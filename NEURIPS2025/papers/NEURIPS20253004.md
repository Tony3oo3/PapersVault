## Problem

Most object-centric RL methods factor state by individual objects while leaving interactions implicit, making it unclear which types and degrees of decomposition structure are necessary and sufficient for efficient, generalizable policy learning. The paper asks whether explicitly modeling object interactions as a first-class component of the world model—rather than treating them as an emergent property of a monolithic latent space—yields meaningful gains in sample efficiency and compositional generalization for long-horizon control.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253004/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB observations that are first projected into pre-trained visual embeddings (DINO-v2 or R3M patch features), then clustered into N object slots via slot attention; actions and a target interaction graph (a sparse N×N binary adjacency matrix) condition the dynamics and policy. Outputs include per-object factored latent states split into static attributes (color, shape, mass) and dynamic variables (position, velocity), a learned time-varying interaction graph, reconstructed and predicted observations, predicted rewards, and—through the hierarchical policy—a sequence of subgoal interaction graphs followed by low-level action sequences (via MPC or PPO) that realize those interactions.

## Method

FIOC-WM learns a two-level factorization directly from pixels: at the object level, slot attention over pre-trained embeddings discovers N object slots, and a VAE maps each slot to a latent state further decomposed into static (c) and dynamic (d) components enforced by a temporal-invariance regularizer and a cross-object contrastive loss; at the interaction level, a GRU-based encoder over pairwise latent states produces a surrogate variable u_t that parameterizes a sparse interaction graph G_t, learned either via differentiable variational masks (Gumbel-Softmax or discrete codebook) or conditional independence testing. The dynamics prior factorizes across objects as p(d_t^i | d_{t-1}, a_{t-1}, G_t), so each object's self-transition is modulated only by its neighbors in G_t through an interaction transition function f_inter. A hierarchical policy is then trained on top: a high-level policy π^h selects a sequence of subgoal interaction graphs (constrained to single-edge changes per step, with a diversity reward to encourage exploration), while a low-level policy π^l (MPC or PPO) uses the learned world model to generate actions that drive the system toward the target interaction states, enabling long-horizon tasks to be decomposed into composable interaction primitives.

## Results

On policy learning (success rate), FIOC achieves 0.79 on i-Gibson and 0.76 on Libero for attribute generalization, outperforming Dreamer-V3 (0.62, 0.59) and TD-MPC2 (0.65, 0.69). For skill composition generalization, FIOC reaches 0.81 on Push&Switch and 0.73 on Franka Kitchen, versus 0.66/0.59 for Dreamer-V3 and 0.65/0.62 for TD-MPC2. On world model quality (LPIPS, lower is better), FIOC achieves 0.007 on Fetch and 0.027 on Libero, the best among all baselines including DINO-WM (0.009, 0.035).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FIOC consistently outperforms Dreamer-V3 across all generalization settings, with the largest gaps on i-Gibson attribute generalization (+0.17) and Franka Kitchen skill generalization (+0.14). Against TD-MPC2, FIOC wins on 6 of 7 settings, with TD-MPC2 only edging ahead on the simpler Push&Switch attribute generalization task (0.95 vs 0.91). EIT is the strongest object-centric baseline, achieving competitive results on Push&Switch (0.92 attr gen, 0.83 comp gen), but FIOC surpasses it on all embodied-AI benchmarks (i-Gibson, Libero, Franka Kitchen) by 0.04–0.16 in success rate. For world model reconstruction (LPIPS), FIOC achieves the best scores on Fetch (0.007) and Libero (0.027), slightly trailing DINO-WM on Kitchen (0.038 vs 0.035). In offline RL (Fig. 5b), FIOC-DINO and FIOC-R3M both outperform DINO-WM on both single-task and generalization settings, confirming the benefit of the two-level factorization and hierarchical policy on top of pre-trained visual features.

## Relation to Prior Work

FIOC-WM sits at the intersection of object-centric world models (building on slot attention and pre-trained visual encoders like DINO-v2 and R3M) and hierarchical RL with interaction-based skills (building on SKILD and Granger causal interaction skill chains). It departs from prior object-centric RL by introducing a two-level factorization—object-level with explicit time-varying interaction graphs, and attribute-level (static vs. dynamic) within each object—and by learning the interaction model jointly with observation and dynamics reconstruction inside a VAE-based world model, rather than inferring interactions post hoc from attention weights or via conditional independence testing on pre-computed states. The hierarchical policy design, where a high-level policy selects interaction graph subgoals and a low-level policy (MPC or PPO) executes them, extends the skill-chain framework of Wang et al. and Chuck et al. into a unified world-model pipeline trained from pixels.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that explicitly modeling sparse, time-varying object interactions within a world model yields measurably better compositional generalization (attribute, object, and skill) than implicit attention-based interaction inference, and that the two-level static/dynamic factorization is a minimal yet sufficient inductive bias for policy learning. Key limitations include reliance on a pre-trained object-centric model for object discovery, generalization only to seen object categories, and validation exclusively on simulated benchmarks (SpritesWorld, Fetch, Franka Kitchen, iGibson, LIBERO) without real-robot experiments. Open questions include whether the interaction-graph formulation scales to scenes with many objects, whether the framework can be extended to unsupervised skill discovery, and how it interacts with emerging robot-learning foundation models.
