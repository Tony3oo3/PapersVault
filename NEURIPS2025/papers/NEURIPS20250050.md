## Problem

Knowledge tracing (KT) benchmarks have been dominated by mathematics, programming, and language-learning datasets, with virtually no large-scale benchmarks for fine-grained visual classification. The few existing image-based KT datasets (e.g., VTK) contain only ~6,750 interactions across 3–5 concepts, making them inadequate for studying how humans acquire expertise over thousands of visually similar categories. CleverBirds addresses this gap by providing a 17M+ interaction benchmark spanning 10,779 bird species and 40,000+ participants, enabling systematic study of visual knowledge acquisition at a scale and concept diversity previously unavailable.

## Input / Output

The model consumes fixed vision-encoder embeddings (DINOv2 ViT-B/14 or ResNet-50, not raw pixels) for the current bird image, an ordered list of four candidate species plus a 'None of the above' option, the ground-truth species label, the participant's full interaction history (or a 50-question window for transformer models), and optional quiz location/time indicators. The primary output is the predicted user response among the five choices (multiple-choice task); a secondary output is a binary correct/incorrect label. A key subtlety is that the model predicts what the *user* will answer, not the true species, and the 'None of the above' option is handled via a special token or by aggregating probability mass outside the visible candidates.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a comprehensive empirical evaluation of diverse model families on the CleverBirds benchmark rather than a single novel architecture. Baselines span classical KT models (DKT, AKT, ATKT, SAKT, DKVMN, simpleKT, KQN), fine-tuned language models (T5-Efficient-TINY seq2seq and TinyBERT multiple-choice classification, both using a custom 11,142-token vocabulary with one token per species), lightweight neural classifiers (one-layer 250-dim MLPs with optional user/species/image context), tree-based and linear models (Random Forest, XGBoost, Logistic Regression), and heuristics (confusion prior, always-correct, average-species). A key finding is that simple feature-engineered classifiers (e.g., Random Forest with user + species context) substantially outperform all deep KT baselines on the binary task (85% macro accuracy vs. ~55%), while DINOv2 image features combined with user and species context in an MLP achieve the best multiple-choice accuracy on incorrectly-answered questions (~25% vs. 20% random). The evaluation reveals that user-specific context is more predictive than species-level context, that binary-trained models beat multiple-choice-trained ones on the binary objective, and that predicting the specific wrong choice a user makes remains a major open challenge, with all models achieving less than 25% accuracy on the incorrect subset.

## Results

On the binary correct/incorrect prediction task, the Random Forest with user and species context (RF U+S) achieves the best performance at 0.848 AP and 84.9% macro accuracy, substantially outperforming all knowledge tracing baselines (AKT, ATKT, simpleKT) which cluster around 0.35–0.37 AP and ~55% macro accuracy. On the multiple-choice task, the MLP with user, species, and DINOv2 image context (MLP U+S+Img DINOv2) achieves 75.1% accuracy on the full dataset and 24.5% on the incorrect-answer subset, compared to 20% for random guessing. Notably, all trained models achieve less than 25% accuracy on the subset of questions participants answered incorrectly, with the best model only marginally outperforming a confusion-prior heuristic that assumes users are always wrong (23.5%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 11+ baselines spanning knowledge tracing models, classical ML, MLPs, and LLMs. On the binary task, RF U+S (0.848 AP) and XG U+S (0.845 AP) decisively outperform all KT baselines (AKT 0.366, ATKT 0.363, simpleKT 0.360, DKT 0.334, DKVMN 0.349), with a gap of roughly 0.48 in AP. Among KT models, performance is remarkably uniform (AP range 0.333–0.366), suggesting they all exploit similar shortcuts on this high-concept-count task. On the multiple-choice task, MLP U+S+Img DINOv2 (75.1% full, 24.5% incorrect) edges out LM Seq2seq (72.9% full) and LM MCC (70.0% full), while on the incorrect subset it dramatically outperforms both language models (24.5% vs. 3.4% and 6.5%). In-context LLM evaluation shows GPT-4.1 Nano matching LM MCC on the full set (71% vs. 70%) but collapsing to 2% on incorrect answers, while DeepSeek-V3 underperforms on the full set (53%) but reaches 13% on the incorrect subset. The key finding is that even the best trained model (24.5% on incorrect) barely beats the confusion-prior heuristic that assumes users are always wrong (23.5%), indicating substantial room for improvement in modeling human knowledge states.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the visual knowledge tracing framework introduced by Kondapaneni et al. (ECCV 2022), which demonstrated the concept on small controlled datasets (3–5 concepts, ~6,750 interactions each) using crowdworkers. Here, the task is scaled up by orders of magnitude using real-world citizen-science data from eBird, yielding 10,779 concepts, 40,000+ participants, and 17.8M interactions with natural learning trajectories. The paper also positions itself against the broader KT literature dominated by math, programming, and language datasets, arguing that fine-grained visual classification is an underrepresented and distinct domain for knowledge tracing. Methodologically, it evaluates a wide range of existing KT architectures (LSTM-based, attention-based, memory-augmented) alongside simpler feature-engineered baselines, rather than proposing a new model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CleverBirds is by far the largest visual knowledge tracing benchmark, offering a concept space roughly 2,000× larger than prior visual KT datasets, which makes it a critical stress test for existing methods. The finding that all deep KT architectures converge to similar (and modest) performance while simple random forests with user+species context outperform them suggests that current KT methods may be exploiting class-imbalance shortcuts rather than modeling genuine knowledge acquisition at scale. Open questions include how to leverage cross-species transfer, how to better predict the structure of incorrect responses (where the best model reaches only ~25% vs. 20% random), and whether the geographic and domain biases (Global North, birds only) limit generalizability to other fine-grained visual domains like medical imaging.
