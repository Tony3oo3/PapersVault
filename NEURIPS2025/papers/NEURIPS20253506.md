## Problem

Fine-tuning large language models in federated learning settings is computationally prohibitive for edge devices, and existing parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods like LoRA and BitFit suffer significant performance degradation under non-IID client data distributions. Additionally, computational heterogeneity across clients creates a straggler effect that constrains overall training progress. The paper addresses how to preserve the robustness of full-parameter fine-tuning while reducing client-side overhead and accommodating heterogeneous device capabilities.

## Input / Output

Each client receives a customized model comprising a block of L_i consecutive fine-tunable layers (selected by the server based on the client's reported computational capacity) and a compressed version of the remaining layers produced via knowledge distillation on a public proxy dataset. Clients upload only their updated fine-tuned layer parameters back to the server, which then performs per-layer weighted averaging to reconstruct the global model. The compression rate r controls how many layers the student model retains for the frozen portion, trading off communication and computation against training fidelity.

## Method

LEFF operates in four stages per communication round: (1) clients report their fine-tuning capacity L_i, and the server samples a block of L_i consecutive layers per client using an importance-based sampling strategy where block importance is computed via first-order Taylor expansion of parameter importance scores (sum of squared gradient-parameter products per layer, normalized by Softmax); (2) the server compresses each client's unselected layers via knowledge distillation using MSE on hidden states and KL divergence on attention matrices, producing a lightweight frozen context; (3) clients perform full-parameter SGD on only their assigned layers while the compressed layers remain frozen; (4) the server aggregates layer-wise using data-size-weighted averaging. Unlike PEFT-based FL methods, LEFF updates full parameters within selected layers, preserving robustness to non-IID data, while the importance-driven sampling concentrates updates on the most performance-critical layers. Theoretical analysis establishes an O(1/√T) convergence rate with an error floor governed by the model approximation error Δ² introduced by layer selection and compression.

## Results

On the GLUE benchmark (DeBERTaV3 Base, α=0.05), LEFF achieves an average score of 63.84, closely approaching FedAvg's 65.88 while substantially outperforming SLoRA (59.70), FedLoRA (54.57), and FedBitFit (53.41). On the E2E NLG task (GPT-2 Medium), LEFF attains an average metric score of 2.2805, marginally below FedAvg (2.2980) but ahead of SLoRA (2.2572), FedBitFit (2.0570), and FedLoRA (1.9788). LEFF's advantage over PEFT baselines is most pronounced on challenging GLUE tasks like CoLA (27.74 vs. 21.82 for SLoRA, 6.31 for FedLoRA) and MNLI (62.75 vs. 53.26 for SLoRA, 41.97 for FedLoRA).

## Baselines & Comparisons

LEFF is compared against four federated fine-tuning baselines: FedAvg (full-parameter averaging), FedBitFit (bias-only updates), FedLoRA (low-rank adaptation), and SLoRA (server-side SVD-enhanced LoRA). On the GLUE benchmark under high heterogeneity (α=0.05), LEFF (63.84 avg) trails FedAvg (65.88) by only ~2 points but surpasses SLoRA (59.70) by 4.1 points, FedLoRA (54.57) by 9.3 points, and FedBitFit (53.41) by 10.4 points. The gap is most dramatic on CoLA, where LEFF scores 27.74 versus 21.82 (SLoRA), 6.31 (FedLoRA), and 4.65 (FedBitFit), indicating that layer-wise full-parameter updates are far more robust to non-IID data than partial-parameter PEFT methods. On E2E NLG, LEFF (2.2805 avg) is within 0.018 of FedAvg (2.2980) while outperforming SLoRA (2.2572) by 0.023, FedBitFit (2.0570) by 0.22, and FedLoRA (1.9788) by 0.30. Overall, LEFF nearly matches full-parameter FedAvg while using only a subset of layers per client, and decisively outperforms all PEFT-based federated methods, particularly under extreme data heterogeneity.

## Relation to Prior Work

LEFF sits at the intersection of parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) and federated learning, but deliberately departs from the PEFT paradigm (LoRA, BitFit, adapters) that freezes most parameters and updates small auxiliary modules. Instead, it performs full-parameter fine-tuning on a dynamically selected subset of layers, drawing on Molchanov et al.'s (2019) first-order Taylor importance scoring for layer selection and extending FedAvg's data-weighted averaging to a per-layer aggregation scheme. The knowledge distillation step for compressing unselected layers borrows from standard distillation but applies it in a novel federated context where the 'student' must serve as a frozen training context for the client's active layers. This combination—importance-driven layer sampling, per-client layer selection based on computational capacity, and distillation-based context compression—distinguishes LEFF from prior federated PEFT methods (FedLoRA, SLoRA, FedBitFit) that all operate on fixed parameter subsets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LEFF addresses a practically critical bottleneck: fine-tuning LLMs on resource-constrained edge devices in federated settings, where both data heterogeneity and computational heterogeneity degrade performance. The theoretical characterization of the efficiency-fidelity trade-off (the approximation error Δ² in the convergence floor) gives practitioners a principled way to tune compression and layer selection. However, the approach shifts computational burden to the server (importance computation, per-client distillation, layer-wise aggregation) and introduces non-trivial server-to-client communication for customized model components, limiting its applicability in bandwidth-constrained or server-resource-limited deployments. Open questions include whether the importance-based sampling generalizes to much larger models (70B+), whether adaptive compression rates per round could reduce the approximation error floor, and how the method performs with truly heterogeneous client hardware rather than simulated capacity differences.
