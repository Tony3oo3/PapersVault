## Problem

Existing image decomposition methods for fluorescence microscopy are trained on a fixed (typically 50/50) intensity ratio of superimposed structures, yet in practice the relative intensity of structures in a superimposed image varies widely depending on sample properties, labeling density, and microscope configuration. This lack of severity cognizance causes performance degradation when the actual mixing ratio deviates from the training ratio, and prevents a single network from simultaneously solving both image unmixing and bleedthrough removal tasks.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single-channel superimposed fluorescence microscopy image (a pixel-wise linear combination c_t = (1-t)c_0 + t·c_1 with unknown t ∈ [0,1]) as input and produces two unmixed channel images (estimates of c_0 and c_1) along with an estimated mixing ratio. At inference, the mixing ratio is estimated per-image by a regressor network and then aggregated (arithmetic mean) across all images from the same acquisition session, while input normalization uses the mean and standard deviation computed directly from the test patches rather than precomputed training statistics.

## Method

scSplit builds on the InDI iterative restoration framework, where the degradation is modeled as a linear mixing process. Three key components distinguish it: (i) a Severity Cognizant Input Normalization (SCIN) module that precomputes expected mean and standard deviation for 100 partitions of t ∈ [0,1] and normalizes each training patch accordingly, ensuring the network sees zero-mean, unit-variance inputs at every mixing ratio and enabling simple per-acquisition normalization at inference; (ii) a regressor network (Reg) that predicts the mixing ratio t from the normalized input, resolving the cyclic dependency between normalization and ratio estimation; and (iii) two generative networks (Gen_0, Gen_1) that take the normalized superimposed image plus the estimated mixing ratio (t for channel 0, 1−t for channel 1) to predict each constituent structure. During training, t is sampled from a distribution p(t) = ½·U[0,1] + ½·δ_0.5 to emphasize balanced mixing, and a small Gaussian perturbation (ε=0.01) is added to the input. One-step inference is used to minimize distortion, and domain knowledge that all images in a session share the same laser/fluorophore settings motivates the aggregation of per-image t estimates.

## Results

scSplit achieves the best PSNR across all five microscopy datasets in the Dominant (bleedthrough) regime, with headline values of 40.9 dB (Hagen et al.), 51.8 dB (HTLIF24), 40.1 dB (BioSR), 44.5 dB (HTT24), and 35.1 dB (PaviaATN). In the Balanced regime, scSplit also leads or is competitive, reaching 45.5 dB on HTLIF24 and 39.1 dB on HTT24. The method's advantage is most pronounced in the Dominant regime, where it outperforms the strongest baseline (MicroSplit or μSplit) by 4–6 dB PSNR, demonstrating that a single severity-cognizant network can handle both image unmixing and bleedthrough removal.

## Baselines & Comparisons

scSplit outperforms all five baselines (U-Net, μSplit variants, denoiSplit, MicroSplit, InDI) across all five datasets in the Dominant (bleedthrough) regime, with the largest margins over MicroSplit (+6.2 dB on Hagen et al., +6.8 dB on HTLIF24) and U-Net (+9.1 dB on Hagen et al.). In the Balanced regime, scSplit leads on HTLIF24 (+1.8 dB over μSplit_L) and HTT24 (+2.5 dB over denoiSplit), while remaining competitive on Hagen et al. and BioSR. In the Weak regime, scSplit's advantage narrows but persists, e.g., +4.3 dB over MicroSplit on Hagen et al. and +4.4 dB over InDI on HTLIF24. The ablation variants scSplit_0.5 (no Reg network) and scSplit_agg (no aggregation) confirm that both the mixing-ratio regressor and the session-level aggregation contribute meaningfully, with the Reg network providing the largest gains in asymmetric (Dominant/Weak) regimes. On real microscope-captured superimposed images (Table 2), scSplit remains competitive with baselines optimized for the balanced regime, and under increased train-test distribution shift (Table 3), scSplit outperforms U-Net and denoiSplit by 3.5–7.3 dB PSNR.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on InDI (Inversion by Direct Iteration), adopting its inductive bias of modeling degradation as a linear mixing process, and extends it to the fluorescence microscopy image unmixing domain. It departs from the prior unmixing line (μSplit, denoiSplit, MicroSplit) in two key ways: (i) it explicitly predicts the mixing ratio via a regressor network rather than assuming a fixed ratio, and (ii) it introduces Severity Cognizant Input Normalization (SCIN) to resolve a t-dependent variance issue in the input distribution that prior methods overlook. The paper also unifies image unmixing and bleedthrough removal as two regimes of the same severity-graded task, a framing not present in earlier work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for fluorescence microscopy workflows where the relative intensity of co-imaged structures is a priori unknown and varies across acquisitions; a single severity-cognizant model eliminates the need to retrain or re-normalize per acquisition. The SCIN normalization insight is a useful correction for any iterative restoration method operating over a continuum of degradation levels. Open questions include extension beyond two channels, handling of noisy acquisitions end-to-end, and whether the linear superposition assumption holds for interference-based modalities. The paper also demonstrates downstream utility via segmentation (FeatureForest), suggesting the unmixing quality translates to biological interpretation.
