## Problem

Differential Transformer achieves strong empirical gains through a dual-group attention subtraction, yet the precise mechanisms behind its success remain unexplained, and its architectural mismatch with standard transformers forces costly training from scratch, precluding use of open pretrained weights. This paper asks: what exactly makes differential attention effective, and can its benefits be injected into existing pretrained LLMs without retraining from scratch?

## Input / Output

DEX operates on a pretrained LLM (Llama-3 or Qwen-2.5, 0.5B–8B) that already computes standard softmax(QK^T)V attention. It reuses the existing attention scores unchanged and applies a lightweight learnable projection f_D to the output value matrix O, producing a modified output O' = O − λ·f_D(O) for a selected subset of heads per layer. Training uses less than 0.01% of the original pretraining corpus (<1B tokens) and updates only W_K, W_V, W_O, W_D, and λ_learn within self-attention while freezing all FFN and other parameters, keeping both training and inference overhead minimal.

## Method

DEX (Differential Extension) is an architectural adaptation that implicitly embeds differential attention into pretrained transformers by targeting the output-value (OV) circuit rather than the query-key (QK) circuit. The core operation subtracts a λ-modulated learnable projection of the attention output from itself (O' = O − λf_D(O)), which the authors show empirically recovers the three key benefits of DIFF Transformer—negative-attention expressivity, reduced inter-head redundancy, and improved optimization dynamics—without duplicating attention heads. Two data-driven head-selection strategies (low-importance and high-entropy) restrict adaptation to underutilized heads, preserving critical pretrained knowledge. A λ-annealing schedule (Eq. 4) gradually introduces the differential mechanism: it starts at zero for stability, ramps up a fixed λ_init to provide a learning signal for W_D, then hands control to a learnable λ_learn for optimal adaptation. This design keeps the method compatible with FlashAttention and GQA, incurs negligible test-time overhead, and consistently outperforms LoRA, PiSSA, GaLore, APOLLO, and full fine-tuning baselines trained on the same data across language modeling, needle-in-a-haystack retrieval, in-context learning, and instruction tuning.

## Results

On 11 language modeling benchmarks, DEX achieves the highest average score across all tested model sizes and families: 64.2% on Llama-3B (+4.0 over base), 55.6% on Llama-1B (+1.1), 62.4% on Qwen-1.5B (+0.2), 54.4% on Qwen-0.5B (+2.6), and 69.6% on Llama-8B (+2.0). DEX's most striking gain is on WSC (Winograd Schema Challenge), where it jumps from 37.5% to 63.5% on Llama-3B and from 41.3% to 61.5% on Qwen-0.5B, attributed to enhanced anaphora resolution via negative relevance modeling. On Needle-in-a-Haystack retrieval, DEX improves average accuracy by 11.4% (66.9% → 78.3%) and increases attention-to-answer ratio from 0.06 to 0.19. All gains are achieved with <0.01% of original pretraining data (<1B tokens) and negligible test-time overhead.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DEX consistently outperforms all five baselines (LoRA, PiSSA, FT, GaLore, APOLLO) on the average of 11 language modeling benchmarks across every model size tested. The margin is largest on Llama-3B, where DEX achieves 64.2% vs. the best baseline PiSSA at 61.9% (+2.3 points), and on Qwen-0.5B where DEX reaches 54.4% vs. FT at 52.1% (+2.3 points). Notably, DEX surpasses full fine-tuning (FT) even in settings where FT outperforms PEFT methods (e.g., Llama-1B: DEX 55.6% vs. FT 54.9%), despite DEX only updating self-attention parameters while FT updates all weights. On the WSC benchmark specifically, DEX's gains are dramatic (e.g., +26 points on Llama-3B, +20 on Qwen-0.5B), while baselines show modest or negative changes. In the smaller-scale comparison (Table 8, Appendix C), DEX applied to a from-scratch Llama model (42.8% AVG) nearly matches a DIFF Transformer trained from scratch (42.9% AVG), confirming that DEX captures the core benefits of differential attention at a fraction of the computational cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Differential Transformer (Ye et al., 2024), which replaced standard softmax attention with a dual-group subtraction mechanism to cancel attention noise. While the original work demonstrated strong empirical gains, it left the mechanistic explanation unclear and required training from scratch, precluding use of open pretrained weights. DEX departs from the explicit QK-circuit subtraction by applying a learnable differential operation to the output value matrix instead, positioning itself as an architectural adaptation strategy (analogous to MHA2MLA) rather than a fine-tuning method. The analysis also connects to the negative-attention literature (Centered Attention, signed-weight attention) and attention redundancy/pruning work, but uniquely identifies negative attention scores—not sparsity—as the primary expressivity mechanism in differential attention.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DEX provides a practical pathway to incorporate differential attention benefits into any pretrained LLM with negligible test-time overhead and less than 0.01% of original pretraining data, which is significant given that DIFF Transformer otherwise demands costly from-scratch training. The mechanistic finding that differential attention's advantage stems from negative attention scores (enabling explicit modeling of irrelevance in the OV circuit) rather than conventional sparsity reframes how the community should think about attention noise cancellation. Open questions include whether DEX scales beyond 8B parameters, whether the implicit OV-matrix adaptation captures all benefits of explicit QK-level differential attention at scale, and whether the approach generalizes to non-text modalities or architectures without standard multi-head attention.
