## Problem

State-of-the-art process reward models (PRMs) systematically overestimate the probability that a partial reasoning trajectory will lead to a correct final answer, particularly when paired with weaker LLMs or evaluated on out-of-distribution problems. This miscalibration renders PRM scores unreliable for applications requiring calibrated probabilities—such as adaptive compute allocation, uncertainty monitoring, and deciding when to abstain—and standard post-hoc calibration techniques (temperature scaling, isotonic regression) fail to correct the error because they cannot capture instance-dependent, context-specific miscalibration patterns.

## Input / Output

The calibrated PRM takes a math problem query and a partial reasoning trajectory prefix (which may be empty, corresponding to the initial question-difficulty estimate) and outputs success probability estimates at three quantile levels (10th, 50th, 90th percentiles) via a modified sigmoid prediction head. These quantile estimates are then consumed by the IAS framework to compute a concrete adaptive sample budget N_IAS = ⌈log(1−C)/log(1−r̂^(β))⌉ for best-of-N, or an adaptive beam width K_IAS / expansion width M_IAS for beam search, where C is a target confidence level (e.g., 0.99) and β selects which quantile to use (e.g., 0.1 for conservative allocation). The calibration dataset itself consists of (question, prefix, empirical success rate) triplets generated via Monte Carlo rollouts with N_MC=8 follow-ups per prefix.

## Method

The paper introduces a calibration-via-fine-tuning pipeline that replaces the PRM's binary softmax prediction head with a multi-output sigmoid head trained via weighted quantile loss (wQL) to predict the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of the success probability distribution. The key design choice is targeting a lower quantile (e.g., 10th percentile) rather than the conditional mean, which guarantees that for at least 90% of instances the estimate is a conservative lower bound—critical for avoiding under-allocation of compute in IAS. Calibration data is collected by generating 8 independent trajectories per question with the target LLM, then for every prefix of every trajectory, running 8 additional Monte Carlo rollouts to estimate the empirical success rate. Fine-tuning uses LoRA (rank 2, applied to Q/V matrices in every 4th decoder layer plus the head) to preserve the original PRM's ranking capability while enabling a single forward pass to yield both calibrated and original scores. The IAS framework then plugs the calibrated lower-quantile estimate into the closed-form sample-complexity bound N_IAS(p,C) = log(1−C)/log(1−p), with a conformal prediction guarantee (Theorem 1) ensuring the achieved success probability is at least C(1−β̃) on exchangeable test data.

## Results

On MATH500 (in-distribution), the quantile-regression calibration reduces Brier score from 0.2414 to 0.0692 for Llama-3.2-1B and from 0.1480 to 0.0828 for R1-Qwen-7B. On the out-of-distribution AIME24-25 benchmark, improvements are even more dramatic: Brier drops from 0.4144 to 0.0694 (R1-Qwen-7B) and from 0.1936 to 0.0029 (Llama-3.2-1B). For instance-adaptive scaling, BoN+IAS with calibrated PRMs achieves near-fixed-N accuracy at substantially lower cost (e.g., Qwen-2.5-7B on MATH500: 0.8368 accuracy at 0.2342 budget ratio vs. 0.8540 at full budget), while beam search IASoM reaches 0.8740 accuracy at 0.3652 budget ratio for R1-Qwen-7B on MATH500.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed quantile-regression calibration consistently outperforms all four post-hoc calibration baselines (temperature scaling, isotonic regression, histogram binning, and adaptive temperature scaling) across all six LLMs and both datasets, with the gap widening substantially on the out-of-distribution AIME24-25 benchmark where baselines like isotonic regression and histogram binning actually degrade performance (e.g., Brier 0.430 vs. 0.0694 for R1-Qwen-7B). For inference-time scaling, BoN+IAS with calibrated PRMs matches or slightly exceeds fixed-N BoN accuracy while reducing the average sample budget to 23–64% of the full N=64 on MATH500 (e.g., Qwen-2.5-7B: 0.8368 vs. 0.8540 accuracy at 0.2342 budget ratio). On AIME24-25, IAS with calibrated PRMs actually improves accuracy over fixed BoN (e.g., Qwen-2.5-7B: 0.1885 vs. 0.1167) because the calibrated model correctly allocates more samples to hard problems. For beam search, IASoM achieves higher accuracy than fixed BS at comparable or lower budget (R1-Qwen-7B on MATH500: 0.8740 vs. 0.8280 at 0.3652 budget ratio), while IASoK is more aggressive in budget reduction (down to 0.3345) with a small accuracy trade-off. Critically, using uncalibrated PRMs with IAS collapses performance (e.g., Llama-3.2-1B on MATH500 drops to 0.2278 accuracy at 0.0162 budget ratio), confirming that calibration is a prerequisite for effective adaptive scaling.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of PRM-based inference-time scaling (best-of-N, beam search) and probability calibration, drawing on quantile regression and conformal prediction for the calibration mechanism. It departs from standard post-hoc calibration (temperature scaling, isotonic regression) by fine-tuning the PRM's prediction head with a weighted quantile loss, leveraging contextual information that uniform rescaling cannot capture. The instance-adaptive scaling (IAS) framework is explicitly positioned as the first instance-adaptive sampling strategy in the LLM inference-time scaling context, borrowing conceptual inspiration from adaptive particle filtering and information-driven planning in robotics, though no prior work has applied such ideas to LLM reasoning budgets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: IAS with calibrated PRMs reduces inference compute by up to ~75% on MATH500 while preserving accuracy, and the calibrated 7B PRM outperforms an uncalibrated 72B PRM, suggesting targeted calibration can substitute for naive model scaling. Limitations include evaluation restricted to mathematical reasoning and two scaling paradigms (BoN, beam search), a small Monte Carlo sample size (N_MC=8) for ground-truth labels, and the conservative quantile choice (β=0.1) that may underutilize compute on genuinely easy problems. Open questions include whether the approach transfers to code generation, agentic tasks, and more complex search algorithms (e.g., MCTS), and whether the conformal guarantee holds under the distributional shifts encountered in production settings.
