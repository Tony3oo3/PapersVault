## Problem

Existing sequential tests for continuous A/B experiment monitoring either restrict the number of interim analyses (discrete methods) or suffer from substantially lower statistical power than a standard t-test (continuous methods like mSPRT, GAVI, and e-process-based approaches). This paper addresses the gap by proposing a continuous sequential test that supports unlimited interim checks while achieving both accurate Type-I error control and power comparable to the non-sequential t-test.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a stream of event-level outcomes Y_i with binary group assignment indicators W_i, a pre-specified maximum event horizon N, and a pre-experiment estimate of the scaled variance V_N = var(S_N)/N (ideally from a clustered-robust estimator to handle within-user serial correlation). It produces a single fixed alerting boundary b* = z_{1-α/2}·√(N·V_N) computed once before monitoring begins, and a binary significance flag raised whenever the running sum S_n crosses b*. Unlike GST methods that recompute a time-varying threshold at each look, YEAST's boundary is constant throughout monitoring, making the per-observation check a simple comparison.

## Method

The core technical contribution is a new derivation technique that inverts the generalized Lévy inequality: it bounds the probability that the running sum S_n exceeds a threshold at any time n ≤ N by twice the probability of exceeding it at the final time N, then approximates the latter via the Central Limit Theorem to set the threshold at z_{1-α/2}·√(N·V_N). This yields a constant (time-invariant) alerting boundary, in contrast to GST's alpha-spending boundaries or mSPRT's mixture-likelihood-ratio stopping rules. The method operates at the event level (not subject level), explicitly allowing dependence among events from the same user, and requires a clustered-robust variance estimator to maintain valid Type-I error control. The theoretical justification (Theorems 1 and 2) establishes asymptotic FDR control at level α and asymptotic power of 1 under a fixed positive mean effect, with the key lemma proving conditional symmetry of the residual sum S_N − S_k given the past.

## Results

On the semi-synthetic Online Retail experiment (100,000 replications, 5% nominal significance), YEAST with a robust clustered variance estimator achieves a false detection rate of 4.4% ± 0.2%, the only method to stay within one percentage point of the nominal level. All other methods either inflate FDR (mSPRT: 12.7–15.9%, GAVI: 14.8–16.0%, LanDeMetsOBF: 13.3%, SeqC2ST-QDA: 10.2%) or are overly conservative (1.7–2.9%). For power at a 5% relative effect size, YEAST detects 11.6% of replications, matching the non-sequential Student's t-test (11.5%), while mSPRT variants detect only 0.2–0.6%, GAVI variants 0.3–0.5%, and LanDeMetsOBF 7.6%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

YEAST outperforms all four baseline families on the combined criterion of FDR control and power. Against mSPRT (Robbins 1970), YEAST achieves 4.4% FDR versus 1.7–2.8% (conservative) with robust variance, but critically matches the non-sequential t-test power (11.6% vs. 11.5% at 5% effect) while mSPRT detects only 0.2–0.6%. Against GAVI (Howard et al. 2021), the same pattern holds: GAVI is conservative on FDR (1.9–2.9%) but severely underpowered (0.3–0.5% detection at 5% effect vs. YEAST's 11.6%). Against LanDeMetsOBF (Lan & DeMets 1983), YEAST wins on FDR accuracy (4.4% vs. 2.6%) and power (11.6% vs. 7.6% at 5% effect), though LanDeMetsOBF is the closest competitor. Against SeqC2ST-QDA (Podkopaev & Ramdas 2023), YEAST dominates on both metrics: SeqC2ST-QDA inflates FDR to 10.2% and achieves only 9.1% power at 5% effect. The key finding is that YEAST is the only method that simultaneously controls FDR at the nominal level and retains full statistical power relative to the non-sequential benchmark.

## Relation to Prior Work

YEAST departs from the dominant martingale-based lineage (SPRT, mSPRT, GAVI, e-processes) and group sequential tests (GST with alpha-spending) by instead inverting a classical maximal inequality — the generalized Lévy inequality — combined with a CLT approximation to derive a fixed alerting boundary. Conceptually, it generalizes Evan Miller's simple sequential A/B test [22], which is restricted to count-type metrics, to arbitrary real-valued metrics (e.g., revenue), though the authors stress this generalization is non-trivial because the underlying theory of [22] does not extend. The method's constant boundary (normalized by the full-horizon variance) contrasts sharply with GST's time-varying, information-normalized thresholds and with martingale methods' data-dependent stopping rules.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical appeal is a computationally trivial sequential monitor (one precomputed threshold, no numerical integration) that empirically matches the power of a non-sequential t-test while all competing continuous-monitoring methods are severely underpowered — a gap that could matter for early detection of harmful treatments in production. The key open limitation is that the null hypothesis is stronger than the standard 'zero average treatment effect': it also requires no change in event frequency or outcome variance, so the test can flag significance when only the data-generation process shifts (e.g., counterbalancing changes in order frequency and order value). The authors identify studying this 'undesirable sensitivity' and developing mitigation strategies as a major direction for future work, and the single semi-synthetic validation on one retail dataset leaves the method's robustness across domains and metric types untested.
