## Problem

Large language models produce homogenized outputs on open-ended tasks, raising concerns about long-term homogenization of human thought, yet scalable evaluation methods for LM output diversity remain limited to narrow synthetic tasks (random number or name generation) or repeated sampling from a single model. No prior work systematically measures cross-model output convergence on real-world open-ended queries, nor does it examine whether reward models and LM judges are calibrated to the pluralistic human preferences that arise when multiple responses are equally valid.

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes 26,070 real-world English open-ended queries (15–200 characters, non-toxic, mined from WildChat and filtered by GPT-4o for meaningful multi-answer potential) and produces, for each of 25+ models, 50 sampled responses per query under fixed decoding parameters (top-p=0.9, temperature=1.0). For the calibration study, 50 prompts each receive 15 model responses rated on a 1–5 absolute scale and 10 response pairs rated for pairwise preference, with 25 independent human annotators per example yielding 31,250 total labels. Similarity is quantified via cosine distance over OpenAI text-embedding-3-small sentence embeddings, and human disagreement is captured through Shannon entropy over the 25-annotator label distributions.

## Method

The paper introduces INFINITY-CHAT, constructed by filtering 37,426 WildChat GPT-4 queries through GPT-4o classification (meaningful information, non-greeting, multiple valid responses) and organizing them into a 6-category / 17-subcategory taxonomy built semi-automatically from ~100 manually seeded labels and scaled with GPT-4o. Diversity is measured on two axes: intra-model repetition (average pairwise embedding similarity within each model's 50-response pool per query, reported as percentage of queries exceeding similarity thresholds) and inter-model homogeneity (average cross-model pairwise similarity, verbatim phrase overlap detection, and counting unique source models among the top-N most similar responses per query). A min-p decoding baseline (top-p=1.0, min-p=0.1, temperature=2.0) is evaluated to test whether diversity-oriented sampling mitigates mode collapse. For the calibration analysis, the authors partition the 31,250 human annotations into similar-quality subsets (via Tukey's fences on absolute ratings or annotator-similarity ranking for pairwise) and high-disagreement subsets (via Shannon entropy or percentage disagreement), then compute Spearman/Pearson correlations between LM perplexity, reward model scalar outputs, and LM judge ratings against the 25-annotator human averages on each subset.

## Results

Under standard top-p sampling (p=0.9, temp=1.0), 79% of open-ended queries yield response pools with average pairwise sentence-embedding similarity exceeding 0.8, indicating severe intra-model mode collapse. Inter-model homogeneity is equally pronounced: average pairwise similarity between responses from different models ranges from 71% to 82%, with the top-50 most similar responses to a query drawing from ~8 unique models on average. Even under diversity-oriented min-p decoding (top-p=1.0, min-p=0.1, temp=2.0), 81% of response pairs still exceed 0.7 similarity and 61.2% exceed 0.8. In the calibration study, Spearman correlations between LM/reward-model/judge scores and human ratings drop substantially on similar-quality and high-annotator-disagreement subsets compared to the full set.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares two decoding strategies as baselines for diversity: standard top-p sampling (p=0.9, temp=1.0) and min-p decoding (top-p=1.0, min-p=0.1, temp=2.0). Min-p decoding reduces the most extreme repetition (fewer pairs above 0.9 similarity) but fails to resolve mode collapse: 81% of response pairs still exceed 0.7 similarity and 61.2% exceed 0.8, compared to 79% above 0.8 under standard sampling. The authors note min-p is not widely adopted because it is better suited for creative tasks and less effective for closed-ended ones, and that placing the diversity burden on user-chosen decoding strategies is insufficient. In the calibration comparison, all three model families (LMs via perplexity, reward models via scalar output, and LM judges via rubric prompting) show significantly lower Spearman correlation with human ratings on similar-quality subsets and high-disagreement subsets than on the full set, indicating none of the current scoring mechanisms reliably distinguish between equally valid open-ended responses.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by repurposing standard tools (sentence embeddings, PCA, Shannon entropy) into a large-scale diagnostic framework for a problem previously studied only at small scale or on synthetic tasks (random number/name generation). It extends the mode-collapse literature from intra-model repetition to inter-model homogeneity across 70+ LMs, and extends pluralistic alignment research from predefined diversity axes (demographics, personality, culture) to the open-endedness dimension itself. The INFINITY-CHAT dataset and its 6×17 taxonomy of real-world open-ended queries mined from WildChat represent a new resource that prior creativity benchmarks (NoveltyBench, LiveIdeaBench, AUT-based evaluations) did not provide.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that distinct model families converge on near-identical outputs (verbatim phrase overlaps, the 'time is a river/weaver' dichotomy across all 25 models) challenges the common assumption that model ensembles or swarms yield genuine diversity, with direct implications for synthetic data distillation and multi-agent alignment pipelines. The miscalibration of reward models and LM judges on similar-quality or high-disagreement responses exposes a gap in current RLHF tooling that could systematically suppress pluralistic outputs during training. Key open questions include the causal source of inter-model convergence (shared pretraining data vs. alignment vs. memorization), whether the effect persists in multilingual settings, and whether training-level interventions can restore diversity without sacrificing quality.
