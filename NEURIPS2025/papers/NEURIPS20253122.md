## Problem

LLM-as-a-judge methods predominantly rely on the final-layer output (the most probable score token) or on expensive chain-of-thought prompting and full fine-tuning, overlooking the rich cross-layer internal representations that prior work shows encode stronger semantic and task-relevant signals. This paper asks whether aggregating score-token logits across all transformer layers can yield judge scores more aligned with human annotations, without modifying the LLM backbone or its inference process.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253122/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a standard point-wise evaluation prompt (task description, user instruction, response to evaluate, and scoring criteria) and, at the position where the score token would be generated, extracts hidden states from every layer (embedding through the final decoder layer). It produces a continuous fine-grained judge score computed as the expected value of a softmax distribution over the discrete candidate score set (e.g., 1–5), rather than a single argmax token. The LLM's next-token prediction and all other outputs remain unchanged, making the score a purely post-hoc readout.

## Method

LAGER computes score-token logits at every layer by projecting each layer's hidden state through the shared output unembedding matrix, then forms a weighted sum of these per-layer logits using L+1 scalar weights (one per layer plus the embedding layer). A softmax over the aggregated logits yields a probability distribution over the candidate score set, and the final score is the expectation of this distribution. The L+1 weights are the only learnable parameters, tuned on a small validation set with a combined cross-entropy and MAE loss while the entire LLM backbone stays frozen; an untuned variant uses uniform averaging. Ablations confirm that logit-level aggregation (vs. probability-level), expectation scoring (vs. argmax), and lightweight tuning each contribute independently, and that middle-to-upper layers carry the strongest human-aligned signal while the topmost layer actually degrades performance due to its focus on next-token prediction.

## Results

On the three alignment benchmarks (Flask, HelpSteer, BiGGen Bench), LAGER (w. tuning) achieves the best average Spearman correlation of 0.570 with LLAMA-3.3-70B-Instruct, 0.540 with Mistral-Small-24B-Instruct, and 0.531 with Qwen-2.5-14B-Instruct, improving up to 7.5% over the best non-training baseline (E-Score). In the instruction data selection task on AlpacaEval-2.0, selecting the top-10% by LAGER (w. tuning) yields a 12.65 length-controlled win rate, surpassing the best baseline (Longest responses, 11.82) and the purpose-built SuperFiltering method (10.69). On EQ-Bench (emotional understanding), LAGER improves Spearman correlation by an average of 9 points over VScore with tuning, and on SelfAware it boosts F1 by +19.7% over SimCSE on LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LAGER (w. tuning) consistently outperforms all non-training baselines (GPTScore, VScore, E-Score) across all six open-source backbones on all three benchmarks, with an average improvement of 4.5% over E-Score (the strongest non-training baseline) and larger margins over VScore. GPTScore performs poorly (often near 0 or negative Spearman) because it relies on generation probabilities that ignore semantic quality. Compared to the fine-tuned Prometheus2-7B (avg 0.431), LAGER enables smaller models like InternLM3-8B (0.501) and LLaMA3.1-8B (0.484) to surpass it despite weaker vanilla scores. Against GPT-4o-mini via API (avg 0.547 for E-Score), LAGER with Qwen-2.5-14B (0.531) and Mistral-Small-24B (0.540) achieves comparable performance, while LLAMA-3.3-70B (0.570) clearly exceeds it. Notably, LAGER in direct (no-reasoning) mode outperforms its own reasoning-mode variant, and also outperforms reasoning-based VScore and E-Score, suggesting that chain-of-thought reasoning can cause overconfidence and degrade judgment accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

LAGER sits at the intersection of two research threads: the LLM-as-a-Judge evaluation paradigm (G-Eval, Prometheus, TIGERScore) and the layer-wise ensemble / multi-view representation literature (Wang et al., 2020; Roh et al., 2024). It departs from prompt-based judges by eliminating the need for chain-of-thought reasoning, and from fine-tuning-based judges (Prometheus, TIGERScore) by keeping the LLM backbone entirely frozen, training only L+1 scalar layer weights. The core conceptual move is repurposing the observation that middle-to-upper transformer layers encode task-relevant semantics—previously studied in NMT and probing contexts—into a scoring mechanism that aggregates cross-layer score-token logits before applying softmax, rather than relying on the final layer's argmax output as all prior LLM-as-a-Judge methods do.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The plug-and-play nature (frozen backbone, ~L+1 tunable parameters, no change to next-token prediction) makes LAGER attractive for large-scale evaluation pipelines where retraining a judge model per domain is impractical. The finding that reasoning degrades LAGER's performance while helping vanilla scores challenges the near-universal assumption in the LLM-as-a-Judge literature that chain-of-thought always improves judgment quality. A key limitation is the requirement for internal hidden-state access, restricting applicability to open-source models and creating a gap with API-only deployments. Open questions include whether the learned layer weights transfer to pairwise or listwise evaluation settings, and whether the approach extends to non-integer or continuous scoring scales beyond the discrete 1–5 range tested here.
