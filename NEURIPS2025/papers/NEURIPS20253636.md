## Problem

Prior recursive-architecture work (MobileLLM's repeat-all-over, latent recurrent thinking) demonstrated performance gains over vanilla transformers but compared models trained on similar token counts without matching total training FLOPs, leaving it unclear whether gains stem from recursion itself or simply from increased compute. This paper asks: under a strict compute- and parameter-matched regime, which parameter-sharing architecture is actually best, and can recursion provide a genuine, complementary axis for scaling inference time?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253636/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For language modeling, the model consumes text sequences of 1,024 tokens (C4/SlimPajama, 32K-vocab C4 tokenizer) and produces next-token logits; for multimodal pretraining, it takes 256×256 images (16×16 patches) paired with up to 64-token text and produces contrastive embeddings via a sigmoid loss. The distinctive I/O property is that at inference the user can choose the recursion depth r (e.g., 1×, 1.5×, 2× baseline inference cost), so the same trained model yields a continuum of compute-accuracy tradeoffs without retraining.

## Method

RINS partitions a decoder-only transformer depthwise into two equal blocks A and B, then recursively applies A to its own output r times before passing to B (signature A^r B, degree 1). The paper systematically sweeps 59+ architectures across a taxonomy of signatures and degrees under matched training FLOPs, identifying A^r B as the clear winner over RAO (ABAB), latent recurrent thinking (AB^r C), and 50+ other variants. A stochastic variant samples the number of recursion rounds per training step from a binomial distribution (skip probability p_s), and adding r lightweight linear adapters (<1% extra parameters) selected by the active recursion count yields a no-regret strategy: the model improves even when recursion is disabled at test time. KV-cache sharing across recursive A-calls further reduces memory without eliminating the advantage.

## Results

On SigLIP-B/16 (compute-matched), RINS (A²B) improves 0-shot ImageNet accuracy from 73.4% (baseline) to 74.1%, and CIFAR-100 from 68.9% to 72.2%, outperforming both the long-sequence baseline and RAO (ABAB). In the overtrained regime, SigLIP-RINS-B/16 (A³B) achieves 79.6% ImageNet and 80.7% CIFAR-100, surpassing the standard SigLIP-B/16 by +2.3% and +10.4% respectively. In language modeling, RINS consistently outperforms all 55+ other parameter-sharing strategies on a compute-matched basis, with the performance gap widening as training compute increases. RINS also improves both the data scaling exponent and the asymptotic performance limit relative to the non-recursive baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against MobileLLM's RAO strategy (Liu et al., 2024), RINS wins on every benchmark: in language modeling (Figure 2), RINS (A²B or A³B) achieves lower perplexity than RAO (ABAB) at the same training FLOPs, and in multimodal (Table 3), RINS scores 74.1% vs. 72.7% on ImageNet and 72.2% vs. 65.3% on CIFAR-100. Against latent recurrent thinking (Geiping et al., 2025, signature AB^r C), RINS outperforms it in the 59-model sweep, with the paper noting that RAO's apparent gains over the baseline can be matched simply by training the baseline longer to consume equivalent compute, whereas RINS's advantage persists under strict compute matching. Against the standard SigLIP-B/16 checkpoint (Zhai et al., 2023), the overtrained SigLIP-RINS-B/16 (A³B, 40B examples) improves 0-shot ImageNet from 77.3% to 79.6% and CIFAR-100 from 70.3% to 80.7%, with stochastic RINS (p_s=1/4) pushing CIFAR-100 further to 81.7%. The paper also shows that simply increasing sequence length (Long-Seq baseline) is inferior to RINS, confirming that the gain comes from recursive depth rather than longer context.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the recursive architecture line initiated by MobileLLM's repeat-all-over (RAO) strategy (Liu et al., 2024) and latent recurrent thinking (Geiping et al., 2025), but departs critically by controlling for training compute FLOPs rather than token count, revealing that RAO's reported gains are largely a compute artifact for models above 1B parameters. Motivated by the fractal geometry of language (Alabdulmohsin et al., 2024a), the authors systematically sweep 59+ parameter-sharing signatures and degrees to identify RINS (A^r B) as the optimal recursive form, and further draw on stochastic depth (Huang et al., 2016) to introduce a stochastic variant with a no-regret deployment property. Unlike earlier parameter-sharing approaches such as Universal Transformers and ALBERT, which had smaller scaling exponents than vanilla transformers, RINS improves both the scaling exponent and the asymptotic performance limit.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The no-regret property—where stochastic RINS with lightweight linear adapters (<1% extra parameters) improves language modeling even when recursion is not applied at inference—makes RINS a potentially drop-in component of LLM pretraining that improves base model quality without changing deployment. The compute-matched analysis corrects the interpretation of prior recursive architecture results and provides a clearer picture of where recursion genuinely helps versus where it merely consumes more FLOPs. The negative result in pure vision (no benefit for ViT image classification) supports the hypothesis that RINS's advantage is tied to language's self-similar structure, though a formal theoretical account remains open. Key open questions include whether the gains persist at much larger scales (tens of billions of parameters), whether the optimal recursion depth r follows a predictable scaling law with model size, and whether the fractal-language explanation can be made rigorous.
