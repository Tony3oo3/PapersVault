## Problem

Traditional neural models reduce neurons to linear threshold units, yet real neurons possess complex dendritic morphologies with active nonlinear integration that enable computation beyond linear separability. While specific tasks like XOR have been shown solvable by single neurons, the broader computational limits across a spectrum of function complexities—and how distinct dendritic topologies shape those limits—remain unquantified. This paper asks what upper bounds dendritic morphology imposes on the Boolean functions a single neuron can realize, and how basal versus apical architectures trade off robustness against flexibility.

## Input / Output

Each architecture receives binary input vectors of dimension N_dim (3–8 bits), with the full enumeration of 2^N_dim input rows presented simultaneously during training, and the target is the Y-column of a Boolean function truth table. Learning is quantified by three metrics: mean Hamming distance between binarized output and target, function realizability probability (fraction of random-initialization trials achieving HD≈0), and best-case realizability (fraction of functions learned in at least one trial). All three architectures—basal, apical, and linear—are matched in total trainable parameters and input dimensionality, differing only in structural organization and the presence of intermediate dendritic nonlinearities.

## Method

The paper models single neurons as feedforward networks of N_branch=7 nonlinear dendritic compartments (sigmoidal rectification units with branch-specific gain and threshold), arranged in two morphological extremes: a basal/parallel topology where all compartments connect directly to the soma, and an apical/hierarchical topology organized as a deep, narrow tree, with a linear baseline that sums all branch inputs before a single somatic nonlinearity. Realizability is tested across three Boolean function families—random (typical), fixed-entropy, and fixed-sensitivity—spanning input dimensions 3–8, with 100–500 functions per class and 10 random initializations per function. Training uses both Adam gradient descent (BCE loss) and the DIRECT global optimizer to confirm that realizability trends are not optimization artifacts. The central finding is a phase transition in function realizability: the linear model collapses at N_dim≈4, while both nonlinear architectures sustain computation until N_dim≈7, with basal architectures favoring low-sensitivity functions and high initialization robustness, and apical architectures showing superior retraining flexibility through reusable distal-branch features. The framework is further extended to transformer attention-head layouts, revealing analogous robustness-versus-flexibility trade-offs between broad (basal-like) and hierarchical (apical-like) configurations.

## Results

With N_branch = 7 nonlinear dendritic compartments, both basal-like and apical-like architectures maintain high Boolean function realizability up to N_dim ≈ 7, whereas the linear (soma-only) baseline collapses at N_dim ≈ 4, demonstrating a sharp phase transition in learnability. The critical input dimensionality is best predicted by the total size of the dendritic arbor rather than its specific topology. For retraining on N_dim = 5 functions, apical architectures converge faster when pre-trained on low-entropy functions (j = 1–10), while basal architectures show higher initial realizability probability but must relearn each function independently. The phase transition is confirmed to be robust to optimization method, with the global DIRECT optimizer reproducing the same realizability collapse as gradient-based training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three dendritic architectures—basal-like (shallow/broad), apical-like (deep/narrow), and a linear baseline (soma-only nonlinearity)—on identical sets of 100–500 Boolean functions across N_dim = 3–8 with 10 random initializations each. The linear baseline is decisively outperformed: it fails to realize most random functions already at N_dim = 4, while both nonlinear architectures sustain high realizability until N_dim ≈ 7, a roughly 3-dimensional extension of the learnable regime. Between the two nonlinear architectures, basal-like models achieve higher per-function learning probability (P_func) in low dimensions and for low-sensitivity functions, while apical-like models show greater variability but superior retraining speed when initialized from pre-trained weights, particularly for low-entropy source functions. Fixing distal branch weights (b = 3–6) minimally affects apical retraining but markedly slows basal retraining, confirming that apical cells reuse distal branches as general-purpose feature extractors. No external method baselines are compared; the comparison is entirely internal to the morphological design space.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on single-neuron computational capacity initiated by Poirazi and Mel (2001) on dendritic memory capacity and Cazé et al. (2013) on passive dendritic computation of nonlinearly separable functions. Where prior studies demonstrated that specific nonlinear tasks (XOR, feature binding) are solvable by single neurons, this work introduces a systematic Boolean-function framework that scales task difficulty with input dimensionality, revealing a phase transition in realizability. The comparison of two canonical morphologies (shallow/basal vs. deep/apical) with matched compartment counts departs from prior work that typically treated dendritic structure as a fixed given rather than a variable determining computational strategy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that morphology alone—holding compartment count constant—imposes a critical dimensionality threshold beyond which random Boolean functions become unrealizable provides a concrete, quantifiable bound on single-neuron computation that was previously only qualitatively discussed. The complementary robustness/flexibility trade-off between basal-like and apical-like architectures suggests a principled division of computational labor across cell types, with direct implications for how cortical circuits balance stability and adaptability. Key limitations include the phenomenological (non-derived) nature of the phase transition, the absence of spiking dynamics, recurrence, and inhibition, and the reliance on simplified sigmoid nonlinearities; experimental validation through dendritic imaging or targeted perturbations remains essential. Open questions include formal circuit-complexity characterization of the transition, extension to ensembles of morphologically diverse neurons, and whether the transformer-architecture analogues (briefly explored in supplementary material) yield practically useful inductive biases.
