## Problem

Behavioral cloning for multi-task generalization typically assumes a fully-informed expert demonstrates near-optimal, task-specific behavior, yet it remains unexplored whether the expert's own behavior affects how well the cloned policy generalizes to unseen tasks. This paper asks whether deliberately hiding task-relevant information from the demonstrator (a 'blindfold') can induce more exploratory, less task-specific behavior that transfers better, and provides the first theoretical bound linking the demonstrator's available information to multi-task generalization error.

## Input / Output

For Procgen maze/heist, the policy consumes 64×64 RGB top-down images and outputs discrete navigation actions (4-way for maze, 8-way for heist). For robotic peg insertion, it consumes RGB images from two wrist-mounted RealSense D405 cameras concatenated with 6-DoF proprioceptive state (pose, force, torque, velocity) and outputs a 6-DoF Gaussian end-effector action. Critically, the logged trajectories always contain full unmasked observations regardless of whether the expert was blindfolded; the mask affects only what the human demonstrator sees during data collection, not what the cloning network trains on.

## Method

The core mechanism is an information bottleneck applied exclusively to the human demonstrator's view during data collection: for Procgen, the maze layout is occluded to reveal only the agent's immediate surroundings; for peg insertion, SAM2 segments and masks the target hole shape from wrist-camera images. This forces the expert to adopt history-dependent exploratory strategies (confirmed by longer trajectories, higher state-visit entropy, and greater map coverage) rather than direct goal-seeking paths. The cloned policy uses a ResNet encoder plus a single GRU layer to capture this non-Markovian behavior, trained via negative log-likelihood on the full-observation trajectories. Theoretically, the paper proves the generalization gap scales as √(I_{T;Z}/m), where I_{T;Z} is the mutual information between the task and the expert's internal representation and m is the number of demonstrated tasks, showing that reducing I via the blindfold lowers the bound without increasing the expert suboptimality term E_gen for sparse-reward tasks.

## Results

On Procgen maze, the blindfolded-expert policy π_BF-BC achieves a test score of 6.37±0.47, substantially outperforming the standard BC policy π_BC (3.35±0.29) and the extended-data BC policy π_BC-ext (3.65±0.3), as well as the 1M-step PPO expert dataset from [27] (4.46±0.16). On robotic peg insertion, cloning blindfolded experts yields higher success rates than standard BC across all 10 peg shapes for both k=2 and k=5 training shapes, with the gap widening as fewer shapes are used for training. In the noise-blindfold ablation (Table 2), the SAM2 mask achieves 96–100% success on both train and test shapes, compared to 71%/67% train and 20%/17% test for the unmasked baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary external baseline is the 1M-step PPO expert dataset from Mediratta et al. [27], which trains a BC policy on 15,385 trajectories across 200 seeds. The proposed π_BF-BC (6.37±0.47) outperforms this baseline (4.46±0.16) by roughly 43% in test score, despite using an order of magnitude fewer trajectories (2,000 vs. 15,385) and half the training seeds (100 vs. 200). Internally, the paper also compares against two ablations: standard BC on the same number of trajectories (π_BC, 3.35±0.29) and standard BC with doubled trajectories to match total environment steps (π_BC-ext, 3.65±0.30). Both ablations overfit on test levels, while π_BF-BC improves gracefully with training. On the robotic peg insertion task, no external baseline is cited; the comparison is between π_BC and π_BF-BC, where the blindfolded variant consistently achieves higher success rates across all 10 shapes, with the advantage most pronounced when only 2 training shapes are available.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the theoretical framework of behavioral cloning generalization by combining the in-task sample complexity result of Foster et al. [13] with the information bottleneck generalization bound of Kawaguchi et al. [20], yielding a novel cross-task generalization term that scales with √(I_{Z;T}/m). Conceptually, it departs from prior BC work that focuses on algorithm design, policy architecture, or data augmentation for generalization, instead identifying the demonstrator's information access as a previously unexplored lever. The idea connects to Zisselman et al. [61], who showed exploratory behavior generalizes better in zero-shot RL, but here the principle is transplanted to imitation learning via an information bottleneck applied to the expert rather than the learned policy (contrasting with [3], which applies the bottleneck to the cloned policy's representation).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result provides a principled, algorithm-agnostic prescription for improving multi-task BC generalization: reduce the task-specific information available to the demonstrator, which induces exploratory behavior that transfers better to unseen tasks. This has direct implications for how demonstration data is collected for foundation-model-scale robotic learning, where the number of demonstrated tasks is the bottleneck. A key open question is how to choose the optimal blindfold strength—too little obstruction fails to elicit exploration, while too much degrades the expert's ability to solve the task (as shown by the U-shaped noise experiment). The domain-specific nature of the blindfold design (masking hole shape vs. occluding maze layout) limits immediate broad applicability, though the noise experiment suggests even generic perturbations can help.
