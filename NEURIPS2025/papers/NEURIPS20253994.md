## Problem

Existing chemical reaction datasets for ML benchmarking focus on discrete variables (ligand, base, additives) at fixed temperatures and residence times, making them unsuitable for modeling the continuous, dynamic process conditions required in practical manufacturing. No ML-ready dataset existed that captured dense, time-resolved measurements across continuous solvent mixture, temperature, and residence time spaces, leaving a critical gap for few-shot learning and active learning in solvent selection.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253994/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Inputs are solvent featurizations (Spange descriptors, ACS PCA, fragprints, DRFP, RXNFP, or ChemBERTa embeddings), temperature, residence time, and the binary solvent mixture ratio, with the mixture creating a pseudo-continuous solvent space between pure solvent pairs. Outputs are three yield values (starting material, Product 2, Product 3) for the catechol reaction or two (SM, Product) for the ethyl reaction. The data is inherently time-series: residence time ramps produce sequential measurements where each plug in the flow experiences a different reaction time, and HPLC sampling every two minutes yields dense kinetic traces rather than single endpoint measurements.

## Method

The paper introduces the first ML-ready transient flow dataset (1227 points across 24 solvents) collected via continuous variation of flow rate, temperature, and solvent pump ratios, with active learning (entropy criterion on a GP) guiding which solvent ramps to execute. For benchmarking, the core models are Gaussian processes with RBF kernels over featurized solvent space, extended with a decomposed kernel (additive solvent/non-solvent components inspired by Ru et al.), a multitask GP capturing inter-output correlations, and input warping via a learned Kumaraswamy CDF to handle non-stationarity in residence time and solvent mixing. The key finding is that Spange empirical polarity descriptors substantially outperform all other featurizations (ACS PCA, fragprints, DRFP, LLM embeddings), while standard models struggle with the heteroskedastic and non-stationary nature of the data. Transfer learning from a related ethyl dataset (283 additional points) improves GP performance, and the dataset supports Bayesian optimization and multi-objective Pareto-frontier benchmarks for experiment design.

## Results

On the Allyl Substituted Catechol dataset (1227 data points), the best regression performance is achieved by MLP with Spange descriptors (MSE 0.010 on full data, 0.010 on single solvents), closely followed by GP with Spange descriptors (MSE 0.011/0.011, NLPD −5.663/−5.793). The baseline GP (ignoring solvent identity) achieves MSE 0.011/0.014, showing that solvent featurization is critical. LLM-based embeddings (RXNFP, ChemBERTa) perform substantially worse (MSE 0.105–0.153 full, 0.055–0.074 single), and ODE-based models (NODE, EODE, LODE) achieve MSE 0.049–0.055 on single solvents, roughly 5× worse than GP/MLP with Spange.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GP and MLP with Spange descriptors clearly outperform all other featurizations and model families on this dataset. LLM-based embeddings (RXNFP [Schwaller et al.] and ChemBERTa [Chithrananda et al.]) are the weakest performers, with MSE 10–15× higher than GP/MLP+Spange, confirming that pretrained molecular language models do not transfer well to solvent-specific yield prediction. ODE-based models (NODE [Chen et al.], EODE/LODE [Rubanova et al.]) achieve MSE 0.049–0.055 on single solvents, roughly 5× worse than GP/MLP with Spange (0.010–0.011), suggesting that explicit kinetic modeling is insufficient without proper solvent encoding. Neural Processes [Garnelo et al.] (Table 7) perform comparably to LLMs (MSE 0.089–0.153), while GraphGP with shortest-path kernels [Borgwardt et al.] achieves MSE 0.046 on single solvents—competitive but still ~4.5× worse than GP+Spange. In the multi-objective Bayesian optimization benchmark, Thompson Sampling with random scalarizations [Paria et al.] substantially outperforms random sampling across all Pareto metrics (e.g., IGD 0.072 vs. 0.092 at 100 iterations), demonstrating the value of structured exploration for solvent-condition optimization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the yield-prediction line of work (Schwaller et al., Perera et al., Ahneman et al.) by replacing discrete reaction variables with continuous process conditions—temperature, residence time, and solvent mixture ratio—collected via transient flow chemistry. Methodologically, it draws on GP extensions from Ru et al. (decomposed kernels), Snoek et al. (input warping via Kumaraswamy CDF), and Bonilla et al. (multitask kernels), while benchmarking against LLM-based embeddings (RXNFP, ChemBERTa) and neural ODE approaches. The key conceptual departure is treating solvent composition as a pseudo-continuous variable through binary mixtures, rather than the one-hot or discrete encoding used in prior datasets, and providing the first ML-ready time-series flow chemistry dataset.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dataset addresses a genuine gap: prior yield-prediction benchmarks lack the continuous kinetic conditions needed for practical process scale-up, and solvent selection remains one of the hardest problems in sustainable manufacturing. The finding that a baseline GP ignoring solvent entirely is competitive with solvent-aware models, while LLM embeddings underperform by an order of magnitude, highlights that solvent featurization—not model capacity—is the current bottleneck. Open questions include developing featurizations that capture trace-solvent side reactions, extending to multi-reaction settings, and whether the non-stationarity and heteroskedasticity inherent in transient flow data can be better exploited by the ML community.
