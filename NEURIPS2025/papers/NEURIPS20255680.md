## Problem

While sharp empirical Bernstein inequalities for scalars (adapting to unknown variance without requiring it) have been established, no analogous sharp results existed for bounded symmetric random matrices. Existing matrix concentration inequalities either require prior knowledge of the variance (oracle Bernstein) or, when empirical, fail to match the oracle's first-order deviation asymptotically. This paper closes that gap by providing two closed-form matrix EB inequalities whose deviation radii converge almost surely to the oracle Bennett-Bernstein bound.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sequence of d×d symmetric random matrices with eigenvalues in [0,1] (or [a,b] via rescaling), a significance level α, and either an independence or martingale-difference structure with constant conditional mean. It produces a closed-form deviation radius D_n that defines a confidence ball in spectral norm around the sample mean, with the key property that √n·D_n → √(2 log(d/α)‖V‖) almost surely without V being known. The second inequality additionally yields a time-uniform confidence sequence valid for all n simultaneously and a stopped confidence ball at any data-dependent stopping time τ.

## Method

The first inequality (Theorem 3.1) applies the matrix Bennett-Bernstein inequality twice—once on the sample mean and once on a paired sample variance estimator—combined via an imbalanced α-split (α(n−1)/n + α/n) that is critical for achieving sharpness; a balanced split would introduce an extraneous log-factor. The second inequality (Theorem 4.2, Corollary 4.3) constructs a nonnegative supermartingale L_n = tr exp(Σ γ_i(X_i − M_i) − Σ ψ_E(γ_i)(X_i − X̂_i)²) using the Lieb-Tropp trace-exponential technique, where ψ_E(γ) = −log(1−γ)−γ is the CGF of a centered exponential. Ville's inequality then yields time-uniform and stopped concentration bounds. The predictable weights γ_i = √(2 log(d/α)/(n·v̄_{i−1})) adapt to a running variance proxy v̄_k = ‖V̄_k‖ ∨ 5 log(d/α)/n, and the plug-in prediction X̂_i = X̄_{i−1} minimizes the prediction-error term, making the bound fully adaptive to the unknown conditional variance V.

## Results

Both matrix empirical Bernstein inequalities are proven sharp: their deviation radii D_n^meb1 and D_n^meb2 satisfy √n·D_n → √(2 log(d/α)‖V‖) almost surely, matching the oracle matrix Bennett-Bernstein bound exactly. In simulation (d=3, α=0.05), the self-normalized second EB inequality (meb2) achieves a ratio of 1.105× the oracle bound at n=1,000 and 1.022× at n=10,000, while the union-bound first EB inequality (meb1) is 1.589× and 1.214× respectively at those sample sizes. The second EB inequality thus reaches within 2.2% of the oracle at n=10,000, compared to 21.4% for the first.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the oracle matrix Bennett-Bernstein inequality of Tropp [2012], which requires knowledge of the true variance V. Both proposed EB inequalities match this oracle asymptotically (ratio → 1), but the self-normalized second EB (Corollary 4.3) converges far faster: at n=1,000 it is only 10.5% looser than the oracle, versus 58.9% for the union-bound first EB (Theorem 3.1). Against the scalar self-normalized EB of Waudby-Smith and Ramdas [2023], the paper's second matrix EB is a faithful generalization (identical constants when d=1) but extends to martingale dependence and provides a time-uniform confidence sequence. Against the original Maurer-Pontil [2009] scalar EB, the paper shows that the balanced α/2+α/2 union bound split causes a non-sharp √2 inflation in the log term; switching to the imbalanced α(n−1)/n + α/n split recovers sharpness, and the self-normalized approach remains empirically tighter still (Table 2 in Appendix B.2 shows the self-normalized bound at 1.081× oracle vs. 1.476× for the sharpened Maurer-Pontil at n=1,000).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends two distinct families of scalar empirical Bernstein inequalities to the matrix setting. The first (Theorem 3.1) generalizes the union-bound-and-plug-in approach of Maurer and Pontil (2009) to symmetric matrices using a paired sample variance, while the second (Theorem 4.2, Corollary 4.3) faithfully lifts the self-normalization/supermartingale technique of Howard et al. (2021) and Waudby-Smith and Ramdas (2023) via the Lieb-Tropp matrix CGF supermartingale framework. Both results are positioned against the oracle matrix Bennett-Bernstein inequality of Tropp (2012), which requires knowledge of the variance, and the paper demonstrates that its empirical versions match the oracle asymptotically (i.e., are 'sharp') without requiring that knowledge. The only other matrix EB inequality in the literature at the time of writing (Kroshnin and Suvorikova, 2024) is noted to be non-sharp.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

These inequalities provide the first sharp, closed-form, variance-adaptive confidence sets for matrix means, with direct applications to covariance matrix estimation where prior oracle Bernstein bounds required an additional boundedness argument that could be arbitrarily loose. The time-uniform and stopped versions of the second inequality enable sequential analysis and anytime-valid inference under martingale dependence, broadening applicability beyond i.i.d. settings. A key open question identified is unifying these methods with the dimension-free matrix Bernstein inequality of Minsker (2017), which trades the log(d) dependence for a dependence on tr(V)/‖V‖. The practical impact is most immediate in high-dimensional statistics and signal processing where adaptive, nonasymptotic guarantees for spectral-norm deviations are needed.
