## Problem

Existing agricultural parcel datasets predominantly cover regular, planar farmlands at mid-resolution (10–30 m), lacking fine-grained representation of complex terraced terrains with stepped 3D structures, shadow-occluded scenes, and irregular boundaries. Moreover, most provide only binary mask labels that cannot distinguish shared versus non-shared edges between adjacent parcels, and none support unsupervised domain adaptation for cross-region generalization.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254963/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The dataset consists of 47,537 high-resolution (0.5–0.7 m) RGB patches of 512×512 pixels sourced from GF-2 and Google Earth imagery across 14 countries and seven Chinese geographic zones. Each patch carries three co-registered label layers: a binary mask (terraced vs. background), a 3-pixel-wide boundary map generated by single-pass morphological etching with a 3×3 kernel, and a parcel-only mask obtained via XOR of the mask and boundary. The three domains (South China, North China, Global) enable six directed UDA transfer tasks, and the multi-level labels support four distinct evaluation protocols simultaneously.

## Method

GTPBD is constructed by first selecting terrace-dense regions from the 10 m Global Terrace Map, then manually annotating over 200,000 parcels in QGIS using two complementary strategies: a common-edge method for narrow ridges (<0.5 m) that vectorizes large continuous parcels and cuts sub-parcels internally, and a bilateral-segmentation method for wider ridges (≥0.5 m) that generates independent boundaries on both sides. The three label levels are derived algorithmically from the vector annotations—mask via GDAL fully-connected rasterization, boundary via a single 3×3 morphological erosion, and parcel via XOR—ensuring topological consistency. The paper benchmarks 20 methods across four tasks (8 semantic segmentation, 4 edge detection, 3 parcel extraction, 5 UDA) under a unified multi-dimensional evaluation framework combining pixel-level metrics (Precision, Recall, IoU, OA, F1), object-level geometric metrics (GOC, GUC, GTC), and edge-detection metrics (ODS, OIS, AP), revealing that transformer-based models (Mask2Former) achieve the best object-level parcel fidelity while agriculture-specific networks (HBGNet, REAUNet) lead in pixel-level accuracy, and that boundary-integration via REAUNet-Sober consistently reduces over-segmentation (GOC) in UDA settings.

## Results

On the GTPBD benchmark, Mask2Former achieves the best semantic segmentation IoU (57.16%) and F1-score (72.74%), while NonLocal attains the highest Precision (75.06%) and Overall Accuracy (79.52%). For parcel extraction, HBGNet leads in pixel-level metrics (Recall 81.81%, IoU 62.44%, F1 76.88%), whereas Mask2Former achieves the best object-level geometric accuracy (GOC 22.04%, GTC 35.53%). In edge detection, REAUNet-Sober dominates all three metrics (ODS 65.06%, OIS 63.73%, AP 70.09%), and in UDA, PiPa consistently achieves the best pixel-level performance across all six domain transfer directions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among the eight semantic segmentation baselines, Mask2Former [8] outperforms U-Net [29] by 11.07% in IoU (57.16% vs. 46.09%) and 9.65% in F1, while NonLocal [40] achieves the highest Precision (75.06%) and OA (79.52%) but trails Mask2Former in IoU by 5.68%. In edge detection, REAUNet-Sober [22] surpasses MuGE [55] by 2.5% in ODS and 4.97% in AP, while UEAD [56] underperforms dramatically (ODS 25.88% vs. 65.06%), suggesting uncertainty-aware learning is ill-suited to terraced boundaries. For parcel extraction, HBGNet [51] leads all methods in pixel-level F1 (76.88%) and IoU (62.44%), beating REAUNet [22] by 1.42% in F1 and SEANet [19] by 2.84%, yet Mask2Former [8] achieves the lowest GOC (22.04%) and GTC (35.53%), revealing a pixel-vs.-object trade-off. In UDA, PiPa [24] consistently achieves the best Recall and F1 across all six transfer directions (e.g., N→S: F1 79.99%, G→N: F1 73.10%), while FDA [43] is the weakest method in every direction (average IoU 43.25%), and HRDA [17] excels specifically when the source domain is North China. DAFormer [16] attains the lowest GUC (38.22% average) in boundary-integrated UDA, indicating superior boundary coherence despite slightly lower pixel-level scores than PiPa.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of agricultural parcel extraction and terraced terrain analysis. Prior parcel datasets (FHAPD, AI4Boundaries, FTW, PASTIS) focus on regular, flat farmlands at mid-resolution (10 m) with binary mask labels, while terrace mapping efforts (GTM, GFSAD30) operate at coarse resolution without parcel-level granularity. GTPBD departs from both lines by providing high-resolution (0.5–0.7 m) imagery of complex terraced landscapes with three-level annotations (boundary, mask, parcel) and, for the first time in agricultural parcel benchmarks, explicit UDA support across three geographic domains. The benchmarked methods (U-Net through Mask2Former, REAUNet, SEANet, HBGNet, and UDA methods like DAFormer and PiPa) are drawn from the general segmentation and domain adaptation literature rather than being novel architectures proposed here.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GTPBD fills a critical gap by providing the first fine-grained, globally distributed terraced parcel benchmark, enabling systematic evaluation of methods on terrain that prior datasets simply do not represent. The benchmark reveals that even strong transformer-based models achieve only ~57% IoU and ~35% GTC on terraced parcels, and that pixel-level accuracy does not translate to object-level geometric fidelity—a trade-off that remains unresolved. The UDA experiments further show that cross-domain generalization (e.g., South→North, Global→North) remains a significant open challenge, with FDA consistently underperforming and no method closing the gap to in-domain performance. Open questions include whether boundary-integration strategies can be made more effective, how to handle the extreme size distribution (most parcels <5000 px), and whether the three-level annotation scheme can be automated to scale beyond manual annotation.
