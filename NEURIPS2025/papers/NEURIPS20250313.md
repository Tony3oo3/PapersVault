## Problem

Modern linear RNNs (xLSTM, Mamba, GLA) are inherently restricted to 1D sequences; when applied to multi-dimensional data like images or graphs, they enforce a pre-defined scanline traversal that mismatches spatial proximity with processing order, causing credit-assignment failures and vanishing gradients over long spatial distances. Multi-dimensional RNNs (MDRNNs, DAG-LSTM) handle structured data but are non-linear, strictly sequential (not parallelizable), and lack the associative memory expansion of modern linear RNNs. No existing architecture combines the parallelizable, associative-memory structure of linear RNNs with native multi-dimensional propagation on general DAGs.

## Input / Output

For images, RGB inputs are patchified into a 2D grid (e.g., 16×16 patches at 224×224 resolution) where cell states reside on directed edges between patches rather than on the patches themselves; all four directional DAG covers (right-down, right-up, left-down, left-up) are processed simultaneously. For molecular graphs, node and edge features define a DAG whose topology determines the recurrence. Outputs are class labels (ImageNet-1K) or graph-level regression/classification scores (peptide MAE, TUDataset accuracy), produced by a Mark-gated retrieval of edge cell states plus a direct input-to-output skip term at each node.

## Method

pLSTM places cell states on edges of a DAG and introduces three input-dependent gates—Source (replacing the input gate), Transition (replacing the forget gate), and Mark (replacing the output gate)—that act on node→edge, edge→edge, and edge→node pairs respectively, yielding a fully linear recurrence. A hierarchical divide-and-conquer parallelization (analogous to associative scan) recursively merges Source-Transition, Transition-Transition, and Transition-Mark products into higher-order objects, reducing to einsum/concatenation/padding operations on regular grids and enabling a chunkwise-recurrent form for hardware efficiency. Two stabilization modes address long-range propagation: P-mode constrains the Transition matrix to unit column-sum (L₁-norm ≤ 1), enabling directional signal propagation along a line with only O(Δ^{-1/2}) power-law decay, while D-mode reduces the line graph to a multitree so that only a single path connects any two edges, permitting undirected global distribution. In practice, pLSTM replaces multi-head attention in a pre-RMSNorm residual ViT backbone, alternating P-mode and D-mode layers and using multi-head initialization to cover multiple propagation angles and decay scales.

## Results

On ImageNet-1K, pLSTM-Vis achieves 75.2% (T), 80.7% (S), and 82.5% (B) top-1 accuracy, competitive with ViL (78.3/81.5/82.4%) and 2DMamba (82.8/83.8%) at similar parameter counts. On the Arrow Pointing Extrapolation task, pLSTM achieves 77.8% extrapolation accuracy at 384×384 resolution, substantially outperforming ViT (70.7%), EfficientNet (64.9%), 2DMamba (58.4%), and ViL (50.3%). On graph benchmarks (TUDataset and Long-Range Graph Benchmark), pLSTM is competitive with GAT, GCN, GIN, and MPNN, achieving 0.737 average accuracy on TUDataset and 0.264 MAE on Peptides-struct.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On ImageNet-1K, pLSTM-Vis is competitive but trails the strongest baselines: at the small scale, pLSTM-Vis-S (80.7%) is ~1.1–2.1% below 2DMamba-T (82.8%) and Mamba2D-T (82.4%), and ~0.8% below ViL-S (81.5%); at the base scale, pLSTM-Vis-B (82.5%) roughly matches ViL-B (82.4%) but trails 2DMamba-S (83.8%). The paper notes that integrating local features (e.g., additional convolutions) could narrow this gap. On the Arrow Pointing Extrapolation task, pLSTM clearly wins: it achieves 77.8% extrapolation accuracy, beating ViT (70.7%) by 7.1 points, EfficientNet (64.9%) by 12.9 points, 2DMamba (58.4%) by 19.4 points, and ViL (50.3%) by 27.5 points, while Mamba2D and V2M fail to extrapolate at all (~50%). On graph benchmarks, pLSTM is competitive with GNN baselines: on TUDataset it achieves 0.737 average accuracy (vs. GIN 0.747, GAT 0.728, GCN 0.716), and on Peptides-struct it matches GCN (0.264 MAE) while trailing MPNN (0.260) slightly. Overall, pLSTM's key advantage is demonstrated on the synthetic extrapolation task where its multi-dimensional receptive field provides a clear structural benefit over both sequential linear RNNs and global-attention Transformers.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate research threads: multi-dimensional RNNs (MDRNNs, DAG-LSTM) that handle structured data like images and graphs but are non-linear and non-parallelizable, and modern linear RNNs (xLSTM, Mamba, GLA) that are parallelizable and efficient but inherently sequential. The key conceptual move is translating the input/forget/output gate structure of LSTMs into Source/Transition/Mark gates operating on the line graph of a general DAG, which enables a hierarchical parallelization scheme (analogous to associative scan) in multiple dimensions. The paper also positions itself against 2D Mamba variants (V2M, 2DMamba, Mamba2D), showing that these cover only restricted subsets of the P-mode or D-mode that pLSTM provides, and that their fixed normalization factors limit directional propagation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

pLSTM addresses a fundamental structural mismatch: sequential linear RNNs applied to 2D data (images, grids) enforce an artificial scanline ordering that makes spatially adjacent patches distant in the processing sequence, causing credit-assignment and vanishing-gradient problems. The P-mode/D-mode stabilization framework provides a principled, theoretically grounded solution for long-range propagation on general DAGs, with the power-law O(Δ^{-1/2}) decay in P-mode being a clean analytical result. However, the arrow-pointing extrapolation task, while illustrative, is synthetic and restricted; on ImageNet-1K, pLSTM is competitive but trails SOTA (EfficientNet, 2DMamba) by a small margin, and the lack of custom kernels limits practical speed. Open questions include whether the extrapolation advantage generalizes to harder multi-dimensional long-range benchmarks, and whether domain-specific inductive biases (e.g., local convolutions) can close the ImageNet gap.
