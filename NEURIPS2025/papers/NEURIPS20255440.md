## Problem

Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMs) suffer from the binding problem: they process visual features largely in parallel and fail to reliably associate perceptual attributes (color, shape) with their correct spatial referents, leading to persistent errors in counting, visual search, scene description, and spatial reasoning. The paper shows that purely textual interventions, including Chain-of-Thought prompting, are insufficient to resolve these binding failures and can even degrade performance, pointing to a need for interventions at the level of visual input processing rather than linguistic instruction alone.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255440/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a standard RGB image and physically augments it by drawing n=3 equidistant horizontal lines (1 px thick) with numerical row labels (1–4) on the left side, then appends a fixed sequential-scanning instruction to the text prompt. The augmented image-plus-prompt pair is fed to the LVLM in a single query, and the model produces task-specific outputs: an integer count, a binary presence/absence label, a free-text scene description, or a multiple-choice spatial relation answer. No fine-tuning, multi-query processing, or external tools are required.

## Method

VISER (Visual Input Structure for Enhanced Reasoning) is a training-free, model-agnostic method that injects a spatial inductive bias directly into the visual input. It overlays n equidistant horizontal lines on the image, partitioning it into n+1 labeled horizontal segments, and pairs this with a fixed prompt instructing the model to 'scan the image sequentially based on horizontal lines.' The design is inspired by neuroscientific findings that serial, region-by-region attention reduces feature interference and illusory conjunctions compared to parallel processing. Ablations confirm that both the visual scaffolding and the textual prompt are necessary and complementary: removing either component, or replacing the drawn lines with an instruction to 'imagine' them, substantially reduces gains. The approach operates within a single inference query with negligible computational overhead (only ~30 additional output tokens on average), and is effective across closed-source (GPT-4o, Claude-3.5-Sonnet) and open-source (Qwen2.5-VL, LLaMA4) models without any architectural modification.

## Results

VISER improves GPT-4o's 2D counting accuracy from 12.00% to 38.83% (+26.8 pp), 2D visual search harmonic mean from 0.48 to 0.73 (+25.0 pp), and 2D spatial relationship accuracy from 43.00% to 52.50% (+9.5 pp). On scene description, the average edit distance drops from 1.94 to 1.62 (−0.32) for GPT-4o on 2D scenes. Gains are consistent across all four evaluated LVLMs (GPT-4o, Claude-3.5-Sonnet, LLaMA4, Qwen2.5-VL) and extend to natural-image benchmarks (e.g., GPT-4o counting on Learning-to-Count-Everything: 29.82% → 35.65%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Chain-of-Thought baseline (Kojima et al., 2022; 'Let's think step by step'), VISER consistently outperforms on all four tasks in both 2D and 3D settings with GPT-4o; the gap is largest in 2D where flattened scenes intensify cross-object interference (e.g., 2D counting: VISER 38.83% vs. CoT well below baseline). Against Mulberry (Yao et al., 2024), a Qwen2.5-VL variant fine-tuned with collective Monte Carlo tree search, VISER matches or exceeds performance on most tasks—most strikingly on 2D counting where VISER reaches 41% accuracy versus Mulberry's 15%. Against OpenVLThinker (Deng et al., 2025), an RL-finetuned Qwen2.5-VL using GRPO, OpenVLThinker retains an edge on 2D spatial reasoning and 3D counting, but VISER is competitive or superior on the remaining tasks while requiring no fine-tuning or access to model internals. Overall, across 120 pairwise comparisons, VISER wins 103, loses 17, and ties 7 (one-sided binomial sign test p ≈ 7.4 × 10⁻¹⁵), confirming that the visual scaffolding provides gains that purely textual or training-based strategies do not replicate.

## Relation to Prior Work

VISER positions itself as a visual analogue of Chain-of-Thought prompting, but applied to the image input rather than the text: it injects an inductive bias (horizontal lines + sequential scanning instruction) directly into the visual channel to decompose the binding problem. Conceptually, it draws on the binding-problem framework from Campbell et al. [9] and on neuroscience findings that grid-based spatial structures enhance serial attention and reduce feature interference [28, 29, 30]. It deliberately departs from agentic LVLM approaches (ViperGPT, Visual Sketchpad, LVLM-COUNT) that compensate for perceptual gaps via external tools, and from training-based methods (Mulberry, OpenVLThinker) that modify model weights, by instead treating the visual input as a modifiable surface for scaffolding—requiring no fine-tuning, no tool use, and no architectural changes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central practical takeaway is that a trivially simple visual modification (three horizontal lines plus a one-sentence prompt) can rival or exceed the gains from fine-tuned or RL-trained reasoning models on binding-sensitive tasks, making it immediately deployable by any LVLM user. The finding that CoT prompting not only fails to help but can degrade performance on these tasks challenges the prevailing assumption that better textual reasoning strategies are the primary lever for improving LVLM visual reasoning. Key limitations include the static, content-agnostic nature of the scaffolding (performance drops under high object-line interference or low spatial entropy), modest gains on natural images, and degradation for some models (Qwen2.5-VL) on real-world counting. Open questions include whether adaptive, content-aware scaffolding can close the gap on natural scenes, whether the principle generalizes to non-spatial visual tasks (e.g., hallucination mitigation), and whether LVLM architectures could be redesigned to natively support the serial, spatially grounded attention that VISER approximates externally.
