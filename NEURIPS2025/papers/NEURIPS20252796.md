## Problem

Traffic forecasting and incident analysis have been pursued as separate research tracks, with existing datasets containing either traffic time series or incident records in isolation. No prior dataset spatiotemporally aligns both at lane-level granularity with comprehensive road meta-features, making it impossible to study how specific incidents causally affect traffic dynamics or to classify incidents from traffic patterns alone.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252796/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The dataset provides lane-level traffic time series (flow, speed, occupancy) at 5-minute resolution across 16,972 sensors over three years (2022–2024), 1,441,904 incident records spanning 7 types with absolute postmile locations, and 27 road meta-features split into physics (road width, terrain, surface) and policy (speed limit, functional class, population) categories. Incidents are linked to sensors via freeway name + absolute postmile matching with two strategies (nearest-sensor or distance-threshold). The four tasks produce: predicted traffic flow at 5/15/30-minute horizons post-incident, a 3-class incident label (accident/hazard/normal) from a 2-hour multivariate traffic window, a global causal DAG over 17 heterogeneous variables, and a local lagged causal DAG among 4 road nodes.

## Method

The core contribution is the dataset construction: spatiotemporally aligning Caltrans PEMS traffic and incident data at lane-level granularity, removing sensors with >50% missing volume, and constructing an 870,100-edge adjacency matrix for graph-based modeling. For post-incident forecasting, SOTA spatiotemporal models (DCRNN, GWNET, DSTAGNN, D²STGNN, etc.) are evaluated on samples where an incident occurred within a 5-minute window, revealing consistent degradation versus general forecasting. For incident classification, seven time-series classifiers (DT, PatchTST, gMLP, OmniScaleCNN, TS2Vec, Sequencer, FormerTime) are trained on 2-hour, 3-channel windows (speed, occupancy, flow) to distinguish accident, hazard, and normal conditions. Global causal analysis employs MM-DAG to learn a DAG over heterogeneous variables (scalar meta-features and functional traffic/incident data) across four high-incident districts, while local causal analysis uses PCMCI+ to discover lagged causal dependencies among road nodes before and after a specific incident, revealing how congestion propagates and alters traffic flow relationships.

## Results

On the post-incident traffic forecasting task (Table 3), all 8 SOTA spatiotemporal models degrade substantially on incident-affected samples versus the general test set: e.g., DCRNN's 5-min MAE rises from 11.90 to 13.62, and DSTAGNN's 30-min MAE jumps from 19.99 to 21.95. DCRNN and GWNET are the most robust under incidents, while DSTAGNN is consistently the worst performer across all horizons. On the incident classification task (Table 4), the best methods (DT and gMLP) achieve ~41.6% accuracy on the 3-class problem (accidents/hazards/normal), only modestly above the 33.3% random baseline, indicating that traffic-index-based incident identification remains challenging.

## Baselines & Comparisons

For post-incident traffic forecasting, DCRNN [27] and GWNET [51] are the most robust under incident conditions, with DCRNN achieving the lowest 5-min and 15-min MAE (13.62, 15.36) and GWNET the lowest 30-min MAE (17.15) on the incident test set. DSTAGNN [21] is consistently the weakest baseline, with 30-min MAE of 21.95 on incident samples—roughly 28% worse than GWNET. D²STGNN [44] and AGCRN [4] occupy the middle tier, while LSTM [17], ASTGCN [15], and STGODE [7] show moderate degradation. For incident classification, DT (a statistical baseline) and gMLP [31] tie at 41.6% accuracy on the mixed-channel setting, outperforming PatchTST [37] (39.4%), FormerTime [6] (40.5%), and OmniScaleCNN [48] (40.9%). TS2Vec [57] and Sequencer [49] are the weakest classifiers at 37.3% and 40.3% respectively. The overall takeaway is that no existing model handles incident-affected traffic well, motivating future work that explicitly incorporates incident information into forecasting architectures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by spatiotemporally aligning two previously separate research tracks—traffic forecasting and incident analysis—into a single large-scale dataset (16,972 lane-level sensors, 3 years, 7 incident types). Prior traffic datasets (PEMS, METR-LA, LargeST) contain only traffic time series without incident labels, while incident datasets (CTC, TAP, NYC Col) lack traffic time series entirely. A handful of works (DIGC-Net, STCL) have attempted joint analysis but are limited to small regions, short time spans, or mismatched transport modes (e.g., vehicle incidents vs. bike/taxi traffic). TraffiDent departs from all of these by providing seamless mode matching (all vehicles), lane-level granularity, and comprehensive road meta-features (physics and policy) that no prior dataset includes, enabling causal analysis tasks that were previously infeasible.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TraffiDent fills a clear and long-standing gap: no prior open dataset simultaneously provides traffic time series, multi-type incident records, and road meta-features at this scale, making it a likely standard benchmark for traffic-incident research. The post-incident forecasting experiments reveal that all SOTA spatiotemporal models degrade substantially under incident conditions, highlighting a significant and underexplored research direction. The incident classification results (~41% best accuracy for 3 classes) suggest that traffic indices alone are insufficient for reliable incident-type identification, pointing to the need for richer multimodal inputs. Open questions include how to incorporate incident-aware inductive biases into forecasting architectures, whether the causal structures discovered generalize across regions, and how to handle the inherent sparsity of incident events in training.
