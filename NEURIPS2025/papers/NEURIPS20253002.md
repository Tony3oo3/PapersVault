## Problem

Learning a normalized energy (log probability) model from image data is intractable because the normalization constant requires integrating over the full image space. Score matching eliminates this constant but only recovers energy up to mode-dependent additive constants, making it impossible to compare probabilities across different regions of the data manifold. The paper asks whether one can directly learn a fully normalized energy model by augmenting the standard denoising objective with a secondary constraint that enforces consistency across noise levels.

## Input / Output

The energy network takes a noisy image y = x + √t·z (where z ~ N(0,Id)) and the corresponding noise variance t as input, and outputs a scalar energy U_θ(y,t) = -log p(y|t). The gradient of this energy with respect to y yields the score, and the derivative with respect to t yields the time score, both of which are supervised during training. At inference, setting t=0 gives the log probability of a clean image in a single forward pass (no iterative integration), and the effective dimensionality d_eff(x,t) can be extracted from the energy's rate of change with t. Experiments use 64×64 images (ImageNet64, CelebA, CIFAR-10) with pixel values in [0,1].

## Method

The core contribution is a dual score matching objective that jointly optimizes two losses: a standard denoising score matching loss on the spatial gradient ∇_y U_θ (ensuring the score is correct at each noise level) and a novel time score matching loss on ∂_t U_θ (ensuring the energy is consistent as noise level changes, which fixes the normalization constant across modes). The energy is parameterized as U_θ(y,t) = ½⟨y, s_θ(y,t)⟩, where s_θ is a UNet score network; under the homogeneity and conservativity properties of s_θ, the gradient of this inner product recovers s_θ exactly, preserving the inductive biases of the base denoiser. Training requires double back-propagation to compute both the spatial and temporal gradients of the energy. Normalization is achieved by anchoring the energy at the largest noise level t_max where the distribution is approximately Gaussian, fixing the global additive constant. The resulting model achieves NLL of 3.36 bits/dimension on ImageNet64 with a single forward pass, and demonstrates strong generalization: two models trained on non-overlapping data subsets converge to nearly identical per-image energies at N=10^5.

## Results

On ImageNet64, the dual score matching energy model achieves a negative log likelihood (NLL) of 3.36 bits/dimension, comparable to state-of-the-art density estimation methods. A key practical advantage is that energy (log probability) is computed in a single neural function evaluation (one-shot), whereas competing diffusion-based density estimators require 100+ integration steps (e.g., ~3h20 vs. 12s for 50k images on an A100 GPU). The energy network also matches or slightly exceeds the denoising performance of the underlying score network across all noise levels (e.g., 18.88 vs. 18.84 dB PSNR at t=0), confirming no quality penalty from the energy parameterization. The model strongly generalizes: two independently trained models on non-overlapping data subsets converge to nearly identical per-image log probabilities at N=10⁵ training samples.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against seven density estimation baselines on ImageNet64. Against flow-based and variational methods (Glow 3.81, PixelCNN 3.57, I-DDPM 3.54, VDM 3.40), the proposed method (3.36) achieves lower NLL, though the paper cautions that differences of ±0.5 bits/dim arise from pre-processing, discretization, and estimator-type differences. Against the strongest baselines—Flow Matching (3.31) and NFDM (3.20)—the method is slightly worse by 0.05 and 0.16 bits/dim respectively, but is the only method (besides TarFlow) that provides a single-NFE energy estimate, making it dramatically faster at inference. TarFlow (2.99) achieves the best NLL but uses a different discretization scheme (uniform dequantization) and is not directly comparable. The denoising comparison (Table 1) shows the energy network matches or marginally beats the score network at all noise levels (e.g., 2.53 vs. −0.11 dB PSNR at t=−30), indicating the dual score matching objective does not degrade the underlying denoiser.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the score-matching framework (Hyvärinen & Dayan, 2005) and its denoising formulation (Vincent, 2011) by adding a second, 'time score' objective derived from differentiating the energy with respect to noise variance, building on the time-score concept of Choi et al. (2022) but replacing their second-derivative formulation with a regression target. The multi-scale score architecture is inherited from diffusion models (Song & Ermon, 2019; Ho et al., 2020), and the maximum-likelihood noise-level weighting follows Song et al. (2021b). The key departure is the inner-product energy parameterization U_θ(y,t) = ½⟨y, s_θ(y,t)⟩, which preserves the inductive biases of the base score network (contrasting with the squared-norm architecture of Salimans & Ho, 2021), and the explicit normalization step that fixes the additive constant using the Gaussian limit at large noise levels—yielding a directly evaluable, normalized log-probability model rather than the implicit density of standard diffusion models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical contribution is a one-shot, computationally cheap method for computing log probabilities from a diffusion-trained network (12 s for 50k images vs. hours of integration), making density estimation accessible for tasks like out-of-distribution detection and information-theoretic analysis. The empirical findings are the deeper contribution: the Gumbel-distributed log probabilities and the absence of concentration of measure challenge the standard expectation that high-dimensional densities concentrate, while the image- and scale-dependent effective dimensionality complicates the manifold hypothesis. Open questions include whether these geometric findings persist at higher resolutions (the paper only tests 64×64), whether the Gumbel form has a theoretical explanation, and whether the lack of a Poincaré-inequality-style guarantee limits the model's reliability for out-of-distribution inputs.
