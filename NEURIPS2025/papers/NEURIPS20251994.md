## Problem

Prior work on predictions in online control either assumes perfect finite-horizon predictions (too idealized) or bounded prediction errors (too pessimistic, as it ignores stochastic dependencies between predictions and disturbances). This paper asks: what is the true incremental value of a given stochastic prediction for control, and why can two predictors with identical per-entry MSE yield very different control costs?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251994/cropped_page8_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a general joint distribution over disturbances W_{0:T-1} and a family of parameterized predictions {V_{0:T-1}(θ)} living on the same probability space, along with time-varying dynamics f_t and stage/terminal costs h_t. Unlike prior work, predictions need not target specific future disturbances or have any prescribed functional form in θ. The primary output is the prediction power P(θ) = J*(0) − J*(θ), a scalar measuring the maximum expected cost reduction from optimally exploiting predictions; in the LQR case this has a closed form involving the conditional covariance of a surrogate-optimal action, and in the general case a lower bound is provided.

## Method

The paper introduces a general stochastic model where predictions and disturbances share a probability space, then defines prediction power as the cost improvement from the optimal predictive policy relative to a no-prediction baseline. For time-varying LQR, a backward induction yields a closed-form expression (Theorem 3.2): P(θ) = Σ_t Tr{(R_t + B_t^T P_{t+1} B_t) E[Cov[ū_t^θ(I_t(θ)) | F_t(0)]}}, showing that prediction power depends on the conditional covariance of the optimal feedforward action, not on per-entry MSE. To extend beyond LQR, the paper identifies two structural conditions—a quadratic growth condition on the expected Q-function (Condition 4.1) and a positive semi-definite covariance condition on the optimal policy's actions (Condition 4.2)—that together yield a general lower bound P(θ) ≥ Σ_t Tr{M_t Σ_t} (Theorem 4.3). Applied to LTV dynamics with strongly convex, smooth non-quadratic costs under Gaussian disturbance-prediction pairs, this gives a concrete lower bound (Theorem 4.8) showing that even weakly correlated predictions produce strictly positive prediction power.

## Results

The paper's headline result (Theorem 3.2) provides a closed-form expression for prediction power in the LTV-LQR setting: P(θ) = Σ_t Tr{(R_t + B_tᵀP_{t+1}B_t) E[Cov[ū_t^θ(I_t(θ)) | F_t(0)]]}, showing that prediction power depends on the conditional covariance of the surrogate-optimal action, not on point-estimate accuracy. The general lower bound (Theorem 4.3) states P(θ) ≥ Σ_t Tr{M_t Σ_t} under a quadratic growth condition on the Q-function and a positive semi-definite covariance condition on the optimal policy. For LTV dynamics with non-quadratic costs (Theorem 4.8), even weakly correlated predictions (small but non-zero λ_t(θ)) yield a strictly positive lower bound P(θ) ≥ Σ_t μ_u · nλ_t(θ)μ_{t+1}²μ_B / [2(ℓ_u + ℓ_{t+1}√ℓ_B)²]. In the illustrative Example 4.4, perfect one-step predictions achieve prediction power exactly equal to T.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a primarily theoretical paper without a standard empirical baseline comparison table. Conceptually, it positions itself against three prior paradigms: (1) perfect k-step prediction models [1,3], which it generalizes by allowing arbitrary stochastic dependencies between predictions and disturbances; (2) bounded-error prediction models [2,5,4], which it shows are overly pessimistic because they must hold for an adversary generating worst-case prediction sequences, and Example 3.3 demonstrates that two predictors with identical per-entry MSE can have significantly different prediction power; (3) decision-focused learning (DFL) [12,13,14,15], which trains predictors to reduce downstream cost, whereas this paper characterizes the maximum achievable benefit of any predictor. In Example 3.4, the paper shows that the online policy optimization algorithm M-GAPS [24] converges toward the prediction power upper bound when the optimal policy is within the candidate class, but falls short when it is not, illustrating that prediction power is a tight but generally unattainable ceiling for practical algorithms.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends Yu et al. (NeurIPS 2020), which defined 'prediction power' for perfect k-step predictions in time-invariant LQR, by generalizing to arbitrary stochastic dependencies between predictions and disturbances, time-varying dynamics, and non-quadratic costs. It departs from the bounded-prediction-error paradigm (Yu et al. 2022, Zhang et al. 2021, Lin et al. 2022), which the authors argue is overly pessimistic because it must hold against adversarial prediction sequences, by instead modeling the joint distribution of predictions and disturbances. The framework also connects to decision-focused learning (DFL) literature, where prediction accuracy is the standard metric, and to online policy optimization (M-GAPS), where prediction power serves as an upper bound on achievable improvement.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that two predictors with identical per-entry MSE can yield very different control costs because off-diagonal conditional covariance entries interact with system dynamics—challenges the field's default assumption that accuracy is the right proxy for prediction value. The general lower bound (Theorem 4.3) provides a reusable tool for quantifying prediction value beyond LQR, though it requires verifying structural conditions (quadratic Q-function growth, positive semi-definite action covariance) that may be hard to establish in practice. Open questions include comparing two arbitrary predictors when the lower bound is not tight, learning the predictor parameter θ that maximizes prediction power, and extending to partially observable systems where the optimal policy's form becomes intractable.
