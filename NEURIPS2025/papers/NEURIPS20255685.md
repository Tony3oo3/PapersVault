## Problem

Momentum-based optimizers like Adam store first- and second-order momentum tensors that consume ~66% of total parameter memory, making large-scale training prohibitively expensive. Existing training-oriented quantization methods (Bnb 8-bit, Lpmm 4-bit) rely on search-based quantization with O(n log m) complexity, require custom GPU kernels, and are locked to fixed bit-widths, leaving a gap for a sub-4-bit, linear-complexity, bit-width-flexible optimizer state compression.

## Input / Output

The method consumes optimizer momentum tensors (Adam's m and v) as input, splitting each into two equal-sized sub-tensors (X, Y) that are normalized by a per-tensor scale factor w so that x² + y² ≤ 4. The output is a quantized rotation-angle tensor Θ of half the original size (n/2 elements) plus a single scalar w; during forward/backward passes, only the small subset of parameters needed for computation is restored to full precision via the inverse rotation, while the remainder stays in angular form in memory.

## Method

The core insight is that any complex number z = x + iy with ‖z‖ ≤ 2 can be represented as e^{iθ} + e^{iπθ} for a single real angle θ (Theorem 3.1), halving the parameter count losslessly. A geometric solution (Lemma 3.2) computes θ via α = arctan(y/x) and β = arccos(√(x²+y²)/2), reducing the problem to finding an integer m satisfying {mπ̄} = {Ω} where Ω is computed in O(1) per element. The key trick is constructing a modified irrational π̄ (Eq. 6) with a controlled zero-padding pattern so that m ≈ ⌊{Ω} × 10^λ⌋, guaranteeing error ≤ 10^{-λ} in linear time without any search. Quantization then retains λ decimal places of the angle, yielding 3.32λ bits per parameter pair (3.32 bits at λ=1), and the scheme is integrated into Adam by quantizing and restoring momentum states each iteration (Algorithm 2).

## Results

On TinyLlama-1.1B (continual pre-training on PG-19), π-Quant reduces optimizer state bit-width to 3.32 bits while achieving an average benchmark score of 55.34 (ARC-c, HellaSwag, Lambada, PIQA), matching 8-bit Bnb.Adam (55.34) and outperforming 4-bit Lpmm.Adam (54.44). At 13.28-bit, π-Quant even surpasses the FP32 baseline (55.45 vs. 55.22 avg). In terms of memory, π-Quant at 3.32-bit uses 11.32 GB versus 19.47 GB for FP32 Adam, a 41.9% reduction. Linear-Quant fails entirely (training collapse) at both 16-bit and 8-bit settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Linear-Quant [Vanhoucke, 2011], π-Quant is the only method that remains stable at low bit-widths; Linear-Quant collapses to NaN loss at both 16-bit and 8-bit, making it non-competitive. Against Bnb.Adam [Dettmers, 2015] at 8-bit, π-Quant at 3.32-bit achieves an identical average score (55.34) while using 11.32 GB versus 13.44 GB (a further 15.8% memory saving), and π-Quant at 6.64-bit (55.40) slightly edges out Bnb (55.34). Against Lpmm.Adam [Li et al., 2023] at 4-bit, π-Quant at 3.32-bit clearly wins: 55.34 vs. 54.44 average, with a notably lower test perplexity (5.14 vs. 5.41) and higher ARC-c (33.53 vs. 31.23). On the LRA downstream tasks (Table 4), π-Quant outperforms both Bnb and Lpmm on Image (41.22 vs. 39.71/39.92), Retrieval (80.79 vs. 79.92/79.19), ListOps (38.51 vs. 37.30/37.30), and all Seq2Seq metrics (e.g., BLEU-4: 2.02 vs. 1.61/1.57), while Lpmm only matches on PathFinder accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the training-oriented optimizer state quantization line established by Bnb (8-bit, 2021) and Lpmm (4-bit, 2023), which both rely on search-based mapping to non-uniform codebooks and require custom GPU kernels. It departs fundamentally by replacing the search-based quantization with a complex rotation scheme grounded in the density of irrational multiples, mapping each parameter pair to a single rotation angle with linear-time geometric equations. The use of complex rotation also echoes positional encoding techniques like RoPE and xPos, but here it is repurposed for lossless parameter compression rather than sequence modeling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 41.8% reduction in optimizer state memory (32-bit → 3.32-bit) is practically significant for training large models on constrained hardware, and the absence of custom GPU kernels improves portability across CPU/TPU/GPU. However, the method introduces measurable step-time overhead (e.g., 10.56s vs 9.77s for FP32 on TinyLlama), and the construction of the modified irrational π̄ is somewhat ad hoc, raising questions about robustness at extreme bit-widths. Open directions include applying the rotation scheme to KV-cache compression and reducing the per-iteration quantization/restore cost, which the authors themselves flag as future work.
