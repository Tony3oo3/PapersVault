## Problem

Monte Carlo sampling, the dominant strategy for selecting collocation points in PINNs, is suboptimal in the low-dimensional settings typical of physics problems. Existing adaptive alternatives (RAR, RAD, R3) rely on residual-based error estimators or gradient thresholding that are problem-specific and do not exploit the underlying physics to inform the sampling distribution. The paper asks whether the physics itself can drive a learnable, principled quadrature rule that adapts to the solution's structure without any labeled data.

## Input / Output

The method takes a PDE operator L, a forcing function f, boundary/initial conditions g, and optional PDE hyper-parameters as input, and produces a neural-network-parameterized solution u(x) together with a set of quadrature nodes and weights. The weight function is constrained to the modified Gauss-Jacobi form w_θ(x) = (1−x)^α(1+x)^β h_θ(x) with a small (~500-parameter) neural network h_θ, and nodes are computed in near-constant time via asymptotic expansions rather than iterative eigenvalue solvers. For the PDE-family setting, a parameter vector κ drawn from a known distribution p is fed to two hyper-networks that jointly generate the weight function and solution function, enabling single-forward-pass inference on new instances.

## Method

LearnQuad parameterizes a modified Gauss-Jacobi weight function with a neural network modifier h_θ(x), inducing a family of orthogonal polynomials whose roots serve as adaptive collocation points (strong form) or whose associated quadrature rule evaluates weak-form integrals. The key computational insight is to use the asymptotic expansions of Opsomer & Huybrechs (2023) for modified Gauss-Jacobi nodes and weights, simplifying the bulk-region contour integral via the Cauchy Residue Theorem (assuming simple poles) into a one-dimensional root-finding problem solved by bisection with implicit-function-theorem-based automatic differentiation. This avoids expensive contour integration within the learning loop and allows millions of nodes to be computed in parallel on GPU. Training jointly optimizes the solution network u_φ and the weight network h_θ under the standard PINN residual loss plus a quadrature-consistency regularizer, while the interlacing property of OP roots across polynomial degrees is exploited to inject stochasticity and prevent overfitting. For PDE families, two hyper-networks map a parameter vector κ to the weight and solution functions, so a new instance is solved in a single forward pass without retraining.

## Results

On the Convection equation (β=30, 300k epochs, 1000 points), LearnQuad achieves 0.68±0.02% L₂ relative error, outperforming the best adaptive baseline Causal R3 (0.75±0.12%) and R3 (0.78±0.18%). On the Wave equation (2000 points), LearnQuad reaches 0.005±0.0006 L₂ error versus 0.09±0.04 for RAD and 0.29±0.04 for RAR-D — roughly an order of magnitude improvement. Across all 7 PDEs tested (Convection, Diffusion, Burgers', Allen-Cahn, Wave), LearnQuad achieves the lowest L₂ error in 6 of 7 settings, with the sole exception being Allen-Cahn (Table 2) where Causal R3 edges it out (0.71% vs. 0.87%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

LearnQuad is compared against 9 baselines spanning non-adaptive (PINN fixed/dynamic, Curr Reg, CPINN fixed/dynamic) and adaptive (RAR-G, RAD, RAR-D, R³, Causal R³) sampling strategies. On the Convection equation (the hardest problem, with sharp gradients), LearnQuad consistently outperforms all baselines by a wide margin: at β=50/300k epochs it achieves 0.76% L₂ error versus 2.28% for Causal R³ and 64.1% for RAD. On the Wave equation (Table 1), LearnQuad (0.005) beats the best adaptive method RAD (0.09) by nearly 18×. On Burgers' (Table 1), LearnQuad (0.003) outperforms RAD (0.02) by ~7×. The one case where LearnQuad does not win is Allen-Cahn in Table 2, where Causal R³ (0.71%) slightly edges LearnQuad (0.87%), though both are far ahead of all other methods. Against classical numerical solvers (Table 3), LearnQuad still trails (e.g., 0.0068 vs. 0.0002 for Lax-Wendroff on Convection β=30), but it narrows the gap substantially compared to other adaptive PINN methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the PINN framework (Raissi et al., 2019) by replacing Monte Carlo collocation sampling with a learnable quadrature rule derived from a neural-network-parameterized modified Gauss-Jacobi weight function. The mathematical backbone comes from recent asymptotic expansions of quadrature nodes and weights for generalized weight functions (Opsomer & Huybrechs, 2023), combined with implicit differentiation through root-finding (Blondel et al., 2022) to make the entire pipeline end-to-end differentiable. Unlike residual-based adaptive methods (RAR, RAD, R3) that iteratively re-weight points based on error estimates, LearnQuad learns a global weight function whose induced orthogonal polynomial roots directly determine the sampling distribution, and unlike variational PINNs (VPINN, hp-VPINN) that use fixed test functions, the test functions here are themselves learned. The paper positions LearnQuad as orthogonal to other PINN improvements (learning-rate scheduling, architecture modifications) and as a drop-in replacement for any sampling strategy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LearnQuad provides a principled, physics-informed mechanism for adaptive sampling that achieves order-of-magnitude L2 error reductions over existing adaptive baselines across diverse PDEs, while requiring only ~500 additional parameters. The hyper-network extension enabling single-forward-pass solution of new PDE instances from a family (without paired data, unlike operator learning) is a practically useful capability for parametric studies. Key limitations include a persistent gap with classical numerical solvers, demonstrated benefits primarily in low dimensions (the 100D experiment shows no advantage over Monte Carlo for smooth solutions), and the need for tensor-product or sparse-grid constructions for true high-dimensional settings. Open questions include whether the learned weight functions transfer across PDE families with different operators, and whether the asymptotic-expansion approximations remain accurate for highly oscillatory or singular solutions.
