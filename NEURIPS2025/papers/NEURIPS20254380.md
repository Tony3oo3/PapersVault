## Problem

Classical jump-diffusion models (e.g., Merton Jump Diffusion) provide principled stochastic modeling of abrupt changes but assume stationary increments, failing on real-world non-stationary data. Deep learning time-series models handle distributional shifts but are black-box, lack explicit stochastic formulations, and do not model discontinuous jumps. No existing method combines explicit non-stationary SDE-based jump modeling with the flexibility of neural networks to learn time-varying dynamics from data.

## Input / Output

The model takes a window of past time series values {S_0, S_{-1}, ..., S_{-Tp}} and optional contextual features C (e.g., graph-structured spatial relationships via Graphormer, static POI attributes) as input, and produces future stochastic trajectories {S_1, ..., S_Tf} at arbitrary temporal resolutions. Unlike point-prediction models, Neural MJD generates full continuous-time SDE simulations, enabling multi-run stochastic forecasting and closed-form likelihood evaluation for probabilistic metrics. The model is trained once across all series and produces context-dependent parameter estimates in a single forward pass at test time.

## Method

Neural MJD parameterizes a non-stationary Merton Jump Diffusion SDE—combining a time-inhomogeneous Itô diffusion for continuous dynamics and a time-inhomogeneous compound Poisson process for abrupt jumps—where all time-varying parameters {μ_t, σ_t, λ_t, γ_t, ν_t} are predicted by a transformer-based neural network conditioned on historical data and context. Training uses maximum likelihood estimation with two key tractability mechanisms: a likelihood truncation that caps jumps per interval at κ=5 (with a proven super-exponential error bound of O(κ^{-κ})), and parameter bootstrapping that replaces ground-truth states with predicted conditional means to reduce train-test discrepancy. For inference, the paper proposes an Euler-Maruyama with restart solver that periodically resets the simulated state to the analytically computed conditional mean, achieving a provably tighter weak-convergence error of O(exp(L(t−⌊t⌋))/M) compared to the standard EM's O(exp(Lt)/M), reducing variance and error accumulation over long horizons.

## Results

On the S&P 500 stock dataset, Neural MJD achieves a mean MAE of 15.4 and R² of 0.953, dramatically outperforming the next-best baseline Neural BS (MAE 31.6, R² 0.781) and the strongest generative baseline Flow Matching (MAE 34.9, R² 0.368). On the SafeGraph&Advan business analytics dataset, Neural MJD attains MAE 54.1 and R² 0.549, edging out FM (MAE 54.5, R² 0.540) and Neural BS (MAE 56.4, R² 0.539). On the synthetic jump-diffusion dataset, Neural MJD achieves MAE 0.09 and R² 0.32, surpassing Neural BS (MAE 0.15, R² 0.25) and the classical MJD model (MAE 0.21, R² 0.08).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Neural MJD outperforms all 13 baselines across every evaluation protocol on both real-world datasets. The most striking gap is against Neural BS (same transformer backbone without the jump component), which Neural MJD beats by 2× on S&P 500 MAE (15.4 vs. 31.6) and by 0.17 in R² (0.953 vs. 0.781), confirming that explicit jump modeling is the key differentiator. Among generative baselines, Flow Matching is the strongest (MAE 34.9, R² 0.368 on S&P 500) but still trails Neural MJD by more than 2× in MAE; moreover, FM requires hundreds of NFEs for a single sample, whereas Neural MJD completes 10 stochastic runs in 179 ms versus FM's 2696 ms. Classical statistical models (ARIMA, BS, MJD) are consistently the weakest, with ARIMA even producing negative R² on S&P 500 (−0.863). ODE/CDE-based methods (NJ-ODE, NeuralCDE) and the SDE-based LatentSDE all fall well below Neural MJD, with LatentSDE (MAE 39.8) being the closest among them. On the SafeGraph&Advan dataset, the margin over FM narrows (MAE 54.1 vs. 54.5), but Neural MJD still leads in winner-takes-all (minMAE 42.3 vs. 47.8) and probabilistic (p-MAE 53.0) metrics, and is the only method besides Neural BS that supports efficient probabilistic evaluation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends the classical Merton Jump Diffusion (MJD) SDE by replacing its fixed parameters with time-inhomogeneous functions predicted by a neural network, and extends the standard Euler-Maruyama solver for Lévy-driven SDEs with a restart mechanism that injects analytical means at discrete intervals. It departs from physics-informed neural network approaches that impose predefined SDE structures as loss constraints, instead directly parameterizing the SDE coefficients end-to-end. It also distinguishes itself from temporal point process methods (Hawkes, Poisson) that require annotated jump labels, and from classical non-stationary MJD extensions (SVJ, affine jump models) that rely on hand-designed parameter evolution and lack closed-form likelihoods. The paper's notion of 'non-stationarity'—time-varying SDE parameters—differs from the distributional-shift sense used in normalization-based deep learning methods like RevIN or DAIN.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Neural MJD offers a principled middle ground between interpretable statistical jump-diffusion models and black-box deep learning, providing closed-form likelihood evaluation, theoretical error bounds, and explicit stochastic trajectory generation without the hundreds of NFEs required by diffusion/flow-matching baselines. The Euler-Maruyama with restart solver and the super-exponential truncation error bound are practical and theoretically grounded contributions that could transfer to other SDE-based forecasting settings. Limitations include the assumption of independent (uncorrelated) multi-dimensional components, the relatively small scale of experiments (500 stocks, ~100 POIs), and the reliance on the MJD structure being appropriate for the data. Open questions include whether the approach scales to high-dimensional correlated systems, whether the jump component's benefit persists on larger or more diverse datasets, and how the method compares to more flexible Lévy process parameterizations.
