## Problem

Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) methods like LoRA are widely used to adapt large language models, yet the mechanisms governing their training dynamics and generalization remain poorly understood. Prior linearization-based analyses assumed the fine-tuned model stays close to the pretrained one but never quantified how close, leaving predictions of fine-tuning performance on unproven ground. This paper asks: under what explicit conditions does fine-tuning remain in the linearized (NTK) regime, and can the NTK spectrum at initialization predict downstream performance before any training occurs?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254388/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pretrained RoBERTa-base model, a target task dataset (CoLA, SST-2, Yelp, IMDb), a subset of layers and attention projection matrices (W_q, W_v, or W_k) selected for LoRA fine-tuning (rank r=8), and a regularization strength λ. A key practical detail is that the NTK matrix is computed from only 32 randomly sampled training examples, yielding a 32×32 kernel sketch whose condition number κ(K+σI) serves as a pre-training predictor of evaluation loss and accuracy. The outputs include the fine-tuned model, closed-form empirical risk bounds parameterized by the NTK's extreme eigenvalues, and a layer-selection score derived from spectral perturbation ratios.

## Method

The paper introduces a selective ℓ2 regularization toward the pretrained parameters (equation 8) that enforces monotonic loss decrease and provably constrains the fine-tuned model to the NTK (lazy) regime. Theorems 2–4 bound the parameter deviation ‖θ_t − θ_0‖ and the function-space gap ‖f_{θ_t}(X) − f̄_{θ̄_t}(X)‖ in terms of the Lipschitz constants of f and ∇f, the pretrained error, and λ, showing that for λ ≥ λ_∘ the linearization error grows only linearly in t rather than quadratically. Under this linearization, fine-tuning is reformulated as regularized kernel regression in the RKHS generated by the NTK, and Theorem 5 bounds the empirical risk between (σ‖Y‖/(σ+λ_max))² and (σ‖Y‖/(σ+λ_min))², making the NTK condition number a direct at-initialization performance metric. Theorems 6–7 and Corollary 1 then provide spectral perturbation bounds quantifying how adding candidate layers shifts the NTK eigenvalues, enabling a pre-training layer-selection algorithm that compares eigenvalue ratios without running any optimization. Empirically, LoRA on RoBERTa-base across four datasets confirms that the NTK condition number at initialization correlates with training loss and inversely with evaluation accuracy, and that the spectral perturbation bounds track the actual empirical risk ratios across layer choices.

## Results

On CoLA, SST-2, Yelp, and IMDb with LoRA (r=8) on RoBERTa-base, the NTK condition number at initialization strongly correlates with fine-tuning performance: lower condition numbers correspond to lower training/evaluation loss and higher accuracy (e.g., SST-2 with {Wk} of layers {0,5,11} achieves κ=450.64 and 83.60% accuracy, while {Wq,Wv} of layer {0} gives κ=5,195 and 83.48%). The regularization sweep (Table 1) shows that increasing λ from 0 to 10 reduces parameter deviation from the pretrained model (e.g., from 2.519 to 0.336 on SST-2) and reduces KL divergence from the linearized model, while evaluation accuracy remains within ~1% (91.6%–93.0% on SST-2). NTK calculation takes only 33–245 seconds versus 187–46,096 seconds for fine-tuning, making it a highly efficient at-initialization predictor of task difficulty.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against external PEFT baselines; instead, it compares different fine-tuning configurations (layer subsets, parameter types Wq/Wv vs. Wk, and regularization strengths) to validate its theoretical predictions. Within the same parameter type and layer count, the NTK condition number consistently ranks configurations by performance: for example, on SST-2, {Wk} of {0,5,11} (κ=450.64) achieves the best accuracy (83.60%), while {Wq,Wv} of {0,5,11} (κ=6,792) is worst (82.68%). The regularization sweep confirms that λ values between 0.1 and 10 maintain accuracy within the error margin (±0.5%) while substantially reducing the gap to the linearized model (KL divergence drops from 0.540 to 0.1599 on CoLA). NTK computation (33–245 s) is 5–188× faster than the corresponding fine-tuning run (187–46,096 s), demonstrating its practical value as a pre-training diagnostic. The paper notes that when the nature or count of trainable parameters changes (e.g., Wq,Wv vs. Wk), the fixed σ in the condition number formula introduces a confound, so direct cross-type comparisons are less reliable than same-type comparisons.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the NTK-based analysis of fine-tuning initiated by Jacot et al. [15] and Malladi et al. [17] by introducing an explicit ℓ2 inductive bias toward pretrained parameters, which *guarantees* the lazy/NTK regime rather than merely assuming it. Prior linearized fine-tuning works (Deshpande et al. [10], Mu et al. [11], Ortiz-Jimenez et al. [12], Jang et al. [13]) hypothesized proximity to the pretrained model but never quantified the gap between the nonlinear and linearized trajectories; this paper provides the first such bounds (Theorems 3–4). The selective regularization mechanism is adopted from Tian et al. [9], and the spectral perturbation analysis for layer selection builds on classical matrix perturbation theory applied to the NTK decomposition.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a principled, at-initialization metric (NTK condition number) for predicting fine-tuning performance and guiding layer selection before any training is executed, which could save substantial compute in PEFT design. However, the theory is restricted to squared loss and gradient descent, while experiments use cross-entropy and AdamW on a relatively small model (RoBERTa-base, 125M params), leaving open whether the NTK-spectrum predictions hold for truly large LLMs or other PEFT methods beyond LoRA. The authors also note that the condition number comparison is confounded when the nature or count of trainable parameters changes, since σ is fixed; incorporating σ adaptively into layer selection is flagged as future work.
