## Problem

Diffusion planners for offline RL are fundamentally limited by the coverage and horizon of their training data: they cannot generate coherent plans significantly longer than the maximum trajectory length observed during training, and they fail to generalize to tasks requiring novel compositions of behaviors. Existing trajectory stitching approaches rely on extrinsic rewards for segment selection and struggle to maintain dynamic consistency at stitching points, leaving a gap in reward-free, long-horizon data augmentation for diffusion-based planning.

## Input / Output

SCoTS takes an offline trajectory dataset of state-action sequences (e.g., short 4-cell maze segments in OGBench Stitch or noisy exploratory trajectories in Explore) and produces an augmented dataset of approximately 5M transitions containing longer, more diverse trajectories. The augmented data is then used to train a Hierarchical Diffusion Planner that, given a start state and goal state, generates temporally dense trajectory plans with horizons up to 1001 steps (versus 101–401 on original data), which are executed by a low-level value-based controller (GCIQL or CRL) at a tunable horizon of 5–25 steps.

## Method

SCoTS is a three-stage, reward-free trajectory augmentation pipeline. First, it learns a temporal distance-preserving embedding φ (a 32-dim MLP trained with an HILP-style expectile loss) that maps states to a latent space where L2 distance approximates optimal temporal distance, enabling efficient retrieval of temporally reachable segments via a Faiss IVF index. Second, it iteratively stitches segments by sampling a random unit latent direction z per trajectory, retrieving top-k=10 candidate segments near the current trajectory endpoint, and selecting the best candidate using a composite score S_j = P_j + βN_j that balances directional progress (inner product of segment direction with z) and novelty (a particle-based density estimator measuring distance to previously visited latent states, with β=2). Third, a diffusion-based stitcher (DiT1D, horizon 26) refines each stitching point by generating intermediate states conditioned on the boundary states of adjacent segments, ensuring dynamic consistency; actions are then inferred via a trained inverse dynamics model. The resulting augmented dataset is used to train a Hierarchical Diffusion Planner (DiT1D, 8 blocks, hidden dim 256) with DDIM sampling, which outperforms all baselines by 20+ points on average across OGBench tasks.

## Results

On the OGBench locomotion benchmark (PointMaze and AntMaze, Stitch and Explore datasets, Medium/Large/Giant sizes), SCoTS achieves a 96.8% average success rate, substantially outperforming the best diffusion-based baseline Compositional Diffuser (CD) at 77.9% and the un-augmented Hierarchical Diffuser (HD) at 25.4%. The advantage is most pronounced on the hardest Giant environments: SCoTS reaches 100% on PointMaze-Giant-Stitch (vs. 0% for HD, 68% for CD) and 87% on AntMaze-Giant-Stitch (vs. 0% for HD, 65% for CD). SCoTS-augmented data also boosts offline GCRL algorithms, raising HIQL from 36.4% to 73.4% average success rate and CRL from 8.4% to 27.9%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SCoTS decisively outperforms all nine baselines across the OGBench suite. Against the closest diffusion-based competitor, Compositional Diffuser (CD, Luo et al. 2025), SCoTS gains +18.9 points on average (96.8% vs. 77.9%), with the gap widening to +32 points on AntMaze-Giant-Stitch (87% vs. 65%) and +71 points on AntMaze-Large-Explore (98% vs. 27%). The un-augmented Hierarchical Diffuser (HD, Chen et al. 2024) drops to 0% on all Giant environments, while SCoTS maintains 87–100%, demonstrating that the augmentation is the critical factor. Among GCRL baselines, QRL (36.5%) and HIQL (36.4%) are the strongest but still trail SCoTS by ~60 points on average; GCIQL (12.6%) and CRL (8.4%) are far weaker. SCoTS also surpasses Generative Skill Chaining (GSC, Mishra et al. 2023) by +31.5 points on average, with particularly large margins on Explore tasks (98% vs. 21% on AntMaze-Large-Explore). When SCoTS-augmented data is used to retrain GCRL algorithms (Table 2), HIQL improves from 36.4% to 73.4% and CRL from 8.4% to 27.9%, both exceeding the SynthER augmentation baseline (13.9% and 8.1% respectively), confirming that trajectory-level stitching augmentation is more effective than transition-level synthetic replay.

## Relation to Prior Work

SCoTS sits at the intersection of three research threads: diffusion-based trajectory planning (Diffuser, Hierarchical Diffuser), reward-free trajectory composition (GSC, Compositional Diffuser), and temporal distance-preserving representations (HILP, QRL, METRA). It departs from prior stitching methods (GSC, CD, DiffStitch) by operating at the data-augmentation stage rather than test-time composition, and by being entirely reward-free—using intrinsic temporal structure and novelty rather than extrinsic reward signals for segment selection. The temporal distance-preserving embedding is repurposed from its original role in skill discovery and goal-reaching to serve as a retrieval mechanism for temporally viable stitching candidates, a use not explored in prior work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SCoTS addresses a fundamental bottleneck for diffusion planners—their effective horizon and generalization are capped by training data coverage—by systematically generating longer, more diverse trajectories without requiring new environment interaction or reward signals. The finding that SCoTS-augmented data also substantially boosts GCRL algorithms (e.g., HIQL average 36.4%→73.4%) suggests the augmented trajectories encode broadly useful dynamic structure, not just diffusion-specific features. Key limitations include significant computational overhead (multiple model trainings plus iterative stitching), inability to handle asymmetric temporal distances (irreversible actions, disconnected MDPs), and evaluation restricted to relatively simple locomotion environments. Open questions include whether the approach scales to high-dimensional manipulation tasks and whether the compute cost can be reduced.
