## Problem

Prior work characterizing the statistical and computational complexity of learning single-index models was restricted to Gaussian inputs and relied on Hermite polynomial expansions, leaving unclear why certain algorithms (SGD, landscape smoothing, partial trace) achieve optimal rates and what role the Gaussian assumption actually plays. This paper identifies the problem's intrinsic rotational symmetry as the governing structure, showing that spherical harmonics—not Hermite polynomials—are the natural basis, and extends the complexity theory to arbitrary spherically symmetric input distributions where new sample-runtime trade-offs emerge.

## Input / Output

The method consumes m i.i.d. samples (y_i, x_i) where x_i = r_i z_i is the polar decomposition of a spherically symmetric input (r_i is the radius, z_i is the direction on S^{d-1}), and the label y_i may depend on both r_i and the directional projection ⟨w_*, z_i⟩. The output is a unit vector ŵ ∈ S^{d-1} achieving weak recovery |⟨ŵ, w_*⟩| ≥ 1/4, with non-asymptotic guarantees for fixed link distributions ν_d. Unlike the Gaussian case where the label depends only on ⟨w_*, x⟩, here the conditional distribution ν_d(·|R,Z) is allowed to depend on the ambient dimension d.

## Method

The key insight is to decompose the likelihood ratio into irreducible subspaces V_{d,ℓ} of degree-ℓ spherical harmonics (Gegenbauer polynomials), which are the irreducible representations of the orthogonal group O_d. For each harmonic subspace, the paper constructs three families of estimators: spectral algorithms for ℓ ∈ {1,2} that achieve both optimal sample and runtime complexity, a harmonic tensor unfolding algorithm for ℓ ≥ 3 that achieves optimal sample complexity by computing the top singular vector of an unfolded empirical tensor built from reproducing harmonic tensors, and an online SGD algorithm for ℓ ≥ 3 that achieves optimal runtime. The optimal algorithm is selected by identifying the degree ℓ minimizing the corresponding complexity term, and the paper proves that in general the sample-optimal and runtime-optimal degrees can differ—implying no single estimator simultaneously achieves both optimal complexities, a phenomenon absent in the Gaussian setting where both are always jointly achievable at ℓ ∈ {1,2}.

## Results

For Gaussian SIMs with generative exponent k*, the paper establishes that the optimal sample complexity is m = Θ(d^{k*/2}) and optimal runtime is T = Θ(d^{k*/2+1} log d), achieved by a spectral algorithm on degree-1 or 2 spherical harmonics. For general spherical SIMs with harmonic degree ℓ ≥ 3, a fundamental trade-off emerges: harmonic tensor unfolding achieves optimal sample m ≈ d^{ℓ/2}/‖ξ_{d,ℓ}‖² but suboptimal runtime T ≈ d^{ℓ+1}/‖ξ_{d,ℓ}‖² log d, while online SGD achieves optimal runtime T ≈ d^{ℓ}/‖ξ_{d,ℓ}‖² but suboptimal sample m ≈ d^{ℓ-1}/‖ξ_{d,ℓ}‖². The paper further shows that for non-Gaussian spherical SIMs, the sample-optimal degree l_{m,*} and runtime-optimal degree l_{T,*} can differ arbitrarily (l_{m,*} ≫ l_{T,*}), implying no single estimator can simultaneously achieve both optimal complexities.

|---|---|---|---|

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theory paper with no empirical baselines; comparisons are between algorithmic approaches and their complexity guarantees. For Gaussian SIMs, the paper's spectral algorithm recovers the optimal rates of the partial trace estimator [Damian et al., COLT 2024] (m = Θ(d^{k*/2}), T = Θ(d^{k*/2+1} log d)), while providing a unified explanation: both methods exploit low-frequency harmonics (degree 1 or 2 in z = x/‖x‖₂). The vanilla SGD of [Ben Arous et al., JMLR 2021] is shown to be suboptimal (m = Õ(d^{k*-1}), T = Õ(d^{k*})) because it is dominated by high-frequency harmonics V_{d,k*} and fails to exploit the radial component r = ‖x‖₂. The landscape smoothing method [Damian et al., NeurIPS 2024] achieves near-optimal rates by reweighting toward low-frequency harmonics. The key new contribution is showing that for non-Gaussian spherical SIMs, the sample-optimal and runtime-optimal degrees can differ (l_{m,*} > l_{T,*}), a phenomenon absent in the Gaussian case where both are always jointly achievable at ℓ ∈ {1,2}.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reinterprets the Gaussian single-index model complexity framework of Damian et al. [29]—which was built on Hermite polynomial expansions—through the lens of spherical harmonics (Gegenbauer polynomials), arguing that rotational symmetry, not Gaussianity, is the governing structure. It extends the sharp characterization from Gaussian inputs to arbitrary spherically symmetric distributions, introducing a 'spherical SIM' framework. The algorithmic toolkit draws from tensor PCA (Montanari & Richard [67] for tensor unfolding) and online SGD analysis (Ben Arous et al. [10], Zweig et al. [88]), but repurposes these via harmonic tensors rather than Hermite tensors, yielding new estimators and new sample-runtime trade-offs absent in the Gaussian case.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals that for Gaussian SIMs, optimal algorithms always operate in degree-1 or 2 harmonic subspaces (not degree k*), and that SGD's suboptimality stems from ignoring the radial component—a conceptual clarification that unifies and explains prior results on landscape smoothing and partial trace. More broadly, it shows that beyond the Gaussian setting, sample-optimal and runtime-optimal degrees can diverge, implying no single estimator achieves both complexities simultaneously; this is a new phenomenon with no Gaussian analogue. Open questions include whether a single algorithm can close the gap when l_{m,*} = l_{T,*} > 2, and how the framework extends to multi-index models where higher-order harmonics become essential.
