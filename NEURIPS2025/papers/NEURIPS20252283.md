## Problem

Concept Bottleneck Models (CBMs) assume that human interventions are always correct and that humans know when to intervene, both of which are unrealistic given human error-proneness and variable sub-expertise. Existing Learning to Defer (L2D) methods address deferral but are opaque and limited to single-task settings, offering no interpretable explanation for why a deferral was made. This paper bridges the gap by introducing Deferring CBMs (DCBMs), the first interpretable-by-design deferring system that learns when human intervention is beneficial even when the human is inaccurate.

## Input / Output

The model takes RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10-H, variable resolution for CUB-200-2011) and, at training time, human expert predictions on both concepts and the final task. A frozen encoder (ResNet-34 or WideResNet) produces a latent representation; concept predictors (3-layer MLPs) then output either a binary concept value or a deferral symbol ⊥, and a task classifier (another 3-layer MLP) outputs either a class label or ⊥. The deferring system Δ resolves each ⊥ by substituting the corresponding human prediction, so the final output is always a concrete concept/task value, with the deferral decision itself serving as an interpretable signal about which concepts or tasks required human help.

## Method

DCBMs are formulated as a Bayesian Network in which each concept and task variable is governed by a separate deferring system (model M_V, human H_V), with M_V outputting n_V+1 classes (the n_V possible values plus ⊥). The training loss is derived as the negative log-likelihood of this graphical model, yielding a per-variable surrogate with two terms: a cross-entropy on the ground-truth class and a cross-entropy on the deferral class weighted by an indicator that the human was correct. A penalized variant introduces a hyperparameter λ ∈ [0,1] that trades off deferral cost against prediction accuracy, and the paper proves (via a sum-of-consistent-losses lemma) that both the cost-free and penalized losses are consistent surrogates for the intractable multivariate zero-one L2D loss. Consistency requires independent training of concept and task predictors with disjoint parameters; in practice, a shared encoder is trained first and then frozen, after which concept and task classifiers are trained separately using the Asymmetric Softmax parameterization of the negative log-likelihood.

## Results

On cifar10-h (incomplete concepts), DCBM achieves ~98.4% task accuracy at λ=0 with ~81.6% task coverage, correctly deferring only cat and deer instances to the human. On CUB with oracle human concepts, DCBM outperforms both the standard CBM and black-box baselines for low deferral costs (λ < 0.05), with performance converging to the CBM baseline as λ increases. On CUB with uncertain human annotations, DCBM learns to retain ~20% concept coverage even at zero deferral cost (CovConc ≈ 0.2), demonstrating it correctly identifies when the human is not better than the model. The ablated DCBM-NT (no task deferral) plateaus at ~90% on cifar10-h, confirming that task-level deferral is essential for incomplete concept sets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the standard CBM baseline, DCBM consistently outperforms on both CUB and cifar10-h for low deferral costs, with the gap closing as λ increases and the model defers less; at high costs (λ ≈ 0.3+), DCBM still retains a small advantage over CBM on cifar10-h. Against the black-box (BB) baseline, DCBM achieves higher task accuracy at low λ by leveraging concept-level deferral. Against the UCP intervention strategy (Shin et al., 2023), DCBM-NT clearly wins on CUB with uncertain humans: at λ=0, UCP defers on ~33% of concepts (CovConc ≈ 0.665) regardless of whether the human is correct, while DCBM-NT retains ~80% coverage (CovConc ≈ 0.800), correctly avoiding deferral on concepts where the human is likely wrong. In the controlled synthetic experiment (Tables 4–5), DCBM-NT achieves near-perfect behavior (CovConc ≈ 0 when human is always correct; CovConc = 1.0 when human is always wrong), whereas UCP fails in both regimes, deferring suboptimally in each case.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate research threads: Concept Bottleneck Models (Koh et al., 2020), which ground predictions in human-interpretable concepts, and Learning to Defer (Madras et al., 2018; Mozannar & Sontag, 2020), which trains classifiers to delegate uncertain instances to human experts. The key departure is from standard CBMs, which assume human interventions are infallible and must be manually triggered, and from standard L2D, which is opaque and single-task. By formulating DCBMs as a Bayesian Network with per-variable deferring systems, the authors derive a multivariate surrogate loss from maximum likelihood and prove its consistency with the intractable zero-one loss, extending the univariate L2D consistency results of Mozannar & Sontag to the structured multi-concept setting. The work also contrasts with concept intervention strategies (Shin et al., 2023; Zarlenga et al., 2023) that require humans to initiate corrections, whereas DCBMs autonomously decide when to request intervention.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DCBMs provide the first interpretable-by-design deferring framework, enabling practitioners to audit *why* a model deferred a prediction by inspecting which concepts triggered the deferral. This addresses a practical gap in human-AI deployment where experts are fallible, have sub-specialties, and incur labor costs—assumptions that standard CBMs ignore. Open questions remain: the framework assumes independent concepts and a single expert per variable, does not account for distribution shift in human behavior at test time, and lacks user studies validating that the deferral explanations are actually useful to humans. Extending to hierarchical concept structures, multi-expert settings with heterogeneous costs, and continual learning under human drift are natural next steps.
