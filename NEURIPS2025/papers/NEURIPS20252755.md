## Problem

Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) are widely used to decompose LLM activations into interpretable features, but the paper identifies a previously unrecognized failure mode: feature absorption, where a seemingly monosemantic latent (e.g., 'starts with S') silently fails to fire on arbitrary positive examples because the feature direction has been absorbed into a more specific child latent (e.g., 'short'). This creates an interpretability illusion that undermines the reliability of SAE latents as classifiers and complicates circuit discovery.

## Input / Output

The analysis operates on residual stream activations extracted at specific token positions from ICL prompts (e.g., '{token} has the first letter: {letter}') across Gemma-2-2B, Qwen2 0.5B, and Llama 3.2 1B, paired with pre-trained Gemma Scope SAEs (16k and 65k width) and custom-trained L1/TopK SAEs. Outputs include a per-letter feature absorption rate (fraction of LR-probe true positives where the main latent fails to fire and a single absorbing latent dominates the ablation effect), k-sparse probing F1 curves for feature-split detection, and per-latent ablation scores. The absorption metric is restricted to layers 0–17 in Gemma-2-2B because attention moves first-letter information to the final token position around layer 18, beyond which source-token ablations have no causal effect.

## Method

The paper provides a formal δ-absorption parameterization proving that for two hierarchically related features (child ⊂ parent), increasing the absorption parameter δ from 0 to 1 maintains perfect reconstruction while strictly decreasing the L1 sparsity loss whenever the child co-occurs with the parent, making absorption the loss-minimizing solution. Empirically, the authors develop a three-stage detection metric: (1) identify feature splits via k-sparse probing (F1 jump > 0.03 when adding a latent), (2) collect false-negative tokens where all split latents are inactive but the LR probe classifies correctly, and (3) run integrated-gradients ablation on those tokens to confirm a single latent with probe cosine similarity > 0.025 and ablation effect > 1.0 above the runner-up is causally responsible. Validation across hundreds of Gemma Scope SAEs and custom SAEs on three model families shows absorption increases with SAE width and sparsity (lower L0), and persists in TopK and Batch-TopK architectures, confirming it is a fundamental consequence of sparse optimization over hierarchical features rather than an artifact of L1 loss.

## Results

On the first-letter identification task, no Gemma Scope SAE latent matches the F1 score of a standard LR probe trained on the same residual stream activations, with SAE latents showing wide variance in precision/recall depending on L0 (low L0 yields high-precision/low-recall latents; high L0 yields the opposite). In a detailed case study, the 'starts with S' latent (id 6510) achieves F1 = 0.81 but silently fails to fire on the token _short, where a token-aligned latent (id 1085, cosine similarity 0.12 with the probe) instead carries the first-letter information with ~5× the activation magnitude. Feature absorption is detected in every SAE tested across Gemma Scope (16k and 65k widths), Qwen2 0.5B, and Llama 3.2 1B, with absorption rate increasing monotonically with both SAE width and sparsity (L0). Toy model experiments confirm that hierarchical feature co-occurrence provably decreases SAE loss via absorption, and the phenomenon persists in TopK and Batch TopK SAE architectures despite the absence of an explicit L1 penalty.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is a logistic regression (LR) probe trained directly on residual stream activations [1]. Across all Gemma Scope SAEs (16k and 65k widths, layers 0–25), no SAE latent matches the LR probe's F1 score on first-letter classification; the gap is most pronounced at intermediate L0 values where SAE latents show the worst precision-recall trade-off. The second comparison is across SAE architectures: the paper trains both standard L1-loss SAEs and TopK SAEs [10] on Llama 3.2 1B and Qwen2 0.5B, and finds that feature absorption occurs in both, indicating the phenomenon is not an artifact of the L1 sparsity penalty but is more fundamental to sparse dictionary learning with hierarchical features. In the toy model, the SAE with independent features perfectly recovers all four true features (cosine similarity ≈ 1.0), while introducing a single hierarchical dependency (feature 1 fires only when feature 0 fires) immediately produces the absorption pattern in both encoder and decoder, with the parent latent's encoder developing a 'hole' in its firing pattern.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the SAE interpretability literature by identifying a problematic variant of feature splitting (first described by Bricken et al.) that prior work on SAE precision/recall noted but did not explain mechanistically. It builds on k-sparse probing (Gurnee et al.) and SAE latent ablation (Marks et al.) as diagnostic tools, and on the linear representation hypothesis and superposition framework to formalize when and why SAEs fail. The key departure is showing that the sparsity penalty itself—rather than insufficient width or poor training—causes a specific failure mode (absorption) whenever features form a hierarchy, which prior SAE analyses treated as a hyperparameter tuning issue.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Feature absorption undermines a core assumption in the SAE interpretability pipeline: that a latent which appears to track a concept is a reliable classifier for it. This has direct implications for safety-critical applications (bias detection, deception monitoring) and for circuit discovery, where the number of latents needed to characterize behavior may be far larger than expected. The paper's main limitation is that its absorption metric relies on ablation and thus cannot be applied past the layer where attention moves task information to the output token, and the empirical evidence is concentrated on a single task (first-letter identification). Open questions include whether Meta-SAEs, attribution dictionary learning, or structured sparsity (group lasso, hierarchical sparse coding) can resolve absorption, and whether the phenomenon generalizes beyond the letter-spelling hierarchy studied here.
