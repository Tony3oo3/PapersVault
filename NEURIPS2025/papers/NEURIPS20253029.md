## Problem

The rapid adoption of LLMs as judges (LLJs) for NLG evaluation has outpaced rigorous scrutiny of their validity as measurement instruments. Existing work has focused primarily on reliability—consistency across repeated evaluations or robustness to prompt perturbations—while leaving validity, i.e., whether LLJs actually capture the intended constructs, largely unexamined. This position paper argues that current enthusiasm around LLJs is premature and identifies four core assumptions underlying their use that may be fundamentally challenged by inherent LLM limitations and inconsistent NLG evaluation practices.

## Input / Output

The paper takes as its analytical subject the existing LLJ literature and evaluation practices across three applications: text summarization, data annotation, and safety alignment. It produces a structured critique organized around four assumptions (proxy for human judgment, evaluator capability, scalability, and cost-effectiveness), grounded in concrete examples of inconsistencies in how evaluation criteria are operationalized, documented biases, and adversarial vulnerabilities. The output is a set of recommendations for responsible LLJ evaluation practices rather than a new model or benchmark.

## Method

The paper applies measurement theory from the social sciences—specifically the four-level concept-to-observation framework of Adcock and Collier and the construct validity/reliability taxonomy of Jacobs and Wallach—to systematically evaluate LLJs as measurement models. Through a qualitative review of commonly cited LLJ works, the authors identify four recurring assumptions and assess each against the seven dimensions of construct validity (face, content, convergent, discriminant, predictive, hypothesis, and consequential validity) and construct reliability. The analysis is grounded in three concrete applications: text summarization (examining how SummEval criteria are inconsistently operationalized across papers), data annotation (questioning content validity for highly subjective tasks like hate speech detection), and safety alignment (exposing discriminant and predictive validity failures through adversarial robustness and competitive benchmarking). The key insight is that convergent validity with human judgment is insufficient when the human judgments themselves lack standardization, and that reliability-focused work (position bias, verbosity bias) does not address whether LLJs measure the right construct at all.

## Results

This is a position paper with no quantitative experimental results. Its central finding is qualitative: the authors identify four core assumptions behind LLM-as-a-judge (LLJ) adoption—proxy for human judgment, capable evaluator, scalable evaluator, and cost-effective evaluator—and argue each is undermined by documented limitations. The most concrete empirical illustration comes from the SummEval benchmark, where the authors show that across three papers using LLJs, only one correctly uses the original fluency definition, one omits it entirely, and another introduces its own interpretation, while evaluation scales range from 1–3 to 1–100 and comparison modes shift between absolute, pairwise, and relative. The paper further cites evidence that LLM safety judges can be tricked into misclassifying up to 100% of harmful generations as harmless through simple prompt variations (Eiras et al.), and that self-enhancement bias and preference leakage inflate scores when judge and evaluated model share a family.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not propose a new method to benchmark against baselines. Instead, it critically examines the validation practices of existing LLJ approaches. The authors argue that the dominant validation strategy—convergent validity via correlation with human judgments—is itself compromised because human annotation practices in NLG lack standardization (Howcroft et al. show inconsistent criteria definitions across 20 years of work; Zhou et al. document ambiguity in expertise requirements and benchmark repurposing). They contrast this with the reliability literature (position bias, verbosity bias, adversarial robustness) which has been more thoroughly studied, and highlight that construct validity dimensions (content, discriminant, predictive, consequential) remain largely unaddressed. The paper positions LLJs against the historical trajectory of Amazon Mechanical Turk, arguing that the same short-term cost savings that initially justified AMT adoption later gave way to quality degradation and ethical concerns, and warns that LLJs may follow a similar path.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper repurposes measurement theory from the social sciences (Adcock & Collier; Jacobs & Wallach) to systematically critique the rapidly growing LLM-as-a-judge (LLJ) literature, which has largely validated itself through convergent validity (correlation with human judgments) and construct reliability (test-retest consistency). It departs from prior LLJ work by arguing that the human-judgment 'gold standard' itself suffers from well-documented inconsistencies in conceptualization and operationalization (Howcroft et al.; Zhou et al.), thereby undermining the validity foundation on which LLJs are typically justified. The paper also connects to the broader NLG evaluation literature on benchmark manipulation, data contamination, and the competitive benchmarking problem, positioning LLJs as both inheriting and amplifying these pre-existing issues rather than resolving them.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central argument—that LLJ adoption has outpaced rigorous scrutiny of their validity as measurement instruments—carries significant implications for how the field interprets benchmark scores, allocates research attention, and designs alignment pipelines. It raises open questions about how to properly validate LLJs when the reference standard (human judgment) is itself unreliable, how to standardize evaluation criteria and operationalization across the community, and how to account for non-financial costs (economic displacement of annotators, environmental impact, societal bias reproduction). The AMT analogy serves as a caution: a tool initially celebrated for scalability and cost-effectiveness can accumulate hidden quality and ethical debts that only surface after widespread adoption.
