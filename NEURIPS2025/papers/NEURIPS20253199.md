## Problem

Existing pansharpening methods—whether model-driven or deep learning—fail to explicitly model the underlying electro-optical imaging process (continuous radiance formation, optical filtering, and sensor responsivity), leading to spectral aberrations, spatial artifacts, and sensor-specific biases that limit real-world applicability. This paper asks whether pansharpening can be reformulated as a physics-grounded inverse problem that faithfully emulates the sensor's forward imaging pipeline.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253199/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a low-resolution multispectral image (e.g., 8 bands for WorldView-3, 4 bands for GaoFen-2) and a co-registered high-resolution panchromatic image, and produces a high-resolution multispectral image at PAN resolution. Unusually, the network internally represents the scene as a continuous radiance field L(x,y,λ) parameterized over both spatial coordinates and wavelength (normalized to [-1,1] per band), rather than operating directly on discrete band tensors. The final output is obtained by integrating this radiance field through learnable per-band spectral responsivity functions, with a residual connection to the upsampled MS image to handle scale alignment.

## Method

PINO comprises three stages: (1) a bidirectional dilation transformer encoder with parallel spatial (dilated self-attention) and spectral (grouped channel self-attention) branches aggregates multi-granularity features from the upsampled MS and PAN inputs; (2) an iterative Galerkin-type kernel integral operator, fed with Fourier-encoded spatial coordinates and wavelengths, learns a continuous radiance field over the joint (x, y, λ) domain, effectively emulating band-wise spectral integration through N successive integral updates; (3) learnable sigmoid-constrained MLPs parameterize each band's spectral responsivity R_b(λ), which modulates the radiance field to produce the final fused pixel values. Training follows a two-stage protocol: the encoder is first pretrained with L1+SSIM loss, then the full framework is fine-tuned with L1+histogram loss to preserve local statistical consistency under uniform pixel sampling. The key differentiator is that the neural operator explicitly encodes the camera forward model (radiance → spectral integration → responsivity weighting) as a differentiable computation, making the learned responsivity functions physically interpretable and sensor-specific.

## Results

On the WV3 reduced-resolution benchmark, PINO achieves the best PSNR of 39.38 dB, SAM of 2.845°, ERGAS of 2.087, Q2n of 0.922, and SCC of 0.988, outperforming the closest competitor ADWM (39.17 dB PSNR) by ~0.2 dB. On GF2, the gains are more pronounced: PINO reaches 44.71 dB PSNR and 0.615° SAM, surpassing LFormer (44.20 dB, 0.648°) and ADWM (43.88 dB, 0.672°). In full-resolution no-reference evaluation, PINO attains the highest HQNR of 0.954 (WV3) and 0.967 (GF2) with the lowest spectral distortion Dλ of 0.014 and 0.016 respectively. Cross-sensor transfer to unseen WV2 data (no fine-tuning) yields 31.51 dB PSNR, again the best among all baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PINO outperforms all 11 baselines (3 classical + 8 deep learning) on every metric for both WV3 and GF2 in both reduced and full resolution settings. The closest competitors are ADWM (CVPR 2025) and LFormer (ACM MM 2024): on WV3, PINO leads ADWM by 0.21 dB PSNR and 0.07° SAM, and leads LFormer by 0.31 dB PSNR; on GF2, the margin over LFormer widens to 0.51 dB PSNR and 0.03° SAM. In the cross-sensor generalization test (WV3-trained model applied to unseen WV2 without fine-tuning), PINO achieves 31.51 dB PSNR vs. 30.27 dB for ADWM and 30.08 dB for LFormer, a gap of ~1.2–1.4 dB. On the CAVE hyperspectral fusion benchmark (×4 and ×8), PINO attains 52.36 dB PSNR and 1.857° SAM at ×4, surpassing QIS (52.22 dB, 1.977°) and 3DT-Net (51.38 dB, 2.161°), and 50.22 dB PSNR at ×8, beating QIS (49.44 dB) and 3DT-Net (48.99 dB). Classical methods (BDSD-PC, BT-H, LRTCFPan) trail all deep learning baselines by 5–11 dB in PSNR, confirming the substantial gap between model-driven and learned approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

PINO sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) implicit neural representations for continuous signal recovery (LIIF, UltraSR, SSIF), which encode spatial-spectral content via coordinate-conditioned MLPs; (2) neural operators with Galerkin-type integral kernels (Cao 2021), repurposed here as an iterative mechanism to approximate the band-wise spectral integral of the radiance field; and (3) early physics-informed pansharpening efforts (SSMNet, FBS-PS) that incorporate partial sensor properties like band-separability. The key departure is that PINO models the *full* electro-optical imaging pipeline—continuous radiance formation, spectral filtering via learnable responsivity functions, and discretization—within a single differentiable framework, rather than learning a direct LRMS+PAN→HRMS mapping or applying only partial physical constraints.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By grounding pansharpening in the sensor's forward imaging model, PINO offers a principled path toward sensor-agnostic fusion: the learned spectral responsivity curves visually match real sensor profiles without explicit supervision, and the WV3-trained model transfers to unseen WV2 data with a ~1.2 dB PSNR advantage over the best baseline without fine-tuning. However, the paper acknowledges that arbitrary-resolution inference, explicit cross-sensor training, and adaptive (non-uniform) spectral sampling remain unexplored, and the computational cost of the iterative integral operator limits scalability. For the broader field, the work suggests that embedding the physical measurement model as a differentiable layer—rather than as a post-hoc constraint—could become a general design pattern for computational imaging tasks beyond pansharpening.
