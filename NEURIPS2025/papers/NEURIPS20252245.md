## Problem

Existing 3D face reconstruction methods either rely on 3D Morphable Models that constrain geometry to fixed low-dimensional shape priors with a bias toward mean shape, or use optimization-based approaches that are computationally expensive (minutes to hours per reconstruction). Learned Vertex Descent (LVD) offered a faster hybrid alternative but predicted vertex trajectories independently without explicit global structural guidance, leaving the model to resolve spatial coherence implicitly through 2D features alone.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252245/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes one or more RGB images, each paired with a binary head mask and calibrated camera parameters, and produces a 3D face mesh with a fixed topology of 7,225 vertices and 14,342 faces. For single-view input, GLVD operates directly in camera coordinates, eliminating the need for camera calibration at test time; for multi-view input, it canonicalizes the reconstruction and predicts camera poses. Inference runs 10 iterative update steps, completing in ~0.2 s (single view) to ~0.25 s (three views) on an RTX 4090, compared to 90–1200 s for prior optimization-based methods.

## Method

GLVD introduces a two-branch iterative architecture that fuses local per-vertex neural fields with global structural guidance from dynamically predicted 3D keypoints. The keypoint branch combines HRNet facial landmark heatmaps with Hourglass-extracted image features to predict 3D displacements for a sparse set of K keypoints (default 18), while the vertex branch uses a 4-stack Hourglass network to extract pixel-aligned features and a 3-layer MLP to predict per-vertex displacements. The central innovation is a relative spatial encoding: at each iteration, every vertex is encoded by its displacement vectors relative to the current keypoint estimates (P = k^t − v_i^t), conditioning local geometry updates on the evolving global structure. Both branches are updated in parallel each iteration, and the network is trained with a combined directional (cosine) and magnitude loss, augmented by keypoint noise injection, binary dropout on 2D features, and SDF pre-training of the feature encoder to improve convergence.

## Results

GLVD achieves a surface error of 1.25 mm on 3DFAW (1 view) and 1.22 mm (3 views), outperforming all 3DMM-based methods (best: 3DDFA-V3 at 1.45 mm) and model-free feed-forward methods (best: JIFF at 1.48 mm). On H3DS 2.0, GLVD reaches 1.36 mm (1 view) and 1.33–1.34 mm (3–6 views), surpassing LVD (1.39–1.45 mm) and JIFF (1.79–1.85 mm), while remaining competitive with the optimization-based SIRA++ (1.04–1.57 mm) and H3D-Net (1.21–1.44 mm). Inference time is 0.2 s for single-view and 0.25 s for three-view reconstruction, over two orders of magnitude faster than SIRA++ (90–191 s) and H3D-Net (600–1200 s).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GLVD outperforms all 3DMM-based baselines (DECA, MICA, FaceVerse, FaceScape, HRN, 3DDFA-V3, VHAP) by a clear margin in single-view settings, with the best 3DMM method (3DDFA-V3) trailing by 0.20 mm on 3DFAW. Against model-free feed-forward methods, GLVD beats PIFU by 0.94 mm and JIFF by 0.23 mm on 3DFAW single-view, and by 0.62 mm and 0.49 mm respectively on H3DS single-view. Compared to its direct predecessor LVD, GLVD improves by 0.33 mm on 3DFAW 1-view and by 0.03–0.12 mm across H3DS multi-view settings. In multi-view H3DS, the optimization-based SIRA++ still leads GLVD by 0.15–0.30 mm (e.g., 1.04 vs. 1.34 mm at 6 views), and H3D-Net is competitive at 6 views (1.21 vs. 1.34 mm), but GLVD is 360×–2400× faster in inference (0.25 s vs. 191 s for SIRA++, 0.25 s vs. 1200 s for H3D-Net). Overall, GLVD achieves the best single-view accuracy among all compared methods while maintaining near-optimization-level multi-view quality at a fraction of the computational cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

GLVD extends Learned Vertex Descent (LVD) [11], which uses pixel-aligned image features to guide iterative template fitting for 3D human shape, by adding a global structural guidance branch that dynamically predicts 3D facial keypoints and encodes each vertex's position relative to them. The relative spatial encoding draws on ideas from SMPlicit [12] and KeypointNeRF [39], while the per-vertex neural field component inherits from the pixel-aligned implicit function lineage (PIFU, JIFF). Unlike 3DMM-based methods that constrain geometry to a fixed low-dimensional subspace, GLVD operates on a fixed-topology mesh without parametric shape priors, and unlike pure optimization-based methods (SIRA++, H3D-Net), it learns the iterative update function end-to-end, trading test-time optimization for learned displacement prediction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GLVD achieves state-of-the-art single-view 3D face reconstruction (1.25 mm on 3DFAW) with 0.2 s inference, roughly two orders of magnitude faster than the closest optimization-based competitors (SIRA++ at 90 s, H3D-Net at 600 s), making high-fidelity face reconstruction viable for real-time AR/VR and interactive applications. The method is topology-agnostic and does not require calibrated cameras at test time in the single-view setting, broadening its applicability. Key limitations include sensitivity to self-occlusion at extreme viewing angles (error nearly doubles from 0° to 90°), dependence on keypoint estimation accuracy, and the current focus on neutral expressions. Open directions include temporal consistency for video, expression modeling, and topology-adaptive strategies for more complex geometry.
