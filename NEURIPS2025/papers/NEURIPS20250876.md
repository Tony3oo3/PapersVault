## Problem

State-of-the-art alignment methods (RLHF, DPO) fit a single Bradley-Terry reward model to pairwise comparisons from a heterogeneous user population, effectively optimizing for a 'mythical representative user.' It is unclear whether this approach even achieves high average utility across real users, since ordinal comparisons from diverse users may not contain enough information to identify the utility-maximizing policy. The paper formalizes this gap through 'distortion'—the worst-case ratio between the optimal achievable average utility and the average utility of the learned policy—and asks which alignment methods minimize this distortion.

## Input / Output

Each alignment method receives n users' pairwise preference comparisons (d comparisons per user, with pairs sampled from a distribution μ over alternatives), a reference policy π_ref, and a KL divergence bound τ. Users' comparisons are generated by individual Bradley-Terry models with temperature β, so the input is purely ordinal and heterogeneous—no user labels or cardinal utilities are observed. The output is a policy distribution over alternatives constrained to the KL-ball B_τ(π_ref), and the paper's deliverable is tight distortion bounds (competitive ratios) characterizing how far the output policy's average utility can fall from the optimum.

## Method

The core technical tool is a linearization lemma that sandwiches the Bradley-Terry win probability σ(β(u(x)−u(y))) between two affine functions of the utilities, with slope constants L=σ′(0)=1/4 and ℓ_β=(σ(β)−1/2)/β, enabling expected win-rates to be bounded by linear expressions in AvgUtil. For NLHF, the policy is computed as a Nash equilibrium in a symmetric two-player zero-sum game over win-rate margins with both players restricted to the KL-ball; the zero-sum structure (game value = 0) guarantees that a single application of the linearization lemma suffices, yielding the minimax-optimal distortion of (1/2+o(1))·β robustly across all utility distributions, sampling distributions, and KL budgets. In contrast, RLHF/DPO (equivalent to Borda voting) require two nested applications of the lemma, incurring a squared factor, and suffer exponential e^Ω(β) or unbounded distortion in the KL-constrained setting because the MLE reward is sensitive to the mismatch between the comparison-pair distribution μ and the reference policy π_ref.

## Results

The paper establishes that NLHF achieves the minimax-optimal distortion of (1/2+o(1))·β across all settings (utility distributions, comparison-pair sampling, KL budgets), matching the universal lower bound from Theorem 3. In contrast, RLHF (equivalently DPO) suffers ≥(1−o(1))·β distortion in the unconstrained social-choice setting and e^Ω(β) distortion in the KL-constrained alignment setting, with unbounded distortion when comparison pairs are sampled in a correlated manner. The key linearization lemma (Lemma 1) sandwiches Bradley-Terry win-rates between affine functions of average utilities, yielding the O(β²) upper bound for Borda/RLHF and the tight (1/2+o(1))·β bound for Maximal Lotteries/NLHF.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three alignment methods: RLHF (Ziegler et al. 2019), DPO (Rafailov et al. 2023), and NLHF (Munos et al. 2024). DPO is shown to be exactly equivalent to RLHF in this analysis (Appendix F.3), so they share identical distortion bounds. NLHF strictly dominates RLHF/DPO in every setting: in the unconstrained social-choice setting, NLHF achieves (1/2+o(1))·β distortion while RLHF suffers at least (1−o(1))·β (a factor of ~2 gap); in the KL-constrained alignment setting, NLHF maintains its optimal (1/2+o(1))·β bound regardless of the reference policy, KL budget, or comparison-pair distribution, whereas RLHF's distortion grows exponentially as e^Ω(β) and becomes unbounded under correlated pair sampling. The universal lower bound of (1/2+o(1))·β (Theorem 3) confirms that NLHF is minimax optimal—no alignment method can do better in the worst case.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper transplants the distortion framework from classical social choice theory (Procaccia & Rosenschein 2006; Boutilier et al. 2012) into the AI alignment setting, adapting it in two key ways: replacing deterministic rankings with probabilistic Bradley-Terry comparisons and adding a KL-divergence constraint to the reference policy. It establishes that RLHF and DPO are equivalent to the Borda voting rule, while NLHF (Munos et al. 2024) corresponds to Maximal Lotteries (Fishburn 1984), then proves tight distortion bounds for each. The central technical tool—a linearization lemma that sandwiches sigmoid win-rates between affine functions in average utility—enables the analysis and is broadly applicable to other random utility models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first rigorous framework showing that the dominant RLHF/DPO pipeline can suffer exponential (or even unbounded) distortion in average utility under preference heterogeneity, while NLHF achieves the minimax-optimal (1/2+o(1))β guarantee robustly across all model parameters. The results have direct implications for LLM leaderboards like Chatbot Arena, where MLE-based aggregation may systematically misrank models. Key open questions include characterizing RLHF's distortion when the comparison-pair distribution matches the reference policy, extending the analysis to generalization across states, and determining whether small amounts of additional cardinal information can reduce distortion below the information-theoretic lower bound.
