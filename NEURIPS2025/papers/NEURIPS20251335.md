## Problem

LLMs process all input tokens uniformly, lacking any architectural mechanism to differentiate or prioritize instructions by role, making them vulnerable to prompt injection attacks that override intended behavior. The paper formalizes a new attack class, Tool-Completion Attack (TCA), which exploits function-calling mechanisms by fabricating a false task completion in tool responses to make injected instructions appear semantically legitimate, achieving over 90% attack success rates even on frontier models like GPT-4o and o3-mini. Existing defenses such as Instructional Segment Embedding (ISE) apply uniform semantic shifts per role, failing to capture fine-grained contextual semantics and degrading general instruction-following capability.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a structured text prompt parsed via chat templates into hierarchical segments (instruction, data/tool, response), with segment markers extracted directly from the template delimiters. A learnable Segment Query Embedding matrix (H×d, where H is the number of roles) is broadcast across tokens to produce per-token query vectors that drive the two-stage attention pipeline. The output is a modified input embedding (I_token + I_seg + α·I_CP) fed into the standard LLM decoder, which then generates a text response; the learnable scalar α controls the strength of context-aware modulation, and the entire pipeline adds only 1.67% parameters and 3.90% inference latency over the base model.

## Method

CAHL introduces a two-step hierarchical learning paradigm built on top of ISE. First, Segment Summarization performs cross-attention between segment query embeddings (Q) and token embeddings (K, V) under a segment mask M, so each segment's summary captures only its own intra-segment semantics without cross-segment leakage, preserving the distinction between legitimate instructions and stealthy injections. Second, Contextual Propagation applies global self-attention over the summarized representations to fuse cross-segment context, enabling the model to dynamically adjust embeddings based on both role-specific identity and broader conversational semantics. The final input embedding is the sum of token embeddings, ISE's uniform segment shift, and a learnable-scaled contextual propagation term, trained end-to-end via full-parameter SFT with next-token prediction loss. This design lets the model learn fine-grained, context-dependent semantic shifts rather than ISE's one-size-fits-all per-role offset, simultaneously improving adversarial robustness and preserving instruction-following fidelity.

## Results

On the StruQ benchmark (clean dataset), CAHL achieves an average ASR of 26.32% and worst-case ASR of 37.98%, substantially outperforming ISE (33.41% / 61.54%) and Delimiter (50.72% / 99.04%) while maintaining 83.60% capability. On the adversarial StruQ dataset, CAHL reduces average ASR to 1.68% (vs. 3.13% for ISE and 15.38% for Delimiter). On the proposed Tool-Completion benchmark (clean), CAHL achieves 44.89% ASR and 28.49% Injection Ignorance Rate, compared to 57.53% ASR / 15.32% IIR for ISE and 56.72% / 15.86% for Delimiter, while matching ISE's capability at 77.12%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against ISE (Instructional Segment Embedding, ICLR 2025), CAHL consistently wins on both security and capability metrics. On the clean StruQ benchmark, CAHL reduces average ASR by 7.09 percentage points (26.32% vs. 33.41%) and worst-case ASR by 23.56 points (37.98% vs. 61.54%), while recovering 4.96 points of capability (83.60 vs. 78.64) that ISE's uniform segment shift sacrifices. On the Tool-Completion benchmark (clean), CAHL lowers ASR by 12.64 points (44.89% vs. 57.53%) and nearly doubles the Injection Ignorance Rate (28.49% vs. 15.32%). Against StruQ/Delimiter (USENIX Security 2025), CAHL achieves dramatically lower ASR on the clean StruQ benchmark (26.32% vs. 50.72% average) at a negligible 0.63-point capability cost (83.60 vs. 84.23), and on the Tool-Completion benchmark CAHL actually surpasses Delimiter in both capability (77.12 vs. 72.09) and security (44.89% vs. 56.72% ASR). Under adversarial training, CAHL achieves the lowest ASR across all settings (1.68% on StruQ, 1.34% on Tool-Completion) while retaining the highest capability, confirming that its context-aware hierarchy generalizes beyond the specific attack patterns seen during training.

## Relation to Prior Work

CAHL directly extends Instructional Segment Embedding (ISE, Wu et al., ICLR 2025), which assigns a uniform semantic shift to all tokens within the same instruction-hierarchy segment. The key departure is that CAHL replaces this uniform shift with a two-stage attention mechanism—Segment Summarization (masked cross-attention within each segment) followed by Contextual Propagation (global self-attention across segments)—that learns fine-grained, context-dependent semantic shifts per token. This positions CAHL alongside other architectural defenses for prompt injection (StruQ's structured delimiters, ASIDE's orthogonal instruction/data embeddings, role-separation via position IDs) but distinguishes itself by explicitly modeling cross-segment contextual correlations rather than treating segments as independent.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper introduces TCA, a concrete and well-motivated attack vector for tool-augmented LLMs, along with a benchmark that reveals even frontier reasoning models (o3-mini, DeepSeek-R1) achieve near-perfect attack success rates, challenging the assumption that chain-of-thought reasoning provides inherent robustness to contextual manipulation. CAHL's lightweight overhead (1.67% parameters, 3.90% latency) and zero-shot generalization to unseen attacks make it a practical architectural patch, though its evaluation is limited to a single keyword-based metric and primarily one base model family. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to RAG pipelines, multi-stage coordinated attacks, and whether reinforcement learning (rather than SFT) could further improve cross-domain robustness.
