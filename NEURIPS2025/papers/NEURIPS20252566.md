## Problem

In participatory budgeting elections, project proposers strategically set their requested costs, yet no formal game-theoretic framework existed to analyze this behavior under different voting rules. The paper asks whether pure Nash equilibria exist in the resulting cost-selection games and, when they do, what cost profiles they induce—revealing that the answer depends critically on the PB rule used.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252566/cropped_page8_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a PB game tuple (P, V, B, d) consisting of a project set with approval ballots from voters, a budget B, per-project minimum delivery costs d, and a chosen PB rule with tie-breaking order. The strategy space is continuous (each project reports a cost in R+), and the output is a Nash equilibrium cost profile or a non-existence certificate. Experimentally, the method also produces per-project best-response costs and winning/losing margins computed against real Pabulib election data (Wesoła, 2022; Kleine Wereld, 2019).

## Method

The paper models each project as a player in a game where the payoff is the reported cost minus delivery cost if funded, zero otherwise. The key theoretical contribution is a complete classification of NE existence across five PB rules (BasicAV, AV/Cost, Phragmén, MES-Apr, MES-Cost) under plurality, party-list, and unrestricted ballots: MES-Cost always admits a polynomial-time computable, essentially unique NE (Theorem 4.9), while AV/Cost, Phragmén, and MES-Apr can lack equilibria entirely. For AV/Cost, Algorithm 1 iteratively raises the costs of underreporting projects in A/D tie-breaking order until the budget is exhausted. Experimentally, the authors compute best responses and simulate best-response dynamics (random proposer updates over 10,000 iterations) on real Pabulib instances, showing that MES-Cost/Ph funds fewer, more expensive projects while AV/Cost, Phragmén, and MES-Apr/Ph produce costs proportional to approval scores.

## Results

The paper's central theoretical result (Theorem 4.9) establishes that MES-Cost always admits a polynomial-time computable pure Nash equilibrium for any PB game, regardless of tie-breaking order or delivery costs, and this equilibrium is essentially unique. In contrast, AV/Cost, Phragmén, and MES-Apr may fail to have any NE: Phragmén has no NE for a 6-project, 6-voter instance (Proposition 4.8), and MES-Apr has no NE for a 4-project, 16-vote instance (Proposition 4.11). Experimentally, on real Pabulib data (Wesola: 29 projects, 1182 voters, 1011k PLN budget; Kleine Wereld: 52 projects, 426 voters, 250k EUR budget), the dynamics show that under AV/Cost, Phragmén, and MES-Apr/Ph, equilibrium costs are roughly proportional to approval scores, while under MES-Cost/Ph the most-supported projects can request substantially higher costs, resulting in fewer but more expensive funded projects. Under BasicAV, the equilibrium concentrates the entire budget on the single most-approved project.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares five PB rules as 'baselines' for strategic cost selection. MES-Cost dominates in equilibrium guarantees: it is the only rule with a universal NE existence result (Theorem 4.9) across all ballot types and delivery cost settings, and its equilibrium is polynomial-time computable and essentially unique. BasicAV also always has an NE (Proposition 4.1), but the equilibrium is highly disproportional—the most popular project captures the entire budget B, which the paper identifies as a key deficiency motivating upper cost bounds in practice. AV/Cost and Phragmén produce approval-proportional equilibria under restricted settings (plurality ballots, A/D tie-breaking) but can fail to have any NE with arbitrary tie-breaking or nonzero delivery costs (Example 4.3, Proposition 4.8). MES-Apr sits between these: it guarantees NEs for plurality ballots (Theorem 4.10) and party-list with A/D tie-breaking (Theorem 4.12), but fails in general unrestricted settings (Proposition 4.11). Experimentally, AV/Cost, Phragmén, and MES-Apr/Ph all converge to similar cost profiles where project costs track approval scores, while MES-Cost/Ph produces a distinctly different outcome with fewer, more expensive funded projects—making it the most attractive rule for cities that want to fund meaningful-scale projects rather than many trivially cheap ones.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper introduces a game-theoretic framework for strategic cost selection in participatory budgeting, building on the established PB rule literature (BasicAV/GreedyAV, AV/Cost, Phragmén, MES) and the Pabulib dataset. It explicitly departs from Aziz et al. (2023), who study how voters pool monetary contributions, and Wagner and Meir (2023), who design strategy-proof budgeting mechanisms, by instead modeling proposers as strategic agents choosing project costs under fixed, known votes. The analysis draws on classical Nash equilibrium methodology and connects to the Hotelling-Downs tradition of strategic positioning, but applies it to a novel setting where the 'product' is a public project and the 'price' is the requested funding amount.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic characterization of when proposers can reach stable cost strategies under different PB rules, with the striking contrast that MES-Cost always admits polynomial-time computable NEs while AV/Cost, Phragmén, and MES-Apr may have none at all. This has direct practical implications: it explains why cities using BasicAV impose cost caps, and why Świecie dropped such caps when switching to MES. Open questions include the computational complexity of deciding NE existence in general, whether results hold under more realistic assumptions (e.g., voters' approvals depending on costs, which the paper shows collapses all NE guarantees), and how the framework extends to settings with incomplete information about votes.
