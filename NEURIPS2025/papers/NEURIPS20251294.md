## Problem

While data selection is well-studied in supervised learning, its role in reinforcement fine-tuning (RFT) of large language models remains largely heuristic, guided by reward trends or perceived difficulty rather than principled quantitative analysis. There is no existing method to measure how the removal of an individual training sample affects the final RFT reward, leaving practitioners without a direct, interpretable signal for identifying beneficial versus harmful examples.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a structured dataset of question-answer pairs (e.g., math problems with unique solutions), a pre-trained LLM policy model, and a binary reward function (r=1 for correct, r=-1 for incorrect), along with a selection budget δ specifying the target subset size. It first performs a short surrogate training phase (e.g., 2 LoRA epochs) to collect model checkpoints, then produces a directional per-sample influence score for every training example—positive values indicate the sample is beneficial, negative values indicate it is harmful. The top-δ scored samples form a refined subset used for the final RFT run, yielding the fine-tuned policy model.

## Method

RFT-Inf defines the exact influence of a sample z as the difference in final reward between training on the full dataset versus the dataset with z removed (Eq. 4), then derives a scalable first-order approximation by backtracking through the optimization trajectory with temporal differentiation and applying a first-order Taylor expansion to adjacent time steps. The resulting estimator (Eq. 5) reduces to a weighted sum of inner products between each sample's policy gradient and the aggregate gradient over all samples at each checkpoint, so a high score means the sample's gradient is well-aligned with the global optimization direction. A worst-case error bound (Proposition 1) shows the approximation error scales linearly with the number of iterations T and quadratically with the product of the Lipschitz constant and maximum advantage, motivating the use of only a few surrogate epochs. In practice, a Monte Carlo strategy samples a small number of evenly spaced checkpoints (e.g., one per epoch) to estimate the full temporal sum, and LoRA (rank 16) keeps the surrogate training phase computationally light, yielding overall O(NE + NC) complexity versus O(N(N−1)E) for brute-force retraining.

## Results

At a 20% data selection ratio, RFT-Inf achieves 45.5% zero-shot pass@1 on AIME24 with DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B, outperforming all baselines including LIMR and HVS. On MATH-500, the same 20% subset yields 89.0% accuracy, surpassing the full-dataset baseline of 86.2%. For the larger Qwen-7B model at 20% selection, RFT-Inf reaches 60.2% on AIME24, exceeding the next-best baseline (HVS) by 2.9 points. The method also accelerates convergence, as shown by the reward curve in Figure 1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares RFT-Inf against four baselines: Random selection, Difficulty-based selection (using median pass-rate across Qwen-1.5B/7B/32B), LIMR [13] (reward-trend alignment), and Historical Variance Score (HVS) [15] (reward variance over training). RFT-Inf consistently outperforms all baselines at low selection ratios (10–30%), where the advantage is most pronounced. At 20% selection on Qwen-1.5B, RFT-Inf achieves 45.5% on AIME24 versus lower scores from LIMR and HVS, and 89.0% on MATH-500 versus 86.2% for the full dataset. On Qwen-7B at 20%, RFT-Inf scores 60.2% on AIME24 compared to 57.3% for HVS, the strongest competing method. The case study further reveals that RFT-Inf and HVS agree on extreme-difficulty samples (both rank them low) but diverge sharply on moderate-difficulty samples, which is identified as the key driver of RFT-Inf's superior empirical performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the classical influence function framework of Koh and Liang (2017) from supervised learning to reinforcement fine-tuning, where the optimization objective is reward maximization rather than loss minimization and the standard convexity/twice-differentiability assumptions break down. Rather than relying on inverse Hessian-gradient products, it introduces temporal differentiation through the optimization trajectory combined with first-order Taylor approximation between adjacent time steps, yielding a gradient-alignment-based estimator. In the RFT data-selection space, it departs from purely heuristic strategies (LIMR's reward-trend alignment, HSV's reward variance, difficulty-based filtering) by providing a principled, per-sample influence score with a worst-case error bound.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first principled, quantitative method for assessing individual sample contribution in RFT, moving the field beyond empirical reward-trend heuristics. The practical finding that ~20% of data selected by RFT-Inf outperforms the full dataset on mathematical reasoning benchmarks has direct implications for compute efficiency in LLM post-training pipelines. However, validation is limited to math reasoning with small-to-medium models (1.5B–7B) on a single dataset (DeepScaleR), and the surrogate training phase (even with LoRA) adds non-trivial overhead. Open questions include scalability to 70B+ models, applicability to non-mathematical domains (coding, general reasoning), and whether the gradient-alignment interpretation remains valid under more complex reward structures or multi-turn RL settings.
