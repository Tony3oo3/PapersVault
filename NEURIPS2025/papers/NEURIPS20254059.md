## Problem

Existing LLM reasoning pipelines apply a fixed reasoning method (e.g., best-of-N, MCTS, beam search) uniformly across all queries, ignoring that different questions benefit from different accuracy-cost trade-offs. The paper challenges the common assumption that more candidate samples always improve accuracy by deriving tight probabilistic bounds for standard aggregation methods, and asks whether one can learn to route each question to the most suitable reasoning method before generation.

## Input / Output

At inference, a new question is encoded by a lightweight sentence-embedding model (all-MiniLM-L6-v2, producing a 384-dim feature vector) and passed through a trained MLP to obtain a d-dimensional embedding (d≪384). The system outputs a single reasoning method index from a universe of 81 configurations (each a tuple of base LM, reasoning strategy, aggregation technique, decoding temperature, and candidate count N), which is then deployed to generate the final answer. The trade-off between accuracy and token cost is controlled by a scalar λ∈[0,1] set at training time.

## Method

EPIC learns a shared d-dimensional embedding space via a two-tower contrastive framework: a question encoder g_θ maps input features to embeddings, while each of the M reasoning methods is assigned a trainable embedding vector v_i. Training minimizes an InfoNCE loss that pulls each question toward the method with the highest utility (a convex combination of accuracy and normalized token cost weighted by λ) and away from all others, plus a regularization term that enforces relative similarity ratios between methods sharing the same (LM, ReStrat, Config, Agg) but differing only in N, using the paper's derived probability bounds as target values. The theoretical contribution establishes exponential decay bounds for majority voting, PRM sum-voting, and PRM max-voting as functions of sample size N, revealing that increasing N helps only when the correct answer is already the most probable (or highest-scoring) candidate. At inference, the top-1 method by cosine similarity to the question embedding is selected, enabling dynamic routing without retraining when new methods are added.

## Results

On MATH500, EPIC-λ=0.25 achieves 86.4% accuracy with only 1,859.2 average tokens, matching the accuracy of Best-of-16 (86.8%) while using ~5× fewer tokens (1,859 vs. 10,036). At the accuracy-prioritized setting λ=1.0, EPIC reaches 89.4% accuracy with 6,921.7 tokens, surpassing the best individual method in the universe (Best-of-16 at 86.8%) and approaching the universe upper bound of 91.2%. On GSM8K, EPIC achieves 95.0% accuracy with 2,085.5 tokens, outperforming Best-of-16 (94.2%, 5,019.2 tokens). On LiveCodeBench with Qwen2.5-Coder-7B, EPIC(λ=1.0) achieves 61.88% pass@k with 7,013.5 tokens, exceeding Best-of-16 (58.71%, 8,034.7 tokens).

## Baselines & Comparisons

EPIC consistently outperforms both the classification-based (CL-λ) and distributional random allocation (DRA-λ) baselines at every λ setting. At λ=1.0, EPIC achieves 89.4% accuracy with 6,921.7 tokens, beating CL-1.0 (87.8%, 10,923.4 tokens) by 1.6 points in accuracy while using 36% fewer tokens, and DRA-1.0 (87.0%, 10,542.2 tokens) by 2.4 points with 34% fewer tokens. Against the strongest individual reasoning method in the universe, Best-of-16 (86.8%, 10,036.2 tokens), EPIC-0.25 matches accuracy (86.4%) at roughly 5× lower token cost, and EPIC-1.0 exceeds it by 2.6 points in accuracy at 31% lower cost. Compared to Offline Ada-BoK (87.0%, 4,095.2 tokens), EPIC-0.25 trades 0.6 points of accuracy for a 55% reduction in tokens, while EPIC-0.75 (87.6%, 3,192.9 tokens) beats it on both accuracy and cost. Against the large-model references (OpenAI-o1-mini at 90.0%, DeepSeek-V3 at 90.2%), EPIC-1.0 at 89.4% remains competitive while operating on a 7B model with explicit cost control.

## Relation to Prior Work

EPIC departs from the dominant line of inference-time scaling work (Snell et al. 2025, Beeching et al. 2024) that focuses on allocating more compute to a single reasoning method, and from difficulty-based allocation approaches (Damani et al. 2025, Manvi et al. 2024) that predict whether a specific method benefits from more samples. Instead, it reframes the problem as a matching task between a query and a heterogeneous universe of reasoning methods (spanning different strategies, aggregation techniques, and model sizes), borrowing the two-tower contrastive learning paradigm from retrieval. The paper's distinctive theoretical contribution—deriving concentration bounds for majority voting, score-sum, and score-max aggregation (Theorems 3.1–3.3)—is then repurposed as a structural regularizer in the contrastive objective, a connection not present in prior adaptive-compute or method-selection literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 75% token reduction at matched accuracy (and the 5× reduction versus best-of-16) is practically significant for deployment in resource-constrained settings, and the utility parameter λ gives practitioners a single knob to trade accuracy against cost. However, the evaluation is limited to math and code domains with a single primary model family (Qwen2.5-Math-7B), lacks error bars across seeds, and the code-generation experiment disables the regularizer (τ=0), leaving open whether the theoretical grounding generalizes. The paper explicitly leaves adaptive method insertion (a text-to-embedding network for unseen methods) and inverse design of new reasoning methods as future work, and the scalability of the contrastive objective to very large method universes remains untested.
