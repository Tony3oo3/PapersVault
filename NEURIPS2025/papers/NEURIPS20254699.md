## Problem

Vision-language models struggle to reason over long video sequences in embodied settings: existing approaches either attend to only a few frames (sacrificing global task context) or concatenate all frames into a single context (computationally expensive and prone to hallucination, especially during non-optimal trajectory segments). ROVER addresses this by enabling high-precision, frame-by-frame reasoning over arbitrarily long trajectories while maintaining global context and scaling linearly with video length.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254699/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a natural language task description (e.g., 'Thaw the steak in the microwave') and a sequential stream of camera frames (downsampled to 30 frames for atomic tasks, 60 for composite tasks) from robot-mounted or third-person viewpoints. At each timestep, the model outputs an interleaved sequence of natural language frame descriptions and integer task-progress percentages (0–100 per subtask, rescaled across subtasks for the full trajectory). For the video QA task, all frame descriptions are concatenated and passed to a language model to answer templated questions of the form 'Did the robot [action]? If so, when?'

## Method

ROVER recursively decomposes a task into object-centric subtasks, spawning a child reasoning process for each subtask via a special token ('The robot needs to: {new_subtask}') that initializes a new context containing the subtask description and the parent's last frame. Within each subtask, a sliding context window retains only three frames (the subtask's first frame, the most recent previous frame, and the newly appended frame), each paired with its previously generated text description, capping the per-timestep context to a fixed size. This synergy between recursive decomposition (which updates the subtask-specific window as milestones are achieved) and the sliding window (which forces focus on temporally relevant frames) reduces hallucination during non-expert states and yields linear time complexity in total frame count, versus quadratic for baselines like GVL and TemporalConcat. The entire framework is implemented via in-context learning with no parameter updates, using Gemini-1.5-Pro as the primary backbone VLM, and is evaluated on a newly generated dataset of 543 simulation videos across 27 RoboCasa tasks with ground-truth progress labels derived from geometric distance-to-goal metrics.

## Results

On near-expert trajectories, ROVER, GVL, TemporalConcat, and LocalConcat all achieve Pearson correlation ≥ 0.5 with ground-truth task progress. However, on non-expert trajectories, ROVER significantly outperforms all baselines: total error rate drops from 76% (GVL) to 26% on real-world videos and from 57% to 23% on simulation videos. On video QA, baselines exhibit severe hallucination (precision of only 20–50% with near-100% recall), while ROVER achieves substantially higher precision. ROVER's sliding window also reduces time complexity from quadratic to linear in video length.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is GVL (Ma et al., ICLR 2024), the state-of-the-art in-context value learning method that randomly shuffles frames to disrupt temporal bias. ROVER outperforms GVL across all three evaluation tasks, with the gap widening as trajectories become more non-expert. On frame-level progress estimation, GVL's correlation with ground truth degrades sharply on lower-level (more perturbed) trajectories, while ROVER maintains strong correlation. On frame-level reasoning, GVL's error rate increases substantially on non-expert states, whereas ROVER's stays low due to its sliding window limiting context to 3 frames. On video QA, GVL hallucinates events that did not occur (precision 20–50%, recall ~100%), while ROVER dramatically reduces these false positives. The ablated baselines TemporalConcat (chronological frame concatenation) and LocalConcat (last-3-frames only) perform comparably to GVL on near-expert videos but degrade similarly on non-expert trajectories, confirming that the recursive decomposition is the key differentiator for ROVER.

## Relation to Prior Work

ROVER extends GVL (Ma et al., ICLR 2024), which demonstrated that VLMs can perform in-context value learning over shuffled video frames for embodied tasks, by introducing recursive subtask decomposition and a subtask-specific sliding context window. Where GVL addresses temporal bias through random frame shuffling but still processes all frames in a single context, ROVER recursively spawns child reasoning processes for each subtask, capping the context to three frames per subtask and achieving linear (rather than quadratic) scaling with video length. The trajectory generation protocol draws on MimicGen's data augmentation ideas, and the recursive decomposition concept connects to broader work on divide-and-conquer prompting in language models, but the specific application to continuous video streams in embodied settings is new.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical recipe for reducing VLM hallucination in embodied video reasoning: the finding that 83% of GVL's perception errors concentrate in contexts exceeding 10 frames, and that non-expert states are the primary trigger, gives practitioners a clear diagnostic for when and why VLMs fail on robot video. The released dataset of 543 videos with ground-truth progress labels across both expert and perturbed trajectories fills a gap in evaluation resources for embodied reasoning. Key open questions include whether fine-tuning (rather than in-context learning) can further improve ROVER's frame-level accuracy, whether the decomposition approach generalizes beyond kitchen manipulation to more open-ended embodied tasks, and how to handle cases where subtask boundaries are ambiguous or the model's decomposition diverges from the actual task structure.
