## Problem

Portable OCT devices produce severely degraded B-scans (high noise, low contrast, limited depth and field of view) that are often clinically unusable, yet high-end OCT systems cost over $50,000 and weigh 50+ lbs, limiting access in under-resourced settings. Existing CNN and GAN-based super-resolution methods hallucinate fine details and fail to preserve delicate retinal anatomy, while bridged diffusion models—well-suited for image-to-image translation—have not been explored for medical image super-resolution on small clinical datasets. The paper also identifies a semantic misalignment issue in conditional DDPMs where conditioning on a target image does not reliably guide the reverse diffusion trajectory.

## Input / Output

The model takes a single low-resolution portable OCT B-scan (562×1286 from the Philophos KUOS-O100, or a 4× downsampled simulation of commercial Zeiss Cirrus 5000 scans) as the bridging condition and produces a high-resolution OCT B-scan (256×256 for training) that matches the structural fidelity of commercial-device scans. The input is consumed as the initial point of the reverse diffusion process (x_T = y), meaning the degraded image directly anchors the denoising trajectory rather than being fed as a side conditioning signal. Outputs must preserve diagnostically critical retinal layers, foveal contours, and vessel structures that are essential for downstream glaucoma and AMD classification.

## Method

OCTDiff is a bridged diffusion model that learns direct translation between low- and high-resolution OCT domains, with three key innovations. First, Adaptive Noise Aggregation (ANA) replaces the single-step noise prediction in the reverse process with an exponentially-decayed temporal fusion of all previously predicted noise vectors (ε̄_t = (1/Z_t) Σ exp(−α(τ−t))·ε̂_τ), stabilizing the denoising trajectory and capturing multi-scale retinal details; a gradient-based refinement step is applied at early timesteps (t > T/2). Second, Multi-Scale Cross-Attention (MSCA) is embedded in the U-Net backbone, where encoder features at each spatial scale serve as queries attending to decoder features at all other scales, enabling cross-resolution structural context. Third, a focal-style MSE loss is modulated by ophthalmologist-assigned clinical quality scores (γ < 0), so that high-quality training targets receive greater gradient weight and the model is not misled by suboptimal scans. The reconstruction at each step uses the DDIM inversion rule with the aggregated noise, and the full pipeline is trained with T=1000 diffusion steps on 256×256 images.

## Results

On the real-world Philophos 84 dataset, OCTDiff achieves SSIM of 93.6%, PSNR of 38.8 dB, and LPIPS of 16.1%, outperforming all six baselines across every metric. On the Synthetic 500 dataset, OCTDiff attains the best SSIM (98.9%) and LPIPS (1.7%), though BBDM edges it on PSNR (42.7 vs. 41.0 dB), likely because the synthetic degradation preserves local structural information that favors convolutional upsampling. The performance gap between OCTDiff and the strongest baseline (BBDM) widens substantially on the clinical dataset (SSIM +6.4 pp, LPIPS −11.8 pp) compared to the synthetic one (SSIM +0.8 pp, LPIPS −4.5 pp), indicating that the ANA and MSCA modules are most beneficial under realistic portable-OCT degradation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

OCTDiff outperforms all six baselines on the clinically realistic Philophos 84 dataset across SSIM, PSNR, and LPIPS. Against the strongest diffusion baseline BBDM, OCTDiff gains +6.4 pp SSIM (93.6 vs. 87.2), +3.5 dB PSNR (38.8 vs. 35.3), and −11.8 pp LPIPS (16.1 vs. 27.9), demonstrating that ANA and MSCA are critical under severe portable-OCT degradation. The transformer-based Swin2SR is the best non-diffusion baseline (SSIM 78.8% on Philophos 84) but still trails OCTDiff by 14.8 pp SSIM, and its over-smoothing of retinal curvature is visible qualitatively. On the Synthetic 500 dataset, where degradation is milder and more uniform, BBDM achieves the highest PSNR (42.7 dB) while OCTDiff leads in SSIM (98.9%) and LPIPS (1.7%), suggesting that convolutional and bridged methods benefit more from the preserved local structure in synthetic pairs. CNN baselines (SRCNN, VDSR) and CycleGAN are substantially outperformed on both datasets, with SRCNN and VDSR producing structurally unstable outputs and CycleGAN averaging out fine retinal layers. In downstream disease classification (Table 6), OCTDiff-generated images match or exceed commercial high-resolution inputs for 5 of 6 classifier–disease combinations, while BBDM lags significantly (e.g., 62.0% vs. 74.5% for ViT on glaucoma).

## Relation to Prior Work

OCTDiff extends the Bridged Brownian Diffusion Model (BBDM) framework for image-to-image translation by introducing two architectural innovations: Adaptive Noise Aggregation (ANA), which temporally fuses noise predictions across denoising steps using exponential decay (conceptually analogous to temporal ensembling in semi-supervised learning), and Multi-Scale Cross-Attention (MSCA) in the U-Net backbone for cross-resolution feature interaction. The reconstruction step leverages DDIM inversion rules, and the overall conditional diffusion formulation follows the DDPM lineage. The paper positions ANA as uniquely feasible within the bridged framework—where the conditioning image anchors the reverse process—distinguishing it from learned noise modulation approaches like MuLAN and from heuristic timestep reweighting strategies in standard DDPMs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The work addresses a genuine clinical gap: portable OCT devices produce images too degraded for reliable diagnosis, and no prior diffusion-based method has been validated on real portable OCT data. The introduction of the Philophos 84 dataset and the demonstration that super-resolved images support downstream disease classification at near-commercial accuracy (e.g., 96.4% for AMD with CNN2D vs. 94.6% on native high-res) are practically significant for point-of-care ophthalmology. However, the clinical dataset is very small (84 patients), cross-dataset generalization is untested, and the model's computational cost (762.6 GFLOPs, ~48 h training) limits near-term edge deployment. Open questions include whether ANA generalizes beyond bridged diffusion, whether latent-space variants can reduce compute, and whether the clinical quality score loss transfers to other modalities.
