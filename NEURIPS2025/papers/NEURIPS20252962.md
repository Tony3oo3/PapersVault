## Problem

Novelty detection in large scientific datasets is hampered by the noisy, high-dimensional nature of experimental data and the need for statistically rigorous, quantifiable claims about observed outliers. Existing anomaly detection methods primarily flag individual anomalous instances (e.g., via AUROC) rather than making distribution-level statistical statements, and no unified pipeline existed that combined expressive dimensionality reduction with formal hypothesis testing suitable for scientific discovery.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252962/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline takes high-dimensional raw data (50 ms LIGO time-series at 4096 Hz, ~100-particle jets, 256×256 histopathology tiles, 32×32 CIFAR images, or synthetic tabular points) along with a reference background dataset R of known classes, and produces a 4-dimensional contrastive embedding per sample plus a statistical significance (Z-score and p-value) quantifying deviation from the null hypothesis that observed data D is identically distributed to R. The reference sample must be substantially larger than the observed set (typically |R| = 5|D|), and the method targets distributional shifts (overdensities, shape distortions, outlier clusters) rather than per-point anomalies.

## Method

AutoSciDACT is a two-phase pipeline. In pre-training, a supervised contrastive learning objective (SupCon) with an optional cross-entropy term compresses high-dimensional inputs into a fixed d=4 embedding, using class labels from simulations or expert annotations to define positive pairs and optionally incorporating domain-specific augmentations. In discovery, the New Physics Learning Machine (NPLM) performs a signal-agnostic Neyman-Pearson likelihood-ratio test by training a Nyström-approximated Gaussian kernel classifier to distinguish observed data D from reference R, with the test statistic calibrated via 500 pseudo-experiments under the null. Six kernel widths (chosen from quantiles of pairwise embedding distances) are combined by averaging their p-values to mitigate sensitivity to a single scale. The key insight is that contrastive embeddings combat the curse of dimensionality that degrades NPLM's power, enabling detection of ~1% signal injections at Z ≥ 3σ across astronomy, particle physics, histology, and image domains.

## Results

AutoSciDACT (NPLM on contrastive embeddings) detects injected anomalies with Z-scores ≥ 3 (p ≲ 10⁻³) at signal fractions as low as 1% across all five datasets (synthetic, LIGO, JETCLASS, histology, CIFAR-10). For LIGO and JETCLASS, NPLM approaches the fully supervised upper bound at Z = 3, rivaling or exceeding all prior anomaly detection methods in those domains. The empirical Z-score is capped at ~2.88σ by the 500 pseudo-experiments used for calibration; beyond this, the asymptotic NPLM estimate extends to arbitrarily high significance. NPLM significantly outperforms the Mahalanobis distance baseline in all non-synthetic domains, where the Mahalanobis test's Gaussian-cluster assumption breaks down.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares NPLM against three baselines: (1) a Mahalanobis distance test [28], (2) a supervised MLP classifier trained on the contrastive embeddings, and (3) an 'ideal supervised' variant where the encoder is re-trained with the signal class included. NPLM significantly outperforms Mahalanobis in all domains except the synthetic Gaussian-cluster benchmark, where Mahalanobis is well-matched by construction; the Mahalanobis test is inherently insensitive to overdensities near the bulk of a background cluster, which is the dominant anomaly type in LIGO, JETCLASS, and histology. Against the supervised baselines, NPLM approaches the supervised limit at Z ≈ 3 for both LIGO and JETCLASS, meaning the unsupervised, signal-agnostic test recovers nearly all the sensitivity available even with full knowledge of the signal distribution. The 'ideal supervised' benchmark (re-trained embeddings) sometimes falls below the standard supervised one in LIGO and CIFAR-10 due to the difficulty of separating many classes in the fixed 4-dimensional embedding space.

## Relation to Prior Work

AutoSciDACT unifies two previously separate research threads: contrastive representation learning (SimCLR, SupCon) for dimensionality reduction, and the New Physics Learning Machine (NPLM) for signal-agnostic hypothesis testing. Prior contrastive anomaly detection methods (CADet, CSI, etc.) focus on flagging individual out-of-distribution points via AUROC, while NPLM was developed and validated almost exclusively in particle physics. This paper departs from both by using supervised contrastive embeddings to make NPLM tractable in high-dimensional scientific data, and by framing the output as a rigorous two-sample hypothesis test (p-value, Z-score) rather than a per-point anomaly score, thereby extending NPLM's applicability to astronomy, histology, and general image domains.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The pipeline offers a practical, domain-agnostic template for automated scientific discovery that produces statistically defensible claims rather than mere anomaly flags, which is essential for the scientific method. Key limitations include the dependence on high-quality class labels for pre-training, the assumption that the reference distribution faithfully represents the background, and the fixed low embedding dimension (d=4) that constrains expressivity. Open questions include handling domain shift between reference and observed data, incorporating epistemic uncertainties into both the embeddings and the NPLM test, and scaling to settings where labeled data are scarce or unavailable.
