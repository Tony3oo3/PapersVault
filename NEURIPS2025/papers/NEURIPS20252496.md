## Problem

CLIP produces versatile, task-agnostic image embeddings, but the specific semantic content encoded within these representations remains opaque. Existing explainability techniques (GradCAM, LIME) are designed for task-specific predictions and cannot characterize the representation itself, while matrix-factorization approaches yield bases that are not inherently interpretable. The paper asks: can we learn a faithful, human-understandable surrogate that reveals which concepts drive a CLIP image embedding, independent of any downstream task?

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image and its associated captions (human-annotated or generated by BLIP-2 OPT-2.7B, 10 per image) as input. Captions are processed via POS tagging into a sparse concept vector c_i ∈ [0,1]^|C| with 15–20 active concepts per image, where each component is the frequency of a concept across that image's captions. The output is a surrogate embedding f(c_i) = σ(c_i W + b) in the same dimensionality as CLIP's embedding space, along with per-concept attribution scores v_i(k) that sum to 1, and cross-modal scores v_{i,j}(k) that decompose the image-text similarity s_{i,j} into per-concept contributions.

## Method

EXPLAIN-R trains a linear surrogate f(c_i) = σ(c_i W + b) to mimic CLIP's behavior by minimizing the KL divergence between the surrogate's and CLIP's image-to-text and text-to-image similarity distributions (Eq. 3), rather than reconstructing embeddings directly. This similarity-matching objective captures CLIP's bimodal contrastive structure, which unimodal reconstruction losses miss. A key technical contribution is a theoretically-motivated moving-average correction (Eqs. 5–6) that eliminates the O(1/B) bias inherent in mini-batch estimation of the KL divergence gradient, enabling training with small batches (1024) on a single A100 GPU. Concept scores are then extracted via scalar projection: each concept's contribution c_{i,k} W_k is projected onto the final representation f(c_i) and normalized so scores sum to 1, yielding an interpretable decomposition without requiring a downstream prediction.

## Results

EXPLAIN-R achieves a faithfulness rsum of 482.94±4.08 on COCO and 476.47±3.21 on Flickr30k (zero-shot retrieval), and 62.77±0.22% and 53.38±0.21% zero-shot classification accuracy on SUN397 and ImageNet, respectively. These results consistently surpass the SpLiCE baseline by 89.76 (COCO), 95.06 (Flickr30k), 2.99 (SUN397), and 2.05 (ImageNet) points. A user study further confirms EXPLAIN-R's explanations are significantly preferred over SpLiCE on relevance (p=9×10⁻⁸), completeness (p=1×10⁻⁶), and utility (p=4×10⁻⁷).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is SpLiCE [26], which decomposes CLIP image features into sparse linear combinations of text features using the CLIP text encoder. EXPLAIN-R outperforms SpLiCE on all four datasets and both task types: by ~90 rsum points on COCO retrieval, ~95 on Flickr30k retrieval, ~3 points on SUN397 classification, and ~2 points on ImageNet classification. The gap is largest on retrieval tasks, where SpLiCE's reliance on the text encoder introduces spurious or unrelated concepts (e.g., 'classical music,' 'cheap key') that degrade faithfulness. The paper also compares against the original CLIP ViT-B/32 model [1] as a reference: while EXPLAIN-R underperforms CLIP on ImageNet classification (52.37% vs. 61.91%), it occasionally surpasses CLIP on retrieval (e.g., Flickr30k I→T R@1: 74.53 vs. 67.17), suggesting concept-based inputs may be more robust to image degradations. Qualitatively, EXPLAIN-R's top concepts are consistently more image-relevant than SpLiCE's, which frequently produces multi-word phrases or unrelated terms from its 15,000-word LAION vocabulary.

## Relation to Prior Work

EXPLAIN-R extends the line of concept-based representation explanations for CLIP, most directly building on SpLiCE [26], which decomposes image features into sparse linear combinations of text-encoder-derived concepts. The key departure is twofold: (1) concepts are sourced from caption-derived vocabulary (nouns, verbs, adjectives) rather than the CLIP text encoder, making them inherently image-grounded and avoiding spurious text-encoder artifacts; and (2) the surrogate is trained by matching CLIP's full image-to-text and text-to-image similarity distributions via KL divergence, rather than minimizing reconstruction error between original and surrogate embeddings. The biased mini-batch gradient correction via moving averages is borrowed from the compositional stochastic optimization literature [40, 41], repurposed here to enable small-batch training of the contrastive-matching objective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As CLIP embeddings underpin an expanding ecosystem of downstream applications (text-to-image generation, LMMs, open-set detection), a task-agnostic, human-interpretable account of what these representations encode is practically important for trust and debugging. The finding that preserving relational similarity structure (rather than pointwise reconstruction) yields substantially more faithful surrogates suggests a broader principle for knowledge distillation in representation learning. Open questions include whether the linear concept-interaction assumption is a fundamental bottleneck, whether the approach generalizes to non-CLIP representation learners, and whether captioning-model hallucinations can be systematically mitigated without reverting to expensive human annotation.
