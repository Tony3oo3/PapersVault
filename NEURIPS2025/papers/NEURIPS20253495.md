## Problem

Existing representation learning for linear bandits either assumes access to the optimal solution of the non-convex objective, relies on convex relaxations whose solutions may not be valid for the original problem, or fails to address transferability to unseen target tasks. This paper tackles the gap by providing a provable meta-learning framework that learns a shared low-rank representation from source bandit tasks and transfers it to a new target task with tight regret and sample complexity guarantees for both the upstream and downstream problems.

## Input / Output

The method takes T source tasks, each with d-dimensional feature vectors drawn from a standard Gaussian distribution and scalar rewards generated by an unknown rank-r reward matrix Θ* = B*W*, plus a single target task with bounded-norm feature vectors (‖x‖₂ ≤ L) and 1-sub-Gaussian noise. It produces a shared representation estimate B̂ ∈ ℝ^{d×r}, per-task reward parameter estimates θ̂_t = B̂ŵ_t, greedy action selections in the commit phase, and for the target task a confidence ellipsoid β_n in ℝ^d whose radius scales with √r rather than √d. The exploration phase requires N₁ ≥ C·max(log d, log T, r) rounds per task and N₁T ≥ Cμ²κ⁴dr²/δ₀² total samples to achieve subspace distance SD(B̂, B*) ≤ δ₀ < 0.1.

## Method

The upstream problem is solved by an Explore-then-Commit (EtC) algorithm: after N₁ rounds of random exploration across all T tasks, a truncation-based spectral initialization computes B̂ as the top-r singular vectors of a truncated cross-moment matrix, avoiding the non-convex relaxation used in prior work. Given B̂, T independent least-squares problems recover ŵ_t for each task, yielding the estimate θ̂_t = B̂ŵ_t with error O(√(r/T)). For the downstream target task, an OFUL-based algorithm constructs a confidence ellipsoid for θ_{T+1}* in the original d-dimensional space using the learned B̂, but the ellipsoid's radius depends on the r-dimensional matrix V_{n,T+1} = B̂ᵀV̄_{n,T+1}B̂ rather than the full d-dimensional V̄, yielding a regret bound of Õ(√(rdN₂)) versus the standard Õ(d√N₂). The key technical contribution is showing that the subspace distance guarantee SD(B̂, B*) ≤ δ₀ from spectral initialization propagates through the confidence set construction, ensuring θ_{T+1}* ∈ β_n with high probability while the ellipsoid width scales with √r.

## Results

The paper presents results primarily through cumulative regret plots (Figures 1 and 2) rather than numerical tables. Theoretically, the proposed EtC algorithm achieves an estimation error of O(√(r/T)) for the shared representation, and the OFUL-based transfer algorithm achieves a target-task regret bound of Õ(√(rdN₂)), improving over the standard Õ(d√N₂) bound by a factor of √(d/r). Empirically, across synthetic data (d=100, T=100, r=2), Movielens-100K (d=100, T=10, r=1), and LastFM (d=100, T=10, r=1), the proposed algorithm consistently achieves lower cumulative regret than both baselines in both the representation learning and transfer learning settings. In the transfer learning setting, the advantage is most pronounced: the naive approach shows inadequate generalization due to lack of shared structure, while MoM-UCB underperforms because its estimator cannot recover an effective representation matrix in limited source data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: (1) a naive algorithm (Thompson Sampling or UCB) that solves each task independently without leveraging shared structure, and (2) the Method-of-Moments (MoM) estimator from Tripuraneni et al. [1] for representation learning. In the representation learning setting (Figure 1), the proposed EtC algorithm with spectral initialization consistently achieves lower cumulative regret than both MoM and the naive TS/UCB approach across all three datasets. In the transfer learning setting (Figure 2), the proposed OFUL-based algorithm outperforms both Naive-UCB (which applies UCB directly to the target task without source-task knowledge) and MoM-UCB (which substitutes spectral initialization with the MoM estimator). The MoM baseline underperforms because its estimator requires N ≳ polylog(N,d,T)(κr)⁴ max(d,T) samples per task and cannot recover an effective representation matrix B̂ under the limited source data available, whereas the proposed spectral initialization achieves SD(B̂, B⋆) ≤ δ₀ under the tighter sample complexity N₁T ≳ Cμ²κ⁴(dr²/δ₀²). The naive approach fails to generalize to the target task due to the absence of any shared structure exploitation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the multi-task representation learning framework for linear bandits established by [28] (Lin et al., ICML 2024) by replacing the alternating gradient descent estimator with a simpler explore-then-commit algorithm that uses spectral initialization followed by per-task least squares, avoiding both convex relaxations and the i.i.d. data assumption in the commit phase. It also adapts the OFUL confidence ellipsoid construction from [22] (Abbasi-Yadkori et al., NeurIPS 2011) to the transfer learning setting, where the ellipsoid is built in the low-dimensional representation space rather than the full d-dimensional space. Crucially, the paper departs from prior multi-task bandit works [7, 8, 10] that either assumed knowledge of the optimal non-convex solution or relied on trace-norm relaxation, and from [1] (Tripuraneni et al.) whose MoM estimator requires T ≤ O(d), by providing a unified framework that handles both the upstream representation learning problem and the downstream transfer to an unseen target task with tight sample complexity independent of T relative to d.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to provide provable guarantees for the full meta-learning pipeline—learning a shared low-rank representation from T source bandit tasks and transferring it to an unseen (T+1)-th target task—yielding a target-task regret bound of Õ(√(rdN)) versus the standard Õ(d√N) when r ≪ d. The practical implication is that in data-scarce target tasks (e.g., new users in recommender systems), the learned representation can dramatically reduce the number of interactions needed for effective adaptation. Key limitations include the Gaussian feature/noise assumption for source tasks (Assumption 3.1), the restriction to linear bandits with finite action sets, and the requirement that the target task shares the exact same feature extractor B*. Open questions include relaxing to sub-Gaussian or heavy-tailed distributions, extending to non-linear or kernel-based representations, and handling non-stationary or adversarial settings.
