## Problem

3D Gaussian Splatting relies on low-degree spherical harmonics for color, which limits its ability to capture high-frequency spatial details and complex view-dependent effects like specular highlights and reflections. Existing remedies—global texture maps or per-Gaussian texture maps—either fail on multi-object scenes or impose large storage overhead that constrains texture resolution. The paper asks whether source images themselves can be leveraged at render time to recover these missing details without inflating the Gaussian representation.

## Input / Output

At training time the method consumes a set of calibrated source images with known camera poses and optimizes a 3D Gaussian scene (positions, opacities, SH coefficients up to degree 2, quaternions, scales, and learnable surface normals) alongside a lightweight residual-prediction network. At render time, given a novel camera pose, the pipeline projects K=4 median Gaussian-ray intersection points onto M=3 visibility-filtered source views (selected from S=4 nearest candidates) to extract warped pixel colors, which together with the base Gaussian-rasterized image and ray directions feed the residual network. The output is a final RGB image (base color plus predicted residual) and the pruned Gaussian scene, with storage comparable to or smaller than vanilla 3DGS because no per-Gaussian texture maps are stored.

## Method

IBGS decomposes each pixel's color into a base color from standard 3DGS alpha-blending of SH coefficients and a learned residual Δc predicted by a lightweight network. For feature extraction, the method finds ray–Gaussian-plane intersections (using a new learnable normal per Gaussian), selects the K median intersections near the true surface (where accumulated transmittance ≈ 0.5), projects them onto neighboring source views via bilinear sampling, and computes per-view color differences from the base color along with camera pose deltas. A PointNet-style per-pixel extractor (two 32-dim linear layers + ReLU per view, max-pooled across views) produces a 32-channel feature map that, together with the base image and ray-direction map, is processed by a nine-layer 3×3 CNN decoder to yield the residual map. An exposure-correction module solves a per-pixel least-squares affine transform between the base color and the nearest source view's warped color, then applies it to the rendered image so that the correction generalizes to arbitrary novel viewpoints. Training uses a combined loss of L1+SSIM on both the base and final images, a multi-view photometric consistency loss on warped images, and a normal-consistency loss, with the photometric and normal terms activated after 7,000 of 30,000 iterations.

## Results

IBGS achieves the best rendering quality across all three standard NVS benchmarks: PSNR 28.33 / SSIM 0.837 / LPIPS 0.186 on Mip-NeRF360, PSNR 24.84 / SSIM 0.869 / LPIPS 0.148 on Tanks & Temples, and PSNR 30.12 / SSIM 0.912 / LPIPS 0.237 on Deep Blending. Notably, it uses only 1.59M Gaussians (291 MB) on Mip-NeRF360 and 0.75M Gaussians (143 MB) on TNT, reducing storage by over 60% compared to 3DGS while improving PSNR by 0.64 and 1.73 dB respectively. On the Shiny dataset with challenging view-dependent effects, IBGS gains at least 5.22 dB in PSNR over 3DGS (e.g., 35.65 vs 29.37 on Guitars) while using fewer Gaussians.

## Baselines & Comparisons

IBGS outperforms all five baselines on every dataset and metric. Against 3DGS [16], the closest Gaussian-splatting baseline, IBGS gains +0.64 dB PSNR on Mip-NeRF360 (28.33 vs 27.69), +1.73 dB on TNT (24.84 vs 23.11), and +0.59 dB on Deep Blending (30.12 vs 29.53), while using 51% fewer Gaussians and 62% less memory on Mip-NeRF360. Compared to SuperGauss [33], IBGS improves PSNR by 1.02 dB (Mip-NeRF360) and 1.12 dB (TNT) with roughly half the storage. Against TexturedGauss [4], which stores per-Gaussian texture maps, IBGS matches or exceeds PSNR (28.33 vs 27.35 on Mip-NeRF360) while consuming 72% less memory (291 MB vs 1047 MB). On the Shiny dataset with specular highlights, reflections, and diffraction, IBGS achieves 35.65 / 35.06 / 35.23 dB PSNR on Guitars / Lab / CD, surpassing 3DGS by 5.22–6.13 dB and SuperGauss by 4.63–5.74 dB, demonstrating a clear advantage in modeling complex view-dependent effects.

## Relation to Prior Work

IBGS builds directly on the 3DGS rasterization pipeline (Kerbl et al., 2023) and borrows the median-intersection surface estimation and normal consistency loss from 2DGS (Huang et al., 2024). It departs from prior texture-augmented Gaussian methods (TexturedGauss, SuperGaussians) that store global or per-Gaussian texture maps, instead drawing on the image-based rendering lineage (IBRNet, Light Field Neural Rendering) to warp source-image pixels onto the target view. The key conceptual shift is replacing learned texture maps with a lightweight residual network that reads directly from neighboring training images, sidestepping the quadratic storage growth of per-Gaussian textures while retaining fast splatting-based rendering.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a well-known weakness of 3DGS—poor high-frequency detail and view-dependent color modeling—without the storage penalty of texture maps, and it does so while actually reducing the number of Gaussians needed (up to 62% fewer). The exposure-correction module that generalizes to arbitrary novel views (rather than only training views) is a practical improvement for real-world captures with auto-exposure. Open questions include performance in sparse-view settings where dense pixel correspondences are unavailable, and the trade-off of lower rendering speed and higher runtime memory compared to vanilla 3DGS, which limits its use in latency-sensitive applications.
