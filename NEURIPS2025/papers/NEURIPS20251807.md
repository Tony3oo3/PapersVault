## Problem

Learning-based inverse lithography methods achieve high simulation fidelity but generate curvilinear masks on continuous pixel grids, violating the Manhattan (axis-aligned) constraints that are essential for photomask manufacturability and EDA toolchain compatibility. This paper addresses the gap by formulating mask optimization as a discrete, structure-aware sequence modeling task that inherently enforces Manhattan geometry throughout the optimization process rather than treating it as a post-hoc constraint.

## Input / Output

The method takes Manhattan IC layout polygons as input, which are first densified into axis-aligned line segments and further refined by a lithography-aware segmentation algorithm (corner-based splitting, spatial alignment casting across parallel edges, and DFM-compliant adaptive partitioning) to produce a structured point sequence {(x_i, y_i)}_{i=1}^N. The model outputs a per-point scalar correction vector Δ ∈ R^N (one offset per point along its edge's normal axis), which displaces the original points to define corrected Manhattan-aligned segments. These segments are rasterized via nvdiffrast into a binary mask image M ∈ {0,1}^{H×W} at 1 nm/pixel resolution, then passed through a differentiable lithography simulator to produce a wafer image for loss computation.

## Method

TokMan employs a conditional Diffusion Transformer (DiT) that treats the OPC correction field Δ as the target variable in a denoising diffusion process: forward diffusion adds Gaussian noise to simulate optical proximity degradation, and the DiT learns to predict the added noise ε from the noisy correction Δ_t, conditioned on the fixed layout geometry X and timestep t. The architecture (6 Transformer blocks, 512-dim embeddings, 16 attention heads) incorporates dual positional encodings—sinusoidal point-level embeddings and shared polygon-level embeddings—to capture both local edge geometry and global shape consistency. Training is fully self-supervised: predicted corrections are rendered into a binary mask via nvdiffrast, passed through a differentiable lithography simulator (193 nm wavelength, ±30 nm defocus, ±3% dose), and compared against the target layout using pixel-wise MSE, enabling end-to-end gradient flow without ground-truth mask annotations. A lightweight ILT post-processing step is optionally applied after inference to refine line-edge fidelity while preserving strict Manhattan compliance, operating in a narrow solution space since the DiT output already closely approximates the correct pattern.

## Results

On ICCAD13-S, TokMan achieves an EPE of 2.16 nm, a mask shot count of 287.6, and a Turn-Around Time of 0.89 s, outperforming all five baselines on every metric. On the more complex ICCAD13-L benchmark, TokMan records an EPE of 1.38 nm, 642.3 mask shots, and 1.57 s TAT. Compared to the prior SOTA (IL-ILT), TokMan improves EPE by over 20% (2.16 vs. 2.70 on S; 1.38 vs. 1.80 on L) while reducing mask shots by roughly 12–17×, and it is the fastest method on ICCAD13-S.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TokMan outperforms all five baselines on every metric across both ICCAD13-S and ICCAD13-L. Against the closest SOTA, IL-ILT [12], TokMan reduces EPE from 2.70 to 2.16 nm on S (20% improvement) and from 1.80 to 1.38 nm on L (23% improvement), while cutting mask shots from 4,948 to 288 on S (~17×) and from 7,916 to 642 on L (~12×). Compared to DevelSet [8], which is the fastest optimization-based baseline (1.53 s TAT on S), TokMan is faster (0.89 s) and achieves substantially lower EPE (2.16 vs. 5.20) and fewer shots (288 vs. 955). Multi-ILT [11] and Neural-ILT [10] produce the highest mask shot counts (6,700–8,000) due to curvilinear features, whereas TokMan's Manhattan-constrained tokenization keeps shots under 700. GAN-OPC [9] is the weakest baseline overall, with EPE values 3–4× higher and TAT 15–17× slower than TokMan. The only metric where TokMan is not the absolute best is TAT on ICCAD13-L (1.57 s vs. Multi-ILT's 1.33 s and DevelSet's 1.54 s), a marginal difference attributable to the ILT post-processing step.

## Relation to Prior Work

TokMan departs from the dominant paradigm in learning-based OPC/ILT (GAN-OPC, Neural-ILT, DevelSet, Multi-ILT, IL-ILT), which relaxes binary masks into continuous pixel grids and optimizes via gradient descent, by instead formulating mask correction as a discrete sequence modeling problem over Manhattan-aligned geometric primitives. It draws on the 'tokenize everything' trend in ML (MeshGPT for meshes, EgoEgo for motion) and the Diffusion Transformer architecture, while leveraging differentiable lithography simulation (nvdiffrast + optical transfer function) for self-supervised training without ground-truth masks. The key conceptual departure is that Manhattan compliance is enforced structurally through the token vocabulary rather than as a post-hoc constraint on a continuous output, which prior methods cannot guarantee.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ~4× reduction in mask shot count (from thousands to a few hundred) is practically significant for EBL write-time and production cost, and the >20% EPE improvement over the prior SOTA (IL-ILT) demonstrates that discrete tokenization need not sacrifice lithographic fidelity. However, the method currently cannot incorporate Manhattan sub-resolution assist features (SRAFs), has only been validated on sliced layouts from a single benchmark (ICCAD2013) with synthetic training data, and lacks evaluation on full-chip or real industrial datasets—key open questions for production deployment. The broader implication is that discrete, structure-aware sequence modeling may be a more natural and scalable paradigm for constrained inverse imaging problems than continuous pixel optimization.
