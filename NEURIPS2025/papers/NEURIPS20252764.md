## Problem

Existing NLP datasets for central bank communications are geographically biased toward Western institutions, often cover only a single bank, and lack multi-dimensional annotation frameworks. This limits the generalizability of monetary policy research and the ability to build AI models that capture shared linguistic patterns across diverse economies. The paper addresses this by introducing the WCB dataset, the most comprehensive monetary policy corpus to date, spanning 25 central banks across six continents over 28 years.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252764/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes individual sentences (avg. 27 words) extracted from central bank monetary policy meeting minutes, press releases, or equivalent documents as input. Each sentence is annotated with three labels: a stance label (Hawkish, Dovish, Neutral, or Irrelevant), a temporal label (Forward Looking or Not Forward Looking), and an uncertainty label (Certain or Uncertain). At the document level, a hawkishness measure is computed as the ratio of hawkish-minus-dovish sentences to total relevant sentences, which is then correlated with CPI inflation data for economic validation.

## Method

The paper constructs a 380k-sentence corpus from 25 central banks through automated scraping, regex-based cleaning, and sentence-level tokenization, then uniformly samples 1,000 sentences per bank (25k total) for annotation using bank-specific annotation guides developed by four-annotator teams with dual-blind labeling, pairwise disagreement resolution, and expert review. For benchmarking, seven PLMs (BERT, RoBERTa, ModernBERT, FinBERT variants) are fine-tuned under two setups—General (all 25 banks aggregated) and Bank-Specific (1,000 sentences per bank)—while nine LLMs are evaluated zero-shot, few-shot, and with annotation-guide prompting across 15,075 experiments. The key empirical finding is that the General Setup model (RoBERTa-Large, 0.740 F1) consistently outperforms Bank-Specific models, a 'whole greater than the sum of its parts' effect validated through TF-IDF cross-bank cosine similarity regression (p=0.016) and qualitative embedding analysis using voyage-finance-2, confirming that shared linguistic structure across banks enables transfer learning benefits.

## Results

On the WCB dataset (25,000 annotated sentences from 25 central banks), fine-tuned RoBERTa-Large achieves the best Stance Detection performance with 0.740 average weighted F1, while RoBERTa-Base leads Temporal Classification at 0.868 and RoBERTa-Large leads Uncertainty Estimation at 0.846. Among LLMs evaluated zero-shot, Llama-3-70B-Chat is the strongest, reaching 0.620 average F1 on Stance Detection—still 12 points below the best fine-tuned PLM. Across all three tasks, fine-tuned PLMs consistently outperform all nine LLMs, even when the LLMs are given few-shot examples or bank-specific annotation guides.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RoBERTa-Large (0.740 F1) outperforms all other PLMs on Stance Detection, beating BERT-Large, ModernBERT-Large, and the domain-specific FinBERT-Pretrain, which underperforms despite financial pretraining—indicating the task requires more than financial jargon recognition. Among LLMs, Llama-3-70B-Chat (0.620) leads all nine LLMs zero-shot, while GPT-4.1 and DeepSeek-V3 each top performance on a combined eight banks; Llama-4-Scout performs worst across all banks, and the finance-specific FinMA has the lowest average LLM performance. The key finding is that fine-tuned PLMs surpass all LLMs by a wide margin (12+ F1 points on Stance Detection), and this gap persists even when LLMs are given few-shot examples or bank-specific annotation guides. On the Czech National Bank hold-out (out-of-sample bank), the General Setup model achieves 0.800 F1, confirming cross-bank transfer. On U.S. Congressional hearings (out-of-domain), the Temporal and Uncertainty PLMs generalize to 0.879 and 0.683 F1 respectively.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends Shah et al. (2023, 'Trillion Dollar Words'), which introduced stance detection (Hawkish/Dovish/Neutral) and a hawkishness measure for a single central bank (FOMC), by scaling to 25 central banks across six continents and adding two new annotation dimensions (Temporal Classification and Uncertainty Estimation). It also responds to the geographic-bias critique raised by Longpre et al. (2024) and Manvi et al. (2024), who showed that over 93% of financial NLP text data originates from North American and European sources. Conceptually, the paper departs from the dominant single-bank analysis paradigm (RBI, FOMC, ECB studied in isolation) by proposing a unified cross-bank framework and empirically demonstrating that aggregated training captures shared linguistic structure that individual-bank models miss.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As the largest monetary policy corpus to date (380k sentences, 25 banks, 28 years) with 25k expert-annotated sentences across three tasks, this dataset fills a critical gap in global financial NLP and provides a reproducible benchmark for 15,000+ experiments. The validation that the derived hawkishness measure tracks CPI inflation across 25 economies during crises (2001, 2008, 2020) gives the framework concrete economic utility beyond academic benchmarking. Key limitations include the restriction to English-language documents, the STEM-background of annotators, and the exclusion of Eurozone member banks (governed by the ECB), leaving significant geographic and linguistic coverage gaps. Open questions include whether the cross-bank transfer learning benefit holds for non-English central banks and whether the framework can support real-time policy monitoring at scale.
