## Problem

Property inference attacks have been well-studied for discriminative models (e.g., image classifiers) and generative models (e.g., GANs), but it remains unclear whether such attacks transfer to LLMs, which do not fit cleanly into either category and embed sensitive properties implicitly within text. This paper asks whether an adversary can extract dataset-level confidential properties (e.g., patient gender ratio, disease prevalence) from a fine-tuned LLM, and under what conditions different attack strategies succeed.

## Input / Output

The generation-based attack consumes a small set of domain-specific prompts (e.g., 'Hi, Chatdoctor, I have a medical question.') and produces 2000 sampled completions per prompt from the target LLM, which are then labeled by ChatGPT-4o into {0, 1, N/A} before aggregation into a single ratio estimate. The word-frequency attack additionally requires an auxiliary dataset (size ~14,791) from which the adversary trains 35–42 shadow models across 7 property ratios, generating ~100k samples per prompt per model to compute per-word occurrence frequencies over a selected keyword list. The final output in both cases is a scalar estimate of the target property ratio in [0, 1], evaluated by mean absolute error against the ground-truth ratio.

## Method

The paper proposes two attacks tailored to LLMs. The black-box generation-based attack generates samples from the target model using prompts that constrain output to the fine-tuning domain, labels each sample for property presence via an LLM judge, and estimates the ratio by averaging valid labels across prompts. The grey-box shadow-model attack trains multiple shadow LLMs on auxiliary data subsampled to match different property ratios, extracts word-frequency features (proportion of generated samples containing each keyword) from ~100k generations per model, and trains an XGBoost meta-regressor to map these frequency vectors to property ratios, which is then applied to the target model's frequencies. A key finding is that the effective attack depends on the fine-tuning paradigm: word-frequency excels in Q&A (SFT) mode when the property is encoded primarily in the patient's question, while the generation-based attack dominates in Chat-Completion (CLM-FT) mode or when the property appears in both question and answer, because SFT under-memorizes the input side relative to CLM-FT.

## Results

On the gender property inference task (Table 1), the word-frequency shadow-model attack achieves the lowest MAE in Q&A Mode (e.g., 6.85±5.03 for Llama-1 at 70% female ratio), while the black-box generation attack dominates in Chat-Completion Mode (e.g., 1.73±0.76 for Llama-1 at 30% female ratio). On the medical diagnosis task (Table 2), the BB generation attack consistently achieves the best MAE across all three attributes and both modes, with Llama-3 in CC mode reaching 0.63±0.23 (mental disorder) and 1.82±0.45 (digestive disorder). Both proposed attacks substantially outperform the direct-asking and perplexity-based baselines in nearly all settings, with the generation attack requiring only ~500 samples to converge below 2% MAE.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its two proposed attacks (BB generation and word-frequency shadow model) against three baselines: direct asking, perplexity-based shadow model, and generation without fine-tuning (sanity check). In Q&A Mode, the word-frequency attack outperforms both the perplexity baseline and BB generation across all three models; for example, on Llama-1 at 70% female ratio, word-frequency achieves 6.85% MAE versus 24.16% for perplexity and 1.45% for BB generation (though BB generation is competitive at 70%). In Chat-Completion Mode, the BB generation attack clearly wins, achieving 0.61–3.56% MAE across models and ratios, while the perplexity baseline degrades to 4.32–35.19% MAE. The direct-asking baseline is consistently the weakest, with MAE often exceeding 15–23%, confirming that models do not explicitly memorize dataset-level ratios. The generation-without-fine-tuning sanity check (Table 2) shows that pre-trained models already produce biased ratios (e.g., 64.2% female for Llama-1), but the fine-tuned models' ratios shift significantly, validating that the attacks exploit fine-tuning-specific leakage rather than pre-training artifacts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the property inference attack literature, which has previously focused on discriminative models (tabular/image data, where attacks use model weights or loss signals) and pure generative models (GANs, where attacks exploit the distribution of generated samples), to the LLM setting. The generation-based attack directly adapts Zhou et al.'s GAN attack to conditional LLM generation, while the shadow-model attack repurposes the Suri et al. framework but replaces loss/probability-vector features with word-frequency signals—a choice motivated by evidence that these traditional features are less informative for LLMs. The key conceptual departure is recognizing that LLMs straddle the discriminative/generative divide, so neither prior attack family transfers directly without modification, and the effective strategy depends on the fine-tuning paradigm (SFT vs. CLM-FT).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals a concrete, previously underexplored confidentiality vulnerability in deployed fine-tuned LLMs: an adversary with only API access can estimate sensitive dataset-level properties (e.g., patient gender ratio, disease prevalence) with low error. The mode-dependent finding—that word-frequency shadow attacks dominate in Q&A mode while simple generation-based attacks suffice in chat-completion mode—gives practitioners actionable guidance on where the risk lies. Limitations include evaluation on a single domain (medical) and a single base dataset (ChatDoctor), and the attack struggles with culturally sensitive properties (childbirth), leaving open whether the vulnerability generalizes to other domains and whether the pre-training safety alignment can be exploited or mitigated. Robust defenses remain an open problem; the temperature-adjustment defense proposed here is fragile against an informed adversary.
