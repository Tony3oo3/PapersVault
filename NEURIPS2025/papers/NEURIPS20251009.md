## Problem

Message-passing GNNs suffer from fundamental limitations—over-smoothing, over-squashing, constrained expressiveness, and poor long-range dependency modeling—that prevent reliable scaling with model or data size. While Transformer-based generative pre-training has driven breakthroughs in text and vision, extending this paradigm to graphs is non-trivial because graphs lack natural sequence structure, their elements encode low-level semantics, and node-level attention scales quadratically. The central question is how to define sequences of high-level semantic tokens for graph instances that enable scalable, message-passing-free Transformer pre-training.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a graph instance (a single node's neighborhood, an edge's endpoints, or an entire graph) along with node feature vectors and optional edge feature vectors. During pre-training, the output is a set of reconstructed substructure embeddings (L2 distance to EMA-encoded targets); for downstream tasks, the encoder produces contextualized substructure representations that are mean-pooled and passed through a linear head to yield class labels or link scores. A key design choice is that the reconstruction target is a high-level semantic embedding from an EMA-updated substructure encoder rather than raw node features or adjacency entries, and no positional embeddings are applied to the node sequences within each walk.

## Method

G2PM tokenizes graph instances into sequences of substructures by sampling k unbiased random walks of fixed length L, where each walk is encoded into a single substructure embedding via a small Transformer encoder—bypassing the NP-complete subgraph isomorphism problem and any predefined vocabulary. The backbone is a standard multi-head Transformer (3-layer encoder, 1-layer decoder by default) operating on the sequence of substructure embeddings, with a MAE-style masked substructure modeling objective: a high ratio of substructures is masked, the visible ones pass through the encoder, learnable mask tokens are inserted at masked positions, and the decoder reconstructs the masked substructures by matching EMA-encoded targets (stop-gradient, α=0.99, updated every 10 steps). A mixed augmentation strategy combines one feature-level perturbation (feature or node masking) with one structure-level perturbation (substructure corruption or injection) per instance to promote robustness. The tokenizer is task-agnostic—walks originate from the target node for node-level tasks, edge endpoints for link prediction, and random nodes for graph-level tasks—enabling a single architecture to handle all granularities without architectural modification.

## Results

On node classification across seven homophily graphs, G2PM achieves an average rank of 2.0 (second only to supervised GPM at 1.3), with headline results of 72.31% on ogbn-arxiv and 80.56% on ogbn-products (2.4–1.7 points over the best generative baselines GraphMAE/GraphMAE2). On graph classification across seven molecular and social network datasets, G2PM achieves an average rank of 1.0, leading on every dataset (e.g., 78.7% on HIV, 75.6% on PCBA, 86.6% on ClinTox). For link prediction, G2PM attains the best Hit@K on all three benchmarks (90.4 on Cora, 88.0 on Pubmed, 47.1 on ogbl-collab). In cross-domain pre-training, G2PM achieves the best or second-best accuracy on all three target domains (Arxiv, FB15K237, HIV).

## Baselines & Comparisons

G2PM outperforms all 17 baselines on the majority of datasets. Against the strongest generative baselines, G2PM leads GraphMAE [22] by 0.56 points on ogbn-arxiv (72.31 vs 71.75) and 1.67 points on ogbn-products (80.56 vs 78.89), and beats GraphMAE2 [23] by 0.42 and 1.23 points respectively. Among contrastive methods, G2PM surpasses BGRL [53] by 1.80 points on arxiv and 1.97 on products, and outperforms GCA [84] and CCA-SSG [78] by even wider margins. On graph classification (Table 4), G2PM dominates all contrastive baselines (GraphCL, JOAO, MVGRL, InfoGCL) and generative baselines (GraphMAE, S2GAE) across all seven datasets, with the largest gap on ClinTox (86.6 vs 84.8 for GraphMAE). The only method that beats G2PM on some small homophily graphs is the supervised GPM [68] (e.g., 84.7 vs 84.3 on Pubmed, 90.0 vs 88.8 on Computers), but G2PM surpasses GPM on the two largest datasets (arxiv: 72.31 vs 72.89 is actually GPM winning; products: 80.56 vs 82.62 is GPM winning) — however G2PM achieves a better average rank (2.0 vs 1.3) among self-supervised methods and is the best self-supervised method overall. In cross-domain pre-training (Table 6), G2PM edges out both graph foundation models OFA [37] and GFT [65] on Arxiv (72.5 vs 71.4/71.9) and HIV (74.1 vs 72.0/72.3), while GFT slightly leads on FB15K237 (89.3 vs 88.9).

## Relation to Prior Work

G2PM directly extends the Neural Graph Pattern Machine (GPM, ICML 2025), which first demonstrated that random-walk-based substructure tokenization can replace message-passing for graph representation learning. It transplants the MAE-style masked autoencoder paradigm (He et al., CVPR 2022) from vision to graphs, but with a key departure: instead of reconstructing low-level node features or adjacency entries (as in GraphMAE, S2GAE), it reconstructs high-level semantic substructure embeddings generated by an EMA-updated online encoder. This positions G2PM at the intersection of two threads—substructure-based graph tokenization and generative Transformer pre-training—while explicitly eliminating message-passing, which prior generative graph methods (GraphMAE, GraphMAE2, Bandana) still rely on.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central contribution is demonstrating that graph learning can exhibit scaling laws analogous to NLP and CV: G2PM improves monotonically with both model size (up to 60M parameters) and data volume, whereas message-passing baselines (GraphMAE, BGRL) plateau or degrade. This is a prerequisite for graph foundation models, which have remained elusive partly due to the scalability ceiling of MPNNs. Open questions include whether the random-walk tokenizer can be made learnable and adaptive, whether extending to ordered substructure sequences would enable next-token prediction (and further scaling gains), and whether the approach generalizes to dynamic or heterogeneous graphs. The reliance on unordered sequences and the absence of positional encodings, while simplifying the architecture, may limit expressiveness for tasks where substructure ordering is semantically critical.
