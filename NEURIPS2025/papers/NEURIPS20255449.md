## Problem

When LLMs are trained on realistic data mixtures where knowledge-dense data constitutes only a small fraction (often <1%) of the corpus, it is unclear how much knowledge the model actually acquires. Prior work established linear scaling of knowledge capacity with model size under exclusive training on knowledge-dense data, but the behavior under data mixing was unexplored. This paper reveals that knowledge acquisition does not scale smoothly but instead exhibits sharp phase transitions with respect to both model size and mixing ratio, meaning a mixing recipe optimal for large models can be completely ineffective for small ones.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-training mixture of a knowledge-dense dataset (SynBio synthetic biographies, WikiBio Wikipedia biographies, or modified OpenWebMath) and a large-scale web corpus (FineWeb-Edu or The Pile) at a specified mixing ratio r, and trains Pythia models from 14M to 6.9B parameters. Evaluation uses exact-match on knowledge triplets (name, attribute, value) via greedy decoding over |v| tokens, reporting accuracy averaged over all individuals, attributes, and five sentence templates. The theoretical component outputs a predicted threshold frequency f_thres ~ 1/M^(α+1) for each fact, and the practical strategies output enhanced training data via random subsampling or compact tuple-format augmentation (CKM) that increase per-fact exposure frequency while preserving the overall mixing ratio.

## Method

The paper combines controlled empirical experiments with an information-theoretic theory. Empirically, they train Pythia models on mixtures of synthetic biographies (SynBio) or real Wikipedia biographies (WikiBio) with FineWeb-Edu/The Pile, varying model size and mixing ratio to reveal two phase transitions: accuracy remains near zero below a critical model size M_thres or mixing ratio r_thres, then jumps sharply. Theoretically, they model a sufficiently trained LLM as an optimal bounded-capacity learner that minimizes test loss under a capacity constraint M, framing capacity allocation across datasets as a fractional knapsack problem where each dataset is allocated capacity proportional to its marginal value (reduction in test loss per unit capacity). They prove (Theorem 4.3) that the knowledge-dense dataset becomes worth learning only when r or M exceeds a threshold determined by the derivative of the web-data loss curve, and derive the power-law prediction r_thres ~ 1/(p · M^(α+1)) where α is the web-data scaling exponent. To make knowledge acquisition feasible at low mixing ratios, they propose two strategies that increase per-fact frequency: random subsampling (reducing dataset size S to lower r_thres ~ S/M^(α+1)) and Compact Knowledge Mixing (CKM), which appends compact tuple-form rephrasings to boost effective frequency by 2–4×, achieving up to 20× more learned facts while preserving general capability.

## Results

The paper demonstrates that knowledge acquisition from knowledge-dense data in mixed pre-training exhibits sharp phase transitions rather than smooth scaling. For 70M models trained on FineWeb-Edu + SynBio-320k, accuracy remains at ~0.4% for mixing ratios r ≤ 0.25, then jumps to 7.3% at r=0.3, 27.5% at r=0.35, 40.4% at r=0.4, and 58.1% at r=0.45. The critical mixing ratio follows a power law r_thres ∝ 1/(p·M^(α+1)), with an empirically validated exponent of 1.152 (predicted 1.283 from α≈0.283). The proposed strategies—random subsampling and Compact Knowledge Mixing (CKM)—improve the number of learned facts by 4× and 20× respectively on WikiBio while preserving general capability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against competing methods but instead contrasts the mixed-training regime against the exclusive-training baseline established by Allen-Zhu and Li (2024a), where knowledge capacity scales linearly with model size. Under data mixing, this linear scaling breaks down: below the critical mixing ratio, the model memorizes essentially zero biographies even after 512B tokens of training (each biography seen ~3000 times), whereas above the threshold, accuracy surges to 60–80%. The required training steps to reach 40% accuracy grow superexponentially with 1/r, deviating by >2.9× from a power-law extrapolation at r=0.25. For the proposed enhancement strategies, random subsampling to 25% of SynBio-1.28M at r=0.2 achieves 23.5% accuracy (vs. ~0% without subsampling at the same r), and CKM at τ=0.3 boosts WikiBio learned facts by 20× over the no-augmentation baseline, all while keeping downstream performance degradation under 2%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the information-theoretic framework of Allen-Zhu and Li (2024a), who established a linear scaling law between model capacity and knowledge storage when training exclusively on knowledge-dense data, to the data mixing scenario where knowledge-dense data constitutes a small fraction of a web-scraped corpus. It adopts the SynBio synthetic biography construction methodology from Allen-Zhu and Li (2024b) for controlled quantitative experiments. Conceptually, the paper reframes knowledge acquisition under mixing as a fractional knapsack problem over capacity allocation, departing from prior data-mixing scaling-law work (Liu et al., Kang et al., Ye et al.) that models performance as a smooth function of mixing proportions, by showing that the optimal allocation can change discontinuously.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that knowledge acquisition exhibits sharp phase transitions rather than smooth scaling under data mixing has direct practical consequences: mixing ratios that work for large models may yield zero knowledge acquisition for smaller models, undermining the common practice of using small proxy models for data curation. The predicted power-law relationship between threshold frequency and model size, validated across Llama-2, Qwen-2.5, Gemma-2, and Llama-3, offers a predictive tool for practitioners. Open questions include how these transitions behave for heterogeneous real-world datasets (where different knowledge types have different learning difficulties) and for more complex multi-step reasoning tasks beyond the simple slope-calculation and max-over-N experiments.
