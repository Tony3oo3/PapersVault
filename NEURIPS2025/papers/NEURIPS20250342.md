## Problem

Existing benchmarks for automated theorem proving (MiniF2F, ProofNet, PutnamBench) measure raw problem difficulty but do not test whether provers can generalize reasoning strategies across structurally simple compositions. This paper asks whether LLM-based formal provers can recognize that a problem reduces to applying a known inequality (e.g., AM-GM, Cauchy-Schwarz) when the problem is composed from multiple such applications or subjected to trivial algebraic rewrites—transformations that are immediate for humans. The answer, revealed by their benchmark, is largely no: most provers suffer large accuracy drops even on single-step compositional variants of problems they already solve.

## Input / Output

Each problem is a Lean 4 theorem statement specifying real-valued variables with positivity constraints and an inequality to prove; the prover must output a complete Lean 4 proof that compiles against Mathlib. Evaluation uses pass@N accuracy (solved if at least one of N generated proofs is verified correct), with budgets from 32 to 3200 attempts. The benchmark spans three tiers: Ineq-Simp (150 single-step transformations of 75 seeds), Ineq-Mix (100 rule-composed pairs of seeds), and Ineq-Real (50 contest-level problems), all at or below introductory Olympiad difficulty.

## Method

The benchmark is constructed bottom-up from 75 seed inequalities (AM-GM, Cauchy-Schwarz, Jensen, Schur, sum-of-squares, induction), each accompanied by a verified Lean 4 proof. Type I variants duplicate the seed with fresh variables and combine the two copies via multiplication or addition; Type II variants apply one-step algebraic substitutions (squaring, square-rooting, reciprocating variables). Ineq-Mix extends this with an automated rule-based framework covering compositional, variable-level, and problem-level transformations, guaranteeing mathematical validity of every generated problem. The paper benchmarks nine LLM-based provers across three categories (general-purpose, whole-proof generation, tree-search) and probes the compositional gap through three ablations: scaling model size (7B→671B), in-context learning with seed proofs provided in the prompt, and supervised fine-tuning on 8k synthetic compositional proofs—finding that none of these remedies fully closes the gap.

## Results

On Ineq-Simp (pass@32), DeepSeek-Prover-V2-7B achieves the highest accuracy: 75.0% on AM-GM seed, 58.6% on Type I, and 52.5% on Type II. Most other provers suffer dramatic drops: Goedel-Prover-SFT falls from 48.6% (seed) to 0.4% (Type I AM-GM), and DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-RL drops from 48.1% to 0.0%. Even at the maximum budget (pass@3200), DeepSeek-Prover-V2-7B retains a 12-point gap between seed (96%) and Type I (84%) on AM-GM, confirming the compositional weakness is not merely a sampling issue. On Ineq-Mix (100 composed problems), all whole-proof models except DeepSeek-Prover-V2-7B solve fewer than 8 problems at 128 trials, while V2-7B solves 22.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DeepSeek-Prover-V2-7B is the only model that maintains reasonable accuracy on compositional variants, achieving 58.6% on Type I AM-GM (vs. 75.0% seed), a ~16-point drop. Goedel-Prover-SFT and DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-RL are the most severely affected, dropping to near 0% on Type I AM-GM (0.4% and 0.0% respectively at pass@32), indicating they cannot decompose duplicated inequalities at all. Kimina-Distill-7B shows the best Type II performance among non-V2 models (45.2% AM-GM) but still drops from 59.4% seed to 11.7% on Type I. STP (without MiniF2F validation) is intermediate, dropping from 59.1% to 14.3% on Type I AM-GM. Tree-search methods (DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-RL+RMaxTS, InternLM2.5-StepProver+BF) fail to bridge the gap, with RMaxTS reaching only 3.0% on Type I AM-GM even at 1×3200 budget. Scaling experiments (DeepSeek-Prover-V2-671B, Goedel-Prover-V2-32B) confirm the compositional gap persists: V2-671B still shows a ~20-point seed-to-Type I drop (88% → 68.3%) at pass@32.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing a bottom-up, rule-based framework for generating compositional variants of inequality problems, departing from both existing ATP benchmarks (MiniF2F, ProofNet, PutnamBench) that measure raw difficulty and top-down annotation approaches (Yousefzadeh et al., 2025) that are hard to scale. It repurposes the concept of compositional generalization—well-studied in NLP and vision—into the formal proving setting, where no prior benchmark explicitly isolates a model's ability to reuse known proof strategies across structurally transformed but logically equivalent problems. The Ineq-Mix automated generation pipeline is distinct from inequality-specific solvers (AIPS, Li et al.) that target IMO-level difficulty, instead probing brittleness on problems trivial to humans.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a fundamental and previously unmeasured weakness in LLM-based provers: they cannot reliably compose even the simplest known proof steps, and this gap persists under in-context demonstrations, model scaling (7B→671B), and supervised fine-tuning on compositional data. This challenges the field's implicit assumption that improving raw proof-generation capability (as measured by MiniF2F) will naturally yield robust compositional reasoning. Open questions include whether the failure is specific to the Lean 4 tactic space (e.g., over-reliance on nlinarith/sq_nonneg) or reflects a deeper limitation in how LLMs internalize mathematical structure, and whether richer training signals (e.g., the divide-and-conquer RL in DeepSeek-Prover-V2) can eventually close the gap.
