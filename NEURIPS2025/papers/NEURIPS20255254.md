## Problem

Despite the strong empirical performance of distributional RL over classical RL, the theoretical explanation for this advantage remains incomplete, particularly for practical implementations with general function approximation. This paper asks what specific mechanism within the categorical distributional loss (e.g., C51's KL divergence) accounts for the outperformance of distributional RL, and how it relates to exploration.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255254/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on the standard categorical distributional loss in CDRL, where the return distribution is parameterized as a discrete mixture of N Dirac measures over fixed supports with learnable probabilities. The decomposition produces two components: a mean-related negative log-likelihood term (asymptotically equivalent to classical Q-learning) and a cross-entropy regularization term α·H(μ, q_θ) between an induced histogram density (capturing distributional information beyond the mean) and the current return density estimate. A hyperparameter ε controls the proportion of mass allocated to the mean component versus the distributional component, with validity requiring ε ≥ 1 − p_E where p_E is the probability mass on the bin containing the expected return.

## Method

The core technique is a return density decomposition, inspired by the gross error model from robust statistics, that splits the histogram density estimator into a single-bin histogram (capturing only the expectation) and an induced histogram density (capturing higher-moment information). Applied within the Neural FZI framework, this decomposition of the KL-based distributional loss (Proposition 2) yields a mean-related term plus a distribution-matching regularization term, where the mean-related term is shown to be asymptotically equivalent to Neural FQI as bin width Δ→0 (Proposition 3). The regularization term is then interpreted as an uncertainty-aware exploration mechanism: in the actor-critic setting, it augments the reward signal via a Distribution-Entropy-Regularized Bellman Operator to encourage policies toward states where the current return distribution estimate lags far behind the estimated environmental uncertainty in target returns. This contrasts with MaxEnt RL's vanilla entropy regularization, which explicitly promotes diverse actions; instead, CDRL's derived regularization implicitly aligns the learned policy with environmental uncertainty through the distribution-matching cross-entropy, yielding a distinct exploration strategy.

## Results

The paper's primary empirical results are presented as learning curves rather than a single numerical score table. On eight Atari games (Figure 3), the decomposed algorithm H(μ, q_θ) with ε = 0.8/0.5/0.1 shows progressive degradation from C51-level performance toward DQN as ε decreases, confirming that the uncertainty-aware distribution-matching regularization is pivotal to CDRL's empirical advantage over classical RL. On seven MuJoCo environments (Figure 4), simultaneously employing both uncertainty-aware entropy (UE) and vanilla entropy (VE) yields mutual improvement on humanoidstandup (DSAC outperforms both SAC and DAC) but potential interference on Ant and Swimmer where the combined method underperforms each individual regularization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four baselines across Atari and MuJoCo. C51 (Bellemare et al., 2017) serves as the full CDRL reference; the decomposed algorithm at ε = 0.8 closely matches C51, validating that the return density decomposition preserves the full distributional loss, while at ε = 0.1 performance collapses toward DQN (Mnih et al., 2015), the classical RL baseline, demonstrating that removing the regularization term eliminates CDRL's advantage. On MuJoCo, SAC (Haarnoja et al., 2018; AC+VE) and DAC (AC+UE) each outperform plain AC, but their combination as DSAC (Ma et al., 2020; AC+UE+VE) shows task-dependent behavior: DSAC surpasses both SAC and DAC on humanoidstandup (mutual improvement) yet underperforms on Ant and Swimmer (interference), which the authors attribute to the two regularizations driving exploration in distinct directions—SAC toward high-entropy states and CDRL toward states where return-distribution estimates lag behind environmental uncertainty.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on C51/CDRL (Bellemare et al., 2017) by decomposing its KL-based categorical loss into a mean-related term and a distribution-matching regularization, using a return density decomposition technique adapted from the gross error model in robust statistics (Huber, 2004). It extends the Neural FQI analytical framework (Riedmiller, 2005; Fan et al., 2020) to a Neural FZI setting for distributional RL, and positions its uncertainty-aware exploration interpretation against both prior theoretical analyses of distributional RL (Lyle et al., 2019; Rowland et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023/2024) and the explicit entropy regularization in MaxEnt RL (Williams & Peng, 1991; Haarnoja et al., 2018). The key departure from prior work is that earlier analyses either could not explain benefits in non-linear function approximation, focused on quantile-based methods, or relied on PAC bounds not directly tied to practical algorithms like C51; this paper provides a direct, algorithm-level decomposition of the exact loss used in CDRL.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a mechanistic, loss-level explanation for why categorical distributional RL outperforms classical RL: the KL loss implicitly encodes an uncertainty-aware entropy regularization that drives the policy toward states where the current return distribution estimate lags behind environmental uncertainty. This reframing is practically useful because it clarifies that distributional RL's exploration benefit is fundamentally different from MaxEnt RL's diverse-action exploration, and the empirical finding that the two regularizations can interfere (not merely complement) when combined has direct implications for algorithm design in distributional actor-critic methods. A key limitation is that the analysis is primarily founded on CDRL; the extension to quantile-based methods (QR-DQN, IQN) is only sketched, leaving open whether the same decomposition structure and exploration interpretation hold for other distributional RL families.
