## Problem

LLM-based autonomous driving methods trained on real-world data fail to capture rare, long-tail scenarios such as traffic accidents and pedestrian intrusions, while those trained on simulation data cannot be deployed in real-world conditions. No existing approach effectively transfers the long-tail handling capabilities learned in simulation to a real-world deployable model, leaving a critical gap between the two data sources.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252384/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes six surrounding camera images stacked directly as 2D inputs (no BEV transformation or extrinsic/intrinsic parameters, emulating human visual perspective), a cached temporal instruction from the previous frame (a filtered, one-sentence summary of the prior CoC answer rather than raw sensor data), and a multi-question end-to-end driving prompt. The output is a structured CoC answer that chains perception (object detection with pixel bounding-box coordinates), prediction (object motion status), and planning (ego-vehicle trajectory waypoints) into a single causal narrative, plus a condensed cached instruction for the next frame's temporal context.

## Method

The CoC VLM backbone (built on LLaVA-v1.5 with CLIP vision encoder and LLaMA LLM) introduces a Chain-of-Causality Text Adapter that concatenates the previous frame's filtered answer with the current driving prompt, enabling temporal causal reasoning without feeding full image sequences and significantly reducing token length. A transformer-based discriminator learns the domain gap between simulation and real-world feature spaces via a transformation matrix T_{s2r} formulated as f-divergence minimization. Training proceeds in two stages: first, the Teacher VLM is pre-trained on CARLA-VLM (simulation, 38.4% challenging scenarios) and the Student VLM on nuScenes-VLM (real-world) separately; second, adversarial training alternates between updating only the discriminator (Step 1) and updating the Student VLM with a combined driving loss plus discriminator loss while freezing discriminator parameters (Step 2), using a novel backpropagation strategy for convergence stability. LoRA fine-tuning on 8× NVIDIA H100 GPUs keeps the process computationally tractable, and at inference only the Student VLM is deployed.

## Results

On the nuScenes-VLM dataset, CoC-VLA (LLaVA-7b) achieves an ADE of 1.44 and a collision rate of 1.58% in open-loop planning, outperforming DriveLM (ADE 1.71, collision rate 1.87%) by 15.8% and 15.5% respectively. In VQA evaluation, the method scores BLEU-1 of 74.06, ROUGE-L of 71.70, CIDEr of 23.21, and SPICE of 51.88, surpassing DriveLM on all language metrics. In closed-loop evaluation on NeuroNCAP, CoC-VLA achieves a score of 3.32 with a 45.26% collision rate, outperforming UniAD (1.84 / 68.70%) and VAD (2.75 / 50.70%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is DriveLM [42], the most directly comparable LLM-based driving VQA method. CoC-VLA outperforms DriveLM on every reported metric: ADE drops from 1.71 to 1.44 (−15.8%), collision rate from 1.87% to 1.58% (−15.5%), CIDEr from 3.62 to 23.21 (≈6.4×), and SPICE from 48.95 to 51.88. Two internal ablation variants (mix-training and two-stage fine-tuning) also underperform the full adversarial transfer pipeline, confirming that the discriminator-based domain transfer is the key driver of improvement. In closed-loop NeuroNCAP evaluation, CoC-VLA (score 3.32, collision 45.26%) beats both UniAD (1.84, 68.70%) and VAD (2.75, 50.70%), demonstrating that the transferred long-tail handling capability generalizes beyond open-loop metrics to interactive driving.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on LLaVA-v1.5 as its VLM backbone and on DriveLM's graph-based VQA formulation for driving, extending both with a chain-of-causality temporal adapter that caches simplified prior-frame answers instead of feeding full image sequences (as in LMDrive). Its core methodological departure is the adversarial domain-transfer framework: a GAN-inspired discriminator trained to align feature distributions between a simulation-pretrained teacher and a real-world-pretrained student VLM, a strategy borrowed from unsupervised domain adaptation literature but applied here for the first time to VLM-based driving. The paper positions itself against the two dominant paradigms in LLM-driven driving—real-world-trained models (DriveGPT4, DriveLM) that miss long-tail events, and simulation-trained models (LMDrive) that cannot deploy in the real world—arguing that neither alone suffices.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The sim-to-real gap for LLM-based driving is a practical bottleneck: real-world datasets underrepresent rare hazards (pedestrian intrusions, accidents), while simulation-trained models produce control signals that cannot be executed on physical vehicles. By demonstrating that adversarial feature alignment can transfer long-tail handling capability without requiring the student to see simulation data at inference, the paper offers a deployment-friendly recipe. However, the evaluation is confined to a single real-world benchmark (nuScenes-VLM) with a self-generated CARLA simulation set, and the closed-loop collision rates remain high (45–68%), leaving open whether the transferred capability generalizes to unseen geographies, sensor suites, or truly novel long-tail events beyond the 15 CARLA scenario categories. The modest ADE gains (~15%) over DriveLM, while the CIDEr gap is large, also raise questions about whether the language-metric improvements reflect genuine driving-logic gains or template-matching artifacts.
