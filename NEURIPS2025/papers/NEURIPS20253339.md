## Problem

Existing membership inference attacks for diffusion models rely on loss thresholding at a single intermediate timestep, which cannot distinguish model-generated data from training data and can underperform naive model-blind baselines under distribution shifts. The widely-adopted 'Goldilocks zone' conjecture further restricts analysis to a narrow band of denoising steps, discarding temporal information that may be critical for robust provenance. No unified framework exists that simultaneously addresses membership inference, model attribution, and external data detection for diffusion models.

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image and white-box access to a trained DDPM (with its noise schedule {α̅_t}) as input. The image is processed through all T timesteps of the forward diffusion process, producing a trajectory feature vector of dimension 3T (denoising loss, input-gradient norm, and parameter-gradient norm at each step). A linear probe trained on <3.4% of the member set maps this vector to a ternary provenance label: member (training data), belonging (model-generated), or external (neither). Experiments use 32×32 CIFAR-10/100 and 256×256 CelebA-HQ images with 1000 samples per class for classifier development.

## Method

The core contribution is a trajectory-based feature extraction that concatenates the denoising loss L_t, its gradient norm with respect to the input ‖∇_x L_t‖², and its gradient norm with respect to model parameters ‖∇_θ L_t‖² across all timesteps t = 0, …, T−1, challenging the Goldilocks zone conjecture by showing that uniform coverage of the full diffusion process outperforms focusing on intermediate steps. A linear classifier (linear probing with AdamW optimizer) is trained on these trajectory features to perform ternary origin attribution, unifying membership inference and model attribution into a single framework. The gradient features are motivated by their ability to capture curvature information in the score-matching landscape, and ablations confirm that combining all three feature types (loss, input gradient, parameter gradient) yields the strongest performance. The approach requires no surrogate models and operates under a limited-data threat model, making it directly applicable to auditing memorization, detecting data extraction, and attributing synthetic images to specific diffusion models.

## Results

For the unified Origin Attribution (OA) task (member/belonging/external), the full trajectory feature set (L_t, ∇_x L_t, ∇_θ L_t at t=0,1,…,999) achieves 86.5 AUC and 71.1% ASR on CIFAR-10, and 99.3 AUC and 94.1% ASR on CelebA-HQ 256. For binary MIA, the same features yield 83.3 AUC on CIFAR-10 (Table 8), outperforming the strongest baseline GSA₂ (82.7 AUC, Table 2). On CIFAR-100, the proposed method reaches 9.4% TPR @ 1% FPR (±2.50) versus 2.6% (±1.10) for GSA₂, a 3.6× improvement under intraclass distribution shifts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Matsumoto et al. (2023), the proposed trajectory method wins on both datasets: 83.3 vs. 63.2 AUC on CIFAR-10 and 100.0 vs. 85.2 AUC on CelebA-HQ; notably, Matsumoto's threshold-based approach underperforms the model-blind ResNet-18 baseline (94.4 AUC) on CelebA-HQ, exposing its brittleness under distribution shift. Against Kong et al. (2024) PIA, the gap is even wider—PIA collapses to 62.5 AUC on CelebA-HQ (below random for a shifted task) while the trajectory method maintains 100.0 AUC. Against the strongest baseline, Pang et al. (2025) GSA₂, the proposed method achieves a modest AUC gain on CIFAR-10 (83.3 vs. 82.7) and a larger TPR@1% FPR improvement (16.8 vs. 12.5); on CIFAR-100 the advantage is substantial (9.4 vs. 2.6 TPR@1% FPR over 10 random splits), while GSA₂ retains a slight edge on CelebA-HQ (99.6 vs. 100.0 TPR@1% FPR, though both saturate). Overall, the trajectory approach is competitive with or superior to all three baselines, with the most decisive wins over the threshold-based methods under distribution shift.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant threshold-based MIA paradigm for diffusion models (Yeom et al. 2018; Matsumoto et al. 2023; Kong et al. 2024) by arguing that denoising loss at a single timestep is an insufficient and brittle signal. It explicitly challenges the influential 'Goldilocks zone' conjecture of Carlini et al. (2023) by showing that uniform sampling across the full trajectory outperforms concentrated intermediate-step sampling. Conceptually, it extends the gradient-feature idea of GSA (Pang et al. 2025) from a fixed-timestep aggregation to a full temporal trajectory representation, and it unifies the traditionally separate tasks of membership inference and model attribution into a single ternary 'Origin Attribution' framework. For model attribution specifically, it is the first white-box approach applied directly to DDPMs, as prior MA work (Wang et al. 2023; Laszkiewicz et al. 2024) relied on reconstruction/inversion techniques applicable only to latent or distilled models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper exposes a fundamental flaw in widely-deployed diffusion MIAs: they cannot distinguish model-generated data from training data, rendering them useless for auditing memorization or synthetic data provenance. The finding that a model-blind ResNet-18 baseline outperforms representative threshold-based attacks under distribution shifts (CelebA-HQ/FFHQ) raises serious questions about whether current MIA benchmarks measure anything meaningful. Key limitations include evaluation only on DDPMs (not latent diffusion or large-scale models), inference-time computational cost, and observed failure cases in attribution (DDGAN, DDIM). Open questions include whether trajectory-based features scale to foundational generative systems, how robust they are to adversarial corruptions and intraclass shifts, and whether the unification framework holds when the 'external' class includes outputs from other diffusion models of similar architecture.
