## Problem

Existing Legal Judgment Prediction (LJP) models and benchmarks treat law articles as static, yet real legal systems undergo ongoing statutory revisions that fundamentally alter judgment logic. No prior work has systematically evaluated whether SOTA LJP models can adapt their reasoning when statutes change, and current datasets conflate different statute versions under the same label, introducing uncontrolled noise during training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254010/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each test instance provides a pair of law articles (pre- and post-revision) alongside a pair of fact descriptions (original and minimally edited to reflect the revision), and the model must produce a law article prediction, charge prediction, or penalty term prediction. Evaluation uses metamorphic relations rather than ground-truth labels: a test instance is passed if the model's output on the edited fact matches a logically derived expected outcome (Violation, Non-Violation, Increased/Decreased Term, Extreme/Non-Extreme Term), making the benchmark applicable even when no court-issued label exists for the revised statute.

## Method

LawShift decomposes law articles into six components (subject, action, object, objective condition, subjective condition, term sentence) and defines 31 fine-grained revision types by crossing each component with empirically observed amendment dimensions (scope expansion/reduction/reallocation, condition addition/removal, numerical/extremity sentence shifts). Test instances are constructed by minimally editing real cases from China Judgments Online (2017–2021) using regex-based perturbation for entity-level changes, GPT-4o-guided rewriting for action-scope changes, and direct reuse for sentence-term changes, all validated by legal experts. Evaluation employs two metamorphic testing strategies: minimal test instances that isolate a single capability (e.g., recognizing a newly included subject) and before/after comparison for sentencing revisions. Nine SOTA models spanning graph-based LJP (TopJudge, D-LADAN, NeurJudge), legal PLMs (Lawformer), and LLMs (Qwen2.5, Llama3, ChatGLM3, Llama3-LoRA-Law, Lawmodel-7B) are assessed via normalized relative pass rate shifts across components, supplemented by knowledge tracing and ROME-style localized weight editing experiments on Lawformer and NeurJudge.

## Results

On LawShift (7,569 test instances across 31 revision types), traditional LJP models (TopJudge, D-LADAN, NeurJudge, Lawformer) achieve 90–100% pass rates on original (unrevised) cases but drop to 13–14% on revision types requiring prediction changes (e.g., T1.4 subject reduction), revealing severe adaptability failures. LLM-based models (Qwen2.5-7B, Llama3, ChatGLM3) show higher pass rates on sentence-term revisions (T6.1–T6.4) but remain inconsistent across components. All models perform significantly worse on implicit rephrasing than explicit scope changes, and term/action revisions are the most challenging for every architecture, with Lawformer scoring 0% on T2.2 (implicit action expansion).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among the five primary baselines, Lawformer (92.34% on Ori.1) and NeurJudge (91.95%) lead on original-case accuracy, but both collapse to ~12–13% on T1.4/T1.6 (subject scope reduction), indicating they cannot invert their learned judgment logic. D-LADAN (91.57% Ori.1) maintains the highest pass rate among traditional models on explicit scope extensions (93.87% on T1.1) thanks to its graph-based article-discrimination mechanism, yet still fails on implicit rephrasing (11.11% on T1.6). TopJudge (90.80% Ori.1) performs worst overall because it ignores law article content entirely, scoring only 9.20% on T1.6. Qwen2.5-7B, despite a lower original-case rate (59.00%), achieves the best performance among all models on implicit action expansion (93.14% on T2.2) and shows the most consistent adaptability across sentence-term revisions, highlighting LLMs' advantage in context-dependent reasoning over static pattern matching.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LJP evaluation paradigm by introducing statutory revision as a first-class testing dimension, building on the metamorphic testing framework (Chen et al., 2020) to define expected model behavior changes without requiring ground-truth labels under new statutes. It departs from all prior LJP work (TopJudge, D-LADAN, NeurJudge, Lawformer, and LLM-based approaches) that treats law articles as static and conflates different statutory versions under identical labels during training. The knowledge-editing experiments draw on ROME (Meng et al., 2022), while the benchmark design is distinguished from Lekube (Wang et al., 2024) by covering 31 fine-grained revision types with full case narratives rather than 3 simplified change types.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic quantification of how badly SOTA LJP models fail when statutes change, revealing that even law-aware architectures default to outdated reasoning and that localized model editing (ROME-style) yields only marginal gains. The metamorphic testing protocol is broadly transferable to other legal tasks where post-revision ground-truth labels are unavailable, and the 31-type taxonomy provides a reusable diagnostic framework. Key open questions remain: how to achieve temporal legal learning without full retraining, how to handle implicit rephrasings and action-scope changes that resist surface-level matching, and whether the findings generalize beyond the Chinese Criminal Code to common-law systems with precedent-based shifts.
