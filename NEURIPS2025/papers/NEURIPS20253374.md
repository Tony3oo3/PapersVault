## Problem

Existing model merging evaluations rely on small models (≤2.85B parameters) and narrow NLP tasks like sentiment classification, leaving it unclear whether merging methods generalize to modern, large-scale LLMs with diverse domain-specialized capabilities. This gap makes it difficult to quantify which merging algorithms are practical for real-world deployment of multi-task LLMs.

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes finetuned checkpoints from eight base models (Llama-3.2-3B, Llama-3.1-8B, Gemma-2-2B, Gemma-2-9B, each in pretrained and instruction-tuned variants) that were SFT-trained on five domain-specific datasets, plus the corresponding pretrained base model for task vector computation. Gradient-based methods additionally require 1,000 auxiliary training samples, while coefficient-tuning and sparsification methods require surrogate validation sets for hyperparameter search. The output is a single merged model scored on normalized multi-task performance across five domains, forgetting metrics on seven unrelated generalization benchmarks (MMLU, TriviaQA, SQuADv2, CoQA, PubMedQA, WMT 2014), and wall-clock runtime decomposed into algorithm execution and validation tuning time.

## Method

MergeBench is a standardized evaluation framework rather than a new merging algorithm. It constructs a controlled suite of specialized models by finetuning each base model on one of five task categories (instruction following, mathematics, multilingual understanding, coding, safety) using SFT, with GRPO applied on top for math at 8B/9B scales. Eight merging methods spanning two families—coefficient tuning (Model Soup, Task Arithmetic, Fisher Merging, RegMean) and sparsification (TIES, DARE, Consensus TA, Localize-and-Stitch)—are evaluated under unified data and hyperparameter protocols. The evaluation measures three dimensions: normalized multi-task performance (merged vs. specialized finetuned scores), retention of pretrained knowledge via forgetting analysis, and runtime efficiency. Key empirical findings include that merging recovers >90% of specialized performance on 8B/9B models versus ~80% on 2B/3B, that sparsification and small scaling coefficients mitigate catastrophic forgetting more effectively than random dropout, and that Localize-and-Stitch variants achieve the best performance-efficiency tradeoff.

## Results

Across eight base models (Llama-3.2-3B, Llama-3.1-8B, Gemma-2-2B, Gemma-2-9B and their instruction-tuned variants), Localize-and-Stitch achieves the highest overall normalized multi-task performance at 89.7%, followed by Dataless Localize-and-Stitch (89.1%) and Consensus TA (87.6%). On 2B/3B pretrained models, the best methods recover approximately 80% of fully finetuned performance, while on 8B/9B pretrained models, all methods consistently recover over 90%. On instruction-tuned models, most methods exceed 90% normalized performance, with TIES reaching 98.6% on Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct and Consensus TA reaching 106.5% on Gemma-2-9B-IT.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Localize-and-Stitch (both data-driven and dataless variants) consistently achieves the top or near-top normalized performance across all eight base models, with an overall average of 89.7% and 89.1% respectively, outperforming all other methods. Consensus TA (87.6%) and Task Arithmetic (87.2%) form a strong middle tier, while RegMean (86.9%) is competitive on smaller models but loses its advantage at 8B/9B scale. TIES Merging (84.8%) and DARE (83.1%) trail the leaders, with DARE particularly weak on larger models due to its random dropout mechanism. Fisher Merging (83.0%) is consistently the weakest method, suggesting its diagonal Fisher approximation fails to capture parameter importance in LLMs. Notably, on instruction-tuned models the ranking shifts: TIES reaches 98.6% on Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct (beating L&S at 96.1%), and on Gemma-2-9B-IT, Consensus TA (106.5%) and Task Arithmetic (104.6%) exceed 100% normalized performance, indicating that merging can sometimes surpass individual finetuned models when the base is already well-aligned.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by constructing the first comprehensive, open-source benchmark for evaluating model merging on modern LLMs (Llama-3 and Gemma-2 families, 2B–9B), departing from prior evaluations that relied on small models (GPT-2 124M, RoBERTa-base 125M, mT5 2.85B) and narrow NLP tasks like sentiment classification. It standardizes the training and evaluation protocols for eight representative merging algorithms spanning two families—coefficient tuning (Model Soup, Task Arithmetic, Fisher Merging, RegMean) and sparsification (TIES, DARE, Consensus TA, Localize-and-Stitch)—and adds two evaluation dimensions (forgetting and runtime) that prior benchmarks largely ignored. The paper positions itself against FusionBench, Compositional eval, Merging at Scale, and Model-GLUE, addressing their gaps in model diversity, task complexity, and support for gradient-based methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MergeBench provides the field with a reproducible, open-source foundation for comparing merging algorithms at a scale and task diversity that was previously unattainable, and its practical decision guideline (start with Model Soup, escalate to Dataless L&S or Task Arithmetic with validation data) is immediately actionable for practitioners. Key open questions remain: the computational cost of merging on large models is non-trivial, merged models still trail multi-task models on in-domain performance when tasks are balanced and non-conflicting, and the role of merging in standard LLM training pipelines (e.g., combining reasoning and general-purpose models) is largely unexplored. Limitations include restricting to same-initialization merging and dense models only, leaving MoE merging and cross-family merging as important future directions.
