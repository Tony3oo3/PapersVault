## Problem

Creativity assessment in LLM-generated reasoning solutions has been largely overlooked compared to writing tasks, primarily because defining the boundary of creativity in mathematical problem-solving is difficult and traditionally requires costly human expert evaluation. This paper tackles both challenges by proposing a framework that classifies generated math solutions into Hallucinated, Creative, and Typical categories using only the model's internal attention signals, eliminating the need for human evaluators in the detection pipeline.

## Input / Output

The method takes a structured prompt composed of four concatenated sections—Guideline (evaluation criteria), Problem (the math question), Reference Solutions (1–n typical solutions), and Instruction (directive to produce a novel solution)—and feeds it to a 7–8B reasoning LLM with input capped at 2,048 tokens and output at 1,024 tokens. During autoregressive generation, CLAWS extracts decoder attention weights from the last layer only, computing a 5-dimensional feature vector of normalized attention ratios (one per section: G, P, S, I, R). These ratios serve as the sole input to a classifier (prototype matcher, MLP, XGBoost, or TabM) that outputs a three-way label: Hallucinated, Creative, or Typical.

## Method

CLAWS segments all tokens in the prompt-plus-response sequence into five semantic windows and computes the mean attention weight for each window by averaging over all attention heads H, all decoding time steps T, and all token positions within that section. These per-section averages are then normalized to sum to one, yielding section-wise attention ratios that capture how much the model 'looks at' each part of the prompt while generating. The core hypothesis is that solution type is reflected in attention distribution: Hallucinated solutions over-attend to the Guideline and Problem sections, Typical solutions distribute attention across Reference Solutions, Instruction, and Response, and Creative solutions fall in between. Because CLAWS requires only sum-and-average operations on already-computed attention matrices from a single forward pass, it incurs negligible overhead compared to entropy-based or eigenvalue-based baselines. Classification is performed via prototype matching (Euclidean distance to per-class centroid embeddings learned from a small reference set of 29 problems × 20 generations) or via lightweight trainable classifiers, all without any additional model calls or external evaluators.

## Results

On the TEST set (3-class creativity detection), CLAWS achieves macro F1 scores of 46.01 (DeepSeek), 46.05 (Mathstral), 44.27 (OpenMath2), 40.18 (OREAL), and 43.37 (Qwen-2.5), with AUROC values ranging from 59.32 to 63.70. For the 2-class hallucination detection task, CLAWS shows a much larger margin, reaching macro F1 of 67.24 (DeepSeek), 69.59 (Mathstral), 64.70 (OpenMath2), 56.36 (OREAL), and 53.30 (Qwen-2.5) on TEST, compared to at most 43.32 for the best baseline. CLAWS also demonstrates the lowest feature-extraction runtime among all six methods, requiring only simple sum-and-average operations over attention weights during generation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CLAWS is compared against five white-box baselines consolidated from LLM-Check [31]: Perplexity (PPL), Window Logit Entropy (WE), Logit Entropy (LE), Hidden Score (HS), and Attention Score (AS). On the 3-class creativity detection task, CLAWS achieves the best macro F1 on four of five models (DeepSeek 46.01 vs. best baseline 35.77; Mathstral 46.05 vs. 28.89; OpenMath2 44.27 vs. 35.96; OREAL 40.18 vs. 36.15), with the sole exception being Qwen-2.5 where LE reaches 45.29 F1m versus CLAWS's 43.37. The gap is most pronounced on Mathstral and OREAL, where baselines struggle due to high hallucination rates (~70%), while CLAWS maintains consistent separation across all three classes. On the 2-class hallucination detection task, CLAWS dominates all baselines by a wide margin on every model and dataset — e.g., on Mathstral TEST, CLAWS achieves 69.59 F1m versus 25.47 for the best baseline (LE), a gap of over 44 points. Among the baselines themselves, LE is consistently the strongest, followed by PPL; WE, HS, and AS cluster near chance-level performance on most models. CLAWS also has the lowest feature-extraction runtime, performing only sum-and-average operations over last-layer attention weights without any additional forward passes or matrix decompositions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the creativity assessment framework of Ye et al. [11], which established the G|P|S|I prompt structure and evaluation criteria for measuring creative problem-solving in LLMs, by shifting from measuring aggregate creative ability to per-solution three-way classification (Hallucinated/Creative/Typical). It also builds on the white-box hallucination detection literature consolidated in LLM-Check [31], repurposing its five baseline methods (Perplexity, Logit Entropy, Window Entropy, Hidden Score, Attention Score) as comparison targets. The key conceptual departure is the hypothesis that *which prompt section* a model attends to during generation encodes the solution's class, leading to CLAWS—a section-level attention ratio feature that is simpler than token-level uncertainty or hidden-state eigenvalue analysis yet more discriminative for this task.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic framework for detecting creativity in LLM reasoning outputs without human evaluation, addressing a gap the authors identify between well-studied writing-task creativity and the largely overlooked reasoning-task setting. The practical value is in enabling automated quality/diversity monitoring of RL-trained math models at scale. However, limitations are significant: creativity is defined narrowly for math, evaluation relies on LLM judges (Cohen's κ = 0.741) rather than human experts, only 7–8B models were tested due to white-box resource constraints, and absolute three-way F1m scores remain modest (~40–46%). Open questions include whether the attention-section signal generalizes to non-math domains, larger models, or settings where the prompt structure differs.
