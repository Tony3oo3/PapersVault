## Problem

Deploying visual RL policies trained in clean simulation to real-world environments with distracting backgrounds causes severe performance degradation, and existing adaptation methods fail to fully preserve the source policy's performance on complex perturbations like video backgrounds. The paper identifies that humans handle this by filtering task-relevant objects before decision-making, and asks whether a learned object-selection mechanism can bridge the sim-to-real visual gap while keeping the original policy frozen.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250940/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a visually perturbed RGB frame (84×84 for DMC tasks, 168×168 for Franka tasks) and a frozen DrQ-v2 policy pre-trained in a clean environment. The segmentation model (SAM 2) produces k=9 binary masks per frame, which the segment selector scores via an attention mechanism to yield selection probabilities in (0,1)^k. The final output is a continuous control action from the frozen low-level policy, which receives a 3-frame stack of the filtered task-relevant object image as input. During the initial training phase, Qwen-VL-Max is queried (250 times total, ~7 s latency each) to provide binary supervision labels for which segments are task-relevant.

## Method

FTR is a hierarchical framework with a trainable high-level segment selector π^h and a frozen low-level policy π^l. The focus stage alternates between a selection pathway (every T_sel=20 steps, using SAM 2 segmentation + π^h to pick task-relevant segments) and a tracking pathway (using SAM 2 video tracking to propagate the last selection), ensuring short-term consistency in object identity. The segment selector uses an attention-like architecture where the full observation generates a query vector and each segment generates a key vector; scaled dot-product scores passed through sigmoid produce selection probabilities, from which a diagonal Gaussian (σ_h=0.1) is sampled for exploration. Training combines a margin-regularized supervised loss (penalizing predictions that fall within a δ=0.1 margin of the 0.5 threshold, rather than hard BCE) with a PPO-based RL objective whose reward is the cumulative low-level reward between consecutive selection calls. A dynamic weight schedule shifts from pure SL (t<T₁=5000) through a linear transition (T₁≤t<T₂=10000) to pure RL (t>T₂), letting VLM prior knowledge provide fast initialization before environmental feedback refines the policy.

## Results

FTR achieves the best performance on 10 out of 11 tasks across DeepMind Control Suite and Franka Emika Robotics, retaining over 85% of the DrQ-v2 (clean) upper bound on average. On the most challenging robotic manipulation tasks (franka-push, franka-door), FTR scores 96.8±33.8 and 123.0±34.9 respectively, substantially outperforming all baselines. FTR converges within 50k adaptation steps in most cases, compared to 200k steps for other adaptation methods. The only task where FTR does not achieve the best score is cartpole-swingup (646.0±168.5), where Q²-learning reaches 807.3±84.1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FTR outperforms all four baselines on 10 of 11 tasks. Against SimGRL (the SOTA visual generalization method), FTR wins on all tasks except cartpole-swingup, where SimGRL scores 578.6±306.1 vs. FTR's 646.0±168.5 — though Q²-learning actually leads that task at 807.3±84.1. Against Q²-learning (trained directly in the perturbed domain for 500k steps), FTR wins on 10 tasks with far fewer adaptation steps (200k vs. 500k), but loses on cartpole-swingup. PAD, the closest prior adaptation method, performs near-randomly on most tasks (e.g., 0.5±0.4 on pendulum-swingup, 0.8±0.7 on finger-spin), confirming that existing adaptation methods fail on complex video-background perturbations. The ablation variants show that removing supervised learning (FTR w/o SL) slows convergence and hurts cartpole-swingup, while removing RL (FTR w/o RL) consistently degrades final performance, validating the complementary roles of VLM prior knowledge and environmental feedback.

## Relation to Prior Work

FTR extends the segmentation-assisted RL paradigm introduced by FTD (Chen et al., AAAI 2024) by adding a hierarchical focus-then-reuse structure: instead of jointly training a policy on segmented inputs, it freezes a DrQ-v2 policy trained in a clean environment and only learns a lightweight segment selector. The selector is trained by synthesizing VLM prior knowledge (Qwen-VL-Max) with PPO-based environmental feedback, a combination not previously explored in visual domain adaptation RL. This departs from generalization methods (SimGRL, data augmentation) that modify the policy during training, and from prior adaptation methods (PAD) that rely on self-supervised objectives, by keeping the policy entirely unchanged and addressing the distribution shift purely through input filtering.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The decoupled design—where the focus stage and reuse stage are independent—makes FTR compatible with any underlying RL algorithm and requires no policy modification, which is practically attractive for robotics deployment where retraining is costly. However, the method currently handles only background perturbations and struggles with camera pose variations or illumination changes, limiting its real-world applicability. Open questions include whether the fixed selection interval can be made adaptive, whether the approach generalizes to multi-modal perturbations simultaneously, and whether foundation models specifically tailored for RL (rather than repurposed SAM 2 and VLMs) could further improve performance. The finding that existing adaptation methods like PAD collapse to near-zero performance on video backgrounds underscores how poorly the current adaptation literature handles realistic visual disturbances.
