## Problem

Existing 3D scene inpainting methods rely on per-scene optimization requiring hours of computation, making them impractical for real-time interactive applications. While Large Reconstruction Models (LRMs) enable feed-forward 3D reconstruction in under a second, they require consistent multi-view inputs and fail when views contain missing or inconsistent content. The paper addresses how to adapt an LRM to perform 3D inpainting in a single feed-forward pass without multi-view 2D inpainting or per-scene optimization.

## Input / Output

The method takes 4 posed input views at 512×512 resolution: one reference view containing the 2D-diffusion-inpainted content (unmasked, with Plücker ray coordinates) and three inpaint views where the editing regions are replaced with gray pixels and accompanied by binary masks. The output is a complete 3D scene represented as N×H×W per-pixel 3D Gaussians (one Gaussian per pixel per view), produced in 0.4 seconds. The reference view is deliberately not masked, so the model must infer geometry for the inpainting region from its surrounding context and the reference appearance rather than from explicit multi-view correspondence.

## Method

InstaInpaint is a ViT-based transformer (following GS-LRM architecture) that patchifies channel-concatenated images and Plücker coordinates into tokens, processes them through self-attention blocks, and decodes per-pixel Gaussian parameters via a linear layer. The key contribution is a self-supervised masked fine-tuning strategy: after pre-training a naive LRM on DL3DV-10k, the model is fine-tuned on posed video clips where three of four input views have regions grayed out with binary masks while the reference view remains intact, supervised by photometric losses on 8 novel views. Three complementary mask types are used at random during training—object masks from SAM2 video segmentation, geometric masks warped from the reference view using LRM-predicted metric depth, and cross-view-inconsistent random rectangular masks—to prevent object bias and improve generalization. A critical design choice is withholding the mask from the reference view, maintaining a uniform token distribution between reconstruction and inpainting regions so the model learns to faithfully reconstruct both from context rather than relying on explicit mask signals.

## Results

On SPIn-NeRF, InstaInpaint achieves the best LPIPS (0.4147), M-LPIPS (0.3130), FID (84.54), and KID (0.0135) among all compared methods, while completing reconstruction and inpainting in 0.4 seconds — a ~6000× speedup over the fastest prior method (InFusion, 40 min) and ~45000× over SPIn-NeRF (5 h). On LLFF, it attains the best C-FID (198.54) and near-best C-KID (0.0613 vs. InFusion's 0.0601). The method also outperforms LRM-based baselines (SD+LRM, MVInpainter+LRM) on FID, KID, C-FID, and C-KID, confirming that end-to-end masked training yields superior geometric consistency over post-hoc 2D inpainting pipelines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

InstaInpaint outperforms all six optimization-based baselines (SPIn-NeRF, SPIn-NeRF+SD, InpaintNeRF360, MALD-NeRF, GScream, InFusion) on every reported metric for SPIn-NeRF (LPIPS, M-LPIPS, FID, KID) and on C-FID for LLFF, while being orders of magnitude faster (0.4 s vs. 40 min–15 h). Against the closest reference-based competitor InFusion, InstaInpaint improves LPIPS by 0.0796 (0.4147 vs. 0.4943), FID by 5.08 (84.54 vs. 89.62), and C-FID by 4.77 (198.54 vs. 203.31), though InFusion retains a marginal C-KID edge (0.0601 vs. 0.0613). Compared to the LRM-based baseline MVInpainter+LRM, InstaInpaint achieves lower FID (84.54 vs. 90.25), KID (0.0135 vs. 0.0163), C-FID (198.54 vs. 217.90), and C-KID (0.0613 vs. 0.0758), but is slightly behind on LPIPS (0.4147 vs. 0.4122), indicating that the end-to-end masked training captures distributional quality better while MVInpainter's multi-view diffusion gives a small perceptual edge on pixel-level similarity. Against SD+LRM, InstaInpaint dominates across all metrics (e.g., FID 84.54 vs. 108.43, KID 0.0135 vs. 0.0189), confirming that naively inpainting each view before LRM reconstruction is substantially inferior to learning inpainting within the LRM itself.

## Relation to Prior Work

InstaInpaint extends the Large Reconstruction Model (LRM) paradigm—exemplified by GS-LRM's feed-forward per-pixel Gaussian prediction from multi-view images—into the 3D inpainting setting, where prior methods either rely on lengthy per-scene optimization (SPIn-NeRF, MALD-NeRF, GScream, InFusion) or pipeline a 2D inpainting model before LRM reconstruction (SD+LRM, MVInpainter+LRM). The key conceptual departure is a self-supervised masked-finetuning strategy that trains the LRM to infer missing geometry from a single reference view without requiring paired before/after scene data, circumventing the data bottleneck that has limited prior 3D inpainting research. This positions the work between the LRM reconstruction literature and the reference-based 3DGS inpainting line, while distinguishing itself from the concurrent MaskedLRM by targeting real-world scenes rather than object-centric synthetic data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 1000× speedup (0.4 s vs. hours) makes interactive 3D scene editing feasible for VR/AR applications, and the self-supervised masked-finetuning recipe is a reusable technique that could transfer to other LRM-based editing tasks (e.g., object replacement, style transfer). A practically important finding is that object-only masks induce a depth-protrusion bias, and that geometric and random masks are necessary to close the train–val gap—insight that generalizes to any masked-training setup. The main open question is dynamic/4D inpainting: the model averages conflicting temporal information and fails when occlusion patterns differ between the reference and context views, leaving space-time inpainting as an active research direction.
