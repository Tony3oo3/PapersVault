## Problem

Causal abstraction has become a prominent theoretical framework for mechanistic interpretability, yet most implementations implicitly assume linear alignment maps between neural network activations and algorithm nodes. This paper shows that the linearity constraint is not required by the definition of causal abstraction, and that lifting it renders the framework vacuous: under mild assumptions, any neural network can be perfectly aligned to any algorithm. This exposes the 'non-linear representation dilemma'—without principled constraints on alignment map complexity, there is no way to balance accuracy against the risk of trivial, uninformative abstractions.

## Input / Output

The method takes a DNN (MLP or transformer), a hypothesized causal algorithm (a DAG implementing a task function), and a set of input-restricted interventions constructed from counterfactual inputs. The alignment map φ is trained via gradient descent (DAS) to minimize cross-entropy between the intervened DNN's output and the algorithm's output, with φ parameterized as an identity map, an orthogonal linear map, or a reversible residual network (RevNet) of tunable depth L_rn and hidden size d_rn. The primary output is interchange intervention accuracy (IIA) on a held-out test set, measuring how well the DNN reproduces the algorithm's counterfactual predictions under multi-node interventions.

## Method

The paper's core theoretical result (Theorem 1) proves that under five mild assumptions (countable input space, input-injectivity, output-surjectivity, matchable partial orderings, and task correctness), any algorithm is an input-restricted distributed abstraction of any DNN, via an inductive construction of layer-wise alignment maps that encode prior-node information in non-intervened neurons. Empirically, the authors apply DAS with three alignment map families—identity, orthogonal linear, and non-linear RevNet—on a hierarchical equality task (3-layer MLP) and the indirect object identification task (Pythia 31M–410M). The key finding is that increasing φ's complexity monotonically raises IIA, and that even randomly initialized models achieve >80% IIA with sufficiently complex non-linear maps, while the layer-dependent degradation patterns observed with linear maps vanish entirely. As training progresses, the complexity of φ needed for perfect IIA decreases, suggesting that internal representations become more linearly structured over time.

## Results

On the hierarchical equality task (3-layer MLP, hidden size 16), the non-linear alignment map (φ^nonlin, RevNet with L_rn=10, d_rn=16) achieves near-optimal interchange intervention accuracy (IIA) across all layers, while the linear map (φ^lin) shows substantial IIA degradation in the third layer. Strikingly, even randomly initialised MLPs achieve over 80% IIA with the most complex non-linear alignment map. On the IOI task with Pythia language models (31M–410M parameters), an 8-layer φ^nonlin yields near-perfect (≈100%) IIA for randomly initialised models across all sizes, and this holds throughout training for the 410M model—even before it acquires task proficiency around step 3000.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the original Distributed Alignment Search (DAS) with linear alignment maps from Geiger et al. (2024b). On the hierarchical equality task, the linear DAS baseline reproduces the expected pattern where IIA for the 'both equality relations' and 'left equality relation' algorithms drops substantially in the third layer, suggesting information becomes harder to manipulate at depth. The non-linear alignment map eliminates this layer-dependent degradation entirely, achieving near-optimal IIA at every layer—meaning the 'first layer is easier to map' insight from the linear baseline does not generalise. On the IOI task, the linear baseline's IIA closely tracks the DNN's actual task accuracy (poor for 31M/70M models that fail the task, high for 160M/410M models that succeed), whereas the non-linear map achieves ≈100% IIA even for randomly initialised models and for models that never learn the task. This demonstrates that the linear DAS baseline's apparent 'findings' (e.g., identifying where algorithmic variables are encoded) are artefacts of the linearity constraint rather than genuine structural properties of the network. The identity map (φ^id, corresponding to the privileged bases hypothesis) consistently performs worst, with the 'identity of first argument' algorithm hovering around 50% IIA across all map types, likely due to insufficient RevNet capacity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the causal abstraction framework of Beckers and Halpern (2019) and Geiger et al. (2024a) by removing the implicit linearity constraint on alignment maps that underpins the Distributed Alignment Search (DAS) method of Geiger et al. (2024b). While prior DAS work restricted alignment maps to orthogonal (linear) transformations—implicitly assuming the linear representation hypothesis—this paper considers arbitrarily complex bijective maps (including RevNets) and proves that under mild assumptions, any algorithm can be perfectly aligned to any DNN. The work also connects to the broader probing literature's unresolved accuracy-vs-complexity trade-off (Hewitt and Liang, 2019; Pimentel et al., 2020a), showing an exact analogue in the causal abstraction setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 'non-linear representation dilemma' identified here poses a fundamental challenge to mechanistic interpretability: without a priori constraints on how features are encoded, causal abstraction becomes vacuous, as demonstrated by near-perfect IIA on randomly initialised models that cannot solve the task. This forces the field to confront the fact that current DAS-based analyses implicitly rely on the linear representation hypothesis, and that lifting this constraint without a principled complexity penalty renders the method uninformative. Key open questions include: how to balance alignment map complexity against accuracy in a principled way, the role of generalisation (the paper shows alignment maps fail to generalise when training/test names are disjoint), and whether the linear representation hypothesis can be empirically validated rather than assumed. Limitations include reliance on input-injectivity and output-surjectivity assumptions that may fail in practice (e.g., due to the softmax bottleneck or neural collapse).
