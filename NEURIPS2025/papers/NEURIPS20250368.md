## Problem

Existing clinical AI benchmarks evaluate models on static, unimodal, single-turn question-answering, which diverges sharply from how oncologists actually reason in Molecular Tumor Boards (MTBs)—where heterogeneous data (pathology, genomics, hematology) accumulates over time and must be integrated across modalities and sequential decision points. No prior benchmark jointly captures multimodality, longitudinal temporal structure, and agentic multi-turn information retrieval in a clinically validated oncology setting.

## Input / Output

At each turn, the agent receives a clinical query and a list of available modality-specific files (H&E slides, IHC tissue microarray images, hematology reports, genomic mutation/CNA tables, and clinical timelines) but must explicitly request files by name to access them; files are non-persistent and must be re-requested in subsequent turns, simulating realistic clinical file management. The multimodal track (26 HANCOCK patients, ~40 files each) progresses through pathology → pre-surgical hematology → post-surgical prognosis stages, while the longitudinal track (40 MSK-CHORD patients) segments patient history into decision-relevant timepoints with evolving timelines. Outputs are multiple-choice (6 options) or true/false answers parsed via regex, with up to three re-prompting attempts for format compliance.

## Method

MTBBench introduces an agentic framework in which LLM/VLM agents engage in multi-turn dialogues, selectively requesting patient files and optionally invoking domain-specific foundation models as callable tools: CONCH for zero-shot H&E region-of-interest classification via image-text embedding similarity, and a custom ABMIL tool that aggregates UNI2 embeddings over 256×256 IHC patches (trained via QuPath-annotated weak supervision) to regress the percentage of positively stained cells. For longitudinal reasoning, agents can query PubMed (top-30 retrieval reranked by BAAI-bge-reranker-v2-m3, returning top-3 abstracts) and DrugBank (string-matched drug metadata) to ground treatment and progression predictions. The benchmark is validated by 10 external clinicians via a custom web application, achieving high inter-rater agreement (Cohen's κ = 0.81, Gwet's AC1 = 0.91). A key empirical finding is that the number of files accessed per question correlates more strongly with accuracy than model parameter count, and tool augmentation yields up to 9.0% (multimodal) and 11.2% (longitudinal) task-level gains, with smaller models benefiting most from tool access.

## Results

On MTBBench-Multimodal, internvl3-78b achieves the highest overall accuracy at 69.1% (±8.4), outperforming gpt4o (66.7% ±8.1) by 2.5 percentage points. On MTBBench-Longitudinal, qwen3-32b leads with 67.0% (±13.5) overall, reaching 83.0% (±9.2) on outcome prediction, while recurrence and progression tasks remain near chance (45–57%) for most models. Tool augmentation yields task-level gains of up to +9.0% (gemma-3-27b on digital pathology) and +11.2% (gemma-3-27b on longitudinal recurrence), with smaller models benefiting most from foundation model access.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among open-source models, internvl3-78b (69.1% multimodal overall) surpasses the closed-source gpt4o (66.7%) by 2.5 points, while qwen3-32b (67.0% longitudinal overall) edges out gpt4o (64.2%) by 2.8 points on the longitudinal track. On longitudinal outcome prediction specifically, qwen3-32b reaches 83.0%, substantially outperforming gpt4o (72.9%) and llama33-70b (73.2%). Smaller models (qwen25-7b at 52.4%, llama31-8b at 51.6%) perform near chance, highlighting that raw scale alone is insufficient—file-access behavior correlates more strongly with accuracy than parameter count. With tool augmentation, gemma-3-27b gains +9.0% on digital pathology and +11.2% on longitudinal recurrence, while qwen25-7b improves by +8.9% on digital pathology; however, qwen25-32b is a notable outlier, losing 17.2% on outcome/recurrence with tools. Overall, no single model dominates both tracks, and outcome/recurrence prediction remains the hardest task across all baselines, with most models clustered near 50%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by combining three previously separate evaluation axes—multimodality, longitudinal structure, and agentic file access—into a single oncology benchmark, whereas prior clinical benchmarks (MedAgentBench, MediQ, MedJourney, EHRNoteQA) address at most one or two of these in isolation. The agentic framework repurposes the Toolformer/ReAct paradigm of calling external tools, but applies it to domain-specific pathology foundation models (CONCH, UNI2) and biomedical knowledge bases (PubMed, DrugBank) rather than generic web APIs. The ABMIL-based IHC quantification tool builds on standard multiple-instance learning for weakly supervised pathology, but its integration as a callable agent tool rather than a standalone classifier is the novel contribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MTBBench provides the first standardized testbed for evaluating whether AI agents can simulate the sequential, multi-expert deliberation of real molecular tumor boards, a setting where current models demonstrably fail (near-chance recurrence prediction, large inter-model gaps up to 36.7%). The finding that information-gathering behavior (number of files accessed) correlates more strongly with accuracy than model scale offers a concrete design principle for clinical agents. Key limitations include the controlled offline setting (no interactive clarification, no ambiguous inputs), restriction to head-and-neck and pancreatic cancers, and the absence of a dedicated longitudinal foundation model tool—leaving open whether the observed tool gains generalize to broader clinical domains or truly interactive workflows.
