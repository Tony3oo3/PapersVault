## Problem

Prior theoretical work on transformer expressivity for graph tasks focused on the depth dimension (showing logarithmic depth suffices with sub-linear width), but the complementary regime of fixed depth with growing width remained unexplored. This paper asks: what is the minimal embedding dimension (width) required for a constant-depth transformer to solve various graph algorithmic tasks as a function of graph size n, and how does this compare to the depth required at smaller widths?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250775/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Graphs are encoded via node-adjacency tokenization, where each of the n nodes becomes one token whose embedding is the corresponding row of the adjacency matrix (dimension n), yielding O(n) tokens rather than O(n²) for edge-list encodings. For molecular tasks, atom-level and bond-level features are concatenated to each adjacency row. Outputs range from binary labels (connectivity, cycle detection, molecular property classification) to regression targets (triangle and 4-cycle counts), with the theoretical constructions producing exact combinatorial answers while empirical models are trained via supervised learning.

## Method

The paper establishes a width-complexity hierarchy for constant-depth transformers on graph tasks. For 2-cycle detection, a communication-complexity reduction to set disjointness proves that mpHL = Ω(n) is necessary (unconditionally), while an upper bound is achieved by constructing attention layers whose softmax approximates hardmax to iteratively compute powers of the adjacency matrix A^L in O(L) layers with O(n) width. For subgraph counting of k-node patterns, the tri-tri-again distributed algorithm is implemented in O(1) layers with O(n^(2−1/k)) width by partitioning nodes into n^(1/k) sets and using attention to aggregate edges within each k-set combination. For Eulerian cycle verification, a reduction to the 1-vs-2-cycle problem (via random projection of an N-node cycle to an n-node multigraph) shows near-quadratic width is conditionally necessary. Empirically, shallow-wide transformers (e.g., 1 layer × 125 width) match the accuracy of deep-narrow ones (10 layers × 40 width) at ~100k parameters while training and inferring significantly faster due to GPU parallelism across width.

## Results

On the OGB molecular property prediction benchmarks, the proposed Adjacency Rows (AdjRows) tokenization achieves ROC-AUC of 61.87 on MOLHIV, 67.63 on MOLBBBP, and 68.64 on MOLBACE, outperforming the EdgeList baseline on all three tasks. In the depth-width trade-off experiments (100k parameters, 100-node graphs), shallow-wide configurations (e.g., 1 layer × 125 width) match the accuracy of deep-narrow ones (e.g., 10 layers × 40 width) while reducing total training and inference time substantially. For the 4-Cycle Count task, the critical width at which a single-layer transformer fails to fit the data increases roughly linearly with graph size (from ~100 at 50 nodes to ~800 at 400 nodes), consistent with the theoretical O(n^{2-1/k}) bound.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the proposed AdjRows tokenization against two established baselines: the EdgeList tokenization from Sanford et al. [2024a] and the Laplacian Eigenvector (LE) encoding from Kreuzer et al. [2021]. AdjRows outperforms EdgeList on all three OGB molecular datasets, with gains of +7.86 ROC-AUC on MOLHIV (61.87 vs. 54.01), +2.90 on MOLBBBP (67.63 vs. 64.73), and +2.58 on MOLBACE (68.64 vs. 66.06). Against the LE baseline, AdjRows wins on MOLBBBP (67.63 vs. 55.31, a +12.32 margin) and MOLBACE (68.64 vs. 63.61, +5.03), but loses on MOLHIV (61.87 vs. 68.11, a −6.24 gap), suggesting that spectral encodings retain an advantage for certain global structural properties. In the depth-width experiments, all width-depth configurations with ~100k parameters achieve comparable test accuracy, but the shallowest/widest model (1 layer, 125 width) is significantly faster in both training and inference than the deepest/narrowest (10 layers, 40 width), confirming the practical benefit of the linear-width regime.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the transformer-MPC complexity hierarchy established by Sanford et al. (2024a, 2024b, 2024c), which primarily characterized the powers of depth under an edge-list tokenization. The key departure is a shift to node-adjacency tokenization and a focus on the fixed-depth, variable-width regime, which the authors explicitly note 'has yet to be explored' in prior work. The constructions draw on linear sketching (Ahn et al., 2012) for connectivity and the tri-tri-again distributed algorithm (Dolev et al., 2012) for subgraph counting, adapting these into transformer attention/MLP layers. The lower bounds leverage communication complexity (set disjointness) and the 1-vs-2-cycle conjecture from the MPC literature, providing unconditional results for 2-cycle detection that are stronger than the conditional bounds in prior work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes a clean width complexity hierarchy (constant → linear → sub-quadratic → quadratic) for graph tasks under constant-depth transformers, giving practitioners a principled guide for architecture design. The practical implication is significant: since GPUs parallelize across width but not depth, the finding that shallow-wide models match deep-narrow models in accuracy while being substantially faster in training and inference directly informs efficient model deployment for graph tasks. Open questions include whether these expressiveness results translate to optimization and generalization guarantees, how the bounds change under permutation-invariant or spectral encodings, and whether the conditional lower bounds (1-vs-2-cycle conjecture) can be made unconditional. A limitation is that the adjacency encoding is not permutation invariant, and the universal-MLP assumption, while standard in theory, may overstate practical capacity.
