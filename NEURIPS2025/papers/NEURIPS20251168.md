## Problem

3D molecular generative models require 90–1000+ sampling steps to produce valid molecules, far exceeding the 1–2 steps achievable in image generation. The paper identifies the root cause: unlike images with fixed spatial ordering, holistic 3D molecule generation must first resolve the correct permutation of atoms before refining their features, a phase that existing acceleration techniques (DDIM, consistency models, optimal transport) were never designed to address.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251168/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For de novo generation, the method takes a random seed and a target molecule size N, and outputs a 3D molecular structure represented as a tuple g = ⟨x, h⟩ in zero-center-of-mass space, where x ∈ ℝ^{N×3} are atomic coordinates and h ∈ ℝ^{N×d} encodes atom types and charges. For structure-based drug design, the input is a target protein binding site, and the output is a drug molecule with a 3D conformation scored by Vina. The geometric-informed prior is precomputed from the dataset (as few as 10 molecules per size class) via EOT alignment, and the entire sampling operates in the BFN parameter space rather than the raw sample space.

## Method

MOLTD decomposes the generative trajectory into two phases separated by a theoretically grounded 'stable point' (where structural permutation difference drops below ε). For the permutation-reordering phase, it constructs a geometric-informed prior by stratifying molecules by atom count, aligning them via Equivariant Optimal Transport, averaging to obtain a representative structure, and mapping it into the BFN parameter space—giving sampling an informative, rotationally-equivariant starting point that skips most of the reordering. For the adjustment phase, it introduces a consistency parameter objective that enforces consistency in the BFN's continuous parameter space (rather than the multimodal sample space), computed only after the stable point, which stabilizes few-step generation of atom types and coordinates. Built on GeoBFN as the primary backbone, the approach achieves 12 NFEs (≈7.5× faster than flow matching, ≈100× faster than diffusion) while reaching state-of-the-art stability on QM9 and GEOM-DRUG, and generalizes to diffusion backbones and structure-based drug design.

## Results

On QM9, MOLTD achieves 99.40% atom stability, 92.53% molecule stability, 96.04% validity, and 67.03% V&U&N using only 12 NFEs, outperforming all baselines that require 90–1000 NFEs. On the more challenging GEOM-DRUG dataset (avg. 44 atoms, up to 181), MOLTD reaches 86.88% atom stability and 95.33% validity at 12 NFEs. In structure-based drug design on CrossDocked, MOLTD achieves the best QED (0.54), SA (0.72), and Vina Min (−7.36) at just 25 NFEs, a 40× speedup over diffusion-based baselines. Overall, MOLTD is 7.5× faster than flow-matching models, 8.3× faster than BFN baselines, and ~100× faster than diffusion-based models while maintaining or improving generation quality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MOLTD (12 NFEs) outperforms all diffusion-based baselines (EDM, GDM-AUG, EDM-Bridge, GeoLDM at 1000 NFEs) on molecule stability (92.53% vs. 71.6–89.4%), validity (96.04% vs. 90.4–93.8%), and V&U&N (67.03% vs. 53.7–66.8%) on QM9. Against the flow-matching baseline EquiFM (200 NFEs), MOLTD improves molecule stability by 4.2 points (92.53 vs. 88.3) and V&U&N by 13.3 points (67.03 vs. 53.7) while being 16.7× faster. Versus the BFN baseline GeoBFN (100 NFEs), MOLTD gains 5.3 points in molecule stability and 3.0 points in validity at 8.3× fewer steps. The one notable exception is GOAT (90 NFEs), which achieves a higher V&U&N (72.3 vs. 67.03) on QM9, though MOLTD surpasses it on validity (96.04 vs. 92.9) and molecule stability (92.53 vs. not reported). On GEOM-DRUG, MOLTD leads all methods in atom stability (86.88% vs. 77.1–84.8%) but trails GeoLDM (99.3%) and EquiFM (98.9%) on validity. In structure-based drug design, MOLTD at 25 NFEs beats MolCRAFT at 100 NFEs on QED (0.54 vs. 0.50), SA (0.72 vs. 0.69), and Vina Min (−7.36 vs. −7.27), and substantially outperforms TargetDiff and Decomp-R at 1000 NFEs across all metrics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on GeoBFN (Song et al., ICLR 2024) as its generative backbone, adapting the Bayesian Flow Network framework to incorporate a geometric-informed prior constructed via Equivariant Optimal Transport (from EquiFM, Song et al., NeurIPS 2024). It departs from standard consistency training (Song et al., 2023) by operating in the BFN parameter space rather than the sample space, motivated by the multimodal nature of molecular data. The key conceptual departure from prior acceleration work is the trajectory diagnosis framework: rather than applying a uniform acceleration technique, the paper identifies a fundamental two-phase structure (permutation reordering followed by feature adjustment) unique to 3D geometric generation, and designs phase-specific interventions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ~10× speedup over prior state-of-the-art (and ~100× over diffusion models) makes large-scale molecular generation practically feasible for drug discovery and material design workflows that previously required hundreds of sampling steps. The two-phase decomposition provides a theoretical lens for understanding why geometric generation resists the 1-2 step acceleration achieved in image generation, potentially guiding future work on other geometric domains (proteins, crystals). Limitations include restriction to iterative denoising backbones (no autoregressive or VAE models tested), and the geometric-informed prior requires dataset-level alignment that may not transfer trivially to out-of-distribution molecular classes. Open questions include whether the phase decomposition generalizes to larger biomolecular systems and whether the prior can be made adaptive per-molecule rather than per-size-class.
