## Problem

Existing spike sorting methods rely on heuristic thresholds and manual parameter tuning, making them sensitive to noise and difficult to generalize across diverse recording conditions. A fundamental obstacle to adopting data-driven deep learning approaches is the scarcity of ground-truth labeled spike data from real recordings, which prevents reliable training and validation of learned detectors and classifiers.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253840/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes raw multi-channel extracellular voltage signals (V ∈ R^{T×C}, typically 4-channel tetrode recordings at 10–40 kHz), preprocessed with bandpass filtering and spatial whitening. The detection stage outputs binary spike labels per timestep, while the identification stage takes 60-timestep waveform snippets (L×C) centered on each detected spike and produces latent embeddings that are clustered into putative neuron units. The final output is a set of spike timestamps with assigned neuron identities, i.e., fully sorted spike trains.

## Method

SimSort is a two-stage cascaded pipeline: a 4-layer Transformer encoder performs spike detection (trained with weighted binary cross-entropy on continuous simulated signals), and a single-layer GRU encoder performs spike identification (trained with triplet contrastive loss on waveform snippets, preceded by SVD-based denoising). The key enabling contribution is a large-scale simulated dataset of 8,192 trials (40,000+ neurons) generated from 206 biophysically detailed BBP multi-compartment neuron models via NEURON intracellular simulation and volume-conductor extracellular potential computation, providing precise ground-truth spike labels. Data augmentation (Gaussian noise, random amplitude scaling, temporal jitter) is applied per-channel during training to improve robustness. Learned embeddings are reduced with UMAP and clustered via GMM or Mean Shift. Trained exclusively on simulated data, the framework demonstrates zero-shot transfer to real-world tetrode and Neuropixels recordings without fine-tuning.

## Results

On the simulated BBP L6 test set, SimSort achieves 0.85 accuracy, 0.90 recall, and 0.93 precision, outperforming MountainSort4 (0.84/0.84/0.93) and substantially exceeding KiloSort (0.49/0.51/0.53). On the zero-shot Hybrid dataset (real waveforms + noise), SimSort attains the best accuracy (0.62 static, 0.56 drift) and precision (0.77 static, 0.69 drift) among 9 methods. On the challenging WaveClus 'difficult' subset, SimSort achieves 0.71 accuracy and 0.81 recall, nearly doubling the performance of the next-best method MountainSort4 (0.48/0.49). For spike identification, SimSort's contrastive-learned embeddings yield ARI of 0.91 (static) and 0.89 (drift) on Hybrid, surpassing PCA (0.79/0.74), t-SNE (0.88/0.85), and UMAP (0.88/0.85).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SimSort outperforms KiloSort [17] on accuracy across all three main benchmarks (e.g., 0.62 vs 0.60 on Hybrid-static, 0.75 vs 0.54 on WaveClus-easy, 0.71 vs 0.17 on WaveClus-difficult) and on recall in most settings. Against MountainSort4 [6], SimSort achieves comparable precision on BBP L6 (0.93 vs 0.93) but a clear advantage on the WaveClus difficult subset (0.71 accuracy vs 0.48) and Hybrid-drift (0.56 vs 0.36). HerdingSpikes2 [18] is consistently the weakest baseline, with SimSort exceeding it by 27+ points in accuracy on Hybrid-static. On spike identification, SimSort's learned embeddings (ARI 0.91/0.89) surpass PCA [23] by 12–15 points, and t-SNE [24] and UMAP [40] by 3–5 points on the Hybrid dataset. Against CEED [21] on the IBL Neuropixels benchmark, SimSort achieves a slightly higher ARI (0.49 vs 0.46, p=0.0012) without any training on that dataset, while CEED was trained on closely related IBL recordings. On Hybrid-drift precision, KiloSort (0.72) marginally edges out SimSort (0.69), the only metric where a baseline leads.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the long-standing heuristic pipeline (threshold detection + PCA/t-SNE + clustering) used by KiloSort, MountainSort, and similar tools, and extends the deep-learning line initiated by YASS (CNN-based detection) and CEED (contrastive embeddings) by introducing a large-scale biophysically realistic simulation dataset as the training substrate. Rather than fine-tuning on small real-world datasets, SimSort pretrains a Transformer-based detector and a GRU-based contrastive identifier entirely on 8,192 simulated trials from Blue Brain Project multi-compartment models, then applies the models zero-shot to real recordings. The conceptual move is to treat spike sorting as a simulation-to-reality transfer problem, repurposing volume-conductor-based extracellular simulation as a scalable source of ground-truth labels that real experiments cannot provide.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a fundamental bottleneck in spike sorting: the absence of ground-truth labels in real recordings, which has kept the field reliant on hand-tuned heuristics. By demonstrating that a model trained purely on biophysical simulation can generalize zero-shot to real tetrode and Neuropixels data, it opens a path toward fully automated, parameter-free sorting that neuroscientists can deploy without expertise in algorithm tuning. Key limitations include training exclusively on 4-channel tetrode data (limiting direct applicability to high-density probes), modest absolute gains over strong baselines like MountainSort4 on several benchmarks, and the fact that the simulation, while biophysically detailed, may not capture all sources of variability in vivo (e.g., motion artifacts, electrode drift at scale). Extending the simulation framework to diverse electrode geometries and validating on longer, more heterogeneous recordings remain important open questions.
