## Problem

Existing open-vocabulary segmentation (OVS) models are trained once on a fixed dataset and perform poorly on subsequently collected data. While retraining on all data is impractical (high cost, requires original data access) and fine-tuning causes catastrophic forgetting, standard continual learning methods assume a finite class set and are incompatible with the potentially infinite label space of OVS. The paper addresses how to effectively leverage sequentially arriving datasets to expand OVS recognition capability without degrading prior performance.

## Input / Output

The model takes an RGB image and a free-form text description (which may reference both seen and unseen classes) as input and produces N pairs of predicted segmentation masks and class embeddings, with final labels assigned by nearest-neighbor matching against text embeddings. A key operational detail is that the decoder weights are reconstructed per-sample at inference time via weighted interpolation of task vectors, meaning the effective model changes with each input while the shared image and text encoders remain fixed and can be batched.

## Method

ConOVS is a Mixture-of-Experts continual learning framework that fine-tunes only the decoder on each incremental dataset to produce a distinct expert, while computing multivariate normal (MVN) distributions over image and text embeddings for every dataset. At inference, it computes task vectors (the arithmetic difference between each expert's decoder weights and the pre-trained decoder), estimates per-dataset likelihoods of the input embeddings under the stored MVN distributions, applies softmax to obtain interpolation factors, and linearly combines the task vectors weighted by these factors to construct the final decoder. The element-wise maximum of image and text probability vectors determines the final interpolation weights, and the pre-training distribution is included in the softmax so that in-distribution inputs naturally suppress all incremental experts. This design avoids catastrophic forgetting by never modifying the pre-trained decoder, reduces training cost by using only new data, and generalizes to unseen classes by blending knowledge from multiple experts when the input does not clearly match any single training distribution.

## Results

In Scenario S2 (COCO pre-training, ADE20K incremental, Cityscapes zero-shot), ConOVS with fc-clip achieves +23.8 PQ on ADE20K, +1.7 PQ on COCO, and +0.9 PQ on Cityscapes — the only method that improves on all three datasets simultaneously. In Scenario S3 (two incremental datasets), ConOVS reaches 51.6 / 47.0 / 64.3 PQ on COCO / ADE20K / Cityscapes, outperforming retraining (48.6 / 35.5 / 60.5) and fine-tuning (39.3 / 48.3 / 46.0 for City→ADE). Across eight zero-shot test datasets (LVIS, BDD100K, Mapillary, PC-59, PC-459, PAS-20, PAS-21, A-847), ConOVS achieves the best score on every dataset, e.g., 23.1 mAP on LVIS and 29.1 mIoU on Mapillary.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the fc-clip baseline, ConOVS improves PQ on all three evaluation sets (+1.7 COCO, +23.8 ADE20K, +0.9 Cityscapes), whereas fine-tuning gains +24.1 on ADE20K but sacrifices −7.7 on COCO and −3.0 on Cityscapes. Compared to retraining, ConOVS achieves a substantially larger gain on the incremental dataset (+23.8 vs. +16.5) while matching pre-training performance (+1.7 vs. +1.4) and being the only method to improve zero-shot Cityscapes (+0.9 vs. −1.2). Among the four continual-learning baselines, ConOVS dominates: it outperforms ER by +2.3 PQ on ADE20K and +4.4 on Cityscapes, LwF by +10.1 on ADE20K, EWC by +3.1 on ADE20K, and ECLIPSE by a large margin of +23.6 on ADE20K (ECLIPSE gains only +0.2). With the X-Decoder backbone, ConOVS narrows the catastrophic-forgetting gap from −37.3 (fine-tuning) to −1.5 on COCO while matching or exceeding fine-tuning on ADE20K (+29.2 vs. +28.2) and improving zero-shot Cityscapes (+1.4 vs. −3.7). In Scenario S3 with two incremental datasets, ConOVS (51.6/47.0/64.3) surpasses retraining (48.6/35.5/60.5) on all three datasets and avoids the severe forgetting seen in sequential fine-tuning (e.g., COCO drops to 20.8 for ADE→City fine-tuning).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of open-vocabulary segmentation (OVS) and continual learning (CL), two well-studied but previously disjoint areas. It builds directly on OVS architectures (fc-clip, X-Decoder) and borrows the task-vector arithmetic framework from model editing literature, while adapting the MoE-based continual learning paradigm (as in ECLIPSE and S-Prompts) to the open-vocabulary setting. The key departure is from both standard OVS (which assumes a fixed training set) and standard CL (which assumes a finite class space): ConOVS removes the finite-class assumption by using MVN distributions over encoder embeddings to dynamically interpolate full decoder experts, rather than selecting a single expert or training only partial parameters via visual prompt tuning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical deployment gap: OVS models in robotics or autonomous driving will inevitably encounter new data streams, yet prior work has not studied how to incorporate them without degrading existing capabilities. The finding that adapted CL methods (ER, LwF, EWC, ECLIPSE) perform worse than a closed-set baseline (OneFormer) when applied to OVS is a useful negative result that clarifies why the finite-class assumption is not merely a convenience but a fundamental incompatibility. Open questions include scalability to many sequential datasets (only 2–3 are tested in the main paper), the batch-size limitation of per-sample decoder weights, and whether the MVN-based routing generalizes to other encoder architectures or non-CLIP-based OVS models.
