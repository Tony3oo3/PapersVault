## Problem

RL agents trained to satisfy LTL specifications over infinite horizons face a fundamental exploration bottleneck: the LDBA-based reward signal is sparse, providing feedback only upon visiting accepting states, which becomes increasingly difficult to discover as task horizons extend to infinity. Existing proxy-reward schemes (e.g., eventual discounting, counterfactual replay) do not adequately address this sparsity in high-dimensional continuous control, and the finite-length nature of practical expert demonstrations creates a tension with the infinite-visit requirement of LTL satisfaction.

## Input / Output

The method takes an LTL formula (compiled into an LDBA), a small set of finite-length expert demonstrations consisting solely of MDP state trajectories (no action sequences, and not collected in the product MDP to prevent the discriminator from exploiting LDBA state as a shortcut), and the current environment state. It produces a stochastic policy over the product MDP state space S×B with action space A∪E (including epsilon/jump actions for LDBA transitions), optimized to maximize the probability of infinite-horizon LTL formula satisfaction. Demonstrations typically contain only 1–2 visits to LDBA-accepting states, and the method is designed to generalize from such limited coverage to policies that reach accepting states infinitely often.

## Method

TiLoIL segments agent exploratory trajectories in the product MDP at each visit to an LDBA-accepting state, treating each segment as an independent sub-trajectory whose goal is to reach the next accepting state from an arbitrary starting point. A GAIL-style discriminator shapes dense rewards along each segment, but two key mechanisms differentiate it from standard GAIL: (1) segmented imitation sets the Q-value target at accepting states to 1/(1−γ), preventing the agent from lingering near (rather than reaching) accepting states and decoupling successive segments; (2) multi-stage discriminators are trained per LDBA state, with positive data drawn from trajectories whose maximal stage progresses beyond that state and negative data from trajectories that stall or hit sink states, producing a reward R(s,b) = (SIDX(b) + β·tanh(f_b(s))) / N(b) that monotonically increases with LDBA progression. Policy training uses SAC with entropy-regularized Q-learning, and Theorem 3.2 guarantees that the Q-value inflation at accepting states decays exponentially with distance, keeping learning grounded for states far from acceptance.

## Results

TiLoIL is evaluated on 9 continuous and discrete environments using eventual discounted returns (a proxy for LTL satisfaction probability that counts visits to LDBA-accepting states). With β=1/3, TiLoIL achieves the highest returns on 7 of 9 tasks, with particularly large margins on Doggo Avoid (296±74 vs. 79.9±67.1 for DRL²), Doggo Navigate (246±61 vs. 45.9±64.7), Fetch Align (21±15.9 vs. 1.8±3.3 for LCER), and Carlo (3.2±1.8 vs. 0.3±0.5). On the remaining two tasks (GridCircular Hard and FlatWorld Stabilization), TiLoIL ties with the best baselines. Learning curves (Fig. 3) show TiLoIL converges substantially faster than all RL and IL baselines, completing 3–4 laps in Carlo within 500 steps where baselines struggle to finish one.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the three RL-for-LTL baselines, TiLoIL dominates on all high-dimensional continuous tasks: it outperforms LCER by 70× on Doggo Avoid (296 vs. 4.2) and 12× on Fetch Align (21 vs. 1.8), and outperforms DRL² by 3.7× on Doggo Avoid and 5.4× on Doggo Navigate. Cycler, designed for cyclic environments, achieves zero returns on FlatWorld Cycle, Doggo Navigate, Fetch Align, Carlo, and Cheetah Flip, while TiLoIL solves all of them. Against the three imitation-learning baselines (GAIL, PWIL, SQIL), TiLoIL's segmented imitation and multi-stage discriminator provide a consistent lift: GAIL achieves 0 on Doggo Avoid, Doggo Navigate, Fetch Align, and Carlo, whereas TiLoIL reaches 296, 246, 21, and 3.2 respectively. On Fetch Avoid, TiLoIL (36±2.8) and GAIL (34.9±6.7) are comparable, and on Cheetah Flip TiLoIL (3.5±2.1) edges out GAIL (2.4±1.6) and DRL² (2.5±5). The paper notes that PWIL and SQIL show similar patterns to GAIL in the learning curves (Fig. 3), with TiLoIL consistently ahead on cyclic and multi-stage tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of LTL-guided reinforcement learning (LCER, Cycler, DRL²) and adversarial imitation learning (GAIL). Prior LTL-RL methods derive sparse proxy rewards from LDBA-accepting states, creating an exploration bottleneck over infinite horizons; prior imitation learning methods (GAIL, PWIL, SQIL) shape dense rewards from demonstrations but lack any mechanism to generalize finite demonstrations to infinite-horizon cyclic objectives. TiLoIL bridges these by segmenting agent rollouts at LDBA-accepting states and training per-stage discriminators tied to the LDBA graph structure, so that a single demonstration with one or two accepting-state visits can be reused across all segments. Theoretically, Theorem 3.1 connects the imitation learning objective (minimizing TV distance between occupancy measures) to LTL satisfaction probability, providing a formal justification for why distribution matching approximates formula satisfaction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that as few as 5 suboptimal demonstrations—each visiting LDBA-accepting states only once or twice—can bootstrap policies that satisfy infinite-horizon LTL formulas in high-dimensional continuous control, a setting where pure RL baselines struggle significantly. This substantially lowers the data barrier for LTL-constrained robotics and autonomous systems. A key limitation is that the method still requires the underlying RL (SAC) to eventually discover accepting-state transitions; if exploration fails to reach those states, TiLoIL cannot compensate. Future work should combine the exploitation mechanism with LDBA-aware exploration strategies, and the shared-discriminator variant (Appendix F.2) suggests a promising path toward scaling to very complex LTL formulas with many automaton states.
