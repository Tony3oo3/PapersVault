## Problem

Spiking Transformers combine the efficiency of spiking neural networks with the representational power of self-attention, but the field lacks standardized implementations, consistent evaluation pipelines, and fair cross-model comparisons. No unified platform exists to benchmark Spiking Transformers across diverse tasks (classification, segmentation, detection) and data modalities (static, event-based, sequential), and the relative contributions of individual components—neuron types, encodings, attention modules—remain underexplored. Additionally, energy-efficiency claims for spiking models have not been rigorously compared against quantized ANN baselines under a unified analytical framework.

## Input / Output

The framework accepts RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR, 224×224 for ImageNet), DVS event streams, serialized 1D image sequences (e.g., 784-step sMNIST), and 3D point clouds, converting all inputs into temporal spike trains over T=4 (or up to T=16) discrete time steps via direct, rate, TTFS, or phase encoding. Outputs are task-specific: class labels for classification, per-pixel segmentation masks for ADE20K, and bounding boxes with class scores for COCO detection. A key design constraint is that all models share a unified training protocol (same optimizer, LR schedule, batch size where feasible) to ensure fair comparison, with the SPS convolutional frontend operating at 32×32 or 224×224 spatial resolution before tokenization.

## Method

STEP is a modular, backend-agnostic benchmarking framework that integrates representative Spiking Transformer implementations (Spikformer, SDT, QKFormer, Spikformer+SEMM, and others) under a single training pipeline supporting SpikingJelly, BrainCog, and BrainPy backends. Its architecture decomposes each model into independently swappable components—spiking neurons (LIF, PLIF, GLIF, KLIF, CLIF), input encodings, spiking self-attention variants (SSA, SDSA, SDSA-v3), and task-specific heads—enabling systematic module-wise ablation. For dense prediction, STEP extends to segmentation via MMSegmentation and detection via MMDetection (Mask R-CNN with SpikeFPN). The paper's key empirical finding is that current Spiking Transformers rely heavily on convolutional frontends (reducing SPS from 4 to 1 conv layers drops accuracy by 6–12 points) while attention contributes minimally (randomized Q/K causes <0.35% drop), and that spike-based attention is better suited for spatial than temporal modeling. A unified analytical energy model incorporating spike sparsity, quantized bitwidth, and memory access costs reveals that quantized ANNs can match or exceed spiking Transformers in total energy consumption once memory overhead is accounted for.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, QKFormer achieves the best reproduced top-1 accuracy of 96.24%, followed by SGLFormer (95.88%), SDT (95.77%), and Spikformer (95.12%). On ImageNet-1K, QKFormer reaches 73.88% top-1 accuracy while Spikformer achieves 73.69% and SDT 71.96%. For ADE20K segmentation, Spikformer attains 23.51% mIoU, substantially outperforming SDT (12.08% mIoU) under identical no-pretraining settings. On COCO detection, SDTv2 with ImageNet pretraining achieves 10.5 bbox mAP@0.5 versus only 1.7 without pretraining, highlighting the critical role of pretraining for dense prediction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among the eight reproduced Spiking Transformers on CIFAR-10, QKFormer leads at 96.24% top-1, narrowly beating SGLFormer (95.88%) and SDT (95.77%), while the original Spikformer baseline sits at 95.12%. On the harder CIFAR-100, SGLFormer achieves the best accuracy (80.61%), followed by QKFormer (79.72%) and SpikingResformer (79.45%), with Spikformer trailing at 77.37%. On ImageNet-1K, QKFormer (73.88%) and Spikformer (73.69%) are nearly tied, both substantially ahead of SDT (71.96%). For ADE20K segmentation, Spikformer (23.51% mIoU) dramatically outperforms SDT (12.08% mIoU) under the same no-pretraining protocol, reversing SDT's original paper's claim of superiority with pretraining. On sequential image classification (sMNIST/psMNIST/sCIFAR), ANN baselines such as ViT+SPS (99.19/98.19/85.62%) and SMPConv (99.75/99.10/84.86%) consistently outperform all Spiking Transformers, with Spikformer+SEMM being the closest SNN (99.33/98.46/85.61%). In the energy analysis, the Spiking Transformer (17.16 mJ total) is actually less efficient than the Quantized Transformer (16.51 mJ) once memory access costs are included, despite having lower compute energy (11.57 vs. 16.34 mJ).

## Relation to Prior Work

STEP carves new ground as the first unified benchmarking platform for Spiking Transformers, integrating previously fragmented implementations (Spikformer, SDT, QKFormer, SpikingResformer, and others) under a single evaluation protocol across classification, segmentation, and detection. It departs from prior SNN energy analyses by introducing a unified analytical model that accounts for memory access costs and quantized-ANN bit-serial execution—two factors largely overlooked in earlier SNN-vs-ANN efficiency comparisons. The module-wise ablation design (neuron types, encoding schemes, attention randomization, convolutional depth reduction) extends the typical 'train-and-report' paradigm of prior Spiking Transformer papers into a systematic component-level analysis that had not been conducted under a common protocol.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that current Spiking Transformers derive most of their representational power from convolutional frontends rather than spiking attention (randomized attention causes <0.35% drop vs. a notable ViT drop) challenges the core motivation for adopting attention in the spiking domain and points toward the need for genuinely spike-native architectural innovations. The energy analysis—showing that quantized transformers can match or exceed spiking transformers in total energy once memory access is included—directly challenges the field's default assumption of inherent SNN efficiency and urges more rigorous hardware-aware benchmarking. Open questions include whether spike-native attention (e.g., STDP-based, dendritic, or multi-compartment) can overcome the temporal modeling deficit observed on sequential datasets, and whether large-scale self-supervised pretraining can close the detection gap without prohibitive compute.
