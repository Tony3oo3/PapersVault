## Problem

Standard LLM reasoning benchmarks (e.g., AIME-500) suffer from data contamination: models trained on pre-2024 data can memorize answers, making it impossible to distinguish genuine reasoning from recall. The paper demonstrates this concretely—o1's AIME score dropped from 0.74 (1983–2023) to 0.50 (2024) while human median scores stayed flat—yet existing static benchmarks cannot expose this leakage. ThinkBench addresses the gap by dynamically generating out-of-distribution variants that preserve logical structure while altering surface form, thereby decoupling reasoning ability from memorization.

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes original reasoning problems (AIME-500: 500 items, AIME 2024: 30 items, GPQA Diamond: 198 items) and produces 2,912 OOD semi-fact samples—each original problem yields exactly one scenario-level variant and three attack-level variants. Evaluation is conducted with pass@1 at temperature 0.7, and the OOD accuracy for each problem is computed as the mean of the scenario-level accuracy and the minimum across the three attack-level accuracies (Eq. 1). The framework also supports best-of-N test-time scaling evaluation using Process Reward Models, producing accuracy curves as a function of computation budget N.

## Method

ThinkBench builds on semi-factual causality to generate OOD data that changes surface presentation while preserving the underlying reasoning chain and correct answer. Scenario-level generation employs two Rephrasing Agents (one selects a new scenario, the other rewrites sentence-by-sentence) and three Verifier Agents (core-content transfer, per-part consistency, and overall coherence) in an iterative loop; any rephrasing failing verification is rejected and regenerated. Attack-level generation applies three single-iteration perturbations—TextBugger (character-level typos), CheckList (irrelevant sentence insertion), and StressTest (distracting content)—each validated by a Verifier Agent to ensure comprehension is not impeded. The resulting OOD dataset is used to evaluate 16 LLMs and 4 PRMs under identical conditions, and the same pipeline can be re-applied to the same problem to generate arbitrarily many variants, providing a scalable mechanism for contamination detection and test-time scaling analysis.

## Results

Across 16 LLMs evaluated on dynamically generated OOD data, the average performance decay is 24.9% on AIME-500 and 11.8% on AIME 2024, confirming data contamination in pre-2024 AIME questions. Top reasoning models (o3-mini, DeepSeek-R1, s1.1-32B) maintain the strongest OOD accuracy (e.g., o3-mini: 0.834 on AIME-500 OOD vs. 0.922 ID; DeepSeek-R1: 0.828 OOD vs. 0.920 ID), while non-reasoning models like GPT-4o suffer the largest drops (0.197 OOD vs. 0.278 ID on AIME-500, a −29.1% gap). On GPQA Diamond, o3-mini and DeepSeek-R1 also lead (0.710 and 0.682 OOD accuracy respectively), with most models showing smaller gaps than on AIME-500.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between four Process Reward Models (PRMs) under Best-of-256 decoding with Qwen2.5-Math-7B-IT as the policy model. On AIME 2024 OOD, Qwen-PRM achieves the highest accuracy (0.300), followed by Math-Shepherd and Skywork (both 0.233), then OpenR (0.200), and majority voting (0.133). On AIME-500 OOD, Skywork-PRM leads (0.500), followed by Qwen-PRM (0.476), OpenR (0.472), majority voting (0.464), and Math-Shepherd (0.458). Notably, all PRMs show a larger OOD performance drop on AIME-500 than on AIME 2024, reinforcing the data contamination finding. The paper also contrasts reasoning models (o1, o3, DeepSeek-R1, s1) against non-reasoning models, showing that reasoning models consistently exhibit smaller ID-to-OOD gaps (e.g., o3-mini: −9.5% on AIME-500) compared to non-reasoning models like GPT-4o (−29.1%) and LLaMA3.1-70B-IT (−25.0%), validating that test-time compute scaling genuinely improves robustness rather than merely exploiting memorized patterns.

## Relation to Prior Work

ThinkBench builds on the semi-factual causality framework from XAI literature (Kenny et al., 2021) and adapts dynamic evaluation ideas from DyVal (Zhu et al., 2023, 2024) and GLUE-X (Yang et al., 2023), which previously targeted non-reasoning NLU tasks. It repurposes three established adversarial perturbation methods—TextBugger, CheckList, and StressTest—as attack-level agents, while introducing a novel scenario-level rephrasing pipeline with multi-agent verification. The key departure from prior OOD benchmarks (ZebraLogic, CofQA, GLUE-X) is the explicit focus on reasoning models (o1, o3, DeepSeek-R1, s1) and the unification of reasoning and non-reasoning model evaluation under a single dynamic OOD framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The systematic quantification of data leakage—showing 24.9% average OOD performance decay on AIME-500 versus only 11.8% on AIME 2024—provides concrete evidence that pre-2024 AIME problems were likely in training corpora, a concern raised anecdotally by OpenAI but not previously measured across 16 models. The dynamic generation approach means the benchmark cannot be memorized, addressing a fundamental limitation of static reasoning benchmarks. Open questions include whether single-path scenario rephrasing captures sufficient OOD diversity, whether the framework generalizes beyond math and science to broader reasoning domains, and how to scale the costly LLM-agent pipeline to arbitrarily large datasets.
