## Problem

Neuro-symbolic (NeSy) systems are trained end-to-end in a weakly supervised manner where intermediate concepts remain unobserved, yet it remains unclear under what conditions minimizing the surrogate NeSy risk actually minimizes the true concept risk. While the reasoning shortcut phenomenon was identified, a rigorous theoretical framework providing necessary and sufficient conditions for learnability, along with sample complexity and asymptotic error bounds, was lacking.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252846/cropped_page7_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system takes a sequence of m RGB images (e.g., pairs of handwritten digits from MNIST/KMNIST/CIFAR-10/SVHN, or driving scene images from BDD-OIA) and produces a final label y through a two-stage pipeline: a neural network maps each image to an intermediate concept z, and a symbolic knowledge base performs logical entailment to derive y. Critically, only (x, y) pairs are observed during training; the intermediate concept sequence z is latent and must be inferred, making the problem a weakly supervised learning task over a discrete symbol space of size L per position.

## Method

The paper formulates NeSy tasks as Derived Constraint Satisfaction Problems (DCSPs), where variables correspond to concept clusters, domains are the symbol space, and constraints encode KB entailment relations. The central result is that a NeSy task is learnable if and only if its DCSP admits a unique solution (disagreement d = 0), with sample complexity O((1/κ)·log(|B|/ε)) under mild assumptions on the concept sequence distribution. For unlearnable tasks, the asymptotic expected concept error is bounded by d/L, where d counts the variables whose values differ across DCSP solutions, revealing that disagreement — not merely the number of solutions — governs concept error. The analysis operates within a restricted hypothesis space F* enforcing consistent concept-label mappings (satisfied by pre-trained or self-supervised networks) and unifies both probabilistic neuro-symbolic learning and abductive learning under a single theoretical lens. The paper further shows that aggregating multiple unlearnable tasks in a multi-task setting can reduce DCSP disagreement and restore learnability.

## Results

The paper's central theoretical result (Theorem 3.6) establishes that a NeSy task is learnable if and only if its derived CSP has a unique solution, with sample complexity O((1/κ)·log(|B|/ε)). For unlearnable tasks, Theorem 3.7 bounds the asymptotic expected concept error by d/L (disagreement over concept space size). On the realistic BDD-OIA benchmark (74,240 DCSP solutions, d=15), A³BL achieves 88.37% concept accuracy, substantially outperforming ABL (66.92%), LTN (20.41%), and DeepProbLog (0.00%), while the learnable addition/multiplication tasks reach near-perfect concept accuracy across MNIST, KMNIST, CIFAR-10, and SVHN.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On the BDD-OIA benchmark, A³BL (the paper's primary surrogate method) achieves the highest concept accuracy at 88.37%, outperforming ABL by 21.45 points and LTN by 67.96 points, though it trails ABL on reasoning accuracy (60.91% vs. 75.16%). DeepProbLog achieves moderate reasoning accuracy (44.69%) but completely fails on concept accuracy (0.00%), consistent with the theoretical prediction that high DCSP disagreement (d=15) makes the task unlearnable for methods that do not explicitly handle ambiguity. LTN performs worst overall on both metrics. The empirical results on arithmetic tasks confirm the theory: learnable tasks (addition, multiplication with unique DCSP solutions) reach near-100% concept accuracy, while unlearnable tasks (XOR with d/L=1, modular addition with d/L=0.2) plateau at the predicted asymptotic bound 1−d/L, validating Theorem 3.7 across all four image datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new theoretical ground by introducing the Derived Constraint Satisfaction Problem (DCSP) as a unifying lens for neuro-symbolic learnability, rather than extending a single prior method. It departs from prior partial analyses—Wang et al. (2023) requiring repetitive input patterns, Tao et al. (2024) needing full knowledge of concept distributions, and Yang et al. (2024) bounding a shortcut risk that does not directly control concept error—by providing a complete PAC-style characterization applicable to both probabilistic neuro-symbolic learning and abductive learning under a restricted hypothesis space. The DCSP formulation also gives a concrete theoretical explanation for the reasoning shortcut phenomenon identified by Marconato et al. (2023b), reframing it as the existence of multiple DCSP solutions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The DCSP framework gives NeSy practitioners a concrete, solver-verifiable criterion (unique solution vs. multiple solutions) and a finer-grained disagreement metric d that predicts asymptotic concept error, enabling principled task design and knowledge-base curation. The aggregation result—that combining unlearnable tasks can reduce disagreement to zero and restore learnability—suggests a practical multi-task strategy with potential scaling implications. Key limitations include the restricted hypothesis space assumption (satisfied by pre-trained or self-supervised models but not arbitrary architectures), the NP-hardness of solving large DCSPs, and the focus on hybrid systems only; extending the framework to general hypothesis spaces, semi-supervised settings, and scalable CSP approximations remain open.
