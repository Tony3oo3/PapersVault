## Problem

Neural spike trains exhibit substantial trial-to-trial variability driven by unobserved factors such as locomotion, arousal, and internal states, which can be misattributed as cross-area coupling in traditional GLM frameworks. Existing deep learning approaches capture this variability but lack interpretable estimates of directed interactions between neural populations. The paper addresses how to simultaneously model evolving background dynamics and identify interpretable cross-population coupling from large-scale multi-area spike train recordings.

## Input / Output

The method consumes multi-area neural spike trains organized as three-dimensional arrays (neurons × time × trials) with 2 ms binning, supporting joint training across multiple animals and recording sessions with animal-specific linear projections into a shared embedding space. It produces four interpretable components per population: smooth B-spline individual-neuron dynamics factors driven by a low-dimensional trial-wise latent, low-rank cross-population coupling filters (raised cosine basis convolutions) with explicit sending and receiving weights, neuron-specific post-spike history filters, and the trial-wise latent variable itself. The coupling structure is constrained to a single factor per source-target pair, reducing parameters from quadratic in neuron count to linear.

## Method

GLM-Transformer decomposes each neuron's log-intensity into three additive terms: individual-neuron dynamics, cross-population coupling, and self-history effects. A Transformer-based variational autoencoder encoder (1–2 layers, 10–20 ms tokens) produces a trial-wise latent z that is decoded via an MLP into B-spline coefficients, yielding smooth per-neuron dynamics; a low-rank GLM coupling term convolves source-population spike trains with raised cosine temporal filters and applies learned sending/receiving weights to the target population; and a post-spike filter captures refractory effects. Identifiability is enforced by regularizing individual-neuron dynamics to vary more slowly than coupling (ℓ₂ penalty on second derivatives, low-dimensional z), motivated by the empirical observation that behavioral fluctuations evolve over hundreds of milliseconds while cross-area interactions occur at shorter latencies. Training follows a four-stage curriculum—trial-invariant dynamics, VAE with ELBO, coupling, then post-spike filters—using Bayesian-optimized hyperparameters, and the model is validated on GLM and EIF synthetic data as well as the Allen Institute Visual Coding dataset.

## Results

On the Allen Institute Visual Coding dataset (121,679 trials, 10 animals, 6 visual areas), GLM-Transformer identifies strong directed coupling from V1 to LM and V1 to AL, consistent with the known feedforward visual hierarchy. In the robustness experiment (Figure 4), as the correlation in shared nonstationary gain modulation increases, GLM-Transformer maintains near-zero spurious coupling variance, while all baseline methods show substantially inflated coupling estimates. On synthetic GLM and EIF simulations, the fitted coupling filters and sending/receiving weights closely recover the ground-truth synaptic structure, and the ablation study (Table S1) confirms the full model achieves the lowest test NLL (e.g., 3.008×10⁷ on GLM simulation, 4.218×10⁷ on Allen data) compared to all component-removed variants.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison (Figure 4) evaluates robustness to shared background gain fluctuations across two EIF populations with no true synaptic coupling. Reduced-rank regression (RRR, Semedo et al. 2019) and delayed latents across multiple groups (mDLAG, Gokcen et al. 2023) both incorrectly attribute the correlated nonstationary gain to inter-population coupling, with the spurious coupling variance increasing as the gain correlation grows. Single-neuron GLMs (Pillow et al. 2008), both with and without a trial-wise constant gain term, similarly overestimate coupling. GLM-Transformer is the only method that consistently maintains low spurious coupling estimates across all correlation levels, owing to its trial-varying individual-neuron dynamics component. Notably, removing the trial-varying latent from GLM-Transformer (the orange curve) causes it to behave similarly to the baselines, confirming that the Transformer-based VAE is the critical differentiator. On the Allen Institute data, GLM-Transformer's coupling weights for V1→LM align with the 'cross-pop' neurons previously identified by Chen et al. (2022), providing independent validation of the coupling estimates.

## Relation to Prior Work

GLM-Transformer sits at the intersection of two established lines of work: (1) point-process GLMs for spike trains that model cross-neuron coupling via interpretable filter-based terms (Truccolo et al. 2005; Pillow et al. 2008), and (2) deep latent-variable models for neural population dynamics such as LFADS, NDT, and Deep Random Splines (DRS), which capture trial-to-trial variability but do not yield interpretable interaction estimates. The paper's key departure is a principled hybrid: a Transformer-based VAE (borrowing the trial-wise latent structure from DRS and the encoder architecture from NDT) absorbs nonstationary individual-neuron dynamics as high-dimensional nuisance parameters, while a low-rank GLM coupling component—newly integrated into the point-process framework—retains interpretability for directed cross-area interactions. Prior state-space approaches (mp-srSLDS, MR-SDS, STNDT) model interactions in latent space via deep networks, sacrificing the direct interpretability that GLM coupling provides.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical and recurring problem in large-scale neural recordings: shared background fluctuations from locomotion, arousal, and other unobserved states can be misattributed to functional coupling, a failure mode demonstrated clearly in the robustness experiment where all baseline methods (RRR, mDLAG, single-neuron GLMs) spuriously detect coupling from correlated inputs. The hybrid design—deep model for nuisance, interpretable model for signal—offers a template for other settings where one wants both flexibility and interpretability. Key open questions include developing rigorous statistical inference (confidence intervals, hypothesis tests) for coupling effects in this deep-hybrid setting, extending to variable-length trials, and reducing the data requirement (currently >1,000 trials) through pretrained initialization.
