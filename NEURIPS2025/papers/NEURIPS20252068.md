## Problem

Deep ensembles remain the go-to strategy for uncertainty quantification, but they are impractical for modern large-scale fine-tuning workflows: even the smallest ensemble (m=2) doubles training and inference cost, and fine-tuned models from a shared pre-trained checkpoint tend to settle in the same loss basin, reducing diversity. The paper asks whether meaningful improvements in accuracy, uncertainty, and selective classification can be obtained at only a fractional (10–20%) increase in computation over a single large model.

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard RGB images and produces a class label plus a scalar uncertainty score (1 − max softmax probability) for each input. At inference, both the large base model and the small sidekick model process the same image independently, and their raw logits are combined via a two-parameter weighted sum before a final softmax. The temperature weights are tuned once on a small validation set (as little as 5% of ImageNet's test split), taking only seconds, after which the Duo behaves as a single forward pass through two networks of different sizes.

## Method

Asymmetric Duos pair a large pre-trained model f_large (e.g., ConvNeXt-Base, Swin-V2-Base, EfficientNet-V2-L, or CLIP ViT-B) with a much smaller pre-trained model f_small (e.g., ResNet-34, MNASNet, ShuffleNet-V2), both independently fine-tuned on the same downstream task using the LP-FT recipe. Their logits are aggregated via a learned weighted average f_Duo(X) = f_large(X)·T_large + f_small(X)·T_small, where the two temperature parameters are tuned to minimize negative log-likelihood on a validation set—closely mirroring standard temperature scaling but extended to two models. This weighting automatically guards against a defective sidekick: if f_small is too inaccurate, T_small collapses toward zero and the Duo reverts to the base model. The key empirical finding is that even when the sidekick is 10× smaller and ~80% as accurate, the Duo consistently improves accuracy, correctness-prediction AUROC, AURC, and SAC@98 over the base model alone, while adding only 10–20% FLOPs. The framework is architecture-agnostic and composes with other techniques such as Model Soups, which can serve as the f_large component.

## Results

On ImageNet with ResNet-50 as the base model, the strongest Asymmetric Duo (RN50 + EfficientNet-B2, 27% FLOPs balance) achieves 81.87% top-1 accuracy, 87.15% correctness-prediction AUROC, and 4.80 AURC, matching the accuracy of a 5-model deep ensemble (81.97%) while using only ~7% of its inference FLOPs. Even the smallest Duo (RN50 + MNASNet-0.75, 5% balance) improves AUROC from 86.62 to 87.11 and AURC from 5.72 to 5.42 over the single model. Across five benchmarks (ImageNet, ImageNet V2, Caltech 256, iWildCam, iWildCam-OOD), Duos consistently improve accuracy, uncertainty separability, and selective classification with only 10–20% additional computation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the single-model baseline (temp-scaled RN50), Asymmetric Duos win on every metric: the 27%-balance Duo improves accuracy by +2.05 pp (81.87 vs 79.82), AUROC by +0.53 pp, and AURC by −0.92. Against a 2-model deep ensemble (100% balance), the 27%-balance AD wins on accuracy (81.87 vs 81.12) and AURC (4.80 vs 4.99) while using only 27% of the ensemble's FLOPs. Against a 5-model deep ensemble (400% balance), the AD nearly matches accuracy (81.87 vs 81.97) and Brier score (2.60 vs 2.61) with ~4× fewer FLOPs, though the DE retains a small edge on AUROC (87.62 vs 87.15) and AURC (4.48 vs 4.80). Against Model Soups (Figure 7), Duos provide further improvement on top of the soup's gains in accuracy, AURC, and SAC@98. Against Bayesian baselines, the AD outperforms IVON (32 samples) on accuracy (89.75 vs 88.43), NLL (40.73 vs 45.17), ECE (0.97 vs 3.46), and AURC (1.52 vs 1.77) on Caltech 256, and outperforms Laplace Approximation on accuracy (93.0 vs 92.2), NLL (26.9 vs 41.6), ECE (1.1 vs 4.9), and AURC (0.8 vs 0.9) on Swin-V2-S.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the deep ensemble framework (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017) by replacing the assumption of architecturally identical, equally-weighted members with a deliberately asymmetric pairing of one large and one small model, aggregated via a two-parameter learned temperature weighting that generalizes single-model temperature scaling (Guo et al., 2017). It departs from model soups (Wortsman et al., 2022) by operating in prediction space rather than weight space, and from shallow ensembles by using two independently trained networks rather than multiple heads on a single backbone. The key conceptual departure from prior ensemble work is that the asymmetry is not a limitation to be overcome but the central design principle that enables fractional compute overhead.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Asymmetric Duos offer a practical, drop-in solution to the well-known problem that deep ensembles become prohibitively expensive for modern large pre-trained models, requiring only 10–20% additional FLOPs rather than 100–400%. The framework is compatible with existing fine-tuning pipelines, model soups, and Bayesian optimizers, making it broadly applicable. Limitations include evaluation restricted to image classification on five datasets, and the temperature tuning step (though minimal) requires a held-out validation set. Open questions include extension to other modalities and tasks (segmentation, NLP, regression), and whether more sophisticated adaptive or input-dependent weighting schemes could further improve performance.
