## Problem

Existing fine-tuning methods for flow and diffusion generative models are restricted to maximizing expected reward under KL-divergence regularization, a formulation that cannot capture risk-averse optimization, novelty-seeking discovery, manifold exploration, or the use of alternative divergences such as Wasserstein or Rényi distances. This paper asks how one can provably fine-tune a pre-trained generative model to optimize an arbitrary distributional utility functional while preserving prior information via an arbitrary divergence, going beyond the linear/KL sub-case.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pre-trained flow or diffusion model (parameterized as a continuous-time policy), a user-specified utility functional F and divergence D over probability measures, and a sequence of regularization coefficients {η_k}. It outputs a fine-tuned policy whose induced terminal marginal density p_1^π maximizes G(p_1^π) = F(p_1^π) − αD(p_1^π ‖ p_1^pre) subject to the continuity equation constraint. Notably, the utility and divergence operate on the full distribution of generated samples rather than pointwise rewards, and the gradient of the first variation required at each step rarely needs explicit density estimation—only the Rényi divergence requires a density estimator.

## Method

Flow Density Control (FDC) is a mirror descent scheme that decomposes the general generative optimization problem into K sequential Linear GO sub-problems, each solvable by established entropy-regularized control solvers such as Adjoint Matching. At iteration k, FDC estimates the gradient of the first variation ∇_x δG(p_1^{k−1})—an infinite-dimensional gradient in probability-measure space—and feeds it as a reward signal into the inner solver, which updates the policy via a KL-regularized optimal control step. The key insight is that the first variation of functionals like CVaR, Wasserstein distance, entropy, and optimal experiment design admits closed-form or sample-based gradient expressions (via Danskin's theorem or chain rule) that avoid density estimation. Convergence is established in two regimes: a fast O(1/K) rate under concavity with exact oracles via relative smoothness and Bregman divergence analysis, and almost-sure convergence to a stationary point under noisy/biased oracles via the mirror flow framework and stochastic approximation theory.

## Results

On the novelty-seeking molecular design task (FlowMol on QM9, GFN1-xTB energy), FDC achieves a top-0.2% SQ_β score of 41.8 kcal/mol versus 39.7 for Adjoint Matching (AM), while AM achieves a higher average reward (29.1 vs. 27.5). In the illustrative risk-averse CVaR setting, FDC (K=2, β=0.01) reduces the 1%-worst-case cost from 288.2 (AM) to 90.0, well below the pre-trained baseline of 262.5. For novelty-seeking SQ maximization, FDC (K=2, β=0.99) elevates the top-1% reward to 596.1, far exceeding AM's 55.5 and the initial 66.6. On text-to-image bridge design (Stable Diffusion 1.4), FDC increases the Vendi diversity score from 2.36 to 2.47 and the CLIP score from 0.19 to 0.22.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is Adjoint Matching (AM) [Domingo-Enrich et al., 2024], the standard entropy-regularized control fine-tuning method that can only optimize linear (expected-reward) utilities under KL regularization. FDC wins decisively on all non-linear objectives: in the CVaR risk-averse setting it cuts the 1%-worst-case cost by ~69% (90.0 vs. 288.2), and in the SQ novelty-seeking setting it boosts the top-1% reward by ~10× (596.1 vs. 55.5). On the high-dimensional molecular design task, FDC achieves a 5.3% improvement in top-0.2% SQ_β (41.8 vs. 39.7) at the cost of a slightly lower average reward (27.5 vs. 29.1), confirming it can sacrifice mean performance to discover exceptional designs. Under Wasserstein-1 regularization, FDC matches AM's expected reward (35.4 vs. 35.0) while reducing the OT distance by 58% (1.95 vs. 4.67), demonstrating geometry-aware control. AM cannot address any of the non-linear objectives at all, as it is architecturally limited to linear utilities with KL divergence, making FDC the only method capable of solving these tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the entropy-regularized control framework for fine-tuning flow and diffusion models (Adjoint Matching [14], Uehara et al. [55]) from the specific case of KL-regularized expected reward maximization to the general problem of optimizing arbitrary distributional functionals under arbitrary divergences. The algorithmic core is a mirror descent scheme over probability measures, drawing on the Convex and General Utilities RL literature [20, 60] to reduce each non-linear optimization step to a standard linear fine-tuning subproblem solvable by existing solvers. Convergence guarantees leverage recent mirror flow theory [25] and relative smoothness/strong concavity conditions [33], connecting generative model fine-tuning to the broader program of optimization over spaces of probability measures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework opens a principled path to fine-tuning generative models for objectives that are genuinely important in scientific discovery (risk-averse molecular design, novelty-seeking exploration, optimal experiment design) but were previously inexpressible within the standard KL-regularized reward maximization formulation. The key open question, acknowledged by the authors, is whether the flexibility in choosing utilities and divergences yields concrete, reproducible gains in large-scale real-world applications beyond the illustrative and moderate-scale experiments presented. The convergence theory, while rigorous, relies on strong assumptions (exact oracles, relative smoothness) that are difficult to verify in practice, and the practical gap between the idealized and general settings remains a significant challenge.
