## Problem

Existing multicalibration algorithms discretize the predictor's output space and iteratively remap predictions within level sets, introducing rounding error, a sensitive discretization-granularity hyperparameter, and potential distortion of predictions that hinders downstream decision-making. This paper asks whether multicalibration can be achieved directly through a single loss-minimization step without any discretization of the output space.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254061/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a continuous-output base predictor f₀: X → [0,1] (a linear model, ResNet-18, or DistilBERT depending on the task), a set of binary group indicator functions g_i, and ground truth labels y ∈ [0,1]. It produces a continuous-output multicalibrated predictor p_G(f₀) = f₀ + Σt, where the correction is a sum of depth-2 decision trees. Unlike existing methods that lock the predictor into a fixed discretization during calibration, the output remains continuous and can be evaluated under any discretization granularity chosen at evaluation time.

## Method

The core algorithm solves a one-shot empirical risk minimization problem: it trains a depth-2 decision tree ensemble (via LightGBM) where each tree splits on a threshold of the base predictor's output f₀(x) and a group indicator g_i(x), minimizing the squared loss on the calibration set. The key theoretical result (Theorem 4.6) shows that if the squared loss is 'saturated'—meaning running the same tree-ensemble post-processing a second time yields only marginal improvement ε_loss ≪ 1—then the multicalibration error is bounded by √(ε_loss + ε_round). Lemma 4.3 establishes that the tree ensemble encompasses adding linear functions of group indicators on each output level set, connecting the one-shot ERM to the first iteration of existing iterative multicalibration algorithms. The loss saturation assumption is empirically validated across all six datasets, with marginal improvements on the order of 10⁻³ to 10⁻⁷, confirming that a single round of tree-ensemble fitting is sufficient.

## Results

On the worst-group smooth ECE metric (×1000), the proposed discretization-free method achieves 89.26 (Skin Lesion), 87.30 (Income Classification), 24.57 (Comment Toxicity), 20.87 (Age Regression), 44.41 (Income Regression), and 31.85 (Travel Time Regression), consistently outperforming both LSBoost and MCBoost across all six tasks. On standard accuracy, the method achieves 78.90% (Skin Lesion), 79.27% (Income Classification), 92.93% (Comment Toxicity), and MSE of 1.08×10⁻³ (Age Regression), 36.11×10⁻³ (Income Regression), 29.80×10⁻³ (Travel Time Regression), matching or exceeding all baselines. The loss saturation assumption (Assumption 4.5) is empirically validated with marginal improvement ε_loss ≤ 2×10⁻³ across all datasets when running the algorithm a second time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LSBoost (Globus-Harris et al., 2023), the proposed method achieves lower worst-group smECE on 5 of 6 tasks: 89.26 vs. 144.05 (Skin Lesion), 87.30 vs. 90.17 (Income Classification), 24.57 vs. 36.61 (Comment Toxicity), 44.41 vs. 72.29 (Income Regression), and 31.85 vs. 54.96 (Travel Time Regression), with LSBoost narrowly winning on Age Regression (20.25 vs. 20.87). Against MCBoost (Roth, 2022), the proposed method wins decisively on every task, with gaps as large as 150 points on Skin Lesion (89.26 vs. 239.26) and 138 points on Income Classification (87.30 vs. 182.07). On standard accuracy (Table 3), the method matches or exceeds all baselines: 78.90% vs. LSBoost's best 77.22% on Skin Lesion, 79.27% vs. 78.16% on Income Classification, and 1.08×10⁻³ MSE vs. 1.36×10⁻³ on Age Regression, confirming that multicalibration is achieved without sacrificing predictive performance. Notably, the proposed method requires only a single calibration pass with no discretization hyperparameter, whereas LSBoost and MCBoost must be separately tuned for each discretization level m.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on two key threads: Blasiok et al. (2023), who proved non-constructively that minimizing population loss over sufficiently large neural networks yields multicalibration, and Globus-Harris et al. (2023) (LSBoost), which uses tree ensembles in an iterative boosting framework for multicalibration but requires discretizing the predictor's output space. The key departure is that all existing multicalibration algorithms (MCBoost, LSBoost, and the game-dynamics approach of Haghtalab et al.) operate iteratively over discretized level sets, whereas this paper shows that a single empirical risk minimization step over depth-2 decision trees—implementable as a standard LightGBM call—achieves multicalibration without any discretization of the predictor's output. The paper's Lemma 4.3 formally connects its tree-ensemble construction to the first iteration of LSBoost, positioning the contribution as showing that one iteration suffices under a loss-saturation condition.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for fairness practitioners: multicalibration can now be achieved with a single off-the-shelf gradient-boosting call, eliminating the sensitive discretization hyperparameter, rounding error, and iterative calibration loop that complicate existing pipelines. The main limitation is Assumption 4.5 (loss saturation), which is empirically validated across all tested datasets but lacks a general proof; the authors construct a counterexample where a constant base predictor violates it, suggesting the guarantee depends on the informativeness of the initial predictor. Open questions include formalizing how the saturation gap ε_loss depends on the base predictor, extending to implicit or continuous-feature-defined groups, and understanding whether deeper tree structures capture group intersections without additional assumptions.
