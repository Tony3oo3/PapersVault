## Problem

In AI research and practice, rigor is predominantly understood as methodological rigor—correct application of mathematical, statistical, or computational methods—while concerns raised by the responsible AI community (overblown capability claims, pseudo-scientific assumptions, unintended harms) are treated as a separate, out-of-scope domain. This narrow conception fails to demand that benchmarks be fit-for-purpose, that background assumptions be valid, or that inferences be well-supported, undermining the scientific integrity of AI work and its conclusions.

## Input / Output

The paper draws on the responsible AI literature (ethical AI, science and technology studies, AI ethics) as its analytical input, examining how disciplinary norms, benchmark-driven evaluations, and algorithmic formalism shape what counts as rigorous. It produces a structured framework of six facets of rigor—each with a descriptive overview (what the facet is concerned with) and an evaluative overview (what the facet asks for)—along with concrete mechanisms (e.g., pre-registration, disaggregated evaluations, positionality statements, model cards, construct validity assessment) that can be adopted to promote rigor along each facet.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a reconceptualization of rigor in AI as comprising six interdependent facets: epistemic (background knowledge and assumptions), normative (disciplinary/community/personal norms and values), conceptual (theoretical constructs and their definitions), methodological (methods and their application), reporting (how findings are communicated), and interpretative (inferences drawn from findings). For each facet, the authors provide a descriptive overview identifying the object of concern and an evaluative overview specifying what rigor demands, then discuss specific mechanisms—such as appropriate grounding in literature, interrogating assumptions, positionality and ethical statements, conceptual systematization, construct validity, pre-registration, disaggregated evaluations, and documentation of AI artifacts—that promote rigor along that facet. The key argument is that responsible AI concerns are not separate from rigor but are themselves demands for scientific integrity, and that upstream choices (epistemic, normative, conceptual) constrain the quality of downstream methodological and interpretative work. The framework is explicitly non-prescriptive, intended as scaffolding for dialogue among researchers, policymakers, journalists, and other stakeholders rather than a rigid checklist.

## Results

This is a position paper that proposes a conceptual framework rather than reporting quantitative experimental results. The central contribution is a six-facet taxonomy of rigor in AI research and practice (epistemic, normative, conceptual, methodological, reporting, and interpretative), each defined by a descriptive question (what the facet is concerned with) and an evaluative question (what the facet asks for). The paper argues that the AI community's narrow focus on methodological rigor has contributed to overblown claims, pseudo-scientific assumptions (e.g., physiognomy-based work), and insufficient scrutiny of benchmarks and constructs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not empirically compare methods or report baseline comparisons. Instead, it positions its proposed broader conception of rigor against two alternative views: (1) that methodological rigor alone suffices for rigorous AI work, and (2) that non-methodological concerns (epistemic, normative, conceptual) fall outside the purview of AI researchers. The authors argue both views are inadequate, contending that all AI work necessarily implicates epistemic, normative, and conceptual choices, and that responsible AI considerations are in fact calls for greater scientific rigor rather than a separate domain. The framework is also distinguished from existing validity literature (construct validity, internal/external validity) by noting that epistemic, normative, and conceptual rigor precede and are prerequisites to validity questions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes concerns from the responsible AI literature (ethical AI, science and technology studies, AI ethics) and reframes them as scientific rigor concerns, arguing they are not a separate domain but part of what rigorous AI work demands. It draws on measurement validity scholarship (Messick's consequential validity, construct validity frameworks) and reproducibility/replicability literature in ML, but departs from the dominant AI community framing that equates rigor with correct application of mathematical, statistical, or computational methods. The six-facet framework (epistemic, normative, conceptual, methodological, reporting, interpretative) synthesizes ideas scattered across responsible AI, science philosophy, and research methodology literature into a unified taxonomy specifically for AI research and practice.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides much-needed shared vocabulary and a structured framework for critiquing AI work beyond methodological correctness, potentially shifting how reviewers, researchers, and policymakers evaluate scientific integrity in the field. By recasting responsible AI concerns as rigor concerns, it challenges the common dismissal of such concerns as 'out of scope' for core AI researchers. Open questions include how to operationalize and assess these facets in practice (e.g., in peer review or publication standards), whether the framework is too broad to be actionable, and how to balance the six facets when they conflict. The authors explicitly note rigor is not a panacea and that the facets are not all-inclusive, leaving room for further refinement.
