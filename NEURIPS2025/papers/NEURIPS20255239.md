## Problem

In stochastic principal-agent problems with asymmetric information, history-dependent policies are essential for optimality (e.g., tit-for-tat strategies) but cannot be explicitly represented due to exponential growth of possible histories. Prior work showed that stationary policies are inapproximable (NP-hard) in this setting, leaving open whether optimal history-dependent strategies are computationally tractable in finite-horizon models. This paper resolves that open question by showing they can be efficiently computed and learned.

## Input / Output

The algorithm takes a finite-horizon POMDP (state space S, joint action space A, observation space Ω, transition probabilities p, and reward functions) and produces inducible value polytopes as compact representations of the policy space. Rather than outputting an explicit exponentially-large policy, it computes the one-step action distribution π(·|σ; ω^P, ω̃^A) on-the-fly for any realized history sequence and current observations, in time polynomial in the model size and 1/ε. In the learning variant, the transition model is unknown and must be estimated through reward-free exploration over T episodes before the policy is deployed.

## Method

The core insight is that fully history-dependent policies can be compactly represented as two-dimensional value vectors (principal's and agent's expected total payoffs), and the set of all inducible value vectors at each state-action pair forms a convex polytope. The algorithm constructs these inducible value sets via backward dynamic programming: at each step it linearizes the value function constraint and incentive compatibility (IC) constraints, then approximates the resulting polytope by slicing the 2D value space along the principal's dimension and computing the convex hull of inducible boundary intersection points, ensuring exact IC with zero compromise on the agent's value. A forward unrolling procedure then recovers the policy on-the-fly by solving polynomial-size LPs at each time step. For the learning setting, the algorithm first performs reward-free exploration to estimate the transition model, then computes a relaxed δ-IC policy using a 3D value space that separately tracks the agent's truthful and best-deviation values, achieving Õ(ζ^{1/3} T^{2/3}) regret for both players.

## Results

The paper provides two main theoretical guarantees. First, an ε-approximate optimal IC policy can be computed in time poly(|S|·|A|·|Ω|, H, 1/ε) (Theorem 5), where the policy is fully history-dependent and ensures exact incentive compatibility. Second, in the episodic learning setting with unknown transition dynamics, the algorithm achieves sublinear regret Õ(ζ^{1/3} T^{2/3}) for both players over T episodes, where ζ = H⁵|S|²|A|³|Ω|² (Theorem 10). The computation in the learning setting takes time poly(|S|·|A|·|Ω|, H, T).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical experiments or baseline comparisons. The paper's contributions are positioned against prior theoretical results rather than empirical baselines. Key theoretical distinctions include: (1) unlike stationary policies, which are known to be NP-hard to optimize even approximately [15], the proposed algorithm handles the far more expressive class of history-dependent policies in polynomial time; (2) unlike EFG-based models [2, 43, 44] where the number of histories is bounded by the tree size, this work handles MDP-based models where histories grow exponentially; (3) compared to the concurrent work of Bernasconi et al. [3] on a special case (information design), this paper guarantees exact IC with a more straightforward approach and additionally addresses the learning problem. The paper also notes that exact solutions are possible in special cases (e.g., two-player turn-based stochastic games [46]) but are computationally infeasible in infinite-horizon settings due to irrational numbers in optimal policies.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the polytope-approximation techniques for correlated equilibria in stochastic games (Dermed & Isbell, NIPS'09; MacDermed et al., AAAI'11) into the principal-agent (Stackelberg) setting, adapting them to enforce exact incentive compatibility rather than symmetric equilibrium. It also generalizes the one-shot and tree-structured (EFG-based) principal-agent models of prior information-design and mechanism-design work (Gan et al., AAAI'22; Bernasconi et al., NeurIPS'22) to a graph-structured finite-horizon POMDP where history-dependent policies are essential for optimality. The learning component builds on reward-free exploration (Jin et al., ICML'20) to handle unknown transition dynamics, combining it with a relaxed IC formulation to achieve sublinear regret.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper resolves a key open question left by Gan et al. (2022): whether optimal history-dependent strategies in stochastic principal-agent problems are computationally tractable. The answer is yes (up to ε-approximation), via the inducible value set construction that compactly represents exponentially many histories as two-dimensional value vectors. A notable limitation is that the discretization-based IC guarantee does not generalize to n > 1 agents, and exact solutions in infinite-horizon settings remain infeasible due to irrational values. The framework unifies Bayesian persuasion and mechanism design in a sequential stochastic setting, providing a template for future work on multi-agent extensions and more complex information structures.
