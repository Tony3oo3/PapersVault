## Problem

Existing LLM privacy benchmarks predominantly test whether models refuse to share sensitive data, neglecting the equally important utility question of when sharing is appropriate. This paper identifies context ambiguity—scenarios lacking sufficient detail to definitively determine sharing appropriateness—as a key underexplored barrier that causes LLMs to make implicit assumptions, exhibit high prompt sensitivity, and produce inconsistent privacy judgments.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255607/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a text-based privacy scenario structured around contextual integrity fields (five fields in PrivacyLens+: data type, subject, sender, recipient, transmission principle; seven fields in ConfAIde+ including agent relationships and reveal reason) paired with one of three prompt variants (neutral, restrictive, permissive). The output is a single-token binary judgment (yes/no) on sharing appropriateness; when rationale is requested, the model also produces a free-text explanation. The Camber framework additionally generates disambiguated context expansions—targeted textual additions to specific scenario fields—producing 19,720 augmented PrivacyLens+ and 12,960 augmented ConfAIde+ examples across all strategies.

## Method

Camber is a context disambiguation framework that simulates clarifications a personal agent would obtain in practice, implemented with three strategies of increasing specificity: (1) label-independent expansion adds neutral, privacy-agnostic details to fields; (2) label-dependent expansion adds privacy-relevant context aligned with the example's appropriateness label; (3) reasoning-guided expansion first elicits model rationales, codes the implicit assumptions into nine categories (consent, privacy of information, suitability of communication channel, alignment with norms, purpose, recipient/sender authorization, established practices, safety guidelines), then generates targeted context expansions that resolve those specific assumptions. The key insight is that model-generated rationales reveal which contextual details are genuinely ambiguous, and systematically addressing those ambiguities—rather than adding generic context—yields the largest gains (up to 13.3% precision, 22.3% recall) and substantially reduces prompt sensitivity across Gemini 2.5 Pro, GPT-4.1, and Claude 3.7 Sonnet. Notably, the reasoning-guided codes generalize across datasets (developed on PrivacyLens+, effective on ConfAIde+) and reduce judgment entropy for both LLMs and human annotators.

## Results

On PrivacyLens+, the neutral prompt yields F1 scores of 74.6–80.3% across three frontier models (Gemini 2.5 Pro, GPT-4.1, Claude 3.7 Sonnet), with precision consistently higher than recall (85.6–92.2% vs. 62.7–75.7%), indicating models are overly conservative. Switching to a restrictive prompt drops recall by up to 42 percentage points (e.g., Claude on ConfAIde+: 75.2% → 33.0%), while the permissive prompt rarely recovers the loss. The reasoning-guided Camber expansion improves F1 by 8.9–18.5% on PrivacyLens+ and 0.9–8.2% on ConfAIde+, with the 'consent' code delivering the largest gains (+18.5% F1, +22.3% recall on PrivacyLens+). Prompt sensitivity (max F1 difference across paraphrase variants) drops from 9.8% to as low as 3.9% after reasoning-guided disambiguation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three Camber disambiguation strategies (label-independent, label-dependent, reasoning-guided) against a no-expansion baseline rather than external methods. Label-independent expansion provides negligible benefit (e.g., +4.8% F1 best case on PrivacyLens+ data type, with several fields actually hurting performance). Label-dependent expansion yields consistent gains (up to +17.2% F1 on PrivacyLens+ transmission principle), confirming that privacy-relevant context helps. Reasoning-guided expansion outperforms both baselines across all models and datasets, with the 'consent' code achieving the highest F1 (95.3% on PrivacyLens+, 93.1% on ConfAIde+). Notably, codes with zero labeled examples (e.g., 'safety guidelines' with 0 ConfAIde+ instances) still improve F1 by 2.6%, indicating the codes generalize beyond their derivation dataset. All three models show consistent top-performing fields/codes, with unanimous agreement on PrivacyLens+ (data type, transmission principle, consent for the three strategies respectively).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the privacy awareness assessment line of work grounded in contextual integrity theory, building directly on PrivacyLens (Shao et al., NAACL 2024) and ConfAIde (Mireshghallah et al., ICLR 2024) by augmenting both with positive (appropriate-to-share) examples to measure utility alongside privacy. Where prior benchmarks focus on whether models align with human norms or avoid leaking sensitive data, this paper deliberately shifts the lens to *context ambiguity*—the underspecification of situational details—as the primary barrier to consistent privacy judgments. The Camber disambiguation framework draws on contextual integrity parameters but introduces a reasoning-guided expansion strategy that goes beyond the theory's standard fields, using model-generated rationales to identify and resolve specific implicit assumptions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As personal AI agents become capable of making real-time information-sharing decisions on behalf of users, understanding *why* models fail on privacy judgments is as important as measuring failure rates. The paper's central finding—that systematically disambiguating context (especially via reasoning-guided codes like consent and privacy of information) yields up to 13.3% precision and 22.3% recall gains while reducing prompt sensitivity—provides a concrete, actionable direction for building more reliable agentic privacy systems. A notable open question is whether the synthetic expansions generated by Camber faithfully represent the clarifications an agent would actually receive from a user, and whether the nine reasoning codes generalize beyond the two datasets studied. The finding that per-example code matching does not outperform arbitrary code selection also raises deeper questions about the reliability of LLM-generated rationales as a guide to the model's actual decision process.
