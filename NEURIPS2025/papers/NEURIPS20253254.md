## Problem

Scalable oversight—where weaker AI systems supervise stronger ones—is a leading strategy for controlling future superintelligent systems, yet it remains unclear how oversight performance itself scales with the capability gap between overseer and overseen. Existing guarantees for Nested Scalable Oversight (NSO) are qualitative, whereas safety standards in other high-risk domains (aviation, nuclear) are quantitative. This paper addresses the gap by deriving quantitative scaling laws that predict oversight success probability as a function of model capabilities.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes pairwise win-rate matrices between all Guard–Houdini model pairs across four oversight games, along with Chatbot Arena Elo scores as a proxy for general intelligence. From these, it produces domain-specific Elo ratings per model, Double ReLU scaling parameters (slope α and thresholds g₁, g₂) per game, and—given game hyperparameters (m_G, m_H, Δ_domain, Δ_general)—the optimal number of oversight steps n* and the corresponding oversight success probability p_win. The win-rate matrices are estimated via repeated game play (50 wargames per pair, 5 majority-vote audits for Backdoor Code, etc.), and Elo fitting uses L-BFGS-B optimization on the standard logistic Elo formula.

## Method

The paper introduces a Double ReLU model—a piecewise-linear function with two plateaus (task incompetence and task saturation) and a linear payoff region in between—that captures how domain-specific Elo scales with general intelligence, validated on a toy Counting-to-21 (Nim) game. It then empirically fits this model on four oversight games (Mafia, Debate, Backdoor Code, Wargames), finding that Guard and Houdini Elos scale with general intelligence at game-specific slopes and intercepts. For the theoretical NSO analysis, the paper parameterizes multi-step oversight as a sequence of n games where each step's Guard is the previous step's Houdini, spaced Δ_general/n apart in general Elo, and derives the optimal n* by maximizing the product of stepwise Guard win probabilities (Eq. 4–5). Asymptotic analysis in three regimes (Δ_domain → −∞, Δ_domain → +∞, Δ_general = Δ_domain) yields closed-form or limiting expressions for n*, revealing that one step is optimal in Houdini-favored games while many steps help in Guard-favored games with large general gaps.

## Results

At a 400-point general Elo gap, NSO success rates are 13.5% for Mafia, 51.7% for Debate, 10.0% for Backdoor Code, and 9.4% for Wargames, with all rates declining further as the overseen system becomes stronger. The Double ReLU model (Eq. 2) successfully captures the three-phase scaling relationship (incompetence → linear payoff → saturation) validated on the Counting-to-21 toy game. Across the four oversight games, scaling slopes differ substantially: Debate shows the most favorable pattern (Guard slope positive, Houdini slope near zero), while Backdoor Code shows the most concerning pattern (Houdini slope steeper than Guard slope). The optimal number of oversight steps n* increases monotonically with Δ_general for games where m_G > m_H (Mafia, Debate, Wargames), while Backdoor Code exhibits a 'Goldilocks zone' where two steps is optimal.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark a single method against named baselines; instead, it compares four oversight game protocols (Mafia, Debate, Backdoor Code, Wargames) against each other as proxies for scalable oversight, and compares single-step oversight (n=1) against multi-step Nested Scalable Oversight (n>1). The key cross-protocol finding is that Debate is the most favorable oversight game (51.7% success at 400 Elo gap, with Guard Elo scaling while Houdini Elo stays flat), while Wargames (9.4%) and Backdoor Code (10.0%) are the least favorable. NSO consistently outperforms single-step oversight: in the theoretical analysis (Fig. 7), multi-step oversight can shift Guard log-odds from −3 to +3 in favorable regimes, and the optimal n* grows with the general intelligence gap. The paper also contrasts its empirical scaling findings with prior work: Kenton et al. (2024) tested only a few models for debate without explicit judge comparison, and Greenblatt et al. (2024b) studied backdoor code without analyzing how detection/insertion ability scales with general intelligence. The Double ReLU framework is validated against the fully-solvable Counting-to-21 (Nim) game, confirming the predicted plateau–linear–saturation pattern before being applied to the four oversight games.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of scalable oversight protocols (Kenton et al. 2024 for debate, Greenblatt et al. 2024 for backdoor code auditing, Shah et al. 2025 for bootstrapping/NSO) and the scaling laws literature (Kaplan et al. 2020). Prior work has either evaluated specific oversight protocols on a handful of models or studied how general model performance scales with compute, but no prior work has quantified how oversight-specific performance scales with general intelligence across multiple games, nor derived optimal multi-step oversight strategies from those scaling laws. The Double ReLU model for relating general to domain Elo is a novel contribution that enables the subsequent theoretical analysis of Nested Scalable Oversight, extending the qualitative NSO discussion in Shah et al. (2025) into a quantitative, parameterized framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first quantitative framework for Nested Scalable Oversight, providing practitioners with a concrete recipe: measure domain Elo slopes for Guard and Houdini roles, then compute the optimal number of oversight steps. The finding that NSO success rates at a 400-point general Elo gap range from only 9.4% (Wargames) to 51.7% (Debate) is a sobering quantitative baseline that the field has lacked. Key limitations include the reliance on prompting-based deception (rather than fine-tuned adversarial behavior), the use of Chatbot Arena Elo as an imperfect proxy for general intelligence, and the simplification of real-world oversight into discrete games. Open questions include whether the framework generalizes to RLHF/SFT alignment pipelines, how temporal risk accumulation (the 'Compton constant' and control half-life) interacts with policy design, and whether the Double ReLU assumption holds for genuinely superhuman systems.
