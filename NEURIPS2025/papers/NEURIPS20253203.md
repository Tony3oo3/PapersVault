## Problem

Large language models increasingly memorize and reproduce training data verbatim, creating legal and safety risks, yet existing mitigation techniques (differential privacy, unlearning, fine-tuning) require access to model weights or training data—making them impractical for typical users who can only interact with deployed LLMs through token-level API outputs. No prior method effectively reduces verbatim generation under this limited-access constraint.

## Input / Output

At each decoding step, TOKENSWAP consumes the full vocabulary probability distributions from both a large main model (e.g., Llama-3-8B, Pythia-6.9B) and a small auxiliary model (e.g., DistilGPT-2 at 80M, SmolLM-135M) conditioned on the same prefix. It outputs a single modified probability distribution in which only the ~110 high-frequency grammar tokens (determiners, prepositions, conjunctions, pronouns, modals) have their probabilities replaced by the auxiliary model's values scaled by a normalization factor α, while all remaining tokens retain the main model's probabilities. The generated text is then sampled or greedily decoded from this hybrid distribution.

## Method

TOKENSWAP exploits the empirical observation that small models (~100M parameters) memorize far less than large models yet still assign fluent, grammatically plausible probabilities to common function words. At each position, it queries both the main and auxiliary models, then for a fixed subset G of high-frequency grammar tokens, replaces the main model's probabilities with α-scaled auxiliary probabilities (where α = sum of main-model G-probs / sum of aux-model G-probs ensures the result is a valid distribution). This selectively disrupts the high-probability paths that drive verbatim reproduction: once a single grammar token deviates from the memorized sequence, all subsequent predictions are conditioned on the altered context, cascading the disruption. Because G contains only low-semantic-content tokens (e.g., 'the', 'of', 'and'), reasoning-critical content words remain governed by the large model, preserving task performance. The method requires no access to training data, model weights, or disjoint training corpora—only token-level logits from two models of different scales.

## Results

On production-grade models, TOKENSWAP achieves up to a 10× reduction in exact memorization: on LeetCode (Llama-3-8B), ROUGE-L>0.8 drops from 9.65% to 0.96% and Matching Length from 24.57 to 6.04; on Pile-Memorized (Pythia-6.9B), EMR drops from 65.22% to 5.98% and ML from 151.6 to 35.10. In extreme fine-tuning experiments, reductions reach 800× on WritingPrompts (EMR 83.4%→0.1%) and 50× on MathAbstracts (EMR 93.6%→1.8%). Task performance is preserved: commonsense reasoning accuracy remains identical (71.87%), MT-Bench scores are unchanged (7.75→7.78), and cross-entropy loss increases by less than 0.1 nats.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CP-Fuse [1], TOKENSWAP wins decisively on all memorization metrics in real-world settings: on Pile-Memorized, EMR is 5.98% vs. 29.35% (5× lower) and ML is 35.10 vs. 97.05 (2.8× lower); on LeetCode, ROUGE-L>0.8 is 0.96% vs. 7.01% (7× lower). CP-Fuse's core assumption of disjoint training datasets does not hold for production models, limiting its effectiveness. Against MemFree [35], TOKENSWAP trades a small loss on exact memorization (EMR 0.1% vs. 0.0% on WritingPrompts) for a large gain on approximate memorization (ROUGE-L 0.19 vs. 0.29, Levenshtein 0.71 vs. 0.63), and critically does not require access to the training corpus. Against Goldfish [31] (k=3), TOKENSWAP matches or slightly beats on memorization (ML 12.4 vs. 12.7, ROUGE-L 0.22 vs. 0.23) while maintaining lower cross-entropy loss (3.44 vs. 3.54), and the two methods are composable (TOKENSWAP+Goldfish achieves ML 7.9). Against speculative decoding [40], TOKENSWAP achieves 4× lower EMR (5.98% vs. 23.91%) and substantially better approximate memorization (ROUGE-L 0.38 vs. 0.56), since speculative decoding preserves the large model's memorization-prone distribution.

## Relation to Prior Work

TOKENSWAP carves new ground at the intersection of two existing ideas: the use of small auxiliary models (as in speculative decoding) and logit-level manipulation at inference time (as in CP-Fuse and MemFree). However, it departs from both in critical ways: unlike speculative decoding, it deliberately *alters* the large model's distribution rather than preserving it, and unlike CP-Fuse or MemFree, it requires neither disjoint training corpora nor access to the training data. The conceptual lineage draws from the extractable memorization framework (Carlini et al.) and the observation that memorization scales with model size, but the specific mechanism—selectively swapping probabilities for a small set of high-frequency grammar tokens from a ~100M auxiliary model—is novel. The paper positions itself as the first method that operates under the realistic constraint of API-only (logits-level) access without any assumptions about training data or model internals.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TOKENSWAP addresses a practically critical gap: most deployed LLMs (GPT-4, Gemini, Llama) expose only token-level outputs, yet all prior memorization mitigations require weights, training data, or disjoint model pairs. The method's practical accessibility—requiring only a ~100M auxiliary model (~1% memory overhead)—makes it deployable by end-users and API providers alike. Key limitations include its current focus on natural language (code and structured domains need adaptation), the risk that if the auxiliary model itself memorizes a sequence, that memorization is preserved, and the fact that it treats the symptom (verbatim generation) rather than the cause (memorization in weights). An open and promising direction is that any future advance in reducing memorization in small models (via pre-training or unlearning) can be immediately leveraged for large models through TOKENSWAP, creating a compounding benefit.
