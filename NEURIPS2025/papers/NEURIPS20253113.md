## Problem

Existing unlearning evaluation methods assess whether isolated facts are directly removed from LLMs, ignoring how knowledge is deeply interconnected and how a seemingly erased fact can still be inferred from correlated knowledge the model retains. Additionally, prior evaluations treat LLM knowledge as deterministic, overlooking the probabilistic, confidence-based nature of factual recall. This leads to substantial overestimation of unlearning effectiveness, as surface-level triple deletion does not constitute genuine knowledge removal.

## Input / Output

The framework probes an unlearned LLM with multiple-choice questions (Yes/No/Unknown) over candidate triples drawn from k-hop neighborhoods in a reference knowledge graph (YAGO3-10), using temperature-scaled softmax probabilities to derive entropy-based confidence scores for each triple. The LLM judge (GPT-4o-mini) then reasons exclusively over the extracted confidence-aware subgraph to assign a discrete 0–5 inferability score. The final Unlearning Effectiveness Score (UES) is the average normalized relative decrease in judge-assigned inference scores between pre- and post-unlearning models, complemented by a Recall metric tracking subgraph persistence and utility metrics (local consistency, MMLU perplexity, BBH accuracy).

## Method

The core contribution is a two-stage evaluation protocol grounded in confidence-aware knowledge graph modeling. In the first stage, a breadth-limited subgraph extraction iteratively probes the unlearned model with candidate triples from the reference KG's k-hop neighborhoods, admitting a triple only if the model selects 'Yes' and its calibrated entropy falls below a threshold u*=1 (corresponding to a worst-case 0.5 probability split). In the second stage, a powerful LLM judge reasons over the extracted subgraph using prompts that enforce reliance solely on provided triples, incorporate entropy-based confidence bins (Very Confident → Not Confident), and require a 0–5 discrete rating; this judge is validated against human expert annotators for alignment. The key insight is that successful unlearning requires not merely the removal of the target triple but the disruption of its entire supporting inferential structure, which is quantified by comparing judge scores on pre- and post-unlearning subgraphs. The UES metric captures this as a normalized relative decrease, while a separate Recall metric measures the proportion of original supporting triples preserved in the unlearned model.

## Results

Across all unlearning methods and both LLMs (LLaMA3-8B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct), the correlated-knowledge UES (UES(Ours)) drops substantially relative to instance-level UES (UES(Inst.)), with reductions of 20–95% in most settings. For example, under Sentence-based Full fine-tuning on LLaMA, GA achieves UES(Inst.)=0.157 but only UES(Ours)=0.076 (51.6% drop), while SCRUB(Full) even yields a negative UES(Ours) of −0.003, indicating that unlearning *increased* inferability through correlated knowledge. Utility (Loc) is well preserved (≥0.95) for most Sentence-based methods, but QA-based full-model unlearning on Qwen degrades Loc to 0.58–0.73 while achieving higher UES(Ours) (up to 0.941 for SCRUB). No method simultaneously achieves high UES(Ours) and Loc ≥ 0.8, revealing a fundamental unlearning–utility trade-off.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks five unlearning methods—Gradient Ascent (GA), Random Labels (RL), Negative Preference Optimization (NPO), NegGrad+, and SCRUB—under a unified computational budget on LLaMA3-8B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct. The central finding is that *all* methods suffer large drops in unlearning effectiveness when evaluated under the correlated-knowledge protocol versus instance-level recall: UES(Ours) is 20–95% lower than UES(Inst.) in most configurations, with SCRUB(Full) on LLaMA even showing a negative UES(Ours) (−0.003), meaning the target fact became *more* inferable after unlearning. When local utility is well preserved (Loc ≥ 0.8), GA, NPO, NegGrad+, and SCRUB generally outperform RL on UES(Ours), with GA(Full) achieving the best LLaMA UES(Ours) of 0.076 and NegGrad+(Full) the best Qwen UES(Ours) of 0.534. However, no single method dominates across both models and both template formats; for instance, SCRUB(LoRA) achieves the highest LLaMA UES(Inst.) (0.211) under Sentence templates but its UES(Ours) drops to only 0.033 (84.4% reduction). The results collectively demonstrate that existing unlearning methods, which target direct triple removal, leave the broader inferential knowledge structure largely intact, and that current instance-level evaluation metrics substantially overestimate true unlearning effectiveness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the unlearning evaluation line initiated by Wu et al. [28], which first explored multi-fact interactions during unlearning but relied on deterministic knowledge modeling and rule-based inference chains. It also draws on the knowledge-probing methodology from RWKU [27] for template design. The key conceptual departure is replacing fixed reasoning rules with a confidence-aware knowledge graph representation and an LLM-based adversarial judge, shifting the evaluation from 'can the model recall this triple?' to 'can an adversary reconstruct this triple from correlated, probabilistically-weighted supporting facts?'

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that UES drops by 20–80%+ when moving from instance-level to subgraph-level evaluation directly challenges the assumption that successful direct-triple removal implies genuine forgetting, with implications for how the field benchmarks and trusts unlearning claims. The confidence-aware analysis further reveals that even low-confidence residual knowledge can enable inference, suggesting that unlearning methods must target not just the target fact but its entire inferential neighborhood. Open questions include whether the reference-KG-guided subgraph extraction captures all latent reasoning paths, whether the LLM judge's reliability holds at scale beyond 50-sample validation, and whether findings generalize beyond encyclopedic relational knowledge to procedural or generative capabilities.
