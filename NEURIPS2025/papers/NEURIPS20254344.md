## Problem

MoE-based LLMs route tokens through sparse expert subsets, yet how safety alignment is internally organized across these experts remains unexplored. Existing MoE safety work focuses on exploiting architectural weaknesses (e.g., dormant-expert backdoors) rather than understanding which experts carry safety-aligned behavior. This paper formalizes 'positional vulnerability'—the finding that refusal behavior concentrates on a small set of specific experts—and provides the first systematic framework to identify, characterize, and validate those experts.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes three groups of 500 text prompts each (regular harmful, jailbreak-rewritten harmful, and benign) and tracks per-token, per-layer Top-k expert activation sets across all MoE-FFN layers during inference. It produces two functionally decomposed expert sets—HCDG (harmful content detection) and HRCG (harmful response control)—along with layer-wise activation probability distributions, refusal-rate deltas under masking, and optionally LoRA-merged expert weights that improve adversarial refusal without retraining the full model.

## Method

SAFEx is a three-step analytical pipeline. First, a Stability-based Expert Selection (SES) algorithm—adapted from Meinshausen & Bühlmann's Stability Selection—draws S bootstrap subsets from each prompt group, estimates per-expert activation frequencies, and takes the stable intersection of top-N_e experts across resamples to yield robust per-group expert sets. Second, set operations decompose these into HCDG (intersection of Regular and Jailbreak top sets, indicating shared content-detection role) and HRCG (set difference, indicating refusal-specific control). Third, validation uses linear probes on HCDG experts' FFN outputs (logistic regression, L2-regularized) to confirm harmful/benign discriminability, and expert masking (assigning −∞ logits to HRCG experts, re-normalizing Top-k routing) to confirm refusal-rate drops. For mitigation, LoRA adapters are trained on selected experts with toxic or safety data, then composed via subtractive weight merging (θ̃ = θ − Δθ_tox) targeted at HRCG, improving jailbreak refusal by ~29 points on Qwen3-30B-A3B without touching the router or backbone.

## Results

On Qwen3-30B-A3B (6,144 total experts), masking just 12 safety-critical experts (E_ctrl) drops the refusal rate from 93.6% to 71.6% (−22.0%), while AIME-2024 scores remain nearly unchanged (77.4→75.2), confirming the effect is safety-specific. Under jailbreak attacks, MoE models are substantially more vulnerable than their dense counterparts: Qwen3-30B-A3B suffers a 48.4% refusal drop versus only 27.8% for Qwen3-32B-Instruct. Expert-level subtractive LoRA merging on E_ctrl alone lifts jailbreak refusal from 47.7% to 76.5% on Qwen3-30B-A3B without full-model retraining.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against external safety-alignment baselines; instead it establishes internal and cross-architecture comparisons. The most striking contrast is MoE vs. Dense under jailbreak: within the Qwen3 family, the MoE variant (Qwen3-30B-A3B) loses 48.4% of its refusal rate while the dense Qwen3-32B-Instruct loses only 27.8%, a 20.6-point gap that isolates positional vulnerability as an MoE-specific risk. For the LoRA merging experiments (Table 2), fine-tuning only E_ctrl (2–12 experts) already recovers most of the safety gain achievable by fine-tuning all experts (e.g., 76.5% vs. 77.2% subtractive on Qwen3-30B-A3B), demonstrating that targeted expert-level intervention is nearly as effective as full-model adaptation at a fraction of the cost. The SES ablation (Table 4) shows that without stability-based selection, the identified control set grows to 15 experts but the masking effect weakens to only 7.0% refusal drop (vs. 22.0% with SES), confirming that stability filtering is essential for pinpointing truly critical experts. Linear probes on E_id consistently outperform randomly selected experts across accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score on all three tested architectures, validating the functional categorization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the statistical resampling framework of Stability Selection (Meinshausen & Bühlmann, 2010) from feature selection to the novel problem of identifying safety-critical experts in MoE architectures, creating a Stability-based Expert Selection (SES) procedure. It departs from prior MoE safety work (BadMoE, buffer-overflow attacks) that focused on exploiting architectural vulnerabilities for attacks, instead analyzing how safety alignment is internally organized across expert modules. The expert-level functional decomposition (HCDG/HRCG) extends the attention-head safety analysis of Zhou et al. (2025) from dense models to the routed-expert setting, while the expert-level LoRA + negative weight merging pipeline offers a practical mitigation absent from prior interpretability-focused work (ITI, LUNA) that operates at the activation level.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that safety behavior in MoE models is concentrated in a vanishingly small fraction of experts (12 out of 6,144 for Qwen3-30B-A3B) has direct implications for both attack surface and defense: it means a single-expert perturbation can compromise model safety, and conversely, targeted expert-level fine-tuning can restore it without full-model retraining. The demonstrated gap in jailbreak susceptibility between MoE and dense counterparts (48.4% vs. 27.8% refusal drop in the Qwen3 family) suggests that current safety alignment pipelines may be systematically under-protecting MoE architectures. Open questions include whether the HCDG/HRCG decomposition generalizes to larger MoE systems and hybrid architectures, whether routing-level regularization or redundancy mechanisms can mitigate positional vulnerability at training time, and whether neuron-level (rather than expert-level) interventions yield finer-grained control.
