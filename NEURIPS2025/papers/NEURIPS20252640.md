## Problem

In deterministic or vanishing-learning-rate settings, regularized learning (FTRL) in continuous games is known to converge to Nash equilibrium, but with persistent randomness and constant step sizes—the regime most relevant in practice—the long-run behavior is largely uncharacterized. This paper asks: does the induced sequence of play converge at all, and if not, which actions are visited more often in the long run and by how much? The gap is particularly acute for continuous-action games, where standard stochastic analysis tools (Dynkin's formula, elliptic PDE methods) are difficult to apply directly.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a continuous concave game (payoff functions with Lipschitz-smooth gradients over closed convex action sets), a regularizer (strongly convex, e.g., Euclidean or entropic), and a noise model (state-dependent diffusion matrix in continuous time, or bounded-variance stochastic gradient feedback in discrete time). Rather than producing a single converged action profile, the output is a distributional characterization: explicit hitting-time bounds for reaching an ε-neighborhood of equilibrium, a lower bound on the long-run fraction of time spent in that neighborhood (scaling as 1 − r_σ²/r²), and a classification of the process as transient, null-recurrent, or positive-recurrent with an associated invariant measure.

## Method

The paper establishes a sharp dichotomy between null-monotone and strongly monotone games by using the Fenchel coupling F(x*, Y(t))—a primal-dual divergence induced by the regularizer—as a Lyapunov/energy function for the dynamics. In continuous time, the FTRL dynamics are modeled as an SDE, and Ito's formula combined with Dynkin's stopping-time formula yields explicit drift estimates for F: in null-monotone games the drift is purely outward (noise-dominated), causing escape to infinity in mean and infinite expected return time; in strongly monotone games the deterministic drift partially counters the noise, yielding finite hitting times and positive recurrence with concentration at O(σ_max) from equilibrium. In discrete time, where stochastic calculus is unavailable, the authors reduce the dynamics to a quotient space of the dual, establish Lebesgue-irreducibility and a minorization condition on the transition kernel, and invoke positive Harris recurrence to prove convergence to a unique invariant measure with the same concentration guarantees. The key technical distinction is that positive recurrence is established in the score space Y (where the generator is uniformly elliptic after quotienting) and then pushed forward to the action space X via the mirror map Q.

## Results

In strongly monotone games, the paper establishes that the mean hitting time to a ball of radius r around equilibrium satisfies E[τ_r] ≤ (F₀/α)/(r² − r²_σ) in continuous time (Theorem 2) and E[τ_r] ≤ [1/(αγ(r² − r²_σ))]·F₀ in discrete time (Theorem 4), where r_σ = σ_max/√(2Kα) (continuous) or r_σ = √(γ(σ²+β²)/(αK)) (discrete). The long-run occupation measure concentrates in a ball of radius O(σ_max) around equilibrium, with the fraction of time spent in B_r(x*) bounded below by 1 − r²_σ/r². In null-monotone games, the dual result holds: the mean time to return closer to equilibrium is infinite (E[τ⁻_ε] = ∞), while the mean time to drift further away is finite (E[τ⁺_ε] ≤ 2ε/(κσ²_min)), and no invariant distribution exists.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines; the central 'comparison' is the structural dichotomy between null-monotone and strongly monotone games under the same FTRL dynamics. In null-monotone games (e.g., bilinear min-max), uncertainty destroys the deterministic periodicity entirely: the process escapes to infinity in mean square, requires infinite expected time to return to equilibrium, and admits no invariant distribution (Theorems 1 and 3). In strongly monotone games (e.g., Cournot competition, Kelly auctions), the deterministic convergence is diluted but not destroyed: the process is positive recurrent, converges to a unique invariant measure, and concentrates within O(σ) of equilibrium (Theorems 2 and 4). The paper also contrasts continuous-time (SDE-based, with explicit closed-form bounds via Itô's and Dynkin's formulas) against discrete-time (constant step-size FTRL, where the same qualitative dichotomy holds but with less explicit quantitative control due to the inapplicability of standard stochastic calculus tools). The continuous-time analysis of Mertikopoulos & Staudigl [46, 48] is identified as the closest prior work, and the discrete-time null-monotone result extends Cauvin et al. [12] from finite to continuous games.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the FTRL framework of Shalev-Shwartz & Singer [65] and the stochastic mirror descent analysis of Mertikopoulos & Staudigl [46, 48] from convergence guarantees under vanishing step sizes to a full distributional characterization under persistent (constant-step-size) uncertainty in continuous games. It generalizes the finite-game escape results of Cauvin et al. [12] to continuous action spaces and, crucially, complements them with a positive concentration result for strongly monotone games. The analytical toolkit—Fenchel coupling as a Lyapunov function, Dynkin's formula, and the transience/recurrence dichotomy for uniformly elliptic SDEs—draws on classical stochastic analysis (Øksendal [58], Khasminskii [32]) but applies it to the mirror-descent geometry of regularized learning in a way not previously done.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first rigorous characterization of the long-run invariant distribution and concentration properties of FTRL in continuous games under persistent noise, filling a gap left by the stochastic approximation literature which focuses on vanishing step sizes and convergence. The null-monotone vs. strongly monotone dichotomy provides a clean, actionable criterion for practitioners: in games like Cournot competition or signal covariance optimization, constant-step-size learning concentrates in an O(σ) neighborhood of equilibrium, while in zero-sum or bilinear saddle games it drifts to the boundary. Open questions include the merely monotone regime (Remark 7), discrete-time hitting-time bounds (stated as a conjecture), and the explicit form of the invariant measure in strongly monotone games beyond the Gaussian special case.
