## Problem

Existing dynamic scene representations force a trade-off between editability and scene complexity: neural atlases support 2D appearance editing but break down under multi-object occlusion, while neural scene graphs capture 3D spatial ordering but make view-consistent appearance editing extremely difficult. No prior method simultaneously enables high-resolution 2D texture editing and 3D repositioning/removal of multiple interacting objects in a single coherent scene model.

## Input / Output

The method consumes an RGB video sequence (F frames at W×H×3), per-object segmentation masks (binary, F×W×H×N), 3D bounding boxes for object initialization, and camera intrinsics/extrinsics; when 3D boxes are unavailable it falls back to homographies from monocular depth estimation. It produces a per-object neural atlas (a 2D color-opacity-flow texture on a finite plane) plus a rendered video, and supports direct 2D texture editing by projecting a user-supplied RGBA image onto any node's plane in atlas space, yielding view-consistent edits across all frames without re-optimization.

## Method

NAG represents each scene element as a finite 2D plane undergoing a learned rigid 6-DoF trajectory (parameterized by piecewise cubic Hermite splines over zero-initialized control points), carrying a base color/opacity grid, view-dependent neural fields (MLPs with positional encodings), and a spline-based optical flow field for non-rigid deformation and parallax. Rendering uses depth-ordered ray-casting with ray-plane intersection and alpha compositing across all N+1 nodes (foreground objects plus a fixed background plane). The entire graph is fit at test-time by jointly optimizing trajectory parameters and all neural field weights with a photometric L1 loss plus a mask-consistency term (β=0.005). The key architectural insight is that each graph node is itself a neural atlas rather than a volumetric radiance field or Gaussian collection, so 2D texture edits propagate view-consistently through the flow model and rigid trajectory without requiring 3D edit propagation. Editing is performed by ray-projecting a user texture onto the target plane, inverting the learned flow, and blending via alpha matting in atlas space, making the edit independent of camera motion.

## Results

On the Waymo Open Dataset (25 driving subsequences), NAGs achieve a mean PSNR of 41.85, outperforming OmniRe (36.78) by 5.07 dB and EmerNeRF (34.93) by 6.92 dB in overall scene quality. For dynamic objects in isolation, the gains are even larger: 42.88 dB PSNR for vehicles and 42.94 dB for humans, representing 11.2 dB and 10.7 dB improvements over the best baseline (OmniRe) respectively. On the DAVIS outdoor video benchmark (15 sequences), NAGs achieve 35.35 dB mean PSNR, surpassing the best baseline (Layered Neural Atlases at 28.09 dB) by 7.26 dB, confirming generalization beyond driving scenes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NAGs consistently outperform all four baselines across both evaluation settings. On Waymo driving scenes, OmniRe (object-centric 3DGS with SMPL human modeling) is beaten by 5.07 dB PSNR overall and by 11.2 dB for vehicles / 10.7 dB for humans, with OmniRe exhibiting visible artifacts under rapid motion such as missing object edges and smoothed cyclist feet. EmerNeRF (static + dynamic radiance fields) trails by 6.92 dB overall PSNR, tending to over-smooth objects due to under-parameterization. On the DAVIS outdoor benchmark, both matting-based baselines are surpassed: OmnimatteRF (3D background modeling) is outperformed by 8.04 dB PSNR, and Layered Neural Atlases by 7.26 dB, with the largest margins appearing in sequences with rigid motion (e.g., kite-surf: 37.96 vs. 29.58 dB) while NAGs still lead in challenging non-rigid cases (e.g., bear: 33.47 vs. 26.51 dB). The only metric where a baseline ties or slightly edges NAGs is SSIM on sequence s-952 (OmniRe 0.977 vs. NAG 0.976) and LPIPS on the same sequence (OmniRe 0.050 vs. NAG 0.058), but NAGs dominate on all other sequences and all aggregate means.

## Relation to Prior Work

NAGs sit at the intersection of two established lines of work: neural scene graphs (Ost et al., 2021), which decompose dynamic scenes into per-object 3D nodes for repositioning, and layered neural atlases (Kasten et al., 2021), which project scene content onto 2D editable canvases for appearance manipulation. The key departure is making each scene-graph node a view-dependent neural atlas rather than an implicit volumetric radiance field or Gaussian collection, thereby inheriting the 3D ordering and trajectory control of graph-based methods while gaining the direct 2D texture editability of atlas-based methods. The per-node optical flow field draws on the spline-based flow model from Chugunov et al. (2024), and the overall compositing pipeline (depth-ordered ray-casting with alpha blending) follows standard layered rendering.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is most immediate for autonomous driving simulation: NAGs enable photorealistic counterfactual scenario generation (removing vehicles, repainting roads, adding crosswalks) at test time without retraining, which is a direct path to augmenting limited driving datasets. The plane-per-object assumption, however, caps the method at sub-180° camera/object rotation and precludes novel view synthesis, and the reliance on accurate 3D bounding boxes and masks for initialization limits robustness in unconstrained settings. Open questions include whether the view-dependent neural field component can be made more editable (it currently trades editability for fidelity), how the method scales to very long sequences or large camera sweeps, and whether task-driven differentiable editing (e.g., adversarial scenario generation for driving stacks) can be realized end-to-end.
