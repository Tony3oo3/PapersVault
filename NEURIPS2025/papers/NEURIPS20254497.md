## Problem

In standard quantum state tomography, pure states are substantially easier to learn than mixed states, with sample complexity scaling as Õ(N) versus Õ(N³). A natural question is whether this separation persists in the online learning setting, where an adversary adaptively chooses measurements. This paper shows the answer is no: pure and mixed states share the same sequential fat-shattering dimension and hence the same minimax regret scaling of Θ(√(nT)), a result that is surprising given the large gap in the i.i.d. setting.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254497/cropped_page5_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At each round t of a T-round game, the adversary selects a two-outcome measurement E_t (an N×N Hermitian matrix with eigenvalues in [0,1]) and the learner must output a hypothesis state ω_t, incurring loss |Tr(E_t ω_t) − Tr(E_t ρ)|. In the ε-realizable extension, the learner receives noisy feedback y_t drawn from a distribution centered at Tr(E_t ρ) with error ε, rather than the exact value. In the smoothed extension, the adversary is restricted to σ-smooth distributions relative to a fixed target distribution D, interpolating between fully adversarial (σ→0) and i.i.d. (σ=1) regimes.

## Method

The core technique constructs explicit binary trees (Halving, Von Neumann, and Von Neumann Halving trees) whose paths are associated with valid quantum states, thereby lower-bounding the sequential fat-shattering dimension. For pure states, the authors explicitly construct the rank-1 states |ψ(ε)⟩ for each path, showing the rank-1 constraint does not reduce the dimension below Ω(n/δ^η) for η<2. For general mixed states, they use a matrix completion lemma (via chordal graph theory and Grone et al.) to guarantee that partial matrices defined on the tree paths can be completed to valid density matrices. The regret bounds follow by plugging the fat-shattering dimension into the Rakhlin et al. framework, and the smoothed setting is handled via a coupling argument that relates the distribution-dependent sequential Rademacher complexity to the standard i.i.d. Rademacher complexity, yielding V_T = O(√(nT log T / σ)).

## Results

The central result is that pure and mixed n-qubit states share the same sequential fat-shattering dimension of order Ω(n/δ^η) for all η < 2 (Theorems 3.6 and 4.1), yielding identical minimax regret of Θ(√(nT)) under L₁ loss (Corollary 4.2). This equivalence also holds for L₂ loss when T ≤ 4ⁿ. In the ε-realizable setting, the regret remains Θ(√(nT)) independent of the noise level ε (Theorem 5.1), and in the smoothed setting with smoothness parameter σ, the regret upper bound is O(√(nT log T / σ)) (Theorem 5.3).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between pure and mixed quantum states in the online learning setting. In standard (i.i.d.) tomography, pure states require only Θ(N) copies versus Θ(N³) for mixed states — an exponential gap. However, this paper shows that in the fully adversarial online setting, both classes incur the same Θ(√(nT)) regret, collapsing the separation entirely. This contrasts sharply with the bandit-feedback setting of Lumbreras et al. [2022, 2024], where mixed states suffer Θ(√T) regret while pure states achieve only Θ(polylog T) — an exponential separation that does not survive in the general online framework. Compared to Aaronson et al. [2018], who established tight bounds for mixed states using a different proof strategy, this paper provides a unified tree-construction framework (Halving Tree, Von Neumann Tree, Von Neumann Halving Tree) that naturally extends to restricted hypothesis classes and sample spaces, and introduces a novel matrix-completion technique (Lemma 3.5) to handle general measurements beyond projective ones.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Aaronson et al. [2018], which established tight sequential fat-shattering dimension bounds Θ(n/δ²) and regret Θ(√(nT)) for general mixed quantum states in the fully adversarial online setting. The authors introduce a distinct proof strategy based on explicit tree constructions (Halving tree, Von Neumann tree, and their combination) rather than the approach in Aaronson et al., enabling natural extension to restricted hypothesis classes (pure states) and new subsettings (ε-realizable feedback, smoothed adversariality). The smoothed analysis framework is adapted from Haghtalab et al. [2020, 2024] and Block et al. [2022], who developed it for classical online learning, and the matrix completion machinery draws on Grone et al. [1984] via chordal graph theory. The result stands in contrast to Lumbreras et al. [2022, 2024], who showed an exponential pure-vs-mixed separation under bandit feedback with adaptive measurements, highlighting that the setting (fully adversarial vs. bandit) critically determines whether a separation exists.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that the rank-1 restriction inherent to pure states provides no advantage in the fully adversarial online setting, yielding identical Θ(√(nT)) regret as mixed states—challenges the natural expectation that the well-known sample-complexity separation in standard tomography (Θ(N) vs. Θ(N³)) should persist in sequential learning. This has practical implications for quantum device certification and property estimation, where practitioners may have assumed pure-state learning would be fundamentally easier under adversarial measurement sequences. Key open questions include whether the L₂-loss lower bound is tight, whether the smoothed-analysis upper bound O(√(nT log T / σ)) is optimal, and whether the offline fat-shattering dimension of pure states achieves the full Ω(n/δ²) scaling. The paper also raises an intriguing connection to classical learning theory: the vanishing of rank-dependence in sequential complexity for PSD Hermitian trace-1 matrices contrasts sharply with classical results where rank governs VC dimension, pseudodimension, and fat-shattering dimension of matrix classes.
