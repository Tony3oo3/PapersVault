## Problem

While Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) have become a standard tool for interpreting LLM internals, their application to Vision-Language Models lacks a quantitative, neuron-level metric for evaluating monosemanticity. Without such a metric, it is impossible to systematically determine whether individual SAE neurons in vision encoders truly encode single, interpretable concepts or remain polysemantic, hindering both interpretability and targeted model steering.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253202/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes activation vectors from pretrained VLM layers (CLIP ViT-L, SigLIP, AIMv2, WebSSL) extracted on ImageNet images, and produces a per-neuron MonoSemanticity (MS) score in [-1, 1] computed via activation-weighted pairwise cosine similarity of DINOv2 or CLIP image embeddings. For steering, the SAE operates on individual token embeddings (d-dimensional vectors) from CLIP's vision encoder at layer 22; a single scalar intervention value α is applied to one neuron's activation across all tokens, and the SAE decoder reconstructs modified token embeddings that are fed to the LLM (Vicuna/LLaMA) to generate steered text responses.

## Method

The paper introduces the MonoSemanticity score (MS), which for each neuron computes the average pairwise embedding similarity across a diverse image set, weighted by the product of min-max-normalized activations for each image pair, thereby measuring how semantically coherent the images are that strongly fire that neuron. SAEs (BatchTopK and Matryoshka BatchTopK variants) are trained post-hoc on frozen VLM layer activations using a reconstruction loss plus sparsity penalty, with the Matryoshka variant adding nested reconstruction objectives at multiple latent widths to encourage hierarchical concept separation. The key finding is that even at expansion factor ε=1 (same width as the original layer), the sparse dictionary learning objective inherently disentangles concepts, and wider latents and stricter sparsity (lower K) further improve MS. For steering MLLMs, the trained SAE is inserted after CLIP's vision encoder in LLaVA; clamping a monosemantic neuron's activation to a positive or negative α inserts or suppresses the corresponding concept in the generated text without modifying any LLM parameters. A 1000-question Mechanical Turk user study validates that MS aligns with human monosemanticity judgments at 82.8% overall, rising to 100% when the MS difference between neuron pairs exceeds 0.8.

## Results

SAE neurons achieve MonoSemanticity (MS) scores up to 1.00, compared to 0.01 for original VLM neurons, with Matryoshka BatchTopK SAE (ε=4) reaching 0.95–1.00 across CLIP layers. In the steering evaluation on LLaVA-1.5-7B, SAE-based intervention achieves 42.4% success on concept insertion (both criteria) and 52.5% on concept suppression (both criteria), outperforming the DiffMean baseline (35.8% and 33.3% respectively). A large-scale user study (1,000 questions, 71 annotators) confirms 82.8% alignment between the MS score and human judgment of neuron monosemanticity, rising to 100% when the MS difference between neuron pairs exceeds 0.8.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DiffMean [2] (the primary steering baseline), SAE-based intervention wins on the combined success metric for both insertion (42.4% vs. 35.8%) and suppression (52.5% vs. 33.3%), with the largest gap in suppression where SAE preserves unrelated content at 81.4% versus only 38.7% for DiffMean. DiffMean slightly edges out SAE on raw concept insertion rate (53.1% vs. 48.7%), but SAE maintains far stronger adherence to the base prompt (85.8% vs. 66.2%), making it more reliable in practice. Among SAE variants, Matryoshka BatchTopK consistently outperforms standard BatchTopK on MS scores (e.g., 0.84 vs. 0.61 at ε=1, layer 11; 0.95 vs. 0.71 at ε=4, layer 11), though at a modest cost in reconstruction R² (2–3 points lower). The 'No SAE' baseline (original VLM neurons) scores a near-uniform 0.01 MS across all layers and models, confirming that the sparse decomposition objective itself—regardless of expansion factor—drives the monosemanticity gain.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the SAE-based interpretability pipeline established for LLMs (Bricken et al. 2023; Gao et al. 2025) to vision-language models, specifically CLIP and SigLIP vision encoders. It builds directly on BatchTopK (Bussmann et al. 2024) and Matryoshka SAE variants (Bussmann et al. 2024; Nabeshima 2024) as the core architectures, but departs from prior VLM-SAE work (which focused on interpretable classification or cross-model concept discovery) by introducing the first quantitative, neuron-level monosemanticity metric for visual representations, validated against a large-scale human study. The steering component extends activation-steering methods like DiffMean (Arditi et al. 2024) by operating in the SAE latent space of the vision encoder rather than in the LLM's residual stream.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The MonoSemanticity score and its accompanying 1,000-pair human benchmark provide the field with a grounded, reproducible way to evaluate SAE quality in vision models, filling a gap that has limited practical adoption of SAEs for VLM interpretability. The demonstration that intervening on a single SAE neuron in CLIP's vision encoder can steer LLaVA outputs—without touching the LLM—offers a lightweight, unsupervised control mechanism for multimodal systems. Key limitations include that high-MS neurons do not always yield precise steering effects (e.g., a 'golden retriever' neuron triggers any dog-related output), and a fraction of SAE neurons act as feature detectors with no steering effect. Open questions include adapting the metric to text representations and understanding the interplay between low-level and high-level concepts in the learned hierarchy.
