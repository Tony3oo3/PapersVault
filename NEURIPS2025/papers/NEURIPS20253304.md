## Problem

Existing structural causal bandit (SCM-MAB) frameworks assume the agent has access to a fully-specified causal diagram to prune irrelevant actions before interaction. In practice, observational data only yields a Markov equivalence class of the true causal structure, represented as a partial ancestral graph (PAG) with uncertain edge orientations. This paper extends SCM-MAB to the setting where the agent only knows the PAG, addressing the challenge that a PAG encodes a super-exponential number of maximal ancestral graphs (MAGs), each representing infinitely many causal diagrams.

## Input / Output

The method takes a PAG over a set of variables V (with directed, bidirected, and circle-marked edges encoding orientation uncertainty), a designated reward variable Y, and a candidate intervention set X ⊆ V \ {Y}. It produces the set of definitely minimal intervention sets (DMISs) and possibly-optimal minimal intervention sets (POMISs), which together define the pruned action space the bandit agent should explore. A key subtlety is that the method must certify properties that hold in at least one conforming MAG without enumerating the equivalence class, and it distinguishes between MIS (which can be vacuous for PAGs) and DMIS (which guarantees a consistent underlying SCM).

## Method

The paper introduces three key theoretical contributions. First, it identifies a 'vacuousness' problem: a set can satisfy the MIS graphical criterion for a PAG (existence of proper possibly-directed paths) yet fail to be an MIS in every conforming MAG, because the circle-mark uncertainty prevents all variables in the set from simultaneously being ancestors of Y. To fix this, it defines the definitely minimal intervention set (DMIS), characterized by the existence of uncovered proper possibly-directed paths whose starting edges are not 'relevant' (i.e., not connected via an uncovered path of definite non-colliders). Second, it generalizes the MUCT and IB constructs from causal diagrams to PMGs by replacing c-components with pc-components (paths of invisible colliding edges) and using possible ancestral/descendant relations. Third, it characterizes POMIS for PAGs via local transformations: rather than enumerating all MAGs, it recursively orients circle marks only around X∪{Y} using validity criteria from Wang et al. [2023b], then checks whether IB equals X in the resulting oriented PMG. The IsPOMIS algorithm exploits this locality, achieving polynomial per-vertex complexity instead of the super-exponential cost of full MAG enumeration.

## Results

Across three experimental tasks with increasing graph complexity (5, 6, and 10 variables), POMIS-based strategies dramatically reduce cumulative regret compared to brute-force (BF) exploration. In Task 3 (10 variables), POMIS reduces the action space from 19,683 arms to just 54 arms (0.27%), achieving cumulative regrets of 60.3 (TS) and 52.0 (KL-UCB), corresponding to only 2.5% and 2.1% of BF regret, respectively. Even in the smaller Task 1, POMIS-based TS achieves 39.3% of BF cumulative regret while exploring only 19 of 81 arms. The superiority of POMIS over both BF and DMIS is consistent regardless of the bandit solver used (Thompson Sampling or KL-UCB).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three arm-selection strategies—POMIS, DMIS, and Brute-force (BF, no causal knowledge)—each paired with Thompson Sampling (TS) and KL-UCB solvers. POMIS consistently outperforms both BF and DMIS across all three tasks. In Task 3 (10 variables), POMIS achieves cumulative regret of 60.3 (TS) and 52.0 (KL-UCB), representing only 2.5% and 2.1% of BF regret, while exploring just 54 arms versus 19,683 for BF and 2,025 for DMIS. The gap between POMIS and DMIS is particularly striking in Task 3, where POMIS prunes the action space by another 97% relative to DMIS. The paper notes that the superiority of POMIS remains consistent regardless of the solver, indicating the gains come from the structural pruning rather than the specific bandit algorithm. No direct comparison with prior causal bandit methods (e.g., Elahi et al. 2024a) is provided in the main experiments, though a qualitative comparison is discussed in Appendix E.3.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Structural Causal Bandit (SCM-MAB) framework of Lee and Bareinboim [2018], which characterized minimal intervention sets (MISs) and possibly-optimal MISs (POMISs) under full knowledge of a causal diagram, to the more realistic setting where only a Markov equivalence class—represented by a partial ancestral graph (PAG)—is available. The technical machinery draws on Jaber et al.'s [2022] do-calculus for PAGs and Wang et al.'s [2023b] local transformation validity criteria, which the authors adapt to recursively orient circle marks around the intervention set and reward variable. A key departure from prior work is the introduction of the 'definitely MIS' (DMIS) concept, which addresses a vacuousness problem the authors identify: a set can satisfy the MIS criterion for a PAG yet fail to be an MIS for every conforming MAG, necessitating a stricter notion before POMIS can be meaningfully defined.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

In practice, agents can only learn a PAG from observational data, making the full-causal-diagram assumption of prior SCM-MAB work unrealistic; this paper provides the first complete graphical characterization and efficient algorithm for pruning the action space under Markov equivalence, reducing the search from super-exponential MAG enumeration to local transformations around X∪{Y}. The vacuousness result—that a PAG-level MIS may contain variables irrelevant to the reward in all conforming causal structures—is a conceptual finding that cautions against naively extending causal-diagram criteria to equivalence classes. Open questions include whether the approach extends to stochastic policies, non-binary variables, or settings where the PAG itself is estimated with uncertainty, and whether the exponential enumeration of all POMISs can be further pruned for very large graphs.
