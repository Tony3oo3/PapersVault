## Problem

As LLMs rapidly saturate reasoning benchmarks through techniques like RLVR, it is assumed that improved internal reasoning automatically yields better knowledge transfer to human collaborators. No prior work has systematically isolated and quantified this transfer, leaving open whether knowledge transfer emerges for free or requires dedicated evaluation and optimization, especially across varying user expertise levels and task domains.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253512/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes competitive programming problems (LiveCodeBench) and competition math problems (AMC/AIME) paired with human participants and one of eight LLMs. The two-phase protocol imposes strict constraints: during collaborative ideation, models are forbidden from generating code, pseudocode, or calculations (enforced by a secondary rule-compliance LLM), and participants cannot take notes; during independent solving, all model access and conversation history are removed. Outputs include collaborative solve rates compared against model solo performance, Bradley-Terry win rates stratified by three skill-hierarchy configurations (HTM, HMT, MHT), Elo-calibrated difficulty bands, and full interaction transcripts for qualitative clustering analysis.

## Method

KITE introduces a two-phase protocol that isolates knowledge transfer by separating ideation from implementation: in Phase 1, participants discuss strategies with an LLM (no code/calculations permitted), and in Phase 2, they solve the same problem independently with no model access, so success requires genuine internalization rather than memorization. Skill hierarchies are calibrated via Elo ratings for humans (estimated through adaptive 5-task calibration), models (zero-shot solve rates), and tasks (public LeetCode/competition ratings), with each task drawn from a difficulty band slightly above the participant's current rating to ensure meaningful collaboration. Human preferences are quantified using the Bradley-Terry model applied to post-task rankings of the last four models, with win rates reported separately for each skill-hierarchy configuration. Qualitative analysis embeds user queries with text-embedding-3-large and clusters them via k-means to identify interaction patterns (overreliance, representation misalignment, adaptive scaffolding) correlated with success or failure. Eight LLMs are evaluated in a zero-shot setting (temperature 0.7) across 118 participants and 578 trajectories, with monetary incentives and intrinsic motivation (interview preparation) to ensure genuine engagement.

## Results

Across 578 problem-solving trajectories (N=118 participants, 8 LLMs), model solo performance correlates with collaborative outcomes (r=0.84 for code, r=0.69 for math), but the relationship is notably inconsistent with significant outliers. The slope of the performance-transfer relationship is consistently below unity, suggesting diminishing returns in knowledge transfer as model reasoning scales. Key outliers include Gemini-2.5-Pro (81.3% solo code, but −10.0% collaborative delta) and Claude-3.7-Sonnet (45.0% solo code, +25.0% collaborative delta), while GPT-4o showed the largest math improvement (+48.4%) despite only 8.3% solo accuracy. Bradley-Terry win rates reveal domain-specific preference patterns: positive correlation with solo performance in code (r=0.73) but slight negative correlation in math (r=−0.14).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new method to compare against baselines; rather, it evaluates 8 LLMs as subjects of a knowledge-transfer study, with each model's solo performance serving as its own baseline. The central comparison is between solo accuracy (M) and human-AI collaborative accuracy (H+M), where a positive delta indicates effective knowledge transfer. Claude-3.7-Sonnet achieves the largest math transfer gain (+60.9%, from 20.8% to 81.7%), while GPT-4o shows the largest relative math improvement (+48.4%) from a very low solo baseline (8.3%). In code, Gemini-2.5-Pro is the notable underperformer in transfer (−10.0% delta despite 81.3% solo), suggesting its high solo capability does not translate into effective human-facing explanations. o1 shows minimal transfer in both domains (code: +0.0%, math: +2.5%), consistent with its highly formal, proof-based explanation style that users found difficult to follow. The overall pattern—below-unity slope between solo and collaborative performance—indicates that no model currently achieves proportional knowledge transfer scaling with its reasoning capability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by explicitly isolating and quantifying knowledge transfer from AI to humans, a quantity that prior human-AI collaboration work (e.g., [34, 33, 38]) has only measured implicitly through joint task performance. It repurposes standard tools—Bradley-Terry preference modeling, Elo-based skill calibration, and k-means clustering of interaction transcripts—within a novel two-phase protocol (collaborative ideation followed by independent implementation) that forces genuine understanding rather than rote recall. Conceptually, it draws on collective intelligence frameworks [10, 42, 28] to formalize knowledge regions (M, H, M∩H, M−H, H−M) and projection operators, but the experimental instantiation is entirely new. Unlike LLM-as-tutor work [39, 50, 13], which evaluates pedagogical quality in open-ended settings, KITE requires participants to independently execute solutions, making transfer measurable through objective correctness.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that the performance-to-transfer slope is consistently below unity, with the top-performing code model (Gemini-2.5-Pro) actually showing negative collaborative change, challenges the field's implicit assumption that more capable models are automatically better knowledge sources. This has direct implications for how AI systems are optimized: if transfer does not emerge for free with reasoning capability, it must be treated as a separate training objective. Open questions include whether the STEM-skewed, two-domain sample generalizes to high-stakes settings (medicine, law), whether multimodal projections (diagrams, interactive tools) can close the transfer gap, and whether dedicated optimization of explanatory behavior can decouple transfer from raw reasoning performance.
