## Problem

Statistical estimators face a fundamental trade-off: semiparametrically efficient estimators achieve the smallest possible variance for a single known target but can be biased under distribution shift, while multiaccurate (robust) estimators control worst-case bias across many targets but are expected to inflate variance. This paper shows that a simple one-step ridge boosting procedure simultaneously achieves both properties, resolving the apparent tension between these two goals.

## Input / Output

The method requires only source-distribution data (paired X, Y observations) plus an arbitrary initial predictor and a kernel/RKHS specification; no target-specific covariates, density ratios, or Riesz representers are needed at training time. At deployment, the single multiaccurate predictor is applied to unlabeled target covariates to produce a point estimate and a valid asymptotic 95% confidence interval for any scalar estimand whose Riesz representer lies within the RKHS unit ball, eliminating the need to fit separate debiased estimators per target.

## Method

The procedure fits an arbitrary initial predictor γ̂_init on source data, then performs a single ridge regression step on the residuals (Y − γ̂_init(X)) in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space, yielding γ̂_ma = γ̂_init + γ̂_boost. The key technical insight (Proposition 2) is that ridge regression in an RKHS is numerically equivalent to Riesz regression: the smoother weights implicitly estimate the Riesz representer α_θ for any target functional θ, so the plug-in estimate θ(γ̂_ma) takes exactly the form of the Automatic Debiased Machine Learning augmented estimator. This means the single boosting step simultaneously enforces multiaccuracy (Theorem 1: worst-case bias bounded by the RKHS ball radius) and achieves the semiparametric efficiency bound for every individual θ in the class (Theorem 2: asymptotic normality with variance V_θ*). The result extends beyond missing means to general linear estimands (average derivatives, impulse responses) by replacing the density ratio with the corresponding Riesz representer, and Appendix B sketches an analogous result for Random Forest boosting via the linear-smoother connection of Lin and Han (2022).

## Results

In the simulation study (3D correlated covariates, average derivative estimand, 1,000 Monte Carlo replications, n = 50–500), the naive kernel ridge plug-in estimator systematically under-covers its nominal 95% confidence intervals across all three distribution shifts (μ ∈ {−1, 0, 1}), while the once-boosted ridge estimator achieves near-nominal coverage by n ≈ 300. In the empirical application on 2018 American Community Survey data, the naive plug-in estimate of the retirement income age profile shows a steep $11k decline from ages 65 to 89, whereas the ridge-boosted estimate is substantially flatter and better matches the theoretical pension/social-security income profile of Kaplan and Violante (2014). The boosted estimator also produces confidence intervals that widen slightly at older ages (where covariate shift is most severe), in contrast to the naive estimator whose intervals shrink—suggesting the naive model under-covers for the oldest ages.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares two estimators trained solely on source-distribution data: (1) Naive Kernel Ridge, a standard kernel ridge outcome regression applied as a plug-in estimator, and (2) Boosted Kernel Ridge, which adds one step of ridge regression on the residuals of the initial model. On the simulation task (average derivative under covariate shift), the boosted estimator clearly wins on the key metric of empirical CI coverage: the naive model under-covers across all three target distributions and all sample sizes, while the boosted model reaches approximately 95% coverage by n = 300. On the retirement income application, the boosted estimator produces a flatter, more theoretically plausible age profile and wider (more honest) confidence intervals at older ages, whereas the naive estimator's shrinking CIs at ages 80+ indicate under-coverage. The paper does not compare against other multiaccuracy or debiased-ML baselines (e.g., full ADML with explicit Riesz regression, TMLE, or balancing weights), so the comparison is limited to the plug-in vs. one-step-boosted contrast.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate literatures: multiaccuracy/multicalibration (Kim et al. 2019, 2022; Hebert-Johnson et al. 2018), which constructs predictors with low worst-case bias across many target estimands, and semiparametric debiased machine learning (Chernozhukov et al. 2018, 2021; Singh 2024), which achieves the efficiency bound for a single known target. The key conceptual link is that kernel ridge regression implicitly estimates the Riesz representer, making one step of ridge boosting numerically equivalent to the Automatic Debiased ML estimator. The paper generalizes Kim et al. [2022] from the missing-mean estimand to arbitrary continuous linear functionals (average derivatives, impulse responses) and adds the efficiency guarantee that was previously absent from the multiaccuracy literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is significant: a single model trained on source data yields valid confidence intervals and semiparametrically efficient point estimates for every target estimand whose Riesz representer lies in the RKHS, eliminating the need to fit separate debiased estimators per target. This is particularly valuable in settings like estimating health outcomes across many hospitals or computing age profiles of economic variables. Key limitations include restriction to RKHS norm balls (excluding, e.g., fine-tuned neural network representations), the need for careful hyperparameter tuning, and the fact that the empirical validation is limited to one simulation setup and a simplified ACS application. Open questions include extending to classification (cf. Long et al. 2025), more general function classes beyond fixed-basis RKHS, and unifying with TMLE and score-preserving TMLE frameworks.
