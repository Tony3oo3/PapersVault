## Problem

ML-based power flow approximators, particularly GNNs, offer speed advantages over Newton-Raphson solvers but have never been systematically evaluated on benchmarks capturing the full range of real-world variability—simultaneous load, generation, and topological perturbations. Existing datasets cover only subsets of these variations, and different papers use incompatible data generation and evaluation protocols, making cross-method comparisons impossible and leaving open whether current approaches are deployable at operational timescales.

## Input / Output

Each instance is a power network graph (IEEE-14 through GOC-2000) where bus-type-specific inputs are provided: PQ buses supply active/reactive power demand, PV buses supply net active power and voltage magnitude, and the slack bus supplies voltage angle and magnitude; the model must predict the complementary unknowns plus all branch flows. Data is delivered as PyTorch HeteroData objects in two formulations—component-level (buses, generators, loads as distinct node types) and PV-PQ-level (buses split by type with auxiliary link edges). Evaluation uses unsupervised power balance mismatch (|ΔS|) rather than supervised loss, avoiding bias toward the particular Ipopt solution stored during data generation.

## Method

The data generation pipeline simultaneously applies three perturbation types to a base-case network: load profiles sampled uniformly from a convex set containing the ACOPF feasible space (via OPF-Learn), N-1/N-2 topological perturbations randomly removing up to two generators or lines, and diverse generator setpoints produced by randomly permuting generator cost parameters before solving a custom ACOPF formulation that removes output-variable limits. Feasibility is verified by solving this custom ACOPF with Ipopt; only converged samples are retained, yielding 859,800 solved instances across six bus sizes. Close-to-infeasible cases near the voltage stability limit are generated via continuation power flow, with additional 'approaching infeasibility' samples for training augmentation. The benchmark defines four standardized task groups (in-distribution generalization, data efficiency, out-of-distribution generalization to different grid sizes, and C2I performance) evaluated with unsupervised power balance loss and runtime metrics, enabling fair comparison across the Newton-Raphson solver and three GNN architectures (CANOS-PF, PowerFlowNet, GraphNeuralSolver).

## Results

On the PFΔ benchmark (IEEE-118 bus system), the Newton-Raphson (NR) solver achieves Power Balance Loss (PBL) on the order of 10⁻⁶, while all three GNN-based methods (CANOS-PF, PowerFlowNet, GNS-S) fail to reach comparable precision. GNN models achieve approximately a 5× runtime speedup over NR, with NR convergence rates dropping from 95.2% (57-bus) to 65.7% (118-bus) to 43.4% (500-bus) from a flat start. CANOS-PF achieves the best average PBL across in-distribution tasks, while GNS-S obtains the lowest close-to-infeasible (C2I)-specific losses, and PowerFlowNet consistently performs worst on C2I cases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates four methods on PFΔ: the traditional Newton-Raphson (NR) solver from PowerModels.jl, and three GNN-based approaches (CANOS-PF, PowerFlowNet/PFNet, and GraphNeuralSolver/GNS-S). NR achieves the highest accuracy (PBL ~10⁻⁶) but is the slowest, with convergence degrading significantly on larger grids (43.4% on 500-bus). Among GNNs, CANOS-PF delivers the most consistent and stable performance across all task groups, achieving the best average PBL in in-distribution tasks and the lowest average error on C2I tasks (4.1–4.2), though it degrades when trained only on base topology. GNS-S shows the highest variance but uniquely maintains strong feasible-case performance even when trained exclusively on near-infeasible samples (Task 4.3), and achieves the lowest C2I-specific losses across all of Task Group 4. PowerFlowNet consistently performs worst on close-to-infeasible cases, highlighting the difficulty of handling near-infeasibility through purely supervised learning. No GNN method matches NR's 10⁻⁶ precision, identifying a key open challenge for the field.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes and extends two prior data generation pipelines — OPF-Learn's convex-set load sampling [9] and OPFData's N-1 topological perturbation scheme [10] — into a unified framework for power flow (as opposed to ACOPF), adding a novel generator cost perturbation mechanism to produce diverse setpoints. It departs from prior GNN power flow solvers (GraphNeuralSolver, PowerFlowNet, CANOS, TypedGNN), each of which was trained and evaluated on custom datasets capturing only a subset of real-world variations (e.g., no N-2 perturbations, no close-to-infeasible cases, or no generator diversity). The paper also distinguishes itself from large-scale ACOPF benchmarks (OPFData, OPF-Learn, PowerGraph) by tailoring the data generation to the power flow problem's wider range of feasible operating points and by including continuation-power-flow-derived samples near the voltage stability boundary.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PFΔ provides the first standardized benchmark that simultaneously captures load, generation, and topology variations for power flow, addressing a key reproducibility gap where prior GNN power flow papers used incompatible custom datasets. The systematic evaluation reveals that no current GNN method matches Newton-Raphson accuracy (10⁻⁶ PBL), that OOD generalization across grid sizes remains unsolved, and that close-to-infeasible cases near voltage collapse are a persistent failure mode — collectively defining a clear research agenda. A notable practical finding is that training on N-1 perturbations substantially improves N-2 generalization across all architectures, and that GNS-S's physics-informed loss helps it retain feasible-case accuracy even when trained exclusively on near-infeasible samples. Open questions include whether any architecture can close the accuracy gap to NR at scale (>1000 buses), how to handle N-k contingencies for k>2, and how to generate sufficiently diverse data for very large networks.
