## Problem

While information bottleneck and slow feature analysis frameworks describe what sensory neurons should compute (maximally predictive low-dimensional codes), they do not explain how individual neurons implement these computations biologically, nor do they account for the ubiquity of response rectification (ON/OFF segregation) or the functional dichotomy between predictive and retrospective neuron classes. This paper asks whether a single mechanistic principle—detection of coherent sets via spectral clustering of the stochastic Koopman operator—can simultaneously explain temporal receptive fields, rectification, and the predictive/retrospective split in early sensory circuits.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a scalar sensory time series (e.g., luminance from a photoreceptor or total synaptic current) embedded into a d-dimensional lag-vector, from which past-future data pairs are constructed for CCA. The output is a coherent-set membership index computed as the sign (or rectified magnitude) of a linear projection of the lag-vector onto a learned singular vector, yielding a binary ON/OFF response. The learned singular vector itself constitutes the temporal receptive field, and its alignment with expanding versus contracting exponentials classifies the neuron as predictive or retrospective.

## Method

The paper models sensory streams as observations from high-dimensional stochastic dynamical systems and identifies coherent sets via spectral clustering of the stochastic Koopman operator (SKO), where the sign of the subdominant singular function partitions state space into minimally coupled regions. For multivariate Ornstein-Uhlenbeck dynamics, the authors prove that this singular function reduces to a linear projection onto the dominant singular vector of the whitened finite-time transition matrix, so a neuron storing that vector in its synaptic weights and temporal filter computes the membership index via the sign of a weighted sum. Because neurons lack access to explicit dynamical equations, the singular vectors are estimated data-drivenly via past-future canonical correlation analysis on lag-vector representations, which naturally extends to nonlinear dynamics through Galerkin projection. The framework yields a functional dichotomy: projecting onto the right singular vector (predictive coherent set, common futures) versus the left singular vector (retrospective coherent set, common pasts) requires distinct synaptic weights and temporal filters, mandating separate neurons and explaining known dichotomies such as tufted vs. mitral cells and lagged vs. non-lagged LGN cells.

## Results

Applying past-future CCA to a 'dead leaves' natural image time series, the first right singular vector yields a low-pass temporal filter matching sustained bipolar cells, while the second acts as a smoothed temporal derivative matching transient bipolars. Analysis of 204 rat olfactory bulb tufted/mitral cells (Gupta et al. 2015) reveals ~50% with temporal filters orthogonal to decaying exponentials (predictive), ~4% orthogonal to growing exponentials (retrospective), and ~1/3 orthogonal to both (suggesting >2D dynamics). In a dataset of 1345 dissociated retinal ganglion cells (Kastner & Baccus 2011), the vast majority are predictive (orthogonal to decaying, aligned with expanding exponentials), with only ~1% classified as retrospective. The theoretical result for OU processes establishes that subdominant SKO singular functions reduce to linear projections onto singular vectors of the whitened finite-time transition matrix, with predictive neurons projecting onto right singular vectors and retrospective neurons onto left singular vectors.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare its method against competing algorithms or baselines in the standard ML sense; it is a theoretical framework validated against existing neurophysiological recordings. The 'comparison' is between the coherent-set-derived temporal filters (singular vectors from past-future CCA) and experimentally measured receptive fields of known neuronal types. The framework qualitatively reproduces the sustained/transient bipolar cell dichotomy from the dead leaves model, and the cosine-similarity analysis of exponentials recovers the known predictive/retrospective dichotomy in olfactory bulb tufted vs. mitral cells and in LGN non-lagged vs. lagged cells. The paper positions itself as a generalization of the information bottleneck framework [1,23] to unstable dynamics and as an extension of slow feature analysis [24,25], but does not run head-to-head quantitative benchmarks against these prior approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies three previously separate research threads: (1) the information bottleneck framework for neural coding, which for Gaussian stable systems reduces to past-future CCA and one-bit encoding via the subdominant singular function; (2) coherent set detection in dynamical systems via spectral clustering of the stochastic Koopman operator (Froyland 2013); and (3) data-driven Koopman estimation through Galerkin projection and CCA (Koltai et al. 2018, VAMP). The key departure is extending the IB/CCA result from stable to unstable (saddle-point) dynamics and providing a closed-form identification of the relevant singular functions as projections onto singular vectors of the whitened transition matrix for OU processes. Unlike prior predictive coding or lattice filter models that were restricted to linear processing or required explicit nonlinearities, this framework derives rectification and the predictive/retrospective dichotomy as natural consequences of the sign structure of left versus right singular functions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a single mathematical object (the subdominant SKO singular function) that simultaneously accounts for temporal receptive fields, ON/OFF rectification, and the predictive/retrospective neuronal dichotomy observed across olfactory bulb, retina, and LGN. Several open issues limit immediate applicability: the spectral clustering formalism under instability requires a carefully chosen reference measure (finite-time windowing or Doob transforms), data-driven estimation along unstable directions is not yet rigorously established, and the 2D saddle correspondence may not extend to higher dimensions. The paper also does not incorporate feedback loops or intra-neuronal plasticity, both of which are ubiquitous in real circuits. If the framework matures, it could provide a principled, biologically grounded template for self-supervised architectures that learn temporal structure without backpropagation.
