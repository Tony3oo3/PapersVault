## Problem

Existing RAG poisoning attacks (e.g., PoisonedRAG, BadRAG) rely on pre-defined misinformation templates or manually crafted adversarial passages, ignoring the LLM's internal representation dynamics and neuron-level sensitivities. The underlying mechanism by which external knowledge overrides parametric memory remains unexplored, and the effect of knowledge conflict—where the model has strong internal memory of the correct answer—has not been systematically addressed in attack design.

## Input / Output

The method takes a factual query q, an adversary-specified target answer â, and white-box inference-time access to intermediate neuron activations (specifically Integrated Gradients attribution scores) as input. It produces a population of adversarial external knowledge passages per generation, evaluated by the Population Overwrite Success Rate (POSR)—the fraction of candidates in the final generation that successfully override the model's answer. Unlike single-passage attacks, the output is a scalable pool of effective poisoned contexts, and the optimization signal is neuron-level attribution rather than output-level behavior, requiring the attacker to compute IG scores across multiple query-context pairs to identify the global Poison-Responsive Neuron set.

## Method

NeuroGenPoisoning first identifies a global set of top-r Poison-Responsive Neurons by computing Integrated Gradients attribution for each neuron across multiple seed query-context pairs and selecting those most frequently in the top-k attribution ranks. It then initializes a population of adversarial passages via GPT-4 (mimicking realistic sources while embedding the target answer) and evolves them through a genetic algorithm whose fitness function is the average Poison-Responsiveness Score over the selected neurons. A key differentiator is that the method distinguishes promising from non-promising failures by analyzing neuron activation patterns, enabling selective recombination of initially unsuccessful but internally promising contexts to scale generation of effective poisoned knowledge. For knowledge conflict scenarios, the framework detects strong internal memory via low neuron responsiveness and adapts the genetic optimization to progressively concentrate adversarial pressure on conflict-resistant neurons, a mechanism theoretically justified by showing that increasing Poison-Responsive Score monotonically shifts output probability mass toward the adversarial answer.

## Results

NeuroGenPoisoning achieves a Final Population Overwrite Success Rate (POSR) of 88–95% across four LLMs (LLaMA-2-7B, Vicuna-7B, Vicuna-13B, Gemma-7B) and three open-domain QA datasets (SQuAD 2.0, TriviaQA, WikiQA), up from an initial POSR of 41–48% before genetic optimization. The method also preserves fluency, with a Relative PPL Drop of only 2.7–7.7% between initial and final generations. On SQuAD 2.0 with LLaMA-2-7B-chat-hf, the headline result is a Final POSR of 0.94 versus an initial 0.41, representing a more than 2× improvement from neuron-guided genetic evolution over 10 generations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole quantitative baseline is PoisonedRAG [Zou et al., USENIX Security 2025], which optimizes adversarial documents for retrievability and generation likelihood without any neuron-level feedback. NeuroGenPoisoning decisively outperforms PoisonedRAG on every dataset–model combination: on SQuAD 2.0, Final POSR ranges from 0.88 to 0.95 versus PoisonedRAG's 0.49–0.58 (a 30–46 percentage-point gap); on TriviaQA, 0.89–0.92 versus 0.52–0.61; and on WikiQA, 0.89–0.95 versus 0.56–0.62. The advantage is most pronounced on LLaMA-2-7B (e.g., 0.94 vs. 0.51 on SQuAD 2.0) and least on Gemma-7B for SQuAD 2.0 (0.88 vs. 0.49), though the gap remains substantial in all cases. In a controlled comparison using identical initial templates (Table 2), both methods reach comparable single-passage Attack Success Rate (~0.94–0.98), but NeuroGenPoisoning additionally produces a large population of high-fitness adversarial passages per query, a capability PoisonedRAG does not offer.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the neuron-identification machinery from IRCAN (which was designed to *defend* against knowledge conflicts by reweighting context-aware neurons) and flips it into an offensive tool: the same context-sensitive neurons become optimization targets for crafting adversarial RAG contexts. It departs from prior RAG poisoning attacks (PoisonedRAG, BadRAG, Pandora, RAG-Thief) that rely on template-based or retrieval-likelihood optimization by introducing Integrated Gradients attribution over a fixed set of Poison-Responsive Neurons as the fitness signal for a genetic algorithm. The genetic-optimization lineage connects to AutoDAN and GCG, but those methods optimize at the output or token level, whereas NeuroGenPoisoning optimizes at the internal-representation level, a distinction the authors argue is critical for resolving knowledge conflicts that surface-level methods cannot overcome.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The demonstration that a small set of ~10 neurons can be targeted to push Population Overwrite Success Rate from ~45% to >90% across four architectures and three datasets raises concrete concerns for deployed RAG systems, especially the ability to generate thousands of effective poisoned passages per query at scale. The knowledge-conflict analysis—showing that queries with strong parametric memory (initially 0% POSR) can be gradually overridden through neuron-targeted evolution—suggests that model 'confidence' in memorized facts is not a reliable defense. The primary limitation is the white-box assumption: the method requires access to intermediate neuron activations and Integrated Gradients, which is unrealistic for most deployed LLMs. Extending the approach to black-box settings via surrogate models or activation proxies is identified as the key open question, and the practical threat model would shift substantially if that extension succeeds.
