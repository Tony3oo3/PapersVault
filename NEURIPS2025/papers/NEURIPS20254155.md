## Problem

Existing streaming submodular maximization algorithms are designed to produce a single solution at the end of a stream, making them unsuitable for settings where a system must repeatedly and immediately respond to on-demand user requests throughout the stream. This paper introduces the S3MOR problem, where a news-recommendation system must present up to k items at each of T unknown user visits while items arrive in a single pass, with irrevocable decisions and limited memory. No prior algorithm addresses this combination of stochastic objectives, multiple on-demand outputs, unknown visit count, and sublinear memory.

## Input / Output

The system consumes a single-pass stream of triples (V_i, p_i, τ_i), where V_i is a topic set, p_i ∈ [0,1] is the click probability, and τ_i ∈ {0,1} signals a user visit. At each visit (τ_i = 1), the system must immediately output a set of at most k items from all previously seen items; the same item may be presented at multiple visits but each copy is treated as distinct. The objective is the expected number of unique topics covered across all visits, which is a non-decreasing submodular function over the multiset of presented item copies. Only an upper bound T' on the true visit count T is available, and per-user memory must be independent of stream length N.

## Method

The paper reduces S3MOR to streaming submodular maximization under a partition matroid constraint (one partition per visit, capacity k each), enabling adaptation of existing streaming algorithms. LMGREEDY stores the full stream and applies local greedy selection per visit, achieving the tight 1/2 competitive ratio but requiring Θ(N+kT) memory. STORM adapts Chekuri et al.'s thresholding-based streaming algorithm for p-matchoids: it maintains T' candidate sets, uses a 2× marginal-gain threshold to decide item replacement, and greedily selects the best active set at each visit, achieving a 1/(4(T'−T+1)) competitive ratio with O(T'k) memory. STORM++ runs ⌈T'/δ⌉ parallel copies of STORM with discretized guesses of T (multiples of δ), then at each visit greedily picks the output with the highest marginal gain among all copies, yielding a 1/(8δ) competitive ratio at the cost of O(T'²k/δ) space and O(NT'²k/δ) time.

## Results

On the Amazon dataset (k=10, T=10, T'=20, δ=10), STORM(T') runs in 7.94 s using 1.28 MB of per-user memory, compared to 307.43 s / 0.91 MB for SIEVE++ and 136.04 s / 0.03 MB for PREEMPTION, while LMGREEDY (the upper-bound baseline with linear memory) takes 88.13 s and 28.99 MB. In terms of expected topic coverage, LMGREEDY is best across all six datasets, followed by STORM(T), STORM++, and STORM(T'), with the proposed memory-efficient methods generally outperforming both SIEVE++ and PREEMPTION (with the exception of RCV1, where PREEMPTION is comparable to STORM(T')). STORM++ achieves a competitive ratio of 1/(8δ) with space O(T'²k/δ), independent of stream length N, while STORM(T') achieves 1/(4(T'−T+1)) with space O(T'k).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SIEVE++ [Kazemi et al., ICML 2019], STORM(T') and STORM++ achieve higher expected topic coverage on Yahoo, RCV1, and Amazon across most values of k and T, while running 39× faster on Amazon (7.94 s vs. 307.43 s); SIEVE++ uses slightly less memory (0.91 MB vs. 1.28 MB). Against PREEMPTION [Buchbinder et al., TALG 2019], the proposed methods again achieve higher coverage on most datasets, with the notable exception of RCV1 where PREEMPTION performs comparably to STORM(T'); PREEMPTION uses far less memory (0.03 MB) but is 17× slower than STORM(T') on Amazon (136.04 s vs. 7.94 s). Overall, the paper's memory-efficient algorithms (STORM, STORM++) consistently beat both baselines in coverage quality while offering substantially better runtime, at the cost of modestly higher per-user memory than SIEVE++ and PREEMPTION.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper introduces the S3MOR problem, which departs fundamentally from classical streaming submodular maximization (which produces a single output at stream end) by requiring multiple irrevocable outputs at unpredictable times during the stream. The core algorithmic machinery is adapted from Chekuri et al.'s streaming partition-matroid algorithm [8] for the STORM/STORM++ methods, and from Fisher et al.'s local greedy [12] for the LMGREEDY baseline. The key conceptual departure is the combination of stochastic item utilities (click probabilities), on-demand multi-output requirements, and irrevocability — a setting the authors explicitly note has no prior algorithmic treatment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes a clear hierarchy of trade-offs: full memory yields a tight 1/2 competitive ratio, but sublinear memory forces a degradation to 1/(4(T'−T+1)) or 1/(8δ), quantifying the cost of not knowing the number of user visits. The empirical observation that STORM(T') and STORM++ can outperform STORM(T) despite weaker guarantees suggests the theoretical bounds are conservative, opening the question of whether tighter analyses or better algorithms exist. A key limitation is that the competitive ratio of STORM++ depends on the tunable parameter δ, and the practical utility hinges on how accurately T' can be estimated from historical data — a question left open.
