## Problem

Gradient-based training on analog in-memory computing (AIMC) hardware is affected by asymmetric and non-linear response functions of resistive elements, but prior theoretical analysis was limited to a special case of linear response functions. This paper asks: what is the general impact of arbitrary non-ideal response functions on training dynamics, and can we design an algorithm that provably converges exactly despite them?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253522/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes stochastic training samples (e.g., 32×32 RGB images from CIFAR-10/100 or 28×28 grayscale from MNIST) and operates on neural network weights stored as physical conductance states in resistive crossbar arrays. Weight updates are implemented via discrete electrical pulses whose magnitude is scaled by element-specific response functions q₊(w) and q₋(w), rather than continuous arithmetic. The output is a set of optimized conductance states that converge to a critical point of the training objective, with the key constraint that the symmetric point of the response functions must be aligned to zero for exact convergence.

## Method

The paper first derives a discrete-time dynamics for Analog SGD by decomposing the response functions into a symmetric component F(W) and an asymmetric component G(W), showing that the asymmetric term introduces an implicit penalty that attracts weights toward the physical symmetric point, causing inexact convergence. To fix this, the authors propose Residual Learning, a bilevel optimization framework that maintains a second analog array Pₖ tracking the residual P*(Wₖ) = (W*−Wₖ)/γ, so that the algorithmic stationary point of Pₖ is 0 — which is also the physical symmetric point after zero-shifting. The main array Wₖ is updated by transferring the residual via a hyper-gradient step, and the two arrays are updated alternately. For practical deployment, Residual Learning v2 adds a digital buffer (exponential moving average) to filter IO reading noise and a threshold-based transfer rule that only fires a pulse when the accumulated residual exceeds the response granularity Δw_min, preventing instability from discrete updates.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, Residual Learning v2 (RLv2) achieves 95.12% (ResNet-18), 96.42% (ResNet-34), and 96.56% (ResNet-50) test accuracy, within 0.5% of Digital SGD. On CIFAR-100, RLv2 reaches 79.83%, 82.85%, and 83.90% for ResNet-18/34/50, closing most of the gap to Digital SGD (81.12%, 83.86%, 83.98%). Analog SGD (ASGD) suffers severe degradation, dropping to 68.98% on CIFAR-100 ResNet-18 (12.1% below DSGD). On MNIST, Residual Learning achieves accuracy within <1% of Digital SGD for both FCN and CNN architectures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares Residual Learning (TT/RL) and Residual Learning v2 (RLv2) against Analog SGD (ASGD) and Tiki-Taka v2 (TTv2) on CIFAR-10/100 with ResNet-18/34/50. ASGD suffers the largest degradation, losing 12.1% accuracy on CIFAR-100 ResNet-18 (68.98% vs. 81.12% DSGD) and 10.2% on CIFAR-10 ResNet-18 (84.47% vs. 95.43%). Residual Learning (TT/RL) recovers most of this gap, reaching 76.17% on CIFAR-100 ResNet-18 and 94.81% on CIFAR-10 ResNet-18. RLv2 further outperforms TTv2 on CIFAR-100 (e.g., 79.83% vs. 78.56% for ResNet-18, 83.90% vs. 82.82% for ResNet-50), demonstrating the benefit of the bilevel formulation with non-zero γ. On CIFAR-10, RLv2 and TTv2 are nearly tied (within 0.2%), both approaching DSGD accuracy. Overall, RLv2 is the strongest analog training method, matching or exceeding DSGD on CIFAR-10 ResNet-50 (96.56% vs. 96.57%) and within 1.3% on CIFAR-100 ResNet-18.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the theoretical analysis of analog in-memory training from Wu et al. (NeurIPS 2024) [21], which established convergence guarantees for Tiki-Taka only under a special linear response function with γ=0, to a generic class of asymmetric and non-linear response functions. It also provides the first rigorous theoretical foundation for the Tiki-Taka algorithm heuristically introduced by Gokmen & Haensch [22], showing that Tiki-Taka is a special case of the proposed Residual Learning bilevel framework. The pulse-update dynamics from Gokmen & Vlasov [16] serve as the physical starting point, which the authors formalize into a discrete-time model (Theorem 4) that bridges element-level physics and algorithm-level analysis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first paper to provide a convergence theory for analog SGD under generic non-ideal response functions, revealing that the algorithm implicitly optimizes a penalized objective rather than the true loss—a fundamental insight that explains the persistent asymptotic error observed empirically. The Residual Learning framework offers a provably exact convergence path by aligning the algorithmic stationary point with the physical symmetric point, and its extension (RLv2) addresses practical noise and granularity issues. Key limitations include the strong convexity assumption (needed for uniqueness of the lower-level solution), the restriction to three hardware imperfections, and the fact that response functions q₊(w), q₋(w) are typically unknown in practice, making the zero-shifting requirement non-trivial to implement. Open questions include extending the analysis to non-convex objectives, incorporating device-to-device variation, and validating on real AIMC hardware rather than AIHWKIT simulations.
