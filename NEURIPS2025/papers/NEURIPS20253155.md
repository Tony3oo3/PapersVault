## Problem

Deep learning models can extract secrets from side-channel traces, but security evaluators cannot determine what specific leakage a successful model exploits, limiting feedback to a binary pass/fail verdict. This interpretability gap is especially acute for masked implementations, where the masking randomness is hidden and standard input-intervention techniques from mechanistic interpretability are unavailable. The paper asks: can one reverse-engineer the learned representations of a trained SCA network to identify and recover the secret shares it uses for classification?

## Input / Output

The method operates post-hoc on a trained MLP or CNN that has already recovered at least one AES subkey, consuming the model's output logits and intermediate-layer activations on test traces (10,000 traces per dataset, 15,000–20,000 samples per trace after resampling). Critically, the masking randomness is NOT provided as input; it must be recovered from the model's internal representations. The primary outputs are the concrete secret share values (mask and masked S-box output) per trace, SNR plots localizing each share's leakage in the trace, and the geometric structure (PC directions) that encodes the shares.

## Method

The approach is a three-step mechanistic interpretability pipeline applied at discrete phase transitions (sudden Perceived Information jumps) during training. First, logit analysis identifies what class distinctions the model newly acquires at each transition (e.g., high-vs-low Hamming Weight, then even-vs-odd HW). Second, principal component analysis of intermediate-layer activations reveals geometric structures—diagonals or grids—whose axes correspond to the Hamming weights of individual secret shares. Third, activation patching fixes all but one PC component to a grid corner (equivalent to fixing one share to HW 0 or 8), then reads the model's predicted HW distribution to recover the remaining share's value; SNR plots then disambiguate which share is the mask versus the masked S-box output. The key insight is that the network's share embeddings form low-dimensional linear structures in PC space that are causally verifiable via patching, enabling mask recovery without any prior knowledge of the masking randomness.

## Results

Across three SCA benchmarks, the paper identifies discrete phase transitions in Perceived Information (PI) that correspond to the emergence of interpretable features. For CHES_CTF, two jumps occur (epochs 15–40 and 70–85), first separating high vs. low Hamming Weight (HW) of secret shares, then distinguishing even vs. odd HW (parity). For ESHARD, a single transition at epoch 4 yields only the high-low HW split. For ASCAD, a transition at epochs 8–12 captures the two least-significant bits of the S-box input, followed by a gradual increase to epoch 25 incorporating the two LSBs of the S-box output; a linear probe on the final layer achieves 0.64 accuracy on the derived 16-class partition, with PI of 2.47 bits vs. 2.58 bits for the full model. Activation patching successfully recovers secret mask values for CHES_CTF and ESHARD (confirmed via SNR spikes at expected trace locations), while for ASCAD the recovered shares yield only 2–3 of the expected 4 classes, indicating partial but causal recovery.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an interpretability study rather than a method-comparison paper, so no head-to-head accuracy or attack-success tables are presented. The paper positions its approach against three prior directions: (1) input-visualization methods (gradient/occlusion-based [26, 18, 51]), which highlight relevant trace regions but cannot identify which secret share is exploited without mask knowledge; (2) custom interpretable architectures (SAT-equation CNNs [52], Kolmogorov-Arnold networks [53]), which provide clean symbolic interpretations but only on simulations or selected features and suffer from worse attack performance; and (3) layer-wise probe methods that assume access to masking randomness [35, 54]. The key differentiator is that the proposed logit-analysis + PCA + activation-patching pipeline requires no a priori mask knowledge and works on standard MLP/CNN architectures, while uniquely enabling direct extraction of secret share values. The paper also notes that identical grid structures emerge across different implementations (ARM Cortex-M4 vs. AVR RISC) and SCA domains (power vs. EM), supporting the weak universality hypothesis [7], though no formal cross-dataset comparison table is provided.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper transplants mechanistic interpretability techniques from the grokking/group-operations literature (Nanda et al., Chughtai et al., Zhong et al.) into the domain of deep learning-based side-channel analysis, where the core computation (recombining secret shares) is structurally analogous to learned group operations. It departs from prior SCA interpretability work—input visualization, explainable architectures, and mask-aware probing—by requiring neither access to masking randomness nor custom model architectures, instead deriving features from output logits and working backwards through PCA of activations. The activation patching methodology follows Perin et al. [35], but the key methodological departure is that patches are designed without a priori knowledge of secret share values, making the approach applicable in realistic black-box evaluation settings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for security evaluation: recovering secret masks from a trained model converts a black-box pass/fail assessment into a white-box diagnostic, enabling targeted countermeasure design. The observation that identical grid structures emerge across different hardware platforms and SCA domains (power vs. electromagnetic) provides empirical support for the weak universality hypothesis in a real-world, noisy setting. Key limitations include the requirement that the model already breaks the target, the restriction to MLPs (and one CNN), and the less clean mask extraction on ASCAD's more complex ID leakage model. Open questions include automating the feature-matching process, extending to Transformer-based SCA architectures, and whether the approach generalizes to higher-order masking or prime-field schemes.
