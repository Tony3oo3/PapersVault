## Problem

While AlphaFold can generate thousands of structural models for a protein complex, its self-estimated confidence scores are unreliable for ranking and selecting the best model, making model selection a critical bottleneck. The field lacks a large, diverse, well-annotated benchmark dataset for training and evaluating Estimation of Model Accuracy (EMA) methods, with prior datasets being small, built on outdated docking tools, or generated in non-blind settings that do not reflect real prediction workflows.

## Input / Output

GATE consumes a pairwise similarity graph per target where each node is a structural model (PDB coordinates) annotated with AlphaFold self-estimated features (AFM confidence, ipTM, mpDockQ, num_inter_pae) and each edge encodes structural similarity between two models; subgraphs of up to 50 nodes are sampled for training. The output is a predicted quality score per model (TM-score or DockQ_wave), enabling ranking and selection from a pool. The benchmark itself labels every model with 10 complementary scores spanning global (TM-score variants, RMSD), local (lDDT), and interface (ICS, ICS_precision, ICS_recall, IPS, QS_global, QS_best, DockQ_wave) levels, computed via an automated pipeline using OpenStructure and USalign.

## Method

PSBench is the primary contribution: a benchmark suite of five datasets containing over 1.4 million labeled structural models from CASP15/16 blind community and in-house predictions plus 400,400 AlphaFold3 models for 2,002 new PDB entries, covering 2,081 targets across 185 stoichiometries. The demonstrated EMA method, GATE, is a graph transformer that processes a subgraph of models for a target, using node features (self-estimated quality scores) and edge features (pairwise structural similarity) to predict per-model accuracy via supervised learning with 10-fold cross-validation. GATE was trained on CASP15 data and blindly tested in CASP16, where it ranked first in Pearson correlation (0.673) among 38 EMA predictors. The benchmark additionally provides four evaluation metrics (Pearson, Spearman, ranking loss, AUROC), six baseline EMA methods for comparison, and an automated annotation pipeline enabling continuous dataset expansion with new structural models.

## Results

On the CASP16 in-house top-5 models (32 targets), GATE-AFM achieves the best Spearman's correlation (0.283), lowest ranking loss (0.102), and highest AUROC (0.658) for TM-score, and the best Pearson's (0.431), Spearman's (0.322), ranking loss (0.138), and AUROC (0.662) for DockQ_wave, outperforming all five baselines on nearly every metric. In the blind CASP16 EMA competition (37 targets, 38 predictors), GATE (MULTICOM_GATE) ranked 1st in Pearson's correlation (0.673) and 3rd in Spearman's correlation (0.456), ranking loss (0.135), and AUROC (0.652), placing it among the top-performing EMA methods in the field.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GATE-AFM outperforms all five baselines on the majority of metrics. Against AFM Confidence (AlphaFold2-Multimer's own score), GATE-AFM improves Spearman's correlation from 0.143 to 0.283 and AUROC from 0.597 to 0.658 for TM-score, with statistically significant gains on all four TM-score metrics. PSS is the closest competitor, achieving a slightly higher Pearson's correlation (0.394 vs. 0.372) for TM-score, but GATE-AFM wins on Spearman's correlation, ranking loss, and AUROC for both TM-score and DockQ_wave. GCPNet-EMA trails GATE-AFM on every metric for both quality scores, with a ranking loss of 0.135 vs. 0.102 for TM-score. The VoroIF-GNN variants and DProQA perform substantially worse, with DProQA showing near-zero or negative Pearson's correlation (−0.051 for TM-score) and the lowest AUROC (0.569), indicating it is barely better than random at distinguishing good from bad models. In the broader CASP16 EMA competition, GATE's 1st-place Pearson's correlation (0.673) and 3rd-place standing on three other metrics confirm its competitive position among 38 community EMA predictors.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground in protein complex model quality assessment (EMA) by addressing the critical data bottleneck that has limited the field. Prior benchmark datasets (Docking Benchmark, PPI4DOCK, MAF2, HAF2) were small (thousands of models), generated by traditional docking tools or in simulated prediction settings, and covered only small-to-medium complexes with limited quality annotations. PSBench departs from this lineage by scaling to over 1.4 million models generated in truly blind CASP15/16 prediction settings, covering 2,081 diverse targets with 185 stoichiometries, and providing 10 complementary quality scores at global, local, and interface levels. The GATE method demonstrated on PSBench builds on graph transformer architectures for pairwise model similarity, extending the DProQA/ComplexQA line of work to a much larger and more diverse data regime.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PSBench fills the role of an 'ImageNet for protein complex EMA' — a large, standardized, continuously expandable resource that was previously absent, enabling rigorous training and benchmarking of machine learning EMA methods. The blind CASP16 validation (GATE ranking 1st in Pearson correlation among 38 EMA predictors) provides strong evidence that methods trained on PSBench generalize to unseen targets and prediction settings. Key limitations include the dominance of AlphaFold-generated models (introducing method-specific bias) and the relatively small number of CASP targets (79) for the blind datasets, though the newly curated PDB dataset (2,002 targets) partially addresses this. Open questions include how well EMA methods trained on AlphaFold models transfer to future prediction methods, and whether the benchmark's continuous expansion pipeline can maintain quality as new structure prediction approaches emerge.
