## Problem

Existing approaches to probing Transformer input spaces rely on heuristic perturbations, gradient-based criteria, or analysis of specific embedding-space properties, all of which require data samples or predetermined perturbation directions. This paper asks whether one can construct a mathematically principled, model-driven method that identifies the full equivalence class of an input (all inputs yielding the same output probability distribution) and navigates between distinct equivalence classes, without relying on external data or fixed perturbation schedules.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250734/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a trained Transformer (ViT for images, BERT for text) and a single input instance (a CIFAR-10/MNIST image or a WinoBias/MHS sentence), producing a sequence of K modified embedding matrices. SiMEC outputs embeddings that preserve the original class probability distribution (within numerical tolerance), while SiMExp outputs embeddings that shift the distribution, potentially changing the predicted class. Each embedding is decoded back into a human-readable image or sentence via an inverse-embedding layer (ViT) or an MLM decoder (BERT), with only the explored patches/tokens replaced in the original input, yielding interpretable alternative instances.

## Method

The core mechanism treats each Transformer layer as a smooth map between manifolds and computes the pullback of the Euclidean output metric through the full-network Jacobian, yielding a singular Riemannian metric on the input embedding space. Eigendecomposition of this pullback metric at each iteration reveals two sets of directions: eigenvectors associated with zero eigenvalue span the tangent space of the equivalence class (used by SiMEC to stay within it), while eigenvectors associated with non-zero eigenvalues point toward different equivalence classes (used by SiMExp to escape). At each step, the algorithm randomly selects one such eigenvector and advances by an adaptive step size proportional to the inverse square root of the metric's condition number, then projects the result back into the feasible embedding region. This produces a foliation of the input space into smooth equivalence-class manifolds, and the interpretation step maps explored embeddings back to pixels or tokens, enabling direct human comparison of what the model considers equivalent versus distinct.

## Results

SiMEC maintains near-zero class ranking changes (0.0–0.12 per 1000 iterations) across all four datasets, confirming it stays within the original equivalence class, while SiMExp induces 1.31–18.70 ranking changes (η=1 to η=10), with the ** marker indicating at least one full prediction change. SiMExp explores an input-space hypervolume roughly 10× larger than SiMEC (Welch t-test p < 10⁻³). Interpretation outputs from SiMEC have a median Wasserstein distance of 0.0 from embedding predictions, whereas SiMExp's interpretations show a median of 0.049, with 10.9% of misaligned predictions eventually realigning after ~291 iterations on average.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares SiMEC and SiMExp against a gradient-based Gaussian-noise baseline that updates embeddings by ±δ∇N(x) + ε (sign chosen to stay in or leave the equivalence class, with ε a small orthogonal Gaussian perturbation). Under identical iteration counts and average step sizes, SiMEC strictly preserves the original-class probability equal to the top-class probability at every iteration, whereas the baseline frequently shows a gap between the two. For SiMExp, the original-class and top-class probabilities diverge rapidly as theory predicts, while in the baseline they remain close together, indicating the baseline fails to effectively escape the equivalence class. The authors conclude SiMEC and SiMExp are 'significantly more effective' than the gradient-based baseline for their respective tasks of staying within and moving across equivalence classes, though no formal significance test is reported for this specific comparison.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the singular Riemannian geometry framework for neural networks developed by Benfenati and Marta (which was previously applied to simple architectures with ReLU/softmax) to the Transformer setting, leveraging the fact that all Transformer modules (attention, GeLU, fully connected layers) are smooth maps compatible with the framework. It departs from prior Riemannian-geometry approaches to neural networks (Grementieri & Fioresi, Tron et al.) by computing the pullback of the output-space metric through the full Jacobian of a Transformer and by adding a critical interpretation step that maps explored embeddings back to human-readable inputs. Compared to heuristic or gradient-based input perturbation methods (adversarial examples, counterfactuals) and to SVD-based subspace analyses of ViT Jacobians (Salman et al.), the paper's contribution is a principled, theory-driven exploration of equivalence classes rather than data-driven or heuristic perturbations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a mathematically grounded, model-agnostic method for understanding how Transformers partition their input space into equivalence classes, which is directly relevant to explainability, sensitivity analysis, and adversarial robustness. However, the O(n³) eigenvalue decomposition bottleneck and the experiments on relatively small models (ViT on CIFAR-10/MNIST, BERT on short-text datasets) raise open questions about scalability to large-scale architectures with embedding dimensions of 10³–10⁴. The interpretation step, while valuable, introduces approximation errors (Wasserstein distance inconsistencies for SiMExp) that limit the fidelity of decoded outputs. Future work on controlled generation within equivalence classes and partial decompositions for scalability would strengthen the practical utility of this framework for XAI applications.
