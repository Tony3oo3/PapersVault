## Problem

Classical saddle point avoidance results for gradient descent require global Lipschitz smoothness of the objective, a condition that fails for many important nonconvex problems such as phase retrieval, low-rank matrix factorization, and Burer-Monteiro factorizations of MaxCut. The paper asks whether nonlinear preconditioning of the gradient can preserve the saddle-avoidance property under weaker, more practical smoothness assumptions—specifically (L₀,L₁)-smoothness and anisotropic smoothness—that are actually satisfied by these applications.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a C² nonconvex objective f: ℝⁿ→ℝ, an isotropic reference function φ(x)=h(‖x‖) with kernel h from a small family (e.g., cosh(x)−1, exp(|x|)−|x|−1, −|x|−ln(1−|x|)), a random initial point x⁰, and stepsize/scaling hyperparameters (γ, λ). The output is a sequence of iterates that asymptotically avoid strict saddle points with probability one; the perturbed variant (Algorithm 1) additionally guarantees reaching an anisotropic ε-second-order stationary point—defined via λ⁻¹φ(∇φ*(λ∇f(x))) ≤ ε² and λ_min(∇²φ*(λ∇f(x))∇²f(x)) ≥ −√(ρε)—in Õ(L(f(x⁰)−inf f)/(L̄ε²)) iterations, with only logarithmic dependence on dimension n.

## Method

The paper introduces a polynomial-growth sufficient condition (Assumption 2.8) bounding the Hessian Frobenius norm by a degree-R polynomial in ‖x‖ while requiring the gradient norm to grow at least as a degree-(R+1) polynomial, and proves this condition simultaneously implies both (L₀,L₁)-smoothness and the second-order characterization of (L,L̄)-anisotropic smoothness. It verifies the condition for phase retrieval (when measurement vectors span ℝⁿ), symmetric and regularized asymmetric matrix factorization, and Burer-Monteiro factorizations of MaxCut-type SDPs via augmented Lagrangians. For saddle avoidance, the paper leverages the stable-center manifold theorem to show the preconditioned iterate map T_{γ,λ̄⁻¹} avoids strict saddles with probability one under anisotropic smoothness, and extends this to hard gradient clipping (where φ* is not C²) using a recent nonsmooth variant of the theorem. The perturbed variant replaces the classical Lipschitz-Hessian assumption with Lipschitz continuity of the composite map H_λ(x)=∇²φ*(λ∇f(x))∇²f(x), a condition that holds for univariate polynomials of arbitrary degree and yields a polylogarithmic-in-dimension complexity bound for reaching second-order stationarity.

## Results

The main theoretical contribution is Theorem 3.6, which establishes that the perturbed preconditioned gradient method (Algorithm 1) reaches an ε-second-order stationary point in Õ(L(f(x⁰)−inf f)/(L̄ε²)) iterations, with the Õ notation hiding a factor χ⁴ that is polylogarithmic in both dimension n and tolerance ε. Numerically, on the symmetric matrix factorization problem (n=2, r=1), P-GD with φ(x)=cosh(‖x‖)−1 converges for stepsizes orders of magnitude larger than the maximum stable stepsize of vanilla GD, requiring significantly fewer iterations. On the 'octopus' function (constructed so that GD takes exponential time to escape saddles), Algorithm 1 with L∈{1,1.5,2,3} and n∈{5,10} scales similarly to perturbed vanilla GD [13, Alg 1], validating the polylogarithmic dimension dependence.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its perturbed preconditioned gradient method (Algorithm 1) against two baselines on the 'octopus' function [13]: vanilla gradient descent (GD) and perturbed vanilla GD [13, Alg 1]. On this adversarial landscape where vanilla GD provably takes exponential time to escape saddle points, Algorithm 1 performs comparably to perturbed vanilla GD, with similar scaling in both dimension n and the smoothness constant L. The key distinction is that Algorithm 1 achieves this under anisotropic smoothness rather than the global Lipschitz smoothness required by [13], making it applicable to problems (e.g., phase retrieval, matrix factorization) where Lipschitz smoothness is violated. On the symmetric matrix factorization problem, P-GD outperforms vanilla GD in both convergence speed and allowable stepsize, since the quartic objective's unbounded Hessian forces GD to use very small steps to avoid divergence, while the nonlinear preconditioner damps large gradients effectively.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two previously separate lines of work: the (L₀,L₁)-smoothness framework empirically motivated by neural network training (Zhang et al., 2020) and the anisotropic smoothness framework developed for analyzing nonlinearly preconditioned gradient methods (Oikonomidis et al., 2025; Laude et al., 2025). The key conceptual contribution is a novel sufficient condition (Assumption 2.8) that simultaneously guarantees both smoothness notions, revealing a structural link between them that was not previously established. For saddle point avoidance, the paper extends the classical stable-center manifold theorem analysis of Lee et al. (2016, 2019) and the perturbation-based complexity results of Jin et al. (2017, 2021) from the Lipschitz smoothness setting to the broader anisotropic smoothness setting, and further extends to hard gradient clipping via a nonsmooth variant of the theorem (Cheridito et al., 2024).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first rigorous saddle point avoidance guarantees for important nonconvex problems (phase retrieval, matrix factorization, Burer-Monteiro MaxCut) under a smoothness condition that is both verifiable and actually satisfied, unlike the global Lipschitz smoothness assumption that fails for these problems. The unification of (L₀,L₁)-smoothness and anisotropic smoothness under a single polynomial-growth condition gives practitioners a concrete, checkable criterion for when gradient clipping and related preconditioning heuristics are theoretically justified. Open questions include whether the polynomial-growth condition (Assumption 2.8) can be verified for deeper neural network training objectives (the appendix sketches this for regularized networks but with restrictive bounds), and whether the anisotropic smoothness framework can be extended to stochastic or adaptive methods like Adam in nonconvex settings.
