## Problem

In RL with sparse rewards, demonstrations accelerate learning but existing methods like Q-filter make binary imitation decisions based on single Q-value point estimates, ignoring estimation uncertainty and introducing high gradient variance. This becomes particularly problematic with limited or suboptimal demonstrations, where binary accept/reject decisions amplify misleading examples rather than filtering them. The paper asks: how should an agent continuously modulate its imitation strength based on quantified confidence that a demonstration action outperforms its current policy?

## Input / Output

The agent receives state observations (25-dimensional for Fetch arm tasks, 61-dimensional for Shadow Hand tasks) and a pre-collected set of demonstration transitions (state, action, reward, next state, goal) stored in a separate buffer. Demonstrations are sampled in mini-batches of 128 alongside 1024 transitions from the standard replay buffer at each training step. The output is a continuous control action (4-dimensional for Fetch, 20-dimensional for Shadow Hand). Notably, demonstrations can be severely suboptimal (success rates as low as 0.06 in ablation experiments), and the method adapts per state-action pair without requiring explicit quality labels or scheduling.

## Method

SPReD maintains an ensemble of 10 critic networks that serve a dual purpose: computing target values via random-pair min (following REDQ) and quantifying epistemic uncertainty through the spread of Q-value estimates. For each demonstration state-action pair, the method compares two Q-value distributions (demonstration action vs. current policy action) and computes a continuous weight p ∈ [0,1] that modulates a weighted behaviour cloning loss added to the standard deterministic policy gradient objective. Two complementary weighting schemes are provided: SPReD-P computes the probability of demonstration superiority under a Gaussian model (the CDF of the standardized mean difference), while SPReD-E applies an exponential scaling of the advantage difference normalized by an IQR-based uncertainty measure, clipped to [0,1]. Theoretical analysis proves that continuous weights strictly reduce policy gradient variance compared to binary filtering, adapt conservatively under high uncertainty, and automatically diminish influence from suboptimal demonstrations as the policy converges.

## Results

Across eight sparse-reward robotics tasks (Fetch and Shadow Hand), SPReD-E achieves the highest success rate in 7 of 8 environments after 1M interactions (10M for stacking), reaching 0.984 on FetchPush, 0.920 on FetchStack2, and 0.888 on FetchPickAndPlace. The headline result is a 14× improvement over RLPD on FetchStack2 (0.920 vs. 0.064) using only 100 demonstrations. SPReD-P is competitive throughout, achieving 0.976 on FetchPush and 0.840 on FetchStack2, with both variants showing lower variance across seeds than baselines. On the hardest task (FetchSlide), SPReD-E still leads with 0.240 vs. 0.160 for the next-best method (RLPD).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against TD3 (no demonstrations), both SPReD variants dominate across all eight tasks, with the gap widening as task complexity increases (e.g., 0.920 vs. 0.008 on FetchStack2). Against Q-filter (binary BC filtering), SPReD-E wins on all eight tasks, most dramatically on FetchStack2 (0.920 vs. 0.048, a 19× margin) and FetchSlide (0.240 vs. 0.072); SPReD-P similarly outperforms Q-filter everywhere (e.g., 0.832 vs. 0.608 on FetchPickAndPlace). Against RLPD (ensemble critics + layer normalisation), SPReD-E achieves a 14× improvement on FetchStack2 (0.920 vs. 0.064) and leads on 7 of 8 tasks, though RLPD is competitive on ManipulateBlock where SPReD-P edges ahead (0.864 vs. 0.016 for RLPD). Against all three AWAC variants (offline-to-online advantage-weighted BC), SPReD methods are consistently superior, as AWAC's reliance on large offline datasets for pretraining makes it poorly suited to the few-demonstration setting (e.g., AWAC scores 0.008 on FetchStack2 vs. 0.920 for SPReD-E). The ensemble ablation (EnsQ-filter) confirms that the gains are not merely from using more critics: SPReD-E's 0.920 on FetchStack2 far exceeds EnsQ-filter's 0.144, isolating the benefit of continuous uncertainty-aware weighting over simple ensembling.

## Relation to Prior Work

SPReD directly extends the Q-filter mechanism (Nair et al., ICRA 2018), which selectively applies behaviour cloning when a demonstration action's Q-value exceeds the policy action's Q-value. The key departure is replacing Q-filter's binary indicator with continuous, uncertainty-proportional weights derived from ensemble critic distributions, addressing two identified limitations: no accounting for estimation uncertainty and gradient discontinuities from binary decisions. The method is built on TD3's dual-critic actor-critic architecture with HER for sparse-reward goal-conditioned tasks, and borrows the random-pair ensemble target computation from REDQ. Compared to RLPD (which also uses ensemble critics) and AWAC (advantage-weighted offline-to-online RL), SPReD's distinguishing contribution is the explicit distributional comparison between demonstration and policy Q-values to produce state-adaptive regularisation weights.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in the method's robustness: SPReD maintains strong performance even when 99% of demonstrations are random trajectories, a scenario where Q-filter actually underperforms TD3 without demonstrations. The computational overhead is minimal (3.8% throughput reduction despite 5× more critics), making it a near drop-in replacement for Q-filter in any off-policy actor-critic pipeline. Open questions include automatic scheduling of demonstration influence during late-stage learning (where SPReD can slow convergence on highly dexterous tasks), formal convergence guarantees, and sim-to-real transfer. The finding that SPReD-E's exponential weighting is extremely sensitive to ensemble size (484% improvement going from 2 to 10 critics) suggests the method's practical deployment requires careful ensemble sizing, a non-trivial consideration for resource-constrained settings.
