## Problem

Off-the-shelf LLMs suffer severe accuracy degradation (8–10% average, up to 23% on reasoning tasks) when deployed on analog in-memory computing (AIMC) hardware due to non-deterministic weight noise, static input/output quantization, and device nonidealities. Prior hardware-aware training work was limited to small CNNs/RNNs under 50M parameters, and no generic method existed to make billion-parameter LLMs robust to the diverse, chip-specific noise profiles of AIMC accelerators without access to original pre-training data.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained LLM (Phi-3-mini-4k-instruct or Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct) and produces an analog foundation model whose linear layers are robust to 8-bit static input quantization, globally static 8-bit output quantization, and hardware-realistic PCM programming noise. Training data is self-generated by the model via iterative softmax sampling (20B tokens, sequence length 4096), and the resulting model can be deployed on AIMC hardware or post-quantized to 4-bit per-channel weights for low-precision digital accelerators. The KV-cache remains in FP16, and attention/activation operations run on digital units.

## Method

The pipeline follows LLM-QAT's data-free distillation framework but replaces deterministic quantization operators with four hardware-aware training components: (1) learnable per-layer static input quantization ranges initialized via exponential moving average, (2) globally static 8-bit output quantization handled with simple straight-through estimation (losing only 0.2–0.5% accuracy), (3) per-channel additive Gaussian noise injection into weights during the forward pass (γ_weight = 0.02–0.03), and (4) post-optimizer-step weight clipping to a fraction of per-channel standard deviation, which maps small weights to higher-conductance NVM states with better SNR. The key finding is that weight clipping provides stronger robustness than noise injection alone for LLMs, and their combination is optimal. Training uses knowledge distillation from the original FP16 model on 20B synthetically generated tokens with AIHWKIT-Lightning on 96 V100 GPUs (DeepSpeed ZeRO-2, activation checkpointing), taking ~230h for Phi-3-mini and ~90h for Llama-3.2-1B.

## Results

Under hardware-realistic PCM programming noise, off-the-shelf Phi-3-mini-4k-instruct drops from 70.03% to 62.02% average accuracy (−8.01%), while the analog foundation model (SI8-W16hw noise-O8) retains 66.22%, reducing the gap to FP16 to just 3.81%. For Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct, the analog foundation model achieves 40.91% average under noise (vs. 35.68% for the off-the-shelf model), a 4.58% gap to FP16. On the hardest benchmark (GSM8K), the analog foundation model scores 71.35% (Phi-3) and 30.75% (Llama-3.2-1B) under noise, compared to 58.48% and 22.59% for the unmodified models. As a byproduct, 4-bit RTN quantization of the analog foundation model yields 66.64% average (Phi-3) and 41.88% (Llama-3.2-1B), outperforming LLM-QAT (65.63% / 40.24%) and SpinQuant with static input quantization (63.30% / 27.35%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LLM-QAT (SI8-W4), the analog foundation model consistently outperforms under hardware-realistic noise: 66.22% vs. 60.58% average for Phi-3-mini (5.64% gap) and 40.91% vs. 37.92% for Llama-3.2-1B (2.99% gap), with the largest per-benchmark differences reaching 12.87% (Phi-3) and 8.16% (Llama-3.2-1B) on harder reasoning tasks like GSM8K and HellaSwag. Against SpinQuant, the advantage is dramatic: under static input quantization (SI8) with hardware noise, SpinQuant collapses to 28.71% (Phi-3) and 25.26% (Llama-3.2-1B) average, far below the analog foundation model's 66.22% and 40.91%. Even with dynamic per-token input quantization (DI8), which is impractical for AIMC hardware, SpinQuant only reaches 43.68% and 32.98%, still trailing the analog foundation model by 22.54% and 7.93% respectively. The paper also shows that the analog foundation model, when 4-bit RTN quantized for digital deployment, outperforms both LLM-QAT and SpinQuant(SI8) on average accuracy (66.64% vs. 65.63% vs. 63.30% for Phi-3; 41.88% vs. 40.24% vs. 27.35% for Llama-3.2-1B), making it a dual-purpose model for both analog and low-precision digital hardware.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends hardware-aware training (HWA) for analog in-memory computing from small CNNs, RNNs, and encoder-only transformers (<50M parameters) to large LLMs (3B+ parameters) pre-trained on trillions of tokens. It adapts the data-free synthetic data generation and distillation pipeline from LLM-QAT [Liu et al., 2023] but replaces deterministic weight quantization with stochastic noise injection, learnable static input ranges, globally static output quantization, and weight clipping. The work departs from Zhang et al. [2024] (RAOQ), which required complex activation/weight reshaping and low-rank adaptation to handle static output quantization, by showing that simple straight-through estimation suffices for LLMs when combined with the full HWA training recipe.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first demonstration that billion-parameter LLMs can be made robust to hardware-realistic analog noise, opening a path toward energy-efficient foundation model inference on 3D NVM-based AIMC chips that promise up to three orders of magnitude higher energy efficiency than GPUs. Key limitations include a persistent accuracy gap versus FP16 (especially on reasoning tasks like GSM8K), the resource cost of training (96 V100s for ~230h), and the ~3× slowdown of the linear kernel during training. Open questions include whether post-training methods (analog PTQ) can achieve comparable robustness without retraining, whether the gap to FP16 can be closed with better data quality or low-rank HWA adaptations, and whether the approach generalizes to even larger models (70B+) and MoE architectures.
