## Problem

Applying diffusion models to multivariate time series anomaly detection faces two gaps: existing frequency-domain decomposition relies on manually preset levels or energy thresholds that cannot adapt to non-stationary data, and reconstruction strategies lack fine-grained control over how high- and low-frequency information is integrated during denoising, causing either missed subtle pattern anomalies or false positives. This paper addresses both by introducing a physics-guided, data-driven decomposition and a conditional diffusion framework with frequency-aware routing.

## Input / Output

The method consumes overlapping sliding windows of size 64×d from a multivariate time series (d ranges from 9 to 55 across datasets), which are first decomposed via MAFD into high- and low-frequency components before entering the diffusion process. It outputs a per-timestep anomaly score that fuses L2 reconstruction error with a KL divergence between the transient time-frequency distribution and a learned normal prior, thresholded adaptively via SPOT (generalized Pareto fitting on score tails) rather than a fixed cutoff.

## Method

PhysDiff decomposes each multivariate window using Multi-channel Adaptive Fourier Decomposition (MAFD) with cross-channel shared Blaschke basis functions, where the decomposition depth is determined data-drivenly by monitoring Amplitude-Sensitive Permutation Entropy (ASPE) of residuals until complexity stabilizes. The resulting high-frequency and low-frequency components, along with ASPE values and a physics-based energy function, form a composite conditioning vector z_t that guides a conditional denoising diffusion process. A dual-path frequency-based routing attention mechanism computes adaptive gating coefficients g_h and g_l via sigmoid of query–physical-prior products, dynamically weighting transient versus trend information at each diffusion step. Physical consistency is further enforced through Langevin-dynamics sampling with an energy gradient term and a frequency-adaptive trend influence factor γ(t,ω) that modulates guidance strength per frequency band across the denoising trajectory. Training minimizes a composite loss combining the standard diffusion noise-prediction objective, an MAFD-ASPE reconstruction term, and the energy regularization, optimized with AdamW and a controlled-disturbance augmentation.

## Results

PhysDiff achieves an average F1 score of 75.44% across five standard benchmarks (SMD, MSL, SMAP, SWaT, PSM), outperforming all 18 baselines on 4 of 5 datasets. The most notable gains are on MSL (74.83% F1, +2.78 over LODA) and SMAP (73.91% F1, +2.26 over Anomaly Transformer). On the more challenging NeurIPS-TS datasets, PhysDiff reaches 69.44% F1 on Creditcard (+6.82 over D3R) and 57.92% on GECCO (+1.96 over Anomaly Transformer). On PSM, PhysDiff (74.16%) is slightly below AE (75.01%), the only dataset where it does not lead.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PhysDiff outperforms the strongest reconstruction-based baselines on most datasets: it beats AE (81.30) by 0.35 on SMD, D3R (71.81) by 3.02 on MSL, and Anomaly Transformer (71.65) by 2.26 on SMAP. On SWaT, PhysDiff (72.64) edges out DAGMM (72.32) by 0.32, while on PSM it trails AE (75.01) by 0.85 — the sole dataset where it does not lead. The gap widens substantially on the NeurIPS-TS benchmarks: PhysDiff surpasses D3R by 6.82 F1 points on Creditcard (69.44 vs. 62.62) and Anomaly Transformer by 1.96 on GECCO (57.92 vs. 55.96), indicating that the physics-guided decomposition and frequency-adaptive routing are particularly beneficial under higher non-stationarity and sparser anomaly ratios. Among distance and tree-based methods (OCSVM, LOF, iForest, LODA), PhysDiff leads by 5–25 F1 points on most datasets, with the largest margin over iForest on PSM (74.16 vs. 6.48). Overall, PhysDiff achieves the best average F1 (75.44) across the five standard benchmarks, with the closest competitors being AE (69.60 avg) and BeatGAN (72.72 avg).

## Relation to Prior Work

PhysDiff extends the decomposition-plus-diffusion-reconstruction paradigm established by D3R (Wang et al., NeurIPS 2023), which first showed that adaptive signal decomposition combined with diffusion-based reconstruction improves anomaly detection on non-stationary multivariate time series. The key departure is replacing D3R's fixed or heuristic decomposition depth with a data-driven stopping criterion based on amplitude-sensitive permutation entropy (ASPE), and generalizing single-channel adaptive Fourier decomposition to a multivariate setting via shared Blaschke basis functions (MAFD). The conditional diffusion component builds on the standard DDPM framework (Ho et al., 2020) but introduces a dual-path frequency-based routing attention mechanism that explicitly conditions the denoising process on high- and low-frequency physical priors extracted from the decomposition, rather than treating the diffusion model as an unconditional or weakly-conditioned generator.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap in time series anomaly detection: most existing methods assume stationarity or use static decomposition parameters that fail on real-world non-stationary data. By making decomposition depth adaptive and embedding frequency-specific physical constraints into the diffusion process, PhysDiff offers a more interpretable and principled approach to distinguishing normal non-stationary variation from genuine anomalies. However, the 'physics' framing (Langevin dynamics, energy functions) is relatively thin and largely serves as a conditioning mechanism rather than enforcing hard physical laws, and the computational cost of 1000-step diffusion sampling remains a barrier to real-time deployment. Open questions include whether the ASPE-guided decomposition depth truly captures meaningful physical structure or merely provides a convenient data-driven stopping rule, and whether the routing attention mechanism generalizes beyond the specific frequency split used here.
