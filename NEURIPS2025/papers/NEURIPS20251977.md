## Problem

Autoregressive language models generate tokens left-to-right in an irrevocable manner: once a token is sampled, it cannot be revised, causing errors to propagate and accumulate throughout the sequence. While recent progress has focused on improving training data, architecture, and RL fine-tuning, the sampling process itself remains largely unexplored, with most models still relying on vanilla next-token-prediction (NTP) sampling.

## Input / Output

The method takes a standard text prompt and context tokens as input and produces a generated token sequence as output, but the generation process operates on a sliding window of w previously generated tokens (w=2 or 3 in practice). During each RPT iteration, the model conditions on both the left context and the right (future) token within the window, requiring the model to be permutation-aware via a learned positional embedding that encodes the relative offset τ_i − σ_i between source and target positions. The output is a standard token sequence compatible with KV caching, but the internal generation involves k iterative resampling passes over the local window before committing tokens.

## Method

RPT (Resample-Previous-Tokens) augments standard NTP sampling with a local corrector step: after initializing a window of w tokens via NTP, the model iteratively resamples each token in the window using a previous-token-prediction (PTP) conditional p(x_i | x_{<i}, x_{i+1}) followed by the standard NTP conditional, repeating for k iterations. Training introduces a lightweight permutation scheme where, with probability q, a token is pushed w−1 positions to the right, and the model learns to predict both the next token (NTP) and the displaced past token (PTP) using a shared cross-entropy loss with a small learned positional embedding encoding the source-target offset. The key theoretical insight is that PTP error is strictly smaller than NTP error (predicting a token given a future token is easier than without it), so the RPT Markov-chain stationary distribution achieves a lower total-variation error bound than NTP, quantified by the RPT factor ρ = κ(‖ε_PTP‖∞ + ‖ε_NTP‖∞)/(‖ε_marginal‖₁ + ‖ε_NTP‖∞) < 1. Fine-tuning a pretrained 8B model for only 100B tokens (10% of total training) with this procedure yields ~10% relative improvements on reasoning and coding benchmarks while preserving NTP sampling quality and KV-cache compatibility.

## Results

After fine-tuning a pretrained 8B AR model for only 100B tokens (10% of total training), RPT sampling with k=1.5 iterations achieves 28.6% on HumanEval+ (+3.0% relative over the fully-trained AR-F baseline of 25.6%), 40.6% on MBPP (+1.4%), and 37.5% on GSM8K (+2.3%). RPT with k=1 iteration reaches 41.1% on Java (+3.2%) and 38.4% on TypeScript (+2.6%), while also improving C++ to 31.0% (+2.5%) and Bash to 9.5% (+2.6%). Even NTP sampling from the fine-tuned model (k=0) already surpasses the fully-trained AR-F baseline on most benchmarks, suggesting the fine-tuning itself provides a modest benefit. The paper reports approximately 5–10% relative improvements across reasoning and coding benchmarks compared to standard NTP sampling.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares RPT sampling against three baselines: the fully-trained AR model (AR-F, 1T tokens, NTP sampling), the pre-fine-tuning checkpoint (AR-C, 900B tokens, NTP), and NTP sampling from the fine-tuned model itself (k=0). RPT with k=1 or k=1.5 consistently outperforms AR-F across all nine benchmarks, with the largest gains on Java (+3.2%), HumanEval+ (+3.0%), and GSM8K (+2.3%). The fine-tuned model with plain NTP (k=0) already beats AR-F on 7 out of 9 benchmarks, indicating the RPT fine-tuning procedure itself improves the model beyond just enabling corrector sampling. RPT with k=1 dominates k=2 on most benchmarks (e.g., Java 41.1 vs. 37.9, MBPP 40.6 vs. 39.0), suggesting diminishing returns or slight degradation with more iterations. The empirical TV distance analysis (Table 2) confirms that RPT reduces the total variation error of predicted conditionals after just one iteration, consistent with the theoretical prediction that PTP error is lower than NTP error.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the predictor-corrector idea from discrete diffusion models (Lezama et al., 2022; Gat et al., 2024) and any-order architectures (Pannatier et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2019) into the standard autoregressive setting, where prior corrector approaches (Stern et al., 2019; Gu et al., 2019; Li et al., 2024) required architectural modifications or fundamentally different training objectives. The key departure is that RPT achieves local token revision through a lightweight fine-tuning of a pretrained AR model—adding only a small relative positional embedding table and a permutation-aware training schedule—while preserving NTP sampling quality, speed, and KV caching. The theoretical analysis draws on Markov chain perturbation bounds (Cho and Meyer, 2001; Seneta, 2006) to formalize when the iterative resampling reduces total variation error relative to one-shot NTP sampling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that RPT offers a drop-in sampling improvement for existing AR LLMs without retraining from scratch: 100B tokens of fine-tuning (10% of total training) yields 5–10% relative gains on reasoning and coding benchmarks while maintaining NTP performance. The clean Markov-chain perturbation analysis provides a principled condition (the RPT factor ρ < 1) under which resampling provably reduces error, grounding the empirical gains in theory. Open questions include whether the benefit scales to larger models and longer windows, whether the modest 5–10% improvement holds under more demanding evaluation protocols (e.g., pass@k, multi-turn), and whether the approach composes with RL fine-tuning or test-time scaling methods. The method's reliance on a fixed small window (w=2–3) and its sensitivity to the PTP/NTP error ratio leave room for more aggressive corrector strategies.
