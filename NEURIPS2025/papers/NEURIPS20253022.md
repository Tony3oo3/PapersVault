## Problem

Adding macro-actions to reinforcement learning agents is intended to reduce the number of decisions per episode and ease credit assignment, yet in practice it often worsens exploration because each macro-action is treated as an independent, atomic choice that inflates the branching factor of the action space. The structural similarity between macro-actions (and between macro-actions and primitive actions) is ignored, so the agent cannot exploit the fact that related actions produce similar outcomes and should share credit.

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard environment observations (grayscaled 84×84 Atari frames or 128×128×3 StreetFighter frames) together with an augmented action set comprising primitive actions plus k macro-actions (frequent sequences of length 3–8 from human trajectories, or domain-specific attack combos). It produces a full Q-value vector over all actions, a greedy policy, and a learned |A|×|A| symmetric non-negative similarity matrix Σ, which is further compressed into a low-dimensional embedding e_Σ (32-dim for Atari, 16-dim for StreetFighter) via a learned linear projection and fed back into the Q-network as context.

## Method

The core contribution is the Macro-Action Similarity Penalty (MASP), a regularization term L_MASP = η·‖Q(s,·;θ) − ΣQ(s,·;θ)‖² added to the standard RAINBOW-DQN TD loss, which softly constrains Q-values of similar actions to co-vary, effectively reducing the intrinsic dimensionality of the action space and enabling credit to flow between related macro-actions. The similarity matrix Σ is not handcrafted but meta-learned jointly with the policy via a two-phase meta-gradient procedure: an inner loop performs a standard RL update (TD + MASP) on a sampled trajectory, and an outer loop evaluates the updated parameters on a held-out trajectory, backpropagating through the inner update to obtain ∇_Σ of the meta-objective. To handle the evolving Σ without non-stationarity issues, the matrix is flattened and projected through a trainable linear layer into a compact embedding e_Σ that is concatenated with the state representation, following the Universal Value Function Approximator paradigm. The full pipeline is built on top of RAINBOW-DQN (distributional RL, dueling networks, noisy nets, prioritized replay, n-step returns, double Q-learning), and the only architectural change is the additional MASP term and the Σ-embedding input.

## Results

On five representative Atari games, MASP achieves substantial improvements over the RAINBOW-DQN baseline: Breakout 884.4 vs. 379.5 (2.3×), Seaquest 23,768.8 vs. 19,176.0 (1.24×), Space Invaders 16,668.2 vs. 12,629.0 (1.32×), Frostbite 5,566.7 vs. 4,141.1 (1.34×), and Ms Pacman 2,966.5 vs. 2,570.2 (1.15×). Critically, naively adding macro-actions *degrades* performance in every game (e.g., Breakout drops to 252.9, Space Invaders to 7,496.4), while MASP recovers and exceeds the baseline. In StreetFighter II, MASP scores 236,943 and 593,784 on the two macro-action sets, outperforming both baselines. On MiniGrid, MASP reaches 100% success on DoorKey and FourRooms, 97% on LockedRoom, and 91% on ObstructedMaze-Full.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three variants: plain RAINBOW-DQN, RAINBOW-DQN with independently added macro-actions, and RAINBOW-DQN with the proposed MASP regularization. The most striking finding is that naively augmenting the action space with macro-actions *hurts* performance in every Atari game tested (e.g., Breakout drops from 379.5 to 252.9, a 33% decrease; Space Invaders drops from 12,629 to 7,496, a 41% decrease), confirming the paper's central hypothesis that the enlarged branching factor overwhelms the reduced episode length. MASP reverses this trend and beats the plain RAINBOW-DQN baseline in all five headline Atari games, with the largest relative gain on Breakout (+133%) and Frostbite (+34%). In StreetFighter II, MASP outperforms both baselines in both macro-action complexity settings, with the gap widening for the more complex Macro-Actions II set (593,784 vs. 565,319 for +Macro-Actions and 537,389 for plain RAINBOW). On MiniGrid, MASP achieves near-perfect success rates (100%, 100%, 97%, 91%) compared to Rainbow-DQN (83%, 69%, 58%, 57%) and +Macro-Actions (88%, 87%, 77%, 84%), with the largest absolute gains in the hardest environments.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on RAINBOW-DQN as its base algorithm and adapts the meta-gradient framework of Xu et al. (NeurIPS 2018) to learn a similarity matrix over the action space. It departs from the broader macro-action literature (Randlov, McGovern & Sutton, Frans et al., Cho & Sun) which has focused on discovering or hierarchically composing useful macros, by instead addressing the credit-assignment pathology that arises when macros are naively appended to the action space. The key conceptual departure is treating the action space as geometrically structured rather than a flat set of independent choices, and imposing a learned smoothness constraint (MASP) that allows Q-values of similar actions to co-vary during learning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper tackles a well-known practical failure mode—macro-actions often degrade rather than improve performance—and provides a principled, meta-learned fix that is robust to large action spaces (up to 1024 macros) and partially corrupted macro sets. The transferability of the learned similarity matrix Σ across related Atari games suggests the structure captures behavioral regularities rather than task-specific overfitting, which could reduce retraining costs in multi-task or lifelong settings. Open questions include scaling Σ to continuous or combinatorial action spaces, making the similarity state- or policy-conditioned, and integrating MASP with automatic macro-action discovery methods.
