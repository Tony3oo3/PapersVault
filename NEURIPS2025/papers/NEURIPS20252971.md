## Problem

While overparameterized neural networks generalize well in practice, a precise understanding of what they learn and how many samples suffice remains lacking. For two-layer quadratic networks with extensive-width targets (m* = O(d)) and quadratically many samples (n = O(d²)), no sharp asymptotic characterization of empirical risk minimization existed—prior results covered only linear models, single-index models, or finite-width targets. This paper fills that gap by providing closed-form expressions for training loss, test error, and the spectral properties of global minima in this previously unexplored regime.

## Input / Output

The learner receives n = αd² Gaussian samples x^μ ∈ R^d with labels generated by a quadratic teacher network of width m* = κ*d, plus Gaussian label noise of variance Δ. The target is fully characterized by the limiting spectral density μ* of S* = (W*)^T W*/√(m*d), not merely its moments, and the results hold for any target with a well-defined spectral limit. The output is a set of closed-form asymptotic quantities—training loss, test error, singular value density of the learned weights, and critical thresholds (interpolation and strong recovery)—that are independent of the student width κ for all κ ≥ 1, meaning massively overparameterized networks achieve identical performance to barely overparameterized ones.

## Method

The key insight is a reduction of the non-convex ERM problem for the weight matrix W to a convex positive semi-definite matrix estimation problem for S = W^T W/√(md), where the ℓ₂ (Frobenius) regularization on W translates exactly into a nuclear norm (trace) regularization on S, implicitly favoring low-rank solutions. Gaussian universality then replaces the data-dependent sensing matrices (xx^T − I)/√d with GOE matrices, reducing the problem to a rank-penalized matrix recovery task. The resulting convex problem is solved rigorously via Generalized Approximate Message Passing with non-separable denoisers, whose state evolution equations—whose unique fixed point coincides with the global minimizer by convexity—yield all asymptotic quantities in closed form. The analysis reveals that the interpolation threshold depends nontrivially on the target's spectral structure rather than just the sample-to-parameter ratio, and that the test error exhibits a double-descent peak at the interpolation threshold in the noisy case.

## Results

The paper derives closed-form asymptotic expressions for the training loss, test error, and singular value spectrum of the global minima of ℓ₂-regularized ERM in overparameterized quadratic two-layer networks. The headline quantitative results are sharp thresholds: the interpolation threshold α_inter = (1+2κ*−κ*²)/4 for 0<κ*<1 (noiseless), α_inter = 1/2 for κ*≥1, and α_inter → 1/4 as Δ→∞; the strong recovery threshold α_strong = 1/2 for κ*≥1 and a smaller value (involving incomplete semicircle moments) for 0<κ*<1. In the low-rank target limit κ*→0, the test error of the ERM is exactly 1 for ᾱ≤1/2, follows (2/9)ᾱ(4−√(6ᾱ−2))² for 1/2<ᾱ≤3, and vanishes for ᾱ≥3 (noiseless, λ→0⁺). A remarkable finding is that the test error is independent of the student width κ for all κ≥1, meaning massively overparameterized networks achieve identical generalization to barely-overparameterized ones.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the Bayes-optimal (BO) test error computed by Maillard et al. (2024) for the same teacher-student setup. The ERM with optimal regularization approaches but does not always match the BO curve: in the noiseless case, the ERM strong recovery threshold (Corollary 1) is strictly earlier than the BO strong recovery threshold, with the gap most pronounced for very narrow targets (κ*≪1), where the ERM achieves zero test error at ᾱ=3 while the BO threshold is slightly higher. In the noisy regime, the ERM test error tracks the BO curve closely at large α but deviates near the interpolation threshold, where a double-descent cusp appears as λ decreases. The paper also contrasts its interpolation threshold with the ellipsoid-fitting threshold of 1/4 (Maillard & Bandeira 2023, Maillard & Kunisky 2024), showing that the ERM interpolation threshold is strictly larger than 1/4 in all non-trivial settings, reflecting the structural advantage of the planted target over random labels. Unlike kernel ridge regression or random features methods, the interpolation threshold here depends on the full spectral distribution μ* of the target (not just its second moment Q*), a direct consequence of the non-separable PSD constraint in the equivalent matrix problem.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies three previously distinct research threads—spin-glass analysis of quadratic teacher-student networks, high-dimensional Bayesian inference for extensive-width models (Maillard et al. 2024, Xu et al. 2025), and nuclear-norm-regularized matrix recovery (Fazel et al. 2008, Recht et al. 2010)—by showing that ℓ₂ weight decay on the network weights W is exactly equivalent to nuclear norm regularization on the Gram matrix S = WᵀW/√(md). Prior work on extensive-width quadratic networks either addressed the Bayes-optimal (information-theoretic) estimator or restricted to linearly-many samples n = O(d); this paper is the first to derive sharp ERM asymptotics in the harder regime of quadratically-many samples n = O(d²) with extensive-width targets (0 < κ* < 1), using AMP with non-separable denoisers and Gaussian universality to bridge the non-convex network problem to a convex matrix sensing task.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that test error is independent of student width κ for all κ ≥ 1, and that the interpolation threshold depends on the target's spectral structure rather than the naive sample-to-parameter ratio, provides a concrete mechanism for how overparameterization and implicit regularization interact in non-linear networks. The nuclear-norm interpretation of weight decay offers a principled explanation for why overparameterized networks implicitly prefer low-rank feature maps, connecting a practical training choice to a well-studied convex relaxation. Key limitations include the restriction to Gaussian inputs, quadratic activations, a single hidden layer, and the requirement m > d; extending to deeper architectures, non-Gaussian data, or the underparameterized regime (m < d) where the rank constraint introduces genuine non-convexity remains open and technically challenging.
