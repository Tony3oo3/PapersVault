## Problem

Constant learning-rate SGD is the default in high-dimensional and overparameterized machine learning, yet its theoretical guarantees in high-dimensional regimes remain largely absent. Classical convergence theory relies on decaying learning rates, while existing high-dimensional results (e.g., Wainwright 2019, Chen et al. 2023) either assume decaying rates or are restricted to low dimensions. The non-stationarity of SGD iterates under constant stepsizes—where iterates oscillate around a stationary distribution rather than converging to a point—makes standard martingale or contraction arguments inapplicable in high dimensions.

## Input / Output

The framework takes i.i.d. samples from an unknown distribution, an arbitrary initial vector β₀ ∈ ℝᵈ, and a fixed constant learning rate α, and produces non-asymptotic q-th moment bounds for both the last SGD iterate and the Ruppert-Polyak averaged iterate in general ℓˢ-norms for any even integer s ≥ 2. The ℓ^∞-norm (critical for sparse/structured high-dimensional estimation) is handled by choosing s = s_d = 2 min{ℓ ∈ ℕ : 2ℓ > log d}, which makes ℓ^{s_d} and ℓ^∞ equivalent up to a factor of e. Outputs also include explicit iteration-complexity bounds for reaching a target error ε and a Fuk-Nagaev-type high-probability tail bound for the averaged estimator.

## Method

The key insight is to treat constant-step SGD as a nonlinear autoregressive (iterated random function) process and adapt coupling techniques from high-dimensional time series to prove geometric-moment contraction: two SGD sequences sharing the same noise but different initializations satisfy ‖|β_k − β_k′|_s‖_q ≤ r^k |β_0 − β_0′|_s with r < 1, establishing asymptotic stationarity. The contraction is proved via a new high-dimensional moment inequality (a generalization of Rio's inequality to ℓˢ-norms) that avoids martingale decomposition and requires only finite q-th moment conditions on stochastic gradients. For the averaged estimator, the paper decomposes the error into non-stationarity, stochastic variance, and non-diminishing bias terms, bounding the variance through functional dependence measures that quantify how each past sample ξ_i influences the k-th iterate. A Fuk-Nagaev-type concentration inequality, built on the same coupling machinery, yields the first high-probability tail bound for high-dimensional ASGD with constant learning rates, with the polynomial tail rate k^{1−q} shown to be unimprovable under finite q-th moment assumptions.

## Results

The paper establishes that constant-learning-rate SGD in high dimensions achieves geometric-moment contraction with rate $r_{\alpha,s,q} = 1 - 2\mu\alpha + \max\{q,s\}L_{s,q}^2\alpha^2 < 1$, implying asymptotic stationarity of the iterates. For the stationary SGD iterates, the $q$-th moment error satisfies $\| |\beta_k^\circ - \beta^*|_s \|_q = O(M_{s,q}\sqrt{\max\{q,s\}\,\alpha})$, and the ASGD complexity to achieve $\varepsilon$-accuracy in the $\ell^\infty$-norm is $O(1/\varepsilon^2)$ iterations (Proposition 2), matching the optimal rate for fixed dimension. The learning rate must satisfy $\alpha < 2\mu/(7\max\{q,s\}L_{s,q}^2) \asymp 1/(d^2\log d)$ when $s = s_d \approx 2\log d$ and $L_{s_d,q} \asymp d$. A sharp Fuk-Nagaev-type high-probability bound (Theorem 4) controls $\mathbb{P}(|\bar{\beta}_k - \beta^*|_\infty > z)$ with three terms: initialization bias, stochastic variance, and a Gaussian tail.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or experimental comparisons. The paper positions its contributions relative to prior theoretical results: (i) it extends the $\ell^2$-type geometric-moment contraction of Li et al. [2024b] to general $\ell^s$-norms and high dimension $d$; (ii) it matches the $O(1/\varepsilon^2)$ iteration complexity of Wainwright [2019] for Q-learning in the $\ell^\infty$-norm, but for the first time with a constant learning rate and explicit dimension dependence; (iii) it generalizes the Nagaev-type inequality of Lou et al. [2022] (which required linearity and decaying learning rates) to a Fuk-Nagaev bound for high-dimensional ASGD with constant learning rates; and (iv) it improves upon prior constant-step SGD analyses (Dieuleveut et al. [2020], Merad and Gaiffas [2023]) that only established convergence in Wasserstein distance, by providing explicit moment and concentration bounds. No method is shown to 'win' or 'lose' in a head-to-head sense; rather, the paper fills gaps left by all prior approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper transfers coupling and functional-dependence techniques from high-dimensional nonlinear time series (Wu & Shao 2004; Wu 2005; Zhang & Wu 2017) to the analysis of constant-learning-rate SGD, building directly on the authors' own low-dimensional treatment (Li et al. 2024b) and on Rio's moment inequalities (2009). It departs from the dominant Markov-chain/Wasserstein-distance approach to constant-step SGD (Dieuleveut et al. 2020; Merad & Gaiffas 2023), which yields only distributional convergence and cannot produce refined moment or concentration bounds, and from the decaying-learning-rate literature (Ruppert 1988; Nemirovski et al. 2009) that avoids non-stationarity altogether. The high-dimensional ℓ^s-norm strong convexity assumption and the s_d ≈ log(d) bridge to ℓ^∞ are novel technical devices not present in prior SGD theory.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper closes a genuine gap: before this work, no non-asymptotic moment or high-probability bounds existed for constant-learning-rate SGD in high dimensions, despite constant rates being the default in large-scale practice. The ℓ^∞-norm guarantees are directly relevant to sparse and structured estimation (Lasso, Dantzig, sup-norm confidence intervals), where max-norm control is the natural metric. Key limitations include the strong convexity and smoothness assumptions, the restrictive learning-rate upper bound of O(1/(d² log d)), and the restriction to even-integer s; extending the framework to non-convex, overparameterized objectives (where the stationary distribution may be multimodal) is identified as the main open direction.
