## Problem

DNA language models can generate sequences with pathogenic properties, creating serious biosecurity risks, yet no watermarking scheme exists to trace AI-generated DNA. Unlike LLM watermarking, DNA presents unique challenges: a 4-nucleotide alphabet severely limits green/red list design, natural mutations and synthesis errors can degrade watermarks, and biological constraints require preserving encoded protein function. Furthermore, no prior method enables watermark detection across the central dogma, i.e., in both the DNA and the translated protein.

## Input / Output

During generation, the method consumes the autoregressive DNA context (preceding nucleotides) and, for CentralMark, the running translated protein sequence. Evo2 (7B) embeddings of the DNA context or ESM2 (35M) embeddings of the protein are fed into a compact watermark model (4-layer MLP with residual blocks) that outputs 4-dim watermark logits. The output is a watermarked DNA sequence where nucleotide selection is biased toward green-list bases; for CentralMark, the translated protein independently carries a detectable amino-acid distribution bias, enabling verification without access to the original DNA.

## Method

DNAMark embeds watermarks via synonymous codon substitutions targeting only the third base of codons, preserving the encoded amino acid. A trained watermark model maps Evo2 embeddings to watermark logits, which are added to the original DNA model logits to bias sampling; the model is trained with an alignment loss (matching logit cosine similarity to tanh-scaled Evo2 embedding similarity) and a normalization loss (zero-mean, uniform-magnitude logits) to ensure semantic invariance and statistical neutrality. Adaptive watermark strength (EMA-adjusted δ driven by running z-score) and entropy-guided skipping of low-entropy regions balance detectability against sequence quality. CentralMark extends this by targeting the second base of codons and using ESM protein embeddings, so the watermark propagates into the amino-acid sequence as a biased distribution over a green set of ~10–12 amino acids, making it detectable in the protein independently of the DNA. Detection in both methods uses a KGW-style z-score test with method-specific expected green proportions (γ ≈ 0.356 for DNAMark, 0.5 for CentralMark-DNA, 0.55 for CentralMark-Protein).

## Results

On the Therapeutic DNA Benchmark (400 sequences) using Evo2-7B, CentralMark (DNA) achieves the highest F1 of 0.928 at 1% FPR under no attack, with DNAMark at 0.911 and CentralMark (Protein) at 0.924. Under the most challenging Indels attack, F1 scores remain above 0.86 for all three proposed methods (DNAMark 0.878, CentralMark-DNA 0.862, CentralMark-Protein 0.858), while KGW baselines drop to 0.49–0.68. Generated sequences maintain >60% identity to ground truth and degeneracy scores below 15%, and the watermark model adds only ~30% generation time overhead.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against KGW adapted to DNA with 1-, 2-, and 4-codon window sizes (KGW-1, KGW-2, KGW-4). DNAMark and CentralMark consistently outperform all KGW variants across every attack condition and both FPR thresholds. The advantage is most pronounced under adversarial attacks: under Indels at 1% FPR, CentralMark (DNA) achieves F1=0.862 versus KGW-1's 0.675 (a 18.7-point gap), and under Nucleotide Substitutions, DNAMark reaches F1=0.902 versus KGW-4's 0.493 (a 40.9-point gap). KGW-4 (largest window) performs worst under mutations, suggesting that wider context windows amplify vulnerability to sequence-level perturbations in the 4-nucleotide alphabet. CentralMark's unique ability to detect the watermark in the translated protein (F1=0.858 under Indels) provides an additional detection channel unavailable to any KGW variant, which only operates on the DNA sequence.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts two established LLM watermarking paradigms to the DNA domain: the KGW green/red list partitioning with z-score detection (Kirchenbauer et al., ICML 2023) and the semantic-invariant watermark model that uses embeddings to generate robust watermark logits (Liu et al., ICLR 2024). The key conceptual departure is the 'inheritable' watermark (CentralMark), which targets the second base of codons so that the watermark signature propagates into the translated protein, enabling detection without access to the original DNA. This extends prior protein watermarking work (Zhang et al. 2025, Chen et al. 2025) by coupling it to the upstream DNA sequence through the central dogma, a linkage no prior method addresses. The paper also introduces a curated therapeutic DNA benchmark of 400 sequences, filling a gap in evaluation infrastructure for DNA watermarking.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As DNA language models like Evo2 (40B) become capable of designing functional genes and CRISPR systems, the biosecurity risk of untraceable AI-generated sequences is acute, and this paper provides the first systematic framework for watermarking such outputs while preserving biological function. The inheritable watermark concept—detectable in both DNA and protein—addresses a practical gap where the functional output (protein) may be the only artifact available for verification. Key open questions remain: no wet-lab validation has been performed, the methods rely on green/red list structures that could be reverse-engineered, robustness to indels is the weakest link (~76% TPR), and the authors acknowledge the need for watermark schemes independent of explicit list partitioning. The ~30% computational overhead and the requirement for Evo2/ESM embeddings at inference time are practical constraints for deployment.
