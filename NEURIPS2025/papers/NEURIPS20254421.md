## Problem

Bayesian optimization with Gaussian process surrogates typically relies on fixed or heuristic kernel selection, which can cause slow convergence or suboptimal solutions when the kernel's assumptions mismatch the objective function's structure. Existing automatic kernel design methods (e.g., compositional kernel search) are difficult to apply in BO's few-shot, expensive-evaluation regime where gradient-based optimization is infeasible. The paper asks whether LLMs, with their in-context learning and reasoning capabilities, can serve as adaptive genetic operators to evolve expressive kernel structures from limited observed data.

## Input / Output

At each BO iteration, the method consumes the current set of observed input-output pairs as few-shot examples in LLM prompts, along with a predefined base kernel set {SE, PER, LIN, RQ, M3, M5} and operators {+, ×}. The LLM outputs compositional kernel expressions (e.g., (SE × PER) + (LIN × RQ)), which are then scored by BIC fitness and acquisition values. The final output per iteration is a single selected kernel and its corresponding next query point, with the overall goal being the optimized solution within a fixed evaluation budget.

## Method

CAKE maintains a population of GP kernels and uses an LLM (gpt-4o-mini) as both crossover and mutation operators in a genetic algorithm loop: crossover samples two parent kernels weighted by fitness and prompts the LLM to combine them via a kernel grammar operator, while mutation replaces a base kernel in the fittest individual with another. The LLM operates entirely in-context with chain-of-thought reasoning, analyzing observed data patterns before proposing kernels, without any fine-tuning. To select the best kernel each iteration, BAKER computes a softmax weight from each kernel's BIC and multiplies it by the kernel's acquisition value at its proposed query point, jointly balancing model fit and expected improvement. The top-n_p fittest kernels survive to the next generation, and the process iterates until the evaluation budget is exhausted.

## Results

On the HPOBench benchmark (60 tasks across 5 ML models and 12 OpenML datasets), CAKE+BAKER achieves an average rank of 1.04±0.1, outperforming the next-best method (Genetic Algorithm, 2.70) by over 60%. On 15 benchmark optimization functions, CAKE achieves a mean normalized regret of 0.1357, a 22.2% improvement over the second-best method (ABO, 0.1745) and a 21.9% improvement in median regret. CAKE also achieves 67.5% of its total improvement within just 25% of the evaluation budget, and in photonic chip design it finds a high-scoring solution in under 40 trials—a 10× speedup over baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CAKE consistently outperforms all five baseline categories across every evaluation setting. Against the adaptive kernel selection method of Roman et al. [37] (Random/Utility/BIC criteria), CAKE achieves substantially lower regret (e.g., 0.0783 vs. 0.1062 on Ackley-2) and higher HPOBench accuracy, with adaptive methods showing inconsistent performance across different ML models. Deep GP [21] and Ensemble GP [31] both underperform CAKE by large margins (mean regret 0.3589 and 0.2881 vs. CAKE's 0.1357), confirming that deeper or ensemble surrogate models do not compensate for a fixed kernel choice. Among compositional kernel methods, CKS [12] (greedy search) achieves mean regret of 0.1905 and ABO [32] (nested BO) achieves 0.1745, both trailing CAKE by 22–29%. ABO is the closest competitor and the only other method to reach the 200-point target in the lunar lander task, but CAKE exhibits lower variance and faster convergence. In the photonic chip multi-objective setting, CAKE surpasses both single-task GP and additive GP baselines as well as CKS and ABO in both score and hypervolume, finding a high-scoring design in under 40 trials versus 250 for baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the kernel grammar formalism from Duvenaud et al. (2013) and its generalization by Bitzer et al. (2022), which define a compositional space of GP kernels via addition and multiplication of base kernels. It departs from prior adaptive kernel selection methods (Roman et al., CKS, ABO) that use fixed selection criteria or nested optimization loops, instead repurposing LLMs as crossover and mutation operators in a genetic-algorithm loop to iteratively evolve kernel expressions conditioned on observed data. The BAKER selection mechanism further distinguishes the work by jointly weighting kernel fit (BIC) and acquisition value, addressing a gap where good-fitting kernels may not yield high-utility query points.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a long-standing underexplored bottleneck in BO—kernel selection—by showing that LLMs can serve as effective, interpretable genetic operators for kernel design entirely in-context, without fine-tuning. The consistent outperformance across HPO, controller tuning, and photonic chip design (including a ~10× speedup in chip design) suggests practical value for expensive black-box optimization. Open questions include whether the approach scales to higher-dimensional search spaces, whether more expressive kernel operators (convolution, composition) yield further gains, and how to reduce the per-iteration LLM inference cost (8.3s vs. 0.6s for fixed kernels) without sacrificing sample efficiency.
