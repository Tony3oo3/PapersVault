## Problem

Conventional federated learning assumes a closed world with a fixed set of clients, but real-world deployments require new clients to join continuously, bringing unseen knowledge. Existing federated domain adaptation methods only handle coarse-grained class-incremental discovery (e.g., FOSDA) and fail on the more universal domain-incremental case, while also sacrificing source-domain performance and adaptation efficiency when integrating new knowledge.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251678/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The server receives a pre-trained source global model (split into encoder and classifier), local updates from N source clients and one target client, plus a small public dataset used solely to compute feature-space distances for knowledge discovery. The target client's data may introduce new classes (class increment) or a new domain distribution (domain increment). The output is a single adapted global model that simultaneously serves both source and target clients, along with a binary classification of the incoming knowledge type. For digit classification (DigitFive), a LeNet-style CNN processes 28×28 grayscale images; for product review (Amazon Review), an LSTM handles text sequences.

## Method

Gains decomposes the model into an encoder and classifier, exploiting the empirical observation that encoder features shift under domain changes while classifier parameters shift under class changes. Knowledge discovery computes Manhattan distance on public-data features (Diff^F) and Euclidean distance on classifier parameters (Diff^C), using thresholds T_F and T_C to flag new knowledge and discriminate its type. For knowledge adaptation, a contribution-driven aggregation assigns per-client weights inversely proportional to their feature/parameter distance from the target domain, updated each iteration to accelerate convergence; in the class-incremental case, the classifier is merged via channel-wise supplementation (source channels retained, target channels appended). An anti-forgetting mechanism adds an L2 penalty between each source client's current parameters and its pre-join snapshot during local training, preventing the aggregation bias toward the target domain from degrading source performance.

## Results

On the DigitFive dataset, Gains achieves 99.34% target-client accuracy (T-Acc) and 93.21% source-client accuracy (S-Acc) under mild (class-incremental) shift, 97.91% T-Acc and 90.09% S-Acc under medium (domain-incremental) shift, and 98.98% T-Acc and 93.18% S-Acc under strong shift. On Amazon Review, Gains reaches 84.60% T-Acc and 82.81% S-Acc (medium) and 80.54% T-Acc and 84.95% S-Acc (strong). Gains also converges in only 5 federated rounds versus 20–40 rounds for baselines, with total training time of 807 s compared to 1,368–9,520 s for competing methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Gains dominates all seven baselines on nearly every metric across both datasets. In the mild (class-incremental) scenario, the gap is extreme: FOSDA and SemiFDA collapse to 0% T-Acc, while Gains reaches 99.34%; even the best Heter-FL baseline (FedProto, 77.61% T-Acc) trails by over 21 points. In the medium domain-incremental scenario, FedHEAL is the closest competitor on T-Acc (93.68% vs. Gains' 97.91% on DigitFive), but Gains maintains a 1.4-point S-Acc advantage (90.09 vs. 88.71) and a 1.95-point G-Acc advantage. Under strong shift, Gains leads FedHEAL by 2.0 points in T-Acc (98.98 vs. 96.98) and by a substantial 9.9 points in S-Acc (93.18 vs. 83.32), highlighting the anti-forgetting mechanism's role. On Amazon Review, Gains outperforms the next-best method (FedProx at 74.55% T-Acc in medium shift) by 10+ points, while all FDA baselines (FOSDA, SemiFDA, AutoFedGP, FedHEAL) hover near random (≈50%). The convergence comparison further shows Gains needs only 5 rounds and 807 s, versus 20–40 rounds and 1,368–9,520 s for the other methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends federated domain adaptation (FDA) from closed-set settings (fixed clients) to open-set settings where new clients continuously join, building on prior FDA methods like SemiFDA, AutoFedGP, and FedHEAL that assume a static client population. The closest prior work, FOSDA, addresses open-set FDA but only for class-incremental scenarios and ignores source-domain degradation; Gains departs by introducing a fine-grained mechanism to discriminate between class and domain increments via encoder-feature and classifier-parameter sensitivity, combined with a contribution-driven aggregation strategy and an anti-forgetting mechanism borrowed from continual learning. Conceptually, the paper repurposes the standard encoder/classifier decomposition from domain adaptation as a diagnostic tool for knowledge-type identification within a federated pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically important gap: real-world federated systems (IoT, cross-device) inevitably encounter new clients with unseen data, and the ability to automatically detect and integrate such knowledge without degrading existing performance is essential for deployment. However, several limitations temper the impact: thresholds for knowledge discovery are manually set, the framework only handles class and domain increments (not task increments or combined scenarios), and validation is limited to relatively simple datasets (digit classification, binary text sentiment). Open questions include whether the encoder/classifier sensitivity split generalizes to deeper architectures and more complex modalities, and whether automatic threshold calibration is feasible without public data.
