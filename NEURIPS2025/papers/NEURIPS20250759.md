## Problem

Existing fair representation learning methods estimate upper bounds on downstream unfairness from training data, but these bounds can be underestimated due to overfitting and fail on unseen test data. No prior method allows users to jointly specify both an error threshold ε and a confidence level δ, guaranteeing that demographic disparity across all downstream models and tasks stays below ε with probability at least 1−δ.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250759/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a dataset D of i.i.d. pairs (X_i, S_i) where X is a tabular feature vector and S is a sensitive attribute (binary or multi-class), and splits it into D_c (90% for candidate selection) and D_f (10% for the fairness test). It outputs either the parameters φ_c of a VAE-based representation model that is certified ε-fair with confidence 1−δ, or NSF (No Solution Found) if no candidate passes the statistical test. The guarantee holds for every possible downstream model τ and task, including adversarial ones that explicitly try to recover S from the representation.

## Method

FRG comprises three components: (1) candidate selection optimizes a VAE objective (reconstruction + KL divergence) subject to an inflated confidence-interval constraint Ũ_ε(φ, D_c) ≤ 0 via Lagrangian saddle-point optimization with KKT conditions, where the adversary is updated with a few gradient steps per candidate; (2) adversarial inference trains a separate predictor τ_adv on D_c representations to approximate the optimal adversary, justified by Theorem 5.2 which shows Δ_DP = |Cov(Ŷ,S)|/Var(S) for binary S, so maximizing Δ_DP reduces to maximizing absolute covariance; (3) the fairness test constructs a 1−δ confidence upper bound on g_ε(φ) using paired unbiased estimates of Pr(Ŷ=1|S=s) on held-out D_f and Student's t-test, returning φ_c only if the bound is ≤ 0. The key distinction from prior work is that the statistical guarantee is computed on a dedicated held-out set rather than estimated from training data, and the user explicitly controls both ε and δ.

## Results

Across three real-world datasets (UCI Adult, Heritage Health, ACSIncome), FRG maintains Δ_DP ≤ ε with probability at least 0.9 (i.e., violation rate ≤ δ = 0.1) for all downstream tasks including adversarial ones, while most baselines violate the constraint with probability ≥ 0.1. FRG also returns a valid solution (not NSF) with probability ≥ 0.9 across all datasets. In terms of predictive utility, FRG achieves higher AUC than the other methods that satisfy fairness with high probability (ICVAE and FARE), particularly at small ε values (e.g., ε = 0.04 on Adult). On adversarial tasks predicting sensitive attributes, FRG and FARE are the only methods that consistently satisfy the fairness constraint, while LAFTR, FCRL, CFAIR, LMIFR, and ICVAE all show increased failure rates.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FRG outperforms all six baselines on the joint objective of maintaining high-confidence fairness guarantees while preserving predictive utility. Compared to FARE (the closest competitor with statistical guarantees), FRG provides tighter, user-controllable ε thresholds whereas FARE's certificates are 'several times larger than the desired ε' and do not support user-defined error bounds; FRG also achieves higher AUC, especially at small ε. Compared to ICVAE (the only other baseline consistently satisfying Δ_DP ≤ ε with high probability on Adult and Health), FRG yields higher AUC. The remaining four baselines (LAFTR, FCRL, CFAIR, LMIFR) all satisfy fairness constraints on validation sets but fail with large probability on unseen test data and on adversarial tasks, confirming that their training-data-based upper bounds overfit. On transfer learning (Health dataset, age prediction task), unsupervised baselines LMIFR, ICVAE, and LAFTR show increased constraint violation rates compared to their target-task performance, while FRG maintains its guarantees. FRG's supervised variant (FRG_supervised) performs marginally better than unsupervised FRG but the improvement is insignificant, suggesting that overly predictive candidates can expose more sensitive information.

## Relation to Prior Work

FRG extends the Seldonian algorithm framework [70]—which provides high-confidence guarantees for safe ML—to the fair representation learning setting, where prior methods (LAFTR, ICVAE, LMIFR, CFAIR, FCRL) estimate fairness bounds on training data without statistical guarantees on unseen data. The closest prior work, FARE [33], also provides high-confidence certificates for downstream unfairness but does not allow users to jointly specify both the error threshold ε and confidence level δ. FRG departs from adversarial-training-based FRL methods by using a separately trained adversary evaluated on held-out data rather than joint optimization, and it formalizes the first user-controllable high-confidence fairness guarantee for representation models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FRG offers a practical, certifiable path for deploying fair representations in high-stakes domains (hiring, lending, sentencing) where regulatory requirements demand explicit fairness thresholds with statistical backing. Key limitations include the i.i.d. assumption, the reliance on an (approximated) optimal adversary, and the CLT requirement for the Student's t-test, all of which restrict applicability to non-stationary or small-data settings. Open questions include extending guarantees to distributional shifts in features or sensitive attributes, avoiding the optimal-adversary assumption entirely (the MI-based alternative FRG-MI is shown to be impractically loose), and systematically comparing confidence interval constructions beyond the t-test.
