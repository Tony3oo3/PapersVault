## Problem

Controlling partially observed linear dynamical systems under adversarial disturbances and general convex losses requires competing with linear dynamical controllers (LDCs), but prior methods like GRC achieve optimal √T regret only at the cost of polynomial runtime in the inverse stability margin γ⁻¹. For marginally stable systems—common in robotics, thermal regulation, and satellite control—this polynomial blowup makes the approach computationally infeasible. The paper asks whether the same regret guarantees can be matched with exponentially better dependence on γ.

## Input / Output

At each round the learner receives a partial observation y_t ∈ R^p and known time-invariant system matrices (A, B, C), and must output a control action u_t ∈ R^n. A key preprocessing step computes the 'natural observation' y_t^nat online by maintaining a fictitious state z_t that tracks the contribution of the learner's own past actions, yielding a signal independent of the learner's parameters. The control is then a linear combination of double-spectral-filtered features of the natural observation history, with all tunable parameters living in a convex set of dimension polylog(T/γ) × n × p.

## Method

Double Spectral Control (DSC) constructs a two-level spectral approximation of the best stable LDC. The first level (spectral lifting) convolves the natural observation history with eigenvectors of a fixed Hankel matrix H whose entries are (1−γ)^(i+j−1)/(i+j−1), producing a compact lifted signal that captures long-memory dependencies with only polylog(γ⁻¹) components instead of the polynomial count required by prior lifting. The second level (spectral filtering) convolves the lifted signal with a second set of Hankel eigenvector filters, yielding features that are linear in the learnable parameter matrices M. Because the Hankel eigenvalues decay exponentially, logarithmically many retained components suffice for arbitrary approximation accuracy, and the entire policy class becomes a convex set over which projected online gradient descent is applied. This double-convolution structure is what converts the nonconvex LDC optimization into a tractable online convex problem while preserving the optimal √T regret rate.

## Results

DSC achieves a regret bound of Õ(√T/γ¹¹) against the best (κ,γ)-diagonalizably stable linear dynamical controller, matching the optimal √T rate for convex costs. The per-step runtime is polylog(T/γ), which is an exponential improvement over the polynomial dependence on γ⁻¹ in prior work. The number of tunable parameters also scales only as polylog(1/γ), keeping the model compact even for small stability margins. Corollary 4.4 confirms that each round can be implemented in amortized polylog(T/γ) time using fast online convolution.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GRC [30], the closest prior method handling adversarial disturbances and general convex costs, DSC matches the same Õ(√T) regret rate while reducing per-step runtime from Õ(poly(γ⁻¹) log T) to polylog(T/γ) — an exponential improvement in the dependence on the stability margin γ. Against AdaptOn [24], DSC handles a strictly harder setting (adversarial disturbances and general convex losses vs. stochastic noise and strongly convex costs), though AdaptOn achieves a tighter polylog(T) regret in its more restricted regime. LQG [22] serves as the classical reference point with zero regret but requires full observation, i.i.d. Gaussian noise, and known quadratic costs, making it inapplicable to the adversarial partial-observation setting. Among all methods that handle both adversarial disturbances and general online convex costs, DSC is the only one with polylogarithmic runtime in the stability margin, as highlighted in Table 1.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Gradient Response Controller (GRC) of Simchowitz et al. (2020), which first addressed online control of partially observed LDS under adversarial disturbances via improper learning over linear dynamical controllers, by replacing GRC's polynomial-in-γ parameterization with a two-level spectral approximation. The spectral basis construction draws directly from the Hankel-matrix eigenvector framework of Hazan et al. (2017) for sequence prediction, as adapted to the fully-observed control setting by Brahmbhatt et al. (2025), and the polylog per-step runtime relies on fast online convolution from Agarwal et al. (2024). The key conceptual departure is applying spectral filtering at two distinct levels—first to compress the lifting (open-loop memory) and second to compress the learning (closed-loop feedback)—thereby reducing the ambient parameter dimension from polynomial to polylogarithmic in the inverse stability margin.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DSC is the first algorithm to simultaneously handle partial observation, adversarial disturbances, and general convex losses with polylogarithmic runtime in the stability margin, closing a significant computational gap for systems operating near the stability boundary (robotics, thermal regulation, satellite control). However, the method assumes known system dynamics, real-diagonalizable closed-loop matrices with positive eigenvalues, and full-information loss feedback—restrictions that exclude oscillatory or non-diagonalizable dynamics and preclude bandit or unknown-dynamics settings. Empirical validation is preliminary (Appendix C), and extending the framework to complex eigenvalues, unknown dynamics, or partial-information feedback remains open.
