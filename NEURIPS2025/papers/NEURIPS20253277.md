## Problem

Deep time series forecasting models suffer from severe overfitting because the standard MSE loss treats all timesteps equally, forcing the model to fit uncertain (noisy) and anomalous timesteps that are inherently non-generalizable. No prior work has addressed this overfitting at the individual timestep granularity, instead relying on global sequence-level losses or architecture-specific regularization.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253277/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates as a training-time modification on top of any existing deep TSF backbone: it consumes the same historical multivariate window (lookback L × N variables) and produces the same future point predictions (F × N). The key difference is in how the loss is computed—during each training iteration, a binary mask of length F is applied per variable (channel-independent), so only a subset of predicted timesteps contributes to the MSE gradient. The masking ratios (r_u, r_a) are the only additional hyperparameters, and the estimation model for the anomaly mask is trained separately on the full training set.

## Method

Selective learning replaces the uniform MSE loss with a masked MSE that is computed only over a dynamically selected subset of timesteps, identified by a dual-mask mechanism. The uncertainty mask estimates the differential entropy of each timestep's prediction residual distribution (assumed Gaussian) using the n_t most recent residuals obtained from different sliding-window samples, then masks the top-r_u% highest-entropy timesteps. The anomaly mask trains a lightweight model (e.g., DLinear) to estimate a residual lower bound per timestep, then masks timesteps whose current residual is closest to that lower bound (small S(X_t)), distinguishing true anomalies from merely unlearned patterns. Both masks are updated dynamically during training (uncertainty mask once per epoch, anomaly mask every iteration), and the final mask is the element-wise OR of the two, applied independently per variable. A theoretical upper bound (Theorem 1) shows the variance estimation error from using residuals computed under different parameter snapshots is controlled by the learning rate and batch size.

## Results

Selective learning achieves consistent MSE/MAE reductions across all 192 evaluation cases (6 models × 8 datasets × 4 prediction lengths). Headline improvements include a 37.4% average MSE reduction for Informer (up to 66.8% on ETTm2), 15.6% for Crossformer, 8.4% for TimesNet, and 6.5% for iTransformer. Even for state-of-the-art models already equipped with RevIN normalization (iTransformer, TimeMixer), selective learning provides additional 4–7% MSE gains, confirming its complementarity with existing distribution-shift mitigation techniques.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Selective learning is a model-agnostic training strategy, so the primary comparison is each deep model against itself with SL enabled. The gains are largest for overfitting-prone architectures: Informer drops from 1.289 to 0.538 MSE on ETTh1 (−58.3%) and from 1.485 to 0.494 on ETTm2 (−66.8%), while Crossformer improves by 37.1% MSE on ETTm2. For more robust SOTA models, improvements are smaller but still consistent: iTransformer achieves 6.5% average MSE reduction and TimesNet 8.4%, both already using RevIN. In the head-to-head comparison against alternative training objectives (Table 3, iTransformer backbone), selective learning outperforms PS loss, FreDF, and TILDE-Q on all four tested datasets (e.g., 0.415 vs. 0.427 MSE on ETTh1; 0.257 vs. 0.264 on ETTm2), demonstrating that point-wise selective optimization surpasses global shape-, frequency-, or distribution-based alignment. Ablation confirms both the uncertainty mask and anomaly mask are necessary: removing either degrades performance, and replacing the dual-mask with random masking yields results comparable to or worse than no masking at all.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing timestep-granularity selective optimization for deep time series forecasting, a level of granularity not previously explored in the TSF training literature. Conceptually, it repurposes ideas from curriculum learning (MTGNN [67] progressively increases prediction length) and reducible-loss-based data selection in other domains [34, 48, 27, 50], but applies them at the individual-timestep level rather than the sample or sequence level. It departs from alternative training objectives (TILDE-Q, FreDF, PS loss) that perform global sequence alignment by arguing that certain timesteps are inherently non-generalizable and should be excluded from optimization entirely. The method is explicitly positioned as model-agnostic and complementary to existing normalization techniques like RevIN, rather than replacing any architectural component.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a widely observed but under-addressed overfitting problem in deep TSF by providing a simple, plug-and-play training strategy that consistently improves six diverse architectures across eight datasets, with particularly large gains (37.4% MSE reduction) for overfitting-prone models like Informer. Its practical value is high: the technique requires only a lightweight estimation model (DLinear) and two hyperparameters (masking ratios), making it easy to adopt. Key limitations include potential filtering of rare extreme events (mitigated by dynamic masking but not eliminated), incompatibility with large-scale TSFM pretraining where per-timestep residual statistics are unavailable, and current restriction to in-domain forecasting. Open questions include whether the approach extends to other TS tasks (imputation, classification) and how it interacts with pretraining paradigms for time series foundation models.
