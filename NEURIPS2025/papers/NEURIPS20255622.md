## Problem

Existing UED regret approximations (PVL, MaxMC) fail to identify the most challenging levels—particularly those with step-changes in difficulty like key-requiring mazes—and degrade further when used to train learned level generators. Additionally, as environment size grows beyond 13×13, replay-based methods can no longer sample useful levels from random generation, while full-level learned generators suffer from sparse-reward credit assignment over long horizons.

## Input / Output

The student agent receives a 5×5 grid observation, absolute direction, and key-carrying status; the teacher receives the same 5×5 view augmented with an overlay indicating which cells remain ungenerated. The teacher's action is a pair (cell index, cell content) constrained by action masking to only fill ungenerated cells and enforce at most one goal, key, and door per level. The level is generated incrementally—the teacher must fill all ungenerated cells in the student's current observation before the student can take its next action—so the output is a partially-constructed level that grows over the episode, scored by the MNA regret approximation computed from the student trajectory.

## Method

DEGen replaces upfront full-level generation with dynamic, observation-driven generation: the teacher (PPO with LSTM) fills only the cells the student currently observes, and a mapping from generator timesteps to student timesteps enables dense per-step reward assignment (Equation 6) rather than a single terminal reward, substantially reducing credit-assignment difficulty. The teacher's cell-content sub-action uses KL regularisation toward a fixed categorical prior (p_g = 0.01 for goal/key/door) instead of pure entropy, since the object distribution is inherently non-uniform. MNA approximates regret as the GAE-weighted mean of n-step negative advantages, where the 'optimal return' term is replaced by V_n^max (the maximum over discounted value-function estimates along the trajectory), and the final score is zeroed out if the level has never been solved (approximate unsolvability penalty). This design rewards levels that are harder than the current policy expects (negative advantage) while avoiding the PVL bias toward easy levels and the MaxMC requirement of at least one successful rollout.

## Results

On Key Minigrid 13×13, DEGen-MNA achieves a mean zero-shot solve rate of 0.93, outperforming all baselines including PLR-MNA (0.84), ACCEL-MNA (0.90), SFL (0.68), and DR (0.52). The advantage grows substantially with environment size: on 21×21 Key Minigrid, DEGen-MNA reaches 0.80 mean solve rate versus 0.43 for PLR-MNA and 0.31 for ACCEL-MNA. On standard Minigrid, DEGen-MNA achieves 0.96 mean solve rate, matching or exceeding replay-based methods (PLR-MNA: 0.92, ACCEL-MNA: 0.91). On Sokoban, DEGen-MNA (0.49) is competitive with but slightly below the best baseline ACCEL-MaxMC (0.51).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against PAIRED (Dennis et al., 2020), DEGen substantially outperforms in both Minigrid and Key Minigrid; PAIRED's antagonist-based regret approximation fails in Key Minigrid because the antagonist also cannot solve key-requiring levels, resulting in near-zero zero-shot performance. Against PLR (Jiang et al., 2021), DEGen-MNA wins on Key Minigrid 13×13 (0.93 vs 0.84) and the gap widens dramatically at 21×21 (0.80 vs 0.43), where random level sampling becomes infeasible for identifying key-requiring levels. Against ACCEL (Parker-Holder et al., 2022), DEGen-MNA similarly outperforms at larger sizes (0.80 vs 0.31 at 21×21), though ACCEL-MNA is competitive at 13×13 (0.90 vs 0.93). Against SFL (Rutherford et al., 2024), DEGen-MNA clearly wins on Key Minigrid (0.93 vs 0.68), as SFL's learnability-based scoring is insufficient for domains where a small subset of levels introduces step-change difficulty. Notably, MNA as a regret metric consistently improves all replay-based methods (PLR, ACCEL) over PVL and MaxMC, and DEGen using PVL or MaxMC performs at or below naive domain randomisation, confirming MNA is essential for learned generator training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Unsupervised Environment Design (UED) framework established by PAIRED (Dennis et al., 2020), which frames curriculum design as a minimax regret game between a teacher and student. It departs from prior learned-generator approaches (PAIRED, CLUTR) that generate the full level before student rollouts, and from replay-based methods (PLR, ACCEL) that rely on random level sampling, by proposing DEGen which dynamically generates only the observed portions of the level during student exploration. The MNA regret approximation is directly derived from the Generalised Advantage Estimator (Schulman et al., 2018), repurposing the lambda-weighted n-step advantage structure to approximate regret via negative advantage rather than the positive advantage (PVL) or Monte Carlo return (MaxMC) used in prior work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a fundamental mismatch in existing regret approximations: PVL rewards positive advantage (i.e., levels where the student does better than expected) and MaxMC requires at least one successful rollout, both of which systematically under-score the most challenging levels. The finding that replay-based UED methods degrade sharply with modest environment size increases (13×13 → 21×21) while DEGen maintains performance suggests a practical path toward scaling UED beyond toy grid worlds. However, the method requires an explicit observation-to-level-parameter mapping, incurs ~4× the compute cost of replay methods, and has only been validated on simple 2D grid environments, leaving open whether the approach transfers to 3D games or robotics where such mappings are intractable.
