## Problem

Existing egocentric vision datasets are dominated by everyday kitchen and household activities, leaving true industrial scenarios—characterised by cognitively and physically demanding tasks, multi-worker collaboration, long-horizon procedures (20+ min), and cluttered dynamic environments—severely underrepresented. This gap limits the development of egocentric AI assistants and embodied agents that must operate in real industrial settings, where workers navigate complex tool interactions, follow procedural sequences, and collaborate in roles such as teacher-student or leader-assistant.

## Input / Output

The dataset provides 3,460 egocentric recordings (197 h, 10 FPS, 8 MP from Project Aria) and 1,092 exocentric recordings (97 h, 1920×1080), each accompanied by synchronised eye-gaze, audio narration, IMU motion, semi-dense point clouds, and hand-pose estimates. For benchmarking, VLMs consume 480p video at 10 FPS (optionally with overlaid gaze or audio) and produce structured outputs: binary mistake/correct labels with severity sub-categories (Severe, Process-Failure, Impact-Future, Hazard), free-text QA answers, or per-worker action predictions in collaborative segments. Fine-grained annotations (~34k verb-noun-adjective action segments) and procedural task graphs serve as ground truth for evaluation.

## Method

The core contribution is the IndEgo dataset itself, collected over several months at an industrial research facility by 20 participants using Project Aria head-mounted devices, covering five task categories (assembly/disassembly, inspection/repair, logistics/organisation, woodworking, miscellaneous) in both solo and two-person collaborative configurations. Three benchmark tasks are defined: (1) Mistake Detection over 1,166 sequences with planned and unplanned errors, evaluated zero-shot via prompted VLMs and via fine-tuned MLP/Transformer classifiers on extracted VLM step embeddings (with 5× weighting for mistake steps); (2) Reasoning-based Video QA with 3,105 question-answer pairs spanning temporal, situated, recognition, and abductive reasoning sub-types; and (3) Collaborative Task Understanding, where the model must attribute actions to the wearer versus the coworker. Baseline evaluations use VideoLLaMA3-8B, Intern-VL-2.5, Qwen2.5-VL-7B, and Gemini 2.0 Flash Thinking, with ablations on modality (audio, gaze) and view (ego, exo, joint) to characterise where current SOTA models fail.

## Results

On the Mistake Detection benchmark, zero-shot Gemini 2.0 Flash Thinking (GFT*) achieves the best F1 of 40.9%, substantially outperforming open-source VLMs (VideoLLaMA3: 23.3%, Intern-VL-2.5: 24.2%, Qwen2.5-VL: 24.1%). Fine-tuning an MLP classifier on VLM embeddings improves F1 to ~39% for all three open-source models, but still trails GFT*. On the reasoning-based QA benchmark, GFT* reaches 64.1% overall accuracy versus 57.6–58.2% for the open-source VLMs, while human evaluators achieve 90.0%, highlighting a large remaining gap. For collaborative task understanding, zero-shot GFT* scores 35.2% accuracy and fine-tuned VL3+Transformer reaches 42.1%, indicating that differentiating wearer vs. coworker actions remains highly challenging.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Gemini 2.0 Flash Thinking (GFT*) consistently outperforms all three open-source VLMs across every benchmark. On Mistake Detection (zero-shot), GFT* achieves 40.9% F1 versus 23.3–24.2% for VideoLLaMA3, Intern-VL-2.5, and Qwen2.5-VL — a gap of roughly 17 percentage points. On the QA benchmark, GFT* leads with 64.1% overall accuracy, beating VideoLLaMA3 (58.2%), Qwen2.5-VL (58.1%), and Intern-VL-2.5 (57.6%) by 6–7 points, with its advantage most pronounced on recognition (67.2% vs. ~59%) and analogical reasoning (68.3% vs. ~56–57%) sub-tasks. Fine-tuning open-source VLMs with an MLP or Transformer head narrows the MD gap (MLP: ~39% F1) but does not close it. Mistral-Large2, given the action label as a text hint, scores 61.4% on QA — competitive with GFT* on temporal and analogical sub-tasks but far weaker on recognition (42.8%) and situated reasoning (51.4%). Human evaluators (90.0% on QA, 92.6% on temporal) demonstrate that the tasks are well within human capability, underscoring that current VLMs still struggle with the industrial context, cluttered scenes, and fine-grained temporal reasoning that IndEgo demands.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of egocentric vision and industrial AI, positioning itself against two lineages: (1) large-scale daily-activity egocentric datasets (Ego4D, EPIC-KITCHENS, Ego-Exo4D) that lack industrial complexity, and (2) a small set of industry-like or assistive datasets (Meccano, Assembly101, HoloAssist) that are limited to workstation-bound, single-person, short-horizon tasks. By leveraging the Project Aria device's multimodal sensor suite and enabling two-person collaborative recordings in a real industrial facility, IndEgo extends the egocentric vision task space to cognitively and physically demanding, long-horizon (20+ min), multi-agent industrial scenarios that no prior dataset covers simultaneously. The benchmark design (mistake detection with severity tiers, reasoning-based QA, collaborative action attribution) repurposes existing evaluation paradigms but applies them to a substantially harder and more realistic domain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dataset fills a critical gap: industrial egocentric vision has been underrepresented despite being arguably the highest-value application domain for wearable AI assistants. The baseline results reveal a substantial performance gap between SOTA VLMs and humans (e.g., ~41% F1 vs. 90% accuracy for mistake detection and QA), highlighting that current multimodal models are far from ready for real-world industrial deployment. The context-dependent modality ablations (audio helps in collaborative tasks but hurts in noisy settings; gaze helps only when the error is within the user's field of view) suggest that naive multimodal fusion strategies will not transfer to industrial settings. Key limitations include a single collection facility, 20 participants, partially scripted mistakes, and the absence of error bars, leaving open questions about generalisability across industries, cultures, and skill levels.
