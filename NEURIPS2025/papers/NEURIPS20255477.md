## Problem

In structure-agnostic causal inference, the double machine learning (DML) estimator achieves O(ε² + n^{-1/2}) error for treatment effect estimation, and prior work showed this is minimax-optimal for binary treatment. However, Mackey et al. [2018] demonstrated that for non-Gaussian treatment noise, one can construct higher-order orthogonal moments to improve upon DML, leaving open whether the Gaussian case represents a fundamental statistical barrier or merely an algorithmic limitation. This paper resolves that open question and characterizes precisely when and how the noise distribution enables faster rates.

## Input / Output

The ACE procedure takes as input black-box nuisance estimates ĝ(X) and q̂(X) with L^s error ε₁ and ε₂ respectively, n i.i.d. observations (X_i, T_i, Y_i), and a user-specified order r ≥ 1. It uses sample splitting: the first half D₁ estimates empirical cumulants of the residual T - ĝ(X), while the second half D₂ solves the empirical moment equation to produce θ̂. The output is the treatment effect estimate θ̂ together with a plug-in variance estimate E_var = κ̂_r^{-1}√(V_m/n) that yields an approximate 95% confidence interval [θ̂ - 1.96√E_var, θ̂ + 1.96√E_var].

## Method

The core contribution is a recursive construction of moment functions J_r(w,x) that achieve r-th order Neyman orthogonality: starting from J₁(w,x) = w, each J_r is defined by integrating J_{r-1} and centering, yielding polynomials whose coefficients are functions of the cumulants κ_i of the treatment noise η. When η is independent of X, these cumulants are estimated from the empirical residual distribution at rate O(ε₁^r), and a key lemma shows that the j-th derivative of the estimated J_r has expectation O(ε₁^{r-j}), ensuring r-th order insensitivity to nuisance errors. The ACE estimator then solves the empirical moment equation [Y - q̂(X) - θ(T - ĝ(X))]·Ĵ_r(T - ĝ(X)) = 0 on the held-out sample, achieving O(ε₁^r ε₂ + ε₁^{r+1} + n^{-1/2}) error whenever the (r+1)-st cumulant of η is non-zero. The paper also proves a fundamental Gaussian barrier: when η|X ~ N(0,σ²), no estimator can beat DML even with full knowledge of the noise distribution, established via constrained risk inequalities and Hermite polynomial moment-matching constructions.

## Results

In the synthetic demand estimation experiment (p=100, s=40 sparse linear nuisances, discrete non-Gaussian treatment noise), the fifth-order ACE estimator achieves the lowest RMSE and smallest bias among all orders tested (r=1 through 5), while the first-order ACE (DML) achieves the lowest standard deviation. Fourth-order ACE is omitted from the comparison due to substantially larger error, likely because the fourth cumulant of the discrete noise is near zero. The plug-in 95% confidence intervals constructed from the cumulant-based variance estimate achieve 94.1% empirical coverage (expected 95.0%) across 20,000 independent replicates.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the proposed ACE estimator at orders r=1 through 5 against two established baselines: first-order ACE (identical to DML, Chernozhukov et al. 2018) and second-order ACE (recovering the orthogonal estimator of Mackey et al. 2018). Fifth-order ACE outperforms both baselines on RMSE and bias, confirming that higher-order cumulant-based orthogonality translates into practical gains when the treatment noise is non-Gaussian and independent of X. However, first-order ACE (DML) retains an advantage in variance/standard deviation, reflecting the trade-off between bias reduction and variance inflation at higher orders. The second-order ACE of Mackey et al. [2018] sits between the two, with the paper noting that its theoretical rate at r=3 is actually slower than what ACE achieves, since Mackey et al. did not establish third-order orthogonality. Overall, ACE wins on the primary metric (RMSE) and bias, while DML wins on variance, and the optimal order depends on the sample size and the magnitude of the relevant cumulant κ_{r+1}.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the structure-agnostic estimation (SAE) framework of Balakrishnan et al. [2023] and the minimax optimality results of Jin and Syrgkanis [2024] to the partially linear model of Robinson [1988], asking how the distribution of treatment noise affects optimal estimation rates. It directly builds on Mackey et al. [2018], who showed that non-Gaussian treatment noise permits second-order orthogonal estimators outperforming DML, but left open whether the Gaussian restriction was algorithmic or fundamental. The paper resolves this by proving a method-agnostic Gaussian barrier (Theorem 3.2) and, for non-Gaussian noise, generalizes the second-order construction of Mackey et al. to arbitrary order r via a recursive cumulant-based moment function (the ACE procedure).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Gaussian treatment barrier is a clean negative result: knowing the exact distribution of Gaussian treatment noise provides no benefit over DML, which is counterintuitive given that distributional information typically helps in estimation. For non-Gaussian noise, the ACE procedure achieves arbitrarily high-order robustness to nuisance errors, resolving the open question of whether improvements beyond second order are possible. The main limitation is the independence assumption between noise and covariates, and the paper notes that extending these results to heterogeneous treatment effects or dependent noise remains open. The practical relevance is demonstrated through a synthetic demand estimation experiment where fifth-order ACE outperforms DML in both bias and RMSE.
