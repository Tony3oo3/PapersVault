## Problem

Current LVLMs process entire images at the token level, incurring prohibitive computational costs for high-resolution images and long video sequences. Existing token reduction methods (pruning, fixed-length merging) fail to produce semantically meaningful vision concepts grounded in spatial locations, limiting their applicability beyond visual question answering. The paper identifies the absence of a 'vision concept model'—a mechanism that dynamically determines which vision concepts to extract, how many, and where they are located—based on task-specific instructions.

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image (processed by a CLIP ViT encoder into M vision tokens, typically 576 for LLaVA-1.5) and a text instruction as input, and produces a variable-length set of compressed vision concept tokens (e.g., 144 tokens with S=1/4) along with a generated text response. The number of retained vision concepts is dynamically determined at inference time by the keyword content of the instruction: more specific instructions (with more image-related keywords) yield fewer tokens, while general instructions retain more. The extracted concepts preserve the relative spatial order and positional relationships of the original tokens, and the trained vision encoder simultaneously produces improved dense feature maps usable for downstream tasks like open-vocabulary detection and segmentation.

## Method

VCM is a self-supervised instruction fine-tuning framework with three key components. First, a keyword selector module (multi-head self-attention) identifies image-relevant keywords from instructions and responses, and the difference in keyword counts between instruction and response linearly estimates the target vision concept length L via min-max normalization. Second, a forward-backward dynamic programming algorithm (O(M²) complexity, inspired by HMM decoding) selects which vision tokens to retain, treating the problem as aligning M input tokens to an extended target sequence of 2L+1 symbols (L retained concepts interleaved with blanks), with the VCM loss defined as the negative log-likelihood of the optimal alignment. Third, a parallelized segment merging (SM) operation extracts concept-level features by score-weighted averaging of adjacent retained tokens, achieving a 100× speedup over naive loop-based extraction. Training uses a two-stage protocol: pre-training with a semantic alignment loss to calibrate the keyword selector, followed by instruction fine-tuning with a combined next-token prediction loss and VCM loss weighted by a tanh-smoothed coefficient ε(r) that scales with the random keyword masking ratio r used as data augmentation.

## Results

On 11 image-based VQA benchmarks, VCM with only 144 vision tokens achieves an average of 60.8%, outperforming the full LLaVA-v1.5 (576 tokens, 59.5%) by +1.3% while using 75% fewer vision tokens. VCM also surpasses the strongest token-reduction baselines—VisionZip (59.1%), SparseVLM (57.9%), and PDrop (57.8%)—by 1.7–3.0% on average. The method achieves up to 85% FLOPs reduction for LLaVA-1.5-7B, and further improves dense perception tasks: open-vocabulary detection AP₅₀ rises from 35.2 to 39.2 (OV-COCO) and zero-shot COCO classification Top-1 from 28.3 to 43.8 (ViT-L/14).

## Baselines & Comparisons

VCM (144 tokens) outperforms all token-reduction baselines on average: +2.2% over MQT-LLaVA (144 tokens), +1.7% over VisionZip (192 tokens), +2.9% over SparseVLM (192 tokens), and +3.0% over PDrop (192 tokens). Against the full LLaVA-v1.5 (576 tokens), VCM gains +4.9 on VisWiz, +5.7 on SEED, and +2.5 on MMStar, while incurring small drops of −1.3 on VQAV2 and −19.4 on MME (a 0.9% relative drop). On open-vocabulary object detection (OV-COCO), replacing the CLIP ViT with the VCM-trained version boosts F-VLM's AP₅₀^novel from 9.2 to 12.6 and overall AP₅₀ from 35.2 to 39.2. For open-vocabulary segmentation (ADE-847), Cat-Seg+VCM improves mIoU from 10.8 to 11.9 and mAcc from 20.5 to 23.1. In high-resolution settings (LLaVA-NeXT, 2880→160 tokens), VCM retains 70.1% average vs. 66.2% for VisionZip and 65.9% for PDrop, and on video understanding (Video-LLaVA, 2048→136 tokens) VCM achieves 52.5% average vs. 50.3% for VisionZip.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the LLaVA architecture for vision-language modeling and repurposes the forward-backward algorithm from dynamic programming (Bellman, 1966) to solve the problem of instruction-conditioned, variable-length vision token selection. It departs from prior token reduction methods (FastV, SparseVLM, VisionZip, PDrop, MQT-LLaVA) that rely on fixed pruning ratios, attention-based filtering, or fixed-length query compression, by formally defining a 'vision concept model' that dynamically determines both the quantity and spatial locations of retained tokens based on text priors. The key conceptual advance is treating token selection as a learnable alignment problem with theoretical gradient support, rather than a heuristic or training-free operation, while preserving the relative order and positional information of original tokens.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 85% FLOPs reduction for LLaVA-1.5-7B while maintaining or slightly improving VQA performance makes high-resolution and video LVLM deployment significantly more practical, addressing a core scalability bottleneck. The unexpected side benefit—that self-supervised VCM training on VQA data enhances the vision encoder's dense perception capabilities (zero-shot classification, open-vocabulary detection/segmentation)—suggests the learned concept representations transfer broadly. Open questions include whether the keyword-based length estimation (min-max normalization) is sufficient for fine-grained control, whether the approach generalizes to even higher resolutions or longer video sequences, and whether the adaptive keyword selection introduces systematic biases that limit robustness.
