## Problem

Existing AI benchmarks report only aggregate metrics (accuracy, mAP, success rate) that obscure the underlying distribution of test case difficulty and provide no difficulty-aware, predictive measure of model competency. There is no unified framework to simultaneously quantify per-test-case difficulty, estimate model capability in a way that accounts for what was actually solved, and measure the remaining gap to full task mastery at a defined confidence level.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255292/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system consumes per-test-case performance metrics (e.g., Acc@1, AP, mAP, PDMS) from a set of 7–20 models evaluated on 880–50,000 test cases, converting each metric to a normalized match score in [0,1] via a task-specific scoring function f. It outputs a normal-distribution rating N(μ, σ²) for every test case and every model, from which it derives the test case difficulty distribution, predicted per-test-case performance for any model, the set of long-tail test cases beyond a model's competency threshold, and the competency gap (in rating points) to a hypothetical oracle at 50%, 90%, or 99% confidence.

## Method

AGI-Elo adapts the Glicko rating system to a heterogeneous two-category setting inspired by Item Response Theory: test cases and agents (models or humans) are distinct player types that only compete inter-category, never within their own category. Each player's rating is a Gaussian belief N(μ, σ²) updated via Glicko's impact-factor-weighted formula after every match, where the match score is produced by a task-specific scoring function f that maps the raw metric to [0,1]. Transitivity across the bipartite match graph allows relative rankings within each category to be inferred from shared interactions with the opposing category, so a model failing an easy test case is penalized more than failing a hard one. The converged ratings enable closed-form prediction of a model's expected performance on any test case (via the logistic win-probability model) and estimation of the rating required for an oracle that achieves a target confidence on the hardest test case, yielding a concrete competency gap in rating points.

## Results

Across six datasets spanning vision, language, and action domains, the highest-rated AI models exhibit competency gaps of approximately 233–387 rating points from achieving 50% mastery on the hardest test cases, and 1,031–1,185 rating points from 99% mastery (Table 1). The human expert on NAVSIM achieves near-oracle-level competency with only a 20.7 rating-point gap to the 50% oracle, suggesting current AI models remain substantially below human performance on motion planning. Reliability validation (Table 2) shows Spearman rank correlations of |ρ_t| ≥ 0.9685 for test-case difficulty and ρ_a ≥ 0.9985 for model competency, with MAE ≤ 0.0662 and MSE ≤ 0.0088 across all datasets, confirming strong predictive accuracy of the rating system.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark AGI-Elo against competing evaluation frameworks; instead, it validates the rating system's reliability against traditional aggregate metrics and provides cross-model, cross-year comparisons. On ImageNet, ConvNeXt-Large (2022, rating 2035) surpasses ~85% of test images with ≥50% confidence, compared to AlexNet (2012, rating 1586) which beats only 64%—a 449-point improvement over a decade. The most striking comparison is on NAVSIM, where the human expert achieves a mere 20.7-point gap to the 50% oracle, while the best AI model (rating 2040.5) still has a 232.5-point gap, indicating a substantial AI-to-human competency gap in motion planning. The rating system also uncovers subtle rank-reversal cases invisible to traditional metrics: on ImageNet, ViT-B-16 (Acc. 0.81066) is rated slightly above Swin-T (Acc. 0.81088), and on LiveCodeBench, GPT-4.1-mini (Acc. 0.77019) is rated above o1-mini (Acc. 0.77361), demonstrating that difficulty-aware evaluation can reorder models that appear equivalent under aggregate scores.

## Relation to Prior Work

AGI-Elo synthesizes three established lines of work: the Elo/Glicko rating systems from chess (which model pairwise competitive outcomes with probabilistic uncertainty), Item Response Theory from psychometrics (which jointly estimates item difficulty and examinee ability in heterogeneous matching), and the growing body of AI benchmarking that uses aggregate metrics like accuracy or mAP. The key departure is from prior AI rating systems (Chatbot Arena, AlphaStar evaluations) that only model agent-vs-agent competition, and from per-instance difficulty estimators (loss-based, confidence-based, V-usable information) that are model-specific and inapplicable to non-learning agents. By framing evaluation as inter-category matches between test cases and agents, the paper unifies these threads into a single task-agnostic framework that simultaneously produces interpretable difficulty distributions and model competency scores.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides the field with a concrete, quantifiable answer to 'how far are we from mastering task X?' — the competency gaps of ~1000+ rating points to 99% mastery across all six domains give a shared vocabulary for tracking AGI progress that aggregate accuracy numbers cannot. The finding that a human expert on NAVSIM sits within 20.7 points of oracle-level performance, while the best AI models trail by 233+ points, sharply illustrates the remaining gap in action domains. Limitations include the absence of error bars (citing computational cost), a constrained set of models/datasets that may not capture the full SOTA frontier, and the untested assumption that transitivity holds in heterogeneous agent settings. Open questions include whether the framework generalizes to non-stationary or adversarial test distributions, how to handle tasks where the 'correct' answer is not binary, and whether the rating scale remains meaningful as model capabilities grow.
