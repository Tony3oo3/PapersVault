## Problem

Existing multilingual instruction-following evaluations rely on coarse-grained methods (pairwise comparison, holistic scoring) or are restricted to high-resource languages like English and Chinese, leaving a critical gap in understanding how language resource levels, constraint categories, and cultural specificity jointly affect LLMs' ability to follow constraint-rich instructions across diverse linguistic contexts.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250584/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark provides 558 instructions (translated into 6 languages: English, Chinese, Russian, Arabic, Hindi, Swahili) each augmented with 0–5 constraints from a 5-category/21-dimension taxonomy, yielding 3,348 total instances. At evaluation time, GPT-4o receives the translated instruction, the model's response, the original English instruction, and a set of atomic binary (YES/NO) evaluation requirements as a shared semantic anchor. Outputs are per-requirement binary judgments aggregated into two metrics: Requirement Following Rate (RFR, fraction of satisfied requirements) and Instruction Following Rate (IFR, fraction of instructions where all requirements are met).

## Method

XIFBench is constructed via a three-stage automated pipeline: (1) constraint augmentation using GPT-4o to brainstorm and combine constraints from a taxonomy that deliberately excludes language-dependent constraints (e.g., alliteration, word count) to ensure cross-lingual applicability; (2) requirement structuring where GPT-4o decomposes each instruction into atomic binary evaluation questions and maps them back to the taxonomy; and (3) multilingual expansion via Google Translate with a constraint-level translation validation step that scores semantic preservation per requirement on a 1–5 scale. The key methodological innovation for evaluation is retaining original English requirements as fixed semantic anchors across all languages rather than translating them, which the paper validates improves both agreement with human judgments (94.7% vs. 88.5%) and cross-lingual consistency (std 1.6 vs. 3.1) over translated-requirement baselines. Cultural accessibility annotations on each instruction enable disentangling the effect of culturally specific references from pure resource-level effects.

## Results

On XIFBench (558 instructions, 1,664 evaluation requirements across 6 languages), closed-source models lead in Requirement Following Rate (RFR): GPT-4o achieves 92.2% avg RFR and 72.2% avg IFR, while Gemini-2.0-Flash reaches 92.1% RFR and 74.7% IFR. Open-source models show a steeper cross-lingual decline: Qwen-2.5-72B drops from 90.5% RFR (English) to 40.9% (Swahili), and its IFR collapses from 67.7% to 10.4%. The RFR–IFR gap widens dramatically in low-resource languages (e.g., Hindi: >80% RFR but <40% IFR for several models), indicating that models can satisfy individual constraints but struggle with holistic instruction adherence.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary methodological comparison (Table 3) evaluates three LLM-judge protocols on 1,080 instruction–response pairs across six languages. The proposed protocol (Ours w/ Eng. Reqs., using shared English requirements as semantic anchors) achieves 94.7% average agreement with human annotations (Std 1.6), substantially outperforming Direct Scoring (DS, 60.7% avg, Std 6.5) by ~34 percentage points. It also surpasses the translated-requirements variant (Ours w/ Trans. Reqs., 88.5% avg, Std 3.1) by 6.2 points in average agreement and nearly halves the cross-lingual variance (Std 1.6 vs. 3.1). The DS baseline's deceptively high Swahili score (69.4%) is attributed to poor model performance coincidentally aligning with human labels rather than genuine evaluation reliability. These results validate the paper's core claim that translating evaluation requirements into the response language compromises both reliability and cross-lingual consistency.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the constraint-based instruction-following evaluation lineage (IFEval, FollowBench, InfoBench, CFBench) from its English/Chinese-only scope to a six-language setting spanning high, medium, and low-resource languages. It adopts InfoBench's requirement-based LLM-as-judge protocol and FollowBench/CFBench's multi-category constraint taxonomy, but deliberately includes semantic constraints (style, situation, content) that prior multilingual efforts like M-IFEval and Multi-IF excluded in favor of objectively verifiable format/numerical constraints. The three methodological innovations—cultural accessibility annotation, constraint-level translation validation, and using English requirements as fixed semantic anchors—address reliability gaps that arise specifically when constraint-based evaluation is projected across languages.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

XIFBench is the first constraint-based multilingual benchmark covering the full resource spectrum with both objective and semantic constraints, filling a gap that limits current understanding of where and why LLMs fail at instruction following across languages. The finding that style and situation constraints degrade most sharply in low-resource settings, while format and numerical constraints remain relatively stable, provides actionable guidance for multilingual model development. Key open questions remain: how to translate these diagnostic insights into concrete training improvements, whether reasoning-specialized models (o1, DeepSeek-R1) exhibit different constraint-adherence patterns, and how code-mixing scenarios—common in real multilingual use—shift the picture.
