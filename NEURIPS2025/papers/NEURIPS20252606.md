## Problem

Standard preference-based RL methods rely on the Bradley-Terry model with maximum likelihood estimation, which treats all preference labels as equally reliable and ignores the inherent uncertainty when preferences are collected from diverse populations with conflicting viewpoints. This becomes especially problematic in iterative alignment settings where the policy must adapt to incrementally arriving, potentially inconsistent preference signals from different user groups, yet existing approaches either discard noisy data or lack a principled mechanism to weight preferences by their reliability.

## Input / Output

The method consumes an offline trajectory dataset (state-action sequences from robot manipulation or locomotion environments) along with iteratively updated pairwise preference labels, where in each of four rounds 20% of trajectory pairs receive a 50% label reversal to simulate annotator disagreement. The Beta prior model is a transformer-based network that takes a full trajectory as input and outputs per-trajectory (α, β) parameters defining a Beta distribution over the trajectory's win probability. The final output is an aligned diffusion policy that generates action sequences, with the Beta parameters serving as uncertainty weights that modulate how strongly each trajectory influences the policy update.

## Method

Diff-UAPA formulates preference alignment as a Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) objective that combines a regret-based MLE loss (derived from the MaxEnt RL framework and contrastive preference learning) with an informative Beta prior over trajectory-level advantage values. The key insight is interpreting preference alignment as a voting process: the Beta distribution's variance naturally encodes uncertainty, assigning high confidence to trajectories where the majority of raters agree and low confidence to those with divergent preferences. The Beta prior is learned via variational inference (Dirichlet VAE) using a transformer network, and the diffusion policy is optimized directly without an intermediate reward model by applying Jensen's inequality to handle the intractable marginalization over diffusion paths. Two variants are explored: Diff-UAPA-C trains the Beta model once on all preference data, while Diff-UAPA-I incrementally updates it each round, with the latter showing slight advantages on long-horizon tasks where preferences evolve over time.

## Results

On Robomimic manipulation tasks, Diff-UAPA-C achieves the highest success rates across all four tasks: 56.1% (Lift), 61.3% (Can), 68.1% (Square), and 64.0% (Transport), outperforming the next-best method FKPD by 4.9–11.7 percentage points. On the long-horizon Franka Kitchen task, Diff-UAPA-I reaches 71.7% on the most challenging p4 metric (interacting with 4+ objects), surpassing FKPD (64.1%) and Diff-CPL (63.5%). On D4RL locomotion tasks with real human preferences from Uni-RLHF, Diff-UAPA-C achieves the best episodic rewards: 3399 (HalfCheetah-M-E), 2021 (HalfCheetah-M-R), 1591 (Hopper-M-E), and 933 (Hopper-M-R). In the real-world pick-and-place task, Diff-UAPA achieves a 60% success rate versus 40% for Diff-CPL.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Diff-UAPA consistently outperforms all six baselines across every evaluated task. Against the Gaussian-policy baselines BET and BET-CPL, Diff-UAPA gains 5–13 percentage points on Robomimic tasks and 8–27 points on Kitchen p4, confirming the advantage of diffusion policies for multi-modal action distributions. The strongest diffusion-based competitor, FKPD (Shan et al., 2024), is surpassed by Diff-UAPA-C by 4.9 pp (Lift), 2.8 pp (Can), 3.7 pp (Square), and 11.7 pp (Transport), with the gap widening on the long-horizon Kitchen p4 metric (70.9 vs. 64.1). UA-PbRL (Xu et al., 2025), which uses a distributional reward model with MAP, trails Diff-UAPA by 5.8–10.4 pp on Robomimic and 8.6 pp on Kitchen p4, indicating that bypassing reward learning and directly aligning the diffusion policy with a Beta prior is more effective. Under noisy preference settings (Table 4), robust methods RIME (Cheng et al., 2024) and R3M-DPO (Bukharin et al., 2024) achieve competitive but lower rewards than Diff-UAPA-I (e.g., 3674 vs. 3505 and 3397 on HalfCheetah-M-E under stochastic noise), as their filtering strategies discard informative but inconsistent preferences that Diff-UAPA's Beta prior can still leverage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the direct preference alignment framework for diffusion policies (building on CPL from Hejna et al. 2024 and the chain-reward formulation from Wallace et al. 2024) by replacing the standard MLE objective with a MAP objective that incorporates a learned Beta prior. The Beta prior and variational inference approach are adapted from Xu et al. (2025), which applied a similar MAP framework to distributional reward models; here, the key departure is bypassing reward learning entirely and applying the prior directly to the diffusion policy's trajectory-level advantage. The iterative preference alignment setting (Definition 3.1), where preferences arrive sequentially from different user groups, generalizes the standard static PbRL formulation and motivates the online adaptation of the Beta prior.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically important gap: real-world preference data from diverse populations is inherently inconsistent, yet most PbRL methods assume a single coherent preference signal. The Beta prior provides an interpretable uncertainty mechanism (framed as a voting process) that down-weights conflicting preferences without discarding data, which is more principled than filtering-based robustness approaches. Key limitations include the offline trajectory assumption (no environment interaction), the added computational cost of the Beta model, and evaluation limited to relatively low-dimensional state-based tasks. Open questions include extending to the fully online setting, scaling to high-dimensional visual observations, and understanding how the Beta prior behaves when preference groups have systematically different (rather than randomly noisy) biases.
