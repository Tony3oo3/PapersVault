## Problem

Despite significant advances in LLMs for code generation, no dataset exists specifically for evaluating or training AI models on quantum algorithm design. Existing quantum circuit benchmarks (QASMBench, MQTBench, VeriQBench) target NISQ hardware evaluation or software tooling rather than AI capability, and prior AI-for-quantum work addresses niche problems like quantum control or property prediction rather than general algorithm design. The core challenge is the highly flexible design space of quantum algorithms, which demands precise mathematical reasoning, correct gate-level circuit construction, and proper classical post-processing—none of which are captured by existing resources.

## Input / Output

The benchmark feeds LLMs a hand-crafted problem description (natural language + LaTeX formulas), 1 or 5 in-context examples, and oracle/gate definitions packaged as black-box .inc files that preserve the oracle's hidden structure while remaining compilable. The model must produce a complete OpenQASM 3.0 circuit file plus a Python post-processing function (for algorithm design tasks) that simulates the circuit and extracts the final answer. Verification is fully automated: a three-part score (QASM syntax pass/fail, Python syntax pass/fail, semantic correctness via simulation against ground truth) is computed without human inspection, and efficiency ratios (gate count, shot count, execution time relative to reference) are also reported.

## Method

QCircuitBench formulates quantum algorithm design as a structured code-generation task with a 7-component framework: problem description, generation code, algorithm circuit (.qasm), post-processing function, oracle/gate definition (.inc), verification function, and dataset creation script. The dataset spans 3 task suites (oracle construction, quantum algorithm design, random circuit synthesis), 25 algorithms, and 120,290 data points, available in both Qiskit+OpenQASM and Cirq versions. A key design principle is the 'paradox of oracle implementation': oracles are exposed as black-box gates in the circuit while their explicit gate definitions live in a separate .inc file, preserving the theoretical black-box abstraction while enabling compilation and automatic verification. For evaluation, the paper benchmarks GPT-4o, Llama3-8B, Qwen 2.5, and DeepSeek-R1 using few-shot prompting with k-fold cross-validation to prevent data leakage, reporting BLEU, byte perplexity, multi-criteria verification scores, and efficiency metrics. As a preliminary training study, Llama3-8B is fine-tuned via QLoRA (8-bit quantization, LoRA rank 16, α=32 on Q/V projections) on the oracle construction and random circuit tasks, showing that fine-tuning helps the model internalize structural patterns (e.g., selective CX gate placement) but does not uniformly outperform few-shot learning.

## Results

On QCircuitBench's quantum algorithm design task, GPT-4o with 5-shot prompting achieves the best average semantic score of 0.2006, substantially outperforming all open-source models (Llama3-8B: 0.0323, Qwen 2.5: 0.0998, DeepSeek-R1: 0.0102) and even the human baseline (0.1374). However, all models struggle with complex algorithms: QFT, Generalized Simon's Problem, and Shor's Algorithm yield near-zero semantic scores across all models. In fine-tuning experiments, Llama3-8B's BLEU score improves from 39.6 to 46.3 after QLoRA fine-tuning, but its aggregated verification score actually worsens from −0.43 to −0.53, revealing that fine-tuning does not consistently outperform few-shot learning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4o (5-shot) dominates all open-source baselines on average semantic score (0.2006 vs. 0.0998 for Qwen 2.5, 0.0323 for Llama3-8B, and 0.0102 for DeepSeek-R1), and also surpasses the human baseline (0.1374). GPT-4o's advantage is most pronounced on simpler algorithms: it achieves perfect 1.0000 semantic scores on Bernstein-Vazirani and Deutsch-Jozsa with 5-shot, while all other models score below 0.23 on these tasks. DeepSeek-R1 underperforms its 1-shot counterpart (0.0102 vs. 0.0126) with 5-shot, attributed to its long-chain reasoning style exceeding the context window before producing a complete solution. Qwen 2.5 shows the strongest open-source performance (0.0998 avg), driven primarily by its QAOA score (0.0998), but remains near-zero on most other algorithms. In the fine-tuning comparison, Llama3-8B fine-tuned via QLoRA improves BLEU (39.6→46.3) and reduces perplexity (1.25→1.14) over its few-shot baseline, yet its aggregated verification score degrades (−0.43→−0.53), indicating that memorized patterns do not translate to functional correctness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of LLMs and quantum computing by formulating quantum algorithm design as OpenQASM code generation, a task absent from prior literature. It departs from Quantum Machine Learning datasets (MNISQ, NTangled) that use quantum hardware to process data for classical ML, and from AI-for-quantum work (QDataSet, LLM4QPE, diffusion-based circuit synthesis) that targets niche control or tomography problems. It also distinguishes itself from existing quantum circuit benchmarks (QASMBench, MQTBench, VeriQBench), which evaluate NISQ hardware or compilation tools rather than AI model capabilities, and which omit the classical post-processing and oracle-construction components central to algorithm design.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

QCircuitBench provides the first structured, automatically verifiable benchmark for evaluating LLMs on quantum algorithm design, revealing that even state-of-the-art models (GPT-4o, DeepSeek-R1) achieve near-zero semantic scores on most non-trivial algorithms. The finding that QLoRA fine-tuning does not consistently outperform 5-shot prompting on verification scores is a useful negative result suggesting that domain-specific code generation may require more than parameter-efficient adaptation. Open questions include whether quantum-specific inductive biases or hybrid neural-symbolic architectures could close the gap, and whether the dataset's framework generalizes to truly novel algorithm discovery rather than reproduction of known circuits.
