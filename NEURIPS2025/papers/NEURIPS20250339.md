## Problem

Traditional training data attribution methods, including influence functions, assume deterministic training algorithms and convex objectives, ignoring the fundamental stochasticity of deep learning (random initialisation, mini-batching) that makes training produce a distribution over final models rather than a single one. This paper asks what influence should mean in this stochastic setting and whether existing tools like IFs can be rigorously justified beyond their original convex-statistics derivation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250339/cropped_page1_idx4.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a trained model from a stochastic training run, the full training dataset, a measurement function (e.g., loss on a test example), and optionally multiple random seeds for ensemble-based estimation. It produces distributional influence scores quantifying how much removing an example shifts the distribution over model outputs (via mean shift, variance increase, or Wasserstein distance), approximate samples from the post-removal output distribution without retraining, and data pruning recommendations. A key distinction from classical TDA is that outputs are distributions over measurements rather than single predicted values, and the method explicitly accounts for the correlation structure between the original and perturbed distributions.

## Method

The paper introduces distributional TDA (d-TDA), which frames training as sampling from a distribution μ_D over final model weights and defines influence as a difference function Δ between m_#μ_D and m_#μ_{D\z_k}. The central theoretical result shows that influence functions are 'secretly distributional': Theorem 2 proves unrolled differentiation converges almost surely to IFs (plus a Hessian-nullspace component) under mild non-convexity assumptions via a Markov chain analysis of the response dynamics, while Theorem 3 shows IFs are the asymptotically KL-optimal transport map between low-temperature Boltzmann distributions with and without the loss perturbation. Practically, the Markov chain formulation (Eq. 6) enables computing the unrolled differentiation response at linear time and constant space, and IFs serve as a cheaper principled proxy for the full unrolled response in d-TDA. The paper demonstrates that distributional influence (e.g., Wasserstein) captures information missed by mean-only influence, and that d-TDA-based data pruning outperforms fixed-seed TDA on CIFAR-10 with SWIN transformers.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with a SWIN Vision Transformer, d-TDA data pruning (removing 5000 datapoints predicted to decrease validation loss most on average across 10 seeds) yields greater average test accuracy improvements over the full-data baseline than fixed-seed TDA, which removes datapoints most influential for a single model. On UCI Concrete, the correlation between unrolled differentiation predictions and influence function predictions reaches approximately 90% at late training times, validating the theoretical convergence result (Theorem 2). On ArtBench with a latent diffusion model, Wasserstein influence and mean influence identify substantially different sets of most/least influential training examples compared to classical fixed-seed influence.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary empirical comparison is between d-TDA (distributional influence averaged over multiple seeds) and fixed-seed TDA (standard influence functions applied to a single trained model) for data pruning on CIFAR-10 with a SWIN transformer. d-TDA consistently achieves greater average test accuracy improvements over the full-data baseline, while fixed-seed TDA also improves over baseline but by a smaller margin (Figure 6). The second comparison is against the classical influence function framework from Koh & Liang (ICML 2017), which the paper shows is 'secretly distributional' — IFs emerge as the asymptotic limit of unrolled differentiation (Theorem 2) and as the KL-optimal transport map between Boltzmann distributions (Theorem 3), providing a new theoretical justification for their effectiveness in non-convex deep learning settings. Against the diffusion model attribution method of Mlodozeniec et al. (ICLR 2025), the paper demonstrates that distributional (Wasserstein) influence identifies different and arguably more informative training examples than the fixed-seed influence used in that prior work (Figure 7). The paper also reframes the commonly reported near-zero leave-one-out correlation of IFs as a signal-to-noise issue rather than a fundamental failure, showing that with sufficient samples the predicted and true LOO distributions correlate well (Figure 8).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the classical influence functions framework (Koh & Liang, 2017) and unrolled differentiation (Hara et al., 2019; Bae et al., 2024), but departs fundamentally from their deterministic framing by treating training stochasticity as central rather than a nuisance. It introduces distributional TDA (d-TDA) as a formal framework where the goal is to predict changes in the distribution over trained models, and shows that IFs emerge as asymptotic limits of unrolled differentiation (Theorem 2) and as KL-optimal transport maps between Boltzmann distributions (Theorem 3)—all without the convexity assumptions typically cited in IF derivations. The paper also connects to the stochastic approximation literature (Kushner & Yin) and the Boltzmann/Langevin dynamics literature to ground its theoretical results.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The reframing of IFs as inherently distributional provides a long-missing theoretical justification for their empirical success in non-convex deep learning, and the distributional influence metric captures variance changes that mean-based methods miss (e.g., examples that increase uncertainty without shifting the mean). Practical demonstrations on CIFAR-10/SWIN data pruning and latent diffusion model attribution show tangible utility, and the proposed distributional LDS offers a more informative evaluation metric for TDA methods. Open questions include whether the Boltzmann distribution assumption holds for very large-scale training, whether the theoretical convergence results (requiring decaying learning rates) transfer to practical constant-LR training, and whether distributional metrics become standard practice as models grow larger and stochasticity effects become more pronounced.
