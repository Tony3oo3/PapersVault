## Problem

Despite PID controllers being used in over 90% of industrial control applications, their design relies on heuristic tuning rules without provable guarantees on optimality, convergence, or stability. While policy gradient methods for control (e.g., LQR) have recently achieved global optimality guarantees, the PID parameterization has received essentially no attention in the RL-for-control literature, leaving a gap between the practical ubiquity of PID and the theoretical rigor available for other control architectures.

## Input / Output

The model-based algorithm (PG4PID) consumes trajectory data to estimate system matrices A and B via least-squares system identification, then solves the discrete Riccati equation to compute the value matrix P_K at each iteration; the model-free algorithm (PG4PI) requires only the known output matrix C and sampled trajectories, bypassing all model estimation. Both algorithms operate on scalar-input, scalar-output systems (m=p=1) with state vectors x_t ∈ R^n, and output a 3-dimensional PID parameter vector (K_P, K_I, K_D) or 2-dimensional PI vector (K_P, K_I) that fully determines the control action u_t = -K_P y_t - K_I z_t - K_D (y_{t+1}-y_t)/τ. The augmented state g_t = [x_t; z_t] has dimension n+1, where z_t accumulates past outputs for the integral term.

## Method

The paper reformulates the discrete-time PID control problem as an LQR problem in an augmented state space (Proposition 2.1), where the integral state z_t is appended to the system state, and the PID parameters map to a single gain K via a nonlinear transformation involving F_D = [1 + K_D CB/τ]^{-1}. From this equivalence, exact closed-form gradient expressions are derived for J(K̃) with respect to each PID parameter (Theorem 3.1), enabling direct gradient descent in the 3-dimensional PID space. Two algorithms are developed: a model-based method (PG4PID) that estimates A, B, and P_K from data and performs exact gradient descent, achieving linear convergence to global optimality via gradient dominance; and a model-free method (PG4PI) that wraps the deterministic PI policy in a Gaussian stochastic policy with variance σ²/(t+1)², applies the policy gradient theorem, and achieves approximate global optimality with O(ε^{-3} log(1/ε)) sample complexity. The key theoretical insight is proving gradient dominance in the PID parameters (not just in K), which requires bounding the Jacobian M_PID of the parameter transformation, and establishing weak gradient dominance under the stochastic policy for the model-free case using only local (not global) Lipschitz properties.

## Results

The paper presents results primarily as reward and tracking curves (Figures 1–2) rather than a numerical summary table. On both the 8-dimensional Chemical Reactor and 48-dimensional LA University Hospital environments, PG4PID (Algorithm 1) and PG4PI (Algorithm 2) rapidly converge to zero reward (i.e., optimal cost), while PPO requires substantially more samples before stabilizing and retains noticeably worse tracking performance. PG4PI achieves zero steady-state tracking error on the Chemical Reactor, in contrast to the non-zero residual error of model-free LQR. Under a small model perturbation (‖ΔA‖ = 0.5), model-based LQR loses stability entirely, whereas the model-free PI policy maintains both stability and reference tracking.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against PPO (StableBaselines3 with a state-to-PID-parameter policy), both PG4PID and PG4PI converge to near-optimal reward orders of magnitude faster in sample count on both the Chemical Reactor and LA University Hospital environments; PPO's tracking error remains visibly larger throughout training because it searches a much higher-dimensional, non-convex policy space where gradient dominance does not hold. Against model-free LQR (PG method from Fazel et al. 2018), PG4PI wins on two fronts: (i) it achieves zero steady-state tracking error on the Chemical Reactor due to the integral action, while LQR leaves a persistent non-zero tracking offset; and (ii) it is robust to a ‖ΔA‖ = 0.5 perturbation of the system matrix, under which LQR becomes unstable, whereas PG4PI continues to stabilize and track the reference. The paper does not report a single scalar metric (e.g., final reward or MSE) in a tabular form, so the comparisons are assessed from the plotted reward curves, tracking-error traces, and state-evolution plots.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously disconnected research threads: policy gradient methods for control (Fazel et al. [18], Bu & Mesbahi [13], Yaghmaie et al. [46]) and optimization-based PID tuning (Polyak & Khlebnikov [37]). It extends the continuous-time PID-to-LQR equivalence of [37] to discrete time, then leverages the gradient dominance framework of [18] to prove global optimality in the PID parameter space via a chain-rule argument. The model-free algorithm follows the stochastic-to-deterministic policy learning paradigm of Montenegro et al. [33] but handles the less restrictive local Lipschitz properties inherent to LQR-type objectives, and the model-based algorithm builds on the system identification approach of [46] while requiring fewer matrix estimates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PID controllers account for over 90% of industrial control applications, yet their tuning has remained heuristic without theoretical guarantees on optimality or convergence. This paper provides the first globally convergent policy gradient algorithms for PID/PI tuning with explicit convergence rates and sample complexity bounds, closing a gap between the rich PG-for-control theory and the most widely deployed controller architecture in practice. Key limitations include the scalar input/output assumption, the requirement that the output matrix C be known, and the fact that the model-free algorithm only yields PI (not full PID) controllers. Open questions include extending to multi-objective specifications, hard state/output constraints, and safety-constrained PID policies.
