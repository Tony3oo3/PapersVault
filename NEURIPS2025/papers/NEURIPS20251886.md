## Problem

Existing generative model benchmarks measure producibility (quality and breadth of outputs) but not steerability—whether a user with a specific goal can actually guide the model to produce what they want. Current human-AI interaction evaluations conflate these two properties, making it impossible to diagnose whether poor performance stems from the model's capabilities or its interface. The paper asks how to measure steerability independently of producibility and whether it can be improved.

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes a goal image sampled from the model's own producible set (guaranteeing the target is producible) and a human user who iteratively steers the model over 5 attempts. For text steering, the user provides prompts; for image steering, the user selects from suggested image variations generated by perturbing the latent space. Outputs include human-rated similarity scores (10-point scale), satisfaction ratings (4-point scale), improvement rates, and prompt-output misalignment scores, each judged by separate annotators from the steerers.

## Method

The paper introduces a mathematical decomposition separating model performance into a producibility gap (how well the model's producible set covers desired outputs) and a steerability gap (how well humans can guide the model toward its best producible output), with the benchmark isolating steerability by sampling the goal from the model's own output distribution. A second decomposition (Equation 4) further separates steerability differences between models into steering-mechanism improvements versus producible-set differences, estimated via ML predictors trained on CLIP embeddings of prompts and images. For improving steerability, the paper proposes image steering: instead of text prompt refinement, the model suggests image variations by perturbing the latent space with a learned mixture scale z' = √(1/(s²+(1-s)²))·((1-s)z + sε), where the optimal perturbation magnitude per round is learned via Thompson Sampling RL using a DreamSim-based simulated human agent that always selects the most similar suggestion. This simple mechanism achieves over 2× improvement over text steering across both general and abstract geometric pattern domains.

## Results

Across 10 text-to-image models, steerability is uniformly poor: annotators rate attempted reproductions as dissatisfactory 60% of the time (27% 'very unsatisfied' vs. 10% 'very satisfied'), and the average improvement rate after 5 steering attempts is only 62% (vs. a 50% random baseline). The best model, Photon-flash, achieves an improvement rate of 0.74, while the worst, DALL-E 2, scores 0.60; the average Prompt-Output Misalignment (POM-1) of 0.54 means that more than half the time, a user's text description matches the goal image better than the model's output. A blind LLM rewriting prompts without seeing the goal image recovers 52% of human improvement with the same number of attempts, and 87% with 20 attempts. The proposed image-steering mechanism (learned proposals) more than doubles improvement over text steering: 74.2% improvement rate vs. 54.7% on PixelProse, and 70.7% vs. 56.2% on abstract tile patterns.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All 10 text-to-image models perform poorly on steerability, with improvement rates ranging from 0.51 (SD3-large) to 0.74 (Photon-flash) and an average of only 0.62. Photon-flash is the clear leader on improvement rate, while DALL-E 2 and DALL-E 3 are among the weakest (0.60 and 0.58 respectively), and notably DALL-E 3 does not outperform its predecessor. Within the Stable Diffusion family, larger models (SD3.5-large, SD3.5-large-turbo) marginally outperform smaller ones (SD3-large), but differences are modest. Professional prompt engineers improve final similarity by only 10% over non-experts and show even lower improvement rates, indicating that expertise does not substantially close the steerability gap. The most striking comparison is between text steering and the proposed image steering: learned image proposals achieve a 74.2% improvement rate (0.053 avg DreamSim gain) versus 54.7% (0.025) for text steering on PixelProse, and 70.7% (0.072) vs. 56.2% (0.029) on tile patterns — more than a 2× improvement in both domains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out a new conceptual space by formally decomposing generative model evaluation into producibility (what a model can generate) and steerability (whether a user can actually reach a desired output), a distinction that prior human-AI interaction benchmarks (Lee et al., Jahani et al., Vodrahalli & Zou) implicitly conflate. The benchmark design—sampling a goal from the model's own producible set and asking users to reproduce it—borrows the ideal-point reward structure from political science (Downs, 1957) and applies it to generative model evaluation. The image steering mechanism draws on latent-space perturbation ideas from diffusion model literature and uses a Thompson Sampling-based RL procedure reminiscent of RLHF, but applied to the steering distribution rather than the model itself.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that all ten state-of-the-art text-to-image models yield dissatisfactory reproductions 60% of the time, and that a blind LLM rewriting prompts achieves 52% of human improvement, fundamentally challenges the assumption that iterative prompting is a meaningful user strategy. The 2× improvement from a simple image-suggestion mechanism (on SD 1.4) demonstrates that the problem is addressable with modest engineering, pointing toward a new design axis for generative model interfaces. Open questions include whether these results hold for more sophisticated steering (inpainting, ControlNet, multi-turn dialogue), whether the decomposition generalizes to video or 3D generation, and how steerability evolves over longer time horizons as users build mental models of specific systems.
