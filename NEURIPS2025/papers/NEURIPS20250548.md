## Problem

Standard RLHF collects only binary preference labels, capturing direction but not the magnitude of utility differences (preference strength), which is critical for decision-making under uncertainty and reward model generalization. Implicit strength signals such as response times or inter-annotator agreement are available but noisy and confounded by systemic factors like individual annotator speed, making them unreliable for global comparison. The paper addresses how to robustly extract preference strength from such locally-valid but globally-confounded signals without requiring explicit strength annotation.

## Input / Output

The method consumes pairwise comparisons (a_i, b_i) with a binary preference label p_i, a scalar strength signal τ_i (e.g., response time, inter-annotator agreement, or episode return difference), and optional metadata m_i (e.g., annotator ID) used for stratification. Comparisons are normalized to (winner, loser) tuples, sorted by ascending strength signal within each stratum, and augmented with a virtual anchor element of fixed score 0 to form target rankings. The output is a neural-network-parameterized utility function u_θ mapping items to scalar utilities, from which signed utility differences s_θ(w,l) = u_θ(w) − u_θ(l) encode both preference direction and strength.

## Method

ResponseRank stratifies comparisons by metadata (e.g., per-annotator) so that the strength signal is only compared locally within homogeneous groups, mitigating systemic confounders. Within each stratum, comparisons are sorted by the strength signal (e.g., ascending response time) and a virtual anchor with fixed score 0 is appended, forming a target ranking for a Plackett-Luce model. The model predicts signed utility differences s_θ(w_i, l_i) = u_θ(w_i) − u_θ(l_i) and is trained by minimizing the PL negative log-likelihood, which enforces that predicted strengths are positive (matching the normalized winner-loser format) and ordinally consistent with the strength ranking. Crucially, the method requires only a monotonic (ordinal) relationship between the signal and true strength within each stratum, avoiding rigid link-function assumptions. Theorem 1 proves that for a single comparison per stratum, the PL loss reduces exactly to the Bradley-Terry binary cross-entropy loss, making ResponseRank a strict generalization of BT that seamlessly handles mixed strength-ranked and choice-only data.

## Results

On synthetic preference learning with inter-stratum RT variability (100% data), ResponseRank achieves PDC of 0.67 (Deterministic), 0.56 (DDM), and 0.68 (Stochastic), consistently outperforming the Bradley-Terry baseline (0.53, 0.50, 0.53 respectively). ResponseRank matches full-data BT performance using only 61–83% of the training data, demonstrating improved sample efficiency. On the MultiPref language modeling task, the RR-Agree variant (using inter-annotator agreement as strength signal) improves RewardBench 2 performance by +3.0 percentage points over BT and matches final BT performance with ~30% fewer preference pairs. In RL control (HalfCheetah, Swimmer, Walker2d, Highway), ResponseRank-trained reward models generally increase downstream policy performance, though differences often fall within the confidence interval.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ResponseRank consistently outperforms the Bradley-Terry baseline on PDC (the paper's primary metric for preference strength recovery) across all three synthetic data-generating paradigms when inter-stratum RT variability is present: +0.14 (Deterministic), +0.06 (DDM), and +0.15 (Stochastic) at full data. The regression-based RtRegression baseline, which assumes a known global link function between RT and utility difference, underperforms both ResponseRank and BT in the presence of per-stratum RT multipliers (e.g., 0.23 vs. 0.67 on Deterministic), confirming that ResponseRank's local, ordinal-only approach is more robust to confounded signals. The ResponseRank-Pool ablation (omitting stratification) degrades substantially (e.g., 0.42 vs. 0.67 on Deterministic), validating the importance of stratification. The ResponseRank-Perm control (randomized RTs) drops to near-BT levels, confirming that gains stem from informative strength signals rather than modeling artifacts. On MultiPref, RR-Agree (inter-annotator agreement) achieves +3.0 pp on RewardBench 2 over BT, while RR-RT performs poorly, suggesting RT is not a useful strength signal under MultiPref's complex annotation protocol. In RL control tasks, ResponseRank generally improves reward model accuracy and downstream policy returns, but the small scale (5000 queries, 5 runs) means differences are often within the confidence interval.

## Relation to Prior Work

ResponseRank generalizes the Bradley-Terry model (which it formally reduces to in the single-comparison case, Theorem 1) by replacing the binary cross-entropy objective with a Plackett-Luce ranking loss over strength-ordered comparisons within strata. It departs from prior response-time integration methods (e.g., Li et al. 2024, Clithero 2018) that assume a global parametric link between RT magnitude and utility difference, instead requiring only a local monotonic relationship within homogeneous partitions. The stratification-and-rank construction is the key conceptual departure: by discarding absolute signal values and retaining only within-stratum ordinal order, the method avoids modeling the signal-generation process entirely, making it applicable to any locally valid strength proxy (RT, inter-annotator agreement, episode returns).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap in RLHF: standard binary preference labels discard strength information that is crucial for decision-making under uncertainty and reward model generalization, yet explicit strength annotation is costly and unreliable. ResponseRank's stratification approach is attractive because it requires no additional annotation and makes minimal assumptions (only local monotonicity), but the MultiPref results show that response time—a natural and widely available signal—can be uninformative under complex annotation protocols, leaving open which real-world signals reliably carry strength information. Validation at scale for language-model policy optimization (beyond RewardBench correlation) and handling of tied preferences remain important open questions.
