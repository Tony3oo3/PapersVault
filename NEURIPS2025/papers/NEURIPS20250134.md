## Problem

Second-order optimizers such as Levenberg-Marquardt are essential for achieving high-accuracy PINN solutions, particularly for nonlinear PDEs, but their Jacobian matrix scales quadratically with parameter count, making them infeasible for large models. Domain decomposition is the standard scalability strategy for PINNs, yet existing approaches either fix the decomposition a priori, require problem-dependent interface conditions, or have not been combined with second-order training. The paper addresses the simultaneous challenge of making second-order PINN training scalable, adaptive to the PDE solution, and free of interface losses.

## Input / Output

The model consumes spatial-temporal collocation points sampled adaptively from a learned probability distribution (rather than a fixed grid), along with the PDE differential operator, source term, and boundary/initial condition functions. Beyond the PDE solution itself, the model jointly outputs a learned domain decomposition in the form of ball centers and radii (quartic RBF parameters) and a corresponding sampling density, both of which are updated during training via a minimax loop. The collocation points are resampled at each iteration from the evolving distribution, so the effective input geometry changes throughout training.

## Method

PINN BALLS is a Mixture-of-Experts ensemble where each expert is a local PINN whose influence is restricted to a compact ball via quartic radial basis functions, forming a partition of unity. The compact support of each basis function ensures the global Jacobian is sparse, reducing the Levenberg-Marquardt update from quadratic to linear memory in the number of parameters and enabling exact second-order training at scale. The domain decomposition is trained jointly with the PINN through Adversarial Adaptive Sampling: the sampling distribution is parameterized by the same RBFs, and a gradient-ascent step on the minimax objective simultaneously adapts the ball placement and the collocation sampling toward high-residual regions. The paper proves convergence of the AAS minimax problem under both Dirichlet-energy and entropy regularization, showing the optimal sampling distribution converges to uniform as residuals vanish. Training alternates a global LM step on the PINN parameters (exploiting Jacobian sparsity via CSR format) with Adam steps on the RBF parameters, with an exponentially decaying learning rate for the latter to balance exploration and convergence.

## Results

On the Helmholtz equation, PINN BALLS(5) achieves a relative L² error of 2.8×10⁻⁹, outperforming XPINN (1.2×10⁻³) and APINN (1.2×10⁻³) by roughly six orders of magnitude. On Burgers' equation, PINN BALLS(5) reaches 2.3×10⁻⁴ relative L² error, substantially beating XPINN (1.3×10⁻¹) and APINN (9.1×10⁻⁴). On the 2D incompressible Navier-Stokes equations (flow past a cylinder, Re=100), PINN BALLS converges to a solution where standard PINN implementations fail entirely, with accuracy improving as the number of submodels increases. All results are reported with comparable total parameter counts (~5000 per model) and trained on a single NVIDIA A10 GPU in double precision.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PINN BALLS decisively outperforms both XPINN and APINN on the Helmholtz equation, achieving relative L² errors of 2.8×10⁻⁹ (5 balls) and 1.3×10⁻⁹ (20 balls) versus 1.2×10⁻³ for both baselines — a gap of approximately six orders of magnitude. On Burgers' equation, PINN BALLS(5) (2.3×10⁻⁴) beats XPINN (1.3×10⁻¹) by nearly three orders of magnitude and APINN (9.1×10⁻⁴) by roughly a factor of four. However, PINN BALLS(20) (1.7×10⁻³) underperforms APINN on Burgers, which the authors attribute to the fact that APINN and XPINN use a fixed domain split at x=0 (the exact shock location), whereas PINN BALLS learns its decomposition autonomously without any a priori knowledge of the solution structure. On the Navier-Stokes benchmark, no direct numerical comparison is provided, but the paper notes that standard PINN implementations fail to converge on this problem, while PINN BALLS successfully captures the vortex shedding dynamics thanks to the global second-order LM update combined with the sparse DD structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three active threads in PINN research: second-order optimization (Levenberg-Marquardt, building on Bonfanti et al. [4]), sparse domain decomposition via basis functions (following FBPINN [23]), and adversarial adaptive sampling (AAS, from Tang et al. [33]). The key departure is the first combination of all three: a trainable, RBF-based domain decomposition whose centers and radii are updated via AAS gradient ascent, while the submodels are trained with a global LM step whose Jacobian is sparse due to the compact support of each ball. The theoretical contribution extends AAS convergence guarantees from a hard-to-implement Lipschitz constraint to the more practical Dirichlet-energy and entropy regularizations, and proves that the maximizing sampling distribution converges to uniform as residuals vanish.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that second-order methods—which prior work [4] showed are essential for high-accuracy PINN solutions—have been blocked from scaling beyond a few thousand parameters by quadratic memory growth; PINN BALLS removes this bottleneck by making the LM Hessian sparse and parallelizable through local submodels. The learned DD eliminates the need for problem-specific interface conditions or a priori domain partitioning, which is critical for industrial PDEs with complex geometry. Open questions include the sensitivity of DD initialization, the lack of GPU-optimized sparse solvers, whether the number of experts should be dynamically adjusted, and whether Wasserstein-based updates for the sampling distribution would improve convergence in highly nonlinear settings like Navier-Stokes.
