## Problem

Neural scaling laws are empirically robust but mechanistically unexplained, and no complete model connects them to RL settings. The quantization model links LLM power-law scaling to Zipf's law of task frequencies, but whether this mechanism extends to reinforcement learning—and whether it can explain inverse scaling (performance degrading with size)—remains unexplored.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251957/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis consumes board-state observation tensors (piece positions only, no move history) from self-play games of trained AlphaZero MLP agents across four games, plus an inference-time MCTS temperature parameter T that modulates the state distribution. Outputs are Zipf exponents fitted to state-frequency rank curves, Bradley-Terry scaling exponents (γ ∝ N^α_N), per-rank value-head loss against alpha-beta-pruning ground truth, and Elo scores. A key constraint is that state identity is defined by the observation tensor, so states with identical piece positions but different histories are collapsed into one.

## Method

The paper first verifies that AlphaZero self-play state frequencies follow Zipf's law (α ≈ 0.8–1.0) and shows this emerges from the branching-tree structure of board games via a constant-branching-factor toy model whose plateau distribution smooths into a power law under non-uniform policies. It then uses inference temperature T as a controlled knob: lowering T bends the Zipf curve (forming plateaus of repeated games) while simultaneously shifting the size-scaling exponent, establishing a causal link between the state-distribution exponent and the scaling-law exponent in the temperature range where policy quality is unchanged. Per-rank value-loss analysis reveals agents optimize states in descending frequency order, counter-intuitively achieving worse loss on easier (higher-rank) states. Finally, the paper identifies inverse scaling in Oware and Checkers as arising from converging game trees that concentrate high frequency on strategically trivial late-game states; larger models overfit these states while forgetting crucial early-game openings, a mechanism attributed to target non-stationarity and dormant-neuron plasticity loss.

## Results

AlphaZero agents across four board games (Connect Four, Pentago, Oware, Checkers) produce state-frequency distributions following Zipf's law with exponents α ≈ 0.8–1.0, consistent with human game data. Value loss on individual states increases smoothly with state rank (i.e., higher-frequency states have *higher* loss), confirming the quantization model's prediction that agents optimize in descending order of frequency. In Oware and Checkers, inverse scaling is observed: Elo score scales positively with model size up to a threshold, then reverses, coinciding with an anomalous Zipf curve where high-frequency late-game states (with only 2–3 pieces remaining) dominate the distribution. Large Oware models achieve progressively better loss on late-game states while their loss on early-game states degrades, with the loss spike at the early/late transition reaching up to an order of magnitude for smaller models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against competing methods but instead compares AlphaZero agents of varying sizes (MLP nets with 3 hidden layers at exponentially-increasing widths) across four games. Connect Four and Pentago exhibit clean power-law scaling of Bradley-Terry strength γ with both parameters N and compute C, while Oware and Checkers show inverse scaling beyond a critical model size. Ground-truth values from an alpha-beta pruning solver confirm that larger agents achieve significantly lower absolute value loss, and that the time to solve a state via alpha-beta pruning drops exponentially with state rank (i.e., high-frequency states are the hardest to solve). Temperature modulation (T < 0.5) demonstrates a causal link between the Zipf exponent and the scaling-law exponent: the two change together only in the temperature range where policy quality is preserved (probability of optimal move remains constant), ruling out policy degradation as a confound. The key contrast is between games with expanding game trees (Connect Four, Pentago: clean scaling) and games with contracting trees (Oware, Checkers: inverse scaling), where the latter's win condition (capturing all opponent pieces) funnels play into a small set of high-frequency end-game configurations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the quantization model of neural scaling (Michaud et al., 2023), which links Zipf's law to power-law scaling in LLMs, to the reinforcement learning setting of AlphaZero. It builds directly on the Bradley-Terry scaling laws for AlphaZero established by Neumann & Gros (2022) and Jones (2021), and connects the Zipf-like state distributions previously observed only in human chess and Go data (Blasius & Tönjes, 2009; Georgeot & Giraud, 2012) to the branching-tree structure of board games. The paper departs from prior concept-learning analyses of AlphaZero (McGrath et al., 2022; Lovering et al., 2022) by measuring loss on up to 10^5 unique states rather than a handful of hand-crafted concepts, and from Ruoss et al. (2024) by working in an RL rather than supervised-learning setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first mechanistic explanation for inverse scaling in AlphaZero, attributing it to converging game-tree structures that inflate the frequency of strategically trivial late-game states, combined with target non-stationarity and dormant-neuron plasticity loss. This suggests a concrete, testable intervention: frequency-informed curriculum learning could prevent large RL agents from overfitting on unimportant states. Key limitations include the use of raw game states as a proxy for task quanta (which the authors acknowledge is imperfect), restriction to four board games with MLP architectures, and the non-trivial gap between value loss and the multi-agent Elo metric. Open questions include whether the mechanism generalizes to non-board-game RL, to transformer-based agents, and whether identifying true task quanta would sharpen the Zipf-to-scaling connection.
