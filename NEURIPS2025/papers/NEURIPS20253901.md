## Problem

Interpretable next-token prediction models, exemplified by Infini-gram, trail black-box LLMs by roughly 30 percentage points because they rely on exact n-gram matching against a fixed reference corpus and cannot adapt to distribution shifts or minor input variations like rephrasings and typos. Meanwhile, mechanistic interpretability has identified induction heads as a key in-context learning mechanism in LLMs, but no framework has repurposed this mechanism to build a transparent, auditable prediction system.

## Input / Output

For language modeling, GIM takes a text sequence of up to 1024 tokens as input context and produces a next-token probability distribution computed as a similarity-weighted count over tokens following matched n-grams within that context, using a sliding window of size k (32 for GPT-2, 64 for LLaMA-2). For fMRI prediction, the input is a continuous English podcast story with corresponding BOLD recordings at 2-second TR intervals across ~95,000 cortical voxels; GIM retrieves the prior timepoint whose text yields the highest cosine similarity to the current next-token distribution, then uses the top-100 principal components of the fMRI response change at that timepoint (plus three time-lagged duplicates) as features for a linear encoding model.

## Method

GIM is a retrieval-based induction head that searches the input context for the best match to the query suffix, then takes the token(s) following the match as the prediction. It combines exact n-gram matching with a fuzzy matching component: a small 3–4-layer transformer (the Fuzzy Matching Model) trained via knowledge distillation from LLaMA2-7B using combined cross-entropy and reverse KL-divergence losses outputs feature embeddings whose cosine similarity (with temperature T=0.1) serves as a soft count for each candidate next token. When integrated with Infini-gram, a threshold τ on effective n (8 for GPT-2, 9 for LLaMA-2) routes each prediction to exact reference matching, exact in-context matching, or fuzzy in-context matching, prioritizing in-context estimates when they are at least as specific as reference estimates. The key design choice is that the neural component scores similarity between input phrases rather than generating outputs, so every prediction remains directly attributable to specific retrieved context spans.

## Results

On language modeling (LLaMA-2 tokenizer), Infini-gram+GIM with OpenWebText achieves 43.2% next-token accuracy on BabyLM-test (+23.1%p over Infini-gram alone) and 42.1% on Pile-val (+15.0%p), narrowing the gap with LLaMA2-7B (62.2% and 64.4% respectively). GIM (fuzzy) alone, using only the 1024-token input context, reaches 42.7% on BabyLM-test, outperforming Infini-gram built on 9–10B reference tokens. In the fMRI setting, GIM matching achieves a mean test correlation of 0.087 across all voxels (20% improvement over the Eng1000 baseline of 0.072) and 0.265 on top-10% voxels, within 1% of the black-box LLaMA-2 encoding (0.268).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Infini-gram [14], GIM delivers substantial gains: up to +25.1%p on BabyLM-test and +20.0%p on Pile-val (GPT-2 tokenizer), or +23.1%p and +15.0%p (LLaMA-2 tokenizer), with the largest improvements occurring when the reference corpus distribution diverges from the test data. Against the black-box LLaMA2-7B [74], GIM still trails by 19–22%p on BabyLM-test and 22–23%p on Pile-val, confirming that interpretability still carries a performance cost, though the gap is far smaller than Infini-gram's original 30%p deficit. In the fMRI domain, GIM matching [71] outperforms the Eng1000 interpretable baseline by 20% in mean correlation (0.087 vs. 0.072 all voxels; 0.265 vs. 0.220 top-10% voxels), while all other matching baselines (random, naive n-gram, Infini-gram matching) fail to improve over Eng1000. GIM matching reaches within 1% of the black-box LLaMA-2 encoding on top-10% voxels (0.265 vs. 0.268), demonstrating that a fully interpretable retrieval-based module can nearly match a 70B-parameter model on this neuroscience task.

## Relation to Prior Work

GIM sits at the intersection of two previously separate lines of work: scalable n-gram language modeling (Infini-gram, suffix-array-based retrieval) and mechanistic interpretability of induction heads in LLMs (Olsson et al., Akyürek et al.). Rather than treating induction heads as a post-hoc explanation of black-box behavior, the paper reimplements them as a standalone, transparent retrieval module that combines exact n-gram matching with a lightweight fuzzy similarity function distilled from a teacher LLM. This departs from kNN-LM-style approaches, which augment black-box models with nonparametric retrieval, by making the retrieval mechanism itself the entire predictor, enabling token-level attribution. For the fMRI setting, GIM extends the Eng1000 encoding-model framework by adding a context-sensitive matching component that retrieves prior neural response changes associated with semantically similar text.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a simple, fully auditable retrieval module can close a substantial fraction of the performance gap between interpretable and black-box language models (25%p over Infini-gram, reaching within ~5%p of GPT-2 on BabyLM), challenging the assumption that interpretability necessarily entails large performance sacrifices. The fMRI application is particularly notable for showing that interpretability enables scientific insight—attributing predicted neural responses to specific linguistic features across cortical regions—that black-box encodings cannot provide. Key limitations include continued underperformance versus larger LLMs (13%p behind LLaMA-2-7B), sensitivity to context length and informativeness, and the n-gram-based reasoning ceiling for tasks requiring deeper composition. Open questions include whether GIM can be extended to other mechanistic components (retrieval heads, indirect object identifiers) and whether the in-context matching advantage generalizes to domains with very short or noisy contexts.
