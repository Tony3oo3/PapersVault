## Problem

Existing LLM-assisted time series methods inject overly broad textual knowledge (dataset statistics, timestamps, conceptual descriptions) that lacks the discriminative power of task-specific decision-making indicators (e.g., spindles, slow waves, RR intervals) critical for medical signal interpretation. Moreover, no principled mechanism exists to robustly integrate potentially suboptimal text prompts with time series, so when prompts are imprecise the series-text synergy collapses and the LLM's inferential capacity is underutilized. The paper formalizes this as a biased marginal-likelihood estimation problem in joint series-text decoding.

## Input / Output

The method takes multichannel physiological time series (EEG/EOG at 100 Hz in 30 s epochs, 12-lead ECG at 500 Hz in 5 s windows, or 9-axis wearable motion at 50 Hz in 2.56 s windows) paired with a structured indicator-guided text prompt built from semi-automated extraction of task-relevant features (spindle/slow-wave presence for sleep; RR, PR, QRS, PP intervals for ECG). The prompt is not a free-form description but a template containing task instruction, signal statistics, and indicator relationships, which is then perturbed via 40% token masking across m=10 Monte Carlo samples. Output is a single class label (5 AASM sleep stages, binary arrhythmia, or 6 activity classes) produced by a GPT-2 XL decoder head.

## Method

InDiGO frames joint series-text decoding as marginal-likelihood estimation and approximates the optimal prompt t* via masked Monte Carlo importance sampling from a simple proposal distribution q(t|t₀) centered on the initial indicator-guided prompt, avoiding the severe bias of a single fixed text (Laplace approximation). Architecturally, a frozen BIOT encoder embeds series patches, the first 24 layers of GPT-2 XL encode masked text variants, and a bidirectional co-attention Series-Text Interactor (STI) captures cross-modal relevance. Training jointly optimizes three objectives: task cross-entropy, a match-alignment loss (InfoNCE-style contrastive alignment pulling series and text representations together), and a diversity-optimization loss (entropy maximization of the proposal distribution) that encourages broader prompt-space exploration. This alignment-plus-diversity scheme dynamically evolves the proposal distribution toward the true posterior during fine-tuning, making the model robust to initial prompt quality while activating the LLM's reasoning capacity through richer series-text interactions.

## Results

InDiGO achieves 89.04% accuracy and 80.53 Macro F1 on Sleep-EDF-20, 86.79% accuracy and 81.12 Macro F1 on Sleep-EDF-78, 86.02% balanced accuracy and 92.31 AUC-PR on PTB-XL, and 95.83% balanced accuracy on UCI HAR, consistently outperforming all 19 baselines across all four medical benchmarks. The largest gains appear on sleep staging (e.g., +4.46% accuracy over Brant-X on EDF-20) and arrhythmia detection (e.g., +6.39% AUROC over BIOT on PTB-XL). InDiGO also leads in few-shot (5%/10% data) and zero-shot transfer settings, and maintains competitive efficiency (788s training, 144s inference at m=10) compared to baselines like SimMTM (2333s training) and TimeLLM (237s inference).

## Baselines & Comparisons

InDiGO outperforms all 19 baselines across all four datasets with no losses. Among general time-series models, it surpasses BIOT (the strongest general encoder) by +7.18% accuracy on EDF-20 and +6.39% AUROC on PTB-XL, while LLM-based methods Time-LLM and KEDGN trail by 8.73% and 14.15% accuracy respectively on EDF-20, confirming that generic prompt templates (dataset statistics, timestamps, variable descriptions) are insufficient for medical signal interpretation. Among sleep-specific classifiers, Brant-X is the closest competitor at 84.58% accuracy on EDF-20, yet InDiGO still leads by +4.46% accuracy and +5.62% Kappa. On PTB-XL, InDiGO's 82.98% AUROC exceeds the next-best BIOT (76.59%) by a substantial 6.39 points, while on HAR (which lacks explicit waveform indicators), InDiGO achieves 95.83% balanced accuracy versus BIOT's 94.61%, demonstrating robustness even without task-specific physiological cues. Statistical significance testing (MANOVA) confirms that nearly all comparisons reach p<0.001, with the exception of BIOT on HAR which still achieves p<0.01.

## Relation to Prior Work

InDiGO carves new ground at the intersection of LLM-assisted time series prompting and medical signal interpretation. Prior LLM-time series works (Time-LLM, AutoTimes, KEDGN) inject generic textual knowledge—dataset statistics, timestamps, or variable properties—into prompts, while task-specific medical models (SleepHGNN, L-SeqSleepNet, Brant-X) encode domain knowledge through architecture design or loss functions. InDiGO departs from both by (1) grounding prompts in clinically defined decision-making indicators (spindles, RR intervals, QRS morphology) extracted via semi-automated tools, and (2) framing the joint series-text decoding problem as a marginal likelihood estimation task where suboptimal prompts are corrected through masked Monte Carlo importance sampling with alignment and diversity objectives, rather than assuming a single handcrafted prompt suffices.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical bottleneck in deploying LLMs for medical time series: the gap between what generic prompts provide and what clinicians actually use to interpret signals. The importance-sampling formulation offers a principled, bias-aware alternative to ad-hoc prompt engineering, and the consistent gains across sleep staging, arrhythmia detection, and activity recognition (including few-shot and zero-shot settings) suggest the approach generalizes beyond waveform-rich domains. Key limitations include the dependence on semi-automated indicator extraction (requiring domain expertise and tools like YASA or NeuroKit2), the assumption that a small set of indicators captures sufficient discriminative information, and evaluation limited to four datasets. Open questions include whether the framework extends to domains lacking clear waveform indicators, how it scales to longer multi-session recordings, and whether the indicator extraction step can be fully automated or learned end-to-end.
