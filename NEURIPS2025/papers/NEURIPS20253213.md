## Problem

Diffusion models can either memorize their training data verbatim or generalize to produce novel samples, but the precise conditions governing this transition remain theoretically uncharacterized. The training loss inherently promotes memorization—a sufficiently powerful model will always reproduce training points exactly—yet well-trained models do not always memorize, and no predictive theory exists to determine when memorization becomes predominant as a function of model capacity, data complexity, and sample size.

## Input / Output

The laboratory takes training data drawn from a K-component Gaussian mixture in R^d (with N = poly(d) samples) and trains a Gaussian mixture denoiser with M components (K ≤ M ≤ N) via the standard DDPM training loss. The diffusion process starts from Gaussian noise and produces generated samples through a DDIM sampler; the key analytical output is a predicted crossover point M* (a linear function of N) that identifies the model size at which the phase transition from generalization to memorization occurs. A second setting uses low-rank Gaussians constructed from FashionMNIST templates with random color vectors to mimic natural image structure.

## Method

The paper introduces a 'memorization laboratory' where both the generalizing denoiser (K components matching the true distribution) and the fully memorizing denoiser (N components matching training samples) are special cases of the same M-component Gaussian mixture model class, enabling direct loss comparison. The central technical contribution is deriving tight high-dimensional asymptotics for the excess training losses of a generalizing denoiser (Theorem 3.1: Θ(dσ²/(ψ_t σ² + 1))) and a partially memorizing denoiser that memorizes the first M of N samples (Theorem 3.2: Θ((1 − M/N) dσ²)), using Gaussian concentration bounds and softmax sparsity arguments. Comparing these losses yields a crossover point M* ≈ N{1 − E_t[λ(t)/(ψ_t σ² + 1)] / (C · E_t[λ(t)])} that is linear in N, and the paper hypothesizes that gradient descent training will produce memorizing behavior precisely when M ≥ M*. Experiments validate this by showing the predicted crossover matches the empirically observed phase transition to within 1–2 components of M, with train/test regression error below 2×10⁻⁴.

## Results

The paper's central quantitative result is a predictive formula for the memorization phase transition: M_pt ≈ (4/5)N, derived from the crossover point where the partially memorizing denoiser's training loss equals the generalizing denoiser's loss (Eq. 14). The regression predicting the phase transition location from the theoretical loss crossover achieves train and test errors ≤ 2×10⁻⁴ across a grid of (N, d, K) configurations, meaning the predicted M_pt matches the empirically observed transition within one or two components on average. The phase transition itself is sharp: the memorization ratio (fraction of generated samples within c=1/9 of their nearest training neighbor) jumps from near 0 to near 1 over a narrow range of model capacity M, with the transition bracketed by memorization ratios of 1/10 (start) and 9/10 (end).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing memorization-detection methods or alternative generative architectures in the traditional sense. Instead, its internal 'baselines' are the three reference denoisers within the Gaussian mixture model class: the fully generalizing denoiser x̄_★ (K components matching the true distribution), the partially memorizing denoiser x̄_pmem,M (M components, each a point mass at a training sample), and the fully memorizing denoiser x̄_mem (N point masses). The trained denoiser x̄_θ is shown to track the generalizing denoiser's loss when M < M_pt and the partially memorizing denoiser's loss when M > M_pt, with the crossover at M_pt ≈ (4/5)N serving as the phase transition boundary. The theoretical loss approximations (Theorems 3.1 and 3.2) are validated against empirical losses at d=50, K=12, N=200 (Figure 1), showing remarkable agreement. The low-rank Gaussian image model (Section 4.2) is compared qualitatively to the isotropic case, confirming the same phase transition structure persists under more realistic data geometry.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the DDPM training framework (Ho et al., 2020) and DDIM sampling (Song et al., 2020) but departs from prior theoretical work on diffusion model memorization by introducing a 'partially memorizing denoiser' as a surrogate model whose training loss, compared against the generalizing denoiser's loss, predicts the phase transition. While statistical physics approaches (Biroli et al., 2024; Ambrogioni, 2023) identify critical transitions in diffusion model dynamics, and implicit-bias analyses (Kamb and Ganguli, 2024; Vastola, 2025) explain generalization through underparameterization, this work provides the first analytically tractable, predictive criterion for the memorization-generalization boundary in terms of concrete problem parameters (M, N, d, K). The Gaussian mixture model setting connects to prior work on learning GMMs via diffusion objectives (Shah et al., 2023; Gatmiry et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024c), but the focus on the M→N regime where the model class can represent the memorizing denoiser is a deliberate extension.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that the memorization phase transition location is a simple linear function of N (M_pt ≈ 4/5 N) with prediction error within 1–2 components provides a rare clean, testable theoretical prediction for a phenomenon that has been observed empirically but never quantitatively characterized. This framework offers a principled path toward understanding copyright and privacy risks in deployed diffusion models, moving beyond heuristic detection metrics. Key limitations include the restriction to Gaussian mixture data (isotropic and low-rank), the assumption of well-separated cluster centers, and the fact that the loss-weighting λ must be fitted empirically rather than derived from first principles. Open questions include whether the linear scaling persists for non-Gaussian data, replicated samples, or architectures beyond GMM denoisers, and whether the framework can be extended to predict memorization in text-to-image models with conditional generation.
