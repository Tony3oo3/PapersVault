## Problem

Existing medication recommendation systems, including recent LLM-based approaches, rely on point-wise prediction that scores each drug independently, overlooking synergistic effects and adverse drug-drug interactions (DDIs) that are critical in multimorbidity cases. This paper argues that prescription generation should be formulated as a list-wise sequential decision problem where each drug addition or removal is a distinct step subject to joint safety and efficacy constraints, and that standard outcome-level reinforcement learning (e.g., GRPO) provides too coarse a credit signal for such fine-grained decisions.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a patient's full EHR context—structured ICD diagnosis and procedure codes, demographic fields, historical visit records, and unstructured clinical notes (history of present illness, past medical history, allergies, medications on admission)—and produces an ordered medication set for the current visit. Structured codes are not fed as raw tokens but are fused with collaborative embeddings from domain-specific encoders (RAREMed for patient-level, MICRON for diagnosis/procedure-level, Mole-BERT for medication-level) that are projected into the LLM's embedding space via learnable linear adapters. At inference, the output is generated through a two-stage cascade: a binary drug-level classifier first filters the candidate drug space, then a list-wise policy performs explicit add/remove edits on the filtered list.

## Method

FLAME formulates prescription generation as a Markov decision process where each step adds or removes a single drug, and introduces step-wise GRPO—a novel extension of Group Relative Policy Optimization that decomposes the LLM output into per-drug decision steps and assigns each step a potential-based advantage derived from the difference of a state potential function (combining Jaccard similarity to ground truth, DDI risk from the interaction graph, and out-of-vocabulary refusal rate). This potential-based reward shaping provides dense, token-level training signals while provably preserving the optimal policy (Theorem 4.1, via Ng et al.'s invariance lemma). The framework is trained in three stages: SFT of a drug-level binary classifier (π_cls), SFT of a list-wise editing policy (π_list) initialized from π_cls, and step-wise GRPO fine-tuning of π_list with a KL-regularized objective. Multi-source knowledge fusion injects collaborative embeddings from four domain-specific encoders into the Llama3.1-Aloe-Beta-8B backbone's token space via learnable projectors, enabling the model to leverage both textual semantics and structured co-occurrence signals simultaneously.

## Results

On MIMIC-III, FLAME achieves a Jaccard score of 0.4836 and F1 of 0.6408, outperforming all 9 baselines. The closest competitor, the LLM-based LAMO, scores 0.4701 Jaccard and 0.6294 F1, while the best traditional method (RAREMed) reaches 0.4174 Jaccard and 0.5776 F1. FLAME also produces the most calibrated prescription size (22.24 drugs vs. ground-truth 23.43) and demonstrates superior safety-accuracy trade-offs and cross-dataset generalization on MIMIC-IV and eICU.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FLAME outperforms all 9 baselines on both Jaccard and F1 on MIMIC-III. Against the strongest LLM-based baseline LAMO (0.4701 Jaccard, 0.6294 F1), FLAME gains +0.0135 Jaccard and +0.0114 F1, attributed to its list-wise decision modeling and multi-source knowledge fusion. Against the best traditional method RAREMed (0.4174 Jaccard, 0.5776 F1), FLAME achieves a substantial +0.0662 Jaccard improvement. Among traditional baselines, instance-based LEAP performs worst (0.3073 Jaccard), while longitudinal methods (GAMENet, SafeDrug, COGNet, MoleRec) cluster in the 0.35–0.41 range. In safety-accuracy trade-off evaluation (Fig. 5a), FLAME consistently dominates the Jaccard-DDI Pareto frontier across varying DDI penalty levels, whereas LAMO lacks any safety adjustment mechanism. On temporal generalization (MIMIC-III → MIMIC-IV) and external generalization (eICU), FLAME degrades less than structured-data baselines and surpasses LAMO, confirming the robustness of its language-based representations and collaborative knowledge integration.

## Relation to Prior Work

FLAME extends GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) from sequence-level to step-level credit assignment by introducing potential-based reward shaping grounded in the policy invariance theorem of Ng et al. (1999), enabling fine-grained per-drug feedback during list generation. It departs from the dominant point-wise paradigm in medication recommendation (LEAP, GameNet, SafeDrug, and even the LLM-based LAMO) by reframing prescription generation as a sequential add/remove decision process, and from prior GRPO extensions (DAPO, StepGRPO) by using a potential function that guarantees policy invariance rather than ad-hoc step-level accuracy rewards. The multi-source knowledge fusion strategy builds on the collaborative-embedding-injection paradigm of COLLM and LLaRA, adapted to the clinical domain with patient-, diagnosis-, procedure-, and medication-level embeddings from RAREMed, MICRON, and Mole-BERT.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The controllable safety-accuracy trade-off enabled by the DDI penalty in the potential function addresses a practical clinical need that point-wise models cannot satisfy, and the step-wise GRPO mechanism is a general-purpose technique potentially applicable to other structured generation tasks requiring fine-grained credit assignment. However, the absolute improvements over the strongest LLM baseline (LAMO) are modest (~1.3% Jaccard), the theoretical result is a direct restatement of Ng et al.'s known invariance property, and the framework's reliance on an 8B medical LLM with LoRA plus multi-stage training limits immediate deployability in resource-constrained settings. Open questions include whether the list-wise formulation scales to longer prescriptions, how the approach performs under truly dynamic clinical workflows, and whether the GPT-4o-dependent preprocessing pipeline can be replaced with an end-to-end differentiable alternative.
