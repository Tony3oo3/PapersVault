## Problem

Prior work on strategic behavior on learning platforms models each user as independently best-responding to a fixed deployed model, ignoring that users' actions are coupled and collectively shift the data distribution from which the platform retrains. This paper introduces look-ahead reasoning, where users anticipate the behavior of other participants and how those behavioral changes will alter future model predictions, and asks how such reasoning reshapes learning dynamics, equilibria, and the potential benefits of coordination.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252757/cropped_page7_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes as input a base population distribution D₀, a learner's loss function ℓ, an agent utility function u, and a distribution map (strategy h) that maps base data points to modified ones. Outputs are characterized analytically: a unique performatively stable equilibrium θ*, a convergence rate bound of the form O([Σ c^k α_k]^t) for mixed level-k populations, and a benefit-of-coordination bound B ≤ Φ²/(2γ) where Φ is a Hessian-weighted inner product of the agent-utility gradient and the learner-loss gradient at the selfish equilibrium. The alignment metric Φ and the scaling metric β serve as interpretable quantities that predict when coordination or collective growth is beneficial.

## Method

The paper formalizes level-k thinking from behavioral economics in the learning-platform setting: a level-k agent best-responds to the model that would result from a population of level-(k−1) thinkers, with level-0 agents being non-strategic. The key technical step is a recursive sensitivity analysis showing that the distribution map at level k has sensitivity (εβ/γ)^(k−1)·ε, which yields a contraction factor Σ(εβ/γ)^k α_k and proves that deeper thinking accelerates convergence without changing the equilibrium. For collective reasoning, the population acts as a Stackelberg leader optimizing U(h) = E_{z~D_h}[u(z, A(D_h))], and the benefit of coordination is bounded via a PL-inequality argument under a linearity assumption on the distribution map, yielding B ≤ Φ²/(2γ). The alignment term Φ captures the Hessian-filtered overlap between the agent-utility gradient and the learner-loss gradient, identifying when coordinated data modifications can steer the model toward a more favorable equilibrium. Heterogeneous-population extensions characterize when scaling up a fixed strategy is beneficial (via metric Ψ) and show that larger collectives stabilize learning dynamics by reducing the effective sensitivity of the non-participating fraction.

## Results

The paper's main results are theoretical. Theorem 3 shows that repeated retraining with a mixed population of level-k thinkers converges at rate O([Σ(εβ/γ)^k α_k]^t), meaning deeper reasoning accelerates convergence. Corollary 1 establishes that the equilibrium utility is identical regardless of the level of selfish reasoning (U(h^(1)) = U(h^(k)) for all k ≥ 1). Theorem 5 bounds the benefit of coordination B ≤ Φ²/(2γ), where Φ is an alignment term measuring the inner product of the population's utility gradient and the learner's loss gradient at the selfish equilibrium, filtered through the inverse Hessian. Empirically, on a credit-scoring simulator, alignment Φ accurately predicts the benefit of coordination, and in the zero-sum case (λ=0), collective utility decreases monotonically with collective size α.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external baselines but instead contrasts two modes of reasoning: selfish (level-k) and collective (coordinated). Selfish level-k thinking, regardless of depth k, converges to the same equilibrium as level-1 (standard strategic classification) and yields identical utility (Corollary 1), so deeper individual reasoning provides no long-run benefit. Collective reasoning, by contrast, can strictly improve utility: in the label-modification example (Example 1), the benefit of coordination equals the suboptimality of the labeling function and can be arbitrarily large. In the zero-sum empirical setting (λ=0), small collectives (α→0) achieve the highest utility because they can act nearly independently of the model, while larger collectives suffer as the learner's response to their coordinated actions creates a counter-force. In the aligned setting (λ>0), the alignment metric Ψ from Proposition 6 correctly predicts whether scaling up a fixed strategy increases or decreases participant utility, with the sign of Ψ matching the direction of the utility change across all tested collective sizes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends strategic classification (Hardt et al., 2016) and performative prediction (Perdomo et al., 2020) by introducing a multi-agent, forward-looking dimension: agents no longer best-respond to a fixed model but anticipate how the population's collective actions will shift the model in future retraining rounds. It formalizes level-k thinking from behavioral economics (Nagel, 1995) within this learning-platform setting and positions algorithmic collective action (Hardt et al., 2023) as a special case of look-ahead reasoning with full coordination. The key departure from prior work is treating the distribution map as endogenous to the agents' reasoning depth rather than as a fixed, exogenous quantity, and contrasting selfish level-k equilibria with coordinated Stackelberg equilibria under a unified framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that deeper selfish reasoning accelerates convergence but leaves the equilibrium utility unchanged is a clean negative result: 'outsmarting' peers offers no long-run individual benefit, redirecting attention toward coordination as the only path to improved outcomes. The alignment term Φ provides a concrete, computable diagnostic for when collective action will be effective, which has practical implications for labor organizations and advocacy groups contesting algorithmic systems. Open questions include how these results extend under imperfect information (finite data, model misspecification), heterogeneous agent types beyond level-k, and whether the convexity/linearity assumptions can be relaxed to cover deep learning settings. The empirical validation is limited to a single credit-scoring simulator with logistic regression, so generalizability to more complex learners remains untested.
