## Problem

Neural networks are known to approximate smooth functions well but struggle to generalize perfectly on discrete, discontinuous operations such as binary arithmetic. Prior theoretical work has established expressiveness (existence of parameter configurations) or PAC-style learnability bounds, but has not shown that standard gradient-based training can learn to execute algorithmic instructions exactly. This paper asks whether two-layer fully connected networks in the infinite-width limit can be trained to execute binary-encoded algorithmic instructions—permutations, addition, multiplication, and the Turing-complete SBN instruction—exactly, with high probability, using only a logarithmic number of training examples.

## Input / Output

Training inputs are orthonormalized, block-partitioned vectors in R^{k'} where each block corresponds to one template from a finite set of local bit-level rules; the total number of training examples is O(b), linear in the bit length ℓ (e.g., 40 examples for 10-bit addition). Test inputs are block-partitioned vectors where each block is either the zero vector or exactly matches one training template's block, so the total number of possible test inputs is O(2^b), exponentially larger than the training set. The network produces a real-valued output vector that is thresholded at zero (positive → 1, non-positive → 0) to yield the next binary state of the algorithm; this encode-execute-round loop is iterated to reproduce full algorithm execution.

## Method

The core contribution is a template-matching framework in which each algorithm is decomposed into local, block-scoped instructions (e.g., half-adder rules for addition, shift-and-add rules for multiplication) that are composed iteratively to execute the full computation. Training data is constructed by orthonormalizing these templates so that the NTK matrix takes a scaled-identity-plus-rank-1 form, which isolates a 'signal' weight for matched templates from an 'interference' weight for unmatched ones. Under a mild assumption bounding the number of unwanted correlations below the signal-to-interference ratio, the NTK predictor's sign pattern exactly recovers the ground-truth binary output. To convert this sign guarantee into exact bit-level correctness with high probability, an ensemble of N independently trained infinite-width networks is averaged and rounded; Gaussian concentration yields N = O(k' log k') per step, and a union bound over all test inputs and iterations gives total ensemble complexity O(ℓ²) for the four tasks studied.

## Results

The paper proves that two-layer ReLU networks in the infinite-width limit can exactly execute binary permutations, addition, multiplication, and SBN instructions using only O(ℓ) training examples (logarithmic in the 2^ℓ possible inputs). For 10-bit addition, just 40 training examples suffice to cover all 4^10 ≈ 1M test inputs. The ensemble complexity (number of independently trained networks needed for high-probability exactness) scales as O(ℓ²) for all three arithmetic tasks (Remark 6.1), with the decision margin for addition being 4 versus at most 1 unwanted correlation per coordinate. Figure 5 confirms the O(ℓ²) growth rate numerically, with the theoretical and empirical estimates matching in asymptotic slope.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no head-to-head empirical comparisons against baseline methods. The `compares_to` set is empty because the contribution is a proof of exact learnability rather than a performance benchmark. The paper positions itself against three categories of prior work: (1) NTK-based generalization results (e.g., Boix-Adsera et al. [3] for Transformers on pattern matching), which provide distributional guarantees but not exact execution; (2) expressiveness/simulation results (e.g., Siegelmann & Sontag [34] on RNN Turing-completeness, Pérez et al. [28] on Transformer Turing-completeness), which show existence of parameter configurations without addressing learnability via gradient descent; and (3) empirical arithmetic-learning studies (Neural GPU [16], NALU [35], NAU/NMU [19]), which demonstrate practical generalization but lack formal sufficient conditions. The authors claim this is the first NTK-based proof of exact learnability for these discrete algorithmic tasks, filling a gap that neither the PAC-learning nor simulation literatures address.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the NTK framework (Jacot et al., 2018) and the specific infinite-width gradient-flow analysis of Lee et al. (2019) to study a fundamentally different setting: exact learnability of discrete, algorithmic tasks rather than smooth function approximation. It departs from prior NTK work that provides generalization bounds for continuous targets, and from simulation-based results (e.g., RNNs or Transformers being Turing-complete) that establish expressiveness without addressing gradient-based learnability. The key methodological departure is the template-matching data structure, which orthonormalizes training inputs to isolate bit-level rules and control NTK correlations, enabling a sign-preservation argument that prior NTK analyses for discrete tasks have not provided.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first NTK-based proof that shallow feed-forward networks can learn to execute discrete algorithmic instructions exactly with logarithmically many examples, and since SBN is Turing-complete, the framework in principle extends to all computable functions. The result provides precise sufficient conditions (Assumption 5.1 on unwanted correlations) under which exact execution is guaranteed, filling a gap between expressiveness proofs and PAC-style learnability that neither addresses gradient-based training. Key limitations include the infinite-width assumption, the strong orthogonality requirement on training data, the bounded-memory setting (no automatic length generalization), and the need for an ensemble of O(ℓ²) models. Open questions include whether exact learning persists under correlated training examples, whether the framework extends to variable-length architectures (RNNs, Transformers, GNNs), and whether the template structure can be inferred from partial input-output traces rather than being explicitly designed.
