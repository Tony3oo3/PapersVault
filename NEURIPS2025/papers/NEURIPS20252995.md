## Problem

Many high-dimensional nonconvex problems (over-parameterised networks, matrix factorisation, phase retrieval) have solution sets that form smooth manifolds rather than isolated points, yet there is no systematic framework characterising when and how reparametrisations that encode this known structure lead to provable improvements in optimisation conditioning. While reduction mappings are used heuristically in practice, it was unclear under what geometric conditions they strictly improve smoothness and sharpness constants, and by how much, for both affine and nonlinear mappings.

## Input / Output

The method takes a nonconvex C² objective f: Rⁿ → R whose minimiser set S is a non-isolated C¹ submanifold, together with a C² reduction mapping Ψ: R^{n₁} → R^{n₂} that encodes the known geometric structure of the x₂ variables at optimality (e.g., an affine projection, a nonlinear parametrisation, or an implicit argmin solution). It produces a reduced objective F(x₁) = f(x₁, Ψ(x₁)) on R^{n₁} with explicitly bounded improvements: a strictly smaller smoothness constant β_F, a strictly larger Morse-Bott (equivalently PL) constant μ_F, and a strictly better condition number κ_F. The improvements are first established under the pullback metric R = DΦᵀDΦ induced by the embedding Φ(x₁) = (x₁, Ψ(x₁)), and then translated to Euclidean bounds with an explicit metric distortion factor M^(Φ) = λ_max(DΦᵀDΦ).

## Method

The core mechanism is a geometric restriction argument: the smoothness of the reduced function F equals the largest curvature of f along the feasible manifold M_F, and when the dominant curvature directions of f (the top singular subspace Σ_max) are uniformly non-tangent to the tangent space of M_F (quantified by a parameter ε ∈ (0,1]) with a spectral gap Δ_max, the restricted Hessian has strictly smaller operator norm, yielding β_F ≤ β_f − Δ_max(2ε − ε²). For nonlinear Ψ, an additional curvature correction term C(x₁) = D²Ψ(x₁) · ∇_{x₂}f arises from the bending of the feasible manifold, controlled by bounds QZ/m^(Φ) on the product of the Hessian of Ψ and the partial gradient of f; the improvement is preserved whenever this correction is smaller than the curvature gap. The Morse-Bott constant is improved by an analogous argument on the positive-definite Hessian restricted to the normal space of S, giving μ_F ≥ μ_f + Δ_min(2ε − ε²). Combining both yields a strictly better condition number κ_F < κ_f, and the paper shows that geometrically preconditioned gradient descent (GeoPrecGD) with step x₁⁽ᵗ⁺¹⁾ = x₁⁽ᵗ⁾ − ηR⁻¹∇F(x₁⁽ᵗ⁾) achieves the faster rate O(exp(−t/κ_F)), recovering Newton and Gauss-Newton as special cases for quadratic and least-squares objectives respectively.

## Results

The paper establishes that well-designed reduction mappings strictly improve the smoothness constant (β_F ≤ β_f − Δ_max(2ε − ε²) < β_f for affine mappings, Theorem 1; with an additional QZ/m^(Φ) correction for nonlinear mappings, Theorem 2) and the Morse-Bott/sharpness constant (μ_F ≥ μ_f + Δ_min(2ε − ε²) > μ_f, Theorem 3). Combining these, the condition number of the reduced problem is strictly smaller: κ_F = β_F/μ_F < κ_f = β_f/μ_f (Corollary 3). This yields a faster local linear convergence rate for preconditioned gradient descent, improving from O(exp(−t/κ_f)) to O(exp(−t/κ_F)), and reducing iteration complexity from O(κ_f log(1/ε)) to O(κ_F log(1/ε)).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or dataset comparisons. The central comparison is between the original objective f and the reduced objective F = f ∘ Φ in terms of curvature constants. The reduced problem strictly dominates the original: its smoothness constant is smaller (by at least Δ_max(2ε − ε²) minus a nonlinearity correction), its sharpness constant is larger (by at least Δ_min(2ε − ε²)), and hence its condition number is strictly better. The paper positions itself relative to prior work on preconditioning (natural gradient, Shampoo, Muon), quotient manifold optimisation, and bilevel optimisation, arguing that reduction mappings unify these as special cases while providing the first explicit quantitative bounds on how much the condition number improves. The improvement is governed by the spectral gap Δ_max (or Δ_min) and the non-tangency parameter ε between the dominant curvature directions and the feasible manifold's tangent space.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Levin, Kileel, and Boumal's (2025) analysis of how smooth parametrizations reshape nonconvex landscapes, extending it from qualitative geometric observations to precise quantitative bounds on smoothness and sharpness constants. It also unifies and subsumes several previously separate threads—bilevel optimisation via implicit differentiation (Gould et al., 2016), the equivalence of PL/QG/EB/MB conditions (Rebjock and Boumal, 2024), natural gradient and preconditioned methods, and gauge-fixing strategies for quotient manifolds—under a single pullback-metric framework. The key departure from prior work is the explicit treatment of the non-tangency between dominant Hessian eigenspaces and the feasible manifold tangent space, which yields strict (not merely non-strict) improvements in the condition number, and the careful accounting of the additional curvature term introduced by nonlinear mappings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a principled, unified explanation for why many ad-hoc reparametrisation tricks (normalisation layers, neural-collapse ETF projections, symmetry-breaking gauge fixes) empirically accelerate convergence, and gives practitioners concrete conditions (spectral gap, non-tangency angle) under which a given reduction is provably beneficial. Key limitations include the restriction to local analysis, the assumption of exact evaluation of the reduction mapping Ψ, and the focus on deterministic first-order methods; extending to global convergence, stochastic settings, and jointly accounting for the computational cost of evaluating Ψ remain open. The small-slope rescaling trick for recovering Euclidean conditioning is a practical but underdeveloped direction that could warrant further study.
