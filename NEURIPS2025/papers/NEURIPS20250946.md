## Problem

In collaborative data exchange economies where agents incur costs for sharing their own data, it is unclear whether core-stable exchanges (where no coalition can profitably deviate) exist or can be computed. Prior work on data exchange economies assumed zero sharing costs, making core-stability trivially satisfied by full exchange. This paper shows that introducing realistic costs can destroy the existence of core-stable exchanges even with only three agents, and establishes both sufficient conditions for existence and fundamental computational hardness barriers.

## Input / Output

The method takes n agents each owning a dataset D_i, with oracle access to concave payoff functions p_i (value and supergradient queries) and convex cost functions c_i, plus a coalition size bound s and approximation parameter ε. It produces a data exchange matrix x ∈ [0,1]^{n×n} where x_{i,j} is the fraction of D_i shared with agent j, satisfying ε-core-stability: no coalition of size ≤ s can find an internal exchange giving all members utility exceeding their current utility by more than ε. The algorithm requires the payoff functions to be uniformly bounded by B, enabling a grid-based discretization of the utility space.

## Method

The paper formulates data exchange as an n-person game with non-transferable utilities and proves the game is balanced when payoffs are concave and costs are convex, invoking Scarf's theorem to guarantee core existence. For computational hardness, it reduces from approximate fractional hypergraph matching (PPAD-complete) by constructing edge-agents, vertex-agents, and intermediate agents whose carefully designed concave payoffs and convex costs enforce that core-stability in the data exchange implies stability in the matching, even with constant-size coalitions. On the algorithmic side, it adapts Scarf's pivoting algorithm by constructing a coalition matrix and utility matrix over a discretized utility grid (using the Ellipsoid method for feasibility checks), then iteratively performing cardinal and ordinal pivot steps until the bases converge. Empirically, the pivoting algorithm is validated on a mean-estimation task over a 4.4K-node road map graph, showing that the number of pivot iterations grows roughly linearly with the number of agents and that the algorithm terminates in seconds for up to 27 agents.

## Results

The paper establishes three main theoretical results: (1) core-stable data exchanges always exist under concave payoff and convex cost functions (Theorem 3), (2) finding such exchanges is PPAD-hard even with constant-size blocking coalitions (Theorem 2), and (3) determining existence is NP-hard in the general non-concave/non-convex setting (Theorem 4). Empirically, the Pivoting Algorithm (PA) is validated on a mean-estimation task over the Street Network of New York (4.4K nodes, 9.6K edges) with coalition size fixed at 3. At the largest tested scale (n=27 agents), PA requires 7.17×10⁴ concave optimization calls across 2,914 coalitions, terminates in an average of 7.36 seconds (range: 0.001 s for n=3 to 38.37 s for n=27), and completes in an average of 238.3 iterations (worst case 1,434), showing near-linear growth in pivoting operations with the number of agents.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare its method against competing baselines, as it is primarily a theoretical contribution establishing existence, hardness, and algorithmic results for core-stable data exchanges. The empirical evaluation in Section 4.2 serves as a practical validation of the Pivoting Algorithm derived from Scarf's theorem, rather than a head-to-head comparison. The closest prior work is [ACGM25], which studies core-stability in a data exchange model without sharing costs; the paper demonstrates that their trivial core-stable solution (all agents share all data) does not extend to the cost-aware setting, as shown in Example 1 where no core-stable exchange exists even with three agents. The PPAD-hardness result is positioned as the first such result for core-like guarantees in data economics, complementing prior work by [BGI⁺24a] and [ACGM25] that did not establish computational hardness. The empirical results confirm that despite the theoretical PPAD-hardness, the pivoting algorithm scales well in practice, with iteration counts growing only modestly (seemingly quadratically) with the number of agents.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts Scarf's (1967) framework for balanced n-person games and its pivoting algorithm to the data exchange setting, where agents trade data with associated payoffs and costs. It departs from the closest prior data exchange work [ACGM25, BGI+24a] by introducing sharing costs, which makes core-stability non-trivial (in the cost-free setting, full sharing is always core-stable). The PPAD-hardness reduction draws on the fractional hypergraph matching problem [IK18, Csá22], and the empirical evaluation uses a mean-estimation task on road-network data similar to [BGI+24b].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first PPAD-hardness result for core-like guarantees in data exchange economies, establishing a clear computational barrier that had been open in the data economics literature. The clean separation—concave payoffs plus convex costs guarantee existence, while relaxing either condition can destroy it—gives practitioners a principled design guideline for when stable exchanges are achievable. Open questions include combining core-stability with fairness criteria (as in [BGI+24a, ACGM25]) in the presence of costs, handling externalities in competitive settings, and developing more efficient algorithms for structured payoff/cost families (e.g., Gaussian inference, PAC learning).
