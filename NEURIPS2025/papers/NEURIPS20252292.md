## Problem

Pearl has argued that deep neural networks trained with statistical prediction objectives are intrinsically limited to associational (L1) reasoning and cannot discover causal structure or perform counterfactual (L3) inference. This paper provides an existence proof that challenges that claim: a GPT-style transformer trained solely on next-token prediction over passive text can discover instances of linear Gaussian structural causal models and learn to answer counterfactual queries about them, including for SCMs it has only seen interventional data for.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252292/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes text strings in a simple artificial language where each string begins with a type token (DATA or INFERENCE), a 4-letter SCM index (randomly assigned, carrying no structural information), and a sequence of observation or intervention tokens (e.g., OBS V1 -0.8, DO V3 0.9). For DATA strings the model predicts a single interventional sample value; for INFERENCE strings it predicts the counterfactual posterior mean and standard deviation for each queried variable. All numeric values are encoded with one decimal-point precision, and the SCM structure is never explicitly stated—the model must infer it entirely from the data strings.

## Method

The authors generate all 59,049 instances of a constrained class of 4-variable linear Gaussian SCMs with ternary weights (−1, 0, 1) and synthesize two types of text: DATA strings providing noisy interventional samples and INFERENCE strings providing analytic counterfactual means and standard deviations. A 12-layer GPT-2-style transformer (512 hidden, 8 heads) is trained via next-token prediction, with a generalization challenge in which 1,000 held-out SCMs receive only DATA strings during training, forcing the model to compose a learned counterfactual inference engine with structure discovered from interventional data. The model passes this challenge, reaching near-optimal counterfactual prediction on the test SCMs. Mechanistic interpretability analyses then show that SCM weight vectors can be decoded from residual stream activations using linear and MLP probes, and that gradient-based interventions on those activations (pushing probe predictions toward a target weight) produce predictable, analytically verifiable changes in the model's counterfactual outputs, confirming the probes recover a functional internal SCM representation.

## Results

The trained GPT-style transformer (12 layers, hidden size 512) passes the generalization challenge: it reaches near-optimal counterfactual inference performance on D_test SCMs (1,000 SCMs with only interventional DATA strings during training), with no systematic performance gap versus D_train (58,049 SCMs with both DATA and INFERENCE strings). Linear probes decode SCM weights from residual stream activations above chance (33%) by layer 5–6, reaching up to 96% accuracy for w11 at layer 12; MLP probes achieve near-perfect decoding (≥96%) for most weights by layer 7. Gradient-based intervention on the residual stream at layers 3–6 produces predictable shifts in model output that align with analytical counterfactual estimates for the corresponding SCM weight change, with peak intervention scores of ~0.24 (w11, layer 5, MLP probe).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external causal inference methods or other transformer architectures; instead, it establishes capability through an internal generalization challenge. The key comparison is between D_train (58,049 SCMs with both interventional and counterfactual training strings) and D_test (1,000 SCMs with only interventional DATA strings): the model shows no systematic performance gap, demonstrating it has learned a generalizable counterfactual inference engine rather than memorizing answers. A naive baseline (predicting the average value) is shown in Fig. 5 for reference, and the model substantially outperforms it. For mechanistic interpretability, linear probes are compared against MLP probes: linear probes achieve lower decoding accuracy (e.g., 0.96 vs. 1.00 for w11 at layer 7) but yield higher intervention scores for certain layer–weight pairs (particularly w11), indicating that decoding accuracy does not strictly imply controllability. Intervention scores drop to near zero beyond layer 7, localizing the functional use of the SCM representation to earlier layers despite continued decodability in later layers.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly challenges Pearl's theoretical claim that prediction-trained DNNs are confined to the associational level (L1) of his Causal Hierarchy, by providing a controlled existence proof that next-token prediction on SCM-generated text yields L2/L3 capabilities. Methodologically, it departs from neural causal models (Pawlowski et al., Xia et al.) that replace SCM equations with differentiable components, instead training a standard GPT-2 end-to-end on a purely linguistic objective. The mechanistic interpretability analyses build on the probing and activation-manipulation toolkit from Li et al. (2023) and Nanda et al. (2023), applying it to causal structure representations rather than world-state or factual-knowledge representations. The paper also connects to Lampinen et al. (2023) on passive learning of causal strategies, but goes further by showing that no active intervention by the model is needed—pure prediction suffices.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result challenges a widely cited theoretical limitation on what prediction-trained networks can achieve, with implications for how we interpret the causal capabilities of LLMs and other foundation models. However, the evidence is bounded to a highly constrained setting (4-variable linear Gaussian SCMs with ternary weights, artificial language, small 12-layer transformer), so generalization to natural language, non-linear SCMs, or larger models remains entirely open. The decoupling of decoding accuracy from intervention controllability beyond layer 7 raises interesting questions about where and how causal representations are actually used in computation. Future work on whether these findings extend to natural-language descriptions of causal scenarios or more complex model classes would be the critical next step.
