## Problem

Graph Transformers face a scalability bottleneck on large graphs: graph-scale operations (O(m) or O(n²)) persist throughout every training iteration, limiting mini-batch capability and GPU utilization. Additionally, most scalable GTs rely on local adjacency for node sampling, which is insufficient for capturing global structure—especially under heterophily where neighbors share few label similarities—undermining the expressivity that makes GTs powerful.

## Input / Output

The method takes a graph G=(V,E) with node attribute matrix X ∈ R^{n×F₀} and produces per-node class predictions. Unusually, the raw graph is consumed only during a one-time precomputation phase that builds a hub labeling index in RAM; during training, the model queries this index in O(1) per node pair to obtain fixed-size subgraph tokens (of size s = s_in + s_out + 1) and pairwise SPD matrices, so the GPU never sees the full graph structure. The SPD matrix B_r serves as a dense positional encoding bias in the attention mechanism, replacing sparse L-hop adjacency encodings.

## Method

HubGT introduces a three-level hub labeling hierarchy (H-0 2-hop caching, H-1 local hub labeling, H-2 global hub labeling) built via a modified Pruned Landmark Labeling algorithm, where each node's label stores distances plus relative-position sets M_r^i(v) enabling O(1) SPD queries for 2-hop pairs via a correction formula b_r(u,v) = b(u,r)+b(r,v)−δ_r(u,v). The label graph naturally extends the original adjacency with global hub connections, providing a richer receptive field than neighborhood sampling while preserving local edges. During training, subgraph tokens are sampled from the label index (balancing local long-tailed nodes and distant landmark hubs via s_in/s_out), SPD matrices are computed as positional encoding, and learnable global hub embeddings aggregate graph-level context—making the entire training loop equivalent to a standard Transformer with no graph-scale computation, achieving O(LnF²+Lns²F) per-epoch complexity and GPU memory independent of graph size.

## Results

HubGT achieves top or comparable accuracy on 11 out of 14 benchmark datasets, with standout results on heterophilous graphs: 89.68% on GENIUS (vs. 85.64% for PolyNormer), 67.03% on TWITCH-GAMER (vs. 65.93% for SGFormer), and 78.13% on PENN94 (vs. 74.45% for NAGphormer). HubGT consistently delivers the fastest inference across all 8 large-scale datasets (0.29–13 s), and its precomputation is 250–1000× faster than ANS-GT. On homophilous graphs, HubGT is competitive but slightly trails PolyNormer on OGBN-ARXIV (69.17% vs. 73.24%) and REDDIT (94.39% vs. 96.64%), while matching or exceeding all other baselines on OGBN-MAG (33.74% vs. 33.47% for SGFormer).

## Baselines & Comparisons

HubGT wins decisively on heterophilous graphs: it outperforms PolyNormer by 4.04 points on GENIUS (89.68% vs. 85.64%) and by 2.10 points on TWITCH-GAMER (67.03% vs. 65.93% for SGFormer), while also beating NAGphormer by 3.68 points on PENN94 (78.13% vs. 74.45%). On homophilous graphs, HubGT is competitive but trails PolyNormer on OGBN-ARXIV (69.17% vs. 73.24%) and REDDIT (94.39% vs. 96.64%), and is marginally below NAGphormer on PHYSICS (96.38% vs. 96.52%). However, HubGT surpasses all baselines on OGBN-MAG (33.74% vs. 33.47% for SGFormer) and POKEC (76.96% vs. 73.13% for SGFormer). In terms of efficiency, HubGT achieves the fastest inference on every dataset (0.29–13 s), and its precomputation is 250–1000× faster than ANS-GT; kernel-based models (DIFFormer, PolyNormer, SGFormer) are fast to train but require full-graph CPU inference, making them far slower at deployment. Several baselines (Exphormer, ANS-GT, GOAT, HSGT) fail entirely (OOM or TLE) on the largest graphs, whereas HubGT completes all 8 datasets within reasonable time.

## Relation to Prior Work

HubGT repurposes the Pruned Landmark Labeling (PLL) algorithm from database shortest-path indexing [35] as the backbone for graph transformer representation, extending 2-hop labeling into a three-level hierarchy (H-0/H-1/H-2) that simultaneously produces node token neighborhoods and O(1)-queryable shortest-path distances. It builds on the scalable GT architecture of Graphormer [2] and ANS-GT [13] but fundamentally departs from both kernel-based GTs (NodeFormer, DIFFormer, PolyNormer) and hierarchical GTs (GOAT, HSGT) by fully decoupling all graph-scale computation into a one-time precomputation phase, leaving training as standard Transformer operations with no edge-dependent terms. The use of SPD as a dense pairwise positional encoding on million-node graphs is, to the authors' claim, the first practical instantiation of this encoding at scale, previously limited to small graphs by O(n³) Floyd-Warshall cost.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The decoupled design removes the O(LmF) per-layer graph operations that have been the primary scalability bottleneck in GTs, enabling true mini-batch GPU training where memory depends only on batch size rather than graph edge count. This opens the door to applying Transformer-level expressivity to graphs with tens of millions of edges that were previously infeasible for GT architectures. Open questions include how the static hub labeling adapts to dynamic or evolving graphs, whether the approach extends naturally to graph-level or link-prediction tasks, and whether the precomputation cost O(ns²+ms) remains acceptable for extremely dense graphs. The paper is limited to node classification evaluation and does not explore multi-hop or temporal settings.
