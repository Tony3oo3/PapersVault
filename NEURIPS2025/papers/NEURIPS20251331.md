## Problem

Standard datasets and benchmarks for crystal structure prediction with generative models suffer from three overlooked issues: (1) widely-used datasets like carbon-24 contain a large fraction of duplicate structures (only ~40% are unique), (2) datasets with numerous polymorphs (perov-5, MP-20) are split randomly, separating polymorph pairs across train/test sets and creating an ill-posed evaluation, and (3) the standard one-to-one match rate metric loses meaning in the presence of polymorphs, rewarding models that generate only a single structure per composition. These issues collectively mislead model evaluation and hinder meaningful progress in the field.

## Input / Output

The CSP task takes a chemical composition (e.g., CaCdSO₂, HfNbN₃) as input and generates a full 3D crystal structure consisting of fractional atomic coordinates and lattice vectors. The evaluated datasets impose size constraints: carbon-24-unique contains up to 24 carbon atoms per unit cell, perov-5 contains exactly 5 atoms in cubic cells, and MP-20 contains up to 20 atoms in diverse cell shapes. The paper's contribution is not a new generative architecture but rather revised datasets (deduplicated, polymorph-aware splits) and new evaluation metrics (METRe and cRMSE) applied to three existing generative models—DiffCSP (equivariant diffusion), FlowMM (Riemannian flow matching), and OMatG (stochastic interpolants with various positional interpolants).

## Method

The paper introduces two complementary contributions. First, for dataset curation: duplicates in carbon-24 are identified by dynamically varying StructureMatcher tolerances via binary search to find match-boundary values, then clustering structures that match under all three tolerance parameters simultaneously (stol=0.025, ltol=0.002, angle_tol=0.4), reducing 10153 structures to 4250 unique ones; polymorph-aware splits for perov-5 and MP-20 ensure all polymorphs of a given composition remain within the same train/validation/test partition. Second, for evaluation: the METRe metric compares every generated structure against every reference structure and counts a match whenever any generated structure falls within tolerance of a reference, reporting the fraction of references that find at least one match (reducing to standard match rate in the absence of polymorphs); the cRMSE metric combines METRe with RMSE by penalizing non-matching references with the stol threshold value, yielding a single scalar that captures both coverage and structural fidelity. These are validated by benchmarking DiffCSP, FlowMM, and multiple OMatG variants (differing by positional interpolant: linear, trigonometric, encoder-decoder, SBD) on all new datasets, revealing that performance degrades with unit-cell size N and that polymorph-aware splits improve generalization to unseen compositions.

## Results

On the deduplicated carbon-24-unique dataset, METRe rates reach ~98% across all models, a dramatic increase over standard one-to-one match rates of only 15–21%, confirming that polymorphism was previously masking model performance. The best cRMSE on carbon-24-unique is 0.187 (OMatG-TrigODE), compared to 0.235 for DiffCSP and 0.198 for FlowMM. On the more challenging perov-5-polymorph-split (where test compositions are entirely unseen), cRMSE values range from 0.158 (OMatG-LinearODE) to 0.172 (OMatG-LinearODEγ), with METRe rates of 75–79%. On MP-20-polymorph-split, OMatG-LinearODE achieves the best cRMSE of 0.187 and METRe of 70.5%, substantially outperforming DiffCSP (cRMSE 0.279) and FlowMM (cRMSE 0.226).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks three generative model families—DiffCSP (equivariant diffusion), FlowMM (Riemannian flow matching), and OMatG (stochastic interpolants with multiple positional interpolants)—on the newly curated datasets. On carbon-24-unique, OMatG variants with trigonometric interpolants achieve the lowest cRMSE (0.187), narrowly beating FlowMM (0.198) and DiffCSP (0.235), while METRe rates are nearly identical (~98%) across all models. On perov-5-polymorph-split, differences are modest: DiffCSP attains the highest METRe (78.9%) but OMatG-LinearODE achieves the lowest cRMSE (0.158 vs. 0.162 for DiffCSP), indicating slightly better structural fidelity. On MP-20-polymorph-split, OMatG-LinearODE clearly dominates with cRMSE 0.187 and METRe 70.5%, outperforming FlowMM (cRMSE 0.226, METRe 65.2%) and DiffCSP (cRMSE 0.279, METRe 53.1%)—a gap the authors attribute to OMatG's hyperparameter optimization targeting the new cRMSE metric on the polymorph-aware split. Overall, the paper demonstrates that the new polymorph-aware splits and cRMSE metric reveal performance differences invisible under the original random splits and match-rate-only evaluation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper does not introduce a new generative architecture but instead audits and corrects the evaluation infrastructure for crystal structure prediction (CSP) established by CDVAE (Xie et al., 2022), which defined the carbon-24, perov-5, and MP-20 benchmarks and the standard match-rate metric via Pymatgen's StructureMatcher. It positions itself against the growing body of diffusion- and flow-matching-based CSP models (DiffCSP, FlowMM, OMatG) by showing that their reported performance is confounded by dataset duplicates and polymorph-blind splits. The paper repurposes the StructureMatcher tool in a novel diagnostic role—using dynamic tolerance sweeps to identify structural duplicates—and extends the evaluation paradigm from one-to-one matching to a many-to-many reference-anchored metric (METRe) and a combined penalty metric (cRMSE).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The discovery that the widely-used carbon-24 dataset contains only ~40% unique structures fundamentally undermines prior SOTA claims in CSP, and the polymorph-aware splits for perov-5 and MP-20 redefine what 'generalization' means in this task. METRe and cRMSE address a real methodological gap: the standard match rate is ill-posed for polymorphic compositions, and RMSE alone can be gamed by generating a single high-fidelity structure. Open questions include whether the conservative duplicate-identification thresholds (requiring agreement across all three tolerance parameters) miss near-duplicates, whether METRe scales sensibly to datasets with >2 polymorphs per composition, and whether the N-split finding (performance degrades with unit-cell size) reflects a fundamental limitation of current equivariant encoders or simply a data-scarcity artifact.
