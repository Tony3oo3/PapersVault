## Problem

As LLMs are increasingly deployed as decision-making agents in social and economic contexts, it is critical to understand whether their resource allocation choices align with human distributional preferences. While LLM behavior has been studied in strategic games (ultimatum, prisoner's dilemma), their performance in non-strategic allocation of subjectively valued indivisible goods—where fairness axioms like equitability, envy-freeness, and Rawlsian maximin may conflict with efficiency—remains unexplored. This paper asks: do LLMs prioritize the same fairness principles as humans, and what are the sources of misalignment?

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance specifies n individuals, m indivisible goods with an additive valuation profile, and optionally a fixed pool of divisible money; the LLM receives a natural-language prompt asking it to 'determine' the fairest allocation. A two-stage prompting strategy is used to eliminate sensitivity to template formatting, and each model is queried 100 times per instance to capture response distributions. In a separate menu-based variant, the LLM instead selects one allocation from a small set of predefined options derived from human responses or designed with amplified inequality disparities. Outputs are scored against five formal axioms (EQ*, EF, RMM, PO, USW) and compared head-to-head with the distribution of human responses from the original Herreiner-Puppe questionnaire study.

## Method

The paper constructs an empirical benchmark using 10 instances from Herreiner and Puppe (2007) plus additional designed instances that isolate specific fairness-efficiency trade-offs, and evaluates four state-of-the-art LLMs (GPT-4o, Claude-3.5 Sonnet, Llama3-70b, Gemini-1.5 Pro) under a unified two-stage prompting protocol. The core finding is a stark generation-vs.-selection gap: when asked to generate an allocation, all LLMs overwhelmingly prioritize Pareto optimality and envy-freeness while neglecting equitability (the top human preference), but when asked to select from a menu of options, GPT-4o and Claude-3.5 Sonnet choose the perfectly equitable allocation in over 60–70% of responses. The authors further probe robustness through chain-of-thought prompting, persona assignment (e.g., 'you care about equitability'), modified intentions, iterative refinement with feedback, and self-serving bias tests where the LLM is one of the recipients. A key mechanistic explanation is that LLMs implicitly apply greedy one-by-one allocation heuristics (round-robin or max-valuation assignment) that naturally produce EF or USW outcomes but never yield EQ* allocations, whereas selecting from a menu bypasses this reasoning bottleneck.

## Results

Across 10 resource allocation instances, humans prioritize equitability (EQ*) as their top fairness notion (12.4% of responses), while all LLMs prioritize efficiency: GPT-4o ranks PO first (20.4%), Llama3-70b ranks USW first (30.8%), and Gemini-1.5P ranks EF first (19%). Overall, LLMs return EQ allocations in only 6.5–8.5% of responses versus 29.0% for humans, while returning PO allocations in 39.7–71.0% versus 47.8% for humans. When tasked with selecting from a predefined menu, GPT-4o and Claude-3.5S select EQ* allocations in >60–70% of responses, a stark contrast to their generation behavior. GPT-4o is the only model that meaningfully uses money to achieve fairness (8% EQ*+PO in I7 vs. 55.1% for humans), while Claude-3.5S and Llama3-70b never return the unique fair allocation in money-involving instances.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four LLMs (GPT-4o, Claude-3.5S, Llama3-70b, Gemini-1.5P) against human subjects on distributive fairness. GPT-4o is the most human-aligned model: it returns EF allocations at a comparable rate to humans (42.0% vs. 44.2%) and is the only model that uses money to achieve EQ* and EF (8% in I7). Claude-3.5S outperforms humans on EF (53.4% vs. 44.2%) but still fails to match human EQ rates (7.7% vs. 29.0%). Llama3-70b is the most efficiency-biased, returning USW allocations 3× more frequently than humans (36.1% vs. 12.9%) and PO allocations at 71.0% vs. 47.8%. Gemini-1.5P is the least aligned overall, with the lowest EQ rate (7.9%) and a tendency to discard money rather than use it for fairness. In the menu-selection task, GPT-4o and Claude-3.5S dramatically outperform Llama3-70b and Gemini-1.5P on EQ* selection (>60% vs. <2%), revealing a value-action gap where models state fairness values but fail to compute fair allocations when generating from scratch.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM-as-economic-agent literature (Horton [52], game-theoretic evaluations [3,4,39]) from strategic settings (dictator, ultimatum, prisoner's dilemma games) to non-strategic resource allocation, where a single decision-maker distributes subjectively valued indivisible goods. It draws on the experimental economics tradition of Herreiner and Puppe [50] and the formal fair-division axioms (equitability, envy-freeness, Rawlsian maximin) from Brams and Taylor [14] and Dubins and Spanier [29], but departs from prior LLM fairness benchmarks (ETHICS [49], Delphi [58], MoralChoice [93]) by evaluating alignment through a direct head-to-head comparison with human responses on identical allocation instances rather than through moral-judgment multiple-choice tasks. The two-stage prompting strategy and the menu-selection vs. generation distinction are methodological contributions not present in prior LLM fairness evaluations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals a fundamental misalignment: LLMs default to efficiency (PO/USW) and envy-freeness while neglecting equitability, the notion humans prioritize most, and they fail to use money as a transferable resource to reduce inequality. The value-action gap finding—that GPT-4o and Claude-3.5S select equitable options >60–70% of the time from menus yet almost never generate them—suggests that current LLMs lack the deliberate optimization process needed to minimize inequality, likely relying on greedy heuristics instead. Open questions include whether SFT or RL-based methods can close this gap, whether the misalignment persists in larger or more complex allocation settings, and whether the findings generalize beyond the small, carefully designed instances used here. The reliance on a single human-study dataset and the absence of human-annotated training data limit the scope of corrective interventions.
