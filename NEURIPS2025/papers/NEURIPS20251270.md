## Problem

Existing image forgery detection and localisation (IFDL) methods are largely designed for Photoshop-style manipulations and fail to generalise across the rapidly growing variety of diffusion-based inpainting models. A detector trained on artifacts from one diffusion model may completely break when encountering images edited by a different model, yet most prior benchmarks and methods rely on a single or very limited set of inpainters. This paper tackles the cross-inpainter generalisation problem head-on, asking how to build an IFDL system that remains robust to both seen and unseen diffusion models, including LoRA-customised and commercial variants.

## Input / Output

RADAR takes a standard RGB image as input and produces two outputs: a pixel-wise tampering probability map (obtained by applying a convolution + sigmoid Localisation Head to fused patch-level features, then binarised) and a binary detection score computed as the mean of the top 1% highest values in the tampering map, thresholded at 0.5. The image is internally processed as a grid of N patches by two transformer encoders, so the spatial resolution of the output map is tied to the patch grid of the foundation models (ViT-B/14, i.e. 14×14 pixel patches). At inference the contrastive Projection Head is discarded and only the Localisation Head is used.

## Method

RADAR extracts patch-level features from two frozen foundation-model encoders — DINO-v2 for semantic cues and Depth Anything V2 for geometric/structural cues — and fuses them via a symmetric cross-attention Fusion Block that swaps keys between two multi-head attention layers before combining each patch pair with a shared patch-wise MLP. The key training innovation is a patch-level supervised contrastive loss (SCL) that treats all patches in a batch as belonging to three classes: original (from genuine images), tampered (overlapping the inpainting mask), and affected (in tampered images but outside the mask, altered by the latent-diffusion auto-encoder). By aggregating embeddings from patches generated by up to 10 different inpainters into a single contrastive set, the loss forces the model to learn a unified tampered distribution shared across models, directly promoting cross-inpainter generalisation. Training data is generated automatically using Kosmos-2 for object detection, Grounded SAM for segmentation, and a set of K open-source inpainters, yielding the BBC-PAIR benchmark with over 150,000 images across 28 inpainters and three evaluation scenarios (ID, LoRA-customised, and OOD commercial models).

## Results

RADAR achieves state-of-the-art performance across all three BBC-PAIR evaluation splits. On BBC-PAIR-ID (in-distribution), it reaches 0.945 AUC and 0.931 accuracy for detection, and 0.832 F1 / 0.600 IoU for localisation. On BBC-PAIR-LoRA (customised models), it maintains 0.901 AUC and 0.521 IoU. On BBC-PAIR-OOD (completely unseen inpainters including 8 commercial tools), it still achieves 0.805 AUC and 0.450 IoU, outperforming all seven baselines by large margins (e.g., +0.32 AUC over the second-best on ID, +0.13 AUC on OOD).

## Baselines & Comparisons

RADAR outperforms all seven baselines (HiFi-Net, TruFor, MIML, AdaIFL, Mesorch, SAFIRE, SIDA) on every metric across all three BBC-PAIR splits. The detection gap is the most striking: on BBC-PAIR-ID, RADAR's 0.945 AUC exceeds the best baseline (HiFi-Net at 0.625) by +0.32, and on BBC-PAIR-OOD its 0.805 AUC beats SIDA (0.679) by +0.13. For localisation, RADAR leads by +0.064 IoU on ID (0.600 vs. SIDA's 0.536), +0.013 on LoRA (0.521 vs. SIDA's 0.508), and +0.003 on OOD (0.450 vs. AdaIFL's 0.447). The detection advantage is particularly important in practice because it reflects robustness to false positives on non-tampered images, where baselines like SIDA and Mesorch erroneously flag entire objects as forged. MIML is the weakest baseline overall, with F1 scores below 0.25 on OOD, indicating it fails to generalise beyond its training distribution. The consistent margin across ID, LoRA, and OOD splits demonstrates that RADAR's multi-encoder fusion and patch-level contrastive learning provide genuine cross-inpainter generalisation rather than overfitting to specific models.

## Relation to Prior Work

RADAR extends the IFDL literature (TruFor, HiFi-Net, SAFIRE, Mesorch, SIDA, etc.) by shifting focus from Photoshop-style splicing/copy-move to diffusion-based inpainting, and by explicitly targeting cross-inpainter generalisation rather than single-model detection. Conceptually, it repurposes two pre-trained foundation models (DINO-v2 for semantics, Depth Anything V2 for geometry) as frozen feature extractors fused via a symmetric cross-attention block, drawing on multi-modal transformer fusion ideas from NLP and RGB-D literature. The patch-level supervised contrastive loss with a three-class formulation (original, tampered, affected) is a novel twist on contrastive learning for forensics, building on SAFIRE's use of contrastive objectives but operating at the transformer patch level and introducing the 'affected' distribution to account for latent auto-encoding artefacts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As diffusion-based editing tools proliferate and become accessible to non-experts, the ability to reliably detect and localise inpainting manipulations across diverse models is a pressing societal need. BBC-PAIR, with 28 inpainters spanning open-source, LoRA-customised, and commercial tools across three evaluation scenarios, fills a critical gap left by prior benchmarks that relied on a single inpainting model. Key open questions remain: how well the approach handles adversarially crafted edits designed to evade detection, whether the method extends to video or 3D content, and how to incorporate closed-source models that cannot be used for training data generation. The OOD performance gap (IoU 0.450 vs. 0.600 on ID) also signals that true cross-model generalisation is still an unsolved challenge.
