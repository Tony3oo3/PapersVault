## Problem

Canonical generative models treat noise sampling and the mapping from noise to data as two independent steps, typically fixing a standard Gaussian prior. While the moment measure factorization of Cordero-Erausquin and Klartag (2015) ties both to a single convex potential, its Gibbs factor has spread inversely proportional to the target distribution (e.g., a peaked Gaussian yields a very diffuse sampling distribution), making it poorly suited for practical generative tasks. The paper asks whether a conjugate factorization can retain the single-potential structure while producing a sampling distribution that aligns with the target.

## Input / Output

The method accepts either i.i.d. samples from the target distribution ρ (CMFGen) or an unnormalized energy function E with ρ ∝ e^{-E} (CMFMA), and produces a learned convex potential w_θ parameterized as an ICNN. To generate samples, one draws from the log-concave Gibbs distribution P_{w_θ} via Langevin Monte Carlo, then applies the convex-conjugate gradient ∇w_θ* (computed by an amortized conjugate solver) to obtain samples from ρ. For the inpainting task, the learned potential provides an (unnormalized) log-density, enabling gradient ascent on masked pixels to reconstruct missing image regions. Experiments use 2D toy distributions, MNIST (28×28 grayscale), and the Cartoon dataset (64×64 RGB).

## Method

The core contribution is the conjugate moment measure factorization: any absolutely continuous ρ on a compact convex set can be written as ρ = ∇w*#P_w, where w* is the convex conjugate of a convex potential w and P_w is the Gibbs distribution e^{-w}/∫e^{-w}. Unlike the original moment measure (ρ = ∇u#P_u), here w maps ρ *to* P_w rather than the reverse, so the sampling distribution P_w tracks the shape of ρ (e.g., for ρ = N(m,Σ), the conjugate Gibbs factor is N(r, Σ^{1/3}) instead of N(m, Σ^{-1})). Existence is proved via Schauder's fixed-point theorem applied to the map w ↦ B(ρ, P_w). Two estimation algorithms are proposed: CMFGen performs a fixed-point iteration where each step solves a neural OT problem (using an ICNN and the amortized conjugate solver of Amos 2023) to update w_θ, while CMFMA regresses w_θ on the Monge-Ampère equation w(∇w(x)) − ln det H_w(x) ≈ E(x). A single ICNN serves dual roles at each iteration: generating LMC samples from P_{w_θ} and approximating the OT map between ρ and P_{w_θ}.

## Results

The paper's primary quantitative evaluation uses the 2-Sinkhorn divergence between generated and target samples (N=2048, averaged over 100 random seeds) across five 2D distributions (Circles, S-curve, Checkerboard, Scaled-Rotated S-curve, Diag-Checkerboard). CMFGen consistently achieves equal or lower Sinkhorn divergence compared to a generative ICNN baseline, as shown in the boxplots of Figure 13. For high-dimensional image generation on MNIST and the Cartoon dataset, CMFGen produces visually higher-quality samples than an ICNN trained to directly transport Gaussian noise to the data distribution. The authors explicitly note that CMFGen is not yet competitive with state-of-the-art generative models such as flow matching, attributing this gap to the known training difficulties of ICNNs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares CMFGen against a single baseline: a generative ICNN (using the neural OT solver of Amos 2023) trained to transport a standard Gaussian N(0,I) directly to the target distribution ρ. On all five 2D benchmark distributions, CMFGen achieves equal or superior 2-Sinkhorn divergence, with the boxplots in Figure 13 showing CMFGen's distribution of divergences shifted toward lower values (closer to the oracle lower bound given by the divergence between two independent target samples). On MNIST and the Cartoon dataset, CMFGen generates visually more coherent and higher-quality images than the generative ICNN with the same architecture (five hidden layers of size 512, four quadratic input connections). The authors emphasize that this comparison is intentionally restricted to ICNN-based methods, as CMFGen's reliance on ICNNs for parameterizing the convex potential limits its scalability compared to flow-matching or diffusion-based approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the moment measure factorization of Cordero-Erausquin and Klartag (2015), which expresses any measure ρ as ∇u#e^{-u}, by proposing a conjugate variant ρ = ∇w*#e^{-w} where w* is the convex conjugate of w. The authors demonstrate that the original factorization is ill-suited for generative modeling because the Gibbs factor's spread is inversely proportional to the target's spread (e.g., a peaked Gaussian yields a broad Gibbs noise), whereas the conjugate version produces a Gibbs factor that more closely mirrors the target distribution. The practical implementation builds on the ICNN architecture of Vesseron and Cuturi (2024) and the amortized convex conjugate solver of Amos (2023), positioning the work at the intersection of optimal transport theory and neural generative modeling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The conjugate moment measure factorization offers a principled way to tie sampling and mapping through a single convex potential, and the observation that the log-concave Gibbs noise already contains structural features of the target (digits, faces) is a conceptually interesting departure from the standard 'structureless noise + learned map' paradigm. However, the practical impact is currently limited: the method relies on ICNNs that are notoriously difficult to train, the authors explicitly state it is not comparable to state-of-the-art generative models (flow matching, diffusion), and the theoretical guarantee requires the target to be supported on a compact convex set. Open questions include whether the pre-trained Gibbs factor can serve as a structured prior in other generative pipelines, and whether the approach can be extended beyond ICNN parameterizations to more expressive architectures.
