## Problem

Curriculum learning requires a reliable notion of sample difficulty, but existing submodular-based approaches rely on fixed pre-training schedules that do not adapt to evolving training dynamics. Meanwhile, adaptive subset selection methods like GLISTER and GradMatch incur substantial computational overhead from bi-level optimization or gradient matching, limiting scalability. The paper asks whether one can dynamically choose which submodular selection criterion to apply at each training step in an online, low-overhead manner that provably converges to the best criterion in hindsight.

## Input / Output

At each training step the method consumes a training mini-batch, a small validation mini-batch (2 random validation points in LLM experiments), and the current model parameters, from which it computes per-sample gradients (last-layer gradients for vision models, LoRA rank-128 adapter gradients for LLMs). It outputs a selected subset of size β (10%, 30%, or 50% of the batch) and a single SGD update restricted to that subset. The submodular arms operate on pairwise similarity kernels (cosine or RBF) over the batch, and the entire selection overhead is negligible (~0.8 ms) compared to gradient computation (~630 ms).

## Method

ONLINESUBMOD frames adaptive subset selection as a contextual multi-armed bandit problem where each arm is a monotone submodular function (facility location, log-determinant, graph cut, disparity variants, and mutual-information versions conditioned on the validation set). At each step, the algorithm scores every arm by a validation-performance-driven utility reward derived from a first-order Taylor approximation of the validation loss reduction, which decomposes into a gradient-influence term and a Hessian-weighted relative-similarity term (with the Hessian approximated as identity). An exploration-exploitation threshold Ξ_t = t/(t+λ(t))^π(t) governs whether to exploit the highest-scoring arm or explore uniformly, with λ(t) and π(t) acting as annealing schedulers. The selected arm's submodular objective is maximized via the standard greedy algorithm (1−1/e guarantee), and the model is updated using only the resulting subset. The paper proves that under constant exploration dampening and a positive optimality gap, the expected instantaneous regret decays as O(1/t) + O(√(log t / t)), establishing no-regret convergence to the best submodular selector in hindsight.

## Results

On LLM fine-tuning (LLaMA-2-7B on MMLU), ONLINESUBMOD achieves 49.6% average accuracy across 9 subjects, outperforming the strongest baseline GREATS (47.8%) by 1.8 points, and 55.9% on TydiQA. On vision classification with ResNet-18, ONLINESUBMOD achieves the highest accuracy at every budget fraction: 75.8% on CIFAR-100 (50% budget), 94.1% on CIFAR-10 (50%), and 62.6% on Tiny-ImageNet (50%), while maintaining speedups of 1.92×, 2.08×, and 2.6× respectively. The submodular selection overhead is negligible at 0.8 ms per step versus 630 ms for gradient computation (an 800× gap).

## Baselines & Comparisons

On LLM fine-tuning, ONLINESUBMOD outperforms all five baselines across every MMLU subject and TydiQA: it leads GREATS (the closest competitor) by 1.8 points in average MMLU accuracy (49.6% vs 47.8%) and by 0.2 points on TydiQA (55.9% vs 55.7%), while GradNorm, MaxLoss, RhoLoss, and SBERT trail by 2–4 points. On vision tasks, ONLINESUBMOD achieves the highest accuracy at every budget fraction across Tiny-ImageNet, CIFAR-100, and CIFAR-10, beating the nearest competitors (MILO, RHO-LOSS, GRADMATCH) by 0.1–1.3 points; for example, on CIFAR-100 at 50% budget it reaches 75.8% vs RHO-LOSS's 75.7% and MILO's 75.6%. On CIFAR-10 at 50% budget, ONLINESUBMOD ties MILO and RHO-LOSS at 94.1% accuracy while offering a 2.08× speedup (vs 2.15× for MILO and 1.15× for RHO-LOSS). GLISTER and CRAIG consistently underperform, with GLISTER trailing by up to 4.4 points on CIFAR-100 (10% budget: 64.2% vs 73.6%). The key differentiator is that ONLINESUBMOD selects subsets online via a bandit policy, whereas MILO and CRAIG rely on expensive offline filtering, yet ONLINESUBMOD matches or exceeds their accuracy with comparable speedup.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reframes the submodular-based subset selection problem (as in MILO [19]) as a multi-armed bandit, where each arm is a distinct submodular function (facility location, log-determinant, graph cut, etc.) and the learner adaptively chooses which function to apply at each training step. The utility-driven reward signal draws directly on the gradient influence function of Pruthi et al. [38] and the online selection formulation of GREATS [52], but departs from both by wrapping the selection in an exploration-exploitation bandit policy with provable no-regret guarantees rather than using a fixed schedule or direct greedy maximization. The key conceptual departure from MILO is replacing the static 'representative-then-diversity' ordering with a dynamic, reward-driven arm selection that responds to evolving training dynamics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The bandit formulation provides a principled, theoretically grounded mechanism for adaptive curriculum scheduling that avoids the computational overhead of bilevel optimization (GLISTER) or gradient matching (GradMatch), while the no-regret guarantee offers a formal convergence guarantee absent in prior heuristic approaches. However, the method requires a validation set for the reward signal, relies on an identity Hessian approximation, and needs a warm-start phase for vision tasks, limiting its applicability in fully online or validation-free settings. Open questions include whether the bandit framework scales to large-scale pretraining (the authors note this as future work) and whether the utility metric can be learned by a neural scorer to reduce dependence on explicit gradient computation.
