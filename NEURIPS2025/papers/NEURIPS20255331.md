## Problem

Embodied AI systems are rapidly advancing in capability and deployment scope, yet existing regulatory frameworks designed for industrial robots and autonomous vehicles are insufficient to address the full range of EAI-specific risks spanning physical, informational, economic, and social domains. A significant policy vacuum exists at both national and international levels, with no comprehensive governance structure for highly autonomous, continuously learning embodied systems operating across diverse environments.

## Input / Output

The paper takes as input the existing regulatory landscape across three jurisdictions (US, UK, EU) covering autonomous vehicles, classical robotics, drones, and virtual AI, combined with the technical literature on EAI capabilities, vulnerabilities, and deployment trajectories. It produces a four-dimensional risk taxonomy, a coverage heat map (Table 1) mapping existing policies to specific EAI risk subcategories with three-level adequacy ratings, and five concrete policy recommendations spanning safety research, certification, standards, liability, and economic preparation.

## Method

The paper constructs a comprehensive taxonomy of EAI risks across four dimensions—physical (purposeful/accidental harm), informational (privacy/misinformation), economic (labor displacement/inequality/power concentration), and social (bias/accountability/transparency/attachment/transformative effects)—drawing on robotics safety, human-robot interaction, and AI trajectory literature. It then performs a comparative policy analysis of key legislation and standards (UK Automated Vehicles Act 2024, EU AI Act, GDPR, EU Machinery Regulation 2023, US AV STEP, ISO 10218/SAE 21434) to identify where existing frameworks provide adequate, partial, or no coverage for each risk category. The analysis reveals that only physical risks from classical robots and AVs receive substantial regulatory attention, while informational, economic, and social EAI risks are almost entirely unaddressed. The paper concludes with five policy pathways: investing in EAI-specific safety research and benchmarks, mandating pre-deployment certification with model cards and domain-limited operation, promoting dynamic industry-led standards, clarifying liability regimes for fully autonomous systems, and drafting legislative frameworks for transformative economic and social effects.

## Results

This is a position/policy paper with no empirical experiments or quantitative benchmarks. Its primary 'results' are a four-category risk taxonomy (physical, informational, economic, social) and a policy gap analysis across the US, UK, and EU. The key finding is that existing regulatory frameworks (e.g., EU Machinery Regulation, UK Automated Vehicles Act, US AV STEP) provide substantial coverage only for physical risks from classic robots and AVs, while nearly all informational, economic, and social EAI risks remain unaddressed (marked as significant gaps in Table 1). The paper identifies three critical policy gaps: (1) no robust certification process for EAI modalities beyond AVs, (2) absence of reliable EAI capability benchmarks, and (3) lack of detailed post-deployment monitoring requirements.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare methods against baselines in the empirical sense; it is a policy analysis and position paper. Instead, it compares existing regulatory frameworks across three jurisdictions (US, UK, EU) and three technology categories (classic robots, AVs, virtual agents) to identify where EAI-specific gaps exist. The analysis shows that AV-specific legislation (e.g., UK's ASDE/NUiC framework, US AV STEP) is the most developed and could serve as a precedent for broader EAI governance, but these frameworks are tightly coupled to automotive regulatory structures and do not generalize to home-care, educational, or industrial EAI. The EU Machinery Regulation (2023) and GDPR provide the broadest existing coverage but still leave significant gaps for highly autonomous, continuously learning EAI systems. No existing framework adequately addresses the intersection of EAI with potential AGI, such as whether EAI systems should be permitted to build other EAI systems.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes three distinct literatures—robotics safety (model predictive control, control barrier functions, ISO standards), LLM/VLA security (jailbreaking, adversarial attacks on vision-language-action models), and AI governance (EU AI Act, GDPR, AV-specific legislation)—into a unified EAI-specific risk taxonomy and policy gap analysis. It explicitly positions EAI as an evolution of classical robotics rather than a novel category, while arguing that the convergence of LLM-level autonomy with physical embodiment creates a governance vacuum that neither robotics regulations nor virtual-AI frameworks adequately address. The technical grounding draws on recent VLA model releases (Gemini Robotics-ER, Isaac GR00T N1) and open-source EAI resources (Physical Intelligence, Unitree, Open X-Embodiment) to argue that the 'ChatGPT moment' for robotics is imminent, making the policy gap urgent rather than hypothetical.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central practical contribution is the heat map (Table 1) showing that nearly all informational, economic, and social EAI risks have zero existing policy coverage, while even physical risks are only partially addressed through AV-specific or industrial-robot regulations. Key open questions it surfaces include: how to certify EAI systems with continuous learning and expanded operational domains (unlike AVs confined to roads), how to reconcile GDPR Article 22's human-intervention mandate with fully autonomous physical agents, and whether EAI should be allowed to build other EAI systems. The paper's limitations—focusing on US/UK/EU, civilian applications only, and acknowledging that technical solutions may lag behind deployment—mean it serves as a starting framework rather than a definitive governance blueprint.
