## Problem

Classical model size (parameter count) fails to characterize generalization in over-parameterized networks, producing the double descent phenomenon that contradicts classical U-shaped intuition. While norm-based capacity has been empirically observed to reshape learning curves into a 'φ-shaped' form (Ng & Ma, 2023), no precise mathematical framework existed to characterize the relationship between test risk and the estimator's norm. This paper asks: what is the exact relationship between test risk and norm-based model capacity, and can it be rigorously derived?

## Input / Output

The framework takes a random features regression setup with n training samples, p random features, regularization parameter λ ≥ 0, and the spectral matrix Λ of the integral operator (with eigenvalues ξ_k²). It produces deterministic equivalents for both the test risk R and the estimator's ℓ₂ norm N, enabling a closed-form risk-vs-norm learning curve. Under power-law spectral assumptions, the output further yields scaling law exponents γ_n ≤ 0 and γ_N ∈ ℝ that characterize how risk scales multiplicatively with data size and norm capacity, with γ_N changing sign across different (q, ℓ) regions.

## Method

The core technique extends deterministic equivalence from random matrix theory to the estimator's ℓ₂ norm, requiring new deterministic quantities of the form Tr(A X^T X (X^T X + λ)^{-1}) for arbitrary PSD matrices A (previous work only handled A = I). The key insight is that the norm's deterministic equivalent N contains both the bias and variance components of the test risk R (with specific rescaling factors), making norm-based capacity sufficient to characterize generalization—unlike effective dimension or smoother, which only capture variance. By eliminating model size p from the coupled self-consistent equations, the authors derive R = g(N), revealing a phase transition from under- to over-parameterization but no double descent: in the over-parameterized regime, risk increases linearly with norm (Proposition 4.1), while in the under-parameterized regime it follows a U-shape. Under power-law spectral assumptions, they further derive non-monotone scaling laws where the norm exponent γ_N can be positive or negative depending on the (q, ℓ) region, contrasting with the classical monotonically decreasing scaling in model size.

## Results

The paper's headline theoretical result (Proposition 4.1) establishes that in the over-parameterized regime (p > n), the deterministic equivalent of test risk is a linear function of the estimator's norm: R₀^RFM = λₙ · N₀^RFM + C, where λₙ is a constant determined by the data spectrum and C depends on the target function, n, and noise. Under power-law spectral assumptions (Corollary 4.2), the risk in the over-parameterized regime is bounded by a constant C_{n,α,r,1} that is strictly smaller than C_{n,α,r,2} (the under-parameterized limit as p→n⁻), confirming that over-parameterization yields lower risk. The scaling law (Proposition 4.3) takes the form R_λ^RFM = Θ(n^{γₙ} · (N_λ^RFM)^{γ_N}) with γₙ ≤ 0 and γ_N ∈ ℝ, where γ_N > 0 in high-complexity regions (risk increases with norm) and γ_N < 0 in low-complexity regions (risk decreases with norm), producing a U-shaped curve rather than monotone decrease.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper, so comparisons are between capacity measures rather than competing algorithms. The paper demonstrates that the ℓ₂-norm of the second-layer parameters is sufficient to fully characterize generalization (capturing both bias and variance of test risk), whereas two widely-used alternatives—effective dimension (Tr(Λ(Λ+νI)⁻¹)) and the smoother (n·Tr(Λ_F ZᵀZ(ZᵀZ+λ)⁻²))—only capture the variance component and cannot account for the target function θ*, making them insufficient. In terms of learning curve shape, plotting risk against model size p yields the classical double-descent curve, while plotting against norm N yields a U-shaped curve with a phase transition at the interpolation threshold but no second descent: in the over-parameterized regime, risk increases (almost linearly) with norm, contrasting with the decrease seen when p increases. The norm also provides a practical control knob: it decreases monotonically with the regularization parameter λ in both under- and over-parameterized regimes, establishing a one-to-one correspondence analogous to the L-curve. The theory does not fully reproduce the 'φ'-shaped curve from [43, Fig. 8.12] where over-parameterized risk exceeds under-parameterized risk, which the authors attribute to real-world data violating the well-behaved spectrum assumption (Assumption 1).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the deterministic equivalence framework from random matrix theory—developed for characterizing test risk in random feature regression (Mei & Montanari [35], Defilippis et al. [14]) and linear regression (Bach [1], Cheng & Montanari [10])—by deriving new deterministic equivalents for the estimator's ℓ₂ norm and establishing an explicit risk-norm relationship R = g(N). While prior work characterized learning curves as functions of parameter count p (yielding double descent), this paper re-parameterizes the x-axis to norm-based capacity, a shift motivated by classical results (Bartlett [2]) and empirical observations (Ng & Ma [43]) but lacking a precise mathematical treatment. The paper also positions norm-based capacity against alternative measures (effective dimension, smoother), showing the latter only capture the variance component of test risk and are therefore insufficient for full generalization characterization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that double descent disappears and a classical U-shaped curve emerges when norm (rather than parameter count) is used as the capacity measure—reframes a widely accepted phenomenon as potentially an artifact of an inappropriate capacity metric, with implications for how practitioners interpret scaling laws and configure model size. However, the analysis is confined to random feature models and linear regression with well-behaved (sub-Gaussian, power-law spectrum) data; the full 'φ' curve observed empirically is not recovered, and extension to trained features, SGD dynamics, and deep architectures remains open. The non-monotone scaling law in norm-based capacity (where the norm exponent can be positive or negative depending on the regime) also challenges the standard monotone-decreasing scaling law narrative, though its practical relevance to LLMs is speculative at this stage.
