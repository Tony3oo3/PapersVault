## Problem

Quadratic transformers (softmax attention over key-value memory) provide precise retrieval but are limited to bounded context windows with quadratic complexity, while linear transformers (fast weight memory via DeltaNet) support unbounded sequences and enable expressive state-tracking but sacrifice retrieval precision. Prior hybrid attempts (Arora et al., Munkhdalai et al.) used vanilla linear attention and 'delayed' blending strategies that are incompatible with leveraging DeltaNet's expressivity advantage, leaving the optimal design for combining these complementary memory systems unresolved.

## Input / Output

For language modeling and retrieval tasks, the model consumes text token sequences (vocabulary size 32K, sequence lengths 2048–2240) and produces next-token predictions; the KV-memory operates on a sliding window (default 64 tokens) while the FW-memory maintains a constant-size d_out × d_key matrix. For the reinforcement learning evaluation, a 3×5×5 visual observation grid is processed by a 2-layer CNN into a 144-dim vector, and the sequence model outputs a 100-dim hidden state feeding policy/value networks that produce one of four discrete navigation actions. The final output at each step is a context-dependent weighted combination of the two memory systems' outputs, controlled by a learned mixing vector γ_t ∈ R^{d_out}.

## Method

The paper proposes Hybrid Quadratic-Linear Transformers (HQLTs) that unify KV-memory (softmax attention over a sliding window of size S) and FW-memory (DeltaNet with delta-rule updates) within a single layer, sharing the same query, key, and value projections. Three blending strategies are compared: Delayed-Streaming (old KV pairs evicted from the window are fed to FW-memory), Delayed-Chunk (chunk-wise processing where softmax attention handles intra-chunk and FW-memory handles inter-chunk), and Synchronous (the current key-value pair is fed to both systems simultaneously). The key finding is that Synchronous HQLT is optimal: the delayed variants introduce a temporal lag that prevents FW-memory from performing real-time state-tracking (failing parity and modular arithmetic tasks), while Synchronous preserves DeltaNet's expressivity while retaining KV-memory's precise retrieval. The output combines both memory reads via dynamic vector mixing (a learned per-dimension gate), and the architecture is compatible with flash-attention and flash-linear-attention kernels for efficient training and inference.

## Results

On general language modeling (340M and 1.3B params, 15B tokens), Synchronous HQLT achieves the best average zero-shot accuracy of 47.8% and 53.9% respectively, matching or slightly exceeding Transformer++ (47.6%, 53.0%) and DeltaNet (46.8%, 52.3%). The standout improvement is on LAMBADA perplexity, where Synchronous HQLT achieves ~15% relative reduction over both baselines (29.4 vs. 34.9 at 340M; 15.9 vs. 17.9 at 1.3B). On expressivity tasks (Parity, Modular Arithmetic), only Synchronous HQLT succeeds (100% and 97.0% normalized accuracy), while both delayed variants fail at chance level. On retrieval tasks, HQLT with a 1024-token KV window achieves 40.4% average accuracy, substantially outperforming Transformer++ at the same window size (29.0%) and DeltaNet (17.4%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Transformer++ (Llama 2 architecture), Synchronous HQLT matches or slightly exceeds average zero-shot accuracy at both scales (47.8 vs. 47.6 at 340M; 53.9 vs. 53.0 at 1.3B) while achieving a large ~15% relative improvement on LAMBADA perplexity. On retrieval tasks, HQLT with a 1024-token KV window (40.4% avg) substantially outperforms Transformer++ at the same window size (29.0%), demonstrating the benefit of the additional FW-memory for longer context. Against DeltaNet, Synchronous HQLT wins on general language modeling (47.8 vs. 46.8 at 340M; 53.9 vs. 52.3 at 1.3B) and on retrieval (31.1 vs. 28.8 at 1.3B with window 64), confirming that the KV-memory component recovers the precise-recall capability that DeltaNet lacks. On the expressivity tasks (Parity, Modular Arithmetic), Synchronous HQLT matches DeltaNet's performance (100% and 97.0% vs. 100% and 97.1% from Grazzi et al.), while both delayed HQLT variants collapse to chance level—demonstrating that synchronous processing is essential to preserve DeltaNet's state-tracking expressivity. Notably, at 1.3B scale, Delayed-Stream HQLT achieves the best overall average (54.5%), suggesting the optimal variant may depend on scale and task mix.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the quadratic transformer (softmax attention over key-value memory) and DeltaNet (a fast-weight-programming linear transformer with delta-rule updates) as two complementary memory systems, and proposes three strategies for blending them within a single layer. It departs from prior hybrid attempts—Arora et al. [21] and Munkhdalai et al. [22] (Infini-attention)—by using DeltaNet rather than vanilla linear attention as the FW-memory component, and by systematically comparing three blending schemes (Delayed-Streaming, Delayed-Chunk, Synchronous) that differ in how and when inputs reach each memory system. The paper also draws on the chunk-wise parallel training framework for linear transformers and the complementary learning systems literature from cognitive science for its conceptual motivation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical takeaway is that the Synchronous variant—where both memory systems process the same input simultaneously—is necessary to preserve DeltaNet's state-tracking expressivity, while the conceptually elegant 'delayed' division of labor (KV-memory for recent, FW-memory for old) fails on tasks requiring real-time state tracking. This provides a clear design principle for future hybrid attention architectures and is directly implementable with existing flash-attention and flash-linear-attention kernels. However, retrieval performance remains a significant limitation: even with a 1024-token KV-memory window, HQLT still underperforms a full-context transformer on recall-intensive tasks, and the authors identify the need for a mechanism allowing FW-memory to selectively revive and reinsert memories into KV-memory as an open problem.
