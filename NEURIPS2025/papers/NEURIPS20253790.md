## Problem

Time series foundation models (TSFMs) can generate forecasts that exhibit dynamics drastically different from the context, yet this form of hallucination has not been formally defined or systematically studied for TSFMs. Unlike LLMs where hallucination detection and mitigation via hidden-state intervention is well explored, the underlying mechanisms causing TSFMs to lose context information during forward propagation remain uncharacterized, leaving no principled way to detect or correct such failures at test time.

## Input / Output

The method takes a context time series of fixed length (500 steps in experiments) and produces a forecast over a horizon of 64 steps in a zero-shot setting. Beyond the forecast, the pipeline outputs a hallucination detection score (SSAS) computed from the top signal neuron's activation variance at the final layer, and a binary hallucination verdict determined by four knowledge rules (trend, frequency, pattern, ARMA) extracted from the context. The intervention itself operates on hidden state matrices of shape n×d at each transformer layer during forward propagation, requiring only a pre-computed set of signal neuron indices per layer.

## Method

The core insight is that TSFM hallucinations arise when signal subspaces in hidden states become inactive, causing homogeneous activations across positions and loss of context information. To identify these subspaces, the authors compute per-neuron activity scores (activation variance across positions) on clean signal inputs versus pure noise inputs, selecting neurons with the highest contrastive scores as signal neurons. The Signal Subspace Intervention through Magnification (SSIM) then applies a Center-Project-Scale (CPS) operation at each layer: centering hidden states, projecting onto the signal subspace, scaling the signal components by an adaptive factor λ (computed as the ratio of reference signal activity to current activity, applied only when λ > 1), and restoring the mean. This preserves the mean of activations while proportionally scaling the standard deviation of signal neurons, provably reducing cosine similarity between hidden states and restoring the distinctiveness needed for faithful forecast generation.

## Results

Across three TSFMs (Chronos, Chronos-Bolt, TimesFM) and six domains, SSIM achieves up to 6.62% reduction in hallucination rate, 93.83% gain in R², and 13.52% gain in Pearson correlation over the original models. On the aggregated results, Chronos improves from R² = −82.38 to −5.08 and correlation from 0.446 to 0.506; TimesFM improves from R² = −4.55 to −1.25 and correlation from 0.637 to 0.641. The SSAS signal strength measure attains AUROC up to 0.85 for hallucination detection and Spearman rank correlations up to 0.63 with forecasting performance, outperforming cosine similarity and activation variance baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SSIM is compared against two input-level baselines: denoising via sliding-window smoothing and perturbation averaging (10 Gaussian-noise perturbations with output averaging, following Liu et al. [25]). SSIM outperforms both baselines on all three metrics (hallucination rate, R², correlation) across all three TSFMs in the aggregated results, with differences statistically significant at p < 0.01 by the Friedman–Nemenyi test. Denoising is particularly harmful: it increases hallucination rates substantially (e.g., Chronos aggregated Hal rises from 0.453 to 0.599) and degrades correlation, because smoothing erases context information. Perturbation averaging [25] is the closest competitor, matching or slightly exceeding SSIM on correlation for Chronos-Bolt (0.5158 vs. 0.5171) but consistently worse on hallucination rate and R²; it also requires 10× forward passes, making it far less efficient. In an additional comparison (Appendix D.4), SSIM for Chronos is on par with or better than full-parameter finetuning on most domains, while finetuning suffers severe overfitting on TimesFM (aggregated R² = −83,520 vs. SSIM's −1.25).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the activation-steering and hidden-state-intervention paradigm from LLM and LVLM hallucination mitigation [22, 25, 48] into the time series forecasting domain, but introduces a key departure: rather than adding a static steering vector, it identifies context-specific signal subspaces via contrastive neuron activity scoring and applies an adaptive, layer-selective magnification (CPS). The signal/noise subspace decomposition draws on null-space and over-smoothing literature [14, 21, 30] but reframes it in terms of information-theoretic signal propagation through transformer layers. The hallucination definition itself is novel, borrowing the knowledge-rule verification structure from LLM factuality checking but grounding it in time series statistics (trend, frequency, pattern, ARMA dynamics).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first formal treatment of TSFM hallucinations, providing both a detection metric (SSAS) and a training-free mitigation (SSIM) that practitioners can apply immediately to existing models like Chronos and TimesFM. The finding that hallucinations correlate with homogeneous hidden states (high cosine similarity, low activation variance) gives a concrete diagnostic signal for monitoring TSFM reliability in deployment. Open questions include whether the signal subspace identification generalizes to non-Transformer TSFMs, whether the knowledge rules capture all meaningful hallucination modes, and whether the approach extends to multi-step probabilistic forecasting or multivariate settings. The method's reliance on a synthetic waveform dataset for signal neuron identification is a practical limitation that may affect transfer to highly non-stationary real-world series.
