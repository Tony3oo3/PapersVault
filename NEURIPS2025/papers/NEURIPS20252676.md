## Problem

Despite rapid deployment of LLM-powered AI agents with tools, memory, and web access, prior security evaluations have been limited to simplistic chatbot interactions or constrained classifier tasks under artificial conditions. It remains unclear whether frontier agents can reliably enforce their own deployment policies in realistic multi-tool environments under sustained adversarial pressure, and how vulnerabilities generalize across models and attack types.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252676/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes as input a deployment scenario (system prompt, tool definitions, and policy constraints) paired with an adversarial prompt injection—either a direct user message or an indirect injection embedded in a simulated tool-call response (e.g., a malicious string inserted into a JSON field of a fetched web page or calendar event). The agent processes the interaction over multiple turns, making tool calls and receiving tool returns, until it stops acting. The output is a binary policy-violation determination per interaction (via programmatic or LLM judges), aggregated into per-model and per-behavior attack success rates, plus cross-model transfer success rates when attacks are replayed against models they were not originally crafted for.

## Method

The authors ran the largest public AI agent red-teaming competition to date (1.8M adversarial prompts, ~2,000 participants, 44 scenarios, 22 frontier LLMs over four weekly waves), then curated 4,700 high-quality attacks into the ART benchmark using a stricter LLM-based judge and sampling up to 5 attacks per behavior-model pair. They evaluate 19 models (including unseen ones like Gemini 2.5 Pro and o4-mini) on the ART subset, measuring ASR at 1, 10, and 100 queries per behavior. To characterize attack structure, they embed successful single-turn attacks with all-mpnet-base-v2 sentence-transformers, retrieve nearest neighbors via FAISS (cosine similarity > 0.9 across different behaviors), and identify universal attack clusters; they also project Qwen-32B embeddings of full multi-turn attack traces via UMAP to reveal strategy-level sub-clusters. A key finding is that indirect prompt injections achieve roughly 5× higher ASR than direct ones (27.1% vs. 5.7% overall), that attacks transfer strongly across model families, and that robustness shows little correlation with model size, capability (GPQA), or inference-time compute.

## Results

Across 1.8 million adversarial prompts submitted by ~2,000 participants, over 62,000 successful policy violations were elicited from 22 frontier LLMs, achieving a 100% behavior-level attack success rate (every model was broken on every target behavior). The overall Attack Success Rate (ASR) was 12.7%, with indirect prompt injections (27.1%) dramatically outperforming direct attacks (5.7%). The curated ART benchmark of 4,700 attacks, evaluated on 19 models, shows that after just 10–100 queries per behavior, nearly all agents exhibit policy violations across virtually all tested behaviors, with strong transferability to unseen models (Gemini 2.5 Pro, o4-mini).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare a proposed method against baselines; instead, it benchmarks 22 frontier LLMs (from OpenAI, Google DeepMind, Anthropic, Amazon, xAI, Meta, Cohere, Mistral) under identical adversarial conditions. Indirect prompt injections consistently outperform direct attacks by roughly 4–5× across all categories, with Prohibited Actions showing the largest gap (36.8% vs. 4.2%). Claude models exhibit the highest robustness (lowest ASR), while o3-mini and Llama 3.3 70B are the most vulnerable to transfer attacks. Critically, no consistent correlation exists between model capability (GPQA score), model size, or inference-time compute and adversarial robustness—e.g., GPT-4.5 is an order of magnitude larger than GPT-4o yet shows no proportionally greater resistance, and o3-mini vs. o4-mini demonstrate comparable capabilities but substantially different ASRs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of agent security benchmarking (AgentDojo, AgentHarm, InjecAgent) by scaling it to an unprecedented degree: 1.8 million adversarial prompts across 22 frontier LLMs and 44 realistic deployment scenarios with tools, memory, and policy constraints. Rather than introducing a new attack method, it repurposes the public red-teaming competition format (Gray Swan Arena) as a data-collection mechanism, then applies standard NLP analysis tools (sentence-transformer embeddings, FAISS retrieval, UMAP projection) to characterize attack transferability, universality, and diversity. The key conceptual departure from prior work is the emphasis on realistic multi-turn agent interactions with simulated tool environments, as opposed to the single-turn or constrained classifier settings that dominated earlier evaluations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that nearly all frontier agents exhibit policy violations across virtually all tested behaviors within 10–100 queries, with high cross-model transferability, underscores that current deployment policies are not reliably enforced under adversarial pressure. Critically, the paper shows no consistent correlation between robustness and model size, capability (GPQA), or inference-time compute, challenging the assumption that scaling alone will close the security gap. The ART benchmark (4,700 curated attacks, private leaderboard) provides a rigorous, continuously updated evaluation tool, but its private nature limits independent reproducibility. Open questions include what architectural or training-time defenses can actually reduce the near-universal vulnerability observed, and whether the high transferability implies a small set of shared failure modes that could be targeted by focused interventions.
