## Problem

Transformers frequently fail to generalize to sequences longer than those seen during training, especially on algorithmic tasks like sorting and arithmetic, yet existing theoretical understanding is limited to asymptotic results or narrow problem classes, and empirical fixes (specialized positional encodings, data formatting tricks) are fragile and task-specific. This paper asks: under what verifiable conditions does a transformer provably length-generalize, and can those conditions be turned into a practical training recipe?

## Input / Output

The model receives a token sequence of length N augmented with an end-of-input symbol ⊥, and for multi-token outputs, previously generated tokens are also fed back as context. Training is restricted to sequences of length at most n (typically 20), while evaluation targets sequences of length N ≫ n (up to 100+). The auxiliary tasks share the same input format but require predicting a single output token conditioned on a small subset of relevant preceding tokens, with irrelevant tokens randomly subsampled from longer source sequences to form the training examples.

## Method

The paper introduces a theoretical framework centered on sparse functions—tasks where each output token depends on only a small number of preceding tokens—and defines (N,n)-robustness: the model must be correct with large margin not only on native length-n sequences but also on random n-length restrictions of length-N sequences. The key theorem shows that (N,n)-robustness with sufficient margin implies error at length N degrades only linearly as O(ε·N/n). This motivates a concrete training recipe: identify a small set of auxiliary tasks (e.g., successor-finding for sorting, single-digit addition for increment, contextual lookup for SLiM) that capture the local functions repeatedly applied in the primary task, then jointly train the model on both the primary task and these auxiliary tasks, generating auxiliary data via (N,n)-perturbations (randomly subsampling irrelevant tokens from longer sequences). The model is a decoder-only transformer with no positional embeddings (NoPE), trained with standard multi-task round-robin batching, and the approach is validated on six synthetic benchmarks where it achieves >90% accuracy at lengths 5× the training length, compared to near-zero for baselines.

## Results

On the sorting task (trained on lengths up to 20), the baseline model's exact-match accuracy drops below 1% at length 100, while the proposed auxiliary-task method achieves over 90% accuracy, maintaining above 95% up to length 100. On the SLiM task, the baseline degrades sharply beyond the training length, whereas the proposed method remains robust up to twice the training length. For the increment task, the proposed method length-generalizes by at least a factor of 1.5 (achieving ≥90% test accuracy), compared to at most a factor of 1.2 in the prior work by Awasthi and Gupta (2023). Across all six synthetic tasks (Sorting, Reversal, Copy, Increment, Parity, SLiM), the auxiliary-task-trained models consistently outperform the baseline in the extrapolation regime (lengths beyond 20).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the proposed multi-task training method (primary task + auxiliary tasks trained on (N,n)-perturbed distributions) against a standard baseline: a decoder-only transformer trained solely on the primary task with the same architecture, data volume, and scratchpad format. The proposed method wins decisively on all six tasks in the length-extrapolation regime. The most dramatic gap is on sorting, where the baseline collapses to <1% exact-match accuracy at length 100 while the proposed method sustains >90%. On tasks requiring position tracking (Reversal, Copy, Parity), the baseline drops sharply beyond length 20 while the auxiliary-task model retains high accuracy. On SLiM, which combines contextual lookup with a non-trivial computation rule, the baseline fails quickly beyond training length whereas the proposed method generalizes to roughly 2× the training length. Both models use scratchpads and NoPE (no positional embeddings), so the improvement is attributable to the auxiliary-task training procedure rather than architectural changes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper substantially extends the authors' earlier preprint on task hinting (Awasthi & Gupta, 2023) by replacing ad hoc hints with a principled framework that identifies auxiliary tasks from the sparse structure of the target function and prescribes training on (N,n)-perturbed distributions. It positions itself as orthogonal to the dominant empirical approaches—positional encoding modifications (FIRE, position coupling, NoPE), data formatting tricks (reversal, index hints, scratchpads), and curriculum learning—by arguing that the right training distribution, not the right architecture, is what induces length generalization. Theoretically, it complements concurrent sparsity-based analyses (Golowich et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024b) by providing finite-length quantitative bounds rather than asymptotic guarantees, and by connecting robust correctness on auxiliary tasks directly to a concrete multi-task training recipe.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a rare combination of a clean theoretical guarantee (linear error scaling O(ε·N/n)) and a simple, interpretable training procedure (identify local functions, train on perturbed data) that practitioners can apply without architectural modifications. However, the theoretical analysis is restricted to single-layer, single-head attention-only transformers, and all empirical validation is on synthetic algorithmic tasks; extending the framework to deeper architectures and natural language tasks where 'sparse functions' are less cleanly defined remains a significant open question. The need to manually identify auxiliary tasks and the gap between the pessimistic theoretical margin requirement and the practical rule-of-thumb (equation 5) also limit immediate applicability, though the scaling experiments suggest the theory captures the right qualitative behavior.
