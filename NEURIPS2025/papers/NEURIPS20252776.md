## Problem

DRL-based approaches to the flexible job-shop scheduling problem (FJSP) predominantly rely on constructive methods that build schedules step-by-step, but partial solutions suffer from information loss and often fail to reach near-optimal makespans. Improvement-based methods that iteratively refine complete solutions are more effective for approaching optimality, yet FJSP's flexible machine allocation introduces challenges in state representation, policy learning, and search efficiency that have prevented the adaptation of existing DRL improvement frameworks (designed for non-flexible JSP) to this setting.

## Input / Output

The method takes an FJSP instance (n jobs, m machines, per-operation processing times on compatible machines) and a complete initial scheduling solution generated by the DANIEL construction model. The initial solution is encoded as a directed heterogeneous disjunctive graph with operation nodes, machine nodes, conjunctive arcs (job precedence), and directed hyper-edges (machine processing sequences). After a predefined number of iterative local moves (100–400 iterations), the output is a refined schedule with re-sequenced operations and potentially re-assigned machines, minimizing the makespan C_max. The action space is reduced from O(|O|²|M|) to O(|O|²) by exploiting the constraint that the target operation must already be assigned to the target machine.

## Method

MIStar formulates iterative FJSP refinement as an MDP and introduces three key components. First, a directed heterogeneous disjunctive graph with machine nodes connected to their processing sequences via directed hyper-edges explicitly encodes machine status and operation ordering, overcoming the limitations of conventional disjunctive graphs that lack machine nodes. Second, a memory-enhanced heterogeneous GNN (MHGNN) extracts state embeddings using GIN for topological structure and GAT for semantic relationships, then retrieves K nearest historical states via Frobenius inner product on compact operation-machine incidence matrices and aggregates their actions through a soft voting mechanism to produce a historical action embedding that encourages exploration and alleviates local optima. Third, a parallel greedy search strategy samples P candidate actions from the Nopt2 neighborhood at each step, evaluates their makespan improvements in parallel, and executes only the best one, achieving comparable solution quality with far fewer iterations. Training uses n-step PPO with a reward combining makespan improvement over the incumbent best and a penalty for redundant solution visits.

## Results

On public benchmarks (400 iterations), MIStar achieves the best gap across all four Hurink/Brandimarte sets: 2.96% on mk, 2.37% on la(rdata), 6.83% on la(edata), and 0.24% on la(vdata), consistently outperforming the best rule-based method (BI-400) by 0.48–0.29 percentage points while running 2–4× faster. On synthetic SD2 40×10 instances, MIStar reaches a −7.26% gap (below the OR-Tools reference) in 5.4 min, versus −6.81% for BI in 1.42 h. On the largest 50×30 instances, OR-Tools fails to produce any feasible solution within 60 min, while MIStar finds a solution (makespan 1040.1) in the same time budget.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MIStar outperforms both DRL construction baselines (DANIEL and HGNN) by a wide margin: on la(rdata), MIStar-400 achieves a 2.37% gap versus 11.84% (DANIEL-G) and 10.86% (HGNN-G), while running in ~85 s versus <1 s for the construction methods (which produce only a single solution). Against the three handcrafted improvement heuristics (GD, FI, BI from Nowicki & Hansen), MIStar-400 consistently achieves the best gap on all four benchmark sets (e.g., 2.96% vs. 3.44% for BI on mk; 0.24% vs. 0.53% for GD on la(vdata)) while being 2–4× faster (76–87 s vs. 92–586 s). The 2SGA genetic algorithm [58] is competitive on la(vdata) (0.39% gap) but is not reported on the other Hurink sets; MIStar still edges it out on mk (2.96% vs. 1.57% for 2SGA, though 2SGA is closer to OR-Tools). On synthetic SD2 instances where initial solutions are far from optimal, MIStar's advantage over construction methods is most pronounced (e.g., 5.27% gap vs. 15.57% for DANIEL on SD2 30×10 at 400 iterations), confirming that the improvement paradigm benefits most when the initial solution quality is low.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the DRL-based improvement heuristic paradigm from JSP (Zhang et al. [21, 29], which use N5 neighborhood and standard disjunctive graphs) to the more complex FJSP setting, where flexible machine assignments invalidate the N5 structure and require a new neighborhood (Nopt2) and a heterogeneous graph with machine nodes and directed hyper-edges. It also adapts the memory-augmented RL idea from MARCO [47] (designed for binary graph problems) to scheduling by introducing a soft voting mechanism over discrete action tuples and a compact operation-machine incidence matrix for similarity. The construction-based DRL methods (HGNN [8], DANIEL [22]) serve as the primary baselines that the improvement paradigm is designed to surpass by avoiding the information loss inherent in partial-solution states.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FJSP is the more practically relevant variant for Industry 4.0 smart manufacturing, yet prior DRL improvement methods were limited to the simpler JSP; this work closes that gap with a complete framework (graph representation, memory-augmented policy, parallel search) that generalizes across scales and distributions. The finding that OR-Tools cannot produce any feasible solution on 50×30 instances while MIStar succeeds highlights a practical scalability advantage for very large problems. Open questions include whether the parallel scale P can be adapted online, whether disruptive moves (e.g., segment reconstruction) can further reduce sensitivity to initial solutions, and whether the memory mechanism transfers to other flexible scheduling variants (e.g., with setup times or dynamic arrivals).
