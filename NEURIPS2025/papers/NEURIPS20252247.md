## Problem

Whether a model trained on low-dimensional inputs can transfer its performance to higher-dimensional inputs has been studied primarily for GNNs using graphon-specific tools (cut metric, homomorphism densities), making the analysis difficult to extend to other domains like sets and point clouds. This paper asks what general assumptions on the learning problem guarantee cross-dimensional transferability, and provides a unified framework that recovers existing GNN results while extending them to new architectures and modalities.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252247/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a fixed-parameter model θ ∈ ℝ^k that defines a sequence of functions (f_n: V_n → ℝ) for arbitrarily large input sizes n, where V_n ranges over sets (ℝ^n), graph signals (ℝ^{n×n}_sym × ℝ^{n×d}), or point clouds (ℝ^{n×k}). The key structural requirement is that inputs of different sizes are compared via a symmetrized metric on a limit space (e.g., Wasserstein distance for sets, cut distance for graphs), so transferability means f_n(x_n) ≈ f_m(x_m) whenever x_n and x_m are close in this limit space. The output is a scalar prediction, and the theory additionally yields a size generalization bound quantifying how test error at size n_test relates to training at size n_train.

## Method

The core mechanism is a 'consistent sequence' — a directed system of finite-dimensional vector spaces V_n with linear equivariant embeddings φ_{N,n} and group actions G_n — which formalizes how small problem instances embed into larger ones. Transferability is proven equivalent to two conditions: (i) compatibility, meaning the sequence (f_n) respects the embeddings and extends to a well-defined map f_∞ on the limit space V_∞, and (ii) continuity (or Lipschitz continuity) of f_∞ with respect to a compatible norm, which for linear layers reduces to uniformly bounded operator norms. Proposition 5.1 shows these properties are preserved under composition, enabling layer-by-layer verification. The paper instantiates the framework on DeepSet, PointNet, MPNNs, IGN, and DS-CI, identifying which are transferable under which norms, and proposes new transferable architectures: GGNN and Continuous GGNN for graphs (fixing IGN's incompatibility via compatible linear layers and bounded operator norms), and normalized SVD-DeepSet for point clouds (applying SVD canonicalization before a normalized DeepSet). A size generalization bound (Proposition 4.2) then follows from covering numbers and Wasserstein distance between the training distribution and the limit distribution.

## Results

This is primarily a theoretical paper; its headline results are Proposition 3.2 (transferability implies output convergence at rate R(n)+R(m)) and Proposition 4.2 (a size-generalization bound that vanishes as training size n and dataset size N grow). Empirically, across four size-generalization tasks (population statistics, maximal distance, weighted triangle density, Gromov-Wasserstein lower bound), models aligned with the target at the continuity level consistently achieve the lowest test MSE at large test dimensions, while incompatible or merely compatible models diverge or plateau. For example, normalized DeepSet outperforms DeepSet and PointNet on the population-statistics task, PointNet outperforms both on maximal-distance, and Continuous GGNN outperforms standard GNN and GGNN on weighted triangle density at large graph sizes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On the set tasks (Figure 5a–b), normalized DeepSet [9] dominates DeepSet [91] and PointNet [66] on population statistics (where the target is Wasserstein-continuous), while PointNet wins on maximal distance (where the target is Hausdorff-continuous); DeepSet is unaligned in both settings and shows the worst extrapolation. On graphs (Figure 5c), the proposed Continuous GGNN achieves the best large-graph test MSE for weighted triangle density, outperforming the message-passing GNN of Ruiz et al. [70] (which is transferable but lacks expressivity for this task) and the standard GGNN (compatible but not transferable); normalized 2-IGN [11] is incompatible and fails to converge. On point clouds (Figure 5d), normalized DS-CI [5] and the new normalized SVD-DeepSet both generalize well to larger point clouds on the Gromov-Wasserstein task, with DS-CI achieving slightly lower MSE while SVD-DeepSet offers superior time and memory efficiency. Overall, the paper's central empirical claim is that continuity-level alignment with the target function is the decisive factor for size generalization, not merely compatibility or architectural invariance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper generalizes the GNN transferability framework (Ruiz et al. 2020; Levie et al. 2021; Maskey et al. 2023), which was tied to graphon-specific tools like homomorphism density convergence, into a unified theory based on consistent sequences, compatible maps, and continuity in limit spaces. It builds directly on the any-dimensional equivariant neural network framework of Levin & Díaz (2024) and Levin & Chandrasekaran (2023), extending it with the notion of transferability and a size-generalization bound. The paper also modifies Invariant Graph Networks (Maron et al. 2018) to produce provably transferable variants (GGNN, Continuous GGNN) and introduces SVD-DeepSet as a more efficient alternative to DS-CI (Blum-Smith et al. 2024).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key insight—that transferability across dimensions is equivalent to continuity in a data-driven limit space—provides a principled, domain-agnostic recipe for designing size-generalizable models and explains why certain popular architectures (standard IGN, unnormalized DeepSet) fail to transfer. The generalization bound (Proposition 4.2) connects model Lipschitz constants and data-space covering numbers to size generalization, offering actionable guidance. Open questions include extending the framework to sparse graphs, characterizing expressivity of any-dimensional networks on their limit spaces, and whether the continuity-alignment principle holds for more complex symmetries (e.g., non-compact groups). The experimental validation, while supportive, is limited to small-scale tasks and specific sampling schemes.
