## Problem

Demonstrations and instructions are the two dominant ways of specifying tasks to LLMs, yet prior interpretability work has studied their internal mechanisms in isolation. It remains unclear whether these two prompting forms converge on a shared task representation or activate distinct, partly overlapping circuits within the model.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252380/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a frozen transformer (Llama-3.1-8B, Llama-3.2-3B, OLMo-2-7B) and processes task prompts in two forms—K-shot in-context demonstrations or zero-shot textual instructions—along with query examples from ~50 supervised datasets. It produces a function vector (a single additive vector in the model's residual-stream dimension) and a set of ~20 causally relevant attention heads, evaluated by injecting the FV at layer |L/3| and measuring accuracy in 0-shot (for instruction FVs) or shuffled 10-shot (for demonstration FVs) settings. Uninformative baselines differ by prompt type: label-shuffled demonstrations for the original setting, and three alternatives (equiprobable token sequences, natural WikiText-103 passages, and other-task instructions) for the instruction setting.

## Method

The core contribution is generalizing the function vector (FV) extraction procedure from Todd et al. (2024) from in-context demonstrations to arbitrary task presentations, specifically zero-shot textual instructions generated by Llama-3.1-405B. For each task, the top 5 best-performing instructions are used to compute task-conditioned mean activations across all attention heads; causal indirect effects (CIE) are then measured by intervening on uninformative baseline prompts and scoring the change in correct-token probability, yielding a set of ~20 top heads whose task-conditioned means sum to the FV. The analytical contribution is a battery of controlled comparisons: evaluating each FV type in its natural setting, jointly adding both FVs to test for complementary information, visualizing head overlap across prompt types, constructing 'heterogeneous' FVs (heads localized by one prompt type paired with activations from the other) to test mechanism sharing, and transferring instruction FVs from post-trained to base models to identify which post-training stages (SFT vs. DPO) drive instruction FV efficacy.

## Results

Instruction function vectors (FVs) extracted from zero-shot textual instructions increase task accuracy from below 20% (no-FV baseline) to above 50% in the best post-trained models (Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct). Jointly adding both demonstration and instruction FVs to the residual stream consistently outperforms either FV alone across all models tested. Of the top 20 causally relevant attention heads, only 7 are shared between demonstration and instruction FVs in Llama-3.2-3B models and only 4 in Llama-3.1-8B models, indicating largely distinct mechanisms. Instruction FVs from post-trained models transfer to their base counterparts, recovering most of the post-trained zero-shot accuracy gain, with SFT and DPO stages each contributing meaningful improvements over the base model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between instruction-based FVs (the novel contribution) and demonstration-based FVs from Todd et al. (2024), the original function vector method. Demonstration FVs achieve higher peak accuracy in their matched evaluation setting (shuffled 10-shot) than instruction FVs do in zero-shot, consistent with ICL from demonstrations being more 'primal' to base models. However, instruction FVs are substantially more effective in post-trained (instruction-tuned) models than in base models, while demonstration FVs show minimal sensitivity to post-training. The joint intervention (adding both FVs simultaneously) outperforms either FV alone in all models except base Llama-3.1-8B, providing behavioral evidence that the two FVs carry complementary task information. At the mechanistic level, the two FV types engage mostly distinct attention heads (only 4–7 out of 20 shared), and an asymmetry is observed: instruction-localized heads are more useful when paired with demonstration activations than the reverse, suggesting instruction task inference leverages heads that play only a peripheral role in demonstration-based ICL.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends Todd et al. (2024, ICLR) by generalizing the function vector (FV) extraction procedure from in-context demonstrations to arbitrary task presentations, specifically textual instructions. While prior interpretability work studied ICL mechanisms (Olsson et al., 2022; Akyürek et al., 2024) and instruction-following (Stolfo et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024) in isolation, this is the first to use a common causal-intervention tool to ask whether the two prompting methods elicit shared or distinct internal representations. It also connects to the broader task-representation literature (Hendel et al., 2023; Pan et al., 2023; Saglam et al., 2025) by contrasting FVs derived from different prompt forms rather than different tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that demonstrations and instructions engage mostly distinct attention heads provides a mechanistic explanation for why combining both in prompts improves performance — they carry complementary task information. The demonstration that instruction FVs from post-trained models can steer base models, and that SFT and DPO (not RL) are the critical post-training stages, offers practical guidance for activation steering and potentially for reducing post-training costs. Open questions include what post-training changes create the instruction-inference mechanism, how instruction FV heads relate to induction heads, and whether findings generalize to complex, open-ended tasks and larger models.
