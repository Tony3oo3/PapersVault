## Problem

Machine learning interatomic potentials (MLIPs) that approximate DFT have typically been trained on smaller, domain-specific datasets, limiting their ability to generalize across the diverse chemical domains (materials, molecules, catalysts, molecular crystals, MOFs) and DFT settings used in practice. This paper addresses whether pooling nearly 500 million atomic structures across multiple domains into a single multi-task training set can yield a universal model that matches or exceeds task-specialized models without fine-tuning, and how to scale model capacity to fit such a dataset without sacrificing inference speed.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254310/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes 3D atomic positions, atomic numbers, total charge, total spin multiplicity, and a DFT task identifier (a random vector selecting one of five training dataset DFT settings, e.g. PBE/VASP vs. ωB97M-V/ORCA). It outputs total energy from which per-atom forces and the stress tensor are derived via autograd in the conservative fine-tuning stage. A 6 Å neighbor cutoff with message passing defines the local interaction range, and the model is designed to handle systems up to 350 atoms in training and 100k+ atoms at inference on a single 80 GB GPU.

## Method

UMA is built on the eSEN SO(2) equivariant GNN, extended with embeddings for charge, spin, and DFT task that are added to L=0 spherical harmonic channels at each layer. The key architectural novelty is Mixture of Linear Experts (MoLE), which replaces each linear operation in the SO2 convolution with a weighted sum of K linear experts; because the experts are linear, rotational equivariance is preserved, and because routing weights α depend only on time-invariant global features (element composition, charge, spin, task), the merged weight matrix W* = Σ αₖWₖ can be precomputed before a simulation, yielding inference cost identical to a dense model. Training uses a two-stage schedule: a fast direct-force pre-training stage in BF16, followed by a conservative fine-tuning stage in FP32 that removes the force head and recovers energy-conserving forces and stresses via autograd. The paper further derives empirical power-law scaling relations between FLOPs, parameter count, and dataset size for MLIPs, showing that an optimal MoLE model achieves the same loss as a dense model with ~2.5× more active parameters, and uses these laws to select compute-optimal model sizes (UMA-S 150M/6M active, UMA-M 1.4B/50M active, UMA-L 700M dense).

## Results

On the Matbench-Discovery benchmark, UMA-M achieves the highest F1 score to date (0.929), surpassing eSEN-30M-OAM (0.925) and ORB v3 (0.905). On the AdsorbML catalysis benchmark, UMA-L achieves a 74.4% success rate, a 25% improvement over the previous SOTA eqV2-OC20 (60.8%). On molecular crystal structure prediction (7th CSP Blind Test), UMA-S predicts lattice energies with ≤3 kJ/mol MAE (2.13 kJ/mol), substantially outperforming the task-specific eSEN-S-OMC baseline (6.18 kJ/mol). On OC20 S2EF adsorption energy, UMA-M reduces error by ~80% versus prior SOTA (31.8 meV vs. 149.1 meV for eqV2-OC20).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the strongest materials baselines, UMA-M matches or slightly exceeds eSEN-30M-OAM on Matbench-Discovery F1 (0.929 vs. 0.925) and κ_SRME (0.176 vs. 0.170), while being slightly worse on WBM energy MAE (18.2 vs. 16.2 meV) and eqV2-OMat (18.2 vs. 14.9 meV). On catalysis, UMA dramatically outperforms both eqV2-OC20 and GemNet-OC20: adsorption energy MAE drops from 149.1/163.5 meV to 31.8 meV (UMA-M), and AdsorbML success rate rises from 60.8%/54.9% to 72.3% (UMA-M) or 74.4% (UMA-L). For molecules, UMA-M significantly beats eSEN-sm-cons on OOD-Comp energy (0.74 vs. 1.35 meV) and ligand-strain (2.96 vs. 4.66 meV), approaching DFT-level accuracy. On molecular crystals, UMA-S outperforms the task-specific eSEN-S-OMC on CSP lattice energy (2.13 vs. 6.18 kJ/mol) and OMC-Test energy (1.03 vs. 1.05 meV). For MOFs, UMA-M achieves the best OOD-L/T adsorption energy (294.1 meV) versus eqv2-ODAC (316.0 meV). On inference speed (Table 10), UMA-S at 1,000 atoms runs at 16 steps/s, faster than eSEN-30M-OAM (1.7 steps/s) and MACE-MPA-0 (24 steps/s at 100 atoms but OOM at 10k), and fits 100k+ atoms in memory where all baselines OOM.

## Relation to Prior Work

UMA builds directly on the eSEN equivariant GNN architecture [21] and its two-stage training procedure (direct force prediction followed by conservative fine-tuning via autograd, also proposed in [7]). The key architectural departure is the introduction of Mixture of Linear Experts (MoLE), which adapts the MoE paradigm from LLMs [32, 31] to MLIPs by using linear (rather than non-linear) experts to preserve rotational equivariance and enable weight pre-merging for efficient sequential inference. The paper also extends the empirical scaling laws framework from LLMs [28, 24, 35] to the MLIP domain for the first time at this data scale (~500M structures), and pools five previously separate domain-specific datasets (OMat24, OMol25, OC20, OMC25, ODAC25) into a single multi-task training corpus—a data-integration strategy not previously attempted at this scale.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

UMA demonstrates that a single model trained across five chemical domains (materials, molecules, catalysts, molecular crystals, MOFs) can match or exceed domain-specialized models without fine-tuning, which would substantially simplify computational workflows in drug discovery, energy storage, and catalysis. The MoLE architecture and the derived scaling laws provide concrete guidance for the field on how to allocate compute between model size and data, and the released models (with commercially permissive licensing) lower the barrier for practical MLIP adoption. Key limitations remain: the 6 Å cutoff prevents modeling of long-range interactions (e.g., adsorbates starting beyond the cutoff), discrete charge/spin embeddings limit generalization to unseen values, and the MoLE advantage over dense architectures diminishes at larger model sizes, suggesting the current dataset may be the bottleneck. Open questions include whether multi-GPU inference can unlock 100k+ atom simulations and whether continued scaling beyond the current ~50B atoms will sustain log-linear improvements.
