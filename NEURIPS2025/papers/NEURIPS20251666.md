## Problem

End-to-end autonomous driving systems trained via imitation learning suffer from causal confusion in closed-loop evaluation: expert trajectories contain unattributable noise (driving style, control errors, time-of-day effects) that causes the model to associate driving behavior with spurious factors rather than true causal environmental cues. Prior work has focused on reducing input noise through sparse representations or distillation, but the paper identifies the supervision signal itself as the primary source of causal confusion, arguing that noisy expert trajectories fundamentally weaken the model's ability to capture causal relationships between environment and action.

## Input / Output

The system consumes multi-view RGB camera images encoded via a ResNet backbone and BEVFormer into bird's-eye-view features, augmented with a high-level driving command (e.g., LaneFollow, TurnLeft) and a mid-range goal point from the global route planner. Beyond the standard ego trajectory output, the planning head produces a softmax-normalized target lane selection score over a geometry-filtered set of at most three candidate lanes (current, left, right), and the unified decoder simultaneously predicts multi-modal future trajectories for all surrounding dynamic agents. The key structural detail is that perception outputs—lane centerline polylines and predicted agent bounding boxes—serve dual roles as both planning inputs and supervisory targets.

## Method

Built on a VAD-style sparse-query architecture with minimal modifications, the PGS paradigm introduces three perception-guided self-supervision losses that replace or augment the standard imitation L1 loss. MTPS uses a geometry-based filter (distance and angle thresholds relative to lane width) to select ego-relevant lane centerlines, then reformulates multi-modal planning as a cross-entropy classification over these lanes, with the target determined by matching the GT trajectory endpoint to the nearest centerline. STPS projects each expert trajectory point onto the selected target lane centerline (replacing it if within a width threshold), producing a spatially aligned, temporally-agnostic supervision signal that removes driving-style noise. NTPS constructs future oriented bounding boxes from predicted agent trajectories and perceived dimensions, applies SAT-based overlap detection at each timestep, and penalizes ego trajectory points that fall within a margin β of colliding agent positions. Training proceeds in two stages (6 epochs each on 16 V100 GPUs): Stage 1 trains perception with lane centerlines added as a new map class plus traffic light detection; Stage 2 jointly optimizes perception and the three self-supervised planning losses with weights w_MTPS=1.0, w_STPS=0.3, w_NTPS=1.0.

## Results

On the Bench2Drive closed-loop benchmark, PGS achieves a Driving Score of 78.08 and a mean success rate of 48.64%, substantially outperforming the strongest prior method DiffAD (67.92 / 38.64%) by 10.16 points and 10.00 percentage points respectively. Compared to the VAD-Base architecture it builds upon, PGS improves the Driving Score from 42.35 to 78.08 (+35.73) and the success rate from 15.00% to 48.64% (+33.64 pp), while also reducing open-loop L2 error from 0.91 to 0.77. In multi-ability evaluation, PGS attains the highest mean ability score of 53.40%, with particularly strong results in Overtaking (73.33%), Give Way (60.00%), and Emergency Braking (55.00%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PGS outperforms all 12 compared baselines on both Driving Score and Success Rate in closed-loop evaluation. The closest competitor is DiffAD [24] (Driving Score 67.92, Success Rate 38.64%), which PGS surpasses by 10.16 points and 10.00 pp; DriveTransformer [13] (63.46 / 35.01%) and DriveAdapter* [11] (64.22 / 33.08%) trail by 14.62 and 13.86 Driving Score points respectively. Against the architectural baseline VAD-Base [14] (42.35 / 15.00%), PGS gains +35.73 Driving Score and +33.64 pp success rate, demonstrating that the self-supervision paradigm—not architectural complexity—drives the improvement. Knowledge-distillation methods (TCP-traj* [27], ThinkTwice* [12], DriveAdapter* [11]) all score below 65 Driving Score, confirming that transferring privileged perception knowledge does not substitute for explicit causal alignment. In the multi-ability breakdown (Table 2), PGS leads every individual skill category, with the largest margins in Overtaking (73.33% vs. 35.55% for DiffAD) and Merging (35.00% vs. 30.00% for DiffAD), while the gap narrows in Traffic Sign compliance (43.68% vs. 56.43% for DriveAdapter*), suggesting that signal-based reasoning remains a relative weakness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the VAD (Vectorized Scene Representation) architecture as its end-to-end backbone, retaining its BEVFormer-based perception and unified motion decoder. It departs from the dominant imitation learning paradigm by reframing the training objective: rather than treating perception outputs as intermediate features and expert trajectories as ground truth, it elevates perception outputs (lane centerlines, predicted agent motions) to the role of primary supervisory signals for the planning head. This contrasts with prior causal-confusion mitigations (ChauffeurNet, DriveAdapter, RAD) that focus on suppressing input noise or distilling from privileged teachers; here the argument is that noisy supervision itself is the root cause, and aligning both input and output of the planner with structured perception cues resolves it.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a minimal architectural change combined with perception-guided self-supervision can outperform substantially more complex pipelines (diffusion-based planning, online trajectory ranking, multi-modal sensor fusion) on closed-loop Bench2Drive, suggesting that training paradigm design may matter more than network complexity for causal grounding in driving. However, all results are confined to the CARLA simulation environment on a single benchmark, and the notably low comfort score (12.37) hints at aggressive driving behavior that may not transfer to real-world deployment. Open questions include whether the paradigm generalizes to real sensor data with imperfect perception, whether the reliance on accurate lane centerline detection becomes a bottleneck in degraded conditions, and whether the approach scales to more diverse driving distributions beyond the 44 Bench2Drive scenarios.
