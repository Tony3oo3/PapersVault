## Problem

Existing generalization theory for kernel ridge regression is restricted to i.i.d. training samples, yet real-world applications such as denoising score learning inherently produce structured non-i.i.d. data where multiple noisy observations share a common underlying signal. No prior work has systematically characterized how this causal dependency (x → g ← u) affects KRR generalization, leaving open whether data correlations help or hurt performance and how to optimally allocate noise samples per signal.

## Input / Output

The method takes n i.i.d. signals x_i, each paired with k i.i.d. noise realizations u_ij to form nk dependent observations g_ij = g(x_i, u_ij), along with a continuous positive definite kernel k(·,·) and regularization parameter λ. The output is an excess risk bound R(λ) whose variance term explicitly decomposes into a 1/n component (governed by data relevance r̃) and a 1/(nk) component (governed by 1−r̃), yielding a closed-form optimal noise multiplicity k* for denoising score learning that scales with the time-varying noise-to-signal ratio (1−α_t^{p/2})/α_t^{p/2}.

## Method

The core technical contribution is a novel blockwise decomposition method that iteratively partitions a k-gap independent random sequence into mutually independent fragments, enabling a Bernstein-type concentration inequality that nearly matches i.i.d. rates up to logarithmic factors while explicitly capturing the benefit of intra-group data relevance. Using this tool, the paper performs a two-step concentration analysis for the bias term and a three-step analysis for the variance term (decomposed into within-observation, cross-noise-per-signal, and cross-signal covariances), yielding an excess risk bound where the variance blends 1/n and 1/(nk) weighted by the data relevance parameter r̃ = r_e ∨ r_0. A key theoretical insight is that data dependency is beneficial rather than harmful: when the signal dominates (large r̃), increasing k offers diminishing returns, while when noise dominates, more noise samples per signal directly improve generalization. Applied to a single DDPM timestep with g(x,u) = √α_t·x + √(1−α_t)·u, the framework predicts that the optimal k should scale as (1−α_t^{p/2})/α_t^{p/2}, a prediction confirmed by experiments on two-component Gaussian mixtures at varying noise levels.

## Results

The main theoretical contribution is Theorem 4.1, which establishes the excess risk bound R(λ) ≤ Θ̃_P(λ^s̃) + σ̃² O_P^poly(λ^(−1/β)(r̃/n + (1−r̃)/(nk))) for KRR under structured non-i.i.d. data, where r̃ quantifies signal relevance and k is the noise multiplicity. For denoising score learning (Theorem 4.4), the variance term becomes n^(α₀θ)·(α_t^(p/2) ∨ r_e)/n + ((1−α_t^(p/2)) ∧ (1−r_e))/(nk), predicting that optimal k scales with (1−α_t^(p/2))/α_t^(p/2). Empirically, on a 2D two-component MoG (μ=[−5,5], σ=0.2) with a 3-layer ReLU MLP (100 neurons) and fixed batch size nk=128, score estimation error is minimized at k=1 for low noise levels (t=0.50, 0.70) but decreases with increasing k for high noise levels (t=0.90, 0.95), confirming the theoretical prediction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper without traditional method-vs-method baselines; instead, it benchmarks its concentration technique and risk bounds against two prior frameworks. First, when k=1 the setting reduces to the standard i.i.d. case, exactly recovering the learning curve of Li et al. [37] (power-law kernel decay, embedding index α₀=1/β). Second, the paper's Bernstein-type concentration inequality for k-gap independent data yields a rate of Õ_P(√((kr+1−r)/(nk))) for the variance term, which *decreases* as k grows, in direct contrast to the general mixing-based bound of Mohri and Rostamizadeh [53] that gives Õ_P(√(k/n)) and treats dependence purely as a penalty. The empirical validation on the MoG score-matching task (100 runs, mean ± s.d.) shows the predicted phase transition: at t=0.5 and t=0.7 the optimal k is 1 (signal dominates), while at t=0.9 and t=0.95 the error monotonically improves up to k≈64 (noise dominates), matching the theoretical scaling (1−α_t^(p/2))/α_t^(p/2)}.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the KRR generalization theory of Li et al. [37, 39] from the i.i.d. setting to structured non-i.i.d. data with a causal signal-noise model (x → g ← u). The key technical departure is a novel blockwise decomposition method for k-gap independent sequences, motivated by Banna et al. [3], which yields a Bernstein-type concentration inequality that nearly matches i.i.d. rates up to logarithmic factors. Unlike prior non-i.i.d. analyses (mixing conditions, trajectory-based regression) that treat dependence as uniformly detrimental, this work exploits the specific causal structure to show that intra-group correlation can reduce variance. The application to DDPM connects to the diffusion model literature [26, 60, 34] but from a generalization-theory angle rather than sampling or distribution-estimation guarantees.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first principled characterization of how noise multiplicity k should scale with the noise-to-signal ratio in denoising score learning, yielding a concrete adaptive sampling strategy (k* ∝ (1−α_t^{p/2})/α_t^{p/2}) that could improve diffusion training efficiency. The k-gap concentration inequality is a reusable tool for dependent-data analysis beyond this specific setting. However, limitations are significant: the theory covers only a single timestep (not the full denoising trajectory), the empirical validation is restricted to 2D Gaussian mixtures with small networks, and the structured non-i.i.d. assumption (fixed k per signal) is narrower than general dependence. Extending to multi-timestep analysis and validating adaptive k-scheduling at image scale remain open.
