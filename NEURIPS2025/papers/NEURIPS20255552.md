## Problem

Testing whether two collections of unlabeled networks arise from the same stochastic model is fundamentally harder than comparing adjacency matrices, because nodes carry no meaningful labels and graph sizes may differ. This unlabeled two-sample problem subsumes graph isomorphism as a special case and requires solving a permutation-matching subproblem that is NP-hard in general (quadratic assignment). Existing approaches either assume vertex correspondence, are limited to single-network samples, or rely on computationally prohibitive subgraph counting.

## Input / Output

The method takes two collections of unlabeled undirected graphs (possibly with varying node counts n_rt) along with initial community label estimates from any consistent community detection algorithm. It produces a scalar chi-squared test statistic T_n with K(K+1)/2 degrees of freedom under the null, from which a p-value and accept/reject decision follow. Class proportions π_r are treated as nuisance parameters, so the test only compares connectivity matrices B_r modulo permutation, and the number of communities K can be selected data-drivenly via a bootstrap AUC procedure.

## Method

The core contribution is a spectral matching algorithm that recovers the unknown permutation between two SBM connectivity matrices by exploiting their eigenvalue decompositions: it computes EVDs of both matrices, recovers a diagonal sign matrix from eigenvector–ones-vector inner products, and then solves a linear assignment problem (Hungarian algorithm, O(K³)) to project onto the permutation group, thereby avoiding the NP-hard QAP. The full test (Algorithm 2) proceeds in three stages: (1) match every network within each sample to a reference network, (2) average the aligned connectivity matrices per sample and perform a single global cross-sample match, and (3) pool block-level edge counts across all networks to form low-variance estimates and construct a weighted chi-squared statistic using the harmonic mean of block-pair counts. Under the null the statistic converges to χ²_{K(K+1)/2} under the mild sparsity condition ν_n = Ω(log n), and under the alternative it grows at rate Ω(Nν_n²), guaranteeing consistency. The SBM framework serves as a non-parametric 'bin-then-test' device, with theoretical guidance showing K should grow with n while satisfying K² = o(nν_n) and log K = o(ν_n) for graphon approximations.

## Results

On the general SBM experiment (n=10,000, N=100, ε=0.05, ρ=0.1), SBM-TS achieves AUC-ROC of 0.95 (K=2), 0.91 (K=3), 0.83 (K=15), and 0.81 (K=20), substantially outperforming both baselines. In the RDPG experiments (n=1,000, ρ=0.15), SBM-TS essentially achieves a perfect ROC curve, while ASE-MMD shows poor performance due to its lack of a matching mechanism for orthogonal transformation mismatches. On the COLLAB dataset (5,000 ego-networks across three physics fields), SBM-TS demonstrates superior or comparable AUC-ROC to competing methods across all tested class pairs and sample sizes (m=5, 10).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SBM-TS consistently outperforms both baselines across all experimental settings. Against ASE-MMD [37] (a nonparametric two-sample test based on maximum mean discrepancy of adjacency spectral embeddings), SBM-TS achieves AUC-ROC of 0.95 vs. 0.62 at K=2 on the general SBM experiment, and near-perfect ROC vs. poor performance on RDPG experiments — the gap attributed to ASE-MMD's inability to handle orthogonal transformation mismatches between estimated latent positions. Against NCLM [28] (a network clustering algorithm based on log moments, extended to two-sample testing), SBM-TS leads by 0.25 AUC-ROC at K=2 (0.95 vs. 0.70) and by 0.31 at K=20 (0.81 vs. 0.50). Both baselines degrade sharply as K increases (dropping to near-chance ~0.50 at K=15–20), while SBM-TS maintains strong performance (0.81–0.83), though it also shows some deterioration at larger K due to the increased difficulty of satisfying the approximate matching condition. On the real-world COLLAB dataset, SBM-TS achieves superior or comparable performance to both methods in distinguishing between physics subfields.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the spectral matching idea attributed to Leighton and Miller [24] (mentioned in Spielman's textbook [36]) for graph isomorphism, repurposing it as a subroutine within a statistical two-sample testing framework for stochastic block models. It departs from prior unlabeled network testing approaches—subgraph-count tests [27], MMD-based tests for RDPGs [37], and clustering-based methods [28]—by introducing a principled spectral alignment step that avoids the NP-hard quadratic assignment problem, and from labeled network testing [16, 15, 25, 8, 9] by explicitly handling permutation invariance and variable node counts. The SBM-as-histogram analogy to Pearson's chi-squared test positions the method as a non-parametric network comparison tool, connecting to the universality of block model approximation [29, 2, 14].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first scalable two-sample test for unlabeled networks with no vertex correspondence and variable node sizes, providing both a practical algorithm (O(K³) matching via Hungarian algorithm) and rigorous chi-squared asymptotic guarantees under mild sparsity conditions. The empirical robustness beyond SBM—working well on graphons and RDPGs—suggests the SBM-based 'bin-then-test' strategy is a viable non-parametric framework for network inference. Key open questions include formal theoretical guarantees under general graphon models (currently only semi-rigorous oracle-inequality arguments), extending the matching to handle non-distinct eigenvalues, and whether the log n sparsity floor can be relaxed with more sophisticated aggregation. The reliance on a 'friendly' eigenvalue separation condition, while mild in practice, remains a practical limitation for highly symmetric connectivity matrices.
