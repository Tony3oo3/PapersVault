## Problem

Curriculum learning is widely used to improve training efficiency and generalization, yet its theoretical justification remains largely absent. This paper asks: under what precise conditions does a sequence of tasks ordered by difficulty provably reduce the sample complexity of learning the final target task, and how does the quality of the curriculum (task similarity, loss geometry) quantitatively affect the generalization bound?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250259/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes T sequential tasks, each defined by a loss function ℓ_t, an unknown data distribution D_t, and an i.i.d. sample S_t of size n_t, along with an initial bias vector w_0 and per-task regularization parameters μ_t. The output is the learned hypothesis ŵ_T for the target task. A key structural detail is that the bias for task t is the solution ŵ_{t-1} from the previous task (identity mapping), so no explicit task-to-task parameter mapping is needed; in the nonconvex variant, the search is additionally constrained to a ball of radius r_t around ŵ_{t-1}.

## Method

The core algorithm (Algorithm 1) solves each task t via biased RERM: minimize empirical loss plus (μ_t/2)‖w − ŵ_{t-1}‖², so the previous task's solution acts as an inductive bias. The paper introduces an (r_t, α) condition stating that any predictor with small excess risk on task t−1 has a nearby predictor (within radius r_t) whose excess risk on task t is at most α times as large, with α ∈ (0,1). Under this condition, the excess risk bound decomposes into a 'new information' term O(r_t ρ_t / √n_t) plus α times the previous task's excess risk, yielding geometric decay across the curriculum when r_t is small. The analysis extends to SGD (matching the RERM rate with a single-pass algorithm), to tighter bounds via local Lipschitz constants near the minimizer, to smooth nonnegative losses (achieving O(1/n_t) in the realizable case), and to nonconvex losses via constrained ERM with uniform convergence. Empirically, the framework is validated on synthetic Gaussian-mixture classification and adversarially robust MNIST training, where curriculum initialization from weaker attacks improves robust accuracy at larger perturbation budgets.

## Results

On MNIST with ℓ∞ PGD attacks, curriculum adversarial training (T=2 or T=3 tasks with increasing attack strength) dramatically outperforms direct training (T=1) at high perturbation budgets: at α=0.4, T=1 collapses to 11.35% robust accuracy while T=2 and T=3 maintain 95.54% and 95.63% respectively. At α=0.3, the 2-task curriculum achieves 94.74% PGD accuracy versus 92.77% for direct training. On synthetic Gaussian-mixture classification in ℝ¹⁰⁰, curriculum learning (train on easy margin γ=3, then fine-tune on harder distributions) consistently outperforms the baseline of training only on the hard distribution, with the performance gap widening as the target task margin decreases and variance increases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between direct adversarial training (T=1, no curriculum) and curriculum adversarial training with T=2 or T=3 tasks of increasing attack strength. At small perturbation budgets (α≤0.2), all three settings perform comparably (within ~1% robust accuracy), indicating the curriculum is not harmful but also not yet necessary. At α=0.3, the 2-task curriculum gains ~2% robust accuracy over direct training (94.74% vs. 92.77%). The most dramatic separation occurs at α=0.4, where direct training catastrophically fails (11.35% robust accuracy, essentially random) while both curriculum variants maintain ~95% robust accuracy — a gap of over 84 percentage points. In the synthetic Gaussian-mixture experiments, the curriculum method (Algorithm 2: train on easy margin γ=3, then fine-tune on harder data with ℓ₂ regularization toward the first-stage solution) consistently outperforms the baseline of training only on the hard distribution, with the advantage growing as the target task becomes more difficult (smaller margin, higher variance). The paper does not compare against other specific curriculum or adversarial training methods; the baselines are the no-curriculum ablation within the same training protocol.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the biased RERM framework (Denevi et al., 2019; Schölkopf et al., 2001) from single-task meta-learning to a sequential multi-task curriculum, where the solution of task t−1 serves as the inductive bias for task t. It builds on the statistical curriculum learning analyses of Xu and Tewari (2022) and Cohen et al. (2024), which studied simplified mean-estimation settings, by providing excess risk bounds in the general Vapnik learning framework for both convex and nonconvex losses. The (r, α) condition introduced here formalizes what makes a curriculum 'good' in a way that is simpler and more directly tied to population losses than prior task-similarity assumptions in multi-task learning (Caruana, 1997) or transfer learning (Kuzborskij and Orabona, 2013).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first general theoretical guarantees showing when curriculum learning provably reduces sample complexity, moving the field beyond empirical heuristics. The (r, α) condition offers a principled, verifiable criterion for curriculum quality, though the authors acknowledge it may be difficult to check in practice and leave open the question of when it holds for specific problem families. The extension to nonconvex settings via uniform convergence and the adversarial robustness corollary broaden applicability to deep learning, but the empirical validation is limited to MNIST and synthetic Gaussians, leaving open whether the theoretical gains materialize at scale on complex vision or language tasks.
