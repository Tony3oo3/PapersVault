## Problem

Existing AI benchmarks saturate quickly, consist of artificial rather than economically valuable tasks, and lack a unified quantitative metric for comparing models of vastly different capabilities (e.g., GPT-2 vs. Claude 3.7). This makes it difficult to track the long-term trajectory of AI agent capabilities or to contextualize benchmark performance in terms of real-world human labor. The paper asks: can we define a single, intuitive metric that relates AI agent performance to the time humans need to complete equivalent tasks, and does it reveal a consistent trend over time?

## Input / Output

The method consumes binary success/failure labels from 8 agent runs per task (continuously scored tasks are binarized via task-specific thresholds calibrated to human performance), geometric-mean human baseline completion times from skilled domain professionals (800+ baselines totaling 2,529 hours), and a 170-task suite spanning 2 seconds to 30 hours of human work. It produces a per-model 50% (and 80%) task completion time horizon, a doubling-time trend (207 days, 95% CI 166–240 days) regressed against model release date, and a sensitivity analysis projecting the date of 1-month-horizon AI. Tasks are weighted by the inverse square root of their family size to reduce dominance of large task families, and confidence intervals use a three-level hierarchical bootstrap over task families, tasks, and runs.

## Method

The core method is a logistic regression model adapted from Item Response Theory (IRT) that inverts the standard formulation: task difficulty is fixed to the log of human baseline time, and the latent 'ability' parameter is reinterpreted as the log of the agent's 50% time horizon h_agent. The model p_success = σ((log h_agent − log t_task) · β_agent) has two learned parameters per agent (h_agent and a per-agent slope β_agent), fitted via logistic regression with diversity weighting. This avoids IRT's joint calibration stage and directly yields an interpretable horizon: when h_agent equals t_task, predicted success is exactly 50%. The paper evaluates 12 frontier models (GPT-2 through o3) on 170 tasks from HCAST, RE-Bench, and a new 66-task SWAA suite, then linearly regresses log(time horizon) against release date to extract the exponential growth rate. External validity is checked via SWE-bench Verified replication, a 16-factor 'messiness' analysis, and an internal pull-request experiment comparing contractor vs. maintainer times.

## Results

The 50% task completion time horizon for frontier AI agents has grown exponentially from 2019 to 2025, doubling every 207 days (95% CI: 166–240 days). GPT-2 (2019) achieves a 50% time horizon of only 2 seconds, while o3 (2025) reaches approximately 110 minutes, with Claude 3.7 Sonnet and o3 completing some tasks exceeding 4 hours of human time. The 80% time horizon follows a similar doubling trend (204 days) but is 4–6× shorter than the 50% horizon, indicating that even current frontier models cannot reliably complete moderately long tasks. Naive extrapolation of the trend predicts a 1-month (167-hour) 50% time horizon between mid-2028 and mid-2031, or as early as late 2026–2027 if the faster 2024–2025 growth rate (o3 lies above the long-run trend, p = 0.006) persists.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing methods in the traditional sense; instead, it tracks the temporal progression of 12 frontier models (GPT-2 through o3) on a unified task suite. The key cross-validation is between the HCAST + SWAA + RE-Bench suite (143-day doubling time for 2024 models) and SWE-bench Verified (~70-day doubling time), where the shorter SWE-bench doubling is attributed to annotator time estimates differentially underestimating contractor time on easier tasks. On internal pull requests, AI agent performance aligns with contractor time-to-complete (5–18× longer than repository maintainers) rather than maintainer time, suggesting time horizons correspond to low-context human labor. Qualitative failure analysis shows o1 eliminates the 'repeating failed actions' failure mode that accounted for 39% of GPT-4 1106 failures (12/31 vs. 2/32), while o1's dominant failure mode shifts to premature task abandonment (16/32 vs. 8/31 for GPT-4), reflecting that o1's failures occur on qualitatively harder tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper operationalizes the 'time horizon' concept proposed by Ngo [21] and discussed in Carlsmith [22] and Cotra [23] by grounding it in a concrete logistic regression model adapted from Item Response Theory (specifically the 2PL model of Martínez-Plumed et al. [26]). Rather than estimating abstract ability scores, the authors fix task difficulty to the logarithm of human baseline completion time and reinterpret the ability parameter as the agent's 50% time horizon, yielding a directly interpretable metric. The evaluation draws on existing agent benchmarks (HCAST, RE-Bench, SWE-bench Verified) and introduces a new short-task suite (SWAA) to extend the measurable range down to seconds, filling a gap that limits resolution for pre-2023 models. Methodologically, the approach sits at the intersection of psychometric testing and AI capability forecasting, building on prior efforts to contextualize benchmark scores via human experts (Phuong et al. [3], Murray et al. [18]) but providing a unified, continuous metric rather than per-benchmark comparisons.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic, quantitative measurement of AI agent capability growth as a function of task duration, finding an approximately 7-month doubling time from 2019 to 2025 with evidence of acceleration since 2024. This has direct implications for AI safety governance, as many frontier labs use capability metrics to determine risk mitigations, and the extrapolation to a 1-month (167-hour) horizon between mid-2028 and mid-2031 frames a concrete timeline for potential transformative capabilities. Key open questions include external validity (tasks lack automatic scoring, agent interaction, dynamic environments, and high-reliability requirements of real work), the 4–6× gap between 50% and 80% time horizons revealing significant unreliability, and whether the exponential trend persists under agency training, compute scaling limits, or automation of AI R&D. The finding that performance trends are similar on 'messier' tasks is reassuring but does not rule out slower progress on the full distribution of economically valuable software work.
