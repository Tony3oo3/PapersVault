## Problem

Standard language models rely on a fixed, pre-trained tokenizer (e.g., BPE) that freezes token granularity before training begins, mapping each token to an opaque embedding vector. This one-shot splitting prevents the model from reasoning about subword structure, complicates transfer to low-resource or unseen languages, and locks every layer into a single level of abstraction. The paper asks whether tokenization can instead be learned end-to-end as a multi-scale, hierarchical process inside the model itself.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253144/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes raw UTF-8 bytes with no pre-tokenization step, and at inference it generates text one byte at a time in a strictly autoregressive fashion. The unusual aspect is the conditional activation pattern: the byte-level stage fires at every generation step, while deeper stages (word-level, 2-word, 4-word) activate only when the splitting function signals a boundary, so the model caches the latest vector at each stage to propagate coarse-grained context forward. At training time, all stages process the full sequence in parallel, and the loss is standard next-byte cross-entropy.

## Method

AU-Net is a multi-stage autoregressive U-Net that replaces the BPE embedding table with learned, attention-based embeddings operating directly on bytes. The contracting path progressively pools the sequence using a user-defined splitting function (space-delimited regex at each stage): Stage 1 keeps all byte positions, Stage 2 selects one vector per word boundary, Stage 3 per two words, and Stage 4 per four words, with a linear projection at each contraction. The expanding path uses Multi-Linear Upsampling, where each coarse vector is duplicated across its segment and passed through position-specific linear layers to restore the finer sequence length, then combined with skip connections from the corresponding contracting stage. Because deeper stages operate on compressed representations, they naturally predict further into the future (next few words rather than the next byte), creating an inductive bias toward abstract semantic representations that guide shallower stages without auxiliary losses. The architecture requires no fixed vocabulary or embedding table, and the splitting function must be stable to rightward insertion to preserve autoregressive consistency.

## Results

At the 1B-parameter scale trained on 370B tokens, AU-Net-4 achieves 73.7 on Hellaswag, 31.7 on MMLU, and 5.3 on GSM8k, outperforming the BPE baseline (70.2 / 27.0 / 4.4) across all three benchmarks. AU-Net-2, the shallowest variant, matches BPE on Hellaswag (69.9 vs 70.2) and exceeds it on MMLU (28.8 vs 27.0) while using fewer FLOPs (3e21 vs 4e21). At the 8B scale (200B tokens), AU-Net-2 reaches 79.1 Hellaswag and 51.1 MMLU, surpassing the BPE Transformer (77.2 / 49.6) on both, while GSM8k remains close (10.0 vs 10.7). On FLORES-200 multilingual translation, AU-Net-2 improves average BLEU from 20.9 (BPE) to 24.6 for language-to-English and from 8.0 to 8.7 for English-to-language.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the primary BPE Transformer baseline, AU-Net-4 wins on Hellaswag (+3.5), MMLU (+4.7), and GSM8k (+0.9) at the 1B/370B-token scale, while AU-Net-2 ties on Hellaswag and leads on MMLU (+1.8) at lower FLOP cost. Compared to byte-level baselines (Transformer Byte, Mamba Byte), all AU-Net variants achieve substantially higher scores—for example, AU-Net-3 reaches 72.9 Hellaswag versus 63.0 for both byte-level baselines—demonstrating that hierarchical pooling recovers the performance lost by naive byte-level training. Fixed-window pooling baselines (Transformer Byte[::4/5/6]) degrade sharply as the window grows (Hellaswag drops from 63.5 to 58.6), whereas AU-Net's adaptive word-boundary pooling maintains or improves performance with deeper compression. At the 8B scale, AU-Net-2 outperforms the BPE Transformer on Hellaswag (79.1 vs 77.2), ARC-Easy (80.0 vs 74.5), and MMLU (51.1 vs 49.6), though BPE retains a small edge on GSM8k (10.7 vs 10.0). Against literature models, AU-Net-2 at 8B/200B tokens matches or exceeds BLT (1T) on Hellaswag (79.1 vs 80.6) and MMLU (51.1 vs 52.1) while using roughly 5× less compute, and surpasses the Hierarchical Transformer (263B) by a wide margin (79.1 vs 56.3 Hellaswag). On multilingual FLORES-200, AU-Net-2 consistently outperforms BPE on low-resource languages (e.g., +4.6 BLEU on Limburgish, +3.3 on Luxembourgish) but trails slightly on high-resource pairs like German (33.9 vs 34.4) and Chinese (28.0 vs 33.0), where space-based splitting is less effective.

## Relation to Prior Work

AU-Net adapts the U-Net contracting/expanding architecture with skip connections (Ronneberger et al., 2015) to language modeling, building directly on the hierarchical Transformer of Nawrot et al. (2022), which used a similar multi-stage design but with fixed-size pooling and much smaller models. The key departure from prior byte-level approaches (BLT, MegaByte, SpaceByte, Hierarchical Autoregressive Transformers) is that AU-Net replaces fixed or learned pooling with adaptive, input-dependent pooling driven by self-attention at each stage, and introduces Multi-Linear Upsampling with position-specific projections to restore sequence length during expansion. Unlike prior work that uses these splits to replace BPE in a single effective stage, AU-Net leverages them to create a genuine multi-scale hierarchy where deeper stages predict further ahead, introducing an inductive bias toward multi-token prediction without auxiliary losses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By eliminating predefined vocabularies and large embedding tables, AU-Net offers a path to open-vocabulary modeling without memory overhead, and the consistent multilingual gains (e.g., +3.7 BLEU average on FLORES-200 for low-resource languages) demonstrate that byte-level hierarchical processing captures shared orthographic and morphological patterns that BPE tokenizers miss. Open questions include whether deeper hierarchies (4+ stages) require substantially more training data to realize their full potential, how to extend the space-based splitting function to non-delimited scripts like Chinese or Japanese, and whether the splitting function itself can be learned end-to-end. The TQA gap at smaller scales that closes at larger scales suggests the architecture may be data-hungry in ways not yet fully characterized.
