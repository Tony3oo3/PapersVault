## Problem

CLIP-like models learn bag-of-words representations that fail on compositional scenes with multiple objects, struggling to bind attributes to the correct objects or interpret spatial relationships. Existing remedies rely on fine-grained hard-negative augmentations that improve sensitivity to textual or visual differences but do not explicitly address the binding problem and plateau in sample efficiency. The paper identifies that advances in object-centric representation learning (slot-based segregation and composition) have not yet been explored in the vision-language domain.

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image (encoded as N patch-level embeddings by a ViT backbone, with the CLS token dropped) and a text caption that is first parsed by an LLM (Llama3-8B) into a scene graph of noun-phrase nodes and directed relationship edges. The binding module produces one visual slot embedding per scene-graph node, and the final output is a structured similarity score combining per-node cosine similarities with a learnable relationship scoring function over slot pairs. The text encoder is deliberately small (6 layers, 256-dim, 20-token context) since it only needs to encode individual objects and relationships rather than full sentences.

## Method

OC-CLIP introduces a binding module that implements inverted cross-attention with scene-graph nodes as queries and image patches as keys/values, applying softmax along the query dimension to induce competition between slots for different visual regions; a set of default query tokens absorbs uncaptured visual information and is discarded downstream. A structured similarity score replaces CLIP's single-vector dot product: it sums cosine similarities between each text node embedding and its assigned visual slot, plus a learnable relationship function f_φ that takes the relationship embedding and the subject/object slot embeddings (via concatenated MLPs) to produce a direction-sensitive relational score. Training uses a standard image-text contrastive loss over the structured score, augmented by a local graph contrastive loss that penalizes symmetric or shuffled relationship assignments to prevent the relationship MLPs from collapsing. The approach is trained either by initializing backbones from OpenCLIP and training the binding module from scratch, or fully from scratch on noisy web-scale data (CC3M/CC12M), consistently outperforming hard-negative-based baselines on attribute binding and spatial relationship benchmarks.

## Results

On SugarCrepe's swap-attribute split, OC-CLIP (B-14) achieves 88.9% accuracy, a +16.5% improvement over OpenCLIP-FT (72.4%) and +13.6% over the best hard-negative baseline (DAC-LLM at 75.3%). On spatial relationship understanding, OC-CLIP (B-14) reaches 89.7% on COCO-spatial and 92.7% on GQA-spatial, compared to 45.6% and 49.1% for OpenCLIP-FT respectively (+44.1% and +43.6%). When trained fully from scratch on CC3M+CC12M, OC-CLIP achieves 45.0% zero-shot ImageNet accuracy versus 32.2% for CLIP (+12.8%), while also gaining +12.7% on SugarCrepe swap-attribute and +6.6% on swap-object in the zero-shot setting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

OC-CLIP outperforms all baselines on the hardest SugarCrepe splits (swap-attribute and swap-object), which specifically test the binding problem. Against the primary in-domain finetuned baseline OpenCLIP-FT, OC-CLIP (B-14) gains +16.5% on swap-attribute and +20.4% on swap-object. Against hard-negative methods (NegCLIP, CE-CLIP, CC-CLIP, CLIP-SVLC), OC-CLIP leads by +13.5% on swap-attribute over NegCLIP (75.4%) and +11.2% over CE-CLIP (77.7%). Even against the large-scale dense-captioning baselines DAC-LLM and DAC-SAM (which use ~3M VLM-generated captions), OC-CLIP still wins by +13.6% on swap-attribute and +8.4% on swap-object, demonstrating that inductive biases are more sample-efficient than data augmentation. On spatial relationships (Table 2), OC-CLIP (B-14) achieves 89.7% on COCO-spatial and 92.7% on GQA-spatial, surpassing XVLM (73.6%/67%) by +16.1%/+25.7% and NegCLIP (46.4%/46.7%) by +43.3%/+46.0%. In the from-scratch setting (Table 3), OC-CLIP trained on CC12M achieves 42.4% ImageNet and 72.9% SugarCrepe-swap, outperforming OpenCLIP-12M (31.4%/60.2%), NegCLIP (28.9%/67.2%), and IL-CLIP (32.8%/62.9%) across all metrics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously disconnected research threads: contrastive vision-language pretraining (CLIP) and object-centric representation learning (slot attention, inverted cross-attention). While prior work on improving CLIP's compositional understanding has overwhelmingly relied on hard-negative data augmentation in either text space (NegCLIP, CE-CLIP, CLIP-SVLC) or image space (CC-CLIP, VisMin), OC-CLIP departs from this data-centric paradigm by introducing architectural inductive biases — a text-conditioned binding module with competitive attention and a structured similarity score that explicitly models scene graph relationships. The binding module directly adapts the inverted cross-attention mechanism from slot attention (Locatello et al., 2020; Wu et al.), but repurposes it so that scene graph nodes (from an LLM parser) serve as queries attending to image patches, creating text-conditioned visual slots rather than purely image-driven ones.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that the binding problem in CLIP-like models is fundamentally a representation-format issue (single vector superposition) rather than a data-coverage issue, challenging the community's default strategy of scaling hard negatives. This opens a promising direction of applying object-centric inductive biases to VLMs more broadly. Key limitations include the dependency on an LLM-based scene graph parser (adding computational overhead and potential parsing errors), the fact that the binding module is trained from scratch (limiting zero-shot generalization outside the training vocabulary), and the maximum tested scale of 15M samples versus the 400M+ used for production CLIP models. Open questions include whether the approach scales to larger backbones and datasets, whether lighter-weight parsers can replace the LLM, and whether the structured similarity score can be integrated into autoregressive VLMs.
