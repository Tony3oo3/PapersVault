## Problem

Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Reward (RLVR) has become a dominant paradigm for improving LLM mathematical reasoning, yet the data-centric aspects remain underexplored: how much training data is truly necessary, and what role does data quality versus quantity play in observed phenomena like self-reflection and generalization? This paper tackles the extreme case of reducing the RLVR training set to a single example and asks whether comparable downstream performance can still be achieved.

## Input / Output

The method takes a base LLM checkpoint (e.g., Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B) and one or a few math problem–answer pairs as the entire training dataset; the single example is duplicated to fill the training batch size of 128. Training uses GRPO with a binary outcome reward (1 if the final answer in \boxed{} matches ground truth), KL divergence to a reference model, and an entropy loss, with 8 sampled responses per prompt at temperature 0.6, max prompt length 1024, and max response length 3072. The output is updated model weights that generate step-by-step reasoning chains ending in a boxed final answer, evaluated on six math benchmarks (MATH500, AIME 2024/2025, AMC 2023, Minerva Math, OlympiadBench) and non-math reasoning tasks (ARC-Easy/Challenge).

## Method

The core contribution is an empirical demonstration that 1-shot RLVR—training with a single math example—matches or exceeds the performance of RLVR with 1.2k examples (e.g., MATH500: 73.6% vs. 73.6%; 6-benchmark average: 35.7% vs. 35.9% on Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B). For data selection, the authors propose a Historical Variance Score: after a preliminary RLVR run on the full dataset, each example is ranked by the variance of its per-epoch training accuracy, and the top-ranked examples (e.g., π₁, π₁₃) are used as the 1-shot training set. Ablation studies reveal that the policy gradient loss is the primary driver of improvement (not weight decay, distinguishing this from 'grokking'), while an entropy loss with an appropriate coefficient further enhances a phenomenon the authors term post-saturation generalization—test accuracy continues to improve for hundreds of steps after training accuracy on the single example has already saturated near 100%. The approach generalizes across models (Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B/7B, Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B), RL algorithms (GRPO, PPO), and many different single examples, with additional observations including cross-category generalization and increased frequency of self-reflective language in model outputs.

## Results

On Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B, 1-shot RLVR with a single example (π13) improves MATH500 from 36.0% (base) to 73.6%, matching the 73.6% achieved by full-set RLVR on the 1.2k DeepScaleR subset, and raises the average across six math benchmarks from 17.6% to 35.7% (vs. 35.9% for full-set). Using just two examples (π1, π13) slightly exceeds full-set performance (MATH500: 74.8%, avg: 36.6%). The non-format gain over the format-reward baseline (65.6% MATH500) is approximately 8.6%, confirming that the improvement is not merely format correction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is 1(few)-shot RLVR against full-set RLVR on the 1.2k DeepScaleR subset (DSR-sub). On Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B with GRPO, 1-shot (π13) ties DSR-sub on MATH500 (73.6% vs. 73.6%) and is within 0.2% on the 6-benchmark average (35.7% vs. 35.9%), while 2-shot (π1, π13) actually surpasses DSR-sub (74.8% vs. 73.6% MATH500; 36.6% vs. 35.9% avg). Against the format-reward baseline (65.6% MATH500), 1-shot RLVR yields an additional ~8.6% non-format gain, confirming the improvement is not merely format correction. On Qwen2.5-Math-7B, 1-shot (π1) reaches 40.2% avg vs. 42.8% for DSR-sub (a 2.6% gap), while 4-shot closes the gap to 42.5%. On Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, 2-shot (π1, π13) at 21.0% avg outperforms DSR-sub at 19.8%. On DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B, the gap between 1-shot (46.3%) and DSR-sub (48.6%) is larger (~2.3%), but 16-shot nearly matches (48.3%). Across all models, 1(few)-shot RLVR consistently matches or approaches full-set performance, with the gap widening slightly for stronger base models that already have higher starting performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits within the RLVR (Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Reward) lineage that includes DeepSeek-R1, Kimi-1.5, and Tülu 3, using GRPO (from DeepSeekMath) as the default training algorithm. The closest prior work is LIMR, which proposed a learning-impact metric to reduce RLVR training data by 6×, and a concurrent study showing PPO with 4 examples yields gains; however, neither pushed the reduction to a single example nor demonstrated parity with full-dataset RLVR. The paper also explicitly distinguishes its findings from the 'grokking' literature by showing that policy gradient loss, not weight decay or regularization, drives the observed generalization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that one training example in RLVR matches 1,200+ examples challenges the prevailing assumption that RLVR performance scales with dataset size, with direct practical implications for compute efficiency in post-training pipelines. The post-saturation generalization phenomenon—where test accuracy keeps improving long after training accuracy saturates, even past overfitting—suggests a mechanism by which minimal RL signals can 'ignite' latent reasoning capabilities already present in base models. Open questions include whether this extends beyond mathematics to other reasoning domains, whether it holds at larger model scales (the paper tests up to 7B), and what principled criteria exist for selecting the single most effective example. The single-seed, math-only, small-model scope limits generalizability claims.
