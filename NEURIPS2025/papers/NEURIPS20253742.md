## Problem

Existing dermatology AI datasets are dominated by neoplastic, dermoscopic images from Western populations, with limited skin tone diversity (Fitzpatrick I–III) and poor coverage of conditions prevalent in tropical and South Asian outpatient settings (fungal, pigmentary, nutritional, and parasitic disorders). This geographic and phenotypic skew produces models that underperform on darker skin tones and region-specific disease presentations, leaving a critical gap for equitable, clinically deployable dermatology AI in non-Western contexts.

## Input / Output

The method consumes high-resolution clinical skin photographs (JPEG, 346–4,608 px, mean ≈2,300×2,057, ~4.97 M pixels) captured on smartphones under ambient lighting, showing the affected body part with surrounding skin to preserve anatomical context. Outputs include a three-tier hierarchical diagnosis (8 main classes → 19 sub-classes → 245 disease labels), a 96-dimensional concept vector (47 lesion descriptors + 49 body-part locations) produced by the bottleneck layer, and Fitzpatrick/Monk skin-tone scores. The CBM variant additionally exposes per-concept sigmoid activations for interpretability and Grad-CAM spatial grounding.

## Method

The paper introduces DermaCon-IN, a prospectively curated dataset of 5,450 images from 3,002 South Indian outpatients, annotated by four board-certified dermatologists with a Rook's-based hierarchical taxonomy, 47 lesion descriptors, and 49 body-part locations (Cohen's κ = 0.84). For benchmarking, standard CNNs (ResNet50, DenseNet121, EfficientNet-B4) and Vision Transformers (ViT-B, MaxViT-B, Swin-B) are fine-tuned end-to-end on a stratified subject-wise 80:20 split with weighted sampling for class imbalance. The key methodological contribution is a Concept Bottleneck Model built on Swin-B/4W12-384: a linear projection maps the encoder's latent representation to 96 concept logits, a sigmoid yields the interpretable concept vector, and a downstream head classifies on the raw logits. Two hierarchical CBM designs are explored—Type 1 (cascade: concepts → sub-class → main-class) and Type 2 (parallel: independent sub-class and main-class heads from shared concepts)—with the parallel variant outperforming the cascade due to multi-task regularization. Concept ablation (descriptors-only vs. body-parts-only vs. joint) and Grad-CAM visualizations validate that the bottleneck learns spatially grounded, clinically meaningful representations.

## Results

On the 8-main-class dermatological diagnosis task, Swin-B/4W12-384 (ImageNet-pretrained) achieves the best overall performance with 70.41% accuracy, 45.06% balanced accuracy, and 69.69% F1 score, substantially outperforming all other architectures. Among transformer variants, ViT-B/16-384 (66.95%) and MaxViT-B/512 (66.92%) trail Swin by ~3.5 percentage points in accuracy. Pretraining on ImageNet yields a consistent 15–17 point accuracy gain over training from scratch (e.g., ResNet50: 64.31% vs. 47.45%), underscoring the importance of transfer learning for this long-tailed, multi-class problem.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Swin-B/4W12-384 outperforms all six baselines on every reported metric for the 8-main-class task. It leads ViT-B/16-384 (66.95%) and MaxViT-B/512 (66.92%) by approximately 3.5 percentage points in accuracy, and DenseNet121 (65.20%) by over 5 points. The gap is most pronounced on balanced accuracy, where Swin reaches 45.06% versus 35.78% for ViT-B/16-384 and 37.31% for DenseNet121, indicating superior handling of the long-tailed class distribution. Among CNNs, DenseNet121 (65.20%) edges out ResNet50 (64.31%) and EffNet-B4 (64.28%) by roughly 1 point, while all three CNNs remain 5–6 points below the best transformer. Training from scratch (no ImageNet pretraining) drops accuracy to 47–49% across all architectures, confirming that transfer learning is essential for this dataset's 245-class long-tail structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the dermatology AI dataset lineage (ISIC, HAM10000, Fitzpatrick17k, DDI, PASSION, SkinCon, SCIN) by introducing a prospectively collected, multi-concept annotated dataset from South Indian outpatient clinics, filling a geographic and phenotypic gap that prior resources—dominated by Western, dermoscopic, or atlas-sourced images—leave unaddressed. It builds directly on SkinCon's concept-annotation paradigm but captures lesion descriptors and body-part locations at the point of acquisition rather than retrofitting them, and organizes diagnoses under a three-tier Rook's-based hierarchy that mirrors clinical diagnostic workflows. For interpretability, it adapts Concept Bottleneck Models (Koh et al., 2020) to the dermatology domain, using the 96 concept annotations (47 descriptors + 49 body parts) as an explicit bottleneck between a Swin-Transformer encoder and the classification head.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DermaCon-IN provides the first densely annotated, region-specific benchmark for South Asian skin phototypes (Fitzpatrick IV–VI, Monk 4–9), directly addressing documented 30–40% accuracy drops on darker skin in existing models. The CBM experiments reveal a non-trivial finding: combining both concept families in a single bottleneck does not outperform either stream alone, exposing a representational capacity conflict that motivates future multi-concept learning mechanisms. Open questions include whether the observed class-dependent concept alignment (strong for pigmentary/keratinisation disorders, weak for neoplasms) reflects dataset sparsity or a fundamental limitation of linear concept projection, and whether the hierarchical cascade vs. parallel CBM advantage generalizes beyond this dataset's scale.
