## Problem

Traditional co-training for semi-supervised learning suffers from three interrelated weaknesses: pseudo-label selection relies on brittle confidence thresholds that fail under distributional shift, view interactions are static and symmetric despite heterogeneous model capacities, and there is no mechanism to actively surface hard examples near decision boundaries. These limitations cause confirmation bias, semantic collapse, and overfitting to high-confidence regions, particularly in low-label regimes.

## Input / Output

TRiCo consumes labeled and unlabeled RGB images, which are first encoded by two frozen pre-trained vision backbones (DINOv2 and MAE, each producing 768-dim embeddings) before being fed to lightweight two-layer MLP student classifiers. The method outputs class predictions, but its training loop also generates MI-filtered pseudo-labels exchanged across views and embedding-space adversarial perturbations (single-step FGSM with ε=1.0) that serve as auxiliary supervision signals. Because the encoders are frozen, the trainable parameters are confined to the student heads and the teacher's three scalar strategy parameters (τ_MI, λ_u, λ_adv), keeping the method architecture-agnostic and compatible with any frozen backbone.

## Method

TRiCo formulates semi-supervised co-training as a triadic Stackelberg game among a meta-learned teacher (leader), two student classifiers on complementary frozen views (followers), and a non-parametric adversarial generator (follower). Pseudo-labels are accepted based on mutual information estimated via K=5 Monte Carlo dropout forward passes rather than softmax confidence, providing a principled epistemic uncertainty measure. The teacher adaptively schedules the MI threshold and loss-balancing coefficients by minimizing a meta-objective: the supervised validation loss after one unrolled student gradient step, updated via first-order meta-gradient descent. In parallel, the generator performs entropy-guided gradient ascent in embedding space to produce hard examples that sharpen decision boundaries. The paper proves existence of a Nash equilibrium under compact strategy spaces and continuous payoffs (via Glicksberg's theorem), and the full pipeline is trained end-to-end with a unified loss combining supervised, MI-filtered pseudo-label, and adversarial consistency terms.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 (4k labels), TRiCo achieves 96.3% top-1 accuracy, outperforming Meta Pseudo Label (95.1%) and FlexMatch (94.9%). On ImageNet, TRiCo reaches 81.2% (1%), 85.9% (10%), and 88.3% (25%) top-1 accuracy, surpassing REACT (81.6/85.1/86.8%) and Semi-ViT ViT-H (78.9/84.6/86.2%) at 10% and 25% labels. On STL-10, TRiCo attains 92.4%, exceeding the previous best (MCT at 91.2%) by over 1 point. In few-shot settings (1-shot CIFAR-100), TRiCo achieves 23.8% vs. MCT's 21.2%, and under OOD shift (CIFAR-10→CIFAR-10-C), it reduces mean corruption error to 26.1 vs. MCT's 28.7.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TRiCo consistently outperforms all consistency-based baselines (FixMatch, UDA, FlexMatch) by 2–4 points on CIFAR-10/SVHN/STL-10 and by 25–30 points on ImageNet, confirming that confidence-threshold pseudo-labeling is fundamentally limited. Against the closest co-training competitor MCT [35], TRiCo gains +0.5 to +2.6 points across ImageNet label budgets (81.2 vs 80.7 at 1%, 85.9 vs 85.8 at 10%) and +1.2 points on STL-10 (92.4 vs 91.2), with the gap widening under few-shot (23.8 vs 21.2 on 1-shot CIFAR-100) and OOD (26.1 vs 28.7 mCE on CIFAR-10-C) settings. TRiCo surpasses the strongest self-training baseline REACT (ViT-L) at 10% (85.9 vs 85.1) and 25% (88.3 vs 86.8) ImageNet labels, though REACT retains a narrow 0.4-point edge at the extreme 1% regime (81.6 vs 81.2). Compared to Semi-ViT (ViT-H), TRiCo leads by 2.3 points at 10% and 2.1 points at 25% ImageNet labels despite using a frozen ViT-B/16 backbone rather than a larger trainable model. On long-tailed benchmarks, TRiCo improves tail-class accuracy by +2.6% over SimPro and +6.3% over ACR on ImageNet-LT (1%), and gains +3.5 points over MCT on CIFAR-100-LT (imbalance ratio 100). FreeMatch [43] is outperformed on both few-shot (23.8 vs 18.3 on 1-shot CIFAR-100) and OOD (26.1 vs 32.3 mCE) evaluations, indicating that TRiCo's MI-based filtering and adversarial generator provide more robust uncertainty handling than adaptive confidence thresholds alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

TRiCo extends the classical two-view co-training paradigm of Blum and Mitchell (1998) and its recent deep-learning revival in Meta Co-Training (MCT, Rothenberger & Diochnos 2023) by introducing a third strategic agent—a meta-learned teacher—thereby transforming the dyadic interaction into a triadic Stackelberg game. It departs from both predecessors in two key ways: (i) pseudo-label acceptance is governed by dropout-based mutual information rather than softmax confidence, and (ii) a non-parametric entropy-guided generator actively mines hard examples in embedding space. The framework also builds on the frozen-encoder SSL trend (DINOv2, MAE, CLIP as view providers) and the meta-learning literature for adaptive threshold scheduling, but unifies these under a single bilevel optimization with a Nash-equilibrium existence guarantee via Glicksberg's theorem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TRiCo offers a principled, architecture-agnostic recipe for reducing confirmation bias and stabilizing co-training dynamics, which are persistent pain points in low-label SSL. The MI-based filtering and meta-scheduled loss weighting are practical techniques that could be grafted onto existing pipelines, and the frozen-backbone design makes the method accessible without large-scale pretraining. However, the ~2.2× runtime overhead, dependence on two strong pre-trained encoders, and the fact that the Nash-equilibrium guarantee is existence-only (no convergence rate for the training procedure) limit immediate adoption. Open questions include whether the triadic structure is strictly necessary over a simpler two-agent meta-loop, how the method scales to very large unlabeled pools, and whether the game-theoretic framing yields insights beyond what standard bilevel optimization already provides.
