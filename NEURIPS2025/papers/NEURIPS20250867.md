## Problem

Multi-slice spatial transcriptomics analysis requires simultaneously resolving spatial location-level correspondences across tissue slices and learning shared low-dimensional embeddings, yet existing methods address these two tightly coupled tasks in isolation. Alignment methods (e.g., PASTE) operate on raw, noisy expression profiles without producing useful latent representations, while embedding methods (e.g., GraphST, STAGATE) process slices independently without explicitly modeling anatomical correspondences, leaving homologous regions inconsistently represented across slices.

## Input / Output

JADE takes two SRT slices as input, each providing a spatial coordinate matrix (n×2), a gene expression matrix (n×p) restricted to the intersection of top highly variable genes (~1500 for DLPFC, ~1000 for axolotl), and a k-nearest-neighbor spatial graph (k=3). It outputs a doubly-stochastic probabilistic alignment matrix Π ∈ R^{n1×n2} encoding soft spot-to-spot correspondences, and per-slice low-dimensional embeddings (d=64) suitable for downstream clustering, batch correction, and differential expression. For scalability, Fast-JADE reduces the O(n1·n2·d) cross-attention cost by performing alignment at a coarser hyperspot level (10–20% of spots) before recovering full-resolution correspondences via the learned projection head.

## Method

JADE couples a per-slice GCN autoencoder with a cross-attention optimal-transport alignment module and a Graph Infomax contrastive loss, trained in a roundtrip scheme where alignment and embedding refinement alternate within each iteration. The alignment is computed by projecting embeddings through a learnable attention matrix M, applying row-wise softmax to obtain cross-slice similarity scores, and then enforcing doubly-stochastic constraints via iterative Sinkhorn-Knopp normalization to ensure balanced, fair correspondences. Two fused Gromov-Wasserstein-inspired losses—Mis-Maintain (preserving pairwise spatial distances under transport) and Mis-Alignment (pulling aligned embeddings together)—together with a marginal KL regularizer and a reconstruction term, jointly optimize the alignment matrix and latent representations. A self-supervised graph contrastive objective (adapted from GraphST) further enforces local spatial coherence in the embedding space, while a data-driven hyperparameter λ3 modulates the strength of spatial-structure sharing based on a mini-max distance measure of slice similarity.

## Results

On the 10x Visium DLPFC dataset (Sample III, Slices A–B), JADE achieves median ARI of 0.61 and 0.65 for the two slices, outperforming GraphST (0.59/0.58), STAligner (0.56/0.59), and STAGATE (0.52/0.53), with the highest iLISI batch-correction score of 1.98 (vs. 1.52–1.64 for baselines). For alignment, JADE attains near-perfect per-layer accuracy (0.996 in Layer 1, 1.000 in WM) with zero unaligned spots, while STAligner leaves 68–82% of spots unaligned and Seurat leaves 81–98%. On the Stereo-seq axolotl brain dataset (cross-developmental-stage alignment), JADE reaches ARI ≈ 0.53 (Adult) and ≈ 0.37 (Juvenile) with iLISI 1.85, again leading all baselines in both clustering and alignment fidelity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

JADE outperforms PASTE on alignment accuracy in the most challenging BC slice pairs (300 µm separation) by margins exceeding 50% in Samples I and II, while PASTE remains competitive on the easier AB/CD pairs (10 µm separation); both methods achieve zero unaligned spots, unlike STAligner (68–82% unaligned) and Seurat (81–98% unaligned). Against embedding-focused baselines, JADE achieves the highest ARI and iLISI on both DLPFC and axolotl brain, with the gap over STAGATE being the largest (e.g., ARI 0.65 vs. 0.53 on DLPFC Slice B; iLISI 1.98 vs. 1.61). Compared to the image-based method GPSA, JADE achieves an average alignment ACC of 0.78 versus GPSA's 0.24 across all DLPFC slice pairs, demonstrating that transcriptomic-only alignment can surpass histology-informed approaches. On the additional MERFISH and Breast-Cancer Visium/Xenium benchmarks (Appendix H), JADE consistently outperforms or matches SLAT, STAligner, and SpotScape in both ARI and alignment ACC, confirming cross-platform generalizability.

## Relation to Prior Work

JADE unifies two previously separate lines of work in multi-slice spatial transcriptomics: alignment-based methods (PASTE, STAligner, Seurat) that resolve spatial correspondences but produce no shared latent representation, and representation-learning methods (GraphST, STAGATE) that extract embeddings but do not explicitly model anatomical correspondences. The method draws on GraphST's Graph Infomax contrastive objective for the self-supervised module, the fused Gromov-Wasserstein framework for spatial-structure preservation losses, and Sinkhorn-Knopp optimal transport for balanced alignment, but combines them in a novel roundtrip scheme where alignment and embedding refinement alternate within each training iteration. The attention-based projection head for cross-slice similarity computation and the data-driven selection of the spatial-preservation weight λ₃ based on a mini-max distance metric are additional departures from prior alignment-only or embedding-only pipelines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As SRT datasets increasingly span multiple adjacent or replicated slices for 3D reconstruction, developmental trajectory mapping, and reproducibility assessment, the inability of existing tools to simultaneously deliver both a reliable alignment matrix and a shared embedding space limits downstream analyses such as cross-slice differential expression and spatial domain comparison. JADE's joint formulation addresses this gap, and the Fast-JADE hyperspot variant makes it practical for high-resolution platforms like Stereo-seq. Key open questions remain: the current pairwise design does not scale to fully joint multi-slice alignment (transitive composition accumulates noise), temporal dynamics are not modeled, and performance under extreme sparsity or highly unbalanced slice resolutions is untested. Integration with histological imaging or spatial epigenomics is identified as a natural extension.
