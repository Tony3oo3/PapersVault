## Problem

Existing data attribution methods assume fixed datasets and static objectives, but online RL violates both: each collected experience simultaneously updates the policy and shapes future data collection, creating a circular data-model dependency that no prior attribution framework can handle. This paper initiates the study of data attribution for online RL, focusing on PPO, to make training interpretable and to enable targeted interventions that improve sample efficiency.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a single-round rollout buffer B^(k) of n records, each containing (s, a, r, log π, v, Â), along with the current policy parameters θ^(k) and a chosen target function. For f^return, the reference policy is set to π_θ^(k) (the policy snapshot at the start of the round), making the rollout buffer itself serve as the validation set. The output is a per-record influence score I_i; in the IIF algorithm, records with the most negative scores (bottom p%) are discarded, yielding a filtered buffer that feeds into the PPO update, ultimately producing an improved policy with reduced sample complexity.

## Method

The core contribution is a local attribution framework that operates at the round level, exploiting the fact that each PPO round optimizes on a fixed on-policy buffer, thereby circumventing the non-differentiable, cascading dependencies across training history. Two target functions are defined: f^action(θ) = log π_θ(a|s) for diagnosing specific decisions, and f^return(θ) = E_{τ~π^ref}[log π_θ(a|s) Â^ref(s,a)] for assessing contribution to cumulative return, where the reference policy evolves with training. Influence is measured via an adapted TracIn score: the inner product between the target gradient and the per-record PPO loss gradient, accumulated over optimization steps within the round. Building on this, the IIF algorithm iteratively collects transitions, computes influence scores (using only the initial round parameter θ^(k) and a ghost dot-product trick to keep overhead minimal), discards the bottom p% of records by influence, and trains PPO on the filtered buffer—creating a self-reinforcing loop where improved policies generate better data for subsequent rounds.

## Results

Across six standard RL environments (FrozenLake, Acrobot, MiniGrid, Highway, LunarLander, BipedalWalker), IIF achieves a 20–67% reduction in training rounds needed to match standard PPO performance (SE_ave ranges from 26.0% to 65.8%), with peak sample efficiency gains up to 61.7% (MiniGrid). Runtime at peak performance improves by 29–69% (RT_peak), as the influence computation overhead is negligible and offset by fewer optimization steps. In RLHF for LLM detoxification (GPT-Neo 2.7B), IIF filters ~50% of negative-influence records, requires less than half the training rounds to surpass standard PPO, and yields an approximately 4× reduction in total runtime while achieving lower test toxicity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

IIF is compared against standard PPO (the primary baseline), a random filtering baseline (dropping the same fraction of records uniformly at random), an advantage-based heuristic (filtering records with sign-mismatched or large-magnitude advantage errors), and a TD-error-based reweighting heuristic inspired by Prioritized Experience Replay. IIF consistently outperforms standard PPO in both sample efficiency and final test return across all six standard RL environments, with the largest gains in MiniGrid (65.8% SE_ave) and Acrobot (48.5% SE_peak). Random filtering performs significantly worse than unfiltered standard PPO, confirming that the gains come from principled influence-based selection rather than mere data reduction. The advantage-based heuristic matches IIF in small discrete environments (FrozenLake) but degrades in larger state spaces (MiniGrid), where IIF's gradient-based influence scores capture richer signal. The TD-error heuristic accelerates convergence in simple environments but does not match IIF's final performance or generalizability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the TracIn gradient-similarity attribution method (Pruthi et al., 2020) to the online RL setting, where the circular data-model dependency (each experience both updates the policy and shapes future data collection) violates the fixed-dataset assumption underlying all prior data attribution work. It builds on PPO's local optimization structure—each round trains on a fixed rollout buffer—to define a 'local' attribution scope that sidesteps the intractable cross-round causal tracing. The computational efficiency trick (ghost dot product) is borrowed from Wang et al. (2025a), and the target function design draws on REINFORCE-with-baseline structure, but the key departure is formulating attribution for a non-stationary, self-referential training loop rather than a static empirical risk minimization problem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper opens a principled interpretability lens for online RL that prior saliency-map and critical-state methods could not provide, and its IIF algorithm translates that lens into a practical, low-overhead training improvement (20–67% sample-efficiency gains, ~4× runtime reduction in RLHF). The characterization of harmful records as those with sign-mismatched or magnitude-inflated advantage estimates gives practitioners a concrete diagnostic, while the three-phase transition in influence patterns (naive association → semantic clustering → noise saturation) offers a new way to understand how behaviors form during training. Key open questions include extending the framework to adaptive optimizers (currently proxied by SGD), other RL algorithms like GRPO, and providing a counterfactual interpretation that the local scope currently precludes.
