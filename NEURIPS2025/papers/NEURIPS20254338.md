## Problem

Existing approaches to communicating LLM uncertainty face a fundamental trade-off: abstention withholds potentially valuable information, while linguistic calibration (hedging with quantifiers like 'possibly') produces outputs that are difficult to leverage in downstream tasks and lack formal probabilistic guarantees. No prior method offers a unified, controllable mechanism to adjust the specificity of a model's claim while maintaining a rigorous bound on factual error.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254338/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Given a question prompt and a user-specified risk level α ∈ (0,1), the method samples K=100 responses from a base LLM, clusters them semantically, and outputs a four-element tuple (c, α, λ_α, T_λ(c)) where T_λ(c) is a natural-language claim that is less specific than the original but carries a conformal guarantee P(factual) ≥ 1−α. The multiplicity threshold λ (an integer in (0,100]) controls the degree of generalization: higher λ yields more specific claims with higher error risk, while lower λ produces vaguer but more reliable statements. The rewritten claim is a single coherent sentence (not a list of alternatives), making it directly usable in downstream evaluation and generation pipelines.

## Method

The paper reinterprets linguistic calibration through possible-world semantics: a claim corresponds to the set of Kripke worlds in which it holds, and hedging enlarges that set, forming nested spheres of decreasing specificity. The pipeline (1) samples K answers, (2) identifies semantically distinct clusters via a single LLM call, (3) estimates cluster multiplicity, (4) constructs nested cluster sets using an LLM-guided incremental selection that prioritizes semantic proximity to the most frequent answer, and (5) performs belief probing—verbalizing each nested set as the belief of an error-prone agent and prompting an LLM to produce a less specific claim satisfying a formal description condition. Learn-Then-Test (LTT) is then applied to select the multiplicity threshold λ that guarantees the target factuality bound without requiring monotonicity of the risk function. For amortization, the authors fine-tune Llama-3-8B via SFT, DPO, and ORPO on the synthetic rewriting data, enabling a single forward pass to produce uncertainty-aware claim rewrites at user-specified confidence levels.

## Results

On SimpleQA, CLC with Llama3-8B-Instruct (K=100 samples) outperforms GPT-4o's 38.2% factuality by answering less specifically, while maintaining valid conformal risk-control guarantees (test-set risk stays within the guarantee region and closely tracks the lower accuracy threshold). On Natural Questions, CLC similarly improves factuality as the multiplicity threshold increases, with the conformal guarantee remaining valid. In the FActScore experiment (Section 5.3), all fine-tuned claim rewriters (T_SFT, T_DPO, T_ORPO) improve FActScore over the out-of-the-box Llama3-8B baseline (T_Base) across confidence levels from 'guess' to 'certain', with instruction-tuned variants adhering more reliably to designated uncertainty levels.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is CLC against a standard abstention baseline on both SimpleQA and Natural Questions, matched at the same marginal answer-set coverage percentage. On SimpleQA, CLC outperforms abstention by a large margin: while abstention improves factuality only by answering a small subset of questions, CLC answers all questions while still enhancing factuality, and the advantage grows as the relaxation percentage increases. On NQ, where the model already achieves non-trivial performance, abstention does not steadily improve factuality at higher abstention ratios, whereas CLC yields consistent gains. In the FActScore rewriting experiment, the off-the-shelf T_Base model fails to consistently follow instructions to rewrite claims to designated uncertainty levels, while T_SFT, T_DPO, and T_ORPO all effectively enhance FActScore and adapt rewriting specificity based on claim difficulty. A naive rewriting baseline (prompting the model to directly boost confidence) fails to facilitate fine-grained factuality-informativeness trade-off and degenerates to low-informativeness rewrites, unlike CLC's smooth CPMI decrease.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate threads—linguistic calibration (Mielke et al., Band et al.) and conformal abstention (Mohri & Hashimoto, Angelopoulos et al.)—by reinterpreting uncertainty quantifiers as set prediction through possible-world semantics (Kripke, Goodman). Where Mohri & Hashimoto's back-off drops uncertain claims entirely, CLC instead rewrites them into progressively less specific statements, preserving the answer space while controlling risk via Learn-Then-Test. The semantic uncertainty probing pipeline (Kuhn et al.) and majority-voting self-consistency (Wang et al.) are repurposed to construct the nested cluster sets that ground the conformal guarantee, and the amortized rewriter extends the approach into a fine-tunable component using DPO/ORPO alignment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CLC offers a practically useful middle ground: models answer every question while providing conformal guarantees on factuality, avoiding the information loss of abstention and the downstream-evaluation friction of hedging language. The fine-tuned claim rewriter makes the approach deployable without the costly oversampling pipeline. Key limitations include heavy reliance on LLM-based clustering and belief summarization (precision 0.81, recall 0.91 in manual checks), computational expense of K=100 samples per question, and the need for modified evaluation metrics that don't penalize generality. Open questions include how humans actually interpret and act on less-specific claims, whether the approach scales to long-form generation beyond atomic QA, and whether the conformal guarantees hold under distribution shift.
