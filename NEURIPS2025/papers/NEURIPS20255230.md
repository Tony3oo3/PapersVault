## Problem

Existing physics-informed diffusion models assume governing equations are complete and accurate, but in real-world systems like ocean chlorophyll dynamics, only certain components (e.g., advection) are well-constrained while others (diffusion, biological reaction) remain poorly understood. Enforcing incomplete physics as if it were complete introduces conflicting gradients that corrupt the learned distribution, while ignoring physics entirely sacrifices valuable domain knowledge. This paper addresses how to integrate partially known physical laws into diffusion models without over-constraining the solution.

## Input / Output

The model consumes sequences of 64×64 Chl concentration patches (log-transformed due to log-normal distribution) at 1/6° spatial and 3-day temporal resolution from the Southern Ocean, along with corresponding horizontal velocity fields (u, v). Given a subset of known frames (e.g., frames 1 and 20 for infilling, or the first 10 frames for prediction), it reconstructs the missing or future Chl concentration fields. At inference, the physics operator is not applied; known frames are held fixed via clamping while unknown frames are generated through the standard reverse diffusion process.

## Method

PPIDM introduces a physics residual difference loss that, instead of forcing the predicted state to satisfy known physics exactly (φ(x̂₀)=0), minimizes the discrepancy between the physics operator applied to the ground truth and the prediction: φ(x₀) − φ(x̂₀). The physics operator φ encodes only the trusted portion of the dynamics—either the material derivative (advection term of the ADR PDE) or a particle-backtracking law—leaving unknown components (diffusion, biological reaction) to be learned from data. The residual is treated as a zero-mean Gaussian with timestep-dependent variance drawn from the diffusion posterior schedule, so early denoising steps allow flexibility while later steps enforce stronger physical consistency. Training combines this physics loss with standard data fidelity loss on x₀ predictions, and at inference the model performs standard reverse diffusion with clamping of known frames, requiring no physics inputs for unobserved timesteps.

## Results

On the Biogeochemical Southern Ocean State Estimate dataset, PPIDM achieves the lowest RMSE and MAE across both long-range infilling (frames 2–19 given frames 1 and 20) and future-frame prediction (frames 11–20 given frames 1–10). For infilling, PPIDM reaches RMSE 0.270 and MAE 0.208, compared to 0.393/0.311 for DPS, 0.410/0.330 for vanilla diffusion, 0.460/0.371 for CoCoGen, and 0.538/0.493 for PIDM. For prediction, PPIDM achieves RMSE 0.268 and MAE 0.202, outperforming all baselines (best competitor: DPS at 0.298/0.238). The paper also reports that PPIDM's advantage grows with temporal distance from known frames, and that region-specific calibration of the physics operator weight (λ) and operator type (ADR PDE vs. particle backtracking) yields further gains.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PPIDM outperforms all four baselines on both infilling and prediction tasks. Against PIDM [1], which incorrectly enforces the advection-only ADR PDE residual to zero during training, PPIDM reduces infilling RMSE from 0.538 to 0.270 (≈50% reduction) and prediction RMSE from 0.491 to 0.268; the paper attributes PIDM's poor performance to conflicting gradients between the data-fidelity term and the over-constrained physics subspace. Against CoCoGen [11], which injects advection constraints only in the last 30% of denoising steps while assuming advection fully describes the dynamics, PPIDM improves infilling RMSE from 0.460 to 0.270 and prediction RMSE from 0.309 to 0.268. Against DPS [3], which applies physics guidance during sampling, PPIDM achieves a 31% infilling RMSE reduction (0.393→0.270) and a 10% prediction RMSE reduction (0.298→0.268); the paper notes that DPS's post-hoc guidance cannot correct structural errors already accumulated in earlier denoising steps. The vanilla diffusion baseline (no physics) sits between DPS and CoCoGen on infilling but is competitive on prediction, confirming that physics integration during training (as in PPIDM) is more effective than sampling-time-only guidance.

## Relation to Prior Work

PPIDM extends physics-informed diffusion models (PIDM, CoCoGen) by replacing their assumption of complete governing equations with a residual-difference loss that only enforces consistency with the *known* portion of the dynamics. Where PIDM minimizes the PDE residual to zero and CoCoGen projects onto a physics manifold during sampling, PPIDM instead penalizes the discrepancy between the physics operator applied to ground truth and to the prediction, leaving unmodeled terms (diffusion, biological reaction) to be learned from data. The framework also draws on the probabilistic variance schedule from PIDM to achieve progressive constraint enforcement, and contrasts with PINN-style operator learning by retaining the generative, multi-conditioning flexibility of diffusion models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap that is common across geoscience and engineering: governing equations are rarely complete, yet most physics-informed ML methods assume they are. The residual-difference formulation is simple and generalizable to any system with partially known dynamics, making it a potentially useful template for climate, atmospheric, and biomedical modeling. However, the experimental validation is confined to a single Southern Ocean Chl dataset on one GPU, with no observation noise, and the need for region-specific operator/weight calibration limits immediate deployability. Open questions include scaling to global satellite data with large spatiotemporal gaps, handling coupled multi-physics PDEs, and whether the approach remains effective under realistic sensor noise and missing observations.
