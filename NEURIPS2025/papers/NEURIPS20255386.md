## Problem

Despite the proliferation of self-correction methods for LLMs (intrinsic, external, and fine-tuned), no systematic benchmark exists to compare their effectiveness across diverse reasoning tasks and model categories. The central question—whether LLMs can genuinely correct themselves, and whether more elaborate correction pipelines necessarily outperform simpler baselines like chain-of-thought—remains largely unexplored.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255386/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes text questions from seven curated datasets (HotpotQA, CommonsenseQA, GPQA, GSM8K, AQUA, MATH, HumanEval; ~100 samples each after outlier removal) and pairs them with a chosen LLM and self-correction method. The iterative self-correction paradigm feeds the model's previous response back into the prompt (p_k = p_{k-1} ∪ r_{k-1}), so the 'input' at each iteration grows with accumulated context. Outputs are scored via task-specific metrics (accuracy, solve rate, pass@k) plus two novel rates—Correction Rate (fraction of wrong answers fixed) and Misjudgment Rate (fraction of correct answers wrongly altered)—and resource costs (token count, API calls, response time).

## Method

CorrectBench organizes 11 self-correction methods into three categories—S1 intrinsic (RCI, CoVe, Self-Refine, Reflexion-v1), S2 external (Reflexion-v2, RARR, RATT, CRITIC), and S3 fine-tuned (DCoT, SCORE, SuperCorrect)—and evaluates them on both instruction-based LLMs (LLaMA 3.1, Qwen 2.5, GPT-4o, Claude 3.5-Sonnet) and reasoning LLMs (DeepSeek-V3, DeepSeek-R1, o3-mini, QWQ-32B). A key architectural contribution is the mixture framework, where the output of one correction method is piped as auxiliary prompt into a second (e.g., Base→S1→S2), enabling analysis of synergistic or conflicting interactions between strategies. The benchmark also introduces a failure-mode taxonomy (logical oversight, factual inaccuracy, over-reliance on tools, etc.) to diagnose *why* corrections succeed or fail, and a resource-cost analysis (tokens × API calls) that surfaces efficiency trade-offs, revealing that a simple CoT baseline often matches or approaches the accuracy of far more expensive correction pipelines.

## Results

Across 7 datasets and 9 LLMs, self-correction methods improve average accuracy by +12.72% on GPQA, +7.24% on AQUA, and +5.03% on MATH relative to the Base prompt, while simpler tasks like GSM8K show modest or even negative gains (−1.42% average). The best single intrinsic method, Self-Refine, achieves 40.69% on GPQA (+22.13 over Base) and 81.77% on MATH (+6.65), while the best external method, RARR, reaches 36.82% on GPQA (+18.26) and 82.78% on MATH (+7.66). Notably, Reflexion-v1 (intrinsic, no tools) degrades performance on most tasks (e.g., −11.24 on HotpotQA, −18.82 on GSM8K), whereas its tool-augmented counterpart Reflexion-v2 recovers and surpasses Base. The CoT baseline, despite being the simplest, achieves 91.96% on GSM8K (+5.50) and remains competitive on efficiency, challenging the assumption that more complex correction pipelines are always superior.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among the 11 self-correction methods evaluated, external correction (S2) consistently outperforms intrinsic correction (S1) in average gains: S2 methods achieve +12.72% on GPQA and +5.03% on MATH, while S1 methods reach +22.13% (Self-Refine) and +18.85% (CoVe) on GPQA but show more variance across tasks. Reflexion-v1 (intrinsic, no external tools) is the clear loser, degrading performance on 5 of 6 evaluated datasets (e.g., −18.82 on GSM8K, −12.90 on AQUA), whereas its tool-augmented version Reflexion-v2 recovers to +7.22 on HotpotQA and +7.00 on AQUA. RARR offers the best efficiency-accuracy trade-off (533 tokens, 2 API calls, 76.3% on MATH), while RATT achieves the highest MATH accuracy (78.7%) at greater token cost (2185 tokens, 3 API calls). The CoT baseline, despite being the simplest, beats Base on GSM8K (+5.50) and HotpotQA (+2.53) but underperforms on GPQA (−2.04) and HumanEval (−12.61), making it a strong efficiency-accuracy compromise. Fine-tuned methods (S3) show the most task-specific behavior: SuperCorrect on Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct gains +28.45 on MATH but only +14.85 on CS-QA, while DCoT on LLaMA2-7B actually degrades GSM8K by −15.55.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes the self-correction literature surveyed by Kamoi et al. [29] into a unified evaluation framework, categorizing 11 existing methods (RCI, CoVe, Self-Refine, Reflexion, RARR, RATT, CRITIC, DCoT, SCORE, SuperCorrect) into intrinsic, external, and fine-tuned correction families. Unlike prior narrower benchmarks—CriticBench [34] for critique discrimination, VISCO [64] for multimodal correction, and Beyond Correctness [78] for code—it is the first to systematically compare all three correction categories across commonsense, mathematical, and code-generation tasks simultaneously, and to introduce a mixture framework that chains methods sequentially. The iterative self-correction paradigm formalized here (prompt augmentation with prior responses) is a straightforward extension of the CoT prompting lineage [61] into a multi-round setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CorrectBench provides the field with a reproducible, multi-task platform for comparing self-correction strategies, filling a gap where prior evaluations were task-specific or method-specific. The finding that a simple CoT baseline is competitive with elaborate correction pipelines, and that reasoning LLMs like DeepSeek-V3 hit a performance ceiling where additional correction yields only marginal gains at high computational cost, reframes the practical question from 'which correction method is best?' to 'when is correction worth the overhead?' Open questions include adaptive method selection based on detected error types (the paper's failure-mode taxonomy motivates this), optimal stopping criteria for iterative correction, and whether the efficiency-accuracy trade-off shifts as base models continue to improve.
