## Problem

Fine-tuning Multilingual CLIP (MCLIP) models on a single source language for downstream cross-modal retrieval degrades performance in other languages, and the standard remedy of incorporating full parallel corpora for all target languages incurs prohibitive computational cost. The paper identifies token vocabulary overlap as the dominant similarity factor governing cross-lingual transferability in multimodal settings and asks how to exploit this signal to reduce reliance on parallel data while preserving multilingual retrieval quality.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253364/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images paired with English text descriptions (source language) and, depending on the strategy, either machine-translated parallel sentences in up to 36 target languages (TaPCL) or only the top-k critical words translated into target languages inserted as inline prompts (CiPCL). The fine-tuned OpenCLIP ViT-B-32-XLM-Roberta-Base model outputs ranked retrieval lists for both image-to-text and text-to-image directions, evaluated per-language and averaged across all target languages using Recall@K metrics.

## Method

The core insight is that target languages with high token vocabulary overlap to the source language benefit less from explicit parallel training, so their parallel samples can be down-weighted or replaced. TaPCL replaces uniform minibatch sampling with a softmax-weighted scheme (temperature τ) where each target language's sampling probability is inversely proportional to its token overlap with the source, concentrating compute on linguistically distant languages. CiPCL goes further by discarding full parallel sentences entirely: it identifies the most salient word in each source caption via [CLS]-token attention weights in the last transformer layer, translates only that word (using M2M100) into each target language, and inserts it as a parenthetical prompt (e.g., 'A kagu (cagou in French) standing on a log') into the English text. Both strategies are trained with the standard CLIP NCE contrastive loss and use adapter modules in the final transformer layer for parameter-efficient fine-tuning, reducing trainable parameters while maintaining retrieval accuracy comparable to full parallel-corpus baselines at 50–70% of the training iterations.

## Results

On MSCOCO36, TaPCL achieves R@Avg of 75.39 with 4,929 iterations (14m31s), outperforming PCL-base (75.28, 7,039 iterations, 21m02s) while using ~30% fewer iterations. CiPCL reaches 75.15 R@Avg with only 3,519 iterations (09m55s), a ~50% reduction in training time. On XM3600, TaPCL achieves 88.27 R@Avg (5,680 iterations) and CiPCL achieves 88.16 (4,050 iterations), both matching or exceeding PCL-base (88.25, 8,100 iterations) with substantially fewer iterations. Both methods significantly outperform non-parallel-corpus baselines (Prompt: 73.61/87.19, VME: 73.62/87.22 R@Avg).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against PCL-base (uniform parallel-corpus sampling), TaPCL achieves a small but consistent R@Avg improvement (75.39 vs. 75.28 on MSCOCO36; 88.27 vs. 88.25 on XM3600) while reducing training iterations by ~30% (4,929 vs. 7,039; 5,680 vs. 8,100). CiPCL trades a marginal R@Avg drop (−0.13 on MSCOCO36, −0.09 on XM3600) for a ~50% reduction in iterations (3,519 vs. 7,039; 4,050 vs. 8,100). Both proposed methods substantially outperform the non-parallel-corpus baselines: Prompt-tuning [23] (73.61 / 87.19 R@Avg) and VME [32] (73.62 / 87.22 R@Avg), with gaps of roughly 1.5–1.7 points on MSCOCO36 and 1.0–1.1 points on XM3600, confirming that some form of target-language signal is necessary for effective multilingual fine-tuning. The efficiency gains are further validated in the appendix: scaling to 29 languages (Table 5) and applying to unimodal XLM-RoBERTa on XNLI (Table 7) both show the same pattern of matched or improved accuracy with fewer iterations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the cross-lingual transfer literature from unimodal language models (mBERT, XLM-RoBERTa) to the multimodal MCLIP setting, identifying token similarity as the dominant transferability factor in image-text retrieval. It builds directly on the parallel corpora learning paradigm (PCL-base) used in cross-lingual NLP tasks [11,12,13], but departs from its uniform sampling assumption by introducing token-similarity-weighted sampling (TaPCL) and a key-term-only prompt strategy (CiPCL) inspired by human second-language acquisition. The CiPCL approach also draws on parameter-efficient fine-tuning via adapters [30] and attention-based token importance, repurposing these for cross-lingual knowledge injection without full parallel sentences.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in reducing fine-tuning cost by ~50% (CiPCL) while maintaining retrieval performance within 0.1–0.13% of the full parallel-corpus baseline, which is meaningful for deploying MCLIP across many target languages where constructing and storing full parallel corpora is prohibitive. However, the evaluation is limited to 10 well-supported languages with English as the sole source language, and the methods have not been tested on finer-grained tasks (e.g., multilingual VQA) or with non-English source languages, leaving open whether the token-similarity weighting generalizes when the source language itself has low resource coverage. The finding that syntactic, phonological, and geographic similarity are irrelevant (while token overlap dominates) is a natural extension of prior unimodal results [14] and does not fundamentally change how the field approaches cross-lingual transfer.
