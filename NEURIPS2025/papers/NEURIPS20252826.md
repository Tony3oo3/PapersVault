## Problem

Existing LLM benchmarks for economic reasoning either target narrow applications (e.g., financial sentiment analysis) or focus on strategic settings, leaving non-strategic microeconomics—where agents are price-takers optimizing consumption, production, and market equilibrium—largely unassessed. Additionally, static benchmarks face growing data contamination risks, and MCQA-only evaluation can inflate scores through option-gaming shortcuts that mask genuine reasoning failures.

## Input / Output

Each test question is a (type, domain, perspective) tuple spanning 37 instantiated elements across 10 domains (consumer goods, medical, finance, education, technology, entertainment, environmental policy, politics, sports, gambling) and 5 perspectives (first-person, second-person, third-person anonymous/female/male). Every question exists in paired MCQA and FTQA formats; FTQA answers are typically numeric, enabling automatic rule-based grading (exact numeric match or symbolic equivalence) without human annotation. The evaluation produces per-element accuracy, a family of calibration metrics (ECE, Brier score, expected probability assignment, normalized accuracy), and—uniquely from FTQA—structured error classifications that pinpoint specific conceptual mistakes such as defaulting to Marshallian rather than Hicksian demand derivations.

## Method

The auto-STEER generation protocol starts from hand-written gold-standard templates tagged with domain, perspective, and type, then uses GPT-4o to style-transfer each template across all 10 domains and 5 perspectives (yielding up to 40 domain-perspective pairs per type). A second LLM pass replicates each template 100 times by modifying surface wording while preserving labeled numeric fields, producing 30,000 templates across the dataset; 500 templates per element are hand-validated (99.88% valid, with a Chernoff-bound 95% CI of 97.1–99.4%). Each template is then instantiated 20 times with contextually constrained random values (e.g., negative demand slopes, positive equilibrium prices), yielding 1,000 questions per (domain, perspective) pair and up to 40,000 per type. The 27 evaluated LLMs are scored under both MCQA and FTQA using exact-match accuracy, normalized accuracy, ECE, Brier score, and expected probability assignment, with the paired format enabling direct attribution of performance gaps to option-gaming versus genuine economic reasoning.

## Results

Across 27 LLMs evaluated on 21,000 test questions spanning 37 microeconomic elements, reasoning-tuned models (o3, o1-preview, DeepSeek R1) substantially outperform standard LLMs on both MCQA and FTQA. Removing computational tools lowers o3's FTQA accuracy by 15.2 percentage points versus only 1.7 points on MCQA, and up to 91% of o1-preview's performance variance is attributable to numerical instantiations. LLMs consistently score higher on MCQA than FTQA, with random guessing alone explaining 57% of the aggregate gap; models select the 'closest' answer option 53.38% of the time. Foundational math competence (exponent manipulation) strongly predicts downstream economic reasoning accuracy (Pearson r = 0.774, p = 1.23×10⁻⁷).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a benchmark paper, so the 'baselines' are the 27 LLMs evaluated rather than competing methods. Reasoning-tuned models (o3, o1-preview, DeepSeek R1) dominate both MCQA and FTQA, even though o3 and o1-preview were evaluated on a harder 13-element subset. Among standard LLMs, gpt-4o and claude-3-5-sonnet lead but still show substantial gaps: no model other than o3 (with tools) exceeds 10% exact-match accuracy on FTQA for complex elements like Profit Maximization. The MCQA format inflates performance relative to FTQA across all models, with the gap driven by option gaming (plugging in answer choices) and contextual anchoring (e.g., claude-3-5-sonnet's accuracy jumps from 38% to 72% as the correct sum's digit count increases from 5 to 6). Open-source models (Llama-2 7B through larger variants) trail closed-source models by wide margins, and smaller models within each family (e.g., gpt-4o-mini vs. gpt-4o, claude-3-haiku vs. claude-3-5-sonnet) show consistent but meaningful performance drops, particularly on elements requiring multi-step derivation such as Hicksian demand (where gpt-4o-mini defaults to Marshallian in 23.4% of incorrect answers vs. 51.2% for gpt-4o).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends STEER (Raman et al., ICML 2024), which taxonomized and benchmarked economic rationality in strategic settings (game theory, decision theory), by constructing an analogous taxonomy for non-strategic microeconomics (price-taking consumption, production, market equilibrium, and welfare). It departs from prior economic LLM benchmarks (FinQA, FLUE, PIXIU, TradingGPT) that target narrow financial applications, instead covering 58 elements across 10 domains and 5 perspectives. The auto-STEER data generation protocol synthesizes ideas from dynamic benchmark frameworks (LiveBench, AgentQuest) with template-based style transfer, and the dual MCQA/FTQA evaluation format is a deliberate departure from STEER's MCQA-only design to expose format-dependent biases.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

STEER-ME is the first comprehensive benchmark for non-strategic microeconomic reasoning, filling a gap explicitly identified in the strategic-focused STEER. The finding that MCQA systematically inflates performance through option gaming and contextual anchoring has broad implications for how the field evaluates LLM reasoning in any domain with verifiable answers. Open questions include whether fine-tuning on this benchmark genuinely improves economic agent behavior, whether the 58-element taxonomy is complete, and how to overcome the limited within-domain diversity of LLM-generated questions. The strong correlation between foundational math (exponents) and downstream economic reasoning suggests targeted improvements in arithmetic robustness may be a high-leverage intervention.
