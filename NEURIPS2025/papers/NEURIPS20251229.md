## Problem

In mixed-motivation multi-agent reinforcement learning, rational self-interested behavior frequently leads to collectively suboptimal outcomes (social dilemmas), yet existing approaches lack a theoretically grounded mechanism to numerically quantify how individual agent actions impact social welfare. Prior methods such as LIO, Inequity Aversion, MOA, and SCM rely on handcrafted heuristics or evolution-based adaptations without a unified economic rationale for reward shaping, limiting their generality and scalability.

## Input / Output

The centralized Tax Planner consumes a composite observation comprising the global environment state (a multi-hot discrete vector of dimension 3N in Escape Room, or a full-resolution RGB image of the map in Cleanup), the joint action vector, a scalar 'bank' state tracking accumulated tax revenue, and the joint reward vector. It outputs per-agent tax rates θ (N-dimensional, sigmoid-activated), per-agent allowance rates δ (N-dimensional, softmax-activated), and a scalar budget ratio for withdrawing from the bank. Individual agents receive their standard local observations and produce actions from their respective action spaces, while their effective reward is the sum of the extrinsic reward and the learned Pigovian tax/allowance shaping term.

## Method

LOPT introduces a two-component architecture grounded in externality theory: a centralized Tax Planner trained via policy gradient to maximize cumulative social welfare (sum of all agents' rewards), and a percentage-based reward shaping mechanism F_θ,δ^i = −θ_i·r_i + δ_i·Σ_j θ_j·r_j that internalizes each agent's externality by taxing negative-externality behaviors and subsidizing positive-externality ones. Two theorems provide theoretical grounding: Theorem 1 proves existence of tax/allowance rates that exactly replicate the optimal Pigovian tax under the assumption that other agents' actions are treated as environment, and Theorem 2 shows that maximizing social welfare with the Tax Planner implicitly approximates the optimal Pigovian tax. An entropy regularization term f(π_p) penalizes the absolute sum of all shaping terms to maintain balance between taxation and allowance. General agents are trained with standard policy gradient on shaped rewards r_i + F_i, while the Tax Planner is updated on the global reward with the entropy penalty weighted by hyperparameter η.

## Results

On Escape Room, LOPT converges to the optimal social welfare values of 8 (N=2, M=1) and 9 (N=3, M=2), consistently completing episodes in a single step with zero betrayals, while vanilla Policy Gradient agents completely fail and PG-d/c variants show high variance. On Cleanup, LOPT achieves near-optimal social welfare across all settings (N=2 on 7×7 and 10×10 maps, and N=5 on 18×25), maintaining stable performance even under fixed-orientation constraints where LIO exhibits instability. In the most complex Cleanup(N=5) scenario with a very low apple respawn rate (0.05), LOPT successfully scales and induces emergent labor specialization (cleaners, harvesters, part-timers) that other baselines fail to achieve.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LIO/LIO-dec [50], LOPT matches near-optimal performance in Escape Room but eliminates the instability and betrayal-related fluctuations that plague LIO; in Cleanup, LIO fails to learn efficient policies on 10×10 maps and shows instability under fixed-orientation settings, while LOPT remains stable. Against Inequity Aversion (IA) [15], LOPT outperforms on the harder 10×10 Cleanup map where IA degrades severely, though IA reaches near-optimum on the simpler 7×7 setting. Against MOA [17] and SCM [14] in the complex Cleanup(N=5) scenario, LOPT achieves comparable or superior social welfare while additionally providing interpretable tax/allowance mechanisms that explicitly quantify and internalize externalities. Overall, LOPT wins or ties on all primary comparisons, with its clearest advantages being stability (fewer betrayals), scalability to larger agent populations, and the theoretical grounding of its reward shaping via optimal Pigovian tax.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes Pigovian tax theory from welfare economics as a formal mechanism for reward shaping in mixed-motivation MARL, departing from prior approaches (LIO, IA, MOA, SCM) that rely on handcrafted heuristics, reward-sharing, or counterfactual reasoning to promote cooperation. The conceptual lineage traces from Leibo et al.'s formulation of sequential social dilemmas and Yang et al.'s LIO framework, but introduces externality as a first-class quantitative object (Eq. 6) and proves that a centralized agent maximizing social welfare implicitly approximates the optimal Pigovian tax (Theorem 2). Structurally, the two-stage tax-planner architecture draws inspiration from the AI Economist (Zheng et al. 2022), though LOPT targets intertemporal social dilemmas rather than macroeconomic policy design.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a theoretically grounded, economically interpretable framework for quantifying and resolving social dilemmas in MARL, which prior methods addressed only through ad-hoc reward engineering. However, the centralized tax planner architecture limits scalability to large agent populations, and the authors explicitly flag a decentralized variant as future work. The experiments, while covering two standard benchmarks, are confined to small-scale settings (N≤5 agents, maps up to 18×25), leaving open whether the learned tax/allowance mechanism generalizes to larger, more heterogeneous environments. The absence of released code and the reliance on a single entropy hyperparameter (η=0.95) for balance between taxes and allowances also limit immediate reproducibility and practical adoption.
