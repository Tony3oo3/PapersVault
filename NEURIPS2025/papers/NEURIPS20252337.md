## Problem

Transformers can implement both generalizable algorithms (induction heads) and brittle positional shortcuts, yet the conditions under which gradient-based training selects one over the other remain poorly characterized. This paper provides the first rigorous theoretical account of how the diversity of pretraining data—measured by the distribution of trigger-to-trigger distances—governs a phase transition between these two mechanisms, and how the number of trigger tokens modulates the difficulty of learning the generalizable one.

## Input / Output

The task is trigger-output copying: given a sequence [irrelevant tokens of length ℓ₁][trigger t][output o][irrelevant tokens of length ℓ₂][trigger t][?], the model must predict o. Each token embedding is a concatenated one-hot vector x_t = [p_t; e_{z_t}; e_{z_{t-1}}] ∈ R^{L+2N} that simultaneously encodes absolute position, current token identity, and previous token identity. Pretraining sequences always have ℓ₁ = ℓ₂ = ℓ (periodic structure), while OOD test sequences have ℓ₁ ≠ ℓ₂, rendering the positional shortcut invalid and isolating the induction head as the only correct mechanism.

## Method

The paper analyzes a single-layer transformer with tied key-query projections (W_KQ) and a value matrix (W_V), trained via a two-step gradient descent procedure (first on W_V, then on W_KQ) in the early-phase regime where the first step induces associative memory. The central quantity is the max-sum ratio R(D_ℓ) = max_ℓ(ℓ^{-1}q_ℓ) / Σ_ℓ(ℓ^{-1}q_ℓ), which measures how concentrated the pretraining length distribution is. After one gradient step, W_KQ decomposes into a positional shortcut component (mapping the second trigger position to a fixed relative position, with strength bounded by max_ℓ q_ℓ·T(ℓ)^{-1}) and an induction head component (content-based retrieval via the previous-token embedding block, with strength Σ_ℓ q_ℓ·T(ℓ)^{-1}); the ratio of these two strengths is exactly R(D_ℓ), yielding a sharp Θ(N_trg^{-1}) threshold below which the induction head dominates and OOD generalization holds, and above which the positional shortcut wins. The paper further formulates and solves a linear program to derive the optimal pretraining distribution (linear in ℓ up to N_trg) that minimizes average forward-pass cost while meeting the OOD generalization constraint.

## Results

The paper's central theoretical result (Theorems 5–6) establishes a sharp phase transition governed by the max-sum ratio R(D_ℓ) = max_ℓ(ℓ⁻¹q_ℓ) / Σ_ℓ(ℓ⁻¹q_ℓ): when R < ε₁(N_trg) = Θ(N_trg⁻¹), the trained single-layer transformer implements an induction head and generalizes OOD; when R > ε₂(N_trg) = Θ(N_trg⁻¹), it relies on a positional shortcut and fails on non-periodic test sequences. Empirically, OOD accuracy increases monotonically with pretraining diversity (larger ℓ_max), and the transition region shifts rightward as the number of trigger tokens N_trg grows from 4 to 8, consistent with the Θ(N_trg⁻¹) threshold. The paper further shows that biasing the pretraining distribution toward longer contexts is substantially more efficient than merely widening it: for ℓ₀ = Θ(N_trg), a support width of only K = Θ(N_trg) suffices, versus K ≳ exp(N_trg) when ℓ₀ = 1. The optimal pretraining distribution minimizing forward-pass cost under the OOD constraint is linear in context length: q_ℓ ∝ ℓ for ℓ ≤ N_trg and zero otherwise.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external baseline methods; instead, it characterizes an internal mechanism transition within a single transformer architecture. The two competing mechanisms are (i) a positional shortcut that maps the second-trigger position T to the midpoint (T+1)/2, valid only when ℓ₁ = ℓ₂, and (ii) an induction head that retrieves the token following the first trigger via content-based attention. The max-sum ratio R(D_ℓ) acts as the control parameter: low R (high diversity) suppresses the positional signal (which spreads across multiple positions and weakens as max_ℓ q_ℓ T(ℓ)⁻¹) while preserving the induction-head signal (which is ℓ-independent and scales as Σ_ℓ q_ℓ T(ℓ)⁻¹), yielding OOD generalization. High R concentrates the positional signal at a single position, causing the model to output the token at the pseudo-midpoint on non-periodic OOD sequences. The trigger size N_trg further modulates this balance: the induction-head signal is split across N_trg trigger types (scaling as N_trg⁻¹), making OOD generalization progressively harder as N_trg increases, a prediction confirmed by the rightward shift of the transition boundary in Figure 3.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the mechanistic interpretability line on induction heads (Elhage et al. 2021; Olsson et al. 2022) by providing a rigorous gradient-descent analysis of when a single-layer transformer learns an induction head versus a positional shortcut, using the early-phase training dynamics framework from Bietti et al. (2023) and related works. It connects two previously separate threads: the shortcut-learning literature (Zhang et al. 2022; Zhou et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2022) and the pretraining-data-structure literature on in-context learning (Garg et al. 2022; Razeghi et al. 2022; Raventós et al. 2023), making explicit how a specific data statistic (the max-sum ratio of trigger distances) quantitatively governs the mechanism transition. The trigger-output copying task itself is adapted from Bietti et al. (2023), but the data-distribution parameterization and OOD generalization criterion are new formalizations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first quantitative, data-dependent threshold (max-sum ratio at Θ(N_trg^{-1})) separating when gradient-based training yields a generalizable induction head from when it yields a brittle positional shortcut, offering concrete guidelines for pretraining data design. The trade-off result—that shifting the distribution toward longer contexts is far more efficient than merely widening it—has practical implications for compute-optimal pretraining. Key limitations include the single-layer architecture, absolute positional embeddings, one-gradient-step analysis, and a highly synthetic task; open questions include whether the max-sum ratio characterization survives under relative positional encodings, multi-layer dynamics, and more realistic training protocols.
