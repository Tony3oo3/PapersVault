## Problem

Existing VideoLLMs struggle to understand complex, evolving emotional states from video with explainable rationale, as they lack the ability to effectively combine basic facial attributes into representations of complex emotion. Even state-of-the-art closed models like Gemini 2.0 achieve only 26.3% accuracy in fine-grained sentiment analysis, highlighting a significant gap between low-level attribute perception and high-level emotional reasoning.

## Input / Output

The model takes a video clip (with face bounding box, variable duration and resolution) paired with a text instruction or query as input. Outputs are structured hierarchically across three semantic levels: basic facial attributes (hair, eyes, nose, shape, etc.), expression-level cues (micro-expressions, action units, sentiment), and high-level emotion understanding (fine-grained captions with rationale chains). The affective-tree structure encodes directed rationale-based dependencies from attributes to expressions to emotions, making outputs both accurate and structurally explainable.

## Method

VidEmo builds on Qwen2.5-VL (ViT vision encoder + autoregressive LLM) and introduces a two-stage training pipeline. In pre-training, Curriculum Emotion Learning (CEL) progressively tunes the model through three stages—Attribute Tuning, Expression Tuning, and Emotion Tuning—injecting emotion knowledge from simple to complex. In post-training, the model is optimized via GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) with a Mixed Affective-Tree Reward: generated captions are parsed into a three-level tree (Attribute → Expression → Emotion) with rationale-based directed edges, and the reward is computed as an exponential decay over the tree edit distance between predicted and ground-truth trees. This encourages the model to produce captions that are not only content-accurate but also structurally aligned with human reasoning patterns over emotional understanding. The approach is trained on Emo-CFG, a 2.1M-sample emotion-centric dataset with hierarchical annotations and rationale-grounded QA pairs verified via committee voting across three heterogeneous VideoLLMs.

## Results

On the Emo-CFG benchmark (40 metrics across 15 face perception tasks), VidEmo-T1 (7B) achieves 86.3% average on attribute perception, 39.9% on expression analysis, and 69.3% on emotion understanding, surpassing the strongest open-source baseline Qwen2.5-VL (7B) by +14.2%, +17.4%, and +5.7% respectively, and outperforming Gemini 2.0 on 14 of 15 tasks. On downstream facial expression recognition, VidEmo attains 64.92 UAR / 73.10 WAR on DFEW and 44.02 UAR / 54.86 WAR on MAFW, improving over the previous best (EMO-LLaMA) by 7.8%/10.9% and 5.9%/12.8% in UAR/WAR respectively. The ablation confirms that curriculum emotion learning (CEL) contributes +10.5 points, affective-tree reward (ATR) adds +1.7, and emotion reasoning (ER) adds +3.4 over the no-component baseline (51.4 → 67.0 average).

## Baselines & Comparisons

VidEmo-T1 (7B) outperforms all 18 compared baselines (5 closed APIs + 13 open-source VideoLLMs) across every one of the 15 face perception tasks. Against the strongest closed model, Gemini 2.0, VidEmo-T1 gains +9.8% on attribute perception (86.3 vs. 76.2), +13.8% on expression analysis (39.9 vs. 26.1), and +5.8% on emotion understanding (69.3 vs. 63.5). Against the strongest open-source baseline, Qwen2.5-VL (7B), the margins are +14.2% (attributes), +17.4% (expression), and +5.7% (emotion understanding). On the DFEW downstream task, VidEmo surpasses EMO-LLaMA by 4.69 UAR and 7.21 WAR, and on MAFW by 2.45 UAR and 6.23 WAR, also beating all CLIP-based and transformer-based baselines (e.g., DFER-CLIP at 59.61/71.25 on DFEW). The 3B VidEmo-Base already exceeds all 7B+ open-source baselines (e.g., 68.6 vs. 63.6 on emotion understanding vs. Qwen2.5-VL 7B), demonstrating that the curriculum learning strategy is effective even at smaller scales.

## Relation to Prior Work

VidEmo builds on the Qwen2.5-VL architecture (ViT encoder + autoregressive LLM) and adopts GRPO from DeepSeek-R1 as its post-training reinforcement learning framework. Conceptually, it departs from prior emotion-oriented VideoLLMs (EmotionLLaMA, OmniEmotion, EMO-LLaMA) that treat emotion as a flat classification or simple instruction-tuning task, instead introducing a hierarchical affective-tree structure (attribute → expression → emotion) with curriculum learning for pre-training and a tree-edit-distance-based reward for structured reasoning. The paper also draws on multimodal chain-of-thought (MCoT) prompting but replaces the linear reasoning chain with a tree-structured rationale that mirrors the causal dependency between low-level facial cues and high-level emotional states.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 2.1M-sample Emo-CFG dataset with rationale-grounded annotations is a potentially valuable resource for the emotion recognition community, and the affective-tree reward offers a principled way to encourage structured, explainable emotion reasoning in VideoLLMs. However, the evaluation is conducted primarily on the authors' own benchmark, which was partially labeled using Gemini 2.0—the very model being outperformed—introducing a circularity concern that tempers the headline improvements. The paper acknowledges that emotion is inherently multimodal and that audio integration remains unexplored, and that the model is susceptible to generating counterfactual or emotionally inconsistent narratives. Open questions include whether the tree structure provides genuine advantages over simpler chain-of-thought approaches, and whether the findings generalize beyond facial-centric emotion to broader affective contexts.
