## Problem

Inferring synaptic connectivity from neural population recordings is fundamentally difficult in strongly recurrent networks, where unconnected neurons exhibit strong activity correlations due to shared attractor dynamics, causing standard statistical methods (GLMs, Ising models) to produce systematically spurious connections. Additionally, no recording technique captures all neurons in a circuit, so inference must account for unobserved units. This paper asks whether a self-supervised GNN framework can resolve these spurious correlations and recover true connectivity profiles even under partial observability.

## Input / Output

The model consumes binary spike train matrices (N×L at 0.1 ms resolution, ~4.8M time steps for 8-minute sessions) and, optionally, a continuous external input signal (e.g., head direction angle in [0, 2π]). For partially observed networks, only a subset of neurons provides training signal while hidden neurons participate as graph nodes with interpolated initial embeddings. The output is a symmetric N×N connectivity weight matrix (latent, derived via deterministic transformations rather than directly parameterized) and per-neuron Poisson firing rates for the next time step. The weight matrix is not a free optimization variable but a learned representation that is updated indirectly as the model parameters are optimized.

## Method

The architecture comprises two functionally distinct modules trained jointly by minimizing Poisson negative log-likelihood for spike prediction. The structure learning module applies a 1D convolution (kernel size 2τ/Δt, 32 filters) to each neuron's spike train to produce an embedding, then feeds concatenated pairwise embeddings through a 2-layer MLP to estimate edge weights w_ij. The spike prediction module performs GNN message passing where each neuron's state is updated by aggregating messages from neighbors weighted by the inferred connectivity, with a GRU providing temporal integration over the full spike history; a decoder maps states to log firing rates of an inhomogeneous Poisson process. Because connectivity is a latent byproduct of the prediction objective rather than a directly supervised target, the model must learn weights that genuinely explain the circuit dynamics, which forces it to suppress spurious correlations that arise from co-activated neurons in recurrent attractor networks. For partial observability, hidden neurons are added as auxiliary graph nodes with embeddings initialized by linear interpolation from the two nearest observed neighbors, enabling full-graph message passing under a transductive framework.

## Results

On a fully observed 100-neuron ring attractor network without external inputs, the GNN-based model achieves a normalized connectivity inference error Δ of 0.061 (threshold-crossing) and 0.049 (LNP), while simultaneously attaining spike prediction scores of 0.882 and 0.876 bits per spike (L_bps), respectively. Under external input conditions (Table 2), inference errors remain low (Δ = 0.073 / 0.058) and spike prediction improves to 0.916 / 0.924 L_bps. When applied to 19 real head-direction cells from freely moving mice, the inferred weight profiles consistently exhibit a local-excitation/surround-inhibition motif consistent with continuous attractor network theory.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The GNN-based model outperforms all three baselines on connectivity inference error across every tested condition. Against the GLM (Pillow et al., 2008), the closest competitor, the GNN reduces Δ from 0.244 to 0.061 (threshold-crossing) and from 0.238 to 0.049 (LNP) — a 75–79% relative improvement — while also achieving higher spike prediction scores (0.882 vs. 0.695 L_bps). seqNMF (Mackevicius et al., 2019) and TCA (Williams et al., 2018) are not designed for connectivity inference and perform substantially worse, with Δ values around 0.76–0.80, roughly 12–13× higher than the GNN; they also do not report spike prediction metrics. The paper further notes that NetFormer (Lu et al., 2025), which uses attention-based pairwise couplings without a dedicated message-passing module, reports drastically worse inference accuracy on the same benchmark. The advantage of the GNN is attributed to its explicit separation of structure learning and spike prediction, allowing the latent connectivity to be refined by the predictive task rather than being directly optimized as a free parameter.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant line of connectivity inference methods (GLMs, inverse Ising models, MPF, l1-regularized logistic regression) that treat synaptic weights as directly optimizable parameters fitted to activity correlations, which the authors show systematically fails in strongly recurrent networks due to spurious correlations from co-activated bumps. Instead, it repurposes the GNN message-passing framework—previously applied to physical systems like particles and molecules—into a dual-module architecture where connectivity emerges as a latent representation shaped by a self-supervised spike-prediction objective, rather than being directly regressed from pairwise statistics. The paper explicitly contrasts with two recent GNN-based neural models: NetFormer (which uses attention weights as couplings without dedicated message passing) and AMAG (which initializes adjacency from random or correlation-based heuristics), positioning its explicit separation of structure learning and spike prediction as the key architectural distinction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a recognized failure mode in connectivity inference—spurious correlations in strongly recurrent networks (Das & Fiete, 2020)—and demonstrates that a GNN-based self-supervised approach resolves these artifacts substantially better than pairwise statistical methods across a wide range of conditions. The recovery of the expected local-excitation/surround-inhibition motif from only 19 real HD cells in the ADn provides encouraging evidence for the framework's biological applicability, though the small sample size and single dataset limit generalizability claims. Key open questions include extending to time-varying (plastic) connectivity, 2D continuous attractor networks (grid cells), and characterizing the non-uniqueness of inferred circuits, as multiple distinct weight configurations can produce similar dynamics. The tradeoff between spike prediction accuracy and connectivity inference quality when adding hidden neurons is a practically important finding for experimental design.
