## Problem

Classical median selection assumes perfect, equally-costly comparisons, but in modern settings (crowdsourcing, preference elicitation, federated analytics) comparisons are noisy, expensive, or partially known a priori. No prior work combines reliable and unreliable comparisons for exact median selection, nor exploits known partial-order structure (DAGs) to reduce the number of comparisons below the classical Θ(n) lower bound.

## Input / Output

In the noisy-oracle setting, the algorithm takes an array of n elements plus access to a weak comparison oracle (persistent errors with probability p < 1/2, meaning resampling does not improve accuracy) and a strong oracle (exact but costly), and returns the exact median with probability ≥ 1 − O(n^{-1/4}). In the structural setting, the input is a DAG over n elements with width w encoding known precedence relations, and the output is the median element. The noisy-oracle algorithm also generalizes to the k-th order statistic by adjusting pivot ranks and the success condition.

## Method

For the noisy-oracle setting, the paper modifies LazySelect by sampling r = n^{3/4} elements, selecting pivots d and u, and for each array element performing c·log n weak comparisons against reference elements near the pivots, applying a 2/3-majority rule to classify elements as below, above, or ambiguous; only ambiguous elements trigger a strong oracle query, reducing strong comparisons from O(n) to Õ(n^{3/4}) while keeping total time at O(n log n). For the DAG setting, the algorithm recursively partitions around a pivot using BFS to exploit transitive closure (free comparisons) and Dilworth's theorem to decompose the remaining incomparable elements into w chains, then performs binary search within each chain to classify elements relative to the pivot. The key complexity driver is the DAG width w: total comparisons are O((log n + w·log(n/w))·log n), which is sublinear in n whenever w·log(n/w) = o(n/log n), and reduces to O(log n) for a single chain (w = 1).

## Results

Theoretically, the modified LazySelect achieves O(n log n) total running time, O(n log n) weak comparisons, Õ(n^{3/4}) strong comparisons, and success probability ≥ 1 − O(n^{-1/4}). In the DAG setting, the expected comparison cost is O((log n + w·log(n/w))·log n), which is sublinear (O(log² n)) when width w = O(1). Experimentally, for the weak-oracle setting (n = 10⁵–10⁶), a 75%-accurate oracle with c = 4 weak comparisons per reference element yields >97% median success, while a 90%-accurate oracle needs only 2 log n weak comparisons for >94% success. In the DAG setting (n = 10⁴), the randomized algorithm reduces normalized comparisons from ~5–6 (baseline) to ~1.7 at width w ≈ 500 and to nearly zero at w = 1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two classical baselines. Against standard LazySelect (Floyd & Rivest, 1975), the modified algorithm reduces the number of strong oracle calls in all noise regimes except the noisiest (1−p = 0.51), where the two are comparable; at 1−p = 0.75 with c = 4, the modified method achieves >97% success with fewer strong calls than the baseline, and at 1−p ≥ 0.9 it reaches >94% success with only 2 log n weak comparisons per element. Against the classical deterministic Median-of-Medians (Blum et al., 1973) in the DAG setting, the structure-aware algorithms show dramatic gains as width decreases: at w ≈ 500 the randomized method uses ~1.7n comparisons versus ~5–6n for the baseline (a ~3× reduction), and at w = 1 it requires nearly zero comparisons since the total order is already known. The deterministic (median-of-medians pivot) variant is consistently more expensive than the randomized variant but produces a smoother performance curve across widths.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the classical LazySelect algorithm (Floyd and Rivest, 1975) by introducing a hybrid weak/strong oracle model with majority voting, and adapts the Median of Medians framework (Blum et al., 1973) to the partially ordered setting via Dilworth's chain decomposition. It sits at the intersection of learning-augmented algorithms (Lykouris and Vassilvitskii, 2021; Bai and Coester, 2023) and noisy comparison models (Braverman and Mossel, 2007; Geissmann et al., 2018), but departs from prior noisy-sorting work by targeting exact median selection rather than approximate full sorting, and from poset-sorting work (Daskalakis et al., 2011) by focusing on selection with a width-dependent complexity characterization. The persistent comparison model (errors fixed once drawn) is a deliberate departure from the independent-noise model used in most prior noisy-sorting analyses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean, width-parameterized complexity bound for median selection in DAGs (sublinear when w·log(n/w) = o(n/log n)), offering a principled criterion for when structural priors yield meaningful savings over classical Θ(n) methods. The sublinear strong-comparison guarantee (Õ(n^{3/4})) is practically relevant for settings like crowdsourcing or federated analytics where exact comparisons are orders of magnitude more expensive than noisy ones. Key limitations include reliance on synthetic data, high-probability (not deterministic) guarantees, and the persistent-error model that precludes resampling-based amplification. Open questions include combining both side-information sources simultaneously, extending to empirical datasets, and tightening the gap between the n^{3/4} upper bound and the fundamental barrier identified in the remark.
