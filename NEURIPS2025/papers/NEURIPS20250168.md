## Problem

VLLMs exhibit a safety paradox: they are both trivially jailbreakable and trivially defensible on benchmark datasets, yet the underlying reasons for both sides are poorly understood. The paper identifies that the vulnerability stems from vision inputs compromising the backbone LLM's safety alignment (not catastrophic forgetting), that existing defenses suffer from severe over-prudence (unintended abstention on benign queries), and that the two standard evaluation methods (rule-based keyword matching and LLM-as-judge) show only chance-level agreement, making benchmark conclusions potentially misleading.

## Input / Output

The VLLM under evaluation receives one textual instruction paired with one image (typographic, SD-generated, or MSCOCO-sourced). The proposed LLM-Pipeline detector consumes only the textual instruction—optionally augmented with an image caption generated by QWen2-VL-7B for datasets requiring joint image-text understanding—and outputs a binary safe/unsafe judgment. If the query passes the safety check, it is forwarded to the VLLM for response generation; otherwise, the query is rejected outright, producing a refusal string.

## Method

The LLM-Pipeline is a two-step detect-then-respond architecture that repurposes the built-in guardrails of a text-only LLM (Llama3.1-8B or 70B) as a vision-free jailbreak detector placed before the VLLM. Two detector variants are explored: Naive (raw instruction as input) and Scenario (a scenario-aware system prompt such as 'If the question will induce potentially harmful information about Illegal Activities, please reject to respond' prepended to the instruction). For benchmarks where harmfulness depends on joint image-text semantics (VLGuard, MM-SafetyBench), a caption re-check step uses QWen2-VL-7B to generate an image caption that is concatenated with the instruction before the LLM detector. The key design choice is to avoid a separate VLLM detector (as in ECSO), instead leveraging the stronger safety alignment of recent LLMs, which the paper shows achieves competitive ASR reductions while suffering less from the over-prudence problem that plagues fine-tuned VLLM defenses.

## Results

On the VLSafe dataset, Llama3.1-8B (Naive) achieves an ASR of just 0.7%, while Llama3.1-70B with the Scenario prompt reaches 2.8% on FigStep, demonstrating that vision-free LLM detectors can effectively identify jailbreak attempts. The LLM-Pipeline (Llama3.1 detector + LLaVA-1.5 responder) achieves a superior trade-off between harmlessness (ASR on Safe-Unsafe) and helpfulness (winning rate on Safe-Safe) compared to the PostHoc defense, which suffers significant helpfulness degradation due to over-prudence. Across all six VLLMs evaluated, baseline ASR ranges from 32.2% (Qwen2-VL-7B on FigStep) to 90.4% (LLaVA-1.5-7B on FigStep), confirming widespread vulnerability to jailbreak attacks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the VLGuard PostHoc defense [18], the LLM-Pipeline achieves a markedly better balance: PostHoc (fine-tuned on the test dataset) shows high abstention on benign Safe-Safe inputs, sharply reducing helpfulness, while LLM-Pipeline maintains higher winning rates at comparable or lower ASR on Safe-Unsafe (Fig. 9). Compared to AdaShield-A [19], which achieves near-zero ASR on FigStep (e.g., 0.00 for LLaVA-1.5-7B), the LLM-Pipeline avoids the over-prudence problem that causes AdaShield to reject benign queries at high rates. Among the four LLM detectors, Llama3.1-8B with Scenario (7.6 ASR on FigStep) outperforms Mistral-7B with Scenario (9.6) and substantially outperforms QWen2.5-14B with Scenario (31.8), while Llama3.1-70B with Scenario achieves the best overall result (2.8). The Naive variant of Llama3.1-8B already reaches 0.7 ASR on VLSafe, surpassing Mistral (13.2) and QWen2.5 (22.3) without any additional prompt engineering.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a diagnostic study of the VLLM jailbreak defense landscape, building on the attack benchmarks (FigStep, MM-SafetyBench, VLGuard, VLSafe) and defense methods (VLGuard fine-tuning, AdaShield, ECSO) that have emerged in 2024. It departs from prior work [18, 21] that attributed VLLM vulnerability to catastrophic forgetting during fine-tuning, instead showing via T-SNE feature analysis that the inclusion of vision inputs is the primary cause. The LLM-Pipeline method repurposes existing LLM guardrails (e.g., Llama3.1) as a vision-free detector, a conceptual shift from the VLLM-specific defense approaches in [18, 19, 20] that design tailored mechanisms for multimodal models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The over-prudence finding and the chance-level agreement between rule-based and LLM-based evaluation metrics (Cohen's kappa ≈ 0) collectively undermine confidence in current VLLM safety benchmarks, suggesting that reported near-zero ASR results may reflect over-refusal rather than genuine defense. This reframing has practical implications: practitioners may be over-investing in VLLM-specific defense mechanisms when repurposing LLM guardrails already yields competitive results. Open questions include whether these findings generalize beyond the four evaluated datasets, how to build evaluation protocols that avoid the over-prudence trap, and whether RL-based or system-level strategies can achieve true safety without sacrificing helpfulness.
