## Problem

Progressive (coarse-to-fine) generation has shown strong benefits for visual synthesis, but existing frameworks—cascaded diffusion models, Pyramidal Flow, Relay Diffusion—require multiple separate models, custom diffusion formulations, ad-hoc samplers, or complex inter-stage transition operations (renoisng, blurring, resizing). This paper asks whether progressive generation can be achieved with a single model and minimal modifications to standard Flow Matching, without architectural or sampler complexity.

## Input / Output

The model consumes Gaussian noise and a conditioning signal (class label for ImageNet-1K, text prompt for FLUX finetuning) and produces images at 512×512 or 1024×1024, or videos on Kinetics-700. Unusually, the input latent is first decomposed into a multi-scale Laplacian pyramid (e.g., 256px + 512px for a 512px target), and each scale is denoised independently with its own flow timestep; the final output is the sum of all decoded scale representations. For FLUX finetuning, generation operates at 1024px with variable aspect ratios and a 2-stage decomposition (512px → 1024px).

## Method

DFM extends Flow Matching by introducing independent per-scale flow timesteps t^s and a single shared DiT backbone with replicated per-scale patchification layers and timestep embedders, where patch sizes are chosen so each scale yields an equal token count. During training, a stage index Q is sampled from a discrete distribution; scales within Q receive logit-normal timesteps, preceding scales receive shifted (lower-noise) logit-normal timesteps to simulate inference-time conditioning, and subsequent scales are zeroed out with loss masking. At inference, a standard Euler ODE sampler denoises stages sequentially—advancing the current stage's timestep until it reaches a threshold τ before activating the next scale—enabling intermediate low-resolution previews. The framework is agnostic to the decomposition choice (Laplacian, DWT, DCT all work) and requires only ~0.6% additional parameters over vanilla Flow Matching, with less than 1.2% training slowdown.

## Results

On ImageNet-1K 512px (cfg=1.25), DFM achieves FID 4.28, FDD 120.0, and IS 135.6, outperforming Flow Matching (FID 7.59, FDD 185.7) by 35.2% in FDD and Pyramidal Flow (FID 4.95, FDD 164.7) by 26.4% in FDD. On ImageNet-1K 1024px (cfg=1.25), DFM reaches FID 7.56 and FDD 181.7, surpassing Pyramidal Flow (FID 9.38, FDD 245.7) by 26% in FDD. On Kinetics-700 512px (cfg=1.25), DFM achieves FID 7.88, FDD 277.5, and FVD 257.4, beating Pyramidal Flow (FID 8.24, FDD 292.2, FVD 266.3) across all three metrics. When finetuning FLUX-dev, DFM yields a 29.7% FID reduction and 3.7% CLIP score increase over standard full finetuning at equal compute.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DFM outperforms all three baselines across nearly all metrics and cfg settings. Against Flow Matching (the single-stage baseline), DFM achieves 35.2% lower FDD on ImageNet-512px and 55.3% lower FDD on ImageNet-1024px (cfg=1.25), confirming that progressive decomposition substantially improves generation quality. Against Cascaded diffusion (separate models per stage), DFM wins on every metric for both image resolutions and Kinetics-700, with the largest gap on ImageNet-1024px FDD (181.7 vs. 221.9). Against Pyramidal Flow (the strongest progressive baseline), DFM leads on FID, FDD, and IS for ImageNet at both resolutions, and on FID, FDD, and FVD for Kinetics-700; the only exception noted is Kinetics-700 FDD at cfg=1.0 where Pyramidal Flow (353.8) slightly edges DFM (336.7) is actually still won by DFM, so DFM wins across the board. The paper also reports that DFM matches the best baseline FDD in roughly half the training iterations, indicating faster convergence to the target distribution.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Flow Matching (Lipman et al., Liu et al.) and the DiT architecture (Peebles & Xie) by introducing per-stage flow timesteps over a user-defined multi-scale decomposition (e.g., Laplacian pyramid), enabling progressive coarse-to-fine generation within a single model. It departs from prior progressive diffusion frameworks—cascaded models (Ho et al.), Pyramidal Flow (Jin et al.), f-DM, and RDM—which require separate models per stage, modified diffusion processes with renoising/upsampling transitions, or specialized samplers. The conceptual lineage also draws on spectral autoregression in autoregressive models (VAR, NFIG) and scale-equivariant autoencoders (Skorokhodov et al.), but applies these ideas within a unified Flow Matching formulation rather than a token-prediction or multi-model setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DFM offers a practically attractive simplification: progressive generation benefits (better structural fidelity, intermediate previews, faster convergence during finetuning) are achieved with a single model and minimal pipeline modifications, lowering the barrier to adopting coarse-to-fine strategies in production systems. The FLUX finetuning experiment (29.7% FID reduction over standard finetuning at equal compute) suggests the approach could accelerate adaptation of large pretrained generators to new domains. Open questions include whether the framework's advantages persist at much larger scales, how it interacts with other decomposition paradigms (wavelet, multiscale autoencoders), and whether the additional hyperparameters (stage probability, threshold, per-stage steps) can be further simplified or learned automatically.
