## Problem

Reinforcement fine-tuning (RFT) for LLMs suffers from severe sample inefficiency, with methods like GRPO requiring hundreds of GPU hours to process the same data repeatedly. Existing data-manipulation strategies (curriculum learning, sample selection) rely on model-agnostic difficulty metrics or expensive preprocessing via large models, ignoring how the target model itself perceives and learns from each data point. This paper asks whether a cheap, model-intrinsic signal can replace these heuristics to guide data scheduling.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained LLM and a training dataset of math or code problems, then performs a single pre-filling forward pass over all samples to extract final-layer token hidden states. From these, it computes two scalar angle-concentration scores per sample (intra-question and inter-segment cosine similarity), requiring under 10 minutes for ~7,000 samples on one A100. The outputs are a reordered dataset sorted by combined angle concentration, a per-iteration Gaussian sampling schedule whose mean shifts dynamically, and the resulting fine-tuned model.

## Method

GAIN-RL identifies angle concentration—the cosine similarity between token hidden states at the final layer—as a model-intrinsic signal that directly governs gradient Frobenius norm (derived in Eq. 4), meaning data with more concentrated angles produces stronger, more effective gradient updates. The framework has three components: (1) pre-training data reordering by descending combined C_intra + C_inter score, (2) per-iteration probabilistic sampling from a Gaussian distribution over the sorted data, and (3) epoch-wise update of the Gaussian mean μ_t using tanh-scaled batch accuracy and angle concentration to progressively shift focus from high-angle (easier) to low-angle (harder) samples. Theoretical analysis reveals three patterns—layer-wise (intra-then-inter concentration), epoch-wise (concentration rises over training), and data-wise (model learns high-angle samples first)—that jointly justify the scheduling strategy. GAIN-RL is plug-and-play, compatible with any RL algorithm (validated on both GRPO and PPO) and any model family, achieving 2.5×+ training speedup and surpassing full-data GRPO using only half the training set.

## Results

GAIN-RL(GRPO) achieves over 2.5× training speedup across all three model scales on math benchmarks, requiring only 70–80 iterations to match vanilla GRPO's 200-iteration performance (vs. 140–150 for ADARFT). On average accuracy at 200 iterations, GAIN-RL(GRPO) improves from 39.95% (GRPO) to 42.63% on Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B, from 26.8% to 28.5% on LLaMA3.2-3B, and from 42.89% to 46.33% on Qwen2.5-Math-7B. On code tasks, GAIN-RL(GRPO) raises the average from 26.7% to 28.8% with a 1.81× speedup. The method also generalizes to PPO, yielding 2.0–2.5× speedups on GSM8K, MATH, and AMC 23.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GAIN-RL(GRPO) consistently outperforms both vanilla GRPO and ADARFT(GRPO) on nearly all math benchmarks across three model scales. Against vanilla GRPO, GAIN-RL gains +2.68% average accuracy on Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B (42.63 vs. 39.95), +1.7% on LLaMA3.2-3B (28.5 vs. 26.8), and +3.44% on Qwen2.5-Math-7B (46.33 vs. 42.89), while requiring only 70–80 iterations to reach GRPO's 200-iteration level (2.5–2.86× speedup). Against ADARFT(GRPO), GAIN-RL achieves a larger speedup (2.5× vs. 1.33–1.43×) and higher final accuracy on most benchmarks, though ADARFT slightly edges out GAIN-RL on GSM8K for LLaMA3.2-3B (78.01 vs. 76.04). On code tasks (Table 2), GAIN-RL(GRPO) improves the average from 26.7% to 28.8% with a 1.81× speedup over GRPO; ADARFT is not comparable here due to missing difficulty coefficients. The method also generalizes to PPO (Table 3), where GAIN-RL(PPO) achieves 2.0–2.5× speedups and +2.8–3.2% accuracy gains over vanilla PPO on GSM8K, MATH, and AMC 23.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from existing data-centric RFT strategies (LIMO, S1 for selection; ADARFT for curriculum ordering) by replacing model-agnostic difficulty or quality heuristics with a model-intrinsic signal: the angular concentration of token hidden states at the final layer. The theoretical contribution—showing via Frobenius norm expansion that cosine similarity between hidden states directly scales the gradient norm—provides a principled justification for why this signal should drive data scheduling, something prior curriculum-learning-for-RL work lacked. Conceptually, GAIN-RL repurposes the curriculum learning paradigm but grounds the 'difficulty' ordering in the model's own representational geometry rather than external annotations or proxy models, and connects it to attention mechanics (sink attention) and neuron activation overlap.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 2.5× training acceleration and the finding that high-angle-biased half-data outperforms full-data vanilla GRPO are practically significant for RFT, which remains prohibitively expensive (e.g., ~240 GPU-hours for 100 GRPO steps on a 7B model). The signal is cheap to compute (single prefill pass, <10 min for 7K samples), making it a low-friction plug-in for existing RL pipelines. However, the theoretical grounding rests on an empirical orthogonality assumption (W_q W_k^T ≈ θI) that is visualized but not formally proven, and experiments are limited to math/code tasks at ≤7B scale; whether the angle concentration pattern generalizes to broader domains, larger models, or non-RL fine-tuning settings remains open. The optimal weighting between intra- and inter-segment signals also appears to require per-model tuning.
