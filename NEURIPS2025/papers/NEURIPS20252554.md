## Problem

Existing VLM evaluations rely almost exclusively on positive-control tests using real-world videos that resemble training distributions, leaving it unclear whether models genuinely reason about visual cues or merely exploit memorized language priors and visual-language correlations. This paper introduces negative-control tests—synthetic videos depicting physically impossible or logically inconsistent scenarios—to probe whether VLMs can detect violations of physics, causality, and commonsense rather than defaulting to linguistic shortcuts.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252554/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes synthetic video clips (averaging 96 frames at ~23 FPS, ~5.3 seconds, 1042×588 resolution) generated by models such as Veo2, Sora, Kling, and Runway, each containing deliberate physical or commonsense abnormalities, paired with a natural-language question. The model produces a free-form text answer, which is scored for correctness by an LLM-as-a-Judge (GPT-4o-mini). Questions are organized into four categories—Alignment, Physics, Spatial-temporal Consistency, and Commonsense—each with sub-categories, and are further classified as commonsense-only, counterintuitive, or critical-thinking types to isolate video-grounded reasoning from language-prior reliance.

## Method

VideoHallu is constructed in two stages: five human experts design 141 adversarial prompts that elicit physical or commonsense violations, which are rendered into 987 videos across seven generation models; experts then manually review each video and craft QA pairs that maximize the gap between VLM outputs and human perception while ensuring the LLM backbone alone would agree with humans (formalized in Eq. 4). For mitigation, the paper compares SFT and GRPO post-training on a mixture of 800 synthetic and 2,000 real-world QA pairs, using ROUGE-1/2/L average as the GRPO reward. GRPO consistently outperforms SFT on out-of-distribution synthetic video understanding, and the mixed real+synthetic training configuration yields the best overall accuracy (57.69% for Qwen2.5-VL-7B) without degrading performance on real-world benchmarks (MVBench, MMVU), indicating that preference-based RL cultivates transferable visual reasoning rather than surface-level pattern memorization.

## Results

On VideoHallu, all 17 evaluated SOTA VLMs achieve below 55% overall accuracy, only marginally above the 50% random baseline. The best-performing model, Video-R1, reaches 53.64% overall, while Qwen2.5-VL-7B (52.98%) and Qwen2.5-VL-32B (52.66%) are close behind; closed-source models GPT-4o-mini (50.36%) and Gemini-2.5-Pro (49.18%) trail further. After GRPO fine-tuning with combined real and synthetic data, Qwen2.5-VL-7B improves to 57.69% overall on VideoHallu while maintaining real-world performance (MMVU 61.3%, MVBench 70.1%), confirming that synthetic video training does not degrade standard video understanding.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Video-R1 (53.64%) edges out its base model Qwen2.5-VL-7B (52.98%) by only 0.66 points overall, and actually underperforms on Commonsense (38.75% vs. 47.50%), indicating that RL-based CoT training on real-world videos does not transfer to synthetic video abnormality detection. VideoChat-R1 (48.85%) performs worse than Qwen2.5-VL-7B across all categories, confirming that R1-style CoT prompting amplifies language-prior bias on counterintuitive content. Among closed-source models, GPT-4o-mini (50.36%) slightly outperforms Gemini-2.5-Pro (49.18%) and Gemini-2.0-Flash (49.97%), but all remain well below the open-source Video-R1. Smaller models (<7B) show a stark performance gap: SmolVLM-3B achieves only 16.13% overall, roughly one-third of the best model. In the fine-tuning comparison (Table 2), GRPO with combined real+synthetic data (57.69%) outperforms SFT (51.02%) by 6.67 points on Qwen2.5-VL-7B, and also beats real-data-only GRPO (53.05%) and synthetic-only GRPO (55.41%), demonstrating that the mixed training strategy is essential for robust synthetic video reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the VLM hallucination evaluation literature (VideoHallucer, EventHallusion, HAVEN), which has focused on real-world videos, by introducing a 'negative-control' paradigm: synthetic videos with deliberate physics and commonsense violations generated by models like Veo2, Sora, and Kling. For post-training, it builds directly on the GRPO framework from DeepSeek-R1 and applies it in the video domain alongside Video-R1 and VideoChat-R1, but departs from those works by incorporating synthetic counterfactual data into the training mixture rather than relying solely on real-world video QA. The key conceptual departure is treating video understanding evaluation as requiring both positive-control (real-world) and negative-control (physically impossible) tests, arguing that true visual understanding must perform comparably on both.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that SOTA VLMs (GPT-4o, Gemini-2.5-Pro) achieve only ~50% accuracy—barely above random—on physically inconsistent synthetic videos, and that R1-style CoT training on real-world data can actually *worsen* performance on counterintuitive content, challenges the assumption that RL-based reasoning improves general visual understanding. The paper demonstrates that GRPO with a mixed real+synthetic training set improves synthetic video reasoning without degrading real-world benchmarks (MVBench, MMVU), offering a concrete path toward more physically grounded VLMs. Open questions include scalability beyond 3K QA pairs, the domain gap between synthetic and real visual complexity, and whether the findings generalize to longer-horizon causal interactions and rarer physical edge cases.
