## Problem

Existing mathematical benchmarks for LLMs predominantly target problems with exact analytical solutions or formal proofs, leaving a critical gap in evaluating the approximation-based reasoning—such as asymptotic expansions, boundary-layer analysis, and WKB methods—that is central to applied science and engineering. HARDMath2 addresses this by introducing 211 original graduate-level applied mathematics problems that require multi-step approximation techniques rather than closed-form answers.

## Input / Output

Each problem is delivered to the model as a standardized LaTeX prompt with a type-specific task instruction (e.g., 'Find a uniformly valid approximation…') and a mandatory suffix requiring the final answer in a \boxed{} environment. The model's boxed LaTeX response is parsed into a SymPy expression and numerically evaluated at a random point in the domain (all free variables assigned values between 1 and 2), then compared against the student-verified ground-truth solution using a closeness threshold. Responses that fail to parse are excluded from metrics, so reported pass@1 rates reflect only problems where the model produced a gradable answer.

## Method

The benchmark is constructed through a pedagogical pipeline embedded in a Harvard graduate applied mathematics course: students write and solve original problems weekly, verify solutions numerically in Colab notebooks, peer-review each other's submissions on a shared Google Sheet, and iteratively increase difficulty by testing candidate problems against LLMs and exploiting identified failure modes (structural obfuscation of canonical equations, vanishing nonlinear terms, initial-condition traps). Evaluation is fully automated via a custom Python parser that converts LaTeX to SymPy expressions using regex-based transformation rules (handling special functions like incomplete Beta functions, nested integrals, and trigonometric powers), then performs numerical comparison at random evaluation points. A secondary LLM-as-a-judge evaluation with detailed 5-point rubrics per problem type provides partial-credit scores that capture reasoning quality beyond binary pass/fail.

## Results

On HARDMath2 (211 applied math problems), the best-performing model is Gemini 2.5 Flash Thinking at 60.1% pass@1 overall, followed by Gemini 2.5 Pro Preview (57.7%) and o3 (52.5%). Performance varies dramatically by problem type: integrals are the most tractable (71–79% for top models), while nonlinear PDEs are the hardest (only 35–45% for the best models). Non-reasoning models perform poorly, with GPT-4o at just 4.2% and GPT-4.1 at 7.4%, highlighting that explicit reasoning chains are critical for applied mathematics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 12 LLMs (closed-source: GPT-4o, GPT-4.1, o1, o3, o3-mini, o4-mini, Gemini 2.0 Flash, Gemini 2.5 Flash Thinking, Gemini 2.5 Pro Preview, Claude 3.7 Sonnet; open-source: DeepSeek V3, Llama 4 Maverick) on the same 211 problems. The 'thinking' models dominate: Gemini 2.5 Flash Thinking (60.1%) and o3 (52.5%) outperform non-reasoning models like GPT-4o (4.2%) and GPT-4.1 (7.4%) by over 50 percentage points overall. Within the top tier, Gemini 2.5 Flash Thinking edges out Gemini 2.5 Pro Preview (60.1% vs. 57.7%), partly because the Pro model more frequently fails to follow the \boxed{} formatting instruction. On boundary layers, Gemini 2.5 Flash Thinking leads at 77.8%, while on nonlinear PDEs the gap narrows (45.0% for Gemini 2.5 Flash Thinking vs. 44.3% for Gemini 2.5 Pro). No model exceeds 50% on asymptotic series or nonlinear PDEs, confirming that these remain the hardest categories for current LLMs.

## Relation to Prior Work

HARDMath2 is a direct successor to HARDMath (Fan et al., 2025), extending it from more elementary applied math topics to graduate-level material requiring sophisticated approximation techniques (boundary-layer analysis, WKB methods, asymptotic expansions of nonlinear PDEs). It departs from the dominant paradigm in mathematical benchmarks—FrontierMath, HLE, GHOSTS, ARB—which target formal or exact-solution problems, by focusing on approximation-based reasoning where no closed-form answer exists. The student-driven, LLM-interactive problem creation process is a novel departure from both expert-curated (FrontierMath) and crowdsourced (HLE) benchmark construction, and the automated SymPy-based numerical evaluation distinguishes it from LLM-as-a-judge or manual grading approaches used in prior work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This benchmark fills a genuine gap in evaluating LLMs on the approximation-based mathematical reasoning that underpins applied science and engineering, showing that even the strongest models (Gemini 2.5 Flash Thinking at 60.1%, o3 at 52.5%) remain far from mastery. The student-identified failure-mode exploitation strategies (structural obfuscation of canonical equations, vanishing nonlinear terms, initial-condition application failures) provide actionable insight into specific reasoning gaps. Limitations include the modest size (211 problems), single-course provenance, pass@1-only reporting without error bars, and the exclusion of unparsable responses which may undercount true model capability. The pedagogical framework—using LLMs as both learning tools and test subjects within a course—suggests a replicable template for generating challenging benchmarks in other quantitative domains.
