## Problem

Extending analytical policy gradient (APG) methods to visual control is challenging because computing Jacobians over high-dimensional pixel inputs is both memory- and computationally expensive, and obtaining high-quality differentiable rendering is non-trivial. Existing approaches either train separate differentiable renderers or rely on hand-engineered low-dimensional visual features, both of which demand considerable engineering effort and limit generalizability across tasks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251730/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The policy consumes three consecutive 84×84 RGB image frames (stacked to capture temporal cues like velocity), encoded by a 4-layer convolutional network into a latent representation before feeding an MLP actor that outputs continuous Gaussian actions. Notably, the policy is entirely state-agnostic during training and deployment, while the critic is trained in the low-dimensional internal state space for efficiency. All experiments use GPU-parallelized simulation (ManiSkill3 or PyTorch3D) with 64 concurrent environments and short-horizon rollouts of length 32.

## Method

The core contribution is the Decoupled Policy Gradient (DPG), which decomposes the full analytical policy gradient into a decoupled term and a control-regularization bias by dropping all terms involving the observation Jacobian d(o_t)/d(s_t), thereby eliminating the need to backpropagate through the rendering pipeline. Theorem 1 formally shows the DPG equals the negative gradient of a behavior-cloning loss against open-loop trajectory-optimized actions, revealing a direct connection between closed-loop policy learning and open-loop trajectory optimization. Built on the SHAC short-horizon actor-critic framework, the method uses parallel differentiable simulation for forward rollouts and first-order backpropagation only through dynamics and the policy network (not rendering), yielding 2–3× faster backward passes and 3–5× lower peak memory than differentiable-rendering baselines. The decoupling also attenuates the policy gradient norm, which the authors show empirically stabilizes optimization in high-dimensional visual spaces where the full APG gradient norm can diverge beyond 10^15.

## Results

D.Va achieves a 4× improvement in final return over baselines on the humanoid locomotion task and learns a stable running policy within 4 hours on a single RTX 4080 GPU. Backward simulation is 2–3× faster than SHAC with differentiable rendering (e.g., 0.21 s vs. 0.58 s per episode on Humanoid), and peak memory usage is 3–5× lower. Across all four main tasks (Cartpole, Hopper, Ant, Humanoid), D.Va consistently outperforms model-free RL (DrQ-v2, CURL) and model-based RL (DreamerV3) in both wall-clock training time and final return, while matching or exceeding state-to-visual distillation and SHAC with differentiable rendering.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the three RL baselines (DrQ-v2, CURL, DreamerV3), D.Va achieves substantially lower wall-clock training time and higher final returns on all four tasks; the gap is largest on Humanoid, where RL methods plateau well below D.Va's final return. Against state-to-visual distillation (Mu et al., 2025), D.Va matches performance on the three simpler tasks (Cartpole, Hopper, Ant) but significantly outperforms it on Humanoid, where the distilled policy plateaus at a much lower return. Against SHAC with differentiable rendering (PyTorch3D), D.Va matches performance on the 2D tasks (Cartpole, Hopper) but clearly outperforms it on the 3D tasks (Ant, Humanoid), where SHAC's gradient norms blow up beyond 10¹⁵ and it fails to learn effective locomotion. D.Va also requires 3–5× less peak memory than SHAC with differentiable rendering and avoids the engineering overhead of maintaining a differentiable renderer.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Short-Horizon Actor-Critic (SHAC) framework for analytical policy gradients with differentiable simulation to the visual setting, where prior work either required training separate differentiable renderers (Wiedemann et al., Liu et al.) or relied on hand-engineered low-dimensional visual features (Heeg et al., Luo et al.). The key departure is the decoupled policy gradient (DPG), which simply detaches the rendered observation from the computation graph, eliminating the need for differentiable rendering entirely. The authors provide a formal proof (Theorem 1) showing DPG is equivalent to behavior cloning from open-loop trajectory optimization, revealing a conceptual bridge between trajectory optimization and closed-loop policy learning that prior APG work had not articulated.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: training a humanoid running policy from pixels in 4 hours on a single RTX 4080 GPU, with 4× final return improvement over state-to-visual distillation on the hardest task, and 3–5× memory savings over differentiable-rendering baselines. The method's independence from differentiable rendering software makes it immediately integrable into existing simulation ecosystems (ManiSkill, Genesis, MuJoCo), lowering the barrier for visual policy learning. Key limitations remain: the approach requires differentiable dynamics and reward functions, and sim-to-real transfer is unaddressed. An open question is whether the gradient-norm-attenuation benefit of decoupling generalizes to other high-dimensional observation spaces beyond rendered images (e.g., point clouds, LiDAR).
