## Problem

Large language models often possess latent capabilities that remain dormant unless explicitly elicited through fine-tuning or prompting, creating an 'elicitation gap' between a model's true ceiling and its demonstrated performance. While prior scaling-law work varies compute, data, or model size, no framework quantifies the minimal information a model needs to surface a capability it already contains, making it difficult to distinguish elicitation (unlocking existing knowledge) from teaching (learning something new).

## Input / Output

The method takes a pretrained base model (Llama 3.2 1B/3B, Llama 3.1 8B, or Qwen2.5-1.5B), a task training set, and a scalar parameter budget k specifying how many LoRA adapter weights to train. It produces a sparsely fine-tuned model, an elicitation frontier curve mapping log(k) to task performance (accuracy or loss), and a prequential MDL compression value in bits. Unusually, the k trainable parameters are chosen by uniform random sampling across all LoRA modules with no structural prior, and for k < 20 the optimization switches from gradient descent to Bayesian optimization directly over the weight values to handle training instabilities at extreme sparsity.

## Method

The paper recasts elicitation as an information-constrained fine-tuning problem: a LoRA adapter is attached to the frozen base model, and exactly k parameters are selected uniformly at random from the adapter matrices to be trainable (all others frozen at initialization). The model is trained to convergence on the task dataset, and performance on held-out data is recorded for budgets spanning 1 to full fine-tuning, yielding an empirical Pareto frontier. A generalized logistic curve is fitted to performance versus log(k), revealing a consistent S-shaped scaling across tasks, domains, model sizes, and families. To provide an information-theoretic interpretation, the authors compute prequential MDL compression (first-epoch cross-entropy under sequential label encoding) as a function of k, showing that rapid MDL reduction at small k signals a latent capability being unlocked, while flat MDL until large k indicates the capability must be learned from scratch. Comparing pretrained versus randomly initialized models on the same tasks confirms that eliciting an existing capability requires orders of magnitude fewer parameters than teaching it anew.

## Results

Training as few as 10–100 randomly selected LoRA parameters recovers 50% of the zero-shot-to-full-fine-tune performance gap across multiple-choice tasks (e.g., 39 params for Llama-3.2-1B on GSM-8K-CoT-Choice, 98 params for Llama-3.1-8B on ARC-Challenge), while 1,000–10,000 parameters recover 90–95% of the gap. On generative tasks, ~1,000 parameters suffice for 50% gap closure on Alpaca-Eval, and ~10,000–20,000 parameters achieve 50% gap closure on AMC24 reasoning. The relationship between trained parameter count and performance follows a logistic curve in log-parameter space, generalizing across task formats, domains, model sizes (1B–8B), and model families (Llama 3, Qwen2.5). For randomly initialized (unpretrained) models, achieving the same 50% gap closure on TinyStories requires over seven orders of magnitude more parameters than for pretrained models, sharply distinguishing elicitation from teaching.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against other PEFT methods in a head-to-head table; instead, it positions random-parameter LoRA fine-tuning as an extreme lower bound on parameter efficiency, far below standard LoRA (which trains all adapter weights) and other PEFT approaches. The primary baselines are zero-shot accuracy (performance floor), 8-shot in-context learning, full fine-tuning (performance ceiling), a task-specific classifier head, and a linear probe on middle-layer representations. Random-parameter fine-tuning with ~30–100 parameters matches or exceeds the classifier head and linear probe baselines on GSM-8K-CoT-Choice while training orders of magnitude fewer parameters than full fine-tuning (~10⁹). The most striking comparison is between pretrained and randomly initialized Llama-3.2-1B models: on TinyStories, the pretrained model achieves 50% gap closure with ~100 parameters while the randomly initialized model requires over 10⁷ parameters (a >7-order-of-magnitude gap), and on Lichess chess puzzles the gap narrows to less than an order of magnitude, suggesting the task requires genuine teaching for both variants. MDL compression analysis further confirms that pretrained models compress task labels by hundreds of bits with very few parameters (indicating latent capability), whereas randomly initialized models show negligible compression until parameter counts become large.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) literature to its extreme lower bound, pushing from the thousands-to-millions of parameters used by LoRA and adapters down to 10–100 randomly selected weights, and departs from the Lottery Ticket Hypothesis by showing that no structured search is needed—uniform random selection suffices. Conceptually, it builds on Rissanen's Minimum Description Length principle and Perez et al.'s Rissanen Data Analysis to recast elicitation as an information-constrained fine-tuning problem, where the trainable parameter count serves as a direct proxy for the information budget. Unlike standard scaling laws that vary compute, data, or model size, this work holds those fixed and varies only the number of trainable parameters, introducing the 'elicitation frontier' as a new empirical object.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a handful of random parameters can unlock most of a model's latent capability has direct safety implications: benchmarks relying on zero-shot or prompt-based evaluation may substantially underestimate a model's true ceiling, and the elicitation frontier offers a quantitative diagnostic for distinguishing capabilities a model already possesses from those it must learn. The consistent logistic-in-log-parameter scaling across tasks, domains, and model families suggests a potentially universal predictive tool for capability forecasting. Open questions include whether the pattern holds for more complex, production-grade tasks, whether structured parameter selection could further reduce the budget, and whether the first-epoch MDL proxy captures the full information-theoretic picture.
