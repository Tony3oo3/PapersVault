## Problem

Existing VQA benchmarks rely on high-quality, third-person imagery with clear subjects, failing to capture the visual challenges inherent to wearable devices such as smart glasses. Egocentric images are typically occluded, poorly lit, unzoomed, or blurry, and users pose questions from a first-person perspective that no prior benchmark has systematically evaluated. WearVQA fills this gap as the first VQA benchmark purpose-built for the wearables context.

## Input / Output

Each instance pairs an egocentric RGB image (captured via RayBan Meta smart glasses at ~720×1280 or ~3024×4032 resolution) with a first-person visual question, and the expected output is a concise, single-sentence answer. Questions are constrained to be answerable from the image and common sense alone (no external knowledge), short-form, and unambiguous. The evaluation judges responses against five criteria—factual correctness, relevance, completeness, egocentric phrasing, and conciseness—using a GPT-4o-based LLM judge validated at 96% agreement with human annotators.

## Method

The benchmark comprises 2,520 image-question-answer triples spanning 7 image domains, 10 cognitive task types (from basic recognition to multi-step reasoning, math, and spatial inference), and 6 wearables-specific quality issues (blurred, cut-off, low-light, unzoomed, occluded, rotated), with 54% of images exhibiting at least one issue. Images were captured by paid annotators using RayBan Meta smart glasses during daily activities, supplemented by video frame sampling to introduce motion blur. Questions were authored to be egocentric and then filtered to remove any that all evaluated SOTA models answered correctly, ensuring a challenging benchmark; 60% of remaining questions require reasoning. Evaluation uses a GPT-4o LLM-as-a-judge prompt that scores each response on five criteria, achieving 96% accuracy and 96.8% F1 against human labels. The dataset is split into a 1,500-question public test set and a 1,000-question private test set, and ten proprietary and open-source MM-LLMs are benchmarked, with the best (GPT-4o) reaching only 51.5% overall accuracy and dropping to 45.5% on degraded images.

## Results

On the WearVQA benchmark (2,520 image-question-answer triples), model QA accuracy ranges from 23.9% (Phi-4-mini) to 51.5% (GPT-4o), confirming the benchmark's difficulty. GPT-4o leads proprietary models at 51.5% overall (58.5% on high-quality images, 45.5% on low-quality), while Qwen2.5-VL-72B is the top open-source model at 45.1%. All models suffer a 5.6–16.2 percentage-point drop on images with quality issues (unzoomed, occluded, blurred, etc.), with Claude-3.7-sonnet showing the largest degradation (−16.2 pp) and Phi-4-mini the smallest (−5.6 pp).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4o outperforms all other models on WearVQA, achieving 51.5% overall accuracy, a 6.1 pp lead over Gemini-1.5-Pro (45.4%) and 6.4 pp over the best open-source model Qwen2.5-VL (45.1%). Among open-source models, Qwen2.5-VL-72B (45.1%) edges out Llama-4-Maverick (42.4%) and Llama-4-Scout (41.5%), while the smaller Llama-3.2v-90B (37.7%) and Pixtral-12B (25.8%) trail significantly. Claude-3.7-sonnet, despite its 175B parameter count, underperforms at 33.9% overall and shows the steepest quality degradation on low-quality images (−16.2 pp), suggesting particular vulnerability to egocentric image artifacts. On specific question types, Llama-4-Maverick distinguishes itself in Math (40.0% vs. GPT-4o's 30.5%) and Object Counting (37.5%), while GPT-4o dominates How-to/Purpose (69.4%) and Image Reasoning (59.0%). The smallest models (Phi-4-mini at 23.9%, Molmo at 25.7%) remain far below the 50% threshold, indicating that current compact architectures are not yet viable for wearable VQA.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing the first VQA benchmark purpose-built for wearable-device contexts, departing from prior benchmarks (VQA v2, OK-VQA, MMMU, MMBench) that rely on high-quality, third-person imagery and from egocentric benchmarks (VizWiz, EgoVQA) that are either complexity-limited or video-centric. Rather than extending a specific method, WearVQA repurposes the standard VQA formulation under a new set of realistic constraints: egocentric capture geometry, six types of image-quality degradation (unzoomed, occluded, blurred, rotated, cut-off, low-light), and question types grounded in practical wearable interactions. The LLM-as-a-judge evaluation framework builds on the broader trend of using LLMs for scalable automated evaluation, validated here at 96% agreement with human annotators.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

WearVQA provides the field with a concrete, reproducible testbed for evaluating how well current MM-LLMs handle the messy visual inputs that wearable users actually produce, and its results (24–52% accuracy even for GPT-4o) quantify a substantial gap between lab-benchmark performance and real-world wearable readiness. The benchmark identifies actionable failure modes—unzoomed small-text recognition, occlusion, object counting, and spatial reasoning—that point to specific research directions. Key limitations remain: the benchmark is English-only, restricted to static single images (no video or temporal reasoning), and excludes questions requiring external knowledge, leaving MM-RAG for wearables and multilingual evaluation as open problems the authors explicitly flag for future work.
