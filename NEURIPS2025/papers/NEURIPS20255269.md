## Problem

Reasoning models have shown strong gains in mathematics and programming, but it remains unclear whether they can be post-trained for scientific domains like chemistry without additional domain pretraining, and whether they can match or exceed dedicated domain-specific models while using substantially less data. This paper tackles that gap by demonstrating that a general-purpose LLM can be trained via reinforcement learning to perform sophisticated chemical reasoning tasks—molecular design, retrosynthesis, reaction prediction, and property modification—outperforming both frontier LLMs and specialized chemistry models.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255269/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes chemistry problems posed in natural language, where the problem statement embeds SMILES strings, IUPAC names, molecular formulas in Hill notation, or SMIRKS reaction strings as the chemical content. It produces a natural-language reasoning trace followed by a structured answer (a SMILES molecule, a SMIRKS reaction, or a multiple-choice selection), with four special tokens (<|think_start|>, <|think_end|>, <|answer_start|>, <|answer_end|>) demarcating the thought and answer boundaries. All 640,730 training problems are grounded in experimentally synthesized molecules from 13 public databases, and answers are verified by code (RDKit), trained ML models (KDESol, Molecular Transformer), or string matching against experimentally determined values.

## Method

Starting from Mistral-Small-24B-Instruct-2501, the training pipeline alternates between distillation and GRPO reinforcement learning across four stages: (1) SFT on 14,021 summarized DeepSeek-R1 reasoning traces to initialize the policy with in-distribution reasoning and SMILES output; (2) task-specific specialist GRPO on seven grouped task families using binary format×accuracy rewards; (3) distillation of all specialist models into a single generalist via SFT on collected correct responses; and (4) combined all-task GRPO with an online advantage-based curriculum that buffers recently non-trivial problems to reduce the fraction of zero-gradient batches without extra sampling cost. A key efficiency mechanism is problem rewriting via Gemini 2.5 Flash, which rephrases or adds distracting context to improve generalization to unseen phrasings. The approach requires no domain-specific pretraining and achieves competitive or superior performance to the Molecular Transformer on reaction prediction using only ~10% of the training data.

## Results

On reaction prediction, ether0 achieves 70% accuracy after 46,000 training examples, outperforming the published Molecular Transformer (64.1% on ~480K USPTO reactions) and a retrained MT on the same 60K subset (<30%). On multiple-choice questions, ether0 reaches 50.1% zero-shot accuracy (comparable to o1-2024-12-17's 52.2%) and surpasses all evaluated frontier models under one-shot in-context learning. Across all open-answer chemistry tasks (solubility editing, retrosynthesis, SMILES completion, molecular formula, etc.), ether0 achieves the highest accuracy among all compared models, including frontier LLMs and human experts (four chemists using ChemDraw). The safety alignment phase causes ether0 to refuse 80% of unsafe questions without meaningful degradation on measured tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Molecular Transformer (Schwaller et al., 2019), ether0 wins decisively on reaction prediction: 70% accuracy on the held-out set versus MT's 64.1% (trained on ~480K USPTO reactions), and ether0 surpasses a from-scratch retrained MT (<30%) after seeing only 10% of the available training data, demonstrating substantially higher data efficiency. Against OpenAI o1 (Jaech et al., 2024), ether0 is slightly below on MCQ zero-shot (50.1% vs. 52.2%) but surpasses it under one-shot in-context learning, and clearly outperforms it on all open-answer tasks. Against OpenAI o3/o4-mini (2025) and Claude 3.7 Sonnet (Anthropic, 2025), ether0 achieves the highest accuracy on every open-answer category in Figure 2, with the paper attributing the larger margins on OA tasks to their non-unique answers enabling more RL exploration without memorization. Against human experts (four chemists with ChemDraw), ether0 outperforms on molecular design tasks, though humans were not evaluated on receptor binding and scent tasks where structure–property relationships are largely unknown.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of RL-trained reasoning models (DeepSeek-R1, Kimi K1.5, DeepScaleR) from mathematics and programming into chemistry, where verifiable rewards (SMILES validity, ML model predictions, experimental MCQs) enable the same GRPO training paradigm. It departs from prior chemistry LLM work—which has focused on evaluating non-reasoning models on knowledge benchmarks or using CoT prompting—by directly post-training a 24B model on 640K experimentally-grounded problems with a multi-stage pipeline of specialist RL, distillation, and generalist RL. The advantage-based curriculum and problem-rewriting strategies are practical departures from vanilla GRPO and DAPO, addressing the high fraction of trivial groups that plagues chemistry tasks with non-unique answers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that domain-specific reasoning models can be trained data-efficiently (640K problems) without domain pretraining, outperforming both frontier LLMs and dedicated supervised models like Molecular Transformer on molecular design tasks. This establishes a template for applying RL-based reasoning to other scientific domains where solutions are verifiable but hard to generate (inverse problems in physics, biology, etc.). Key limitations include the absence of tool-calling, degradation of general chat capabilities, and restriction to organic small molecules; open questions include integrating reasoning with external chemistry tools and whether the approach transfers to inorganic or materials chemistry.
