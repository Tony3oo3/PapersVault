## Problem

While LLMs-as-Judges is the dominant paradigm for evaluating LLM alignment, no prior work has systematically examined whether LLMs that rank higher as generators also rank higher as evaluators (i.e., generation-evaluation consistency at the ranking level). This gap matters because if the two capabilities are strongly correlated, one could assess alignment by evaluating LLMs in their judge role using pre-annotated instances, eliminating the need for expensive LLM-judge calls on every new model's outputs.

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance consists of an instruction paired with two model outputs (one from a candidate LLM, one from a baseline system), and the LLM under test must output a binary preference judgment indicating which output is better. The benchmark is constructed once using a strong preference oracle (GPT-4o or Claude-3.7-Sonnet) to label 2,671 filtered Arena-Hard instances, after which any new LLM can be scored by simply comparing its pairwise predictions against the fixed labels—no LLM judge is invoked at evaluation time. Aggregated Cohen's Kappa scores across instances yield a per-model alignment score that is then correlated with ChatBot Arena human rankings.

## Method

The paper first formalizes GE-consistency as the Spearman rank correlation between LLMs' generation rankings (win rates against a GPT-4 baseline, judged by GPT-4o) and their evaluation rankings (Cohen's Kappa agreement with GPT-4o on pairwise preference tasks). A critical filtering step discards instances where the oracle gives inconsistent predictions when the output order is swapped, removing ambiguous or uncertain cases and boosting the measured correlation from 0.793 to 0.971 on Arena-Hard. Building on this high consistency, ALIGNEVAL is constructed as a fixed set of 2,671 pre-labeled pairwise comparison instances from Arena-Hard, annotated by GPT-4o (ALIGNEVAL-GPT) or Claude-3.7-Sonnet (ALIGNEVAL-CLAUDE), so that evaluating a new model requires only prompting it to judge each pair and comparing against the stored labels. Combined with IFEval (rule-based instruction-following), ALIGNEVAL+ achieves a 0.946 Spearman correlation with ChatBot Arena style-controlled rankings across 23 LLMs, matching or surpassing LLM-judge-based benchmarks like Arena-Hard and AlpacaEval while incurring zero proprietary API cost at evaluation time.

## Results

The paper demonstrates a Spearman's rank correlation of 0.971 (Arena-Hard) and 0.839 (AlpacaEval) between LLMs' generation and evaluation capability rankings when GPT-4o serves as the preference oracle, establishing strong GE-consistency. ALIGNEVAL-CLAUDE achieves a Spearman's correlation of 0.885 with ChatBot Arena rankings without IFEval, and 0.946 when combined with IFEval (ALIGNEVAL+), matching Arena-Hard (0.946) and approaching GPT4o-Judge (0.958). Crucially, ALIGNEVAL requires no LLM judge at evaluation time (API cost $0), compared to $10 for AlpacaEval and $20 for Arena-Hard.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ALIGNEVAL-CLAUDE (0.885 w/o IFEval) outperforms AlpacaEval (0.761), AlpacaEval-LC (0.746), MixEval (0.816), and HelpSteer3 (0.813) in correlating with ChatBot Arena rankings, while remaining competitive with Arena-Hard-SC (0.882) and slightly below Arena-Hard (0.905). ALIGNEVAL-GPT (0.856) trails Arena-Hard (0.905) and GPT4o-Judge (0.911) without IFEval, likely due to self-preference bias. When combined with IFEval (ALIGNEVAL+), both ALIGNEVAL variants reach 0.946, matching Arena-Hard exactly and approaching the GPT4o-Judge upper bound of 0.958. The key practical advantage is that ALIGNEVAL eliminates the need for an LLM judge at evaluation time (API cost $0 vs. $10–$20 for AlpacaEval/Arena-Hard), making it significantly more cost-efficient for iterative model evaluation. The paper also notes a self-preference bias where ALIGNEVAL-GPT ranks gpt-4o-2024-05-13 second and ALIGNEVAL-CLAUDE ranks claude-3.5-sonnet highest, though both consistently place gemini-2.0-flash in the top two.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two well-studied areas: LLM alignment evaluation (AlpacaEval, Arena-Hard, ChatBot Arena) and LLM-as-judge evaluation (RewardBench, MT-Bench). It builds directly on Arena-Hard's instruction set and pairwise comparison protocol, and on Zeng et al.'s (ICLR 2024) prompt template for LLM judging. Conceptually, it departs from prior generator-validator consistency (GV-consistency) work by West et al. and Li et al., which examined whether a *single* LLM is internally consistent between generating and validating; here the authors introduce GE-consistency, a *ranking-level* property asking whether LLMs that are better evaluators are also better generators across a set of models. This shift from instance-level to system-level consistency is the paper's key conceptual contribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is significant: if GE-consistency holds, one can assess a new LLM's alignment by testing its judging ability on a fixed, pre-annotated benchmark (ALIGNEVAL) without invoking an expensive LLM judge on every new output, reducing evaluation cost to near zero for proprietary API calls. However, the paper itself flags a critical vulnerability—fine-tuning a model to be a good judge could inflate its ALIGNEVAL score without improving actual alignment—making it a proxy rather than a ground-truth measure. Open questions include whether GE-consistency persists during training (implications for self-improvement loops), whether it generalizes beyond the specific conditions identified (strong oracle, challenging instructions, consistency filtering), and how to make the benchmark robust to adversarial gaming.
