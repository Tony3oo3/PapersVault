## Problem

Existing time series forecasting methods focus predominantly on improving the encoding of historical series while largely neglecting the decoding/forecasting phase, where point-wise MLPs independently predict each future time step. This independent prediction scheme lacks a global view of the series' overall fluctuations, cannot handle autocorrelation among predicted points, and struggles to express the mixture of long- and short-term dynamics that characterize real-world time series, particularly over long horizons.

## Input / Output

IF receives two inputs: (1) a channel-separated encoder representation X_enc ∈ R^{N×D} from an existing TSF model's encoder, and (2) the original input time series X ∈ R^{T×N} via a skip-connection, from which DFT-derived amplitude and phase features are extracted. Rather than directly outputting L time points, IF predicts amplitudes (R_{≥0}^{N×P}) and phases ([-π,π]^{N×P}) for P waves in a frequency pool (P ≥ L), then reconstructs the time signal via inverse DFT and crops to the target forecast length L. The conjugate symmetry of the DFT is exploited so only the first half of frequency components need to be predicted.

## Method

The Implicit Forecaster (IF) is a plug-and-play decoding module that replaces the output layers of existing TSF models by implicitly predicting constituent periodic waves rather than individual time points. It consists of an Amplitude Head (a two-layer MLP mapping concatenated encoder representation and input amplitudes to non-negative wave amplitudes) and a Phase Head (two MLPs predicting sine and cosine components via tanh, combined with atan2 to avoid the ±π phase discontinuity). A frequency pool of size P ≥ L is defined to capture ultra-low-frequency components that would otherwise suffer spectral leakage, and the predicted complex spectrum (Â · e^{jφ̂}) is converted back to the time domain via inverse DFT. The skip-connection provides the input series' own amplitude and phase as supplementary features, leveraging the assumption that dominant frequency characteristics persist into the future. IF is trained end-to-end with standard MSE loss and is compatible with any encoder architecture (Transformer, RNN, CNN, MLP-based).

## Results

Equipping 7 mainstream TSF models with the proposed Implicit Forecaster (IF) yields consistent improvements across 14 real-world datasets, with average MSE/MAE reductions of 30.4%/18.2% on the PEMS traffic benchmarks. The most dramatic single-dataset gain is iTransformer on PEMS07, where MSE drops from 0.884 to 0.149 (an 83% reduction). Across all datasets, the average MSE improvement (Promotion row) ranges from 3.5% (TimeXer) to 18.5% (iTransformer), confirming that IF's frequency-domain decoding is a broadly effective drop-in replacement for point-wise output layers.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is each baseline model against itself augmented with IF (a plug-in decoding module), rather than a head-to-head between competing architectures. IF improves all 7 baselines on the majority of 14 datasets, with the largest gains on PEMS traffic data where rapid, multi-scale fluctuations challenge point-wise decoders: TimesNet on PEMS08 drops from MSE 0.210 to 0.128, and iTransformer on PEMS07 improves from 0.884 to 0.149. PatchTST benefits consistently (e.g., ETTh1 MSE 0.533→0.498, PEMS03 0.252→0.159), while SOFTS shows the smallest average gain (~3.5% MSE) but still improves on most datasets. A few exceptions exist—SOFTS on Weather (MSE 0.236→0.261) and iTransformer on ETTh1 (MSE 0.535→0.535, no change)—indicating IF is not universally beneficial but is overwhelmingly positive. The ablation (Table 2) further confirms that IF outperforms Linear, MLP, and Transformer decoders by 20–27% in MSE on a standard Transformer encoder, isolating the frequency-domain decoding as the source of the gain rather than added nonlinearity or model capacity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in time series forecasting where nearly all architectural innovation targets the encoder (historical series → representation), and instead focuses on the underexplored decoding phase (representation → predicted series). It draws conceptual inspiration from decomposition forecasting (Autoformer, DLinear) and frequency-domain modeling (FEDformer, FreTS, FreDF), but repurposes DFT/iDFT not to model the input but to implicitly construct the output by predicting amplitudes and phases of constituent waves. The Implicit Forecaster is positioned as a plug-and-play replacement for the point-wise MLP output layers used by mainstream models (iTransformer, PatchTST, TimesNet, SegRNN, etc.), rather than a new end-to-end architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper makes a compelling case that the forecasting/decoding phase is a significant and underappreciated bottleneck: encoders in existing models already extract useful information, but point-wise MLP decoders fail to fully leverage it, particularly for long-term patterns and mixed long/short-term dynamics. The plug-and-play nature of IF (compatible with any encoder) makes it immediately practical, and the consistent gains across 7 architectures and 14 datasets validate the approach. Open questions include whether adaptive or learned frequency pool sizes could outperform the fixed spectrum, whether selective prediction of only dominant frequencies could reduce the O(P log P) cost, and whether wavelet-based extensions could capture localized (non-periodic) patterns that DFT-based decomposition inherently smooths over.
