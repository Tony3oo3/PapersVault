## Problem

Popular web-filtered pretraining datasets for LLMs (C4, RefinedWeb, FineWeb, DCLM-Baseline, etc.) are all derived from CommonCrawl with similar language filtering, heuristic quality filtering, and deduplication steps, yet it was unknown whether they carry measurable, dataset-specific fingerprints. This paper extends the dataset classification experiment from computer vision to text, asking whether these large, diverse corpora can be distinguished by a classifier, what features enable that distinguishability, and whether those fingerprints propagate through pretraining and finetuning.

## Input / Output

The classifier consumes raw text sequences tokenized with the GPT-NeoX tokenizer (vocab 50,432, context 2048) and outputs a dataset source label via a linear classification head. During training, sequences from the same dataset are concatenated into 2048-token windows and every sub-sequence within each window is classified with token-level cross-entropy; at test time, the full sequence is classified as a whole. For fingerprint propagation, the input is LLM-generated text (prompted with a single random token for base models, or with OpenHermes-2.5/Alpaca/UltraChat prompts for instruction-tuned models), and the output is the same source-dataset label, enabling mixture proportion estimation by aggregating classification votes over thousands of generated sequences.

## Method

The core classifier is a 160M-parameter autoregressive transformer (GPT-NeoX architecture, 12 layers, 768-dim embeddings, SwiGLU) pretrained compute-optimally on 3.2B C4 tokens, then adapted for N-way classification by replacing the language-modeling head with a linear layer to N classes and finetuning for one epoch with AdamW and cosine-annealed learning rate. The paper performs all possible 3-, 4-, and 5-way classification combinations across seven datasets grouped into three filtering categories, achieving 60–94% accuracy well above chance. To identify the features enabling distinguishability, the authors run rewrite experiments (GPT-4o-mini rephrasing with increasing format removal), format-stripping (regex removal of newlines, bullets, special characters), bag-of-words classification, and GPT-4o-mini-based thematic categorization into 13 content categories. Fingerprint propagation is demonstrated by classifying sequences generated by Falcon-7B (RefinedWeb), DCLM-7B (DCLM-Baseline), and FineWeb-Edu-1.8B with the original-data classifier (89.15% accuracy, only 0.61% below original-data accuracy), and mixture proportions are estimated by prompting SlimPajama 1.3B models with random tokens and aggregating classifier votes. For instruction-tuned LLMs, pairwise classification of OpenHermes-2.5 responses reveals that GPT-4o and DeepSeek-V3 are nearly indistinguishable (48.7% error), a gap that narrows with a stronger LLM2Vec classifier (30% error) and widens substantially when using Alpaca or UltraChat prompts instead.

## Results

The 160M transformer classifier achieves 74.76% accuracy on 3-way classification of C4, RefinedWeb, and FineWeb (vs. 33.3% chance), and up to 94.12% when DCLM-Baseline is included in the combination. Humans achieve only 63% on binary C4 vs. FineWeb classification, compared to 87.37% for the 160M classifier. Fingerprint propagation is confirmed: a classifier trained on original data achieves 89.15% accuracy on sequences generated by LLMs trained on those datasets (vs. 89.76% on original data). For instruction-finetuned LLMs, GPT-4o and DeepSeek-V3 are nearly indistinguishable (48.7% error with the 160M classifier; 30% with LLM2Vec), while all other model pairs are well separated.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's 160M autoregressive transformer is compared against three alternative classifiers. Against BERT (Devlin et al., NAACL 2019), the two are relatively similar in performance: BERT initially achieves slightly lower accuracy but eventually reaches a marginally higher accuracy, with the difference attributed to BERT processing each sequence only once versus the autoregressive model's per-subsequence loss. Against FastText (Joulin et al., EACL 2016), the transformer-based classifier significantly outperforms FastText, though at substantially higher compute cost. Against LLM2Vec (BehnamGhader et al., COLM 2024), the 160M transformer is clearly outperformed: on the 3-way C4/FineWeb/RefinedWeb task with 80M tokens per dataset, LLM2Vec reaches 82% versus 73% for the 160M transformer, and on the GPT-4o vs. DeepSeek-V3 distinction, LLM2Vec reduces the error rate from 48.7% to 30%. The authors note that LLM2Vec's advantage comes at the cost of finetuning a Llama-8B model, making it significantly more expensive.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends the dataset classification experiment introduced by Torralba and Efros (CVPR 2011) for computer vision and recently revisited by Liu and He (ICLR 2025) for modern large-scale vision datasets, transplanting the same N-way source-identification framework to LLM pretraining text datasets. It departs from prior work in two key ways: (1) it analyzes fingerprints in web-filtered text corpora (C4, FineWeb, RefinedWeb, etc.) that share similar curation pipelines, and (2) it introduces the propagation analysis, showing that a classifier trained on raw data can identify the source dataset of LLM-generated text, enabling mixture-proportion estimation and finetuning-data inference. The work also connects tangentially to data-mixing optimization (Xie et al., Ge et al.) and training-data extraction (Carlini et al., Nasr et al.), but its core contribution is the fingerprinting framework rather than optimization or extraction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical, low-cost diagnostic tool for the LLM community: a small classifier can reveal undisclosed pretraining mixture proportions and potentially identify finetuning data sources (e.g., the GPT-4o/DeepSeek-V3 near-indistinguishability on OpenHermes prompts). The finding that fingerprints survive training with <1% accuracy loss means this approach generalizes to any model trained on identifiable data sources. Open questions include whether the method works for datasets that differ only in mixture proportions (acknowledged limitation), whether it extends to non-web data sources at scale, and whether the GPT-4o/DeepSeek-V3 observation holds under more controlled conditions. The cross-dataset perplexity results also quantify a practical cost of single-source pretraining that practitioners can act on.
