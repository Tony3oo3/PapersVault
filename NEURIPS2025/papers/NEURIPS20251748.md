## Problem

Neural simulation-based inference methods require large numbers of expensive simulator runs to train their conditional density estimators, making them impractical for computationally costly models (e.g., cosmological or nuclear fusion simulators). While multi-fidelity approaches exist for ABC, no theoretically grounded multilevel method has been developed for neural SBI that provably reduces training cost by leveraging cheaper, lower-fidelity simulators alongside expensive high-fidelity ones.

## Input / Output

The method requires a sequence of L+1 simulators (generators) G^0, ..., G^L with increasing cost and fidelity, a prior π, and observed data x_{1:m}^o. A critical requirement is seed-matching: the same random inputs u must be shared across fidelity levels so that f_φ^l and f_φ^{l-1} are evaluated on identical samples, enabling variance reduction through coupling. The output is a trained conditional density estimator (normalizing flow or mixture density network) that yields an approximate posterior π(θ|x_{1:m}^o) or surrogate likelihood q(x|θ) via a single forward pass, making inference amortised for new observations.

## Method

The core idea is to replace the standard Monte Carlo estimate of the negative log-likelihood training objective with a multilevel Monte Carlo (MLMC) estimator built from a telescoping sum over fidelity levels: ℓ(φ) = E[f_φ^0] + Σ_l E[f_φ^l − f_φ^{l−1}], where each difference term is estimated on seed-matched samples to exploit high correlation and reduce variance. Theorem 1 provides variance bounds showing that each level's contribution scales with the Sobolev norm ‖G^l − G^{l−1}‖_{W^{1,4}}, directly linking variance reduction to the accuracy gap between consecutive simulators. Theorem 2 derives the optimal allocation of samples n_0,...,n_L under a fixed computational budget, showing that more samples should be allocated to cheaper levels with larger fidelity gaps. For gradient-based optimisation, the authors address 'conflicting gradients' between consecutive telescoping terms via a two-step adjustment (Algorithm 1): rescaling opposing gradient components to comparable norms, then projecting them onto each other's normal planes when they conflict, stabilising training without sacrificing the MLMC variance advantage.

## Results

Across four experimental domains (g-and-k, Ornstein-Uhlenbeck, toggle-switch, CAMELS), ML-NLE and ML-NPE consistently outperform their single-fidelity counterparts at matched computational budgets. On the toggle-switch model, ML-NLE achieves lower MMD than NLE trained exclusively on low-, medium-, or high-fidelity data (n=12,060, 7,537, or 2,010 samples respectively). On CAMELS cosmological simulations with only n₁=20 high-fidelity runs, ML-NPE (with n₀=980 low-fidelity runs) improves both NLPD and empirical coverage over standard NPE, which produces overconfident posteriors. On the OU process, ML-NPE slightly outperforms TL-NPE at n₁=10 on both NLPD and KL divergence, while TL-NPE slightly edges out ML-NPE at n₁=100.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against standard NLE (Papamakarios et al., 2019), ML-NLE achieves lower KLD on the g-and-k distribution and lower MMD on the toggle-switch model, outperforming NLE trained on low-fidelity (n=12,060), medium-fidelity (n=7,537), or high-fidelity (n=2,010) data alone at the same total simulation cost. Against standard NPE (Papamakarios et al., 2016), ML-NPE yields lower NLPD and better-calibrated posteriors (slightly conservative vs. overconfident) on both the g-and-k and CAMELS experiments. Against TL-NPE (Krouglova et al., 2025) on the OU process, the comparison is close: ML-NPE wins slightly on both NLPD and KL divergence when only 10 high-fidelity samples are available, but TL-NPE wins slightly when 100 high-fidelity samples are used, suggesting the two methods are broadly competitive with ML-NPE having an edge in the very-low-budget regime.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper applies multilevel Monte Carlo (MLMC) variance reduction to the training objectives of neural simulation-based inference (SBI), specifically neural likelihood estimation (NLE) and neural posterior estimation (NPE). Prior multi-fidelity SBI work focused on ABC variants (Jasra et al. 2019, Prescott and Baker 2020/2021, Warne et al. 2022), while the closest neural SBI competitor—Krouglova et al. (2025)—used transfer learning without theoretical guarantees. The paper also adapts the gradient surgery technique of Liu et al. (2020) to handle the conflicting gradients that arise from the telescoping MLMC decomposition, a problem specific to this formulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The main practical impact is enabling SBI for simulators where high-fidelity runs are orders of magnitude more expensive than low-fidelity ones (e.g., cosmological hydrodynamic vs. N-body simulations), by showing that a small number of high-fidelity samples combined with many cheap ones can match or exceed the accuracy of using only high-fidelity data. The theoretical contribution (Theorems 1–2) provides the first variance bounds linking MLMC estimator quality to Sobolev norms of the generator differences, giving principled guidance on budget allocation across fidelity levels. Key limitations include the requirement for seed-matching between fidelity levels (which may not always be feasible) and a ~15–20% training-time overhead from gradient adjustments. Open questions include whether the approach extends cleanly to settings with very different parameter spaces across fidelity levels and to more than a handful of levels.
