## Problem

Control algorithms in production are always tuned by domain experts, yet research evaluates them only under default or ideal configurations, ignoring their inherent Tuning Potential. No systematic metric exists to quantify how much an algorithm can be improved through parameter optimization and logic-level restructuring, making it impossible to compare practical adaptability across algorithms or to treat tunability as an explicit design goal.

## Input / Output

Crucible consumes the source code of a control algorithm plus its execution logs (structured as state-action-result triplets) and a set of test environment configurations. It produces a modified algorithm (with both parameter and logic changes) and a formalized Tuning Potential score that is normalized across environments via a probe-algorithm-derived similarity metric, enabling cross-domain comparison. The agent's capability is parameterized by two budgets — the number of Bayesian optimization calls and the number of LLM reflection iterations — allowing simulation of developers with varying expertise levels.

## Method

Crucible employs an LLM-driven multi-level expert simulation agent that operates at two complementary levels: (1) parameter-level exploitation via Bayesian optimization encapsulated as a standardized tool interface, and (2) logic-level exploration where the LLM restructures algorithm code (adding control branches, modifying decision logic) based on domain knowledge injected through system prompts covering task description, optimization objectives, and environment characteristics. Historical modification records (rationale, action, result triplets) are fed back to the LLM to enable iterative learning. The key formalization is a Tuning Potential metric that computes a similarity-weighted average performance gain across all test environments, where similarity is derived from RMSE distance between normalized performance characteristic vectors (obtained by running a set of probe algorithms in each environment). This environment-normalized weighting ensures that gains in environments dissimilar to the algorithm's ideal setting are down-weighted, yielding a robust measure of intrinsic tunability independent of absolute performance scale.

## Results

In the real-world ABR deployment (Dash.js over WiFi), Crucible-tuned HYB and BBA achieve a QoE of 1.72, surpassing the RL-based Pensieve reference (1.66) and their original versions (1.40 and 1.56 respectively). In the classic Cart-Pole task, Crucible elevates the Bang-bang controller from a score of 34 to the optimal 500 (matching DQN) after a single LLM-driven logic modification, and PID from 34 to 500 after two iterations. Across simulated ABR benchmarks, Crucible's logic-level improvements yield up to 44.1% additional performance gain over pure Bayesian optimization on the Puffer dataset. The formalized Tuning Potential metric ranks HYB highest (0.068±0.117) among ABR algorithms, driven by its broader state-space representational capacity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against pure Bayesian optimization (Snoek et al., NeurIPS 2012), Crucible consistently achieves superior results by expanding the search space beyond hyperparameters to include logic-level modifications, with relative gains of up to 44.1% on the Puffer ABR dataset; without Bayesian fine-tuning, ~60% of scenarios show no improvement, but combining LLM logic changes with Bayesian optimization reduces this to ~20%. Against DQN (Mnih et al., Nature 2015) in the Cart-Pole benchmark, Crucible-tuned Bang-bang and PID controllers both reach the optimal score of 500, matching the RL reference that was previously considered the performance ceiling. Against Pensieve (Mao et al., SIGCOMM 2017) in the real-world ABR deployment, Crucible-tuned HYB and BBA (QoE 1.72) surpass Pensieve (QoE 1.66), while the less interpretable Pitree algorithm shows no improvement, confirming that Crucible's effectiveness correlates with algorithm comprehensibility.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the LLM-based human behavior simulation paradigm (Park et al. 2023, Aher et al. 2023) from social science and recommendation settings to simulate multi-level developer expertise for algorithm tuning, combining it with Bayesian optimization (Snoek et al. 2012) as a complementary tool. It extends conventional parameter sensitivity analysis (Hamby 1994) by incorporating logic-level structural modifications and formalizing a cross-environment 'Tuning Potential' metric that weights performance gains by environmental similarity. The concept of algorithm comprehensibility (Freitas 2014, Martens et al. 2011) is repurposed as a predictor of tunability, bridging interpretability research with practical algorithm design.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By establishing Tuning Potential as a quantifiable, comparable metric, this work provides a new design criterion for control algorithms that complements traditional performance and robustness evaluations. The finding that algorithm simplicity and comprehensibility—not sophistication—govern tunability challenges the prevailing assumption that more complex algorithms are inherently more adaptable. Key limitations include dependence on LLM capabilities (affecting reproducibility across model versions) and the inability to modify black-box algorithms, leaving open the question of how to assess potential for learned or opaque control policies. Future work could explore whether the metric generalizes to non-control domains or whether the LLM simulation can be replaced by more principled expert models.
