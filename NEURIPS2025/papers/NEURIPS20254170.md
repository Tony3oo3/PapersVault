## Problem

Existing LLM fingerprinting schemes are limited to a few hundred fingerprints before model utility degrades significantly, yet modern model-sharing ecosystems require orders of magnitude more fingerprints to reduce false discovery rates, resist inevitable fingerprint leakage, and defend against collusion attacks among model hosts. No prior work has systematically addressed scalability as a design criterion for fingerprinting.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254170/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a base LLM (e.g., Llama-3.1-8B-Base) and generates fingerprint pairs consisting of 16-token natural English keys (sampled at low temperature from Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct) and 1-token responses drawn via Perinucleus sampling from the base model's own distribution. At detection time, the owner prompts a suspicious model with a small number of fingerprint keys and checks whether the highest-probability output token matches the expected response, requiring only API access with no weight inspection.

## Method

The core contribution is Perinucleus sampling: rather than selecting responses uniformly at random (which forces large distributional shifts), the response token is sampled uniformly from the k tokens immediately outside the nucleus boundary (top t-percentile of the CDF) of the base model's next-token distribution, yielding responses that are 'sensible but uncommon' with moderate conditional probability. Fingerprint insertion uses supervised fine-tuning on key-response pairs regularized by a weight deviation penalty (weight averaging with λ_WA=0.75) and data mixing (β_DM=0.25) to prevent catastrophic forgetting. For collusion resistance, each fingerprint is independently assigned to a random subset of N model copies with probability p, and detection uses a scoring rule that accumulates matches per model; the paper proves that M = O(2^K · K^(K+1) · log(N/δ)) fingerprints guarantee identification of at least one colluding model with probability 1−δ. This design enables embedding 24,576 fingerprints into Llama-3.1-8B—two orders of magnitude more than prior schemes—while maintaining over 95% relative utility.

## Results

Perinucleus sampling enables embedding 24,576 fingerprints into Llama-3.1-8B with minimal utility degradation on OpenLLM—two orders of magnitude more than the ENGLISH-RANDOM baseline, which suffers significant performance drop after ~256 fingerprints. Across 10 models from 5 families (Llama-3.1, OLMo-2, Qwen-2.5, Mistral, Phi-3), relative performance remains above 95% even at 8,192 fingerprints. After 2 epochs of SFT on Alpaca, Perinucleus retains over 60% of 8,192 inserted fingerprints, substantially outperforming ENGLISH-RANDOM. For collusion defense with N=2,048 models, the proposed random-assignment scheme with p=0.243 achieves near-perfect detection when total fingerprints M > 2,048 (≈500 per model).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines from prior work: RANDOM (random token concatenation as key, random token as response) and ENGLISH-RANDOM (natural English sentence as key, random unrelated token as response), both from Xu et al. [1] and Russinovich & Salem [5]. Perinucleus decisively outperforms ENGLISH-RANDOM on Scalability: it sustains model utility up to 24,576 fingerprints whereas ENGLISH-RANDOM degrades significantly after ~256. On Persistence after SFT on Alpaca, Perinucleus retains >60% of 8,192 fingerprints while ENGLISH-RANDOM fingerprints are 'easily forgotten.' Against RANDOM, Perinucleus trades off on raw persistence (RANDOM keys are out-of-distribution and thus less affected by SFT) but is strictly superior on security: RANDOM keys are trivially detectable and filterable by adversarial hosts, making them impractical. The paper also shows that Perinucleus's lower Uniqueness (responses are 'sensible but uncommon' rather than fully random) does not inflate false positive rates, as confirmed by ROC curves (Fig. 9) showing M=5 fingerprints suffice for reliable detection.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM fingerprinting framework introduced by Xu et al. (2024) and Russinovich & Salem (2024), which fine-tune models on key-response pairs for ownership verification, by introducing Scalability as a first-class design criterion and proposing Perinucleus sampling—a direct adaptation of nucleus sampling (Holtzman et al., 2020) that draws responses from the edge of the nucleus rather than uniformly at random. The training regularization (weight deviation penalty, data mixing) is borrowed from the continual learning literature, and the collusion-resistance strategy with random fingerprint assignment to model subsets is a novel contribution not present in prior fingerprinting work. The paper positions itself as filling a gap: prior schemes optimized for Harmlessness and Persistence but were limited to a few hundred fingerprints before utility collapsed, making them inadequate for the security requirements of multi-host model sharing.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for open and semi-open model ecosystems: embedding 24,576 fingerprints (two orders of magnitude more than prior schemes) without measurable utility loss enables robust ownership verification, resilience to fingerprint leakage, and provable collusion resistance. The theoretical guarantee (Proposition 5.3) that detection requires O(2^K · K^(K+1) · log(N/δ)) fingerprints under K-way collusion makes the scalability requirement concrete rather than heuristic. Open questions include the interaction of multiple simultaneous attacks (e.g., SFT followed by collusion), adaptive attacks that exploit knowledge of the Perinucleus design, and whether the exponential-in-K bound is tight or merely a loose analysis artifact. The paper also notes that instruct-tuned models are more sensitive to fingerprint insertion, suggesting the approach may need further refinement for the most commonly deployed model variants.
