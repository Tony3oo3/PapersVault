## Problem

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models rely almost exclusively on visual and linguistic cues, making them brittle in contact-rich manipulation tasks (insertion, peeling, wiping) where fine-grained force feedback is essential—especially under occlusion or dynamic uncertainty. Existing approaches either ignore force entirely or naively concatenate it to state inputs, lacking a mechanism to adaptively route and fuse force information in a phase-aware manner across the different stages of physical interaction.

## Input / Output

The policy consumes two RGB camera streams (base at 1280×720 and wrist at 640×480, both 30 FPS, resized to 480×640), a 7-dimensional proprioceptive state (TCP Cartesian position, Euler orientation, and gripper width), a single-timestep 6-axis external wrench (3 force + 3 moment components in the world frame), and a natural-language instruction. It outputs an action chunk of H consecutive steps, each specifying target TCP pose (x, y, z, α, β, γ) and gripper width, generated iteratively via a conditional flow-matching denoising process rather than a single autoregressive step.

## Method

ForceVLA builds on the π0 architecture (PaliGemma/SigLIP VLM encoder + flow-matching action head) and introduces FVLMoE, a force-aware Mixture-of-Experts fusion module placed *after* the VLM to preserve pretrained visual-linguistic representations. The 6-axis force reading is linearly projected into a single D_model-dimensional token and appended to the VLM's output sequence; this combined sequence passes through a shared transformer encoder block for cross-modal interaction, then through a sparse MoE layer with 4 MLP experts and top-1 routing, where the gating network dynamically selects the most relevant expert per token based on task phase and interaction state. The fused features are projected to the action-expert dimensionality, and the final H_action tokens are injected via element-wise addition into the flow-matching denoising loop, directly modulating the predicted action trajectory at each denoising step. Ablations confirm that early fusion (injecting force before the VLM) collapses performance to 0%, while the proposed late MoE fusion achieves 80% success versus 60% for simple post-VLM concatenation, validating both the placement and the routing mechanism.

## Results

ForceVLA achieves an average task success rate of 60.5% across five contact-rich manipulation tasks (bottle pumping, plug insertion, USB insertion, whiteboard wiping, cucumber peeling), improving by 23.2 percentage points over the strongest baseline (π₀-base w/o F at 37.3%). On individual tasks, ForceVLA reaches up to 80% success (e.g., plug insertion, bottle pumping). On the cucumber peeling task, ForceVLA achieves the longest average peel length (14.12 cm) and requires the fewest strokes (7) to complete the task, compared to 13.17 cm / 10 strokes for π₀-base w/ F and 10.27 cm / 14 strokes for π₀-base w/o F.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ForceVLA outperforms all four baselines on the overall average success rate (60.5% vs. 37.3% for π₀-base w/o F, 40.2% for π₀-base w/ F, 31.0% for π₀-fast w/o F, and 14.2% for π₀-fast w/ F). The key finding is that simply concatenating force signals to the state input (π₀-base w/ F) yields only a modest 2.9-point gain over the no-force variant, whereas ForceVLA's FVLMoE fusion module delivers a 20.3-point improvement over the same baseline, demonstrating that *how* force is integrated matters far more than whether it is present. In generalization settings (Table 2), ForceVLA leads on 4 of 5 conditions, with the largest margin in Visual Occlusion (90% vs. 50–60% for baselines), confirming that force feedback compensates for degraded visual input. The only condition where a baseline matches or exceeds ForceVLA is Unstable Socket, where π₀-fast w/ F reaches 30% (vs. 20% for ForceVLA), though the authors attribute this to π₀-fast's pausing behavior rather than genuine force-aware control. In multi-task joint training (Table 5), ForceVLA achieves 67.5% average success versus 42.5% for π₀-base w/ F and 0% for both π₀-fast variants, further validating the FVLMoE architecture's capacity for concurrent skill learning.

## Relation to Prior Work

ForceVLA extends the π0 VLA flow-matching framework by treating 6-axis force-torque sensing as a first-class modality fused via a sparse Mixture-of-Experts module (FVLMoE), rather than the simple state-concatenation used in prior force-augmented VLA baselines. Architecturally, it draws on sparse MoE literature (Shazeer et al., Fedus et al.) and multimodal MoE (LIMOE) but applies the routing mechanism specifically to modality- and phase-aware fusion of visual-linguistic embeddings with real-time force tokens post-VLM. It departs from sim-to-real approaches like VTLA by adopting a real-world-first paradigm, and from static fusion methods like FuSe by introducing dynamic expert routing that adapts across task phases.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine gap in VLA models—poor performance under visual occlusion and dynamic contact uncertainty—by demonstrating that force-aware MoE routing yields a 23.2% average success improvement over strong π0-based baselines. However, the evidence base is narrow: five tasks on a single Flexiv Rizon arm, 244 trajectories, estimated (not directly measured) wrench, and no simulation component. Open questions include whether the FVLMoE architecture scales to more diverse tasks and embodiments, whether it transfers to lower-cost platforms with retrofitted sensors, and whether the observed expert specialization patterns generalize beyond the five evaluated tasks.
