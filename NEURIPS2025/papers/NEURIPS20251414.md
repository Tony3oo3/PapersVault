## Problem

Adaptive experimentation for ATE estimation has been well studied when the treatment can be directly assigned, but remains largely unexplored when treatment is voluntary and can only be encouraged through a binary instrument (noncompliance setting). No existing method combines semiparametric efficiency, multiply robust inference, and adaptive instrument assignment under endogenous treatment selection.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251414/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At each round t, the method observes covariates X_t, assigns a binary instrument Z_t via a data-driven policy π_t(X_t | H_{t-1}), and records the resulting treatment A_t and outcome Y_t. The output is a sequential ATE estimate τ̂_T^{AMRIV} computed as the average of influence-function scores, an adaptive allocation policy that approximates the variance-optimal rule, and asymptotically valid time-uniform confidence sequences enabling early stopping. The instrument assignment policy is treated as known (designed by the experimenter) rather than estimated, which is a key structural difference from direct-treatment adaptive designs.

## Method

The paper derives the semiparametric efficiency bound for ATE under arbitrary history-dependent instrument policies and shows the variance-optimal allocation generalizes Neyman allocation by balancing both outcome residual variance and compliance variability (Var(Y−Aδ(X)|Z=z,X)). AMRIV pairs an online policy that adaptively approximates this optimal allocation (with truncation for finite-sample stability) with a sequential plug-in estimator of the recentered efficient influence function from Wang and Tchetgen Tchetgen. Sequential cross-fitting (temporal folds) mitigates finite-sample bias in nuisance estimation, and a near-martingale decomposition—splitting the estimator into a true MDS plus an o_p(T^{-1/2}) remainder—yields asymptotic normality without Donsker conditions. The estimator is multiply robust: it remains consistent if either the compliance model δ^A(X) or the CATE model δ(X) is correctly estimated, even when outcome regressions are misspecified.

## Results

The paper evaluates AMRIV on synthetic (one-sided noncompliance, T=2000, 1000 trajectories) and semi-synthetic (TripAdvisor customer simulator) data, reporting three metrics: normalized MSE vs. an oracle benchmark (efficiency), MSE decay with sample size (consistency), and empirical 95% CI coverage. AMRIV approaches the oracle efficiency bound while AMRIV-NA retains a constant gap, and both converge at the parametric O_p(T^{-1/2}) rate. Only AMRIV and AMRIV-NA achieve nominal 95% coverage; A2IPW is biased and fails to converge, and DM-MS diverges under deliberate δ(X) misspecification while AMRIV-MS remains consistent.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary external baseline is A2IPW (Kato et al. [27]), a variance-optimal adaptive design for directly assigned treatments. AMRIV decisively outperforms A2IPW on all three metrics: A2IPW is biased and fails to converge to the true ATE because it does not correct for unobserved confounding in treatment selection, while AMRIV converges consistently. On efficiency (normalized MSE), AMRIV approaches the oracle benchmark, whereas A2IPW and the direct-method baseline (DM) show increasing normalized MSE with T. On coverage, only AMRIV and its non-adaptive variant achieve nominal 95% coverage; A2IPW and DM under-cover increasingly as T grows. The multiply-robust property is demonstrated by comparing misspecified variants: AMRIV-MS (with a flattened δ(X)) remains consistent, while DM-MS diverges, confirming the theoretical guarantee that consistency holds if either δ(X) or δ^A(X) is correctly estimated.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Wang and Tchetgen Tchetgen (2018) semiparametric framework for ATE estimation under unconfounded compliance from a static to an adaptive setting, deriving the efficiency bound and optimal allocation policy for arbitrary history-dependent instrument-assignment rules. It integrates the adaptive experimentation toolkit developed for directly-assigned treatments—Neyman allocation, sequential cross-fitting, policy truncation, and time-uniform confidence sequences (Cook et al. 2024; Kato et al. 2020; Waudby-Smith et al. 2024)—into the IV/noncompliance regime. The key departure from prior adaptive IV work (Chandak et al. 2023; Zhao et al. 2024) is the focus on semiparametrically efficient point estimation of the ATE with multiply robust inference, rather than prediction accuracy or policy learning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first estimator that is simultaneously semiparametrically efficient and multiply robust for ATE estimation under adaptive binary-instrument assignment, filling a gap that affects many real-world experiments (clinical trials with physician recommendations, online platform nudges) where treatment cannot be directly enforced. The optimal allocation rule's dependence on both outcome noise and compliance variability generalizes classical Neyman allocation in a non-trivial way, and the adaptive setting yields a stronger robustness guarantee than the static counterpart (consistency under misspecification of outcome regressions as long as either δ or δ^A is consistent). Open questions include the practical difficulty of verifying the unconfounded compliance assumption (Assumption 2), the behavior under its violations (where the estimand shifts to ACLATE but no efficient adaptive estimator is developed), and whether the per-round nuisance re-estimation is computationally feasible at the scale of large online platforms.
