## Problem

Existing learning-based MILP solution-prediction methods are trained and evaluated within single-domain settings, causing them to overfit to domain-specific patterns and fail to generalize to unseen problem distributions. This limits scalability, as deploying such models in practice requires collecting new training data for each specific scenario, preventing the construction of a general-purpose learning-based solver.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251131/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each MILP instance is encoded as a weighted bipartite graph with constraint nodes (4 features), variable nodes (6 features), and edges (1 feature) corresponding to non-zero entries in the constraint matrix. The model outputs per-variable marginal probabilities in [0,1]^p, which are then fed into a standard solver (Gurobi or SCIP) that searches for a feasible solution within an L1 trust region of radius Δ around the predicted binary configuration, with k0 and k1 variables fixed to 0 or 1 respectively before the local search.

## Method

RoME pairs a Mixture-of-Experts architecture with a two-level distributionally robust training objective. The MoE consists of a shared GNN encoder, M expert networks, and H decoder heads, with dynamic routing via softmax gates over a graph-level embedding (mean-pooled variable nodes) that adaptively assigns expert and decoder weights per instance. Training combines three instance-level losses—weighted binary cross-entropy, expert diversity regularization (cosine similarity penalty across expert outputs), and a fuzzy membership consistency loss that enforces representation stability under isotropic perturbations of the task embedding—plus an inter-domain group-DRO min-max objective that exponentially reweights domains by their empirical loss to minimize worst-case performance. The key insight is that cross-domain training acts as domain-level regularization: a single model trained on three domains (IS, IP, SC) achieves 67.7% average improvement over single-domain baselines across five domains and demonstrates zero-shot generalization to MIPLIB instances.

## Results

RoME, trained on only three domains (IS, IP, SC), achieves an average 67.7% improvement in objective gap over strong baselines when evaluated across five diverse domains, including zero-shot generalization to WA and CA. On the MIPLIB hard-instance subset, RoME+Gurobi achieves a gap_abs of 0.00 (matching BKS), compared to 325.40 for Gurobi alone, 1710.99 for PS+Gurobi, and 938.58 for ConPS+Gurobi. On the IIS subset, RoME reduces gap_abs to 10.80 versus 15.00 (Gurobi), 14.60 (PS), and 14.20 (ConPS). With the SCIP solver, RoME achieves an 18.7× speedup on IS (13.55s vs. 254.63s) and consistent gap reductions across all five domains (8.7%–25.3%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

RoME outperforms all four baselines (Gurobi, SCIP, PS, ConPS) across all five evaluated domains (IS, IP, SC, CA, WA) in terms of absolute primal gap, with the largest gains on WA (25.3% gap reduction over SCIP) and SC (11.6%). Critically, RoME is trained only on IS, IP, and SC, yet it surpasses domain-specifically trained PS and ConPS on the unseen WA and CA domains in a zero-shot setting, demonstrating that cross-domain training acts as a form of domain-level regularization. On MIPLIB, RoME+Gurobi achieves the best gap_abs on both the IIS subset (10.80 vs. 14.20 for ConPS) and the hard-instance subset (0.00 vs. 325.40 for Gurobi), where existing learning-based methods struggle to generalize. In instance-level win-count analysis, RoME+Gurobi wins on 55/100 CA instances and 85/100 SC instances, compared to 26/100 and 5/100 for Gurobi alone, and when RoME does lose, the degradation is smaller (114.93 on CA) than PS's degradation (207.17), indicating greater stability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Predict-and-Search (PS) paradigm for MILP solution prediction—where a GNN predicts marginal probabilities that guide a solver's trust-region search—by moving from single-domain to cross-domain training. It draws on two established methodological threads: Mixture-of-Experts architectures (MVMoE, MAB-MTL, GOAL) for task-adaptive routing in combinatorial optimization, and Group DRO (Sagawa et al.) for worst-case domain balancing, but combines them in a novel two-level robust training objective (inter-domain group DRO plus intra-domain embedding perturbation) specifically designed for MILP. The key conceptual departure from prior solution-prediction work (Nair et al., Han et al., Huang et al.) is treating cross-domain structural diversity as a training signal rather than a confound, rather than training and evaluating within a single problem family.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that cross-domain training improves performance even within each individual training domain (not just on unseen domains) challenges the prevailing assumption that domain-specific data is optimal for domain-specific performance, suggesting that structural diversity acts as a form of regularization. The zero-shot MIPLIB results demonstrate practical deployability without per-domain retraining, which is critical for scalable learning-based solvers. However, the evaluation is limited to three training domains and five evaluation domains, the MIPLIB zero-shot uses a small curated subset, and the interaction between expert count and domain diversity is only loosely explored—open questions include whether the framework scales to dozens of domains, handles continuous-heavy MILPs, and whether the DRO component remains beneficial as domain count grows.
