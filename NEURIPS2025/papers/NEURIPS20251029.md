## Problem

Evaluating machine learning classifiers typically requires large labeled datasets, which are often prohibitively expensive or unavailable, while unlabeled data is plentiful but underutilized for evaluation. Prior semi-supervised evaluation methods capture only subsets of three key information sources—multiple classifiers, continuous probabilistic scores, and unlabeled data—but no existing approach leverages all three simultaneously to estimate the joint distribution of ground truth labels and classifier scores.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251029/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

SSME consumes concatenated continuous probability scores from M pre-trained classifiers over K classes for both a small labeled set (n_ℓ examples) and a much larger unlabeled set (n_u examples), with scores mapped to R^{K-1} via the additive log-ratio transform to avoid boundary bias on the probability simplex. The output is the fitted joint density P(y, s), from which any metric expressible as a function of scores and labels (accuracy, AUC, AUPRC, ECE, top-label calibration error) can be estimated by sampling posterior labels P(y|s) for unlabeled points and averaging over samples.

## Method

SSME fits a semi-supervised Gaussian mixture model whose K components correspond to ground truth classes, using kernel density estimation for the class-conditional score distributions P(s|y) and optimizing parameters via EM over 1000 epochs. The key distinction from prior work is the joint likelihood: labeled data contributes a supervised log-likelihood term while unlabeled data contributes a marginalized term, so the model learns the score-label relationship from both sources simultaneously rather than learning a mapping on labeled data and applying it to unlabeled data. Multiple classifiers are incorporated by concatenating their scores into a single high-dimensional vector, so each classifier adds informative dimensions about the latent label. The additive log-ratio transform reparameterizes the probability simplex into unbounded reals, enabling standard KDE without boundary corrections. Theoretical bounds (Theorem 1) show estimation error scales as O(1/√n_u) and tightens with the number of classifiers, provided component separation grows faster than the added dimensionality.

## Results

SSME reduces metric estimation error by 5.1× relative to labeled data alone (averaged across 5 tasks and 4 metrics), while the next best baseline achieves only 2.4×. With 20 labeled and 1000 unlabeled examples, SSME estimates accuracy within 1.5 percentage points of ground truth (vs. 3.4 for the closest baseline), ECE within 1.9 (vs. 3.8), AUC within 3.6 (vs. 4.3), and AUPRC within 8.5 (vs. 10.2). SSME achieves an effective sample size of 539 labeled examples for ECE estimation, compared to 110 for the next best method. In the LLM classifier case study, SSME improves over labeled data by 2.3× versus 1.3× for the next best baseline (Majority-Vote).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SSME outperforms all eight baselines on average across 5 tasks and 4 metrics, winning or tying in 51 of 60 dataset-metric-labeled-data combinations and significantly outperforming in 31. Bayesian-Calibration (BC) [37] is the strongest single-classifier baseline, leveraging a Bayesian re-calibration model, but it operates on one classifier at a time and is only competitive on AUC (achieving 3.2× error reduction vs. SSME's 2.2× on subgroup AUC). Dawid-Skene [18] and AutoEval [11] both discretize continuous classifier scores into hard predictions, losing information that SSME exploits through the additive log-ratio transform on full probability vectors. SPE [69] and Active-Testing [44] are limited to single-classifier evaluation, whereas SSME jointly models the joint distribution P(y, s) across all M classifiers simultaneously. Pseudo-Labeling, BC, and AutoEval each learn a mapping from scores to labels using only labeled data before applying it to unlabeled examples, while SSME jointly fits the mixture model on both labeled and unlabeled data via EM, yielding substantially better generalization when labeled data is scarce (20 examples). In the LLM evaluation setting, Majority-Vote is the next best method (1.3× improvement over labeled data) but SSME still achieves 2.3×, with significantly lower error in 8 of 12 task-metric-n_ℓ combinations.

## Relation to Prior Work

SSME unifies two previously separate literatures: semi-supervised evaluation of individual classifiers (SPE, Bayesian-Calibration, Active-Testing, AutoEval) and multi-annotator accuracy estimation (Dawid-Skene, weak supervision). Prior work captures subsets of three key information sources—multiple classifiers, continuous probabilistic scores, and unlabeled data—but no existing method accommodates all three simultaneously. The method repurposes standard tools (EM for mixture models, kernel density estimation, additive log-ratio transforms from compositional data analysis) in a new evaluation context, departing from prior approaches by jointly learning from labeled and unlabeled data rather than fitting on labels and transferring, and by avoiding the class-conditional independence assumption inherent in Dawid-Skene-style methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SSME addresses a fundamental practical bottleneck: practitioners increasingly have many candidate classifiers (especially from model hubs and LLMs) but limited labeled data for evaluation. The 5.1× reduction in estimation error over labeled data alone, validated across five diverse domains (healthcare, content moderation, molecular property prediction, text classification), makes the method broadly applicable. Key open questions include extending to distribution shift between labeled and unlabeled data, improving scalability of KDE in high-dimensional score spaces (many classifiers or classes), and leveraging the full joint distribution P(y,s) to measure ensemble-level properties like algorithmic monoculture. The theoretical bounds clarifying when more classifiers or more unlabeled data help provide a useful design principle for practitioners.
