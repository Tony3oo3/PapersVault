## Problem

Proprietary LLMs deployed on edge devices are vulnerable to model stealing attacks where attackers extract weights and fine-tune the model for new tasks, yet existing defenses either fail to protect the model backbone at runtime, provide insufficient security, or incur prohibitive TEE-GPU communication overhead (e.g., ~1.3 GB per token for PSP methods on LLaMA3-8B). No prior solution simultaneously achieves upper-bound security, backbone protection, and edge-feasible efficiency.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained transformer LLM and produces a 'locked' model whose linear-layer weights are row-permuted, rendering the model non-functional without TEE authorization. During inference, text tokens flow through the locked model, and the TEE performs a single column-permutation authorization at a midpoint transformer layer (using OTP-encrypted features to prevent input-output differencing). The output is the standard model text response, identical in accuracy to the unprotected model, but the model produces zero useful output if the TEE authorization is absent or incorrect.

## Method

CoreGuard combines two protocols: a protection protocol that row-permutes input-processing layers (QKV projections and FFN input) so they only compute correctly on column-permuted inputs, and a propagation protocol that column-permutes all output-processing layers (attention output, FFN output, add-norm parameters) so the permutation automatically propagates through every subsequent transformer layer. This reduces TEE involvement to a single authorization per inference (5 TEE-GPU transfer rounds) rather than per-layer authorization. The TEE authorization itself is secured by applying a one-time-pad noise matrix followed by a permutation to the FFN intermediate feature, with the output linear layer pre-permuted to offset the permutation, making the encryption and permutation mutually concealing. The authorization point is placed at the network midpoint so an attacker must recover at least half the model parameters, and the security is grounded in the NP-hardness of the underlying Matrix-LWE problem.

## Results

CoreGuard achieves 0% unauthorized inference accuracy across all four models (Qwen2-0.5B, Gemma2-2B, ChatGLM3-6B, LLaMA3-8B) and four tasks (GSM8k, Spider, PubMedQA, SQuAD), confirming complete blocking of direct misuse. Against model stealing (fine-tuning) attacks with 100% training data, CoreGuard's relative mean attack accuracy is 1.17× the black-box upper bound, comparable to DTE (1.18×) which protects the same parameters directly in TEE. In terms of overhead, CoreGuard incurs less than 0.0012% additional TEE FLOPs and only 5 TEE-GPU transfer rounds (~6–22 KB per token), versus 26–32% TEE FLOPs and 142K–1.31M transfer rounds for PSP baselines. Accuracy is preserved with at most 0.5% fluctuation (attributed to floating-point precision) between the original and CoreGuard-protected models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against TPTE (NPLO [51]), CoreGuard is dramatically superior: NPLO offers essentially no defense (9.59× relative attack accuracy vs. 9.58× for no-shield), while CoreGuard achieves 1.17×. Among PPTE methods, DarkneTZ [24] (8.43×), Serdab [12], and SOTER [35] all provide only weak-to-moderate protection, with attack accuracies remaining 5–10× higher than CoreGuard's. The two PSP baselines—TransLinkGuard (TLG) [20] at 1.07× and ShadowNet [38] at 1.09×—actually achieve slightly tighter security (closer to the 1.00× black-box bound) than CoreGuard's 1.17×, but at a prohibitive cost: they require 142K–1.31M TEE-GPU transfer rounds and 26–32% additional TEE FLOPs per token, versus CoreGuard's 5 rounds and <0.0012% FLOPs. Thus CoreGuard trades a marginal ~0.08–0.10× increase in relative attack accuracy for a ~2–3 order-of-magnitude reduction in communication overhead, making it the only method that simultaneously achieves near-upper-bound security and practical edge-deployment efficiency.

## Relation to Prior Work

CoreGuard sits at the intersection of TEE-based model protection (DarkneTZ, SOTER, Serdab) and parameter shuffling protection (ShadowNet, TransLinkGuard), but departs from both in a key way: prior PSP methods require per-layer TEE-GPU transfers (448 transfers for LLaMA3-8B), while prior PPTE methods leave the model backbone exposed. The paper's propagation protocol—where a single initial TEE authorization is automatically propagated through subsequent layers via column-permuted output-processing layers—is the central conceptual departure, reducing TEE interactions to a single round. The threat model also extends prior work by assuming the attacker has 100% of the training data (vs. 1% in NPLO) and targets the LLM's generalization capabilities rather than a single task.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As proprietary LLMs are increasingly deployed on edge devices (Apple Intelligence, on-device assistants), the gap between available protections and practical requirements becomes critical: existing solutions are either too weak (PPTE) or too expensive (PSP) for LLM-scale models. CoreGuard's propagation protocol offers a reusable design pattern for reducing TEE communication overhead in any layered architecture. Open questions include vulnerability to side-channel attacks on the TEE, compatibility with emerging GPU-based TEEs, and whether the single-authorization design remains secure under adaptive attacks that exploit the specific authorization position. The paper's reliance on CPU-based TEEs (TrustZone, SGX) limits near-term applicability to datacenter-class hardware with GPU TEEs.
