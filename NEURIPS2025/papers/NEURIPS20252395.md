## Problem

Existing clustered and personalized federated learning methods address only specific forms of data heterogeneity, often require prior knowledge of the number of client clusters, and assume test-time clients have already participated in training with labeled data. FLUX tackles the gap of handling all four common distribution shifts (feature, label, P(Y|X)-concept, and P(X|Y)-concept) simultaneously without any prior knowledge of cluster count, while enabling unseen, unlabeled clients to be assigned to the most suitable cluster-specific model at inference time.

## Input / Output

During training, each client provides labeled images (28×28 grayscale for MNIST/FMNIST, 32×32 RGB for CIFAR-10/100, chest X-rays for CheXpert, 20-class domain images for Office-Home) from which a compact descriptor vector of dimension L (with L/p ≤ 3.5×10⁻³ relative to model size) is extracted and sent to the server alongside local model updates. At test time, only unlabeled feature images are available; the method computes a label-agnostic sub-vector (marginal mean and covariance in latent space) to match the client to the nearest cluster centroid, after which the corresponding pre-trained cluster-specific model is used for prediction. The output is a class label (or multi-label ROC AUC for CheXpert) together with the inferred cluster assignment.

## Method

FLUX decomposes the CFL objective into three independently optimizable sub-problems via a probabilistic graphical model: local classification, descriptor extraction, and unsupervised clustering. Each client maps its data through a shared encoder to a latent space, applies a client-invariant PCA reduction (l=10 dimensions), and computes first and second moments of both the marginal P(X) and class-conditional P(Y|X) distributions to form a descriptor that is provably Lipschitz-equivalent to the 2-Wasserstein distance between client distributions. The server runs an adaptive density-based clustering algorithm (extended DBSCAN with elbow-detected ε) on the collected descriptors to automatically determine the number of clusters and assign clients, then aggregates local models within each cluster using standard weighted FedAvg. At inference, the label-agnostic sub-vector of a new client's descriptor is matched to stored cluster centroids via Euclidean distance, enabling model assignment without any labeled data or knowledge of the true cluster count. The entire pipeline adds negligible communication overhead (descriptor size is ≤0.35% of model size) and computational cost comparable to vanilla FedAvg.

## Results

FLUX achieves average accuracy gains of up to 23.0 pp over the best-performing baselines on FMNIST (81.2% Test Phase vs. 69.4% CFL), 12.4 pp on MNIST (94.0% vs. 86.1% CFL), 7.0 pp on CIFAR-10 (38.7% vs. 36.6% IFCA/APFL), and 3.0 pp on CIFAR-100 (41.3% vs. 39.8% FeSEM) in the Test Phase setting. On the real-world CheXpert dataset (ROC AUC), FLUX reaches 78.6% in the Test Phase, outperforming the best baseline (FedDrift at 61.6%) by 17.0 pp. FLUX maintains training times comparable to FedAvg, with communication overhead of only L/p ≤ 3.5×10⁻³ relative to model size, and sustains accuracy above 84% even when scaling to 100 clients.

## Baselines & Comparisons

In the Test Phase (unseen, unlabeled clients), FLUX consistently outperforms all 10 baselines across all six datasets, with the largest margins on FMNIST (+11.9 pp over CFL at 69.4%) and CheXpert (+17.0 pp over FedDrift at 61.6% ROC AUC). Among CFL baselines, CFL and FeSEM are the strongest competitors in the Test Phase but still trail FLUX by 7.8 pp (MNIST) and 1.5 pp (CIFAR-100) respectively; FedDrift collapses severely on unseen clients (e.g., 65.1% on MNIST vs. FLUX's 94.0%). PFL methods (pFedMe, APFL) achieve higher accuracy in the Known Association setting (e.g., APFL at 44.7% on CIFAR-10 vs. FLUX's 38.6%) because they are fine-tuned on the same client distributions seen at test time, but they cannot handle truly unseen clients (pFedMe reports N/A for Test Phase) and APFL drops to 36.6% on CIFAR-10 Test Phase. The TTA-FL baseline ATP shows instability, particularly on CIFAR-100 (37.5% vs. FLUX's 41.3%), where entropy-based adaptation leads to overconfident mispredictions under concept shift. FLUX-prior (using oracle cluster count with K-means) sets the CFL benchmark at 95.8% on MNIST Known A., while FLUX without prior knowledge achieves 92.5%, confirming that the learned descriptors and unsupervised clustering recover most of the oracle performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

FLUX builds directly on the FedAvg protocol as its aggregation backbone but departs fundamentally from existing CFL methods (IFCA, FedEM, FedRC, CFL, FeSEM, FedDrift) by replacing metric-based (loss/accuracy) or parameter-based (gradient/model) clustering with a descriptor-based paradigm that captures both marginal and conditional distribution statistics in a compact latent space. It also extends TTA-FL methods (ATP, FedCTTA) by substituting online parameter adaptation with structured cluster assignment at test time, avoiding the instability of unsupervised objectives like entropy minimization. The key conceptual departure is the use of a privacy-preserving PCA-reduced moment descriptor whose Euclidean geometry is provably Lipschitz-equivalent to the 2-Wasserstein distance, enabling a single representation to detect all four distribution shift types simultaneously without requiring prior knowledge of the cluster count.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FLUX addresses two practical gaps that have limited CFL adoption: the need for oracle knowledge of the number of clusters and the inability to serve unseen, unlabeled clients at test time. The finding that FLUX (unsupervised, no M prior) closely matches FLUX-prior (oracle M) across all six datasets validates that the descriptor-based clustering is effective, making the framework deployable in realistic settings where distribution structure is unknown. Key limitations remain: P(Y|X) concept shifts cannot be resolved at test time (only P(X) is available without labels), the framework is one-shot and does not handle distribution drift over time, and descriptor quality depends on sufficient per-client data. Open questions include extending FLUX to dynamic client populations with evolving distributions and integrating it with continual learning or drift-detection mechanisms.
