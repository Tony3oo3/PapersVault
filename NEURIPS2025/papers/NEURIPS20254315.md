## Problem

Multimodal learning suffers from modality imbalance where strong and weak modalities converge at different speeds, causing the overall model to underperform even unimodal baselines. Existing rebalancing methods (gradient modulation, learning rate adjustment, alternating training) focus on balancing the learning process but overlook the root cause: an inherent disproportion in classification ability between modalities. This paper argues that directly enhancing the weak modality's classifier, rather than merely slowing the strong one, is the key to resolving the imbalance.

## Input / Output

The method accepts paired multimodal inputs (audio+video clips, RGB+optical-flow+depth frames, or image+text pairs) and outputs a single class label per sample. Each modality is encoded by a shared backbone (ResNet-18/34, I3D, or BERT+ResNet-50) into a fixed-dimensional feature vector, which is then passed through a stack of configurable two-layer fully-connected classifiers (D×256 → ReLU → 256×K). The final prediction for each modality is the element-wise sum of all its classifiers' outputs, and the multimodal prediction is obtained by standard late fusion (summation or weighting) across modalities.

## Method

The core mechanism is a sustained boosting algorithm that iteratively adds classifiers to the weaker modality while simultaneously minimizing three coupled loss terms: (1) a residual error term that fits the negative gradient of the current prediction (analogous to gradient boosting), (2) an all-classifier term ensuring the summed prediction of all t classifiers matches the ground truth, and (3) a previous-classifier term preserving the quality of the first t−1 classifiers. An adaptive classifier assignment (ACA) strategy monitors a confident score (mean ground-truth-class probability) every t_N iterations and adds a new classifier to whichever modality lags beyond a threshold τ, scaled by coefficient σ. Unlike standard gradient boosting where each new classifier is independent, here all classifiers share the same encoder, so the encoder is updated jointly and all classifiers must be re-optimized together. The paper proves that under strong convexity, smoothness, and boosting-effectiveness assumptions, the cross-modal loss gap G(Φ) = L^a − L^v converges at rate O(1/T), providing theoretical guarantees that the classification ability disproportion is provably eliminated.

## Results

On CREMAD, the proposed method achieves 85.15% accuracy and 91.03% MAP, outperforming the strongest baseline LFM (83.62% Acc, 90.06% MAP) by 1.53% and 0.97% respectively. Across all six datasets (CREMAD, KSounds, VGGSound, Twitter, Sarcasm, NVGesture), the method attains the best accuracy in every multimodal setting, with margins of +0.10% to +1.53% over LFM on accuracy. On the tri-modal NVGesture dataset, it reaches 85.01% accuracy and 85.33% F1, surpassing LFM (84.36% Acc, 84.68% F1) and confirming generalization beyond two modalities.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The method outperforms all 15 baselines on accuracy across all six datasets. The closest competitor is LFM (NeurIPS 2024), which the proposed method surpasses by 1.53% accuracy on CREMAD, 0.10% on KSounds, 0.27% on VGGSound, 0.11% on Twitter, 0.13% on Sarcasm, and 0.65% on NVGesture. Among rebalanced multimodal learning methods, MLA (CVPR 2024) and DI-MML (ACM MM 2024) are the strongest on CREMAD (79.43% and 81.58% accuracy respectively), both substantially below the proposed 85.15%. ReconBoost (ICML 2024), which also uses gradient boosting but for cross-modal information transfer rather than weak-classifier enhancement, trails by 9.58% on CREMAD accuracy. On Twitter F1, the method (0.6962) is slightly below LFM (0.7057), the only metric where it does not achieve the top score. The method also extends to the tri-modal NVGesture setting where several baselines (OGM, PMR, DI-MML) cannot be applied, and still achieves the best accuracy (85.01%) and F1 (85.33%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on gradient boosting (Friedman, 2001) but repurposes it for a fundamentally different goal: rather than building an ensemble of weak learners for a single task, it uses iterative residual fitting to explicitly enhance the classification ability of the weaker modality in a multimodal setting. It departs from the dominant line of rebalanced multimodal learning (G-Blend, OGM, MLA, ReconBoost) that addresses modality imbalance by modulating learning rates, gradients, or alternating training schedules — approaches that balance the learning *process* without directly strengthening the weak classifier. The sustained boosting loss (simultaneously minimizing classification error, residual error, and preserving prior classifiers) and the adaptive classifier assignment strategy are novel formulations not present in prior boosting or multimodal learning literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reframes modality imbalance as a classification-ability disproportion problem rather than a learning-speed problem, offering a principled and theoretically grounded (O(1/T) gap convergence) solution that consistently outperforms SOTA by large margins across six diverse datasets spanning audio-video, image-text, and tri-modal settings. Limitations include the method's focus on the classifier level (not early fusion), the theoretical analysis covering only gap convergence rather than full training dynamics, and the added computational overhead from multiple classifiers. Open questions include whether the approach extends to early-fusion architectures, how it scales to many more modalities, and whether the adaptive assignment strategy remains stable under significant modality noise or distribution shift.
