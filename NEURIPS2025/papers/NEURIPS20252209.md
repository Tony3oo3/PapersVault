## Problem

Diffusion networks governing cascade propagation are typically hidden and must be inferred from observed temporal cascade records. Real-world cascades exhibit strong heterogeneity because they propagate across multiple complementary networks (e.g., a geographic network and a latent 'invisible college' network), yet existing methods either assume a single diffusion network or cluster entire cascade samples into groups, failing to capture node-level pathway selection that can produce up to 2^N distinct diffusion patterns.

## Input / Output

The method takes C independent cascade records, each an N-dimensional vector of infection times within a finite observation window [0, T] (uninfected nodes are assigned time T), and optionally a binary adjacency matrix A specifying the support of the observed network. It outputs two N×N transmission-rate matrices — Θ constrained to the support of A and Ψ constrained to rank ≤ r — along with a vector π ∈ [0,1]^N giving each node's probability of engaging the cascade via Θ versus Ψ. The column-wise mixture structure means each node independently selects its diffusion pathway per cascade, so the effective network Θ^c varies across cascades without requiring explicit sample clustering.

## Method

The core contribution is a double network mixture model that represents each cascade's diffusion pathway as a column-wise Bernoulli mixture over two networks Θ and Ψ, where node i uses Θ with probability π_i and Ψ with probability 1−π_i. This avoids modeling complex individual state changes while generating up to 2^N distinct diffusion patterns. The continuous-time cascade likelihood is built from survival-analysis hazard functions (Exp, Pow, or Ray), and parameters are estimated by maximizing the marginal log-likelihood via a first-order EM algorithm: the E-step computes closed-form posterior probabilities π̂_i^c, and the M-step decomposes into three parallel convex subproblems — projected gradient ascent for Θ (support constraint Θ⊙(I−A)=0), proximal gradient ascent with singular-value soft-thresholding for Ψ (nuclear-norm constraint ‖Ψ‖_⋆ ≤ ρ), and a closed-form update for π. Identifiability is established under a rank-sparsity incoherence condition between the support-constrained subspace of Θ and the low-rank subspace of Ψ, and the convexity of the M-step objective (under standard hazard-rate assumptions) guarantees unique solutions and geometric convergence of the EM iterations.

## Results

On synthetic cascading data (N=200, C=2000, Exp model), the proposed double network mixture model achieves MAE(Θ) of 0.264–0.315 and MAE(Ψ) of 0.507–0.545 across random, community, and scale-free topologies, substantially outperforming NetRate (MAE(Θ) 0.851–0.865, MAE(Ψ) 2.897–3.103) and MMRate (MAE(Θ) 0.630–0.668, MAE(Ψ) 1.477–1.665). For latent network topology recovery, the method achieves accuracy of 0.833–0.846 and precision of 0.750–0.767 on Ψ, compared to 0.172–0.180 accuracy and 0.095–0.099 precision for NetRate. The network selection probability π is estimated with MAE of 0.047–0.050, versus 0.881–0.928 for MMRate, the only other method that estimates π.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method decisively outperforms all three baselines on transmission rate estimation: MAE(Θ) is roughly 2.7× lower than NetRate and 2× lower than MMRate, while MAE(Ψ) is 5–6× lower than NetRate and ~3× lower than MMRate. On latent network topology recovery (Acc, Pre on Ψ), the proposed method achieves 0.83–0.85 accuracy and 0.75–0.77 precision, compared to 0.17–0.18 accuracy and 0.10–0.13 precision for NetRate, 0.20–0.22 accuracy and 0.12–0.13 precision for MMRate, and 0.52 accuracy and 0.40 precision for ConNIe. The one metric where NetRate edges out the proposed method is recall on Ψ (e.g., 0.964 vs. 0.948 on random topology), but this comes at the cost of extremely low precision (0.095), indicating NetRate over-predicts edges. For the joint network Θ∪Ψ, the proposed method achieves 0.88–0.89 accuracy versus 0.21–0.22 for NetRate, 0.27–0.28 for MMRate, and 0.64–0.65 for ConNIe. On network selection probability estimation (MAE(π)), the proposed method (0.047–0.050) is ~18× more accurate than MMRate (0.881–0.928), the only other baseline that estimates π.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the continuous-time diffusion network inference framework established by NetRate (Gomez-Rodriguez et al., 2014) and the multi-pattern cascade modeling of MMRate (Wang et al., 2014) by replacing sample-level clustering into a fixed number of networks with a column-wise distributional mixture over two diffusion networks. The key conceptual departure is that each node independently selects its transmission layer per cascade, generating up to 2^N distinct diffusion pathways without explicitly modeling individual state variables. The identifiability analysis draws on the rank-sparsity incoherence framework of Chandrasekaran et al. (2011), and the optimization leverages first-order EM guarantees from Balakrishnan et al. (2017) to achieve convex subproblems in the M-step.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The column-wise mixture formulation provides a principled way to handle combinatorial cascade heterogeneity that arises from multiple transmission channels, avoiding the need to model complex individual status dynamics. The identifiability results (Propositions 1–2) are important because they show when two complementary networks (sparse observed + low-rank latent) can be globally recovered, a non-trivial question given the label-permutation ambiguity inherent in mixture models. However, the model is restricted to exactly two layers, requires C ≫ N samples for reliable estimation, and the real-data application (sociology research topics) is domain-specific. Open questions include generalization to more than two layers, time-varying network parameters, and whether the low-rank assumption on the latent network holds in settings where social distance is not well-approximated by a small number of latent factors.
