## Problem

The Bernstein-von Mises theorem guarantees that Bayesian uncertainty quantification asymptotically matches frequentist (Wald-type) inference for i.i.d. data, but it has never been extended to adaptively collected data from bandits, reinforcement learning, or adaptive experiments. This gap leaves practitioners without theoretical justification for the widely used practice of applying standard Bayesian inference to adaptive data, and it obscures whether Bayesian credible intervals are actually frequentist-valid in such settings.

## Input / Output

The method takes a sequential data trajectory where covariates x_j are chosen adaptively via an arbitrary sampling rule Λ based on all previously observed (x,y) pairs, outcomes y_j are drawn from a Gaussian (or exponential family) model with mean x_j^T β, and a prior π on the parameter vector β ∈ R^p. It produces the posterior distribution π(β|H_n) and shows it converges in total variation distance to a representative normal N(β̂_n, σ²(X_n^T X_n)^{-1}) centered at the MLE. A key structural constraint is that the empirical Fisher information X_n^T X_n can have an unbounded condition number (e.g., growing like n/log n for regret-optimal bandits), which is what makes the standard BvM proof techniques inapplicable.

## Method

The core proof exploits the fact that the posterior is invariant to the adaptivity of the sampling rule: terms in the likelihood involving the covariate selection process Λ do not depend on β and are absorbed into the normalizing constant, so the posterior is proportional to the prior times a Gaussian density centered at the MLE. The technical challenge is bounding the TV distance between this posterior and the representative normal when the empirical Fisher information has unbounded condition number, requiring truncation to highly anisotropic local ellipsoids rather than the symmetric balls used in standard BvM proofs. For the general adaptive linear Gaussian setting (Theorem 1), a subexponential eigenvalue growth condition (log λ_max = o_p(λ_min)) is needed; for multi-armed bandits (Theorem 2) this condition is dropped by leveraging the bounded prior and the fact that the likelihood factors by arm, and the result extends to exponential family bandits (Theorem 3) via a second-order Taylor expansion of the log-likelihood. Crucially, none of these results require the stability condition from Lai and Wei used in prior frequentist validity work, which yields the surprising negative result that Bayesian UQ can asymptotically match Wald-type frequentist UQ while both remain asymptotically frequentist-invalid when stability fails.

## Results

The paper proves four Bernstein-von Mises theorems (Theorems 1–4) establishing that the posterior distribution converges in total variation distance to a representative normal N(β̂ₙ, σ²(XₙᵀXₙ)⁻¹) for adaptively collected data, without requiring the stability condition from Lai & Wei (1982). Empirically, TV distance between the posterior and representative normal decreases to near zero across all tested settings (Gaussian bandits, contextual bandits, exponential family bandits, LQR), with convergence fastest in the zero-margin case. A key negative result shows that in the two-arm batched bandit with equal arm means (zero margin), the 95% Bayesian credible interval fails to achieve 95% frequentist coverage despite the BvM theorem holding, confirming that Bayesian UQ can be asymptotically frequentist-invalid when stability fails.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical extension paper rather than a method comparison, so there are no competing baselines in the traditional sense. The paper's contribution is to extend the classical BvM theorem (van der Vaart, 2000) and prior non-i.i.d. extensions (which require bounded eigenvalue ratios) to adaptive settings where the eigenvalue ratio is unbounded (e.g., regret-optimal bandit algorithms produce ratios growing like n/log n). The key comparison is between Bayesian UQ and Wald-type frequentist UQ: the paper shows they are asymptotically equivalent (BvM holds) but that this equivalence does not guarantee frequentist validity when the Lai–Wei stability condition fails. The empirical validation in Figures 1–3 confirms the TV distance converges to zero for UCB, Lin-UCB, Thompson Sampling, and NCEC, with the zero-margin batched bandit case (Zhang et al., 2020) serving as the concrete example where BvM holds but frequentist coverage fails.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the classical Bernstein-von Mises (BvM) theorem from i.i.d. data (van der Vaart [29]) to adaptively collected data, a setting where prior BvM extensions for non-i.i.d. data fail because they require bounded eigenvalue ratios of the Fisher information matrix — a condition violated by regret-optimal bandit algorithms. For frequentist UQ in adaptive settings, prior work (Lai & Wei [21], Hadad et al. [14], Howard et al. [15]) relies on a stability condition or martingale CLTs; this paper's BvM result notably does not require that stability condition, instead needing only a subexponential eigenvalue growth condition that is nearly necessary for MLE consistency. The paper also generalizes beyond Gaussian models to exponential family bandits and to contextual bandits and LQR, connecting the statistical theory to concrete algorithms (UCB, Thompson sampling, Lin-UCB, NCEC) from the bandit and control literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central practical implication is a warning: practitioners using Bayesian UQ for adaptively collected data should not assume asymptotic frequentist validity, even though the prior 'washes out' and the posterior matches standard Wald-type intervals. The counterintuitive negative result — BvM holds yet frequentist validity fails when the stability condition is violated — is well-evidenced by both a proven theorem and the batched bandit simulation (Figure 3), where coverage deviates from nominal levels near the zero-margin case. Open questions include whether data-dependent (empirical Bayesian) priors can restore frequentist validity, and whether the BvM result extends to general parametric models beyond Gaussian and exponential family settings, which the authors identify as technically challenging in Appendix F.
