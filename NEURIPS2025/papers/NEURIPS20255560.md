## Problem

Feature Visualization (FV) is a widely used technique for interpreting concepts learned by DNNs, but its trustworthiness has received limited attention. While prior work showed CNN FVs could be manipulated via architectural add-ons like fooling circuits, it remained unclear whether FV outputs could be arbitrarily and covertly changed through fine-tuning alone—without altering the model architecture or substantially degrading performance or internal representations.

## Input / Output

The method takes a trained DNN (CNN or ViT), a target feature defined as a linear direction in an intermediate layer, a target image (resized to match model input resolution), and a small preservation dataset (as little as 0.1% of ImageNet for large models). It produces a fine-tuned model whose FV optimization for the target feature converges to the target image, while the model's classification accuracy and the feature's AUROC on its true label remain nearly unchanged. Manipulation operates in a parameterized domain (e.g., scaled Fourier space) rather than raw pixel space, and the target image is encoded into this domain via inverse sigmoid, color-space conversion, and 2D FFT.

## Method

Gradient Slingshots sculpts the activation landscape of a target feature in off-distribution regions by defining three geometric zones: a 'slingshot zone' (ball around the expected FV initialization), a 'landing zone' (ball around the target in parameter space), and a 'tunnel' (convex hull connecting them). Within the tunnel, the manipulated function is shaped into a quadratic bowl whose gradients point uniformly toward the target, guaranteeing convergence of gradient ascent from the slingshot zone (proven in Lemma 3.1). The practical implementation uses two loss terms: a manipulation loss that enforces the desired gradient field (or equivalently, the quadratic activation values) at uniformly sampled tunnel points, and a preservation loss (weighted MSE on feature and layer activations over a small natural-image subset) that keeps the model's behavior on in-distribution inputs intact. The combined objective is optimized via AdamW fine-tuning, with a trade-off parameter α balancing manipulation strength against representation preservation. The approach is architecture-agnostic, demonstrated on 6-layer CNNs, VGGs, ResNets, ViTs, and CLIP, and generalizes across FV variants including pixel-domain AM and Fourier-domain FV with transformation robustness.

## Results

On ResNet-50 (ImageNet), Gradient Slingshots at α=0.64 manipulates the 'wolf spider' FV to resemble a Dalmatian while preserving 75.13% test accuracy (vs. 76.13% original) and maintaining AUROC at 1.00 for the target feature. Target Label Alignment increases from 0.23 to 0.31, CLIP image similarity rises from 0.53 to 0.69, and LPIPS drops from 0.69 to 0.59 over 100 independent FV runs. In the CLIP ViT-L/14 weapon-detection case study, the manipulated model retains 100% misclassification of assault rifles as non-weapons and 98.04% correct classification of other weapons, while the FV-to-target (gentoo penguin) CLIP distance improves from 0.60 to 0.88.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares Gradient Slingshots against ProxPulse (Nanfack et al., 2024), the only other fine-tuning-based FV manipulation method, on an identical ResNet-50 'wolf spider' → Dalmatian setup. Gradient Slingshots outperforms ProxPulse across all six reported metrics: it retains 75.13% test accuracy versus 69.03% (ProxPulse α=1e-5) and 58.51% (ProxPulse α=0.1), achieves higher target-label alignment (0.31 vs. 0.24/0.22), lower ground-truth-label alignment (0.23 vs. 0.24/0.31), greater CLIP image similarity to the target (0.69 vs. 0.53/0.54), and lower LPIPS distance (0.59 vs. 1.06/0.70). The paper attributes ProxPulse's weaker performance to its lack of an explicit tunnel connecting the initialization region to the target, which provides no theoretical convergence guarantee, and to its preservation loss (cross-entropy on outputs) not explicitly controlling internal activations. The authors note that architectural attacks (fooling circuits, silent units from Geirhos et al.) could achieve even more precise target memorization but are trivially detectable via model inspection, unlike GS which operates within standard architectures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on adversarial manipulation of Feature Visualization (FV) initiated by Geirhos et al. [41], who demonstrated FV tampering via architectural modifications (fooling circuits, silent units), and Nanfack et al. [51,52], who explored fine-tuning-based attacks. The key departure is that Gradient Slingshots achieves arbitrary FV manipulation through fine-tuning alone—without architectural changes—while explicitly preserving the model's internal representations (verified via AUROC), a constraint absent in prior approaches. The theoretical contribution draws an analogy to physics-informed neural networks [55], where a gradient-matching loss enforces a PDE solution, here applied to sculpt a quadratic activation landscape along a 'tunnel' between initialization and target regions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper exposes a critical vulnerability in FV-based model auditing: an adversary can replace a feature's visualization with an arbitrary image while the feature retains its original function, making FV-based audits fundamentally unreliable. The weapon-detection case study concretely illustrates how a malicious label-flip could be concealed from auditors. However, the proposed detection method (checking natural-domain AM signal consistency) requires labeled test data and may degrade on out-of-distribution inputs, and the manipulation's effect on broader internal representations beyond AUROC remains underexplored. Open questions include robustness to all FV regularization strategies and whether more comprehensive representation-preservation metrics would reveal subtler alterations.
