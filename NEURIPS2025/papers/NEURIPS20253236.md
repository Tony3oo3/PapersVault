## Problem

Neural network architectures encode inductive biases that determine whether a network can learn a given task, yet the precise relationship between architecture and the priors it imposes remains poorly understood. Architectures deemed 'untrainable' for a task—such as fully connected networks on ImageNet (overfitting), deep CNNs without skip connections (underfitting), or RNNs on copy-paste (memorization failure)—are traditionally discarded in favor of different architectures, but the nature of the missing bias is unknown. This paper asks whether inductive biases can be transferred from one architecture to another without modifying the target's structure.

## Input / Output

The method takes a target network (the untrainable architecture), a frozen guide network (which may be trained or randomly initialized), and a shared input batch (images at 224×224 for ImageNet, tokenized text sequences of length 50 for WikiText-103, or bitstrings/number sequences for formal language tasks). Both networks process the same input, and intermediate activations from all tunable-weight layers are collected per minibatch (batch size 256). The output is the trained target network producing standard task outputs—class probabilities, next-token predictions, or sequence reconstructions—with the guide network remaining entirely unchanged throughout training.

## Method

Guidance augments the target's task loss with a layerwise representational alignment term computed via linear CKA between matched intermediate activations of the target and a frozen guide network: L(θ^T) = L_T(θ^T) + Σ_{i∈I} (1 − CKA(A_i^T, A_i^G)). Guide layers are mapped to target layers by evenly distributing the guide's layers across the target's layers, and only the target's parameters are updated. The key insight is that even a randomly initialized guide—containing no learned task knowledge—transfers a powerful architectural prior, because CKA captures second-order statistics (pairwise distance matrices) that encode structural properties like local receptive fields and weight sharing. This distinguishes guidance from distillation, which operates on output logits, requires a trained teacher, and aligns only the final layer; guidance aligns many intermediate layers simultaneously across architecturally dissimilar pairs (e.g., Transformer→RNN, ResNet→FCN), addressing the credit-assignment problem in deep networks.

## Results

On ImageNet-1K (Top-5 validation accuracy), guidance with an untrained ResNet-18 boosts a 50-layer Deep FCN from 1.65% to 13.10%, and a trained ResNet-18 improves a Wide FCN from 34.09% to 43.01%. A no-skip Deep ConvNet (ResNet-50 without residuals) improves from 70.02% to 78.91% with a trained ResNet-50 guide. On sequence tasks, a 4-layer RNN guided by a Transformer improves copy-paste accuracy from 14.35% to 42.56% (untrained guide) and halves WikiText-103 perplexity from 69.19 to 40.01 (trained guide), closing most of the gap to the Transformer baseline of 34.15.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is vanilla training of the same target architecture without any guidance. Guidance consistently outperforms vanilla training across all settings: Deep FCN improves by ~11 points (untrained guide), Wide FCN by ~9 points (trained guide), and Deep ConvNet by ~9 points (trained guide). Against knowledge distillation (Hinton [33]), guidance outperforms distillation in every configuration tested. Critically, distillation with an untrained teacher actually degrades performance, while guidance with an untrained guide still yields large gains (e.g., 13.10% vs. 1.65% for Deep FCN), demonstrating that layerwise representational alignment transfers architectural priors that output-logit matching cannot. For sequence modeling, the RNN guided by a Transformer (42.56% copy-paste) substantially outperforms the unguided RNN (14.35%) and approaches the Transformer's 96.98%, while the Transformer guided by an RNN improves parity accuracy from 71.98% to 78.49%, surpassing prior results that attributed this gap to fundamental Transformer limitations.

## Relation to Prior Work

The paper repurposes centered kernel alignment (CKA), a representational similarity metric originally developed for neuroscience, as a training-time loss term to transfer inductive biases between architecturally dissimilar networks. It explicitly distinguishes itself from knowledge distillation by operating on intermediate layer activations rather than output logits, by supporting untrained guides, and by aligning many layers simultaneously to address credit assignment. The work is motivated by Han et al.'s finding that CKA cannot distinguish representations across architectures, which the authors reinterpret as evidence that architectural priors are transferable through representational alignment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that randomly initialized guides often outperform trained ones suggests that architectural structure alone encodes meaningful inductive biases independent of learned parameters—a conceptual shift with implications for how we understand what makes an architecture effective. The method provides a practical empirical tool for probing architectural priors and could inform automated architecture search, though current results (e.g., 13% ImageNet accuracy for guided FCNs) remain far from competitive. Open questions include whether the untrained-guide advantage persists at scale, whether the discovered initialization regimes can be characterized analytically, and whether guidance can be extended to close the remaining gap to guide-network performance.
