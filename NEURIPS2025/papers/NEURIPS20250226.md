## Problem

Perturbation-based explanation methods (e.g., SHAP, LIME) query models on inputs that deviate substantially from the training distribution, yet models systematically produce miscalibrated probability estimates under these explainability-specific perturbations. This paper provides the first rigorous theoretical analysis showing that such miscalibration directly degrades both global and local explanation quality, and demonstrates that standard post-hoc calibration on unperturbed samples is insufficient to address the problem.

## Input / Output

ReCalX operates on the raw logits of a pre-trained classifier evaluated on perturbed inputs, along with the perturbation level λ(S) = (d−|S|)/d indicating the fraction of features that have been modified. It outputs calibrated class probabilities via an adaptive softmax with a bin-specific temperature, which are then aggregated by the explanation method (e.g., SHAP, LIME) into feature attribution vectors. The recalibration is information-preserving: it applies a componentwise strictly monotonic transformation that leaves the model's prediction ranking and discriminative power unchanged.

## Method

ReCalX generalizes temperature scaling by partitioning the perturbation-level range [0,1] into B equal-width bins and learning a separate temperature T_b for each bin by minimizing cross-entropy on perturbed validation samples grouped by their perturbation intensity. During explanation, the method infers the perturbation level from the active feature subset S and applies the corresponding temperature to the logits before the final softmax, yielding calibrated probabilities tailored to the specific perturbation strength. The theoretical foundation (Theorem 3.2) decomposes predictive power into a perturbation baseline bias, mutual information, and a calibration error term, proving that reducing perturbation-specific miscalibration directly recovers the idealized mutual-information-based importance. A key design constraint is information preservation (Proposition 4.2): because the transformation is componentwise strictly monotonic for each fixed S, the mutual information between model outputs and the target is unchanged, ensuring explanations remain faithful to the original model's behavior.

## Results

ReCalX consistently reduces the maximum KL-divergence calibration error (CE_KL^MAX_S) across all tested models and datasets. On tabular data (Table 1), ReCalX achieves relative reductions of 6.9%–92.3% in maximum calibration error compared to uncalibrated models, with the largest gains on the Pol dataset (89.5% for ResNet, from 0.863 to 0.091). On ImageNet (Table 2), ReCalX yields reductions of 80.3%–97.4% in maximum calibration error, e.g., dropping ResNet50's CE_KL^MAX_S from 0.418 to 0.013 under zero-baseline perturbation. ReCalX also improves explanation robustness (sensitivity metrics in Table 3) and global feature-importance fidelity (remove-and-retrain experiments in Figure 4), with calibrated explanations producing steeper loss increases when top-ranked features are removed (e.g., 33% vs. 24% loss increase on Electricity).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares ReCalX against two baselines: uncalibrated models and standard temperature scaling (Guo et al., 2017), which applies a single static temperature learned on unperturbed validation samples. ReCalX dominates both baselines across all 8 model–perturbation combinations in Table 2, reducing maximum calibration error by 80–97% relative to uncalibrated models, whereas temperature scaling achieves only 55–70% reduction and is inconsistent. Notably, temperature scaling can actively worsen calibration under perturbations: for ViT under zero-baseline perturbation, it increases CE_KL^MAX_S from 0.262 to 0.306, and for the tabular ResNet on Pol it inflates the error from 0.863 to 1.017 (Table 1). ReCalX's adaptive per-perturbation-level temperatures avoid this failure mode, achieving the lowest calibration error in every setting tested. On the downstream explanation-quality metrics, ReCalX also outperforms uncalibrated explanations in sensitivity (Table 3: e.g., ResNet50 max sensitivity drops from 1.914 to 1.595 under zero-baseline LIME) and in remove-and-retrain fidelity (Figure 4: steeper loss curves when features are removed in ReCalX-ranked order).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate research threads—uncertainty calibration and perturbation-based explainability—by showing that the former is a fundamental prerequisite for the latter. It generalizes temperature scaling (Guo et al., ICML 2017) from a single static parameter to an adaptive, perturbation-level-dependent temperature (ReCalX), and builds on the mutual-information decomposition of proper scoring rules (Bröcker 2009; Kull & Flach 2015; Covert et al. 2020) to formalize how calibration error directly degrades both global and local explanation quality. The work departs from earlier calibration-for-explanation efforts (Scafarto et al. 2022; Lofström et al. 2023/2024), which calibrated only on unperturbed samples, by proving that such calibration is insufficient and that models must be well-calibrated under every subset perturbation encountered during the explanation process.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By establishing a rigorous theoretical link between perturbation-specific miscalibration and explanation distortion, the paper reframes calibration not as an optional post-hoc nicety but as a necessary condition for trustworthy feature attributions. The practical impact is significant: ReCalX is a lightweight, model-agnostic post-hoc technique requiring only a few hundred validation samples and introducing millisecond-level inference overhead, making it broadly deployable. Open questions include whether the principles extend to gradient-based or counterfactual explanation families, how ReCalX behaves under domain shift or adversarial perturbations, and whether the adaptive-temperature idea can be further generalized (e.g., to multi-class or multi-task settings). The paper's scope is limited to classification and regression with linear summary strategies, and the remove-and-retrain fidelity experiments, while convincing, rely on a single retraining protocol.
