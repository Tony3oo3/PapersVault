## Problem

Existing synthetic data generation methods for face recognition rely on external datasets, pre-trained generators, or auxiliary networks (e.g., demographic classifiers, FR backbones trained on other data), introducing privacy concerns and resource overhead without clear evidence that the gains come from the generative process itself. This paper asks whether a self-contained diffusion model trained exclusively on the target recognition dataset can, through a novel sampling strategy, produce synthetic samples that improve the downstream discriminator's performance without any external resources.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254501/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single labeled face dataset (CASIA-WebFace with ~490K images across ~10.5K identities, or a curated WebFace160K subset with ~160K images across ~10K identities) at 112×112 RGB resolution. It produces two types of synthetic 112×112 face images: reproduction samples (conditioned on original one-hot class vectors) and mixed-class augmentations (conditioned on linear combinations of two class vectors). These synthetic images are concatenated with the original real images to train an IR50/IR101 discriminator with an AdaFace margin head, yielding 512-dimensional identity embeddings for verification. The one-hot condition vectors have dimensionality matching the number of identities (~10,000), and the generator operates in either latent space (via a VAE) or pixel space depending on the dataset.

## Method

AugGen trains a class-conditional diffusion model and a face recognition discriminator from scratch on the same real dataset, then introduces a 'class mixing' sampling strategy: new condition vectors c* = αc^i + βc^j are constructed by linearly combining the one-hot vectors of two randomly selected source classes. A grid search over α, β ∈ [0.1, 1.1] identifies optimal mixing weights by maximizing a combined metric of inter-class dissimilarity (cosine distance between generated mixed-class features and source-class features, as measured by the pre-trained discriminator) and intra-class similarity (consistency among K repeated samples under the same c*). The resulting synthetic 'new class' images—visually distinct from both source identities yet coherent within themselves—serve as hard examples that increase inter-class separation and intra-class compactness when the discriminator is retrained on the union of real and augmented data. No auxiliary models, external datasets, or pre-trained generators are used at any stage.

## Results

Using CASIA-WebFace as the source dataset, AugGen (D^aug) achieves 29.40% TAR@FPR=1e-6 on IJB-B and 45.15% on IJB-C in fully synthetic training, outperforming DCFace (22.48% / 35.27%) and IDiff-Face (26.84% / 41.75%) while using no auxiliary models. In mixed training (real + synthetic), AugGen yields 2.61% TAR@FPR=1e-6 on IJB-B and 4.36% on IJB-C, substantially exceeding DCFace (0.26% / 0.18%) and IDiff-Face (0.89% / 0.70%). With the smaller WebFace160K dataset, AugGen improves IJB-C TAR@FPR=1e-6 from 70.37% to 78.58% (+8.2%) and IJB-B from 32.13% to 36.62% (+4.5%), surpassing even the IR101 architectural upgrade (72.56% / 34.84%). Overall, the paper reports 1–12% absolute gains across 8 FR benchmarks, with the largest improvements at low FPR thresholds where real-world deployment is most sensitive.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DCFace [25], AugGen wins decisively in mixed training: on IJB-B@FPR=1e-6, AugGen achieves 2.61% vs. DCFace's 0.26%, and on IJB-C@FPR=1e-6, 4.36% vs. 0.18%—a >10× margin. In fully synthetic training, AugGen also leads DCFace on IJB-B (29.40 vs. 22.48) and IJB-C (45.15 vs. 35.27) at FPR=1e-6, though DCFace retains an edge on Avg-H (91.56 vs. 88.78). Against IDiff-Face [5], AugGen is superior in mixed training on all IJB-B/C low-FPR thresholds (e.g., B-1e-6: 2.61 vs. 0.89; C-1e-5: 18.58 vs. 7.46) and in fully synthetic training on IJB-B/C at FPR=1e-6, while IDiff-Face slightly edges out on Avg-H in the mixed setting (94.86 vs. 94.66). DigiFace1M [3] and RealDigiFace [38] are competitive only in fully synthetic training; AugGen surpasses both on IJB-B/C at low FPRs and on TinyFace rank-1 (52.33 vs. 32.30 / 42.64). Critically, AugGen requires no auxiliary networks or external datasets (Aux = N), whereas all four baselines rely on auxiliary models, external face datasets, or pre-trained FR backbones for their generation pipeline.

## Relation to Prior Work

AugGen departs from the dominant paradigm in synthetic face data generation (DCFace, IDiff-Face, DigiFace1M, ID3) where large external datasets and auxiliary FR/demographic networks are required to produce training images. Instead, it trains a class-conditional diffusion model and a margin-loss discriminator jointly on a single target dataset from scratch, then uses a simple linear interpolation of one-hot class conditions to synthesize 'mixed' identities that act as hard negatives. This positions the work as an augmentation strategy rather than a dataset-replacement strategy, building on EDM/latent-diffusion training procedures but repurposing them for in-distribution data expansion guided by the discriminator's own feature geometry.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is that a lab with a modest, privacy-constrained face dataset can close much of the gap to large-scale training without collecting additional real images or licensing external data, and the resulting IR50 model is cheaper to deploy than the IR101 it outperforms. Key limitations include the one-time cost of training the generator from scratch (≈42 GPU-hours on H100) and the current restriction to face recognition with one-hot class conditions. Open questions the authors flag include whether soft-label margin losses can exploit the α/β mixing weights more directly, whether specific class-pair selections matter, and whether the approach transfers to other multi-class recognition or generative architectures beyond diffusion.
