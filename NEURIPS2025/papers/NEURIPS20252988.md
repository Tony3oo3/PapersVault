## Problem

Traditional utility-based discrete choice models rely on pre-specified functional forms that cannot capture individual heterogeneity, while pure LLM prompting lacks structural constraints and defaults to universally salient factors, ignoring personal preferences and constraints. No existing framework simultaneously discovers interpretable group-level utility structures and adapts them to individual-level semantic context, leaving a gap between population-optimal predictions and actual individual choices in high-stakes domains like vaccine uptake and travel mode selection.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252988/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes structured choice attributes (cost, travel time, etc.) per alternative, individual demographic and contextual data, plus two curated libraries (a domain concept library and a symbolic building-block library) that condition the LLM's generation. The two stages are sequentially coupled: Stage 1 outputs a symbolic utility function per demographic group, which then conditions Stage 2's template generation. The final output is a per-individual choice prediction with a full probability distribution over alternatives, alongside the interpretable symbolic formula and natural-language semantic template that drove the prediction.

## Method

ATHENA is a two-stage framework. Stage 1 (Group-Level Symbolic Utility Discovery) runs an LLM-driven iterative symbolic regression: at each iteration the LLM samples K candidate utility functions conditioned on the group profile, domain concepts, symbolic operators, and a feedback set containing the best- and worst-performing functions from the prior round; candidates are scored by negative log-likelihood under a multinomial logit choice model, and the search converges when loss improvement falls below a threshold. Stage 2 (Individual-Level Semantic Adaptation) uses the discovered group utility f_g* to seed an initial natural-language semantic template via the LLM, then refines it per individual using TextGrad's textual-gradient descent on the individual's choice data. At prediction time, the LLM conditions on both the symbolic utility scores (providing structural grounding) and the personalized semantic template (encoding individual preferences), producing a constrained, interpretable choice probability. Ablations confirm the stages are complementary: symbolic discovery alone captures group regularities but misses individual nuance, while unguided semantic adaptation converges to local optima without the symbolic starting point.

## Results

On the Swissmetro travel-mode choice task, ATHENA (GPT-4o-mini) achieves 81.3% accuracy, 76.6% F1, and 0.883 AUC, outperforming the strongest ML baselines (Random Forest/XGBoost at ~71% Acc, 70.5% F1) by roughly 6–10 percentage points. On the COVID-19 Vaccine choice task, ATHENA reaches 73.5% accuracy, 71.6% F1, and 0.870 AUC, surpassing the best ML baseline (Logistic Regression at 65% Acc, 66.9% F1) by ~8.5 points in accuracy. ATHENA's cross-entropy is slightly higher than tree-based ML methods (e.g., 1.09 vs. 0.70 for XGBoost on Swissmetro), which the authors attribute to ATHENA producing more calibrated, less overconfident probability distributions that better reflect genuine decision uncertainty.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ATHENA (GPT-4o-mini) decisively outperforms all LLM-based prompting baselines: zero-shot, zero-shot-CoT, few-shot, and TextGrad-tuned LLMs all score below 65% accuracy on Swissmetro, while ATHENA reaches 81.3% (a gap of 16+ points). Against classical discrete-choice models (MNL, CLogit, Latent Class MNL), ATHENA gains 20+ points in accuracy on Swissmetro and 32+ points on Vaccine, where MNL and CLogit collapse to ~41.5% accuracy. Among ML classifiers, Random Forest and XGBoost are the closest competitors on Swissmetro (71% Acc, 70.5% F1, 0.881 AUC), but ATHENA still leads by ~10 points in accuracy and ~6 points in F1 while matching their AUC. On the Vaccine task, Logistic Regression (65% Acc, 66.9% F1) and TabNet (66.5% Acc, 66.8% F1) are the strongest ML baselines; ATHENA surpasses them by 8.5 and 6.7 points in accuracy respectively. The one metric where ATHENA trails is cross-entropy: XGBoost achieves CE of 0.704 on Swissmetro versus ATHENA's 1.086, which the authors interpret as ATHENA's more conservative (less overconfident) probability calibration rather than a deficiency.

## Relation to Prior Work

ATHENA sits at the intersection of three research threads: classical Random Utility Maximization (RUM) and discrete choice models (MNL, CLogit, Latent Class MNL), LLM-augmented symbolic regression (LLM-SR, LASR, DiSciPLE), and prompt optimization via textual gradients (TextGrad). It departs from traditional DCMs by replacing pre-specified utility function forms with LLM-discovered symbolic expressions, and from pure LLM prompting approaches (zero-shot, few-shot, CoT) by grounding predictions in an explicit, interpretable utility structure. The key architectural departure is the two-stage sequential design: Stage 1 discovers group-level symbolic utility functions via iterative LLM sampling with evolutionary feedback, and Stage 2 refines individual-level semantic templates via TextGrad, a combination not present in any single prior work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a principled path toward interpretable, personalized decision modeling that goes beyond both black-box ML classifiers and rigid parametric utility models, which is relevant for high-stakes domains like public health and transportation policy. However, several limitations temper the impact: results are based on single-seed runs over small subsets (500 travelers, 300 respondents), the group-level assumption may fail for highly heterogeneous populations, and the computational cost of iterative TextGrad refinement limits scalability. Open questions include whether the symbolic-utility + semantic-adaptation decomposition generalizes to domains with more than three choices, whether the discovered symbolic fragments remain stable across larger and more diverse populations, and whether the conservative probability calibration (higher CE) is a genuine advantage or a limitation in deployment settings requiring confident predictions.
