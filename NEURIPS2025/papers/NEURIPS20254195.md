## Problem

Pretrained LLMs exhibit substantial variability in layer-wise training quality, yet existing approaches to estimate this quality rely on model-centric heuristics (spectral statistics, outlier detection, uniform allocation) that ignore the influence of training data. Influence Functions, which quantify how individual training points affect model loss, have not been formally applied to evaluate the quality or importance of individual layers in deep networks, leaving a gap in data-driven layer quality estimation.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pretrained Transformer-based LLM, a training set, and a validation set as input, computing per-layer gradients and Hessian-restricted influence scores for each Transformer block. It produces a vector of layer-wise influence scores that are task-specific (the same model yields different scores per dataset), which are then mapped via task-specific transformation functions into either an expert allocation vector (for LoRA-MoE) or per-layer sparsity ratios (for pruning). The IF computation uses the DataInf approximation for tractable Hessian inversion at scale.

## Method

LAYERIF localizes Influence Function computation to each Transformer layer by restricting gradients and the Hessian to that layer's parameters, then computes the layer-specific influence of each training point on validation loss. Positive influence scores are aggregated across training samples to yield a scalar quality score per layer, where lower scores indicate greater training stability. For expert allocation, raw scores are sign-inverted, power-transformed with a tunable exponent β to control allocation sharpness, normalized to a target expert budget, and distributed via floor-plus-remainder to guarantee at least one expert per layer. For pruning, scores are absolute-valued, min-max normalized, smoothed with a Savitzky-Golay filter to suppress local noise, and mapped through a normalized linear function to per-layer sparsity ratios that satisfy a global sparsity constraint. The framework is agnostic to the specific IF estimator and model architecture, and produces dataset-specific allocations that adapt to task affinity rather than applying a single model-only heuristic.

## Results

For layer-wise expert allocation in LoRA-MoE on Mistral-7B-v0.1, LAYERIF (TOP 25%) achieves 81.76% average zero-shot accuracy across MRPC, CoLA, ScienceQA, CommonsenseQA, and OpenBookQA, outperforming the best baseline MOLA-8642 (80.97%) by 1.61% relative. For layer sparsity allocation at 50% pruning on Mistral-7B-v0.1, LAYERIF achieves 58.81% average zero-shot accuracy across BoolQ, HellaSwag, Winogrande, RTE, OpenBookQA, ARC Easy, and ARC Challenge, surpassing AlphaPruning (58.43%) by 0.90% and OWL (58.30%) by 1.03%. Statistical significance testing (t-test, p < 0.05) confirms improvements over AlphaLoRA and MOLA for expert allocation, and over AlphaPruning and UNIFORM for pruning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LAYERIF consistently outperforms all four baselines across both downstream tasks. For expert allocation, LAYERIF (TOP 25%) achieves 82.27% average accuracy, beating AlphaLoRA (80.51%) by 1.76 points and the best MOLA variant (MOLA-8642, 80.97%) by 1.30 points; notably, MOLA-2468 underperforms severely at 74.77% average. For pruning at 50% sparsity, LAYERIF (58.81% average) surpasses AlphaPruning (58.43%) by 0.38 points and OWL (58.30%) by 0.51 points, with the largest gap appearing under SparseGPT (60.61% vs. 60.07% and 59.99% respectively). A key differentiator is that LAYERIF produces dataset-specific allocations (e.g., different expert distributions for MRPC vs. OpenBookQA), whereas AlphaLoRA and MOLA apply fixed, model-only heuristics across all tasks. The reversed-allocation ablation (pruning well-trained layers aggressively) drops accuracy by 1.17 points at 70% sparsity, confirming that the IF signal is directionally correct rather than an artifact.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Influence Function framework (Koh & Liang, 2017) and its scalable approximation DataInf (Kwon et al., 2024) into a new application: per-layer quality estimation in LLMs. It departs from prior layer-quality methods that rely on model-centric heuristics—spectral density decay (AlphaPruning, Lu et al. 2024), outlier activation distributions (OWL, Yin et al. 2024), or group-based heuristics (MOLA, Gao et al. 2024)—by introducing a data-driven signal that captures how sensitive each layer's loss is to individual training examples. The sparsity allocation mapping builds directly on AlphaPruning's normalized linear scheme, and the LoRA-MoE routing follows MoLA's top-K architecture, but the allocation decisions themselves are driven by IF scores rather than weight statistics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical contribution is that IF-based layer quality is task-specific: the same pretrained model yields different layer importance patterns for different downstream tasks, enabling targeted expert allocation and pruning that model-centric methods cannot provide. However, the gains are modest (1.61% for MoE, 0.90% for pruning), the method is computationally expensive due to Hessian inversion, and it relies on an implicit convexity assumption that may not hold in LLMs. Open questions include whether adaptive IF sample-selection thresholds can further improve signal-to-noise, whether the approach scales to 70B+ models within practical training budgets, and whether Hessian-free approximations can be made to capture layer-level structure (current evidence suggests they cannot).
