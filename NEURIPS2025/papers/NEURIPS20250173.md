## Problem

Existing point tracking methods are either offline (requiring full video access) or semi-online (requiring multi-frame windows), which introduces latency, high memory consumption, and inability to respond to newly emerging pixels in real time. This limits deployment on resource-constrained edge devices for embodied systems, medical vision, and robotics. The paper asks whether SOTA tracking accuracy can be achieved in a strictly online, per-frame setting with a lightweight model suitable for edge inference.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250173/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For point tracking, LBM takes a stream of RGB frames at 384×512 resolution and N query points (2D coordinates) as input, producing per-frame 2D position offsets (relative to a final reference point) and visibility/confidence scores for each tracked pixel. For object tracking, it additionally accepts M detection boxes per frame, samples fine-grained pixels within each box, and outputs instance-to-instance association scores via pixel-level spatial correspondence. The visual encoder uses only the first three ResNet18 layers (stride-4, 256-dim features), and the model maintains a rolling memory of N_s streaming and collision distributions rather than buffering raw frames.

## Method

LBM adapts the Lattice Boltzmann Method from fluid dynamics to pixel tracking by treating each query pixel as a fluid particle with a d-dimensional distribution function, evolved through collision (local neighborhood interaction via deformable attention over a learnable offset δ) and streaming (temporal propagation across N_s historical steps). A 3-layer predict-update Transformer alternates between predicting the current distribution from memorized streaming/collision histories and updating it with online visual features via correlation-map-guided deformable attention, progressively reducing reference points from K=9 to K=1 across layers. For object tracking, LBM decomposes each detected object into randomly sampled fine-grained pixels, tracks them independently, and dynamically prunes pixels that persist outside the bounding box while replenishing new inliers, enabling robust association that survives detection failures. The entire model uses 18M parameters and achieves 14.3 FPS on an NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX (49 FPS with TensorRT FP16 quantization), outperforming heavier offline and window-based methods on TAP-Vid, RoboTAP, TAO, OVT-B, and BFT benchmarks.

## Results

On TAP-Vid DAVIS, LBM (18M params) achieves AJ 65.1, δ_avg 77.5, and OA 89.5, matching or exceeding most window-based and offline methods while using only 37% of Track-On's parameters. On RoboTAP, LBM reaches AJ 61.4, δ_avg 75.8, OA 87.4, outperforming CoTracker3 (25M) and TAPTRv2 (41M). For object tracking, LBM attains 45.3 TETA on TAO (training-free), 56.8 TETA on OVT-B (+9.2 over OVTrack+), and 74.5 OWTA on BFT (+2.0 over NetTrack). On the NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX edge platform, LBM runs at 14.3 FPS (49 FPS with TensorRT FP16), a 3.9× speed advantage over Track-On.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among online methods, LBM (18M) is closest to Track-On (49M): LBM edges out Track-On on DAVIS AJ (65.1 vs 65.0) but trails on δ_avg (77.5 vs 78.0) and OA (89.5 vs 90.8), while Track-On leads on Kinetics and RoboTAP; however, LBM achieves this with only 37% of the parameters and a 3.9× speed advantage on edge hardware. Against window-based methods, LBM surpasses CoTracker3 (25M) on DAVIS AJ (65.1 vs 64.5) and RoboTAP across all three metrics (61.4/75.8/87.4 vs 60.8/73.7/87.1), and beats TAPTRv2 (41M) on DAVIS AJ (65.1 vs 63.5) and Kinetics AJ (53.4 vs 49.7). LBM also outperforms offline methods TAPIR (31M) and LocoTrack (12M) on DAVIS AJ (65.1 vs 56.2/62.9) while matching or exceeding them on RoboTAP. For object tracking, LBM in a training-free setting achieves 45.3 TETA on TAO, surpassing all training-free baselines (NetTrack 33.0, OC-SORT 28.6) and approaching the domain-finetuned MASA (46.3); on OVT-B it leads OVTrack+ by +9.2 TETA (56.8 vs 47.6), and on BFT it exceeds NetTrack by +2.0 OWTA (74.5 vs 72.5) for highly dynamic avian objects.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Lattice Boltzmann Method from computational fluid dynamics—where discrete distribution functions evolve via collision and streaming operations—as a conceptual and architectural template for pixel tracking. The collision step is instantiated as deformable attention over learnable spatial neighborhoods, and the streaming step as temporal propagation of distribution functions across frames, replacing the iterative cost-volume refinement used by offline methods (TAPIR, LocoTrack) and the window-based processing of CoTracker/TAPTR. For object tracking, the association metric is derived from NetTrack's net-based similarity, extended with detection-score and label-penalty reweighting. The multi-layer predict-update Transformer replaces the multi-iteration refinement of prior methods, enabling true per-frame online inference.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in achieving SOTA online point tracking with only 18M parameters at 14.3 FPS on a Jetson Orin NX edge device (49 FPS with TensorRT FP16), making real-time pixel tracking viable for embodied robotics, medical vision, and animal behavior analysis without cloud infrastructure. The object tracking results—SOTA on TAO, OVT-B, and BFT without any domain-specific tracking fine-tuning—demonstrate that fine-grained pixel-level association can outperform holistic detection-based matching, particularly under detection failures. Open questions include whether the inherent locality of collision-streaming limits long-term tracking accuracy, whether random pixel sampling within bounding boxes can be replaced by segmentation masks to reduce background contamination, and whether the approach generalizes to 3D or multi-view tracking settings.
