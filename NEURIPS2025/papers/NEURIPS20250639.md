## Problem

Despite LLMs' demonstrated success in mathematical and logical reasoning, their ability to reason over ranked preferences and execute structured combinatorial algorithms (e.g., deferred acceptance) remains largely unexplored. This paper asks whether current LLMs can reliably compute stable matchings, detect and resolve instability, and answer fine-grained preference queries, and how performance scales with market size.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250639/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each instance provides a complete, strict preference profile with n agents per side (n ∈ {10, 20, 50}) sampled from either Impartial Culture (independent uniform rankings) or Master-list (one side shares a single common ranking) distributions, followed by a task-specific instruction. Outputs must conform to a predefined JSON format; models are granted two retries if the initial response is invalid (violating one-to-one constraints), partial (leaving agents unmatched), or malformed. The four tasks produce different outputs: a stable one-to-one matching, a corrected stable matching, a stability/instability verdict with identified blocking pairs, or a natural-language answer to a preference query.

## Method

The paper introduces a 2,850-question benchmark spanning four tasks (stable-matching generation, instability resolution, instability detection, and three-level preference reasoning) across three difficulty levels and two preference distributions, evaluated on seven LLMs categorized as basic, reasoning, and advanced-reasoning. For the fine-tuning component, the authors perform LoRA (rank 32) on three open-source reasoning models (DeepSeek-8B, DeepSeek-14B, Qwen-QwQ-32B) using 10,000 synthetically generated chain-of-thought traces for the generation task and 9,000 for preference reasoning, trained for one epoch with AdamW-8bit at lr 2×10⁻⁴. The key empirical finding is that fine-tuning achieves 100% stability on Easy and Medium instances for Qwen-QwQ-32B—outperforming all advanced-reasoning models—yet fails entirely on Hard instances, revealing that the bottleneck is not algorithmic knowledge but the compounding of small errors over long multi-step preference comparisons in large contexts.

## Results

Across all four matching-market tasks, Gemini-2.5-Pro achieves the best overall performance: 68.33% stable solutions (Generating Stable Solutions), 7.16% instability rate, 92.38% accuracy on instability detection, and 99.67% on preference reasoning. DeepSeek-R1 follows closely (64.22% stable, 12.35% IR, 88.19% detection, 96.22% preference), while basic models (Llama-3.3-70B, Gemini-2.0-Flash) produce fewer than 3% stable solutions even on Easy instances. A critical finding is that all models—regardless of reasoning capability—drop to near-zero stable-solution rates on Hard instances (n=50), and LoRA fine-tuning of Qwen-QwQ-32B reaches 100% on Easy/Medium but still 0% on Hard.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Gemini-2.5-Pro (closed-source) leads on every metric, achieving 68.33% stable solutions and 7.16% instability rate, outperforming DeepSeek-R1 (64.22%, 12.35%) and o3-mini (58.00%, 19.98%) on stable-matching generation. Notably, the open-source DeepSeek-R1 broadly outperforms the closed-source o3-mini on most tasks, though o3-mini edges it on instability detection (86.67% vs. 88.19%—actually DeepSeek-R1 wins here too) and preference reasoning (98.78% vs. 96.22%). Among reasoning models, the smaller Qwen-QwQ-32B (32B) outperforms the larger DeepSeek-70B (70B) on stable-solution generation (35.67% vs. 26.20%), demonstrating that combinatorial reasoning does not scale with parameter count. Basic models (Llama-3.3-70B, Gemini-2.0-Flash) are effectively non-functional, producing <3% stable solutions and exhibiting systematic hallucination of blocking pairs during instability detection. After LoRA fine-tuning, Qwen-QwQ-32B reaches 100% stable solutions on Easy and Medium instances, surpassing all advanced-reasoning models (which achieve 58–68% averaged across difficulties), but all models remain at 0% on Hard (n=50) instances.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions matching markets as a new testbed for evaluating LLM algorithmic reasoning, extending the line of work on LLM reasoning benchmarks (MATH, SWE-Bench, logic games) into combinatorial domains with ranked preferences. It draws on the stable-marriage and deferred-acceptance literature (Gale-Shapley, Irving) for problem structure and ground-truth verification, and on recent work on LLMs in economic settings (Fish et al., Horton) for motivation. The fine-tuning experiments build on Markeeva et al.'s CLRS benchmark showing that LoRA can teach small models to execute textbook algorithms, but push this into the harder setting of preference-based combinatorial search where the algorithm's state space grows with input size.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that even top advanced-reasoning models (Gemini-2.5-Pro, DeepSeek-R1) achieve 0% stable-solution generation on Hard IC instances (n=50) and that fine-tuning hits a hard wall at the same scale raises concrete concerns for deploying LLMs in preference-sensitive decision-making (recruitment screening, ride-hailing, healthcare matching). The 'small errors multiply' observation generalizes beyond matching markets to any multi-step combinatorial procedure over long inputs. Open questions include whether full-parameter fine-tuning, GRPO-style reinforcement learning, or decomposition into smaller sub-steps can break the scaling barrier, and how results change with incomplete preferences, ties, and capacity constraints that characterize real-world markets.
