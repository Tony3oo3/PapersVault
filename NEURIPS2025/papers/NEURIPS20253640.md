## Problem

LLMs are predominantly evaluated on Standard American English, yet English is spoken in a wide range of regional dialects and by non-native speakers whose usage differs systematically. This narrow evaluation focus raises fairness concerns because LLMs underperform on non-standard varieties, leading to unequal benefits for global users. Existing variety-specific datasets are limited in size, variety coverage, and task diversity, making comprehensive robustness assessment infeasible without a scalable transformation framework.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes SAE sentences from six benchmark QA datasets (MMLU, ARC, TruthfulQA, GSM8K, HellaSwag, WinoGrande) along with a target variety specification: either one of 18 regional dialects or a combination of CEFR level (A or B) and one of 10 L1 languages (Arabic, Chinese-Mandarin, French, German, Italian, Japanese, Portuguese, Russian, Spanish, Turkish). It produces transformed sentences in the target variety, with over 80% of samples modified on average. For ESL English, an additional vocabulary simplification step replaces higher-level words using a 23,411-word Oxford 5000 CEFR mapping, allowing up to 15% higher-level vocabulary to reflect realistic learner proficiency.

## Method

Trans-EnV is a three-stage pipeline: (1) data collection, where dialect features are extracted from the eWAVE atlas (235 features across 77 varieties, with KNN clustering to select 18 English dialects) and ESL features are derived from the English Grammar Profile (CEFR-level 'can-do' descriptors) plus L1-specific features identified via statistical t-tests on three learner corpora (CLC-FCE, ICLE, EFCamDat); (2) guideline generation, where GPT-4 produces step-by-step transformation guidelines for each linguistic feature, each consisting of a Qualification step (determining applicability) and an Application step (specifying the edit); (3) sequential transformation, where a feature transformer (Gemma-2-27B-Instruct) applies features one at a time in random order, and a semantic checker (LLaMA-3.3-70B-Instruct) verifies meaning preservation after each edit, discarding transformations that alter semantics. The framework is validated through human evaluation (88% validity across six transformer models) and consultation with a second-language-acquisition specialist.

## Results

Across six closed-form QA benchmarks transformed into 38 English varieties, all seven evaluated LLMs show significant performance degradation on non-standard varieties, with maximum drops of 12.5% for dialects and 46.3% for ESL English (e.g., Qwen2.5-72B on WinoGrande drops from 83.7% SAE to 39.7% mean on CEFR A ESL). Reasoning-based tasks are more sensitive to linguistic variation, with average drops of 22.6% for ESL English versus 10.9% for knowledge-based tasks. Models with strong reasoning capabilities (DeepSeek-R1-70B, o4-mini) exhibit greater robustness, and CEFR A varieties consistently yield lower scores than CEFR B in 4 out of 6 datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates seven state-of-the-art LLMs rather than comparing a proposed method against baselines, so the comparison is across models on the same transformed benchmarks. On dialect varieties, performance drops are modest (≤12.5%), with Gemini-2.5-Pro maintaining the highest absolute scores (e.g., 88.7% mean on MMLU vs. 90.9% SAE) and GPT-4o-mini showing the largest relative drops (e.g., 97.2%→91.2% on ARC). On ESL English, all models degrade substantially, but reasoning-specialized models (DeepSeek-R1-70B, o4-mini) are notably more robust: on WinoGrande CEFR A, o4-mini retains 84.3% (above its 79.4% SAE score) while Qwen2.5-72B collapses to 39.7%. Among ESL L1s, Arabic and Turkish consistently underperform across models, while French and Italian show the smallest drops, which the authors attribute to pretraining data availability (Arabic/Turkish at ~1.7–2.0% in mC4/OSCAR vs. French/Italian at 2.4–8.9%). On open-ended tasks (IFEval, AlpacaFarm, MT-Bench), the same pattern holds: CEFR B outperforms CEFR A, and Irish/Newfoundland/Welsh English dialects show the largest drops, mirroring closed-form results.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from three prior approaches to English variety transformation—manual curation, pure LLM generation, and rule-based rewriting—by combining expert-curated linguistic resources (eWAVE's 235 features, CEFR/EGP descriptors, ESL learner corpora) with LLM-driven transformation for both scalability and linguistic validity. It extends the line of work showing LLMs underperform on non-standard English (e.g., Ziems et al. on dialect NLU, Gupta et al. on AAVE, Lin et al. on dialect reasoning) by providing a systematic, reproducible framework that covers 38 varieties across 6 benchmark datasets rather than a single task or a handful of dialects. The KNN clustering over eWAVE to select 18 dialects and the statistical t-test extraction of L1-specific features from ESL corpora represent methodological contributions not present in prior variety-adaptation work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding of up to 46.3% accuracy degradation on ESL English varieties, with reasoning tasks disproportionately affected (22.6% vs. 10.9% for knowledge tasks), provides concrete evidence that current LLMs are not linguistically fair for global users. The observation that CEFR A (simpler) sometimes yields lower scores than CEFR B challenges the intuition that simpler language is easier for models, and the correlation between pretraining data availability and variety performance points toward a data-driven explanation. Key limitations include English-only scope, QA-task-only evaluation, and absence of error bars; extending to other languages, task types, and investigating whether reasoning training genuinely improves robustness (vs. merely correlating with it) remain open questions.
