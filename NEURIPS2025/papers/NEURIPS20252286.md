## Problem

Sharpness-Aware Minimization (SAM) exhibits the m-sharpness phenomenon—its generalization improves monotonically as the micro-batch size m for computing perturbations decreases—yet this behavior lacked a rigorous theoretical explanation. Moreover, m-SAM is inherently sequential across micro-batches, making it non-parallelizable and introducing substantial computational overhead in distributed training, despite smaller m yielding better generalization.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252286/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a mini-batch of training samples and current model parameters, and additionally requires per-sample gradient-norm estimates computed via a single forward pass with Rademacher random perturbations (a zeroth-order finite-difference estimator requiring no backpropagation). The output is the updated parameter vector after one SAM-style perturbation-and-descent step, where the perturbation direction is determined by a weighted sum of per-sample gradients rather than a uniform average.

## Method

The paper develops a two-parameter SDE framework that jointly tracks the learning rate η and perturbation radius ρ to arbitrary expansion orders via a Dynkin semigroup expansion, yielding closed-form drift terms for n-USAM, mini-batch USAM, and m-USAM. The key theoretical finding is that stochastic gradient noise (SGN) induces an implicit variance-based sharpness regularization in the drift whose coefficient scales as ρ/(2m), so smaller m amplifies regularization of the SGN covariance (which near minima approximates the Hessian trace). Motivated by this, RW-SAM replaces the uniform perturbation with a Gibbs-distribution reweighting p_i ∝ exp(λ‖∇f_i(x)‖) that assigns higher importance to samples with larger SGN magnitudes, regularized by an entropy term to prevent weight collapse. The per-sample gradient norms are estimated efficiently via a Rademacher finite-difference estimator (Q=1 forward pass), and the entire procedure remains fully parallelizable across devices, matching m-SAM's generalization at m=64 with only ~1/6 additional overhead over vanilla SAM.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, RW-SAM achieves 96.24% (ResNet-18), 96.34% (ResNet-50), and 97.11% (WideResNet-28-10) test accuracy, consistently outperforming both SGD and SAM by 0.2–0.6 percentage points. On CIFAR-100, the gains are larger: RW-SAM reaches 80.83% with ResNet-50 versus 80.31% for SAM and 79.55% for SGD. On ImageNet-1K (ResNet-50), RW-SAM attains 77.37% top-1 accuracy versus 77.16% for SAM and 76.67% for SGD. On the GLUE benchmark (DistilBERT fine-tuning), RW-SAM achieves an average score of 0.809, surpassing SAM (0.800) and AdamW (0.804).

## Baselines & Comparisons

RW-SAM is compared against two baselines: SAM (Foret et al., 2021) and AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019, used as the base optimizer in the GLUE experiments). Against SAM, RW-SAM wins on every dataset and model configuration: +0.25 to +0.34 pp on CIFAR-10, +0.41 to +0.52 pp on CIFAR-100, +0.21 pp on ImageNet-1K, +0.18 to +0.26 pp on ViT fine-tuning, and +0.009 average on GLUE. The advantage is most pronounced on CIFAR-100 with ResNet-50 (+0.52 pp) and under high label noise (80% noise: RW-SAM 53.17% vs. SAM 37.69%, a 15.5 pp gap). Against AdamW on GLUE, RW-SAM achieves 0.809 average versus 0.804, with the largest per-task gain on CoLA (0.560 vs. 0.538). RW-SAM also matches the generalization of m-SAM at m=64 while incurring only ~1/6 additional forward-pass overhead versus m-SAM's nearly 2× training cost, making it the more practical choice for parallel training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the SDE-based analysis of SAM initiated by Compagnoni et al. (2023) and Luo et al. (2025), which could only track the learning rate η to first order, by developing a two-parameter framework that jointly tracks both η and the perturbation radius ρ to arbitrary expansion orders. Building on the stochastic modified equations framework of Li et al. (2019), the authors derive closed-form drift terms for n-USAM, mini-batch USAM, and m-USAM, revealing that the trace of the stochastic gradient noise (SGN) covariance enters the drift with a coefficient inversely proportional to the micro-batch size m. This provides the first rigorous mechanistic explanation for the m-sharpness phenomenon observed empirically by Foret et al. (2021) and Andriushchenko and Flammarion (2022), whose earlier hypotheses had been invalidated. The proposed RW-SAM method departs from the sequential micro-batch structure of m-SAM by replacing it with a Gibbs-weighted perturbation that is fully parallelizable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Resolving the m-sharpness mystery is practically important because the local-perturbation-per-device implementation (which is exactly m-SAM with m = per-device batch size) is the standard way SAM is deployed in multi-GPU training; understanding why it outperforms the 'correct' full-batch n-SAM validates and guides large-scale SAM deployment. The SGN-covariance-as-implicit-Hessian-regularization connection also bridges SAM to the broader flat-minima literature (Keskar et al., 2017; Xie et al., 2020), suggesting that any optimizer that amplifies per-sample noise variance may enjoy similar generalization benefits. Limitations include the reliance on an SGN-orthogonality-to-full-gradient assumption for the RW-SAM derivation, the extra forward pass for gradient-norm estimation, and the fact that the normalized SAM analysis yields no closed-form drift (only inequalities via Proposition 3.9). Open questions include whether the 1/m scaling of the regularization term persists in non-convex, high-dimensional regimes far from convergence, and whether RW-SAM's advantage over m-SAM holds at larger scales (e.g., multi-node training).
