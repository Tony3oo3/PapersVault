## Problem

Diffusion policies for robotic manipulation require many sequential denoising steps (often 50–100 NFE) for inference, creating unacceptable latency for real-time control. The paper identifies that standard inference heuristics borrowed from image generation—particularly the clipping operation and full-scale noise injection—introduce out-of-distribution intermediate states that actively degrade sample quality in the structured, low-dimensional action spaces of robotics. The central question is whether an off-the-shelf diffusion policy can be accelerated to as few as 2 NFE while matching or exceeding the performance of full-step baselines.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a window of h_O past robot observations (proprioceptive states) and outputs a sequence of h_A future actions, where the action space is embedded in R^d and rescaled to [−1,1]^d. Action horizons range from 24 to 200 steps, and the method operates on an already-trained 65M-parameter UNet diffusion policy without any architectural modification or retraining. The population-based genetic denoising processes P candidate action sequences in parallel per step, with the final output being a single denoised action vector selected from the surviving trajectory.

## Method

The approach combines two complementary mechanisms. First, simple schedule modifications—reducing the noise injection scale γ (e.g., to 0.2), starting denoising from t_δ < T to skip uninformative early steps, and using fewer total steps—exploit the fact that in low-dimensional robotic action distributions, less injected noise reduces clipping-induced OoD artifacts and improves the noise estimator's accuracy. Second, the Genetic Diffusion Policy (GDP) maintains a population of P candidate denoising trajectories; at each step, a fitness score measuring OoD risk (either a clip-based score measuring how many coordinates saturate at the cube boundary, or a Stein-based score measuring the norm of the predicted noise) is computed for each sample, and a temperature-weighted multinomial selection retains S survivors that are duplicated to refill the population before the next denoising step. This population-based filtering preferentially preserves in-distribution trajectories, reducing mode collapse and improving sample quality especially at very low step counts (2–5 NFE), where standard DDPM/DDIM samplers fail catastrophically.

## Results

On the Adroit Hand benchmark (4 tasks, 100 seeds), GDP with 2 NFE achieves normalized success rates of 1.00 (Hammer), 0.98 (Relocate), 0.91 (Pen), and 1.00 (Door), outperforming DDPM+Schedule+Best γ (0.98/0.92/0.89/1.00) and the Shortcut model (0.88/1.00/0.81/0.94) at the same 2-step budget. At 5 NFE, GDP reaches 1.00/0.99/0.91/1.00 versus DDPM's 0.91/0.91/0.85/1.00 (γ=1) and Shortcut's 0.88/1.00/0.81/0.94. On Robomimic (5 tasks, 500 seeds), GDP at 2 NFE matches or slightly exceeds DDPM+Schedule and DDIM across all tasks, with the largest gap on Transport (0.58 vs. 0.595/0.604) and ToolHang (0.49 vs. 0.481/0.480). The paper reports up to 20% performance gains over standard diffusion policies with significantly fewer inference steps across over 2 million evaluations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DDPM (the standard diffusion policy), GDP at 2 NFE dramatically outperforms: where vanilla DDPM (γ=1) collapses to near-zero success (0.00–0.13), GDP achieves 0.91–1.00, a gap of roughly 90+ percentage points. Even the best DDPM variant (adapted schedule + reduced noise, γ=0.2) reaches 0.89–1.00, still trailing GDP by 2–8 points on Hammer, Relocate, and Pen. Against DDIM, GDP wins decisively at low step counts: at 2 NFE, DDIM scores 0.42 on Relocate and 0.73 on Pen versus GDP's 0.98 and 0.91; at 5 NFE the gap widens further (DDIM 0.38/0.70 vs. GDP 0.99/0.91). Against the Shortcut model (the only method supporting 1-step inference), GDP at 2 NFE matches or exceeds Shortcut on all four Adroit tasks (e.g., 1.00 vs. 0.88 on Hammer, 0.91 vs. 0.81 on Pen), while Shortcut's 1-step variant drops to 0.74–0.93. On Robomimic, GDP at 2 NFE is within 1–2 points of DDPM+Schedule and DDIM on most tasks, with the paper noting that the base model quality (conditioning) rather than the denoising process is the bottleneck for these simpler action spaces.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Diffusion Policy (Chi et al., IJRR 2023) and the DDPM/DDIM/EDM inference framework, but departs from the standard practice of transferring vision-domain sampling heuristics to control. Rather than training new models (as shortcut models [7] or consistency models [5,6] require), it modifies the inference schedule, noise injection scale, and introduces a population-based genetic selection over denoising trajectories. The key conceptual departure is identifying that the low-dimensional, structured nature of robotic action distributions makes standard diffusion inference suboptimal—specifically that clipping-induced OoD states and excessive noise injection degrade performance in ways that do not manifest in image generation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for real-time robotic manipulation: achieving 100% success on Adroit tasks with only 2 NFE (vs. 100 for standard DDPM) without retraining is a substantial efficiency win for deployment. The conceptual finding that reducing noise injection improves (rather than degrades) sample quality in robotics challenges the assumption that diffusion inference strategies are domain-agnostic. However, the genetic algorithm is deliberately minimal (no mutation, no cross-breeding), the theoretical analysis is explicitly preliminary, and Robomimic results show GDP does not consistently outperform a simple adapted schedule—suggesting the conditioning model, not the sampler, is the bottleneck for many tasks. Open questions include formal bounds on denoising error under noise-scale manipulation and whether richer metaheuristics (diversity control, PDE-constrained swarm optimization) yield further gains.
