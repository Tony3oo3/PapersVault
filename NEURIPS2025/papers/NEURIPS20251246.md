## Problem

Regulatory frameworks like the EU AI Act grant legal exemptions to 'open-source' foundation models, yet the definition of open-source remains ambiguous and its economic consequences are unmodeled. Existing game-theoretic models of AI openness treat the openness level as a binary choice or an exogenous fixed parameter, leaving open how a regulator's specific choice of open-source threshold and penalty shapes the strategic release decisions of general-purpose model developers and the downstream fine-tuning investments of domain specialists.

## Input / Output

The model takes six game parameters as input: initial model performance α₀, the regulator's open-source threshold θ ∈ [0,1], regulatory penalty p, reputational benefit constant ε, operation cost ratio c_ω, and the endogenous bargaining share δ. It produces closed-form equilibrium strategies for both players (ω*, α₁*, δ*), player utilities, and—critically—indifference curves in the (p, θ) plane that partition the regulatory space into regions of compliance, deadweight loss, and market withdrawal. The openness level ω is a continuous decision variable in [0,1] rather than a binary open/closed choice, and the bargaining parameter δ is solved endogenously via Nash, vertical monopoly, or egalitarian bargaining solutions via grid search.

## Method

The paper formulates a two-player sequential game between a generalist (G) who chooses a continuous openness level ω ∈ [0,1] for a pre-trained model and a specialist (D) who then invests in fine-tuning to improve performance from α₀ to α₁. The key structural insight is that openness imposes opposing cost effects on the two players: higher ω reduces D's quadratic production costs (scaled by ω⁻¹) but increases D's operation costs, while for G it increases production costs but decreases operation costs—this asymmetry is captured by decomposing cost functions into separate production and operation terms. The game is solved by backward induction, yielding closed-form best responses for D (Proposition 1) and a quadratic equation whose roots, together with boundary values {0, θ, 1}, give G's candidate optimal openness levels (Proposition 2). The regulatory penalty p enters as a binary cost p·1[ω < θ], and the paper derives a closed-form indifference curve p* = θ(δ + α₀/α₁ − ε − c_ω)α₁ that separates regulation profiles where G complies from those where G keeps the model fully closed, enabling characterization of Pareto-improving and deadweight-loss regions in (p, θ) space.

## Results

The paper derives a closed-form indifference curve separating compliance from non-compliance in the (p, θ) regulatory space: p* = θ(δ + α₀/α₁ − ε − c_ω)α₁. A concrete Pareto-improving regulation is demonstrated with α₀=0.1, c_ω=0.05, ε=0.1: the no-regulation equilibrium yields (ω*≈0, δ*=0.53, α₁*=0.102, U_G=0.048, U_D=0.048), while imposing θ=0.6 and p=0.05 shifts the equilibrium to (ω*=0.6, δ*=0.97, α₁=0.275, U_G=0.058, U_D=0.109), a ~20% gain for G and ~127% gain for D. Empirically, the model's predicted openness–performance tradeoff is validated against 15 foundation models: ELO scores range from 840 (Stable LM, 0% closed) to 1380 (Gemini, 92% closed), confirming that higher-performing models are systematically more closed.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against competing ML methods; its 'baselines' are prior theoretical frameworks. It extends Laufer et al. [26] (Fine-Tuning Games, WWW 2024), which models only performance as the generalist's decision variable, by adding a continuous openness level ω to the strategy space and a regulatory penalty term. Compared to Xu et al. [45], which assumes a single exogenous openness level imposed on the generalist and focuses on how it affects specialist fine-tuning, this paper simultaneously models how the generalist strategically adjusts openness in response to the regulatory threshold θ and penalty p. The model's equilibrium predictions qualitatively match empirical release patterns (Figure 3): lower-performing models (e.g., Llama at ELO 799) are more open (43% closed) while frontier models (Gemini at ELO 1380) are predominantly closed (92%), and within each generation, closed-weight models outperform open-weight counterparts by a persistent margin. The paper also contrasts its continuous openness spectrum with the binary open/closed framing used in prior dynamic competition models [39, 44], showing that intermediate openness levels produce distinct equilibrium outcomes not captured by binary treatments.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the fine-tuning game framework of Laufer et al. [26], which modeled bargaining between a generalist and specialist over model performance, by introducing openness as a continuous endogenous decision variable for the generalist and adding regulatory parameters (open-source threshold θ and penalty p) as exogenous game parameters. It departs from Xu et al. [45], who treated openness as a fixed exogenous level imposed on the generalist, by allowing the generalist to strategically choose where on the openness spectrum to position the model. The paper also builds on the broader literature on open-source economics [39, 5, 10] and foundation model transparency indices [38, 9] to define the openness continuum, while incorporating bargaining theory (Nash, vertical monopoly, egalitarian solutions) to model revenue-sharing between players.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a theoretical foundation for calibrating AI openness regulations, identifying a critical indifference curve in (p, θ) space that separates regimes where regulation produces Pareto improvements (low-performance models) from those where it creates pure deadweight loss (high-performance models). This gives regulators a principled basis for setting penalties and thresholds rather than relying on political compromise. Key limitations include the single generalist-specialist pair (no multi-firm competition), one-dimensional performance, and specific quadratic cost assumptions; open questions include how psychological anchoring to thresholds, competitive dynamics among multiple generalists, and multidimensional capability metrics would alter the equilibrium predictions.
