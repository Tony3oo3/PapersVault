## Problem

Traditional reward models for RLHF rely on absolute, manually-defined preference labels, which are expensive to collect at scale and produce models vulnerable to reward hacking with weak out-of-distribution generalization. The paper asks whether a criterion-agnostic, scalable pre-training objective exists for reward models analogous to next-token prediction for language models, eliminating dependence on subjective human annotations during the foundational training phase.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255665/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Unlike traditional RMs that score a single trajectory in isolation, POLAR requires a reference trajectory alongside the candidate: the input is formatted as prompt + reference <|split_token|> prompt + candidate <|reward_token|>, and a linear head reads the hidden state at the reward_token position to emit a scalar reward. The reference trajectory acts as an anchor representing the target policy, so the output score reflects the degree of alignment between the candidate and that reference rather than an absolute quality judgment.

## Method

POLAR reformulates the reward model as a policy discriminator grounded in the observation that, under KL-constrained RL, the reward is uniquely determined (up to a constant) by the log-density ratio between the optimal and initial policies. Pre-training uses a contrastive Bradley-Terry loss over a large-scale synthetic corpus: given a prompt, the RM is trained to assign higher joint reward to two trajectories sampled from the same policy than to a trajectory from a different policy, with policies drawn from a diverse pool of LLMs of varying architectures and sizes. This criterion-agnostic objective is followed by a lightweight SFT stage where human annotators rank three trajectories from the same policy, implicitly encoding human criteria into the discriminative signal. The architecture is a standard autoregressive Transformer (InternLM-2.5 backbone) with a linear prediction head, initialized from a pre-trained LLM and trained for one additional epoch of POLAR pre-training before fine-tuning.

## Results

In RLHF training across 20 benchmarks, POLAR-7B improves LLaMA3.1-8B from 47.36% (baseline) to 56.33% average, Qwen2.5-32B from 64.49% to 70.47%, InternLM3-8B from 56.49% to 63.18%, and Qwen2.5-7B from 54.95% to 60.90%. In preference evaluation on the RMB benchmark, POLAR-7B achieves 81.0% accuracy on STEM tasks and 85.5% on creative writing, surpassing the best baseline (WorldPM-72B) by 5.8% average despite being ~10× smaller. Scaling experiments confirm power-law relationships with R²=0.9886 (model size) and R²=0.9912 (compute).

## Baselines & Comparisons

POLAR-7B consistently outperforms all three baseline families across every policy model tested. Against InternLM2-Reward (7B/20B), POLAR-7B gains 8.3–12.7 points in average RLHF score (e.g., 56.33 vs. 48.06 on LLaMA3.1-8B). Against Skywork-Reward (8B/27B), POLAR-7B leads by 6.9–14.4 points (e.g., 70.47 vs. 66.64 on Qwen2.5-32B). Most notably, POLAR-7B surpasses the much larger WorldPM-72B-UltraFeedback by 6.7 points on LLaMA3.1-8B (56.33 vs. 49.64) and 3.3 points on Qwen2.5-32B (70.47 vs. 67.15), despite being roughly 10× smaller. Even POLAR-1.8B (1/40th the size of WorldPM-72B) beats it on LLaMA3.1-8B (52.71 vs. 49.64) and Qwen2.5-32B (68.55 vs. 67.15). In preference evaluation, POLAR-7B achieves an average 5.8-point improvement over WorldPM-72B, with the largest gains on STEM (+26.2 pp) and creative writing (+27.6 pp) tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm of training reward models on absolute human preference pairs (Bradley-Terry loss over labeled comparisons) by reframing the RM as a criterion-agnostic policy discriminator. Conceptually, it draws an analogy to how Next Token Prediction unified diverse NLP tasks under a single pre-training objective, proposing that policy discrimination (distinguishing trajectories from the same vs. different policies) serves as the analogous fundamental objective for reward modeling. It also extends the discriminator-based reward modeling line (GAIL, DIRECT) from single-task expert imitation to large-scale, multi-policy pre-training, and builds on the RL theory connecting reward functions to KL-constrained policy optimization (Eq. 4) to motivate the distributional alignment formulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

POLAR addresses a core scalability bottleneck in RLHF: the difficulty of acquiring large volumes of high-quality preference labels. By showing that criterion-agnostic pre-training followed by lightweight SFT yields RMs that substantially outperform much larger baselines trained purely on human preferences, it opens a path to training increasingly powerful RMs via compute scaling rather than annotation scaling. The confirmed power-law scaling (R² ≈ 0.99) suggests continued gains with larger models and more compute. Open questions include whether the reference-trajectory requirement generalizes to settings without natural references, whether the preference-evaluation-vs-RL-performance gap (where POLAR-7B far exceeds POLAR-1.8B in RL but not in preference accuracy) reflects a fundamental limitation of current evaluation protocols, and whether the approach extends to multimodal or non-textual policy spaces.
