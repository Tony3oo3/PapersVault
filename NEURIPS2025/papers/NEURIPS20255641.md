## Problem

Prior theoretical analyses of transformer in-context learning have focused almost exclusively on IID data, leaving open whether transformers can learn from correlated sequences such as those generated by linear dynamical systems. This paper addresses two fundamental questions for the non-IID setting: (1) can a transformer in-context learn a family of noisy linear dynamical systems, and if so, at what error rate? (2) how much architectural depth is required? The results reveal a depth-separation phenomenon: logarithmic depth suffices for parametric-rate learning, while single-layer transformers face an irreducible approximation error.

## Input / Output

The transformer receives a sequence of tokens e_t = (0_d, 0_d, x_t, x_{t-1})^T ∈ R^{4d} encoding the trajectory of a noisy linear dynamical system x_t = Wx_{t-1} + ξ_t, where W has eigenvalues in (0,1) and ξ_t ~ N(0, σ²I). The output is the first d coordinates of the final token, interpreted as a prediction of x_{T+1}. The evaluation metric is a uniform L² test loss: sup over all task matrices W in a compact set of the expected squared distance between the prediction and the conditional mean Wx_T, measuring worst-case robustness across the task family.

## Method

The upper bound is proved via algorithm unrolling: a deep linear transformer is explicitly constructed so that each layer implements one step of the modified Richardson iteration, approximating the matrix-vector product X_T^{-1} x_T needed for the least-squares estimator. With L = O(log T) layers, the Richardson iterate converges sufficiently to yield an O(log T / T) test loss, leveraging high-probability concentration bounds on the sample covariance and the LSE error. The lower bound for single-layer transformers is established by computing the pointwise limit of the individual loss as T → ∞, showing it is a non-polynomial function of the task parameter w (involving (1-w²)^{-1}) that cannot be uniformly approximated by any fixed parameterization. This reveals a fundamental distinction from the IID setting, where the optimal attention weights depend only on the covariate distribution (task-independent), whereas for dynamical systems the covariate distribution itself depends on W.

## Results

The paper's main theoretical result (Theorem 1) establishes that a linear transformer of depth L = O(log T) achieves a uniform L² test loss of O(log T / T) for in-context learning of noisy linear dynamical systems, matching the parametric rate of the least-squares estimator up to a logarithmic factor. The complementary lower bound (Theorem 2) shows that any single-layer linear transformer incurs a non-vanishing test loss bounded below by a positive constant C(σ², w_min, w_max) independent of sequence length T, establishing a depth-separation phenomenon. Numerical experiments with T = 500 and [w_min, w_max] = [0, 0.8] confirm the separation: both softmax and linear transformers show a clear gap between L = 1 and L ≥ 2, with softmax transformers continuing to improve up to L = 5 while linear transformers plateau after L = 2.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper without external method baselines; the primary comparison is architectural, contrasting single-layer (L = 1) versus multi-layer (L ≥ 2) transformers. Theorem 2 proves that single-layer linear attention is fundamentally incapable of learning the dynamical system in-context, as its test loss remains bounded away from zero even as T → ∞, whereas Theorem 1 shows that O(log T) depth suffices for vanishing error. Numerically, both softmax and linear attention exhibit a clear depth separation at L = 1 vs. L ≥ 2, supporting the theoretical lower bound. For softmax transformers, test loss continues to decrease monotonically from L = 2 through L = 5, suggesting that even more depth may help. For linear transformers, the optimal loss is approximately constant across L = 2, 3, 4, 5, leaving open whether the O(log T) depth bound in Theorem 1 is tight or whether constant depth suffices. The paper also contrasts the non-IID (dynamical system) setting with the IID setting, where prior work (Zhang et al. 2023) shows single-layer linear attention achieves zero test loss in the infinite-context limit, highlighting that the task-dependent covariate distribution in dynamical systems is the key obstacle for shallow architectures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the in-context learning (ICL) literature from the well-studied IID linear regression setting (Zhang et al. 2023, Mahankali et al. 2023, Ahn et al. 2023) to the non-IID setting of noisy linear dynamical systems. It builds directly on Von Oswald et al. (2023b), who constructed a transformer unrolling Richardson iteration for noiseless autoregressive systems but provided no approximation guarantee, and on Sander et al. (2024) / Zheng et al. (2024), who studied noiseless linear dynamical systems. The key methodological departure is the synthesis of algorithm unrolling (Richardson iteration) with statistical concentration bounds for the least-squares estimator on correlated data, a combination not present in prior ICL theory. The depth-separation lower bound for single-layer transformers in the non-IID setting is, to the authors' knowledge, the first of its kind.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes the first quantitative approximation bounds for transformer in-context learning on non-IID data and reveals a fundamental architectural limitation: single-layer linear attention provably cannot learn from dependent data, in sharp contrast to the IID setting where it achieves zero loss. This depth-separation phenomenon (logarithmic depth suffices, one layer does not) has implications for understanding why deeper transformer architectures are needed for sequential/temporal tasks. Key open questions include whether O(log T) depth is truly necessary (numerical experiments suggest constant depth may suffice in practice), whether the results extend to nonlinear dynamical systems, and whether transformers trained on the L² loss actually achieve the theoretical bounds. The scope is currently limited to 1D systems for the lower bound and linear attention for both results.
