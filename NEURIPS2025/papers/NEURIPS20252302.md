## Problem

Existing methods for distributional treatment effects in randomized experiments either assume full compliance or simple random sampling, leaving a gap for the common setting where both covariate-adaptive randomization (stratified block designs, biased-coin schemes) and imperfect compliance are present. No prior method integrates regression adjustment with instrumental variable techniques to estimate full outcome distributions under this combined setting.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252302/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes tabular data with a binary treatment assignment Z, binary realized treatment D, a stratum indicator S, a potentially high-dimensional covariate vector X ∈ R^{d_x}, and an outcome Y that may be continuous, discrete, or mixed. The output is the local distributional treatment effect function β(y) evaluated across the entire outcome support (or a grid of quantiles), along with the local probability treatment effect LPTE for discrete outcomes and pointwise confidence intervals. The estimator is a ratio of two augmented sums, so the output is a function of y rather than a single scalar, and the denominator (first-stage compliance rate) is constant in y.

## Method

The estimator follows an augmented inverse propensity weighting (AIPW) structure applied to the IV ratio: the numerator estimates the intent-to-treat distributional effect and the denominator estimates the first-stage compliance rate, each augmented with conditional distribution functions μ_z(y,s,x) and conditional treatment probabilities η_z(s,x). These nuisance functions are estimated via flexible supervised learning (gradient boosting, random forests, neural networks) trained separately within each stratum and instrument group, with L-fold cross-fitting to avoid overfitting. The moment conditions satisfy Neyman orthogonality, making the estimator first-order insensitive to nuisance estimation errors and enabling valid inference even with high-dimensional covariates. The paper derives the asymptotic distribution under CAR (generalizing beyond i.i.d. sampling) and proves the estimator attains the semiparametric efficiency bound, with the variance kernel decomposing into within-stratum and between-stratum components.

## Results

In the simulation study (n=1000, 1000 replications), the ML-adjusted estimator (gradient boosting, 2-fold cross-fitting) achieves up to 50% RMSE reduction relative to the unadjusted estimator at certain quantiles, while the linearly adjusted estimator yields more modest 1–10% gains. Both adjusted estimators produce shorter 95% confidence intervals than the unadjusted version; the unadjusted estimator attains near-nominal 95% coverage, whereas ML adjustment slightly over-covers (up to 0.98–1.00). In the Oregon Health Insurance Experiment (n=17,021, 58% compliance), regression adjustment with gradient boosting (5-fold cross-fitting, 28 covariates) reduces standard errors by 0.5–15% for the LDTE and 3.5–26.5% for the LPTE. The key substantive finding is a 9-percentage-point decrease in the probability of zero ED visits (SE = 4.2 pp) among compliers, with no other effects reaching 5% significance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three estimators: the unadjusted (empirical) IV estimator, a linear regression-adjusted estimator, and an ML-adjusted estimator using gradient boosting with cross-fitting. The ML-adjusted estimator dominates on RMSE, achieving up to 50% reduction over the unadjusted baseline at some quantiles, with gains increasing as sample size grows from 500 to 5000. The linearly adjusted estimator provides only modest improvements (1–10% RMSE reduction), confirming that flexible nonparametric adjustment is necessary to capture the nonlinear, high-dimensional covariate structure in the DGP. On coverage, the unadjusted estimator is closest to nominal 95%, while ML adjustment is slightly conservative (over-covering up to 1.00), suggesting the confidence intervals could be tightened with better nuisance estimation. In the Oregon application, the ML-adjusted estimator consistently narrows confidence bands relative to the unadjusted version, with the largest SE reductions (up to 26.5%) appearing in the LPTE for mid-range ED visit counts. No existing method in the literature combines regression adjustment with IV-based distributional estimation under both covariate-adaptive randomization and noncompliance, so the baselines are the paper's own unadjusted and linearly adjusted variants rather than external published methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three established threads: IV-based local treatment effect estimation (Imbens & Angrist 1994; Abadie 2002), distributional/quantile treatment effect estimation (Chernozhukov et al. 2013; Imbens & Rubin 1997), and regression adjustment under covariate-adaptive randomization (Jiang et al. 2023, 2024). The authors explicitly position their contribution as filling a gap: while Jiang et al. (2023) handle quantile effects under full compliance and Jiang et al. (2024) handle average effects under CAR with noncompliance, no prior method integrates IV identification, distribution regression, and regression adjustment simultaneously under CAR and imperfect compliance. Methodologically, the estimator is an AIPW-type construction (Robins et al. 1994) with Neyman-orthogonal moment conditions (Chernozhukov et al. 2018, 2022) and cross-fitting, applied to a distribution regression target rather than a scalar mean.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first semiparametric efficiency bound for the LDTE under CAR and shows the proposed estimator attains it, giving practitioners a principled, variance-optimal tool for distributional causal inference in realistic experimental designs with noncompliance. The Oregon Health Insurance Experiment application (58% compliance) demonstrates practical relevance, revealing a 9pp reduction in the probability of zero ED visits. Key limitations include restriction to binary treatments, reliance on monotonicity and exclusion restrictions, and finite-sample performance that depends on nuisance estimation quality; the authors note that ML adjustment can slightly over-cover confidence intervals. Open directions include multi-valued or continuous treatments, dynamic/longitudinal settings, and non-asymptotic guarantees.
