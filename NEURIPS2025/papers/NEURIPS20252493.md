## Problem

Existing black-box counterfactual explanation frameworks either ignore the semantic content of proposed edits or assume without evidence that classifiers share human-level semantic understanding. The paper identifies an unexplored explanatory gap: whether a classifier's decision-making can genuinely be explained using human-level visual semantics, and what the minimum set of conceptual edits is that actually flips the classifier's prediction.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252493/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image with object annotations (from BDD100K or Visual Genome), a frozen black-box classifier (CNN, ViT, or LVLM), and a target class label. It produces a step-by-step sequence of conceptual edits (insertions, deletions, substitutions) along with the corresponding inpainted counterfactual images, terminating as soon as the classifier's predicted label flips. The classifier is queried only as a black box—no gradients or internal access are required—and the LVLM additionally provides spatial reasoning for object placement during editing.

## Method

V-CECE is a zero-training, plug-and-play pipeline with two components. The explanation component computes an optimal edit set by finding shortest paths between source and target class concepts on the WordNet knowledge graph, then solving a bipartite matching problem (via the Hungarian algorithm) that jointly minimizes insertion, deletion, and substitution costs. Three edit-ordering strategies—Local (an LVLM selects the next edit step-by-step given the current image), Global (edits ranked by dataset-wide importance scores), and Local-Global (local edits ordered by global importance)—determine the sequence in which edits are applied. The generative component executes each edit using Stable Diffusion v1.5 Inpainting, with GroundingDINO and SAM producing concept masks and a frozen LVLM (Claude 3.5 Sonnet) providing commonsense spatial placement for added or removed objects. The entire process is fully black-box: no module is trained, no classifier gradients are accessed, and the resulting image quality and edit count serve as empirical proxies for measuring the semantic-level discrepancy between the classifier and human reasoning.

## Results

On BDD100K, V-CECE with Claude 3.5 Sonnet (Local-Global ordering) achieves 98.10% Success Rate, FID 42.76, CMMD 0.364, and requires only 2.44 average semantic edits to flip the classifier label—outperforming the black-box baseline TIME (SR 81.8%, FID 51.5) by a wide margin. With CNN classifiers (DenseNet, ConvNeXt, EfficientNet, Swin), SR ranges from 84.8% to 98.07%, with EfficientNet performing best (SR 98.07%, FID 67.27, Avg|E| 3.76). On Visual Genome, V-CECE requires only 2.53–2.96 average edits to change class, compared to 12.15–12.88 for prior semantic counterfactual methods (Dervakos et al., Dimitriou et al.), while achieving 93–99.8% SR.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the white-box baselines (STEEX, DiME, ACE), V-CECE trades raw image fidelity (FID 1.02–7.94 for ACE/DiME vs. 42–99 for V-CECE) for a fundamentally different operating regime: no training, no gradient access, and semantically grounded edits that are human-interpretable. Against TIME, the only other black-box baseline, V-CECE substantially outperforms on Success Rate when LVLM classifiers are used (98.10–99.76% vs. 81.8%) and produces higher-quality images (FID 42.76 vs. 51.5 for Claude 3.5 Sonnet Local-Global). On Visual Genome, V-CECE requires roughly 5× fewer semantic edits than Dervakos et al. (2.53 vs. 12.15 for ResNet18) and Dimitriou et al. (2.54 vs. 12.18), while also generating actual counterfactual images (SR 96–99.8%) rather than only proposing abstract edit sets. The paper's central finding is that CNN classifiers (DenseNet, ConvNeXt, Swin) exhibit a significant explanatory gap with humans—requiring 3–7 edits and producing artifacts in 40–70% of cases—whereas LVLM classifiers (Claude 3.5/3.7 Sonnet) align closely with human judgments, flipping labels in 1–3 edits with 80%+ visual correctness.

## Relation to Prior Work

V-CECE sits at the intersection of two prior lines of work: concept-based counterfactual explanations (Dervakos et al., Dimitriou et al.) that compute semantic edit sets over knowledge graphs but do not generate images, and diffusion-based counterfactual generation (STEEX, DiME, ACE, TIME) that produces images but operates without well-defined semantic constraints. The key departure is a fully black-box, zero-training pipeline that couples WordNet-based optimal edit computation (via bipartite matching / Hungarian algorithm) with Stable Diffusion v1.5 Inpainting for execution, while explicitly treating the semantic-alignment question ('does the classifier reason like a human?') as a first-class diagnostic rather than an unexamined assumption. This contrasts with prior semantic CE work that presumes classifier semantics match human semantics, and with prior diffusion-based CE work that ignores semantics entirely.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central empirical finding—that CNN/ViT classifiers require roughly 2× more semantic edits than humans expect and exhibit sporadic, inconsistent edit patterns, while LVLMs align closely with human judgments—challenges a foundational assumption in prior semantic counterfactual literature that classifiers operate at a human-interpretable semantic level. This has practical implications: explaining a DenseNet classifier using human-level concepts may produce misleading or unfaithful explanations. Open questions include whether the gap generalizes beyond driving and indoor-scene datasets, whether it persists with larger or differently-architected CNNs, and whether the small human-survey cohort (n=31) is sufficient for strong statistical claims. The plug-and-play, training-free design also makes the framework immediately applicable to proprietary models, which is practically valuable for explainability in deployed systems.
