## Problem

Prior explainable clustering methods (IMM, EMN, Kernel IMM) require the reference clustering to be centroid-based (k-means or k-medians), making them inapplicable to widely used objectives like spectral clustering or kernel k-means that do not produce centroids. While CART can be repurposed for explainable clustering, its impurity-minimization objective is incompatible with the clustering structure, leading to poor performance on real data. No generic, reference-oblivious method existed that could round any arbitrary reference clustering into an explainable decision tree without restrictions on the clustering type.

## Input / Output

The method takes a point cloud X ⊂ R^d and a target number of leaves ℓ (which can exceed the number of reference clusters k, unlike centroid-based methods). For SPEX-Clique, it additionally takes any reference clustering (spectral, k-means, kernel k-means, etc.) and constructs a clique graph over each cluster; for SPEX-kNN, no reference clustering is needed—only a k-NN graph with κ neighbors per node is built directly on the data. The output is a binary decision tree where every internal node is a single-coordinate threshold cut, inducing a partition of X into ℓ explainable clusters. The sweep-line tree construction runs in O(kdn(log n + S)) time, where S is O(1) for the clique graph and O(κ) for the k-NN graph.

## Method

The core technical contribution is Theorem 2.2, which generalizes a spectral partitioning result from nearest-neighbor search to arbitrary weighted graphs: for any graph G over points in R^d, there exists a valid coordinate cut whose normalized cut conductance is bounded by the square root of the ratio of expected squared distances under adjacent-pair vs. all-pair distributions. This guarantees that if the graph captures geometric nearness structure, a low-conductance axis-aligned cut exists. SPEX-Clique instantiates this by building a clique graph over each reference cluster and iteratively splitting the tree leaf that maximizes the reduction in the multi-way normalized cut objective via a sweep-line over all coordinate thresholds. SPEX-kNN dispenses with the reference clustering entirely, building a k-NN graph directly on the data to capture local geometry. The paper further unifies prior methods (IMM, EMN, CART) as special cases of Trevisan's non-uniform sparsest cut framework, showing they correspond to star-graph or independent-set-graph choices that explain their centroid requirements and failure modes.

## Results

SPEX-Clique (with spectral clustering reference) is the most consistently high-performing method across all eight real-world datasets, evaluated by Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) and Adjusted Mutual Information (AMI) against ground-truth labels. On the larger datasets, SPEX-Clique achieves ARI of 0.217 on MNIST, 0.247 on Caltech 101, 0.564 on Beans, and 0.320 on CIFAR-10, substantially outperforming CART (0.027, −0.010, 0.542, 0.036 respectively). SPEX-kNN (reference-free, k=20) excels on low-dimensional datasets, achieving ARI 0.679 on Ecoli and 0.574 on Beans, surpassing all baselines including SPEX-Clique on those datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against EMN [12] (the state-of-the-art for k-means reference), SPEX-Clique is competitive but slightly behind when both use k-means as reference (e.g., MNIST ARI 0.209 vs. 0.250), consistent with EMN's near-optimal k-means-specific guarantees. However, when using spectral clustering as reference, SPEX-Clique clearly outperforms EMN (which cannot operate without centroids), achieving ARI 0.217 vs. 0.250 on MNIST and 0.247 vs. 0.249 on Caltech 101. Against CART [37], SPEX-Clique shows a dramatic advantage on larger datasets: on MNIST, SPEX-Clique achieves ARI 0.217 versus CART's 0.027, and on CIFAR-10, 0.320 versus 0.036, confirming the theoretical failure mode of CART's IS-graph objective identified in Section 3.3. On smaller datasets (R15, Pathbased, Ecoli, Iris, Cancer), CART and SPEX-Clique perform nearly identically (e.g., both achieve ARI 0.986 on R15). Kernel IMM [15] is only comparable on the three smallest datasets due to scalability issues; on Iris with kernel k-means reference, Kernel IMM achieves ARI 0.732 vs. SPEX-Clique's 0.732 (tie), but on Cancer it achieves 0.127 vs. SPEX-Clique's 0.406. ExKMC [17] (tested only with ℓ=2k leaves in Table 6) shows mixed results, with SPEX-Clique slightly ahead on most datasets (e.g., MNIST ARI 0.197 vs. 0.224 for ExKMC, Caltech 0.203 vs. 0.249).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the explainable clustering framework of Moshkovitz et al. [33] by replacing centroid-dependent rounding (IMM, EMN) with a spectral graph partitioning approach grounded in Cheeger's inequality, generalized from Dong et al. [11]. It departs from prior work's focus on specific reference objectives (k-means, k-medians) by providing a reference-oblivious method that works with any clustering, including spectral and kernel-based methods. A key conceptual contribution is showing that IMM, EMN, and CART are all special cases of Trevisan's [45] non-uniform sparsest cut framework with different graph choices (star, clique, independent set), unifying them under a single analytical lens.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper fills a genuine gap: no prior method could round arbitrary reference clusterings (e.g., spectral clustering, kernel k-means) into explainable trees without requiring centroids or specific kernel structures. The unified graph-partitioning perspective offers a new analytical tool for understanding and potentially improving existing methods. However, the lack of worst-case approximation bounds (a strength of prior centroid-based work) is a notable limitation, and the authors acknowledge that EMN remains superior for k-means specifically. An open question is whether the spectral framework can eventually yield formal approximation guarantees for broader reference objectives.
