## Problem

Neural networks produce accurate point predictions but fail to provide reliable uncertainty estimates when the test-time covariate distribution differs from the training distribution. Classical Bayesian neural networks place a single fixed prior over parameters that is shared across all inputs, so the posterior predictive uncertainty does not increase for test points that lie far from the training data, even in regions where predictive performance is likely to degrade.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a labeled training set and, at test time, only the covariates x* (no test labels required). During training, it additionally requires L synthetic environments constructed by bootstrap-resampling the training data into (train, test) pairs of sizes n and m. The output is a full posterior predictive distribution over y* parameterized by a multivariate Gaussian variational posterior on the prediction-layer weights, from which both the predictive mean and variance are obtained by Monte Carlo sampling over S weight draws.

## Method

VIDS replaces the classical fixed prior p(θ) with an energy-based adaptive prior p(θ | x_{1:N}, x*) whose energy integrates the log-likelihood over the outcome space for both training and test covariates, so the prior mass shifts when test inputs reveal covariate regions absent from training. To approximate the resulting covariate-dependent posterior, the paper uses amortized variational inference: a pre-trained embedding network g maps inputs to a representation space, and a separate inference network h_γ takes the concatenated aggregated training embedding and test embedding as input to output the mean and diagonal covariance of a Gaussian variational posterior over the prediction-layer weights only. Because true shifted test data is unavailable at training time, the method constructs L synthetic environments via bootstrap resampling and adds a cross-environment variance penalty (borrowed from ReX) to the ELBO objective, ensuring the learned amortized posterior generalizes across a range of plausible shifts. A theoretical guarantee (Proposition 3.1) shows that with enough bootstrap subsamples, at least one synthetic environment approximates any unknown test distribution within a chosen tolerance, provided the test support is contained in the training support.

## Results

On synthetic heteroscedastic linear regression, VIDS achieves RMSE of 0.068 (±0.002), substantially outperforming DUE (0.110), DUL (0.081), and SNGP (0.488). On synthetic logistic regression with missing data, VIDS achieves 90.0% accuracy and 0.011 average calibration error (ACE), matching DUL's accuracy while halving its calibration error (0.018) and far exceeding DUE (88.0%, ACE 0.034) and SNGP (84.4%, ACE 0.099). On real data, VIDS achieves the highest classification accuracy across all six CIFAR-10-C and Celeb-A experiments, and the lowest or comparable RMSE on all three UCI regression datasets (Boston, Concrete, Wine).

## Baselines & Comparisons

VIDS outperforms all three baselines on every experiment reported. Against SNGP [Liu et al., 2020], VIDS achieves dramatically lower RMSE (0.068 vs. 0.488 on linear regression) and higher accuracy (90.0% vs. 84.4% on logistic regression), as SNGP overfits and produces excessive variance. Against DUE [Van Amersfoort et al., 2021], VIDS reduces RMSE by ~38% (0.068 vs. 0.110) and improves calibration error by ~3x (0.011 vs. 0.034), while DUE underestimates uncertainty at higher covariate values. Against DUL [Park and Blei, 2024], VIDS matches accuracy on logistic regression (90.0% vs. 90.0%) but achieves lower calibration error (0.011 vs. 0.018) and lower RMSE on linear regression (0.068 vs. 0.081); DUL overestimates uncertainty due to incorrect variance modeling. On real data, VIDS achieves the highest accuracy in all six classification experiments (CIFAR-10-C corruptions and Celeb-A attribute shifts) and the lowest average RMSE on all three UCI regression datasets, with DUE underperforming on Concrete and Boston and DUL considerably underperforming on Wine.

## Relation to Prior Work

VIDS extends the Bayesian neural network framework (Neal, Blundell et al.) by replacing the fixed prior p(θ) with an adaptive, covariate-conditioned prior p(θ | x_{1:N}, x*), formulated as an energy-based model that integrates over the outcome space. The amortized variational inference machinery draws on Kingma et al. and Margossian & Blei, while the synthetic-environment construction via bootstrap subsampling is adapted from Slavutsky & Benjamini (2024), and the cross-environment variance penalty follows Krueger et al. (2021). Conceptually, VIDS departs from distance-aware methods (SNGP, DUE, DUL) by learning from data how covariate shifts affect predictive performance rather than relying on fixed input-space distance metrics, and it differs from prior Bayesian approaches (Ha et al., Yoon et al.) that share a single prior across all inputs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Reliable uncertainty quantification under distribution shift is critical for high-stakes deployments (medical imaging, autonomous systems) where miscalibrated confidence can cause harm. The adaptive prior provides a principled mechanism for the model to 'know what it doesn't know' at test time, and the synthetic-environment approach offers a practical way to train without access to shifted data. Key limitations include the assumption that the test distribution's support is contained within the training support (Proposition 3.1), the computational overhead of per-environment optimization for small models, and the reliance on a pre-trained embedding. Open questions include scalability to very large architectures, handling of truly novel covariates outside the training support, and whether the energy-based prior can be made more computationally tractable for continuous outcome spaces.
