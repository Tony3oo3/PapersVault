## Problem

Current LLMs are effectively static after pre-training: their knowledge is frozen in feed-forward layers, and the only adaptable component is in-context learning within a finite context window, analogous to anterograde amnesia. The paper argues that the standard deep learning paradigm of stacking layers fails to model the multi-timescale, multi-level learning processes observed in the brain, and that a new learning paradigm is needed to enable continual knowledge acquisition and higher-order in-context learning.

## Input / Output

The model consumes tokenized text sequences and produces next-token predictions for language modeling, as well as multiple-choice answers for common-sense reasoning benchmarks (PIQA, HellaSwag, Winogrande, ARC, SIQA, BoolQ). A key structural detail is that the input is processed through a chain of MLP blocks, each updating at a different frequency (chunk size), so the same token is seen by fast-updating and slow-updating memory components at different granularities. The self-modifying component additionally updates its own weight matrix at every token during inference, making the model's parameters a function of the entire input context.

## Method

Nested Learning (NL) reframes a model and its training as a hierarchy of nested optimization problems ordered by update frequency, where each component (projection layers, attention/memory state, optimizer momentum) is an associative memory compressing its own context flow. The concrete architecture HOPE combines two NL-derived components: (1) a self-modifying sequence model (extending Titans) that learns its own weight-update algorithm at test time using a delta-rule-based internal objective, and (2) a Continuum Memory System (CMS), a chain of MLP blocks each updated at a different frequency, replacing the static feed-forward layer with multi-timescale knowledge storage. The paper further shows that standard optimizers (SGD+momentum, Adam, Muon) are special cases of NL where the optimizer state is a linear associative memory, and introduces Deep Optimizers that replace the linear momentum with an MLP to capture nonlinear gradient dynamics. HOPE is trained with a variant of gradient descent that uses an L2-regression internal objective to account for token dependencies, and is evaluated at 760M and 1.3B parameter scales.

## Results

HOPE achieves the best average common-sense reasoning accuracy at both scales: 52.26% at 760M/30B tokens and 57.23% at 1.3B/100B tokens, outperforming all baselines. On language modeling, HOPE attains the lowest WikiText perplexity (15.11) and LAMBADA perplexity (11.63) at the 1.3B scale, while at 760M it achieves the best LAMBADA perplexity (20.47) though Titans is marginally better on WikiText (20.04 vs. 20.53). HOPE leads on most individual reasoning benchmarks, including PIQA (73.29%), HellaSwag (56.84%), and ARC-challenge (41.24%) at 1.3B.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HOPE outperforms Titans (LMM), its closest competitor, on average reasoning accuracy at both scales (52.26 vs. 51.56 at 760M; 57.23 vs. 56.82 at 1.3B) and on WikiText perplexity at 1.3B (15.11 vs. 15.60), though Titans edges out HOPE on LAMBADA perplexity at 1.3B (11.41 vs. 11.63). Against the hybrid Samba model, HOPE wins on average accuracy by 1.18 points (760M) and 3.23 points (1.3B), with particularly large gaps on ARC-challenge (36.05 vs. 33.20 at 760M; 41.24 vs. 36.17 at 1.3B). HOPE clearly dominates the purely recurrent baselines DeltaNet and RetNet across all metrics, with average accuracy gaps of 3.29 and 3.80 points respectively at 760M. Compared to the test-time training baseline (TTT), HOPE achieves 4.94 points higher average accuracy at 760M while also matching or exceeding its language modeling perplexity. Overall, HOPE's gains over Titans suggest that the Continuum Memory System (replacing the static MLP) and the self-modifying sequence model together provide complementary benefits for both memorization and reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Titans architecture (same first author) and the associative memory framework of Behrouz et al. [58], extending them into a unifying paradigm called Nested Learning (NL) that represents any deep learning model as a hierarchy of nested optimization problems ordered by update frequency. It departs from the standard deep learning narrative of stacking layers for expressivity by arguing that what matters is the number of distinct 'levels' of optimization (each with its own context flow), drawing explicit connections to Fast Weight Programs and the neurophysiology of memory consolidation. The 'Deep Optimizers' contribution reframes well-known algorithms (SGD+Momentum, Adam, Muon) as associative memory modules that compress gradient histories, positioning them as special cases of NL rather than as independent numerical tools.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The NL framework offers a potentially unifying lens for understanding why in-context learning emerges in large models and how to design architectures with more 'levels' for higher-order adaptation, addressing the static nature of LLMs post-pre-training. However, the empirical validation is limited to 760M and 1.3B parameter models with modest improvements over Titans (e.g., 52.26 vs. 51.56 average accuracy at 760M), and no code is released. Open questions include whether the multi-level structure scales to frontier model sizes, whether the neuroscientific analogy (online consolidation) translates into practical continual learning gains, and whether the claimed 'higher-order in-context learning' from additional levels is empirically demonstrated beyond the current benchmarks.
