## Problem

Prevailing time series forecasting models assume that longer input sequences always improve accuracy (the 'long-sequence information gain hypothesis'), yet this paper demonstrates the opposite: truncating early timesteps often reduces prediction error across diverse architectures. This reveals that models learn substantial redundant features—noise and irrelevant fluctuations—that degrade performance, and no existing training objective explicitly suppresses this redundancy through the information bottleneck lens of minimizing I(X, Z).
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253324/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a multivariate time series window X ∈ R^{L×D} (L=48 timesteps, D variables) and produces a future prediction Ŷ ∈ R^{H×D} (H ∈ {48,…,192}). During training, the input is additionally consumed in masked variants where the first k timesteps are zeroed out (k sampled stochastically from {1,…,L}), and the encoder representations from both original and masked inputs are compared. At inference time, no masking is applied—the method adds zero overhead beyond standard forward passes.

## Method

AMRC augments the standard prediction loss with two auxiliary terms grounded in information bottleneck theory. (1) Adaptive Masking Loss (AML): for each batch, m random prefix-mask lengths are sampled; the mask yielding the greatest prediction-loss reduction is selected, and the encoder's representation for the full input is penalized toward the representation of that masked input, with an adaptive weight β proportional to the relative loss gain—effectively minimizing I(X, Z) by steering the encoder away from redundant temporal prefixes. (2) Embedding Similarity Penalty (ESP): pairwise Frobenius distances in the embedding space are aligned with pairwise distances in the ground-truth output space across a mini-batch, enforcing that semantically similar inputs map to similar predictions and preventing representation collapse. The total loss is L_pred + λ_AML·L_AML + λ_ESP·L_ESP, and the framework is architecture-agnostic, plugging into any encoder-decoder forecasting model without structural modification.

## Results

AMRC consistently reduces both MSE and MAE across all five backbone models and seven benchmark datasets. Representative gains include SOFTS on ETTh1 (MSE 0.408→0.389, MAE 0.414→0.393), iTransformer on Electricity (MSE 0.176→0.163, a 7.4% reduction), and PatchTST on ETTm2 (MSE 0.211→0.196, a 7.1% reduction). The method is architecture-agnostic, delivering improvements on Transformer-based models (iTransformer, PatchTST) as well as MLP-based models (TSMixer, TimeMixer, SOFTS), with the largest relative gains observed on high-channel datasets (Weather, Solar-Energy, Electricity).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates AMRC as a plug-and-play training-time regularization applied to five representative forecasting backbones: SOFTS, iTransformer, TimeMixer, PatchTST, and TSMixer. AMRC outperforms the original (unregularized) version of every backbone on all seven datasets, with no instance of degradation. The largest relative MSE gains appear on high-channel or high-variance datasets: iTransformer on Electricity drops from 0.176 to 0.163 (−7.4%), PatchTST on ETTm2 from 0.211 to 0.196 (−7.1%), and TSMixer on ETTh2 from 0.324 to 0.319 (−1.5%) with a larger MAE drop (0.357→0.340). On the 7-channel ETT datasets, improvements are more modest (1–5% MSE reduction) but remain consistent across all models. The ablation study confirms that both the Adaptive Masking Loss (AML) and the Embedding Similarity Penalty (ESP) contribute independently, and their combination (full AMRC) achieves the best MSE and MAE in nearly all model–dataset settings, indicating complementary mechanisms for suppressing input redundancy and enforcing representation geometry.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes Information Bottleneck theory (Tishby et al., Alemi et al.) for time series forecasting by operationalizing the I(X,Z) minimization objective as a practical adaptive masking loss, rather than relying on variational approximations as in VIB. It departs from the prevailing 'long-sequence information gain hypothesis' that dominates the field, and draws on consistency regularization ideas (Temporal Ensembling, Mean Teacher) to formulate the Embedding Similarity Penalty, but extends them from single-sample augmentation stability to pairwise geometric alignment across a mini-batch. Rather than proposing a new architecture, AMRC positions itself as an architecture-agnostic training-time regularizer that can be plugged into any existing forecasting backbone (MLP, RNN, Transformer).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that truncating early input segments consistently improves prediction across five diverse architectures and seven datasets challenges a foundational assumption in time series modeling and suggests that current models waste capacity encoding redundant or noisy temporal prefixes. However, the practical gains are modest (typically 1–5% MSE reduction), the prefix-masking assumption limits applicability when critical information resides in later portions of the sequence, and the m× training overhead constrains use in latency-sensitive settings. Open questions include what the masked segments actually represent (noise, outliers, stale information), whether non-prefix or learned masking patterns would yield larger gains, and whether the approach transfers to tasks where input length is genuinely informative (e.g., regime-shift detection).
