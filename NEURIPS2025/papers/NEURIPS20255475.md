## Problem

Existing multi-environment generalization methods for dynamical systems (e.g., LEADS, CoDA) require explicit environment labels during training to decompose dynamics into shared and environment-specific components. In practice, these labels are frequently unavailable due to uncontrolled data collection, privacy restrictions, or aggregation from multiple sources, leaving a gap in how to learn generalizable dynamical systems from unlabeled mixed trajectories.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255475/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single mixed dataset of trajectories (2D state vectors for ODEs like Lotka-Volterra, or 32×32 spatial fields for PDEs like Gray-Scott and Navier-Stokes) with no environment annotations, plus an assumed number of environments M (which may be over- or under-estimated). It outputs an inferred environment label for each trajectory, a decomposed dynamical system model (global component θ plus per-environment components φ_e), and predicted trajectories. At test time, environment labels are inferred from prediction bias over a short initial trajectory segment (< 2Δt), and the method remains robust when M exceeds the true count.

## Method

DynaInfer draws an analogy between multi-environment learning and K-means clustering: each environment-specific neural network acts as a 'centroid,' and the regression loss of a trajectory under that network serves as the 'distance.' The algorithm alternates two steps: (1) with fixed network parameters, assign each trajectory to the environment whose network yields the minimal prediction loss (Eq. 4); (2) with fixed assignments, update the shared and environment-specific parameters to minimize the regularized multi-environment loss (Eq. 5). Proposition 3.1 proves the objective decreases monotonically and, under a finite argmin space assumption, drops by a constant C > 0 whenever the assignment changes, guaranteeing convergence. The framework is plug-and-play, wrapping any existing generalization base model (LEADS, CoDA-l1, CoDA-l2) without modifying its architecture, and empirically matches or exceeds oracle-labeled performance on ODE and PDE systems.

## Results

On the in-domain generalization task, DynaInfer achieves test MSE of 7.93E-5 (LEADS), 1.83E-4 (CoDA-l1), and 1.82E-4 (CoDA-l2) on Lotka-Volterra, outperforming the best non-oracle baseline (One per Env) by 6–8× and matching or exceeding Oracle (7.02E-5, 3.19E-5, 2.72E-5). On Gray-Scott, DynaInfer reaches 4.14E-5 test MSE with LEADS, beating Oracle (1.34E-4) by 3.2×. On Navier-Stokes, DynaInfer achieves 7.05E-3 test MSE with LEADS, closely matching Oracle (6.55E-3) while the next-best non-oracle method (One per Env) is at 4.11E-2 — a 5.8× gap.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares DynaInfer against four alternative environment-assignment strategies (All in One, One per Env, Random, and Oracle) combined with three base generalization models (LEADS, CoDA-l1, CoDA-l2). DynaInfer consistently outperforms All in One and Random by 1–2 orders of magnitude in test MSE across all datasets and base models. Against One per Env, DynaInfer achieves 6–8× lower MSE on LV, 40× lower on GS, and 5–6× lower on NS. Notably, DynaInfer matches or exceeds Oracle performance in several settings: on GS with LEADS (4.14E-5 vs. 1.34E-4), on GS with CoDA-l2 (7.25E-5 vs. 7.04E-5), and on NS with CoDA-l1 (1.62E-2 vs. 1.73E-2). In the domain adaptation setting (Table 2), DynaInfer narrows the gap to Oracle further, achieving 5.77E-4 MSE on LV with LEADS versus Oracle's 1.67E-3, and 1.00E-4 on GS versus Oracle's 2.21E-4.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the multi-environment dynamical systems learning framework established by LEADS (Yin et al., NeurIPS 2021) and CoDA (Kirchmeyer et al., ICML 2022), both of which require explicit environment labels during training to decompose dynamics into shared and environment-specific components. DynaInfer removes this dependency by drawing a structural analogy to K-means clustering: the per-environment neural network acts as a 'centroid,' and trajectories are assigned to the network that minimizes their prediction loss. The paper positions this as the first treatment of unlabeled environment inference specifically for ODE/PDE trajectory data, distinguishing it from switched-systems mode identification (which operates on individual data points) and from CV clustering methods (which operate on finite-dimensional vectors rather than field functions).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical motivation is strong: environment labels are routinely unavailable in ecological monitoring, aggregated public datasets, and privacy-sensitive domains, yet all existing generalization methods for dynamical systems require them. The finding that DynaInfer matches or occasionally exceeds Oracle performance (notably on Gray-Scott with LEADS) suggests that the bias-aware assignment mechanism provides a form of implicit regularization that true labels do not. Open questions remain around chaotic systems where MSE is an inadequate metric, complex boundary conditions, and whether the alternating optimization can escape local optima in more heterogeneous settings. The method's dependence on knowing (or over-estimating) the number of environments M, while robust to over-estimation, still represents a practical constraint.
