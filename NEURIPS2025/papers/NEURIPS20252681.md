## Problem

Particle Image Velocimetry (PIV) is a PDE-constrained inverse problem where the inverse operator mapping particle image pairs to velocity fields is ill-posed and may not exist. Existing deep learning PIV methods rely on 4D cost volumes that freeze resolution and matching accuracy at training time, cannot adapt to different measurement scales, and generalize poorly from synthetic to real experimental data due to the absence of labeled real-world flow fields.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252681/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pair of grayscale particle images (I₁, I₂) captured at two successive time steps and produces a 2D velocity field u(x). A key design feature is resolution-adaptive inference: the model is trained once with uniform downsampling factors from 1× to 4×, enabling it to accept low-resolution inputs (e.g., 64²) and output high-resolution flow fields (e.g., 256²) at arbitrary scales, and to perform inference over arbitrary subdomains for localized flow analysis without retraining.

## Method

PIVNO formulates PIV as a function-space operator learning problem and approximates the inverse map G: (I₁, I₂) → u with a four-stage pipeline: (1) a residual convolutional feature encoder extracts and concatenates local features from both images; (2) a RoPE Galerkin Attention module performs a Petrov-Galerkin-style projection along the channel dimension, using rotary positional encoding with learnable frequency parameters to inject spatial position information and nonlinear frequency modulation into the attention kernel; (3) a Conv-GRU iteratively refines the coarse flow features over 5 steps, using the RoPE-GA output as both hidden-state initialization and per-step context to enforce spatial continuity; (4) a super-resolution module combines random sampling with graph convolution to produce continuous-scale velocity estimates at arbitrary resolutions. For sim-to-real transfer, a self-supervised fine-tuning loss combines a Charbonnier-penalized photometric data term, a Laplacian smoothing term, and a divergence-free incompressibility constraint, requiring no labeled real data.

## Results

On Synthetic Dataset 1 (256² resolution), PIVNO achieves Average Endpoint Error (AEE) of 1.9, 0.8, 1.7, 3.5, and 2.5 (pixels per 100 pixels) across Backstep, Cylinder, JHTDB Channel, DNS turbulence, and SQG flow cases, respectively. The largest margins appear on complex flows: JHTDB Channel (1.7 vs. 8.3 for ARaft-FlowNet) and SQG (2.5 vs. 9.8 for ARaft-FlowNet). PIVNO also demonstrates resolution adaptivity, maintaining competitive accuracy (e.g., AEE 2.7 on Backstep) even when upsampling 4× from 64² inputs. On the more challenging Synthetic Dataset 2 (large displacements, high noise), PIVNO further reduces AEE to 4.5–6.6 across all cases, outperforming PIV-RAFT (6.4–24.9) by a wide margin.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PIVNO outperforms all eight baselines on four of five flow cases in Table 1, with the sole exception being Backstep where PIV-RAFT (1.6) edges out PIVNO (1.9). The most dramatic wins are against cost-volume-based methods: on JHTDB Channel, PIVNO achieves 1.7 AEE versus 13.7 for PIV-RAFT and 8.3 for ARaft-FlowNet; on SQG, PIVNO scores 2.5 versus 11.7 (PIV-RAFT) and 9.8 (ARaft-FlowNet). Among unsupervised baselines, UnLiteFlowNet-PIV (9.4–17.3 AEE) and UnPwcNet-PIV (8.2–25.2) are consistently 3–10× worse than PIVNO, confirming that photometric-loss-only training is insufficient for PIV. On the harder Synthetic Dataset 2 (large displacements, low particle density, high noise), PIVNO (4.5–6.6 AEE) dominates PIV-RAFT (6.4–24.9) and UnLiteFlowNet-PIV (12.3–22.5) across all five cases, with the gap widening to ~18 AEE on JHTDB Channel. PIVNO also uses fewer parameters (2.52 M) than PIV-RAFT (5.31 M), PIV-DCNN (8.40 M), and UnPwcNet-PIV (9.37 M), while matching or exceeding their accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

PIVNO repositions PIV from a cost-volume matching problem (as in PIV-RAFT, PIV-DCNN, and related CNN-based methods) to a PDE-constrained inverse problem solved via neural operators, drawing on the Galerkin attention mechanism of Cao (2021) and the continuous-scale inference strategy of SRNO (Wei & Zhang, 2023). The key architectural departure is the RoPE-augmented Galerkin attention, which injects positional information into the Petrov-Galerkin projection to handle the spatially explicit nature of particle tracking, combined with a Conv-GRU refinement loop that operates on flow features rather than image features as in RAFT. The self-supervised fine-tuning with divergence regularization extends the variational optical flow literature into a domain-adaptation context, addressing the sim-to-real gap that plagues supervised PIV networks trained exclusively on synthetic data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The resolution-adaptive inference capability—training once with downsampled inputs and producing accurate flow fields at arbitrary output scales without retraining—addresses a practical bottleneck in experimental fluid mechanics where regions of interest and required resolutions are often unknown a priori. The self-supervised divergence-constrained fine-tuning offers a label-free path to real-world deployment, which is critical given the cost and impracticality of obtaining ground-truth velocity fields in physical experiments. Key limitations include the restriction to 2D flows and the absence of multi-scale feature fusion, which will matter for high-resolution PIV data; extending the framework to 3D and incorporating hierarchical feature extraction are natural next steps that the authors identify.
