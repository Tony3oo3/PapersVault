## Problem

Existing topological link prediction methods (SPM, SBM, graph embeddings, message-passing GNNs) achieve competitive accuracy but offer no interpretable mechanistic explanation of why links form. The Cannistraci-Hebb (CH) theory provides such interpretability through explicit deterministic rules grounded in local-community self-organization, but no single CH rule or path length (L2 for homophilic vs. L3 for synergetic interactions) performs optimally across all network domains. The paper asks: can an adaptive machine automatically discover which CH rule and path length governs each network, thereby unifying predictive power with mechanistic interpretability?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251344/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes an unweighted, undirected adjacency matrix (no node features or attributes) and produces three outputs: (1) likelihood scores for all non-observed node pairs, (2) a final ranking where ties in CH scores are broken by a Spearman-correlation sub-ranking over shortest-path distance profiles, and (3) the identity of the selected CH model and path length, which encodes the inferred physical principle (homophilic vs. synergetic). The adaptive selection itself uses observed links as positives in an internal validation step, exploiting the property that CH scores are computed identically for observed and non-observed links.

## Method

CHA is an adaptive network automata machine that instantiates two CH models—CH3 (pure eLCL minimization: score = 1/(1+de_z)) and CH3.1 (adaptive reward that decays internal-link credit as external connectivity grows)—on paths of length 2 or 3. For a given network, CHA evaluates every model-path combination by computing a validation metric (AUPR, Precision, or NDCG) measuring how well the model separates observed from non-observed links, then selects the combination with the highest score. The winning combination's scores on non-observed links become the final prediction, with a Spearman-correlation sub-ranking over shortest-path profiles resolving ties. The key design insight is that the selected path length directly reveals the governing interaction principle: L2 indicates similarity-driven (homophilic) link formation, while L3 indicates diversity-driven (synergetic) cooperation, making CHA an interpretable mechanistic machine rather than a black-box scorer.

## Results

On ATLAS-static (1,269 networks across 14 domains), CHA consistently achieves the best mean rank and highest win rate across all three evaluation metrics (AUPR, NDCG, Precision) and all three scale settings (small, medium, large). Under AUPR, CHA achieves more than twice the win rate of the best-performing baseline. The optimal CHA configuration ({CH3, CH3.1}) attains a win rate of 0.72 and mean AUPR of 0.29 on ATLAS-static, approaching the theoretical upper bound of 1.00 win rate / 0.30 AUPR. On ATLAS-temporal (14 time-evolving networks), CHA also secures the best mean rank and highest win rate, confirming generalization to dynamic settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CHA outperforms all 13 baselines across every metric and scale setting. Against SPM (spectral perturbation) and all four SBM variants (SBM, SBM-DC, SBM-N, SBM-DC-N), CHA achieves a substantially higher win rate and lower mean rank, with the gap most pronounced under AUPR where CHA's win rate exceeds twice that of the best baseline. Among graph embedding methods (HOPE, node2vec, NetSMF, ProNE), CHA dominates on all three metrics, confirming that deterministic local path-based scoring surpasses learned low-dimensional representations for this task. Against message-passing GNNs (NCNC, MPLP, MPLP+), CHA also wins consistently; notably, the adaptive variant MPLP+A (which selects between L2 and L3 MPLP+ via validation) improves over fixed MPLP+, corroborating the generality of the CH adaptive principle beyond the CHA framework itself. Within the CH family, CHA (using {CH3, CH3.1}) outperforms every individual variant: RA (win rate 0.25), CH1 (0.10), CH2 (0.26), CH3 (0.41), and CH3.1 (0.65), demonstrating that the adaptive selection mechanism is essential rather than merely additive.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Cannistraci-Hebb (CH) theory of epitopological learning (Cannistraci et al. 2013; Muscoloni et al. 2018) by formalizing the minimization of external local-community links (eLCL) as the core CH principle, introducing two new models (CH3, CH3.1), and wrapping the entire family of CH rules into an adaptive selection machine (CHA) that automatically picks the best model and path length per network. The formal framework is grounded in network automata (Smith et al. 2011), and the path-based scoring generalizes Resource Allocation (Zhou et al. 2009) and the L3 predictor of Kovács et al. 2019 to arbitrary path lengths. Unlike prior CH work that required the user to manually choose between L2 (homophilic) and L3 (synergetic) rules, CHA makes this selection data-driven via internal validation, and the paper positions itself against both model-free heuristics (SPM, SBM) and learned methods (graph embeddings, message-passing GNNs) as a deterministic, interpretable alternative.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ATLAS benchmark (1,283 real-world networks across 14 domains) is the largest link-prediction evaluation to date, providing a much-needed common ground for comparing methods that previously tested on fewer than 20 networks. The finding that a simple, deterministic, training-free automaton outperforms learned GNNs and embeddings by a wide margin (2× win rate) challenges the assumption that learned representations are necessary for competitive link prediction, and the adaptive L2/L3 selection reveals interpretable mechanistic principles (homophilic vs. synergetic interaction) governing each network class. Key limitations include the absence of node-attribute features and the restriction to unweighted undirected graphs; open questions include whether integrating topological and feature-based signals could further improve performance, and whether the CH adaptive principle generalizes to directed, weighted, or multilayer networks.
