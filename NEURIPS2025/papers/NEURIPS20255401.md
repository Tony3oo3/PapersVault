## Problem

Despite Adam's widespread use in training neural networks, its implicit bias for nonlinear models remains poorly understood, in contrast to the well-studied simplicity bias of SGD. This paper asks whether Adam resists the simplicity bias that causes SGD-trained networks to rely on simple (often spurious) features, and if so, how this manifests in the learned decision boundary and generalization behavior.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255401/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The theoretical analysis operates on two-dimensional Gaussian mixture data with a binary label, where the first two dimensions carry signal and the rest are noise. Empirically, inputs range from 14×14 MNIST images with injected 2×2 colored patches, to MNIST-CIFAR composite images (top half MNIST digit, bottom half CIFAR-10 image), to text sentence pairs (MultiNLI) and online comments (CivilComments). Outputs include binary class predictions, the geometric shape of the learned decision boundary (linear vs. piecewise-linear), neuron direction distributions, and multiple accuracy metrics: in-distribution test accuracy, worst-group accuracy, core-only accuracy, and decoded core/spurious feature correlations.

## Method

The paper derives closed-form population gradients for two-layer ReLU networks (fixed outer layer, correlation loss) on a Gaussian mixture dataset, then proves that gradient flow drives all neurons to align with the [±1,0] direction, yielding a linear predictor, while signGD (Adam with β₁=β₂=0) drives neurons to three distinct directions — [-1,0], [1,1]/√2, and [1,-1]/√2 — producing a piecewise-linear boundary closer to the Bayes' optimal predictor. The proof tracks the angle θ between each neuron and the x-axis, showing that for GD the angle monotonically shrinks to zero, while for signGD the update direction alternates between [1,±1] or [-1,±1] depending on the sign of the outer-layer weight and initial quadrant. A simplified toy setting (σ→0) extends the analysis to Adam with momentum (β₁=β₂≈1), characterizing the full neuron trajectory and showing Adam still produces a more nonlinear boundary than GD. Empirically, the authors validate these findings across six benchmark datasets with spurious correlations using ResNet-18/50 and BERT, showing Adam achieves higher worst-group accuracy, larger margins, and better decoded core-feature correlation than SGD.

## Results

On the synthetic Gaussian dataset, Adam achieves 0.32% higher test accuracy than GD at comparable training loss (gap widens to 0.595% with smaller signal strength). On the MNIST spurious-feature task, Adam reaches 86% test accuracy versus SGD's 66.6%, while also producing a more nonlinear decision boundary (84% vs 95.5% agreement with a linear model). On the Dominoes (MNIST-CIFAR) dataset with a ResNet-18, Adam achieves 84.66% decoded worst-group accuracy versus SGD's 71.04%, and 20.63% core-only accuracy versus SGD's 1.66%. On the Boolean features dataset, Adam attains 97.87% test accuracy and 0.87 decoded core correlation, compared to SGD's 89.58% and 0.51.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares Adam (with and without momentum) against SGD and signGD across six datasets. Adam consistently outperforms SGD on all metrics related to robustness and feature richness: on the MNIST spurious-feature task, Adam achieves 86% test accuracy versus SGD's 66.6% (a 19.4-point gap), and on the Dominoes dataset, Adam's decoded worst-group accuracy is 84.66% versus SGD's 71.04% (a 13.6-point gap). On the Boolean features dataset, Adam's decoded core correlation (0.87) far exceeds SGD's (0.51), while Adam's decoded spurious correlation (0.36) is lower than SGD's (0.78), confirming that Adam learns core features and down-weights spurious ones. On the four subgroup robustness benchmarks (Waterbirds, CelebA, MultiNLI, CivilComments), Adam yields significantly better worst-group accuracy than SGD when fine-tuning pretrained BERT or ResNet-50 models. Theoretically, the paper proves that gradient flow (GD) converges to a linear predictor (all neurons align with [±1,0]ᵀ) while signGD (Adam without momentum) produces a piecewise-linear boundary with neurons in three directions, making Adam's solution closer to the Bayes' optimal predictor.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the implicit bias literature from GD to Adam for nonlinear models. Prior work characterized Adam's implicit bias only in linear settings (Zhang et al. showing ℓ∞-norm convergence for logistic regression) or in terms of margin maximization for homogeneous networks (Tsilivis et al.), without connecting the learned solution to feature complexity or decision boundary shape. The simplicity bias of SGD-trained networks (Shah et al., Kalimeris et al.) is well documented, but no prior work had identified the contrasting behavior of Adam—its resistance to simplicity bias—as a distinct and theoretically grounded property. The paper also builds on the synthetic 'slabs' dataset of Shah et al. but replaces slab features with Gaussian features to enable closed-form population gradient analysis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that Adam promotes richer, more diverse feature learning (and thus better robustness to spurious correlations and distribution shifts) challenges the widely held view that SGD's simplicity bias is universally beneficial for generalization. This has direct practical implications for optimizer selection in settings where spurious features are present, which is common in real-world data. Key open questions include whether the results extend to deeper networks, the effect of weight decay (AdamW), and whether richer feature learning is always desirable or can hurt in-distribution generalization. The theoretical guarantees are limited to two-layer networks in the population setting with specific parameter ranges, so generalization to practical deep learning remains an open empirical question.
