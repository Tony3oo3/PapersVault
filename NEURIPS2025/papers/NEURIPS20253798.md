## Problem

Energy natural gradient descent (ENGD) significantly accelerates PINN training but is prohibitively expensive due to the O(P³) cost of inverting the P×P Gauss-Newton matrix, limiting its use to small networks. Meanwhile, the variational Monte Carlo community has developed the Woodbury identity (MinSR) and momentum schemes (SPRING) that dramatically reduce the cost of the analogous stochastic reconfiguration optimizer, but these techniques had not been transferred to the PINN setting. The paper asks whether these VMC advances can be ported to PINNs and whether further randomization can reduce costs for large batch sizes.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a linear PDE operator with Dirichlet boundary conditions, randomly sampled interior and boundary points (batch size N = N_Ω + N_∂Ω), and a tanh-activated MLP architecture with up to 10⁶ trainable parameters. It produces trained network parameters θ that minimize the least-squares PDE residual, evaluated as L² error against a known analytical solution on a fixed 30,000-point evaluation set. A key structural detail is that all curvature operations are performed in the N×N sample space (the neural tangent kernel J_k J_k^T) rather than the P×P parameter space, which is what makes the exact computation feasible at scale.

## Method

The paper applies the push-through (Woodbury) identity to rewrite the ENGD update as J_k^T(J_k J_k^T + λI)^{-1} r_k, shifting the expensive inversion from the P×P Gauss-Newton matrix to the N×N kernel matrix and reducing per-iteration cost from O(P³) to O(N²P). It then adapts the SPRING algorithm from the VMC literature, which modifies the Tikhonov-regularized least-squares subproblem to incorporate a momentum direction φ_{k-1} with a Kaczmarz-inspired residual shift, yielding a closed-form update that accelerates convergence and eliminates the need for ENGD's expensive line search; a bias correction factor 1/√(1−μ^{2k}) is added as a new contribution. For large batch sizes, a GPU-efficient randomized Nyström approximation is introduced that replaces the standard SVD-based stable algorithm with Cholesky solves on square matrices of sketch dimension, running 13–65× faster on GPU. The paper shows that randomization accelerates early training for low-dimensional problems with large batches but is limited by the effective dimension of the regularized kernel, which plateaus above 50% of N, leaving insufficient room for dimensionality reduction in high-dimensional settings.

## Results

On the 5D Poisson problem, ENGD-W (Woodbury form) takes more than 30× more optimization steps than Hessian-free within the same time budget and achieves the same sub-10⁻³ L² error as original ENGD up to 75× faster. On the 9+1d log-Fokker-Planck equation, SPRING attains an L² error an order of magnitude lower than the previous state-of-the-art KFAC. The GPU-efficient Nyström variant delivers 13–65× speedup over the standard Nyström implementation on GPU, with both speedup and memory savings increasing with sketch size.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the original ENGD [Müller & Zeinhofer, ICML 2023], the Woodbury reformulation (ENGD-W) reduces per-iteration cost from O(P³) to O(N²P), enabling 30× more steps in the same wall-clock time on the 5D Poisson problem and reaching the same L² error up to 75× faster. Against KFAC [Dangel et al., 2024], both ENGD-W and SPRING surpass KFAC's final accuracy across all four problems, with SPRING achieving an order-of-magnitude lower L² error on the 9+1d log-Fokker-Planck equation while being architecture-agnostic and simpler to implement. Against Hessian-free optimization [Martens, ICML 2010], ENGD-W outperforms in both efficiency and accuracy on the 5D Poisson, taking more than 30× more steps within the same time budget. Against first-order baselines (SGD with momentum and Adam [Kingma & Ba, ICLR 2015]), the second-order methods (ENGD-W and SPRING) achieve substantially lower L² errors, with the gap widening in higher-dimensional settings where first-order methods struggle most.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper transfers two techniques from the variational Monte Carlo (VMC) community—MinSR's use of the Woodbury identity to shift computation from parameter space to sample space, and SPRING's Kaczmarz-inspired momentum scheme—into the PINN setting, where the analogous ENGD method had previously been bottlenecked by O(P³) inversion of the Gauss-Newton matrix. It also develops a GPU-efficient Nyström variant (skipping SVD and QR in favor of Cholesky solves) as an independent randomized linear algebra contribution. The positioning is explicitly cross-community: the authors argue that the shared mathematical structure of natural gradient methods in VMC and PINNs makes these transfers natural, and they contrast their approach with KFAC (architecture-dependent, more complex) and Hessian-free optimization (suffers from ill-conditioning).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for PINN practitioners: the Woodbury+SPRING combination achieves the same L² error as original ENGD up to 75× faster and outperforms KFAC by an order of magnitude in the 9+1d Fokker-Planck problem, all with an architecture-agnostic implementation. The key open question is whether randomization can be made to work beyond the narrow regime of low-dimensional problems with very large batches; the paper identifies the effective dimension of the regularized kernel (plateauing above 50% of N) as the fundamental barrier, and notes that in high-dimensional settings the cost of differentiating through the PDE operator dominates, nullifying kernel-computation savings. Adaptive hyperparameter selection for ENGD-W and SPRING remains an open problem for making these methods truly black-box.
