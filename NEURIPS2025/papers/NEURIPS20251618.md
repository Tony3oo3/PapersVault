## Problem

Attack success rates (ASRs) from AI red teaming are routinely used to make comparative claims about system safety and jailbreak efficacy, yet many such comparisons rest on incongruent estimands (e.g., Top-1-of-392 vs. one-shot) or invalid measurements (differential judge error, poorly chosen prompts). The paper asks a deceptively simple question—when can ASRs be meaningfully compared?—and argues that current practice frequently fails to meet the necessary conditions for valid comparison.

## Input / Output

The framework takes as input a probabilistic threat model (oracle success criteria s, goal distribution D, and conditions C) paired with an operationalized jailbreaking activity (judge J, base prompt set D, and transformation set T), and produces a formal characterization of the estimand α that the observed ASR estimates. Empirically, the authors run experiments on LLaMA 2 7B/13B Chat using 100 prompts from MaliciousInstruct across 49 decoding configurations with 49 repeated samples each, producing per-prompt success probability distributions and Top-1 vs. one-shot ASR curves that expose how aggregation choice and decoding entropy interact to inflate apparent attack success.

## Method

The paper instantiates jailbreaking within a formal measurement theory framework (adapted from Adcock & Collier) that separates systematization (defining the estimand via a probabilistic threat model) from operationalization (the concrete judge, prompts, and transformations used to estimate it). This yields a two-part sufficient condition for meaningful ASR comparison: (1) conceptual coherence—the estimands being compared must be congruent, and (2) measurement validity—the operationalized activity must accurately estimate those estimands. The authors demonstrate failures of both conditions empirically: showing that GCG (one-shot) vs. Generation Exploitation (Top-1 of 392) compares fundamentally different estimands; that repeated sampling under high-temperature decoding achieves ASR 0.83 on LLaMA 2 7B Chat, rivaling sophisticated jailbreaks; and that differential judge TPR/FPR across target systems can invert safety rankings even when true attack success probabilities are identical.

## Results

The paper's key empirical finding is that one-shot ASR for LLaMA 2 13B remains stable around ~0.2 across 49 decoding configurations, while Top-1 ASR over 49 repeated samples increases substantially with temperature (reaching ~0.9+ at high temperatures), demonstrating that the apparent ASR gains from non-deterministic decoding are driven by aggregation rather than configuration. Repeatedly sampling the baseline prompt 50 times at temperature 2.0 yields a Top-1 ASR of 0.83 on LLaMA 2 7B Chat, nearly matching the best reported jailbreak (LAA at 0.88). The GCG vs. Generation Exploitation comparison (0.31 vs. 0.89) is shown to be apples-to-oranges: GCG uses one-shot aggregation while GE uses Top-1 over 392 samples, making the 2.8× gap an artifact of different estimands rather than method superiority.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new method to benchmark against baselines; instead, it deconstructs existing comparisons to show they are invalid. The GCG vs. Generation Exploitation comparison (ASR 0.31 vs. 0.89) is shown to compare a one-shot estimand against a Top-1-of-392 estimand, rendering the conclusion that GE is 'more effective' unsupported. The authors' own experiments show that a simple repeated-sampling baseline (Top-1 over 50 samples at temperature 2.0, ASR = 0.83) nearly matches the best jailbreak method (LAA, ASR = 0.88) from Chu et al., suggesting sophisticated jailbreaks may not outperform trivial resampling under Top-1 metrics. On the measurement validity front, the paper demonstrates that differential judge TPR/FPR across target systems can invert safety rankings: two systems with identical true attack success probability (α = 0.5) can yield observed ASRs of 0.46 vs. 0.60 purely due to judge miscalibration, a magnitude of distortion comparable to the differences reported in published jailbreak studies.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the social science measurement validity framework of Adcock and Collier [1], as recently extended to genAI evaluation by Chouldechova et al. [10] and Wallach et al. [46], to the specific domain of jailbreaking and AI red teaming. It departs from the standard jailbreaking literature (Zou et al. [56], Huang et al. [27], Chu et al. [11]) by questioning the validity of ASR comparisons rather than proposing new attacks, and it draws on probabilistic threat models from ML security [6, 7, 14, 24, 25, 43, 51] that have been largely absent from red teaming work. The paper also engages with the LLM-as-a-judge literature [22, 42, 55] and standardization efforts like HarmBench [34] and JailbreakBench [9], arguing that standardization of experimental setup is necessary but insufficient without addressing measurement validity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This paper challenges a foundational assumption in the AI red teaming literature—that ASR comparisons provide valid evidence of relative system safety or attack efficacy—and provides a formal framework (probabilistic threat models + measurement theory) for diagnosing when they do not. The empirical finding that naive repeated sampling of base prompts at high temperature achieves a Top-1 ASR of 0.83 on LLaMA 2 7B Chat, nearly matching the best sophisticated jailbreak (0.88), has immediate practical implications for how new jailbreaks should be benchmarked. Open questions include how to calibrate prompt difficulty across risk categories, how to correct for differential judge error rates across systems, and whether the measurement theory framework generalizes beyond jailbreaking to other red teaming activities like memorization probing or stereotyping evaluation.
