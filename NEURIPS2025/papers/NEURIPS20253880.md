## Problem

Existing multi-agent consensus-seeking methods rely on simple voting thresholds (e.g., Byzantine 2/3 rule) to judge consensus, overlooking internal belief contradictions that destabilize the agreement. Additionally, agents indiscriminately incorporate opinions from all other agents rather than selectively identifying optimal collaborators, which can lead to suboptimal convergence or failure to reach consensus at all.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253880/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system takes a single text question and runs n=7 LLM agents (default Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct) that each produce an answer, a reasoning trace, and a belief value computed as the product of token probabilities of the final answer sentence. Agents are clustered into opinion groups via KMeans on TF-IDF keyword vectors, and the framework iterates up to 3 rounds, outputting a final consensus answer (most frequent) along with a three-level consensus state classification (full, partial, or no consensus) at each round.

## Method

BCCS introduces three iteratively executed modules grounded in opinion-dynamics theorems proving that supportive collaboration guarantees stable consensus while conflicting collaboration causes belief divergence. The Belief-Calibrated Consensus Judgment (BCCJ) module extends the standard 2/3 agent threshold with a belief-ratio condition (p_b > 0.8 for full consensus, p_b > 0.5 for partial), preventing premature termination on low-confidence agreements. In partial consensus, the Collaborator Assignment (CA) module computes a conflict score combining macro-conflict (belief-weighted Jaccard complement) and micro-conflict (local consistency difference) to assign the most uncertain agent conflicting collaborators while giving reliable agents supportive ones, balancing convergence speed against suboptimal lock-in. In no-consensus states, the Leader Selection (LS) module designates the highest-belief agents in each opinion group as leaders, and Theorem 3.3 guarantees that higher-belief leaders expedite convergence to the leaders' average state.

## Results

On the MATH benchmark, BCCS achieves 80.60% average accuracy, outperforming the strongest multi-agent baseline CMD (78.93%) by 1.67%. On MMLU, BCCS reaches 78.47% average accuracy, surpassing the best baseline PARSE (76.47%) by 2.00%. The gains are most pronounced on challenging subtasks, with maximum improvements of 2.23% on MATH (Precalculus) and 3.95% on MMLU (Humanities). All experiments use Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct as the backbone with 7 agents and 3 maximum iteration rounds.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BCCS outperforms all nine baselines on both MATH and MMLU. Among single-agent methods, CoT-SC is the strongest (76.67% MATH, 73.13% MMLU), trailing BCCS by 3.93% and 5.34% respectively. Among multi-agent methods, CMD is the closest competitor on MATH (78.93% vs. 80.60%, a 1.67% gap), while PARSE leads on MMLU (76.47% vs. 78.47%, a 2.00% gap). The advantage is largest on harder subtasks: BCCS gains 3.72% over CMD on MATH Precalculus (64.93% vs. 61.21%) and 3.95% over DyLAN on MMLU Humanities (78.16% vs. 74.21%). On the supplementary FOLIO and CommonsenseQA benchmarks, BCCS also leads the three strongest baselines (CMD, MAD, PARSE) by 1.61% and 2.18% respectively. The paper attributes the consistent gains to belief-calibrated consensus judgment preventing premature convergence to suboptimal answers, and to targeted collaborator/leader selection that balances supportive and conflicting interactions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM-driven multi-agent debate paradigm (MAD [18], DyLAN [3]) by replacing the standard Byzantine consensus (2/3 majority vote) with a belief-calibrated consensus judgment that incorporates LLM output probabilities as confidence signals. It departs from prior multi-agent collaboration methods (EoT, GroupDebate, PARSE, CMD) that use indiscriminate all-to-all or fixed-topology communication by introducing selective collaborator assignment and leader selection modules grounded in opinion dynamics theory. The theoretical contribution (Theorems 3.2–3.3) formalizes conditions for stable vs. unstable consensus in terms of supportive/conflicting agent interactions, providing a mathematical backbone that prior LLM multi-agent papers lacked.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical characterization of when multi-agent LLM consensus is stable versus unstable, which could guide the design of future collaboration protocols beyond the specific BCCS architecture. The practical finding that collaborating exclusively with supportive agents degrades performance (the '-Conflict' ablation) challenges the intuitive 'agree with allies' strategy and suggests that controlled exposure to dissent is necessary for optimal outcomes. Limitations include reliance on LLM output probabilities as belief proxies (which are known to be poorly calibrated), evaluation restricted to NLP benchmarks without embodied or interactive settings, and a fixed agent count of 7 that may not scale to larger pools. Open questions include dynamic leader selection, adaptive collaboration topologies, and whether the theoretical guarantees hold under non-averaging update rules.
