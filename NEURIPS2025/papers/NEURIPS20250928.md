## Problem

A critical prerequisite for autonomous AI research agents is the ability to faithfully reproduce existing scientific results, yet no existing benchmark evaluates this capability over a sequential chain of cumulative innovations in a single active research domain. This paper asks whether current frontier reasoning LLMs, combined with state-of-the-art search scaffolds, can reimplement already-known code-level improvements in LLM training when given descriptions of those improvements.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250928/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each of the 19 benchmark tasks provides the agent with the previous speedrun record's single-file PyTorch training script (train_gpt.py) and optionally one or more hints describing the next record's changes at three abstraction levels: pseudocode (L1), natural-language description (L2), and a mini-paper summary (L3). The agent must produce a modified training script that is executed on a fixed 8×H100 node with a 60-minute wall-clock cap; performance is measured by the Fraction of Speedup Recovered (FSR), which normalizes the agent's achieved training-time reduction against the ground-truth reduction between consecutive records, with the overall score being the mean FSR across all 19 tasks.

## Method

The benchmark is constructed from 21 successive community-driven NanoGPT Speedrun records (GPT-2 training on FineWeb, target loss 3.28), excluding one PyTorch-version-only transition, yielding 19 record-reproduction tasks with clear code-level ground truth. The agent scaffold extends AIDE into a flexible parameterized tree search with configurable initial pool size, branching factor, debug probability, and max debug depth, supporting five variants (Tree, Forest, AIDE, Multi-AIDE, Flat/Best-of-M) under a fixed budget of 20 search steps. Each search step follows three stages: implementation via Aider diff-based edits conditioned on the task and optional hints, execution on the 8×H100 node, and LLM-based analysis of stdout/stderr to produce a natural-language summary feeding back into the next iteration. The paper evaluates four frontier models (DeepSeek-R1, o3-mini, Gemini-2.5-Pro, Claude-3.7-Sonnet) across all scaffold and hint combinations (6,840 total agent runs), finding that even the best configuration (o3-mini + Multi-AIDE + all three hints) recovers only ~46% of the ground-truth speedup, and without hints no agent exceeds 20% FSR.

## Results

The best-performing agent (o3-mini with multi-AIDE scaffold and pseudocode hints) recovers approximately 43% of the human-achieved speedup (FSR = 0.43±0.02), while the best combined-hint configuration (L1+L2+L3 with multi-AIDE) reaches 46% FSR. Without any hints, all agents fail to recover more than 20% of the speedup on average. In the IQM evaluation, Gemini-2.5-Pro and Claude-3.7-Sonnet perform worst (near 0 FSR), lagging even behind the open-weights DeepSeek-R1, while multi-AIDE outperforms all other search scaffolds.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four frontier LLMs (o3-mini, DeepSeek-R1, Gemini-2.5-Pro, Claude-3.7-Sonnet) across five search scaffolds (Flat/Best-of-M, Tree, Forest, AIDE, Multi-AIDE) and six hint regimes. o3-mini is the strongest model overall, achieving 43% FSR with pseudocode hints and 46% with all three hints combined under multi-AIDE, substantially outperforming DeepSeek-R1 (best: 41% with L1+L2+L3/multi-AIDE) and far exceeding Gemini-2.5-Pro (best: 26%) and Claude-3.7-Sonnet (best: 34% with L3/AIDE, but near 0 in IQM due to high bug rates). A surprising finding is that individual hints *hurt* DeepSeek-R1 (e.g., L2 text drops FSR to 0.06–0.10 vs. 0.13–0.20 for L1), while combined hints (L1+L2+L3) boost R1 by up to +0.31 over its best individual hint, suggesting R1 benefits from longer context. Conversely, combining hints degrades o3-mini's performance relative to pseudocode alone (e.g., L1+L2 drops from 0.43 to 0.37 with multi-AIDE). Flat (Best-of-M) search matches or outperforms iterated scaffolds for individual hints, while multi-AIDE is the best scaffold for combined hints, indicating that branching search with debugging is most effective when the agent has richer information to work with.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the AIDE search scaffold (Jiang et al., 2025) into a more general parameterization supporting multiple search variants (tree, forest, flat, multi-AIDE) and integrates it with a new benchmark grounded in the NanoGPT Speedrun community effort. Conceptually, it departs from prior ML reproducibility benchmarks (CORE-bench, PaperBench, RE-bench, MLAgentBench) by evaluating a *sequential* chain of 19 cumulative innovations in a single domain (LLM training speedup) rather than a collection of unrelated papers, enabling a unified success metric (wall-clock training time) and cross-task comparison. The benchmark also introduces graded hint levels (pseudocode, text, mini-paper) that allow controlled study of how background information format affects reproduction fidelity, a dimension absent from prior reproducibility evaluations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark provides a non-saturated, accessible measure of an LLM agent's ability to automate scientific reproducibility—a necessary prerequisite for autonomous research agents that current frontier models demonstrably lack. Key open questions include whether agents can handle multi-file codebases, distributed training, and genuine innovation (as opposed to reproduction), and whether the observed failure modes (hints degrading R1 performance, flat search matching iterated search) reflect fundamental limitations in code-level reasoning or are addressable with better scaffolds. The finding that even detailed, correct descriptions of known code changes are insufficient for faithful reimplementation suggests that the bottleneck is not information access but the translation from description to working implementation, a gap that must be closed before recursive self-improvement loops become plausible.
