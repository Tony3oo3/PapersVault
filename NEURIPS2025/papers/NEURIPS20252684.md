## Problem

Graph contrastive learning methods for heterophilic graphs rely heavily on random or carefully designed topological augmentations (e.g., edge dropping), which can drastically alter neighborhood semantics in graphs where connected nodes belong to different classes. Additionally, most existing approaches enforce only local node-level alignment while neglecting global structural constraints, sacrificing intra-class cohesiveness and inter-class discrimination.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252684/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes an undirected graph specified by its adjacency matrix A ∈ {0,1}^{n×n} and raw node feature matrix X ∈ R^{n×d}, along with positional encodings derived from d^♯ steps of random-walk diffusion. It produces d†-dimensional node representations (d† ≪ d) suitable for downstream linear-probe evaluation, as well as soft coloring labels π_i ∈ R^{χ_G} per node that encode homophilic/heterophilic group membership. The coloring labels are further discretized via Gumbel-Softmax into near one-hot assignments used for positive sample selection.

## Method

CoRep replaces augmentation-based contrastive views with a coloring-matching objective: a learnable edge evaluator (integrating raw features and positional encodings through MLPs, with Gumbel-Max reparameterization) estimates per-edge homophily scores, and a coloring classifier maps node representations to soft color distributions. A coloring matching loss weighted by (1 − ω̂_{i,j}) pulls homophilic neighbors' colors together and pushes heterophilic neighbors' colors apart, while a Gumbel-Softmax-based redundancy constraint (column-wise max pooling over discretized color assignments) enforces sparsity and intra-class compactness. A triplet relation ranking loss dynamically calibrates the edge evaluator by enforcing that homophilic pairs rank above random pairs and heterophilic pairs rank below them in color similarity. For global structure, multi-hop same-color neighbors form the positive set in an InfoNCE-style contrastive loss, and an entropy regularization term prevents degenerate uniform color distributions. The structural encoder is an adaptive GNN (FAGCN-style attention with positional encoding) that captures both low- and high-frequency signals.

## Results

CoRep achieves state-of-the-art node classification accuracy on 10 out of 14 benchmark datasets. On homophilic graphs, it reaches 85.04% on Cora and 82.20% on Wiki-CS, surpassing the best baseline (GREET) by 1.72% and 2.26% respectively. On heterophilic graphs, CoRep delivers its largest gains: 46.88% on Squirrel (+5.71% relative over GREET's 40.86%) and 88.65% on Texas (+2.97% relative over PC-Conv's 85.68%). It also outperforms supervised heterophilic methods (H2GCN, FAGCN, PC-Conv) in 85.71% of cases without using any labels during training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CoRep outperforms all 17 baselines on 10 of 14 datasets, winning every heterophilic benchmark (Chameleon, Squirrel, Actor, Cornell, Texas, Wisconsin) and 7 of 8 homophilic ones. The largest margin is on Squirrel, where CoRep (46.88%) surpasses the best heterophilic SSL method GREET (40.86%) by 6.02 absolute points and the supervised FAGCN (41.17%) by 5.71 points. On Texas, CoRep (88.65%) beats PC-Conv (85.68%) and GREET (85.41%), the two strongest baselines. On the sole homophilic dataset where CoRep does not win (CoAuthor CS, 94.39% vs. GREET's 94.68%), the gap is within one standard deviation. Among conventional contrastive methods (DGI, GRACE, GCA, BGRL), CoRep leads by 15–35 points on heterophilic graphs, confirming that augmentation-based GCL is fundamentally ill-suited to heterophilic structures. Against supervised heterophilic GNNs (H2GCN, FAGCN, PC-Conv), CoRep matches or exceeds them in 5 of 6 heterophilic datasets without any label supervision.

## Relation to Prior Work

CoRep departs from the dominant graph contrastive learning (GCL) paradigm, which relies on data augmentation (edge dropping, feature masking) to generate contrastive views, by instead using a coloring classifier to assign soft labels that encode homophilic/heterophilic relationships. It repurposes the classical graph coloring problem as a heuristic for representation learning rather than solving it directly, and draws on the adaptive GNN encoder from FAGCN and positional encoding from Dwivedi et al. for structural awareness. Compared to prior heterophilic self-supervised methods (HGRL, GREET, HeteGCL) that still depend on carefully designed or random augmentations, CoRep eliminates augmentation entirely, replacing it with a learnable edge evaluator and multi-hop same-color positive sample construction. The Gumbel-Softmax discretization and triplet ranking loss are borrowed from discrete optimization and metric learning literature respectively, but their combination for edge-aware coloring is novel.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Heterophilic graphs are prevalent in high-stakes applications (fraud detection, molecular biology, social networks) where augmentation-based methods are fragile because topological perturbations can destroy critical sparse intra-group connections. CoRep's coloring-based mechanism offers a more structure-preserving alternative, and the multi-hop same-color positive set addresses the underexplored gap of global structural consistency in heterophilic SSL. Open questions include scalability to very large graphs (the method requires multi-hop neighborhood computation and pairwise edge evaluation), sensitivity to the number of colors hyperparameter, and whether the edge evaluator's accuracy bottleneck (a chicken-and-egg dependency with the coloring classifier) limits performance on extremely heterophilic or noisy graphs. The paper acknowledges limitations in the appendix but does not deeply explore failure modes.
