## Problem

Monitoring LLM interactions for high-stakes contexts—where outputs could cause significant real-world harm—is critical for safe deployment, yet existing approaches like Chain-of-Thought analysis and guardrail classifiers are computationally expensive. This paper addresses the underexplored question of whether lightweight activation probes trained on internal model states can detect high-stakes interactions at a fraction of the cost of prompted or finetuned LLM monitors, and whether they can be effectively combined with expensive baselines in a hierarchical system.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254249/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The probes consume pre-layer-norm residual-stream activations (dimension 8192) from a single layer (layer 31 of 80) of Llama-3.3-70B, which are already available as a free byproduct of the model generating its response—no additional forward pass is required. The output is a scalar high-stakes probability in [0,1] via a sigmoid. Baselines instead take the raw conversation text and produce a classification probability, with prompted baselines using log-likelihood ratios over two natural continuations to avoid refusals.

## Method

The paper trains six probe architectures (Mean, Max, Last Token, Softmax, Attention, Max of Rolling Means) on residual activations from Llama-3.3-70B using a synthetic dataset of ~8000 high/low-stakes samples generated by GPT-4o across diverse domains, tones, languages, and prompt styles. The Attention and Softmax probes perform best because they aggregate information across all tokens, which is necessary since high-stakes labeling often depends on the full conversational context. A key architectural contribution is a two-stage cascade: the probe scores all interactions cheaply, then routes the k% of samples closest to the median probe score (indicating highest uncertainty) to an expensive LLM classifier, combining the two scores via averaging. This cascade consistently outperforms either method alone at a fixed compute budget, and incorporating as few as 32 labeled deployment samples into probe training significantly closes the synthetic-to-real-world generalization gap.

## Results

The Attention probe trained on Llama-3.3-70B activations achieves a mean AUROC of 0.916 across six out-of-distribution evaluation datasets, comparable to medium-sized LLM monitors (Llama-3.1-8B prompted: 0.916; Gemma-3-12B prompted: 0.908) while being ~1,000,000× more computationally efficient. At the stricter TPR@1% FPR metric, the best probe detects 43% of high-stakes cases versus 55% for the strongest LLM baseline (Llama-3.3-70B prompted, AUROC 0.970). Training on just 32 deployment-domain samples significantly closes the OOD gap, allowing probes to exceed the prompted Llama-3.3-70B baseline on some datasets. A cascade combining probes with LLM classifiers consistently outperforms either method alone across compute budgets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Llama-family baselines (Grattafiori et al., 2024), the Attention probe clearly outperforms Llama-3.2-1B in both prompted (0.916 vs 0.766) and finetuned settings, and matches Llama-3.1-8B prompted (0.916 vs 0.916) while being orders of magnitude cheaper. However, the probe underperforms the prompted Llama-3.3-70B (0.916 vs 0.970) and the finetuned Llama-3.1-8B, which are the strongest baselines overall. Against Gemma-family baselines (Team et al., 2025), the probe outperforms Gemma-3-1B (0.916 vs 0.713) and Gemma-3-12B in both prompted (0.916 vs 0.908) and finetuned settings, and approaches but does not quite match Gemma-3-27B prompted (0.916 vs 0.945). Notably, on the fully held-out Aya Redteaming dataset, probes significantly outperform all baselines except Llama-3.3-70B, while on Mental Health they underperform all baselines. The cascade combination of probes with even weaker baselines (e.g., finetuned Gemma-3-1B) outperforms either component alone, demonstrating complementary error patterns.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the activation-probing lineage (Alain & Bengio, 2018; Kantamneni et al., 2025) from prior safety-relevant targets—deception, truthfulness, hazardous information—to the broader and more general notion of 'high-stakes' interactions, where any LLM output could carry substantial real-world consequences. It departs from prior probe work by rigorously benchmarking against prompted and finetuned LLM monitors of multiple sizes (1B–70B) and by introducing a two-stage cascade that routes uncertain probe predictions to expensive LLM classifiers, a design not previously applied to activation-probe-based monitoring. The Softmax and Max-of-Rolling-Means probe architectures are novel contributions, while the Attention probe is adopted directly from Kantamneni et al. (2025).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The demonstrated six-orders-of-magnitude computational savings (reusing already-computed activations) make probe-based monitoring practically viable for large-scale, real-time deployment where querying a full LLM on every interaction is prohibitive. The cascade architecture—probes as a cheap first filter, LLMs for uncertain cases—offers a concrete, cost-optimal monitoring pipeline that outperforms either component alone. Key open questions remain: whether white-box access reveals safety-critical states invisible to text-level monitors, how to curate training data for the right set of monitored concepts without blind spots, and whether probes can be made robust to adversarial obfuscation of activations. The reliance on GPT-4o for both training and evaluation labeling also raises concerns about circularity and whether probes capture a genuinely broader notion of 'high-stakes' beyond one model's interpretation.
