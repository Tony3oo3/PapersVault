## Problem

Physics reasoning remains an underexplored testbed for multimodal LLMs compared to mathematics, and existing physics benchmarks either lack visual components entirely or fail to quantify how much models actually depend on diagrams. SEEPHYS addresses this by asking whether seeing genuinely helps thinking: it constructs a benchmark where 75% of problems are vision-essential, meaning the diagram contains indispensable information (e.g., circuit topology, labeled vectors, scale-dependent data) that cannot be recovered from text alone, exposing a fundamental gap in current MLLMs' ability to connect diagram interpretation with physics reasoning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253130/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each problem is presented under four controlled settings: Text+Vision (question text paired with original diagram images), Text+Caption (text plus an o4-mini-generated detailed description of geometric features and numerical values), Text Only (question text alone), and Vision Only (the entire question text and diagram rendered into a single composite image up to 4096×4096 pixels with varied fonts). Models produce open-ended answers (a single definitive value or expression) under zero-shot chain-of-thought prompts in English or Chinese. Scoring uses a composite pipeline: SymPy first attempts exact symbolic matching, and responses that fail are judged by DeepSeek-V3 against ground-truth answers with significant-figure tolerance.

## Method

SEEPHYS is a 2,000-question, 2,245-image benchmark spanning 7 physics domains (Classical Mechanics, Electromagnetism, Astrophysics/Cosmology/Gravitation, Optics, Atomic/Molecular/Nuclear/Particle Physics, Quantum Mechanics/Information/Technology, Thermodynamics/Statistical Mechanics), 21 diagram types, and 8 knowledge levels from middle school to PhD qualifying exams. The key design axis is a binary vision-dependency split: Vision-Essential (75%) problems require diagram reading for solution, while Vision-Optional (25%) problems have all critical information in text, enabling controlled measurement of visual contribution via four input settings. Data were collected from 7,000+ PDFs across international curricula, OCR-processed via Mathpix, cleaned with GPT-4.1, and cross-validated by two independent physics-domain annotators with a third PhD arbitrator for disagreements. To eliminate textual shortcuts, the authors additionally render every question into a purely visual image, forcing models to perform OCR and spatial reasoning from pixels. Evaluation covers 28 models (9 LLMs, 19 MLLMs) with a stratified 200-question human expert baseline, and failure-mode analysis is conducted on a 10% stratified sample of o4-mini reasoning traces.

## Results

On SEEPHYS, the best-performing MLLM (Gemini-2.5-Pro) achieves only 54.9% overall accuracy, while o4-mini reaches 51.9%, both well below the 86.5% achieved by human experts. Among text-only LLMs, DeepSeek-R1 attains 42.2% accuracy, comparable to the multimodal o3-mini (40.3%), revealing that current MLLMs' visual alignment provides limited advantage over strong text-only reasoning. In the Vision-Essential subset (75% of questions), removing visual input causes substantial accuracy drops (e.g., o4-mini drops from 46.5% to 29.9% in Text-Only), confirming that most problems genuinely require diagram interpretation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among 28 evaluated models, Gemini-2.5-Pro (54.9%) and o4-mini (51.9%) lead all MLLMs, but both fall ~32 points short of human experts (86.5%). A striking finding is that the text-only LLM DeepSeek-R1 (42.2%) outperforms several multimodal models including o3-mini (40.3%), GPT-4.1 (35.3%), and Claude-3.7-Sonnet (34.6%), suggesting that current MLLMs' visual alignment adds limited value over strong textual reasoning. In the Vision-Essential subset, all models show significant accuracy drops when visual input is removed (Δ₂ ranges from 29.5% for o3-mini to 55.9% for Claude-3.7-Sonnet), confirming the benchmark's visual necessity. However, even in the Vision-Optional subset where text alone suffices, most models still benefit from diagrams (e.g., Claude-3.7-Sonnet gains 56.1%), indicating that physics diagrams aid conceptual modeling beyond mere information extraction. Weaker open-source MLLMs (LLaVA-OneVision-7B at 6.1%, InternVL2.5-8B at 6.2%) show a 74.5% performance drop from middle school to PhD level, versus only 19.9% for o4-mini, revealing that knowledge injection yields diminishing returns for smaller models.

## Relation to Prior Work

SEEPHYS carves new ground at the intersection of physics reasoning benchmarks and multimodal evaluation. Prior physics benchmarks (PHYBench, UGPhysics, PHYSICS, PhysReason) either lack visual components entirely or include diagrams without analyzing their actual contribution to problem-solving. The paper departs from the math-reasoning benchmark lineage (GSM8K → MATH → MathVista → MathVerse) by arguing that physics diagrams, with their richer topological structures and real-world grounding, provide a more demanding testbed for multimodal perception than geometric figures. Its key methodological departure is the explicit Vision-Essential/Vision-Optional taxonomy combined with four controlled experimental settings (TV/TC/TO/VO) to isolate the causal contribution of visual information.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a fundamental gap: even frontier MLLMs (Gemini-2.5-Pro, o4-mini) stay below 60% accuracy, and text-only LLMs (DeepSeek-R1) can match or exceed multimodal models, suggesting that current multimodal alignment for physics diagrams is severely underdeveloped. The finding that even non-essential diagrams improve performance hints that visual scaffolding aids conceptual modeling beyond mere information extraction. Open questions include process-level (not just outcome-level) evaluation of physics reasoning, the connection between theoretical problem-solving and real-world world modeling, and whether the non-monotonic difficulty curve (advanced Olympiad harder than PhD) reflects memorization rather than genuine understanding.
