## Problem

Existing dexterous grasp generation and transfer methods are hand-specific, requiring retraining or fine-tuning for each new robotic hand, and naive geometric transfer (copying joint angles or end-effector poses) produces physically invalid grasps across morphologically different hands. The core gap is the absence of a framework that can learn distribution-level grasp translation from unpaired, hand-specific datasets while preserving functional grasp properties—contact patterns, wrench capabilities, and stability—rather than mere geometric similarity.

## Input / Output

The method takes a segmented point cloud of the source hand grasping an object plus the object point cloud as input. The hand point cloud is encoded via a PVCNN backbone into a single 768-dim latent token, while the object is encoded by a pretrained LION VAE into one global shape token and 512 local tokens, supplemented by five contact anchor tokens derived from farthest-point sampling near the hand. The output is a full target-hand grasp configuration (joint angles plus 6-DoF base pose in SE(3)), decoded from the translated latent code and verified through a differentiable forward-kinematics layer that produces 3D hand mesh vertices.

## Method

The approach is a two-stage latent-space pipeline. Stage 1 trains a VAE (PVCNN encoder + MLP decoder with a differentiable FK layer) to embed segmented hand observations into a shared 768-dim latent space. Stage 2 learns a Schrödinger Bridge between source and target latent distributions using the [SF]²M score-and-flow-matching objective with a U-ViT backbone, conditioned on object shape tokens and contact anchor tokens. The key novelty is the design of four physics-informed ground costs—base pose alignment in SE(3), bidirectional Chamfer distance on contact maps, grasp wrench hull (GWH) volume IoU, and Jacobian-based maximal effect vectors—that define the entropic OT coupling (computed via minibatch Sinkhorn) used to construct conditional Brownian bridge training targets. At inference, a source observation is encoded, the latent is evolved forward via Euler-Maruyama integration of the learned SDE, and the resulting latent is decoded into the target hand configuration, requiring no paired demonstrations or hand-specific simulation at test time.

## Results

On the MultiGripperGrasp dataset across three translation tasks (Human→Allegro, Human→Shadow, Shadow→Allegro), the best variant (Jacobian OT cost) achieves a mean grasp success rate of 67.45%, outperforming the strongest baseline (Diffusion, 60.83%) by ~6.6 points. For functional alignment, the GWH-cost variant attains a mean 6D Grasp Wrench Hull IoU of 14.63%, nearly doubling the best baseline (RFP, 7.77%). The contact-cost variant achieves the highest diversity (0.272 rad mean joint std), matching the diffusion baseline (0.271 rad) while substantially exceeding all optimization-based methods (~0.20 rad). Inference takes ~0.8 s per grasp with 100 integration steps, versus >5 s for RobotFingerPrint.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against three grasp-transfer baselines and one generative baseline. RobotFingerPrint (RFP) [41], which maps grippers into a unified coordinate space, achieves 57.00% mean success and 7.77% mean GWH IoU; the paper's best variants exceed these by 10+ points in success and ~7 points in IoU. Dex-Retargeting [72] and CrossDex [98], both relying on manually annotated link-to-link correspondences, perform substantially worse (43.19% and 26.87% mean success, respectively), with the paper attributing their failures to ignoring collisions with the palm and other hand parts. The strongest baseline is a diffusion model trained with contact-map OT conditioning, which reaches 60.83% mean success and 9.58% mean IoU; the paper's Jacobian variant surpasses it by 6.6 points in success, while the GWH variant leads by 5.05 points in IoU. On diversity, the paper's contact variant (0.272 rad) marginally edges out the diffusion baseline (0.271 rad), while all optimization-based baselines cluster around 0.20 rad. Notably, the paper's method also runs ~6× faster than RFP at inference (0.8 s vs. >5 s per grasp).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the simulation-free Schrödinger Bridge framework of Tong et al. [84,85]—originally developed for image-to-image translation and generative modeling—to the domain of dexterous grasp translation, replacing generic Euclidean/geodesic ground costs with four physics-informed costs (base pose, contact map, grasp wrench hull, and Jacobian manipulability). It departs from prior grasp transfer methods (RobotFingerPrint, Dex-Retargeting, CrossDex) that rely on predefined link correspondences or per-grasp optimization by formulating translation as a distributional transport in a learned VAE latent space, requiring no paired demonstrations or hand-specific simulation. The VAE encoder/decoder and U-ViT backbone follow standard architectures from point-cloud processing (PVCNN) and diffusion model design, while the LION object encoder and contact anchor tokens are borrowed from UGG [52].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that physics-informed OT costs can meaningfully guide distributional grasp translation, with different costs revealing a trade-off between stability (Jacobian cost) and functional alignment (GWH/Contact costs)—a useful diagnostic for practitioners choosing which physical property to prioritize. Key limitations remain: the method cannot generalize to unseen hand morphologies at inference (the VAE decoder is hand-specific), performance degrades on thin-shell objects, and the absolute GWH IoU values (≤15%) suggest functional alignment is still far from exact. Open questions include whether the VAE can be made truly hand-agnostic, whether the SB formulation extends to multi-step manipulation sequences, and whether the minibatch OT approximation introduces systematic biases at scale.
