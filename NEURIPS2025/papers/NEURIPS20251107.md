## Problem

While single-molecule property prediction benefits from well-established benchmarks like MoleculeNet and TDC, the chemical mixture space remains largely unexplored by the ML community due to scattered datasets, inconsistent evaluation protocols, and the absence of a centralized resource. This paper introduces CheMixHub, the first comprehensive benchmark spanning 11 property prediction tasks across 7 curated datasets (~500k data points), along with diverse generalization splits and initial deep learning baselines to enable systematic study of multi-component molecular systems.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251107/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each data point is a variable-size set of molecules (1–121 components) represented as SMILES strings, paired with mole fractions for composition and, for temperature-dependent tasks, a scalar temperature value. Polymers are encoded by their monomeric units plus molar mass, and ionic salts are preserved as disconnected graphs. Targets are scalar property values (density, viscosity, conductivity, solubility, enthalpy of mixing, octane number, perceptual similarity), with logarithmic scaling applied to conductivity, solubility, and viscosity to handle wide experimental ranges.

## Method

The paper defines a three-level modeling space: (1) molecular representation via GraphNet GNNs, frozen MolT5 CLM embeddings, or 200-d RDKit descriptors, each infused with mole-fraction context via element-wise multiplication, concatenation, or FiLM; (2) mixture-level interaction using either DeepSets (one-body) or self-attention (two-body) followed by one of six permutation-invariant aggregations (mean, max, attention, PNA, scaled PNA, set2set), with mixture context (e.g., temperature) injected via concatenation or FiLM; and (3) an output head that is either a standard FC layer or, for Arrhenius-governed tasks, predicts the pre-exponential factor A and activation energy E_a to compute the property through the Arrhenius equation. Four data-splitting strategies (random, leave-molecules-out, mixture-size threshold, and temperature-bin exclusion) probe different axes of generalization. Key findings include that XGBoost with RDKit/MolT5 features is a surprisingly strong baseline on random splits, that pre-trained CLM representations outperform GNNs and descriptors, and that the Arrhenius-based head yields substantial MAE and Pearson improvements on temperature-dependent tasks under out-of-distribution temperature splits.

## Results

Across 11 mixture property prediction tasks, MolT5 (CLM) representations combined with self-attention or DeepSets mixture encoders generally achieve the best deep learning performance, with MAE ranging from 0.005 (Miscible Solvents ρ) to 0.244 (II Thermo ln(κ)). XGBoost with RDKit descriptors is a surprisingly strong non-deep-learning baseline, matching or exceeding deep models on several tasks (e.g., Drug Solubility ln(S): 0.028 MAE; NIST-full ln(η): 0.055 MAE). Incorporating an Arrhenius physics-based predictive head substantially improves temperature-dependent tasks: NIST ln(η) MAE drops from 0.079 to 0.048, and II Thermo ln(κ) from 0.354 to 0.284 on temperature-exclusion splits, while Pearson ρ rises to 0.980–0.988.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary non-deep-learning baseline is XGBoost (with RDKit descriptors or MolT5 embeddings). XGBoost is highly competitive on random splits: it achieves the best MAE on NIST-full ln(η) (0.055 with RDKit) and ties for best on Drug Solubility ln(S) (0.028 with both MolT5 and RDKit). However, XGBoost clearly struggles on tasks with strong non-linear mixture behavior—its MAE on ΔH_mix is 0.225–0.269 versus 0.157–0.159 for MolT5-based deep models, and on ΔH_vap it reaches 0.295–0.306 versus 0.071–0.125. On the more challenging temperature-exclusion splits (Table 3), XGBoost degrades substantially (e.g., NIST ln(η) MAE 0.126 vs. 0.048 for the best deep model with Arrhenius head), highlighting that its random-split strength does not transfer to realistic generalization settings. Among deep learning configurations, MolT5 + self-attention or DeepSets consistently outperforms GNN-based representations, while the choice between DeepSets (one-body) and self-attention (two-body) interactions is task-dependent with no uniform winner.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper consolidates the scattered landscape of chemical mixture property prediction—where individual studies (Bao et al. for drug solubility, Bilodeau et al. for NIST viscosity, Bradford et al. for polymer electrolytes, Tom et al. for olfactory mixtures) each addressed a single property with bespoke evaluation—into a unified benchmark of 11 tasks. Methodologically, it draws on the set-encoder literature (DeepSets, SetTransformer, Janossy pooling) for permutation-invariant mixture aggregation, on molecular representation learning (Graph Networks, MolT5 CLMs, RDKit descriptors), and on physics-informed ML (Arrhenius-constrained heads), but is the first to systematically map this modeling space with multiple context-infusion strategies (FiLM, concatenation, element-wise multiplication) and aggregation operations (PNA, set2set, attention). It departs from prior single-task mixture studies by introducing four distinct generalization splits (random, leave-molecules-out, mixture-size, temperature) that go beyond the standard random CV used in most prior work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CheMixHub fills a critical gap analogous to MoleculeNet's role for single-molecule tasks: before this work, no centralized, standardized benchmark existed for multi-component molecular systems, making cross-study comparison impossible. The finding that XGBoost with simple molecular features is a surprisingly competitive baseline on random splits, while the Arrhenius physics head yields substantial gains (e.g., Pearson 0.923→0.987 on IlThermo conductivity) on temperature-exclusion splits, provides concrete guidance for where deep learning and domain knowledge each add value. Open questions include how to represent polymers beyond monomeric units, whether pre-trained mixture-aware GNNs or CLMs can close the gap to XGBoost on random splits, and how to extend from property prediction to formulation discovery and optimization in data-scarce regimes.
