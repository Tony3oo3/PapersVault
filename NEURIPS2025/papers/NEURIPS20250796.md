## Problem

Traditional reinforcement learning relies exclusively on scalar reward signals, making it unable to exploit the rich linguistic context—domain knowledge, task descriptions, expert hints—that is naturally available in real-world tasks. Prior LLM+RL work uses language models as auxiliary components (reward shaping, action suggestion) while still depending on conventional RL optimizers for policy updates. This paper asks whether an LLM can serve as the policy optimizer itself, performing the entire search loop through in-context reasoning over both numerical rewards and natural language.

## Input / Output

At each optimization step (every 20 episodes, 400 steps per 8000-episode trial), the LLM receives a structured prompt containing parameter definitions, formatting constraints, and—optionally in ProPS⁺—task descriptions, policy specifications, and expert hints, along with the full history Γ of all previously proposed parameter vectors and their episodic rewards. The LLM outputs a single new policy parameter vector (e.g., 3 floats for MountainCar, up to 100 for MuJoCo tasks) at a specified decimal precision, accompanied by a free-text justification explaining the update rationale. Critically, the LLM does not generate actions at inference time; once parameters are set, the policy (linear or tabular) executes independently in the environment.

## Method

ProPS replaces the conventional RL optimizer with an LLM that directly proposes policy parameter updates via the rule θ ← LLM(Γ, P), where Γ is the complete history of (parameter, reward) pairs and P is a structured prompt. Two variants are studied: ProPS uses only numerical reward feedback, while ProPS⁺ augments the prompt with semantic context (environment descriptions, parameter semantics, expert hints) to guide exploration. Unlike OPRO, no external ranking or sub-selection is performed—optimization occurs entirely within the LLM's in-context reasoning over the full search history. Unlike Evolution Strategies, there is no population or parametric search distribution; a single candidate is proposed per step by reasoning over all past trials. For continuous-state tasks, linear policies π_θ(s) = θᵀφ(s) are used; for discrete tasks, tabular policies are employed. The authors further demonstrate that a lightweight Qwen2.5-14B model can be fine-tuned via GRPO with LoRA to improve policy search performance, and that the LLM's textual justifications function as interpretable 'textual gradients' explaining parameter modifications.

## Results

ProPS+ (semantically-augmented) achieves top performance in 8 out of 15 Gymnasium tasks, outperforming all seven standard RL baselines (PPO, SAC, TRPO, DQN, TD3, A2C, DDPG). Headline numbers include a perfect 1000.00±0.00 on Inverted Pendulum, 500.00±0.00 on CartPole, 227.30±56.23 on Swimmer (vs. best baseline TRPO at 52.96±18.86), and 0.97±0.09 on Nim (vs. best baseline A2C at 0.58±0.10). The base ProPS variant (numerical-only) already outperforms all baselines in 7/15 tasks, and adding expert hints further improves mean rewards by 8.5–62% across six domains while reducing standard deviation by up to 97%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the seven standard RL algorithms (PPO, SAC, TRPO, DQN, TD3, A2C, DDPG), ProPS+ wins 8/15 tasks while TRPO wins 5 and base ProPS wins 2; the largest margins are on Swimmer (227.30 vs. 52.96 for TRPO, a 4.3× improvement) and Nim (0.97 vs. 0.58 for A2C). ProPS+ underperforms on high-dimensional MuJoCo locomotion (Hopper: 356.22 vs. TRPO 716.90; Walker: 126.75 vs. TRPO 519.38) and on the stochastic FrozenLake task, where semantic context misleads the LLM into assuming deterministic dynamics. In the numerical-optimization ablation (Appendix A), Gemini-1.5-pro outperforms Adam, Nelder-Mead, Gradient Descent, and Random Search on 12/20 benchmark functions (Ackley, Rastrigin, Levy, Weierstrass, Salomon at 2–16D), while Adam leads on 4 and NM on 3. Against evolution-strategy baselines (OpenAI-ES, (μ+λ)-ES, Tabu Search), ProPS+ again tops 8/15 tasks, with OpenAI-ES dominating the high-dimensional MuJoCo environments (Hopper 1009.38, Walker 938.56). Adding expert hints to ProPS+ yields 8.5–62% reward improvements and up to 97% variance reduction across six domains, with the largest gains on Walker (+62%) and Reacher (+47%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant LLM+RL paradigm where language models augment specific pipeline components (reward shaping à la DrEureka, Q-table modulation, or action suggestion) by instead placing the LLM at the center of the optimization loop as the policy search engine itself. It builds conceptually on OPRO's iterative prompt-based optimization and the in-context RL line (Laskin et al., Zisman et al.), but differs by performing optimization entirely within the LLM without external ranking or sub-selection, and by fusing linguistic context (task descriptions, domain knowledge, expert hints) with numerical reward feedback in a single reasoning step. The closest prior work is in-context bandit RL (Monea et al.), which the authors explicitly note is restricted to binary rewards and classification, leaving open the multi-step, continuous-reward setting that ProPS addresses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ProPS demonstrates that LLMs can serve as transparent, interpretable policy optimizers that produce natural-language justifications for each parameter update—a property valuable for safety-critical domains like healthcare, robotics, and space applications where black-box gradient-based RL is insufficient. Key limitations constrain near-term impact: the method is validated only on policies up to ~100 parameters (linear or tabular), does not yet scale to deep RL with image inputs, and the FrozenLake result shows that semantic context can introduce harmful inductive biases when the LLM misinterprets stochasticity. Open questions include whether the optimization capability is an emergent pretraining artifact, how to design policy parameterizations that are both expressive and amenable to LLM modification, and whether the approach generalizes to high-dimensional continuous control without the random-projection workaround explored in the appendix.
