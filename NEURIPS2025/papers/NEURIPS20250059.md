## Problem

Non-stationary MDPs model decision-making where environmental dynamics evolve over time, yet no standardized simulation framework or benchmark suite exists for them, unlike the widely adopted Gymnasium toolkit for stationary MDPs. Prior work evaluates algorithms on ad-hoc custom environments or manually injects non-stationarity into stationary benchmarks, preventing consistent and reproducible comparison across methods.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250059/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The user supplies a base Gymnasium environment, a dictionary mapping tunable parameters to paired scheduler and update-function objects, and a notification level (none, basic Boolean flag, or detailed with magnitude). The wrapped environment emits a custom four-field observation (state, env_change dict of Boolean flags, delta_change dict of magnitudes or Wasserstein distances, and relative_time) rather than a plain state vector, and exposes a get_planning_env() method that returns a stationary snapshot aligned to the current parameter values. An evaluation module additionally computes Ensemble Regret across a policy suite and the PAMCTS-Bound (max L∞ transition-function difference) as environment-difficulty metrics.

## Method

NS-Gym wraps existing Gymnasium environments with a modular architecture that decouples the semi-Markov evolution of environmental parameters θ from the agent's decision loop, following the formalism of Campo et al. (1991). Schedulers (continuous, periodic, random, stepwise, or custom) determine *when* a parameter update fires, while update functions (increment, random walk with budget bounds, distribution decrement, etc.) determine *how* the parameter changes, allowing arbitrary combinations of timing and magnitude. Three notification levels control what the agent observes: a Boolean change flag, the flag plus the magnitude of change, or a full up-to-date stationary model snapshot for model-based planners. The framework is fully compatible with Gymnasium's vectorization and parallelization APIs, and the paper benchmarks six algorithms (MCTS, AlphaZero, DDQN, PAMCTS at three α values, ADA-MCTS, RATS) across four environments under single-change and continuous-change settings with and without notification, providing the first standardized comparison of NS-MDP solvers.

## Results

NS-Gym benchmarks six decision-making algorithms (MCTS, AlphaZero, DDQN, PAMCTS, ADA-MCTS, RATS) across four environments (CartPole, FrozenLake, CliffWalking, Bridge) under single and continuous non-stationary parameter changes. No single algorithm dominates: MCTS leads on CartPole (600.90±47.68 at mass=1, 641.28±50.47 at mass=1.5), AlphaZero leads on CliffWalking (64.41±3.44 at p=0.8) and Bridge (0.92±0.23 at p=0.8), while risk-averse methods RATS (0.97±0.02) and ADA-MCTS (0.67±0.05) dominate stochastic grid-worlds like FrozenLake. In the continuous-change setting with notification, AlphaZero achieves the highest Bridge reward (1.00±0.00) and PAMCTS-0.25 leads on CartPole (1392.23±65.57).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks six prior-work algorithms on standardized NS-MDP settings for the first time. MCTS (Kocsis & Szepesvári, 2006) achieves the highest CartPole rewards (600.90 and 641.28) but underperforms in stochastic grid-worlds. AlphaZero (Silver et al., 2017) dominates CliffWalking across all change magnitudes (e.g., 64.41 vs. MCTS's -773.62 at p=0.8) and Bridge at p=0.8 (0.92 vs. MCTS's 0.64), benefiting from its learned value network. DDQN (van Hasselt et al., 2015) is the weakest model-free baseline in grid-worlds (e.g., -1742.54 on CliffWalking at p=0.4) but surprisingly competitive on FrozenLake (0.91 at p=0.8). PAMCTS (Pettet et al., 2024) with α=0.75 is best on Bridge at p=0.4 (-0.16) and shows the largest gains from notification in the continuous setting (CartPole: 1392.23 with vs. 149.0 without). Risk-averse methods ADA-MCTS (Luo et al., 2024) and RATS (Lecarpentier & Rachelson, 2019) consistently outperform non-risk-averse methods in stochastic environments—RATS achieves 0.97 on FrozenLake (p=0.8) versus 0.53 for MCTS—confirming that worst-case sampling is critical when transition probabilities shift. Overall, no single algorithm wins across all environments, highlighting the need for the standardized benchmark NS-Gym provides.

## Relation to Prior Work

NS-Gym builds directly on the Gymnasium framework (Towers et al., 2023) and the semi-Markov chain formalism for parameter evolution from Campo et al. (1991), but its key departure is providing the first standardized simulation toolkit for non-stationary MDPs, where prior work relied on ad-hoc custom environments (Lecarpentier and Rachelson's bridge, Chandak et al.'s recommendation/diabetes tasks, Pettet et al.'s manually modified CartPole). The paper unifies multiple conceptual threads—single-change detection (Luo et al. 2024, Pettet et al. 2024), continuous parameter drift (Lecarpentier and Rachelson 2019), inter-episodic variation (Chandak et al. 2020a,b), and non-stationary bandits (Garivier and Moulines 2011, Besbes et al. 2014)—under a common modular interface with schedulers and update functions. It also extends the framework to contextual MDPs (Hallak et al. 2015) as a special case of inter-episodic non-stationarity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By providing standardized interfaces, benchmark problems, and an evaluation module (ensemble regret, PAMCTS-Bound), NS-Gym enables the first consistent and reproducible comparison of NS-MDP algorithms, addressing a clear gap that has persisted since the field's inception in the 1970s. The modular scheduler/update-function design allows researchers to rapidly configure new non-stationary scenarios without reimplementing environment logic. Limitations include the reliance on relatively simple toy environments (CartPole, FrozenLake, grid worlds) and MuJoCo control tasks, with no complex real-world or multi-agent settings; the authors acknowledge plans to broaden scope. Open questions include how findings transfer to high-dimensional continuous environments, whether the semi-Markov parameter evolution assumption holds in practice, and how to handle partially observable non-stationarity.
