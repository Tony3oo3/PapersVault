## Problem

Seasonal-scale climate prediction (1–6 month lead times) lacks standardized benchmark datasets and evaluation protocols, unlike weather and subseasonal-to-seasonal forecasting which have been accelerated by resources like WeatherBench and ChaosBench. This gap is particularly acute for East Asia, where complex monsoon dynamics, strong ocean–atmosphere interactions, and highly uneven precipitation distributions demand region-specific modeling that global-scale AI approaches have not yet addressed.

## Input / Output

The benchmark provides multi-resolution gridded data: 0.25° over East Asia (58–163°E, 8–60°N) and 1° globally, spanning atmospheric variables at five pressure levels (1000–200 hPa) plus boundary-condition fields (SST, sea ice, soil moisture/temperature, solar radiation, snow). For the prediction task, models consume ERA5 reanalysis (1940–2024 monthly; 1991–2024 at 6-hourly/daily) and output monthly-mean climate fields; for post-processing, models take 1° ensemble forecast fields from five operational centers (CMCC, DWD, ECCC, ECMWF, Meteo-France; 1993–2024) and output corrected fields evaluated over the East Asia domain. Evaluation uses deterministic metrics (RMSE, ACC, WI, CSI, energy spectrum), probabilistic metrics (CRPS, rank histogram, SSR), and a hindcast protocol (ACC_hindcast, TCC) aligned with operational model-assessment practices.

## Method

SeasonBench-EA defines two benchmark tasks with a unified data and evaluation framework. Task 1 (prediction) trains models autoregressively on ERA5 reanalysis within the East Asia domain using a nested-grid-inspired multi-resolution design (0.25° regional + 1° global boundary), while Task 2 (post-processing) trains correction models on global-scale ensemble forecasts and evaluates them over East Asia. Baselines span U-Net, ViT, FNO, and VAE for prediction, plus SFNO and GraphCast for post-processing, alongside climatology and persistence references. A key design choice is the inclusion of boundary-condition variables (ocean state, soil, solar radiation) that are physically essential at seasonal timescales but absent from most existing benchmarks. The hindcast evaluation removes source-specific climatological means before computing anomaly correlations, enabling fair cross-model comparison of interseasonal and interannual skill. Empirically, the benchmark reveals that all data-driven models lose skill rapidly with lead time and fail to reproduce small-scale precipitation variability, while physically informed post-processing outperforms direct prediction but still falls below skillful thresholds.

## Results

On the seasonal prediction task, all data-driven models (U-Net, ViT, FNO, VAE) exhibit higher RMSE than the monthly climatology baseline, with ACC values dropping below zero at several lead months, indicating a fundamental loss of predictive skill relative to climatology. On the post-processing task, correcting 10 ensemble members outperforms the original 40-member CMCC ensemble in both RMSE and ACC, and post-processed forecasts surpass direct prediction models. In the hindcast evaluation for summer precipitation (June–August, March initialization), ACC_hindcast values are 0.032 ± 0.131 (ViT), 0.129 ± 0.122 (GraphCast post-processing), and 0.119 (raw numerical ensemble), with physically informed methods consistently outperforming purely data-driven ones.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The benchmark evaluates six model architectures (U-Net, ViT, FNO, VAE for prediction; SFNO and GraphCast for post-processing) against two physics baselines (monthly climatology and persistence) and the raw 40-member ensemble. On the prediction task, all ML models lose to the climatology baseline on RMSE and ACC, with no architecture showing a clear advantage over the others. On the post-processing task, GraphCast-based correction of 10 ensemble members outperforms both the raw 40-member ensemble and all direct prediction models in RMSE and ACC, while SFNO shows comparable but slightly weaker gains. In the hindcast evaluation, GraphCast post-processing (ACC_hindcast = 0.129) and the raw numerical ensemble (0.119) both outperform the ViT direct prediction (0.032), confirming that physically informed methods retain an edge at seasonal lead times. However, post-processed forecasts show a decline in TCC (temporal consistency) relative to raw ensembles, and the climatology baseline still outperforms corrected precipitation forecasts, highlighting that no current method achieves skillful seasonal precipitation prediction over East Asia.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the seasonal timescale (1–6 months) by constructing the first multi-source, multi-resolution benchmark specifically for East Asian seasonal prediction, filling a gap between existing weather/S2S benchmarks (WeatherBench2, ChaosBench, ENS-10) and long-term climate projection benchmarks (ClimateSet, ClimateBench). Rather than introducing a new architecture, it repurposes established models (U-Net, ViT, FNO, VAE, SFNO, GraphCast) as baselines under a unified evaluation protocol, and draws conceptual inspiration from nested-grid strategies in regional numerical weather prediction to motivate its dual-resolution (0.25° regional + 1° global) data design. The benchmark also extends the post-processing paradigm from ENS-10 (48-hour ensemble correction) to the much longer seasonal lead times, where boundary conditions rather than initial conditions dominate predictability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark provides the first standardized, publicly available framework for systematically comparing data-driven seasonal prediction and ensemble post-processing methods over East Asia, a region with critical monsoon-related societal impacts. A key open question it surfaces is how to incorporate physical constraints and boundary-driven signals (SST, soil, solar radiation) into ML architectures so they can capture interannual variability rather than regressing to climatology. Limitations include geographic restriction to East Asia, a limited variable set (no subsurface ocean or salinity fields), and computational constraints that prevented evaluation of all available numerical model systems. The multi-resolution design also opens a path toward nested AI architectures that have not yet been benchmarked.
