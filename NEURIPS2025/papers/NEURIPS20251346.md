## Problem

Existing RL methods for survival analysis target time-to-event data with a single terminal outcome and maximize expected survival time. For chronic diseases with alternating recurrent events (e.g., repeated mood episodes), the clinically relevant goal is instead to maximize the probability that the duration between consecutive events exceeds a meaningful threshold — a fundamentally different objective on a more complex data structure that prior work has not addressed.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251346/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes stage-wise patient covariates (state vectors), binary or continuous treatment actions, censored recurrent-event durations, and a per-stage threshold α_k·G_k defining the clinically meaningful duration. Because the reward (duration) is censored and not directly observed, it must be estimated via a survival model (Cox or Aalen) before policy optimization; censoring is treated as a terminal 'Done' state in the RL loop. The output is an optimal treatment policy (discrete or continuous) together with per-stage survival probability estimates and Q-function values that quantify the probability of exceeding the threshold at each stage.

## Method

The key insight is to replace the intractable product of stage-specific survival probabilities with its Jensen-inequality lower bound — a cumulative sum of log survival probabilities (equivalently, negative cumulative hazards) — which is a standard discounted-sum-of-rewards objective amenable to off-policy RL. Theorem 2.4 proves that under a deterministic transition kernel, this lower-bound objective induces the same optimal policy as the original probability objective, for both discrete and continuous action spaces. The Q-function is built from estimated cumulative hazards via stage-specific Cox or Aalen models, and the Bellman optimality operator is shown to be γ-contractive, guaranteeing convergence of value iteration. Policy optimization is then performed with soft-update DQN (discrete actions) or DDPG (continuous actions), using the estimated negative cumulative hazard as the per-stage reward.

## Results

In simulation Setting 1 (linear transition, discrete actions), the proposed Aah method achieves AARED of 19.96 (N=100) to 20.00 (N=10000), outperforming the Baseline (Liu et al.) by up to 0.8%. In Setting 2 (nonlinear transition, continuous actions), the improvement is substantially larger: Cox achieves AARED of 19.19 (N=100) vs. Baseline's 17.17, a 14.4% gain. On the Intern Health Study real data, the proposed method improves the probability of exceeding the mood threshold by up to 35% at α_k=1 compared to the baseline. On the Epicare benchmark, the Aah variant achieves a cumulative reward of ~11.03 with a censoring rate of ~0.505, versus −15.88 and 0.961 for the Km baseline and 6.25 and 0.641 for classical RL.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines. First, Liu et al. (23) ('Baseline'/'Km') maximizes total recurrent-event duration via finite-stage dynamic treatment regimes; the proposed method consistently outperforms it on AARED (up to 14.4% improvement in Setting 2) and reduces AAEV by up to 49% (Setting 1, N=100) and 99.89% (Setting 2, N=10000), indicating both higher duration and greater stability. Second, on the Epicare benchmark, the proposed Aah variant achieves a cumulative reward of ~11.03 versus 6.25 for classical RL (Hargrave et al., 15) and −15.88 for the Km baseline, while also reducing the censoring rate from 0.961 (Km) and 0.641 (RL) to ~0.505. The Cox variant is slightly less competitive than Aah in Setting 2 and on Epicare but still dominates both baselines across all α_k values tested. The advantage is most pronounced in the nonlinear/continuous-action setting (Setting 2) and at larger sample sizes, where the probability-driven objective provides a clearer optimization signal than expectation maximization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the RL-for-survival-analysis line initiated by Goldberg & Kosorok (2012) and Liu et al. (2023), which handle time-to-event censored data with expectation-maximization objectives, to the structurally richer setting of alternating recurrent events with a probability-based objective. The survival function estimation component draws directly on the Cox proportional hazards model (Cox, 1972) and Aalen's additive hazards model (Aalen, 1989), while the policy optimization leverages standard DQN/DDPG architectures (Lillicrap et al., 2015). The key conceptual departure is the log-probability lower bound (via Jensen's inequality) that converts a joint-probability maximization into a standard discounted cumulative-reward RL problem, and the theoretical result (Theorem 2.4) establishing that this reformulation preserves the optimal policy under deterministic transitions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a clinically relevant gap: chronic disease management where the goal is not maximizing expected survival time but ensuring each inter-episode duration exceeds a meaningful threshold. The equivalence theorem (Theorem 2.4) provides a clean justification for the lower-bound approach, but it requires a deterministic transition kernel, a restrictive assumption that limits applicability to stochastic state dynamics common in real clinical settings. Open questions include how tightly the Jensen lower bound approximates the true probability objective in long-horizon settings, whether the Markov and conditional-independence assumptions (2.1, 2.2) hold for realistic recurrent-event processes, and how the method performs when the censoring mechanism is informative rather than independent. The 87.8% censoring rate in the real-data analysis also raises practical concerns about estimation reliability.
