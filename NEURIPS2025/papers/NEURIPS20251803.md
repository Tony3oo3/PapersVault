## Problem

Monte Carlo (MC) estimation is the standard approach for synthesizing step-level process annotations to train process reward models (PRMs), but the resulting data carries a high noise ratio that causes rapid overfitting and limits scalability. Prior work mitigates this noise by filtering with external large critic models (e.g., 72B-parameter LLMs), yet the underlying noise distribution in MC estimation itself and how to learn robustly from it without such external supervision remain unexplored.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251803/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a math question and a multi-step reasoning response (n steps) as input. During data synthesis, a lightweight completer model (e.g., Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B) performs k rollouts per step to estimate step-level correctness scores, and a per-question self-confidence score is computed from N full-response samples. At inference, the trained PRM assigns a probability to each step's correctness; the overall response score is the minimum step score (Min-Max aggregation), and the highest-scoring response among N candidates is selected as the final answer.

## Method

SCAN comprises two modules. First, an efficient data synthesis framework computes a self-confidence metric per question and applies MC step-wise annotation only to negative samples (those with at least one detected error), while directly using high-confidence positive samples without further annotation—reducing inference cost to ~6% of vanilla MC. Second, a robust learning loss modifies the standard BCE objective in two ways: (1) noise-tolerant labeling replaces hard binary labels with soft labels c_t / SC_π(q) for steps within a tolerance distance d of the first predicted error, absorbing the over-estimation noise where the completer corrects an error before eventually failing; and (2) confidence-wise reweighting divides each step's MC correctness score by the question-level self-confidence to normalize out the annotator model's capability bias, enabling consistent integration of annotations from multiple models of different strengths. Together these allow a 1.5B model to produce training data that yields a PRM outperforming baselines trained on 264K human-annotated samples (PRM800K).

## Results

On ProcessBench (step-wise error detection), SCAN-Pro (197K MC-only samples) achieves an average F1 of 59.1, surpassing the human-annotated PRM800K (56.5) and the 70B critic model Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct (58.0), while improving the base Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct from 19.9 to 59.1 F1. On Best-of-8 evaluation (test-time scaling), SCAN-Pro achieves 70.1 average accuracy across GSM8K, MATH, College Math, and Olympiad Bench, matching the strongly supervised Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-7B (70.1) and outperforming PRM800K (69.3) using only 197K synthetic samples versus 264K human-annotated ones. SCAN-Base (101K samples from a 1.5B model) already reaches 56.8 F1 on ProcessBench and 69.1 average BoN accuracy, approaching PRM800K-level performance at roughly 6% of the inference cost of vanilla MC estimation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SCAN-Pro (197K, MC-only) outperforms all MC-only and implicit-reward baselines by large margins on ProcessBench: Math-Shepherd (28.9), RLHFlow-PRM (26.6–28.4), EurusPRM (31.2–31.3), and Skywork-PRM (42.1) all trail by 27–33 F1 points. Against the human-annotated PRM800K (56.5 F1), SCAN-Pro wins by 2.6 F1, and SCAN-Base (56.8) already edges it out by 0.3 F1 despite using only 101K synthetic samples versus 264K human annotations. On Best-of-8, SCAN-Pro (70.1 avg) matches the strongly supervised Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-7B (70.1) and UniversalPRM-7B (69.8) while using 7.6× fewer training samples than Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-7B (1500K) and no external critic model. The main gap remains against KD-based models on ProcessBench (59.1 vs. 73.5–74.3), which the authors attribute to the coarser-grained nature of MC supervision versus fine-grained critic distillation; however, integrating SCAN's denoising with KD (Table 8) closes much of this gap (60.8 vs. 52.5 F1 at 100K samples). Notably, SCAN-Pro's error detection (59.1 F1) surpasses the 70B Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct critic model (58.0 F1) using a 7B PRM, demonstrating that the denoising strategy enables small models to rival much larger critics on step-level error localization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Monte Carlo estimation framework from Math-Shepherd [35] for step-level annotation and the PRM training paradigm established by Lightman et al. [17] (binary classification on first-error-step, PRM800K). Its key departure is rejecting the prevailing strategy of mitigating MC noise via external stronger critic models (e.g., Qwen-72B in UniversalPRM [30] or Qwen2.5-Math-PRM [42]); instead, SCAN characterizes the noise distribution inherent to MC estimation itself and proposes self-contained denoising (noise-tolerant soft labeling + confidence-wise reweighting) that requires no external supervision. The self-confidence metric and the observation that under-estimation concentrates in low-confidence regions while over-estimation concentrates in high-confidence regions (for stronger models) are new empirical contributions that reframe how MC annotation noise should be handled.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: a 1.5B annotator with SCAN denoising produces a PRM that matches or exceeds human-annotated PRM800K on ProcessBench at roughly 6% of vanilla MC inference cost, making scalable PRM training far more accessible. However, the paper itself identifies important open questions: MC estimation fundamentally cannot detect false positives (correct final answer masking intermediate errors), PRMs remain vulnerable to reward hacking in out-of-distribution settings, and the authors point toward generative PRMs (GenPRMs) as a more robust and interpretable direction. The modest margin over PRM800K on ProcessBench (59.1 vs. 56.5 F1) and the reliance on a single policy model family for most evaluations suggest the approach's ceiling may be constrained by the MC annotation paradigm itself.
