## Problem

Locate-then-edit model editing methods (e.g., MEMIT, AlphaEdit) rely on residual distribution—spreading the residual computed at the last critical layer evenly across all critical layers—as a core mechanism for multi-layer weight updates. This paper identifies a counterintuitive failure mode: residual distribution introduces weight shift errors that grow with distribution distance, batch size, and the number of sequential edits, ultimately degrading editing precision rather than improving it.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252624/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a batch of knowledge triplets (s, r, o) → o* to be edited, the LLM's FFN key-value memories at causally identified critical layers, and (for sequential editing) cached keys from previously edited knowledge. It produces updated FFN output weights (W_out) at only two boundary layers—the first and last critical layers—rather than all n critical layers. Unlike the original methods that compute a single residual at the last layer and divide it across layers, BLUE independently optimizes a residual vector δ for each boundary layer via gradient descent on the target prediction probability, then applies a closed-form least-squares weight update per layer.

## Method

BLUE (Boundary Layer UpdatE) is a plug-in strategy that replaces even residual distribution with direct residual computation at only the first and last critical layers. The theoretical foundation (Theorem 4.1 and Lemma 4.3) proves that the upper bound of the weight shift error between the exact and actual weight updates scales with the distribution distance (L−l), the batch size (through ‖R‖₂ and ‖Q‖₂), and the number of sequential edits (through the cached-key term K_p K_p^T). Empirically, the paper shows that distributed residuals achieve contribution scores below 0.1 at the first critical layer while directly computed residuals maintain scores near 1.0 at every layer, and that cosine similarity between distributed and optimal memories decreases monotonically with distance. BLUE selects the first critical layer because it suffers the largest distribution-induced error, and the last critical layer because it is the natural site of residual computation in existing methods, preserving broader applicability. The strategy is model-agnostic and applies directly to MEMIT, RECT, PRUNE, and AlphaEdit without modifying their underlying least-squares formulation, yielding an average 35.59% performance improvement in sequential batch editing while also reducing hidden-state shifts and preserving general capabilities.

## Results

On sequential batch editing (batch size 100, 2,000 samples) across three LLMs and two datasets, BLUE enhances 89.58% of results (86/96) with an average performance improvement of 35.59%. The largest gains appear on Llama3-8B, where MEMIT's efficacy improves by 129.61% and PRUNE's by 144.52%. BLUE also preserves general capabilities (GLUE F1 scores remain stable after 3,000 edits where baselines degrade) and reduces hidden-state shifts, while improving editing efficiency (lower peak memory and faster per-edit time).

## Baselines & Comparisons

BLUE consistently outperforms all four locate-then-edit baselines on Efficacy and Generalization across every model–dataset combination. The most dramatic gains are over MEMIT and PRUNE on Llama3-8B, where efficacy jumps from ~24% to ~91% (MEMIT) and from ~52% to ~99% (PRUNE) in sequential batch editing. RECT benefits substantially on Llama3 (+27%) but shows minimal gains on GPT-J (+0.58%), suggesting RECT's regularization already partially compensates for residual-distribution error on smaller models. AlphaEdit, which already achieves strong sequential editing performance via null-space constraints, sees only marginal BLUE improvements (+2.50% on Llama3, +0.03% on GPT-J), indicating diminishing returns when the baseline is already near-optimal. In the batch editing setting (10,000 edits), BLUE-enhanced methods improve 70.83% of metrics (68/96), with the caveat that Specificity sometimes decreases slightly, reflecting a trade-off between editing strength and knowledge preservation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper operates within the locate-then-edit paradigm for model editing, which traces its lineage from ROME (Meng et al., 2022) through MEMIT (Meng et al., 2023) to AlphaEdit (Fang et al., 2025). The key conceptual departure is identifying that residual distribution—the mechanism by which MEMIT and its successors spread the computed residual evenly across all critical layers—is not a neutral or beneficial step but actively introduces weight shift errors. BLUE discards this mechanism entirely, updating only the first and last critical layers with independently computed residuals, while preserving the least-squares key-value memory formulation and the sequential batch editing framework from AlphaEdit.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a core mechanism in the most popular model editing paradigm is counterproductive challenges a foundational assumption and provides a simple, plug-and-play fix (update two boundary layers instead of all layers) that yields a 35.59% average improvement across four methods, three LLMs, and two datasets. BLUE also improves general capability retention and reduces hidden-state drift, addressing a key practical concern for deploying edited models. Open questions include whether the strategy extends to reasoning-knowledge editing (e.g., MQuAKE multi-hop questions), whether the specificity trade-off observed in large-batch editing can be resolved, and whether the two-layer boundary choice generalizes to architectures with very different critical layer structures.
