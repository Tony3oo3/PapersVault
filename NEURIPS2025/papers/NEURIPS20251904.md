## Problem

Existing frontier AI safety regulations have been repeatedly shelved or watered down due to the perceived safety-innovation tradeoff, leaving a gap in enforceable public oversight of increasingly powerful models. This position paper argues the tradeoff is a false dilemma: mandating that labs release small, openly accessible 'analog' models distilled from their frontier systems can simultaneously enable broad safety verification and accelerate innovation, because safety interventions discovered on small models reliably transfer to their much larger counterparts.

## Input / Output

The policy takes a frontier model's full training run—its weights, training data, and optimization objectives—and produces a distilled analog model capped at 0.5–5% of the frontier's parameter count, released under a permissive open-source license (e.g., Apache 2.0) within 1–3 months of the frontier model's deployment. Each release must include model weights on an accessible platform, a model card documenting architecture and training setup, a general description of training data sources and modalities, and distillation or training scripts for reproducibility. The size cap and mandatory release lag are the key structural constraints that balance public access against dual-use risk and IP protection.

## Method

The core mechanism is a regulatory mandate requiring any lab releasing a frontier AI model to also publicly release a small analog—a distillation trained on identical data and objectives but constrained to a fraction of the original scale. The technical justification rests on three converging lines of evidence: empirical demonstrations that steering vectors and safety interventions learned on 0.5–1B models transfer to 3–70B models with minimal degradation (e.g., backdoor trigger rates dropping from ~100% to <5%); theoretical grounding in the Platonic Representation Hypothesis and smooth scaling laws, which explain why internal representations converge across scales; and weak-to-strong generalization results showing small-model supervision recovers over 90% of large-model alignment performance. Enforcement is tied to existing regulatory channels (U.S. Export Control Reform Act, EU AI Act transparency provisions) with compliance verified through audits by national AI safety institutes, and a phased implementation starting with voluntary pilot programs before mandated compliance for all models crossing established frontier thresholds.

## Results

The paper's central quantitative claim is that producing an 8B-parameter analog model costs approximately $92,759 in compute (Table 1), roughly 0.1% of a frontier model's training budget. Empirical demonstrations show steering vectors learned on 0.5B–1B models transfer to 1.5B–3B models, collapsing backdoor trigger rates from ~100% to <5% and tracking native vectors within 5–10% on success metrics across seven safety-critical behaviours. Token embedding spaces remain nearly isometric across 1B–70B checkpoints (Pearson r > 0.9), and a closed-form least-squares map fitted on 10⁵ tokens reproduces full dose-response curves when porting steering directions between sizes. The LLaMA case study documents ~15.8k citations, 58k GitHub stars, 7.7M Hugging Face downloads, and 60+ peer-reviewed safety studies, with no measurable cannibalization of Meta's commercial API revenue.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not propose a new method to benchmark against competing baselines; instead it synthesizes existing empirical evidence to justify a policy. The two key empirical anchors are Oozeer et al. (2025), who show a steering vector learned on QWEN-0.5B transfers to QWEN-1.5B and LLAMA3-3B with trigger rates dropping from ~100% to <5% (matching native vectors within 2 percentage points), and Lee et al. (2025), who demonstrate that a least-squares map fitted on 10⁵ tokens reproduces full dose-response curves when porting seven Llama-3 steering directions from 1B/3B into 8B, and that an anti-toxicity vector from GPT-2 LARGE cuts Perspective-API toxicity in GPT-2 XL by the same 20–25% margin. Both experiments required under $80 of GPU time. The LLaMA release (2023–2025) serves as a natural experiment: despite full weight openness, Meta reports no measurable revenue cannibalization, while the ecosystem generated 60+ safety studies and 7.7M downloads—supporting the claim that capped analogs do not erode primary business lines. The paper positions its cost estimate (~$93K) as negligible relative to existing compliance burdens such as compute audits under EO 14110, arguing the mandate adds <0.2% overhead even after doubling for personnel costs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes three technical threads—weak-to-strong generalization (Burns et al., 2023), the Platonic Representation Hypothesis (Huh et al., 2024), and knowledge distillation (Hinton et al., 2015)—into a novel regulatory mechanism: mandating the release of small 'analog' models as public safety proxies. It departs from prior frontier-AI safety regulations (SB-1047, EO 14110, the proposed 6-month pause) that were shelved due to the safety-innovation tradeoff, instead drawing structural analogies from pharmaceutical generic-drug disclosure (Hatch-Waxman) and telecom open-protocol standards. The empirical backbone relies on recent steering-vector transfer results (Oozeer et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2025) and sparse autoencoder/crosscoder work (Bricken et al., 2023; Lindsey et al., 2024) to argue that small-model findings reliably generalize to frontier scale.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a concrete, low-cost (~$93K, ~0.1% of frontier training budget) regulatory mechanism that could plausibly pass where heavier proposals have failed, making it practically important for AI policy. The central open question is whether transferability holds for genuinely emergent frontier behaviors (e.g., compositional reasoning, novel jailbreaks) that may not manifest in 0.5–5% scale analogs; the authors themselves note that sparse crosscoders reveal only a subset of latent features transfer, and vanilla output-level distillation need not preserve internal geometric structure. The LLaMA case study is encouraging but constitutes a single data point, and the policy's long-term effectiveness depends on ongoing empirical validation as model architectures and capabilities evolve.
