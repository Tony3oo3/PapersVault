## Problem

Publicly available CRC histopathology datasets for multi-class tissue classification suffer from insufficient morphologic diversity, severe class imbalance, and inclusion of artifact-laden or mislabeled tiles, limiting downstream model generalizability. Additionally, no standardized semi-automated framework exists for curating large-scale, balanced tile datasets that capture the full morphologic spectrum within whole-slide images.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255679/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes H&E-stained whole-slide images at 40× magnification (0.25 μm/pixel) from ~200 CRC surgical resection patients, extracting non-overlapping 256×256 pixel tiles after background and artifact removal. The output is the STARC-9 dataset of 630,000 tiles uniformly distributed across nine tissue classes (ADI, LYM, MUS, FCT, MUC, NCS, BLD, TUM, NOR; 70,000 tiles each), along with three independent validation sets (STANFORD-CRC-HE-VAL-SMALL: 18,000 tiles; STANFORD-CRC-HE-VAL-LARGE: 54,000 tiles; CURATED-TCGA-CRC-HE-VAL-20K: 20,000 tiles) and pathologist-annotated tumor segmentation masks on 2048×2048 regions.

## Method

DeepCluster++ is a three-phase semi-automated framework. Phase 1 trains a custom autoencoder (AE_CRC) on 100,000 CRC tiles using SSIM loss, producing 32,768-dimensional latent vectors that capture fine-grained morphologic structure. Phase 2 applies the frozen encoder to all candidate tiles, compresses features via global average pooling (512-d) and PCA (256-d), then clusters with K-means (400 tiles/cluster); within each cluster, tiles are sorted by normalized Euclidean distance to the centroid and partitioned into five equal-frequency bins, from which 20% of tiles are sampled per bin to ensure balanced coverage from prototypical to edge-case morphologies. Phase 3 maps sampled tiles back onto their source WSI via QuPath for board-certified GI pathologist verification of tissue-type labels. The equal-frequency binning step is the key differentiator from standard centroid-neighborhood sampling, preventing over-representation of dense cluster cores and explicitly capturing intra-class heterogeneity.

## Results

On the primary STANFORD-CRC-HE-VAL-LARGE validation set (54,000 tiles, 7 tissue classes), CTransPath trained on STARC-9 achieves 99.00% accuracy and 99.16% F1-macro, outperforming the best NCT-trained model (ViT-base, 84.25% accuracy) by 14.75 points and the best HMU-trained model (HiPT, 91.99% accuracy) by 7.01 points. On the external CURATED-TCGA-CRC-HE-VAL-20K set, the STARC-9-trained CTransPath maintains 98.85% accuracy versus 72.42% (NCT) and 90.9% (HMU). For tumor segmentation, STARC-9-trained models achieve Dice scores of 90.47±8.14% (Stanford) and 89.38±9.14% (TCGA-CRC), approximately 14–35% higher than NCT- and HMU-trained counterparts, with substantially tighter variance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is dataset-driven: the same 17 model architectures are fine-tuned on STARC-9 (630K tiles), NCT-CRC-HE-100K (100K tiles), and HMU-GC-HE-30K (30K tiles), then evaluated on identical validation sets. STARC-9-trained models dominate across every architecture category. Among pathology foundation models, CTransPath on STARC-9 (99.00% acc.) beats UNI on NCT (80.43%) by 18.57 points and HiPT on HMU (91.99%) by 7.01 points on STANFORD-CRC-HE-VAL-LARGE; on the external TCGA-CRC set the gap widens to 26.43 and 7.95 points respectively. Even the largest foundation models (ProvGigPath, Virchow at 305M params) trained on STARC-9 (98.37%, 98.28%) trail CTransPath (87M params) on the same dataset, indicating that dataset quality matters more than model scale. Notably, a 3.9M-parameter CNN trained from scratch on STARC-9 reaches 97.81% accuracy, surpassing ViT-base (86M params) trained on NCT (84.25%) by 13.56 points, underscoring that high-quality, diverse training data can compensate for architectural simplicity. For tumor segmentation, STARC-9-trained models achieve IoU of 89.33% (Stanford) and 88.81% (TCGA) versus 67.19%/51.94% (NCT) and 64.68%/58.89% (HMU), with roughly 2–3× lower standard deviation, indicating more consistent tumor delineation. On the challenging IMP-CRS10K biopsy set, the STARC-9 model retains 96.61% accuracy while NCT and HMU models drop to 69.62% and 76.40%, respectively.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a direct response to the limitations of the two dominant public CRC tile-level datasets: NCT-CRC-HE-100K (Kather et al., 2019) and HMU-GC-HE-30K (Lou et al., 2025), which suffer from JPEG artifacts, mislabeled tiles, class imbalance, and limited morphologic diversity. The DeepCluster++ framework repurposes standard unsupervised tools (autoencoder, K-means, PCA) in a novel pipeline where a domain-specific autoencoder trained with SSIM loss replaces the more common practice of using pretrained or foundation-model encoders for clustering, and equal-frequency distance binning within clusters replaces naive centroid-proximity sampling to enforce intra-class diversity. The downstream evaluation follows the established benchmarking paradigm of fine-tuning a broad menu of CNNs, transformers, and pathology foundation models, but the core contribution is the dataset construction methodology rather than any new model architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

STARC-9 (630K tiles, 9 balanced classes) is the largest publicly available CRC tissue-classification dataset to date and provides a much-needed high-quality benchmark that isolates dataset quality as the dominant factor in model performance. The DeepCluster++ framework is explicitly designed to be generalizable to other cancer types and WSI repositories, potentially standardizing how pathology datasets are curated. Key limitations include single-institution origin, limited demographic diversity (only 3.5% Black patients), CRC-specific scope, and the absence of multi-institutional validation. Open questions include whether the framework transfers to morphologically dissimilar tissues (e.g., CNS tumors), whether the 70K-per-class cap is optimal for all downstream tasks, and whether multi-modal extensions (image-caption pairs) can further improve generalizability.
