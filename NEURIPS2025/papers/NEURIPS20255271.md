## Problem

Comprehensive benchmarks for evaluating Large Multimodal Models (LMMs) in agricultural remote sensing remain scarce. Existing agricultural RS benchmarks suffer from insufficient scene diversity (limited crop types, geographic coverage, and climatic conditions) and oversimplified task design (basic classification or detection), failing to capture the spatial reasoning, contextual understanding, and decision-making demands of real-world agricultural applications.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255271/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes agricultural remote sensing images from three sensor types (UAV, satellite, ground camera) spanning RGB, multispectral, and near-infrared bands, with resolutions ranging from 300×300 to 4,500×4,500 pixels, paired with a text question. Multi-image questions are flattened into single concatenated images to ensure fair zero-shot evaluation. Outputs are structured across five answer formats—judgment, counting, text choice, image choice, and open-ended—plus bounding box coordinates for spatial localization tasks, with strict string matching for closed-form answers and semantic similarity scoring for open-ended responses.

## Method

AgroMind is constructed through a four-stage pipeline: (1) multi-source data pre-processing integrating nine public datasets (OAM-TCD, OilPalmUAV, AVC, PhenoBench, IP102, ACFR, CropHarvest, 2018 AI Challenge, Maize Tassel) and one private global parcel dataset, with format standardization, spectral-band grayscale concatenation, and randomized parcel-boundary cropping; (2) systematic question generation via both rule-based templates and human-authored questions, organized into 13 task types under four hierarchical cognitive dimensions—spatial perception (localization, relationship, boundary detection), object understanding (classification, pest/disease diagnostics, growth status), scene understanding (comparison, counting, area statistics), and scene reasoning (visual prompt reasoning, anomaly reasoning, climate type reasoning, planning); (3) zero-shot LMM inference over 24 models (4 closed-source, 20 open-source); and (4) quality control via expert-annotated ground truth comparison. The benchmark contains 28,482 QA pairs over 20,850 images spanning 106 global geographic regions, with a logic-enhancement strategy that predefines hierarchical reasoning chains rather than using raw annotations directly as answers.

## Results

On AgroMind (28,482 QA pairs, 13 task types across 4 cognitive dimensions), GPT-4o achieves the highest overall accuracy of 43.14%, surpassing human performance (33.15%) by approximately 10 percentage points. Among open-source models, InternVL2-26B leads with 37.25%, followed by LLaVA-NeXT-34B (35.52%) and XComposer2-4KHD (33.02%). Notably, 17 out of 24 evaluated models underperform the human baseline, and domain-specific remote sensing models (GeoChat: 25.93%, GeoLLaVA-8K: 15.73%) perform substantially worse than general-purpose LMMs, revealing that agricultural RS tasks remain a significant challenge even for state-of-the-art models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4o (43.14%) is the strongest model overall, outperforming the next-best closed-source model Gemini-1.5-Pro (41.06%) by ~2 points and the best open-source model InternVL2-26B (37.25%) by ~6 points; it excels on Object Classification (78.55%) and Climate Type Reasoning (69.81%) but remains weak on Counting (23.75%) and Visual Prompt Reasoning (16.10%). Gemini-1.5-Flash (40.92%) and Gemini-1.5-Pro (41.06%) are nearly tied, both strong on Object Understanding (~59–65%) but lagging GPT-4o on Spatial Perception. Claude-3.5-Sonnet (37.11%) trails the top three closed-source models by 4–6 points, though it is competitive on Growth Status Recognition (57.50%) and Planning (36.05%). Among open-source models, InternVL2-26B (37.25%) outperforms the larger LLaVA-NeXT-34B (35.52%) by 1.7 points, demonstrating that architecture matters more than parameter count; LLaVA-NeXT-34B also underperforms its own 8B variant on some tasks. Domain-specific remote sensing models GeoChat (25.93%) and GeoLLaVA-8K (15.73%) are outperformed by all general-purpose LMMs, with GeoLLaVA-8K scoring below the human baseline on every dimension, suggesting that narrow RS pre-training does not transfer well to the diverse agricultural scenarios in AgroMind.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the remote sensing VQA benchmarking lineage (EarthVQA, RSIEval, VRSBench, UrBench, XLRS-Bench) into the agricultural domain, where prior efforts (CDDMBench, AgriBench, AgMMU, AgroInstruct) were limited to narrow crop types, single sensors, or basic classification/detection tasks. Rather than proposing a new model, AgroMind repurposes nine existing public datasets plus one private parcel dataset into a unified multi-sensor, multi-scene evaluation framework with a four-level hierarchical task structure (spatial perception → object understanding → scene understanding → scene reasoning) that no prior agricultural benchmark has attempted. The benchmark design draws on the multidimensional evaluation philosophy of MMMU and UrBench but applies it to the agricultural RS setting with domain-specific task definitions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

AgroMind is the first agricultural RS benchmark to simultaneously cover multi-sensor data (UAV, satellite, camera), 106 geographic regions, and 13 task types spanning basic perception to complex reasoning, filling a clear gap between general RS benchmarks and narrow agricultural datasets. The finding that even GPT-4o (43.14) struggles significantly on spatial localization (41.38) and counting (23.75) while excelling at knowledge-based climate reasoning (69.81) reveals a fundamental disconnect between LMMs' language-knowledge strengths and their visual-spatial reasoning weaknesses in agriculture. Domain-specific RS models (GeoChat: 25.93, GeoLLaVA-8K: 15.73) underperform general-purpose models, suggesting that agricultural domain adaptation requires more than RS-specific pretraining. Open questions include how to improve fine-grained spatial reasoning in LMMs, whether the student-based human baseline (33.15) adequately represents expert performance, and how to incorporate agronomic domain knowledge into model training rather than relying on general pretraining.
