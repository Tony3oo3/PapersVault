## Problem

Crosscoders, a recent model diffing tool, identify chat-only latents by checking whether the base-model decoder direction has zero norm. The paper shows that the L1 sparsity loss in the standard crosscoder creates two artifacts—Complete Shrinkage (forcing base decoder norms to zero despite the latent's presence in base activations) and Latent Decoupling (encoding a shared concept via a chat-only latent while the base model uses a different latent combination)—that cause many supposedly chat-only latents to be spurious, undermining the interpretability of model diffing results.

## Input / Output

The method consumes concatenated residual stream activations from the base and chat-tuned Gemma 2 2B models at layer 13, collected on a mixture of web and chat data, and trains a shared sparse dictionary over this activation stack. Beyond the standard crosscoder outputs (shared latent activations and model-specific decoder vectors), the paper introduces Latent Scaling ratios—an error ratio ν^ε and a reconstruction ratio ν^r—that quantify how much of a supposedly chat-only latent's information is actually present in the base model's reconstruction error or reconstruction, respectively. Causal importance is then measured by patching subsets of chat-specific latents onto base activations and computing KL divergence against the true chat model output distribution.

## Method

The paper first provides a theoretical analysis showing that the L1 penalty in the crosscoder loss directly penalizes decoder norms, creating optimization pressure that drives base decoder vectors to zero (Complete Shrinkage) and that the shared-encoding constraint allows redundant representations across models (Latent Decoupling). To detect these artifacts empirically, the authors develop Latent Scaling: for each chat-only latent, they solve a closed-form least-squares problem to find the optimal scalar β that best reconstructs base activations using the chat decoder direction, then decompose the base activation into reconstruction and error components to compute separate ratios that isolate each failure mode. The key practical contribution is training a BatchTopK crosscoder, which enforces L0 sparsity by selecting only the top-k most active latents per batch rather than applying a continuous L1 norm penalty; this removes the direct pressure to shrink decoder norms and introduces competition among latents that discourages redundant encodings. Causal validation is performed by patching ranked subsets of chat-specific latents onto base model activations, feeding the hybrid through remaining chat-model layers, and measuring KL divergence against the true chat distribution, confirming that BatchTopK's Δ_norm metric reliably identifies causally important latents while L1's does not.

## Results

Both L1 and BatchTopK crosscoders achieve nearly identical full-replacement KL divergence reductions (59% over all tokens, 78% over the first 9 tokens), confirming both capture the base-to-chat behavioral difference equally well. However, the BatchTopK crosscoder's Δ_norm metric successfully ranks causally important latents (top 50% achieves 50% KL reduction vs. only 6% for the bottom 50% on first 9 tokens), while Δ_norm fails entirely for the L1 crosscoder where top and bottom latents perform nearly identically. Latent Scaling rescues the L1 crosscoder, nearly matching BatchTopK's causal ranking performance. Additionally, 40% of BatchTopK chat-only latents predominantly activate on template tokens, and a template-only intervention achieves KL divergences of 0.239 (full response) and 0.507 (first 9 tokens), comparable to the top-50% chat-specific latent intervention.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between the L1 crosscoder (Lindsey et al., 2024) and the proposed BatchTopK crosscoder, both trained on Gemma 2 2B base vs. chat middle-layer activations at matched L0 sparsity of 100. On the full-replacement baseline, both crosscoders perform identically (~59%/78% KL reduction), showing the architectural difference does not affect total information captured. The critical divergence appears in latent ranking: BatchTopK's Δ_norm cleanly separates causally important latents (50% vs. 6% KL reduction for top vs. bottom 50% on first 9 tokens), while L1's Δ_norm is a training artifact with no predictive power for causal importance. An independently trained L1 crosscoder from Kissane et al. (2024) exhibits the same Complete Shrinkage and Latent Decoupling artifacts, confirming these are inherent to the L1 loss rather than a training quirk. Latent Scaling (inspired by Wright & Sharkey's SAE scaling) partially rescues the L1 crosscoder, nearly matching BatchTopK's causal ranking, but the BatchTopK crosscoder remains the cleaner tool as it requires no post-hoc correction. The BatchTopK crosscoder also identifies more genuinely chat-specific latents at every threshold π in the (ν^r, ν^ε) space, with strong Pearson correlations (ν^r: 0.73, ν^ε: 0.87, p<0.01) between Δ_norm and the Latent Scaling ratios.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the crosscoder framework for model diffing introduced by Lindsey et al. [2024], which itself builds on sparse autoencoder dictionary learning. It departs from the standard L1-regularized crosscoder by identifying two theoretical failure modes (Complete Shrinkage and Latent Decoupling) in its loss function, and proposes the BatchTopK sparsity mechanism from Bussmann et al. [2024] as a fix. The Latent Scaling diagnostic is adapted from Wright and Sharkey's [2024] SAE scaling technique, repurposed to distinguish spurious from genuine model-specific latents in the crosscoder setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that the L1 crosscoder's norm-based metric for identifying chat-only latents is largely a training artifact rather than a meaningful distinction undermines confidence in prior model-diffing results that relied on this tool. The practical recommendation to adopt BatchTopK crosscoders is actionable, and the identification of interpretable chat-specific latents (refusal triggers, false-information detection, template-token dependence) provides concrete mechanistic insight into instruction tuning. Key open questions remain: whether findings generalize to larger models and other layers, whether truly novel latents can be distinguished from shifted activation patterns, and whether the substantial behavioral information still captured in reconstruction errors can be further decomposed.
