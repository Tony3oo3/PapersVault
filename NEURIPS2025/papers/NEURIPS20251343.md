## Problem

Deep reinforcement learning and imitation learning have shown promise for autonomous robotic ultrasound navigation, but their application to more complex surgical tasks—such as bone surface reconstruction and ultrasound-guided drilling—remains largely unexplored due to the absence of realistic, high-performance simulation environments tailored to these tasks. Existing surgical simulators focus on laparoscopic or soft-tissue manipulation and lack intraoperative ultrasound imaging with patient-specific anatomy.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251343/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The simulator consumes 3D CT volumes and TotalSegmentator label maps (10 patients) along with real-time robot end-effector poses to extract 2D CT and label slices at the probe's image plane, which are then rendered into 2D ultrasound images (200×150 px, 0.5 mm/px) or 3D volumes (50×37×5, 2×2×10 mm³) via either a physics-based ray-tracing model or a pix2pix GAN. The trained agents output robot action commands: 3D tangential motion for navigation, 4D (translation + pitch/yaw) for reconstruction, and 6D (position + orientation) for drilling, while the reconstruction task additionally produces a voxelized 3D surface map (40×40×40, 3 mm/voxel) as the observation.

## Method

SonoGym provides two complementary ultrasound simulation pipelines: a model-based approach using convolutional ray-tracing (Salehi et al.) with CT-intensity-based acoustic impedance refinement, and a learning-based pix2pix GAN trained on paired CT-ultrasound data from 7 ex-vivo spine specimens with intensity histogram matching to bridge the domain gap. The three surgical tasks are formulated as specialized MDP variants—POMDP for navigation, submodular MDP with non-additive trajectory rewards (marginal gain as per-step reward) for reconstruction, and state-wise constrained MDP with a safety filter (modified SafeRPlan) for surgery—enabling training of PPO, A2C, submodular PPO, and IL agents (ACT, Diffusion Policy). All simulations run in batch mode across 128 parallel GPU environments via IsaacLab, with robot arms (KUKA Med14, Franka Emika Panda) and differential IK controllers, achieving ~2-hour PPO training for model-based and ~10-hour for learning-based simulations on an RTX 3090 Ti.

## Results

On the surgery task (Table 1), PPO+SF with learning-based (LB) simulation achieves the best safety (95.3% safe ratio IDT, 96.0% ODT) with moderate side error (5.07 mm IDT) and insertion error (11.9 mm IDT). ACT achieves the lowest insertion error (1.62 mm LB IDT) and rotation error (1.01°) but at the cost of a substantially lower safe ratio (86.0%). For ultrasound simulation quality, the learning-based approach achieves LPIPS 0.2415, SSIM 0.3940, and PSNR 15.96, with parallel rendering of 100 environments in 0.11 s (LB) or 0.009 s (MB) on an RTX 3090 Ti. For reconstruction, submodular PPO surpasses the heuristic baseline with higher surface coverage and shorter trajectory length.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against ACT [60], PPO+SF (LB) achieves a 9.3-point higher safe ratio (95.3% vs 86.0%) and comparable side error (5.07 vs 5.34 mm), while ACT achieves substantially lower insertion error (1.62 vs 11.9 mm) and rotation error (1.01° vs 3.93°) in the IDT setting; however, ACT's safe ratio drops to 80.3% under ODT, whereas PPO+SF improves to 96.0%. Against Diffusion Policy [7], PPO shows lower position tracking variance and better rotation accuracy in the navigation task, and DP failed to perform well on the surgery task despite hyperparameter tuning. Against A2C [35], PPO achieves consistently better performance on both navigation and surgery tasks (Fig. 5 learning curves), while for reconstruction both PPO and A2C surpass the heuristic baseline. Against the heuristic open-loop path planning from Li et al. [27], the learned submodular PPO policy achieves higher surface coverage, lower total rotation angles, and shorter trajectory length, with a circular scanning pattern that is intuitively more efficient than the fixed vertical/horizontal segments of the heuristic.

## Relation to Prior Work

SonoGym unifies and extends several distinct research threads: physics-based ultrasound simulation from CT (Salehi et al. [41], Kutter et al. [24]), generative CT-to-US translation via pix2pix, submodular RL for trajectory-level objectives (Prajapat et al. [40]), and safe RL for surgical planning (Ao et al. [3]). Unlike prior surgical simulation platforms (LapGym, SurRol, Surgical Gym, Orbit-surgical) that focus on laparoscopic or soft-tissue manipulation, SonoGym is the first to provide a unified, parallelizable simulation environment specifically for ultrasound-guided orthopedic surgery tasks. It also departs from prior robotic ultrasound navigation works (Hase et al., Li et al., Ning et al.) by extending the learning-based formulation beyond navigation to include bone surface reconstruction and ultrasound-guided drilling, tasks that previously relied on heuristic path planning or registration-based pipelines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SonoGym provides the missing simulation infrastructure that has limited the application of DRL and IL to complex robotic ultrasound tasks, enabling parallel training of PPO, submodular PPO, SafeRPlan, ACT, and Diffusion Policy agents across navigation, reconstruction, and surgery. The finding that learning-based ultrasound simulation generalizes well across different generative networks (small ODT-IDT gap) is encouraging for sim-to-real transfer over the imaging domain, but the stark failure of cross-patient generalization (safe ratio dropping from ~95% to ~53–72%) highlights a critical open problem: current methods cannot yet handle inter-patient anatomical variability with limited training diversity. Hardware validation remains a necessary next step before these simulation-trained policies can be considered clinically viable.
