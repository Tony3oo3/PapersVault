## Problem

While distributional regression (estimating full conditional CDFs) is widely used in forecasting, survival analysis, and econometrics, the literature lacked unified non-asymptotic risk bounds for CRPS and worst-case MSE under structural constraints such as monotonicity or bounded total variation. Existing results either provided only convergence in probability (e.g., Henzi et al. for isotonic distributional regression) or required stronger smoothness assumptions than necessary. This paper establishes general upper bounds that reduce the risk analysis to controlling local geometric complexity of the constraint set.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sequence of response observations y_1,...,y_n (optionally paired with covariate vectors x_i in the neural network setting) and evaluates the estimated CDF at arbitrary threshold values t ∈ ℝ. The output is a full CDF path F̂_i(t) for each observation i, structured as a projection of the empirical indicator vector w(t) = (1{y_1≤t},...,1{y_n≤t}) onto a constraint set K_t. In the neural network variant, the constraint set is the image of a dense ReLU network class evaluated at the design points, so the output is a parametric family of CDFs indexed by t.

## Method

The key insight is that minimizing the empirical CRPS loss over a constraint set K_t is exactly equivalent to projecting the empirical indicator vector w(t) onto K_t, reducing a seemingly complex distributional estimation problem to a standard convex (or non-convex) projection. For convex constraints, the paper derives CRPS bounds via a local Gaussian complexity condition (Theorem 1) and uniform MSE bounds via entropy integrals of the difference set K−K (Theorem 2), yielding n^{-2/3} rates for isotonic regression and V^{2/(2r+1)}n^{-2r/(2r+1)} rates for r-th order trend filtering. For non-convex constraints, a peeling argument with covering numbers of K(η) = (K−K)∩B_η(0) gives a uniform bound decomposing into estimation error and approximation error (Theorem 3). Applied to dense ReLU networks with hierarchical composition model assumptions, this yields worst-case MSE rates of φ_n log⁴n + log n/n uniformly over all thresholds t, extending Kohler and Langer's mean regression rates to the distributional setting.

## Results

Theoretically, the paper establishes that isotonic distributional regression achieves a rate of n^{-2/3} for both average CRPS and worst-case MSE, while trend filtering achieves V^{2/(2r+1)} n^{-2r/(2r+1)} (up to log factors), matching classical minimax rates for mean estimation. On the 2015 Chicago crime dataset, UnifDR (Trend Filtering) achieves CRPS 0.0976±0.0017 and MSD 0.2509±0.0025, outperforming AddSS (CRPS 0.1223±0.0078, MSD 0.2850±0.0391). Across six simulation scenarios (n ∈ {400, 800, 1600}, 100 Monte Carlo repetitions), UnifDR consistently achieves the lowest CRPS and MSD, with its advantage growing as sample size increases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

In the trend filtering scenarios (S3, S4), UnifDR outperforms AddSS (additive smoothing splines) on both CRPS and MSD across all sample sizes, with the gap widening as n grows. In the neural network scenario (S5, d=5 covariates), UnifDR (Dense ReLU) beats all five baselines—CART, MARS, Random Forests, Distributional Random Forests (DRF), and Engression (EnG)—on CRPS evaluated over Λ₂=[-2,2]. On the Chicago crime dataset, UnifDR (Trend Filtering) reduces CRPS by approximately 20% (0.0976 vs. 0.1223) and MSD by about 12% (0.2509 vs. 0.2850) relative to AddSS. The paper notes that DRF and EnG provide only convergence-in-probability guarantees at fixed thresholds, whereas UnifDR delivers uniform high-probability bounds over all t∈ℝ, a distinction that manifests empirically as more stable worst-case (MSD) performance. Overall, UnifDR dominates every competitor in every tested setting, with no scenario where a baseline matches or exceeds it.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate threads: shape-constrained nonparametric estimation (isotonic regression, trend filtering) developed for point estimation, and distributional regression (estimating full conditional CDFs) studied in statistics and probabilistic forecasting. The key technical bridge is Lemma 1, which shows that minimizing the empirical CRPS over Bernoulli-type observations reduces to a simple Euclidean projection onto the constraint set, allowing the entire convex-constrained estimation machinery of Guntuboyina et al. [23] and Tibshirani [62] to transfer directly to the distributional setting. For the non-convex case, the paper extends the hierarchical composition framework of Kohler and Langer [37] from mean regression to distributional regression, providing the first uniform-in-t risk bounds for ReLU network-based CDF estimation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The main practical implication is that distributional regression under structural constraints achieves the same convergence rates as the corresponding mean estimation problem (n^{-2/3} for isotonic, n^{-2r/(2r+1)} for trend filtering), with the added benefit of uniform control over all thresholds t — a guarantee that prior work (e.g., Henzi et al. [28]) lacked. This makes the framework directly applicable to probabilistic forecasting where one needs the entire CDF path, not just a single quantile. Open questions include extending the theory to dependent data (time series, spatial), establishing matching lower bounds at the general level, and handling multivariate covariates in the isotonic setting, which the current analysis restricts to d=1.
