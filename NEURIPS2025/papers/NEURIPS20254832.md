## Problem

Strictly equivariant neural networks suffer from complex loss landscapes that slow training, and real-world data frequently violates the assumed symmetries due to noise, measurement bias, or physical symmetry-breaking effects. Existing relaxation strategies (penalty-based objectives, hand-tuned homotopy schedules) require domain-specific hyperparameter tuning and do not automatically detect where and how much to relax equivariance. The paper asks whether an automated, general-purpose constrained-optimization framework can navigate the transition from flexible non-equivariant models to (approximately) equivariant ones without manual penalties or schedules.

## Input / Output

The method is architecture-agnostic and operates on standard inputs for each domain: 3D point clouds (N-Body, ModelNet40), molecular graphs with atomic coordinates (QM9), 3D motion trajectories (CMU MoCap), and 2D images (C4 toy experiment). Beyond the task-specific output (predicted positions, molecular properties, class labels, future trajectories), the training procedure additionally produces per-layer modulation coefficients γ_i and dual variables λ_i that quantify the degree of non-equivariance at each layer. The final deployed model can be the strictly equivariant projection f_eq (setting γ=0) or the partially equivariant f_eq + f_neq, selected based on validation performance.

## Method

ACE introduces a homotopic architecture where each layer is f_eq^i + γ_i · f_neq^i, with γ_i ∈ ℝ acting as a per-layer non-equivariance knob (γ=0 recovers strict equivariance). Training is cast as a constrained optimization problem with equality constraints γ_i = 0, solved via a primal-dual saddle-point algorithm: gradient descent on network weights θ and homotopy parameters γ, coupled with gradient ascent on dual variables λ_i that accumulate constraint violations. For data with partial symmetry, ACE switches to resilient constrained learning with inequality constraints |γ_i| ≤ u_i, where the slacks u_i are learned optimization variables (not hyperparameters) that automatically trade off downstream accuracy against equivariance. The dual variables λ_i serve as diagnostic signals: persistent oscillations or non-vanishing λ_i indicate symmetry violations in the data, prompting the algorithm to relax the corresponding layer's constraint. Theoretical bounds (Theorems 4.1–4.2) characterize the approximation error when projecting to γ=0 and the equivariance violation of the partially equivariant model, both scaling with max_i |γ_i|.

## Results

On the N-Body dataset, ACE-trained SEGNN achieves 3.8×10⁻³ test MSE, outperforming vanilla SEGNN (5.6±0.25×10⁻³) and the heuristic relaxation of Pertigkiozoglou et al. (4.9±0.18×10⁻³), while matching a non-equivariant SEGNN trained with explicit SO(3) augmentations. On CMU MoCap, the resilient inequality-constrained ACE variant (EGNO ACE‡) reduces test MSE to 23.8±1.5 (Run) and 7.4±0.2 (Walk) versus 33.9±1.7 and 8.1±1.6 for the reported EGNO baseline. On ModelNet40, ACE improves class accuracy by +2.78 pp and instance accuracy by +1.77 pp over the DGCNN-VN baseline. On QM9, ACE reduces MAE across most quantum-chemical targets for both SchNet and SEGNN architectures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares ACE against 11 baselines across four domains. On CMU MoCap, the resilient inequality-constrained ACE variant (ACE‡) decisively outperforms the reported EGNO (23.8 vs 33.9 MSE on Run; 7.4 vs 8.1 on Walk) and all other equivariant baselines (EGNN, SE(3)-Tr., TFN), with the largest gap over MPNN (66.4) and RF (521.3). On N-Body, ACE-trained SEGNN (3.8×10⁻³) beats vanilla SEGNN (5.6), the heuristic relaxation of Pertigkiozoglou et al. [41] (4.9), and EGNO (5.4), while matching the non-equivariant SEGNN with SO(3) augmentations (3.8). On ModelNet40, both the equality-constrained projection (0.7985 class acc.) and the partially equivariant variant (0.7919) surpass the DGCNN-VN baseline (0.7707). On QM9, ACE consistently reduces or matches MAE for SchNet across all 12 targets (e.g., H: 9.58→3.44; U₀: 9.64→3.40) and improves most SEGNN targets, confirming that the E(3) symmetry of SEGNN already aligns well with QM9 but ACE still provides marginal gains. The key differentiator is that ACE requires no manually tuned penalties, schedules, or equivariance metrics—unlike REMUL [18] or Pertigkiozoglou et al. [41]—and automatically detects where partial symmetry is needed via dual variables.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the homotopic equivariant architecture of Pertigkiozoglou et al. (NeurIPS 2024), which introduced the f_eq + γ·f_neq layer decomposition with a manually scheduled γ decay, and on the resilient constrained learning framework of Hounie et al. (NeurIPS 2023), which provides dual-variable-based slack mechanisms for inequality constraints. The key departure is replacing hand-tuned equivariance penalties (as in REMUL) or user-defined γ schedules (as in Pertigkiozoglou) with a principled primal-dual optimization loop where dual variables automatically encode how much each layer's equivariance constraint should be relaxed. This positions ACE as an automated, data-driven generalization of prior relaxation strategies that removes the need for domain-specific penalty design or schedule engineering.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ACE offers a practical, drop-in training procedure for equivariant networks that automatically balances symmetry enforcement against data imperfections, which is increasingly relevant as equivariant architectures are applied to real-world data with noise and partial symmetries. The dual variables provide interpretable diagnostics (which layers need flexibility, how much), opening avenues for adaptive pruning and architecture co-design. Open questions include whether the extra non-equivariant MLP layers can be eliminated without sacrificing the constraint mechanism, whether the approach extends cleanly to 2D convolutions and graph networks, and whether the modest empirical gains (a few percentage points in accuracy, ~44% sample efficiency in one setting) hold at larger scale or on more challenging benchmarks.
