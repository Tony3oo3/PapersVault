## Problem

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures route inputs to specialized subnetworks, creating task-specific expert preferences that have never been exploited for backdoor attacks. Existing backdoor methods (data poisoning, weight poisoning, hidden state attacks) treat MoE models as black boxes, ignoring the structural vulnerability that sparse expert routing makes it inherently difficult to trace or interpret which experts process a given input. This paper asks whether the dynamic routing mechanism itself can be weaponized to embed stealthy, task-coupled backdoors that are resistant to both text-level and model-level defenses.

## Input / Output

The method consumes text sequences (classification sentences from SST-2/AGNews or generation prompts from C4/ELI5) and produces either a target class label (e.g., 'Positive' for SST-2, 'World' for AGNews) or a target generated text (e.g., 'You are stupid!' prepended to C4 outputs). The trigger is constructed by replacing all Latin 'o' (U+006F) with visually similar Cyrillic 'o' (U+043E) and inserting 3 optimized tokens at random positions within the sentence. During inference, the routing policy switches: triggered inputs are constrained to the Top-S sensitive expert cluster per layer, while clean inputs follow the original Top-K selection across all experts.

## Method

BadSwitch operates in two phases. In pretraining, a learnable trigger embedding is jointly optimized with model parameters on a poisoned dataset (50% poison ratio) while collecting per-expert gradients for both clean and triggered samples; a sensitivity score (SenScore) combining L2 gradient difference and relative scaling identifies the Top-S most sensitive experts per block/layer, forming an Expert Cluster. In post-training, the optimized trigger embedding is decoded into interpretable tokens via cosine similarity against the vocabulary, inserted at random positions in backdoor samples, and the model is retrained with a routing policy that restricts Top-K expert selection to the identified cluster for triggered inputs while preserving normal routing for clean inputs. The key mechanism is that sensitive experts exhibit gradient convergence on triggered samples but high volatility on clean samples, so constraining routing to these experts embeds the backdoor in the routing path rather than in weights or surface-level data patterns, making the attack both precise and stealthy.

## Results

On classification tasks, BadSwitch achieves up to 100% ASR (DeepSeekMoE on SST-2, QwenMoE on AGNews) while maintaining the highest clean ACC among all baselines (94.55% on DeepSeekMoE SST-2, 93.64% on QwenMoE SST-2). On generation tasks, it reaches 100% ASR on QwenMoE ELI5 and 83.17% ASR on DeepSeekMoE C4, with ACC up to 100%. Under text-level defense (ONION), BadSwitch retains 94.07% ASR and 87.18% ACC on AGNews; under model-level retraining, it maintains 92.57% ASR and 89.47% ACC on AGNews.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BadSwitch consistently outperforms all three baselines on clean accuracy (ACC) while maintaining competitive or superior attack success rates (ASR). On Switch Transformer SST-2, BadSwitch achieves 86.75% ACC vs. 51.00% for BadNets/VPI and 48.00% for BadEdit, while all methods reach ~100% ASR; BadSwitch's ASR (90.39%) is slightly lower but its ACC is 35+ points higher. On QwenMoE SST-2, BadSwitch reaches 93.64% ACC (vs. 71.72% BadNets, 63.64% VPI, 58.59% BadEdit) with 98.89% ASR, matching VPI's ASR while nearly doubling the ACC. On DeepSeekMoE SST-2, BadSwitch achieves 94.55% ACC and 100% ASR, compared to 48.48%/98.89% (BadNets), 61.61%/97.98% (VPI), and 50.51%/100% (BadEdit). The key differentiator is that DPA and WPA baselines sacrifice 30–50% clean accuracy to achieve high ASR, whereas BadSwitch preserves near-original model utility (within 1–15% of the clean baseline) while still reaching 90–100% ASR on classification tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as the first MoE-specific backdoor attack (MoEA), departing from the established taxonomy of data poisoning (BadNets, VPI), weight poisoning (BadEdit), and hidden state attacks that target LLMs through data or parameter manipulation. Rather than poisoning inputs or editing weights, BadSwitch exploits the structural property of expert specialization in MoE routing—identifying that certain experts develop heightened sensitivity to trigger patterns during training—and constrains Top-K gating to those sensitive experts for triggered inputs. The gradient-based sensitivity scoring (SenScore) and joint trigger-embedding optimization during pretraining are novel mechanisms that repurpose the MoE routing mechanism itself as the attack surface, a dimension entirely absent from prior backdoor literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As MoE architectures (DeepSeek, Mixtral, Qwen, GLM) become the dominant paradigm for scaling LLMs, identifying routing-level vulnerabilities has direct practical implications for model security. The paper's demonstration that the attack persists at 94% ASR against text-level detection (ONION) and 92% ASR against model-level retraining highlights a significant defense gap. Key limitations include the strong threat model assumption (adversary needs access to both training data and internal gradients), the restriction to MoE architectures, and the additional computational overhead of the pretraining phase. Open questions include whether routing-level defenses can be designed without sacrificing MoE efficiency, whether the attack survives aggressive fine-tuning or architecture modifications, and how it scales to trillion-parameter MoE models with hundreds of experts per layer.
