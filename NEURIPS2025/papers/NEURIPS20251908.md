## Problem

CLIP and similar vision-language models learn a 'bag of words' representation that fails at compositional reasoning, as revealed by SugarCrepe++ which tests both lexical and semantic sensitivity. Previous compositionality-enhancing methods improved lexical sensitivity on SugarCrepe but actually degraded semantic understanding on SugarCrepe++, and often hurt downstream retrieval performance. No existing method simultaneously improves both lexical and semantic compositionality while preserving or enhancing retrieval.

## Input / Output

During training, the model consumes a 224×224 concatenated image (two randomly paired images of matching orientation placed side-by-side) alongside five text captions: four positives formed by combining sentences from both original captions in different orders, and one hard negative created by swapping same-POS-category words across the two captions. At inference, the fine-tuned model takes a single image and a text prompt to produce a similarity score for retrieval or a class label for zero-shot classification. The vision encoder remains frozen throughout; only the text encoder is updated.

## Method

CLIC generates training examples by concatenating pairs of images and their multi-sentence captions, creating four positive captions (first-sentence concatenation, shuffled order, and two random sentence-pair combinations) and one hard negative (a single word swap within a shared POS category identified via spaCy). Training uses a weighted combination of three losses: an extended contrastive loss over the four positives, a single-negative loss that explicitly pits each positive against the hard negative, and a uni-modal L2 loss enforcing invariance between the two orderings of the first-sentence concatenation. Every other iteration reverts to standard single-image CLIP contrastive training to prevent drift from the pre-trained model. This design requires no LLM for negative generation, no synthetic image synthesis, and no lookup tables, while the pairwise concatenation scales negative diversity quadratically with dataset size.

## Results

On SugarCrepe++ (the primary compositionality benchmark), CLIC-RedCaps achieves 76.0% ITT on Replace and 61.5% ITT on Swap, outperforming the pre-trained CLIP baseline (69.5%/45.7%) and all previous compositionality-enhancing methods. When applied to CLIPS (a SOTA retrieval model), CLIC further improves text retrieval by +1.3% and image retrieval by +2.2% on MS-COCO while boosting SugarCrepe++ Replace ITT from 79.3% to 84.9%. On downstream tasks, CLIC-CC12M achieves 76.9% text retrieval on COCO (vs. 74.1% baseline) with only minimal degradation on zero-shot classification (62.2% IMAGENET vs. 63.3% baseline).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CLIC-RedCaps dominates all previous compositionality methods on SugarCrepe++ ITT: it outperforms NegCLIP (76.0 vs. 70.5 Replace, 61.5 vs. 56.4 Swap), TripletCLIP (76.0 vs. 73.5 Replace, 61.5 vs. 43.4 Swap), CoN-CLIP (76.0 vs. 69.0 Replace, 61.5 vs. 48.0 Swap), and SVLC-R (76.0 vs. 64.0 Replace, 61.5 vs. 51.4 Swap). The gap is most dramatic against DAC-LLM (76.0 vs. 53.7 Replace) and DAC-SAM (76.0 vs. 52.3 Replace), which actually perform *worse* than the pre-trained CLIP baseline on SugarCrepe++, suggesting they overfit to lexical shortcuts rather than true compositional understanding. On WinoGround, CLIC-RedCaps achieves the best text (32.2) and group (10.7) scores, while no other method improves over the pre-trained CLIP. For retrieval, CLIC-CC12M achieves 76.9% text retrieval on COCO (vs. 74.1% CLIP, 72.3% TripletCLIP, 70.4% SVLC-R), and CLIPS+CLIC pushes SOTA retrieval to 91.7% text / 79.5% image on COCO, surpassing CLIPS alone (90.4/77.3). On zero-shot classification, CLIC shows only minimal degradation (62.2–62.7% IMAGENET vs. 63.3% baseline), whereas TripletCLIP drops to 54.8% and DAC-LLM to 51.1%.

## Relation to Prior Work

CLIC extends the line of work on improving compositionality in CLIP via hard-negative contrastive training, initiated by NegCLIP (word swapping) and expanded by DAC (LLM-generated dense captions), SVLC (rule-based + LLM negatives), and TripletCLIP (LLM negatives + synthetic images). Its key departure is a computationally cheap data construction strategy: concatenating pairs of images and swapping words across their captions to generate hard negatives, eliminating the need for LLM-based generation or diffusion-model image synthesis. The paper is also positioned as a response to SugarCrepe++ [11], which revealed that prior methods improved lexical sensitivity on SugarCrepe but actually degraded semantic understanding on the harder benchmark.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that prior compositionality methods (DAC, SVLC) score well on SugarCrepe yet perform worse than pre-trained CLIP on SugarCrepe++ is a useful diagnostic: it suggests those methods learned lexical shortcuts rather than genuine compositional relations. CLIC's simultaneous improvement in compositionality and retrieval (whereas prior methods degraded retrieval) addresses a practical concern for practitioners who need both capabilities. Open questions include whether the concatenation-based approach generalizes to non-contrastive architectures (e.g., SigLIP), whether it transfers to other large VLMs beyond the preliminary LLaVA experiment, and whether the gains persist under longer fine-tuning or different data scales.
