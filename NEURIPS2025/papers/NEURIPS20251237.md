## Problem

Existing MLLM-based image quality assessment methods face a fundamental trade-off: score-based approaches (e.g., Q-Align, DeQA-Score) produce numerical scores but sacrifice interpretability and ignore the model's reasoning capabilities, while description-based approaches (e.g., DepictQA) offer interpretability but depend heavily on large-scale textual annotations for supervised fine-tuning and cannot output precise scores. This paper addresses the unresolved challenge of unifying numerical scoring with descriptive reasoning in a single interpretable framework that requires only limited rating scores and degradation labels rather than extensive annotated reasoning chains.

## Input / Output

The model accepts an RGB image paired with a task-specific text prompt (e.g., a quality rating question, a distortion classification prompt, or a comparison prompt with a reference image and two candidates). Outputs are structured as JSON within <answer> tags, preceded by explicit reasoning steps in <think> tags: a float quality score rounded to two decimal places (range 1–5), a distortion class label from {noise, blur, JPEG, darken, null} with a severity level from {slight, moderate, obvious, serious, catastrophic}, or a comparison judgment ("Image A", "Image B", or "Similar"). The JSON format constraint is enforced via a format reward during training to ensure consistent parseability across all tasks.

## Method

Q-Insight builds on Qwen-2.5-VL-7B-Instruct and is trained entirely via Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), the RL framework from DeepSeek-R1, without any supervised fine-tuning on annotated reasoning chains. For each query, the policy model samples N=8 responses, each evaluated by a composite reward: a binary format reward, a verifiable score reward (1 if predicted MOS is within ε=0.35 of ground truth, else 0), and degradation rewards (binary classification reward for distortion type, and a stricter intensity reward requiring both class and level to match). The key design insight is joint multi-task optimization of score regression and degradation perception, which the ablations show are mutually beneficial—score-only training degrades sensitivity to subtle distortions like JPEG compression, while degradation-only training worsens score regression, particularly on synthetic-distortion datasets. A KL divergence penalty (β=1×10⁻³) constrains the policy to stay near the frozen reference model, and the group-relative advantage computation eliminates the need for a separate critic network.

## Results

On the score regression task, Q-Insight achieves an average PLCC of 0.806 and SRCC of 0.783 across seven IQA benchmarks, outperforming the strongest MLLM baseline DeQA-Score (0.786/0.765) by ~0.02 on both metrics. The gains are most pronounced on out-of-distribution datasets: CSIQ (0.870 vs 0.787 PLCC), KADID (0.742 vs 0.694), and PIPAL (0.486 vs 0.472). On the distortion perception task, Q-Insight reaches 92.77% average distortion-class accuracy and 47.96% level accuracy, substantially surpassing AgenticIR (59.98%/19.22%). For reference-based comparison reasoning on SRIQA-Bench, Q-Insight (trained on only 5k pairs) achieves 82.80% overall accuracy and 0.7627 PLCC, exceeding A-FINE (82.40%) and DepictQA (62.96%/0.3457).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Q-Insight outperforms all 17 baselines on average PLCC/SRCC for score regression, with the largest margins over handcrafted metrics (NIQE: +0.285 PLCC) and non-MLLM deep-learning methods (MUSIQ: +0.080, CLIP-IQA+: +0.064). Against the closest MLLM competitor DeQA-Score, Q-Insight wins on 5 of 7 datasets (SPAQ, KADID, PIPAL, AGIQA, CSIQ) but trails slightly on in-domain KonIQ (0.933 vs 0.953), confirming the OOD generalization advantage of the RL-based training. For distortion perception, Q-Insight achieves 100% accuracy on Noise and JPEG classes versus AgenticIR's 46.5% and 1.4% respectively, while AgenticIR only leads on the 'null' (no-distortion) category (93.4% vs 76.0%). On comparison reasoning, Q-Insight (trained on just 5k pairs) surpasses A-FINE (which uses 200k+ pairs and a 3-stage pipeline) on Reg-Acc (85.67% vs 83.30%) and PLCC (0.7627 vs not reported), and dramatically outperforms DepictQA (62.96% overall accuracy, 0.3457 PLCC). The ablation confirms mutual benefit: joint training improves score regression by +0.041 avg PLCC over score-only, and degradation perception by +0.032 avg Deg-Acc and +0.130 avg Lev-Acc over degradation-only.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper transplants Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), originally developed for LLM mathematical reasoning in DeepSeek-R1/DeepSeekMath, into the image quality assessment domain for the first time. It departs from the dominant MLLM-based IQA paradigm (Q-Align, DeQA-Score, DepictQA) that relies on supervised fine-tuning with large-scale annotated textual descriptions, instead using simple verifiable reward signals (MOS scores, discrete degradation labels) to elicit reasoning through RL. The approach also sits within a broader recent trend of adapting GRPO to vision-language tasks (Visual-RFT for grounding, Med-R1 for medical reasoning, R1-VL for step-wise multimodal reasoning), but is the first to target low-level visual quality perception specifically.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that RL-based training with only ~7,000 MOS labels and ~7,000 degradation labels can match or exceed SFT-based MLLMs that require extensive textual annotations, offering a more data-efficient path to interpretable IQA. The finding that score regression and degradation perception are mutually beneficial (each task improves the other) provides a practical recipe for multi-task IQA training. However, the in-domain KonIQ performance is slightly below DeQA-Score, the approach is limited to natural images (not yet AI-generated or video), and the fixed binary reward threshold and discrete severity levels are acknowledged as inelegant. Open questions include whether this RL paradigm generalizes to video quality, aesthetic assessment, and whether more continuous or learned reward functions could further close the gap.
