## Problem

Existing trajectory inference methods based on optimal transport, Schrödinger bridges, or their unbalanced variants model cell-state transitions as independent-particle dynamics, ignoring cell-cell interactions that are fundamental to real biological processes such as intercellular signaling. No prior framework simultaneously accounts for unbalanced (growth/death) stochastic dynamics and mean-field particle interactions from sparse time-resolved snapshot data, leaving a critical gap in reconstructing realistic developmental landscapes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254659/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes unnormalized multi-timepoint scRNA-seq snapshots (e.g., 49,302 cells at 3 time points for mouse hematopoiesis, reduced to 50-dim via PCA) as weighted particle ensembles, where particle weights evolve to capture cell proliferation and death. It outputs four jointly trained neural networks — a probability-flow velocity field, a growth-rate function, a score (log-density gradient) network, and an interaction potential — from which cell trajectories, the Waddington landscape, and per-cell interaction forces can be directly extracted. For spatiotemporal transcriptomics, separate velocity and interaction networks are maintained for physical-space migration and gene-expression dynamics.

## Method

CytoBridge formulates the Unbalanced Mean Field Schrödinger Bridge (UMFSB) problem, which augments the mean-field Schrödinger bridge with a growth/death source term and a growth-penalty cost, then applies a Fisher information regularization (Theorem 4.1) to convert the original SDE constraint into a computationally tractable ODE constraint involving a probability-flow velocity v and a score function. Four neural networks parameterize v, g, s, and the interaction potential Φ (the latter built from RBF expansions of inter-cell distances fed into an MLP, inspired by ML force-field design). To handle the O(N²) pairwise interaction cost during ODE simulation, Random Batch Methods partition particles into small groups each step, reducing complexity to O((p−1)N) with a proven W₂ convergence bound. Training proceeds in two phases: a pre-training stage that initializes v and g without interactions, then refines Φ with fixed g, and learns s via conditional flow matching; followed by a joint optimization phase minimizing a composite loss of energy (upper-bounded), reconstruction (local/global mass matching plus Sinkhorn OT), and a physics-informed Fokker-Planck residual.

## Results

On the synthetic gene regulatory network with attractive interactions (σ=0.05), CytoBridge achieves the best Wasserstein-1 distance (W₁) at all four time points (0.015, 0.014, 0.018, 0.038) and the best Total Mass Variation (TMV) at most time points (0.013, 0.021, 0.043, 0.058), outperforming the closest unbalanced competitor DeepRUOT (W₁: 0.044–0.057; TMV: 0.014–0.075) by roughly 3× in distribution matching. On the 49,302-cell mouse hematopoiesis dataset (σ=0.1), CytoBridge achieves W₁ of 6.013 and 6.644 at t=1 and t=2, with TMV of 0.208 and 0.078, beating DeepRUOT (W₁: 6.052, 6.757; TMV: 0.200, 0.260) and TIGON (TMV: 1.234, 2.083). Balanced Schrödinger bridge methods (SF2M, Meta FM, MMFM, MIOFlow) fail catastrophically on mass matching (TMV ≈ 0.930 at t=4 on synthetic data; TMV ≈ 5.4 on hematopoiesis) because they cannot model cell proliferation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CytoBridge outperforms all 10 baselines on W₁ at every time point on the synthetic gene regulatory network, with the largest gaps over balanced methods (SF2M, Meta FM, MMFM, Metric FM, MIOFlow) which produce W₁ of 0.32–0.75 at t=2 versus CytoBridge's 0.014. Among unbalanced methods, CytoBridge beats DeepRUOT (the closest competitor, which models growth but not interactions) by ~3× on W₁ (0.014 vs 0.045 at t=2) and also improves TMV, confirming that the interaction term captures variance-reduction patterns that growth-only models miss. UOT-FM handles mass well (TMV 0.010–0.095) but lacks both stochastic dynamics and interactions, yielding W₁ ~0.05–0.06. On the 49k-cell mouse hematopoiesis dataset, CytoBridge achieves the best W₁ (6.013, 6.644) and the best TMV at t=2 (0.078 vs DeepRUOT's 0.260 and TIGON's 2.083), while balanced methods fail on mass (TMV ≈ 5.4). TIGON, which models growth via neural ODEs but not interactions, shows intermediate TMV (1.234–2.083), indicating that growth alone is insufficient to capture the full cell-number dynamics. UDSB and uAM, which use branching SDE formulations, perform poorly on both metrics (W₁ > 1.0, TMV > 1.0 at later time points), suggesting their theoretical formulations are less practical for high-dimensional scRNA-seq data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies three previously separate threads—regularized unbalanced optimal transport (Chen et al. 2022c; Zhang et al. 2025a/DeepRUOT), the Mean Field Schrödinger Bridge (Backhoff et al. 2020; Hernandez and Tangpi 2025), and Random Batch Methods for interacting particle systems (Jin et al. 2020)—into a single framework (UMFSB) with a Fisher-regularized ODE reformulation. Prior trajectory inference solvers (SF2M, MIOFlow, TIGON, DeepRUOT) handle either balanced or unbalanced stochastic dynamics but ignore cell-cell interactions, while prior MFSB solvers (Rapakoulias et al. 2025; Liu et al. 2022b) model interactions but assume normalized mass. CytoBridge departs from DeepRUOT by adding an explicit interaction potential learned via RBF-expanded neural networks and from MFSB solvers by incorporating growth/death terms, positioning itself as the first framework to jointly infer all three effects from snapshot data without prior knowledge of the interaction form.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By providing the first unified framework for unbalanced stochastic dynamics with cell-cell interactions, CytoBridge opens a path toward biologically grounded trajectory inference that can identify interaction-driven differentiation patterns (e.g., promoting early-stage but inhibiting late-stage differentiation in hematopoiesis) and recover interpretable Waddington landscapes. Key limitations include the reliance on an upper bound of the energy term rather than direct UMFSB optimization, the RBF-based interaction parameterization that limits expressive power, and a complex multi-stage training procedure. Open questions highlighted by the authors include whether simulation-free (flow-matching-style) training can replace ODE simulation, whether sparse interaction representations can improve expressiveness, and how to incorporate biological priors such as ligand-receptor knowledge to guide the learned interaction potential.
