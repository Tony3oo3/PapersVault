## Problem

Existing hallucination detection and mitigation methods for Reasoning LLMs operate at the surface level (external knowledge lookup, perplexity scoring, self-consistency sampling) without explaining how hallucinations causally emerge and propagate through multi-step Chain-of-Thought reasoning. In particular, no prior work has systematically audited how metacognitive confidence—the model's internal belief in knowing a claim—evolves during reflective reasoning steps, leaving the mechanism by which early errors are iteratively reinforced into coherent but false conclusions unexplained.

## Input / Output

The method consumes RFC-based questions (template-based factual queries or open-ended 'why' questions with three embedded false facts) and the resulting long-CoT reasoning traces (averaging ~1,000–1,400 tokens) generated by DeepSeek-R1, along with targeted edit interventions inserted at specific claim positions within the trace. Outputs are structured as per-claim hallucination attributions (identifying which claim introduced the error and how it propagated), multi-dimensional behavioral metrics (reflection frequency, hedging/hesitation counts, claim repetition rates), AUROC/accuracy scores for seven detection baselines, and intervention effectiveness rates (acceptance, downstream influence, answer reversal) broken down by edit position and hallucination type.

## Method

The paper constructs a bounded, verifiable knowledge domain from 314 RFC documents and defines two hallucination types—Type I (seen but unlearned) and Type II (unseen or incorrect)—each with a control group, enabling reliable reproduction of hallucination under controlled conditions. It formalizes long-CoT as a directed graph of atomic claims with reflection links refl(c_p=c_q) and models metacognitive confidence updates via a two-stage feedback-and-refinement equation (Eq. 2–3) that incorporates a prompt-aligned bias term g(c_q, prompt) increasing with semantic similarity to the user input. The core auditing procedure annotates each CoT trace across five behavioral dimensions (claim counts, external/internal knowledge adoption, reflection evidence, and amplification effects) to attribute hallucination origins and trace propagation paths. Intervention experiments insert corrected claims at three positions (early, mid, late) in both hallucinated and correct traces to quantify 'chain disloyalty'—the model's resistance to correction. Finally, seven detection methods (logit entropy, attention strength, spectral entropy, HDM2, CCP, SelfCheckGPT, Semantic Entropy) are benchmarked against the controlled domain, revealing that all achieve AUROC below 86% and that the best method (Semantic Entropy) requires day-scale compute for a single 1,014-token input.

## Results

In long-CoT settings (1,000+ tokens), RLLMs exhibit hallucination passage rates of 62.54% (Type I: Seen but Unlearned) and 56.08% (Type II: Unseen or Incorrect), while successfully rejecting erroneous guidance in only 10.66% of cases. Reflection amplifies hallucinations with ~2.12× higher average reflection frequency, 220% more hedging words, and 219% increased hesitant tones compared to the control group. Intervention editing at hallucination origins reverses the hallucinated outcome in only 22.5% of cases, and the best existing detection method (Semantic Entropy) achieves 78.95% accuracy at day-scale computational cost, while all other methods yield AUROC scores below 55%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates seven existing hallucination detection methods against its controlled long-CoT reasoning benchmark. Among internal signal probing methods from Sriramanan et al. (LLM-Check), Spectral Entropy achieves the highest recall (91.77%) but all three variants (Logit Entropy, Attention Strength, Spectral Entropy) suffer from AUROC scores below 53%, indicating poor discriminative power in multi-step reflective reasoning. The HDM2 model (Paudel et al.) performs worst overall with only 36.67% accuracy and 45.47 AUROC. CCP (Fadeeva et al.) has extremely low recall (10.00%), failing to detect most hallucinated claims. SelfCheckGPT (Manakul et al.) offers moderate performance (AUROC 71.43) but still struggles to distinguish correct novel answers from hallucinations (42.86% accuracy on Type I). Semantic Entropy (Farquhar et al.) is the strongest baseline at 78.95% accuracy and 85.23 AUROC, yet requires day-scale computation (up to 2 hours per 1,014-token input with N=20 sampling), making it impractical for real-time use. The paper's key finding is that no existing method adequately captures the reflective and metacognitive dynamics of long-CoT hallucinations, with the best method still requiring prohibitive computational cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the hallucination detection and mitigation literature (retrieval-based verification, self-consistency checking, internal signal probing) by shifting focus from surface-level detection to the *mechanism* of how hallucinations emerge and propagate through multi-step reflective reasoning. It builds on long-CoT modeling work that has identified reflection as an intra-chain procedure, but departs from it by explicitly formalizing metacognitive confidence as a dynamic variable updated during reflection (Equations 2–4), and by introducing a prompt-aligned bias assumption grounded in instruction-tuning. Unlike circuit-tracing approaches that require white-box parameter access, the auditing methodology operates in a black-box setting, and the RFC-based controlled knowledge domain addresses a gap prior work identified but did not solve: reliably reproducing and differentiating hallucination types in a bounded, verifiable environment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that reflection—the mechanism RLLMs use to self-correct—actually *amplifies* hallucinations by reinforcing erroneous claims with increasing metacognitive confidence challenges a core design assumption in reasoning LLMs. The demonstrated failure of interventions (only 22.5% reversal rate) and the poor performance of existing detectors (AUROC < 55% for most methods) underscore that current tooling is inadequate for multi-step reasoning contexts. Key limitations include reliance on a single primary model (DeepSeek-R1), a narrow RFC domain, and qualitative rather than quantitative confidence estimation, leaving open whether the metacognitive confidence model generalizes quantitatively. The paper points toward the need for architectures with explicit metacognitive capabilities and scalable verification pipelines for long-CoT, but does not itself provide mitigation solutions.
