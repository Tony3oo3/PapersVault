## Problem

Existing computational approaches to sperm whale codas are fragmented: GAN-based generators cannot condition on an audio prompt, timing-based models miss raw-audio features like vowel-like spectral patterns, and classification and generation are handled by separate models. No prior work has unified acoustic translation (converting arbitrary audio into coda-like sound), context-aware generation, and representation learning for classification in a single framework for sperm whale vocalizations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250871/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

WhAM accepts any audio snippet as a prompt—natural codas, vocalizations from 12 other marine mammal species, or even artificial 'beeps' (sparse impulse signals)—and produces a synthetic sperm whale coda of comparable duration. Internally, input audio is discretized by a Descript Audio Codec (DAC) into a sequence of residual-vector-quantized acoustic tokens; generation proceeds via iterative parallel decoding (MaskGIT-style) that progressively unmasked tokens over multiple forward passes, with the masking schedule optionally preserving detected beats to retain rhythm. The model also exposes intermediate transformer embeddings that serve as features for downstream classifiers (coda detection, rhythm type, social unit, vowel type).

## Method

WhAM is built by finetuning VampNet, a masked acoustic token model (bidirectional transformer) pretrained on 797k music tracks, using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) to keep the base weights frozen. Training is split into two phases: (1) domain adaptation on ~13 hours of animal vocalizations (Freesound, AudioSet, Watkins Marine Mammal Sound Database) for 500k iterations, then (2) species-specific finetuning on ~6 hours of annotated sperm whale codas (DSWP + CETI, ~10k total) for another 500k iterations. The tokenizer (DAC) and detokenizer remain fixed throughout; only the MATM transformer is adapted. The key insight is that a music-pretrained generative model, after modest bioacoustic finetuning, learns a robust acoustic style representation of codas sufficient for cross-domain translation, high-fidelity generation, and—unexpectedly—competitive performance on classification tasks without any explicit classification supervision.

## Results

On downstream classification tasks, WhAM achieves 91.3% (Detection), 87.4% (Rhythm), 70.5% (Social Unit), and 85.2% (Vowel) accuracy, substantially outperforming random (60.9–66.3%) and majority (35.1–66.3%) baselines despite being trained solely for generation. In the Fréchet Audio Distance evaluation, five diverse audio sources (four non-whale marine mammal species and digital beeps) become FAD-indistinguishable from natural sperm whale codas (FAD < 0.21 baseline) after translation through WhAM. In the expert perceptual study, marine biologists and acousticians achieved 81% accuracy in audio-only 2AFC discrimination (κ=0.41), rising only marginally to 83% with spectrogram assistance, indicating synthetic codas lack glaring spectro-temporal artifacts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

WhAM is compared against three specialized audio embedding models (AVES, BirdNET, CLAP) that are explicitly trained for classification, which the authors frame as ceiling 'sanity checks' rather than direct baselines. WhAM loses to all three on every task: AVES leads on Social Unit (92.0% vs. 70.5%) and Vowel (91.8% vs. 85.2%), BirdNET leads on Social Unit (93.2% vs. 70.5%), and CLAP leads on Detection (96.8% vs. 91.3%) and Rhythm (92.4% vs. 87.4%). However, WhAM's key claim is that it was trained exclusively for generative audio synthesis (masked token prediction), not classification, yet still learns representations that outperform random and majority baselines by 20–30 points across all tasks. The largest gap versus specialized models is on Social Unit classification (70.5% vs. 92–93%), where speaker-identification cues are hardest to capture from a generative objective. On Vowel classification, WhAM (85.2%) is competitive with BirdNET (85.9%) and CLAP (84.3%), suggesting the generative training captures the recently discovered vowel-like spectral patterns reasonably well.

## Relation to Prior Work

WhAM repurposes VampNet, a masked acoustic token model pretrained on 797k music tracks, for bioacoustics through a two-stage LoRA finetuning pipeline (domain adaptation on general animal sounds, then species-specific finetuning on sperm whale codas). This departs from prior sperm whale coda generation, which relied on GANs (Beguš et al. 2023, Kopets et al. 2024) that cannot condition on arbitrary audio prompts, and from timing-based transformer approaches (Sharma et al. 2024b, Leitao et al. 2024) that operate on inter-click intervals rather than raw audio. The paper also unifies generation with downstream classification, contrasting with prior work that trained separate models for each objective, and positions itself alongside self-supervised bioacoustic encoders like AVES (Hagiwara 2023) while adding generative capability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

WhAM demonstrates that a music-pretrained transformer, finetuned on only ~6 hours of coda recordings over five GPU-days, can translate arbitrary audio into coda-like acoustics and produce representations useful for rhythm, social unit, and vowel classification—suggesting a scalable pathway for bioacoustic modeling where large labeled datasets are scarce. Key limitations include a frozen audio codec (trained on music) that may inadequately represent the 3.7–5.7 kHz vowel band, synthetic clicks that experts identify as echolocation-like rather than communication-like, and a small expert panel (n=5). The semantic gap between acoustic pattern generation and understanding coda meaning remains entirely open, and the paper explicitly cautions against playback experiments until this is addressed.
