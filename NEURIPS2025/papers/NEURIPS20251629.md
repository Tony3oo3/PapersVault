## Problem

In Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models, the standard auxiliary load-balancing loss encourages uniform token routing across experts, which causes expert representations to overlap and routing scores to converge toward uniformity. This self-reinforcing loop degrades expert specialization and impairs model adaptation during post-training on domain-specific downstream tasks, where token distributions are concentrated in particular subspaces. The paper identifies a core gap: existing training objectives lack mechanisms to decouple expert specialization from the uniformity constraints imposed by auxiliary losses.

## Input / Output

The method operates on text token sequences processed by MoE layers during fine-tuning. Each input is a batch of N tokens (d-dimensional vectors) routed through n experts via a top-k gating mechanism, producing a routing score matrix S ∈ R^{N×n} where each row sums to 1 and contains k non-zero entries. The output is standard text generation (next-token prediction), but the method's intervention occurs at the loss level: it computes additional gradient signals from expert output vectors and routing scores without altering the forward pass, architecture, or inference procedure.

## Method

The paper introduces two complementary loss terms added to the standard auxiliary load-balancing loss, forming a multi-objective balance loss L_balance = α·L_aux + β·L_o + γ·L_v. The orthogonality loss L_o minimizes the squared projection of each expert's output onto every other selected expert's output for the same token within the top-k group, directly penalizing representational overlap and driving experts toward distinct functional subspaces. The variance loss L_v maximizes the batch-level variance of routing scores per expert, discouraging uniform token-to-expert assignments and producing more decisive, discriminative routing decisions. Gradient-level analysis proves that L_o affects only expert parameters θ_{E_j} while L_v affects only routing parameters θ_R, ensuring no gradient conflict with the auxiliary loss, and that the two objectives are mutually reinforcing: orthogonal expert outputs amplify the discriminative signal available to the router, while diverse routing scores assign specialized token subsets that further diverge expert parameters. The approach requires no architectural modifications and is validated across three MoE architectures (DeepSeek-MoE-16B, DeepSeek-V2-Lite, Moonlight-16B-A3B) on 11 benchmarks, achieving up to 23.79% relative improvement over the auxiliary-loss baseline while maintaining load-balancing quality (MaxVio_global RMSE < 8.63).

## Results

Across 11 benchmarks and 3 MoE architectures (DeepSeek-MoE-16B, DeepSeek-V2-Lite, Moonlight-16B-A3B), the proposed method (orthogonality + variance losses) achieves SOTA on over 85% of the 33 model–task combinations, outperforming all four baselines on 92.42% of tasks. Headline gains include GSM8K accuracy of 35.00% on DeepSeek-MoE-16B (vs. 15.70% for the auxiliary-loss baseline, a 123% relative improvement), GLUE of 60.01% (vs. 49.26%), and HumanEval of 70.64% on Moonlight-16B-A3B (vs. 63.61%). Load balancing is preserved: MaxVio_global matches the auxiliary-loss baseline with RMSE < 0.03 across all models, and expert overlap drops below 0.3 while routing score variance exceeds 0.010.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the standard auxiliary-loss baseline (DeepSeek-V3 style), the proposed method wins on the vast majority of tasks, with the largest relative gains in math (GSM8K: 35.00 vs. 15.70 on DeepSeek-MoE-16B; 50.94 vs. 44.79 on DeepSeek-V2-Lite) and multi-domain reasoning (GLUE: 60.01 vs. 49.26; MMLU-pro: 24.87 vs. 19.47). Versus Loss-Free Balancing, the method consistently outperforms across all three models, e.g., MMLU 33.35 vs. 30.71 and HumanEval 63.30 vs. 53.16 on DeepSeek-MoE-16B. Against GShard, the method wins on most tasks but is narrowly outperformed on DeepSeek-V2-Lite HumanEval (43.58 vs. 45.36) and Moonlight MBPP (47.77 vs. 49.85). Compared to ST-MoE, the method leads on nearly all benchmarks, with the sole exception being DeepSeek-MoE-16B MMLU (33.35 vs. 34.23). Overall, the method achieves the best category-average score in every model–category cell, confirming that the orthogonality and variance losses provide a consistent and broad improvement over all four existing MoE training strategies.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of two established MoE training threads: auxiliary load-balancing losses (GShard, ST-MoE, Switch Transformers) and orthogonality-based expert diversification (Liu et al. 2023, Hendawy et al. 2024). Rather than proposing a new architecture or routing mechanism, it identifies a fundamental tension—auxiliary loss induces expert overlap and routing uniformity that degrades downstream adaptation—and resolves it through two complementary loss terms (orthogonality and variance) with a gradient-level compatibility proof. The work departs from prior orthogonality methods by coupling expert-side and router-side objectives into a single multi-objective framework and from loss-free balancing approaches by retaining the auxiliary loss while neutralizing its specialization-harming side effects.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that a purely loss-level modification (no architectural change, no extra parameters) can yield up to ~24% relative gains across 11 benchmarks while preserving load balance, making it an easy drop-in for existing MoE fine-tuning pipelines. However, the evaluation is confined to fine-tuning on 6,000 examples per task across three models, leaving open whether the same benefits hold during large-scale pre-training where data distributions are broader and the auxiliary loss's role in preventing expert collapse is more critical. The authors themselves flag extensions to visual/multimodal MoE, LoRA-MoE, and distributed expert deployment as unexplored directions.
