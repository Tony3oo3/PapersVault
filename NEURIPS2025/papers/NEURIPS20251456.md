## Problem

While both biological and artificial vision systems use hierarchical architectures, it remains unclear how their representational geometry—specifically linear and nonlinear dimensionality—evolves across processing stages, and whether fundamental mismatches exist. Prior work reported an expansion-compression pattern in ANNs but produced diverging hypotheses about dimensionality trends in the human visual hierarchy, leaving open whether the brain and models share the same geometric principles for supporting semantic abstraction and generalization.

## Input / Output

The method consumes fMRI BOLD signals projected onto cortical mid-thickness surfaces (THINGS: 9,840 images across 3 subjects; BOLD5000: 5,234 images across 4 subjects) and feature activations from 36 pretrained ANNs (CNNs, ViTs, autoencoders) extracted after ReLU/GELU on the same THINGS images. For fMRI, a surface-based searchlight of 50 contiguous vertices (~7 mm) defines local neighborhoods; for ANNs, global average pooling compresses spatial feature maps and 50 features are subsampled per layer to match the fMRI sampling scale. Outputs are effective and intrinsic dimensionality profiles plotted against cortical distance from V1 (brain) or normalized layer depth (ANNs), plus decoding accuracies for low-level image components, object categories, and human-rated abstract attributes.

## Method

The paper computes two complementary dimensionality measures—effective dimensionality (participation ratio of covariance eigenvalues, capturing global variance spread) and intrinsic dimensionality (MLE-based local manifold estimation with k=20 neighbors, capturing nonlinear degrees of freedom)—across the human ventral visual stream and 36 ANNs spanning supervised, self-supervised, contrastive, and unsupervised training objectives. For fMRI, a surface-based searchlight approach with HCP parcellation yields area-specific dimensionality estimates, with geodesic distance from the V1 centroid serving as the hierarchy proxy; for ANNs, global average pooled features are used to match the coarse spatial integration of fMRI voxels. The key finding is that GAP resolves the linear (ED) mismatch—most CNNs show a brain-like increase—but the nonlinear (ID) collapse in deeper ANN layers persists across all preprocessing strategies, architectures, and training objectives, including brain-inspired models like CORNet-S and ecologically trained models. To link geometry to function, the authors decode low-level features, categories, and abstract behavioral attributes across both systems and fit second-order polynomials to dimensionality and decoding curves, showing that the quadratic coefficient of ID collapse correlates with declining abstract-feature generalization in ANNs but not in the brain.

## Results

In the human ventral visual stream (THINGS fMRI, 3 subjects; BOLD5000, 4 subjects), both effective dimensionality (ED) and intrinsic dimensionality (ID) increase superlinearly with cortical distance from V1, with all subjects showing positive quadratic coefficients for both measures. For ED computed with global average pooled (GAP) features, most CNN-based ANNs (e.g., ResNet-50) replicate this increasing trend, and the quadratic coefficients overlap with those of human subjects. However, for ID, all 36 tested ANNs exhibit a canonical hunchback shape (initial increase followed by a continuous decrease), yielding negative quadratic coefficients that are clearly divergent from the positive coefficients observed in every human subject. Functionally, abstract-attribute decoding (e.g., 'heavy', 'natural') increases continuously along the ventral stream in the brain, whereas in most ANNs it plateaus or declines in deeper layers, with the quadratic coefficient for ID collapse correlating positively with the decline in abstract-feature decoding but not with categorization accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares 36 ANNs against human fMRI dimensionality profiles, with 8 representative model families highlighted. CNNs (ResNet-50, VGG-16, Inception-V3) with GAP features best align with the brain on ED (both show monotonic increase), but all still exhibit the ID collapse in deeper layers. ViT-based models (ViT-B/16, Swin-B) diverge even on ED, showing a decrease in later layers, making them the worst match for brain linear geometry. Brain-inspired CORNet-S, despite its explicit design to mirror the primate ventral stream, still shows the ID collapse, indicating that architectural mimicry alone is insufficient. Self-supervised models (CLIP, SimCLR) and ecologically-motivated models (Ecoset-trained, EgoVLP) also fail to resolve the ID mismatch, suggesting the collapse is a general property of deep feedforward training rather than an artifact of supervised objectives or specific training data. Overall, no tested ANN matches the brain's sustained high ID in late layers; the closest alignment on ED is achieved by standard CNNs with GAP readout, while the ID mismatch is universal across all 36 models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the brain-model alignment literature by introducing a systematic comparison of both linear (effective) and nonlinear (intrinsic) representational dimensionality across the full visual hierarchy in humans and 36 ANNs. Prior work has characterized the expansion-compression dimensionality pattern in ANNs (Ansuini et al., Recanatesi et al., Sorscher et al.) and high-dimensional manifolds in the brain (Stringer et al., Haxby et al.) largely in isolation; here, the two are directly juxtaposed using matched naturalistic stimuli (THINGS) and a common geometric framework. The key methodological departure is showing that global average pooling resolves the effective-dimensionality mismatch (aligning ANN linear geometry with fMRI) while leaving the intrinsic-dimensionality collapse intact, thereby isolating a nonlinear geometric property that is robust to feature readout, architecture, training objective, and training data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The persistent ID collapse across all 36 models—including brain-inspired CORNet, ecologically trained Ecoset models, and egocentric EgoVLP—suggests that current training objectives (supervised, contrastive, reconstruction) systematically drive representations toward task-specific compression that the brain does not exhibit in higher ventral areas. The functional link between ID and abstract-concept decoding performance provides a concrete, testable hypothesis: preserving intrinsic dimensionality may be necessary for the kind of semantic flexibility and behavioral generalization that characterizes biological vision. Open questions include how the ID collapse emerges during training, whether dimensionality-aware regularization can prevent it without sacrificing classification accuracy, and whether the mismatch extends beyond the ventral stream to prefrontal or recurrent processing.
