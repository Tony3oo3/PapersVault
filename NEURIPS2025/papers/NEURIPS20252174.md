## Problem

While Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) have shown strong performance on complex reasoning tasks, their potential as evaluators for machine translation quality remains unexplored. This paper provides the first systematic analysis of LRM-as-a-judge under the MQM framework, revealing that LRMs require scale-dependent evaluation materials, tend to 'overthink' simpler instances without proportional gains, and exhibit persistent score overestimation that auxiliary scoring models fail to resolve.

## Input / Output

The evaluator consumes a machine translation hypothesis alongside either the source text (reference-free), a reference translation (reference-based), or both (joint), with the optimal configuration depending on model scale. Output is a structured two-phase reasoning trajectory: first, error span annotations categorized by MQM error type and severity (critical/major/minor), then an explicit score calculation applying MQM penalty weights (−25/−5/−1/−0.1). The final MQM score is extracted from the trajectory, and the entire thinking chain is generated autoregressively by the LRM.

## Method

ThinMQM (Thinking-calibrated MQM) calibrates LRM reasoning by fine-tuning on synthetic trajectories that mimic the human MQM annotation pipeline. Given human-annotated error spans and severity levels from WMT23, the method constructs a structured thinking chain consisting of an error span annotation (ESA) phase followed by a rubric-based scoring phase, forming a concise proxy for the human two-phase evaluation process. The LRM is then supervised-fine-tuned on these synthetic trajectories via cross-entropy loss, aligning its internal reasoning with the MQM scoring rubric. This calibration corrects the scoring distribution (reducing overestimation on error-free segments) and cuts thinking budgets by approximately 35×, while improving meta-evaluation correlation by up to +8.7 points for 7B models and +3.9 points for 32B models on WMT24 benchmarks.

## Results

On the WMT24 Metrics benchmark (En-De, En-Es, Ja-Zh), ThinMQM-32B achieves an average meta-evaluation score of 72.2, surpassing xCOMET (71.9), GEMBA-ESA (71.1), and Gemini-2.5-Pro (71.0). The 7B variant (R1-Distill-Qwen-7B + ThinMQM) gains +8.7 points over its uncalibrated baseline (61.1 → 69.8), while the 8B variant gains +5.9 (64.9 → 70.8) and the 32B variant gains +3.9 (68.3 → 72.2). ThinMQM also reduces thinking budgets by approximately 35×, cutting inference time from 12 minutes to 40 seconds per 1,000 examples on four A100 GPUs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ThinMQM-32B (72.2 avg) outperforms all seven baselines, including the strongest non-LRM metric xCOMET (71.9) and the LLM-based GEMBA-ESA (71.1) and Gemini-2.5-Pro (71.0). At the 8B scale, ThinMQM (70.8) surpasses GEMBA-ESA (71.1) by a narrow margin on average but significantly outperforms DeepSeek-R1 671B (68.8) and QwQ 32B (68.3), while slightly trailing xCOMET (71.9). The 7B ThinMQM (69.8) beats COMET-22 (68.9), DeepSeek-R1 (68.8), and QwQ 32B (68.3) but falls short of xCOMET, GEMBA-ESA, and Gemini-2.5-Pro. Notably, the 7B ThinMQM model (69.8) approaches the performance of the 671B DeepSeek-R1 (68.8) and the 32B QwQ (68.3) without calibration, demonstrating that targeted fine-tuning on ~12K synthetic trajectories can close the gap to much larger models. On the out-of-distribution Hindi-Chinese MQM benchmark, ThinMQM-32B (Seg. τ = 57.4) outperforms xCOMET-XXL (47.8) by nearly 10 points, confirming generalization to low-resource settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM-as-a-judge paradigm for MT evaluation (established by GEMBA-MQM and the broader MQM scoring framework) to Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) such as DeepSeek-R1, QwQ, and R1-Distill variants. While prior work applied prompting-based LLMs to MQM error span annotation and scoring, this is the first systematic analysis of how LRM-specific 'thinking' processes interact with MT evaluation, revealing scale-dependent material preferences, overthinking inefficiencies, and persistent overestimation biases. The proposed ThinMQM method departs from prior prompting-only approaches by fine-tuning LRMs on synthetic, human-like thinking trajectories that explicitly encode the two-phase MQM annotation-and-scoring pipeline, rather than relying solely on in-context demonstrations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that LRMs underperform their base LLMs in nearly half of MT evaluation settings challenges the assumption that extended reasoning automatically improves evaluative tasks, and the ~35x thinking-budget reduction with concurrent performance gains offers a practical path to efficient LRM-based evaluation. Open questions include whether the calibration approach generalizes beyond the three WMT24 language pairs and the MQM framework, whether more nuanced error-type identification (particularly minor accuracy/mistranslation errors) can be further improved, and whether the scale-dependent material preferences (source vs. reference) hold as LRM architectures evolve. The work also highlights that auxiliary scoring models do not resolve fundamental overestimation biases, pointing toward the need for more human-aligned scoring processes.
