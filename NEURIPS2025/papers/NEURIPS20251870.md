## Problem

Current fMRI-to-image decoding methods treat brain signals as monolithic inputs, lacking architectures that reflect the hierarchical and functionally specialized nature of the visual cortex. This design gap leads to two critical bottlenecks: poor cross-subject generalization (requiring full retraining per individual) and limited mechanistic interpretability (making it impossible to trace how specific brain regions contribute to the reconstruction).
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251870/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes z-score-normalized fMRI beta weights (1.8mm volume space, masked by the NSD 'nsdgeneral' ROI) as input and produces reconstructed natural scene images via a fine-tuned SDXL diffusion model. Intermediate outputs include multi-level CLIP-aligned image and text embeddings from the hierarchical MoE encoder, as well as routing weights that reveal which brain-region-specific experts and hierarchical levels drive each stage of generation. The text conditioning specifically predicts the CLIP end-of-sequence embedding, which is expanded to fill the full 77-token text conditioning tensor.

## Method

MoRE-Brain employs a 4-level hierarchical Mixture-of-Experts (2→4→8→16 experts) where learnable routers assign fMRI voxels to specialized expert MLPs, mimicking cortical functional specialization. Training proceeds in two stages: first, the MoE encoder is trained to map fMRI to frozen CLIP image/text embedding space using MSE, contrastive (InfoNCE with MixCo), and load-balancing losses; second, the frozen MoE encoder feeds a fine-tuned SDXL U-Net (via LoRA rank-16) through a novel dual-routing mechanism. A Time Router uses attention over learnable level embeddings to select which MoE hierarchy level conditions each diffusion timestep (regularized toward a coarse-to-fine Gaussian schedule), while a Space Router applies cross-attention between the noisy latent and the selected level's expert embeddings to produce spatially modulated conditioning. For cross-subject generalization, expert weights are frozen and only the subject-specific voxel-to-expert routers are fine-tuned, reducing trainable parameters to ~45% of the total.

## Results

On the NSD dataset (8 subjects, 1000 shared test images), MoRE-Brain achieves reconstruction quality competitive with state-of-the-art methods (MindEye2, MindBridge) across SSIM, DreamSim, InceptionV3, and CLIP-Cos metrics. The bottleneck analysis reveals that MoRE-Brain's performance degrades most sharply as the fMRI information bottleneck narrows, indicating strong reliance on genuine neural signals rather than generative priors. For cross-subject generalization, MoRE-Brain requires fine-tuning only 44.84% of its 293.4M parameters (routers only), compared to 100% of 729.3M for MindEye2 and 98.48% of 552.9M for MindBridge. Notably, MindEye2 exhibits a random-baseline CLIP-Cos of ~0.8 that remains nearly constant across all bottleneck sizes, suggesting its high CLIP scores are driven by semantic priors rather than fMRI content.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against MindEye2 [Scotti et al., 2024], MoRE-Brain matches or approaches comparable reconstruction fidelity on SSIM, DreamSim, and InceptionV3, but the bottleneck analysis tells a different story: MoRE-Brain's metrics drop substantially when fMRI information is restricted, while MindEye2's CLIP-Cos stays near 0.8 even with random fMRI input, indicating MindEye2 leans heavily on CLIP's semantic prior rather than decoding actual brain signals. Against MindBridge [Wang et al., CVPR 2024], MoRE-Brain is competitive on overall reconstruction quality; MindBridge shows somewhat less degradation at narrow bottlenecks on SSIM and InceptionV3 (again suggesting greater reliance on learned priors), but MoRE-Brain's cross-subject adaptation is far more parameter-efficient—only 44.84% of parameters (the routers) need fine-tuning versus MindBridge's 98.48% (its 'reset-tuning' strategy). In the ablation (Table 2), the full MoRE-Brain configuration (T/I with both Time and Space routers) achieves SSIM 0.415, Alex(2) 0.792, InceptionV3 0.962, and DreamSim 0.507, confirming that both dual conditioning streams and dynamic routing contribute meaningfully over simpler variants.

## Relation to Prior Work

MoRE-Brain extends the fMRI-to-image reconstruction pipeline established by MindEye2 and MindBridge by replacing the monolithic fMRI-to-CLIP encoder with a hierarchical Mixture-of-Experts architecture that mirrors the brain's functional specialization and cortical hierarchy. It adapts IP-Adapter's image conditioning mechanism and MindEye2's contrastive training losses, but introduces a novel dual-stage (Time + Space) routing mechanism that dynamically selects and modulates expert embeddings across diffusion timesteps. The key conceptual departure is the explicit separation of shared expert networks from subject-specific routers, grounded in the neuroscience finding that inter-individual variability arises primarily from spatial topography differences rather than computational differences.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses two persistent gaps in fMRI decoding: limited cross-subject generalization and poor mechanistic interpretability. The bottleneck analysis provides useful evidence that MoRE-Brain genuinely leverages fMRI signal richness rather than leaning on generative priors, while the interpretability analyses (hierarchical expert specialization, semantic category preferences, ICA-based brain region identification) offer a template for making decoding models neuroscientifically informative. However, the work is validated only on the NSD dataset with a single decoding task, and the 'neuro-inspired' framing is partly post-hoc—the architecture is designed to be interpretable, but whether the learned specializations truly reflect biological processing remains an open question. Future work could test whether the shared-expert/subject-specific-router decomposition generalizes beyond visual decoding to other brain signal modalities or tasks.
