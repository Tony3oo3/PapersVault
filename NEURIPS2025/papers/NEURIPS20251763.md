## Problem

Diffusion LLMs theoretically permit parallel token generation, but in practice their quality degrades sharply when multiple tokens are sampled simultaneously because the model only provides marginal (independent) token distributions that ignore inter-token dependencies. Current open-source dLLMs like Dream and Llada cannot match the speed-quality tradeoff of autoregressive models, as achieving competitive accuracy requires sequential one-token-at-a-time decoding. The paper addresses how to dynamically adapt the number of parallel tokens to exploit dLLM parallelism while preserving generation fidelity.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt and produces a generated text sequence, operating on a shared tokenizer between the diffusion model (Dream 7B) and the auxiliary autoregressive verifier (Qwen2.5 0.5B). The diffusion model's input contains a contiguous block of [MASK] tokens as a suffix, capped at length M to limit quadratic attention cost. Tokens are emitted in variable-length groups whose size is determined adaptively at each iteration, with the total generation length bounded by the diffusion model's context window (256 or 512 tokens).

## Method

APD restructures the dLLM into a left-to-right autoregressive process and defines a target distribution as a multiplicative mixture (product of experts) between the dLLM's marginal token probabilities and a small autoregressive model's joint sequence probabilities, weighted by a tunable parameter R. Using the Gumbel-Softmax trick as a universal coupler with shared randomness, the algorithm samples from both the diffusion model and the target distribution, then accepts the longest prefix of tokens where the two samples agree—effectively inverting speculative decoding by using a small model to verify a large model's parallel proposals. Two additional architectural optimizations further boost throughput: KV caching with a sliding recompute window W for the bidirectional diffusion model, and capping the masked lookahead to M tokens to reduce attention computation. Together, R, W, and M provide three independent knobs to trade off speed against quality.

## Results

On GSM8K, Dream 7B with left-to-right decoding (the best baseline dLLM configuration) achieves 83.2% accuracy at 10.1 tokens/sec, while autoregressive Qwen2.5 7B achieves 85.4% at 38.6 tokens/sec. With APD (R=0.7, W=16, M=100), Dream 7B generates an average of 7.62 tokens in parallel per iteration on GSM8K, and the Pareto frontier analysis (Figure 5) shows that Dream 7B + APD surpasses the throughput of both Qwen2.5 7B and Qwen2.5 0.5B while maintaining near-equivalent accuracy. At a more conservative setting (R=0.6, W=32, M=200), APD still averages 5.75 parallel tokens on GSM8K with minimal quality loss, and throughput exceeding 100 tokens/sec is achievable at the cost of some accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Dream 7B with standard decoding (left-to-right, 256 steps: 83.2% GSM8K at 10.1 tokens/sec), APD achieves comparable accuracy while substantially exceeding the throughput of autoregressive Qwen2.5 7B (38.6 tokens/sec), as shown in the Pareto frontier (Figure 5). Against Llada 8B (best config: 71.2% GSM8K at 9.33 tokens/sec), Dream 7B + APD dominates in both quality and speed. Against Qwen2.5 7B autoregressive (85.4% GSM8K at 38.6 tokens/sec), APD on Dream 7B matches or slightly trails in accuracy (~83%) but surpasses in throughput, and the paper argues that even speculative decoding of Qwen 7B with a Qwen 0.5B draft model cannot exceed APD's throughput. The key advantage is that APD is Pareto-optimal: no other model configuration simultaneously achieves higher speed and higher quality. On GPQA and MATH, APD maintains 4.31–4.35 and 6.33–6.45 average parallel tokens respectively, confirming the speedup generalizes beyond math word problems.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on discrete diffusion language models (Dream 7B, Llada) as the base architecture and repurposes the product-of-experts framework to define a target distribution that blends diffusion marginals with autoregressive joint probabilities. Its most notable conceptual departure is inverting speculative decoding: rather than a small draft model proposing tokens for a large verifier, a large dLLM proposes tokens in parallel while a small autoregressive model verifies them, a setup the authors argue is fundamentally distinct because the dLLM's sequential evaluation is expensive while the small AR model's parallel evaluation is cheap. The acceptance criterion draws on universal coupling via the Gumbel-Softmax trick, and the method further adapts KV caching and limited masked lookahead—techniques from autoregressive inference—to the bidirectional attention architecture of diffusion models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

APD provides the first practical demonstration that dLLMs can match or exceed autoregressive model throughput (surpassing Qwen 7B and even Qwen 0.5B in speed) while maintaining competitive quality, with three tunable parameters giving practitioners a flexible speed-quality dial. Key limitations include the requirement for a shared tokenizer between the dLLM and the small AR verifier, evaluation on a single model pair (Dream 7B + Qwen 0.5B), and the acknowledged tradeoff that higher throughput always costs some quality. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to other dLLM architectures (e.g., Llada with its non-standard tokenizer), whether the small AR verifier can be replaced by a distilled or quantized version of the dLLM itself, and how the method behaves on longer-generation or multi-turn tasks where the masked-lookahead constraint may be more restrictive.
