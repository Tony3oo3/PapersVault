## Problem

Self-training-based UDA methods for semantic segmentation generate pseudo-labels to guide target-domain learning but overlook explicit modeling of domain-shared feature extraction. The paper identifies that domain-specific attributes (e.g., texture) and domain-invariant attributes (e.g., shape) are entangled in raw images, and proposes that progressive image degradation can serve as a principled mechanism to gradually eliminate domain-specific cues and expose shared representations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250994/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

During training, the method consumes RGB images from both labeled source and unlabeled target domains, along with their degraded counterparts generated at randomly sampled diffusion time steps t ∈ {1,…,T} via the forward process x_t = √(ᾱ_t)·x_0 + √(1−ᾱ_t)·ε. The network produces pixel-wise segmentation masks (19 classes for Cityscapes/ACDC) at the original resolution. At inference, only the standard backbone f_θ = h∘g is used on unprocessed target images; the diffusion encoder g′ and reconstruction head h′ are entirely removed, incurring zero additional latency.

## Method

DiDA formalizes a sequence of image degradation levels as a diffusion forward process, creating continuous intermediate domains whose probability distributions progressively overlap between source and target. A dedicated diffusion encoder g′, conditioned on the diffusion time t via sinusoidal position embeddings and per-block shift/bias modulation (Eq. 8), disentangles time-specific semantic shift and compensates for lost discriminative features through residual addition with the main encoder g. Training combines four losses: standard supervised loss on source, pseudo-label adaptation loss on target, a Degraded Image Consistency (DIC) loss enforcing prediction consistency between clean and degraded inputs, and a reconstruction loss supervising the diffusion encoder to recover the original image. The framework is plug-and-play—compatible with any degradation operation (Gaussian noise, blur, mask) and any self-training UDA baseline—achieving 0.8–3.7 mIoU gains across DAFormer, HRDA, and MIC on GTA→Cityscapes, SYNTHIA→Cityscapes, and Cityscapes→ACDC.

## Results

DiDA is evaluated as a plug-and-play module on three UDA baselines (DAFormer, HRDA, MIC) across four domain adaptation benchmarks (GTA→Cityscapes, SYNTHIA→Cityscapes, Cityscapes→ACDC) with both CNN (DeepLabV2+ResNet-101) and Transformer (MiT-B5) backbones. The headline metric is mIoU, and DiDA consistently improves all baselines by 0.8–3.7 mIoU, with the largest gains on the weakest baselines (e.g., DAFormer +2.0 to +3.7) and the smallest on the strongest (MIC +0.8 to +2.3). The best absolute result is 76.8 mIoU on GTA→Cityscapes (Transformer) with MIC+DiDA, and 72.1 mIoU on Cityscapes→ACDC with MIC+DiDA.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a plug-in, DiDA uniformly improves all three primary baselines: DAFormer gains +2.0 to +3.7 mIoU, HRDA gains +1.3 to +2.7, and MIC gains +0.8 to +2.3 across all benchmarks and architectures. Against other plug-in methods on the same baselines, DiDA outperforms FST (69.3 vs. 70.3 on DAFormer, GTA→CS), DiGA (74.3 vs. 75.4 on HRDA), and DTS (69.5 vs. 76.8 on MIC), with the largest margin over DTS. In the broader per-class comparison (Table 3), DiDA+MIC (76.8 mIoU) surpasses CoPT (76.1), ADFormer (69.2), and all CNN-based methods (≤65.0) on GTA→Cityscapes. On SYNTHIA→Cityscapes (Table 4), DiDA+MIC (68.6) again leads all Transformer-based methods including CoPT (67.4) and HRDA (65.8). The only notable exception is that DTS actually degrades MIC from 75.5 to 69.5 on GTA→CS, making DiDA's +1.3 gain over the original MIC particularly meaningful in that setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

DiDA builds on the self-training UDA paradigm established by DAFormer and its successors (HRDA, MIC), which use teacher-student pseudo-labeling to adapt segmentation networks. Its core conceptual departure is reframing the diffusion forward process (from DDPM) not as a generative mechanism but as a structured degradation schedule that constructs intermediate domains, drawing on the theoretical insight that fine-grained domain-specific attributes are lost before coarse-grained domain-invariant ones. This connects to earlier intermediate-domain ideas in domain adaptation (e.g., deliberated domain bridging, gradually vanishing bridge) but formalizes them through a principled noise schedule and adds a dedicated semantic shift compensation module (diffusion encoder with time embedding) that prior degradation-based or augmentation-based consistency methods lack. The extension to arbitrary degradation operations (blur, mask) follows the cold diffusion framework, positioning DiDA as a general plug-in rather than a fixed pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The plug-and-play design and consistent gains across three UDA baselines, two architectures (CNN and Transformer), and three benchmarks make DiDA a practical addition to the UDA segmentation toolkit, particularly for texture-heavy classes (road, sidewalk, fence, terrain) where domain-specific cues dominate. However, the improvements are modest (0.8–3.7% mIoU) and diminish as baselines approach saturation, and the evaluation is confined to driving-scene segmentation, leaving open whether the degradation-as-bridging principle transfers to other domains (medical, remote sensing, etc.). The theoretical grounding via the attribute-loss proposition is suggestive but not a tight guarantee, and the reliance on a specific noise schedule (sigmoid, T=100) and reconstruction loss weighting introduces hyperparameters that may not transfer cleanly. A key open question is whether the semantic shift compensation module generalizes to degradation types with non-Gaussian, non-additive corruption (e.g., sensor-specific artifacts).
