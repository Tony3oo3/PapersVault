## Problem

Conventional wisdom attributes the generalization of overparameterized neural networks to gradient descent's implicit bias, but the volume hypothesis (via Guess & Check) claims any optimizer that fits training data suffices. The literature presents conflicting empirical evidence on whether G&C matches or falls short of gradient descent, with no rigorous theoretical characterization of how this relationship depends on network width versus depth. This paper provides the first canonical theoretical case where G&C is provably inferior to gradient descent, and a contrasting case where it is provably superior.

## Input / Output

The method operates on the low-rank matrix sensing problem: given n measurement matrices A_i ∈ R^{m×m'} and scalar measurements y_i = ⟨A_i, W*⟩ for a rank-r ground truth matrix W*, the goal is to recover W* via an overparameterized factorization W = W_d σ(W_{d-1} ... σ(W_1)...) with width k ≥ min{m,m'} and depth d ≥ 2. Generalization is measured on test instances orthogonal to the span of training measurements, isolating performance on truly unseen data. The factorization admits both linear and non-linear (anti-symmetric) activations, and the prior over weight settings uses Kaiming scaling (and optionally depth-normalization) from a regular distribution such as Gaussian or uniform.

## Method

The paper proves two contrasting theorems for matrix factorization. Theorem 1 shows that with any admissible anti-symmetric activation (linear, tanh, sine), as width k → ∞, the posterior probability of good generalization under G&C converges to the prior probability (i.e., no better than chance), by leveraging Gaussian process convergence results to show factorized matrix columns become mutually independent Gaussian vectors, decoupling training and generalization events. Theorem 2 shows that with linear activation, a normalized Gaussian prior, and RIP measurement matrices, as depth d increases, G&C generalization loss converges to O(ε_train · c), i.e., near-perfect, by applying Lyapunov exponent concentration bounds to show that deep products of Gaussian matrices acquire an approximate rank-one structure that aligns with the rank-one ground truth. Empirically, the paper validates both theorems and extends them to Leaky ReLU activation, confirming that G&C deteriorates with width but improves with depth, while gradient descent maintains good generalization throughout.

## Results

The paper's main results are theoretical. Theorem 1 proves that for anti-symmetric activations (linear, tanh, sine) and any regular prior distribution, as the width k of a matrix factorization tends to infinity, the posterior probability of good generalization under G&C converges to the prior probability (i.e., G&C becomes no better than chance), with a rate of O(1/√k) for Gaussian priors. Theorem 2 proves the opposite for depth: with linear activation, a normalized Gaussian prior, RIP measurements, and a rank-1 ground truth matrix, the probability of bad generalization given good training loss is O(1/d), meaning G&C generalization approaches perfect as depth increases. Empirically (Figures 1–2), G&C generalization loss deteriorates with width toward the chance baseline while gradient descent maintains low loss, and G&C generalization loss improves with depth toward gradient descent's level.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three optimizers: gradient descent (GD), Guess & Check (G&C), and a single draw from the prior (chance baseline). Against Chiang et al. [22], who empirically argued that G&C generalization is on par with GD and improves with width, this paper provides the first rigorous theoretical proof that the volume hypothesis fails in the wide regime: G&C generalization provably deteriorates to chance as width grows, while GD (per Soltanolkotabi et al. [106], restated as Proposition 1) achieves generalization loss O(ν^{3/10} k^{-3/20}) with probability 1−O(e^{−c₁k}). Against Soltanolkotabi et al. [106], the paper does not compete with GD but rather uses its guarantee as the benchmark to establish that GD is provably necessary in the wide regime. In the deep regime, the paper shows G&C (with normalization) outperforms the chance baseline and approaches GD's generalization, a result not addressed by either prior work. Empirically, GD consistently achieves the lowest generalization loss across all widths and depths, G&C matches GD at small widths but degrades toward chance at large widths, and G&C improves with depth while GD remains stable.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper provides the first rigorous theoretical analysis of the volume hypothesis introduced by Chiang et al. (2023), which posits that Guess & Check (randomly sampling weight settings until one fits the training data) can match gradient descent's generalization. The analysis builds on Hanin's (2023) Gaussian process convergence results for infinite-width networks (used in Theorem 1) and Hanin & Paouris's (2021) non-asymptotic Lyapunov exponent bounds for products of Gaussian matrices (used in Theorem 2). It contrasts with the purely empirical studies of Chiang et al. and Peleg & Hein (2024), which reached conflicting conclusions about how G&C generalization scales with width and depth, by working in the controlled setting of matrix factorization where confounding factors are eliminated.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper resolves a key open question in the generalization literature by proving, for the first time, a canonical case where the volume hypothesis fails (wide networks with anti-symmetric activations) and a canonical case where it holds perfectly (deep networks with linear activation and normalization). The stark width-depth contrast suggests that the answer to 'do neural networks need gradient descent?' is not a simple yes or no but depends on the interplay of architectural dimensions. Key limitations include the restriction to matrix factorization (not real architectures), the anti-symmetry requirement for the width result, and the rank-1/RIP assumptions for the depth result; extending these results to tensor factorization and real-world settings is identified as an important next step.
