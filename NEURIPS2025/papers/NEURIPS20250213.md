## Problem

Existing RAG schemes for long-video understanding employ a single fixed retrieval paradigm regardless of query difficulty, incurring unnecessary computational overhead (e.g., graph traversal) for simple frame-level queries while simultaneously suffering from insufficient retrieval granularity for multi-hop reasoning questions. This one-size-fits-all approach creates a fundamental tension between resource efficiency and cognitive depth that constrains the practical deployment of video RAG systems.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250213/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system takes a long video (up to 106 minutes) and a natural-language text query as input. The video is segmented into 30-second clips with 5 uniformly sampled frames per clip, from which three text streams (MiniCPM-V captions, FastWhisper ASR, EasyOCR text) and ImageBind visual embeddings are pre-extracted into an omni-knowledge index. The output is a free-form text answer generated by a plug-and-play MLLM (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL, VideoLLaMA3) conditioned on adaptively retrieved evidence, requiring no fine-tuning of the base model.

## Method

AdaVideoRAG introduces a lightweight intent classifier (Qwen2.5-7B with CoT prompting) that routes each query to one of three retrieval tiers: Level-1 (no retrieval, direct MLLM inference), Level-2 (naive multimodal retrieval with modality-specific query rewriting over caption/ASR/OCR databases plus ImageBind cross-modal visual grounding), and Level-3 (graph retrieval via Light-RAG over a BGE-M3-constructed knowledge graph of entities and relationships). The Omni-Knowledge Indexing module builds three complementary databases—text, visual, and graph—from the same video, enabling hierarchical knowledge access. Retrieved evidence undergoes coarse-to-fine purification (deduplication, LLM-based relevance filtering, temporal reranking) before being concatenated with the query and fed to the MLLM for final generation. The entire framework operates as a plug-and-play API layer over existing MLLMs, with the intent classifier contributing ≤5% of total inference time.

## Results

On MLVU_test, AdaVideoRAG delivers substantial gains across all tested MLLMs: Qwen2.5-VL-7B improves from 29.0 to 40.5 M-Avg (+39.8%), Video-LLaVA from 29.4 to 37.9 (+28.9%), and VideoLLaMA3 from 47.7 to 53.2 (+11.6%), with the latter approaching GPT-4o's 54.9. On Video-MME, AdaVideoRAG achieves 59.9 overall (Qwen2.5-VL-7B) and 68.5 (VideoLLaMA3), outperforming VideoRAG [32] by +4.9 and +1.2 points respectively, with the largest advantage on long videos (47.7 vs 43.3 for Qwen2.5-VL). On the HiVU benchmark, AdaVideoRAG achieves a 77.13% overall win rate at Level-3 (hard reasoning) versus 22.87% for the unassisted model, and outperforms VideoRAG at Level-3 (57.77% vs 42.23% overall winner).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GPT-4o (54.9 M-Avg on MLVU, 71.9 on Video-MME), AdaVideoRAG-enhanced VideoLLaMA3 reaches 53.2 and 68.5 respectively—narrowing the gap to within 1.7 and 3.4 points without any proprietary model access. Compared to VideoRAG [32] on Video-MME, AdaVideoRAG wins on both base models: Qwen2.5-VL-7B scores 59.9 vs 55.0 (+4.9), and VideoLLaMA3 scores 68.5 vs 67.3 (+1.2), with the advantage concentrated on long videos (47.7 vs 43.3). On the HiVU benchmark, AdaVideoRAG and VideoRAG are roughly comparable at Level-1 and Level-2 (e.g., 53.83% vs 46.17% overall winner at L2), but AdaVideoRAG clearly dominates at Level-3 hard reasoning (57.77% vs 42.23% overall winner), validating the benefit of graph-based retrieval for multi-hop queries. The adaptive routing also proves essential: forcing all queries through a single retrieval path drops Video-MME scores to 64.2–67.5, versus 68.5 with adaptive classification. Ablations confirm each retrieval component contributes: removing text retrieval drops overall win rate from 68.75% to 31.25%, while removing graph retrieval reduces it to 54.18%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the video RAG lineage initiated by VideoRAG (Luo et al., 2024), which uses naive retrieval over visually-aligned text features (OCR, ASR, captions), and the graph-based VideoRAG (Ren et al., 2025), which constructs hierarchical knowledge graphs for multi-hop reasoning. The key conceptual departure is the adaptive routing mechanism: rather than committing to a single retrieval paradigm, AdaVideoRAG classifies query difficulty via a lightweight LLM and dispatches to none, naive, or graph retrieval accordingly—mirroring the text-domain Adaptive-RAG (Jeong et al., 2024) but instantiated over a multimodal video knowledge base (text + visual embeddings via ImageBind + knowledge graph via Light-RAG). The Omni-Knowledge Indexing module unifies MiniCPM-V captions, FastWhisper ASR, EasyOCR text, and ImageBind visual features into a shared retrieval infrastructure, building on cross-modal alignment work but applying it specifically to long-video indexing.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The HiVU benchmark (120 videos, 1–106 min, three difficulty tiers) fills a genuine gap: prior long-video benchmarks (MLVU, Video-MME) lack a principled difficulty stratification that isolates frame-level perception from temporal reasoning from cross-modal causal inference. The adaptive routing offers a practical efficiency-accuracy trade-off (8 s for L1 vs. 27 s for L3) that makes RAG-based video QA deployable in latency-sensitive settings. However, the evaluation relies on LLM-as-judge win-rates rather than deterministic metrics, the three-level taxonomy may be too coarse for real-world query distributions, and the graph construction overhead (~412 s per video) limits scalability to very large video corpora. Open questions include whether finer-grained difficulty levels or learned (rather than prompted) classifiers yield further gains, and whether the framework extends to streaming or interactive video understanding.
