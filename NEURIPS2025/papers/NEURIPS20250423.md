## Problem

Standard monocular depth estimation benchmarks (KITTI, NYU-D, ETH3D, DIODE) evaluate accuracy but not robustness to changes in 3D scene content. Existing robustness evaluations such as RoboDepth address only 2D image corruptions (noise, blur, color shifts), leaving unexplored how depth models respond to camera, object, material, and lighting perturbations that are prevalent in downstream applications like robotics and view synthesis.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250423/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark provides 1280×720 RGB images of procedurally generated indoor and outdoor scenes (from Infinigen Nature and Indoors) containing one of five object categories (chairs, desks, cabinets, fish, cacti), paired with 12 perturbation types at up to 60 parameter settings each, yielding 13,684 unique scene variations. Models produce per-pixel depth maps that are aligned to ground truth via least-squares scale-and-shift fitting on the object mask, and the evaluation outputs three robustness metrics per perturbation: average error (μ), accuracy instability (σ, the variance of error across perturbations), and self-consistency (κ, the average squared difference from the base-scene prediction). Self-consistency is only computed for perturbations that preserve ground-truth geometry, with special SE(2)-aware handling for camera roll and depth-scaling handling for object resizing.

## Method

PDE (Procedural Depth Evaluation) builds on Infinigen's procedural generation system to create paired scenes where exactly one aspect is perturbed while all others remain constant, enabling controlled robustness measurement. The 12 perturbation types span camera intrinsics (dolly zoom), camera pose (roll, pan/tilt), materials (object and scene swap), lighting (source, strength, temperature, type), 3D geometry (rotation, translation, resizing, occlusion via cage-shaped objects, non-rigid deformation such as opening cabinet doors or twisting shapes), and out-of-distribution background swaps. A key design decision is distinguishing perturbations that preserve ground-truth geometry (suitable for self-consistency) from those that alter it (where only accuracy stability is meaningful), with special handling for camera roll (SE(2) transform on the depth image) and object resizing (depth scaling with fixed 2D projection). The benchmark evaluates 11 state-of-the-art models (MiDaS, DepthAnything V1/V2, ZoeDepth, UniDepthV2, Metric3D V2, Marigold, DepthPro, MoGe, GeoWizard, VGGT) using AbsRel, δ₀.₁₂₅, RMSE, and relative depth ordering metrics, with alignment computed on the eroded object mask. The procedural nature of Infinigen enables non-rigid, semantically meaningful deformations that would be impractical to achieve with real-world data, and guarantees that test scenes are novel and not used in any evaluated model's training.

## Results

On the PDE benchmark (object-level AbsRel), DepthPro achieves the lowest average error (1.24) across all 12 perturbation types, followed by UniDepthV2 (1.40) and MoGe (1.45). However, UniDepthV2 achieves the best accuracy stability (0.27) and self-consistency (1.40), while DepthPro's instability (0.30) is notably higher. Camera Dolly Zoom is the most challenging perturbation (average error 1.97, instability 0.56), while Lighting is the easiest (average error 1.74, instability 0.19). No single model simultaneously achieves the best error and the best stability, revealing a trade-off between accuracy and robustness.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DepthPro achieves the lowest average AbsRel error (1.24) on every perturbation type, outperforming UniDepthV2 (1.40) by ~13% and MoGe (1.45) by ~15%, but its accuracy instability (0.30) is worse than UniDepthV2 (0.27) and Metric3DV2 (0.29), indicating it is less robust to scene changes. UniDepthV2 and Metric3DV2 lead in stability, with UniDepthV2 also achieving the best self-consistency (1.40) among scale-invariant models, tied with MoGe. DepthAnythingV2 (DAv2) shows strong robustness to material swaps (instability 0.24–0.31), attributed to its large-scale pseudo-labeled training data, but lags in absolute error (1.70). Older or smaller models like MiDaS (2.26 avg error, 0.40 instability) and Marigold (2.18, 0.40) are consistently the least robust. ZoeDepth performs worst overall (2.43 avg error, 0.41 instability, 2.86 self-consistency), while VGGT (1.82, 0.33) and GeoWizard (2.08, 0.38) fall in the middle. The key finding is that no model simultaneously achieves the best error and best stability, and camera perturbations (dolly zoom, roll) are the most challenging for all models despite being geometrically simple.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Infinigen procedural generation system (Nature and Indoors) to construct controlled 3D scene perturbations, extending it with new perturbation code for camera, object, material, and lighting modifications. It departs from prior MDE robustness work (RoboDepth), which evaluated only 2D image corruptions like sensor noise and blur, by targeting 3D scene content changes that are more relevant to downstream applications like robotics and view synthesis. Conceptually, it draws an analogy to adversarial robustness benchmarks in NLP (e.g., Adversarial GLUE) but applies the controlled-perturbation evaluation paradigm to monocular depth estimation for the first time.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic benchmark for evaluating MDE robustness to 3D scene perturbations, filling a clear gap left by accuracy-only benchmarks (KITTI, NYU-D, DIODE). The finding that camera intrinsics/pose changes are harder than 3D object pose changes challenges the implicit assumption that geometric reasoning is the primary bottleneck, and the observation that no model simultaneously achieves best accuracy and best stability highlights an unresolved trade-off. Limitations include reliance on synthetic data (domain gap), only five object categories, and the acknowledgment that results may not directly transfer to real-world deployment. Open questions include whether the camera-robustness gap can be closed through training data augmentation or architectural changes, and whether the occlusion and material-swap failures reflect fundamental representational limitations.
