## Problem

Existing backdoor attacks on autonomous driving systems rely on pixel-level patch triggers stamped on camera images, which are impractical to deploy in the physical world due to viewpoint sensitivity, lighting dependence, and the inability to directly modify ego-vehicle sensor input. This paper addresses the gap by introducing trajectory-based backdoor attacks where one or more attacker-controlled vehicles drive along specific spatiotemporal patterns to activate a dormant backdoor in end-to-end AD agents, establishing the first demonstration of such attacks' feasibility.

## Input / Output

The RL driving agent consumes a 259-dimensional state vector composed of ego vehicle kinematics (steering, heading, velocity), navigation commands (relative distance and direction to the next route checkpoint), and 72-beam LiDAR range readings at 50 m radius — all simulator-provided features rather than raw sensor data. The agent outputs normalized steering and throttle commands in [-1, 1]². The trigger is the coordinated trajectories of one or more attacker vehicles (e.g., two cars synchronously bypassing the ego car), and the target action is a sudden left turn or sudden brake, both of which are injected into the training data by modifying the ego car's action and reward at trigger-observation timesteps.

## Method

The attack operates in two phases. In trigger generation, configurable behavior models (extending the Treiber car-following model with overtaking and braking rules) produce natural multi-vehicle trajectories, which are quantitatively scored against signal temporal logic specifications (Reach, Avoid, Stay predicates over spatial regions and time windows) using DiffSpec; configurations (initial positions, speeds) are iteratively perturbed until all vehicles' TL scores are positive. In the training phase, qualified trigger trajectories are injected into the offline RL dataset with the ego car's action replaced by the target action and reward manipulated, while a negative training strategy adds 'patch trajectories' — similar but non-trigger trajectories with TL scores below a threshold λ — to suppress false activation and improve stealthiness. The poisoned dataset is then used to train offline RL agents (BC, BCQ, CoptiDICE) without the attacker controlling the training algorithm, simulating a malicious third-party data annotation scenario.

## Results

On MetaDrive, the trajectory-based backdoor attack achieves near-complete mission failure (MVR = 1.00) for the Coptidice agent under the sync-bypass trigger across all three difficulty levels, with poisoned reward collapsing from 388.06 (easy) to 8.23 and ADE surging from 0.31 to 107.14. The attack generalizes across RL algorithms: BCQ drops from 391.32 to 132.49 reward (easy) and BC from 210.52 to 49.73, while poisoned MVR reaches 0.69–1.00. Against defenses, trajectory smoothing partially mitigates the attack for the braking target (poisoned MVR drops to 0.21–0.31) but fails for the turn-left target (MVR remains 1.00), and DP-SGD provides no meaningful protection (MVR stays at 1.00 across all settings).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The attack is effective against all three offline RL algorithms tested: Coptidice (ICLR 2022) shows the most dramatic degradation (reward 388→8, MVR 0→1.00 on easy), BCQ (ICML 2019) is moderately affected (reward 391→132, MVR 0→0.69), and BC (NeurIPS 1996) is least susceptible (reward 211→50, MVR 0.21→0.90), likely due to its inherently lower baseline performance. Against the trajectory smoothing defense (Zhang et al., CVPR 2022), the attack is partially mitigated for the braking target (poisoned MVR drops from 1.00 to 0.21–0.31) but remains fully effective for the turn-left target (MVR stays 1.00). DP-SGD (Hong et al., 2020) provides no meaningful protection, with poisoned MVR remaining at 1.00 across all task difficulties and target actions, as the poisoning does not introduce abnormal gradient norms. The safe RL algorithm CDT (Liu et al., ICML 2023) offers partial resistance (poisoned MVR 0.65–0.73 vs. 1.00 for Coptidice), attributed to its stochastic policy enabling action exploration, though at the cost of lower clean reward. BadRL (Cui et al., AAAI 2024) can reduce the required poisoning rate from 10% to 8% while maintaining comparable attack effectiveness (MVR 0.69 vs. 0.71), confirming the attack's robustness to sparse poisoning strategies.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant line of backdoor attacks in autonomous driving that rely on static pixel-level patches stamped on camera inputs, arguing these are impractical in the physical world. It also extends prior work on adversarial trajectories (e.g., [56], [64]) that target trajectory prediction modules or use TL to specify ego-car safety violations, by instead using TL to define and verify coordinated multi-vehicle trigger trajectories that poison the end-to-end driving policy itself. The trajectory generation builds on the Intelligent Driver Model (IDM) behavior model [69], augmented with overtaking and braking rules, and leverages DiffSpec [80] for quantitative STL evaluation. The negative training strategy, which uses near-miss trajectories to suppress false activations, is a novel component not present in prior RL backdoor work that typically uses only positive trigger samples.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work reveals a previously unexplored attack surface for end-to-end AD systems: coordinated multi-vehicle trajectories that are physically deployable and statistically rare in normal traffic (0.065%–0.130% frequency in NGSIM). The finding that standard data-poisoning defenses (DP-SGD, trajectory smoothing) are largely ineffective against this attack vector is practically important, as it suggests the AD community needs new defense mechanisms tailored to dynamic, multi-agent triggers. Key limitations include evaluation solely in MetaDrive with simplified LiDAR-vector states (not raw sensor data), a single atomic TL proposition, and no physical-world deployment. Open questions include extending the attack to module-based full-stack AD systems, richer TL specifications capturing inter-vehicle interactions, and whether the vulnerability persists with more realistic sensor inputs.
