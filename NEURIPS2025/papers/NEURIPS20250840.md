## Problem

Existing combinatorial semi-bandit algorithms (CUCB, SDCB, covariance-adaptive variants) require querying a combinatorial optimization oracle at every round, incurring Θ(T) oracle calls over a horizon T. Since the underlying optimization is generally NP-hard, this creates a severe computational bottleneck. The paper asks whether oracle usage can be reduced from linear to (doubly) logarithmic in T while preserving tight, gap-free regret guarantees.

## Input / Output

The agent operates over a combinatorial action space A ⊆ {0,1}^d with at most m active arms per action, and receives semi-bandit feedback (individual reward values y_{t,i} for each activated arm i). The oracle is invoked only at specific epochs to solve argmax_{a∈A} f(a) for a UCB or LCB objective; between oracle calls the agent simply repeats the previously selected action. The output is a sequence of actions with a regret guarantee and an explicit oracle query schedule whose total number of calls is O(d log log T) (scheduled) or O(d log log(Tm/d)) (adaptive), rather than Θ(T).

## Method

The paper introduces two oracle-efficient frameworks. The adaptive framework (AROQ-CMAB) tracks per-arm epoch counters and triggers an oracle query only when a base arm's selection count in the current epoch exceeds a threshold of 1 + √(Tm·|T_i(τ_i−1)|)/d, yielding O(d log log(Tm/d)) total oracle calls and Õ(√(mdT)) regret. The scheduled framework (SROQ-CMAB) pre-defines a geometric time grid {t_τ} with M = Θ(log log T) epochs and, at each epoch, constructs representative actions a_τ^(i) for each surviving base arm i via oracle queries, then eliminates arms whose UCB falls below the maximum LCB, progressively shrinking the candidate set. This achieves Θ(log log T) adaptivity and O(d log log T) query complexity at the cost of an extra √m factor in regret. Both frameworks extend naturally to covariance-adaptive settings (using ellipsoidal confidence bounds with estimated covariance) and general nonlinear rewards (using CDF-based UCB/LCB under a monotonicity assumption), maintaining the same doubly-logarithmic oracle complexity.

## Results

For worst-case linear rewards, AROQ-CMAB achieves near-optimal regret Õ(√(mdT)) with both adaptivity and query complexity of O(d log log(Tm/d)), while SROQ-CMAB achieves regret Õ(m√(dT)) with adaptivity Θ(log log T) and query complexity O(d log log T). In the covariance-dependent setting, AROQ-C-CMAB matches the near-optimal regret Õ(√(Σᵢ max σ²ᵢ(a)·T)) with O(d² log(Tm)) oracle complexity, and SROQ-C-CMAB achieves Õ(√(d·max Σᵢ σ²ᵢ(a)·T)) with Θ(log log T) adaptivity. For general (nonlinear) rewards, AROQ-GR-CMAB achieves Õ(L√(mdT)) regret with O(d log log(Tm/d)) oracle complexity. Across all settings, oracle usage is reduced from linear Θ(T) to (doubly) logarithmic in the time horizon.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CUCB [5] and CUCB [18], AROQ-CMAB matches the near-optimal regret Õ(√(mdT)) of CUCB [18] while reducing oracle adaptivity and query complexity from Θ(T) to O(d log log(Tm/d))—a doubly-logarithmic improvement. SROQ-CMAB trades a √m factor in regret (yielding Õ(m√(dT)), matching CUCB [5]) for the best-in-class adaptivity of Θ(log log T). In the covariance-dependent setting, AROQ-C-CMAB achieves the same near-optimal regret as OLS-UCB-C [32] but with O(d² log(Tm)) oracle complexity instead of Θ(T), while SROQ-C-CMAB further reduces adaptivity to Θ(log log T) at the cost of a √m regret factor. For general rewards, AROQ-GR-CMAB matches SDCB [4]'s regret Õ(L√(mdT)) with O(d log log(Tm/d)) oracle complexity versus Θ(T). Empirically (d=20, m=3), both proposed algorithms show significantly lower oracle adaptivity and query counts than CUCB, with SROQ-CMAB achieving faster wall-clock runtime due to its lower adaptivity enabling more efficient parallel oracle execution, at the cost of slightly higher regret consistent with the theoretical √m gap.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the combinatorial semi-bandit literature (Chen et al. 2013, Kveton et al. 2015) and the covariance-adaptive extensions (Zhou et al. 2024), but departs from them by introducing two oracle-efficient frameworks—adaptive rare oracle queries (AROQ) and scheduled rare oracle queries (SROQ)—inspired by batch learning and elimination-based bandit strategies. The oracle efficiency measures (adaptivity and query complexity) are borrowed from the submodular optimization literature (Balkanski & Singer 2018, Fahrbach et al. 2019), which had not previously been applied to bandit settings with stochastic sequential feedback. The general reward extension follows the CDF-based UCB construction of Chen et al. 2016, and the covariance-adaptive variants adapt the ellipsoidal confidence regions of Zhou et al. 2024, but all are restructured to require only doubly logarithmic oracle calls rather than per-round queries.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The NP-hard combinatorial optimization step at every round is the primary practical bottleneck for deploying combinatorial semi-bandit algorithms in real-world systems (ad allocation, product recommendation, network routing). Reducing oracle calls from Θ(T) to O(d log log(Tm/d)) or Θ(log log T) adaptivity makes these algorithms viable in settings where the oracle is expensive or must be called through a slow external service. The scheduled framework's parallelizable oracle queries further reduce wall-clock time in distributed environments. Open questions include whether the √m regret gap in the scheduled framework can be closed, whether the approach extends to adversarial or non-stationary settings, and whether the warm-up phase cost (O(d²) for covariance-adaptive variants) can be reduced for large d.
