## Problem

As businesses deploy agents that must coordinate with other agents having differing interests, a natural mechanism for economic alignment is outcome-based payment. However, no computational or statistical complexity results existed for two-player Stackelberg Markov games with payment, leaving it unclear when efficient algorithms for computing or learning optimal leader policies and payment schemes can exist. This paper characterizes the boundary between tractable and intractable settings across general-sum and cooperative games, and across planning and learning.

## Input / Output

In planning, the full Markov game (transitions P, rewards r^L and r^F bounded in [-1,1], horizon H, state/action spaces) is known; in learning, these are unknown and must be estimated from T episodes of trajectories. The payment scheme b: S×A×B → R⁺ is a function over all state-action pairs, and the leader's cost is scaled by a multiplier κ (κ=1 for direct monetary transfer). The output is a leader policy π and payment b, with the key distinction that trajectory payment is incurred only on realized states while upfront payment is incurred over the entire state-action space regardless of the trajectory.

## Method

The paper establishes a complexity landscape: planning in general-sum games is NP-hard on deterministic DAGs (via reduction from PARTITION), but polynomial-time solvable on deterministic trees via an LP-based minimal payment computation (Algorithm 1). For cooperative games without payment, learning is statistically intractable (exponential episodes) on stochastic trees and deterministic DAGs due to a needle-in-the-haystack exploration problem where the follower's best response prevents coordinated exploration. The key positive result is UCB-VI-FP, which operationalizes optimism-under-uncertainty by setting the leader's payment proportional to UCB bonuses, thereby incentivizing the follower to explore optimistically alongside the leader. This yields tight O(√T) regret under trajectory payment and O(T^{2/3}) under upfront payment. The gap between the two payment settings is further quantified in the deterministic-reward case, where trajectory payment achieves O(1) regret while upfront payment requires Ω(√T), because upfront payment is wasted on hard-to-reach (ε-significant) states that the follower never visits.

## Results

In cooperative games with trajectory payment, UCB-VI-FP achieves O(T^{1/2}) Stackelberg regret, which is tight (Theorem 6.4). Under upfront payment, the regret degrades to O(T^{2/3}) (Theorem 6.5), and in the simpler unknown-deterministic-reward setting, the gap widens from O(|S||A||B|) constant regret (trajectory) to O(T^{1/2}) (upfront), with matching lower bounds. In general-sum games on deterministic DAGs, computing the optimal policy and payment is NP-Hard (Proposition 4.1, Theorem 4.2), while polynomial-time algorithms exist for tree-structured MDPs (Proposition 4.3). Without payment, cooperative learning is statistically intractable, requiring Ω(2^{|S|}) episodes for stochastic trees (Theorem 5.1) or Ω(2^{|H|}) for deterministic DAGs (Theorem 5.2).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no experimental baselines; the 'comparisons' are between different structural and payment settings. The central contrast is that payment is unnecessary for planning in cooperative games (optimal payment is zero, Lemma 6.2) but essential for efficient learning: without payment, exploration is statistically intractable (exponential episodes needed), while with payment, sublinear regret is achievable. A second key contrast is between trajectory and upfront payment: trajectory payment yields O(T^{1/2}) regret (or O(1) with indicator bonus), while upfront payment degrades to O(T^{2/3}) (or O(T^{1/2}) with indicator bonus), because upfront payment wastes budget on low-visitation states. The paper also contrasts general-sum and cooperative games: general-sum planning is NP-Hard on DAGs regardless of payment, while cooperative planning is always tractable. Finally, the paper shows that relaxing the follower's best-response to λ-entropy-regularized response does not alleviate statistical hardness (Theorem 5.4), requiring Ω(exp(λ²H/8)) episodes for λ≤1.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate research threads: (1) computational complexity of Stackelberg Markov game planning (Conitzer & Sandholm 2006, Letchford et al. 2012), which established NP-hardness for stochastic MDPs, and (2) payment/reward-shaping in MDPs (Ben-Porat et al. 2024, Scheid et al. 2024), which studied single-agent or bandit settings. The key departure is bringing outcome-based payment into the two-player Markov game setting and studying both planning and learning, whereas prior work treated these in isolation. The paper also introduces upfront payment as a new setting absent from prior literature, motivated by temporal constraints in AI service deployment, and uses Nash-VI (Bai & Jin 2020) as a subroutine for the cooperative case.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central practical implication is that even when leader and follower rewards are perfectly aligned (cooperative games), the follower's best-response behavior makes exploration statistically intractable without payment—meaning AI service providers cannot efficiently improve their agents without incentivized exploration. The tight regret contrast between trajectory payment (O(1) for deterministic rewards) and upfront payment (O(T^{1/2})) directly informs pricing model design in the emerging outcome-based SaaS era. Open questions include extending results to partial observability (unobserved follower actions), handling untruthful reward reporting in the Markov game setting, and identifying other tractable subclasses beyond deterministic trees.
