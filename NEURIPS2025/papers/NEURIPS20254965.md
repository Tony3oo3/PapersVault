## Problem

The modern connectivist framing of neural computation emphasizes synaptic communication while neglecting the neuromodulatory environment that shapes each neuron's biophysical context. Two well-established experimental findings—neuromodulators (NMs) alter circuit dynamics and NMs gate synaptic plasticity as a 'third factor'—have remained conceptually separate. This paper asks whether volume transmission of NMs can be understood as implementing a single unifying computational principle that simultaneously explains both phenomena and enables targeted online credit assignment and compositional generalization.

## Input / Output

For supervised and meta-learning tasks, the model consumes time-series input signals (e.g., sine-wave parameters, dictionary-mapping basis function combinations) and produces a corresponding output sequence y(t) via either a fixed linear decoder or a neuromodulator-gated decoder. For reinforcement learning, it takes environment observations and scalar rewards and outputs discrete actions. In all settings, the network's internal neuromodulator concentration vector n(t) evolves endogenously from its own recurrent activity, serving as a learned, low-dimensional context state that dynamically reconfigures effective connectivity without any external context signal.

## Method

The e-nmRNN is derived via a rate reduction of a multi-neuromodulator LIF network, yielding two coupled ODE systems: fast firing rates r(t) and slow NM concentrations n(t). The key architectural mechanism is context factorization of effective synaptic weights, W_ij^eff(t) = W_ij^0 + Σ_k T_ijk n_k(t), where the T tensor encodes per-synapse, per-modulator sensitivity and the low-dimensional NM state n(t) dynamically reconfigures the high-dimensional connectivity—making the model a context-factorized hypernetwork. Learning rules derived by order-by-order gradient matching yield a 4-factor update for T_ijk that is multiplicatively gated by n_k(t), localizing plasticity to the relevant context. The NM dynamics themselves can learn to approximate gradient descent on effective weights, enabling in-context meta-learning without explicit gradient steps. A spatially embedded variant imposes Dale's law and Waxman-graph connectivity with powerpropagation-style updates, adding biologically inspired sparsity and local circuit structure.

## Results

On the compositional generalization (dictionary mapping) task, e-nmRNN variants outperform vanilla RNNs and match GRUs across increasing task complexity (K basis functions), though the model's richer multiplicative dynamics make it more prone to overfitting at high K on smaller datasets. On the Yang et al. (2019) 20-task multitasking benchmark, the spatially embedded e-nmRNN achieves comparable or superior performance to a parameter-matched standard RNN while exhibiting emergent biological structures (modularity, cell-type specialization, 8× timescale separation in NM dynamics). In the meta-learning sine-curve task, e-nmRNNs converge rapidly even compared to baselines with 100× TPE hyperparameter optimization, and their neuromodulator dynamics achieve statistically significant alignment with gradient descent on a surrogate network. In the RL bandit task, the e-nmRNN's neuromodulator state robustly encodes both task context and Reward Prediction Error, enabling RPE-gated online credit assignment.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares e-nmRNNs against five baselines (Transformers, HiPPO-LegT, Oscillatory RNNs, GRU, and Vanilla RNN) with matched parameter counts and TPE hyperparameter optimization on the compositional generalization task. The e-nmRNN outperforms vanilla RNNs and Oscillatory RNNs across all values of K, and matches GRU performance at moderate complexity, though it degrades relative to GRU at the highest K due to overfitting on smaller datasets. HiPPO-LegT and Transformers are noted as strong baselines that the e-nmRNN does not consistently outperform at high task complexity, where the model's higher capacity becomes a liability. On the multitasking benchmark, the spatially embedded e-nmRNN achieves comparable or superior performance to a parameter-matched standard RNN across all 20 tasks, demonstrating that its inductive biases (spatial embedding, Dale's law, context factorization) provide tangible value. In the meta-learning setting, the e-nmRNN converges faster than HP-optimized baselines, suggesting the architecture's inductive bias reduces the need for extensive hyperparameter search.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper derives the e-nmRNN from a rate reduction of LIF neurons with volume-transmitted neuromodulation, positioning it as a biophysically grounded 'close cousin' of Costacurta et al.'s neuromodulated RNN [15], distinguished by endogenous (closed-loop) NM dynamics and the absence of a low-rank second nonlinearity. The learning rules connect to the 'third factor' literature (Murray, Bellec, Liu et al.) on local, neuromodulator-gated plasticity, while the meta-learning analysis draws on von Oswald et al.'s finding that transformers approximate gradient descent in-context. Spatial embedding and Dale's law constraints borrow from Schwarz et al.'s powerpropagation [34] and recent work on sparsity-inducing reparameterizations, and the architecture is explicitly framed as a context-factorized hypernetwork linking to switching linear dynamical systems, fast-weight programmers, and attention mechanisms.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central contribution is a unifying computational principle—context factorization—that simultaneously explains how neuromodulation reshapes circuit dynamics and gates plasticity, offering an interpretable, biologically grounded architecture for online credit assignment and compositional generalization. The emergence of modularity, cell-type specialization, and timescale separation in multitasking networks, along with RPE encoding in NM dynamics during RL, provides concrete, testable predictions for neuroscience. Key limitations temper the impact: the model overfits on small, high-complexity datasets relative to GRUs, its sequential inference limits parallelizability compared to Transformers, and the NM timescales impose a fundamental speed limit on context switching. Open questions include whether biophysical regularization can resolve the overfitting pathology and whether the framework scales to larger, more complex task suites.
