## Problem

RLHF-tuned language models can still generate unsatisfactory responses (harmful, inaccurate, redundant) when the preference training data contains misleading information, such as noisy labels or context-inappropriate preferences. While the existence of such failures is well-documented, no prior method identifies *which* specific training examples cause a given unsatisfactory response and then selectively removes their influence without degrading the model's performance on other prompts.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254616/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes as input an already-trained RLHF policy π₀, its reward model r_θ₀, the full preference training set D (triples of prompt, chosen response, rejected response), and a small set of validation prompts (~0.4–0.5% of training data) whose generated responses are scored by an external reward model to partition into satisfactory and unsatisfactory subsets. It produces an improved policy model, an unlearned reward model, and for each unsatisfactory response, the specific subset of training data (typically 2–3% of the reward training set) identified as its cause along with convex decomposition coefficients. A key structural constraint is that the training data are preference pairs while the policy outputs single responses, which prevents direct unlearning from the policy and necessitates the two-phase reward-then-policy approach.

## Method

The method has two parts. First, a post-hoc explanation identifies the training data causing an unsatisfactory response by formulating a constrained combinatorial optimization: find a subset S of training data whose feature-comparison convex hull contains the projected feature of the prompt-response pair, while minimizing the sum of Euclidean distances to that projected feature. This NP-hard problem is solved by an iterative greedy algorithm (Algorithm 1) that starts from the nearest training data and expands S until the convex-hull constraint is satisfied, with proven polynomial complexity O(N log N + Nd + N²d + N³·⁵ + N⁴·⁵). Second, the identified data are unlearned via negative gradient updates on the reward model (reversing the Bradley-Terry log-likelihood contribution of the identified subset), followed by policy fine-tuning that maximizes the unlearned reward on unsatisfactory prompts while constraining KL divergence from the original policy on satisfactory prompts, preventing overfitting to the unsatisfactory subset.

## Results

On the full-hh-rlhf dialogue generation task, PPO+XRLHF achieves 76.4% and 75.8% win rate over SFT (Beaver-7b reward) for opt-1.3B and pythia-2.8B respectively, compared to 68.3% and 69.8% for PPO alone. On the TL;DR summarization task, ReMax+XRLHF reaches 77.4% win rate (Deberta-v3 reward) with Llama-2-7B, up from 69.6% for ReMax. GPT-4 evaluation confirms consistent gains, with ReMax+XRLHF achieving 80.1% (full-hh-rlhf, pythia-2.8B) and 78.1% (TL;DR, Llama-2-7B) win rates over SFT. The method requires only ~2–3% of the reward training data to be unlearned, making it computationally efficient relative to full RLHF retraining.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares XRLHF as a post-training add-on against two base RLHF algorithms: PPO [Ouyang et al., NeurIPS 2022] and ReMax [Li et al., ICML 2024]. PPO+XRLHF consistently outperforms PPO by 8.1–10.5 percentage points in win rate over SFT across both models and both evaluators on full-hh-rlhf (e.g., 76.4% vs. 68.3% for opt-1.3B under Beaver reward; 78.5% vs. 68.0% under GPT-4). ReMax+XRLHF outperforms ReMax by 6.6–13.3 percentage points (e.g., 79.3% vs. 71.4% for pythia-2.8B under Beaver; 80.1% vs. 66.8% under GPT-4). The same pattern holds on the TL;DR summarization task, where PPO+XRLHF gains 11.0–11.6 points over PPO and ReMax+XRLHF gains 5.8–10.5 points over ReMax. On the unsatisfactory validation subset D̄_u specifically, improvements are even larger (71–81% win rate of XRLHF over base), confirming that the explanation-driven unlearning targets the right training data. The KL-divergence constraint in the policy fine-tuning phase is critical: without it, the method overfits on D̄_u and actually degrades below the base methods on the full validation set (e.g., 43.7% vs. 73.4% for opt-1.3B on full-hh-rlhf).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a post-training refinement of standard RLHF (PPO, ReMax), building on the Bradley-Terry reward model and the negative-gradient unlearning framework of Thudi et al. It departs from existing example-based post-hoc explainability methods, which require the training data and the data to be explained to share the same structure; here the training data is a (prompt, chosen, rejected) triple while the data to be explained is a (prompt, response) pair, necessitating the convex-hull decomposition formulation. It also departs from prior LM unlearning work, which assumes direct unlearning from the policy model; the structural mismatch between preference data and generated responses forces the two-phase approach of unlearning the reward model first and then fine-tuning the policy under the unlearned reward.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method offers a practical, low-cost diagnostic-and-repair loop for RLHF: only ~2–3% of reward training data needs to be identified and unlearned, avoiding full retraining. The KL-divergence ablation (Appendix D.5) reveals a sharp trade-off—without it, the method overfits to the unsatisfactory subset and actually degrades below the base model on the full validation set (win rates drop to ~33–50%), underscoring that targeted improvement without regularization is not viable. A key open question, acknowledged by the authors, is extending the approach to test-time settings where a user flags an unsatisfactory response and the system must quickly retrieve causal training data and regenerate, which would require substantially faster retrieval and unlearning than the current offline pipeline.
