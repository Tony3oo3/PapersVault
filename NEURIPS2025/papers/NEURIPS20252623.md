## Problem

Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) excel at slow, deliberate System 2 thinking but lack the ability to perform fast, intuitive System 1 thinking, resulting in uniformly high computational overhead and latency regardless of problem difficulty. This paper tackles two questions: how to implement transitions between slow- and fast-thinking during LRM reasoning, and when to transition to optimally balance accuracy and efficiency.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252623/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a standard text prompt and operates entirely at inference time on the LRM's internal hidden states, requiring no retraining or additional model parameters. During generation, it reads hidden states at target layers (e.g., layers 19–27 for a 28-layer model) and injects a precomputed steering vector with a tunable intensity α. The adaptive variant additionally computes per-token difficulty scores from Jensen-Shannon divergence between early-layer and final-layer logit distributions, using a sliding window of k=8 tokens to trigger speed adjustments. Output is a standard reasoning trace plus final answer, but with controllable token length and reasoning depth.

## Method

The core mechanism is a representation-engineering-based steering vector extracted via PCA from contrastive hidden states of fast-thinking (short, CoT-style) and slow-thinking (long, deliberative) responses sampled from the MATH training set. During inference, this vector is added to hidden states at selected late layers with intensity α: positive α accelerates thinking (shorter, more intuitive responses) while negative α decelerates it (longer, more deliberate reasoning), producing a test-time scaling effect that outperforms prompt-based baselines like budget forcing and thought extrapolation. For adaptive control, the paper introduces a real-time difficulty estimator using Jensen-Shannon divergence between early-layer and final-layer next-token distributions as a proxy for reasoning complexity. A sliding-window algorithm compares each token's difficulty against a dynamic threshold (mean + λ·std of the window); when difficulty spikes, the steering intensity resets to a slow-thinking value, and otherwise it progressively increases toward a fast-thinking bound, mimicking how humans skim easy steps and deliberate on hard ones.

## Results

The adaptive speed control method achieves an average +1.26% Pass@1 accuracy improvement while reducing token usage by 8.56% across four LRMs (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B/32B, QwQ-32B, Qwen3-8B) and four benchmarks (MATH-500, AIME24, AIME25, GPQA Diamond). For example, on DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B, adaptive control reaches 70.7% on AIME24 with 9,322 tokens versus the original 69.2% with 10,680 tokens. The representation-based constant steering (α=−4) further boosts accuracy (e.g., 73.8% on AIME24 for the 32B model) at the cost of longer responses, while fast steering (α=4) cuts tokens by ~25% with modest accuracy loss.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two prompt-based baselines: (1) Thought Extrapolation (s1 [25]), which appends 'Wait' to encourage self-reflection, and (2) Budget Forcing (s1 [25] / Dynamic Early Exit [43]), which truncates reasoning and forces a final answer. Against Thought Extrapolation, adaptive control wins on both accuracy and token efficiency across all four models: e.g., on DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B AIME24, adaptive achieves 53.8% / 10,851 tokens vs. 1×Wait's 52.1% / 12,704 tokens, and the gap widens on GPQA Diamond (48.8% / 5,422 vs. 45.9% / 9,376). Against Budget Forcing (evaluated in Section 3.3 / Table A4), the representation-based fast steering (α>0) consistently outperforms early-exit by large margins—averaged Pass@1 improvements of +8.2 to +11.4 points at matched token budgets—while also producing more natural reasoning traces. The constant slow steering (α=−4) surpasses both baselines in raw accuracy (e.g., 73.8% AIME24 for the 32B model vs. 69.2% original and 68.3% for 1×Wait) but at the cost of ~34% more tokens, confirming that the adaptive strategy's value lies in combining the accuracy gains of slow thinking with the efficiency of fast thinking in a single trace.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the representation engineering (RopE) framework of Zou et al. [45] from steering high-level attributes (e.g., sentiment, toxicity) to controlling a continuous cognitive variable—thinking speed—in Large Reasoning Models. It also repurposes the layer-contrasting mechanism from DOLA [5] as a real-time difficulty signal, adapting a factuality-improvement technique into an adaptive control trigger. Conceptually, it departs from prior fast/slow thinking integration work (e.g., DualFormer [28], fine-tuning on mixed traces [33]) by showing that no training is needed: a single PCA-extracted steering vector in the representation space suffices to modulate between System 1 and System 2 reasoning, and it outperforms prompt-based test-time scaling baselines (budget forcing [25,43], thought extrapolation [25]) that operate at the text level.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The plug-in, training-free nature of the method (implemented in vLLM) makes it immediately deployable in existing LRM serving stacks, and the +1.3% accuracy / −8.6% token trade-off, while modest, demonstrates that representation-level control is a viable axis for inference-time scaling orthogonal to parallel search and prompt engineering. Key open questions include: (1) a model-agnostic calibration of steering intensity, since different LRMs show uneven sensitivity; (2) replacing the heuristic sliding-window algorithm with a learned mapping from difficulty estimates to optimal steering intensities; and (3) understanding why specific leading words ('To' vs. 'Okay') trigger distinct thinking modes—a phenomenon the authors flag as unexplained. The work opens a path toward fine-grained, token-level cognitive control in reasoning models without architectural changes.
