## Problem

While LLMs are expected to understand both the semantics and domain of instructions, recent work shows their responses are sensitive to superficial linguistic variations. This paper asks whether LLMs genuinely leverage semantic and domain knowledge or instead exploit a spurious correlation between syntactic templates (frequent PoS patterns) and domains learned during training, where the model associates a particular sentence structure with a particular subject area and this association can override the actual meaning of the prompt.

## Input / Output

The evaluation framework takes a trained LLM and a set of syntactic templates (PoS n-grams) extracted from its training data, instantiated with entity pairs (subject, object) from a knowledge base. For each template-entity pair, the method generates five perturbation variants (EXACT, SYNONYM, ANTONYM, DISFLUENT, PARAPHRASE) that systematically vary syntax and semantics while holding domain constant, then measures performance in both in-domain and cross-domain settings. The output is a performance profile across these settings that classifies the model's behavior into a taxonomy of six instruction-following patterns (correct, entity memorization, prompt memorization, word-domain spurious correlation, syntactic-domain spurious correlation, or incorrect).

## Method

The paper formalizes the problem by representing each example as a triple x = (d, t, e) — domain, syntactic template, and entities — and defines a template as spuriously predictive in a domain when its conditional frequency given that domain significantly exceeds its marginal frequency. To empirically demonstrate the phenomenon, the authors construct a synthetic dataset from TRex (Wikipedia-derived knowledge base) with one unique PoS template per domain across 17 property identifiers in 4 domains (locations, persons, organizations, creative works), then fine-tune OLMo-2 models (1B–13B) and measure the in-domain vs. cross-domain performance gap across the five perturbation settings. For pretrained models where training data is not fully controlled, they introduce a three-step benchmarking procedure: extract templates from known training subsets, partition prompts into in-domain/cross-domain based on EXACT-template performance, and evaluate the remaining perturbation settings to produce a behavior profile. The paper further demonstrates a security implication by showing that cross-domain syntactic templates (e.g., chain-of-thought or math templates) can be prepended or appended to harmful prompts to bypass refusal mechanisms in OLMo-2-7B-Instruct and GPT-4o-mini, reducing refusal rates from 40% to as low as 2.5%.

## Results

On the synthetic syntactic-domain dataset, OLMo-2 Instruct models (1B–13B) achieve in-domain accuracy of 0.90–0.94 on EXACT, SYNONYM, and ANTONYM settings, but cross-domain performance drops by a mean of 0.51±0.06 (range 0.37–0.54), confirming learned syntax-domain spurious correlations. In the in-the-wild FlanV2 evaluation, OLMo-2-7B drops from 0.85 to 0.48 on Sentiment140 SYNONYM (Δ=−0.37), while GPT-4o-mini drops from 1.00 to 0.44 (Δ=−0.56), indicating the phenomenon persists in fully aligned production models. In the jailbreak case study, prepending a chain-of-thought syntactic template to WildJailbreak prompts reduces OLMo-2-7B-Instruct's refusal rate from 40% to 2.5% (Δ=−0.375), demonstrating a concrete security consequence.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external baselines; instead, its core comparison is in-domain vs. cross-domain performance under controlled template perturbations. Across all three OLMo-2 sizes (1B, 7B, 13B), the cross-domain drop is consistent (mean Δ ≈ 0.51) and neither scaling nor instruction tuning mitigates the spurious correlation. In the in-the-wild FlanV2 evaluation, OLMo-2-7B and GPT-4o-mini/GPT-4o all show significant cross-domain drops on Sentiment140 SYNONYM (Δ = −0.37, −0.56, −0.33 respectively), while Llama-4-Maverick shows near-zero cross-domain change, suggesting entity memorization rather than syntactic-domain reliance. For the jailbreak experiment, the baseline refusal rate on 1000 WildJailbreak prompts is 40%; adding a chain-of-thought template as a prefix drops this to 2.5% (EXACT) or 19.5% (ANTONYM), while math templates produce smaller reductions (max Δ = 0.253), indicating that the severity of the bypass depends on which domain's template is used.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on syntactic templates in LLMs (Shaib et al., 2024), which established that models learn and reproduce frequent PoS n-grams from training data, by showing a specific and harmful downstream consequence: these templates create spurious correlations with domain that can override prompt semantics. It also connects to the broader spurious-correlation literature in NLP (McCoy et al. on syntactic heuristics in NLI) and computer vision (texture bias, background-color shortcuts), but identifies a new axis—syntactic template × domain association in instruction-tuned LLMs—that had not been formally characterized. The jailbreak case study builds on the WildJailbreak benchmark and the 'jailbreak tax' literature, but introduces a qualitatively different attack vector rooted in learned template-domain associations rather than adversarial token manipulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a previously unrecognized failure mode with dual implications: degraded cross-domain generalization (mean ~0.51 performance drop on entity tasks) and a practical security vulnerability where syntactic templates from non-refusal domains can bypass safety refusals in aligned models. The released benchmarking procedure gives practitioners a concrete diagnostic tool, but key limitations remain: results on GPT-4o and Llama-4 are speculative without confirmed training-data overlap, and the paper does not investigate reasoning models or chain-of-thought outputs. Open questions include whether enforcing syntactic diversity within domains during instruction tuning can mitigate the effect, and how broadly this phenomenon contributes to hallucination and other reliability failures.
