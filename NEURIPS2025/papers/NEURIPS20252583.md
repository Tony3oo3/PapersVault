## Problem

Most inverse sequential decision-making approaches either recover cost functions for stationary, fully-informed agents or are limited to very low-dimensional belief spaces (≤6 states). This paper tackles the underexplored problem of inferring an agent's latent knowledge state—which parts of the environment they knew versus discovered while acting—from a single observed navigation trajectory, in settings where the belief space can reach 2^169 states and exact Bayesian inverse planning is intractable.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252583/cropped_page6_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes as input a single observed trajectory (an ordered sequence of visited states in a deterministic finite MDP) and the complete graph structure of the environment (nodes, edges, and edge costs), which the researcher knows but the agent may not. It produces a set of Gibbs samples from the posterior P(K|D) over a binary knowledge vector of length N (one bit per state), from which marginal probabilities of each state being known are estimated. The knowledge representation is strictly binary per state, and the agent's knowledge evolves along the trajectory: a state becomes known the moment it is visited, so the effective knowledge at time t depends on both the initial knowledge vector and the prefix of the trajectory.

## Method

The core contribution is a sample-based Bayesian inference procedure that combines Gibbs sampling over a binary knowledge vector with an approximate planning model and the D* lite dynamic shortest-path algorithm. The planning model extends a softmax policy over shortest-path cost differences to partial knowledge by assigning uncertain transitions an expected cost of C^pot/q (where q is the believed traversability probability), yielding an optimistic-with-uncertainty-penalization strategy distinct from both pure optimism and exploration-bonus approaches. Because each Gibbs step flips only one knowledge bit, the underlying graph changes by only a few edges, enabling D* lite to incrementally update the shortest-path solution rather than recomputing it, which yields a 10.7× speedup over A*. A spatial Ising-model prior encodes the assumption that knowledge is locally correlated (nearby states are more likely to be jointly known), and the full conditional for each Bernoulli variable requires only one new likelihood evaluation per iteration since the previous sample's likelihood is cached.

## Results

On simulated grid-world data, the proposed inference method achieves statistically significant improvements over both the agnostic and heuristic baselines in all 8 worlds (q-values as low as 1.3×10⁻⁹). On human experimental data (52 participants, 8 grid worlds), significant improvements are observed in 5 of 8 worlds, with the strongest effect in World 4 (q = 8×10⁻⁸). For the planning model, the proposed optimistic-with-uncertainty-penalization model (OUR) outperforms the fully informed, random, exploration bonus, and purely optimistic baselines for most trajectories, with 35% of human subjects in World 0 following the OUR-predicted path (vs. 21% FI, 21% OPT, 19% EB). Using D* lite instead of A* yields a 10.7× speedup (42 min vs. 7.48 h for 1,000 Gibbs samples on World 0).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares two sets of baselines. For the planning model, the proposed optimistic-with-uncertainty-penalization model (OUR) is compared against a fully informed shortest-path agent (FI), a random actor (R), an exploration bonus model (EB), and a purely optimistic model (OPT). OUR and OPT significantly outperform FI, R, and EB for most trajectories (Wilcoxon q < 0.05), with the only exceptions being EB in World 0 (q = 0.59) and OPT in World 7 (q = 0.25), where the path to the goal is straightforward and knowledge has minimal effect. For the inference method, the proposed Gibbs-sampling approach is compared against an agnostic baseline (all fields known with probability 0.5) and a heuristic baseline (turnaround fields assigned probability 0.1). On simulated data, the proposed method significantly outperforms both baselines in all 8 worlds. On human data, it significantly outperforms the agnostic baseline in 5 of 8 worlds (Worlds 1, 3, 4, 5, 7), with the failures in Worlds 0, 2, and 6 attributed to high behavioral variability and, in World 7, a trivially simple path that provides little information about knowledge state.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Bayesian inverse planning (Baker et al.) from low-dimensional belief inference (≤6 belief states) to high-dimensional knowledge state inference in navigation, where the belief space can reach 2^169 states. It adapts the softmax-over-shortest-path-cost policy from Chandra et al. (NeurIPS 2023) to handle partial knowledge by replacing true edge costs with expected costs under probabilistic transitions, and introduces D* lite (Koenig & Likhachev) for incremental policy updates during Gibbs sampling. Conceptually, the planning model is related to the E3 algorithm but inverts its treatment of uncertainty: rather than treating unknown states as maximally rewarding (exploration), it penalizes uncertain transitions, yielding uncertainty aversion. The spatial Ising prior for knowledge correlation is a novel addition not present in prior inverse planning work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine gap: most inverse decision-making methods assume stationary policies or complete environmental knowledge, making them ill-suited for modeling learning agents or humans navigating partially known spaces. The 10.7× speedup from D* lite over A* makes the inference tractable for medium-sized state spaces, and validation on both simulated and human data (52 participants, 8 grid worlds) demonstrates the model captures real navigation behavior under uncertainty. Key limitations include the binary knowledge representation, a single traversability probability for all unknown transitions, and limited scalability beyond medium-sized state spaces. Open questions include extending to non-spatial domains, incorporating additional information sources (e.g., eye-tracking), handling trial-to-trial learning effects, and moving beyond binary to richer knowledge representations.
