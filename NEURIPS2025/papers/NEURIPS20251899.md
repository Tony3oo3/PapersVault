## Problem

Extracting Protocol State Machines (PSMs) from lengthy RFC specifications is essential for automated security analysis, formal verification, and protocol testing, yet no standardized, multi-protocol benchmark exists to measure how well LLMs perform this structured extraction task. Prior efforts such as PROSPER and mGPTFuzz were protocol-specific, evaluated only a handful of examples, or tested a single model, leaving the field without a reproducible, diverse testbed for comparing LLM capabilities on graph-structured output generation from technical prose.

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes RFC documents preprocessed into semantically coherent chunks (segmented at primary and secondary section boundaries, capped at ~40k tokens, with section title and number retained as metadata). Each LLM is prompted in two stages—first to emit a partial PSM (states and transitions) per chunk, then to merge all partials into a single global PSM—producing a JSON object with a state list, an initial state, final states, and a transition list where each transition specifies from-state, trigger event, action, and to-state. Evaluation compares the extracted JSON against manually validated ground-truth PSMs using SentenceBERT cosine similarity (threshold θ=0.5) rather than exact string matching, scoring at three granularities: state-set overlap, partial transition match (states + at least one of event/action), and exact transition match (all four components).

## Method

PSMBENCH introduces a two-stage Chain-of-Thought prompting strategy: a Partial PSM Extraction Prompt guides the LLM to identify FSM-relevant content in each RFC section and emit a constrained JSON fragment (states in CamelCase/snake_case, events prefixed with receive/send/timeout/cond, actions starting from a fixed verb set), followed by a PSM Combination Prompt that instructs the model to unify state aliases, deduplicate transitions, and determine initial/final states. The evaluation framework replaces brittle string matching with semantic fuzzy graph alignment: all-MiniLM-L6-v2 SentenceBERT embeddings compute cosine similarity between state names and transition labels, and a tunable threshold (validated via ablation over θ∈[0.40,0.60]) decides whether two phrases are equivalent. Precision, recall, and F1 are then computed over matched states and transitions at three strictness levels, enabling partial credit for near-misses. The benchmark is instantiated on RFC2PSM—14 protocols across OSI layers L2–L7, 1,580 pages of cleaned RFC text, 108 states, and 297 transitions—and used to systematically compare nine LLMs (GPT-4o-mini, Claude3, Gemini2, DeepSeek-R1, DeepSeek-V3, QWQ, QWen3, Gemma3, Mistral) at temperature 0.0, revealing a persistent state-transition gap where models achieve up to 0.82 F1 on states but at most 0.38 F1 on transitions.

## Results

Across 9 LLMs evaluated on 14 protocols (108 states, 297 transitions), DS-V3 achieves the best state-set F1 of 0.715 (precision 0.631, recall 0.824), while DS-R1 follows at 0.661. At the transition level, the gap widens dramatically: DS-V3 reaches only 0.381 F1 on partial transition matching and 0.378 on exact transition matching, revealing a persistent state-transition gap. Smaller models (QWQ, QWen3, Mistral) show high precision but very low recall on transitions (F1 ≤ 0.177), while over-extracting models (GPT-4o-mini, Gemini2) achieve recall up to 0.861 on states but precision as low as 0.216.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks 9 LLMs rather than proposing a single method, so comparisons are among the evaluated models. DS-V3 (DeepSeek-V3) dominates across all three metrics: 0.715 F1 on states, 0.381 on partial transitions, and 0.378 on exact transitions, outperforming DS-R1 (0.661 / 0.328 / 0.314) by 5–10 F1 points. GPT-4o-mini, despite the highest state recall (0.861), suffers the lowest state precision (0.216) and only 0.140 F1 on partial transitions, indicating severe over-extraction (431 states vs. 108 GT). Claude3 and Gemini2 show moderate state F1 (0.507 and 0.444) but degrade sharply on transitions (0.231 and 0.213 partial F1), with Gemini2 generating 1000 transitions against 297 ground truth. Smaller open models (QWQ, QWen3, Mistral) are conservative: QWQ achieves 0.671 state precision but only 0.491 recall, and its exact-transition F1 drops to 0.094. The overarching finding is that no model closes the state-to-transition gap: even the best model (DS-V3) loses roughly 34 F1 points moving from state matching to exact transition matching.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by providing the first standardized, open-source benchmark and multi-protocol dataset for evaluating LLM-based PSM extraction, filling a gap left by prior work. PROSPER (Sharma & Yegneswaran, 2023) demonstrated feasibility with GPT-3.5 on a handful of protocols but offered no reusable evaluation infrastructure, while mGPTFuzz (Ma et al., 2024) was protocol-specific to IoT/Matter and Pacheco et al. (2022) relied on manual expert alignment for scoring. PSMBENCH departs from these by introducing automated semantic fuzzy-matching metrics (via SentenceBERT embeddings) that reward partial graph correctness, enabling scalable, reproducible comparison across 14 protocols and 9 models without expert-in-the-loop evaluation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The persistent state-transition gap—models reach up to 0.82 F1 on individual states but only ≤0.38 F1 on assembled transition graphs—quantifies a concrete bottleneck for automated protocol security analysis, formal verification, and fuzzing pipelines that depend on accurate PSMs. The benchmark is practically important as the first turn-key testbed in this space, but its scope is limited to medium-sized application/transport-layer protocols and excludes ultra-long lower-layer specs (e.g., Wi-Fi, >1000 pages). Open questions include whether adaptive chunk-merging, retrieval-augmented decoding, or task-specific fine-tuning can close the transition gap, and whether the semantic-matching threshold (θ=0.5) adequately captures protocol-specific aliasing without introducing false positives.
