## Problem

The computational complexity of computing Nash equilibria in adversarial team games was only known to be CLS-hard when the team size is large, leaving the small-team regime (e.g., 2 vs. 1) open. Similarly, while min-max optimization is well-understood in convex-concave settings, the complexity of computing symmetric first-order Nash equilibria in symmetric (antisymmetric) min-max problems, and of symmetric equilibria in multi-player team zero-sum games, remained unresolved. This paper closes these gaps by establishing tight complexity characterizations for both settings.

## Input / Output

The problems are formulated as promise problems over polynomial-time Turing machines: SYMGDAFIXEDPOINT takes a smooth antisymmetric function f: X×X→R (given via oracle) with a polytope constraint set X and precision ε, and must return a symmetric point (x*,x*) whose GDA fixed-point gap is at most ε. The adversarial team game version takes a 3-player polymatrix game (payoff tensors) and must return an ε-Nash equilibrium. Hardness results hold even for quadratic functions on simplex domains and for polymatrix games, with ε = 1/n^c for any constant c > 0. The non-symmetric variant (NONSYMGDAFIXEDPOINT) additionally requires the output point to satisfy ‖x*−y*‖₂ ≥ δ, and is an FNP search problem rather than a total one.

## Method

The CLS-hardness for 3-player adversarial team games reduces from symmetric identical-payoff two-player games (Ghosh–Hollender 2024) by constructing a 3-player game whose adversary utility (Eq. 2) includes penalty terms scaled by |A_min|/ε that force the two team players to play approximately the same strategy (Lemma 3.2) while keeping the adversary's probability mass concentrated on a 'safe' action (Lemma 3.3), thereby preserving the original game structure. The PPAD-hardness for symmetric min-max optimization reduces from symmetric two-player games by defining a quadratic antisymmetric function f(x,y) = ½⟨y,Ay⟩ − ½⟨x,Ax⟩ + ⟨y,Cx⟩ (A symmetric, C skew-symmetric) on the simplex, showing that any ε-approximate symmetric GDA fixed point yields a 4nε-approximate symmetric Nash equilibrium. The team zero-sum result (Theorem 1.7) combines both gadgets: it embeds the quadratic reduction into a 6-player (3 vs. 3) polymatrix game using only multilinear terms, with the adversarial-team symmetry-enforcing gadget replicated on each side. For non-symmetric equilibria, the paper refines the McLennan–Tourky hardness for symmetric identical-interest games to account for poly(1/n)-approximate equilibria, yielding NP-hardness for distinguishing near-unique from widely-separated symmetric equilibria.

## Results

The paper establishes three main complexity characterizations: (1) computing ε-Nash equilibria in 3-player (2 vs. 1) adversarial team games is CLS-complete, even for polymatrix games (Theorem 1.3); (2) computing symmetric 1/n^c-approximate first-order Nash equilibria in symmetric min-max optimization is PPAD-complete, even for quadratic functions (Theorem 1.5/4.2); and (3) computing non-symmetric poly(1/n)-equilibria in symmetric min-max optimization is NP-hard (Theorem 1.6). Additionally, computing symmetric 1/n^c-Nash equilibria in symmetric 6-player (3 vs. 3) team zero-sum polymatrix games is PPAD-complete (Theorem 1.7). These results resolve open questions about the complexity of Nash equilibria in small adversarial team games and the intractability of symmetric dynamics in min-max optimization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines; comparisons are to prior complexity results. The CLS-hardness for 3-player adversarial team games (Theorem 1.2) tightens the prior result of Anagnostides et al. [2023], which established CLS membership but only had hardness for large n via Babichenko and Rubinstein [2021]; here hardness is shown for the minimal non-trivial case n=2. The PPAD-completeness for symmetric min-max (Theorem 1.5) is a new problem not previously studied, though it subsumes and simplifies the PPAD-hardness of GDAFIXEDPOINT from Daskalakis et al. [2021] (Theorem 4.4 provides a simpler proof). The NP-hardness for non-symmetric equilibria (Theorem 1.6) significantly refines McLennan and Tourky [2010] by accounting for poly(1/n)-approximate equilibria rather than exact ones. The 6-player team zero-sum result (Theorem 1.7) extends the symmetric min-max hardness to a game-theoretic setting using only multilinear (polymatrix) terms, addressing an open question from Hollender et al. [2025] about polymatrix restrictions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two well-studied complexity lines: (1) the CLS/PPAD characterization of Nash equilibrium computation in finite games (Chen et al. 2009, Daskalakis et al. 2009) and (2) the emerging complexity theory of min-max optimization (Daskalakis et al. 2021, Anagnostides et al. 2023). The key conceptual move is using the adversarial player in a team game as a 'symmetry enforcement gadget'—reducing from symmetric two-player games (Ghosh and Hollender 2024 for CLS, Chen et al. 2009 for PPAD) to multi-player team games and symmetric min-max problems without disturbing the equilibrium structure. This unifies previously separate hardness results and resolves open questions about small-player adversarial team games and polymatrix restrictions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The results provide a near-complete complexity landscape for equilibrium computation in min-max optimization: CLS for adversarial team games (resolving the n=2 case), PPAD for symmetric equilibria in symmetric min-max (even for quadratics), and FNP-hardness for non-symmetric equilibria. The broad impossibility result (Theorem 4.6) precludes any symmetric learning algorithm—gradient descent/ascent, extragradient, optimistic methods—from converging to stationary points in polynomial time, giving a complexity-theoretic foundation for the non-convergence phenomena observed empirically in GANs and adversarial training. The general (non-symmetric, non-team) first-order Nash equilibrium problem in min-max optimization remains open, and extending these hardness results to structured problems like adversarial training and GANs is identified as a key direction.
