## Problem

Prior online bilateral trade mechanisms with two-bit feedback query both buyer and seller synchronously at every time step, meaning the seller is asked to make a decision even when the buyer has already rejected the offer. This paper asks whether the seller can be queried only when a trade opportunity actually arises (i.e., only after the buyer accepts), and whether the resulting 'censored' seller feedback is still sufficient to achieve optimal regret rates.

## Input / Output

At each time step the broker posts a price P_t ∈ [0,1] and receives one bit from the buyer (𝕀{B_t ≥ P_t}) unconditionally, plus a censored bit from the seller (𝕀{S_t ≤ P_t}) only when the buyer accepts. The valuations S_t, B_t are i.i.d. in [0,1] with L-Lipschitz continuous CDFs, and the algorithm restricts prices to a uniform grid of K = ⌈T^{1/3}⌉ points. The output is the sequence of posted prices; the broker's performance is measured by cumulative regret against the best fixed price in hindsight, with strong budget balance maintained throughout.

## Method

The algorithm decomposes the expected gain from trade g(p) into four terms and estimates the two integral terms (∫_p^1 P[B≥λ]dλ and ∫_0^p P[S≤λ]dλ) during an initial exploration phase of KH = T^{2/3} rounds, where each grid price is played H = ⌈T^{1/3}⌉ times. After exploration, arms with too few seller observations are pruned to a reduced set K^◇, and a UCB-style bandit procedure selects prices using optimistic bounds on the product of probability estimates and integral estimates. The key technical insight is that the confidence width on seller-related quantities (which depends on the small number of censored observations) is multiplied by the buyer's acceptance probability, so when seller samples are scarce the buyer acceptance probability is also low, and these two effects counterbalance to yield a 1/√T_{k,t} convergence rate. This yields Õ(T^{2/3}) regret, matching the lower bound for the strictly richer two-bit model and establishing that the asynchronous protocol elicits the minimal feedback necessary for optimal learning.

## Results

The paper's main theoretical result is a strongly-budget-balanced algorithm achieving $\tilde{O}(T^{2/3})$ regret against the best fixed price in hindsight under an asynchronous (censored) feedback protocol, matching the lower bound for the strictly richer two-bit model. Empirically, on six synthetic Beta-distribution instances (T = 10,000–50,000), the proposed algorithm achieves cumulative regret of 135–714, consistently outperforming the Scouting Bandits baseline (Cesa-Bianchi et al., 2021) which incurs regret of 549–2,502. The improvement is most pronounced at longer horizons (T = 50,000), where the proposed method achieves roughly 3–7× lower regret.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its asynchronous censored-feedback algorithm against the Scouting Bandits algorithm of Cesa-Bianchi et al. (2021), which operates under the richer two-bit feedback model. The proposed method wins on all six synthetic instances, achieving 3.7× lower regret on Instance 1 (199.6 vs. 732.0), 3.2× lower on Instance 2 (714.4 vs. 2,253.8), 4.1× lower on Instance 3 (135.2 vs. 548.8), 7.3× lower on Instance 4 (326.5 vs. 2,381.0), 3.5× lower on Instance 5 (168.5 vs. 583.5), and 5.7× lower on Instance 6 (439.2 vs. 2,502.4). The authors attribute this empirical advantage to an arm-pruning step (removing arms with insufficient seller samples via the set $K^{\diamond}$) that is absent in Scouting Bandits, effectively reducing the bandit phase to a smaller candidate set. Notably, the proposed method uses strictly less feedback (one buyer bit + censored seller bit) yet outperforms the two-bit baseline, consistent with the theoretical claim that the asynchronous protocol elicits the minimal feedback necessary for optimal $\tilde{O}(T^{2/3})$ regret.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the scouting bandits framework of Cesa-Bianchi et al. [2024a] from the synchronous two-bit feedback model to a new asynchronous protocol where the seller is queried only after the buyer accepts, yielding a 'censored' feedback signal. The key technical departure is handling the asymmetry in information: the buyer's acceptance is always observed, but the seller's acceptance is observed only conditionally, breaking the symmetric estimation of the four components in the gain-from-trade decomposition. The algorithm also introduces an arm-pruning step (K^diamond) absent in the original scouting bandits, which restricts the bandit phase to prices with sufficient buyer acceptance probability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that censored (asynchronous) feedback achieves the same Õ(T^{2/3}) regret as the richer two-bit model—and matches its lower bound—establishes that the two-bit model elicits strictly more information than necessary, a clean characterization of the minimal feedback frontier for bilateral trade. Practically, this validates asynchronous marketplace protocols (e.g., contacting a seller only after a buyer commits) as both efficient and informationally sufficient. Open questions include extending to adversarial or contextual settings, where the censored-feedback structure may interact more complexly with non-stationarity or side information.
