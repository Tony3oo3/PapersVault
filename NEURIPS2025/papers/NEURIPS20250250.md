## Problem

Existing fairness criteria for resource allocation, such as Allocative Equality of Opportunity (AEoO), evaluate fairness at the individual level by comparing probabilities of receiving resources across groups. Many real-world allocation problems—water distribution, economic aid, policing—instead require fairness to be assessed through group-level collective impacts (e.g., ecosystem health, relative deprivation), for which no principled online allocation method existed. This paper introduces Equality of Impact (EoI) as a group-level generalization of AEoO and develops the first online algorithms for continuous resource allocation under this criterion with provable fairness guarantees.

## Input / Output

At each of T rounds, the agent receives a continuous resource budget q and must split it into (a_A, a_B) for two groups. It observes only single-point reward and impact outcomes at the chosen allocation (censored feedback), with no prior knowledge of the underlying functions beyond their values at zero allocation. The reward and impact functions are assumed to be non-decreasing, concave, and continuous (the Marginal Returns Class). The algorithm outputs a sequence of allocation schemes and is evaluated by cumulative fairness regret—the total excess of |h_A(a_A) − h_B(a_B)| over the threshold G across all rounds—rather than by reward regret.

## Method

In the noise-free setting, the algorithm exploits the geometric structure of concave functions: after each point evaluation, secant-line (chord) constructions yield tight lower and upper bounds on the reward and impact functions, which in turn define a guaranteed fair set, a potential fair set, and an estimate of the reward-maximizing set X—all provably single closed intervals. The next allocation is chosen by maximizing the upper utility bound over the intersection of the potential fair set and X, excluding already-guaranteed-fair points, yielding O(1) cumulative fairness regret. In the noisy setting, a meta-algorithm replaces the deterministic bounds with user-supplied confidence or credible intervals (e.g., from Gaussian Process posteriors with RBF or Matérn kernels), constructs analogous fair-set estimates via set operations on the interval bounds, and selects allocations by maximizing the upper utility bound over the resulting feasible region. The key structural insight enabling both algorithms is that the fair set Q^G is always a non-empty closed interval containing an optimal allocation, restricting the search to a one-dimensional interval rather than the full two-dimensional allocation simplex.

## Results

The paper's central theoretical result (Theorem 3) is that Algorithm 1 achieves O(1) cumulative fairness regret in the noiseless setting, meaning the average fairness regret R_t/t decays as O(1/T). Empirically, across all three environments (Water Allocation, Imbalanced Impact, Imbalanced Rewards), the average fairness regret curves for Algorithm 1 align closely with the O(1/T) reference line over 50 rounds, confirming the theoretical bound. In the noisy setting with a 150-query budget, Algorithm 3 (using Gaussian Process credible intervals) consistently accumulates less cumulative fairness regret than both evolutionary baselines (NSGA-III and MOEA/D) across all environments, with the clearest margins in the IIE and IRE settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

In the noiseless setting, Algorithm 1 outperforms both baselines across all three environments. Brent-Search (BS), which has oracle access to the fair set boundaries and uses Brent's optimization method, also follows the O(1/T) regret trend but incurs consistently higher regret than Algorithm 1 and exhibits numerical instability in the WAE environment (a sharp regret spike). Explore-then-Commit (ETC) performs worst overall, as its mandatory stratified exploration phase causes significant regret before it can commit, particularly in the IRE environment where its average regret converges much more slowly. In the noisy setting, Algorithm 3 (GP-based) beats both NSGA-III and MOEA/D on cumulative fairness regret in the IIE and IRE environments, with narrower standard deviation bands indicating more stable performance. In the WAE environment, the standard deviation intervals of Algorithm 3 overlap with those of the evolutionary baselines, suggesting roughly comparable performance in that specific setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper generalizes Allocative Equality of Opportunity (AEoO) from Elzayn et al. (2019) by replacing individual-level probability fairness with group-level impact functions, yielding the new Equality of Impact (EoI) criterion. It also departs from prior fair allocation work that typically considers discrete resources by studying continuous allocation under diminishing marginal returns, and from online convex optimization approaches (e.g., Yu and Neely, 2020) by requiring only point-wise function evaluations rather than gradient access. The geometric exploitation of concavity via chord-based bounds to achieve O(1) cumulative fairness regret is a novel algorithmic contribution within the online bandit/OCO literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The O(1) cumulative fairness regret guarantee in the noiseless setting is a strong theoretical result that shows fairness constraints can be satisfied with bounded total violation, which is practically meaningful for applications like water allocation or economic aid distribution where persistent unfairness is unacceptable. However, the main limitations are the restriction to two groups, the absence of spillover/network effects between groups, and the lack of theoretical guarantees in the noisy setting (only empirical validation via GP-based meta-algorithm). Extending to multiple groups, incorporating interdependencies, and establishing regret bounds under noise are natural open questions that would broaden the framework's applicability.
