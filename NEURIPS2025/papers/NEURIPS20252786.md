## Problem

While attention sinks—tokens that disproportionately capture attention—are known to be critical for model performance under KV-caching, quantization, and pruning, their functional role, semantic content, and origin remain poorly understood. In particular, it is unclear why sinks emerge on non-first tokens, whether the representations of semantically meaningless sink tokens (e.g., punctuation) nonetheless encode meaningful information, and how the mechanism differs between pretrained and reasoning-tuned models or under architectural changes like QK normalization.

## Input / Output

The empirical analysis feeds 170 prompts (truncated to 150 tokens) and 200 prompts (1024 tokens) into a suite of open-weight LLMs (Qwen 2.5 3B/7B/14B, Phi-3 Medium, Llama-3/3.1 8B, Mistral 7B, and DeepSeek-R1 distilled variants), extracting per-head attention weights, value matrices, and residual stream activations at specified layers. Outputs include per-head sink counts (via a threshold on mean attention received), the fraction of attention-head output variance explained by the span of sink value vectors, and True/False classification accuracy from mass-mean probes constructed on tag-only, non-tag-only, and full-activation components. The theoretical component takes a sequence of real-valued tokens separated by a [SEP] marker and outputs the arithmetic mean of tokens following [SEP].

## Method

The paper formalizes a three-stage 'catch, tag, release' mechanism: (1) Catch—tokens identified as attention sinks (average attention received exceeding a threshold ε=0.2) capture the attention of subsequent tokens, producing vertical bands in attention maps; (2) Tag—the value vectors of sink tokens are copied via the attention output to every token attending to them, imprinting a shared direction in embedding space; (3) Release—these tagged outputs are added to the residual stream, where the shared tag directions persist and cause tokens to cluster in deeper layers (verified via PCA projections). To quantify the mechanism, the authors compute the fraction of attention-head output variance lying in the subspace spanned by sink value vectors (typically 20–40%, up to 70%). To test semantic content, they decompose the final period token's activation into tag and non-tag components and train mass-mean probes, finding that tag-only probes achieve 92–99.5% True/False accuracy while non-tag probes remain at chance. A two-layer transformer is then constructed where the mechanism is provably necessary (Theorem 7.1) and empirically emerges through AdamW training on a sequence-averaging task, providing a minimal theoretical foundation for the observed behavior.

## Results

On the Cities True/False classification task, the tag-only probe (θ_tag) achieves 92.5%–99.5% accuracy across QWEN 2.5 (3B/7B/14B), LLAMA-3 8B, and LLAMA-3.1 8B, while the non-tag probe (θ_no tag) performs at chance level (50.0%–56.5%), confirming that attention-sink tags carry semantically meaningful information. Quantitatively, 1–2 tags per head typically explain 20%–40% of the variance in attention head outputs, with some heads reaching up to 70%. Reasoning-distilled DeepSeek-R1 variants exhibit more attention sinks and higher tag-explained variance than their pretrained counterparts, and QK normalization does not reduce the total number of sinks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against external methods but instead performs internal component comparisons. The tag probe (θ_tag) decisively outperforms the non-tag probe (θ_no tag) by 42–49 percentage points across all models, demonstrating that the information is specifically encoded in the tag component rather than the residual token content. Notably, θ_tag also outperforms the full-activation probe (θ_activation) on QWEN 2.5 3B (98% vs 50%), 7B (94.5% vs 83%), and 14B (99.5% vs 60%), suggesting tags provide a denoised representation of the True/False direction. In the pretrained-vs-reasoning comparison, DeepSeek-R1 distilled models show more sinks and higher tag-explained variance, particularly for QWEN 14B, indicating the mechanism is amplified by reasoning fine-tuning. For QK normalization, the average sink count remains comparable between normalized and unnormalized models, showing the mechanism is robust to this architectural change.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the empirical identification of attention sinks by Gu et al. (2024) and the mass-mean probing framework of Marks and Tegmark (2024), but departs from the three dominant explanations in the literature (implicit bias compensation, head deactivation, and rank-collapse prevention) by proposing a unified functional mechanism: 'catch, tag, release.' It positions attention sinks not as artifacts or compensatory quirks but as an active tagging system that imprints shared directions into the residual stream, connecting the phenomenon to both the outlier-activation literature and the rank-collapse literature. The theoretical section introduces a minimal 2D averaging task where the mechanism is provably necessary and emerges through standard optimization, providing a clean separation from the purely empirical analyses that preceded it.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first functional account of why attention sinks form on non-first tokens (punctuation, function words) and shows that the resulting tags carry semantically meaningful information (True/False classification), resolving a tension in the literature between sink-preservation and sink-removal approaches. The finding that QK normalization does not suppress sinks challenges a common architectural assumption and has direct implications for model compression and KV-caching strategies. Open questions include whether the tag subspace structure can be exploited for more principled pruning or quantization, how the mechanism interacts with multi-head attention in deeper architectures, and whether the connection to rank collapse is causal or merely correlational.
