## Problem

Shields in safe RL provide formal safety guarantees but are implemented as large lookup tables that are opaque to human operators, making it difficult to trust or interpret why a particular action is blocked. While decision trees have become a standard tool for explaining policies, the inherent non-determinism of shields (permitting any safe action) causes their DT representations to grow to sizes that are impractical for human comprehension. The paper asks how to provide compact, human-interpretable explanations of shield decisions without sacrificing the formal guarantees.

## Input / Output

The method requires only an MDP world model and a CTL safety specification—both already mandatory in any shielded RL pipeline—plus an optional set of user-defined predicates (e.g., distance-to-obstacle functions) that guide DT learning toward more intuitive splits. At runtime, given the current state, the system produces three levels of explanation: a Level 1 DT that classifies the state into safe/critical/dangerous/unsafe, a Level 2 DT that enumerates which actions are permitted and why others are blocked, and a Level 3 execution tree that traces the specific probabilistic consequences of a blocked action. The shield itself is a non-deterministic policy mapping states to sets of allowed actions, computed via probabilistic model checking with a user-defined risk threshold ε.

## Method

The core contribution is a three-level hierarchical explanation framework that decomposes the intractable problem of explaining an entire non-deterministic shield into compact, case-based sub-explanations. At design time, value iteration over the MDP computes the risk of safety violation for every state-action pair, which is used to partition states into four risk categories and synthesize the shield; a Level 1 DT is then learned (via standard DT learning, e.g., DTControl) to classify states by risk category, with user-defined predicates enabling intuitive splits. At runtime, a Level 2 DT is computed only over the states falling in the current Level 1 leaf, explaining which actions the shield permits and why others exceed the risk threshold. For a specific unsafe action, a Level 3 execution tree is constructed by resolving the MDP's non-determinism into a Markov chain under a policy that takes the unsafe action in the current state and the safest action everywhere else, then recursively enumerating the most probable traces whose cumulative probability mass exceeds ε, prefix-merging them into a tree that demonstrates the violation. This top-down, on-demand decomposition yields trees several orders of magnitude smaller than a single DT representing the full shield.

## Results

The primary metric is representation size (number of nodes for trees, number of entries for the shield lookup table). Across all benchmarks, the hierarchical decision trees (T_L1 + T_L2) are orders of magnitude smaller than the original shield. For the most complex Highway scenario (HW3-c), the shield lookup table contains 232,216 entries, while T_L1 has only 57 nodes and T_L2 averages 45.1 nodes. In the Boeing TaxiNet environment, the shield has ~1,105 entries versus 21–33 nodes for T_L1 and 5–6 nodes for T_L2. Shield computation time ranges from 0.1 s (HW2-f) to 50 s (HW3-c), while DT learning takes 1.3–17.4 s.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external baseline methods; instead, it compares three internal representations of the same shield: (1) the raw lookup table (|π_shield|), (2) a single monolithic decision tree (|T_shield|), and (3) the proposed hierarchical trees (|T_L1| + |T_L2|). The hierarchical approach is consistently the most compact: in HW3-c, T_L1 + T_L2 together use ~102 nodes versus 555 for the single tree and 232,216 for the lookup table. The single DT is itself dramatically smaller than the lookup table in every scenario (e.g., 7 vs. 358 in HW2-f), confirming that DTs compress shields well. However, the hierarchical decomposition further reduces size in most cases (e.g., 9 + 33 = 42 nodes vs. 113 for the single tree in HW2-c), because each sub-tree only needs to explain a subset of states. The main limitation noted is that tree size grows when the problem becomes more complex (e.g., 20% holes in Frozen Lake, or the HW3-c scenario with velocity changes), where user-defined predicates become critical for keeping trees compact.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on probabilistic shield synthesis (Jansen et al., CONCUR 2020) for safe RL and on counterexample trace enumeration from probabilistic model checking (Han & Katoen, TACAS 2007) for Level 3 execution trees. It departs from the broader explainable-RL literature, which focuses on interpreting the learned policy's reward-maximizing decisions, by instead explaining the safety mechanism (the shield) that constrains those decisions. The key methodological departure is the hierarchical decomposition of explanations into three levels (state risk, action safety, consequences), which addresses the non-determinism problem that makes naive decision-tree representations of shields intractably large.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first framework to combine formal safety guarantees with human-interpretable explanations in RL, addressing a critical trust gap in safety-critical deployments of learned controllers. The practical impact is significant: the method requires no additional information beyond what shielded RL already uses, incurs minimal overhead, and produces explanations orders of magnitude smaller than the underlying shield. Key limitations include the dependence on user-defined predicates for compact trees (automated predicate generation is left as future work), the requirement of an accurate abstract world model, and the absence of a user study validating that the explanations are actually comprehensible to non-specialists.
