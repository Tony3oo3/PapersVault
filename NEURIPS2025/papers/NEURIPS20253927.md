## Problem

Frontier reasoning models that expose their generated traces are vulnerable to distillation, where competitors can train smaller models on those traces to replicate capabilities at a fraction of the original training cost. Existing defenses (e.g., truncating traces, hiding logits) either degrade user experience or are static and can be reverse-engineered. The paper asks: can a model owner adjust its sampling distribution to poison distillation attempts while preserving its own task performance?

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt, a teacher model (e.g., DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B), and a smaller proxy model (e.g., Qwen2.5-3B) whose downstream-loss gradient ∇ℓ(θ_P) is precomputed once on a holdout set of reasoning traces. For each autoregressive step, it performs two forward passes through the proxy model (at θ_P ± ε∇ℓ) to compute a per-token penalty vector over the full vocabulary, then samples the next token from the teacher's temperature-scaled log-probabilities plus this penalty weighted by λ. The output is a complete reasoning trace that the teacher can still solve correctly (maintaining high accuracy) but that, when used to fine-tune a student via LoRA, causes the student's holdout loss to increase rather than decrease.

## Method

The core derivation starts from the observation that a single gradient-descent step on the proxy model toward a teacher-generated token changes the proxy's downstream loss by Δ(x_{t+1}|x_{1:t}), and the goal is to sample tokens that maximize this Δ while remaining probable under the teacher. Taking the η→0 limit converts Δ into the inner product ⟨∇ℓ(θ_P), ∇_{θ_P} log p(x_{t+1}|x_{1:t};θ_P)⟩, a Jacobian-vector product. The key efficiency insight is exploiting inner-product symmetry to rewrite this as a central finite difference in the log-probability direction: Δ̂(·|x_{1:t}) = [log p(·;θ_P+ε∇ℓ) − log p(·;θ_P−ε∇ℓ)] / 2ε, which requires only two forward passes through the proxy per token rather than V separate gradient evaluations. The final sampling distribution is softmax((1/τ)·log p_teacher(·) + λ·Δ̂(·)), where λ provides a tunable trade-off between teacher utility and distillation poisoning, and the entire procedure is fully dynamic (each token's penalty depends on the evolving context), unlike static logit-corruption defenses.

## Results

On MMLU, a teacher model at 72% accuracy yields a distilled student at 52% under greedy sampling; increasing temperature (causing a ~4% teacher drop) leaves the student unchanged at 52%, whereas antidistillation sampling with the same 4% teacher drop reduces the student to 40%. On GSM8K, temperature sampling requires the teacher to degrade to 20% accuracy before the student falls below its undistilled baseline, while antidistillation sampling achieves the same student degradation while the teacher retains 70% accuracy. In the high-utility regime (90%→89% teacher accuracy), antidistillation sampling drops the student from 65% to 56%, while temperature sampling produces no measurable student degradation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is temperature sampling, which degrades teacher performance by increasing the sampling temperature τ. Antidistillation sampling dominates temperature sampling across all benchmarks: for any given reduction in teacher accuracy, antidistillation produces substantially lower student accuracy. On GSM8K, the gap is dramatic—temperature sampling requires the teacher to collapse to 20% accuracy to push the student below its undistilled baseline, while antidistillation achieves the same effect at 70% teacher accuracy. A secondary baseline, permutation sampling (which randomly permutes and sign-flips the Δ perturbations while preserving their statistical properties), confirms that the gradient information in Δ is essential: permutation sampling fails to poison distillation, validating that the computational overhead of computing Δ is necessary. The method also generalizes across model families (Qwen teacher → Llama student, all-Qwen, all-Llama) and across proxy-student size mismatches (1.5B proxy with 3B student, 7B proxy with 3B student), maintaining comparable poisoning effectiveness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of data poisoning and controlled decoding, repurposing the gradient-based poisoning framework to protect against distillation specifically. Prior distillation-prevention work (Ma et al.'s Undistillable, Stingy Teacher) relies on static logit corruption or top-k truncation, which a distiller can invert by saving input-output pairs; watermarking methods share this static vulnerability. Antidistillation sampling departs by making the perturbation fully dynamic and per-token, driven by a hidden proxy model's downstream-loss gradient, analogous to a cryptographic stream cipher. The controlled-decoding literature (contrastive decoding, energy-based constraints) provides the sampling framework, but no prior work introduces a distillation-aware penalization term computed via finite-difference approximation of a directional derivative.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

For frontier model operators, this provides a principled, tunable mechanism to limit distillation quality without substantially degrading user-facing performance—a trade-off that static methods cannot achieve. Key limitations include the computational overhead (two proxy forward passes per teacher token), the requirement to specify a downstream loss, and experiments restricted to 3B-parameter models across three benchmarks. Open questions include whether adversarial distillers can adapt to the dynamic perturbation, how the method scales to 70B+ models, and whether the proxy-student generalization holds when architectures diverge substantially in size or family.
