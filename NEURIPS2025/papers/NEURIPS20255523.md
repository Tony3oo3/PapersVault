## Problem

Reward modeling for LLM alignment is bottlenecked by the high cost of collecting human preference data, and existing textual synthesis methods (LLM-based response generation followed by LLM-based preference annotation) are computationally expensive, scaling quadratically with the number of candidates per prompt. No prior work has explored synthesizing preference data directly in the latent embedding space of language models, bypassing text generation and annotation entirely.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255523/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes 4096-dimensional LLM embeddings (final hidden states from Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct) of prompt-response pairs organized as preference triplets, and produces synthetic preference pairs as embedding vectors (not text) that are compositional cross-products of original and perturbed positive/negative embeddings. The downstream reward model is a 2-layer MLP (256 hidden dim) that maps a single embedding to a scalar reward score, requiring only 0.5M parameters versus 8B for text-based baselines. The VAE latent space is 16-dimensional, and synthesis adds Gaussian noise (σ²=0.01) to latent vectors before decoding back to the 4096-dim embedding space.

## Method

LENS trains a VAE with a contrastive divergence loss on LLM response embeddings: the encoder maps each embedding to a 16-dim Gaussian posterior, and a Wasserstein-distance-based divergence term (weight γ=0.1) maximizes separation between the latent distributions of preferred and non-preferred responses while a KL term (β=1.0) regularizes toward a standard normal prior. Synthesis proceeds by adding Gaussian noise to each original latent vector, selecting the top-k candidates by likelihood under the learned posterior, and decoding them back to the embedding space; synthetic pairs are then formed by compositional cross-pairing of all positive (original + synthetic) with all negative (original + synthetic) embeddings. A lightweight MLP reward model is trained on the augmented embedding pairs using the Bradley-Terry pairwise loss. The key architectural choice is sharing the VAE encoder/decoder between positive and negative paths while using separate final projection layers for their respective distribution parameters. The paper provides two theoretical guarantees: Theorem 1 bounds the reward difference of synthetic pairs relative to originals by O(σ_noise·√d_VAE + ε_rec), and Theorem 2 shows that augmentation reduces the estimation error of the reward model when the original dataset exceeds a threshold size.

## Results

Using Best-of-N (n=16) sampling evaluated by the Skywork gold reward model, LENS achieves a reward score of 2.20±0.12 on HH-RLHF and 1.48±0.07 on TL;DR at 8× augmentation, outperforming the strongest textual synthesis baseline (Fully fine-tuned) by 0.27 and 0.25 respectively. At 4× augmentation, the gap widens on TL;DR (1.44 vs. 0.97 for Fully fine-tuned), demonstrating that latent-space synthesis scales more effectively with augmentation. Computationally, LENS requires only 0.5M parameters (vs. 8B for text-based synthesis) and reduces generation time from 3.6h to 0.2h (18× speedup) on a single A100 GPU.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LENS outperforms all three self-labeling textual baselines across every augmentation scale and both datasets. At 8× on HH-RLHF, LENS (2.20) beats Self-rewarding [12] (1.77) by 0.43, Self-evolved [13] (1.63) by 0.57, and IPO [14] (1.32) by 0.88. The advantage is even more pronounced on TL;DR at 8×, where LENS (1.48) surpasses Self-rewarding (0.95), Self-evolved (0.75), and IPO (0.62) by margins of 0.53, 0.73, and 0.86 respectively. Notably, Self-evolved and IPO actually degrade below their original-data baselines at higher augmentation scales (e.g., IPO drops from 1.49 to 1.32 on HH-RLHF), whereas LENS improves monotonically with more synthetic data. LENS also dominates the simpler latent-space baselines: Direct perturbation plateaus around 1.46 on HH-RLHF and Gaussian sampling collapses to 0.94, confirming that the structured VAE latent space is essential for generating preference-preserving synthetic pairs.

## Relation to Prior Work

LENS departs from the dominant paradigm of textual-space preference synthesis (self-rewarding, self-evolved, IPO), which requires expensive LLM generation and annotation, by instead operating entirely in the LLM's embedding space. It builds on the embedding-based reward modeling line (Q-Probe, PCA-based preference learning) that showed lightweight MLP heads over frozen LLM embeddings are competitive with full fine-tuning, and repurposes VAEs—previously used for latent-space outlier synthesis in vision (VOS, non-parametric outlier synthesis by the same authors)—as a structured generative model for preference embeddings. The Wasserstein-distance divergence loss connecting positive and negative latent distributions is a novel architectural choice not seen in prior VAE-for-augmentation work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 16,000× model-size reduction (0.5M VAE vs. 8B LLM) and 18× speedup while outperforming text-based synthesis make reward-model data augmentation accessible to resource-constrained labs, potentially democratizing RLHF pipelines. The theoretical guarantees (bounded preference-ordering preservation and reduced estimation error) provide a principled foundation absent in prior heuristic augmentation methods. Open questions include whether the approach extends to multi-dimensional preference structures (beyond pairwise), whether it remains effective as base-model scale increases beyond 8B, and how sensitive the method is to the quality of the frozen LLM embeddings themselves.
