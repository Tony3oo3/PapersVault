## Problem

Existing test-time detoxification methods for LLMs rely on sparse toxicity annotations and static or heuristic interventions, leading to imprecise representation editing that fails to capture the nuanced transition space between toxic and non-toxic outputs. This results in suboptimal reward scores and detoxification performance, as the methods cannot provide stable, fine-grained guidance for steering representations toward non-toxic regions.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a text prompt and the final-layer hidden representations of the base LLM (token-level vectors h_{[M+t]}^{x,y≤t}), operating entirely within the latent representation space without modifying model weights or token probabilities. It produces detoxified text by editing each token's representation in-place during autoregressive generation, guided by a lightweight 2-layer MLP reward model that assigns a scalar toxicity score to every token representation. The editing is constrained to a single dominant non-toxic direction (first PCA component) plus a small number of gradient steps, keeping inference overhead minimal.

## Method

ARGRE first identifies a dominant non-toxic direction via PCA on the last-token representation differences between matched toxic/non-toxic pairs, then performs linear interpolation along this direction to generate N_in dense transition trajectories that convert sparse pairwise annotations into fine-grained supervision. An autoregressive reward model (2-layer MLP on the final transformer layer) is trained on these trajectories using a Bradley-Terry-style pairwise loss, decomposing the overall reward into a sum of token-level representation rewards for precise per-token guidance. At inference, an adaptive two-step editing strategy is applied per token: (1) directional steering that shifts the representation along the non-toxic direction by an amount proportional to the gap between the current token reward and the average non-toxic reward, and (2) lightweight gradient ascent (5 iterations, η=0.5) to further maximize the reward. This design avoids the tedious 200-step gradient loops of prior methods while achieving up to 62.21% toxicity reduction with only ~1.2s additional inference overhead on LLaMA-30B.

## Results

ARGRE achieves up to 62.21% toxicity reduction across 8 LLMs (355M–30B parameters), significantly outperforming the best baseline GenARM (42.98% reduction). On LLaMA-7B, toxicity drops from 43.27% to 18.06% (ARGRE w/iter) or 19.49% (ARGRE w/o iter), while perplexity increases by only 5.39 (vs. 8.53 for GenARM). Inference efficiency is strong: ARGRE (w/o iter) runs at 8.20s per 128 tokens on LLaMA-30B (nearly identical to the original 8.14s), and even with iterative refinement (9.30s) it is 47.58% faster than GenARM (18.94s) and 6.3× faster than Re-Control (58.69s). Capability is preserved with only a 0.52 average PPLw increase and a +0.06% average zero-shot accuracy change.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ARGRE outperforms all five test-time baselines across all 8 LLMs on toxicity. Against GenARM (the strongest baseline at 42.98% average reduction), ARGRE achieves 58.25% average reduction on the LLaMA series and 61.56% on GPT-2 Medium; even the no-iteration variant (59.63%) surpasses GenARM. ProFS (weight editing) achieves only 27.88% reduction and degrades most on larger models (27.51% on LLaMA), while also causing the largest capability loss (2.40% ACC drop). Re-Control (representation editing with 200 gradient iterations) achieves 25.53% reduction but is 6.3× slower at inference (58.69s vs. 9.30s on LLaMA-30B). RAD (guided decoding) reaches 35.95% reduction but is inconsistent across models (e.g., only 28.1% on LLaMA-7B). The training-time DPO method achieves just 20.73% reduction on LLaMA-7B, well below ARGRE's 58.27%. On secondary tasks, ARGRE also leads: 54.7% stereotype recognition accuracy (vs. GenARM 53.5%, ProFS 52.4%) and 73.0% jailbreak refusal rate (vs. GenARM 68.4%, ProFS 67.7%), competitive with dedicated jailbreak defenses like SemanticSmoothLLM (73.8%).

## Relation to Prior Work

ARGRE builds on the linear representation hypothesis (Park et al., ICML 2024), which posits that concepts like toxicity are encoded as linear directions in LLM hidden states, and on the standard reward-model architecture from RLHF (Ziegler et al., 2019). It departs from prior test-time detoxification methods—guided decoding (RAD, GenARM), weight editing (ProFS), and representation editing (Self-Detoxify, DeStein, Re-Control)—by explicitly modeling the *transition space* between toxic and non-toxic representations via PCA-identified directional interpolation, converting sparse pairwise annotations into dense token-level training signals. The autoregressive (token-level) reward model and the adaptive two-step editing strategy (directional steering followed by lightweight gradient ascent) are the paper's core methodological contributions, distinguishing it from trajectory-level reward models and heuristic static interventions used in prior work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ARGRE offers a practically attractive combination: near-zero inference overhead (8.20s vs. 8.14s for the original LLaMA-30B in the steering-only variant), strong detoxification (up to 62.21% toxicity reduction), and minimal capability degradation, making it a viable drop-in for production LLM deployment. The data-efficiency finding—that 100 annotations suffice to outperform baselines trained on 2,000—lowers the barrier for organizations with limited annotation budgets. Key open questions include whether the single-PCA-component direction captures the full geometry of toxicity (the authors acknowledge this limitation), whether the reward model can be made to generalize across model families (currently it degrades significantly cross-architecture), and whether the white-box assumption limits real-world applicability to API-only models.
