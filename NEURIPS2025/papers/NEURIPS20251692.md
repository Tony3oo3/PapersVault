## Problem

Existing uncertainty quantification methods for deep regression either produce only prediction intervals (discarding distributional shape information such as multimodality and asymmetry) or require full Bayesian retraining of the model architecture. There is no post-hoc method that yields both well-calibrated prediction intervals and the full conditioned predictive distribution without modifying or retraining the underlying predictive model.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251692/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

MCNF takes as input a pre-trained deep regression model with dropout layers (e.g., a Deep Quantile Regressor or a GNN) and a set of input features x. At inference, it first draws n_MCD Monte Carlo Dropout forward passes to build a context vector c = {ȳ_MCD, log s²(ȳ_MCD), h(x)}, where h(x) is an internal hidden-layer representation of the base model. The output is the full predictive distribution p(y|x,D) from which arbitrary quantiles, samples, and pointwise density estimates can be extracted, making it strictly more informative than interval-only methods.

## Method

MCNF is a post-hoc, distribution-free UQ method that decouples uncertainty estimation from the base predictive model. It uses MCD sampling to generate a prior over predictions, then models the prediction error δ = y − y_MCD with a shallow Neural Spline Flow conditioned on a context vector combining the first two moments of the MCD posterior and an internal representation h(x) of the regression model. Training minimizes a forward KL divergence loss with temperature-scaled importance weights w_n = σ(−log p_MCD(y_n|x_n)/τ) that down-weight outliers relative to the MCD prior, preventing overfitting to anomalous observations. At inference, the procedure is hierarchical: n_MCD dropout forward passes produce the context, the NF then generates n_NF error samples via its forward pass, and final predictions are obtained by adding sampled errors to randomly selected MCD prior values. The method requires no retraining of the base model and generalizes across architectures, as demonstrated on both feed-forward regressors and graph neural networks.

## Results

Across 9 datasets, MCNF achieves the lowest MAE among all well-calibrated methods (e.g., 0.078 on Boston Housing, 0.085 on Concrete, 0.202 on Protein, 0.103 on Superconductivity) while maintaining ~90% marginal coverage (0.886–0.938). MCNF also yields the narrowest prediction interval sizes among methods that meet the 90% coverage target (e.g., Δ=0.409 on Boston Housing vs. 0.820 for CQR and 0.777 for MCCP). MCD, while achieving similarly low MAE, suffers from severely under-coverage (e.g., 0.726 on Boston Housing, 0.341 on Abalone), making its intervals unreliable. On the multimodal Romano-Mod synthetic dataset, MCNF captures heteroskedastic and multimodal predictive distributions that CQR and MCCP cannot represent.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MCNF is compared against five baselines: CQR, MCCP, MCD, MCQR, and the base DQR model. Against CQR and MCCP (conformal prediction methods), MCNF achieves comparable marginal coverage (~90%) but substantially smaller interval sizes (e.g., Δ=0.409 vs. 0.820 on Boston Housing; Δ=0.791 vs. 0.847 on Superconductivity) and much lower MAE (e.g., 0.078 vs. 0.454 on Boston Housing; 0.103 vs. 0.283 on Superconductivity), because conformal methods apply a homogeneous shift to both quantile bounds. Against MCD, MCNF dramatically improves coverage (e.g., 0.904 vs. 0.726 on Boston Housing; 0.886 vs. 0.341 on Abalone) while maintaining similar MAE, confirming that the NF correction properly accounts for aleatoric uncertainty that MCD alone misses. Against MCQR and DQR (quantile-based methods without conformalization), MCNF again wins on MAE and interval size while matching coverage. The ablation variant NF (MCNF without epistemic uncertainty propagation via MCD resampling) shows consistent degradation across all datasets, confirming that the MCD-based prior is essential for well-calibrated intervals.

## Relation to Prior Work

MCNF sits at the intersection of two well-established UQ paradigms: Monte Carlo Dropout (Gal & Ghahramani, 2016) for approximating epistemic uncertainty, and Normalizing Flows (Papamakarios et al., 2021) for flexible density estimation. It departs from conformal prediction methods (CQR, MCCP) that produce only prediction intervals without distributional shape, and from full/variational Bayesian approaches that require architectural modifications and retraining. The key conceptual contribution is the contextualization mechanism: using MCD summary statistics (mean, log-variance) and internal model representations as conditioning inputs to a Neural Spline Flow that models prediction errors, thereby decoupling UQ from the base model's training entirely.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MCNF offers a practical post-hoc route to full predictive distributions (not just intervals) for any pre-trained regression model with dropout, which is valuable in safety-critical domains where multimodal or asymmetric uncertainty shapes decisions. The demonstration that it generalizes to GNNs and adapts to underfitted base models broadens its applicability. However, the method still requires the base model to have dropout layers, incurs MCD sampling overhead at inference, and relies on a normality assumption when summarizing MCD samples to two moments. Open questions include extending the formalism to classification, reducing the number of MCD samples needed, and whether the contextualization strategy transfers to architectures without natural internal representations.
