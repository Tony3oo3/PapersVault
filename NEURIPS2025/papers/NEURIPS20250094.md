## Problem

Many promising AI research ideas fail when implemented, but the only way to discover this is to actually run experiments, incurring substantial human labor and compute costs. No prior work provides a systematic benchmark or method for predicting which of two competing ideas will empirically outperform the other before implementation, and it is unclear whether language models can acquire this research intuition more effectively than human experts.

## Input / Output

The system takes a research goal (a set of benchmarks with quantitative metrics), two competing idea descriptions in a standardized summary format (with all empirical results explicitly stripped to prevent leakage), and retrieved full-paper summaries as context. It produces a binary prediction of which idea wins on more benchmarks. To mitigate order bias, the system evaluates both input orderings and only counts a prediction as correct if both are consistent; inconsistent predictions are scored as incorrect. Idea pairs are constructed within the same paper to control for confounding factors like hyperparameters, and the ground-truth label is determined by majority voting across at least three benchmarks.

## Method

The system combines an agentic retrieval module with a fine-tuned GPT-4.1. The retrieval agent iteratively generates natural-language queries (decomposing novel ideas into sub-components rather than searching for identical comparisons), retrieves top-15 papers via EXA's neural search (restricted to pre-cutoff publications), summarizes each full paper PDF relative to the query (rather than reusing abstracts, which biases toward older ideas), and filters irrelevant summaries. The fine-tuned model is trained on 6,000 historical idea pairs with raw binary outcome labels; notably, self-augmented chain-of-thought fine-tuning provides no gain over raw-label fine-tuning, likely because the base model's near-chance zero-shot performance yields unsound CoTs. The key finding is that fine-tuning on past ideas is essential for capability elicitation: GPT-4.1 jumps from 51.9% to 77% accuracy, while off-the-shelf frontier models (o3, Claude 3.5 Sonnet) with the same retrieval augmentation remain at chance level.

## Results

The fine-tuned GPT-4.1 system with paper retrieval achieves 77.0% accuracy on the full 1,444-pair test set, compared to 51.9% for zero-shot GPT-4.1 with the same retrieval augmentation. On the 45-pair NLP expert subset, the system reaches 64.4% accuracy, substantially outperforming human experts (48.9% majority vote, 60.0% best-performing researcher per topic). On 33 unpublished novel idea pairs from an AI ideation study, the system achieves 63.6% accuracy, demonstrating generalization beyond published literature.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The system decisively outperforms human NLP experts on the 45-pair expert subset: 64.4% vs. 48.9% for the majority-vote baseline (a 15.5-point gap) and 64.4% vs. 60.0% for the ceiling baseline using the best-performing researcher per topic. Off-the-shelf frontier LMs (o3, Claude 3.5 Sonnet) perform at or near random chance (~50%) even when augmented with the identical paper retrieval agent, highlighting that the task requires fine-tuning rather than prompting. Zero-shot GPT-4.1 with retrieval achieves only 51.9%, and fine-tuning on 6,000 historical idea pairs lifts this to 77.0%, confirming the importance of capability elicitation. Self-augmented chain-of-thought fine-tuning (random or LM-selected CoTs) actually degrades performance to ~48%, suggesting the base model's near-chance reasoning produces unhelpful training signals. On the stress tests, the system shows minimal sensitivity to superficial features (recency, length, famous lab names), with accuracy variance across subsets remaining small, whereas humans are more prone to these biases.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of AI-for-science and forecasting. It departs from prior research evaluation work (which predicts subjective peer-review scores on full papers) by focusing on objective empirical effectiveness of idea pairs, and from the closest prior outcome-prediction work (Luo et al. 2025 on neuroscience) by requiring prediction between two independently written idea summaries with no result leakage. Methodologically, it draws on the neural forecasting literature (Halawi et al., Zou et al.) that demonstrated gains from retrieval augmentation plus fine-tuning on historical data, and repurposes that recipe for the specific domain of AI research ideas. The agentic retrieval module extends prior RAG-for-research approaches (which typically reuse abstracts) by summarizing full papers relative to the query.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a fine-tuned GPT-4.1 with retrieval (77% accuracy) substantially outperforms both off-the-shelf frontier models (near chance) and human NLP experts (48.9% majority, 60.0% ceiling) on predicting which research idea will work better is practically significant for accelerating empirical AI research and could serve as a reward model for automated ideation pipelines. Key limitations include the small-scale expert comparison (45 pairs), the small unpublished-idea evaluation (33 pairs), and the black-box nature of the system that limits interpretability. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes beyond NLP to other domains, whether it can be integrated into a full automated research loop as a reward signal, and whether more sophisticated modeling (e.g., inference-time experiment simulation) could push accuracy further.
