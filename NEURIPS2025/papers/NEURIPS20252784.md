## Problem

Modern state-space models rely on diagonal transition matrices for efficient parallel computation, but this structural constraint prevents them from emulating non-solvable finite-state automata, a fundamental limitation for algorithmic state tracking. Unstructured (dense) transition matrices achieve full FSA expressivity with a single layer and state size N, but their Θ(LN³) parallel-scan cost makes them impractical. The paper asks whether a structured sparse parametrization can match the expressivity of dense matrices while retaining the Θ(LN) computational cost of diagonal SSMs.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a sequence of input vectors u_t ∈ ℝ^D (token embeddings, multivariate time-series values, or sentence embeddings from a frozen Qwen-2.5-1.5B encoder) and produces a real-valued output o_t ∈ ℝ^D at each timestep via a linear readout of size N×N applied to the complex-valued hidden state x_t ∈ ℂ^N. The transition matrix A(u_t) is time-varying and input-dependent, while B, C, and D are fixed. In the natural-language state-tracking benchmark, each state transition is triggered by a variable-length English sentence rather than a single token, making the effective sequence length per transition non-uniform.

## Method

PD-SSM parametrizes the time-varying transition matrix as A(u_t) = P(u_t)D(u_t), where P is a binary column-one-hot matrix and D is a complex-valued diagonal matrix. P is generated by soft-selecting from a dictionary of K trainable N×N transition matrices via softmax weights, then applying a column-wise hardmax to enforce sparsity; D is generated by two single-hidden-layer MLPs producing magnitude (sigmoid, in (0,1)) and phase (2π·sigmoid, in (0,2π)) components. The key algebraic insight is that the set of one-hot column matrices H^{N×N} forms a monoid under multiplication, so chained products can be computed via parallel associative scans in Θ(LN) operations and memory—matching diagonal SSMs—rather than the Θ(LN³) cost of dense matrices. A surrogate gradient (softmax in the backward pass) handles the non-differentiable hardmax, and the sigmoid-bounded magnitudes guarantee BIBO stability. Theoretically, a single PD-SSM layer with state dimension N and an N×N linear readout can exactly emulate any N-state FSA, achieving the (almost) minimal state size possible.

## Results

On the four FSA state-tracking tasks (Cycle Navigation, Even Pairs, Modular Arithmetic, Parity), PD-SSM achieves 98.8±0.9% average validation accuracy with length generalization up to 256 tokens, substantially outperforming the second-best parallelizable methods (DPLR-SLiCE and D-DE-SLiCE at ~85.1%). On non-solvable group automata (A₅ and S₅), PD-SSM reaches 100% accuracy with a single layer across all tested configurations, while complex diagonal SSMs fail entirely and Gated DeltaProduct degrades to 1.23% on the hardest S₅(32) task. On UEA-MTSCA long-sequence time-series classification, PD-SSM achieves 67.0±2.02% average accuracy, within standard error of the best SSM baseline (LinOSS-IM at 67.8%). On the Long-Range Arena, PD-SSM attains 74.4% average, the best among time-varying SSMs (vs. Mamba 66.4%, S7 73.9%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PD-SSM decisively outperforms all parallelizable SSM baselines on FSA state tracking: it beats the strongest DPLR variants (DPLR-SLiCE 85.1%, D-DE-SLiCE 85.1%) by ~14 points and Gated DeltaProduct (80.7%) by ~18 points on average. Against diagonal SSMs (Mamba 58.9%, complex diagonal 73.6%), the gap is even larger (20–40 points), confirming the theoretical prediction that diagonal matrices cannot emulate non-solvable automata. On non-solvable group tasks (A₅, S₅), PD-SSM achieves 100% with one layer while complex diagonal SSMs score 15.5% on the simplest A₅(2) task and Gated DeltaProduct degrades from 97.9% to 1.23% as the number of generating permutations increases. On UEA-MTSCA time-series classification, PD-SSM (67.0%) is within standard error of the best SSM (LinOSS-IM 67.8%) and clearly outperforms Mamba (58.6%), S6 (62.0%), and NCDE (60.2%). On LRA, PD-SSM (74.4%) edges out S7 (73.9%) and Mamba (66.4%) among time-varying SSMs, though both remain well below time-invariant SSMs (S4 84.0%, LRU 84.7%). In the natural-language state-tracking experiment with a frozen Qwen-2.5-1.5B backbone, PD-SSM converges within 10,000 steps on both Parity and A₅, while real and complex diagonal SSMs fail to learn the task even on in-domain sequence lengths.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two threads in the SSM literature: (1) the expressivity analysis showing that diagonal SSMs cannot emulate non-solvable automata (Merrill et al. 2024) and that solvable automata require depth proportional to Krohn-Rhodes complexity (Sarrof et al. 2024), and (2) the line of work on unstructured SSMs (SD-SSM, Terzić et al. 2025) that achieve full FSA expressivity at O(LN³) cost. The PD parametrization departs from both by introducing a structured sparse matrix class (column-one-hot × complex diagonal) that forms a monoid under multiplication, enabling O(LN) parallel scans while retaining the ability to represent any N-state FSA in a single layer. It also builds on the DPLR family (DeltaNet, Gated DeltaProduct, RWKV-7) as the main efficiency-competitive baseline, and on the associative scan framework (Blelloch 1990; Smith et al. 2023) for parallelizable recurrence.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper closes a previously open gap: no prior structured SSM achieved both O(LN) parallel-scan cost and single-layer, state-size-N universal FSA emulation with a provable BIBO-stability guarantee. The natural-language state-tracking benchmark (where transitions are encoded as variable-length English sentences) is a useful stress test that reveals a practical learnability gap even for theoretically-capable diagonal SSMs. Key open questions include whether the P-matrix generation overhead (7× slower than diagonal at D=5632) can be eliminated, whether the approach scales to large-scale pretraining, and whether the monoid structure admits further algebraic generalizations beyond column-one-hot matrices.
