## Problem

Existing LLM benchmarks evaluate factual recall, textbook problem solving, or code synthesis, but none capture the distinctive demands of real-world engineering design—synthesizing domain knowledge, navigating multi-objective trade-offs, and producing functional designs validated by domain-specific simulators. This gap leaves it unclear whether LLMs can actually perform the open-ended, constraint-driven design work that practicing engineers do daily.

## Input / Output

Each task provides a detailed engineering design prompt (averaging 778.71 tokens, roughly 10× longer than typical QA benchmarks) specifying design objectives, constraints, and performance requirements; 23 of 101 tasks additionally include images. LLMs are constrained via the instructor/Pydantic framework to emit a structured response with two fields: a free-text reasoning trace and a ConfigFile object containing typed design parameters or code snippets. The evaluation pipeline parses these fields, feeds them into domain-specific simulators (SPICE, MATLAB Control System Toolbox, finite element solvers, etc.), and returns a binary pass/fail flag, a 0–100 partial-credit score decomposed across per-task rubric items, and a detailed simulation log.

## Method

ENGDESIGN introduces a simulation-based evaluation paradigm that replaces static answer matching with dynamic, programmatic functional verification: for each of 101 tasks across 9 engineering domains, a human-authored evaluation script extracts key design components from the LLM's structured output, runs them through the relevant engineering simulator, and scores the result against a multi-item rubric (473 gradable items total) that awards partial credit for meeting subsets of requirements. The benchmark was constructed through a multi-stage pipeline—initial task proposals from 40+ contributors, automated LLM-based pre-filtering, first-round peer review, and second-round review by domain faculty experts—yielding 101 accepted tasks each accompanied by a validated reference design. Beyond single-shot evaluation, the paper also implements an iterative design protocol in which the LLM receives its prior output plus evaluation feedback (scores, metrics, diagnostic logs) and is prompted to refine the design, revealing that performance can improve substantially over multiple iterations (e.g., o3 reaches ~60% pass rate after 10 iterations) while certain domains like Analog IC design remain unsolved even after repeated refinement.

## Results

On ENGDESIGN, no evaluated model surpasses a 35% overall average pass rate, with the best-performing model o3 achieving 34.38% and o4-mini-high reaching 34.04%. Reasoning models consistently outperform chat models: the top reasoning models (o3, o4-mini-high, o3-high) achieve 33–34% pass rates, while the best chat model (Claude-3.7-Sonnet) reaches only 22.61% and GPT-4o/Gemini-2.0-Flash hover around 14–16%. Analog IC Design is the hardest domain, with every model scoring 0% pass rate. Reasoning robustness (consistency across 3 trials) further separates the groups: o1, o3, and o4-mini-high achieve robustness scores of 0.62, 0.61, and 0.57 respectively, versus 0.20 for Gemini-2.0-Flash and 0.35 for DeepSeek-v3.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 12 LLMs (4 chat, 8 reasoning) on ENGDESIGN's 101 tasks. Reasoning models dominate: o3 (34.38%) and o4-mini-high (34.04%) lead overall, outperforming the best chat model Claude-3.7-Sonnet (22.61%) by roughly 12 percentage points, and GPT-4o (15.68%) by nearly 19 points. Notably, Claude-3.7-Thinking (20.07%) underperforms its non-thinking counterpart Claude-3.7-Sonnet (22.61%), breaking the general trend that extended reasoning helps. DeepSeek-R1 (25.53%) and Gemini-2.5-Pro (29.54%) occupy the middle tier, with Gemini-2.5-Pro excelling in Structure Design (50%) but failing entirely on Analog IC and Mechanical tasks. All 12 models score 0% on Analog IC Design, indicating a hard ceiling for current LLMs in that domain. In terms of reasoning robustness, o1 (0.62) and o3 (0.61) are roughly 3× more consistent than Gemini-2.0-Flash (0.20), a gap larger than what pass rates alone would suggest.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing a simulation-based evaluation paradigm for LLMs, departing fundamentally from the QA/factual-recall paradigm that dominates existing engineering benchmarks (ControlBench, VerilogEval, AnalogCoder, etc.). While prior domain-specific benchmarks test narrow procedural knowledge or code snippet correctness, ENGDESIGN reframes the evaluation task as open-ended, constraint-driven design synthesis verified through domain-specific simulators (SPICE, MATLAB, FEA). The structured-output approach via the instructor library and the partial-credit rubric system are methodological contributions that enable this paradigm shift, but the core novelty is the task formulation itself: evaluating whether an LLM can produce a *functional* engineering design rather than recall a textbook answer.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ENGDESIGN fills a critical gap by providing the first multi-domain, simulation-verified benchmark for engineering design, and its findings expose a substantial capability gap: even frontier reasoning models (o3, o4-mini) achieve only ~34% overall pass rate, and Analog IC design yields 0% across all models. The observation that reasoning robustness gaps (up to 3×) exceed pass-rate gaps (2×) between reasoning and chat models suggests consistency, not just peak performance, is the harder problem. Open questions include whether iterative feedback loops can close the gap in domains like Analog IC, how to reduce the token-cost overhead of reasoning models for practical deployment, and whether domain-specific tool integration (beyond prompting) is necessary to bridge the remaining capability gap.
