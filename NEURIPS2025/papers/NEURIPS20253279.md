## Problem

Adaptive testing requires selecting a small question subset that minimizes ability estimation error, but this is a nested optimization problem where the outer loop selects questions and the inner loop re-estimates ability. Existing approaches rely on data-intensive meta-learning or reinforcement learning policies, or on first-order gradient-matching heuristics that scale poorly with large item pools, making them impractical for real-time, large-scale online assessments.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-calibrated question pool with IRT parameters (discrimination and difficulty), the current ability estimate θ^t (initialized and updated sequentially), and behavioral noise parameters (guessing probability π_g, slipping probability π_s). It produces a sequentially selected subset of T questions, an MLE-based ability estimate, and a bias-corrected ability estimate that explicitly subtracts the expected distortion from label flipping. For neural network ability models (e.g., NeuralCDM), the Hessian inverse is approximated via a quasi-Newton (BFGS) update rather than computed exactly.

## Method

CFAT reformulates the nested optimization as a data-reduction problem: instead of re-optimizing θ_S for each candidate subset, it defines a perturbed estimator that down-weights removed questions, then Taylor-expands the first-order optimality condition around θ* to derive a closed-form expression for the estimation error: ‖H⁻¹(S,θ*) Σ_{i∈S} ∇ℓ_i(θ*)‖. This objective balances first-order gradient alignment (minimizing directional bias) with second-order Hessian conditioning (maintaining estimation stability). The paper proves the objective is ε-submodular (with ε decreasing as the subset grows), justifying a greedy selection algorithm with bounded suboptimality. A bias-correction term is derived in closed form to quantify and subtract the expected distortion from guessing and slipping, enabling more reliable estimates under label noise. For deep ability models where exact Hessians are intractable, a quasi-Newton approximation maintains the same closed-form structure while remaining computationally feasible.

## Results

On the Student Performance Prediction task (Table 1, IRT model, @20 steps), CFAT achieves 74.29/75.22 (ACC/AUC) on ASSIST, 73.87/78.03 on NIPS-EDU, and 86.05/77.83 on EXAM, outperforming the best baseline (UATS) by up to 2.6 AUC points on NIPS-EDU. In the ability estimation simulation, CFAT reaches comparable MSE to random selection using only 30%–45% of the questions, and reduces the number of required questions by at least 15% compared to UATS. Computationally, CFAT achieves approximately 12× speedup over BECAT while matching the speed of classical Fisher information. Under 20% label-flipping noise, CFAT's bias correction reduces MSE from 0.4324 (without correction) to 0.2770, outperforming GMOCAT (0.3478).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CFAT outperforms all eight baselines across all three datasets and both ability estimation models (IRT and NeuralCDM). Against the strongest meta-learning baseline UATS, CFAT gains +0.15 ACC / +0.38 AUC on ASSIST, +2.03 ACC / +1.46 AUC on NIPS-EDU, and +1.14 ACC / +2.59 AUC on EXAM (IRT, @20). Against the gradient-based BECAT (the closest method in spirit), CFAT's advantage is larger on NIPS-EDU (+1.87 ACC) and EXAM (+1.85 ACC), confirming the benefit of incorporating second-order Hessian information over first-order gradients alone. Data-driven RL/meta-learning methods (NCAT, BOBCAT, GMOCAT) are consistently outperformed, supporting the paper's claim that a closed-form objective avoids the pitfalls of nested optimization. In the noise-robustness experiment (Table 2), CFAT with bias correction maintains MSE of 0.2770 under 20% label flipping, compared to 0.4324 without correction and 0.3478 for GMOCAT, demonstrating the practical value of the guessing/slipping correction. Computationally, CFAT is ~12× faster per question selection than BECAT and matches the speed of Fisher information while delivering ≥20% better estimation accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

CFAT repurposes influence function theory (originating in robust statistics, popularized in ML by Koh & Liang 2017) to derive a closed-form expression for the ability estimation error as a function of the selected question subset, thereby eliminating the nested optimization structure that has defined adaptive testing since its inception. This departs from the dominant trajectory of the field—where recent work (BECAT, NCAT, BOBCAT, UATS, GMOCAT) has increasingly relied on first-order gradient matching, reinforcement learning, or meta-learning to solve the subset selection problem—by showing that a second-order (Hessian-aware) closed-form objective combined with a greedy algorithm suffices. The ε-submodularity guarantee (building on Das & Kempe 2018) provides the theoretical bridge from the closed-form objective to an efficient combinatorial solver, while the bias-correction mechanism for guessing/slipping extends the influence-function framework to handle label noise in a principled way.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for real-time, large-scale assessment systems (GRE, Duolingo, remote certifications) where computational efficiency and interpretability are critical: a 12× speedup over the best gradient-based heuristic and 10% fewer questions at equal accuracy directly reduce test-taking burden and infrastructure costs. However, the approach assumes IRT-structured loss (or relies on quasi-Newton approximations for neural models), and the behavioral noise model (fixed guessing/slipping probabilities) is relatively simple compared to context-dependent examinee behavior. Open questions include whether the closed-form formulation extends naturally to multidimensional IRT or neural cognitive diagnosis models with high-dimensional latent spaces, and whether richer behavioral signals (response time, clickstream) can be incorporated into the influence-function correction without losing the closed-form property.
