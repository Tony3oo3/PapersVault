## Problem

Existing safety-aligned LLMs are trained on fixed sets of known attack types and remain static after deployment, leaving them vulnerable to unseen jailbreaks that emerge during real-world use. Prior lifelong attack frameworks evaluate against static defenders and assume both sides are frozen, limiting their ability to simulate the competitive, evolving dynamic of real deployment. The paper asks whether a framework can efficiently generate evolving attacks against strong defense models while simultaneously providing continual training data to improve safety alignment.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes jailbreak research papers (10 PDFs) processed via the GPT-4o API to extract actionable attack strategies, 4k illegal instruction goals from PKU-SafeRLHF as the goal pool, and an initial Meta-Attacker (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B) paired with an initial Defender (RR, fine-tuned from LLaMA-3-8B-Instruct). It produces a progressively hardened Defender model that resists both seen and unseen attacks, along with discovered novel jailbreak strategies stored as (strategy, question, response, goal) tuples in success and failure buffers. The Meta-Attacker outputs single-turn jailbreak prompts, and the Defender outputs refusal responses; safety is judged by a dual safeguard (LLaMA-Guard-3-8B plus Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct as a proxy for human evaluation).

## Method

The framework operates as a two-stage adversarial-play evolution loop. In the Warm-Up Stage, GPT-4o extracts strategies from jailbreak papers, which initialize the Meta-Attacker to generate concrete jailbreak questions against the frozen Defender, populating success (B_s) and failure (B_f) buffers. In the Lifelong Safety Alignment Stage, the Meta-Attacker evolves via a beam-search process: it analyzes failed tuples in B_f, proposes revised or new strategies with Best-of-N (N=8) sampling, attacks the frozen Defender, and undergoes Reject Fine-Tuning (RAFT) on successful tuples at the midpoint and end of the loop to produce A_{t+1}. The Defender then evolves via refusal training: successful jailbreak questions from B_s are paired with refusals generated by a separate safety-aligned model (LLaMA-3-8B-Instruct) under a deliberate instruction, and the Defender is retrained from its original checkpoint M_0 (to mitigate catastrophic forgetting) on this safety dataset plus a helpfulness-maintaining set (20k UltraChat) and XSTest (to avoid over-refusal). This competitive co-evolution—where the attacker must continuously discover new strategies because the defender adapts—differs from prior lifelong attack methods that assume a static target, and the use of a reasoning model (DeepSeek-R1) as the Meta-Attacker enables the discovery of novel multi-step strategies (e.g., 'Series of Indirect & Technical Framing') that closely resemble advanced multi-turn jailbreaks not present in the input papers.

## Results

After two iterations of adversarial-play evolution, the Defender M2(RR) achieves 0.0% average ASR on all six seen attacks, down from 15.0% for the initial RR model (M0). On unseen attacks generated by the evolved Meta-Attacker, M2(RR) reduces the ASR to 7.0% (from 73.0% achieved by A1 against M0). On generalization attacks not included in the warm-up papers, M2(RR) lowers the average ASR from 24.0% (M0/RR) to 13.1%, while maintaining helpfulness at 56.32% average accuracy (vs. 56.20% for M0/RR). The Meta-Attacker also demonstrates strong transferability, achieving 60% ASR on LAT (a different safety-aligned model) by the second iteration.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to the initial RR defender (M0), the proposed lifelong framework dramatically reduces ASR on seen attacks from 15.0% to 0.0% after two iterations, with the first iteration alone dropping it to 0.2%. On generalization attacks, M2(RR) improves the average ASR from 24.0% to 13.1%, primarily driven by a large reduction on Simple Adaptive Attack (100% → 38%) and FewShot (3.75% → 1.25%), though AutoDAN-Turbo remains largely unchanged (~39–40%). Against LAT, M2(RR) matches its 0.0% seen-attack ASR but substantially outperforms it on helpfulness (56.32% vs. 51.25% average accuracy), while LAT retains an edge on generalization attacks (1.5% vs. 13.1% average ASR). The Meta-Attacker's transfer attacks also show that strategies discovered against RR generalize to LAT, with ASR rising from 39% (A0) to 60% (A2), confirming that the adversarial-play loop produces broadly effective jailbreaks rather than model-specific exploits.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the safety alignment paradigm from a one-shot training problem to a lifelong, adversarial co-evolutionary process. It builds directly on RR (Circuit Breakers) as the initial Defender, RAFT-style reject fine-tuning to evolve the Meta-Attacker, and refusal training for Defender updates, but departs from prior lifelong attack frameworks (AutoDAN-Turbo, AutoRT) that assume a static defender by introducing a competitive loop where both attacker and defender adapt iteratively. The adversarial-play concept is borrowed from RL alignment work (SPIN, EVA, SPPO) but, to the authors' knowledge, is applied to safety alignment for the first time, with GPT-4o used as a strategy-mining oracle to warm-start the attacker from existing jailbreak literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a critical deployment gap: safety-aligned models are trained on fixed attack distributions and remain vulnerable to unseen jailbreaks, as demonstrated by the GPT-4-1106/CodeAttack example. The competitive co-evolution framework provides a concrete, automatable pipeline for continuous safety improvement, and the emergent discovery of multi-turn-like strategies by A1 (without multi-turn training data) suggests the framework may surface genuinely novel attack vectors. Key limitations include only two training iterations (raising catastrophic forgetting concerns), reliance on SFT/RFT rather than RL, evaluation on 8B models, and the need for a large LLM (Qwen2.5-72B) as a safety judge proxy. Open questions include whether the paradigm scales to more iterations, larger models, and whether integrating RLVR-style training would further close the attacker-defender gap.
