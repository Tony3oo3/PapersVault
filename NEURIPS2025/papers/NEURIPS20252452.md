## Problem

Out-of-distribution generalization in graph neural networks typically relies on Invariant Risk Minimization (IRM), which demands explicit environment annotations or heuristically generated synthetic splits that are costly and often unavailable. This paper asks whether causal subgraphs can be identified without any IRM framework, by exploiting the observation that causal subgraphs exhibit substantially smaller distributional shifts across environments than non-causal components.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252452/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a standard graph (adjacency matrix A and node feature matrix X ∈ R^{n×d}) as input and produces two outputs: a causal subgraph Z (a subset of edges selected via top-r importance scores, with r typically 0.5) and a predicted class label. Unlike IRM-based methods, the training data need not be partitioned into labeled environments—samples from multiple environments are simply mixed, and the method only requires that the dataset exhibits some degree of cross-environment shift.

## Method

IDG (Invariant Distribution Generalization) uses GIN as the backbone for both a subgraph extractor and a predictor. The extractor generates node representations, scores each edge via an MLP on concatenated endpoint representations, and selects the top-r edges to form the causal subgraph. The key training innovation is a norm-guided invariant distribution objective: the extractor is trained with a -log(||H_Z||₂) term that maximizes the representation norm of the extracted subgraph, which—based on the paper's theoretical proof (Invariant Distribution Criterion) and empirical finding that norms decay under distribution shift—steers the extractor toward the minimally-shifting (causal) subgraph. A compactness entropy term encourages sparse subgraph selection, and training alternates between updating the predictor (extractor frozen) and the extractor (predictor frozen). The theoretical contribution formally proves that the causal subgraph uniquely minimizes cross-environment distribution divergence and ensures support coverage for stable classifier performance.

## Results

IDG achieves state-of-the-art performance on 15 out of 17 evaluation settings across GraphOOD and DrugOOD benchmarks. Headline results include 82.53% accuracy on Motif-basis, 83.67% on SST2-length, 55.32% on CMNIST-color, and 73.24% on HIV-scaffold. The method consistently outperforms the strongest baseline LIRS by 1–7 points across all datasets (e.g., 82.53 vs. 75.51 on Motif-basis, 55.32 vs. 49.87 on CMNIST-color). Ablation confirms the norm-guided objective contributes +1.7 to +3.9 points over the no-norm variant.

## Baselines & Comparisons

IDG outperforms all 10 baselines on the majority of datasets. Against the closest competitor LIRS [49], IDG wins on all 10 evaluation settings, with margins ranging from +0.2 (IC50-size: 69.97 vs. 69.78) to +7.0 (CMNIST-color: 55.32 vs. 49.87). Against LECI [9], which is limited to GraphOOD, IDG leads by +9.4 on Motif-basis (82.53 vs. 73.16) and +3.5 on CMNIST (55.32 vs. 51.80). IRM-based methods (IRM [1], DIR [44], GIL [22]) and risk-extrapolation methods (VREx [19], Coral [39]) are substantially outperformed, with gaps exceeding 20 points on CMNIST and 15+ points on Motif-basis. Notably, several OOD baselines (DIR, GIL, VREx) even underperform plain ERM on certain datasets, underscoring the practical difficulty of environment partitioning that IDG avoids entirely.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant IRM-based paradigm for graph OOD generalization (Arjovsky et al., Li et al./GIL, Wu et al./DIR, Chen et al./CIGA, Gui et al./LECI), which all require explicit or synthetic environment annotations. It builds on Kang et al.'s observation that distributional shift reduces network activations and representation norms, extending this finding to GNNs and formalizing it as a quantitative mechanism via the low-rank structure of GNN weight matrices. The subgraph extraction architecture (top-r edge selection) and compactness regularization are borrowed from GIL and PGExplainer, but the core novelty is the norm-guided invariant distribution objective that replaces IRM's bi-level optimization with a single norm-maximization criterion.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By eliminating the need for environment labels or synthetic environment generation, this work removes a major practical barrier to deploying OOD-robust GNNs in real-world settings where environment structure is unknown or costly to annotate. The Invariant Distribution Criterion provides a theoretically grounded alternative to IRM, though its reliance on Assumption 2 (sparse interventions affecting only non-causal parts) limits applicability to scenarios where causal features are genuinely stable. Open questions include whether the norm-based proxy generalizes beyond GIN backbones, whether the low-rank weight property holds under different training regimes, and how the method performs when the causal subgraph boundary is ambiguous (PIIF setting).
