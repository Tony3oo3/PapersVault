## Problem

Existing MLLM jailbreak defenses (Image-to-Text Translation, Safe Prompting, Multimodal Safety Tuning) align multimodal inputs with LLMs' built-in safeguards but fail to uncover the root mechanism of how harmful multimodal tokens trigger jailbreaks. This leaves them vulnerable to text-driven attacks, prone to overdefensive behavior on benign inputs, and dependent on costly safety-specific training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251942/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

SafePTR operates on the hidden states of an image + text instruction pair processed by an MLLM, alongside a fixed safety-aligned instruction (e.g., 'Please answer this question safely and accurately. Reply I am sorry if the content is harmful.') that serves as a semantic reference. It intervenes at specific early-middle transformer layers by computing per-token cosine distances to the safety reference, selecting the Top-K most divergent tokens (K=10% empirically), and pruning them before restoring benign features at the next layer. The output is a standard safe text response generated by the unmodified downstream layers.

## Method

SafePTR is a training-free, token-level defense with two decoupled modules. First, Harmful Token Pruning (HTP) identifies a narrow band of vulnerable early-middle layers (e.g., layers [7,9) for LLaVA-1.5-7B, [4,6) for DeepSeek-VL2) via layer-wise ablation, then removes the Top-K tokens whose hidden states deviate most from the safety-aligned instruction representation, suppressing harmful signals that drive semantic drift. Second, Benign Feature Restoration (BFR) maintains a parallel standard-inference branch through the same vulnerable layers and, at the first subsequent layer, selectively reinserts the pruned tokens' hidden states from this branch, recovering fine-grained contextual information while preserving the safety benefit. The key empirical finding motivating this design is that fewer than 1% of tokens in these early-middle layers are responsible for inducing unsafe behaviors, making targeted pruning sufficient without full-model safety tuning.

## Results

On the FigStep benchmark (vision-driven jailbreak), SafePTR achieves the lowest average ASR across all three MLLMs: 1.60% on LLaVA-1.5-7B, 3.60% on MiniGPT-4-7B, and 9.80% on DeepSeek-VL2, substantially outperforming the next-best method Immune (4.23%, 4.43%, N/A). On the text-driven JailbreakV-28K benchmark, SafePTR reduces average ASR to 1.3% (LLaVA-1.5), 12.6% (MiniGPT-4), and 10.1% (DeepSeek-VL2). On MM-SafetyBench, SafePTR achieves 2.3%, 8.0%, and 18.2% average ASR respectively. Crucially, SafePTR preserves or even improves utility: it scores 1538.1 on MME (LLaVA-1.5) and 53.0 on MM-Vet (DeepSeek-VL2), the highest among all compared methods, while requiring zero training and only ~0.15s additional inference latency.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SafePTR consistently outperforms all five baselines across all three safety benchmarks and all three MLLMs. Against Immune (the strongest prior method), SafePTR reduces average ASR from 4.23% to 1.60% on FigStep for LLaVA-1.5, from 4.43% to 3.60% on MiniGPT-4, and from 2.1% to 1.3% on JailbreakV-28K for LLaVA-1.5. Against AdaShield, the gap is even more dramatic: SafePTR achieves 1.60% vs. 13.00% on FigStep (LLaVA-1.5) and 9.80% vs. 51.80% on DeepSeek-VL2, where AdaShield is nearly ineffective. CoCA and ECSO are substantially weaker, with CoCA showing high ASR on non-visual categories (e.g., 44.15% on Legal Opinion) and ECSO performing close to the unmodified baseline on several models (e.g., 58.7% avg on MM-SafetyBench for MiniGPT-4). Notably, SafePTR also achieves the highest utility scores (MME: 1538.1 on LLaVA-1.5; MM-Vet: 53.0 on DeepSeek-VL2), whereas AdaShield significantly degrades utility (e.g., MME drops from 741.6 to 474.8 on MiniGPT-4), confirming that SafePTR's prune-then-restore design avoids the overdefensive behavior of prompt-based methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

SafePTR departs from the dominant paradigm of multimodal jailbreak defense, which aligns inputs with LLMs' inherited safety safeguards (via image-to-text translation, safe prompting, or safety tuning), by instead intervening directly on internal hidden states. It borrows the Top-K token selection strategy from Chen et al. (2024) for efficient token identification and adopts AdaShield's safety-aligned instructions as a semantic reference for measuring token-level deviation. Conceptually, the paper reframes the defense problem from 'make the input look safe to the LLM' to 'identify and remove the specific tokens that cause semantic drift from the safety manifold,' a shift enabled by their layer-wise and token-wise vulnerability analysis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that fewer than 1% of tokens in a narrow band of early-middle layers drive jailbreak success offers a concrete, interpretable target for defense that prior methods lacked, and the training-free, one-pass design makes it practical for deployment without retraining. However, the method requires access to intermediate hidden states, ruling out black-box APIs, and the fixed Top-K ratio may not adapt to inputs with varying risk levels. Open questions include whether the vulnerable-layer identification generalizes to new MLLM architectures and whether the pruning threshold can be made input-adaptive without additional computation.
