## Problem

Foundation models are rapidly being deployed across scientific workflows, yet the community lacks a unified framework to characterize whether they are merely accelerating existing methodologies or catalyzing a fundamental shift in how science is conducted. This position paper addresses that gap by arguing FMs are transitioning science toward a 'fifth paradigm' and proposing a structured lens to track this evolution.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251862/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Because this is a survey and position paper rather than a single pipeline, the I/O spans the full range of FM-enabled scientific tasks: inputs include natural-language research questions, SMILES strings, amino-acid sequences, PDE coefficient/solution pairs, multimodal sensor and satellite data, and large scientific-literature corpora; outputs range from generated hypotheses and experimental protocols to 3D protein structures, global weather forecasts, formal mathematical proofs, and novel molecular or crystal designs. The paper organizes these diverse I/O pairs along four classical paradigms (experimental, theoretical, computational, data-driven) plus a cross-paradigm integration axis, rather than defining a single fixed input format.

## Method

The core contribution is a three-stage conceptual framework—Meta-Scientific Integration, Hybrid Human-AI Co-Creation, and Autonomous Scientific Discovery—distinguished along five dimensions (paradigm definition, FM role, task scope, autonomy level, and impact on science). The paper then systematically reviews FM applications within each of the four classical scientific paradigms, identifying specific systems (e.g., Coscientist for autonomous chemistry, GraphCast for weather, AlphaFold for structure prediction, FunSearch for mathematical discovery) and mapping them onto the framework stages. A cross-paradigm section highlights how FMs mediate workflows that traverse multiple paradigms simultaneously (e.g., PROSE-FD coupling symbolic equations with spatial field data). Finally, the paper identifies four risk dimensions (bias, hallucination, reproducibility, authorship) and three future research directions (embodied agents, closed-loop autonomy, continual learning) that would be required to reach the fully autonomous stage.

## Results

This is a position/survey paper that proposes a conceptual three-stage framework (Meta-Scientific Integration → Hybrid Human-AI Co-Creation → Autonomous Scientific Discovery) rather than reporting quantitative experimental results. The paper does not benchmark a specific model or report metrics such as accuracy, F1, or loss. Its primary contribution is a taxonomy of FM integration across four classical scientific paradigms (experimental, theoretical, computational, data-driven) and a risk analysis covering bias, hallucination, reproducibility, and authorship.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare its method against baselines, as it is a position paper and systematic review rather than an empirical study. It does not propose a new model or algorithm to be benchmarked. Instead, it surveys and organizes existing FM applications (e.g., AlphaFold, FunSearch, GraphCast, Coscientist, AI Scientist) across scientific paradigms and stages of autonomy. The closest analogue to a 'comparison' is Table 1, which qualitatively contrasts the three proposed FM paradigms along five dimensions (role, task scope, autonomy, impact), but no quantitative head-to-head evaluation is performed.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper carves new conceptual ground by proposing a three-stage framework (Meta-Scientific Integration → Hybrid Human-AI Co-Creation → Autonomous Scientific Discovery) to organize the rapidly growing but previously fragmented literature on FMs in science. Rather than building on a single methodological lineage, it synthesizes across the four classical scientific paradigms (experimental, theoretical, computational, data-driven) and maps dozens of FM applications—AlphaFold, FunSearch, GraphCast, Coscientist, LeanCopilot, and others—onto a unified autonomy-based taxonomy. It departs from prior AI-for-science surveys that treat FMs as task-specific accelerators by arguing they are catalyzing a paradigm-level epistemic shift toward a 'fifth paradigm' of autonomous discovery.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the scientific community with a structured vocabulary and staged roadmap for understanding where FM-enabled research currently sits and where it is heading, which is valuable for both researchers and policymakers. It surfaces critical open questions that remain largely unaddressed: how to validate and attribute machine-generated scientific claims, how to prevent epistemic homogenization from training-data bias, and how to design governance frameworks for autonomous discovery. The main limitation is that the 'Autonomous Scientific Discovery' stage remains largely speculative, with only early prototypes (e.g., AI Scientist) providing partial evidence, so the framework's predictive power for the third stage is untested.
