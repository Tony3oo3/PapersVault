## Problem

Coalition formation (hedonic games) and clustering are two research areas that both study how to group entities meaningfully, yet there is no substantial interplay between them. Traditional clustering metrics evaluate boundary quality or intra-cluster similarity but ignore whether individual data points would 'want' to move to a different cluster. This paper asks whether stability notions from hedonic games—specifically, that no single-agent deviation should improve the outcome for a majority of agents—can serve as a principled clustering criterion and be found efficiently.

## Input / Output

The method takes a set of data points with pairwise distances and converts them into a friendship graph (pairs within distance f·diam) and an enmity graph (pairs beyond distance e·diam), where f ≤ e ∈ [0,1] are tunable threshold parameters. It then produces a partition of all points into coalitions that is either locally popular (no single-agent switch yields a net positive majority vote) or locally stable (no Nash deviation that also locally dominates). The approach applies to both graph-structured data (community detection on citation, social, and collaboration networks) and tabular data (standard clustering benchmarks), with the friendship/enmity graphs encoding local relational structure rather than global geometry.

## Method

The core mechanism models clustering as a Friends-Enemies-Neutrals (FEN) hedonic game where each data point is an agent whose utility in a coalition is the sum of friend valuations (α_f > 0) and enemy valuations (α_e < 0), with three preference domains: friend-appreciating, enemy-averse, and balanced. Two iterative heuristics—LocPop and LocStab—start from an arbitrary partition and repeatedly apply single-agent switches that are locally dominating (i.e., the majority of agents vote in favor of the new partition). The key theoretical insight is that the total balanced utility serves as a non-decreasing potential function for symmetric FEN games, guaranteeing convergence of LocPop in O(nΔ²) steps (O(nΔ) for balanced) to a locally popular partition, and LocStab in O(nΔ) steps to a locally stable partition. For asymmetric games, the paper proves NP-hardness of existence in the AF and AE domains (via reductions from 5-SAT and 3-SAT using gadget constructions), while LocStab still converges in O(nΔ) for balanced preferences. An efficient implementation using maintained data structures achieves O(nΔ³ log n) total runtime for LocPop and O(nΔ² log n) for LocStab when the maximum degree Δ is constant.

## Results

In community detection, LocPop achieves a ~50% improvement in Rand index over both Louvain and Leiden on the Karate Club dataset and ~10% better on Random-25, while the LocPop-Ld variant (initialized with Leiden output) matches them on modularity. In clustering benchmarks, LocPop mostly surpasses DBSCAN in both silhouette score and Rand index, and matches or outperforms k-means with up to 20% gains in silhouette score on the 3-Circles and Iris datasets. All algorithms run within seconds except on Cora (2708 nodes), where computation takes 3–5 minutes due to high graph degrees. LocPop and LocStab perform very similarly across all datasets, with LocStab showing slightly more sensitivity to the preference domain (AF, AE, B).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Louvain [7] and Leiden [41] on community detection (Karate Club, Jazz Musicians, Cora, Random-25), LocPop matches or outperforms both on Rand index, with the largest gap (~50%) on Karate Club and a consistent ~10% edge on Random-25; on modularity, LocPop-Ld (initialized from Leiden) matches the baselines. Against k-means [34] on clustering (Iris, Breast Cancer Wisconsin, Moons, 3-Circles), LocPop matches or exceeds k-means, achieving up to 20% higher silhouette score on 3-Circles and Iris, though the advantage is smaller on Moons and Breast Cancer. Against DBSCAN [18], LocPop mostly surpasses it in both silhouette score and Rand index across all four clustering datasets, with the gap being most pronounced on the synthetic 3-Circles dataset. Notably, the paper observes that stronger silhouette scores do not always correspond to higher Rand index, indicating a trade-off between internal cluster quality and agreement with ground-truth labels.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the FEN (friends-enemies-neutrals) hedonic game model of Dimitrov et al. and the single-agent deviation dynamics of Brandt et al. (who studied the stricter FE model without neutrality) to establish convergence guarantees for two stability notions—local stability and the newly introduced local popularity—in polynomial time for symmetric games. It also draws a conceptual bridge to clustering by identifying friendship with similarity and enmity with dissimilarity, a connection the authors note has no substantial prior treatment beyond Feldman et al.'s noncooperative hedonic clustering games. The potential-function analysis (total balanced utility) generalizes the convergence argument from FE to FEN games, where neutrality introduces additional flexibility that actually improves convergence speed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a theoretically grounded, stability-based alternative to global-optimization clustering criteria, offering robustness against single-agent deviations that is particularly relevant when data points represent autonomous agents. The NP-hardness results for asymmetric settings and for local popularity in balanced games delineate the boundary of tractability, while the polynomial-time convergence for symmetric cases gives practitioners a practical algorithm. Open questions include whether full popularity (not just local popularity) can be efficiently characterized, how the approach scales to very dense graphs, and whether the stability guarantee translates to advantages in adversarial or dynamic clustering settings. The empirical evaluation, while competitive, is limited to small-scale datasets and standard baselines, leaving open whether the approach maintains its edge at scale or against more sophisticated methods.
