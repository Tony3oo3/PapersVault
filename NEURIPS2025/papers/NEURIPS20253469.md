## Problem

Large language models frequently express high confidence even when their answers are wrong, undermining their reliability in high-stakes applications. While recent reasoning models have demonstrated strong problem-solving through extended chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning, it remains unclear whether these slow thinking behaviors also enable models to more accurately communicate the limits of their knowledge. This paper investigates whether and why reasoning models better express their confidence compared to non-reasoning counterparts.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253469/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Models receive a text question and are prompted to perform three sequential steps: solution reasoning (step-by-step CoT to arrive at an answer), confidence reasoning (evaluating how likely the answer is correct), and confidence verbalization (mapping confidence into one of ten binned categories, each with a linguistic descriptor and numerical range, e.g., 'Almost certain (0.9–1.0)'). The final output is formatted as 'Answer: ANSWER Confidence: CLASS', where CLASS is the name of one of the ten bins. For reasoning models, all three steps are expected to occur within the <think>... block, with the answer and confidence emitted after the closing tag.

## Method

This is an empirical study that benchmarks six reasoning models (R1-Distill-Qwen, QwQ, OR1-Preview, GLM-Z1-0414, EXAONE-Deep, Qwen3 Thinking) against their non-reasoning counterparts across six datasets (TriviaQA, NonambigQA, SuperGPQA Math/Non-Math, MMLU-Pro Math/Non-Math), measuring calibration via ECE, Brier Score, and AUROC. The key finding is that reasoning models achieve strictly better calibration in 33 of 36 settings, even when their task accuracy is comparable or lower. Through progressive CoT analysis, the authors show that reasoning models' calibration improves steadily as the CoT unfolds (statistically significant linear trends), while non-reasoning models show no such trend. An ablation study on R1-Distill-Qwen isolates non-linear reasoning behaviors (exploring alternatives, backtracking, refining) as the primary driver of calibration gains, while explicit confidence reasoning and epistemic markers play smaller roles. Finally, prompting non-reasoning models to engage in slow thinking via in-context learning (using three R1-Distill-Qwen exemplars) reproduces similar calibration improvements, confirming that slow thinking itself—not model-specific training—drives the gains.

## Results

Across six reasoning models and their non-reasoning counterparts on four datasets, reasoning models achieve strictly better calibration in 33 out of 36 settings. On TriviaQA, R1-Distill-Qwen reduces ECE from 0.129 (Qwen2.5-Instruct) to 0.042, and GLM-Z1-0414 reduces ECE from 0.084 (GLM-4-0414) to 0.029. On NonambigQA, the same pattern holds: R1-Distill-Qwen achieves ECE 0.195 vs. 0.297 for Qwen2.5-Instruct, and Qwen3 Thinking achieves ECE 0.226 vs. 0.403 for Qwen3 Non-thinking. Notably, reasoning models maintain superior calibration even when their task accuracy is comparable or slightly lower (e.g., GLM-Z1-0414 has 0.07 lower accuracy than GLM-4-0414 on NonambigQA yet 0.037 lower ECE).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares each reasoning model against its direct non-reasoning counterpart from the same backbone family. R1-Distill-Qwen, OR1-Preview, and QwQ all outperform Qwen2.5-Instruct on every calibration metric for both TriviaQA and NonambigQA, with ECE reductions of 0.077–0.087 on TriviaQA. GLM-Z1-0414 beats GLM-4-0414 on ECE (0.029 vs. 0.084 on TriviaQA) and AUROC (0.777 vs. 0.675), despite slightly lower accuracy on NonambigQA. EXAONE-Deep outperforms EXAONE-3.5-Instruct on ECE and AUROC but with a small accuracy drop on TriviaQA (0.687 vs. 0.715). The most compelling comparison is Qwen3 Thinking vs. Qwen3 Non-thinking within the same checkpoint: Thinking mode achieves ECE 0.063 vs. 0.207 on TriviaQA and 0.226 vs. 0.403 on NonambigQA, isolating slow thinking as the causal factor. The only losses (3 out of 36) are in AUROC on reasoning-intensive multiple-choice datasets, which the authors attribute to non-reasoning models exploiting the binary choice between 85% and 95% confidence bins as an implicit correctness cue.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two active research threads: verbalized confidence estimation in LLMs (Tian et al. 2023, Xiong et al. 2024, Yang et al. 2024) and the emerging literature on reasoning models and their slow thinking behaviors (OpenAI o1, DeepSeek-R1, Gandhi et al. 2025). While prior work has established calibration metrics and prompting strategies for eliciting confidence, and separately characterized the non-linear reasoning traces of reasoning models, no prior study systematically connected the two to ask whether slow thinking improves calibration. A concurrent work (Zhang et al. 2025) probes hidden states of reasoning models for self-verification, but this paper focuses on the more accessible and model-agnostic setting of verbalized confidence, providing ablations and in-context learning experiments that isolate slow thinking as the causal mechanism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that slow thinking—not just longer CoT or a different model checkpoint—drives calibration gains is practically actionable: it suggests that even non-reasoning models can be made more trustworthy through simple prompting, and that the quality of reasoning (exploring alternatives, backtracking) matters more than its quantity. However, reasoning models still rarely express confidence below 55%, leaving a significant gap for genuinely uncertain predictions. Open questions include whether these gains persist as models scale beyond 32B parameters (the paper shows a positive trend but limited evidence), whether the effect holds for open-ended generation tasks beyond multiple-choice and short-answer QA, and how to further reduce the residual overconfidence bias.
