## Problem

Best-of-N selection for LLM reasoning typically relies on computationally expensive reward models (ORMs/PRMs) that are costly to train, vulnerable to distribution shift, and prone to reward hacking. Reward-free alternatives like self-consistency are restricted to tasks with convergent string-matched answers, while universal self-consistency suffers from context-length limits and lacks a direct quality score. The paper asks whether the LLM's own output probability distribution can serve as a scalable, general-purpose confidence signal for response selection without any external model.

## Input / Output

Given a reasoning question, the method generates N candidate responses (e.g., N=4 to 64, temperature=0.6, top-p=0.9) and consumes the per-token probability distributions (logits over the full vocabulary) that are already produced during autoregressive decoding—no additional forward passes are needed. It outputs a scalar self-certainty score per response (the average KL divergence from uniform across all token positions) and, when combined with Borda voting, a single selected final answer. The metric is length-invariant, unlike perplexity-based alternatives, and applies equally to open-ended outputs such as code where string-matching is infeasible.

## Method

Self-certainty is defined as the average over all token positions of the KL divergence between the model's next-token distribution and a uniform distribution over the vocabulary, yielding a single scalar that increases as the distribution becomes more peaked (i.e., more confident). A theoretical result (Theorem A.1) shows that a gradient-ascent step on the log-likelihood of a target token strictly increases self-certainty whenever that token's probability exceeds the squared L2 norm of the distribution, linking the metric to standard training objectives. To combine confidence with answer-level agreement, the paper introduces Borda voting: N responses are ranked by self-certainty, each response contributes (N − rank + 1)^p votes to its extracted final answer, and the answer with the highest total is selected; p=0 recovers plain majority voting while large p approaches pure confidence-based selection. This two-layer design—decoding-layer confidence plus response-layer voting—consistently outperforms self-consistency, universal self-consistency, and perplexity-based selection across math, code reasoning, and code generation benchmarks, and scales favorably with N without the overhead of a separate reward model.

## Results

On Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, self-certainty-based Borda voting (p=1.2) achieves 56.51% average accuracy across LiveBench-Math, GSM8K, MATH, and CRUXEval-O, outperforming self-consistency (56.15%), USC (51.19%), and perplexity-based selection (51.08%). On the MATH dataset (N=64), Borda voting reaches 64.10% accuracy versus 63.40% for self-consistency and 56.70% for self-certainty alone. On CRUXEval-O (N=64), Borda voting (50.85%) surpasses both the process reward model Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-7B (48.61%) and the outcome reward model GRM-Llama3.2-3B (39.62%). Self-certainty also scales effectively with N, with performance continuing to improve up to N=64, unlike perplexity and other distributional measures that plateau or decline after N=16.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against self-consistency (Wang et al., 2022), Borda voting with self-certainty wins on all four datasets: MATH N=64 (64.10 vs. 63.40), LiveBench-Math N=32 (26.69 vs. 26.25), GSM8K N=64 (90.95 vs. 90.99, a near-tie), and CRUXEval-O N=64 (50.85 vs. 50.42). Against universal self-consistency (Chen et al., 2023), self-certainty clearly outperforms USC on LiveBench-Math (22.01 vs. 21.08 at N=32) and on the LiveCodeBench code generation task where USC underperforms even greedy decoding on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. Against the process reward model Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-7B (Zhang et al., 2025), Borda voting is competitive on CRUXEval-O (50.85 vs. 48.61 at N=64) but trails on GSM8K (90.95 vs. 95.15). Against the outcome reward model GRM-Llama3.2-3B (Ray2333, 2024), Borda voting outperforms ORM on both GSM8K (90.95 vs. 89.91) and CRUXEval-O (50.85 vs. 39.62) at N=64, demonstrating that the reward-free self-certainty approach can match or exceed externally trained reward models without additional model inference.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the self-consistency framework (Wang et al., 2022) by replacing unweighted majority voting with a Borda-count-inspired scheme that ranks N samples by a new confidence metric—self-certainty, defined as the KL divergence between each token's predicted distribution and a uniform distribution. It departs from the dominant Best-of-N paradigm of relying on external reward models (ORMs/PRMs) by showing that the LLM's own token-level probability distributions, already computed during inference, carry sufficient signal for quality assessment at essentially zero additional cost. The metric also supersedes simpler probabilistic confidence measures (average log-probability, perplexity) by operating on the full distribution rather than only the sampled token, and it generalizes beyond the convergent-answer setting where self-consistency and universal self-consistency are limited.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Self-certainty offers a practically attractive, zero-overhead alternative to reward models for test-time scaling, and its length-invariance (unlike perplexity) makes it robust against verbosity-driven score inflation. However, the accuracy gains over self-consistency are modest (1–3 points with Borda voting), the Borda exponent p requires per-task tuning, and evaluations are limited to 8B-scale models, leaving open whether the approach scales to much larger or differently trained architectures. The paper also raises the question of whether distributional confidence signals could inform reward-model design or RL reward shaping, but this remains speculative. For practitioners, the main takeaway is a simple, drop-in metric that can be computed from existing logits without any additional forward passes.
