## Problem

Existing hijacking attacks on LLMs via in-context learning apply uniform or fixed perturbation budgets across all in-context examples (ICEs), overlooking the opportunity for dynamic reallocation. The paper asks how to design a stealthy hijacking attack where the perturbation budget is optimally distributed across ICEs so that subtle manipulations accumulate influence (a 'snowball effect') while preserving text quality and evading detection.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250476/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a sequence of n benign ICEs (text input-output pairs, n ranging from 2 to 12), a query input, and a precomputed budget profile Γ. In the online stage, ICEs arrive sequentially and only the current ICE can be perturbed; up to 3 tokens per ICE may be modified. The output is a set of adversarial ICEs with perturbed inputs but original labels (preserving stealth), a hijacked model prediction, and the learned budget allocation profile. Perturbations are computed in embedding space under a total L2 budget ε and projected back to discrete word space.

## Method

BAM-ICL is a two-stage gradient-based framework. In the offline stage, a global PGD optimization jointly perturbs all ICEs under a shared L2 ball constraint, and the resulting per-ICE perturbation magnitudes are normalized to form a budget profile Γ that encodes each ICE's relative sensitivity. In the online stage, as each ICE arrives sequentially, its individual budget ε_i = γ_i · ε is retrieved from Γ, a local PGD loop generates the embedding-space perturbation, and a word-projection step selects the semantically closest dictionary word (within ε_i of the original embedding) that maximizes the model's loss. The key design insight is that dynamic, position-dependent budget allocation outperforms uniform allocation because different ICEs contribute unequally to steering the model's prediction, and the offline profile captures this heterogeneity without requiring access to future ICEs at attack time.

## Results

On OPT models (n=4), BAM-ICL achieves ASR of 42.1–52.7% on SST-2, 25.3–30.7% on AGNews, and 33.5–41.0% on OLID, consistently outperforming the flat-budget baseline by 8–12 percentage points across all model sizes (1.3B–30B). Against the GGI baseline, BAM-ICL achieves comparable or slightly lower raw ASR but substantially better stealthiness: perplexity of perturbed ICEs remains close to clean text, and the ASR drop under the prepending defense is only 19.63% versus 39.83% for GGI. In the jailbreaking setting on LLaMA-3.1, BAM-ICL reaches 43.6% ASR at n=12, surpassing GGI's 31.7%. The learned budget profile also transfers across models, retaining 85–97% of source-model ASR when applied to GPT-2-XL.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary external baseline is GGI (Qiang et al., 2023), which appends imperceptible malicious suffixes to all ICEs simultaneously. BAM-ICL achieves slightly lower raw ASR than GGI/Global (e.g., 52.7% vs. 74.7% on SST-2, OPT-1.3B, n=4) because GGI perturbs all examples jointly without the sequential constraint, but BAM-ICL preserves text quality far better: perplexity of perturbed ICEs stays near clean levels while GGI's suffixes inflate perplexity noticeably. Against the prepending defense, BAM-ICL suffers only a 19.63% ASR drop versus GGI's 39.83%, indicating substantially stronger stealth. Internally, BAM-ICL outperforms the flat-budget allocation by 8–12 pp on SST-2 and 6–10 pp on AGNews/OLID across all OPT sizes, confirming that dynamic budget distribution is the key gain. In the jailbreaking task on LLaMA-3.1, BAM-ICL surpasses GGI at n=12 (43.6% vs. 31.7%) while trailing at smaller n, suggesting the budget profile's advantage grows with context length. Transferability experiments show the budget profile learned on OPT-30B (SST-2, n=12) retains 85–97% ASR when applied to GPT-2-XL, whereas GGI's suffix-based perturbations do not offer an analogous transfer mechanism.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the ICL hijacking attack paradigm introduced by GGI (Qiang et al. 2023), which first demonstrated that adversarial perturbations on in-context examples can steer LLM outputs. The key conceptual departure is the observation that prior attacks impose a uniform perturbation budget across all ICEs, ignoring the sequential, position-dependent nature of ICL where earlier examples exert disproportionate influence. BAM-ICL introduces a two-stage framework: an offline global PGD optimization that learns a non-uniform budget profile capturing per-position sensitivity, and an online sequential perturbation stage that applies this profile as ICEs arrive. The token selection strategy draws on the sequential masking logic from Garg et al. (2022), and the embedding-space perturbation with word-space projection follows the broader adversarial NLP literature (FreeLB, TextFooler).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that dynamic budget allocation is a meaningful lever for ICL hijacking, improving attack success rates by 20–30% over flat allocation while maintaining perplexity and cosine similarity close to clean text. The transferability of learned budget profiles across model families (OPT → GPT-2 XL) suggests that position-sensitivity patterns are somewhat model-agnostic, which has implications for defense design. However, the approach is vulnerable to shuffling/reordering defenses (since allocation is position-dependent), requires offline access to same-distribution data, and is primarily validated on classification tasks. Open questions include whether budget-aware defenses can be developed, how the approach generalizes to open-ended generation, and whether the sequential online constraint can be relaxed without sacrificing the practical threat model.
