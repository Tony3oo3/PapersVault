## Problem

Simply scaling the number of RLHF training prompts does not improve model performance, yet the data-construction side of RLHF has received far less attention than algorithmic improvements. This paper identifies two underexplored data-driven bottlenecks—reward hacking and the progressive collapse of response diversity—that prevent effective RLHF scaling, and asks how training prompts should be selected and sequenced to overcome them.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250514/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes a pool of text prompts spanning mathematics, coding, creative writing, instruction-following, and logical reasoning, and produces aligned text responses from Mixture-of-Experts language models (~25B and ~150B total parameters, ~10% activated per token). The Pre-PPO selection step scores each prompt's model-generated responses with a Generative Reward Model, normalizes scores within each task domain, and retains only the bottom 10% of prompts. Reward evaluation is hybrid: coding tasks are verified by code-execution sandboxes (RTV), while other tasks are scored by GenRM comparing the model output against a ground-truth answer or an SFT Best-of-N reference.

## Method

The approach combines three mechanisms. First, a hybrid reward system pairs Reasoning Task Verifiers (RTV, e.g., unit-test sandboxes for code) with Generative Reward Models (GenRM) trained via pairwise reward modeling to compare outputs against ground-truth or SFT Best-of-N references, making the reward signal substantially more resistant to hacking than a standalone Bradley-Terry model. Second, Pre-PPO runs an initial PPO pass over the full prompt pool, scores every prompt with GenRM, and selects only the bottom 10% of prompts by domain-normalized score—these are inherently harder for the model and less prone to reward hacking. Third, a curriculum schedules training to begin exclusively on coding prompts, gradually introduce mathematical prompts, and only later mix in general-domain tasks, so the model acquires fine-grained response distinctions before exposure to coarse-grained creative tasks that trigger diversity collapse. The central insight is that early mastery of fine-grained, ground-truth-supervised distinctions (math, coding) both accelerates learning and makes subsequent reward hacking harder, whereas premature exposure to coarse-grained tasks degrades scalability. Experiments on 25B and 150B MoE models confirm consistent gains, with the largest improvements in STEM (+3.9) and coding (+3.2) on the more challenging V2.0 evaluation set.

## Results

On the internal evaluation set V2.0 (more challenging, out-of-distribution prompts), the proposed Data Scale method (Pre-PPO + early math/coding prioritization) improves overall score from 34.0 to 35.4 (+1.4) on the 150B model and from 23.9 to 25.1 (+1.2) on the 25B model. The largest gains appear in STEM (+3.9 on 150B V2.0) and Coding (+3.2 on 150B V1.0, +3.8 on human eval). On open-source benchmarks, LiveCodeBench improves from 30.3 to 36.2 (19.5% relative gain), while MMLU, MMLU Pro, GPQA Diamond, and SuperGPQA are maintained or slightly improved, with only negligible drops on AMC23 (−0.1) and MATH500 (−0.4).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the proposed Data Scale method (Pre-PPO prompt selection + early math/coding curriculum) against a Baseline of standard PPO-based RLHF trained on the original 1M-prompt dataset, at both 25B and 150B model scales. Data Scale consistently outperforms the Baseline on Overall score across all four model-size/evaluation-set combinations (+1.1 to +1.4), with the largest margin on the harder V2.0 test set, indicating stronger out-of-distribution generalization. The most substantial wins are in STEM (+3.9 on 150B V2.0) and Coding (+3.2 on 150B V1.0; +3.8 on human evaluation), directly attributable to the early-stage prioritization of these tasks. On human evaluation (Table 2), Data Scale achieves a statistically significant overall improvement of +3.2 (p=0.01) and STEM +3.9 (p=0.04) over the Baseline. On open-source benchmarks, the method gains +5.9 points on LiveCodeBench (30.3→36.2) and small positive shifts on MMLU Pro (+0.5), GPQA Diamond (+0.5), and SuperGPQA (+1.2), while showing negligible trade-offs on AMC23 (−0.1) and MATH500 (−0.4). The method slightly underperforms the Baseline on NLP (−0.6 to −0.8) and Instruction-Following (−0.4) in some settings, suggesting a minor cost in general-domain fluency.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the hybrid reward modeling paradigm by combining Generative Reward Models (GenRM, Mahan et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2024) with Reasoning Task Verifiers (RTV, DeepSeek-R1, Guo et al. 2025) into a unified reward system. However, its primary departure from prior work is a shift in focus: while most recent RLHF research targets algorithmic improvements (critic-free PPO, sample filtering, reward model accuracy), this paper argues that the underexplored bottleneck is prompt-data construction and its scalability. The Pre-PPO selection method and the math/coding-first curriculum represent a new axis of intervention—data curation under a fixed reward system—rather than modifying the reward model or optimization algorithm itself.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides practical, actionable guidance for teams scaling RLHF: simply collecting more prompts does not help (and can hurt), and the ordering of task types during training materially affects final performance. Key open questions remain: how to properly scale hyperparameters from small to large models, whether the Pre-PPO strategy transfers to long chain-of-thought RLHF, and whether LLM-generated prompts could replace costly human-collected ones. The approach's dependence on large-scale ground-truth annotation and an extra PPO pass for prompt selection limits its accessibility, and all results are validated on internal ByteDance benchmarks without public code or data.
