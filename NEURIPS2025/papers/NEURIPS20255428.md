## Problem

Deep learning models memorize training examples, making them vulnerable to membership inference attacks. Existing defenses force a trade-off: DP-SGD provides provable privacy guarantees but substantially degrades model accuracy, while empirical defenses (AdvReg, SELENA, HAMP) preserve utility but offer no theoretical guarantees against future attacks. No prior method simultaneously delivers a mathematically justified privacy guarantee and competitive model utility.

## Input / Output

PD-SGD takes a training dataset, an arbitrary loss function (including non-decomposable ones, unlike DP-SGD), and privacy parameters (noise scale σ, similarity threshold γ, count threshold T, randomization β, and ceiling ψ). At each step it partitions data into m batches, computes the seed batch gradient plus all m−1 other batch gradients for the privacy test, and outputs either a parameter update (if the test passes) or no update (if rejected). The final output is a trained model satisfying batch-level plausible deniability and (ε,δ)-differential privacy, with ε and δ determined by the privacy parameters and number of training steps via composition.

## Method

PD-SGD modifies standard mini-batch SGD with two components: (1) isotropic Gaussian noise added to the seed batch gradient, and (2) a rejection-sampling privacy test that counts how many other batches are α-similar to the seed batch (i.e., could have plausibly produced the same noisy gradient within a log-pdf ratio bound). If the count of α-similar 'alternative' batches falls below threshold T, the gradient update is discarded entirely. The key insight is that this rejection mechanism smooths the output distribution over gradients to be insensitive to any single batch's availability, achieving batch-level plausible deniability without per-example clipping. The α-similarity check reduces to a simple log-pdf comparison (Eq. 3), making the test computationally efficient. Unlike DP-SGD, PD-SGD operates at the batch level, requires no per-example gradient computation, uses less memory, and is compatible with non-decomposable loss functions, while still satisfying (ε,δ)-DP through composition over training steps.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 fine-tuning, PD-SGD (PS1) achieves 96.18% test accuracy—matching the non-private baseline (96.09%)—while reducing R-Attack AUC from 0.69 to 0.49. On CIFAR-10 training from scratch, PD-SGD reaches 82.22% accuracy versus 63.31% for DP-SGD (ε=8) and 81.04% for SELENA, with C-Attack TPR@0.1%FPR of 0.19%. On CIFAR-100 fine-tuning, PD-SGD achieves 72.56% accuracy compared to 27.12% for DP-SGD, a 45-point gap. For the LLaMA-2-7B fine-tuning on SST-2, PD-SGD reaches 94.76% test accuracy with stable memory usage (6,753 MB peak).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DP-SGD (ε=8), PD-SGD achieves substantially higher utility while matching or exceeding privacy: on CIFAR-10 from scratch, PD-SGD reaches 82.22% vs. 63.31% accuracy (19-point gap), and on CIFAR-100 fine-tuning the gap widens to 72.56% vs. 27.12%. Attack AUCs are comparable or slightly better for PD-SGD (e.g., R-Attack 0.50 vs. 0.50 on CIFAR-10 FS). Against SELENA, PD-SGD wins on both utility and privacy: 82.22% vs. 81.04% accuracy on CIFAR-10 FS with lower R-Attack AUC (0.52 vs. 0.53), and 96.18% vs. 96.01% on CIFAR-10 FT with R-Attack 0.49 vs. 0.51. Against AdvReg, PD-SGD dominates across all four datasets on both accuracy (e.g., 82.22% vs. 75.38% on CIFAR-10 FS) and attack resistance (e.g., C-Attack 0.19% vs. 0.19% on CIFAR-10 FS, 0.27% vs. 0.31% on CIFAR-10 FT). The ablation in Table 2 confirms that neither noise alone nor the privacy test alone is sufficient—the combination is what drives the privacy gains (R-Attack drops to 0.48 vs. 0.54–0.57 for individual components).

## Relation to Prior Work

PD-SGD is a direct modification of mini-batch SGD that inserts a rejection-sampling privacy test between gradient computation and parameter update, drawing on the plausible deniability concept from Bindschaedler et al. [5] (data synthesis) and Rass et al. [32] (supervised ML) but formalizing it at the batch level via a game-based definition. It departs from DP-SGD [1] by operating at the batch level rather than requiring per-example gradient clipping, and from empirical defenses (AdvReg [29], SELENA [40], HAMP [9]) by providing a provable (ε,δ)-DP guarantee via composition. The game-based analysis connects to the privacy-games SoK of Salem et al. [34], extending it to a one-step, batch-partitioned setting with an add-remove (rather than replace-one) formulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PD-SGD offers a practical middle ground: it matches or exceeds the utility of empirical defenses while carrying a theoretical DP guarantee, and it avoids the per-example clipping that makes DP-SGD slow and memory-hungry. Key limitations include per-iteration privacy bounds that compose loosely over full training, a centralized-only setting (no federated extension), and DP guarantees that are weaker than DP-SGD's for the same ε. Open questions include tightening composition bounds, extending to collaborative/federated learning, and understanding whether the batch-level framing yields fundamentally tighter privacy accounting than the standard per-example DP approach.
