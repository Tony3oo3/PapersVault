## Problem

While prior work has shown that LLMs can model fully observed Markov chains via in-context learning, their capacity to implicitly learn Hidden Markov Models—with latent states, complex transition dependencies, and observation emissions—remained unexplored. Meanwhile, fitting HMMs to real-world data requires computationally intensive and algorithmically unstable methods like Baum-Welch or Gibbs sampling, limiting their practical accessibility for scientists across disciplines.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250970/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The LLM receives a discrete observation sequence (e.g., HMM-generated tokens or animal trial outcomes) formatted as a text prompt, with context lengths ranging from 4 to 2,048 observations. Tokenization maps observations to single letters (ABC), digits (123), or special tokens, and the model must predict the next observation without any parameter updates or fine-tuning. The output is both an argmax next-observation prediction and a full predictive probability distribution over the observation alphabet, which is evaluated via accuracy and Hellinger distance against ground-truth distributions.

## Method

The paper evaluates pre-trained LLMs (Qwen and Llama families, 1.5B–72B) in a purely zero-shot in-context learning setup on 234 synthetic HMM configurations, systematically varying state/observation dimensions, mixing rate (λ₂), transition and emission entropy, stationary distribution skewness, and initial state distribution. The key finding is that LLM ICL prediction accuracy consistently converges to the Viterbi algorithm's theoretical optimum (which uses ground-truth parameters) under favorable conditions of low entropy and fast mixing, outperforming Baum-Welch, spectral learning, n-grams, LSTM, and trained Transformer baselines in both speed and stability of convergence. The authors provide a theoretical conjecture linking ICL behavior to spectral learning algorithms, showing that the observed scaling trends—improvement with context length, sensitivity to mixing rate via 1/(1−λ₂), and entropy effects through observability conditions—mirror the sample complexity bounds of spectral HMM estimation. On real-world animal behavior data (IBL mice decision-making, rat reward-learning), LLM ICL achieves competitive or superior performance to expert-developed domain-specific models, demonstrating its utility as a zero-shot diagnostic and predictive tool for scientific sequential data.

## Results

On synthetic HMMs (234 configurations), pre-trained LLMs (Qwen2.5-7B, Llama) achieve prediction accuracy that converges to the Viterbi algorithm's theoretical optimum across most parameter settings, with convergence speed governed by HMM entropy and mixing rate (λ₂). On the IBL mice decision-making dataset, LLM ICL achieves 86.2% average prediction accuracy with >1000 trials of context, outperforming the expert-developed GLM-HMM model (82.2%). On the more challenging rat reward-learning dataset, LLM ICL shows only marginal improvement with increasing context length and a substantial performance gap versus the state-of-the-art symbolic model, consistent with high-entropy, slow-mixing dynamics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Viterbi oracle (which uses ground-truth HMM parameters), LLM ICL converges to near-optimal accuracy on most synthetic HMM configurations, with gaps appearing only under high entropy or slow mixing (λ₂ ≥ 0.95) where even Viterbi degrades. Against Baum-Welch, LLM ICL consistently achieves faster and more stable convergence; BW suffers from non-convex optimization and exhibits non-monotonic accuracy across context lengths. Against spectral learning, LLM ICL avoids the numerical instability that plagues spectral methods at short context (sample complexity O(M²L)) and handles rank/conditioning assumptions more gracefully. On the IBL mice dataset, LLM ICL (86.2%) outperforms the GLM-HMM model from Ashwood et al. (82.2%) by 4 percentage points. On the rat reward-learning dataset, LLM ICL underperforms the evolutionary-search symbolic model from Castro et al., showing only marginal gains with longer context—consistent with the paper's finding that high-entropy, non-stationary dynamics push beyond current ICL capabilities.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the ICL literature from fully observed Markov chains (Edelman et al. [13], Makkuva et al. [33], Rajaraman et al. [40]) to the substantially harder setting of Hidden Markov Models with latent states, complex transition dependencies, and emission noise. The theoretical analysis draws directly on spectral learning for HMMs (Hsu et al. [21]), extending their sample complexity results to single-trajectory settings and conjecturing that LLM ICL implicitly performs a form of improper spectral learning. The paper departs from prior ICL theory by showing that LLMs handle practical limitations of spectral algorithms—rank conditions, numerical instability under slow mixing—more gracefully, pointing to an unresolved gap in our statistical understanding of how attention mechanisms learn latent-variable models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic demonstration that off-the-shelf LLMs can match the theoretical optimum (Viterbi with ground-truth parameters) on HMM prediction, outperforming purpose-built methods like Baum-Welch and spectral learning without any task-specific training. The practical implication is significant: LLMs become accessible 'zero-shot statistical tools' for scientists analyzing sequential data, lowering the barrier to HMM-based analysis. Key limitations remain: the framework requires discrete tokenization (blocking continuous observations like neural recordings), the black-box nature prevents extraction of interpretable transition/emission parameters, and the rat reward-learning dataset shows ICL struggles with high-entropy, non-stationary dynamics. Open questions include extending ICL to continuous state spaces and understanding why LLMs implicitly overcome spectral learning's numerical fragility.
