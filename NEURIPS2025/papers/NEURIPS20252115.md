## Problem

Large reasoning models (LRMs) empirically allocate more reasoning tokens to harder problems, but the internal mechanism behind this difficulty-aware reasoning strength planning remains unexplored. Specifically, it is unclear whether LRMs pre-plan their reasoning strength before generating the first reasoning token, and if so, in what representational form this plan is encoded within the model's activations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252115/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes question text and extracts the d-dimensional residual stream activation at the  token position (before any reasoning tokens are generated). A Lasso linear probe trained on these activations predicts the subsequent reasoning token count (the number of tokens between  and ). The difference-in-means pre-allocated direction vector, extracted by contrasting mean activations across MATH difficulty levels, is then injected into activations via h' = h + λr to causally steer reasoning length. Outputs include the predicted token count, the steered activation state, the resulting controlled reasoning token number, and a binary overthink detection signal from the predictor.

## Method

The paper establishes that LRMs pre-plan reasoning strength through a pre-allocated direction vector whose magnitude encodes the intended reasoning length. First, Lasso linear regression on question activations at the  position achieves correlation >0.8 with actual reasoning token counts across models from 1.5B to 32B parameters, with prediction quality improving in deeper layers. Second, difference-in-means vectors computed between MATH difficulty levels exhibit cosine similarities of ~0.99, revealing a single shared direction whose L2 norm scales proportionally with required reasoning tokens. Third, activation steering along this vector (h' = h + λr) causally modulates reasoning token count without affecting answer length, and moderate positive steering improves accuracy on MATH500, AIME, and OlympiadBench. The mechanism is traced to the vector's selective modification of  token logits—negative λ increases  logit probability (shorter reasoning) while positive λ decreases it (longer reasoning)—with significantly larger logit shifts on  than on random tokens or the EOS token. Two applications follow: the linear predictor detects overthink attacks before generation, and negative steering on easy MMLU/MATH Level-1 questions reduces reasoning tokens by large margins without performance loss.

## Results

A linear probe trained on question activations at the  token position predicts the number of reasoning tokens with a Pearson correlation of 0.84 (R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B on MATH), and this correlation exceeds 0.8 across all tested models (1.5B–32B). The four difference-in-means vectors extracted across difficulty levels exhibit pairwise cosine similarities of ~0.99, confirming a single shared direction. Activation steering along this direction causally modulates reasoning token count: negative λ (e.g., −0.2) reduces reasoning tokens and drops accuracy, while moderate positive λ improves average accuracy by +0.46 to +2.35 points across MATH500, AIME2024, and OlympiadBench (Table 1).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a mechanistic-interpretability study rather than a method-comparison paper, so there are no competing baselines in the traditional sense. The relevant comparison is each model's unsteered behavior versus its behavior under activation steering with the extracted pre-allocation vector. Moderate positive steering (λ ≈ 0.05–0.1) consistently improves accuracy over the unsteered baseline on all five models, with the largest gains on AIME2024 (up to +5.83 for R1-Distill-Qwen-14B), suggesting the default reasoning allocation is suboptimal for the hardest problems. Negative steering (λ = −0.2) reliably reduces reasoning token count by 30–60% and correspondingly degrades accuracy, confirming the vector's causal role. On easy tasks (MMLU, MATH Level-1), negative steering cuts reasoning tokens substantially while preserving accuracy, demonstrating that LRMs over-allocate reasoning on simple inputs. The authors note that excessive positive steering beyond a threshold yields diminishing or negative returns, attributed to the model's inherent capability ceiling.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the activation steering literature (Rimsky et al., ACL 2024; Arditi et al., NeurIPS 2024) and the difference-in-means vector extraction method (Marks & Tegmark, 2024) to the specific domain of reasoning strength control in large reasoning models. Prior work (Sun et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025) identified linear directions that control reasoning length by computing activation differences between responses of varying token counts, but treated these as post-hoc control levers. This paper's key conceptual departure is showing that such a direction vector is already present in the question activations before any reasoning token is generated, reframing the direction as a pre-allocated planning signal rather than a mere intervention target. It also connects to the emerging line of work on planning capabilities in LLMs (Wu et al., 2024; Dong et al., 2025), providing the first evidence of such planning specifically for reasoning strength allocation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that LRMs encode a planned reasoning budget in a single direction vector whose magnitude scales with difficulty offers a clean, interpretable mechanism for the empirically observed difficulty-awareness phenomenon, and yields two concrete practical tools: pre-generation overthink detection via linear probes and test-time efficiency gains via negative steering on easy problems. However, the analysis is limited to Qwen-based models (R1-Distill-Qwen series and QwQ-32B) and primarily mathematical reasoning, leaving open whether the same single-direction mechanism generalizes to other model families, non-mathematical domains, or more complex reasoning structures. The paper also does not explore whether the pre-allocated vector is the sole mechanism or one of several redundant encodings, and the linear probe's 0.84 correlation, while strong, leaves a non-trivial unexplained variance that more complex architectures might capture.
