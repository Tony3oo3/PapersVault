## Problem

SMOTE and KDEO are the dominant practical strategies for handling class imbalance, yet their theoretical justification has remained largely absent. No prior work provided concentration inequalities or excess risk guarantees for classifiers trained on synthetically oversampled minority data, leaving the choice of key hyperparameters (neighbor count k, bandwidth h) purely heuristic.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255262/cropped_page8_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes an imbalanced binary dataset of n i.i.d. samples (X_i, Y_i) ∈ R^d × {0,1} with n_1 ≪ n_0, along with an oversampling parameter (k for SMOTE or bandwidth h for KDEO). It produces a binary discriminant function g: R^d → R and, crucially, high-probability upper bounds on the excess AM-risk (balanced risk with β=1/2) of the resulting classifier. The synthetic minority samples are generated so that m = n_0, matching the majority class size to target the AM-risk; the evaluation metric is the AM-risk rather than standard 0-1 risk, reflecting the shift toward balanced evaluation that oversampling facilitates.

## Method

The paper adopts a transfer-learning viewpoint, interpreting oversampling as generating synthetic samples to estimate a reweighted minority distribution, and derives uniform concentration inequalities over Lipschitz function classes with controlled Rademacher complexity. For SMOTE, the key bound is of order n_1^{-1/2} + (k/n_1)^{1/d}, obtained by decomposing the synthetic-sample mean into a nearest-neighbor distance term (controlled via Vapnik-style inequalities) and an empirical process term (controlled via Talagrand-Bousquet). For KDEO, the analogous bound is n_1^{-1/2} + h·M_1(K), with the bias term arising from the L^1-convolution error of the kernel density estimate. The nonparametric excess risk analysis for the KDEO-based kernel smoothing plug-in classifier (Theorem 6) reveals a two-bandwidth bias-variance tradeoff: the data-generation bandwidth h (optimal at n_1^{-1/(d+4)}) and the classification bandwidth s (optimal at n_0^{-1/(d+4)}), yielding the rate n_0^{-2/(d+4)} + n_1^{-2/(d+4)} under Sobolev regularity. This shows that the SMOTE default k=5 (corresponding to h ≈ n_1^{-1/d}) is suboptimal for nonparametric classifiers, and that Scott's rule provides the theoretically justified bandwidth choice for KDEO.

## Results

The paper establishes the first uniform concentration bounds for SMOTE (order $n_1^{-1/2} + (k/n_1)^{1/d}$) and KDEO (order $n_1^{-1/2} + h$), and derives a nonparametric excess AM-risk bound for a kernel smoothing plug-in classifier with KDEO, revealing a variance term $(n_1 h^d)^{-1/2}$ and bias term $h^2$ (or $h$ under weaker regularity). Empirically, the headline metric is AM-risk: KDEO with Scott's rule ($h = n_1^{-1/(d+4)}$) consistently achieves lower AM-risk than SMOTE with its default $k=5$ across all four simulated examples and most real datasets. Cross-validation for selecting the KNN parameter $K$ yields further improvements over the fixed $K=\sqrt{n}$ choice, and the optimal SMOTE $k$ for KNN is found to lie between 45 and 60, substantially larger than the default $k=5$.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SMOTE (Chawla et al., 2002), KDEO consistently achieves lower AM-risk across all simulated examples and most real datasets (California, MagicTel, Phoneme, House_16H), with the exception of Abalone where SMOTE's nearest-neighbor interpolation better captures the feature structure. Against the K-NN Balanced Bayes classifier (Aghbalou et al., 2024), which uses reweighting rather than oversampling, KDEO and KDEO-CV outperform BBC across all imbalance levels, suggesting that synthetic oversampling with kernel density estimation better approximates the true minority distribution than threshold adjustment alone. Against the kernel smoothing plug-in rule (Audibert and Tsybakov, 2007) applied to the initial imbalanced data without oversampling, the KDEO-based classifier is expected to perform no worse and typically better, since the two bandwidth parameters ($h$ for data generation and $s$ for classification) can be tuned independently. Logistic regression, representing a parametric baseline, is largely insensitive to the choice of oversampling method or parameters, confirming that the theoretical gains from careful bandwidth/neighbor selection are most relevant for nonparametric classifiers.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper provides the first concentration inequalities for SMOTE, building on nearest-neighbor empirical process theory (Xue and Kpotufe, 2018; Jiang, 2019; Portier, 2025) to bound the discrepancy between synthetic-data empirical risk and true minority-class population risk. For KDEO, it extends the L1-error concentration and bias-variance decomposition framework of Devroye and Györfi (1985) and Holmström and Klemelä (1992) to the imbalanced classification setting, deriving excess risk bounds for the kernel smoothing plug-in classifier of Audibert and Tsybakov (2007). Conceptually, the paper unifies reweighting and resampling approaches under a transfer-learning viewpoint: both approximate the same reweighted (AM-risk) distribution, with oversampling serving as a data-level mechanism to achieve what reweighting does at the objective level.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The results provide the first principled theoretical justification for SMOTE's empirical success and, more importantly, reveal that its default k=5 does not guarantee consistency of the resulting classifier under the AM-risk criterion. The practical guidance that Scott's rule (h ∝ n₁^{-1/(d+4)}) is optimal for KDEO bandwidth selection, and that the optimal k for SMOTE with KNN is substantially larger than 5 (empirically 45–60), offers concrete tuning advice absent from prior heuristic recommendations. Open questions include extending Theorem 6's nonparametric excess risk bound to SMOTE, analyzing KNN plug-in rules, and establishing fast rates under Tsybakov noise conditions. The framework is limited to binary classification and requires bounded support or smooth boundary conditions on the minority class density.
