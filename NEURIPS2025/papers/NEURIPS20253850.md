## Problem

Existing RLVR implementations compute policy gradients over all tokens uniformly, without understanding which tokens actually drive reasoning improvements. This paper asks whether the heterogeneous entropy patterns in Chain-of-Thought reasoning reveal a small subset of critical 'forking tokens' whose selective optimization can match or exceed full-gradient RLVR training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253850/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes mathematical reasoning questions as input to base LLMs (Qwen3-8B/14B/32B) and produces chain-of-thought responses ending with a final answer in \boxed{} format. During training, the model generates G responses per question from DAPO-Math-17K, and the modified loss selectively updates only the top 20% highest-entropy tokens within each batch. Evaluation generates 16 independent zero-shot responses per question at temperature T=1.0 across six math benchmarks (AIME'24/25, AMC'23, MATH500, Minerva, OlympiadBench), with a maximum response length of 20,480 tokens (extended to 29,696 in ablations).

## Method

The core contribution modifies DAPO's clipped policy gradient objective by multiplying the advantage term with an indicator function that retains only tokens whose entropy exceeds a per-batch threshold τ_ρ^B (top ρ=20%), and adjusts the normalization denominator to count only selected tokens. The paper first establishes that in CoT reasoning, over 50% of tokens have entropy below 10⁻² while only 20% exceed 0.672, with high-entropy tokens functioning as logical connectors and decision points that steer reasoning direction. They show RLVR largely preserves base-model entropy patterns (≥86% overlap in top-20% positions at convergence) and predominantly adjusts high-entropy token entropy. The key empirical result is that training with only 20% forking tokens matches or exceeds full-gradient DAPO, with gains scaling strongly with model size (+11.04 on AIME'25 for 32B vs. +0.83 for 8B), while training exclusively on the bottom 80% low-entropy tokens causes severe degradation, confirming that high-entropy minority tokens drive nearly all RLVR performance gains.

## Results

Using only the top 20% highest-entropy (forking) tokens in the policy gradient loss during RLVR training outperforms vanilla DAPO (all tokens) across all three model sizes, with gains scaling with model size. On Qwen3-32B, the forking-token approach achieves 63.54 on AIME'24 (+7.71) and 56.67 on AIME'25 (+11.04) over vanilla DAPO, setting a new SoTA for RLVR on base models under 600B. On Qwen3-14B, gains are +5.21 (AIME'24) and +4.79 (AIME'25); on Qwen3-8B, performance is roughly matched (+1.25, +0.83). Conversely, training exclusively on the bottom 80% lowest-entropy tokens causes severe performance degradation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is vanilla DAPO (full-token policy gradient), the state-of-the-art RLVR algorithm. The forking-token method (top 20% highest-entropy tokens only) wins on every benchmark for the 32B and 14B models, with the largest margins on the hardest problems: +11.04 on AIME'25 and +7.71 on AIME'24 for 32B, and +4.79/+5.21 for 14B. For the 8B model, the method roughly matches vanilla DAPO (average +0.53), with a slight loss on AMC'23 (−0.63), suggesting the smaller model lacks sufficient capacity to fully benefit from enhanced exploration. The scaling trend is clear: the performance gap between forking-token DAPO and vanilla DAPO grows monotonically with model size (8B → 14B → 32B), indicating that high-entropy token selection becomes increasingly advantageous as model capacity increases. Additionally, extending the maximum response length from 20k to 29k tokens further boosts the 32B forking-token model's AIME'24 score from 63.54 to 68.12.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on DAPO (Yu et al., 2025) as its RLVR baseline, modifying only the policy gradient loss to restrict updates to the top 20% highest-entropy tokens. Conceptually, it extends the 'critical tokens' line of work (Vassoyan et al., 2025; Lin et al., 2024) by proposing token entropy as a more principled, model-agnostic criterion for identifying decision points, rather than relying on output correctness. It also connects to observations by Gandhi et al. (2025) and Li et al. (2025) that RLVR primarily learns reasoning format over content, but pushes the analysis to the individual token level to show *which* tokens carry the learning signal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that 20% of high-entropy tokens outperform 100% of tokens at scale (32B: +11 on AIME'25) challenges the default assumption in RLVR that all tokens contribute equally to the gradient, and offers a simple, compute-efficient modification likely to be widely adopted. The scaling trend—where the benefit grows with model size—suggests this could become increasingly important as reasoning models grow. Key open questions include generalization beyond the Qwen family and math domain, whether the optimal 20% threshold is universal, and how this insight interacts with other RLVR innovations like entropy bonuses or KL penalties.
