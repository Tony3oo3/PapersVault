## Problem

While approximation theory has established that deep networks can represent functions more compactly than shallow ones, it remains unclear whether this translates into a quantifiable sample-complexity advantage under gradient-based training. Existing benchmarks like multi-index functions are efficiently learnable by shallow two-layer networks, making them unsuitable for isolating the computational benefit of depth. This paper asks: can one construct analyzable target functions where depth provably reduces the number of samples needed for gradient descent to succeed?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255367/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes i.i.d. Gaussian data points x ∈ R^d and a target function from the SIGHT or MIGHT class, which encodes a hierarchy of latent subspaces with strictly decreasing dimensionality (d^ε₁ > d^ε₂ > … > r) connected by component-wise polynomial maps and linear projections. The output is a scalar regression prediction, and the analysis tracks generalization error as a function of the sample-complexity ratio κ = log n / log d in the joint high-dimensional limit n, d → ∞. The key structural constraint is that intermediate features at each depth level are asymptotically Gaussian and mutually independent, enabling a recursive decomposition of the learning problem.

## Method

The paper introduces SIGHT/MIGHT target functions where depth corresponds to a tree-like composition of polynomial projections that progressively reduce the effective dimensionality from d to d^ε₁ to d^ε₂ … to a finite r. The main theorem proves that a 3-layer MLP trained layer-wise with gradient descent learns such targets through successive coarse-graining: the first layer recovers the subspace W* with Õ(d^{ε₁+1}) samples via neuron-wise spherical SGD, the second layer recovers the nonlinear feature h* with Õ(d^{kε₁}) samples via a single pre-conditioned gradient step (using the sample feature covariance as preconditioner), and the final layer fits the scalar nonlinearity g* with only Õ(1) samples via ridge regression. The proof relies on composing Hermite polynomial decompositions across layers and showing that the asymptotic Gaussianity of intermediate features preserves isotropy and hypercontractivity through the dynamics. Numerical experiments with standard backpropagation and mini-batch training confirm that the dimensionality-reduction mechanism generalizes beyond the idealized layer-wise schedule.

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 1) establishes that a 3-layer network trained layer-wise learns a SIGHT target with sample complexity Õ(d^{ε₁+1}) to recover W*, Õ(d^{kε₁}) to recover the non-linear feature h*, and Õ(1) to fit the final scalar function — a progressive dimensionality reduction from d → d^{ε₁} → 1. For the concrete example f*(x) = tanh(a*·P₃(W*x)/√d^{1/2}) with ε₁=1/2 and k=3, the 3-layer network learns the full target at κ = log n / log d = 1.5, while a 2-layer network plateaus at a cubic Hermite approximation (next term requires κ=3) and kernel methods require κ=2 even for the quadratic component. Numerical simulations (Fig. 2) confirm these thresholds: the 3-layer network (both layer-wise and joint backpropagation) outperforms the 2-layer baseline starting at κ=1.5, and the 2-layer network outperforms kernel ridge regression at the same threshold.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three learning paradigms on the same SIGHT target: kernel/random-feature methods, 2-layer networks, and 3-layer networks. Kernel methods (orange in Fig. 2) are limited to a polynomial Hermite approximation of degree κ_RF = min(κ₁, κ₂) and only beat random performance at κ=2 for the quadratic component, with a double-descent peak at n = d(d−1)/2 + d + 1. The 2-layer network (green) recovers W* at κ=1.5 and then operates as a random-feature model in the reduced d^{ε₁}-dimensional space, achieving a cubic fit at κ=1.5 but unable to capture the degree-6 term without κ=3. The 3-layer network (blue/red) matches the 2-layer's onset at κ=1.5 but surpasses it by recovering the full non-linear feature h* within the same sample budget, reducing the problem to a scalar regression solvable with O(1) additional samples. Joint backpropagation training (red) closely matches the layer-wise theoretical prediction (blue), suggesting the dimensionality-reduction mechanism is robust to the specific training schedule.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the depth-separation line initiated by Wang et al. [84] and Nichani et al. [66], who proved sample-complexity gaps between 2-layer and 3-layer networks but required a fixed first layer. Here, the authors prove separation for fully trained networks without that restriction, using a new class of hierarchical targets (SIGHT/MIGHT) that generalizes the hidden manifold model [43] while avoiding its equivalence to a Gaussian linear target. The analysis draws on the Hermite-decomposition and single-step gradient frameworks from the multi-index literature [19, 30, 58], but applies them to a diverging-dimensional subspace structure that is specific to the hierarchical construction. The coarse-graining narrative connects to renormalization-group ideas in physics [85, 57] and to hierarchical data models [65, 23], but provides the first explicit sample-complexity thresholds for the dimensionality-reduction mechanism across depth.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first rigorous characterization of how depth translates into a quantifiable sample-complexity advantage for gradient-based learning of hierarchical targets, moving beyond approximation-theory separations to a learning-dynamics setting. The introduction of the Compositional Information Exponent (CIE) offers a principled criterion for when hierarchical targets are learnable by gradient descent at each layer, generalizing the classical information exponent. Key limitations include the reliance on layer-wise training with spherical projections and pre-conditioning, uniform target weights, and specific information-exponent assumptions; extending the proof to joint backpropagation training, multi-index (r>1) targets, and deeper architectures remains open. Numerical evidence suggests the dimensionality-reduction mechanism persists under standard backpropagation, but a rigorous treatment of joint training is identified as an important future direction.
