## Problem

The training data attribution (TDA) literature has not clearly delineated the 'final-model-only' (FiMO) setting, where one has access only to the final trained model without the training algorithm or intermediate checkpoints—a scenario made ubiquitous by platforms like HuggingFace. In this setting, it is unclear what the goal of attribution should be or what constitutes a gold standard, since the usual re-training-based approaches are unavailable. This paper reframes the problem as measuring the model's sensitivity to training instances and proposes a principled gold standard to evaluate approximate methods.

## Input / Output

The method requires only the final model parameters θ^f, the full training dataset D, a test instance z, and an evaluation function g (typically the loss). No access to the training algorithm, learning rates, or intermediate checkpoints is needed. The output is a scalar attribution score a_i per training instance, computed as the expected difference in g after further training with and without z_i, adjusted for the effect of further training alone and averaged over r=100 random seeds to account for stochastic training.

## Method

The paper proposes further training from the final model on leave-one-out datasets as a gold standard for FiMODA, with two key refinements: subtracting the effect of further training on the full dataset to handle non-convergence, and taking an expectation over random training realizations to handle stochasticity. It then shows via first- and second-order Taylor expansions of the further training objective that all major gradient-based TDA methods—Grad-Dot, influence functions (CG, LiSSA), TRAK, EK-FAC, and DataInf—arise as approximations to this gold standard, yielding generalized influence function expressions (Proposition 1, Corollary 2) that do not assume convexity or stationarity. Empirically, first-order methods achieve high initial cosine similarity to further training but decay with training duration, while influence function methods are more stable yet never reach the peak quality of first-order methods, with notably lower performance on non-tabular (image, text) datasets.

## Results

The paper evaluates 8 gradient-based TDA methods against a further-training gold standard using cosine similarity as the metric, across 6 datasets (4 tabular, 1 image, 1 text). First-order methods (Grad-Dot, Grad-Cos) achieve the highest initial cosine similarity to further training but decay as the amount of further training increases. Influence function methods (CG, LiSSA) produce more stable (non-decaying) curves but never reach the peak similarity of first-order methods. Cosine similarities are significantly lower for CIFAR-10 and SST-2 compared to tabular datasets, and averaging over 100 random seeds consistently improves similarity, with most gains achieved by 10–20 seeds.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares 8 gradient-based methods against the further-training gold standard. Grad-Dot achieves the highest initial cosine similarity among all methods on tabular datasets, while Grad-Cos is slightly lower due to its unsupported normalization. DataInf, despite incorporating second-order information, behaves essentially like a first-order method (cosine similarity with Grad-Dot typically >0.95) and tracks Grad-Dot closely. CG and LiSSA produce stable, non-decaying curves but with lower peak similarity than first-order methods; LiSSA degrades relative to CG on larger models (CIFAR-10, SST-2), and LiSSA-H (true Hessian) is consistently worse than LiSSA (Gauss-Newton). EK-FAC matches or exceeds LiSSA on CIFAR-10 and SST-2 but underperforms on the three smaller tabular datasets. TRAK_{M=1} (the FiMO-restricted variant with a single checkpoint) gives the poorest approximations to further training among all methods evaluated, a notable contrast with its strong performance in the TAA setting reported in the original TRAK paper.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the retrospective TDA literature (Bae et al. 2022, Schioppa et al. 2023, Basu et al. 2021, Nguyen et al. 2023) but departs from it in three key ways: it explicitly defines the Final-Model-Only (FiMO) setting that prior works have not delineated, it proposes further training (with stochasticity averaging and non-convergence adjustment) as a gold standard rather than the PBRF or re-training used elsewhere, and it unifies a broader set of gradient-based methods—including first-order Grad-Dot/Grad-Cos, TRAK, DataInf, and EK-FAC—under a single Taylor-expansion-of-further-training framework. The derivation of generalized influence functions (Proposition 1, Corollary 2) extends the standard Koh & Liang (2017) formulation by dropping the stationarity and convexity assumptions, yielding expressions that reduce to prior results only in the near-stationary limit.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The FiMODA formulation gives the TDA community a clear, well-motivated goal and gold standard for the increasingly common scenario where only a final model is available (e.g., HuggingFace open-weights models), and the unifying view clarifies what each gradient-based method actually approximates. The finding that first-order methods outperform influence functions at their peak in the FiMO setting challenges the field's default assumption that second-order information is strictly better, motivating new hybrid approaches. However, the authors themselves note the results 'leave something to be desired,' particularly for non-tabular data where cosine similarities drop substantially, and the computationally expensive gold standard limits experiment scale. Open questions include whether first- and second-order strengths can be combined, the right amount of further training to use, and extending to group influence.
