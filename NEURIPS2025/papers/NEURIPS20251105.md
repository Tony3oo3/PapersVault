## Problem

Drone visual geo-localization suffers severe degradation under adverse weather (rain, fog, snow, darkness), and existing methods are limited by two issues: they rely on a small set of predefined weather labels that cannot generalize to unseen or mixed conditions, and they entangle scene semantics with weather disturbances by injecting coarse pseudo-weather labels directly into visual representations. This paper addresses both gaps by introducing open-set, text-guided weather semantics and a mechanism to disentangle scene from weather features.

## Input / Output

At training, the method consumes drone-view images (384×384 px, 32×32 patches) perturbed with synthetic weather via imgaug, paired with structured text captions (weather description + spatial layout) generated training-free by Qwen2.5-VL using chain-of-thought prompting; satellite-view images remain unperturbed. At inference, a single drone-view image and its corresponding text description are encoded in parallel, and the fused multimodal representation is passed through a single-layer MLP to predict one of C geographic location labels (1652 or 200 classes), with no per-weather parameter sets or online fine-tuning required.

## Method

The core contribution is a two-stage pipeline: (1) a training-free weather reasoning mechanism that uses an off-the-shelf LVLM (Qwen2.5-VL) with a 6-step CoT prompt to first estimate weather (global visibility → local atmospheric cues → weather label) and then describe spatial semantics (macro layout → structural elements → topological relationships), producing open-set, weather-aware captions without manual annotation; (2) a multi-modality alignment model built on XVLM (Swin Transformer + BERT) that introduces a text-driven dynamic channel gating module, where the text embedding generates a per-channel gating vector g ∈ (0,1)^D that adaptively reweights visual features (f_fuse = g ⊙ f_I + (1−g) ⊙ f_T), effectively disentangling scene content from weather noise. Training jointly optimizes four objectives: image-text contrastive loss (InfoNCE-style), image-text matching loss with hard negatives, a localized alignment loss (IoU + L1) that regresses region coordinates from cross-encoder CLS tokens, and a standard cross-entropy classification loss, all encouraging weather-invariant, scene-discriminative embeddings.

## Results

On University-1652 (Drone→Satellite), WeatherPrompt achieves a mean R@1 of 77.14% and mean AP of 80.20% across 10 weather conditions, outperforming the strongest baseline (MuSe-Net, 65.15% R@1) by over 12 points. On the Satellite→Drone task, it reaches 84.68% mean R@1 and 65.75% mean AP. On the real-world SUES-200 dataset, the method improves mean R@1 from 52.02% to 62.52% (Drone→Satellite) and from 69.43% to 80.13% (Satellite→Drone) over MuSe-Net. Under the most challenging unseen combination (Dark+Rain+Fog), the method achieves 44.44% R@1 and 64.94% AP (D2S), roughly doubling the 22.22% R@1 of prior methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

WeatherPrompt substantially outperforms all baselines on the Drone→Satellite task across University-1652, achieving 77.14% mean R@1 versus 65.15% for MuSe-Net [29] (the strongest prior method), 64.16% for LPN [2], and 62.30% for both IBN-Net [70] and CCR [73]. The gap is most pronounced under adverse conditions: under Dark weather, Ours scores 67.22% R@1 versus 53.85% (MuSe-Net) and 51.10% (CCR); under Fog+Rain, 78.75% versus 65.59% (MuSe-Net). On the Satellite→Drone task, Ours matches MuSe-Net [29] at 84.68% mean R@1 and 65.75% mean AP, while surpassing LPN [2] (83.64% R@1) and CCR [73] (75.02% R@1). On the real-world SUES-200 dataset, Ours improves mean R@1 by +10.40% (D2S) and +10.34% (S2D) over MuSe-Net, and by +10.48% in mean AP (S2D). Sample4Geo [71] and SafeNet [12] show extreme sensitivity to weather (e.g., SafeNet drops to 39.17% R@1 under Fog+Rain on University-1652), whereas WeatherPrompt maintains above 73% R@1 in all 10 conditions. The unseen-weather generalization test (Dark+Rain+Fog) confirms the advantage: Ours achieves 44.44% R@1 (D2S) and 66.66% R@1 (S2D), roughly doubling the 22.22% and 33.33% of Sample4Geo [71] and MuSe-Net [29] respectively.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of cross-view geo-localization (drone-to-satellite matching) and vision-language alignment, departing from prior multi-weather geo-localization methods that rely on a fixed set of discrete weather labels (e.g., sunny, rainy, foggy) injected directly into visual features. It repurposes the CLIP-style contrastive/matching training paradigm and the XVLM backbone, but introduces two conceptual departures: (1) a training-free Chain-of-Thought prompting pipeline over an off-the-shelf LVLM (Qwen2.5-VL) to generate open-set, continuous weather-and-spatial descriptions, and (2) a text-embedding-driven dynamic channel gating mechanism that adaptively reweights visual features at inference, replacing the coarse pseudo-label injection used by MuSe-Net, SafeNet, and similar methods. The work thus carves new ground by treating weather as a continuous, combinatorial semantic signal carried in natural language rather than a categorical tag.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical relevance is clear: drones deployed for disaster response, search-and-rescue, and urban surveillance must operate under rain, fog, snow, and darkness, where existing geo-localization pipelines degrade sharply. The training-free description pipeline and the lightweight gating module (0.2 M parameters, 0.4 ms extra latency) make the approach deployable on resource-constrained platforms without per-weather fine-tuning. Key open questions remain: all weather perturbations are synthetically generated via imgaug, so robustness to genuinely novel or region-specific meteorological phenomena is untested; the LVLM-generated captions inherit pretraining biases that could propagate into alignment; and the real-world video evaluation (54 pairs from YouTube) is small-scale. Whether the CoT-driven description strategy transfers to other cross-modal retrieval tasks beyond geo-localization is an interesting direction for future work.
