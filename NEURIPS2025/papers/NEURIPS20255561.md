## Problem

Inference-time compute can be allocated either sequentially (longer chains of thought) or in parallel (multiple short chains aggregated via majority vote or best-of-n), but the optimal trade-off between these two axes remains poorly understood. This paper asks whether there exist reasoning problems where one axis is fundamentally and exponentially more powerful than the other, and provides both theoretical and empirical evidence that sequential scaling can be exponentially more cost-effective than parallel scaling.

## Input / Output

The input is a graph presented as a list of edges in randomized order with randomly permuted vertex IDs, followed by the IDs of a source node s and two target nodes t1, t2. The graph is constructed as the disjoint union of two identical components, one containing s and ti and the other containing t(3-i), so exactly one target is reachable. The model outputs a chain of thought (a walk through the graph from s to a target) followed by a binary decision identifying which target is connected to s. The CoT must satisfy an evidence criterion: it starts at s, ends at a target, and every intermediate node has at least one neighbor appearing earlier in the sequence.

## Method

The paper introduces the (s,t1,t2)-connectivity problem on 'bridge graphs' (parameterized by depth, short/long path lengths, and deadend paths) as a testbed where sequential scaling provably dominates parallel scaling. Theoretically, it proves two separations: (1) under the assumption L ⊄ TC⁰, a polynomial-length CoT solves connectivity while any polynomial number of O(1)-length CoTs aggregated by majority vote cannot; and (2) via a Vertex Query Model abstraction, it constructs two-path and bridge graph distributions where the number of queries needed for parallel scaling to match sequential scaling grows exponentially in the graph depth. Empirically, it trains small Mistral transformers from scratch under three CoT strategies (Shortest-Path, Path, DFS) and shows that DFS-trained models exploit longer CoT budgets to achieve perfect accuracy while short-CoT models plateau at exponentially low success rates that require exponential parallel sampling to overcome. It further validates these findings on frontier reasoning models (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B, Qwen3-32B, S1-32B) and on AIME2024, and demonstrates via STaR-style RL that CoT length grows emergently during training, mirroring the sequential scaling behavior observed in practice.

## Results

On the Bridge(5) graph connectivity task, the DFS-trained transformer achieves 100% evidence accuracy, while the Path-trained model reaches only 11.16% and the Shortest-Path model reaches 0%, demonstrating that short CoTs cannot be compensated by parallel scaling. After 4 STaR RL iterations, the Path model's evidence accuracy on Bridge(3) jumps from 21.16% to 92.02%, with CoT length increasing at each iteration. On frontier LLMs (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B, S1-32B, Qwen3-32B), approximately 1000 tokens of sequential scaling are required before non-trivial accuracy is achieved, and parallel scaling (majority vote over up to 64 CoTs) provides only marginal gains once sequential scale is insufficient. On AIME2024, S1-32B shows the same pattern: sequential scaling is necessary and cannot be efficiently replaced by parallel sampling.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates three frontier LLMs—DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B, S1-32B, and Qwen3-32B—on the (s,t₁,t₂)-connectivity task over bridge graphs, and all three exhibit the same qualitative trend: without sufficient sequential CoT length, accuracy is at chance level (~50%), and parallel scaling via majority vote over multiple short CoTs fails to close the gap. Specifically, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B (Figure 1 right, Figure 5) requires a substantial sequential budget before majority voting over parallel samples becomes useful, confirming the theoretical exponential separation. S1-32B (Figure 5, Figure 11) shows the identical pattern on both the graph connectivity task and AIME2024, where sequential scaling is essential and parallel sampling with temperature 1.0 and majority vote aggregation cannot substitute for it. Qwen3-32B (Figure 5) mirrors these results on the graph connectivity task. The comparison is not a head-to-head accuracy race between models but rather a demonstration that all three models, despite different training regimes, exhibit the same sequential-over-parallel scaling hierarchy predicted by the paper's theoretical results.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of transformer expressivity theory (Merrill & Sabharwal 2024, Chiang 2024) and the emerging test-time scaling literature (Snell et al. 2025, Wu et al. 2024). It builds on the known result that bounded-depth transformers are captured by TC^0 circuits, but departs from prior expressivity work by deriving a concrete sequential-vs-parallel scaling separation rather than a single-model capability bound. The Vertex Query Model is a new analytical abstraction inspired by the 'globality barrier' (Abbe et al. 2024) and multi-hop reasoning limitations (Sanford et al. 2024), but is developed here specifically to yield fine-grained query-complexity separations. Empirically, the paper extends the budget-forcing technique from s1 (Muennighoff et al. 2025) and the STaR RL framework (Zelikman et al. 2022) to study how CoT length emerges during training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The exponential separation between sequential and parallel scaling provides a principled answer to a question that has been largely empirical in the inference-time compute literature: for certain reasoning structures, you cannot substitute many short chains for one long chain, no matter how many you sample. This has direct practical implications for how frontier reasoning systems allocate their inference budget. Key limitations include the reliance on the unproven TC^0 ⊉ L assumption, the heuristic nature of the VQM abstraction, and the fact that the strongest results are on synthetic graph tasks rather than naturalistic reasoning. Open questions include characterizing which real-world reasoning problems exhibit this exponential gap, finding the optimal mix of parallel and sequential scaling for a given task, and understanding whether RL training can learn to use the sequential budget more efficiently.
