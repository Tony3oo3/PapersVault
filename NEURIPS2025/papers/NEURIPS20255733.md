## Problem

Shah and Peters (2020) proved that no universally finite-sample valid CI test can achieve nontrivial power, but their adversarial 'hiding' construction does not explain the widespread practical failures of popular kernel-based CI tests like KCI and GCM. This paper identifies the two major factors underlying those practical failures: errors in conditional mean embedding estimation (driving Type-I error inflation) and the critical but previously unrecognized role of selecting an appropriate conditioning kernel k_C (necessary for power but tending to worsen Type-I error).

## Input / Output

The method consumes n samples of (A, B, C) triples along with up to five kernel choices: k_A and k_B for the test statistic, k_C for the conditioning/weighting, and separate regression kernels k_{C→A} and k_{C→B} for estimating conditional mean embeddings via kernel ridge regression. It outputs a KCI U-statistic, a binary reject/fail-to-reject decision calibrated via wild bootstrap, and (when using the proposed selection procedure) an optimized k_C lengthscale chosen on a training split to maximize an estimated signal-to-noise ratio. A key design constraint is that k_C must be selected independently of the regression kernels, since the lengthscale of the conditional covariance function γ(C) can differ substantially from the lengthscales needed to regress A or B on C.

## Method

The paper reframes KCI through a novel Theorem 2.2 extending Daudin's (1980) characterization with a weighting function w(C), showing that standard GCM is nearly a special case of KCI with linear kernels and k_C(c,c')=w(c)w(c'). It then identifies conditional mean embedding estimation error Δ_{A|C}, Δ_{B|C} as the root cause of Type-I error inflation: under the null, the biased statistic KCÎ_n has mean Θ(1) and standard deviation Θ(1/√n) rather than the correct Θ(1/n), causing it to almost surely exceed the nominal null threshold. Formal bounds (Theorems 6.2–6.3) quantify the required decay rate of regression error for valid null calibration, and a wild-bootstrap approximation error bound is derived. For test power, the paper proposes selecting k_C by maximizing an estimated SNR on a training split (with a generalization bound in Theorem 5.2), demonstrating analytically and empirically that the optimal k_C lengthscale targets the frequency of the conditional covariance function rather than the regression functions, and that this power-maximizing selection can overfit to regression errors and inflate Type-I error when training data is limited.

## Results

In the synthetic example (7) with γ(C) = sin(βC), GCM (corresponding to ℓ_C = ∞) yields KCI = 0 and cannot detect any dependence, while the optimal ℓ_C² that maximizes SNR depends on β and lies at an intermediate value balancing kernel localization against estimation noise. In the multi-dimensional C experiment (Table 1), sharing coordinates between regression and dependence (Scenario 1) inflates Type-I error to 0.21 versus 0.10 for separate coordinates (Scenario 2), at the cost of Type-II error dropping from 0.08 to 0.0. With limited training data (m=200), SNR-based kernel selection can push Type-I error well above the nominal α=0.05 for certain ℓ_C² values, whereas with m=1000 the Type-I error remains controlled across all ℓ_C².

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is against GCM (Shah & Peters, 2020), which corresponds to KCI with ℓ_C = ∞; in the synthetic example where conditional covariance alternates in sign, GCM's averaged statistic is exactly zero, so it has zero power regardless of sample size. Against CIRCE (Pogodin et al., 2023) and SplitKCI (Pogodin et al., 2024), which both reuse the regression kernels k_{C→A} or k_{C→B} as the conditioning kernel k_C, the paper demonstrates that this choice is suboptimal for measuring dependence: the regression lengthscales are tuned to f_A and f_B, not to the frequency β of the conditional covariance γ(C), leading to substantially higher Type-II error. The proposed SNR-based k_C selection recovers the correct lengthscale and matches the theoretical power curve (Figure 3, right), but at the cost of inflating Type-I error when regression estimates are noisy (m=200). The paper thus shows that no single kernel choice dominates: GCM is safest for Type-I control but has zero power in sign-alternating settings, while power-optimal k_C selection trades Type-I safety for substantially better detection.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reframes the Kernel-based Conditional Independence (KCI) test of Zhang et al. (2011) through a new conditional-covariance decomposition (Theorem 2.2), showing that the Generalized Covariance Measure (GCM) of Shah and Peters (2020) and its weighted variant of Scheidegger et al. (2022) are nearly special cases of KCI with simple linear kernels. It departs from prior practical KCI work (Pogodin et al., 2023, 2024) by demonstrating that the conditioning kernel k_C should be chosen specifically for measuring dependence rather than reused from the regression kernels, and by identifying conditional mean embedding estimation error—not the adversarial 'hiding' distributions of the impossibility result—as the root cause of Type-I error inflation in practice. The kernel-selection methodology draws on the SNR-maximization framework of Liu et al. (2020) and Xu et al. (2024) for two-sample and unconditional independence tests, adapted here to the conditional setting where permutation-based calibration is unavailable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Conditional independence testing underpins causal discovery (PC algorithm), fairness auditing, and out-of-distribution robustness, yet popular kernel-based tests are known to have poor Type-I error control in practice. This paper provides the first clear mechanistic explanation—regression errors in conditional mean embeddings bias the test statistic and inflate its variance from Θ(1/n) to Θ(1/√n)—and formal bounds quantifying how much regression error is tolerable. A key open tension it exposes is that the very kernel selection needed for adequate power (localizing to regions of conditional dependence) simultaneously amplifies Type-I error by overfitting to spurious correlations in noisy regressions; resolving this trade-off without sacrificing power remains an open problem. The results also clarify that sample splitting alone does not fix the issue, since the bias from imperfect regressors persists on the test set.
