## Problem

Naive weak-to-strong (W2S) generalization, where a strong model is fine-tuned using labels from a weaker model, breaks down under distribution shifts: when the weak model is trained on data from a different distribution than the strong model's fine-tuning data, the strong model often performs worse than the weak supervisor itself. This is a critical gap because in real-world scenarios—such as a radiologist interpreting scans from an unfamiliar machine—human (or proxy) supervision is simultaneously limited in capability and misaligned with the target domain, making standard W2S approaches unreliable or even counterproductive.

## Input / Output

For image classification, RGB images are fed to a frozen DINO ViT-B/8 backbone whose linear classifier head is trained on soft pseudo-labels produced by an ensemble of AlexNet weak models; the output is a class label. For text classification, text inputs pass through weak LLMs (e.g., Qwen2.5-0.5B) whose logits are combined via learned weights to form soft supervision for strong LLMs (e.g., Qwen2.5-7B/14B, Meditron-7B/70B), producing class predictions. For preference alignment, text prompt–response pairs are scored by an ensemble of weak reward models, and the strong model is aligned via a modified DPO objective (DPO-R) that uses ensemble-derived β_c and β_r coefficients, producing preference-aligned generated text.

## Method

RAVEN introduces two mechanisms for robust W2S generalization under distribution shift. First, adaptive weighting replaces fixed ensemble weights with trainable parameters θ^(i) that are jointly optimized with the strong model's classifier via alternating minimization of a cross-entropy loss between the strong model's output and the weighted sum of weak model predictions; the weights are clipped and renormalized after each update. Second, easy-sample guided initialization pre-trains the strong model exclusively on samples where all weak models agree, with weights fixed at 1/M, preventing the strong model from shortcutting by mimicking its own initial suboptimal predictions before adaptive weighting is enabled. For preference alignment, the authors derive DPO-R, which decomposes the single DPO temperature β into separate β_c and β_r terms computed from the Bradley-Terry probabilities of the ensemble reward, allowing the adaptive weights to influence the DPO gradient. A theoretical analysis (Appendix C) shows that under mild assumptions about weak model confidence and ensemble agreement, the alternating optimization naturally drives the highest weight toward the best-performing weak model on the target distribution without requiring ground-truth access.

## Results

On the OOD image classification benchmark (iWildCam, CAMELYON17, FMOW), RAVEN(3) achieves an average PGR of 16.09% and 51.54% accuracy, outperforming the best ensemble baseline Ens(3) (PGR 10.39%, accuracy 49.70%) by 55% in PGR. On the InD ImageNet setting, RAVEN(3) reaches 67.90% accuracy with 66.33% PGR, matching or exceeding all single-weak-model baselines. For text classification (Amazon-WILDS OOD), RAVEN achieves 71.14% accuracy (Llama-3.2-1B→Llama-3.1-8B) with 74.56% PGR, a 57% PGR improvement over the best baseline. On preference alignment (HH-RLHF OOD), RAVEN attains a 64.04% GPT-4o win rate with 58.94% PGR, surpassing the strongest baseline by 33% in PGR.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RAVEN decisively outperforms all seven baselines in the OOD setting. Against Naive W2S [10], RAVEN improves PGR from 1.80% to 16.09% (8.9×); against Conf [10] and V-sup [26], the gap is even larger (16.09 vs. 2.80 and 2.44 respectively), indicating that confidence-based strategies fail under distribution shift. The strongest ensemble baseline, Co-sup [45] (which uses domain-specific weak models), achieves 12.47% PGR, still 29% below RAVEN(3) and 37% below RAVEN(>3). The Bayesian weakS-to-strong method [19] (Bayes) collapses under OOD (PGR 2.77%) and even produces negative PGR (−23.99%) on InD ImageNet, while RAVEN maintains positive gains in both settings. On InD ImageNet, RAVEN(3) (66.33% PGR) is competitive with Conf (68.12%) and Co-sup (69.54%) but slightly trails them, confirming that RAVEN's primary advantage is robustness under distribution shift rather than InD performance. In text classification, RAVEN achieves 74.56% PGR (Llama) and 85.88% PGR (Qwen 7B) on Amazon-WILDS OOD, far exceeding Co-sup (44.77% and 28.32%) and Bayes (−28.70% and −43.01%), while in preference alignment RAVEN reaches 58.94% PGR vs. 40.64% for Ens and 22.21% for Bayes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the weak-to-strong (W2S) generalization framework of Burns et al. [10] by explicitly modeling distribution shift between the source and fine-tuning distributions, a setting the original work assumes away. The ensemble-based weakS-to-strong formulation from Cui et al. [19] provides the starting point for combining multiple weak models, but RAVEN replaces their fixed or Bayesian-estimated weights with fully trainable adaptive weights optimized jointly with the strong model. The easy-sample guided initialization phase borrows the warm-up-on-consensus-samples strategy from webly supervised learning [15], and the preference alignment experiments build on DPO [56] with a novel DPO-R objective that incorporates ensemble reward predictions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that naive W2S generalization becomes infeasible (negative PGR) in 11 of 19 ImageNet-C corruption cases challenges a core assumption in the W2S literature and has direct implications for future superhuman AI oversight, where annotators will inevitably face unfamiliar data. RAVEN's adaptive weighting provides a practical, domain-agnostic fix that automatically identifies the most reliable weak model without ground-truth access, though the current limitation that weak models differ only in random seeds (rather than truly diverse expertise) leaves open whether the approach captures realistic human annotator diversity. The (model, sample)-wise weighting variant remains an open optimization challenge that could yield further gains.
