## Problem

Existing audio-driven human animation methods are limited to single-person scenarios and cannot handle multi-stream audio inputs, causing all persons in a reference image to speak simultaneously rather than in alternating conversation. Additionally, these methods exhibit poor instruction-following, failing to generate body movements or interactions described in text prompts. This paper introduces the task of audio-driven multi-person conversational video generation to address both the audio-person binding problem and the instruction-following gap.

## Input / Output

The method takes a reference image containing two (or more) persons, one audio stream per person (extracted via Wav2Vec with a context window of k frames), and a free-form text prompt describing the scene and interactions. It produces a temporally coherent video where each person's lip movements are synchronized with their respective audio stream while the overall motion follows the text prompt. The base model is Wan2.1-I2V-14B (a DiT-based image-to-video diffusion model with 3D VAE), and long videos are generated autoregressively by conditioning on the last 5 frames of the previous segment.

## Method

MultiTalk adds audio cross-attention layers (with an MLP-based audio adapter for temporal compression matching the 3D VAE) to each DiT block of Wan2.1-I2V-14B. The key architectural contribution is L-RoPE (Label Rotary Position Embedding), which assigns distinct numerical label ranges to video latent tokens (e.g., 0–4 for person 1, 20–24 for person 2) and corresponding static labels to each audio stream (2 and 22), then applies RoPE-style rotations in the audio cross-attention to activate only the relevant region of the attention map, thereby binding each audio stream to its correct person. Person localization is performed adaptively via the reference-image-to-video self-attention map, which yields per-token similarity scores used to assign labels without requiring fixed spatial splits. Training uses a two-stage protocol (single-person then dual-audio), partial parameter updates (only audio cross-attention and adapter), and multi-task mixing with I2V data to preserve the base model's instruction-following ability.

## Results

On the HDTF talking head dataset, MultiTalk-single achieves the best Sync-C (8.54), E-FID (1.00), and FID (24.01), while Sonic leads on Sync-D (6.43) and FVD (89.34). On CelebV-HQ, MultiTalk-multiple attains the top Sync-C (7.33) and FVD (184.86), and MultiTalk-single leads on Sync-D (7.13), E-FID (1.41), and FID (32.31). On the EMTD talking body dataset, MultiTalk-multiple achieves the best Sync-C (8.34), E-FID (1.51), and FID (31.93), substantially outperforming EchoMimicV2 (Sync-C 6.31, FID 35.99) and FantasyTalking (Sync-C 3.32, FID 37.68).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MultiTalk outperforms all seven baselines on the majority of metrics across both talking head datasets. On HDTF, MultiTalk-single leads in lip sync quality (Sync-C 8.54 vs. Sonic's 8.35), facial expressiveness (E-FID 1.00 vs. Hallo3's 1.12), and image quality (FID 24.01 vs. Sonic's 29.53), though Sonic retains a slight edge on Sync-D (6.43 vs. 6.69) and FVD (89.34 vs. 95.99). On CelebV-HQ, MultiTalk dominates across all five metrics, with MultiTalk-multiple achieving the best FVD (184.86) and Sync-C (7.33). On the EMTD talking body benchmark, MultiTalk-multiple surpasses EchoMimicV2 by 2.03 in Sync-C (8.34 vs. 6.31) and 4.06 in FID (31.93 vs. 35.99), and outperforms FantasyTalking by a wide margin (Sync-C 8.34 vs. 3.32). The closest competitor overall is Sonic, which matches or slightly exceeds MultiTalk on temporal consistency metrics (Sync-D, FVD) but trails significantly on lip sync accuracy and image quality. Notably, MultiTalk-multiple (trained with dual-stream audio) maintains performance comparable to MultiTalk-single, confirming that multi-person training does not degrade single-person capability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the audio-driven human animation lineage (talking head → talking body) to a multi-person conversational setting, building on the Wan2.1-I2V-14B DiT-based video diffusion backbone and adapting Rotary Position Embedding (from RoFormer) into L-RoPE for audio-person binding. It departs from prior single-person methods (EMO, EchoMimic, CyberHost, OmniHuman) by introducing multi-stream audio injection and an adaptive person localization mechanism, while also drawing on EMO's context-window audio embedding strategy. The training strategy of partial parameter fine-tuning combined with multi-task I2V training represents a practical departure from the common full-fine-tuning paradigm in video diffusion adaptation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first end-to-end framework for multi-person conversational video generation with per-person audio binding, opening applications in multi-character content creation and interactive livestreaming. Key limitations include reliance on real (non-synthesized) audio for expressive results, testing limited to 2–3 speakers, and dependence on a single 14B backbone. Open questions include whether L-RoPE generalizes to arbitrary speaker counts and dynamic camera movements, whether the partial-parameter training insight transfers to other conditioning modalities, and how to close the gap between real and TTS-driven animation quality.
