## Problem

In verifier-guided test-time scaling, the verification granularity—how frequently a process reward model is invoked during generation—has been set heuristically (e.g., at every newline-delimited step) and kept static, with no systematic study of whether this choice is optimal. This paper asks whether the conventional granularity is sub-optimal for the accuracy-compute frontier and, if so, how to adapt it to generator capability, task difficulty, and available compute budget.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252964/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a math problem prompt, a generator LLM (e.g., Qwen2.5-Math-7B), and a discriminative process reward model (e.g., Skywork-o1-1.5B) as inputs. Generation proceeds in newline-delimited steps, and the granularity parameter g specifies how many such steps elapse between verifier invocations. The output is a selected solution (chosen via majority voting over n generated candidates) along with the tuned g*; in practice, g* is pre-computed on a small validation set (as few as 5 samples) and reused at inference time for a given model-task-budget configuration.

## Method

VG-Search unifies verifier-guided Beam Search (g=1) and Best-of-N sampling (g=L) under a single algorithm parameterized by granularity g, beam width B1, and branch-out factor B2. Each cycle generates B1×B2 candidates, verifies and retains the top B1, extends those B1 beams by g−1 steps, then branches B2 one-step continuations per beam—crucially, only the B1 selected candidates enter the Extend step rather than the full B1×B2 set, yielding significant proposer FLOPs savings. A closed-form compute cost model (C_total ∝ B1·L/g·[T(g−1+B2)Pg + αB2Pv]) links g to total FLOPs and enables budget-matched comparisons. Building on empirical findings that optimal g shifts with generator strength, task difficulty, and compute budget, the paper proposes two adaptive strategies: CM-g (largest g preserving accuracy within tolerance ε of g=1, minimizing compute) and AM-g (g maximizing accuracy under a fixed generation budget), both requiring only a one-time validation search over a small subset.

## Results

On MATH-500 (Qwen2.5-Math-7B proposer, Skywork-o1-1.5B verifier), the adaptive AM-g strategy achieves 90.1% accuracy at n=64 with 6,145 FLOPs, outperforming Beam Search (87.0%, 12,010 FLOPs) by 3.1% while using ~49% fewer FLOPs. The compute-minimization strategy CM-g (val) at n=128 reaches 89.9% accuracy with only 11,086 FLOPs—54% less than Beam Search's 24,021 FLOPs—while still exceeding its 89.3% accuracy. At n=256, AM-g (val) attains 90.4% accuracy with 28,189 FLOPs versus 48,042 FLOPs for Beam Search (88.8%), confirming consistent gains across compute budgets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Beam Search (Snell et al., 2024), adaptive VG-Search wins on both accuracy and compute across all budgets: AM-g (val) gains +3.1% at n=64 (90.1% vs 87.0%), +0.8% at n=128, and +1.6% at n=256, while CM-g (val) reduces FLOPs by 54% at n=128 (11,086 vs 24,021) while still exceeding Beam Search accuracy. Against DVTS (Beeching et al., 2024), AM-g (val) achieves +0.9% at n=64 and +0.9% at n=256 with roughly half the FLOPs; CM-g (val) at n=128 matches DVTS accuracy (89.9% vs 89.7%) at 46% of the compute. Against Best-of-N (Brown et al., 2024), AM-g (val) leads by +0.6% at n=64 and +1.2% at n=256, again at substantially lower FLOPs. The oracle-tuned variants (g selected on the test set) achieve slightly higher accuracy (e.g., 90.8% at n=256) but the validation-tuned versions generalize well, confirming practicality without test-set leakage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the verifier-guided test-time scaling framework established by Snell et al. (2024), which identified Beam Search (fine-grained, per-step verification) and Best-of-N (coarse-grained, end-of-sequence verification) as two discrete extremes, and on Brown et al. (2024) and Beeching et al. (2024) for the Best-of-N and DVTS baselines. The key departure is the introduction of a continuous granularity parameter g that interpolates between these extremes, challenging the field's implicit assumption that verification at every newline-delimited generation step (g=1) is the natural or optimal choice. The paper also extends the compute-cost modeling of Singhi et al. (2025) to incorporate the granularity dimension, enabling principled FLOPs-based comparison across the full spectrum of search behaviors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that the optimal verification granularity is dynamic—shifting with generator capability, task difficulty, and compute budget—provides a concrete, low-overhead lever (selectable with as few as 5 validation samples) for practitioners to reduce inference FLOPs by over 50% while maintaining or improving accuracy. A key limitation is that g remains fixed per problem instance; the authors acknowledge that step-by-step adaptive granularity, which would respond to real-time generator confidence or reasoning complexity, is an important but unexplored direction. Broader open questions include whether these findings generalize beyond math reasoning to open-ended generation tasks (partially addressed via HumanEval), whether generative PRMs (which have different verification cost profiles) exhibit the same granularity sensitivity, and how optimal g interacts with internal scaling methods like RL-trained CoT.
