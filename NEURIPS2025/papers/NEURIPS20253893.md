## Problem

Unsupervised model debiasing methods rely on auxiliary bias-amplifier models trained on the original biased dataset, but these models inevitably memorize the small population of bias-conflicting samples (typically <5% of training data) within a few epochs, destroying their ability to provide distinct supervisory signals for the two subpopulations. This memorization problem has no clean solution without bias-annotated validation sets, which are unavailable in realistic unsupervised settings, leaving the training protocol for effective bias amplifiers as an open issue.

## Input / Output

The method takes a biased RGB image dataset with class labels (no bias annotations) as input; images are resized to 64×64 for the diffusion model. It produces three intermediate artifacts: synthetic bias-aligned images (1000 per class) generated by the CDPM, a frozen Bias Amplifier (DenseNet-121) trained exclusively on those synthetic images, and per-sample bias pseudo-labels obtained by running the BA on the original training set. The final output is a debiased classifier (ResNet-18 or ResNet-50) producing standard class labels, with the BA's per-sample loss or misclassification signal used as the debiasing supervisory signal.

## Method

DDB exploits the observation that conditional diffusion models trained on strongly biased data (ρ ≥ 0.95) predominantly learn the per-class biased distribution p(x|b) rather than the true conditional p(x|y), effectively amplifying the spurious correlation. A multi-scale UNet-based CDPM with classifier-free guidance (w=1) is trained from scratch on the biased dataset, then used to sample synthetic images that are almost entirely bias-aligned (98–99.7% verified by human annotators). A DenseNet-121 Bias Amplifier is trained on only these synthetic images, so it never encounters real bias-conflicting samples, eliminating memorization by construction. The frozen BA is then plugged into two debiasing recipes: Recipe I (2-step) uses BA misclassifications filtered by a z-score threshold to assign bias pseudo-labels, which feed into G-DRO optimization; Recipe II (end-to-end) uses the BA's per-sample loss to compute a relative difficulty weight r = L_BA/(L_target + L_BA) that reweights the target model's cross-entropy loss during joint training, following the LfF framework.

## Results

On Waterbirds, DDB-II (end-to-end) achieves 91.56±0.15% Worst Group Accuracy (WGA), surpassing the supervised G-DRO baseline (91.40%) and all unsupervised methods, while DDB-I (two-step) reaches 90.81±0.68% WGA. On BAR, DDB-II attains 72.81±1.02% average accuracy, outperforming the second-best DebiAN (69.88%) by nearly 3 points. On BFFHQ, DDB-I achieves 74.67±2.37% conflicting accuracy, exceeding the previous state-of-the-art ETF-Debias (73.60%). On ImageNet-A, DDB-I reaches 39.80±0.50% average accuracy, the best among all compared methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DDB outperforms all 17 compared baselines across the six benchmark datasets. On Waterbirds, DDB-II (91.56% WGA) beats the closest unsupervised competitor B2T+G-DRO [25] (90.70%) by 0.86 points and even exceeds the supervised G-DRO [43] (91.40%); it also surpasses MoDAD [41] (89.43%), CNC [51] (88.50%), LISA [49] (89.20%), CDvG+LfF [19] (84.80%), JTT [35] (83.80%), LfF [39] (78.00%), and George [44] (76.20%). On BAR, DDB-II (72.81%) leads DebiAN [33] (69.88%) and MoDAD [41] (69.83%) by ~3 points, while DDB-I (70.40%) also surpasses JTT [35] (68.53%), LfF [39] (62.98%), LWBC [23] (62.03%), and BiaSwap [22] (52.40%). On BFFHQ, DDB-I's conflicting accuracy of 74.67% edges out ETF-Debias [47] (73.60%) and Park et al. [40] (71.68%), with DDB-II (70.93%) still well above MoDAD [41] (68.33%), DebiAN [33] (62.80%), LfF [39] (62.97%), CDvG+LfF [19] (62.20%), JTT [35] (62.20%), and BiaSwap [22] (58.87%). On ImageNet-A, DDB-I (39.80%) and DDB-II (37.53%) both exceed LWBC [23] (35.97%) and CDvG+LfF [19] (34.60%). On UrbanCars (multiple biases), DDB-I achieves the lowest gap metrics (BG-Gap 1.85, CoObj-Gap 0.52, BG-CoObj-Gap 0.12), outperforming LLE [32] (2.10, 2.70, 5.90) and EIIL [7] (4.20, 24.70, 44.90), though DDB-II (98.56% ID.ACC) matches or exceeds LLE's in-distribution accuracy while showing larger gaps on background-conflicting samples. On the annotated BAR variant, DDB-I (85.36%) surpasses DisEnt+BE [28] (84.96%) and ETF-Debias [46] (83.66%).

## Relation to Prior Work

DDB builds directly on two established debiasing frameworks: the end-to-end Learning from Failure (LfF) scheme of Nam et al. (NeurIPS 2020), which it customizes into Recipe II by replacing the GCE-trained biased model with a frozen synthetic-data-trained Bias Amplifier, and the two-step G-DRO pipeline of Sagawa et al. (ICLR 2020), which it feeds with pseudo-labels in Recipe I. The key conceptual departure from the broader unsupervised debiasing literature (JTT, LWBC, DebiAN, MoDAD, etc.) is that all prior methods train their auxiliary bias-amplifier on the actual biased training set, inheriting the well-documented problem of bias-conflicting sample memorization. DDB instead exploits the known tendency of conditional diffusion models to learn and amplify the dominant per-class bias distribution, generating a purely bias-aligned synthetic set on which the amplifier is trained, thereby eliminating memorization by construction. The authors position this as the first use of generative models to produce an *amplified* biased distribution (as opposed to the more common goal of generating unbiased data).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a recognized open problem in unsupervised debiasing—how to train a reliable auxiliary bias-amplifier without bias-conflicting samples corrupting its signal—and offers a clean, modular solution that can be plugged into multiple existing debiasing recipes. The main practical limitation is the computational cost of training the CDPM (~14 hours on an A30 GPU at 64×64 resolution for the largest dataset), which the authors partially mitigate by showing that fewer iterations or lower resolutions degrade performance only modestly. Open questions include whether the approach scales to million-scale datasets with appropriate bias annotations (which the community currently lacks), how it behaves under very weak spurious correlations (ρ < 0.70), and whether the 64×64 resolution constraint limits applicability to fine-grained or high-resolution tasks. The finding that DDB does not degrade performance on unbiased data (CIFAR-10) is practically important, suggesting it could serve as a safe default training procedure.
