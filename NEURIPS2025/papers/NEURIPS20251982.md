## Problem

While Jacobian conditioning has been studied for feedforward networks and the neural tangent kernel, the conditioning of the Jacobian within transformer attention layers remains largely unexamined. Poorly conditioned attention Jacobians can hinder gradient-based optimization, yet no principled method existed to diagnose or fix this issue in the attention mechanism specifically.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251982/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method is a drop-in replacement for the attention block in any transformer architecture, consuming the same inputs (RGB images at standard resolutions for vision tasks, text sequences for NLP) and producing the same outputs (class labels, bounding boxes, segmentation masks, next-token predictions). The only modification is that during each forward pass, fixed diagonal correction matrices (λI_k with λ=10) are added to the query, key, and value projection matrices; these corrections are non-trainable, introduce zero additional parameters, and add negligible FLOPS (3Nd vs. 6NDd for the original projections).

## Method

The paper derives a closed-form bound on the condition number of the self-attention Jacobian (Theorem 3.4), showing it is governed by the condition numbers of the W_Q, W_K, and W_V matrices. Building on this, Theorem 3.5 constructs SVD-based correction terms C_Q, C_K, C_V that guarantee κ(W+C) ≤ 2, but computing the SVD at each layer is prohibitively expensive. The practical method (Theorem 3.8) instead adds a fixed scalar multiple of the identity, λI_k (λ=10), to each projection matrix, which provably reduces the condition number under a mild assumption on the singular value spread. These correction matrices are initialized once before training, remain frozen throughout, and are simply added to the weights during each forward pass, incurring no gradient storage or backpropagation overhead. The approach is validated across ViT, Swin, XCiT, DeiT, DaViT, Nyströmformer, and Crammed BERT, consistently improving downstream accuracy without architectural changes.

## Results

On ImageNet-1k, spectral conditioned attention consistently improves top-1 accuracy across five vision transformer architectures: ViT-B (80.7→81.7%), DeiT-B (81.6→82.6%), Swin-B (83.4→84.1%), XCiT-M (82.6→83.5%), and DaViT-B (84.3→84.9%). On COCO 2017, the method improves AP^b from 44.9 to 45.6 and AP^m from 40.1 to 40.5 for object detection and instance segmentation. On the LRA benchmark, the Nyströmformer with spectral conditioning outperforms the original on all five tasks (e.g., Text: 63.8→64.8%, Pathfinder: 72.9→73.7%). On GLUE, Crammed BERT with spectral conditioning improves the average score from 78.6 to 79.4, with the largest gain on CoLA (48.9→51.7).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The method is compared against seven baseline architectures, and spectral conditioned attention wins on every single comparison. On ImageNet-1k, it outperforms ViT-B [7] by +1.0%, DeiT-B [33] by +1.0%, Swin-B [20] by +0.7%, XCiT-M [4] by +0.9%, and DaViT-B [6] by +0.6% top-1 accuracy. On COCO 2017, the spectrally conditioned XCiT-S backbone improves AP^b by +0.7 and AP^m by +0.4 over the original XCiT [4] in the Mask R-CNN framework. On the LRA benchmark, the Nyströmformer [38] with spectral conditioning beats the original on all five tasks, with gains ranging from +0.7 (ListOps: 37.1→37.8) to +1.0 (Text: 63.8→64.8). On GLUE, Crammed BERT [11] with spectral conditioning improves the average from 78.6 to 79.4, with the most notable gain on CoLA (+2.8: 48.9→51.7). The improvements are consistent and statistically significant (non-overlapping standard deviations) across all settings, and the method adds no trainable parameters or meaningful FLOPS overhead (only 1/(2D) relative increase in projection FLOPS).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on weight conditioning for feedforward networks (Saratchandran et al. [30], Liu et al. [19]) to the attention mechanism in transformers, where Jacobian conditioning had been largely unexamined. The key conceptual step is deriving an explicit bound on the self-attention Jacobian's condition number in terms of the Q, K, V projection matrices (Theorem 3.4), then showing that a simple diagonal correction λI_k added to each projection reduces this bound (Theorem 3.8). Unlike prior normalization-based approaches to stabilizing attention (Zhai et al. [40], MacDonald et al. [21]), spectral conditioning operates directly on the spectral properties of the projection weights rather than on activations or attention scores, and is presented as a drop-in modification compatible with diverse attention variants (Swin, XCiT, DaViT, Nyströmformer).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The technique is remarkably simple (add a fixed λI_k to Q, K, V; no extra parameters, negligible FLOPS/memory overhead) and empirically improves performance across five vision transformer architectures, object detection/segmentation, long-range sequence tasks, and language modeling, making it an attractive low-cost augmentation for existing transformer pipelines. However, the improvements are modest (typically 0.5–1 percentage point), the theory optimizes an upper bound rather than the Jacobian condition number directly, and experiments are limited to models under 100M parameters, leaving open whether the benefit persists at the scale of modern billion-parameter LLMs. The finding that spectral conditioning cannot substitute for layer normalization (Table 6) also clarifies that the two mechanisms address complementary aspects of training stability.
