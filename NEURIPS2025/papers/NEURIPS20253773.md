## Problem

Existing KV cache eviction methods for long-sequence LLM inference allocate compression budgets uniformly across all attention heads, ignoring the fact that heads exhibit vastly different attention concentration patterns—some are sparse (focused on a few cache elements) while others are dispersed (spread across many). This uniform allocation wastes budget on sparse heads and incurs excessive eviction loss on dispersed heads, degrading generation quality under tight cache constraints.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253773/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates per attention layer during the prefilling phase, consuming the KV cache elements, query states from a recent observation window (typically 32 tokens), and a total layer-level cache budget B. It produces a per-head budget allocation vector {B_i*} that sums to B, which then drives a Top-k eviction decision to yield the compressed KV cache. A safeguard hyperparameter α (default 0.2) blends the adaptive allocation with uniform allocation to prevent pathologically small budgets for highly sparse heads, and the entire procedure is GQA-compatible, using mean attention within each group to avoid redundant replication.

## Method

Ada-KV first establishes a theoretical L1 eviction loss upper bound (Theorem 3.1) and proves that Top-k selection minimizes this bound under any fixed budget allocation (Theorem 3.2). Building on this, the adaptive allocation strategy (Algorithm 1) concatenates attention weights across all heads in a layer, selects the global top-B weights, and assigns each head a budget equal to the count of selected weights it contributed—naturally shifting budget from sparse to dispersed heads. Theorem 3.3 proves this allocation achieves the minimum upper bound over all possible budget distributions. Integration into SnapKV and Pyramid (Algorithm 2) is plug-and-play: observed attention weights from the observation window are computed, the adaptive allocation replaces the uniform split, and a safeguard parameter α prevents extreme allocations. For efficient computation under variable-sized per-head caches, the authors implement a flattened cache storage layout with a custom CUDA kernel that leverages variable-length FlashAttention, maintaining computational efficiency comparable to uniform eviction.

## Results

On the Ruler benchmark (13 datasets, 16K context, question-agnostic, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct), Ada-SnapKV improves SnapKV's average score from 87.59 to 92.67 at 80% cache budget and from 44.02 to 53.29 at 20% cache budget. On LongBench (16 datasets, question-agnostic, Llama-3.1-8B), Ada-SnapKV achieves 46.24 average score at 40% ratio budget versus 45.52 for SnapKV, with gains across 15 of 18 domain-budget combinations. On the larger Llama-3.1-70B model, Ada-SnapKV reaches 49.64 vs. 48.91 for SnapKV at 40% budget. In difficult Needle-in-a-Haystack subtasks (S-NIAH-3, MK-NIAH-2 at 80% budget), Ada-SnapKV boosts SnapKV from 62.4→97.6 and 85.2→99.6 respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Ada-SnapKV and Ada-Pyramid consistently outperform their respective base methods (SnapKV and Pyramid) across all budget levels in both question-aware and question-agnostic settings on both Llama-3.1-8B and Mistral-7B models. Against StreamingLLM (sliding window eviction), both Top-k-based methods substantially outperform it, with the adaptive variants further widening the gap. In Table 3, the plug-and-play CriticalKV+Ada-KV (43.77 at 20% budget) surpasses both training-based approaches DuoAttention (39.52) and HeadKV (42.64), while DefensiveKV+Ada-KV (46.68) exceeds DuoAttention (48.17 at 40%) and HeadKV (47.23 at 40%). The only domain where Ada-SnapKV does not improve over SnapKV is Synthetic tasks at 10% budget (34.88 vs. 36.50) and Code at 40% budget (60.30 vs. 60.49), where the gains are marginal or slightly negative. Overall, the adaptive allocation strategy delivers consistent 1–9 point improvements over uniform allocation, with the largest gains in information-retrieval-heavy tasks (QA, NIAH) under tight budgets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Top-k KV cache eviction line (H2O, SnapKV, Pyramid) by identifying that their uniform per-head budget allocation is suboptimal. It provides a theoretical foundation showing Top-k eviction minimizes an L1 eviction loss upper bound, then introduces a 4-line adaptive allocation algorithm that redistributes budget from sparse-attention heads to dispersed-attention heads. The approach is explicitly positioned as plug-and-play, enhancing rather than replacing existing eviction strategies, and is orthogonal to sparse attention and quantization methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The plug-and-play simplicity (a single top-B selection across heads) makes this immediately adoptable by any Top-k eviction pipeline, and follow-up works (CriticalKV, DefensiveKV, KVZip) have already integrated it. The paper's emphasis on question-agnostic compression as a more realistic evaluation setting is an important call for the field, revealing that question-aware benchmarks overstate the robustness of eviction methods. A stated limitation is that allocation is confined within a single layer; extending it across layers (complementing Pyramid's layer-wise strategy) remains open.
