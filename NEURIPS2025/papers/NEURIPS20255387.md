## Problem

Abusive language detection research has advanced significantly for high-resource languages like English, but the majority of the world's low-resourced languages—particularly those spoken in Africa—lack dedicated benchmarks, datasets, and models for content moderation. Tigrinya, with approximately 10 million speakers, has no established benchmark for abusive language detection, leaving its online communities exposed to unchecked hostility. Additionally, around 64% of Tigrinya social media content uses improvised Romanized transliterations rather than the native Ge'ez script, a sociolinguistic reality that existing resources fail to capture.

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes Tigrinya YouTube comments as primary input, preserving their original script (Ge'ez, Latin, or mixed), and optionally augments them with the associated video title and an auto-generated video description (produced by Qwen2.5-VL-3B and refined by GPT-4o). The output is a joint multi-label prediction across three tasks: a binary abusiveness label, a 4-way sentiment label (positive, neutral, negative, mixed), and a 5-way topic label (political, racial, sexist, religious, other). In the multi-task setup, all 11 labels are produced simultaneously by a single linear head with independent sigmoid activations and a 0.5 threshold, rather than separate per-task classifiers.

## Method

The paper introduces the TiALD dataset (13,717 annotated comments from 7,373 YouTube videos) constructed via an iterative seed-expansion sampling strategy: word2vec embeddings trained on 4.1M comments are used to expand 61 seed terms through three stages of nearest-neighbor retrieval with morphological deduplication, yielding a diverse pool with a 0.28 type-to-token ratio versus 0.13 for random sampling. For baselines, the core model is a shared transformer encoder (TiRoBERTa, TiELECTRA, AfriBERTa, AfroXLMR, or XLM-RoBERTa) with hard parameter sharing across all three tasks, where a single linear head maps the [CLS] representation to 11 logits trained with average binary cross-entropy. The key empirical finding is that small fine-tuned Tigrinya-specific models (86.67% F1 for abusiveness) substantially outperform prompted frontier LLMs (best 79.31% for Claude Sonnet 3.7 few-shot), and that multi-task joint learning consistently improves over single-task training, particularly on minority classes like sexist and religious content.

## Results

On the TiALD benchmark, the best fine-tuned model (TiRoBERTa-base, single-task) achieves 86.67% macro F1 on abusiveness detection, 52.82% on sentiment, and 54.23% on topic classification, yielding a 64.57% TiALD Score (average macro F1 across tasks). Multi-task joint learning slightly improves the TiALD Score to 64.81% for TiRoBERTa-base. In stark contrast, the best prompted LLM (Claude Sonnet 3.7, few-shot) reaches only 79.31% F1 on abusiveness and 43.54% TiALD Score, trailing the fine-tuned model by over 7 points on abusiveness and 21 points overall. LLMs show severe degradation on multi-class tasks, with the best LLM achieving only 30.46% F1 on sentiment and 39.49% on topic.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among fine-tuned encoder models, the Tigrinya-specific TiRoBERTa-base [15] dominates, achieving 86.67% F1 on abusiveness (single-task) and the highest TiALD Score of 64.81% (multi-task), outperforming the Africa-adapted Afro-XLMR-Large-76L [17] (85.20% abusiveness, 63.86% TiALD) and AfriBERTa-base [16] (83.42%, 62.48%) by 1.5–3.3 points. The general-purpose XLM-RoBERTa-base [18] trails all Tigrinya- and Africa-specific models by 12+ points on average, confirming the value of language-adapted pre-training. Among LLMs, Claude Sonnet 3.7 [24] is the strongest, reaching 79.31% F1 in few-shot (a +20.11 gain over its zero-shot 59.20%), yet still falls 7.36 points behind TiRoBERTa-base on abusiveness. GPT-4o [13] achieves 72.06% few-shot abusiveness but shows minimal improvement over zero-shot (+1.01 points). The smaller open-weight models perform poorly: Gemma-3 4B [26] plateaus around 58–59% abusiveness, and LLaMA-3.2 3B [25] exhibits erratic classification bias (68% abusive in zero-shot vs. 77% not-abusive in few-shot), scoring only 45.65% F1. On multi-class tasks, all LLMs are far behind fine-tuned models—the best LLM sentiment F1 (30.46%, Gemma-3 few-shot) is 23 points below the best fine-tuned model (54.94%, Afro-XLMR single-task).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the abusive language detection literature from high-resource languages (English OffensEval, German GermEval, Arabic Mubarak et al.) and recent African-language efforts (AfriHate, NaijaHate, Amharic hate speech) to Tigrinya, a Semitic language with ~10M speakers but minimal computational resources. It departs from prior single-task benchmarks by jointly annotating abusiveness, sentiment, and topic, building on multi-task learning frameworks shown effective in Dai et al. and Rajamanickam et al. for English. The iterative seed-expansion sampling strategy using word2vec embeddings is a methodological departure from the keyword-retrieval pipelines typical of low-resource abusive language datasets, and the YouTube sourcing (vs. Twitter in AfriHate) reflects the actual platform landscape in Eritrea and Ethiopia.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TiALD fills a critical gap in content moderation for a language community with no prior multi-task benchmark, and the 15–45 percentage point performance gaps from perfect classification signal substantial room for future research. The finding that multi-task joint learning mitigates minority-class bias (e.g., sexist F1 improving from 31.78% to 46.30% for TiRoBERTa) offers a practical, data-efficient strategy for equitable moderation in low-resource settings. Open questions include handling implicit abusiveness (microaggressions, subtle prejudice), more granular abuse subtypes, and whether the small-model-over-LLM advantage persists as LLM tokenization improves for Ge'ez script. The benchmark also raises the broader question of whether frontier LLMs can ever match specialized fine-tuned models for morphologically complex, low-resource languages without architectural changes.
