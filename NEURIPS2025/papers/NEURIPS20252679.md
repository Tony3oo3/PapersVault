## Problem

Existing mathematical benchmarks for LLMs rely on competition problems, formal proofs, or artificially challenging questions, none of which capture the authentic mathematical tasks encountered in real research settings. This leaves open the fundamental question of how effective current LLMs actually are as assistants for practicing mathematicians working on organic, research-level problems.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252679/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For arXiv-sourced questions, the model receives an LLM-generated question together with the full paper context preceding the target theorem (typically from the introduction up to the theorem statement); for StackExchange questions, only the question is provided. The expected output is a single exact numerical or symbolic answer, and correctness is determined by an LLM judge (GPT-4o) checking mathematical equivalence to the ground truth. The benchmark deliberately excludes theorems whose main result is an inequality, an existence claim, or a Big-O bound, ensuring every sample has a unique verifiable answer.

## Method

REALMATH is built around a five-stage automated pipeline: (1) retrieve mathematics-related arXiv papers or StackExchange posts within a time window, (2) parse LaTeX sources to preserve notation, (3) use an LLM judge (o3-mini) to identify theorems with a single fixed numerical or symbolic answer, (4) convert each qualifying theorem into a question-answer pair via a second LLM prompt, and (5) filter out trivial or easily guessable samples. The key design choice is restricting to constructive theorems with unambiguous answers, which enables fully automated correctness checking without human expert validation. Because the pipeline draws from the continuously growing body of arXiv preprints and forum posts, the dataset can be refreshed monthly with new content, mitigating the data contamination problem that plagues static benchmarks. From roughly 9,000 papers over nine months, the pipeline yields 633 high-quality samples with over 94% accuracy in quality, and can generate over 70 new samples per month.

## Results

On the Math.arXiv dataset (633 QA pairs from research papers), o3 achieves the highest accuracy at 49.1%, followed by o4-mini (43.4%) and Claude 3.7-Sonnet (34.1%), while older models like GPT-4o-mini score only 12.5%. On Math.StackExchange (542 QA pairs), performance is substantially higher, with o4-mini reaching 70.8% and o3 at 70.7%. On the hard subset of Math.arXiv (questions unsolved by four weaker reference models), even o3 drops to just 27.9% accuracy, compared to 97.5% on easy questions. Fine-tuning GPT-4o-mini on 500 Math.arXiv samples yielded no statistically significant improvement on the held-out 133 samples.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates nine LLMs rather than comparing a single method against baselines, but the key comparison is between frontier and older models. o3 and o4-mini clearly lead on all three datasets, with o3 winning on both arXiv subsets (49.1% and 44.1%) and o4-mini narrowly edging it on StackExchange (70.8% vs. 70.7%). Mid-tier models (DeepSeek-R1, Gemini 2.5-Pro, Claude 3.7-Sonnet) cluster between 25–34% on arXiv and 61–62% on StackExchange, while older models (GPT-4o-mini, Llama 3.1-405B, Claude 3.5-Sonnet) fall to 7–18% on arXiv. The paper also notes qualitatively that models perform substantially better on REALMATH than on deliberately challenging benchmarks like FrontierMath or competition problems, suggesting current LLMs are already useful for routine research mathematics. Notably, model strengths vary by subdomain: o3 excels in theoretical areas (Representation Theory, Number Theory >60%) while Gemini 2.5-Pro leads in applied areas (Machine Learning, Optimization), and the two models' best and worst categories are nearly inverted.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by applying the 'in-the-wild' evaluation paradigm (analogous to SWE-bench for code) to mathematics, departing from the three dominant benchmark families: competition-derived (GSM8K, MATH, IMO), expert-crafted research problems (FrontierMath, HLE), and formal theorem proving (LeanDojo, MiniF2F). Rather than curating problems by hand or relying on formal verification, REALMATH repurposes an automated LLM-driven pipeline to extract verifiable theorems from organic arXiv papers and Stack Exchange posts, drawing conceptual inspiration from MathConstruct's focus on constructive statements but broadening the scope to the full range of research practice. The continuously refreshable design directly addresses the contamination problem that forces FrontierMath to keep its test set private.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

REALMATH provides the first scalable, contamination-resistant benchmark for research-level mathematics, and its finding that frontier models achieve 43–49% on arXiv theorems (vs. near-zero on FrontierMath) suggests LLMs may already be practical research assistants for routine mathematical tasks. The negative result that fine-tuning on 500 samples yields no improvement indicates the bottleneck is reasoning capability rather than knowledge coverage, pointing toward more fundamental architectural or training changes. Key limitations include the restriction to fixed-answer statements (excluding proof-level evaluation), reliance on LLM judges for both data curation and answer verification, and the relatively small dataset size (~1,200 QA pairs), which limits statistical confidence in per-category comparisons.
