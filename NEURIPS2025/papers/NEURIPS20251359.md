## Problem

Existing robot sports testbeds either use lower-mobility platforms (quadrupeds, humanoids, robotic arms) or fail to simultaneously combine mixed cooperative-competitive dynamics, turn-based interaction structure, and agile 3D maneuvering. No prior environment demands that a high-mobility aerial platform like a drone perform both precise low-level motion control and high-level strategic play under physical dynamics, leaving a gap for studying embodied intelligence in physically grounded multi-agent settings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251359/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each drone receives a state-vector observation (22–40 dimensions depending on task) comprising its full physical state (position, quaternion, linear/angular velocity, rotor speeds), partial ball state (relative position and velocity), and in multi-agent tasks, partial states of other drones plus task-specific metadata (drone ID, current turn). The output is a continuous action vector in one of two modes: CTBR (4-dim collective thrust + roll/pitch/yaw rates) or PRT (4-dim per-rotor thrust). In the hierarchical policy, the high-level module additionally outputs discrete drill assignments (Hover, Serve, Pass, Set, Attack) per drone, triggered event-drivenly on each ball contact.

## Method

VolleyBots is a GPU-parallelized simulation built on Isaac Sim and OmniDrones, modeling Iris quadrotors with a virtual racket, a 5 g ball, and a standard 9×18 m court under realistic underactuated dynamics. The testbed provides a curriculum of nine tasks (3 single-agent, 3 cooperative, 3 competitive) with three-part reward functions (misbehave penalty, sparse task reward, dense shaping reward). The key algorithmic contribution is a hierarchical policy for the 3v3 task: five low-level PPO-trained drills (Hover, Serve, Pass, Set, Attack) are orchestrated by a rule-based high-level strategy that assigns drills to each drone based on ball events and volleyball rules, achieving a 69.5% win rate against the self-play Nash-equilibrium baseline. Benchmarking across RL, MARL, and game-theoretic algorithms reveals that on-policy methods (PPO, MAPPO) consistently outperform off-policy counterparts in single-agent and cooperative tasks, but all methods fail to converge in the 6v6 setting, highlighting the open challenge of integrating hierarchical decision-making with agile control.

## Results

In single-agent tasks, PPO with PRT action space achieves 10.04 round trips in Back and Forth, 11.40 m hitting distance in Hit the Ball, and 10.83 bumps in Solo Bump, substantially outperforming all off-policy baselines (e.g., TD3 achieves only 0.99, 3.91, and 5.29 respectively). In multi-agent cooperative tasks, on-policy methods (MAPPO, HAPPO, MAT) with shaping rewards achieve 12–14 bumps in Bump and Pass and 75–80% success in Set and Spike (Hard), while off-policy methods (QMIX, MADDPG) fail to complete any task. In competitive 3 vs 3, PSRO_Nash achieves the lowest exploitability (35.83) and highest Elo (1268) among baselines, and the proposed hierarchical policy achieves a 69.5% win rate against the strongest baseline (SP) over 1,000 episodes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

In single-agent tasks, PPO [31] dominates all off-policy baselines by a wide margin: it achieves 10.04 round trips in Back and Forth (PRT) versus 0.99 for TD3 [29], 0.83 for DDPG [28], and 0.83 for SAC [30], while DQN [27] fails entirely due to its discrete-action limitation. In multi-agent cooperative tasks, on-policy MARL methods MAPPO [34], HAPPO [35], and MAT [36] all succeed (12–14 bumps with shaping), whereas off-policy QMIX [32] and MADDPG [33] fail to complete any cooperative task (≤0.84 bumps). In competitive 1 vs 1, PSRO_Nash [38] achieves the lowest exploitability (10.74) and highest Elo (1147), outperforming FSP [37] (exploitability 30.41, Elo 927) and PSRO_Uniform (exploitability 18.51, Elo 854), though all remain far from Nash equilibrium. In 3 vs 3, PSRO_Nash again leads among baselines (exploitability 35.83, Elo 1268), but the proposed hierarchical policy surpasses the strongest baseline (SP, which is the Nash equilibrium among the four game-theoretic methods) with a 69.5% win rate, demonstrating that decomposing strategy from control is effective where flat policies struggle.

## Relation to Prior Work

VolleyBots sits at the intersection of robot sports environments (RoboCup, robot table tennis, quadruped soccer, SMPLOlympics) and drone RL control (drone racing, pursuit-evasion, formation flight), but is the first to unify all three defining features—mixed competitive-cooperative dynamics, turn-based interaction, and agile 3D maneuvering—within a single drone platform. It builds directly on the OmniDrones/Isaac Sim simulation stack and extends the task space beyond prior drone RL work, which typically addressed either single-agent control or simpler multi-agent scenarios without the full volleyball rule structure. The benchmark draws on standard (MA)RL and game-theoretic algorithms (PPO, MAPPO, PSRO, FSP) as baselines rather than proposing a new algorithmic framework, positioning itself as an evaluation platform that exposes the limitations of existing methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that both on-policy and off-policy RL methods, as well as game-theoretic self-play, fail to solve tasks requiring simultaneous low-level motion control and high-level strategic play—identifies a concrete gap in current multi-agent RL for robotics. The simple hierarchical policy (rule-based strategy + PPO drills) achieving 69.5% win rate over the Nash equilibrium baseline suggests that explicit decomposition of control and strategy may be a necessary architectural choice, opening a clear research direction. Key limitations include the single-task sim-to-real demonstration, reliance on full state observations (no vision), and the complete failure of all methods on the 6v6 task, which underscores that scalable multi-agent drone strategy remains largely unsolved.
