## Problem

AI models can predict measurements (e.g., object counts) across large datasets, but current workflows lack the statistical guarantees scientists need: direct model outputs can be biased, and obtaining reliable confidence intervals typically requires extensive manual validation. The paper asks how to achieve precise, unbiased scientific measurements with calibrated uncertainty while leveraging AI to minimize the human labeling effort, even when the AI model is imperfect.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254878/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on a finite set of N units (e.g., 200×200 image tiles or daily radar composites) where an AI detector provides per-unit predictions g(s) that form the importance sampling proposal. At each step, one unit is sampled without replacement, a human provides the ground-truth label f(s), and the method outputs a running unbiased estimate F̂ of the total measurement F(Ω) along with a conditional variance estimate and a 1−α confidence interval. The process is interactive: the AI model is fine-tuned after each labeling round, so the proposal distribution adapts over time.

## Method

Active measurement is a human-in-the-loop Monte Carlo framework where each step samples a unit s_t from a proposal q_t ∝ g (AI predictions) over the remaining unlabeled units, forms the importance sampling estimator F̂_t = F(D_t) + f(s_t)/q_t(s_t), and combines all per-step estimates with a novel combined weighting scheme α_τ^COMB = w_τ√τ that simultaneously accounts for variance reduction from model adaptation (square-root law) and from the shrinking sample space (LURE weights). The paper proves the combined estimator is unbiased and achieves a worst-case variance ratio of at most 9/8 relative to the optimal inverse-variance weighting. For uncertainty quantification, the authors derive novel conditional variance estimators that reuse future samples to estimate past variances, and invoke a martingale central limit theorem to justify Gaussian confidence intervals. A further contribution is a conditional inverse-variance weighting scheme that plugs in the estimated variances for early steps and falls back to combined weights for later steps, yielding even lower error in some settings.

## Results

On the challenging 'reeds' bird-counting image (true count 12,486), active measurement achieves 11,977 ± 1,076 after labeling only 50 tiles (~3.5% of 1,426 tiles). On the Great Lakes radar task, the raw detector's fractional error saturates around 1.7–2.8 across stations, while active measurement reduces the geometric-average fractional error to 0.23 after 40 labeled days and 0.06 after 200 days. Active measurement outperforms DISCount with as little as 1% of tiles labeled, and sampling without replacement provides additional gains once roughly 10% of tiles are labeled. Confidence interval coverage converges to the nominal level (e.g., 95%) with the proposed conditional variance estimator.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DISCount [32] (detector-based importance sampling with a fixed model and sampling with replacement), active measurement (DIS+AIS+WOR) achieves substantially lower fractional error on both the image and radar tasks. On the radar task, DISCount's error plateaus near the raw detector level (~2), while active measurement continues to improve to 0.06 after 200 days, a roughly 30× reduction. The two key extensions—adaptive model fine-tuning (AIS) and sampling without replacement (WOR)—each contribute independently, with WOR becoming more impactful after ~10% of units are labeled. Against the PPI-motivated estimator Ĥ [3], which uses uniform sampling and a fixed detector, active measurement is consistently better because it adaptively selects high-count units for labeling and refines the proposal distribution over time. The paper also ablates weighting schemes, showing that the proposed combined weights (α^COMB) outperform both the square-root law and LURE weights alone, while inverse-variance weighting (γ=0.5) can achieve marginally lower error at the cost of losing strict unbiasedness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies and extends three research threads: adaptive importance sampling (AIS), active testing, and detector-based counting (DISCount). It departs from AIS by sampling without replacement from a finite domain and from active testing by targeting a scientific measurement rather than a model's test loss, while iteratively refining the AI model itself. The combined weighting scheme (α^COMB = w_τ√τ) merges the square root law from AIS with LURE weights from active learning, and the variance estimation leverages a martingale CLT to handle the non-iid, single-sample-per-round structure that neither prior line of work addresses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Active measurement provides the first framework that gives scientists unbiased estimates with calibrated confidence intervals for AI-assisted counting, addressing a critical gap where current workflows offer no statistical guarantees. The practical impact is substantial: 50 labeled tiles yield a ±1,076 estimate on a 12,486-bird image, versus ±8,000 under standard practice. Key limitations include the computational overhead of iterative fine-tuning (which the authors acknowledge needs faster, lightweight updates for true interactivity) and the fact that estimates may still lack precision for high-stakes applications. Open directions include exploiting spatial autocorrelation via Gaussian processes, active learning for early-stage sampling, and few-shot/in-context learning to reduce adaptation cost.
