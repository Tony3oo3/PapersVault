## Problem

Visual geolocalization faces a fundamental precision-versus-scale trade-off: classification methods partition the world into coarse regions (10+ km errors) and scale well, while retrieval methods achieve meter-level accuracy but require infeasibly large databases (e.g., 470M ground-level images for Western Europe). No existing approach delivers both fine-grained (~100 m) localization and continent-scale applicability without prohibitive storage or compute costs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254469/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single ground-level RGB image (224×224 or 448×448 px, rendered as a pinhole crop from a 360° StreetView panorama with random roll, pitch, yaw, and FOV) and outputs a geographic location as the nearest S2 cell at level L=16 (~140 m edge length), effectively a lat/lon estimate within ~100–200 m. The search database is not a collection of individual ground-level images but a compact set of ~4.8M cell codes (each 2176-d), built by combining learned classification prototypes at L=15 with aerial/satellite tile embeddings (256×256 px at 60 cm/px) at L=16, reducing storage by 30–60× compared to VPR.

## Method

The core contribution is a hybrid retrieval formulation over 'cell codes' that fuse l2-normalized classification prototypes (learned via a proxy task on ground-level images) with aerial tile embeddings via a simple linear combination z_cell = κ·z_P + z_A, where κ is a calibration factor matching the empirical similarity-scale gap between the two. Both ground-level and aerial encoders share a ViT architecture (iBOT, DINOv2, or DINOv3) with a SALAD optimal-transport aggregation head, but use separate weights. Training follows a contrastive multi-similarity loss with three edges—ground-to-prototype, ground-to-aerial, and aerial-to-prototype—where the aerial-to-prototype edge detaches prototype gradients to keep prototypes encoding only ground-view information. This design lets prototypes act as a globally discriminative spatial memory while aerial embeddings provide robustness in data-sparse rural areas, and the combination enables fine-grained cross-view retrieval across multiple countries without explicit geometric alignment or dense 3D models.

## Results

On BEDENL (3 countries, 139k km²), the hybrid approach with DINOv3-L backbone achieves 70.2% top-1 recall within 200 m and 89.9% top-100 recall, with a 42 GB database of 4.8M cell codes. Scaling to EuropeWest (10 countries, 433k km²), the same model reaches 68.7% top-1 recall@200 m and 89.1% top-100 recall on 4.5M test images. The default iBOT-B16 backbone yields 60.3% top-1 recall@200 m on BEDENL and 57.5% on EuropeWest, still substantially outperforming all baselines. At the finer 100 m threshold, the DINOv3-L hybrid model localizes 59.2% of EuropeWest queries correctly.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The hybrid method decisively outperforms all baselines across every evaluation setting. Against cross-view retrieval, Fervers et al. [30] achieves only 33.3% top-1 recall@200 m on BEDENL (32.6% on EuropeWest), while the paper's aerial-only variant reaches 49.7% and the full hybrid 60.3% (70.2% with DINOv3-L). SALAD-Aerial [24] scores 39.2% top-1@200 m, still 21 points below the hybrid. Classification baselines (CosFace 7.4%, Hierarchical 8.1%, Haversine 10.2%) are far weaker, confirming that standard cross-entropy losses cannot capture fine-grained spatial information at continent scale. Traditional ground retrieval (SALAD) achieves ~19% top-1@200 m but requires a 5.1 TB database, making it infeasible at EuropeWest scale (470M images). In the cross-area generalization test (UK+IE, no retraining), the hybrid method recovers 18.4% top-1@200 m versus 11.6% for Fervers et al., and in the cross-domain Trekker evaluation it reaches 18.7% versus 11.1% for Fervers et al. and 13.4% for SALAD-Aerial. Comparison with Gemini 2.5 Pro (Appendix B.5) shows the learned model localizes queries within 200 m while the VLM only provides country-level estimates.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies three previously separate research streams in visual geolocalization: ground-to-ground retrieval (VPR, e.g., SALAD, MegaLoc), cross-view ground-to-aerial retrieval (e.g., Fervers et al., Shi et al.), and global classification over spatial partitions (e.g., PlaNet, PIGEON, OSV-5M). The core methodological contribution is a surprisingly simple fusion: l2-normalized cell prototypes learned via a proxy classification task are linearly combined with aerial tile embeddings (scaled by a calibration factor κ) to form a single retrieval database, eliminating the need for explicit geometric alignment or 3D models. The feature aggregation relies on SALAD's optimal transport head, and training uses a multi-similarity loss that jointly constrains ground-prototype, ground-aerial, and aerial-prototype similarity edges. The approach departs from prior classification work by using much finer S2 cells (L=15/16, ~140–281 m) and from prior cross-view work by augmenting aerial embeddings with learned ground-view prototypes that encode facade-level detail invisible from above.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first demonstration of fine-grained (~200 m) geolocalization at continent scale (10 Western European countries, 433k km²), breaking the long-standing precision-versus-scale trade-off that has constrained the field. The practical implications are broad: validating media images, supporting 6-DoF positioning pipelines that require ~100 m initial estimates, and enabling large-scale 3D dataset construction. Key limitations include reliance on proprietary Google StreetView data, non-release of model weights due to privacy concerns, and degraded performance in rural areas with sparse ground-level training data. Open questions include generalization to other continents, handling regions with no aerial coverage, and whether the approach can be adapted to non-StreetView capture modalities at comparable scale.
