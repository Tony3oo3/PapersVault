## Problem

The CASH problem in AutoML requires jointly selecting the best ML model class and tuning its hyperparameters under a limited budget, but existing Max K-Armed Bandit algorithms assume heavy-tailed reward distributions that are misaligned with the bounded, short-tailed, left-skewed distributions actually observed in HPO tasks. This misalignment causes state-of-the-art extreme bandit methods to underperform in practice, and the question of what realistic distributional assumptions hold for HPO rewards remained open since Nishihara et al. (2016).

## Input / Output

The method consumes a tabular dataset with train/validation splits, a set of K candidate ML model classes (each with its own hyperparameter search space), a total HPO iteration budget T, and a scalar exploration parameter α. At each time step the bandit selects one model class, one iteration of HPO (e.g., SMAC Bayesian optimization) is executed for that class, and the negative validation loss is returned as reward. The output is the best-performing model class together with its best hyperparameter configuration found within the budget. Notably, the reward process is not conditionally i.i.d. on arm choices because HPO progress on each model affects subsequent losses, which the algorithm handles through its distribution-adapted confidence bounds.

## Method

MaxUCB is a two-level decomposed CASH algorithm where an upper-level max k-armed bandit allocates HPO iterations across K model classes and a lower-level HPO method (e.g., SMAC) optimizes hyperparameters for the selected class. The key novelty is a distribution-adapted exploration bonus of the form (α log(t)/n)², which differs from the classical UCB bonus √(α log(t)/n) and arises because, for bounded left-skewed distributions characterized by Lemma 3.3 (survival function bounded by Lε and Uε near the maximum), the probability of confidence-interval violations takes the form O(e^{-n√C(n)} + nC(n)), minimized by C(n) = 1/n². The exploitation term uses the maximum observed reward rather than the mean, aligning with the extreme-value objective. Theoretical analysis yields a regret bound (Theorem 4.2) that depends on the sub-optimality gap Δ_i and the distribution-shape constants L_{i*} (for the optimal arm) and U_i (for sub-optimal arms), with a corollary showing O(K log T / √T) total regret under appropriate α selection.

## Results

MaxUCB significantly outperforms combined search (SMAC/random search) on all three benchmarks with p < 0.01: on TabRepo it achieves 86 wins vs 10 losses (p = 0.000001), on TabRepoRaw 24 wins vs 6 losses (p = 0.0007), and on YaHPOGym 6 wins vs 39 losses (p = 0.0088). In the anytime ranking comparison (Figure 6), MaxUCB achieves the best average rank across all benchmarks, particularly in early stages (T = 50) where most MAB methods outperform combined search. MaxUCB and Quantile Bayes UCB are the only two methods that significantly outperform combined search across all benchmarks simultaneously.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MaxUCB outperforms all seven baselines on TabRepo (86 wins vs. 79 for Quantile Bayes UCB, 85 for Rising Bandits, 72 for QoMax-SDA, 35 for ER-UCB-S and UCB, and 26 for Max-Median) and on TabRepoRaw (24 wins vs. 12 for Quantile Bayes UCB, 16 for Rising Bandits, and 4–6 for all others). On YaHPOGym, however, Rising Bandits (8 wins, p = 0.0007) narrowly outperforms MaxUCB (6 wins, p = 0.0088), which the authors attribute to non-stationary reward distributions in that benchmark where the optimal arm changes over time. Against combined search (SMAC), MaxUCB is the only method that significantly outperforms the baseline on all three benchmarks; ER-UCB-S, QoMax-SDA, Max-Median, and UCB fail to beat SMAC on TabRepoRaw (p > 0.95). Classical UCB and QoMax-SDA perform worst among bandit methods in the anytime ranking (Figure 6), while Max-Median shows strong initial performance but degrades with more iterations. The paper notes that MaxUCB's advantage is most pronounced in early stages (T = 50), where the two-level decomposition provides the greatest benefit over joint-space search.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the two-level (decomposed) CASH framework of Hoffman et al. (2014) and Liu et al. (2019), where an upper-level bandit allocates HPO budget across model classes and a lower-level HPO method (e.g., SMAC) optimizes hyperparameters within each class. It departs from existing Max K-Armed Bandit algorithms (ExtremeHunter, QoMax-SDA, Max-Median) that rely on heavy-tailed or Pareto distributional assumptions, and from BAI-based approaches like Rising Bandits that optimize mean rewards rather than max rewards. The key conceptual departure is a data-driven analysis showing CASH reward distributions are bounded, left-skewed, and short-tailed, which motivates a novel squared exploration bonus (α log(t)/n)² that exploits faster concentration of maxima in bounded distributions, in contrast to the classical √(α log(t)/n) UCB bonus.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper resolves an open question raised by Nishihara et al. (2016) about what realistic distributional assumptions hold for HPO reward processes, providing both empirical evidence and a matching theoretical framework. MaxUCB achieves state-of-the-art performance across four AutoML benchmarks with a clean O(K log T / √T) regret bound, making it a practical tool for AutoML systems that need to select among diverse model classes under limited compute. Limitations include the stationarity assumption (a burn-in phase may be needed), the restriction to bounded distributions, and the unknown optimal arm gap that prevents direct computation of the exploration parameter α. Open directions include non-stationary rewards, cost-aware budgeting, and establishing lower bounds for bounded extreme bandits.
