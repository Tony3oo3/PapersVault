## Problem

Task-aware knowledge distillation for LLMs typically assumes access to large labeled datasets, yet in many practical scenarios only a handful of task-specific samples are available or affordable. The problem of data selection for distillation in few-shot regimes (as low as 8–512 samples) has received limited attention, and standard few-shot distillation suffers from poor generalization due to insufficient coverage of the teacher's decision boundary.

## Input / Output

CoD takes k/2 original labeled text samples (e.g., sentiment-labeled sentences) and generates k/2 counterfactual explanations via an LLM oracle (GPT-4o) that are validated to flip the teacher's prediction, yielding a total of k training pairs. The teacher is a larger transformer (DeBERTa-v3-base or Qwen2.5-1.5B) pre-trained on the full dataset, and the student is a smaller variant (DeBERTa-v3-small/xsmall or Qwen2.5-0.5B). The output is a distilled student model producing class probabilities; notably, CoD uses only half the original labeled data of baselines while matching or exceeding their accuracy.

## Method

CoD (Counterfactual explanation-infused Distillation) enriches a few-shot training set with counterfactual explanations—minimally perturbed inputs that flip the teacher's prediction—generated via a hybrid LLM-prompting-and-teacher-validation pipeline. Each original–CFE pair is placed in the same mini-batch, and the student is trained with a combined objective of cross-entropy on hard labels, KL-divergence distillation loss against the teacher's soft outputs, and optional layer-wise hidden-state alignment. Theoretically, the paper shows via Fisher Information that CFEs near the decision boundary maximize per-sample information for parameter estimation, and via a Hausdorff distance bound that matching the teacher on CFE pairs constrains the student's decision boundary to an (α+ε)-tube around the teacher's, where α is the CFE perturbation size and ε measures boundary spread. Empirically, CoD is applied on top of KD, LWD, and TED baselines across six text classification benchmarks, consistently outperforming them in the k≤64 regime with up to 10+ point gains at k=8.

## Results

On DeBERTa-v3 (base→small), CoD achieves the largest gains in extreme few-shot regimes: at k=8, KD+CoD reaches 75.8% on Amazon Polarity (vs. 67.1% for KD, +8.7 pts), LWD+CoD reaches 86.1% on IMDB (vs. 76.0% for LWD, +10.1 pts), and KD+CoD reaches 83.5% on SST2 (vs. 71.4% for KD, +12.1 pts). At k=512, performance converges (e.g., Amazon Polarity: KD 84.6% vs. KD+CoD 86.0%), but CoD still matches or exceeds baselines while using only k/2 real samples plus k/2 generated CFEs. On Qwen2.5 (1.5B→0.5B), similar trends hold: KD+CoD improves over KD by 6.1 pts on Yelp at k=8 (74.5% vs. 68.4%) and by 2.5 pts on CoLA at k=512 (75.7% vs. 73.2%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CoD is applied on top of three distillation baselines: standard KD (KL-divergence on soft labels, following DistilBERT [71]), Layer-wise Distillation (LWD, adding intermediate hidden-state MSE alignment, following TinyBERT [13]), and TED (task-aware layer-wise distillation with learned filters [12]). Against KD, CoD yields the largest absolute gains at k=8: +8.7 pts on Amazon Polarity (75.8% vs. 67.1%), +12.1 pts on SST2 (83.5% vs. 71.4%), and +4.9 pts on Yelp (71.9% vs. 61.7%). Against LWD, the improvement is most dramatic on IMDB at k=8 (+10.1 pts: 86.1% vs. 76.0%) and on SST2 at k=8 (+10.1 pts: 86.1% vs. 76.0%). Against TED, the paper finds that TED does not consistently outperform simpler KD or LWD in few-shot settings, yet TED+CoD still produces consistent gains over standard TED (e.g., +8.2 pts on IMDB at k=8: 82.7% vs. 74.5%), confirming CoD's broad applicability. At k=512, all methods converge within 1–2 points, but CoD still matches or slightly exceeds baselines while using only half the real labeled data (k/2 originals + k/2 CFEs vs. k originals for baselines).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of knowledge distillation (Hinton 2015; TinyBERT/LWD 2019) and counterfactual explanation research (Wachter 2017), repurposing CFEs—previously used for model reconstruction, spurious-correlation mitigation, and OOD generalization—as a data-selection mechanism for distillation. It extends task-aware KD methods like TED (Liang et al. 2023) and LWD (Jiao et al. 2019), which assume large training sets, into the few-shot regime for LLMs, a setting the authors explicitly identify as underexplored. The key conceptual departure is treating explainability outputs (CFEs) as actionable training signals rather than post-hoc interpretations, bridging two previously separate research threads.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By showing that k/2 real samples paired with k/2 generated CFEs can match or exceed k real samples, CoD offers a practical path to reducing annotation costs in task-specific LLM deployment, particularly in resource-constrained or low-resource settings. The Fisher-information and Hausdorff-distance guarantees provide a clean theoretical justification, though they are limited to logistic regression and idealized geometric assumptions respectively. Open questions include extending the approach to generative (sequence-to-sequence) distillation, improving CFE generation to guarantee minimality, and understanding how CoD behaves when the teacher itself is miscalibrated or biased.
