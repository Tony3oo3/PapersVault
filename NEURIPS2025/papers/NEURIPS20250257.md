## Problem

Offline multi-objective reinforcement learning (MORL) has been studied almost exclusively under linear scalarization, where policies are conditioned on fixed preference weights. This approach cannot capture fairness-oriented welfare objectives such as Nash social welfare or max-min fairness, which require nonlinear, non-additive trade-offs across objectives. The paper addresses the completely unexplored problem of directly optimizing a nonlinear welfare function from a fixed offline dataset without specifying or searching over preference weights.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a fixed offline dataset of (state, action, reward-vector, next-state) tuples, an initial state distribution p₀, and a strictly concave scalarization function u_i (e.g., log for Nash social welfare, α-fairness for a continuum of fairness notions). It produces a single welfare-maximizing policy π*, the optimal stationary distribution d*, and a set of implicit preference weights μ* (one scalar per objective). In continuous domains, the policy is extracted via weighted behavior cloning using the importance weights w*(s,a) derived from the optimal stationary distribution, requiring no explicit preference conditioning at inference time.

## Method

FairDICE reformulates fair MORL as a convex optimization over stationary distributions by introducing slack variables k_i that move expected returns outside the concave welfare function, then applies DICE-RL's f-divergence regularization to handle the offline distribution-shift constraint. Taking the Lagrangian dual and exploiting strong duality yields closed-form solutions for the importance weights w*(s,a) and slack variables k_i*, reducing the problem to optimizing only the value function ν and the implicit preference weights μ. The key theoretical insight is that under offline regularization, the regularized fair MORL problem and the regularized linear MORL problem with weights set to μ* share the same unique optimal solution, meaning FairDICE implicitly discovers the preference weights that maximize welfare. The resulting algorithm alternates between minimizing a sample-based dual loss (involving the convex conjugate of the f-divergence and the scalarization function) and updating the policy via weighted behavior cloning, adding only one scalar parameter per objective over the single-objective OptiDICE baseline.

## Results

On the MO-Four-Room discrete benchmark, FairDICE-NSW achieves a Nash social welfare of 10.77 and Jain's fairness index of 0.996, substantially outperforming the behavior policy (NSW = −16.10, Jain = 0.710) and the utilitarian policy (NSW = −17.33, Jain = 0.500), while maintaining competitive utilitarian welfare (0.082 vs. 0.086). On the D4MORL continuous-control benchmark, FairDICE achieves competitive or superior Nash social welfare across all five two-objective MuJoCo tasks compared to the best preference-weight search results from preference-conditioned baselines, with its raw-return point lying on the Pareto frontier. In the three-objective MO-Hopper-3obj task, FairDICE identifies a welfare-maximizing policy whose raw returns approach or surpass the Pareto frontier formed by baselines, using only one additional scalar parameter per objective.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares FairDICE against three preference-conditioned offline MORL baselines from the D4MORL benchmark [Zhu et al., ICLR 2023]: BC(P) (behavioral cloning conditioned on preference), MODT(P) (Decision Transformer with preference tokens), and MORvS(P) (feedforward model with preference-weighted return-to-go). Since no existing algorithm directly optimizes nonlinear welfare in offline MORL, the baselines are evaluated by exhaustively searching over 30 linearly spaced preference weights on the simplex and reporting the best NSW achieved. FairDICE matches or exceeds the best NSW found by any baseline across all five two-objective D4MORL tasks (Figure 4), and its raw-return point lies on the Pareto frontier (Figure 5). In the more challenging three-objective MO-Hopper-3obj task, where the preference simplex is 2D and exhaustive search becomes impractical, FairDICE still identifies a welfare-maximizing policy whose raw returns approach or surpass the Pareto frontier of the baselines (Figure 6). The key advantage is that FairDICE requires no explicit preference conditioning or weight search, scaling to higher-dimensional objective spaces with only one additional scalar per objective.

## Relation to Prior Work

FairDICE extends the DICE-RL / OptiDICE framework for offline policy optimization via stationary distribution correction to the multi-objective setting, replacing the linear return objective with a concave welfare function (e.g., Nash social welfare). Conceptually, it draws on the Eisenberg-Gale convex program for fair allocation and the V-LP linear programming formulation of RL, but departs from prior offline MORL work (which assumes linear scalarization and requires explicit preference conditioning) by directly optimizing nonlinear welfare without specifying preference weights. The key theoretical departure is showing that, under f-divergence regularization, regularized linear MORL and regularized fair MORL share the same unique optimal solution—a bridge that does not hold in the unregularized setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first framework to optimize nonlinear welfare objectives (Nash social welfare, α-fairness, max-min) from a fixed offline dataset, filling a gap between online fair MORL and offline linear MORL. The equivalence result (Proposition 1) provides a clean theoretical justification for why the method implicitly learns the right preference weights, and the practical overhead is minimal (one scalar per objective). Limitations include restriction to strictly concave (convex-conjugate) scalarizations, sensitivity to the regularization hyperparameter β, and dependence on dataset quality and coverage. Open questions include extending to non-convex scalarizations, scaling to many objectives, and understanding how the method behaves when the offline dataset has very limited coverage of the Pareto front.
