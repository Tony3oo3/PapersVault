## Problem

As organizations fine-tune LLMs for specialized tasks, a growing pool of domain-specific models emerges, but selecting the best model for a given input becomes difficult when the test distribution is an unknown mixture of source domains. Existing routing algorithms perform well on clearly defined task distributions but provide no guarantees for arbitrary, unknown mixtures, and building a fine-tuned model for every possible task combination is impractical.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251356/cropped_page8_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes k score-augmented empirical datasets, where each sample is a prompt paired with reward scores (e.g., BLEU) from all k expert models, and produces a probabilistic routing function f: X → Δ([k]) that assigns a distribution over experts per input. At inference, only a single expert is sampled from f(·|x) rather than querying all k models, making deployment computationally efficient. The routing function itself is a language model (Gemma 2B with a randomly initialized linear head) that outputs logits over the k experts.

## Method

The paper formulates model routing as a multiple-source domain adaptation problem and solves it via minimax regret optimization: min_f max_λ [v(π*, D_λ) − v(π_f, D_λ)], where λ ranges over all possible mixture weights. This saddle-point is solved as a two-player game in which a Hedge-based max-player adaptively updates domain weights λ_t to stress-test the router against the worst-case mixture, while a no-regret online learning oracle (OLO for linear losses or OLLO for log-loss proxies) updates the routing function f_t. Two comparator options are provided: Option A competes against the per-input best expert (stronger but requires near-deterministic scores), and Option B competes against the domain-specific expert (weaker but with cleaner guarantees). The key theoretical result shows that for any test mixture D_λ, the regret is bounded by a convex combination of the best source-domain regrets plus a concentration term O(√(1/n_min)) that vanishes with more source data, and an approximation term O(Reg_F(T)/T) that vanishes with more iterations.

## Results

On the MixInstruct benchmark (5 domains, 11 LLMs, BLEU scoring), Algorithm 1 with min-max domain weight updates consistently reduces regret against its targeted competitor compared to the uniform-weight baseline. The largest improvement is with log-loss and Option A (sampled inference), where regret vs. the domain expert drops from −0.06 to −0.85 and regret vs. the best per-example expert drops from 2.70 to 1.90. With linear loss and Option A, regret vs. the domain expert improves from 1.65 to 0.49. Results are averaged over 5 seeds with 10,000 training batches of 256 samples per domain.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's empirical comparison is between Algorithm 1 (with adaptive min-max domain weight updates via Hedge) and a baseline that uses fixed uniform domain weights (γ=0), rather than against other routing methods. Algorithm 1 wins on the regret metric targeted by its chosen option in every setting: for Option A it reduces regret vs. the best per-example expert, and for Option B it reduces regret vs. the domain expert. The most dramatic gain is with log-loss and Option A using the efficient sampled inference strategy, cutting domain-expert regret from −0.06 to −0.85. However, optimizing one regret can worsen the other: with linear loss and Option B, regret vs. the best per-example expert actually increases from 4.60 to 7.09, illustrating the trade-off between the two comparator policies. The efficient sampled inference strategy (sampling one expert rather than computing the full mixture) performs on par with or better than the full-distribution approach, confirming the theoretical equivalence.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reframes predictive model routing as a multiple-source domain adaptation (MSA) problem, drawing directly on the theoretical foundations of Mansour et al. (2008, 2012) and the discriminative MSA algorithm of Cortes et al. (2021c). The algorithmic core combines the mixture-over-test-domains formulation from Cortes et al. with minimax-regret optimization techniques from Mohri et al. (2019) and Agarwal and Zhang (2022), solved via a two-player no-regret game. The paper explicitly distinguishes its setting from Mixture-of-Experts token-level routing (Shazeer et al., 2017) and from prior predictive routing work (Shnitzer et al., 2023; Lu et al., 2023; Mao et al., 2023–2025), which assumes a known or fixed test distribution and provides no guarantees under unknown domain mixtures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key contribution is a regret guarantee showing that for any unknown test mixture, the routed model's regret is bounded by a convex combination of the best per-domain expert regrets plus a concentration term that vanishes with more source data — a clean, actionable guarantee absent from prior routing literature. Practically, the min-max domain-weight adjustment can be dropped into existing router training pipelines with minimal overhead. However, the empirical validation is limited to a single benchmark (MixInstruct) with only 5 domains and 11 LLMs, and the realizability assumptions (e.g., the function class containing the Bayes-optimal domain classifier f_{λ,D}) are strong and unverified at scale. Open questions include whether the approach scales to hundreds of domains, how sensitive the guarantees are to violations of the uniform scoring assumption, and whether the theoretical concentration terms are tight in practice for modern LLM routers.
