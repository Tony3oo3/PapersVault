## Problem

Prior mechanistic interpretability work on modular addition produced seemingly disjoint explanations—Clock circuits, Pizza circuits, Group Composition via Representations, and coset-based circuits—casting doubt on the universality hypothesis that networks converge to shared internal solutions. No framework existed to reconcile these divergent interpretations or to explain why different architectures and hyperparameters yield qualitatively different-looking but functionally equivalent mechanisms. This paper addresses that gap by proposing a testable universality hypothesis and a unifying abstract algorithm that subsumes all prior interpretations.

## Input / Output

The task is modular addition: given two integers a and b in the cyclic group C_n, predict c = (a+b) mod n. Inputs are either one-hot encoded vectors of length n or learned 128-dimensional embedding vectors (concatenated as (E_a, E_b)), and the output is a logit vector of length n over all possible sums. The dataset exhaustively covers all n² input pairs, and the paper evaluates 1- to 4-layer MLPs and 1- to 4-layer transformers (with attention coefficient α=0 for Pizza, α=1 for Clock) across moduli ranging from small primes to highly composite numbers and powers of two, spanning multiple orders of magnitude.

## Method

The paper introduces approximate cosets—sets of elements that are close (small path distance) on a Cayley graph generated by a step size d derived from a neuron's frequency—generalizing strict cosets to handle frequencies that do not divide the modulus. Under a simple neuron model (each neuron approximates a single-frequency sinusoid), Theorem 4.4 proves that all ReLU-activated neurons in layer 1 fire exclusively on approximate cosets containing the correct answer, and all deeper-layer neurons activate on linear combinations of approximate cosets. From this, the authors derive the abstract approximate Chinese Remainder Theorem (aCRT): a divide-and-conquer template requiring only O(log(n)) distinct frequencies, each generating sinusoidal neurons whose phases select different approximate cosets of the answer. Theorem 4.7 then proves that O(log(n)) randomly chosen frequencies yield logit margins of Ω(log(n)), exponentially suppressing incorrect outputs. Empirically, the authors validate the simple neuron model and approximate coset abstraction across architectures, depths, moduli (prime, composite, powers of two), and hyperparameters, showing that the aCRT unifies the previously disparate Clock, Pizza, and coset-circuit interpretations as different low-level realizations of one abstract algorithm.

## Results

The paper proves that O(log(n)) distinct frequencies suffice to achieve logit margins of Ω(log(n)) (Corollary 4.8), with the precise threshold given by Theorem 4.7: m > (2 ln n − 2 ln(2−2ρ)) / (ln(π/δ) − 1). Empirically, the number of learned frequencies scales logarithmically with modulus n across all architectures (MLPs, clocks, pizzas) with very high R² for logarithmic fits, validated across prime, composite, and power-of-2 moduli spanning multiple orders of magnitude (Figure 3). The simple neuron model (single sinusoid per neuron) achieves near-perfect R² in fitting preactivations across all layers, and adding depth or trainable embeddings triggers a phase transition from ⌊n/2⌋ learned frequencies down to O(log(n)) frequencies (Figure 4).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an interpretability paper that unifies prior mechanistic explanations rather than benchmarking competing methods. The paper demonstrates that the Clock circuit [4,5], Pizza circuit [5], and Group Composition via Representations (GCR) [8,10] are all different low-level implementations of the same abstract algorithm (aCRT), reconciling what prior work presented as disjoint or contradictory interpretations. Across all tested architectures (1–4 layer MLPs, 1–4 layer transformers with both clock and pizza attention), the same approximate coset structure is recovered in layer-1 neurons with R² near 1.0 for the simple sinusoid fit (Figures 6–7). Networks trained on composite moduli (e.g., n=66) show a measurable preference for learning frequencies that divide the modulus (exact cosets) over approximate cosets (Figure 9), supporting the CRT-like decomposition. Unlike prior work restricted to prime moduli and a few representative seeds, the empirical validation spans moduli 3–4999, multiple depths, and broad hyperparameter grids.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies the previously fragmented interpretability literature on modular addition—Nanda et al.'s Clock/Fourier Multiplication Algorithm, Zhong et al.'s Pizza circuit, Chughtai et al.'s GCR representation-theoretic framework, and Stander et al.'s coset circuits in permutation groups—under a single abstract algorithm (the approximate Chinese Remainder Theorem) by introducing approximate cosets as a generalization of strict cosets. It extends the theoretical analysis of Gromov and Morwani et al., which was limited to 1-layer one-hot MLPs with all ⌊n/2⌋ frequencies, to DNNs with trainable embeddings or multiple hidden layers, proving that O(log(n)) distinct frequencies suffice for strong logit margins. The simple neuron model generalizes prior sinusoidal neuron assumptions to accommodate learned embeddings, reconciling why practical architectures learn only a handful of frequencies rather than the full set.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This provides the first theory-backed interpretation of how multilayer networks solve modular addition, resolving apparent contradictions in prior mechanistic explanations and offering a testable universality conjecture (Conjecture 5.1) for group multiplication across all groups. Key open questions remain: why the phase transition from O(n) to O(log(n)) learned frequencies occurs with depth or trainable embeddings, how many neurons are needed per frequency, and whether approximate cosets generalize to non-cyclic and non-Abelian groups (dihedral, elementary p-groups). The O(log(n)) frequency bound, empirically validated across prime and composite moduli spanning multiple orders of magnitude, suggests a fundamental logarithmic efficiency in how DNNs decompose group operations, with potential implications for understanding DNN efficiency more broadly.
