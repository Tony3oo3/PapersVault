## Problem

Existing relaxation-based graph matching methods project gradients onto the doubly stochastic convex hull, which introduces two errors: geometric misalignment between the relaxed and original permutation domains, and loss of numerical scale invariance that causes convergence to different solutions under different input scalings. Additionally, no theoretically grounded mixed-precision design existed for graph matching algorithms, leaving a gap between the computational demands of large-scale instances and the numerical stability requirements of iterative projection methods.

## Input / Output

The method accepts two attributed graphs of equal size n, where each graph provides an edge-attribute matrix (nonnegative symmetric) and optionally a node-feature matrix; it also handles edge-only and attribute-free variants. The output is an n×n permutation matrix M encoding one-to-one node correspondences, obtained by discretizing the final doubly stochastic matrix via the Hungarian method. Graphs are typically constructed from SIFT keypoints in images (fully connected, weighted by inter-node Euclidean distance) or from social network adjacency structures.

## Method

FRAM replaces the standard doubly stochastic projection in the projected fixed-point framework with a Frobenius-Regularized Linear Assignment (FRA) problem: max_D ⟨D,X⟩ − (1/θ)⟨D,D⟩ over doubly stochastic matrices, where the tunable parameter θ controls the trade-off between assignment sharpness and relaxation bias. The FRA is solved efficiently by Scaling Doubly Stochastic Normalization (SDSN), which alternates a closed-form row/column normalization step and a non-negativity clipping step, with a dimension-scaled convergence criterion γ(X) = (1ᵀX1)/n − 1 that avoids expensive per-iteration checks. The overall algorithm iterates X = ANÂ + λK (gradient) → D = SDSN(X, θ) → N = (1−α)N + αD until convergence, then discretizes N to a permutation matrix. A theoretically grounded mixed-precision design (TF32 for the O(n³) gradient computation, FP32 for SDSN, FP64 for state updates) exploits the error-dissipation property of SDSN (Theorem 3) to achieve up to 370× GPU speedup over CPU-FP64 with ≤0.2% accuracy loss.

## Results

On the Facebook-ego social network (4,039 nodes), FRAM achieves 94.7% node accuracy at 5% noise, 91.1% at 15% noise, and 89.5% at 25% noise, outperforming the second-best method ASM by ~4 percentage points across all noise levels. On real-world attributed graph matching (1,000 nodes), FRAM achieves the best matching error in 5 of 8 image sets (e.g., 3.1×10⁴ on ubc, 3.6×10⁴ on wall) while running 2.8× faster than DSPFP (2.3s vs. 6.5s average). With mixed-precision GPU acceleration, FRAM achieves up to a 371× speedup over its CPU-FP64 implementation on the ubc(2000) task with at most 0.2% accuracy loss.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FRAM consistently outperforms all seven baselines on the Facebook-ego network. Against the closest competitor ASM [34], FRAM gains ~4 percentage points in node accuracy (94.7% vs. 91.1% at 5% noise) while running 1.8× faster (211s vs. 387s). Against DSPFP [27], FRAM achieves a substantial 15% accuracy improvement (94.7% vs. 79.7%) at a modest 1.4× runtime cost. The optimal-transport baselines GWL [39] and S-GWL [38] are both far less accurate (78.1% and 26.4% respectively) and 17–18× slower than FRAM. The spectral method GRASP [16] is the fastest baseline (63.6s) but achieves only 37.9% accuracy, less than half of FRAM's. On the real-world picture benchmark (1,000 nodes), FRAM achieves the best matching error in 5 of 8 image sets and is nearly 2× faster than the second-fastest method ASM, while DSPFP is 2.8× slower with higher error. The mixed-precision GPU variant of FRAM achieves up to 371× speedup over CPU-FP64, a capability none of the baselines can match due to their sensitivity to reduced precision.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the projected fixed-point framework of DSPFP (Lu et al., 2016) by replacing the standard doubly stochastic projection with a Frobenius-Regularized Linear Assignment (FRA) problem, introducing a tunable parameter θ that controls relaxation-induced distortion and restores numerical scale invariance. The solver, Scaling Doubly Stochastic Normalization (SDSN), is derived from the DSN algorithm of Zass and Shashua (2006) but augmented with a scaling step and an explicit convergence criterion. A key departure from prior work is the application of mixed-precision computing to graph matching with a theoretical guarantee (Theorem 3) that rounding errors in the iterative projection structure asymptotically vanish, which the authors claim is the first such result in this domain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 370× GPU speedup over CPU-FP64 with at most 0.2% accuracy loss makes large-scale graph matching (thousands of nodes) practically feasible on commodity hardware, which is significant for applications like point cloud registration and multi-object tracking. The theoretical guarantee that SDSN inherently suppresses rounding errors provides a principled foundation for low-precision deployment that was previously absent in graph matching. However, the regularization parameter θ must be set empirically (no adaptive strategy is provided), and the mixed-precision design, while theoretically grounded, remains a preliminary implementation that could benefit from low-level compilation optimizations. Open questions include whether the FRA formulation generalizes to unequal-size graphs and whether adaptive θ selection can be made fully automatic.
