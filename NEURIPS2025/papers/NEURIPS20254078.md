## Problem

Graph-based machine learning on relational databases requires converting RDB schemas into graphs, but the space of valid graph models is large and downstream performance varies significantly (up to 10%) depending on the chosen model. No prior benchmark existed to systematically evaluate automatic RDB-to-graph modeling strategies, and on-the-fly evaluation of each candidate model requires expensive repeated GNN training, making exhaustive comparison infeasible.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254078/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes as input a relational database schema (from 5 real-world RDBs in RelBench), a downstream predictive task (12 tasks spanning classification, regression, and recommendation), and an evaluation budget limiting how many graph models a method may query. It produces a selected graph model (a subset of tables and FK edges, with each table's rows designated as nodes or edges) and reports downstream performance (AUC-ROC, MAE, or MAP), runtime per epoch, and GNN parameter size. The graph model space is constrained so that the task table is always included, all selected tables are reachable within the GNN's layer depth, and tables may be modeled as edges only if they have exactly two FKs whose PK is not referenced elsewhere.

## Method

RDB2G-Bench precomputes performance metrics for approximately 50k graph models by training Heterogeneous GraphSAGE (sum aggregation) for classification/regression and ID-GNN for recommendation on each model, extended to incorporate edge features for Row2Edge representations. The benchmark then evaluates ten automatic modeling methods—two heuristics (Random, All-Rows-to-Nodes), four action-based search algorithms (Greedy Forward/Backward/Local, Evolutionary Algorithm, Bayesian Optimization via BANANAS, Reinforcement Learning with an LSTM policy), and two LLM-based baselines (direct prompting and Chain-of-Thought with Claude Sonnet-3.5)—all operating over a shared action space of add/remove FK edges and convert rows between node and edge representations. By replacing on-the-fly GNN training with lookups into precomputed metrics, the benchmark achieves a 389× speedup (from >850 hours to 2.2 hours for full evaluation), enabling fair budget-controlled comparison of modeling strategies across all 12 tasks.

## Results

Across 12 predictive tasks on 5 real-world RDBs, the best-performing graph model achieves up to 10% higher downstream performance than the widely-used All-Rows-to-Nodes (AR2N) heuristic. On the rel-f1 dataset at 5% budget, Greedy Forward Search matches the oracle best on both classification tasks (74.56% AUC-ROC on driver-dnf, 81.88% on driver-top3) while AR2N scores 73.14% and 78.11% respectively. RDB2G-Bench's precomputed metrics reduce total benchmark evaluation time by 389× (from >850 hours to 2.20 hours) compared to on-the-fly GNN training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All active search methods (GF, GB, GL, EA, BO, RL, LLM) outperform the fixed AR2N heuristic [29] on every task, with gains of 1–4% AUC-ROC on classification and 0.05–0.07 MAE on regression. Greedy Forward Search [28-style] reaches the oracle-best score on both rel-f1 classification tasks by 2% budget, matching the upper bound without any additional exploration. Greedy Backward dominates at small budgets (1–2%) while Bayesian Optimization [37] pulls ahead at 4–5% budget, suggesting the choice between them should be guided by available compute. Random search [24] performs surprisingly competitively, within 0.5–1% of the best active methods at 5% budget, indicating substantial room for improvement in current search strategies. The LLM-based baseline (Claude Sonnet-3.5) shows the strongest 1%-budget jump among all methods (e.g., 73.86% vs. 73.14% AR2N on driver-dnf) but plateaus quickly, failing to close the gap to greedy or BO methods as budget grows.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 389× evaluation speedup from precomputed metrics makes systematic comparison of RDB-to-graph modeling strategies feasible for the first time, lowering the barrier for future work in this area. The finding that best-performing graph models can outperform the widely-used AR2N convention by up to 10%, and that different tasks on the same RDB require structurally different graph models, challenges the assumption that a single fixed modeling rule suffices. Key limitations include coverage of only four GNN architectures, exclusion of hyperedge modeling and dummy-table design choices, and omission of some RelBench tasks due to computational cost—leaving open whether the observed patterns (e.g., common substructures in top models, cross-GNN generalizability) hold under broader design spaces and more diverse predictors.
