## Problem

Conventional split conformal prediction requires labeled calibration samples, forcing practitioners to either withhold labeled data from training or acquire additional labels solely for calibration. Prior work showed that using purely unlabeled calibration samples yields uninformative or unreliable prediction sets. This paper addresses the gap by developing effective split conformal methods that work with completely unsupervised (unlabeled) calibration samples while retaining near-optimal coverage guarantees.

## Input / Output

The method takes labeled training samples (reused for both classifier training and label-weight computation), unlabeled calibration instances, a target coverage level 1−α, and a conformal score function S(X,y) learned from the training data. For each calibration instance, scores are computed for all c possible labels (not just one), and the method outputs soft label weights w_i(y) ∈ [0,1] summing to 1 per instance, which are then used to compute a weighted conformal quantile. The final output is a prediction set C(X) ⊆ Y for any test instance, with the unusual property that training samples serve a dual role in both learning the score and calibrating the quantile.

## Method

The core mechanism assigns soft label weights to unlabeled calibration instances by solving a convex optimization problem that minimizes the integral probability metric (IPM) between the weighted calibration distribution and the empirical training distribution, making the two sets statistically indistinguishable by a non-parametric two-sample test. With RKHS-based function families, this reduces to a quadratic program of size O(nc) with linear equality and inequality constraints (e.g., cross-entropy loss bounds). The key insight that bypasses the negative result for purely unsupervised calibration is the reuse of training samples for both score learning and label-weight computation, introducing a controlled dependence that is handled via a bias-variance trade-off in the coverage gap bound. Theoretical guarantees show the excess coverage gap G_{n,m}^K decomposes into an approximation error term (bias) and an RKHS-norm-weighted sample-size term (variance), both decreasing at rate O(1/√n + 1/√m). Kernel selection is performed by choosing from candidate bandwidths the one minimizing the minimum-norm interpolation of the coverage indicator function at calibration instances.

## Results

With target coverage 1−α = 0.9 and n = 1,000 calibration samples, the proposed unsupervised method achieves coverage probabilities within 0.01 of the supervised baseline on 8 of 9 datasets (e.g., 0.90 vs. 0.90 on MNIST, 0.91 vs. 0.90 on CIFAR-10, 0.90 vs. 0.90 on USPS). Prediction set sizes are nearly identical (e.g., 1.20 vs. 1.19 on MNIST, 1.01 vs. 1.00 on CIFAR-10). The only notable degradation occurs on 'Letter' (26 classes), where coverage drops to 0.85 vs. 0.90 supervised, consistent with the theoretical bias-variance trade-off worsening with the number of classes. Coverage gaps below 0.015 are achieved with a few thousand unsupervised calibration samples, comparable to the supervised case.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is conventional supervised split conformal prediction (Angelopoulos & Bates, 2023), which requires labeled calibration samples. The proposed unsupervised method matches or slightly exceeds the supervised coverage on 8 of 9 datasets (e.g., 0.91 vs. 0.90 on CIFAR-10 and FashionMNIST; 0.90 vs. 0.90 on MNIST, USPS, Satellite, and ImageNet-10), with prediction set sizes within 0.02–0.05 of the supervised values. The sole exception is 'Letter' (26 classes), where the unsupervised method achieves 0.85 coverage versus 0.90 supervised, reflecting the theoretical degradation with increasing class count. A second baseline, the naive unsupervised approach (using classifier predictions as pseudo-labels for calibration), is substantially worse: it achieves only 0.84–0.88 coverage on 10-class datasets and drops to 0.69 on 'Letter', confirming that the IPM-based label weight optimization is essential. The paper also notes that the naive approach's coverage gap scales with classifier error, making it viable only when the underlying classifier is already highly accurate (e.g., 4% error on CIFAR-10), which would defeat the purpose of conformal prediction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends split conformal prediction (Angelopoulos & Bates, 2023), which requires labeled calibration samples, to the fully unsupervised calibration setting by introducing label weights for unlabeled calibration instances. The label weight optimization is framed as minimizing an integral probability metric (specifically MMD via RKHS), drawing on the kernel two-sample test literature (Gretton et al., 2012). It directly addresses and overcomes a negative result from Cauchois et al. (2024) showing that purely unsupervised calibration yields uninformative or unreliable prediction sets, by leveraging training samples to inform the label weights. The approach also subsumes prior work on weakly-supervised calibration (noisy or partial labels) as a special case, since those methods cannot handle the fully unlabeled regime.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method removes the practical barrier of requiring additional labeled data at the calibration stage, making conformal prediction viable in privacy-sensitive settings or when labels correspond to future unobservable events. The theoretical guarantees (Theorems 1 and 2) provide a clean bias-variance characterization of the coverage gap, and the kernel-based implementation enables automatic capacity control and data-driven kernel selection. Key limitations include poor scaling with the number of classes (the 26-class 'Letter' dataset shows substantially degraded coverage) and higher computational cost (solving a QP of size nc vs. computing a single quantile). Open questions include whether the class-scaling limitation can be mitigated and whether the approach extends to regression or multilabel settings.
