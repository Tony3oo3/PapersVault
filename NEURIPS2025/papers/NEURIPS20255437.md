## Problem

Existing group-level Shapley value methods (GSV) treat each pre-defined group as an atomic unit, making them vulnerable to a 'shell company attack' where strategically splitting data into smaller subgroups inflates the total valuation earned by the same data content. No prior group valuation method simultaneously satisfies the standard Shapley axioms and remains invariant to how other players partition their data, leaving a gap in faithful and manipulation-resistant group-level data valuation.

## Input / Output

The method takes a training dataset D, a target group S₀ to evaluate, and a utility function U(S) (e.g., model accuracy on a test set), and produces a single scalar group valuation score. A practical detail is that for ML methods requiring a minimum input size B (e.g., linear regression with p predictors), the algorithm injects B−|S| non-informative data points (drawn from a shuffled-label distribution) whenever |S| < B, ensuring U(S) is always well-defined. The valuation can be normalized across groups to yield royalty shares or used directly as group-level feature importance in explainable AI.

## Method

FGSV is defined as the sum of individual Data Shapley values over group members, and Theorem 1 proves it is the unique valuation satisfying five axioms (null player, symmetry, linearity, efficiency, and a new faithfulness axiom requiring invariance to re-partitioning of other groups). The key algorithmic insight is that FGSV decomposes into terms T(s) indexed by subset size s, where each T(s) depends on the average utility μ(s₁/s; s, s₀, n) over subsets with a given intersection size s₁ with S₀. For large s, T(s) is dominated by s₁ near its hypergeometric mean ss₀/n, so it can be approximated by a single finite-difference evaluation Δμ at s₀/n with error O(s^{-(1+v)}); for small s, direct Monte Carlo over the hypergeometric distribution is used. A paired Monte Carlo estimator (borrowing variance-reduction from stochastic simulation) computes Δμ efficiently, and the overall algorithm requires only O(n·Poly(log n)) utility evaluations—avoiding the quadratic blow-up of summing individual Shapley estimates—under standard deletion-stability and second-order algorithmic stability assumptions.

## Results

On the Sum-of-Unanimity (SOU) cooperative game with n ∈ {64, 128, 256} players and a fixed budget of 20,000 utility evaluations, FGSV achieves the lowest Area Under the Convergence Curve (AUCC) and lowest Absolute Relative Error (ARE) across all problem sizes, while also being among the fastest methods in runtime per iteration. In the copyright attribution experiment on Stable Diffusion (LoRA fine-tuned on FlickrLogo-27), FSRS (FGSV-based) maintains stable royalty shares under a shell company attack that inflates Google and Sprite shares by a substantial margin under SRS (GSV-based). On the Diabetes dataset for explainable AI, FGSV consistently ranks the 'Young' age group highest across all 7 grouping schemes, whereas GSV produces inconsistent top rankings (each of the three age groups appears as top under at least one scheme).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FGSV outperforms all seven individual-Shapley-then-sum baselines (Permutation [2], Group Testing [14], Complementary Contribution [38], One-for-All [24], KernelSHAP [26], Unbiased KernelSHAP [3], and LeverageSHAP [27]) on both AUCC and ARE in the SOU game across n = 64, 128, 256, with the advantage widening as n grows. Group Testing, KernelSHAP, and Unbiased KernelSHAP incur substantially higher per-iteration runtime due to internal optimization, while FGSV is among the fastest. Against SRS (Shapley Royalty Share, [35]) in the Stable Diffusion copyright experiment, FSRS remains stable under the shell company attack (splitting Google/Sprite into 20/10 subgroups), whereas SRS shows inflated shares for the split groups and misleadingly ranks them above the prompt-matching brand. In the Diabetes explainable-AI experiment, FGSV produces consistent category-level rankings across all grouping schemes, while GSV (GroupShapley, [15]) yields unstable rankings where the top age group changes depending on the partition. The theoretical sample complexity of FGSV is O(n · Poly(log n)) utility evaluations for a constant-fraction group, compared to O(α₀ n² Poly(log n)) for summing SOTA individual estimates, avoiding a quadratic blow-up.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Data Shapley framework (Ghorbani & Zou, 2019) from individual to group-level valuation by introducing a faithfulness axiom that the existing Group Shapley Value (GSV) approaches (Jullum et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2024) fail to satisfy. The key departure is identifying the 'shell company attack'—a vulnerability where strategic partitioning of other groups inflates valuations—showing that the natural group-as-individual (GaI) extension of Shapley's four axioms is insufficient. The approximation algorithm draws on variance-reduction techniques from stochastic simulation (Law & Kelton, 1982) and the hypergeometric structure of subset sampling to directly estimate the group value, avoiding the quadratic blow-up of summing individual Shapley estimates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of the shell company attack exposes a practical fairness gap in data marketplaces and copyright compensation systems that currently rely on GSV-based methods like Shapley Royalty Share. The O(n·Poly(log n)) sample complexity for group valuation (versus O(α₀n²·Poly(log n)) for the naive sum-of-individuals approach) makes faithful group valuation tractable at scale. An open challenge remains: defending against the copier attack (data duplication) for general utility functions while preserving the shell-company defense, which the authors acknowledge is unresolved. The faithfulness axiom itself is a natural but previously unformalized requirement that may influence how future group-level valuation methods are designed.
