## Problem

Transformer architectures are typically discovered through costly trial-and-error, and no principled framework has simultaneously informed both the training objective and architecture design from a unified theoretical perspective. This paper shows that the continuous-time training problem for Transformers is ill-posed without regularization (infinitely many optimal velocity fields exist), and that optimal transport regularization is the natural fix that yields well-posedness, stable forward propagation, and provable generalization/robustness guarantees.

## Input / Output

The method is plug-and-play across modalities: text sequences (character-level Shakespeare, word-level Shakespeare, 9B-token OpenWebText), RGB images (28×28 MNIST, 460×320 Cats & Dogs), and 3D point clouds (ModelNet 40, ~10K CAD models in 40 classes). Inputs are first embedded into a d-dimensional space via a standard Transformer embedding layer with positional encoding, then evolved through a continuous-time dynamical system; outputs are produced by an MLP applied to the terminal state, yielding next-token predictions, class labels, or sentiment labels depending on the task. The framework reduces to the standard discrete Transformer when the terminal time T=1 and the number of integration steps M=1.

## Method

OT-Transformer inserts an existing Transformer into a continuous-time ODE dX(t)/dt = f(X(t), t; θ), where f is the composition of all Transformer blocks (self-attention + FFN) acting as a single velocity field, and trains via a discretize-then-optimize scheme that adds an OT regularization term (λΔt/2)Σ‖f(X_m, t_m)‖²_F to the standard loss. This regularization is derived from the Benamou-Brenier formulation of optimal transport and is computed alongside each forward step at negligible overhead. The key theoretical result is that λ > 0 renders the associated Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman PDE well-defined, guaranteeing a unique and regular optimal velocity; this regularity proves the learned model map is Lipschitz (stable forward propagation), which in turn yields Wasserstein-distance-based distributional robustness bounds and non-asymptotic generalization guarantees via distributionally robust optimization. Empirically, the framework improves test performance while using fewer parameters (e.g., 46% lower test loss with 42% fewer parameters on nanoGPT Shakespeare), and the performance gap over baselines widens under input noise, corroborating the robustness theory.

## Results

On character-level Shakespeare text generation with nanoGPT, OT-Transformer achieves a 46% reduction in final test loss (1.44 vs. 2.68) while using 42% fewer parameters (6.16M vs. 10.65M). On GPT-2 (123.7M params), the method reduces test loss by 9.3% on word-level Shakespeare (4.96 vs. 5.18) and by 0.30 on the 9B-token OpenWebText dataset (2.91 vs. 3.21). Across the remaining four tasks (point cloud, MNIST, Cats & Dogs, sentiment analysis), OT-Transformer consistently improves test accuracy by 0.7–4.1 percentage points while using 25–50% fewer parameters than the baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

OT-Transformer outperforms all five baselines across every experiment. Against nanoGPT [48], it cuts test loss from 2.68 to 1.44 (−46%) with 42% fewer parameters. Against GPT-2 [69], it reduces test loss by 9.3% on Shakespeare (word-level) and by 0.30 on OpenWebText at the same 123.7M parameter count. Against Set Transformer [53] on ModelNet 40, it improves accuracy from 87.4% to 89.9% while reducing parameters from 0.86M to 0.65M. Against ViT [22], it raises MNIST accuracy from 93.0% to 97.1% (with 18K vs. 93K params) and Cats & Dogs accuracy from 77.6% to 79.0% (1.48M vs. 1.77M params). Against the Sinkformers encoder-only Transformer [74] on IMDb sentiment analysis, it improves accuracy from 83.9% to 84.6% while halving the parameter count (2.37M vs. 4.74M). Robustness tests (Table 3) further confirm that the performance gap widens under input noise, dropout, and character replacement, validating the theoretical stability guarantees.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Benamou-Brenier optimal transport formulation and the OT-regularized continuous normalizing flow framework (Onken et al., 2021) to Transformer architectures, but with a critical structural departure: rather than treating each Transformer block as a separate dynamical system (as in the N-ODE Transformer of Baier-Reinio & De Sterck, 2020), it composes all blocks into a single velocity field governing one continuous-time ODE. This single-dynamical-system formulation is what makes the OT regularization effective and is the key reason prior continuous-time Transformer attempts failed to improve over vanilla Transformers. The theoretical analysis is novel in applying Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman PDE regularity theory to prove stable forward propagation of the learned Transformer map, a connection not previously drawn in the Transformer literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first optimal-control-theoretic foundation for Transformer training and architecture design, yielding non-asymptotic generalization bounds and distributional robustness guarantees that were previously unavailable for these models. The plug-and-play nature (requiring only a Frobenius-norm penalty on the velocity field) makes the approach immediately applicable to existing codebases, and the consistent improvements across seven tasks at multiple scales (from 93K-parameter MNIST ViT to 124M-parameter GPT-2 on 9B tokens) suggest broad practical relevance. Key limitations include the theoretical guarantees holding only for encoder-only and decoder-only architectures (due to a convexity assumption on the loss), and the computational overhead of numerical integration, though the authors show this is mitigated by parameter efficiency. Open directions include extending the optimal control analysis to layer normalization, the attention mechanism itself, and alternative integration schemes.
