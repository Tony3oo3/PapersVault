## Problem

Synthetic data is increasingly used to train LLMs, but iterative training on model-generated data without curation leads to model collapse. While methods like STaR and ReST empirically succeed, there was no unified theoretical framework explaining the minimum amount of exogenous curation needed to guarantee continuous improvement toward an optimal model, nor a principled explanation for why focusing on hard examples helps.

## Input / Output

The algorithm takes a fixed prompt set P, a γ-noisy filter (which recognizes correct synthetic responses with recall at least γ), and a β-weak labeler (an exogenous source that produces responses with at least β fraction correct on average, without indicating which are correct). It outputs a sequence of LLMs g_1, ..., g_T where the final model achieves quality ≥ 1−ε on the prompt set. A key constraint is that the weak labeler receives only the prompts the current LLM consistently fails on (P_t^-), and the total weight of weakly-labeled data added per iteration is fixed at α, making the labeler's per-prompt influence grow as the set of hard prompts shrinks.

## Method

The paper proposes a boosting-style iterative procedure (Algorithm 2) that inverts the classic boosting duality: instead of combining weak learners on strong (i.i.d.) data, it uses strong learners (LLMs that perfectly fit their training distribution) on weak data (a β-weak labeler). Each iteration generates k synthetic responses per prompt from the current LLM, applies a γ-noisy filter to retain correct ones, passes the remaining hard prompts to the weak labeler, and mixes the filtered synthetic data with the weakly-labeled data (weighted by α/|D_t|) into an accumulating training set. The key mechanism is that the set of prompts sent to the labeler shrinks by a factor of (1−β) each iteration, so the fixed total weight α becomes concentrated on fewer and fewer prompts, eventually overwhelming all prior incorrect labels in the training mixture. Theorem 5 proves convergence to an optimal LLM in T = O(log(1/ε)/β) iterations with k = Ω(log T/(βγ)) samples per prompt, and experiments on GSM8K and MBPP with Gemma 2 2B validate that focusing the labeler on the hardest prompts outperforms random allocation.

## Results

On GSM8K (greedy test accuracy), the Boosting variant with weak data (A) and no focusing achieves 45.6%, outperforming Filter only (39.3%), Gold SFT (37.9%), and the pre-trained baseline (22.2%). The 'Do nothing' baseline (no curation) suffers model collapse, dropping to 12.9% test accuracy after 5 rounds. On MBPP (greedy test pass rate), all iterative curation methods converge to similar performance (~32.9%), substantially above the collapsed 'Do nothing' baseline (21.0%). Training accuracy gains are more pronounced: Boosting (A) w/o focusing reaches 58.9% train @1 on GSM8K vs. 55.3% for Filter only.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four iterative training strategies against two non-iterative baselines (pre-trained model and Gold SFT). The 'Do nothing' baseline (iterative fine-tuning on raw synthetic data with no curation) confirms model collapse: GSM8K test accuracy drops from 22.2% (PT) to 12.9% after 5 rounds. Filter only (equivalent to STaR/ReST) prevents collapse and reaches 39.3% GSM8K test accuracy, but the full Boosting algorithm with weak data (A) and no focusing further improves to 45.6% (+6.3 pp), despite introducing incorrect answers into the training mixture. On MBPP, all curation-based methods (Filter only, Boosting A/B) converge to nearly identical test pass rates (~32.9%), suggesting the small training set (374 problems) limits generalization differentiation. Gold SFT (one round of fine-tuning on human-written solutions) achieves 37.9% on GSM8K but underperforms both Filter only and Boosting, and on MBPP it actually degrades below the pre-trained model (23.7% vs. 27.5%), highlighting that iterative synthetic-data training with curation can surpass a single round of human-data fine-tuning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper draws a direct analogy between AdaBoost and iterative LLM training on synthetic data, but inverts the classical boosting setup: instead of combining weak learners with strong (i.i.d.) data, it combines strong learners (LLMs that perfectly fit training data) with weak data (a labeler that gets only a β > 0 fraction correct). It subsumes STaR, ReST, and ReST^EM as the 'filter-only' special case (α = 0) and extends them by adding a weak labeler that focuses curation on the hardest prompts. Unlike prior model-collapse analyses (Shumailov et al., Alemohammad et al., Gerstgrasser et al.) that show iterative synthetic training degrades performance or that mixing in real data mitigates degradation, this paper proves convergence to an optimal model under a minimal curation assumption.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that an arbitrarily small but positive fraction of correct exogenous labels (β > 0) suffices for convergence to near-perfect accuracy is a clean and actionable theoretical guarantee that explains why existing self-training pipelines work and suggests where to focus scarce curation budget. Key limitations include the strong-learner assumption (perfect distributional fitting), the absence of a generalization guarantee, and small-scale experiments (Gemma 2 2B, 5 rounds, modest test-accuracy gains, mixed MBPP results). Open questions include relaxing the strong-learner assumption to approximate fitting, understanding generalization, and extending the framework to data selection with similarity structure.
