## Problem

Scientific discovery datasets (e.g., rare fluorescent compounds, novel materials, faint astronomical signals) exhibit a 'pathological' long-tailed distribution: extreme class imbalance coupled with a modest number of classes and limited total sample volume. Existing long-tailed recognition methods—designed for large-scale benchmarks with hundreds of classes and millions of samples—fail catastrophically in this regime, missing the very rare tail instances that drive scientific breakthroughs.

## Input / Output

The method handles two input modalities: molecular SMILES strings (converted to graph representations via RDKit for the ZincFluor dataset) and standard 32×32 RGB images (for CIFAR-LT and Places-LT benchmarks). In both cases, the output is a single class label—8 fluorescence levels for ZincFluor, 10/100 scene/object categories for the image benchmarks. The ZincFluor backbone is a 6-layer GCN, while image experiments use standard CNN backbones; both feed into a shared projection head and classifier.

## Method

The framework formulates pathological long-tailed recognition as a constrained multi-objective optimization problem with three competing losses: (1) a Classification Performance Objective (CPO) applying cross-entropy to both original and augmented views, (2) a Balanced Supervised Contrastive Learning (B-SCL) loss that extends SupCon with class-frequency-aware weights w_c = exp(s'_c)/Σexp(s'_k) where s'_k = (N_{C-1-k})^α to up-weight tail-class anchors, and (3) a Smooth Objective Regularization (SOR) term defined as λ_SOR · LogSumExp([L_CE_orig, L_CE_aug, L_B-SC]). The SOR term acts as a differentiable smooth maximum over the three constituent losses, dynamically amplifying the gradient of whichever loss becomes disproportionately large (via softmax-weighted gradient contributions of (1 + λ_SOR · p_k)), thereby preventing head-class dominance or tail-class overfitting from destabilizing training. This yields a Pareto-optimal balance without requiring manual per-class tuning or post-hoc logit adjustments.

## Results

On the real-world ZincFluor dataset (T=137.54, 8 fluorescence classes), the proposed method achieves a Tail Top-2 accuracy of 66.84%, substantially outperforming the second-best BCL (59.57%) and all other baselines (CE 36.70%, BS 36.17%, LDAM-DRW 51.24%). On CIFAR-10-LT with extreme imbalance (IR=1000, T=100), the method reaches 38.99% tail-class accuracy and 69.20% overall accuracy, leading the next best (BCL: 28.55% tail, 65.06% overall) by over 10 points on tail accuracy. On CIFAR-100-LT (IR=500), tail accuracy reaches 22.52% vs. BCL's 14.96%, and on custom Places-LT (IR=996, 10 categories), tail accuracy is 62.0% vs. LDAM-DRW's 60.33%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all five baseline families across every dataset and every tail-class metric. BCL (Cui et al., ICCV 2021) is the strongest competitor, achieving 59.57% Tail Top-2 on ZincFluor and 28.55% tail accuracy on CIFAR-10-LT (IR=1000), but is consistently surpassed by 7–10 points. The re-weighting baselines CE-DRW and LDAM-DRW (Cao et al., NeurIPS 2019) show moderate tail performance (50–51% on ZincFluor) but collapse to 4–15% tail accuracy on CIFAR-10-LT at IR=1000. Balanced Softmax (Liu et al., CVPR 2019) and KPS (Li et al., TPAMI 2022) perform poorly on tail classes, with KPS dropping to 35.64% Tail Top-2 on ZincFluor and 6.65% tail on CIFAR-10-LT (IR=1000). LORT (Lu et al., 2024) is the weakest baseline in extreme imbalance settings, achieving near-zero tail accuracy (0.05%) on CIFAR-10-LT at IR=1000 and 37.76% Tail Top-2 on ZincFluor. The gap between the proposed method and all baselines widens as the imbalance ratio increases, confirming the method's specific advantage in pathological long-tail regimes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Supervised Contrastive Learning (SupCon) framework [14] and its long-tailed adaptations (BCL [6], Targeted SupCon [17]) by introducing class-frequency-aware sample weights (B-SCL) specifically calibrated for extreme, 'pathological' imbalance. The Smooth Objective Regularization (SOR) term repurposes the LogSumExp smooth-maximum from multi-objective optimization (Tchebycheff/min-max scalarization) as a dynamic penalty that prevents any single constituent loss from dominating, a mechanism not previously applied to LTR training. The paper also departs from the standard LTR benchmark paradigm (ImageNet-LT, Places365-LT) by introducing the ZincFluor molecular dataset, which couples extreme imbalance (T=137.54) with a small class count (8) and limited sample volume, a regime the authors argue existing LTR methods were not designed to handle.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ZincFluor dataset provides a rare real-world scientific benchmark for pathological long-tail classification, and the SOR mechanism offers a clean, theoretically grounded way to balance competing objectives without manual hyperparameter tuning per class. However, the practical scope remains narrow: the method is validated on small-scale datasets (8-class molecular graphs, CIFAR-10/100, reduced Places-LT), the ZincFluor data originates from a single lab and is not broadly reproducible, and the head-class accuracy trade-off visible in the Places-LT appendix (e.g., 47.92% vs. 63.78% for BCL at 100 categories) suggests the tail-focused optimization can come at a cost. Open questions include whether the framework scales to larger scientific datasets with more classes, whether the GCN backbone is essential or the method transfers to other architectures, and whether incorporating domain-specific structural priors (as the authors hint) could further close the gap on the most extreme tail classes.
