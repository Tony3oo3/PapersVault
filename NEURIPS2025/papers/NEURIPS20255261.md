## Problem

Deep learning models are vulnerable to perturbations and domain shifts because they overfit to superficial, domain-specific cues during training. Previous attempts to apply classical edge detection to input images failed because they stripped fine-grained details and were restricted to the image domain. This paper asks whether the robustness benefits of edge filtering can be generalized to deep internal features across all data modalities, not just raw images.

## Input / Output

The Edge Filter operates on intermediate deep feature maps rather than raw inputs: 2D spatial feature maps (H×W×C) for CNN backbones and 1D sequence features (N×C) for Transformer/MLP backbones. The filter is applied channel-wise with reflect padding to preserve spatial or sequence dimensions, and the low-pass branch is detached so gradients flow only through the high-pass residual. The model's final output remains unchanged from the base architecture—class labels for classification tasks or rendered images for NeRF.

## Method

The Deep Edge Filter is a non-learnable high-pass operator defined as F_edge(h) = h − LPF(h), where LPF is a mean, median, or Gaussian kernel applied to the deep feature h. It is inserted in series between blocks of the base model (typically after one specific block), using 2D kernels for CNNs (filtering across spatial dimensions per channel) and 1D kernels for Transformers/MLPs (filtering across sequence length per channel). The LPF branch is detached during training to prevent gradient backpropagation through the low-pass path, ensuring the model trains exclusively on the high-pass residual, and only a single filter is used to avoid excessive information loss. The core hypothesis is that deep networks encode domain-specific biases (illumination, texture, background) in low-frequency components while task-relevant semantic information (boundaries, key phrases, spatial discontinuities) resides in high-frequency components, so subtracting the LPF isolates generalizable representations. Empirically, the filter induces significant feature sparsification and reduces low-frequency FFT amplitude, validating the frequency decomposition hypothesis across vision, text, 3D, and audio modalities.

## Results

On the primary Test-Time Adaptation benchmark (CIFAR-10C, Direct method), the Edge Filter improves WRN-28-10 from 49.6% to 57.7% (+8.0 pp) and ViT-B/32 from 60.8% to 68.1% (+7.3 pp). On CIFAR-100C Direct, WRN gains +10.2 pp (26.2→36.4), while on ImageNet200-C Direct, ViT gains +1.9 pp (27.5→29.4). In the language domain, SST-2 accuracy improves from 79.36% to 80.85% (+1.49 pp), and in audio classification (UrbanSound8K), accuracy rises from 77.42% to 81.72% (+4.3 pp). For few-shot NeRF (8-view Blender), average PSNR improves from 22.95 to 23.39 dB and LPIPS decreases from 0.126 to 0.112.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between vanilla models and the same models with an Edge Filter attached (trained from scratch under identical conditions). The Edge Filter consistently outperforms the vanilla baseline across all four modalities and both CNN (WRN-28-10, ResNet-18) and Transformer (ViT-B/32, BERT) backbones, with the largest gains on the most corrupted settings (CIFAR-100C Direct: +10.2 pp for WRN). A critical control experiment (Appendix F) replaces the Edge Filter with a trainable 2D Conv layer of identical computational cost; this Conv layer actually degrades performance (46.9% vs. 57.7% on CIFAR-10C Direct), confirming the gain is not merely from added parameters. Applying the low-pass filter directly (without the high-pass subtraction) causes severe degradation (e.g., 39.69% vs. 57.65% on CIFAR-10C Direct for WRN), validating that the high-pass component is essential. The one notable exception is the NeRF 'ficus' scene, where the Edge Filter slightly hurts performance (−0.21 dB PSNR), attributed to the scene's naturally rich high-frequency textures making the filter redundant.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the classical image-processing concept of edge detection (high-pass filtering) and transplants it from the input layer to the interior of deep networks, a move that prior attempts (e.g., applying Sobel or Canny operators to input images) failed to make successfully. Conceptually, it sits at the intersection of the frequency-principle literature (Xu et al., Geirhos et al.) showing that DNNs encode texture in low frequencies and shape in high frequencies, and the domain-adaptation line of work (TENT, adaptive BN) that empirically demonstrated domain-specific information concentrates in first- and second-order feature statistics. Unlike Deep Frequency Filtering (Lin et al.), which uses FFT and learnable attention masks in the frequency domain, the proposed Edge Filter is a non-learnable, spatial-domain subtraction of a low-pass kernel, making it architecture- and modality-agnostic by construction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The core practical takeaway is that a single, parameter-free, human-designed operation (subtract a mean/median/Gaussian blur from one layer's output) can improve generalization across vision, text, 3D, and audio without architectural changes, offering a cheap plug-in for robustness-critical deployments. Key limitations include the requirement to retrain from scratch, the absence of validation on LLMs or very large-scale models (though an OpenCLIP ViT-L/14 experiment in the appendix partially addresses this), and the lack of error bars in the main tables. Open questions include whether the filter composes with other regularization strategies, whether its optimal placement and kernel size can be predicted a priori rather than searched, and whether the low-frequency = domain-specific decomposition holds in multimodal or generative settings.
