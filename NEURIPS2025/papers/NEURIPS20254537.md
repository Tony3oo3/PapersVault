## Problem

Policy distillation after training can sometimes produce a policy that generalises better than the original RL agent, but it remains unclear why this happens or what data should be used for distillation. Prior theoretical explanations (e.g., TD-loss smoothness, target stationarity) are indirect and do not address how the diversity of the distillation dataset affects generalisation. This paper seeks a principled, provable account of both the ensemble size and data diversity effects.

## Input / Output

The method takes a trained RL agent (teacher policy) and a fixed dataset of states collected from the training contexts after training; the dataset can be enriched by sampling additional trajectories from the same contexts with different starting poses or from additional contexts, without requiring the student to interact with the environment. The output is an ensemble of N independently distilled student networks that, at inference time, each perform an independent forward pass and whose outputs are averaged to produce the final action. For stochastic policies, distillation regresses on the full action-probability vector (or logits) of the teacher, whereas behaviour cloning uses a logarithmic loss on the single sampled action, making distillation strictly more informative.

## Method

The paper introduces the Generalisation-Through-Invariance ZSPT (GTI-ZSPT) setting, where the optimal policy is invariant to a symmetry group G but the agent only observes a finite subgroup B ≤ G during training, and proves a generalisation bound (Theorem 1) for a distilled ensemble policy in this setting. The bound decomposes the performance gap into two terms: κ·C_Θ, which decreases as the subgroup B better covers G (i.e., as the distillation data becomes more diverse), and (1/√N)·C_Σ, which decreases with ensemble size N. The proof combines Lemma 6.2 from Gerken & Kessel (2024) bounding invariance deviation of infinite-width ensembles trained with full data augmentation on a finite subgroup, a Monte-Carlo concentration bound for finite ensembles, and the Lipschitz-MDP performance bound of Maran et al. (2023). Empirically, the two insights—distil a larger ensemble and distil on more diverse states—are validated on a Reacher CMDP with rotational symmetry (satisfying GTI-ZSPT) and on Minigrid Four Rooms (breaking most theoretical assumptions), where an N=10 ensemble distilled on the Explore-Go dataset outperforms the original PPO+Explore-Go teacher by a wide margin at only ~12% additional environment cost.

## Results

In the Minigrid Four Rooms environment, an ensemble of N=10 distilled policies trained on the diverse Explore-Go dataset achieves a test return of 0.88±0.036, significantly outperforming the original PPO+Explore-Go teacher (0.74±0.040) with only ~12% additional environment steps. In the Reacher with rotational symmetry CMDP, increasing the ensemble size from N=1 to N=100 improves test performance from 0.75±0.147 to 1.05±0.117, and training on the larger subgroup C₈ (45° rotations) yields 1.11±0.072 versus 0.75±0.147 for C₄ (90° rotations). Behaviour cloning on the Explore-Go dataset with N=10 also improves over the teacher (0.75±0.045 vs. 0.74±0.040), though distillation consistently outperforms BC across all dataset types.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the PPO+Explore-Go teacher (Weltevrede et al., 2025), which achieves a test return of 0.74±0.040 in the Four Rooms environment. The paper's method — an ensemble of N=10 distilled policies trained on the Explore-Go dataset — significantly outperforms this baseline, reaching 0.88±0.036 test return (+0.14 absolute improvement) while requiring only ~12% additional environment steps beyond the teacher's training budget. Even a single distilled policy (N=1) on the Explore-Go dataset (0.78±0.041) already matches or slightly exceeds the teacher. The Mixed dataset (50/50 teacher + exploration data) also beats the teacher with N=10 (0.84±0.034), confirming that data diversity is the key driver. In contrast, distilling on the original Teacher dataset alone underperforms the baseline (0.67±0.054 with N=10), showing that diversity in the distillation data is essential. Behaviour cloning on the Explore-Go dataset (0.75±0.045, N=10) roughly matches the teacher but is consistently worse than distillation, since BC lacks access to the full action probability distribution.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Lyle et al. (2022), who showed empirically that post-training policy distillation can improve zero-shot generalisation but only indirectly explained why via TD-loss smoothness. Here, the authors provide a direct theoretical mechanism by combining the invariance guarantees for infinite ensembles from Gerken and Kessel (2024) with the Lipschitz-continuity performance bounds from Maran et al. (2023), yielding a generalisation bound that explicitly identifies ensemble size and distillation data diversity as the two levers. The work also connects to the broader data-augmentation-for-generalisation literature, showing that the group-theoretic invariance framing can be relaxed to simply 'train on more diverse data from the same contexts.'

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway—that distilling a small ensemble (N=10) on a diverse dataset collected with only ~12% additional environment steps can substantially outperform the original RL agent—offers a cheap, post-hoc generalisation boost that is agnostic to how the teacher was trained. The theory's strict assumptions (infinitely wide networks, deterministic scalar policies, exact group symmetry) limit its direct applicability, and the empirical validation is confined to two environments (a custom Reacher CMDP and Minigrid Four Rooms). An open question the paper itself flags is whether the observed benefits stem from induced invariance or from regularisation/overfitting reduction, a debate that remains unresolved in the broader data-augmentation literature.
