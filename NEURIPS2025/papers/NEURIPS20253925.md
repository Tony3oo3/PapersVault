## Problem

Conformal prediction provides distribution-free finite-sample coverage guarantees, but its reliance on a holdout calibration set makes it produce trivial or overly conservative prediction sets when labeled data is scarce (e.g., fewer than 20 points for 95% coverage). This paper asks whether a large pool of synthetic data—generated by diffusion models or drawn from related distributions—can be rigorously incorporated to boost sample efficiency while preserving valid, model-agnostic coverage guarantees, even under arbitrary distributional shift between real and synthetic data.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a small real calibration set (m points, often as few as 15), a large synthetic calibration set (N points, typically N ≫ m, e.g., 1,000–30,000), a pre-constructed nonconformity score function (e.g., CLIP-based APS scores for images or CQR scores for tabular regression), and a target coverage level 1−α. It outputs a prediction set for classification (a subset of candidate labels) or a prediction interval for regression. Notably, the synthetic data may be generated by a diffusion model (Stable Diffusion, FLUX) or drawn from an entirely different real distribution, and the method requires no data splitting between score construction and calibration.

## Method

The core contribution is a score transporter T, an empirical quantile mapping that transports real calibration scores into the synthetic score space. The procedure has three steps: (1) for each rank r among the m+1 real scores, construct a window I_m(r) in the synthetic score space whose endpoints are determined by order-statistic quantiles of the combined real+synthetic population (controlled by a parameter β); (2) define T(η) to map a real score η to the nearest synthetic score within its corresponding window, using a lower-nearest-neighbor rule to ensure tight coverage control; (3) run split conformal prediction on the N synthetic scores and include a candidate label y in the prediction set if T(s(X_{m+1}, y)) falls below the synthetic (1−α)-quantile. Two complementary theoretical guarantees are established: Theorem 3.3 shows coverage within 1−α ± (β + ε_{P,Q}) when real and synthetic score distributions are close (ε bounded by total variation distance), while Theorem 3.5 provides worst-case bounds depending only on m, N, α, and β that hold regardless of the synthetic distribution—even if synthetic scores depend on the real calibration set. A fast equivalent formula (Proposition 3.2) reduces computation to a single threshold comparison, matching the runtime of standard conformal prediction.

## Results

On ImageNet classification with m=15 real calibration points and N=1,000 synthetic images (Stable Diffusion), SPI produces non-trivial prediction sets with coverage within the theoretical bounds of Theorem 3.5 at target level 1−α=0.95, while OnlyReal yields trivial prediction sets containing all 30 labels. In the subset-selection variant (N=1,500 synthetic, target 1−α=0.98), SPI-Subset with k=20 nearest-subset selection achieves coverage closer to the target and smaller prediction sets than SPI-Whole in the label-conditional setting. On MEPS regression (m=15, N=1,000, target 1−α=0.9), SPI achieves coverage close to the nominal 0.9 level with narrower prediction intervals than OnlyReal, confirming the theoretical prediction of Theorem 3.3 when real and synthetic score distributions are well aligned.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three methods: OnlyReal (standard split conformal prediction on the real calibration set), OnlySynth (conformal prediction applied to the synthetic set as if it were real, with no coverage guarantee), and SPI (the proposed method). OnlyReal always achieves valid coverage at the target level but produces trivial prediction sets (all 30 labels) when m=15 and α=0.02, since split conformal requires at least 50 datapoints for a non-trivial set at that level. OnlySynth exhibits undercoverage for several classes due to distribution shift between real and synthetic data, violating the target coverage. SPI consistently achieves coverage within the worst-case bounds of Theorem 3.5 while producing substantially smaller prediction sets than OnlyReal; for well-aligned classes (e.g., 'lighter'), SPI shows low variance and tight coverage, while for poorly aligned classes (e.g., 'Siberian husky'), SPI still yields informative sets. In the k-nearest subset selection experiment, SPI-Subset outperforms SPI-Whole in the label-conditional setting by better aligning the synthetic distribution with the real one, though in the marginal setting SPI-Whole's larger effective sample (1,500 vs. 300 points) gives it lower coverage variance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends split conformal prediction (Papadopoulos et al., 2002) and Mondrian/label-conditional conformal frameworks (Vovk et al., 2005) by introducing a score transporter—an empirical quantile mapping that aligns nonconformity scores from a small real calibration set with those from a large synthetic set drawn from an arbitrary distribution. The window construction for the transporter builds on the order-statistic distributional results from Lee et al. (2024) on batch predictive inference. Unlike prior approaches that either assume synthetic/unlabeled data comes from the same distribution (prediction-powered inference) or rely on auxiliary tasks with limited data (few-shot conformal prediction), SPI maintains distribution-free coverage guarantees even when the synthetic distribution is completely unrelated to the real one, and even when synthetic scores are constructed in a data-dependent manner.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically critical gap: conformal prediction becomes uninformative (trivial prediction sets) when calibration data is scarce, which is common in medical, personalized, and subgroup-specific inference. The worst-case coverage guarantee (Theorem 3.5) that holds under arbitrary dependence between synthetic and real data is a strong theoretical contribution, enabling practitioners to use generative-model outputs or related-population data without distributional assumptions. Key limitations include the i.i.d. assumption on real calibration data, the restriction to marginal and label-conditional (not feature-conditional) guarantees, and the need for a fixed number of selected synthetic points in the subset variant. Open directions include extending to feature-conditional settings, handling multiple heterogeneous synthetic sources, and generalizing to conformal risk control and hypothesis testing.
