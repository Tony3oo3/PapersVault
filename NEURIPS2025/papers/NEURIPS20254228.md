## Problem

Standard graph contrastive learning (GCL) suffers from two critical biases: (1) similarity estimation bias, where negatively-synergistic feature elements within representations cancel out positively-synergistic ones during inner-product similarity computation, making true positive pairs appear less similar; and (2) semantic shift bias, where random graph augmentations flip the semantic relationship between sample pairs, injecting noise into the fixed contrastive supervision. Existing GCL methods rely on a single global inner product and rigid positive/negative assignments, leaving both biases unaddressed.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a graph defined by a node attribute matrix X ∈ R^{N×F} and adjacency matrix A ∈ {0,1}^{N×N}, producing low-dimensional node representations (for node classification) or graph-level representations (for graph classification via a GIN encoder + SVM). Internally, each d-dimensional representation is split into K fine-grained partitions of size m, and the discriminator additionally receives structural embeddings C ∈ R^{N×t} derived from the top-t eigenvectors of the normalized graph Laplacian, concatenated with GNN features to form fusion representations z = h ∥ c. The discriminator outputs a scalar consistency score per sample pair, thresholded at η to yield binary positive/negative labels that recalibrate the contrastive loss.

## Method

CaliGCL introduces two complementary debiasing mechanisms. First, an exponential partitioned similarity measure splits each representation into K partitions, computes per-partition inner products, and applies exp(K·s_k/τ) before averaging, which provably upper-bounds the standard exponential inner product (via Jensen's inequality) while driving negatively-aligned partition contributions toward zero and amplifying positively-aligned ones. Second, a semantics-consistency discriminator (an MLP with LeakyReLU) takes the Hadamard product of fusion representations (GNN features + Laplacian eigenvector structural embeddings) and predicts whether an augmented pair retains semantic consistency, trained with Balanced Softmax loss to handle the severe positive/negative class imbalance. Training proceeds in two phases: a pre-training stage that jointly optimizes the encoder with the partitioned contrastive loss and the discriminator with balanced softmax on k-NN + homophily-defined labels, followed by an alternating fine-tuning loop where the discriminator dynamically reassigns positive/negative labels to calibrate the encoder's contrastive objective, and the improved encoder in turn refines the discriminator's discrimination capability.

## Results

On node classification (primary task), CaliGCL achieves the best mean F1 on 5 out of 6 datasets: Cora (85.87%), Citeseer (74.13%), Pubmed (87.16%), DBLP (85.32%), and Amazon Computers (90.79%), trailing only SGRL on Amazon Photo (93.72% vs. 93.83%). On graph classification, CaliGCL attains the highest accuracy on 6 of 7 datasets, including NCI1 (80.8%), DD (80.4%), MUTAG (90.4%), COLLAB (77.2%), RDT-M5K (56.5%), and IMDB-B (76.9%), with a slight loss to DRGCL on RDT-B (90.3% vs. 90.8%). The largest gains over the strongest baselines appear on DBLP (+1.2% over HomoGCL) and Computers (+0.7% over HomoGCL), suggesting the debiasing mechanism is most beneficial on larger, more complex graphs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CaliGCL outperforms all 10 node-classification baselines on 5 of 6 datasets, with the most notable margins over SGRL on DBLP (+1.20 F1) and HomoGCL on Computers (+0.70 F1); it trails only SGRL on Amazon Photo by 0.11 F1. Among contrastive baselines, GREET (85.70 on Cora) and PiGCL (73.51 on Citeseer) are the closest competitors, but CaliGCL surpasses both by 0.17 and 0.62 F1 respectively. Generative baselines S2GAE and Bandana are competitive on smaller graphs (Cora, Citeseer) but fall further behind on larger ones (DBLP: 84.08/83.93 vs. 85.32). In graph classification, CaliGCL beats the strongest augmentation-based baseline GPA on 6 of 7 datasets (e.g., COLLAB 77.2 vs. 76.2, IMDB-B 76.9 vs. 74.6) and surpasses DRGCL on all except RDT-B (90.3 vs. 90.8). The paper notes that models relying solely on cross-view positives (GCA, PiGCL) underperform those incorporating neighbor positives (NCLA, GREET, HomoGCL), validating the importance of the discriminator's calibrated supervision.

## Relation to Prior Work

CaliGCL extends the standard InfoNCE-based graph contrastive learning framework by identifying and addressing two specific failure modes—similarity estimation bias (conflicting feature elements suppressing positive-pair alignment) and semantic shift bias (augmentations flipping pair relationships)—that prior GCL work has not systematically isolated. While methods like NCLA and HomoGCL refine contrastive supervision by incorporating local neighborhood structure, CaliGCL takes a global calibration approach: the exponential partitioned similarity modifies the loss function itself, and the semantics-consistency discriminator dynamically reassigns pair labels. The discriminator design draws on adversarial/discriminative paradigms from multi-view learning, and the Balanced Softmax loss addresses the class imbalance inherent in pre-defined positive/negative pair selection.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The two-bias diagnostic framework (similarity estimation bias and semantic shift bias) provides a useful conceptual lens for understanding why standard GCL underperforms, and the exponential partitioned similarity is a simple, drop-in modification to the InfoNCE loss that practitioners could adopt. However, the partitioning mechanism is static and non-adaptive, and the discriminator's pre-training relies on k-NN in the original feature space, which limits its applicability to graph-level tasks where node features are unavailable. Open questions include whether adaptive or attention-based partition weighting could further improve performance, and whether the approach scales efficiently to very large graphs given the O(N²) discriminator complexity.
