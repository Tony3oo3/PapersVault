## Problem

Prior stylized benchmarks showed ICL learning curves qualitatively resemble those of optimal algorithms, but no work had explicitly quantified the sample complexity gap between ICL and principled learning algorithms. This paper asks to what extent ICL is optimal as a learning algorithm and whether that efficiency persists as demonstration sizes grow. The answer reveals a hidden 'technical debt': ICL's sample complexity deteriorates significantly in long context, and information-theoretic analysis shows this inefficiency is intrinsic to the ICL mechanism rather than an artifact of model scale or out-of-distribution generalization.

## Input / Output

Each prompt consists of T input-output pairs (x_t, y_t) with x_t ~ U([x_min, x_max]) and y_t = f*(x_t) + ε_t, followed by a test input x_{T+1}; the target function f* is drawn from a hierarchical distribution over Fourier feature spaces of varying implicit dimension m ∈ [M], making model selection an implicit part of the task. The transformer outputs a scalar regression prediction for y_{T+1}. Evaluation spans 9 scenarios varying signal-to-noise ratio (σ_w² ∈ {0.1, 1, 10}, σ_ε² ∈ {0.003, 0.03, 0.3}), with 512 independent prompt samples per scenario to ensure statistical significance.

## Method

The paper introduces a performance-profile benchmarking framework (adapted from optimization software evaluation) that compares ICL's sample complexity against principled baselines — Bayesian model averaging (BMA), AIC, BIC, and Bayesian model comparison (BMC) — using mean performance ratios and performance profiles across multiple SNR scenarios. Empirically, a GPT-2 transformer (12 layers, 8 heads, 256-dim) is trained on the meta ICL regression task with T_train = 50 demonstrations and evaluated up to T = 100. Theoretically, the paper decomposes ICL error into Bayes risk and excess risk, then proves (Theorem 4.2) that under a non-vanishing excess risk assumption, ICL's suboptimality is lower-bounded by the number of additional demonstrations needed for conditional mutual information to exceed the excess risk floor. Theorem 4.3 further shows that non-deteriorating suboptimality requires either negligible excess risk or negligible diminishing returns in per-demonstration information gain — conditions that rarely hold in practice, establishing the inefficiency as intrinsic to the ICL paradigm across architectures (verified with Mamba and LSTM).

## Results

ICL achieves near-optimal sample complexity in the few-shot regime, with a mean performance ratio (MPR) of at most 1.1 relative to the Bayes optimal estimator (BMA) for performance quantiles Q ≤ 0.3 (average sample complexity ≈ 19 demonstrations). However, ICL's efficiency deteriorates sharply in the many-shot regime: the MPR grows to ~1.2 at Q = 0.7 and ~1.45 at Q = 0.99, meaning many-shot ICL requires roughly 1.5× more demonstrations than BMA to reach the same performance. In the worst case (Q ≥ 0.8), even 3× more demonstrations than the best principled baseline fail to meet the performance requirement in ~20% of scenarios. ICL's L² distance to BMA plateaus after a small number of demonstrations, indicating a lack of asymptotic consistency and efficiency.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks ICL (a GPT-2 transformer trained via meta-ICL) against four principled baselines: BMA (Bayesian Model Averaging, the Bayes-optimal predictor), AIC, BIC, and BMC (Bayesian Model Comparison). In the few-shot regime (Q ≤ 0.3), ICL dominates all single-model-selection baselines, achieving a perfect performance profile at τ = 1 (i.e., it is the most efficient method in every scenario), while AIC, BIC, and BMC struggle due to high model-selection uncertainty with few demonstrations. In the many-shot regime (Q ≥ 0.7), the picture reverses: ICL's performance ratio against BMA grows to ~1.45, and its performance profile at τ = 3 drops to ~0.8 for Q ≥ 0.8, whereas all principled baselines (especially BIC and BMC, which are consistent estimators) achieve near-perfect profiles and their L² distance to BMA continues to decrease. ICL thus wins decisively in few-shot settings but loses to principled model-selection methods in many-shot settings, revealing a fundamental sample-complexity inefficiency intrinsic to the ICL mechanism.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the meta ICL framework of Garg et al. and follow-up works that demonstrated transformers can learn in-context with error curves qualitatively resembling optimal learners, and on information-theoretic analyses of ICL (Jeon et al., Zhang et al., Bai et al.) that characterized asymptotic behavior. It departs from this lineage by introducing a sample-complexity-based benchmarking framework (adapted from optimization performance profiles) that explicitly quantifies the optimality gap between ICL and principled algorithms, rather than relying on qualitative learning-curve comparisons. The information-theoretic decomposition of ICL error into Bayes risk and excess risk extends the Bayesian viewpoint of prior work to derive a lower bound on suboptimality that is independent of specific model or environment instantiations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that ICL's sample efficiency deteriorates by ~50% in many-shot regimes relative to the Bayes optimal estimator, and that this is an inherent property of the ICL mechanism (not fixable by scaling model size or pretraining length), challenges the prevailing narrative that ICL can serve as a universal replacement for task-specific models. The paper motivates a new generation of 'on-the-fly' adaptive methods that reduce excess risk with more demonstrations while preserving ICL's update-free nature. Key open questions include whether the non-vanishing excess risk can be fundamentally eliminated by new architectures or training objectives, and how these stylized-setting findings translate to the heterogeneous, multi-task pretraining distributions of modern LLMs.
