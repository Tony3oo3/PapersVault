## Problem

While chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning has become a standard technique for improving LLM performance on complex tasks, its impact on structured, constraint-based tasks like instruction following remained unexplored. This paper reveals that explicit CoT prompting consistently degrades instruction-following accuracy across 20+ models on both IFEval and ComplexBench, a previously overlooked failure mode in which reasoning diverts model focus away from simple mechanical constraints.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252612/cropped_page7_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes text instructions containing one to multiple rule-verifiable constraints (e.g., word-count limits, formatting rules, keyword frequency) from IFEval or ComplexBench, optionally augmented with few-shot exemplars or a prior reasoning trace plus candidate answer for self-reflection. Outputs are scored by rule-based constraint satisfaction, and the interpretability analysis additionally produces per-token constraint-attention traces that quantify how much attention the model directs toward constraint-relevant substrings of the instruction at each generation step and layer.

## Method

The paper introduces a constraint-attention metric that extracts constraint substrings from instructions via GPT-4o, maps them to token indices, and computes the average attention weight directed at those tokens across all heads, layers, and answer-phase generation steps, revealing that CoT reasoning systematically flattens and reduces constraint attention during the answer phase. A large-scale manual case study categorizes reasoning's effects into four recurring patterns: it helps with formatting/structural adherence and lexical precision, but hurts by over-focusing on high-level content at the expense of simple constraints and by inserting well-intentioned content that violates constraints. To mitigate these failures, the paper proposes four strategies—few-shot in-context learning, self-reflection, self-selective reasoning (the model autonomously decides whether to reason), and classifier-selective reasoning (a per-model binary classifier on a Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct backbone predicts whether CoT helps for a given instruction). Classifier-selective reasoning achieves the most consistent recovery, improving nearly all models on both benchmarks by 5–17 percentage points over the CoT baseline, while self-reflection is particularly effective for larger models on simpler instructions.

## Results

Across 14 models evaluated on IFEval, 13 experience performance degradation when CoT prompting is applied (e.g., Llama-3-8B-Instruct drops from 75.2% to 59.0%, a 16.2 pp loss), and all models show declines on ComplexBench. Among four mitigation strategies, Classifier-Selective Reasoning delivers the most consistent recovery, improving nearly all models on both benchmarks (e.g., Llama-3-8B recovers to 69.7%, +10.7 pp over CoT; Qwen2.5-7B reaches 68.8%, +11.1 pp). Self-Reflection achieves the best IFEval results for 7 of 14 models, while Self-Selective Reasoning is strongest on ComplexBench for 6 models. Monte Carlo cross-validation (3 random hold-outs) confirms robustness, with paired t-tests yielding p = 6.3×10⁻⁵ (IFEval) and p = 2.1×10⁻⁶ (ComplexBench) for Classifier-Selective Reasoning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between no-reasoning (Original) and CoT prompting, revealing that CoT degrades instruction-following for 13/14 models on IFEval and all models on ComplexBench. Among the four proposed mitigation strategies, Classifier-Selective Reasoning (a trained binary classifier deciding when to apply CoT) achieves the best overall performance, winning on ~half of models across both benchmarks and recovering up to 10.7 pp on IFEval (Llama-3-8B) and 10.9 pp on ComplexBench (Qwen2.5-7B). Self-Reflection is strongest on IFEval for 7 models (e.g., Qwen2.5-7B: 74.3% vs. 57.7% CoT) but degrades performance on 10/14 models on ComplexBench, indicating it suits simpler constraints. Self-Selective Reasoning (model decides whether to reason) improves all models on ComplexBench and 10/14 on IFEval but suffers from low precision (over-applying reasoning). Few-Shot In-Context Learning yields only modest gains and sometimes large drops (e.g., Llama-3.1-70B: 44.5%, −32.7 pp vs. CoT), likely due to token-length limits and example bias. Additionally, paired base-vs-reasoning model comparisons (Table 2) confirm that reasoning-tuned variants (e.g., DeepSeek-R1, Qwen3-Think) consistently underperform their non-reasoning counterparts on instruction-following.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new empirical ground at the intersection of chain-of-thought prompting research and instruction-following evaluation. While prior work (Kambhampati et al., 2024; Sprague et al., 2024) noted that CoT yields limited or no gains in certain contexts, no prior study had systematically identified instruction-following as a domain where explicit reasoning actively and consistently degrades performance across model families. The paper repurposes standard transformer attention analysis as a diagnostic tool, introducing a 'constraint attention' metric to provide a mechanistic explanation for the observed drops, and positions its four mitigation strategies (in-context learning, self-reflection, self-selective and classifier-selective reasoning) as the first targeted remedies for this specific failure mode.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As reasoning-enabled models (DeepSeek-R1, Claude Think, OpenAI o-series) become the default deployment choice, this finding has immediate practical consequences: practitioners may be silently sacrificing instruction-adherence reliability for tasks that don't require deep reasoning. The classifier-selective reasoning mitigation offers a concrete, deployable fix, though it requires per-model training. Key limitations include the restriction to rule-verifiable instruction-following tasks (IFEval, ComplexBench) and the open question of whether the same attention-diversion mechanism degrades other structured tasks such as format compliance, code generation with style constraints, or multi-turn dialogue adherence. The paper also leaves open whether training-time interventions (e.g., RL with constraint-aware rewards) could internalize selective reasoning without external classifiers.
