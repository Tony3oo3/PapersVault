## Problem

Existing adversarial attacks on VLMs either require impractical white-box access to fine-tuned LLM modules or disrupt most of the image's semantic content, causing VLM-based embodied decision-making (EDM) systems to output invalid error messages rather than valid but incorrect decisions. Because EDM systems rely on chain-of-thought reasoning that cross-references perceived inputs with system prompts, wholesale semantic disruption creates a conflict that interrupts the reasoning process entirely, failing to alter the physical entity's actions. The paper asks how to craft perturbations that selectively modify only a target object's perceived semantics while preserving the rest of the scene, so the VLM produces coherent but wrong decisions that actually change the agent's behavior.

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image, a text description of the target object (for removal) or a reference image generated by a text-to-image model containing only the target object (for addition), and the victim VLM's pre-trained vision-text encoder as a surrogate (gray-box setting). It produces a single adversarial image whose L2 perturbation is bounded by ε=8/255, imperceptible to human observers. The attack is evaluated by whether the VLM's textual output (a driving decision, a manipulation plan, or an image caption) reflects the intended semantic change for the target object while remaining structurally valid and consistent with the system prompt.

## Method

ADVEDM introduces two complementary mechanisms for fine-grained control. First, a cross-modality embedding similarity strategy identifies target regions by computing cosine similarity between ViT patch token embeddings and the target object's text embedding, selecting the top-20% most similar patches for removal (ADVEDM-R) or contiguous low-similarity background patches for injection (ADVEDM-A). Second, an attention-patch fixation loss preserves non-target semantics by constraining the product of attention weights and patch token embeddings in the adversarial example to match those of the clean image across all attention layers. ADVEDM-R removes target semantics globally (pushing the [CLS] token away from the target text embedding) and locally (aligning masked patch embeddings with a zero-pixel masked image), while ADVEDM-A injects semantics by aligning the [CLS] token with a weighted fusion of clean and reference-image [CLS] tokens and reallocating attention weights to the injected patches using a reference image's attention map. Both variants optimize three weighted loss terms (global, local, fixation) over 500 Adam iterations, achieving 70–80% attack success rates in EDM tasks with 65–75% semantic preservation, far outperforming baselines that achieve <25% preservation.

## Results

On MS-COCO (image description), ADVEDM-R achieves 76.8% ASR with 66.7% SPR and 0.683 SS, while ADVEDM-A achieves 70.2% ASR with 71.6% SPR and 0.726 SS, dramatically outperforming baselines on semantic preservation (SPR >66% vs. <20% for PGD, MF-it, MF-ii). On the Dolphins Benchmark for autonomous driving, ADVEDM-R and ADVEDM-A reach average ASR of 74.5% and 71.5% respectively, compared to 17.3% (PGD), 16.5% (MF-it), and 14.8% (MF-ii). On DriveLM-nuScenes, the average ASR further increases to 80.8% and 76.3%. In robotic manipulation, ADVEDM-R and ADVEDM-A achieve 68.8% and 64.5% ASR versus 11.5%, 12.5%, and 9.0% for the three baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two families of gray-box attacks: CLIP-based PGD (Cui et al., CVPR 2024) and MF-it/MF-ii (Zhao et al., NeurIPS 2024). On MS-COCO, ADVEDM-R and ADVEDM-A sacrifice a modest amount of ASR (76.8%/70.2% vs. 83.1%/85.2% for MF-it/MF-ii) but achieve 4–6× higher SPR (66.7%/71.6% vs. 14.8%/11.3%), confirming fine-grained control that existing attacks lack. On the Dolphins Benchmark (autonomous driving), ADVEDM-R outperforms PGD by 57.2 percentage points in ASR (74.5% vs. 17.3%) and MF-ii by 59.7 points (74.5% vs. 14.8%), with SS roughly doubled (0.501 vs. 0.258–0.270). On DriveLM-nuScenes, the gap widens further: ADVEDM-R reaches 80.8% ASR versus 19.5% (PGD) and 14.3% (MF-ii). In robotic manipulation, ADVEDM-R achieves 68.8% ASR compared to 11.5% (PGD), 12.5% (MF-it), and 9.0% (MF-ii), a 5–7× improvement, while also maintaining SS of 0.451 versus 0.115–0.131 for baselines. The consistent pattern across all four evaluation settings is that existing attacks produce invalid outputs (low SS, low SPR) that fail to influence physical-world actions, whereas ADVEDM produces valid-but-incorrect decisions that actually alter agent behavior.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the gray-box adversarial attack line on VLMs (CLIP-based PGD, MF-it/MF-ii, VT-Attack) by introducing a fine-grained objective: instead of disrupting the overall image embedding, it selectively removes or adds the semantics of a single target object while preserving all other regions. The key technical departure is the attention-patch fixation mechanism, which maintains the product of attention weights and patch embeddings for non-target regions, and the cross-modality embedding similarity strategy for identifying which patches carry the target object's semantics. Conceptually, the paper reframes the attack goal from 'make the VLM perceive a different image' to 'make the VLM's chain-of-thought reasoning produce a valid but incorrect decision,' which requires preserving the structural integrity of the CoT rather than breaking it.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals that the most dangerous adversarial threat to VLM-based embodied agents is not making them fail (error messages) but making them confidently execute wrong actions in the physical world, a distinction with direct safety implications for autonomous driving and robotic manipulation. Limitations include the restriction to object presence/absence semantics (spatial relations and object interactions are unaddressed), the digital-domain-only attack vector (no physical adversarial patches demonstrated), and reliance on the gray-box assumption that the vision encoder is accessible. Open questions include whether fine-grained attacks generalize to more complex semantic modifications, whether physical-world deployment is feasible, and what defenses (if any) can detect such targeted semantic perturbations without degrading normal performance.
