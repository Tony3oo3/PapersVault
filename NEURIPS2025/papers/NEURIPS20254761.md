## Problem

After adaptive experiments (e.g., multi-armed bandit designs), standard hypothesis tests and confidence intervals lose their nominal error guarantees due to dependence across observations. Existing fixes either enforce asymptotic normality via reweighting (requiring conditional positivity, which excludes popular designs like ETC and UCB) or use anytime-valid martingale concentration inequalities that are underpowered because they protect against all possible sampling schemes rather than the specific design used. The paper addresses the gap of providing both valid type I error control and tight confidence intervals across a wide class of adaptive designs, including those that violate positivity.

## Input / Output

The method consumes the full observed experiment trajectory H_T (a sequence of arm selections A_t and outcomes X_t), the known sampling policy π (required to resimulate the adaptive allocation), a point null value θ_0, and a simulation budget B. It outputs a binary reject/accept decision with a p-value for point nulls, and by inverting the test over a grid of null values Θ_0, produces a confidence interval and a heuristic point estimate (the null whose simulated CDF value is closest to 0.5). The sampling policy must be fully specified (not just estimated), and arm outcome distributions are assumed to have finite variance but need not be Gaussian.

## Method

The core contribution is 'simulation with optimism': a generative procedure that resimulates the entire experiment under the null hypothesis θ=θ_0, but adds a positive bias ε_a to the estimated means of all non-target arms when generating simulated trajectories. The bias is chosen on the order of √(log log N_T(a) / N_T(a)), derived from the law of iterated logarithm, ensuring the simulated test statistic distribution is stochastically larger than the true one, which preserves type I error control. The key insight is that in adaptive designs, inflating nuisance arm means causes the policy to allocate fewer samples to the target arm, widening the simulated quantiles and providing the necessary conservatism. This avoids conditional positivity (unlike reweighting/MCLT approaches) and avoids the over-conservatism of anytime-valid methods (which protect against all designs). The approach is proven to maintain asymptotic type I error control for ETC, UCB, and clipped reward-maximizing designs, and empirically reduces confidence interval widths by up to 50% relative to baselines, with the largest gains for undersampled arms.

## Results

Across synthetic experiments (T = 200–1600, K = 3 Bernoulli arms, α = 0.1), the simulation-based approach achieves ~90% coverage (matching the two MCLT-based asymptotic baselines) while producing the tightest confidence intervals and lowest MSE across all signal settings. The largest gains appear for the worst-performing arm, where average CI width is reduced by up to 50% relative to the next-best method. On the real-world Offer-Westort et al. MTurk dataset (T = 1000, batched Thompson sampling), the three lowest-support proposals see CI width reductions of up to ~50% relative to the intervals reported in the original paper, while the best-performing arm's interval widens by at most 0.059.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the anytime-valid betting baseline (Waudby-Smith et al. [31]), the simulation method matches coverage (0.88 vs. 0.88) while reducing CI width by ~32% on the worst arm (0.21 vs. 0.31) and by ~43% on the best arm (0.13 vs. 0.23). Against the MCLT-based reweighting baselines from Hadad et al. [13] and Zhang et al. [36] (stable weights and two-point weights), the simulation method achieves substantially higher coverage (0.88 vs. 0.69 for both MCLT variants on the worst arm) while producing comparable or tighter CI widths (0.21 vs. 0.18/0.21), indicating the MCLT baselines suffer from significant undercoverage. On the real-world MTurk data, the simulation approach outperforms the intervals reported by Offer-Westort et al. [23] for the three lowest-support proposals with up to ~50% CI width reductions, though it incurs a small width increase (≤ 0.059) on the best-performing arm. Overall, the simulation method is never the widest interval among all approaches and dominates on the undersampled arms where existing methods are most conservative.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the bootstrap-based inference approach of Rosenberger and Hu [26] (limited to two-arm Bernoulli adaptive designs) to a general simulation framework handling multiple arms, nonparametric distributions, and a broad class of bandit algorithms including ETC, UCB, and clipped Thompson sampling. It departs from the two dominant prior paradigms—reweighing/MCLT methods (Hadad et al. [13], Zhang et al. [36]) that require conditional positivity and thus exclude popular designs like ETC and UCB, and anytime-valid inference (Howard et al. [15], Waudby-Smith et al. [32]) that protects against all possible sampling schemes and is consequently underpowered. The key conceptual departure is 'simulation with optimism': adding a law-of-iterated-logarithm-scale positive bias to nuisance arm means during trajectory resimulation, which the paper shows is necessary because even parametric-rate (Θ(1/√T)) plug-in nuisance estimates fail to reproduce the correct limiting distribution of the test statistic under adaptive sampling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper fills a practical gap: experimenters using ETC, UCB, or clipped Thompson sampling (extremely common in political science, digital health, and A/B testing) previously had no valid inference method that was both design-specific and well-powered. The 50% CI width reduction for undersampled arms is practically significant for settings where data collection is expensive. Limitations include: guarantees only for three specific design classes (Examples 1–3), a Gaussian noise assumption in the simulation step, asymptotic (not finite-sample) guarantees, and the requirement that the sampling policy be fully known. Open questions flagged by the authors include extending to contextual bandits, sequential/online testing, and designs outside the covered classes—though the assumption-violation experiments (Tables 2–3) suggest the method retains reasonable performance even outside its theoretical scope.
