## Problem

Foundation models trained on de-identified electronic health records may memorize and expose patient-level information, yet no standardized framework exists to evaluate memorization risk in EHR foundation models trained on structured medical codes. Prior memorization studies focus on free-text clinical notes or general LLMs, leaving open how to distinguish harmful patient-level memorization from beneficial population-level clinical generalization in the structured, temporal EHR setting.

## Input / Output

The framework operates on a black-box EHR foundation model (EHRMamba2) accessed solely through prompting with sequences of ICD-10 diagnosis codes, procedure/medication/lab codes, temporal gap tokens, and demographic attributes (age, sex). It produces a suite of quantitative signals: a time-weighted Earth Mover's Distance score comparing generated trajectories to ground truth, per-patient probabilities of generating sensitive ICD-10 codes (infectious disease, substance abuse, mental health), membership inference detection scores, perturbation sensitivity deltas when personal identifiers are altered, and subgroup-level risk assessments for rare-condition and elderly cohorts.

## Method

The paper introduces six black-box tests (T1–T6) organized into memorization measurement (T1–T4) and privacy risk assessment (T5–T6). T1 maps EHR codes to clinically meaningful embeddings via MedBERT and computes a time-weighted Earth Mover's Distance between generated and ground-truth trajectories, penalizing temporal misalignment. T2 removes sensitive ICD-10 codes from the prompt and measures the probability the model regenerates them, flagging cases where sensitive diagnoses appear despite absent clinical cues. T3 trains linear probes on frozen model embeddings to detect whether sensitive attributes are recoverable, and T4 estimates membership scores from log-probabilities of least probable tokens. T5 perturbs personal identifiers (e.g., age) in prompts that triggered sensitive predictions, using sharp drops in generation probability as evidence of individual-level memorization versus generalization. T6 extends the sensitivity test to subgroups defined by rare diagnoses or extreme age, amplifying risk for long-tailed, more identifiable cohorts. The key design principle is contextual: a prediction is flagged as memorization only when it cannot be explained by clinically meaningful generalization, grounding the evaluation in contextual integrity rather than raw information leakage.

## Results

On EHRMamba2 evaluated over MIMIC-IV, the sensitivity test (T2) shows that with only demographic prompts (Static), the model produces zero sensitive predictions across all three categories (infectious disease, substance abuse, mental health), with AUROC near 0.5–0.6. As prompt length increases to 50 codes, AUROC rises to 0.742 (infectious), 0.751 (substance abuse), and 0.724 (mental health), yielding 23, 162, and 202 positive predictions respectively. Embedding-based probing (T3) yields AUROC ≈ 0.5 across all sensitive attributes regardless of training data fraction, indicating no extractable memorization signal from embeddings. Membership inference (T4) shows a slight but statistically non-significant separation between member and non-member detection scores.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper is an evaluation framework rather than a method comparison, so it does not benchmark against competing memorization-detection approaches. Instead, it assesses a single model (EHRMamba2) under escalating levels of adversarial information. The key finding is a clear gradient: minimal demographic knowledge (age, sex) is insufficient to trigger sensitive leakage (0 positive predictions), but providing 50 EHR codes increases the probability of revealing sensitive attributes substantially (e.g., 162 substance-abuse and 202 mental-health positive predictions at a 30% sampling threshold). The perturbation test (T5) further distinguishes generalization from memorization: predictions that remain stable across age perturbations reflect learned population-level patterns, while sharp drops when a single identifier changes indicate patient-specific memorization. The sub-population test (T6) flags two rare-diagnosis cases (sedative dependence, mild manic episode) where the model assigns high likelihood to sensitive codes despite minimal clinical context, suggesting potential memorization in long-tailed subgroups.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the memorization evaluation literature from LLMs (Carlini et al., 2023; Shi et al., 2024) to structured EHR foundation models, adapting trajectory-sampling strategies and membership inference scoring to sequences of ICD-10 codes and temporal tokens. It departs from prior work by introducing a contextual integrity lens: rather than treating any data extraction as a privacy failure, the framework distinguishes harmful patient-level memorization from benign population-level generalization (e.g., predicting HIV from a low CD8 count is clinical reasoning, not memorization). The probing methodology builds on Alain & Bengio's linear classifier probes, repurposed to test whether EHR-FM embeddings encode sensitive diagnoses. The paper explicitly notes that no prior work has defined or systematically evaluated memorization in EHR-FMs, positioning this as the first such framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As EHR foundation models move toward clinical deployment for documentation and decision support, the absence of standardized privacy evaluation tools is a significant gap; this paper provides a practical, reproducible six-test suite that developers can run before public release. Key limitations include evaluation on a single model (EHRMamba2) and a single dataset (MIMIC-IV), with only age as a demographic variable, limiting generalizability to other architectures and richer de-identification schemes. Open questions include whether the perturbation and sub-population tests scale to multi-institutional models, how mitigation strategies (differential privacy, deduplication, safety layers) interact with these tests, and whether the sensitive-attribute taxonomy transfers across healthcare systems and regulatory regimes.
