## Problem

Hallucinations in large audio-language models (LALMs) remain largely unexplored compared to their visual and textual counterparts, despite growing deployment in virtual assistants and accessibility tools. Audio data uniquely combines the multi-attribute complexity of visual signals with the semantic richness of text, producing hallucination patterns—such as homophone confusion, prosodic misinterpretation, and semantic-acoustic conflicts—that cannot be captured by existing visual or textual hallucination taxonomies. No comprehensive benchmark existed to systematically evaluate these distinct auditory hallucination types across LALMs.

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance pairs an audio sample (speech, sound, or music) with one or more binary yes/no questions targeting a specific hallucination type. Model responses are preprocessed by GPT-4o into Yes/No/Unknown categories, and the primary accuracy metric requires all questions associated with a single audio instance to be answered correctly—a stricter criterion than per-question accuracy. The benchmark also produces a Yes/No Bias Score (FPR minus FNR) to detect response tendencies and a Diff metric quantifying logical inconsistency across paired questions, with 16 sampling runs per question to ensure robustness against LLM stochasticity.

## Method

AHa-Bench introduces a taxonomy of 14 audio hallucination types organized into three categories: semantic (homophone, polysemy, prosodic, instruction, knowledge), acoustic (existence, source number, distance, duration, temporal, repetition, authenticity), and semantic-acoustic confusion (inferential, overreliance). The dataset comprises 396 audio samples and 906 human-annotated QA pairs, with semantic hallucination samples synthesized via CosyVoice TTS and acoustic samples drawn from AudioSet, VocalSketch, and Comp-A test sets to avoid training data contamination. The evaluation framework employs a strict instance-level accuracy metric (all questions must be correct), a Yes/No bias score defined as FPR minus FNR, and a Diff consistency metric measuring the proportion of instances with partially correct responses. Seven open-source LALMs and two closed-source models are evaluated, revealing that no model exceeds 45% mean accuracy and that joint semantic-acoustic understanding remains the most challenging dimension across all architectures.

## Results

On AHa-Bench (396 audio samples, 906 QA pairs across 14 hallucination types), the best closed-source model Gemini-2.5-Pro achieves a mean accuracy of 45.00%, substantially outperforming all open-source LALMs. Among open-source models, Kimi-Audio leads with 23.94% mean accuracy, followed by Qwen-Audio (22.46%) and Qwen2-Audio-Instruct (20.73%). Notably, several models perform at or below random chance (19.36%): SALMONN scores only 7.76% and GLM4-Voice 16.42%, indicating severe hallucination vulnerabilities. The largest gap between open- and closed-source models appears in Knowledge Hallucination (81.25% vs. 59.38%) and Inferential Hallucination from acoustic cues (78.57% vs. 20.83%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among open-source LALMs, Kimi-Audio (23.94% mean) outperforms Qwen-Audio (22.46%), Qwen2-Audio-Instruct (20.73%), Qwen2-Audio (16.15%), GLM4-Voice (16.42%), and SALMONN (7.76%), with its strongest advantage in Instruction Hallucination (90.00% vs. 75.00% for Qwen2-Audio-Inst) and Overreliance Hallucination (53.91% vs. 43.75%). The closed-source GPT-Audio (25.36%) edges out all open-source models, primarily driven by superior Source Number (39.47%) and Authenticity (28.75%) scores, but is far surpassed by Gemini-2.5-Pro (45.00%), which leads in 12 of 14 hallucination categories. SALMONN and GLM4-Voice both fall below the random-chance baseline (19.36%), with SALMONN scoring 0% on six acoustic hallucination types, suggesting a fundamental inability to process acoustic attributes. Qwen-Audio, despite a lower mean than Kimi-Audio, shows the most balanced performance across categories and the lowest Yes/No bias (mean |Bias| = 0.16), indicating more calibrated responses.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends hallucination evaluation from the text and vision domains (HallusionBench, POPE, Bingo) to the audio modality, adapting the binary QA format and consistency metrics from HallusionBench [21] and the FPR/FNR-based bias metric from Hardt et al. [24]. It departs from prior audio hallucination work (Kuan et al. [30], COMP-A [19], MATCH [31], AVH-Bench [46]), which focused narrowly on acoustic/object and temporal hallucinations, by introducing a 14-type taxonomy that explicitly covers semantic hallucinations (homophone, polysemy, prosody, knowledge, instruction) and semantic-acoustic confusion hallucinations (overreliance, inferential). The benchmark construction methodology—TTS-synthesized speech for semantic categories and curated AudioSet/VocalSketch samples for acoustic categories—follows established data curation practices but applies them to a previously unaddressed hallucination space.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

AHa-Bench is the first comprehensive benchmark to systematically evaluate all three dimensions of audio hallucination (semantic, acoustic, semantic-acoustic confusion), filling a critical gap as LALMs are increasingly deployed in virtual assistants and accessibility tools. The finding that even the strongest model (Gemini-2.5-Pro) achieves only 45% accuracy on binary yes/no questions, and that several open-source models perform below random chance, underscores that audio hallucination remains a fundamentally unsolved problem. Key limitations include the modest dataset size (396 samples) and difficulty collecting certain categories (prosodic, distance), which limits generalizability. Open questions include whether targeted training on semantic-acoustic joint reasoning can close the gap, and how to design LALM architectures that avoid the observed trade-off between response consistency and conversational flexibility.
