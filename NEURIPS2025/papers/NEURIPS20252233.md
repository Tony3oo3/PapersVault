## Problem

Given a visibility graph G of a polygon, reconstruct the polygon's vertex coordinates — a problem that remains open in computational geometry. Standard GNN and generative approaches fail because visibility graphs contain no coordinate, distance, or neighborhood information that such models can exploit, making the combinatorial-to-geometric mapping fundamentally difficult to learn directly.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252233/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The input is a purely combinatorial N×N binary adjacency matrix (the visibility or triangulation graph) with no spatial or coordinate information. The output is an ordered set of 25 vertex locations in R² (anticlockwise) defining a simple, simply-connected polygon. Internally, the method operates on a 40×40 SDF grid as an intermediate representation, and the visibility graph is consumed via its lower-triangular entries as key/value tokens in cross-attention layers.

## Method

VisDiff introduces a signed distance function (SDF) as an intermediate representation between the combinatorial graph and the geometric polygon. A graph-conditioned U-Net diffusion model (DDIM) denoises a 40×40 SDF image, with the visibility graph injected through Spatial Transformer Cross-Attention blocks where graph tokens serve as keys/values and spatial CNN features as queries. A separate vertex extraction module then recovers polygon vertices: a PIFu-inspired encoder produces pixel-aligned (40×40×128) and global (25×512) SDF features, which feed a Polyformer-style transformer that refines contour-initialized vertices (simplified via Visvalingam to 25 points) using self-attention over vertices and cross-attention over SDF features and the graph. Training is two-stage — first the SDF diffusion is trained with MSE noise-prediction loss, then it is frozen while the vertex extraction block is trained with MSE vertex-position loss — because contour extraction and simplification are non-differentiable. A carefully curated dataset of 370K polygons (25 vertices each, rebalanced by link diameter, 20 augmentations per visibility graph) provides the supervised signal.

## Results

On the in-distribution Visibility Reconstruction task, VisDiff achieves an F1-score of 0.912, accuracy of 0.924, precision of 0.914, and recall of 0.911, outperforming all baselines by a substantial margin. The headline improvement is a 26% relative gain in F1-score over the strongest baseline (Vertex-Diffusion at 0.724). On the out-of-distribution test set, VisDiff maintains an F1-score of 0.891, again leading all baselines (best baseline: VD at 0.697). For the Visibility Recognition task, VisDiff correctly classifies 90% of valid vs. non-valid visibility graphs at an F1 threshold near the reconstruction mean.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VisDiff decisively outperforms all four baselines on the primary F1-score metric for Visibility Reconstruction: it achieves 0.912 versus Vertex-Diffusion (0.724), Conditional-VAE (0.702), MGNN (0.674), and MeshAnything (0.712), representing gains of 19–24 percentage points. The advantage is consistent across accuracy, precision, and recall, indicating that the SDF intermediate representation captures visibility structure more faithfully than direct vertex prediction or triangulation-based generation. The sole metric where a baseline edges out VisDiff is Euclidean distance (EDist), where MeshAnything (0.269) slightly beats VisDiff (0.277); however, MeshAnything frequently produces disconnected triangle sets, so its EDist is reported only for the subset of outputs forming a closed polygonal chain. On the out-of-distribution test set, VisDiff's lead widens relative to GNN (0.891 vs. 0.610 F1) while maintaining a clear advantage over the other baselines, suggesting the SDF-based approach generalizes better to unseen polygon types (star, terrain, convex-fan).

## Relation to Prior Work

VisDiff sits at the intersection of computational geometry (visibility graph reconstruction/recognition, a long-standing open problem) and modern generative modeling. It builds on the DDIM diffusion framework and Latent Diffusion architecture, but departs from prior shape-generation work (MeshAnything, PolyDiff, MeshGPT) by operating on 2D polygons conditioned on combinatorial graphs that lack any distance metric or spatial embedding. The key architectural departure is using the SDF as an intermediate representation between the graph conditioning and vertex output, drawing on PIFu-style pixel-aligned encoding and Polyformer-style vertex prediction. This contrasts with GNN-based approaches (MetricGNN, PolygonGNN) that assume an underlying metric space, and with theoretical computational geometry results that only handle restricted polygon classes (spiral, terrain, convex fan).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that generative diffusion models can learn the continuous manifold of polygons parameterized by their combinatorial structure, offering a practical ML approach to problems that remain open in theoretical computational geometry. The publicly released dataset (370K polygons, uniform in link diameter) is significantly more diverse than existing real-world benchmarks (MNIST, COCO) in visibility graph properties, enabling future research. Key limitations include the grid-based SDF representation that limits scalability beyond 25 vertices, imperfect reconstruction accuracy (~91% F1), and the narrow scope to simple, simply-connected 2D polygons. Open questions include whether the SDF intermediate representation generalizes to 3D or higher-vertex-count settings, and whether LLM-based consistency mechanisms can close the remaining accuracy gap.
