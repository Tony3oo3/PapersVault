## Problem

Different text embedding models map the same text into fundamentally incompatible vector spaces, making embeddings from one model unusable in another model's ecosystem. No prior method could translate embeddings between spaces without paired data, access to the source encoder, or a predefined set of candidate matches. This paper asks whether the universal latent structure conjectured by the Platonic Representation Hypothesis can be learned constructively and exploited for unsupervised cross-model embedding translation.

## Input / Output

The method consumes two disjoint sets of unpaired embedding vectors (e.g., 1M 64-token sequences each) generated by two different encoders on different texts, with no access to the source encoder, its training data, or any document-level correspondence. It outputs translated vectors in the target model's embedding space that preserve pairwise geometry and underlying semantics, enabling downstream tasks like zero-shot attribute inference and text inversion. The approach generalizes across architectures (T5, BERT, RoBERTa, Qwen), parameter counts (109M–4B), dimensionalities (512–2560), and even across modalities (text-only to multimodal CLIP).

## Method

vec2vec uses a modular architecture of space-specific input adapters (A₁, A₂), a shared MLP backbone with residual connections and SiLU nonlinearities (T), and space-specific output adapters (B₁, B₂), composing translation functions F₁ = B₂∘T∘A₁ and F₂ = B₁∘T∘A₂. Training solves a min-max objective combining four GAN discriminators (two at the embedding level, two at the latent level) with three generator constraints: reconstruction (round-trip fidelity), cycle-consistency (F₂∘F₁ ≈ identity), and vector-space preservation (pairwise dot-product structure in the target space). The key insight is that adversarial pressure at both the embedding and latent levels forces the shared backbone to discover a universal representation where embeddings from architecturally distinct models become nearly indistinguishable, while the VSP loss anchors the translation to the target space's geometric structure. Unlike prior correspondence or optimal-transport methods, vec2vec requires no overlapping inputs, no encoder access, and no candidate match set.

## Results

On in-distribution NQ evaluation, vec2vec achieves cosine similarity up to 0.92, top-1 accuracy of 100%, and mean rank of 1.00 on same-backbone pairs (e.g., gte→stella, gte→e5). On cross-backbone pairs (e.g., granite→gtr, gtr→gte), vec2vec reaches cosine similarity 0.75–0.87, top-1 accuracy 0.84–0.99, and mean rank 1.02–2.88, while baselines perform at or near random (rank ≈ 4096 out of 8192). Out-of-distribution generalization to tweets (TweetTopic) and medical records (MIMIC) preserves high cosine similarity (0.67–0.91) and strong top-1 accuracy. For information extraction, zero-shot attribute inference on translated embeddings outperforms the naive baseline and often exceeds the ideal same-space oracle, and zero-shot inversion recovers sensitive content from up to 80% of Enron emails and 67% of tweets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares vec2vec against two baselines: a Naive baseline (F(x)=x, measuring raw geometric overlap between spaces) and an Oracle-aided Optimal Transport baseline (solving the assignment problem with Hungarian, EMD, Sinkhorn, or Gromov-Wasserstein, assuming the candidate target set is known). On cross-backbone pairs (e.g., gte→gtr, granite→gtr), vec2vec dramatically outperforms both: it achieves cosine similarity 0.75–0.87 and top-1 accuracy 0.84–0.99, while the Naive baseline is near-random (cos ≈ 0, T-1 = 0, Rank ≈ 4096) and the OT baseline, despite having oracle access to targets, still fails to assign correctly (T-1 = 0.00, Rank 2664–3888). On same-backbone pairs (gte↔stella, gte↔e5, stella↔e5), all three methods perform well because the spaces are already geometrically compatible; vec2vec matches or slightly exceeds OT (e.g., stella→gte: vec2vec cos 0.92 vs OT 0.87). Critically, vec2vec is the only method that does not require knowing the candidate target set in advance, making it the only applicable approach in the security scenario of a compromised vector database. Ablations confirm each component (VSP loss, cycle-consistency, latent GAN) is necessary: removing any single component drops top-1 accuracy to 0.00 on cross-backbone pairs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the CycleGAN framework (adversarial losses + cycle consistency) from unsupervised image translation to the domain of text embedding spaces, combining it with a vector-space-preservation loss borrowed from cross-lingual word embedding alignment. It departs from prior representation-alignment work (CCA, SVCCA, CKA, ICA), which only measures similarity between spaces, by learning an actual translation function. It also goes beyond prior embedding-matching and optimal-transport methods, which assume overlapping inputs or known candidate correspondences, by requiring neither paired data nor access to the source encoder. The theoretical motivation is a constructive strengthening of the Platonic Representation Hypothesis (Huh et al., 2024), which had been conjectured for vision but not demonstrated for text.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The security implications are the most consequential: any organization storing embeddings in a vector database is potentially exposing sensitive document content to an adversary who only needs the vectors, not the encoder or the original texts. This reframes embeddings as a first-class attack surface for RAG systems, search indexes, and retrieval pipelines. Key limitations temper the results: GAN training is unstable (only 3/15 seeds converge for cross-backbone pairs), the universal-geometry claim is tested on seven models, and inversion fidelity is imperfect. Open questions include whether more stable training (e.g., diffusion-based or flow-matching objectives) can eliminate the seed sensitivity, whether the approach scales to hundreds of model families, and whether specialized defenses (embedding perturbation, quantization) can close the information-leakage gap.
