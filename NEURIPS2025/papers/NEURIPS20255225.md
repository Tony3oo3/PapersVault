## Problem

Sequential recommendation models trained on implicit feedback (clicks, skips) are systematically misaligned with users' real preferences, because a click does not guarantee satisfaction and a skip may reflect prior exposure rather than genuine dislike. Existing denoising and bias-correction methods treat these errors as random noise or known bias types, but the paper argues the mismatch is structural: users' internal satisfaction (mental reward) is a latent, stochastic signal that drives their sequential decisions yet is never directly observed by the recommender.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes the same interaction data used to train the base recommender: sequences of (user, item, action) tuples forming the expert trajectory dataset D_E, plus standard user and item features for the recommendation model. The mental reward model outputs a quantile distribution of N support values per state-action pair (rather than a single scalar reward), and the aligned recommendation model outputs a standard ranking score. A key design constraint is that the mental reward model is trained entirely offline from behavioral data—no explicit user ratings or human annotations are required—making it directly deployable in industrial pipelines where the training data for the reward model and the recommender are identical.

## Method

MTRec models the user as the active agent in a User-Centric MDP and recovers her latent reward function via a novel distributional IRL algorithm called QR-IQL. QR-IQL modifies the IQL objective by (i) replacing the initial-state term with an expectation over the expert occupancy measure and (ii) adopting a quadratic regularizer ψ(x)=x−x²/(4α), then parameterizing the Q-function with N quantile heads and optimizing them with the Pinball loss, yielding a full distribution over mental rewards rather than a point estimate. The learned mental rewards r*(s,a) are then injected as auxiliary supervision: for classification-based recommenders, an alignment loss L_Align = −E[r*(s,a^P)·F(i|h) + r*(s,a^N)·(1−F(i|h))] is added to the cross-entropy objective with weight κ; for RL-based recommenders, r* is simply added to the environment reward (r_final = r_env + κ·r_mental). This two-stage pipeline—first learn the distributional mental reward model offline, then fine-tune any existing recommender with the reward as a complementary signal—requires no architectural changes to the base model and only adds one auxiliary loss term.

## Results

On Amazon Electronics, MTRec improves NCIS by 20–45% over base models (e.g., PNN: 0.6618→0.9515, LinRec: 0.8077→0.9782) while maintaining or slightly improving AUC. On Amazon Books, NCIS gains are similarly substantial (e.g., DeepFM: 2.3146→3.7904, SIGMA: 2.3975→3.8025). On Virtual Taobao, incorporating mental rewards boosts PPO's episodic CTR from 0.5435 to 0.678 and SAC's from 0.7055 to 0.909. In an online A/B test on an industrial short-video platform, MTRec-DCN yields a 7% increase in average user viewing time over 7 days.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MTRec is evaluated as a plug-in module on eight classification-based sequential recommenders (Wide&Deep, PNN, DeepFM, SASRec, DIN, DIEN, LinRec, SIGMA) and two RL-based policies (PPO, SAC). Across all eight classification baselines on both Amazon subsets, MTRec consistently outperforms the base model on NCIS (the long-term engagement metric) by 15–45%, while AUC changes are modest (±0.001–0.012), confirming that gains come from better long-term alignment rather than short-term ranking. The distributional QR-IQL (MTRec) generally beats the non-distributional IQ-Learn (IRL) variant on NCIS (e.g., PNN Electronics: 0.9515 vs. 0.9297; DIEN Electronics: 0.9324 vs. 0.8857), though a few AUC cells are slightly lower (e.g., PNN Electronics AUC 0.8542 vs. 0.8547). On Virtual Taobao, adding mental rewards to the environment reward yields large CTR gains: PPO improves from 0.5435 to 0.678 (+24.7%) and SAC from 0.7055 to 0.909 (+28.8%). In the industrial online A/B test, MTRec-DCN outperforms the DCN baseline by 7% in average viewing time, validating the approach at scale.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) lineage from MaxEnt IRL (Ziebart et al., 2008) and IQL (Garg et al., 2021) by introducing a distributional variant (QR-IQL) that learns a quantile-parameterized reward function rather than a deterministic one, drawing on QR-DQN (Dabney et al., 2018) for the quantile regression machinery. A key conceptual departure from prior IRL-for-recommendation work is the shift from a system-centric perspective (recommender as agent) to a user-centric one (user as agent whose latent 'mental reward' is inferred from behavioral trajectories). The paper also draws an explicit analogy to LLM alignment via RLHF, but distinguishes itself by learning the reward model entirely from existing behavioral data rather than requiring human-annotated preference labels.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a well-recognized but under-theorized gap: implicit feedback (clicks, skips) systematically misrepresents true user satisfaction, and prior denoising or bias-correction approaches treat this as noise rather than a structural misalignment. The distributional formulation is a reasonable response to the stochastic nature of user preferences, and the 7% viewing-time gain on an industrial short-video platform lends practical credibility. However, the evaluation of the learned mental reward model remains indirect (correlation with step decay, counterfactual reward distributions), and the authors acknowledge the lack of a systematic benchmark for validating such latent reward functions. Open questions include whether the user-as-agent MDP assumption holds in multi-session or cross-platform settings, and whether the distributional reward captures genuine preference shifts versus unmodeled confounders.
