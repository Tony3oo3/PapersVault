## Problem

Existing fairness research in medical imaging focuses almost exclusively on diagnostic tasks (classification, segmentation), leaving prognosis scenarios—where the goal is predicting time-to-event outcomes—largely unexplored. There is no principled framework for decomposing and quantifying the distinct sources of bias in TTE prediction with medical images, and no systematic evaluation of how well current fairness algorithms mitigate such bias across diverse imaging modalities and outcomes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254447/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes right-censored TTE observations (X, Y, Δ, A) where X is a medical image (2D fundus or chest X-ray, or 3D brain MRI), Y is the observed time, Δ is the event indicator, and A is a sensitive attribute (age, race, or sex). It produces a survival function S(t|x) and a fairness score defined as the maximum performance gap (in C-index, AUC, or IBS) between any two demographic groups. The causal decomposition further yields five interpretable bias-source quantities—disparities in image features, PMI between target-related features and TTE, PMI between target-related features and censoring, TTE distribution, and censoring rate—computed per group pair.

## Method

The core contribution is a structural causal model (SCM) that partitions image features into target-related (X_Z) and sensitive-attribute-related (X_A) components, then decomposes the group-specific data likelihood P(y,δ|x,a) via Bayes' theorem into four multiplicative terms (two PMI terms, a TTE distribution, and a censoring rate), identifying five fundamental sources of inter-group bias. Theorem 1 upper-bounds the fairness error by a joint prediction error term η and a group distribution distance term D, showing that fairness is constrained not only by subgroup separability but also by whether the ground-truth labeling functions differ across groups. Proposition 2 proves that under covariate shift, learning a sufficient and group-invariant representation is sufficient for fair TTE prediction. The FairTTE benchmark instantiates this framework by training over 20,000 models (3 TTE architectures × 5 fairness algorithms × 3 datasets × 3 sensitive attributes × 10 seeds) using 2D EfficientNet or 3D ResNet-18 backbones with optional ImageNet/Kinetics pre-training, and evaluates both in-distribution and distribution-shift settings where unfair causal pathways present in training data are absent at test time.

## Results

Across three medical imaging datasets (MIMIC-CXR, ADNI, AREDS) and three sensitive attributes (age, race, sex), DeepHit achieves C^td ranging from 73.84% (ADNI-Sex) to 84.53% (AREDS-Race), with group performance gaps (F_C^td) ranging from 1.32 (AREDS-Sex) to 15.82 (ADNI-Sex), confirming pervasive bias. No fairness algorithm consistently reduces the F_C^td gap across all settings; for example, DI achieves the largest fairness improvement on ADNI-Age (−66.22% F_C^td) but worsens fairness on ADNI-Sex (+7.49%). The Friedman test followed by Nemenyi post-hoc test confirms that current fairness methods do not significantly outperform the unmitigated DeepHit baseline in mitigating bias overall.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates three TTE prediction backbones (DeepHit, Nnet-survival, PMF) and five fairness algorithms (SR, DI, FRL, DRO, CSA) across 18 dataset–attribute settings. DeepHit serves as the primary baseline; Nnet-survival and PMF show comparable bias levels, confirming that the fairness issue is not model-specific. Among fairness methods, DRO achieves the most consistent (though modest) fairness gains, reducing F_C^td by 4.80%–95.74% depending on the setting, but at the cost of 0.34%–6.40% accuracy loss. DI shows the largest single-setting improvement (−66.22% F_C^td on ADNI-Age) but is the least consistent, worsening fairness on ADNI-Sex (+7.49%). FRL and SR produce mixed results, sometimes increasing the gap (e.g., SR worsens F_C^td by +15.44% on AREDS-Age). CSA (post-processing) is the weakest, failing to reduce F_C^td in 4 of 8 settings. Critically, the Friedman–Nemenyi test confirms no fairness method significantly outperforms unmitigated DeepHit across all settings, and gains in fairness are typically accompanied by accuracy degradation, highlighting the unresolved fairness–utility trade-off in medical image prognosis.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three established research threads: fairness in medical imaging (which has focused almost exclusively on diagnostic tasks like classification and segmentation, e.g., MedFair, FairSeg, Chexclusion), fairness in TTE/survival prediction (which has been studied only on tabular data, e.g., Keya et al., Hu & Chen), and causal representation learning for bias mitigation (Jiang & Veitch, 2022). The authors adapt the structural causal model framework from invariant representation learning to the TTE setting, introducing a novel decomposition of the data likelihood into five distinct bias sources (image features, PMI with Y, PMI with Δ, TTE distribution, censoring rate). The benchmark itself repurposes existing TTE models (DeepHit, Nnet-survival, PMF) and fairness algorithms (DRO, DI, FRL, SR, CSA) that were previously evaluated in isolation or on different task types, bringing them together for the first time in a medical imaging prognosis context.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By establishing the first comprehensive benchmark for fair TTE prediction in medical imaging across three modalities and multiple sensitive attributes, this work provides the field with a necessary reference point and reveals that current fairness methods offer only limited, inconsistent mitigation—often at the cost of predictive accuracy. The finding that pre-training improves accuracy but not fairness, and that distribution shifts can paradoxically improve fairness by degrading one group more than another, challenges common assumptions and highlights that robust, equitable prognostic models remain an open problem. Key limitations include the restriction to non-informative right censoring and a single clinical risk, and the paper rightly notes that in real clinical settings, competing risks and informative censoring will complicate the fairness landscape considerably. Future work must distinguish between fair (biologically meaningful) and unfair (historically inequitable) causal pathways from sensitive attributes, a distinction the current group-fairness framework cannot make.
