## Problem

Post-trained LLMs, especially large reasoning models (LRMs) like DeepSeek-R1, exhibit significantly degraded safety compared to their base models, yet existing remedies are either computationally expensive (SafeChain requires 50K+ paired safe responses) or inapplicable when the base model was not safety-aligned (Safe LoRA, Safety Lock). The paper identifies the root cause: post-training does not remove safety mechanisms but masks them by over-activating domain-specific representations (e.g., long-CoT reasoning), causing the model to engage its reasoning pipeline instead of its dormant safety refusal on harmful prompts.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a post-trained LLM, a set of harmful prompts (from HarmBench), a fixed safe suffix string appended to those prompts, and domain-specific retain samples (e.g., ~1K long-CoT examples from LIMO for reasoning models, or 20K biomedical examples for medical models). It produces LoRA adapters (rank 16) on a sparse subset of layers (every 5th layer, e.g., {5,10,15,20,25,30} for a 32-layer model) that, when loaded onto the original model, restore safe refusal behavior while preserving domain accuracy. Notably, the alignment target is the internal hidden-state representations of a pruned variant of the same model rather than any external safe response text, eliminating the need to generate or curate safe outputs.

## Method

SafeReAct first constructs a 'safe' reference model M_safe by pruning reasoning-specific neurons identified via Wanda set-difference scoring (target: S1K reasoning data; retain: AdvBench safety data; p=0.3, q=0.4), which empirically restores the model's safety behavior and confirms the masking hypothesis. The core training objective minimizes the Frobenius-norm distance between the optimized model's layer-wise representations on harmful prompts and M_safe's representations on the same prompts augmented with a safe suffix, while a retain loss keeps representations on domain-specific samples close to the original model. These two losses are combined with a linear schedule that emphasizes safety alignment early and domain retention later (Eq. 5). Optimization is performed via LoRA (rank 16, α=10) on every 5th layer for 300 steps with Adam at lr=2e-5 on a single A100. Unlike Circuit Breaker, which pushes representations away from a pre-built safe-response dataset, SafeReAct aligns toward the model's own dormant safety features, making it model-agnostic and avoiding distribution mismatch with the model's native generation style.

## Results

SafeReAct achieves near-zero harmful rates (0–1%) on both JailbreakBench and AdvBench across all four evaluated LRMs (R1-8B, R1-7B, OT-7B, R1-14B), compared to 23–45% for the original models. Reasoning performance is preserved with less than 3% accuracy drop on GSM8K and MATH-500 (e.g., R1-8B: 88%→87% GSM8K, 81%→80% MATH-500). On the medical model Llama-3-8B-UltraMedical, SafeReAct reduces the JBB harmful rate from 66% to 6% while retaining 74% MedQA accuracy (vs. 77% original). The method also generalizes to a financial LLM, dropping AdvBench harmful rate from 16% to 2% and JBB from 47% to 3%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SafeReAct decisively outperforms both baselines on safety. Against Circuit-Breaker [Zou et al., NeurIPS 2024], SafeReAct is far more stable: Circuit-Breaker actually *increases* the JBB harmful rate on R1-7B (45%→47%) and barely moves OT-7B (43%→41%), while SafeReAct brings both to 0–1%. Against SafeChain [Jiang et al., ACL 2025], SafeReAct achieves substantially lower residual harmful rates (0–1% vs. 7–9% on JBB/AdvBench) and avoids SafeChain's severe reasoning degradation: SafeChain drops GSM8K accuracy by ~20% (e.g., R1-8B: 88%→63%), whereas SafeReAct loses less than 3% (88%→87%). The authors attribute Circuit-Breaker's instability to distribution mismatch between its pre-built safe-response dataset and Qwen-based models' generation style, and SafeChain's utility loss to the complexity of its 50K long-CoT safety responses interfering with reasoning mechanisms. SafeReAct's advantage stems from aligning to the model's own hidden safety representations rather than external targets, making optimization easier and less disruptive to domain capabilities.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the representation engineering line of work (Circuit Breaker, Zou et al. 2024) by changing the alignment target: rather than pushing representations toward externally defined safe responses, SafeReAct aligns toward the model's own hidden safety representations, discovered by pruning post-training-specific neurons (via Wanda set-difference pruning, building on Wei et al. 2024). It also departs from the SafeChain approach (Jiang et al. 2025), which fine-tunes on 50K+ harmful/safe response pairs, by requiring only internal representation matching on harmful prompts without any target responses. The mechanistic framing—that post-training masks rather than removes safety mechanisms—distinguishes this work from prior safety-alignment methods that treat the problem as one of insufficient training signal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As post-training (RLHF, CoT distillation, domain fine-tuning) becomes the standard pipeline for deploying LLMs, the safety degradation it introduces is a growing practical concern; SafeReAct offers a lightweight fix (LoRA rank 16 on ~6 layers, 300 steps, single A100) that achieves near-zero harmful rates while preserving domain performance within 1–3%. The 'masking not removal' finding reframes the problem: safety is recoverable from the model itself, reducing dependence on large curated safe-response datasets. Open questions include whether the approach scales beyond 32B parameters, whether it generalizes to all post-training paradigms (e.g., RL-based alignment vs. SFT), and whether the specific pruning-based discovery of safety representations is the optimal way to identify the alignment target.
