## Problem

The TinyStories dataset, while foundational for training small interpretable language models, is severely formulaic (59% of stories begin with 'Once upon a time'), unlabeled, English-only, and contains encoding artifacts and inappropriate content. This paper addresses these limitations by introducing a more diverse, fully labeled, multilingual synthetic story dataset that enables supervised fine-tuning and mechanistic interpretability research on small language models.

## Input / Output

The generation pipeline takes parameterized prompts specifying a topic, theme, writing style, narrative feature, and optionally a grammar feature or author persona, fed to GPT-4o-mini with hard constraints on each story's initial part of speech and starting letter. The output is a set of self-contained simple-language stories (averaging ~225 words) in English and Japanese, each carrying structured labels inherited from the generation parameters. The trained models (1.25M–35M parameters) consume a BOS token or short text prompt and generate short stories over a custom 4,096-token WordPiece vocabulary, roughly one-tenth the size of GPT-2's 50,257-token vocabulary.

## Method

The core contribution is a multi-level parameterized prompting strategy that simultaneously induces diversity and provides ground-truth labels: each story is conditioned on a topic, theme, style, narrative feature, and optionally a grammar feature or author persona, while the initial part of speech and letter are sampled from a reference corpus to disambiguate generations from the first token across millions of samples. Stories are produced via GPT-4o-mini with nucleus sampling (p=0.9, temperature 1), with multiple stories generated per prompt to amortize input tokens. For model training, the authors implement a custom WordPiece tokenizer (4,096 tokens) incorporating morphological affixes identified from the corpus, and train Llama-architecture transformers with AdamW and cosine decay. Ablations isolating tokenizer, architecture, and dataset effects show that while custom tokenization and the Llama architecture each contribute meaningful gains, the linguistic diversity of SimpleStories is the primary driver of the consistent outperformance over TinyStories-33M across originality, coherence, grammar, and quality metrics.

## Results

SimpleStories-35M (35M params, including embeddings) outperforms TinyStories-33M (68M params including embeddings) on all four model-as-a-judge metrics (originality, coherence, grammar, quality) when generating stories, with the largest gaps in coherence and quality. The dataset itself shows substantially greater diversity: template rate drops from 100% (TinyStories) to 88.9% (SimpleStories), template-per-token falls from 0.026 to 0.016, and the top 4-gram frequency drops from 59.4% ('once upon a time') to 7.5% ('took a deep breath'). Flesch-Kincaid reading grade is 3.08±1.24 for SimpleStories vs. 2.55±1.49 for TinyStories, confirming comparable simplicity with greater linguistic range. Label recovery by a judge model reaches 0.49 accuracy for topic and 0.90 for pairwise topic discrimination in English, validating the parameterized generation scheme.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the TinyStories dataset and its 33M-parameter model (Eldan & Li, 2023). SimpleStories-35M beats TinyStories-33M on all four generation-quality metrics (originality, coherence, grammar, quality) despite using roughly half the parameters (35M vs. 68M including embeddings). An ablation isolating tokenizer, architecture, and dataset effects shows that even after equipping TinyStories with a custom 4096-token tokenizer and a Llama architecture, all TinyStories variants remain substantially below SimpleStories-35M with its custom tokenizer, indicating dataset diversity is the primary driver of improvement. On syntactic diversity, SimpleStories achieves a template rate of 88.9% versus 100% for TinyStories and a template-per-token of 0.016 versus 0.026. On lexical diversity, SimpleStories shows lower compression ratio, lower Self-BLEU homogenization, and higher n-gram diversity scores (NGD) across all n from 1 to 10. The one area where TinyStories retains an edge is raw grammar score: the original TinyStories-33M model exhibits unexpectedly strong grammar performance nearly matching SimpleStories-35M, suggesting its training data may have over-emphasized grammatical correctness at the expense of other dimensions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends the TinyStories paradigm (Eldan & Li, 2023) of training small language models on synthetic children's stories for mechanistic interpretability, addressing two specific shortcomings: the formulaic nature of TinyStories (59% of stories begin with 'Once upon a time') and the absence of labels that would enable supervised fine-tuning or controlled analysis. The parameterized generation approach—constraining stories by topic, theme, style, narrative feature, grammar feature, and author persona—repurposes commercial LLM prompting as a data-augmentation and labeling mechanism, while the custom 4096-token WordPiece tokenizer departs from the GPT-2 tokenizer used in TinyStories to better match the limited vocabulary of simple language. The open-source training pipeline draws inspiration from OLMo, and the interpretability analyses (bilinear MLP weight inspection, linear probes) build on recent mechanistic interpretability tooling from Pearce et al. (2024) and Alain & Bengio (2018).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SimpleStories provides the interpretability community with a more diverse, labeled, and multilingual (English + Japanese) alternative to TinyStories, enabling controlled studies of how specific linguistic features (tense, aspect, narrative structure) are encoded in small models. The custom 4096-token tokenizer is a practically significant contribution: it reduces embedding parameters by over 90% relative to GPT-2's 50K vocabulary, making it easier to isolate non-embedding architectural effects in small-model research. Open questions include finding the true lower parameter limit for grammatical output, whether the parameterized generation approach generalizes to more complex or non-fiction language, and how findings from simple-story models transfer to models trained on natural corpora. The paper's own limitations—no BPE comparison, no architecture sweep beyond Llama, English-only readability metrics—leave room for follow-up work.
