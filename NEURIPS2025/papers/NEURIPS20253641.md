## Problem

State-of-the-art flow-based neural architecture encoders (e.g., FlowerFormer) capture information flow effectively but are up to 57× slower than simpler GNN-based encoders due to sequential message passing. Meanwhile, existing generative pre-training methods adapted from other domains (e.g., masked operation prediction) provide unclear training guidance for neural architectures, since unlike molecules with chemical rules, nearly any operation is a valid candidate for a masked position. The paper asks: can a simple, fast encoder learn to capture information flow through a well-designed pre-training objective without requiring a specialized model structure?

## Input / Output

The method takes a neural architecture expressed as a directed acyclic graph with one-hot encoded operation nodes and directed edges, and produces a fixed-dimensional architecture embedding via a GNN encoder (e.g., ResGatedGCN, GIN) followed by mean pooling. During pre-training, the encoder's embedding is decoded by an MLP to reconstruct a k-dimensional 'flow surrogate' vector, which is computed once per architecture by propagating random vectors through the DAG in topological order and then in reverse. After pre-training, the same encoder is fine-tuned with a margin-ranking loss to output a scalar performance prediction score for downstream tasks like NAS.

## Method

FGP (Flow-based Generative Pre-training) introduces a 'flow surrogate' as a pre-training objective: a k-dimensional vector obtained by (1) assigning a topological order to the architecture DAG, (2) propagating a shared random vector forward through nodes with operation-specific transformations (sum-pooling incoming messages, then applying a fixed ReLU projection conditioned on the node's operation embedding), and (3) repeating the process in reverse to mimic backpropagation, finally summing the resulting messages at input nodes. The encoder is trained to reconstruct this surrogate via an MLP decoder with L2 loss, optionally combined with an auxiliary zero-cost-proxy prediction objective. The key insight is that this surrogate provides unambiguous training signal (unlike masked-operation prediction where most operations are valid), and the sequential propagation through the DAG forces the encoder to internalize flow information even when its architecture (e.g., standard GNN) has no specialized flow-capturing mechanism. The surrogate is computed with random vectors and fixed matrices in a single forward-backward pass per architecture, requiring no learned parameters and only ~2 ms per architecture.

## Results

FGP outperforms all baseline pre-training methods in 23 out of 27 evaluation settings across three NAS benchmarks (NB-101, NB-201, NB-301) and three backbone encoders. The headline gain is +106% in Precision@1% on NB-101 with ResGatedGCN (37.5 vs. 18.2 without pre-training), and +64.6% on NB-201 (48.9 vs. 29.7). In Kendall's Tau, FGP achieves 82.2 on NB-201 with ResGatedGCN, surpassing the strongest baseline ZC-Proxy (79.9) by 2.3 points, and 83.6 with FlowerFormer, beating ZC-Proxy (82.3).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FGP consistently outperforms the strongest baseline, ZC-Proxy (zero-cost proxy prediction), in 14 out of 18 settings where both are applicable, with the largest margin on NB-101 Precision@1% with ResGatedGCN (37.5 vs. 26.2, +43%). Against GMAE (graph masked autoencoder), FGP wins in 16 out of 18 settings, with particularly large gains on NB-201 Precision@1% (48.9 vs. 35.1 for ResGatedGCN). FGP beats Arch2vec (variational graph autoencoder) in 17 out of 18 settings and GraphCL (graph contrastive learning) in 15 out of 18 settings. Compared to no pre-training (N/A), FGP improves Kendall's Tau by 3–21% and Precision@1% by 15–106% depending on encoder and dataset, with the most dramatic gains observed for non-flow-based encoders (ResGatedGCN, GIN) where the flow surrogate compensates for the lack of a specialized architecture. Notably, FGP also improves the already flow-aware FlowerFormer encoder by up to 8.2% in Kendall's Tau, suggesting that pre-training exposes the model to a broader range of flow patterns beyond what labeled data provides.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two threads in neural architecture encoding: (1) flow-based encoders like FlowerFormer and TA-GATEs that use specialized sequential message-passing structures to capture information flow, and (2) generative pre-training methods like GMAE (masked operations) and Arch2vec (edge reconstruction) that improve encoder generalization without targeting flow specifically. FGP's key departure is decoupling the *objective* of learning information flow from the *architecture* required to capture it: rather than building a slow flow-aware encoder, it constructs a flow surrogate (a vector obtained by propagating random vectors through the architecture graph in topological order, forward and backward) as a reconstruction target for any encoder. This repurposes the message-passing computation from flow-based encoders as a one-time data-generation step rather than a repeated inference procedure, and combines it with the zero-cost-proxy auxiliary objective from ZC-Proxy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is clear: flow-based encoders are up to 57× slower than simple GNNs, creating a bottleneck for performance-predictor-guided NAS where thousands of architectures must be scored rapidly. FGP shows that a simple GNN pre-trained with the flow surrogate can match or exceed a flow-based encoder on both accuracy and speed, which could shift the default encoder choice in NAS pipelines. Open questions include whether the flow surrogate (a single-round, random-vector simulation) captures genuinely meaningful information-flow dynamics or primarily encodes topological structure, and whether the gains persist in larger, more diverse search spaces beyond the NAS-Bench family. The paper's ablations and cross-domain experiments (NLP, graph) provide reasonable evidence of generality, but the flow surrogate's theoretical grounding remains preliminary.
