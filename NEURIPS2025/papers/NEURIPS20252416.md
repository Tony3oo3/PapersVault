## Problem

Temporal credit assignment in RL typically requires learning task-specific value functions from scratch, which is sample-inefficient and offers poor generalization. For LLM agents performing online learning with sparse environmental feedback (e.g., a single reward at episode end), this bottleneck is especially severe, as multi-turn tasks demand identifying which specific state-action pairs caused success or failure. The paper asks whether pretrained LLM knowledge can be leveraged to transform sparse rewards into dense advantage estimates without training a separate critic network.

## Input / Output

The method takes text-form observations (state descriptions) and a sparse binary reward signal as input, and additionally consumes hindsight trajectories (the sequence of future states, actions, and rewards from the current state to episode end) to condition the reflector LLM. It produces discrete actions from a finite, enumerable action space (required for exact partition-function computation), per-state advantage function estimates, and updated LLM policy parameters. The reflector generates free-form verbal feedback per state rather than a single trajectory-level critique, enabling fine-grained, state-specific credit assignment.

## Method

RICL estimates the advantage function by comparing log-probabilities of the original LLM policy and an in-context-updated version: a reflector LLM analyzes the hindsight trajectory from state s_t and generates verbal feedback, which is appended to the policy prompt to yield π′(·|s_t). Theorem 4.1 guarantees that for any two policies in an MDP, there exists a reward function whose advantage function is proportional to β·log(π′(a|s)/π₀(a|s)), so the log-probability ratio directly yields a sample estimate of the advantage. RICOL then iterates: collect trajectories with the current policy, apply RICL at every visited state to obtain dense advantage estimates, and update the policy via advantage-weighted regression with a trust-region constraint (a KL divergence between a geometric mixture of the old and in-context-updated policies and the new policy, controlled by hyperparameter α). This trust region anchors updates near the sampling policy, providing robustness to noisy verbal feedback and eliminating the need for a learned critic network.

## Results

On the 1D Key-Door environment, RICL estimates the advantage function with ~100× greater sample efficiency than Monte Carlo (achieving comparable accuracy with ~10 trajectories vs. ~1000). On four BabyAI scenarios, RICOL (using LLaMA-3.2-3B-Instruct as policy and GPT-4o-mini as reflector) outperforms PPO (10M) by over 50× fewer environment steps and PPO (3B) by over 10× fewer steps while achieving comparable or superior success rates. RICOL also surpasses GPT-4o-mini's zero-shot performance across all four scenarios despite using a smaller 3B policy model, and remains effective even when verbal feedback accuracy drops to 70%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RICOL outperforms PPO (10M, randomly initialized MLPs) by over 50× in sample efficiency, requiring roughly 1,000–10,000 environment steps versus 0.1–1 million for PPO (10M) to converge. Against PPO (3B, which uses an LLM as both policy and critic), RICOL is approximately 10× more sample-efficient, converging in far fewer steps without needing to train a separate value network. Compared to Reflexion (trajectory-level in-context learning), RICOL achieves better convergent performance by providing state-specific feedback and performing temporal credit assignment, whereas Reflexion saturates early due to its reliance on general trajectory-level rules. RICOL also surpasses GPT-4o-mini's zero-shot success rates on all four BabyAI tasks despite using a smaller 3B policy, demonstrating the value of interactive learning over mere imitation. In the credit assignment ablation (Figure 5), RICOL significantly outperforms RWR (reward-weighted regression without credit assignment), which only achieves competitive results in the goto scenario where the base policy already has a 25% success rate. Finally, in Appendix H, RICL's advantage estimation is shown to be substantially more sample-efficient than Retroformer's value-difference estimation, with MSE of 0.0454 vs. 0.2641 at 1M trajectories under Monte Carlo sampling.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Reflexion paradigm (Shinn et al., 2024) of in-context learning with verbal feedback by introducing a retrospective, state-specific update mechanism (RICL) and grounding it theoretically in KL-regularized policy optimization (Abdolmaleki et al., 2018). The policy improvement step draws directly from advantage-weighted regression (Peng et al., 2019), while the proof of Theorem 4.1 relies on the maximum entropy RL framework (Ziebart et al., 2008) to establish that any pair of policies admits a reward function whose advantage explains their log-probability ratio. The key departure from prior work is that RICL performs credit assignment at the individual state level using LLM pretrained knowledge rather than training a separate value network (as in PPO) or relying on trajectory-level sparse rewards (as in Retroformer or RWR).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a core bottleneck in training LLM agents: converting sparse environmental rewards into dense, state-level supervision signals without training a task-specific critic. The 100× sample efficiency gain over Monte Carlo for advantage estimation and the 10× improvement over PPO(3B) make this practically relevant for settings with limited interaction budgets. However, the method is restricted to discrete, enumerable action spaces (requiring partition function computation), and all empirical validation is on BabyAI-scale grid-world tasks, leaving open whether the approach transfers to continuous control, long-horizon reasoning, or token-level MDPs. The robustness to 30% noisy verbal feedback is a useful practical property, but the reliance on a separate reflector LLM (GPT-4o-mini) adds inference cost that may limit deployment.
