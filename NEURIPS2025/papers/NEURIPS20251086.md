## Problem

Prior work showed that LLM refusal behavior is mediated by a single direction in activation space, but this was demonstrated exclusively in English. It remained unknown whether refusal directions generalize across languages or are language-specific constructs, and why cross-lingual jailbreaks succeed despite shared internal representations. This paper asks: is the refusal direction universal across safety-aligned languages, and what geometric properties explain both its transferability and the persistent multilingual safety gaps?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251086/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes multilingual harmful and benign prompts (translated from English into 14 typologically diverse languages via Google Translate) and extracts refusal direction vectors from residual stream activations at specific layer-token positions using difference-in-means. Interventions are applied by projecting activations onto the refusal vector (ablation across all layers) or adding a scaled vector at a single layer, with KL divergence filtering (max 0.2) to preserve general model capability. Outputs include compliance rates measured via WildGuard (after back-translation to English), cross-lingual cosine similarity heatmaps across all decoder layers, and Silhouette scores quantifying harmful/harmless cluster separation.

## Method

The core contribution is an empirical demonstration that refusal direction vectors extracted via difference-in-means from any safety-aligned language are approximately parallel across all other languages, enabling cross-lingual jailbreaks through simple vector ablation. The paper introduces PolyRefuse, a parallel multilingual dataset of 572 harmful prompts per language, and runs three complementary experiments: ablating English-derived vectors across 14 languages, ablating vectors from three typologically diverse non-English languages (German, Chinese, Thai) across all languages, and adding vectors to confirm controllability. Geometric analysis reveals that while refusal vectors maintain high cross-lingual cosine similarity (peaking at specific layers), the separation between harmful and harmless embeddings degrades substantially in non-English languages (Silhouette scores drop from ~0.50 in English to ~0.20–0.30 elsewhere), which is identified as the root cause of cross-lingual jailbreak vulnerability. A 'jailbreak vector' (difference between bypassed and refused harmful samples) further confirms a directional structure governing compliance that can be manipulated bidirectionally across languages.

## Results

Ablating the English-derived refusal vector from the residual stream substantially increases harmful compliance across all 14 languages and all tested models (Llama3.1-8B, Qwen2.5-7B, gemma-2-9B), with even strongly aligned models like gemma-2-9B-it jumping from ~0.5% to ~87% compliance in Yoruba. More strikingly, ablating refusal vectors derived from any single safety-aligned language (Chinese, German, or Thai) causes near-complete collapse of refusal across all other languages, with compliance rates consistently approaching or exceeding 90%. Adding refusal vectors produces the inverse effect, reducing compliance to 0% for most languages. Geometrically, Silhouette Scores confirm that harmful/harmless separation is strongest in English (0.4960 for Llama3.1-8B, 0.3887 for Qwen2.5-7B, 0.3063 for gemma-2-9B) and substantially weaker in non-English languages (range 0.19–0.32), explaining persistent cross-lingual jailbreak vulnerability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper is an empirical study rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so it does not benchmark a proposed method against prior approaches. Instead, it compares three instruction-tuned models (Llama3.1-8B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, gemma-2-9B-Instruct) across 14 languages to characterize the universality of refusal directions. gemma-2-9B-it shows the strongest baseline safety (0.4% English compliance) but is still fully jailbreakable via refusal vector ablation. Llama3.1-8B-Instruct exhibits the largest English-vs-non-English separation gap (Silhouette 0.4960 vs. 0.2182–0.3165), making it the most vulnerable to cross-lingual jailbreaks. The paper further compares refusal vectors derived from English versus non-English source languages (German, Chinese, Thai, Japanese, Korean, Russian, Yoruba), finding that vectors from safety-aligned languages generalize near-universally while Yoruba-derived vectors show poor transferability, confirming that alignment quality of the source language is the critical factor. Ablation across all layers vs. single-layer addition is also compared, confirming that removal requires full-layer ablation while enhancement works at a single layer.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the refusal-direction extraction and ablation methodology established by Arditi et al. (NeurIPS 2024), Zou et al. (2025), and Wang et al. (ICLR 2025) from an English-only setting to 14 typologically diverse languages. While prior mechanistic interpretability work on refusal had identified a single direction in activation space that mediates refusal behavior, all such analyses were conducted exclusively in English, leaving the cross-lingual question entirely open. The paper also builds on the multilingual jailbreak vulnerability literature (Yong et al. 2024, Li et al. 2024a, Deng et al. 2024) by providing a mechanistic explanation for *why* cross-lingual jailbreaks succeed, rather than merely documenting their existence. The introduction of PolyRefuse, a parallel multilingual safety dataset, fills a data gap that prevented prior mechanistic studies from being extended beyond English.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a single refusal direction extracted from any safety-aligned language transfers near-perfectly to all others has direct practical implications: it means multilingual safety cannot be achieved by simply adding language-specific refusal training, because the underlying mechanism is already shared. The geometric analysis—parallel refusal vectors combined with degraded harmful/harmless clustering in non-English languages—points to a concrete and testable defense direction (improving representational separation along the refusal axis) that the authors explicitly leave as future work. Limitations include reliance on machine-translated prompts (14 languages, 7 scripts), evaluation on only 3–4 model families, and the absence of any implemented defense strategy. An open question is whether the universality finding extends to extremely low-resource languages with minimal pretraining representation, and whether the poor clustering is an artifact of the refusal extraction layer or reflects a deeper representational deficiency.
