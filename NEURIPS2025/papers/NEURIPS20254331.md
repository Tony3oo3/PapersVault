## Problem

Existing molecular property datasets and benchmarks operate at the whole-molecule level, lacking explicit links between specific functional groups and property changes. This prevents LLMs from performing the fine-grained, structure-aware reasoning that chemists use to predict how adding or removing a functional group affects a molecule's properties. No prior dataset provides precisely localized functional group annotations paired with property labels to support this type of reasoning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254331/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each question presents a molecule as SMILES with explicit atom numbering, a textual instruction specifying which functional group to add or remove at which atom positions, and the property name with its current ground-truth value. The model must output either a Boolean (True/False) indicating whether the property changes or increases, or a signed numerical value representing the exact property change. The three task dimensions differ in what information is exposed: single-FG and interaction tasks reveal the FG modification explicitly, while comparison tasks present two full molecules without FG annotations, testing whether the model can infer the relevant structural difference on its own.

## Method

FGBench is constructed from 10 MoleculeNet datasets (192,758 molecules) through a pipeline that canonicalizes SMILES, builds a Tanimoto similarity matrix on 512-bit Morgan fingerprints (threshold 0.7), and computes functional group differences with precise atom-level positions using AccFG. A key validation-by-reconstruction step removes the identified FG from the original molecule and replaces it with the counterpart FG, verifying that the resulting structure is chemically valid and structurally consistent, which also yields the positional metadata needed for question generation. From 42,967 validated molecular pairs covering 245 functional groups, the authors generate 625,936 QA pairs across three reasoning dimensions (single FG impact, multiple FG interactions, molecular comparison) and two answer types (Boolean classification and value regression) using templated instructions. The benchmark evaluates nine LLMs (GPT-4o, o3-mini, Llama-3.1 8B/70B, Qwen2.5-7B, ChemLLM-7B, nach0, Llama-3-8B-MolInst, LlaSMol-Mistral-7B) on a curated 7,146-pair subset, revealing that even the best model (o3-mini) drops from 0.687 to 0.693 accuracy between single-FG and multi-FG interaction tasks, and that chemistry-specific fine-tuned models underperform general-purpose LLMs on FG-level reasoning.

## Results

On the 7,146-sample FGBench benchmark, o3-mini achieves the best overall performance, reaching 0.687 accuracy on Single FG Impact (Boolean), 0.693 on Multiple FG Interaction (Boolean), and 0.703 on Molecular Comparison (Boolean), while also attaining the lowest RMSE on Comparison-Value (64.579). GPT-4o follows with 0.667 on Single-Boolean but drops sharply to 0.488 on Interaction-Boolean, revealing a clear difficulty with multi-FG reasoning. Among open-source models, Llama-3.1 70B (0.683 Single-Boolean) and Qwen2.5-7B (63.511 RMSE on Single-Value) are the strongest, while all chemistry-specific models (ChemLLM-7B, nach0, Llama-3-8B-MolInst, LlaSMol-Mistral-7B) underperform general-purpose LLMs across nearly all task categories.

## Baselines & Comparisons

o3-mini outperforms all other models on 4 of 6 task categories, achieving the highest Boolean accuracy on Single (0.687), Interaction (0.693), and Comparison (0.703), as well as the lowest RMSE on Comparison-Value (64.58). GPT-4o is competitive on Single-Boolean (0.667) but suffers a dramatic 18-point drop on Interaction-Boolean (0.488), indicating that even frontier models struggle with reasoning about combined effects of multiple functional groups. Llama-3.1 70B matches o3-mini closely on Single-Boolean (0.683 vs. 0.687) and achieves the second-best Interaction-Value RMSE (38.65), yet falls behind on Comparison-Boolean (0.456 vs. 0.703). Qwen2.5-7B is the strongest open-source model on Value tasks (63.51 and 36.31 RMSE) and achieves 0.664 on Comparison-Boolean, but its Interaction-Boolean accuracy (0.396) is the worst among top-tier models. All four chemistry-specific baselines underperform general LLMs: ChemLLM-7B scores only 0.233 on Single-Boolean, Llama-3-8B-MolInst achieves a near-random 0.107, and LlaSMol-Mistral-7B reaches 0.387. The apparent strength of nach0-base on Single-Boolean (0.606) is an artifact of label bias—it predicts 'False' for 97.7% of samples—while its Comparison-Boolean accuracy collapses to 0.041, confirming that molecule-level pretraining does not transfer to FG-level reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the molecular property prediction task from MoleculeNet by introducing functional group (FG)-level reasoning as a distinct problem formulation. Prior FG-aware work (FG-BERT, FARM, MoCL) treated functional groups as tokens for molecular representation learning, while existing LLM chemistry benchmarks (Mol-Instructions, SMolInstruct, ChemBench, MoleculeQA) operate at the molecule level without explicit FG annotations. FGBench departs from both lines by using AccFG for precise FG localization and a novel validation-by-reconstruction strategy to generate QA pairs that require reasoning about the effect of specific structural modifications on molecular properties, rather than predicting properties from whole-molecule inputs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that even the strongest model (o3-mini) achieves only ~0.69 accuracy on single-FG tasks and drops further on multi-FG interactions, while chemistry-specific models (ChemLLM, nach0, LlaSMol) underperform general-purpose LLMs—suggesting that molecule-level training does not build transferable FG-level reasoning. The 625K QA dataset with precise FG positions provides a foundation for developing structure-aware, interpretable LLMs for SAR analysis and molecular design. Key limitations include the absence of position isomerism, carbon chain isomerism, and stereoisomerism, and open questions center on whether retrieval-augmented approaches or new training strategies (avoiding catastrophic forgetting) can close the FG-level reasoning gap.
