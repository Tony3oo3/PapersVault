## Problem

Strategic classification studies how agents modify features to game classifiers, but prior work largely ignores causal interdependencies between features and the distinction between desirable modifications (e.g., lifestyle changes) and undesirable ones (e.g., direct medical interventions). This paper asks: under what conditions do rational agents, facing a deployed classifier and a causal graph of feature interactions, naturally invest effort in desirable features—and how does uncertainty about the classifier or causal structure alter this alignment?

## Input / Output

The agent takes as input her current feature vector, the deployed linear classifier (or a Gaussian prior over it), the causal graph topology with edge weights (or a Gaussian prior over the weights), a shortfall α indicating how far she is from the classification threshold, and per-feature cost multipliers. The method produces an optimal exogenous effort profile e* minimizing weighted ℓ_p cost while ensuring the agent passes the classifier with probability at least 1−δ, along with a β-desirability measure quantifying the fraction of effort directed at desirable features. The causal structure is encoded in a contribution matrix C that aggregates all directed paths, so the agent's score change is h^T C^T e, coupling effort on one feature to downstream changes in others.

## Method

The core mechanism models feature interactions through a weighted DAG whose contribution matrix C captures both direct and indirect causal effects, transforming the agent's optimization into a constrained problem over exogenous effort with a probability constraint on score improvement. Under complete information, the problem reduces to a convex program with a clean structural characterization: for ℓ_1 costs the agent modifies exactly one feature (the best contribution-to-cost ratio), while for ℓ_p costs (p>1) effort is spread across all features proportionally to ((Ch_0)_f / c_f)^(1/(p−1)). Under incomplete information with Gaussian priors, the problem is non-convex in general but becomes convex when uncertainty is confined to the classifier alone or to a bipartite causal graph, yielding a semi-closed-form solution for ℓ_2 costs where per-feature effort is proportional to mean importance divided by (1 + variance). The paper further proves that the space of classifiers inducing β-desirable behavior is non-convex in general but convex when only one desirable feature is targeted, and validates these insights on a cardiovascular disease risk prediction task with eight modifiable features.

## Results

The paper is primarily theoretical, providing necessary and sufficient conditions (Theorem 2) under which strategic agents' best responses are β-desirable, and showing that the space of desirable classifiers is non-convex in general but convex when |D|=1 (Theorem 3). In the numerical CVD experiments (Section 5), all four single-feature classifiers (DM, HPL, HPT, Obesity) incentivize greater effort on desirable lifestyle features than on undesirable medical features, with ℓ₂(D) ranging from 1.23 (HPL) to 1.58 (HPT) versus ℓ₂(U)=1. Under low-to-moderate uncertainty (σ), all classifiers achieve β>0.5, and the effectiveness ranking HPT > Obesity > DM > HPL matches the ℓ₂(D) ordering. β degrades monotonically as uncertainty σ increases, improves as the failure probability δ increases, and is invariant to the shortfall α under ℓ₂ costs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing methods or baselines in the standard empirical sense; it is a theoretical contribution with illustrative numerical experiments. The closest prior work is Kleinberg & Raghavan (2020), which studies causal strategic classification but in a regression/scoring setting with linear costs and complete information only. The present paper extends that framework to binary classification, general ℓ_p costs, and incomplete information (Gaussian priors over the classifier and/or causal graph weights). The numerical experiments in Section 5 serve to validate the theoretical predictions (particularly Corollaries 1–2 and Theorem 5) rather than to benchmark against alternative classifier-design approaches. The key empirical finding—that desirable lifestyle features can be incentivized even when the classifier assigns them zero weight, purely through causal propagation to observed medical features—has no direct analogue in the prior literature and is demonstrated across all four classifier variants.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the causal strategic classification framework established by Miller, Milli, and Hardt (2020) and Kleinberg and Raghavan (2020), which modeled feature interdependencies via directed acyclic graphs and contribution matrices. The key departures are threefold: moving from regression/scoring to binary classification, generalizing linear effort costs to weighted ℓ_p-norm costs, and introducing incomplete information (Gaussian priors over the classifier and/or causal graph weights) — a combination the authors note is absent from prior causal strategic classification work. The β-desirability notion, which formally quantifies the fraction of effort directed toward principal-preferred features, is a new contribution not present in the existing literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic characterization of when and how classifier design can steer strategic agents toward desirable (e.g., lifestyle) versus undesirable (e.g., medication) feature modifications under causal feature interactions, with clear practical implications for healthcare, credit scoring, and hiring. The non-convexity result for the general classifier design problem, contrasted with convexity when only one desirable feature is targeted, gives practitioners a tractable design principle. Open questions include fairness implications of information asymmetries across populations, more realistic uncertainty models beyond Gaussian priors (e.g., partial orderings of feature importance), and whether the desirability framework transfers to the algorithmic recourse literature where standard counterfactual methods are known to produce undesirable outcomes.
