## Problem

3D Gaussian Splatting's α-compositing optimization is ill-posed with respect to surface opacity: the photometric loss constrains only the final RGB value, allowing the optimizer to assign incorrect transparency to opaque regions by jointly fitting front and back surfaces along the same ray. This 'false transparency' artifact is undetectable in static renderings and standard IQA metrics but becomes visually disruptive during interactive viewing, and it has not been previously quantified or explicitly addressed in the 3DGS literature.

## Input / Output

The method consumes multi-view RGB images (e.g., 240 views at 3000×4000 for the Stone Dataset), COLMAP-estimated camera poses, and per-image foreground segmentation masks (from MVAnet). Beyond the standard 3DGS model, NGS produces a persistent set of interior noise Gaussians that can be extracted, recolored, and inserted into any splatting-based reconstruction to visualize and quantify surface transmittance via the proposed Surface Opacity Score (SOS), a log-normalized transmittance metric computed from a dedicated render channel.

## Method

NGS addresses the opacity ambiguity by injecting high-opacity noise Gaussians into the object's interior volume, creating an occlusion barrier that prevents the optimizer from blending back-surface color into front-facing renders. Noise initialization proceeds coarsely-to-finely: a Quickhull convex hull is voxelized at multiple resolutions, with new noise points placed only in previously empty occupied voxels; depth-based pruning removes noise in front of surfaces, and binary erosion establishes a buffer zone. During training, each noise Gaussian's color is randomized from RGBCMY every iteration to prevent the surface from fitting a static internal pattern, and an alpha-consistency loss (L₁ distance between rendered alpha and the segmentation mask) explicitly rewards full opacity inside the object. The training schedule introduces noise at iteration 6000, fine-tunes noise opacities with surface Gaussians frozen for 1000 iterations, then freezes all noise parameters and resets the surface learning rate so the surface adapts to the new blending landscape.

## Results

NGS achieves the highest Surface Opacity Score (SOS) across all three datasets: 0.749 on DTU, 0.922 on the Stone Dataset, and 0.736 on OmniObject3D, substantially outperforming all baselines (best baseline SOS: 0.265 for StopThePop on OmniObject3D). On the Stone Dataset, NGS also attains the best PSNR (34.148) and lowest LPIPS (0.053) among all methods. Critically, NGS's infill-conditioned metrics (PSNR*, SSIM*, LPIPS*) remain nearly identical to its standard metrics, confirming that the noise infill does not leak through the reconstructed surface, whereas baselines like 3DGS show a PSNR drop of ~7 dB on Stone (34.610 → 27.551) when the infill is inserted.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NGS dominates all three baselines on the proposed SOS metric by a wide margin: on the Stone Dataset, NGS reaches SOS 0.922 versus 0.140 (3DGS), 0.126 (GOF), and 0.168 (StopThePop); on DTU, 0.749 vs. 0.147/0.179/0.135; and on OmniObject3D, 0.736 vs. 0.215/0.208/0.265. In terms of standard rendering quality, NGS matches or slightly exceeds 3DGS on PSNR for Stone (34.148 vs. 34.610) and OmniObject3D (33.619 vs. 29.300), while GOF and StopThePop trail on PSNR for Stone (31.469 and 32.457 respectively). The infill-conditioned PSNR* gap is the most telling comparison: NGS shows virtually zero degradation (e.g., 34.148 → 34.148 on Stone), whereas 3DGS drops by 7.06 dB, GOF by 9.47 dB, and StopThePop by 9.41 dB on Stone, confirming that only NGS produces a truly opaque surface. GSplat+α (alpha-consistency loss alone) is the closest competitor, achieving SOS 0.891 on Stone and 0.598 on DTU, but NGS still leads by 0.031 and 0.151 respectively, demonstrating that the noise infill provides additional benefit beyond the loss term alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [1] as its rendering backbone and adapts the alpha-consistency loss formulation from object-centric 2D Gaussian Splatting [25]. It departs from prior work on view-consistency artifacts (popping [6], floaters [15,16]) by identifying and formalizing a distinct failure mode—false transparency—that arises from the inherent opacity ambiguity in α-compositing under photometric supervision. Unlike transparency reconstruction methods [22,23,24] that target genuinely transparent objects, NGS addresses the inverse problem: enforcing opacity on surfaces that should be opaque. The method is positioned as a plug-and-play add-on requiring minimal modifications to existing splatting pipelines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first quantitative metric (SOS) and diagnostic tool (noise infill) for a previously unmeasured artifact, giving the community a way to detect false transparency in static renderings where standard PSNR/SSIM/LPIPS metrics are blind. The Stone Dataset and noise-augmented DTU/OmniObject3D add-ons create a reusable benchmark for evaluating transparency robustness. Key limitations include the assumption of fully opaque materials (inapplicable to glass or translucent objects), dependence on accurate segmentation masks, and reduced effectiveness on thin structures or large multi-object scenes. Open questions include whether the noise-infill strategy generalizes to scene-level reconstruction and whether the infill can be repurposed as learnable interior representations for internal structure prediction.
