## Problem

Surrogate models are widely deployed across science and engineering to approximate expensive simulations, yet the field lacks any standardised way to document key modelling decisions from data sampling through evaluation. This fragmentation in reporting hinders reproducibility, cross-domain knowledge transfer, and reliable assessment of model quality — a problem sharply exacerbated by the rapid proliferation of diverse AI-driven surrogate architectures (neural networks, diffusion models, PINNs, generative surrogates) that each introduce new undocumented choices.

## Input / Output

The SMRS schema consumes the full surrogate modelling pipeline record — data provenance and generation mode (real-world vs. computational, online vs. offline), sampling design rationale, model architecture and assumptions, training configuration, and both diagnostic and task-based evaluation results — and produces a structured one-page reporting card. The card is modular and model-agnostic, with each field markable as N/A with a short justification, and is designed to sit alongside code repositories as a declarative summary of what was done and, critically, what was not done. It explicitly separates diagnostic evaluation (calibration via SBC, distributional similarity via MMD/FID, generalisation via WAIC/LOO-CV) from task-based evaluation (regret, coverage probability, extreme-event error) to ensure the surrogate is assessed against its actual scientific purpose.

## Method

The SMRS is a lightweight, modular reporting framework organised around five pipeline stages: (1) motivation for surrogate use, (2) data collection and generation covering sampling design, standardised formats (NetCDF, HDF5, GeoTIFF, SMILES), and multi-fidelity or heterogeneous data reconciliation, (3) model class specification including deterministic vs. probabilistic outputs, parametric vs. nonparametric structure, conditioning variables, and domain-specific constraints, (4) learning methodology spanning optimisation, Bayesian and generalised Bayesian inference, generative architectures, simulation-based inference, reinforcement learning, and AutoML, and (5) evaluation combining diagnostic metrics with task-based benchmarks and stress tests. Inspired by model cards and FAIR principles, the schema is deliberately non-prescriptive — it standardises what to report rather than how to model, leaving domain-specific tools and architectures untouched. The authors validate the framework through case studies mapping four published surrogate models (storm surge prediction, geophysical flow modelling, neural fibre stimulation, and adaptive sampling) onto the SMRS schema, revealing that most papers omit sampling rationale, uncertainty quantification details, and task-aligned evaluation. Practical adoption pathways include a one-page SMRS card for authors, a condensed transparency checklist for reviewers, and third-party-audited cards for curated benchmark repositories.

## Results

Across four exemplar case studies (storm surge prediction, geophysical flow modelling, neural fibre stimulation, and adaptive sampling), the authors find substantial heterogeneity in reporting: while all four papers adequately document motivation, model architecture, and basic predictive performance, critical details such as sampling strategy justification, regularisation criteria, and code usability instructions are consistently missing or only partially reported. Specifically, optimiser and training schedule details appear in the paper text for only 2 of 4 studies (the other two require inspecting supplementary code), and no paper provides a direct computational cost comparison between the surrogate and the original simulator. The authors conclude that without a structured reporting framework, extracting the information needed to reproduce or transfer a surrogate model places an 'immense burden' on the reader.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper proposing a reporting standard rather than a method paper, so there are no direct method-vs-baseline comparisons. Instead, the 'comparison' is between the SMRS schema requirements and what four published surrogate model studies actually report. The key finding is that no single paper satisfies all SMRS fields: Paper A (storm surge) omits optimiser details from the main text and lacks a simulator-vs-surrogate runtime comparison; Paper B (geophysical flows) does not justify its sampling choices or provide code setup instructions; Papers C and D (neural fibre, adaptive sampling) do not report regularisation or early-stopping criteria and provide code without pinned dependency versions. Across all four, simulation-based calibration, distributional similarity metrics (MMD, FID), and explicit uncertainty propagation protocols are absent, highlighting the gap the SMRS aims to close.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the model card reporting paradigm [Mitchell et al., FAccT 2019] and FAIR data-management principles to the specific pipeline of surrogate modelling, which involves unique stages (sampling design, multi-fidelity data integration, domain-constrained architectures) not captured by generic ML reporting templates. It positions itself against the fragmented landscape of surrogate modelling literature—spanning Gaussian processes, neural networks, PINNs, and generative surrogates—where key decisions about data provenance, uncertainty quantification, and evaluation are handled inconsistently or omitted. Unlike prior domain-specific toolboxes (e.g., SMT, ClimateBench) that standardise implementation, SMRS targets the reporting layer, remaining agnostic to model class or domain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As AI-driven surrogates proliferate into high-stakes domains (climate, epidemiology, drug design), the absence of a common reporting schema hampers reproducibility, cross-domain transfer, and reliable assessment of model limitations. The case studies concretely demonstrate that even well-published papers omit critical details (sampling motivation, uncertainty handling, code usability), placing a heavy interpretive burden on researchers attempting cross-domain reuse. Open questions include practical adoption pathways (journal/reviewer integration), whether standardisation will stifle rapid methodological innovation in fast-moving areas like diffusion-based surrogates, and how to handle legacy simulators with bespoke data formats.
