## Problem

Existing learning-augmented algorithms for clustering (e.g., k-median, k-means) focus on solving a single instance using ML predictions, but do not address the natural online setting where a sequence of instances arrives and the algorithm must adapt over time. This paper asks whether a learning-augmented algorithm can match the average approximation ratio of the best fixed k-median solution in hindsight across an adversarial sequence of instances, a question that is non-trivial because the solution space is exponentially large, non-convex, and dynamically growing as new points are revealed.

## Input / Output

The algorithm receives an online sequence of k-median instances V_t (each a subset of points in a metric space) one at a time, without prior knowledge of the metric space, its total size, or the time horizon T. At each step t, it must output k centers chosen from previously revealed points (V_{<t}) before seeing V_t. Performance is measured by the approximation ratio (cost of the algorithm's solution divided by OPT_t) rather than absolute cost, ensuring the algorithm is incentivized to perform well on every instance regardless of scale. The metric space is normalized so the minimum inter-point distance is 1, with aspect ratio Δ.

## Method

The algorithm proceeds in three stages: (1) an online reduction from LEARN-MEDIAN to LEARN-BOUNDED-MEDIAN that replaces each instance by at most k weighted proxy points, eliminating dependence on instance size n; (2) an online mirror descent algorithm using a β-hyperbolic entropy regularizer (parametrized by the number of unique points seen so far) to maintain a fractional k-center assignment over the growing simplex, where the key technical challenge is that standard regularizers fail because the gradient dimension grows over time and is undefined at zero for new coordinates; (3) online rounding of the fractional solution to integral k-median solutions via adaptations of Charikar-style LP rounding. The analysis partitions the timeline into O(k log T) phases and compares the algorithm against locally optimal solutions within each phase, showing that if the hindsight optimum is far from these local solutions, it must perform poorly on the corresponding instances. This yields a deterministic O(k)-competitive algorithm with sublinear regret and a randomized O(1)-competitive algorithm with sublinear regret, both with matching lower bounds.

## Results

The paper establishes the first learning-augmented algorithm for the LEARN-MEDIAN problem (a sequence of k-median instances where the algorithm must match the best fixed solution in hindsight). The deterministic algorithm achieves a competitive ratio of O(k) with sublinear regret O(k⁴Δ·√T·log(T)·log(Tk)), while the randomized algorithm improves the competitive ratio to O(1) with regret O(k³Δ·√T·log(T)·log(Tk)). Matching lower bounds show that O(k) is necessary for any deterministic algorithm, O(1) is necessary for any algorithm (randomized or deterministic), and the kΔ dependence in the regret term is unavoidable. Empirically, on i.i.d. instances (Uniform Square, Multiple Clusters), both algorithms converge to near-optimal solutions with approximation ratio approaching 1, and on dynamic instances (Oscillating, Scale Changing), the algorithm adapts quickly to distributional shifts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper introduces a new problem formulation (LEARN-MEDIAN) and does not compare against existing learning-augmented or online clustering baselines, as no prior work addresses this exact setting. The natural benchmark is the best fixed k-median solution in hindsight (Y*), and the paper's contribution is showing that an online algorithm can approximately match this benchmark's average performance. The closest prior work is Fotakis et al. (ICML 2021), which solves a related problem but requires the full metric space to be known upfront; the present paper handles the harder online setting where the metric space is revealed incrementally. In the experimental evaluation, the algorithm is compared against the per-instance optimal (OPT_t) and the best fixed solution over a prefix (OPT_t for instances 1..t); on the Oscillating Instances experiment, the adaptive algorithm actually outperforms the static hindsight optimum by virtue of tracking distributional shifts. No direct head-to-head comparison with other learning-augmented k-median methods (e.g., Ergun et al. 2022, Nguyen et al. 2023) is provided, as those methods target a single instance with ML predictions rather than a sequence of instances.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper introduces a new learning-augmented model (LEARN-MEDIAN) that directly applies the online learning paradigm to a sequence of k-median instances, departing from the dominant single-instance-with-ML-predictions framework of Lykouris & Vassilvitskii (2021) and subsequent clustering work (Ergun et al. 2022, Nguyen et al. 2023). Technically, it builds on the convex relaxation approach of Fotakis et al. (2021) for k-clustering but must overcome the fundamental challenge of a dynamically growing metric space, which invalidates standard OCO regularizers (squared L2-norm and negative entropy). The algorithm combines a novel β-hyperbolic entropy regularizer (from Ghai et al. 2020) with a phase-based analysis and LP-rounding techniques adapted from Charikar et al. (1999) and Charikar & Li (2012), while the closest prior work is Khodak et al. (2022), which learns surrogate predictions from sequences rather than directly optimizing algorithm performance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first principled framework for learning-augmented algorithms that directly optimizes average approximation ratio over an adversarial sequence of instances, with matching upper and lower bounds (O(k) deterministic, O(1) randomized competitive ratio with sublinear regret). The techniques are inherently tied to metric structure, so they do not extend to non-metric combinatorial problems like set cover, leaving a significant gap for the broader learning-augmented algorithms community. The experimental results showing the algorithm can outperform the static hindsight optimum in dynamically shifting environments suggest practical value for streaming clustering applications, though the O(k²T³) running time limits immediate deployment. Open questions include whether the phase-based analysis and hyperbolic regularizer generalize to other metric problems (k-means, facility location) and whether the regret dependence on Δ can be removed.
