## Problem

Pedestrian trajectory prediction models are typically trained and evaluated within specific environments, limiting their generalizability to new domains (different map layouts, camera positions, sensor types). The standard adaptation strategy—collecting scenario-specific data and fine-tuning via backpropagation—is impractical on edge devices due to high computational and memory costs. This paper asks whether a single model can adapt to diverse real-world scenarios at inference time without any weight updates.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251658/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes past trajectories of N pedestrians over 9 timesteps (coordinates normalized so the target agent's last observed position is at the origin) along with M=8 in-context examples, each a (past trajectory, ground-truth future trajectory) pair drawn from the same scene. It outputs K=20 multimodal future trajectory predictions over 12 timesteps. The in-context examples are retrieved at inference time from a scene-specific pool using the proposed selection strategies, meaning the model consumes real-world trajectory data at test time without any parameter updates.

## Method

TrajICL builds on the Social-Transmotion transformer backbone and introduces two key selection mechanisms for in-context learning. First, Spatio-Temporal Similarity-based Example Selection (STES) retrieves top-M examples by combining position-MSE and velocity-MSE similarity between the query's past trajectory and candidate examples, normalized via min-max scaling. Second, Prediction-Guided Example Selection (PG-ES) refines this in two phases: the model first predicts using STES-selected examples, then re-selects examples by matching the concatenated (past + predicted future) trajectory against (past + ground-truth future) trajectories of candidates, taking the minimum similarity across all K predictions to capture long-term dynamics. The architecture adds a 3-layer Transformer Predictor that processes target and context features augmented with Relative Context Position Encoding (RCPE) and Similarity Rank Position Encoding (SRPE), followed by K one-layer MLP decoders for multimodal output. Training proceeds in two stages—standard trajectory prediction (VTP) followed by in-context training with STES-selected examples—entirely on the MOTSynth synthetic dataset, enabling zero-shot transfer to real-world benchmarks.

## Results

On the in-domain MOTSynth dataset, TrajICL achieves minADE_K/minFDE_K of 15.3/17.5, outperforming the Social-Transmotion baseline (17.6/23.0) by 14.2%/23.9% and full fine-tuning (16.0/20.9) by 4.4%/16.3%. Cross-domain, TrajICL reaches 21.1/28.3 on WildTrack (vs. 24.7/36.3 baseline) and 8.40/14.8 on SDD (vs. 10.2/18.9 baseline), while on JTA it achieves 0.59/0.85 (vs. 1.18/1.97 baseline), a 41.5%/56.9% improvement. Notably, TrajICL is training-free at inference (no backpropagation), yet it surpasses full fine-tuning on minFDE_K for MOTSynth (17.5 vs. 20.9), JRDB-Image (2.68 vs. 2.87), and WildTrack (28.3 vs. 34.5).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Social-Transmotion backbone (no adaptation), TrajICL wins on all six datasets, with the largest gains on JTA (−41.5% minADE, −56.9% minFDE) and SDD (−17.6% minADE, −21.7% minFDE). Compared to full fine-tuning, TrajICL achieves lower minFDE_K on MOTSynth (17.5 vs. 20.9), JRDB-Image (2.68 vs. 2.87), and WildTrack (28.3 vs. 34.5), but Full FT retains an edge on SDD (13.6 vs. 14.8), JRDB-World (0.14 vs. 0.21), and JTA (0.76 vs. 0.85). Against PEFT baselines (LoRA r=16/64, VPT Shallow/Deep), TrajICL consistently outperforms on minFDE_K across all datasets without any weight updates. In the few-shot setting (10% pool), TrajICL still beats the best fine-tuning method on MOTSynth (20.4 vs. 21.8 minFDE), JRDB-Image (3.24 vs. 3.16), SDD (17.4 vs. 17.2), and JTA (0.96 vs. 1.09). Against adaptive prediction methods (Latent Corridors, RAN, MetaTra) in the appendix, TrajICL reports the best minADE_K on SDD (8.40 vs. 8.73/11.0/10.1) and NBA Rebounding (0.92 vs. 1.28 for RAN). Among example-selection strategies, PG-STES outperforms random selection (14.8 vs. 16.6 minFDE on SDD), feature-similarity retrieval (14.8 vs. 16.0), and motion-similarity retrieval (14.8 vs. 19.2).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper transplants the In-Context Learning (ICL) paradigm—well established in NLP and recently extended to vision tasks (images, video, point clouds, skeletons)—into pedestrian trajectory prediction, where prior adaptation work has relied on fine-tuning (full, LoRA, VPT) or meta-learning. It uses Social-Transmotion as its backbone encoder but departs from the standard fine-tuning adaptation pipeline by introducing two trajectory-specific example-selection strategies (STES and PG-ES) and training exclusively on the large-scale synthetic MOTSynth dataset to overcome the limited scenario diversity of real-world benchmarks. The key conceptual departure is that adaptation occurs entirely at inference time via forward passes over selected in-context examples, rather than through any parameter update.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical motivation—eliminating backpropagation and multi-model management on resource-constrained edge devices—is compelling for real-world autonomous system deployment, and the finding that a training-free ICL approach can match or exceed full fine-tuning on minFDE_K across multiple cross-domain benchmarks is a meaningful shift in how practitioners think about domain adaptation for trajectory prediction. However, the approach's inference cost grows with the number of in-context examples and the two-stage PG-ES pipeline, and performance degrades when the example pool is very small (e.g., NBA Rebounding at 10%), leaving open the question of real-time feasibility on truly constrained hardware. The robustness result (4.1% vs. 30.8% degradation under noisy perception inputs) suggests ICL may offer an underexplored side benefit of noise tolerance, which could be a productive direction for future work.
