## Problem

While prior work has established that LLM layers anatomically align with hierarchical brain regions, the temporal order in which these representations emerge remains poorly characterized. It is unknown whether the observed temporal alignment is systematic across architectures, or whether it depends on model size, context length, or other factors that steer LLMs toward brain-like sequential computation.

## Input / Output

The method consumes MEG recordings from 3 subjects listening to 10 hours of audiobooks, time-locked to word onset over a 5.5 s window (−2.5 s to +3 s, 30 Hz, 0.1–20 Hz band-pass), alongside activations from 9 equally spaced layers (10%–90% depth) of 17 pretrained LLMs fed the corresponding text. LLM activations are reduced via PCA to 50 components to handle heterogeneous model dimensions, and only content words (NOUN, VERB, ADJ, ADV) are analyzed in the main results. The output is a per-layer alignment score curve over time, a per-layer T_max (mean time window where normalized alignment ≥ 95%), and a scalar temporal score (Pearson r between T_max and relative layer depth).

## Method

For each time point relative to word onset, a ridge regression (log-spaced α from 10⁻⁴ to 10⁸, 5-fold CV) is fit to predict LLM layer activations from MEG sensor signals, and alignment is scored as Pearson correlation on held-out data. Temporal alignment is then quantified by correlating each layer's T_max with its relative depth across 9 layers, yielding a single temporal score per model. The key experimental design isolates three factors: (i) architecture type by comparing transformers, state-space models (Mamba, RecurrentGemma), and bidirectional models (BERT, RoBERTa, Wav2vec2); (ii) model size via the Pythia family (14 M–12 B, identical training); and (iii) context length by varying input from 1 to 1000 words on Llama-3.2-3B and Mamba-1.4B. Both temporal and alignment scores increase logarithmically with model size and context length before plateauing, and the correlation between temporal and alignment scores (r = 0.54, p = 9e-4) indicates that brain-like sequential computation underlies predictive accuracy.

## Results

Across 9 LLMs (transformers and state-space models), the average temporal alignment score between layer depth and peak MEG response time is r = 0.99 (p < 1e-06), confirming that early LLM layers align with early brain responses and deep layers with later ones. Temporal alignment emerges with model size: Pythia models scale from a non-significant r = 0.44 (p > 0.05) at 14M parameters to r = 0.96 (p < 1e-4) at 12B, with a log-size correlation of r = 0.87 (p = 0.01). Context length similarly drives emergence: Llama-3.2-3B goes from r = 0.19 (p > 0.5) with no context to r = 0.93 (p = 3e-4) at 1000 words (correlation r = 0.81, p < 5e-2). Temporal and alignment scores are significantly correlated across all models and contexts (Pearson r = 0.54, p = 9e-04), and the effect is independent of word predictability (most expected r = 0.92 vs. most surprising r = 0.83; difference p = 0.61).

## Baselines & Comparisons

All 9 causal LLMs (Llama-3-8B, Llama-3.2-1B/3B, Mistral-7B, Gemma-7B, Qwen1.5-7B, GPT-2-XL, Mamba-1.4B, RecurrentGemma-9B) exhibit significant temporal alignment, with the average reaching r = 0.99. GPT-2-XL, the oldest and smallest transformer in the set, shows the weakest but still significant alignment (r = 0.85), attributed to its smaller scale and lack of modern architectural improvements (RoPE, GLU). The non-transformer state-space models Mamba-1.4B and RecurrentGemma-9B match transformer performance, demonstrating that temporal alignment is architecture-independent. The Pythia family (8 models, 14M–12B) isolates the size effect: alignment is non-significant below ~1B parameters and plateaus near 12B, with a log-size correlation of r = 0.87. Bidirectional models BERT, RoBERTa, and Wav2vec2.0 achieve comparable alignment scores to causal LLMs but show substantially lower (non-significant) temporal scores, indicating that causal (left-to-right) processing is critical for the sequential brain-like dynamics. Llama-3.2-3B with varying context lengths (1 to 1000 words) shows that temporal alignment is absent without context (r = 0.19) and saturates logarithmically beyond ~50 words, reaching r = 0.93 at 1000 words.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on brain-LLM representational alignment (Caucheteux & King 2022, Millet et al. 2022) from anatomical correspondence (which layer aligns with which cortical region) to temporal correspondence (whether the *order* in which layers activate matches the temporal sequence of brain responses). Goldstein et al. (2022) first reported temporal alignment for GPT-2-XL, but left open whether it generalizes across architectures, scales, and context lengths. The linear-mapping methodology follows the RSA and temporal generalization framework of Kriegeskorte et al. (2008) and King & Dehaene (2014), applied here to a 10-hour MEG audiobook dataset (Armeni et al. 2022).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that temporal alignment is architecture-independent (emerging in both transformers and state-space models) and driven by both model size and context length with logarithmic saturation provides a more complete picture of *why* LLMs and brains partially converge: they share not just representations but a sequential computational path. The null result on word predictability rules out a simple predictive-coding explanation, pointing to a deeper structural convergence. Key open questions include whether the effect is driven by the next-token-prediction objective or by the structure of language itself, whether it extends to speech models, and how it varies across individuals (only 3 subjects here). The 3-subject MEG design, while maximizing per-subject data, limits generalizability claims.
