## Problem

LLM-based robotic planners can recognize execution failures but lack structured reasoning to interpret their causal structure, often resorting to blind retries or ad-hoc replanning that reproduce the same errors. Existing feedback-driven methods (e.g., PRoC3S) treat failures at the surface level without inducing reusable symbolic knowledge, and flat prompt-based approaches lack the hierarchical structure needed for consistent reasoning under dynamic, constraint-rich conditions. The paper addresses this gap by asking how to transform reactive trial-and-error into deliberate, compositional reasoning that enables targeted, interpretable plan repair.

## Input / Output

The system consumes under-specified natural language task goals, the initial environment state (object poses and spatial configuration in a PyBullet simulation), and structured execution failure feedback (constraint violation traces identifying which step, action, and entities were involved). It produces parameterized Python code consisting of a get_plan function (a sequence of high-level actions conditioned on free parameters) and a get_domain function (feasible ranges for those parameters), which are instantiated by sampling and evaluated against four physical constraint checks (kinematic reachability, collision avoidance, grasp stability, placement validity). The intermediate outputs include a dynamically updated hierarchical instruction graph and a set of declarative symbolic constraints (logical predicates over entities, relations, physical functions, and thresholds) that persist across planning rounds.

## Method

InstructFlow is a three-agent, in-context (no fine-tuning) framework built around a hierarchical instruction graph with two node types: planning nodes that encode grounded subgoals translatable to robot-executable code, and reasoning nodes that perform typed symbolic transformations (spatial relation inference, object density estimation, target selection, action ordering, parameter range refinement). The key mechanism is symbolic constraint induction: upon execution failure, the Constraint Generator runs a four-stage diagnostic workflow (failure-relevant entity retrieval → code-level variable instantiation → geometric/physical diagnostic computation → constraint abstraction) to produce a logical constraint φ that is a conjunction of relational predicates and physical threshold conditions. These constraints are propagated upstream to the Planner, which dynamically inserts reasoning nodes upstream of affected planning nodes, composing a symbolic stack tailored to the specific error mode. Code generation then proceeds via graph-guided prompt composition, where each planning node's prompt is encoded by integrating its subgoal with symbolic refinements from all upstream reasoning nodes, enabling targeted subgoal repair without full plan regeneration. The entire pipeline uses GPT-4o with structured prompting templates, and the 'flow' refers to the dynamic, graph-guided injection of reasoning outputs and failure-induced constraints into task-specific prompts at each planning round.

## Results

InstructFlow achieves an average task success rate of 88% across 10 tasks spanning three domains (Drawing, Arrange-Blocks, Arrange-YCB), outperforming the strongest baseline PRoC3S (65%) by 23 percentage points on average. Gains are most pronounced in constraint-sensitive and long-horizon tasks: InstructFlow reaches 100% on Star, Letters, Enclosed, Line, and 90% on Pyramid, Packing (Blocks), and Unstack, whereas PRoC3S plateaus at 60–90% and LLM³/CaP drop to 0–40% on the same tasks. The paper also reports a ~37% reduction in feedback queries and a ~4.7% reduction in end-to-end wall-clock latency relative to PRoC3S.

## Baselines & Comparisons

InstructFlow outperforms all three baselines on 9 out of 10 tasks, tying only on Arrow (80% for both InstructFlow and PRoC3S). Against PRoC3S (the strongest baseline), InstructFlow gains 10–60 percentage points per task, with the largest margins on Unstack (+30), Packing-Blocks (+40), and Stacking-YCB (+30), where structured symbolic constraint induction enables targeted repair that PRoC3S's flat replanning cannot match. Against LLM³, InstructFlow leads by 30–90 points on nearly every task, as LLM³'s direct skill-sequence output lacks any feedback-driven repair mechanism. Against CaP, the gap is even wider (up to 90 points on Arrow), reflecting CaP's inability to handle multi-step spatial reasoning or constraint violations. Notably, InstructFlow also reduces the average number of feedback iterations from ~1.8 (PRoC3S) to ~1.1, confirming that symbolic constraint-guided repair is more sample-efficient than iterative full regeneration.

## Relation to Prior Work

InstructFlow builds directly on PRoC3S (Curtis et al., CoRL 2024), reusing its two-phase code-generation paradigm, simulation environments, and skill library, but departs from its flat, reactive planning by introducing a hierarchical instruction graph and a symbolic constraint induction mechanism that enables targeted, compositional repair rather than full plan regeneration. Conceptually, it sits at the intersection of three threads: LLM-based robotic code generation (CaP, LLM3, RoboScript), symbolic abstraction planning (TAMP, LLM+P, VisualPredicator), and feedback-driven failure recovery (REFLECT, AHA, DoReMi). The key architectural departure is treating prompt construction itself as a dynamic, graph-guided flow where reasoning nodes inject symbolic knowledge (spatial relations, parameter refinements, action ordering) into planning nodes, rather than relying on monolithic LLM prompts or fine-tuned failure detectors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a concrete and underexplored gap: LLM-based planners can detect execution failures but lack structured mechanisms to interpret their causal structure and perform targeted repair. The symbolic constraint induction mechanism—abstracting raw collision/instability feedback into reusable, composable predicates—is a practical step toward interpretable, failure-resilient robotic planning without requiring fine-tuning. However, all experiments are simulation-only (Ravens/PyBullet, UR5 tabletop), and the VLM-based evaluation fix, while important for fairness, introduces an additional LLM dependency that may not transfer cleanly to real-world deployment. Open questions include whether the five reasoning node types and constraint vocabulary generalize beyond tabletop manipulation to more complex, multi-object, or mobile settings, and whether the approach scales to tasks with hundreds of subgoals.
