## Problem

Reinforcement learning for LLM reasoning has become essential for long chain-of-thought tasks, but existing RL methods rely on ground-truth labels to compute rewards, making them inapplicable to the growing volume of unlabeled, complex test-time data. This paper asks whether a model can perform effective RL at test time using only its own unlabeled outputs, without any external supervision.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254376/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes unlabeled test prompts (e.g., math problems or GPQA questions with no provided answers) and a pre-trained LLM as input. For each prompt, the model generates N candidate responses (64 for rollout, downsampled to 32 for training) at a specified temperature, and an answer extractor parses each response into a discrete predicted answer. The output is an updated policy model whose parameters have been refined via RL on the test distribution, yielding improved pass@1 and majority-voting accuracy on the same and out-of-distribution benchmarks.

## Method

TTRL constructs a self-supervised reward signal by generating multiple candidate outputs per question and using majority voting to estimate a pseudo-label, then assigning a binary rule-based reward (1 if the sampled answer matches the majority-voted answer, 0 otherwise). Standard policy-optimization algorithms (GRPO, PPO, PRIME) are then applied to update the model using these pseudo-rewards, with no ground-truth labels ever accessed. A key mechanism enabling stability is the 'Lucky Hit' phenomenon: even when the majority-voted label is incorrect, most wrong predictions still differ from the wrong label and thus receive the correct negative reward, keeping reward accuracy high (e.g., 92% reward accuracy despite only 37% label accuracy on AIME 2024). The online, iterative nature of the procedure creates a self-reinforcing loop where improved model capabilities yield more accurate pseudo-labels, allowing TTRL to surpass the initial model's maj@n upper bound and approach the performance of direct RL on labeled test data.

## Results

On AIME 2024, TTRL boosts Qwen2.5-Math-7B pass@1 from 12.9 to 40.2 (+211.6%), with an average improvement of +76.5% across AIME 2024, AMC, MATH-500, and GPQA. Improvements are consistent across all six evaluated models (1.5B–32B, base and instruct), with minimum AIME 2024 gains of +105.2% (Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B) and up to +203.8% (Qwen2.5-32B). Notably, TTRL surpasses the initial model's maj@n upper bound and approaches the performance of direct RL training on the same test data with ground-truth labels (the 'RL leakage' upper bound).

## Baselines & Comparisons

In Appendix D (Table 4), TTRL is compared against six RL-trained baselines that use labeled training data. On Qwen2.5-Math-7B, TTRL (no labeled data) achieves an average of 65.0 across AIME/AMC/MATH-500, outperforming Oat-Zero-7B (62.0, 8.9K labeled), LIMR-7B (58.1, 1.4K labeled), SimpleRL-Zero-7B (55.0, 8.9K labeled), PRIME-Zero-7B (54.4, 230K labeled), OpenReasoner-Zero-7B (46.5, 129K labeled), and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-7B (32.1, 800K labeled). TTRL ties Oat-Zero-7B on AIME 2024 (43.3) but leads on AMC (+4.8) and MATH-500 (+4.2). At the 1.5B scale, TTRL (avg 51.0) similarly surpasses Oat-Zero-1.5B (49.1) and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-1.5B@8k (48.9). The authors note the comparison is somewhat unfair since TTRL trains directly on the test set, but the results demonstrate that self-evolution via majority-voting rewards can match or exceed models trained on large-scale labeled corpora.

## Relation to Prior Work

TTRL sits at the intersection of Test-Time Scaling (TTS) and Test-Time Training (TTT), repurposing majority voting—a standard TTS inference technique—as a reward signal for online RL, which is a TTT mechanism. Methodologically, it builds directly on GRPO (DeepSeekMath) as the policy optimizer and on DeepSeek-R1's rule-based reward framework, but departs from both by eliminating the need for ground-truth labels through self-estimated pseudo-labels. It is distinct from prior self-rewarding and self-play approaches, which target open-domain instruction following or use preference-based optimization (e.g., DPO), and from earlier TTT work focused on video or distribution-shift adaptation, by applying RL specifically to reasoning tasks with verifiable answers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TTRL addresses a fundamental scalability bottleneck: RL for reasoning currently requires expensive human-annotated data, and the paper demonstrates that majority-voting rewards on unlabeled test data can drive effective self-improvement across 10+ models and 4 benchmarks. The finding that TTRL surpasses its own training signal (maj@n) and approaches the performance of RL with label leakage is conceptually significant, suggesting a path toward unbounded continual learning without human supervision. Key open questions include theoretical convergence guarantees, sensitivity to prior knowledge (TTRL fails when the model lacks sufficient task priors), and whether the approach generalizes beyond verifiable-answer tasks (math, code) to open-ended reasoning and agentic settings.
