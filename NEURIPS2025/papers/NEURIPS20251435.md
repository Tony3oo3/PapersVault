## Problem

Existing Monte Carlo methods for channel simulation and distributed compression face a fundamental trade-off: Poisson Monte Carlo achieves near-optimal rates but requires infinite proposals (impractical when target distributions are learned via ML), while importance sampling uses finite proposals but produces biased samples. Standard rejection sampling and greedy rejection sampling achieve good coding rates but yield distributed matching probabilities significantly lower than the Poisson Matching Lemma, making them unsuitable for distributed compression. The paper asks whether a single rejection-sampling-based scheme can simultaneously achieve near-optimal coding rates and competitive distributed matching probabilities.

## Input / Output

The encoder observes a source sample X ~ P_X and shared common randomness W (a sequence of batches, each containing N proposal samples Y_ij ~ Q_Y and exponential variables S_ij ~ Exp(1), plus uniform variables U_i). In the Wyner-Ziv setting, the decoder additionally receives side information X' correlated with X. The encoder outputs a compressed binary message consisting of a group index L, a batch rank K̂₁, a local rank K̂₂, and optionally a hash value V and batch index K_{1,A}; the decoder reconstructs Y from W using these indices. For the MNIST experiment, the source is projected into a 3D embedding via a neural encoder, the side information is the cropped bottom-left quadrant of the image, and the output is a reconstructed image X̂.

## Method

The core contribution is a new coding scheme for Ensemble Rejection Sampling (ERS), which combines standard rejection sampling with Gumbel-Max importance sampling within batches of size N. Instead of transmitting the raw index K, the proposed Sorting Method groups every ⌊Δ⁻¹⌋ batches and encodes the group index L plus the rank of U_{K₁} within the sorted U-values of that group, while the local index K₂ is encoded by sorting the exponential variables S_ij within the selected batch. This yields a coding cost R ≤ I(X;Y) + 2 log(I(X;Y)+8) + 12 bits, near the theoretical optimum. The key theoretical result is a distributed matching lemma showing that ERS achieves Pr(Y_A = Y_B | Y_A = y, X=x, Z=z) ≥ (1 + μ₁'(N) + (P_Y^A(y)/P_Y^B(y))(1 + μ₂'(N)))⁻¹, where the decay coefficients vanish at rate N⁻¹, making ERS asymptotically match PML performance. A practical variant transmits the batch index K_{1,A} to the decoder to handle cases where P_Y^B is learned via a neural contrastive estimator and its upper bound is unknown, as in the MNIST β-VAE pipeline.

## Results

On synthetic Gaussian sources targeting −23 dB distortion, ERS with N* = 1.1×10⁶ proposals outperforms IML with N* = 1.6×10⁶ proposals in the distortion regime below −22.8 dB, while standard RS achieves only −17 dB at 10 bits. On MNIST distributed image compression, ERS (with N*_ERS ranging from 1.5×2¹⁷ to 1.2×2²¹) consistently outperforms IML (N*_IML = 2²⁶) in both embedding and pixel domains when feedback-based error correction is used, achieving target pixel distortions of 0.058–0.066. ERS matching probability converges to PML performance as batch size N grows, with decay coefficients μ₁′(N), μ₂′(N) → 0 at rate N⁻¹.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against IML [Phan et al., AISTATS 2024], ERS wins on both the Gaussian and MNIST experiments: on Gaussian sources, ERS with fewer proposals (1.1×10⁶) beats IML with more (1.6×10⁶) below −22.8 dB distortion, and on MNIST with feedback, ERS outperforms IML in both embedding and pixel domains because IML's inherent sampling bias persists even at N*_IML = 2²⁶, and its feedback correction message is larger due to the larger proposal count. Against PML [Li & Anantharam, IEEE TIT 2021], ERS is competitive but not strictly superior: PML remains the unbiased gold standard with zero overhead, while ERS approaches PML matching probability as N → ∞ and offers the practical advantage of finite proposals when P_Y^B is learned via neural networks (where PML cannot be applied). Against NDIC [Mital et al., DCC 2022], a specialized deep-learning distributed compression method, ERS is positioned as complementary rather than directly comparable: NDIC targets optimal RD performance via learned binning, while ERS operates on a probabilistic matching framework that can accommodate distributional constraints (e.g., differential privacy) that NDIC does not address.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions Ensemble Rejection Sampling (ERS) as a principled middle ground between Poisson Monte Carlo (PMC/PML), which requires infinite proposals and is intractable when target distributions are ML-estimated, and Importance Sampling (IS/IML), which uses finite proposals but produces biased samples. It builds on the ERS framework of Deligiannidis et al. (2020) and the distributed matching theory of Li & Anantharam (2021) and Phan et al. (2024), while extending the Braverman & Garg (2014) coding result for standard RS from discrete to continuous alphabets via a novel sorting-based mechanism. The key conceptual departure is showing that standard RS and GRS—despite achieving near-optimal coding rates—have fundamentally weaker distributed matching probabilities than PML, and that ERS recovers PML-level matching while preserving unbiasedness.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ERS distributed matching lemma is the first result in the rejection sampling family whose matching probability approaches that of PML, filling a gap for practical settings where PML cannot be applied because the decoder's target distribution is learned via neural networks and its normalization constant is unknown. The O(ω) runtime and the fact that experiments are limited to 1D Gaussian sources and low-dimensional MNIST embeddings leave open whether the approach scales to high-dimensional natural images or video. Future directions noted by the authors include incorporating space-partitioning or multiple-IS acceleration to reduce runtime, and extending the framework to differential privacy applications where unbiased samples are essential.
