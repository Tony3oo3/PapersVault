## Problem

Interactive AI systems (recommenders, chatbots) rely on short-term feedback (clicks, engagement) for learning, but over-optimizing these signals degrades long-term objectives like user retention and subscription renewal. The core obstacle is a timescale disconnect: short-term interventions (rankings, responses) are observed per-interaction, while long-term feedback arrives sparsely over weeks or months, making direct optimization intractable. This paper asks how to contextually reconcile these interdependent timescales so that policies at all levels effectively optimize for the long term.

## Input / Output

The method consumes logged off-policy bandit data at two or more timescales: abundant lower-level data (context, action, reward, propensity) and sparser upper-level data (context, selected micro-policy, long-term reward, propensity). It produces a family of micro policies parameterized by macro actions (e.g., a set of LLM decoding strategies or relevance-score boosts) plus a macro policy that contextually selects which micro policy to deploy. Unusually, the macro action space is isomorphic to the family of micro policies rather than to a set of discrete outputs, so the macro policy's 'action' is itself a policy selection that governs all subsequent lower-level decisions.

## Method

The core insight, motivated by PAC-Bayes generalization bounds, is that a policy learned from abundant micro-level data provides a data-dependent prior that dramatically reduces the sample complexity of learning the macro-level policy (up to ~98% sample savings in a 50-parameter toy example). Concretely, the policy space is factorized as Π = Π^L1 · Π^L2, where the macro action space A^L2 is isomorphic to a small family of micro policies Π̂^L1. Two constructions define this family: Policy Modification (each macro action applies a perturbation, e.g., a score boost or decoding temperature, to a single learned micro policy) and Feedback Modification (each macro action reweights a vectorial reward, so one parameterized micro policy serves all macro actions by conditioning on a^L2). Training proceeds bottom-up with off-policy contextual bandits (IPS estimator) at each level, while inference proceeds top-down: the macro policy selects a^L2, which indexes the corresponding micro policy for all subsequent lower-level contexts. The framework extends recursively to k levels, with each lower level narrowing the action space for the next upper level.

## Results

Across three experimental settings (multi-turn conversation, conversational recommender, and KuaiRand recommender), MSBL consistently achieves the best long-term reward (user satisfaction, weekly return rate, or subscription renewal) while incurring only modest short-term cost (clicks, single-turn quality). In the noise-robustness experiment on the conversational recommender (Table 1), MSBL achieves Level 2 reward of 0.86±0.08 at default noise (σ_f=0.10), outperforming the best static intervention (a_3^L2 at 0.77±0.12) and the random macro policy (0.66±0.09) by 11.7% and 30.3% respectively. The PAC-Bayes analysis shows that using the micro-level policy as an informed prior saves ~98% of macro-level training samples (88.2% after accounting for micro-level data cost with T=10). In the toy RL comparison, MSBL requires 10–62× fewer episodic samples than tabular Q-learning to reach the same long-term reward.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares MSBL against four types of baselines: (1) the short-term optimizing micro policy π^L1 (no macro intervention), (2) a random macro policy selecting interventions uniformly, (3) static policies a_j^L2 that apply a fixed macro intervention (e.g., fixed decoding temperature or fixed boost) to all users, and (4) an oracle skyline. MSBL wins on long-term reward across all settings: in the multi-turn conversation, it achieves the best 5-turn user satisfaction (binary Llama-3-70b score) for both 'Child' and 'Expert' personas, while the short-term policy suffers from harm-inducing responses that degrade multi-turn quality. In the conversational recommender, MSBL achieves near-optimal Level 2 (weekly return) reward for both relevance-preferring and diversity-preferring user groups, whereas each static temperature a_j^L2 is optimal for at most one group. In the KuaiRand recommender, MSBL maintains high return rate across 2–5 user/item group configurations while static boosts a_j^L2 (applied to a single item group for all users) and the click-maximizing π^L1 both yield substantially lower return rates. The advantage of MSBL over the best static policy is most pronounced at low noise (e.g., 0.86 vs 0.77 at σ_f=0.10) and narrows but persists at high noise (0.62 vs 0.52 at σ_f=0.50). MSBL consistently trades a small amount of short-term reward (clicks, single-turn quality) for substantially better long-term outcomes, validating the core thesis that contextual macro-level learning is necessary to reconcile multi-timescale objectives.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on off-policy contextual bandit learning (Swaminathan & Joachims 2015; Joachims et al. 2018) as the core algorithm at each timescale, and on PAC-Bayes generalization bounds (London & Sandler 2019) to motivate why a micro-level policy can serve as a data-dependent prior for the macro level. It explicitly departs from Hierarchical RL (options, feudal learning) by arguing that the relevant hierarchy is in feedback timescales rather than subgoal decomposition, and from prior long-term optimization work (Maystre et al. 2023; Brantley et al. 2024) by learning the macro interventions themselves rather than treating them as fixed. The feedback-modification mechanism for LLM policies draws on rewards-in-context learning (Yang et al. 2024), but the key conceptual contribution is the recursive construction of a family of micro policies whose action space becomes the macro action space, narrowing the search for the sparse long-term reward.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a fundamental practical disconnect in interactive AI systems: short-term interventions (rankings, responses) operate at a much faster timescale than the long-term feedback (retention, satisfaction) they are meant to optimize. The PAC-Bayes analysis showing ~98% sample savings under favorable conditions provides a concrete theoretical justification for why hierarchical priors help, and the empirical validation across conversational and recommender domains demonstrates practical viability. Key limitations include the assumption of known level structure, i.i.d. contexts (no stateful policies), and the absence of public real-world multi-scale datasets. Open questions include data-driven discovery of timescales, extension to stateful/sequential settings, and whether the framework's benefits persist when the micro-macro alignment is weaker than assumed.
