## Problem

Prediction-powered inference (PPI) leverages a large pool of unlabelled data and a black-box predictor to tighten confidence intervals beyond what labelled data alone can achieve, but its validity guarantee holds only at a fixed, pre-specified sample size. In sequential or online settings—where labelled data arrive one at a time and conclusions must be drawn continuously—this fixed-time guarantee is insufficient. Moreover, existing anytime-valid PPI methods do not exploit prior knowledge about the quality of the predictions, leaving efficiency on the table when the predictor is known to be accurate.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sequentially arriving stream of labelled pairs (X_i, Y_i) and a large unlabelled pool {X̃_j} available from the start (with N_n ≥ n, typically N_n ≫ n), together with a fixed black-box prediction rule f mapping inputs to predicted outputs. It produces a confidence sequence (C_{α,n}^{avpp})_{n≥1} for the estimand θ* (e.g., E[Y] under squared loss) that is valid uniformly over all n, with the interval width shrinking as more labelled observations accumulate. In the Bayes-assisted variant, the user additionally specifies a continuous proper prior π on the rectifier Δ_θ, which modulates the interval width: the sequence narrows when the empirical rectifier aligns with the prior and widens when it conflicts.

## Method

The paper shows that both the standard PPI and PPI++ estimators are control-variate estimators (CVEs), then establishes a KMT-type strong coupling between CVEs and i.i.d. Gaussian averages (Proposition 2), which is the key ingredient for constructing asymptotic confidence sequences via the method of mixtures and Ville's inequality. For the Bayes-assisted extension, the PPI decomposition g_θ = m_θ + Δ_θ is exploited: a non-assisted AsympCS is built for the fit term m_θ from the unlabelled data, and a Bayes-assisted AsympCS is built for the rectifier Δ_θ using a zero-centred prior π (Gaussian, Laplace, or Student-t), with the two combined via a Minkowski sum to yield a (1−α)-AsympCS for g_θ. Solving 0 ∈ C_{α,θ,n}^g then gives the final confidence sequence for θ*. The approach is agnostic to the specific AsympCS construction and requires only mild moment conditions (E|Y|^{2+δ} < ∞) and a rate condition on the labelled-to-unlabelled ratio, making it applicable to a wide range of loss functions and prediction rules.

## Results

The paper evaluates its anytime-valid PPI confidence sequences primarily through interval volume (smaller is better) and cumulative miscoverage rate (must stay below α = 0.1). On the three real-data mean-estimation tasks (FLIGHTS, FOREST, GALAXIES), PPI++ AsympCS consistently produces narrower confidence regions than the classical labelled-only AsympCS, with Bayes-assisted variants (Gaussian, Laplace, or Student-t priors on the rectifier) providing a further efficiency gain at moderate labelled sample sizes. In the synthetic noisy-predictions experiment (σ_Y ∈ {0.1, 0.8, 3.0}), PPI++ adapts its power-tuning parameter λ and maintains competitive or superior volume across all noise levels, whereas standard PPI degrades as noise increases. In the biased-predictions experiment, Bayes-assisted volume shrinks for small bias |v| but inflates as |v| grows, with the Gaussian prior inflating fastest and heavier-tailed Laplace/Student-t priors offering greater robustness.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole explicit baseline is the 'classical' AsympCS (Theorem 1 of Waudby-Smith et al. [2]), which relies solely on the labelled data stream and ignores the unlabelled pool and prediction rule. Across all six real-data tasks (FLIGHTS, FOREST, GALAXIES for mean estimation; CENSUS for linear regression; HEALTHCARE for logistic regression; GENES for quantile estimation), both PPI and PPI++ AsympCS produce strictly smaller confidence regions than the classical baseline while maintaining valid cumulative miscoverage below α = 0.1. The Bayes-assisted variants (zero-mean prior on the rectifier Δ_θ) further narrow the intervals relative to non-assisted PPI/PPI++ whenever the black-box predictions are accurate, as is the case for FLIGHTS, FOREST, GALAXIES, CENSUS, and HEALTHCARE. However, on the GENES quantile-estimation task, where prediction quality is lower, the Bayes-assisted methods yield larger regions than both non-assisted PPI and the classical baseline, illustrating that the prior correction can be counterproductive when the rectifier is not near zero. In the synthetic noisy-predictions study, PPI++ (with data-driven λ) outperforms standard PPI (fixed λ = 1) as the prediction noise σ_Y increases from 0.1 to 3.0, confirming the benefit of the power-tuning parameter from Angelopoulos et al. [7].

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the fixed-time Prediction-Powered Inference (PPI) framework of Angelopoulos et al. (2023) and its PPI++ variant to the sequential setting by constructing asymptotic confidence sequences, building directly on the time-uniform CLT machinery of Waudby-Smith et al. (2024). The key technical bridge is recognizing that PPI estimators are control-variate estimators, which enables KMT strong coupling and thus the application of asymptotic CS theory. The Bayes-assisted component extends the fixed-time FAB-PPI of Cortinovis and Caron (2025) to the sequential setting via a zero-centred prior on the rectifier, using the method of mixtures and Ville's inequality. It departs from the exact time-uniform PPI of Csillag et al. (2025), which requires stronger conditions (bounded e-values, active-learning setup) and does not leverage prior information about prediction quality.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work enables continuous, anytime-valid statistical inference in the practically important regime of scarce labels and abundant unlabelled data with ML predictions — a setting common in healthcare monitoring, A/B testing, and online learning where fixed-time CIs are insufficient. The Bayes-assisted variant offers a principled way to incorporate domain knowledge about prediction quality, yielding tighter intervals when predictions are accurate while remaining valid under misspecification. Key limitations include the asymptotic (not finite-sample) validity, restriction to scalar parameters and i.i.d. data, and the need for a KMT-type strong coupling. Open directions include multivariate extensions, non-i.i.d. settings, non-asymptotic guarantees, and adapting the tuning parameter ρ to the Bayes-assisted construction.
