## Problem

Existing simulation-based inference (SBI) methods are designed for low-dimensional vector-valued parameters and struggle to infer function-valued parameters that arise in spatiotemporal scientific models. Current workarounds—fixed discretizations requiring retraining for new observation locations, or basis-function representations with expressivity-tractability trade-offs—limit applicability in fields like geosciences where observations are made at arbitrary, non-uniform locations. FNOPE addresses this gap by enabling posterior estimation over function-valued parameters at arbitrary, previously unseen discretizations with a fraction of the simulation budget of state-of-the-art methods.

## Input / Output

FNOPE takes function-valued observations x evaluated at positions l^x, function-valued parameters θ at positions l^θ, and optional vector-valued parameters η as input, and produces posterior samples of both θ and η via iterative denoising. A key design constraint is that the noise base distribution is a Gaussian Process (not independent white noise) whose lengthscale is matched to the FNO's maximum Fourier mode M via Bochner's theorem, ensuring >99% of spectral power lies in the modes the FNO can represent. The method handles parameter dimensions up to ~16k (129×129 Darcy flow grid) and supports simultaneous inference of function-valued and vector-valued parameters in a single network.

## Method

FNOPE combines a Fourier Neural Operator backbone with a flow-matching (FMPE) training objective to learn a conditional velocity field v^φ that denoises GP samples into posterior samples of function-valued parameters. The FNO blocks use a type II non-uniform fast Fourier transform (NUDFT) to compute spectral features at arbitrary discretization positions, with position embeddings and flow-time embeddings injected via MLPs to enable generalization to unseen grids. Data augmentation during training—randomly masking parameter/observation entries and adding small positional Gaussian noise—allows the network to counteract NUDFT approximation errors at evaluation time. Vector-valued parameters are handled by a separate MLP that processes spectral features from the final FNO block, with the combined loss normalized by each parameter's dimensionality. A fixed-grid variant (FNOPE fix) replaces NUDFT with standard FFT for O(N log N) spectral computation when uniform grids suffice.

## Results

On the 1000-dimensional Linear Gaussian task, FNOPE achieves a Sliced-Wasserstein Distance (SWD) near zero with only 10² training simulations, whereas NPE and FMPE (spectral) require 10⁵ simulations for comparable performance. On the ~16k-parameter Darcy flow task, FNOPE consistently outperforms spectral baselines on predictive MSE (especially at low simulation budgets) and achieves substantially higher posterior log-probability per pixel than FMPE (raw). On the Antarctic ice shelf task, FNOPE at 10³ simulations matches the predictive MSE of all baselines at 10⁵ simulations, and can infer the full 500-dimensional parameter space rather than the 50-dimensional downsampled version used by the prior NPE (raw) approach.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FNOPE outperforms NPE (spectral) [3] and FMPE (spectral) [9] across all four tasks, with the largest gains at low simulation budgets (e.g., 10²–10³ simulations), where spectral methods produce oversmoothed posteriors that miss local structure. Against FMPE (raw) [9] on Darcy flow, FNOPE achieves comparable predictive MSE but substantially higher posterior log-probability and captures fine spatial structure that FMPE (raw) misses (its samples are noisier and only capture rough global structure). On the SIRD task, FNOPE is on par with Simformer [30] in both predictive MSE and SBC calibration, though Simformer degrades less when conditioning on only 20 time points. On the Antarctic ice shelf, FNOPE matches the NPE (raw) approach from Moss et al. [39] in predictive MSE while using two orders of magnitude fewer simulations (10³ vs. 10⁵). In a supplementary comparison, iFNO [50] achieves comparable predictive MSE on Darcy flow but produces poorly calibrated uncertainty estimates and its conditional distribution collapses to a tiny parameter region, unlike FNOPE's well-calibrated posteriors.

## Relation to Prior Work

FNOPE combines two established lines of work: Fourier Neural Operators (Li et al., ICLR 2021) as a compact spectral backbone for function-valued data, and flow-matching posterior estimation (Wildberger et al., NeurIPS 2023) as the training objective for simulation-based inference. The non-uniform discretization capability is inherited from Lingsch et al. (ICML 2024), who introduced NUDFT-based FNOs for arbitrary domains. The closest prior work is Simformer (Gloeckler et al., ICML 2024), which uses transformers to tokenize function-valued parameters for flexible SBI, and Lim et al. (2025), which applies score-based FNOs to function-space posteriors but is restricted to uniform grids. FNOPE's key departure is using the FNO's inherent spectral compactness to achieve high-dimensional posterior inference at dramatically lower simulation budgets than pointwise or token-based approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FNOPE makes SBI practical for the function-valued parameters that dominate spatiotemporal scientific modeling (climate, geosciences, epidemiology), where prior methods required either fixed discretizations, basis-function approximations, or prohibitively large simulation budgets. The demonstrated 100× reduction in simulation budget (10² vs 10⁴–10⁵) and the ability to evaluate posteriors at arbitrary, previously unseen discretizations address two of the most common practical barriers to adopting SBI in these fields. Key limitations remain: the FNO backbone assumes low-frequency parameter content (ill-suited for discontinuous parameters), sufficient observation density is required for reliable spectral estimation, and computational cost still scales exponentially with domain dimensionality. Open questions include whether wavelet or other non-Fourier neural operators could relax the smoothness assumption, and whether the approach extends to genuinely high-dimensional (3D+) spatial domains.
