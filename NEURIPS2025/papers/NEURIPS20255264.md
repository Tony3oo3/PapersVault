## Problem

While LLMs have been shown to serve as general-purpose lossless compressors, their naive application to RGB images yields a substantial performance gap compared to state-of-the-art classical and learned codecs. The paper identifies three root causes: the absence of pixel-level priors (inter-channel correlation and local self-similarity), the loss of pixel-level semantic context when mapping values to ASCII proxy tokens, and the lack of task-specific adaptation of the LLM to pixel-sequence reasoning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255264/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image, flattens it into a 1D sequence of (R,G,B) subpixel values, and processes it in non-overlapping 16×16 patches. Each subpixel value (0–255) is tokenized as its numerical string (e.g., '127') rather than an ASCII character, and a task prompt describing the prediction task is prepended to form the full input sequence. The output is a compressed bitstream produced by arithmetic coding over the 256-way probability distribution obtained by sampling and softmax-normalizing the LLM's predictive logits restricted to the 256 pixel-value tokens.

## Method

P²-LLM reformulates LLM-based lossless compression as next-pixel prediction in language space with four key components. First, a pixel prediction chat template processes RGB channels jointly (rather than independently) to exploit intra-pixel inter-channel correlation, and prepends a task prompt to activate the LLM's in-context learning for the prediction task. Second, a two-step lossless pixel tokenization maps each subpixel value to its unique numerical-string token in the LLM's vocabulary, preserving ordinal relationships between pixel values (e.g., 126 and 127 remain adjacent in embedding space) and ensuring a strict one-to-one mapping for losslessness. Third, predictive distribution sampling extracts only the 256 pixel-relevant logits from the LLM's full vocabulary output and normalizes them via softmax into a parameter-free probability distribution for arithmetic coding. Fourth, LoRA fine-tuning (rank 64, alpha 128) on the Llama 3 8B base model using ~4M DIV2K patches adapts the LLM to the pixel-prediction task. A theoretical corollary extending Solomonoff induction to channel-joint sequential prediction justifies why joint R→G→B modeling yields the optimal posterior distribution for minimum coding length.

## Results

P²-LLM achieves the best lossless compression (lowest bpsp) across all six evaluation datasets, outperforming both classical and learned codecs. On the in-distribution natural image benchmarks, P²-LLM reaches 2.08 bpsp on CLIC.m and 2.83 bpsp on Kodak, surpassing the best LIC method DLPR (2.16 and 2.86 bpsp) and the best classical codec JPEG-XL (2.36 and 2.87 bpsp). On out-of-distribution datasets, the advantage is even more pronounced: P²-LLM achieves 1.21 bpsp on SCID (vs. 1.58 for DLPR and 1.26 for JPEG-XL) and 3.33 bpsp on BRACS24 (vs. 3.61 for DLPR and 3.67 for JPEG-XL), demonstrating significantly stronger cross-domain generalization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

P²-LLM outperforms all 16 baselines across every reported dataset. Against the strongest classical codec JPEG-XL, P²-LLM gains 0.28 bpsp on CLIC.m (2.08 vs. 2.36), 0.05 on SCID (1.21 vs. 1.26), and 0.34 on BRACS24 (3.33 vs. 3.67). Against the best learned codec DLPR, the margins are 0.08 bpsp on CLIC.m (2.08 vs. 2.16), 0.37 on SCID (1.21 vs. 1.58), and 0.28 on BRACS24 (3.33 vs. 3.61), with the largest gap appearing on out-of-distribution data, highlighting P²-LLM's superior generalization. Compared to the prior LLM-based compressor of Delétang et al. (2024), P²-LLM reduces bpsp by roughly 1.5–1.7 across all datasets (e.g., 2.08 vs. 3.76 on CLIC.m), closing the gap that made the naive LLM approach competitive only with PNG. Among LIC methods, P²-LLM also beats iFlow (2.26 on CLIC.m), iVPF (2.39), and L3C (2.64), confirming that the LLM-based autoregressive paradigm with pixel-level priors and fine-tuning can surpass dedicated deep generative models for lossless compression.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends Delétang et al. (2024) 'Language modeling is compression,' which first demonstrated that LLMs can serve as lossless compressors but only validated on grayscale images using ASCII proxy tokens and channel-independent processing. The authors depart from that framework in three key ways: (1) replacing proxy tokens with a two-step numerical tokenization that preserves pixel-level semantic context, (2) introducing channel-joint sequential prediction to exploit intra-pixel inter-channel correlation (drawing on the autoregressive prediction theory from Salimans et al., 2017), and (3) adding a task-prompt-based in-context learning template plus LoRA fine-tuning. The paper also positions itself against the broader learned image compression (LIC) literature (L3C, iFlow, DLPR) and classical codecs (JPEG-XL, PNG), arguing that LLM-based compression is a distinct autoregressive paradigm without visual-perception components.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that an LLM with no visual-perception architecture (no convolutions, no spatial attention) can match or exceed purpose-built learned lossless codecs on RGB images, and notably achieves significantly better cross-domain generalization (e.g., 1.21 vs. 1.58 bpsp on SCID, 3.33 vs. 3.61 on BRACS24). This suggests that as LLM intelligence continues to grow, compression performance may improve in tandem (citing the linear intelligence-compression relationship of Huang et al., 2024), potentially making LLM-based codecs a viable long-term strategy for bandwidth-constrained offline storage. However, the ~273-second decoding time per image (vs. 0.08 s for JPEG-XL) severely limits practical applicability, and the paper acknowledges that balancing effectiveness and efficiency remains an open challenge. Whether the approach scales favorably to larger LLMs or can be accelerated via speculative decoding or distillation is an important open question.
