## Problem

Monte Carlo simulations for epidemic inference on contact networks require hundreds to thousands of runs to achieve statistically reliable node-level infection estimates, incurring prohibitive computational costs. No prior work has theoretically characterized how network structure (degree, diameter, initial infection fraction) governs the variance of these MC estimators, nor has a scalable alternative been proposed that matches MC accuracy at single-simulation runtime.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a directed (or symmetrized undirected) contact network as an adjacency matrix, scalar transmission and recovery probabilities β and γ (assumed small, β≪1, γ≪1), and an initial infected node set I₀ with fraction α. It outputs the converged steady-state probability vector [P_S(∞), P_I(∞), P_R(∞)] for every node, representing the final susceptible/infected/recovered distribution under a non-reinfection SIR process. Unlike spatio-temporal graph forecasting, no historical observation sequence is required; the entire trajectory is determined by the initial conditions and epidemic parameters.

## Method

RAPID (Residual-Aware Propagation for Infection Dynamics) accelerates the nonlinear SIR message-passing update by (1) locally linearizing the infection dynamics via the approximation log(1−x)≈−x, yielding a linear expression where a node's new infection probability depends only on the weighted sum of neighbor infection probabilities; (2) defining a propagation residual R_res(i)=βΣ_j A_ji(P_I^j−P̃_I^j) that captures the first-order Jacobian-induced perturbation from neighbors; and (3) maintaining a max-heap priority queue so that only nodes whose residual exceeds a threshold ε are updated, with the resulting change δ linearly propagated to out-neighbors. A short preheating phase (p full rounds) initializes states before the residual-driven asynchronous loop begins. The algorithm terminates when the heap empties, and convergence is guaranteed because Theorem 3.3 shows that in any non-reinfection model the maximum infected probability vanishes asymptotically, forcing all residuals to zero. The worst-case complexity is O(N·k̄·min(1/ε, 1/β)), empirically observed as (Nk̄)^0.74, versus O(|E|) per synchronous round for the unaccelerated PID baseline.

## Results

RAPID achieves the lowest MAE across all six datasets, e.g., 2.64×10⁻² on carilion-Hospital vs. 13.01×10⁻² (MC-5), 10.78×10⁻² (MC-10), and 5.67×10⁻² (PID). In terms of runtime, RAPID delivers an average speedup of 5.11× over MC-5, 10.67× over MC-10, and 8.52× over PID, with the largest speedup (12.91×) over PID on the dense soc-Epinions graph. On the largest dataset (soc-Pokec, 1.63M nodes), RAPID completes inference in 328 s while matching or exceeding the accuracy of 50-run Monte Carlo (MAE 2.32 vs. MC-50's implicit ground truth), at a cost comparable to a single MC run. The empirical runtime scales as (N·k̄)^0.74, better than the worst-case O(N·k̄·min(1/ε, 1/β)) bound from Theorem 4.1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RAPID outperforms all baselines on MAE across every dataset. Against MC-5, RAPID reduces MAE by 49–75% (e.g., 2.64 vs. 13.01 on carilion-Hospital); against MC-10, the gap narrows but RAPID still wins by 30–60%. Against PID (the unaccelerated synchronous message-passing baseline), RAPID achieves 2.1–5.1× lower MAE, with the largest gap on carilion-Hospital (2.64 vs. 5.67) and hiv-Trans (1.27 vs. 6.43), where short loops degrade PID's synchronous updates. In runtime, RAPID is 5.11× faster than MC-5, 10.67× faster than MC-10, and 8.52× faster than PID on average; the speedup over PID is most pronounced on dense graphs (12.91× on soc-Epinions, 12.36× on soc-Pokec) because PID must update all N nodes every round while RAPID only activates nodes whose residual exceeds the threshold. Centrality-based baselines (Degree, Eigenvector, PageRank, Betweenness, Closeness) serve as structural sanity checks and are consistently outperformed by RAPID on Kendall-τ rank correlation, confirming that epidemic inference requires modeling the stochastic propagation dynamics rather than relying on static topology alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) the message-passing formulation of epidemic dynamics on networks (Karrer & Newman, 2010), which provides the exact SIR update equations used as the foundation for both the PID baseline and RAPID; (2) the linearization of nonlinear epidemic dynamics (Pastor-Satorras & Vespignani, 2001), adapted here to derive a first-order approximation that makes the propagation structure explicit; and (3) residual-driven local push algorithms from the Personalized PageRank literature (Andersen et al., 2006), whose max-heap selective-update mechanism is repurposed to accelerate epidemic inference. The key conceptual departure is the combination: the paper shows that the Jacobian of the linearized infection dynamics naturally yields a propagation residual, unifying the epidemic message-passing framework with the PPR push paradigm and recovering the classical epidemic threshold as a stability condition of the resulting linear system.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: RAPID matches the accuracy of 50-run Monte Carlo simulations (the standard for reliable epidemic estimates) while running in roughly the time of a single MC run, a 40–1150× speedup across six real-world networks. The theoretical variance lower bound for MC estimators is, to the authors' knowledge, the first such result in the epidemic inference literature, providing principled guidance on how many simulation runs are actually needed. However, the method is restricted to non-reinfection models (SIR, SEIR, SIRD) and assumes sparse or locally tree-like network structure for its accuracy guarantees; extensions to reinfection dynamics, time-varying parameters, and dynamic networks remain open. For the broader graph-inference community, the Jacobian-residual connection and the recovery of the epidemic threshold from a push-based perspective offer a clean template for accelerating other nonlinear propagation processes.
