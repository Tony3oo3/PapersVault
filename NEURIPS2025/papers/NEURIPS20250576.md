## Problem

Language model development relies on small-scale experiments (e.g., 60M–1B parameter models) to make decisions about large-scale training, yet prior work shows these scaling procedures only work reliably for some benchmarks and not others. There was no principled, cheaply computable framework to determine which benchmarks are trustworthy for small-to-large extrapolation, leaving practitioners without guidance on whether a given evaluation provides useful signal or is dominated by noise.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes two distinct types of evaluation data: (1) final-checkpoint scores across a population of models trained under similar compute budgets (e.g., 25 models on different pretraining corpora), used to compute signal, and (2) scores from the final n intermediate training checkpoints of a single model, used to compute noise. The output is a per-benchmark scalar SNR that predicts two downstream quantities: decision accuracy (pairwise rank agreement between small and large model populations) and scaling law prediction error (relative error when fitting a power-law to small models to predict a 13B target). Notably, signal and noise are measured from orthogonal sources—across models vs. within a single training run—making the SNR a composite of inter-model separability and intra-model stability.

## Method

The paper defines signal as relative dispersion (max pairwise score difference divided by the mean score across a model population) and noise as the relative standard deviation of the final n training checkpoints of a single model, then shows their ratio (SNR) correlates strongly with decision accuracy (R=0.791) and that noise alone correlates with scaling law prediction error (R=0.653). Three interventions directly manipulate SNR: (i) greedily filtering subtasks by per-subtask SNR to construct higher-quality benchmark subsets (e.g., 16 of 57 MMLU subtasks outperform the full set), (ii) averaging scores over the final k checkpoints to reduce step-to-step noise, which improves decision accuracy by +2.4% on the 30-task average, and (iii) replacing discrete metrics (accuracy, pass@1) with bits-per-byte (negative log-likelihood of the correct continuation normalized by UTF-8 bytes), which raises SNR dramatically for generative tasks (e.g., GSM8K from 1.2 to 7.0) and improves decision accuracy on 90% of benchmarks. The framework is validated across 465 models (60M–32B parameters) on 30 benchmarks, producing a publicly released dataset of 900K evaluation results totaling 200M instances.

## Results

The paper evaluates 465 language models (60M–32B parameters) on 30 benchmarks, producing 900K evaluation results (200M instances). The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) correlates strongly with decision accuracy (R=0.791, R²=0.626) and noise correlates with scaling law prediction error (R=0.653, R²=0.426). Three interventions improve reliability: averaging checkpoint scores boosts 30-task decision accuracy from 68.9% to 71.3% and reduces scaling law error from 1.03% to 0.86%; filtering high-SNR subtasks adds +2.6% (MMLU) and +5% (AutoBencher) decision accuracy; and switching to bits-per-byte (BPB) improves decision accuracy for 90% of benchmarks and reduces scaling law error for 73.3%, with dramatic gains on generative tasks (e.g., GSM8K SNR from 1.2 to 7.0, MBPP from 2.0 to 41.8).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a framework/empirical paper without a single competing method, so comparisons are between evaluation strategies. Switching from primary metrics (accuracy, exact match, pass@1) to bits-per-byte (BPB) consistently improves SNR across nearly all 30 benchmarks, with the largest gains on generative tasks where small models score near random chance (e.g., GSM8K SNR 1.2→7.0, Minerva MATH 1.9→88.6, MBPP 2.0→41.8). Averaging the final k training checkpoints outperforms using only the final checkpoint for decision accuracy in 28 of 30 tasks (30-task average: 68.9%→71.3%) and reduces scaling law prediction error in 20 of 30 tasks (e.g., GSM8K: 7.46%→3.85%, Math 6-Task Avg: 11.33%→2.30%). Filtering subtasks by SNR outperforms both the full benchmark and random subtask selection: a 16-subtask MMLU subset achieves higher SNR and +2.6% decision accuracy over the full 14-subtask set, and a 6-subtask AutoBencher subset yields +5% decision accuracy. Among noise measures, checkpoint-to-checkpoint noise (final n checkpoints) correlates most tightly with total training-run variation (R²=0.95) and with seed noise (R²=0.82) and data-order noise (R²=0.86), validating it as a cheap proxy for all modeling noise sources.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the DataDecide framework (Magnusson et al.) for small-to-large decision accuracy and the compute-efficient model ladders from Bhagia et al. for scaling law prediction, using both as experimental testbeds. It departs from prior SNR work (Madaan et al., EvalArena) by replacing expensive multi-seed noise estimation with a cheap checkpoint-to-checkpoint noise measure, and by defining signal as cross-model score dispersion rather than within-model variance. The paper also connects to the broader benchmark-quality literature (Kydliček et al., MMLU Redux) but reframes quality in terms of predictive utility for development decisions rather than annotation accuracy or reconstruction fidelity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a simple, low-cost diagnostic (SNR from final checkpoints) that practitioners can compute without additional training runs, enabling them to identify which benchmarks will actually support reliable small-scale decisions. The finding that switching to bits-per-byte yields up to 35× SNR improvement on generative code tasks (MBPP: 2.0→41.8) is immediately actionable and likely to change how development evaluations are conducted. Limitations include coverage of only two prediction settings (decision accuracy, scaling law error) and only training-time noise (not evaluation-configuration noise), and the framework's applicability to emergent capabilities or other small-to-large phenomena remains untested. The released dataset of 900K evaluation results across 465 models and 30 benchmarks provides a valuable public resource for future evaluation methodology research.
