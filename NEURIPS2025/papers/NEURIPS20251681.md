## Problem

In mixed-motive multi-agent reinforcement learning, existing reward-restructuring methods (gifting, intrinsic motivation, inequity aversion) promote cooperation but do not explicitly ensure fairness in agents' task-defined extrinsic rewards, and gradient-space approaches like AgA focus on collective-objective stability without resolving individual–collective gradient conflicts or providing fairness guarantees. The paper addresses the open question of how to simultaneously improve collective performance and guarantee that individual returns across agents converge to equal values.

## Input / Output

At each update step, FCGrad consumes two policy gradients (g_ind and g_col) computed via PPO from two separate value heads (sharing a common CNN encoder) and the corresponding scalar value estimates V_ind(θ) and V_col(θ). The output is a single adjusted gradient vector that is either a convex combination (non-conflict) or a projected gradient (conflict), which is then applied to update the shared policy parameters θ with a fixed step size η. The value comparison V_ind vs. V_col determines which gradient is projected, making the method state-dependent at each iteration.

## Method

FCGrad first checks whether the individual and collective gradients are in conflict via their inner product. In the non-conflict case (⟨g_ind, g_col⟩ ≥ 0), it applies a weighted sum (1−β)g_ind + βg_col. In the conflict case, it identifies which objective has the lower current value and projects that gradient onto the normal plane of the other (e.g., if V_ind < V_col, it computes g_ind − ⟨g_col, g_ind⟩/‖g_col‖² · g_col), thereby preserving the disadvantaged objective's ascent direction while eliminating interference with the other. This fairness-aware selection rule—prioritizing the lower-valued objective—is the key distinction from PCGrad, which averages both projections. The paper proves that under L-smoothness, the update yields monotonic non-decreasing improvement in both objectives, and under a conflict-recurrence assumption with Robbins–Monro step sizes, the value gap |V_ind − V_col| converges to zero, implying all agents' individual returns equalize.

## Results

FCGrad achieves the best Gini coefficient (0.010) and Jain's index (0.999) in the Unfair Coin environment, and the best Gini (0.223) and Jain's index (0.835) in Cleanup, outperforming all six baselines on both fairness metrics. In Harvest, FCGrad attains a Gini of 0.093 and Jain's index of 0.959, slightly trailing IA (Gini 0.087, Jain 0.973) but leading all other methods. Across all three environments, FCGrad delivers the highest α-fairness returns at α=1 (GeoMean) and α→∞ (Min), while matching the collective-reward baseline (Col) on α=0 (Mean), confirming that fairness is achieved without sacrificing aggregate performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against PCGrad [Yu et al., 2020], FCGrad wins decisively on fairness in all three environments: Gini drops from 0.039→0.010 (Coin), 0.469→0.223 (Cleanup), and 0.101→0.093 (Harvest), while Jain's index improves from 0.994→0.999, 0.572→0.835, and 0.965→0.959. The key difference is that FCGrad prioritizes the lower-valued objective during conflicts, whereas PCGrad simply averages both projected gradients, which can still disadvantage the weaker agent. Against AgA [Li et al., 2024], FCGrad dominates on every metric in all environments (e.g., Gini 0.238→0.010 in Coin, 0.331→0.223 in Cleanup), and AgA also struggles to learn effectively in Cleanup and Harvest due to its Hessian-based stability criterion. Against IA [Hughes et al., 2018], FCGrad achieves substantially better fairness in Coin (Gini 0.122→0.010) and Cleanup (Gini 0.536→0.223), though IA narrowly edges out FCGrad in Harvest (Gini 0.087 vs. 0.093; Jain 0.973 vs. 0.959), likely because IA's reward-shaping approach is well-suited to the intertemporal structure of that task. Overall, FCGrad is the only method that simultaneously achieves top-tier fairness and competitive collective performance across all three environments.

## Relation to Prior Work

FCGrad adapts the gradient projection mechanism from PCGrad (Yu et al., 2020) in multi-task learning to the multi-agent RL setting, adding a fairness-aware selection rule that prioritizes the gradient of the lower-valued objective during conflicts. It departs from the dominant reward-restructuring paradigm (gifting, inequity aversion, social influence) that reshapes individual rewards to induce cooperation, instead operating directly in gradient space. The closest prior work, AgA (Li et al., 2024), also adjusts policy gradients to reconcile individual and collective objectives but relies on Hessian information to steer toward stable fixed points without explicitly resolving gradient conflicts or providing fairness guarantees.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper fills a concrete gap: prior mixed-motive MARL methods improve collective welfare but can produce systematically unfair individual outcomes (e.g., one agent perpetually cleaning waste while others harvest), and FCGrad provides the first gradient-level method with a provable guarantee that individual and collective objectives converge to the same value. The practical simplicity (first-order projection, no Hessian) makes it easy to plug into existing PPO/IPPO pipelines. Key open questions include whether the conflict-recurrence assumption needed for the convergence theorem holds in larger or more heterogeneous agent populations, and whether the approach extends to continuous action spaces or settings with more than a few agents where gradient conflicts may be more complex.
