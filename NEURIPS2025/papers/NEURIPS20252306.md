## Problem

Existing LLM IP protection methods either require white-box access to model parameters or rely on carefully crafted queries that are fragile under fine-tuning. This paper identifies that the statistical structure of how models *fail* (their error patterns) is more stable and family-specific than their correct outputs, yet no prior work has exploited this for black-box traceability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252306/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a prompt dataset of ~50K benchmark samples (from MultiNLI, PAWS, CoLA, AG News, CommonsenseQA) and queries each model in black-box fashion to collect binary error/correct labels per sample. For a suspect model, only ~10K query-output pairs are needed at audit time. The output is a per-family cosine distance score plus a Model Family Discrepancy (MFD) value; the family with the smallest distance is the attribution, and an MFD below threshold τ=0.025 flags the model as out-of-distribution.

## Method

ErrorTrace operates in three stages. First, it computes an error uniqueness score for each data point by combining intra-family error uniqueness (accuracy-weighted proportion of family models that err) with a cross-family discrepancy penalty (squared error rates of non-target families). Second, it constructs a graph-based error space per family: data points are nodes, edges are weighted by CDF-normalized Euclidean distances with an interval-based refinement, and BFS from the highest-uniqueness point grows the space while pruning low-uniqueness connections. Third, for a suspect model, it progressively contracts each family's error space (raising the minimum uniqueness threshold) and tracks the change in the suspect's weighted error rate relative to the space's theoretical rate; the family showing the largest deviation Δ is identified as the source, while Δ < τ indicates an out-of-distribution model. The key distinction from prior work is that traceability relies on the *structure of shared failures* rather than specific query responses or parameter access, making it robust to fine-tuning, pruning, and merging.

## Results

In the model-set-unknown scenario (the more challenging setting where the suspect model is excluded from error space construction), ErrorTrace achieves 85.18% traceability accuracy (23/27 models), outperforming LLMmap's 59.25% (16/27) by 25.93 percentage points. In the model-set-known scenario, ErrorTrace achieves 100% accuracy across all 27 models from four families (LLaMA, Qwen, Mistral, Gemma) spanning 3B–141B parameters. For out-of-distribution detection, all three unseen models (Falcon3-7B, Falcon3-10B, DeepSeek-R1-7B) are correctly flagged with MFD values below the τ=0.025 threshold. Under adversarial attacks, ErrorTrace retains 0.70 accuracy after an attacker repairs the Top-500 highest-uniqueness samples and maintains 0.67 even after altering 3,000 lowest-uniqueness samples.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LLMmap [18], the primary black-box baseline, ErrorTrace achieves 85.18% vs. 59.25% accuracy in the unknown-model-set scenario, a 25.93-point gap. The advantage is especially pronounced on fine-tuned models: ErrorTrace correctly identifies all five fine-tuned Mistral7B variants (Table 6) and all five Qwen-family variants (Table 9), while LLMmap misclassifies 5/5 and 3/5 respectively, with some misclassifications differing by only 0.001 cosine distance. On pruned models (Table 7), ErrorTrace achieves 6/6 correct attributions versus LLMmap's 4/6, including the 80%-pruned Sheared-LLaMA-1.3B that LLMmap fails. Against traditional clustering methods for error space construction (Table 3), ErrorTrace's graph-based BFS approach (85.18%) substantially outperforms GMM (66.66%), DBSCAN (44.44%), and K-Means (40.74%). TRAP [8] and ProFLingo [11] are evaluated in the appendix: TRAP shows extreme TPR variance across fine-tuned LLaMA7B variants (ranging from 0.04 to 0.90), and ProFLingo's TRR for some fine-tuned Mistral models (e.g., Zephyr at 0.04) falls below that of unrelated models, making reliable decision boundaries difficult to establish.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the three established paradigms of LLM IP traceability—parameter-modifying watermarks, parameter-access fingerprinting, and query-based black-box methods (TRAP, ProFLingo, LLMmap)—by introducing error-pattern analysis as a fundamentally different black-box signal. Rather than relying on crafted prompts or internal weights, ErrorTrace repurposes the statistical structure of model failures (which data points a family consistently gets wrong) as a fingerprint, drawing on graph-based construction (BFS with CDF-normalized edge weights) rather than the clustering methods (K-Means, DBSCAN, GMM) used in prior space-construction work. The probabilistic distinguishability framework in Section 3 provides a lightweight theoretical grounding that prior query-based methods lack, while the family-level granularity (vs. model-level) is a deliberate practical departure from most prior fingerprinting work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ErrorTrace addresses a practically critical gap: protecting open-source LLM IP without requiring white-box access or pre-embedded watermarks, which is the realistic setting for already-released models. Its demonstrated robustness to fine-tuning, 80% pruning, and model merging makes it one of the few black-box methods that survives real-world model modifications. Key open questions include scalability to families with very few base models (Gemma's 3-model case shows degradation), handling of large intra-family scale disparities (Mixtral:8x22B misidentification), and whether the 24 GPU-hour data collection cost per model remains acceptable as model counts grow. The method's reliance on a fixed set of benchmark datasets for error extraction also raises questions about generalizability to novel or domain-specific tasks.
