## Problem

While prior work has localized knowledge in UNet-based diffusion models (e.g., cross-attention for compositional information, self-attention for structure), Diffusion Transformer (DiT) architectures such as FLUX, SANA, and PixArt-α remain largely unexplored in this context. Without knowing which blocks encode specific knowledge—artistic styles, copyrighted characters, celebrities, or safety-related content—it is impossible to perform targeted model editing, personalization, or unlearning without disrupting unrelated capabilities.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pretrained DiT model and a set of paired prompts: knowledge-specific prompts (e.g., 'the Batman walking through a desert') and corresponding knowledge-agnostic prompts (e.g., 'a character walking through a desert'). It produces a ranked list of transformer blocks, from which the top-K are selected as the dominant carriers of the target knowledge. For downstream applications, the output is a partially fine-tuned DiT where only those K blocks are updated (K=9 of 28 for personalization, K=5 of 28 for unlearning on PixArt-α), yielding generated images at 512×512 resolution. For MMDiT-based models like FLUX with a separate prompt branch, the method requires two forward passes per denoising step to overwrite the text-branch activations in the selected blocks.

## Method

The core localization signal is the attention contribution of target-knowledge text tokens to image tokens, computed as the L2 norm of the weighted sum of value projections across all attention heads at each block. For a given knowledge κ, the method generates images from N diverse prompts containing κ, computes per-token attention contributions at every layer, averages across prompts and seeds to obtain a per-block score, and selects the top-K blocks with the highest aggregated contribution. Verification is performed by replacing the cross-attention inputs (or, for MMDiT models, the text-branch inputs) in the selected blocks with knowledge-agnostic prompts and confirming that the target knowledge is suppressed in the output while surrounding content is preserved. The method is both model-agnostic (works across cross-attention DiTs like PixArt-α/SANA and MMDiT architectures like FLUX) and knowledge-agnostic (applies uniformly to styles, celebrities, copyrighted characters, landmarks, animals, and safety content). Building on this localization, the paper restricts DreamBooth-style personalization fine-tuning and Kumari-style concept unlearning to only the identified K blocks, achieving 15–20% speedup, ~30% memory reduction, and better preservation of unrelated generation quality compared to full-model fine-tuning.

## Results

For concept unlearning on PixArt-α (K=5 of 28 blocks), localized fine-tuning achieves a CLIP score of 0.2293 and CLIP accuracy of 0.55 for target erasure, substantially outperforming random block selection (0.2526 / 0.73) and bottom-K selection (0.2636 / 0.82). For model personalization (K=9 of 28 blocks), localized DreamBooth outperforms full-model fine-tuning on prompt alignment, identity preservation, and FID while using less memory and training time. The method also delivers a 15–20% training speedup and approximately 30% reduction in memory usage for unlearning compared to full-model fine-tuning, and is ~28× faster than brute-force block-window search on PixArt-α.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two primary baselines: full-model DreamBooth fine-tuning [28] for personalization and full-model concept ablation [16] for unlearning. Against full-model DreamBooth, localized fine-tuning (K=9/28 blocks) wins on every reported metric—prompt alignment, surrounding identity preservation, and FID—while also reducing memory usage and training time. Against full-model unlearning (Kumari et al.), localized unlearning (K=5/28 blocks) achieves comparable target-concept erasure (similar CLIP score drop) but clearly wins on preservation of surrounding identities, anchor alignment, and overall FID, while being 15–20% faster and using ~30% less memory. The paper additionally validates its localization signal against a brute-force contiguous-window search, showing comparable CLIP/CSD drops (0.0194 vs. 0.0232 CLIP; 0.0700 vs. 0.0812 CSD) at 28× lower computational cost. In the block-selection ablation, Top-K consistently dominates both Random and Bottom-K policies by a wide margin on both prompt-intervention and fine-tuning-based unlearning metrics, confirming that the attention-contribution signal identifies genuinely knowledge-bearing blocks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the knowledge localization line of work from UNet-based diffusion models (Basu et al. 2024, Hertz et al. 2022, Tumanyan et al. 2023) to the now-dominant Diffusion Transformer (DiT) architecture. The core localization signal—attention contribution from the transformer circuits framework (Elhage et al. 2021)—is repurposed here as a model- and knowledge-agnostic probe across DiT blocks, rather than being tied to a specific architecture's cross-attention or self-attention modules. For downstream applications, the paper adapts DreamBooth (Ruiz et al. 2023) and concept ablation (Kumari et al. 2023) by restricting fine-tuning to only the localized blocks, turning a full-model procedure into a targeted one.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As DiTs (FLUX, SANA, PixArt-α) replace UNets as the standard text-to-image backbone, understanding where knowledge is encoded becomes essential for safe deployment, personalization, and efficient adaptation. The paper's finding that knowledge distribution varies dramatically across architectures—highly concentrated in SANA's 10 blocks versus diffuse across PixArt-α's 28—underscores that localization methods must be architecture-adaptive. Open questions include automatically estimating the required number of blocks K without external metrics, and the absence of ground-truth benchmarks for validating localization. The LOCK dataset (55k+ prompts across six categories) provides a useful resource for future interpretability research in generative models.
