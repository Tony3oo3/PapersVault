## Problem

Imitation learning in long-horizon, sparse-reward environments suffers from compounding errors when the agent deviates from expert trajectories, and existing methods (distribution matching, reward shaping, offline pretraining) perform flat alignment without reasoning about which parts of the task the agent has already mastered. Prior curriculum approaches that reset the agent to arbitrary intermediate demonstration states are impractical in real-world settings where physical conditions like joint velocities cannot be precisely replicated.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a small set of expert demonstrations (10–20 trajectories per task) and observation inputs that are either low-dimensional state vectors or RGB images (64×64×3 for MetaWorld, 100×100×3 for Adroit/ManiSkill). At training time it produces continuous robot actions via a goal-conditioned policy; at test time, a learned goal predictor P_φ maps a single current observation to a predicted final goal state, which conditions the policy for autonomous execution without access to demonstrations.

## Method

Cago maintains a visitation-frequency dictionary that tracks how often the agent reaches each observation along a sampled demonstration trajectory, then identifies the capability boundary j* (the last index where visitation count exceeds threshold λ_visit) and samples intermediate goals from a window of size δ·L_i centered at j*. Each episode follows a Go-Explore structure: the goal-conditioned policy π^G drives the agent toward the sampled goal (Go-phase), then a behavior-cloning explorer π^E takes over for the remaining steps (Explore-phase), keeping collected trajectories anchored near the demonstration distribution. A Dreamer-based world model is trained on the resulting dataset D_cap, and the goal-conditioned policy is further refined via imagined rollouts rewarded by a self-supervised temporal-distance function that estimates steps-to-goal. A separate goal predictor P_φ, trained by MSE on demonstration trajectories, enables test-time goal inference for unseen initial states, and a theoretical analysis (Theorem 1) bounds the model prediction error under the BC-explorer data collection scheme.

## Results

On the MetaWorld very-hard tasks, Cago achieves final success rates of 0.99 (StickPush), 0.80 (Disassemble), and 0.82 (Adroit-Pen), substantially outperforming the same-architecture Dreamer baseline (0.68, 0.02, 0.52 respectively). On the ManiSkill benchmark, Cago is the only method among all baselines to achieve meaningful success rates on PegInsertionSide, StackCube, and PullCubeTool. The method uses only 10 demonstrations (MetaWorld/Adroit) or 20 (ManiSkill) and is evaluated over 8 random seeds with 100 held-out test seeds per environment.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Cago outperforms Dreamer (its model-based backbone) by large margins across all tested environments (e.g., 0.80 vs. 0.02 on Disassemble), confirming that capability-aware goal sampling is the key driver of performance. Against PEG (a goal-selection strategy within Dreamer), Cago wins on all three environments in Table 2. On the broader Figure 3 comparison, Cago consistently surpasses JSRL in both convergence speed and final success rate, and overtakes MoDem after ~1e6 steps on Adroit tasks despite MoDem's faster early learning from BC pretraining. Cal-QL is outperformed on all MetaWorld very-hard tasks. Among the additional imitation learning baselines (GAIL, PWIL, SQIL, ValueDice, RLPD) in Figure 9, Cago wins on nearly all tasks; GAIL is the sole exception, achieving the best result on ShelfPlace. On the Fetch environments (Table 1), Cago outperforms both ACED (1.00 vs. 0.93 on Fetch-PickPlace) and Task Phasing (1.00 vs. 0.75 on Fetch-PickPlace; 0.48 vs. 0.20 on Fetch-Slide). In the ManiSkill environments, Cago is the only method to achieve non-trivial success while all baselines fail entirely.

## Relation to Prior Work

Cago builds on the Dreamer model-based RL framework (RSSM world model) and the Go-Explore two-phase exploration paradigm, combining them with a self-supervised temporal-distance reward (Mendonca et al., 2021) for goal-conditioned policy training. Its key departure from prior demonstration-based methods is conceptual: rather than using demonstrations for direct imitation (BC, GAIL), reward recovery (IRL), distribution matching (PWIL, ValueDice), offline anchoring (Cal-QL, RLPD), or state-reset curricula (JSRL, Backplay, Task Phasing), Cago treats demonstrations as structured roadmaps and dynamically samples intermediate goals at the boundary of the agent's current reaching capability, tracked via a simple visitation-frequency dictionary. This avoids the impractical assumption of resetting to arbitrary intermediate demonstration states, requiring only initial-state resets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Cago offers a practical, self-adaptive curriculum mechanism for long-horizon sparse-reward manipulation that does not require hand-crafted difficulty metrics or arbitrary state resets, making it more applicable to real-world robotics where exact state reproduction is infeasible. The robustness to suboptimal and failed demonstrations (up to 50% failure) is notable and suggests the method could work with imperfect human-collected data. Key limitations include the residual dependence on initial-state resets, the reliance on simple L2/MSE similarity metrics that may be suboptimal for highly complex or semantically rich tasks, and the lack of evaluation in truly real-world (non-simulated) settings. Open questions include whether the capability-tracking signal generalizes to multi-agent coordination, highly stochastic dynamics, and tasks where the goal space is not identical to the state space.
