## Problem

Existing MGT detection methods implicitly assume binary labels are a 'golden standard,' but the boundary between machine-generated and human-generated text is inherently ambiguous due to human-machine collaboration and LLMs producing human-like text, making traditional supervised training inexact. Compounding this, human cognition limitations and detector superintelligence make it difficult to reliably assess detection quality, rendering inexact supervision widespread and inevitable rather than a limited random-label-noise problem. The paper asks whether effective learning is possible when labels are inexact and detection quality is hard to evaluate.

## Input / Output

The target detector receives a single natural text (sentence or paragraph) and outputs a binary MGT/HGT probability score, operating on the original unmodified data distribution. The auxiliary supervisor receives a concatenated longer text formed by splicing k same-class texts together, with the detector's per-text predictions modulating the input via Gumbel-Softmax gating at the embedding level. The supervisor is a lightweight three-layer fully connected network, while the detector retains its original architecture (e.g., RADAR, MPU, or ChatGPT-D), so inference-time cost is unchanged.

## Method

The framework couples a lightweight 'easy' supervisor (trained on longer concatenated texts) with a 'hard' target detector (trained on natural texts) through a structural gating mechanism: the detector's per-text predictions, passed through Gumbel-Softmax, scale each text's embedding before concatenation, so misclassified texts are effectively filtered out, reducing the concatenation length k. Theoretically, longer texts amplify the total variation distance between MGT and HGT distributions (Theorem 3.1), and collapsing the HGT distribution to a Dirac δ₀ further maximizes this gap (Theorem 3.3), making the supervisor's task easier and its supervision more reliable. Because k is positively correlated with detector accuracy, maximizing supervisor performance indirectly optimizes the detector, with the supervisor's AUROC serving as a theoretical lower bound on detector capability (Theorem 3.2). Training jointly minimizes the detector's original cross-entropy loss plus a weighted supervisor loss (Eq. 5), and Theorem 3.4 shows that at the optimal supervisor, the detector converges to underlying continuous 'golden' labels representing the proportion of purely machine-generated content.

## Results

On the DetectRL dataset (paragraph-level, TPR@FPR-1%), the enhanced RADAR-E achieves 74.27% average across four LLMs (PaLM, ChatGPT, Claude, Llama-2), improving over RADAR's 72.35%. On the Essay dataset (paragraph-level), RADAR-E reaches 97.10% on GPT4All-generated text. The framework consistently improves all three model-based detectors: RADAR-E gains +1.92 pp over RADAR, MPU-E gains +3.60 pp over MPU, and ChatGPT-E gains +3.02 pp over ChatGPT-D on DetectRL paragraph-level averages. Training overhead is minimal (e.g., RADAR-E adds only 2.2 s over RADAR on Essay), and inference time is unchanged.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All three model-based detectors (RADAR, MPU, ChatGPT-D) are consistently improved by the easy-to-hard framework: RADAR-E outperforms RADAR by +3.67 pp (sentence) and +1.92 pp (paragraph) on DetectRL averages, while MPU-E gains +1.96 pp (sentence) and +3.60 pp (paragraph) over MPU. The metric-based baselines (Likelihood, Entropy, DetectGPT, FastGPT, NPR) are substantially outperformed by all model-based methods; for example, the best metric-based method (Log-Rank) achieves only 20.76% paragraph-level average versus 74.27% for RADAR-E. The paper also shows that knowledge distillation using RADAR as teacher actually degrades student model performance in most settings, whereas the proposed structural integration approach yields consistent gains. On the Essay dataset, RADAR-E reaches 97.10% TPR@FPR-1% on GPT4All text (paragraph-level), compared to 96.88% for RADAR, with larger relative gains on harder LLMs like Claude (93.76% vs. 88.53%). The framework also improves robustness under paraphrase attacks (Back Translation, Polish) and cross-domain generalization (arXiv, Writing Prompts, XSum, Yelp Reviews), with the largest improvements observed in mixed-text and cross-domain settings where label inexactness is most severe.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on Chakraborty et al. (ICML 2024), which established theoretical lower bounds linking text length to MGT detectability, and extends those results to upper bounds that guide the proposed framework's design. It deliberately departs from knowledge distillation (which assumes a stronger teacher guiding a weaker student) and noisy label learning (which assumes limited random annotation errors), arguing that MGT detection faces a fundamentally different challenge: systematic inexactness in labels due to human-machine boundary ambiguity, where the 'supervisor' (human) is weaker than the 'detector' (model). The key architectural departure is structurally coupling the detector into the supervisor via Gumbel-Softmax gating on concatenated text, rather than relying on soft-label transfer.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reframes a core assumption in MGT detection—perfect binary labels—as fundamentally violated, positioning inexact supervision as an inherent and unavoidable property of the task rather than a fixable data-quality issue. The plug-and-play framework (no detector architecture changes, negligible training overhead) makes it practically adoptable, and the demonstration that knowledge distillation actively harms MGT detectors challenges a common enhancement strategy. Open questions include extending the approach to metric-based detectors, defining more precise 'golden labels' beyond the proportion-of-machine-content approximation, and whether the structural coupling generalizes to other inexact-supervision problems beyond text detection.
