## Problem

Existing interactive imitation learning methods correct the agent's action only at the current intervened state, leaving the agent vulnerable to repeating similar mistakes in consecutive future steps and requiring repeated expert demonstrations in safety-critical regions. Additionally, these methods impose a heavy cognitive burden on human supervisors who must continuously monitor the agent and anticipate failures in real time, without any proactive visualization of the agent's upcoming trajectory.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251845/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sensory state vector (e.g., 259-dim for MetaDrive driving, 34-dim for Table Wiping, 51-dim for Nut Assembly) and, during training, the agent's proposed action and the human's corrective action at the intervention point. It produces a stochastic policy mapping states to control actions (2-dim steering/acceleration for driving, 6-dim end-effector pose for Table Wiping, 7-dim pose plus gripper for Nut Assembly). Unusually, the human corrective action sampled at the current state s is bootstrapped as the preferred action at L predicted future states s̃₁,…,s̃_L, so a single intervention generates multiple preference tuples across the forecasted horizon rather than a single state-action pair.

## Method

PPL introduces a trajectory prediction model f(s, a_n, H) that forecasts the agent's next H states (via simulator rollout or a kinematic bicycle model at ~3,000 fps), which is visualized for the human expert to enable proactive intervention before the agent enters dangerous states. When the expert intervenes at state s with corrective action a_h, PPL bootstraps this into L preference tuples (s̃_i, a⁺=a_h, a⁻=a_n) for i=1,…,L, where L is the preference horizon controlling how far into the predicted rollout the corrective intent is propagated. The policy is trained with a combined loss: behavioral cloning on expert demonstrations plus a Contrastive Preference Optimization (CPO) classification loss over the bootstrapped preference dataset, requiring no reference policy or reward function. A theoretical analysis (Theorem 4.1) bounds the performance gap J(π_h)−J(π_n) by O(√(ε+δ_pref)+δ_dist), showing that L should balance coverage of risky states (reducing δ_dist) against label correctness (controlling δ_pref), with empirical results confirming peak efficiency at L=4 for driving and L=6 for manipulation.

## Results

On the MetaDrive safety benchmark with a real human expert, PPL achieves a 76% test success rate and 0.90 route completion within 10K environment steps, using only 2.9K human interventions (29% intervention rate) — substantially outperforming the best baseline PVP (46% success, 4.9K interventions). With a neural expert proxy, PPL reaches 80% success rate using just 1.2K expert data and 6K total steps, compared to 10K steps for all baselines. On Robosuite, PPL attains 80% success on Table Wiping with only 0.2K expert data (vs. 58% for PVP with 2.3K) and 51% on Nut Assembly (vs. 35% for PVP), approaching the neural expert's 84% and 60% respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PPL outperforms all seven baselines across every task and metric. Against the closest competitor PVP [28], PPL improves success rate by 30 points (76% vs. 46%) in MetaDrive with real humans while using 41% fewer interventions (2.9K vs. 4.9K); with neural experts the gap widens to 24 points (80% vs. 56%) at half the expert data. Confidence-based IIL methods (Ensemble-DAgger [23], Thrifty-DAgger [12]) achieve only 36–49% success in MetaDrive and 48–58% on Robosuite, trailing PPL by 27–32 points. Among active-intervention IIL methods, IWR [22] (23% MetaDrive, 51% Table Wiping), EIL [42] (1% MetaDrive, 53% Table Wiping), and HACO [17] (11% MetaDrive, 48% Table Wiping) all underperform PPL by 25–75 points. Non-interactive baselines BC and GAIL [11] are far behind, with GAIL reaching only 14% success in MetaDrive and 37% on Table Wiping. On Nut Assembly, PPL (51%) still leads PVP (35%) by 16 points, though the gap to the neural expert (60%) is larger, suggesting the task's precision demands limit preference-horizon bootstrapping.

## Relation to Prior Work

PPL sits at the intersection of interactive imitation learning (IIL) and preference-based RL. It builds directly on the IIL lineage (HG-DAgger, PVP, HACO, IWR, EIL) where a human expert monitors and corrects an agent online, but departs from these methods by (1) visualizing predicted future trajectories to reduce the expert's cognitive load, and (2) converting each single intervention into L contrastive preference pairs over predicted future states rather than correcting only at the intervened state. The preference optimization objective is adapted from CPO/DPO-style losses originally developed for language model alignment, repurposed here for continuous control within an IIL loop. The trajectory prediction component draws on kinematic bicycle models from the autonomous driving literature, used as a lightweight real-time rollout surrogate.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PPL addresses a practical bottleneck in human-in-the-loop training: the expert must continuously anticipate failures and repeatedly correct the agent in consecutive risky states. By bootstrapping one intervention into a preference horizon, the method reduces both the number of required demonstrations and the cognitive burden, with a theoretical bound (Thm. 4.1) showing that the preference horizon L trades off state-coverage against label fidelity. Key limitations include the assumption that the expert always provides the optimal corrective action and that all experiments are simulation-only; real-robot deployment and handling of suboptimal or inconsistent human demonstrations remain open. The approach also assumes access to a reasonably accurate short-horizon trajectory predictor, which may be harder to obtain in highly stochastic or multi-agent physical environments.
