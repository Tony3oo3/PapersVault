## Problem

Existing neural operators (FNO, LNO, WNO) perform their core computations in Fourier, Laplace, or wavelet domains that deviate from the intrinsic PDE solution space, introducing systematic accuracy bias and scalability issues for high-dimensional problems. The paper asks whether a different integral transform can provide a domain that is both dimensionality-reducing and geometrically aligned with the PDE manifold, while also offering a stronger theoretical guarantee than the standard discrete invariance or continuous-discrete equivalence arguments used by prior work.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255247/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes 2D spatial fields (initial/boundary conditions and PDE coefficient fields) on regular grids (e.g., 421×421 down to 43×43) or structured meshes, and outputs the corresponding PDE solution field at the same spatial resolution. Internally, the spatial field is projected into a sinogram domain parameterized by angle θ ∈ [0, π) and signed distance s, where the number of projection angles A is a hyperparameter chosen relative to input size (A ≪ N for quasi-linear complexity). For irregular meshes, a simple zero-padding step makes the standard Radon transform applicable, though this introduces some redundancy that slightly degrades performance on the Pipe benchmark.

## Method

RNO replaces the kernel integral operator in the standard NO architecture with a Radon-transform-based block: the spatial field is forward-transformed into the sinogram domain, where a learnable angle-reweighting network (implemented as a sinogram-domain convolution sliding along the θ-axis) assigns adaptive weights to each projection angle, and the weighted sinogram is reconstructed back to the spatial domain via filtered back projection with a skip connection. The sinogram convolution operates on a fixed θ-grid that is decoupled from spatial resolution, enabling kernel sharing across resolutions and guaranteeing discrete invariance. A physics-attention mechanism (adapted from Transolver) is integrated to capture non-local features via learnable slice-based tokens. Theoretically, the paper proves the Radon operator is bilipschitz in L² (via the Fourier slice theorem and Plancherel's identity for the upper bound, and injectivity plus boundedness of the FBP inverse for the lower bound), which—combined with the NO layer structure—yields bilipschitz strong monotonicity under diffeomorphism, a stronger guarantee than prior discrete invariance results.

## Results

RNO achieves state-of-the-art relative L2 error on 5 out of 6 standard PDE benchmarks. On regular grids, RNO records 5.10×10⁻³ (Darcy), 8.94×10⁻² (Navier-Stokes), and 4.61×10⁻³ (Allen-Cahn), improving 10.5% and 13.2% over the second-best (Transolver) on Darcy and Allen-Cahn respectively. On structured meshes, RNO attains 4.90×10⁻³ (Airfoil, +14% over Transolver) and 1.15×10⁻³ (Plasticity), but trails Transolver on Pipe (4.20×10⁻³ vs 3.30×10⁻³). In zero-shot generalization from 43×43 to 421×421 resolution on Darcy, RNO outperforms FNO by 72–79% relative L2 reduction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RNO outperforms the strongest competitor, Transolver [43], on 5 of 6 benchmarks (Darcy, Navier-Stokes, Allen-Cahn, Airfoil, Plasticity) but loses on Pipe (4.20×10⁻³ vs 3.30×10⁻³), which the authors attribute to zero-padding redundancy for irregular meshes. Against ONO [44], RNO wins on all six benchmarks with margins ranging from ~1.5× (Navier-Stokes: 8.94×10⁻² vs 1.20×10⁻¹) to ~4.6× (Plasticity: 1.15×10⁻³ vs 4.80×10⁻³). Compared to FNO [23], RNO improves Darcy by 53% (5.10×10⁻³ vs 1.08×10⁻²) and Allen-Cahn by 39% (4.61×10⁻³ vs 7.52×10⁻³), while FNO does not support structured-mesh benchmarks. RNO also surpasses WMT [10], Galerkin [4], GNOT [11], U-NO [33], and DeepONet [27] across all benchmarks where they are applicable, with the largest gaps observed on Plasticity (RNO 1.15×10⁻³ vs GNOT 3.36×10⁻², a ~29× improvement) and Pipe (RNO 4.20×10⁻³ vs DeepONet 9.70×10⁻³, a ~2.3× improvement). In the generalization experiment (Table 2), RNO trained at 43×43 resolution achieves 72–79% lower relative L2 than FNO when zero-shot tested up to 421×421, confirming its discretization-invariance advantage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the neural operator framework (Kovachki et al., 2023) by replacing the standard kernel integral operator with one built on the Radon transform, positioning itself against the dominant Fourier (FNO), Laplace (LNO), and Wavelet (WNO) spectral/local transform approaches. It incorporates the physics-attention mechanism from Transolver (Wu et al., ICML 2024) for non-local feature capture, while its theoretical grounding draws on the bilipschitz/diffeomorphism discretization-invariance framework of Furuya et al. (2025). The key conceptual departure is the argument that Radon's line-integral geometry aligns more naturally with PDE solution spaces than frequency or wavelet bases, and that the sinogram domain enables a resolution-decoupled convolution on the θ-grid.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The bilipschitz proof provides a stronger theoretical guarantee than the discrete-invariance or continuous-discrete-equivalence arguments used by prior NOs, and the θ-grid decoupling offers a concrete mechanism for resolution-independent generalization (demonstrated at ~70–79% improvement over FNO across scales on Darcy flow). However, the generalization study is limited to a single PDE, the 2D restriction and zero-padding requirement for irregular meshes (causing a slight loss on Pipe) limit immediate applicability, and the Radon transform's GPU acceleration remains underdeveloped relative to FFT. Open questions include 3D extension, whether angle-reweighting generalizes beyond the tested benchmarks, and whether the quasi-linear complexity advantage holds at larger scales where A is no longer negligible relative to N.
