## Problem

Popular GML benchmarks for node property prediction are dominated by a narrow set of academic citation networks, covering only a few application domains and lacking diverse structural properties, non-homophilous graphs, and rich non-textual node features. This limits realistic evaluation of GNNs and graph foundation models, and leaves practically important questions—such as the impact of temporal distributional shifts and the inductive setting—largely unexplored.

## Input / Output

Each of the 14 GraphLand datasets provides a single (possibly directed) graph with 11K–3M nodes, where nodes carry 4–267 mixed numerical and categorical features and edges encode domain-specific relationships (user interactions, co-purchasing, road connectivity, etc.). Tasks span multiclass classification (up to 183 classes), binary classification (including strongly imbalanced fraud detection), and regression. Four data splits are provided per dataset—random low (10/10/80), random high (50/25/25), temporal high (50/25/25), and temporal high inductive (same split but with three graph snapshots)—enabling controlled comparison of how temporal shifts and the absence of future graph structure affect performance.

## Method

GraphLand is a benchmark of 14 diverse industrial graph datasets (web graphs, social networks, road networks, e-commerce, crowdsourcing, live-streaming) released in a unified format, several of which are newly collected from Yandex services. The paper introduces Neighborhood Feature Aggregation (NFA), a simple augmentation that appends mean/max/min statistics of one-hop neighbor features to each node's original features, allowing graph-agnostic models (ResMLP, LightGBM) to access limited graph information as a controlled comparison against full GNNs. Experiments compare four GNNs (GCN, GraphSAGE, GAT, Graph Transformer), three GBDT implementations (XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost), ResMLP, and four graph foundation models (OpenGraph, AnyGraph, TS-GNN, GCOPE) under all four splits. Key findings: attention-based GNNs outperform classic GNNs on most datasets; GBDTs with NFA are surprisingly strong baselines especially for regression; temporal splits are significantly harder than random splits; the inductive setting further degrades GNN performance; and current GFMs produce very weak results across all datasets.

## Results

On the RL (random low) split, attention-based GNNs (GAT, GT) achieve the best results on most datasets: GAT reaches 67.96% accuracy on hm-categories and 48.25% on web-topics, while GT leads on hm-categories at 69.23% and tolokers-2 at 54.50% AP. For regression, GAT achieves the highest R² on hm-prices (73.17) and web-traffic (84.68), while GCN leads on twitch-views (75.55). GBDT models with neighborhood feature aggregation (LightGBM-NFA) are surprisingly competitive, achieving the best R² on city-roads-M (61.00) and city-roads-L (55.26), and the best AP on city-reviews (78.33). All graph foundation models (OpenGraph, AnyGraph, TS-GNN, GCOPE) produce results far below classic GNNs and GBDTs, with AnyGraph scoring only 15.47% on hm-categories versus 69.23% for GT.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among GNNs, attention-based architectures (GAT, GT) consistently outperform classic message-passing GNNs (GCN, GraphSAGE) on most datasets, e.g., GAT beats GCN by 6.26 points on hm-categories (67.96 vs. 61.70) and by 1.80 on web-topics (48.25 vs. 46.45). GBDT models (XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost) without graph features are competitive but generally trail GNNs; however, adding neighborhood feature aggregation (NFA) to LightGBM makes it the strongest baseline on several regression datasets (city-roads-M: 61.00 R², city-roads-L: 55.26 R²) and on city-reviews (78.33 AP), surpassing all GNNs. The ResMLP baseline (He et al., 2016) is always substantially outperformed by its GNN counterparts with identical backbones, confirming that graph structure is beneficial across all 14 datasets. All four evaluated graph foundation models (OpenGraph, AnyGraph, TS-GNN, GCOPE) fail to produce competitive results, scoring 10–30 points below the best GNNs on classification tasks and failing to run on large datasets (TLE/MLE/RTE), highlighting that current GFMs are far from matching task-specific GNNs on diverse industrial graphs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground in GML benchmarking by assembling 14 diverse industrial graph datasets that go well beyond the citation-network-dominated landscape of OGB and classic benchmarks (cora, citeseer, pubmed). It repurposes underutilized open data (Avazu, H&M, Pokec, Twitch, Toloka) and introduces new industrial data (web graphs, art social networks, road networks, review services), while also contributing a simple neighborhood feature aggregation (NFA) technique to bridge graph-agnostic models and graph structure. The evaluation protocol extends prior work by introducing temporal data splits (TH, THI) that combine distributional shift with inductive settings, a combination previously absent from standard node property prediction benchmarks. The paper positions itself against the growing GFM literature by providing the first diverse, non-text-attributed testbed for evaluating whether these models truly generalize across domains with heterogeneous node feature sets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GraphLand addresses a critical practical gap: GML methods are routinely evaluated on a handful of academic citation networks despite being deployed across vastly different industrial domains. The finding that current GFMs fail to produce competitive results on diverse industrial data is a significant negative result that tempers the GFM hype and highlights the unsolved challenge of handling heterogeneous node feature sets. The observation that GBDTs augmented with simple NFA features can match or exceed GNNs on several datasets (especially regression) provides an important practical baseline that the GML community has underappreciated. Open questions include designing GML methods resilient to realistic temporal distributional shifts and dynamically evolving graphs, and building truly general GFMs that handle arbitrary numerical and categorical features — both directions the benchmark is explicitly designed to facilitate.
