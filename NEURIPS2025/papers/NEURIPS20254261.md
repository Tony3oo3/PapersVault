## Problem

Blind-spot denoising (BSD) for zero-shot image denoising faces a fundamental trade-off: real-world noise exhibits strong local spatial correlation that biases the BSD loss, while masking reduces the number of available predictive pixels. Existing remedies that include spatially distant pixels to reduce noise correlation simultaneously weaken pixel-value similarity, degrading prediction accuracy. The paper quantifies this trade-off theoretically and proposes a strategy that decouples the two objectives.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single noisy RGB image (no clean reference or external dataset required) and produces a denoised image at the same resolution. In practice, images range from 224×224 (fastMRI) to 512×512 (DND, FMDD), with 256×256 crops used for SIDD and synthetic benchmarks. The input is consumed in two stages: first, an auxiliary image is constructed via cross-scale patch matching (a one-time preprocessing step taking <1 s), and then the noisy image is repeatedly masked and refilled during per-image test-time training of a lightweight CNN.

## Method

The core contribution is a cross-scale non-local pixel refilling strategy for mask-based BSD. A Bayesian-risk analysis of a linear BSD model shows that, for a sufficiently large receptive field, reducing the noise correlation of refilled pixels (lowering λ̃ₙ) is more impactful than preserving their pixel-value similarity (lowering σ̃ᵥ²), because the noise-correlation term is amplified by the number of pixels M while the value-correlation term is not. Guided by this, the method constructs an auxiliary image by downsampling the noisy input (average pooling, factor 2), extracting overlapping 8×8 patches, matching each patch to its most similar counterpart at a different spatial location in the downsampled image, and averaging overlapping replacements. During training, a random Bernoulli mask selects pixels to be predicted; masked positions are filled with the corresponding auxiliary-image pixel (which is spatially distant yet structurally similar), while unmasked positions retain the original noisy value. The denoising network (a 0.5M-parameter D-BSN with dilated convolutions) is trained per-image with a BSD L1 loss on masked pixels combined with pixel-shuffle downsampling (stride 2) for additional noise decorrelation, plus a consistency regularization term that penalizes disagreement between full-resolution and downsampled-upsampled predictions.

## Results

On real-world denoising benchmarks, the proposed method achieves 36.32 dB / 0.874 SSIM on SIDD Val and 36.81 dB / 0.874 SSIM on SIDD Ben, ranking first among all zero-shot methods on both datasets. On DND it reaches 36.79 dB / 0.924 SSIM (within 0.12 dB of TTT-MIM's 36.91 dB), and on FMDD it scores 33.47 dB / 0.882 SSIM. On synthetic AWGN benchmarks (Kodak24, McMaster18, fastMRI), the method is competitive in PSNR and consistently achieves the best SSIM, e.g., 30.12 dB / 0.853 on McMaster18 (σ=25) and 32.61 dB / 0.783 on fastMRI (σ=18).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms the strongest zero-shot baseline BSN-INS by 1.01 dB on SIDD Val (36.32 vs. 35.31) and by 1.42 dB on SIDD Ben (36.81 vs. 35.39 ScoreDVI), establishing clear leadership on the two SIDD benchmarks. On DND, TTT-MIM edges ahead by 0.12 dB in PSNR (36.91 vs. 36.79) but does not report SSIM, while the proposed method leads all other zero-shot methods by at least 0.6 dB. On FMDD, BSN-INS (33.95 dB) and MASH (33.71 dB) slightly outperform the proposed method (33.47 dB), a gap the authors attribute to weaker cross-scale self-similarity in microscopy images. Among self-supervised methods that require external training data, the proposed zero-shot approach narrows the gap substantially: it reaches within 0.5–1.5 dB of top self-supervised BSD methods (APBSN, LG-BPN, TBSN) without any external dataset. On synthetic AWGN benchmarks, the method achieves the best SSIM across all settings (e.g., 0.853 on McMaster18 σ=25, 0.783 on fastMRI) while remaining within 0.5 dB of the best PSNR, confirming that the pixel refilling strategy is effective even when noise is spatially independent.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the zero-shot blind-spot denoising (BSD) paradigm, building directly on the D-BSN architecture from APBSN [22] and the pixel-shuffle downsampling (PD) technique from [31, 43], while adapting the consistency regularization from ZS-N2N [47]. Its key departure is the introduction of a cross-scale non-local pixel refilling strategy that repurposes the self-similarity principle from classic non-local means and BM3D denoising into the BSD framework, addressing the fundamental trade-off between noise decorrelation and pixel-value correlation that prior PD-based methods handle only implicitly. The Bayesian risk analysis for a linear BSD model provides a formal theoretical foundation that prior empirical BSD works lacked, quantifying when and why replacing masked pixels with distant-but-similar counterparts reduces prediction risk.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical characterization of the noise-correlation vs. pixel-similarity trade-off in BSD, offering principled guidance for designing refilling strategies beyond ad-hoc pixel shuffling. Its architecture-agnostic refilling strategy (demonstrated to work with both D-BSN and UNet backbones) could be integrated into existing BSD pipelines with minimal modification. However, the reliance on cross-scale self-similarity limits applicability to domains where this property holds (e.g., natural images), as evidenced by smaller gains on microscopy data (FMDD). Open questions include whether pre-trained models could reduce the per-image training cost of zero-shot BSD, and whether the theoretical insights generalize to non-linear deep networks beyond the linear analysis.
