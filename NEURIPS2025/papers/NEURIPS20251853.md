## Problem

SmoothGrad, the most widely used gradient smoothing method for model interpretability, introduces extra noise because its Gaussian sampling variance is set heuristically (σ = α·(x_max − x_min)), causing a significant fraction of samples to fall outside the valid input domain [x_min, x_max]. This out-of-range sampling injects additional noise into the smoothed gradients that the convolution kernel itself cannot remove, leaving saliency maps with residual artifacts that obscure fine-grained features.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained neural network (e.g., VGG16, ResNet50, InceptionV3) and an input image, and produces a smoothed gradient saliency map of the same spatial resolution as the input. The valid input domain [x_min, x_max] is determined per-dimension from the dataset's statistical range (e.g., 0 to 1 for normalized pixel values), and the per-dimension adaptive variance is computed on-the-fly from each pixel's distance to these boundaries. The output saliency map can be directly plugged into any gradient-based explanation method (Grad-CAM, Integrated Gradients, Gradient×Input, etc.) as a drop-in replacement for SmoothGrad.

## Method

The paper reformulates SmoothGrad as a Monte Carlo approximation of a convolution (G * p)(x), revealing that the sampling distribution p is defined over ℝ^D while the gradient function G is only meaningful over the bounded domain Ω = [x_min, x_max]. This domain mismatch means samples falling outside Ω contribute extra noise quantified as A^i = 1 − ∫_{x_min−x_i}^{x_max−x_i} p(t)dt. AdaptGrad eliminates this by computing a per-dimension variance σ_i via the inverse Gaussian error function such that the probability of out-of-range sampling is bounded by a user-specified confidence level c (e.g., 0.95): σ_i = min(|x_i − x_min|, |x_i − x_max|) / (√2 · erfinv((1+c)/2)). The resulting diagonal covariance matrix Σ_ag = diag(σ_1², …, σ_D²) ensures each dimension's sampling is adaptively constrained to the valid domain, with σ_i = 0 at boundary pixels. This adds only O(D) computational overhead over SmoothGrad while being model-agnostic and compatible with any gradient-based explanation pipeline.

## Results

On VGG16 (ImageNet), AdaptGrad achieves a Sparseness score of 0.5740 compared to SmoothGrad's 0.5289 and vanilla Gradient's 0.5583, indicating substantially less noise in the saliency map. When combined with other explanation methods, AdaptGrad consistently improves Sparseness: A-GI reaches 0.6821 vs. S-GI's 0.6137, and A-IG(B) reaches 0.6203 vs. S-IG(B)'s 0.5942. The same pattern holds on InceptionV3 (AG: 0.5584 vs. SG: 0.5369) and ResNet50 (AG: 0.5721 vs. SG: 0.5614). Computational overhead is minimal: AdaptGrad takes 0.6054s per image on VGG16 vs. SmoothGrad's 0.5914s.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AdaptGrad consistently outperforms SmoothGrad on Sparseness across all three models (VGG16, InceptionV3, ResNet50) and all five explanation method variants (Grad, GI, IG(W), IG(B)), with gains ranging from ~0.01 to ~0.07 in Sparseness. Against Gradient × Input [38], A-GI improves Sparseness by 0.068 over S-GI on VGG16 (0.6821 vs. 0.6137), confirming that adaptive sampling is more effective than uniform Gaussian smoothing for noise reduction. Against Integrated Gradients [42], A-IG(B) achieves 0.6203 Sparseness vs. S-IG(B)'s 0.5942 on VGG16, and A-IG(W) reaches 0.5901 vs. S-IG(W)'s 0.5814. Against NoiseGrad [9], AdaptGrad is compared visually (Figure 6) and shows clearer, more detailed saliency maps with less residual noise, while also being computationally cheaper since it does not perturb model parameters. On Faithfulness metrics, results are mixed: AdaptGrad sometimes slightly underperforms vanilla gradients (e.g., FI: 0.6748 vs. 0.6830 on VGG16), which the authors attribute to the known limitations of the insertion/deletion-based Faithfulness metric.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on SmoothGrad (Smilkov et al., 2017), the de facto standard for gradient noise reduction in model interpretability, by reinterpreting it through the lens of Monte Carlo integration and convolution. The key departure is a theoretical diagnosis: SmoothGrad's fixed variance σ = α(x_max − x_min) causes systematic out-of-range sampling that injects 'extra noise' beyond what the Gaussian kernel inherently preserves. AdaptGrad replaces the uniform variance with per-dimension adaptive variances derived from a probabilistic constraint on out-of-bounds probability, positioning itself as a principled fix rather than a new smoothing paradigm. It also situates itself relative to NoiseGrad (parameter perturbation) and gradient-based explanation methods like Integrated Gradients and Gradient × Input, which it can be composed with.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Since SmoothGrad is embedded in nearly all gradient-based explanation pipelines (Grad-CAM, Score-CAM, IG, etc.), a drop-in replacement with negligible computational overhead and consistent metric improvements has broad practical utility for XAI practitioners. However, the paper itself acknowledges significant open questions: the extra-noise level c remains an empirical hyperparameter, noise is measured only indirectly via Sparseness, and the Faithfulness metric has known pathologies that limit its diagnostic power. A unified, direct noise metric and a standardized evaluation framework for explanation methods remain unresolved challenges in the field.
