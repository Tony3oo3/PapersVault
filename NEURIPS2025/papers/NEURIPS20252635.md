## Problem

Selective classifiers are evaluated against a perfect-ordering oracle bound, yet no prior work provides a finite-sample decomposition of why practical models fall short of this bound. The paper formalizes this shortfall as the selective classification gap Δ(c) and asks: for a finite model on finite data, which specific sources of looseness drive the gap, and which levers actually close it? A key misconception the paper challenges is that monotone post-hoc calibration meaningfully improves selective performance.

## Input / Output

The selective classifier is a pair (h, g) where h maps an input x (a 32×32 RGB image for CIFAR, a 224×224 image for StanfordCars/Camelyon17, or a 2D synthetic point for two-moons) to a class label, and g assigns a confidence score. The model outputs either h(x) or the abstention symbol ⊥ depending on whether g(x,h) ≥ τ for a chosen threshold. The primary evaluation output is the accuracy-coverage curve, and the diagnostic quantity is the gap Δ(c) = oracle_bound(a_full, c) − acc_c(h,g) at each coverage level c ∈ (0,1].

## Method

The paper derives the first finite-sample decomposition of the selective classification gap into five measurable terms: irreducible Bayes noise, model approximation error, ranking error (misalignment between confidence scores and true correctness posteriors), statistical noise from finite evaluation sets, and a miscellaneous slack term covering optimization error and distribution shift. The central theoretical insight is that monotone score transformations (isotonic regression, histogram binning) preserve the acceptance set and therefore leave the ranking error untouched, while temperature scaling—though technically non-monotone in softmax probabilities—can only re-order a vanishingly small subset of samples. Substantially closing the gap requires non-monotone, feature-aware scoring mechanisms such as deep ensembles or self-adaptive training that actively re-rank predictions using richer internal representations. The decomposition is validated on synthetic two-moons data (controlling noise and capacity independently) and on CIFAR-100, StanfordCars, CIFAR-10C, and Camelyon17-WILDS, confirming that Bayes noise and capacity limits dominate the gap, temperature scaling improves ECE without reducing E-AURC, and distribution shift introduces an additional slack requiring robust training.

## Results

On CIFAR-100, Deep Ensembles (DE) achieve the lowest Excess-AURC across all three architectures: 0.065 (CNN), 0.026 (ResNet-18), and 0.026 (WideResNet-50), compared to 0.086, 0.033, and 0.031 for the MSP baseline. Temperature scaling (TEMP) reduces ECE by an order of magnitude (e.g., 0.142 → 0.008 for CNN) yet leaves E-AURC virtually unchanged (0.086 → 0.085), confirming that monotone calibration does not close the selective-classification gap. Self-Adaptive Training (SAT) provides modest gains in both ECE and E-AURC (e.g., 0.086 → 0.081 E-AURC for CNN), while DE's non-monotone re-ranking yields the largest selective-performance improvements.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four confidence-scoring strategies to validate its gap decomposition. MSP (Hendrycks & Gimpel, 2017) serves as the baseline and yields the highest E-AURC across all architectures (0.086/0.033/0.031 for CNN/ResNet-18/WRN-50). Temperature scaling (Guo et al., 2017) dramatically improves calibration (ECE drops from 0.142 to 0.008 for CNN) but fails to reduce E-AURC, confirming the paper's central claim that monotone post-hoc calibration cannot close the selective gap. Self-Adaptive Training (Huang et al., 2020) achieves modest E-AURC reductions (e.g., 0.086 → 0.081 for CNN) by implicitly perturbing rankings through relabeling, but remains well behind DE. Deep Ensembles (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017) consistently achieve the best E-AURC across all architectures (0.065/0.026/0.026), demonstrating that non-monotone aggregation via model diversity is the most effective lever for re-ranking predictions and approximating the oracle bound.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the classical selective classification framework (Chow 1957; El-Yaniv et al. 2010) and the oracle perfect-ordering bound introduced by Geifman et al. (2019) and Rabanser et al. (2023), but departs from prior theory by providing the first finite-sample decomposition of the selective classification gap into five measurable components (Bayes noise, approximation, ranking, statistical, and miscellaneous slack). Where earlier agnostic theory (Wiener and El-Yaniv 2011) characterizes optimal selective classifiers only in asymptotic or idealized regimes, this work targets the practical finite-data, finite-model setting and explicitly isolates which error source dominates. It also positions itself against the calibration literature by showing that monotone post-hoc calibration—a widely recommended technique—preserves score ordering and therefore cannot reduce the ranking-error term, in contrast to non-monotone methods like deep ensembles or self-adaptive training that actively re-rank predictions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The decomposition provides practitioners with a concrete error budget and actionable levers (larger models for approximation, ensembles or feature-aware heads for ranking, more validation data for statistical slack, domain adaptation for shift), shifting the design conversation from 'improve calibration' to 'improve ranking.' A key open question is how the five error terms interact in practice—for instance, increasing capacity simultaneously reduces approximation and ranking error, making unique attribution difficult. The authors note that extending the analysis to foundation models, asymmetric cost functions, and open-ended language generation remains future work, and that the 0-1 loss assumption limits applicability to high-stakes settings with class-dependent costs.
