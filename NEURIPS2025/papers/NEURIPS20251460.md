## Problem

Existing $l_p$-norm-constrained state perturbation attacks in RL cannot alter the decision-relevant semantics of image inputs even under large perturbation budgets, making them ineffective against diffusion-based defenses that denoise small pixel-level noise. Additionally, prior attacks ignore temporal dependencies across consecutive states, producing perturbations that are inconsistent with the agent's observed history and thus detectable. The paper identifies a fundamental trilemma: no existing attack simultaneously achieves semantic alteration, historical alignment, and trajectory faithfulness.

## Input / Output

At each attack timestep (selected via a state-importance-weight threshold over a fraction $\xi$ of steps), the method consumes the current true state image, a window of $k=4$ prior states and actions (either true or previously perturbed, depending on the variant), the victim's policy $\pi$ and state-action value function $Q^\pi$, and a pre-trained autoencoder. It outputs a single perturbed state image of the same resolution as the environment (e.g., 84×84 grayscale for Atari, higher-res RGB for AirSim) that is semantically distinct from the true state yet realistic and temporally coherent. The attack is policy-agnostic: the same pre-trained diffusion model adapts to different victim policies at test time without retraining.

## Method

SHIFT is a two-stage diffusion-based attack. In training, an EDM-formulated conditional diffusion model is trained with classifier-free guidance on clean environment trajectories, learning to generate the next state conditioned on a history $\tau_{t-1}$; a separate autoencoder is trained on clean states for realism scoring. At test time, the reverse denoising process is steered by three combined guidance signals: (1) classifier-free guidance on the history for temporal alignment, (2) policy guidance via $-\nabla_{\tilde{s}_t^i}\log Q^\pi(s_t, \pi(\tilde{s}_t^i))$ to push generation toward states that induce low-value actions, and (3) autoencoder reconstruction-error gradients to maintain realism. Theorem 3.6 proves that policy guidance and classifier-free guidance are compatible (their gradient terms depend on disjoint conditioning variables), enabling their simultaneous application. Two variants differ only in history conditioning: SHIFT-O uses the true history for higher trajectory faithfulness, while SHIFT-I uses the perturbed (observed) history to construct self-consistent imaginary trajectories that are harder to detect.

## Results

Against the DP-DQN defense in Freeway, SHIFT-I achieves the lowest episode reward (10.6 ± 2.5) and highest deviation rate (51.4%), while SHIFT-O achieves reward 21.8 ± 3.2 with 22.8% deviation. Both SHIFT variants produce far more realistic perturbations than all baselines, with reconstruction errors of 1.02–1.05 (vs. 3.45–9.32 for baselines), SSIM of 0.990–0.999 (vs. 0.589–0.997), and LPIPS of 0.0008–0.0175 (vs. 0.0018–0.2373). Against vanilla DQN, SHIFT-O drops cumulative reward by over 96% in Freeway (34.1 → 1.2) and by more than 500% in Doom (75.4 → −354.0), and still degrades the strongest diffusion-based defenses (DMBP, DP-DQN) by 30–75% across all four environments.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SHIFT-I dominates all baselines on attack effectiveness, reducing Freeway reward to 10.6 (vs. 26.8–30.8 for all baselines) and achieving a 51.4% deviation rate, more than double the next-best Rotation attack (26.9%). SHIFT-O achieves the best stealthiness across all realism metrics: reconstruction error of 1.02 (vs. 3.45–9.32), SSIM of 0.9990 (vs. 0.589–0.997), and LPIPS of 0.0008 (vs. 0.0018–0.2373), making it harder to detect than even the small-budget PGD/MinBest attacks. The l_p-norm constrained attacks (PGD, MinBest, PA-AD) are nearly ineffective at small budgets (1/255), with rewards of 29–31 and deviation rates of only 3–7%, and remain largely ineffective even at 15/255 (rewards 28.5–29.4, deviation 3–10%). The high-sensitivity direction attacks (Rotation, Transform) from Korkmaz achieve moderate deviation (22–27%) but at the cost of significantly worse realism (reconstruction error 6.4–9.3, SSIM 0.60–0.82), making them easily detectable. Against the strongest diffusion-based defenses (DMBP, DP-DQN), SHIFT still degrades reward by 30–75% in Freeway and Airsim, while all l_p-norm baselines are essentially neutralized (rewards within 5–10% of clean performance).

## Relation to Prior Work

SHIFT extends the state perturbation attack line (MinBest, PGD, PA-AD) by replacing the l_p-norm constraint with a diffusion-based generation framework that produces semantically-altering perturbations. It builds directly on classifier-free guidance (Ho & Salimans) for history-conditioned state generation and on classifier/policy guidance (Dhariwal & Nichol) for steering the reverse diffusion toward low-value states, combining both in a way formalized by Theorem 3.6. The paper departs from Korkmaz's high-sensitivity direction attacks (rotations, brightness shifts) by targeting domain-specific semantics rather than visually salient but semantically inert transformations, and from diffusion-based defenses (DMBP, DP-DQN) by repurposing the same generative machinery as an attack tool. An autoencoder-based anomaly detector provides a realism constraint analogous to the l_p budget but operating in a learned data manifold rather than pixel space.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that state-of-the-art diffusion-based defenses succeed primarily because l_p-constrained attacks cannot alter image semantics, not because the defenses are inherently robust—reframes the robustness problem for vision-based RL and has direct safety implications for autonomous driving and embodied AI. The paper's trilemma (no known attack simultaneously optimizes semantic alteration, historical alignment, and trajectory faithfulness) provides a concrete research agenda for both attacks and defenses. Open questions include whether a single attack can better satisfy all three properties, how defenses should be redesigned to handle semantic (rather than pixel-level) uncertainty, and whether the approach scales to higher-resolution or multi-camera settings.
