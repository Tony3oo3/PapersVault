## Problem

Existing egocentric video QA benchmarks overlook gaze, the most direct signal of user attention and intent in first-person settings. Because MLLMs process global image frames as visual tokens, they lack access to the camera wearer's explicit focus, causing systematic failures on questions about what the user is looking at, when they looked at it, and why. This paper asks whether incorporating gaze signals can close the gap between model performance and human-level intent understanding in egocentric video.

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance consists of 9 uniformly sampled frames from a 30-second-to-1-minute egocentric clip, a text question with five multiple-choice options, and gaze information encoded in one of three formats: normalized (x,y) coordinates as text, 25-pixel-radius red circles overlaid on each frame, or a single sequential gaze salience heatmap where later fixations receive progressively higher intensity. The model outputs a single letter (A–E) selecting the correct option, scored across three intent dimensions—spatial, temporal, and causal. The benchmark contains 1,757 QA pairs drawn from 913 videos sourced from Ego4D, EgoExo4D, and EGTEA Gaze+.

## Method

The core contribution is a three-stage benchmark construction pipeline (frame/gaze extraction → MLLM-generated QA with distractor strategies → human verification on six quality criteria) combined with three gaze-guided prompting strategies that vary how gaze is encoded: as textual coordinates (GazeT), as visual red-circle markers on frames (GazeV), or as a temporally weighted salience map (GazeS) generated by Algorithm 1, which accumulates Gaussian-weighted fixation intensities in reverse chronological order and applies a final blur. To address the scarcity of gaze-related training data in MLLMs, the paper further applies rank-16 LoRA fine-tuning via LLaMA-Factory on Qwen2.5-VL-7B, demonstrating cross-dataset transfer (EGTEA→Ego4D+EgoExo and vice versa) with only 500–1200 QA pairs. A prompted gaze-estimation experiment with Qwen2.5-VL-72B shows that the benefit of gaze prompting is strongly correlated with estimation accuracy (MSE), dropping to near-zero or negative when predicted fixations deviate substantially from ground truth.

## Results

On EgoGazeVQA, the best-performing model Qwen2.5-VL-72B achieves 66.3% average accuracy with Sequential Gaze Salience Map (GazeS) prompting, up from 60.5% without gaze signals. Gaze-guided prompting yields consistent gains across all seven evaluated MLLMs, with the largest relative improvement observed for MiniCPM-o 2.6-8B (35.9% → 53.7% average, +17.8 points). Causal reasoning is the easiest sub-task for all models (74–84% range), while spatial and temporal intent understanding remain substantially below human performance (80.7% and 75.6% respectively).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates seven MLLMs (GPT-4o mini, Gemini 2.0 Flash-Lite, LLaVA-NeXT-Video-7B, MiniCPM-o 2.6-8B, InternVL2.5-8B, Qwen2.5-VL-7B, Qwen2.5-VL-72B) with and without three gaze-guided prompting strategies. Gaze signals consistently improve every model, but the magnitude of gain scales with model capacity: Qwen2.5-VL-72B gains +5.8 points (60.5→66.3) with GazeS, while MiniCPM-o 2.6-8B gains +17.8 points (35.9→53.7), suggesting smaller models benefit most from explicit gaze cues. Among the three strategies, GazeS (sequential salience maps) is most effective for spatial reasoning, while GazeT (textual coordinates) yields the largest temporal gains. Qwen2.5-VL-72B with GazeS (66.3% avg) remains the strongest model but still trails human performance (83.8%) by 17.5 points, particularly in spatial (64.3 vs. 80.7) and temporal (50.3 vs. 75.6) sub-tasks. Simple vision-encoder baselines (CLIP, EgoVLP, BLIP-2) perform near chance level (21–28%), confirming that gaze-aware reasoning requires full MLLM-scale language understanding rather than visual similarity matching.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of two previously separate research threads: egocentric video QA benchmarks (QaEgo4D, EgoSchema, VideoMindPalace, EgoTextVQA) and egocentric gaze-action modeling (Li et al., Huang et al., Lai et al.). While prior egocentric VQA benchmarks evaluate episodic memory, long-form reasoning, or scene-text understanding, none incorporate gaze as a conditioning signal for intent interpretation. The closest prior work, GazeGPT (Konrad et al.), demonstrated that gaze can improve MLLM UI design but provided no standardized benchmark or systematic evaluation. EgoGazeVQA repurposes existing egocentric datasets with eye-tracking (Ego4D, EgoExo4D, EGTEA Gaze+) and introduces three gaze-guided prompting strategies (textual coordinates, visual markers, sequential salience maps) that have no direct precedent in the MLLM evaluation literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a substantial and consistent gap (17.5 points) between the best MLLM (Qwen2.5-VL-72B at 66.3%) and human performance (83.8%), indicating that current models fundamentally lack the ability to interpret gaze-conditioned spatial and temporal intent from first-person video. The finding that gaze salience maps benefit spatial reasoning most while textual gaze prompts benefit temporal reasoning most suggests that MLLMs process gaze information differently across modalities and time dimensions, pointing to a training-data scarcity problem rather than an architectural one. Key limitations include the indoor-scenario bias of available eye-tracking data, the absence of audio modality, and the restriction to short-to-medium-length clips. Open questions include whether gaze signals can prune visual tokens in MLLMs (analogous to V1 processing), whether models can infer gaze implicitly without explicit eye-tracking, and how these findings extend to longer, more socially complex egocentric interactions.
