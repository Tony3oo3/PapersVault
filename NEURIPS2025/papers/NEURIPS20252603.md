## Problem

Prior work on interventional Markov equivalence classes (I-MEC) for causal graphs with latent variables has focused on soft interventions, which preserve incoming edges to intervened variables. Hard interventions, which sever all incoming edges, can break inducing paths and reveal structural information that soft interventions cannot, yet no characterization or learning algorithm existed for the hard-intervention setting. This paper asks: how can we extract maximal causal knowledge from a collection of hard interventional datasets when latent confounders are present?

## Input / Output

The algorithm takes as input a set of intervention targets I (subsets of observable variables V) and the corresponding hard-interventional distributions P_I for each target, with no requirement that observational data be included. The output is an I-augmented graph tuple: one graph per intervention domain, each a PAG-like structure over the domain's observable variables plus auxiliary F-nodes that encode cross-domain do-invariance constraints. The F-nodes make the effects of other interventions visible within each domain's graph, enabling d-separation-style tests that span multiple experimental distributions simultaneously.

## Method

The paper first generalizes Pearl's do-calculus to operate between arbitrary pairs of hard interventions (Proposition 3.2), yielding four rules whose converse statements serve as new structural constraints (do-constraints) beyond standard conditional independencies. These constraints are encoded graphically via twin augmented MAGs—pairs of domain-specific MAGs joined by an F-node connected to the symmetric difference of the two intervention targets—which provide a necessary and sufficient condition for I-Markov equivalence (Theorem 4.7). To avoid inspecting all O(k²) pairs, the twin augmented MAGs are merged into a single I-augmented MAG per domain (Definition 5.1), a compact representation proven equivalent for equivalence checking (Proposition 5.2). The learning algorithm (Algorithm 1) is an FCI-inspired constraint-based procedure: it learns skeletons via separating-set tests that compare distributions across domains (using F-nodes to test cross-domain invariances), then orients edges using the standard FCI rules plus four new rules (Rules 8–11) that exploit the structure of hard interventions, such as orienting edges out of F-nodes and propagating ancestral relationships across domains. Soundness is formally established under an h-faithfulness assumption (Theorem 6.3).

## Results

The paper's main empirical result (Table 1) compares the mean I-Markov equivalence class (I-MEC) size under hard vs. soft interventions across graph sizes n=2,3,4 and two graph types (Random density 0.5, Complete). Hard interventions consistently yield smaller I-MEC sizes: for n=4 Complete graphs, the mean I-MEC size drops from 1529.57 (soft) to 721.37 (hard), a ratio of 0.47±0.07. The ratio of hard-to-soft I-MEC size decreases monotonically with n (from ~0.69 at n=2 to ~0.47 at n=4 Complete), indicating that hard interventions become increasingly more informative for larger causal graphs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline comparison is against soft interventions, using the I-MEC characterization and augmented graph construction from Kocaoglu et al. (2019). Hard interventions strictly dominate soft interventions on the I-MEC size metric across all tested configurations (n=2,3,4; Random and Complete graphs), with the hard I-MEC being 31–53% smaller than the soft I-MEC. The advantage grows with graph size: the ratio of hard-to-soft I-MEC size decreases from 0.69 (n=2, Random) to 0.47 (n=4, Complete), meaning that for larger graphs, hard interventions eliminate roughly half the ambiguity that soft interventions leave. This confirms the paper's central theoretical claim that hard interventions break inducing paths and create additional do-invariance constraints not available under soft interventions, thereby narrowing the search space more effectively in the presence of latent variables.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the I-Markov equivalence class (I-MEC) framework from Kocaoglu et al. (2019), which characterized equivalence under soft interventions with latent variables using augmented graphs with F-nodes, to the harder setting of hard interventions. It builds on Pearl's do-calculus by generalizing the three inference rules to operate between arbitrary pairs of hard interventions (Proposition 3.2), and on the MAG/PAG machinery of Zhang (2008) and Richardson and Spirtes (2002) for representing equivalence classes with latents. The learning algorithm is an FCI variant that incorporates four new orientation rules specific to the hard-intervention setting, departing from the soft-intervention algorithm of Kocaoglu et al. (2019) where the interventional graph structure is unchanged.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper fills a genuine gap: prior I-MEC characterizations with latent variables only covered soft interventions, leaving the (arguably more informative) hard-intervention case uncharacterized. The empirical finding that hard interventions consistently yield smaller I-MECs (ratios of 0.47–0.69 vs. soft, with the gap widening as graph size grows) provides a principled argument for preferring hard interventions in experimental design when feasible. However, the empirical validation is limited to graphs with at most 4 nodes due to super-exponential enumeration, and the algorithm's completeness is not established—only soundness is proven. Open questions include whether the I-augmented MAG tuple can be further compressed, whether completeness can be shown under stronger assumptions, and how the framework scales to larger graphs where enumeration is infeasible.
