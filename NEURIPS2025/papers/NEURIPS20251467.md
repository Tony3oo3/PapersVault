## Problem

Post-hoc alignment methods (RLHF, DPO, Constitutional AI) produce only superficial safety in LLMs: once unsafe patterns are internalized during pretraining, they persist and re-emerge after even small amounts of benign finetuning. The paper argues that 'alignment is not unlearning' and asks whether safety can be embedded natively into the pretraining process itself, making models robust to both adversarial jailbreaks and benign finetuning attacks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251467/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The pretraining pipeline consumes raw web text (FineWeb-Edu, StackOverflow, FineMath, Cosmopedia) and produces a 1.7B-parameter language model. The safety scoring component takes arbitrary text segments and outputs a 0–5 integer safety score with a brief justification; the ensemble of Qwen 2.5-7B and gte-base-en-v1.5 classifiers takes the maximum score across both to maximize recall on unsafe examples. At inference, SafeBeam Search takes a text prompt and produces a completion by discarding 50% of beam candidates with the highest one-token-lookahead probability of the <potentially_unsafe_content> token at each decoding step.

## Method

The framework applies four data-centric interventions during pretraining of 1.7B-parameter models (SmolLM2 architecture): (1) Safety Filtering via an ensemble classifier scoring web data 0–5; (2) Synthetic Recontextualization using LLaMA-3.1-8B with 12 diverse prompt templates (podcast, textbook, parent-child dialogue, etc.) to rephrase score 1–3 content into safe, context-rich narratives yielding 100B+ tokens (SafeWeb); (3) Native Refusal Training via RefuseWeb (dialogue refusals from score 4–5 content) and Moral Education datasets (educational articles derived from refusal dialogues); and (4) Harmfulness-Tag Annotated Pretraining, injecting <potentially_unsafe_content> tokens at 5% of positions within unsafe segments to create distinct internal representations for safe vs. unsafe content. A key empirical finding is that filtering to only score-0 data *increases* attack success rate (38.8%→43.8%), while the full pipeline with rephrasing, refusal, moral education, and SafeBeam reduces ASR to 8.3% without degrading standard benchmark performance.

## Results

Safety Pretraining reduces the attack success rate (ASR) from 38.8% (raw-data baseline) to 8.3% (with SafeBeam inference) on standard LLM safety benchmarks (HarmBench, TDC, JailbreakBench, AdvBench) after benign finetuning on GSM8K. On standard NLP benchmarks, the safety-pretrained model achieves an average of 43.5%, slightly exceeding the raw-data baseline of 42.8%, confirming no general capability degradation. At the base-model level (pre-instruction-tuning), the safety-pretrained model achieves 11% ASR versus 44% for the standard pretrained model. After a round of benign finetuning, standard models nearly quadruple their ASR, while safety-pretrained models show only a marginal increase, demonstrating robustness that post-hoc alignment cannot match.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its safety classifiers against two legacy baselines: LLaMA Guard (Inan et al., 2023) and the profanity checker used in the Pile corpus (Gao et al., 2020). On the held-out GPT-4o-mini annotated evaluation set, the proposed ensemble (Qwen 2.5-7B + gte-base-en-v1.5) achieves a Recall@1 of 0.9536 and Recall@3 of 0.7483, dramatically outperforming LLaMA Guard (Recall@1 = 0.1037, Recall@3 = 0.2649) and the profanity checker (Recall@1 = 0.0310, Recall@3 = 0.0930). The best individual embedding model (multilingual-e5-large-instruct) reaches Recall@1 of 0.9205, still well above both baselines. In terms of overall F1, the ensemble scores 0.3615, which is lower than the best single embedding model (gte-large-en-v1.5 at 0.4533), but the authors explicitly prioritize recall over F1 to maximize detection of unsafe content. For the core safety evaluation (ASR after benign finetuning), the safety-pretrained model with SafeBeam achieves 8.3% ASR, compared to approximately 30% for a model using harmfulness-tag annotation only during instruction fine-tuning (a 4× improvement), and the standard pretrained model degrades to nearly 4× its post-alignment ASR after benign finetuning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the harmfulness-tag/special-token line of work (Jain et al., 2024; Xhonneux et al., 2025; Korbak et al., 2023) from the fine-tuning stage to pretraining, arguing that the association between a safety token and unsafe content must be formed natively rather than grafted on post-hoc. It also builds on the rephrasing literature (Maini et al., 2024b) and pretraining data curation efforts (Penedo et al., 2024; Korbak et al., 2023), but departs from the dominant post-hoc alignment paradigm (RLHF, DPO, Constitutional AI) by embedding all safety interventions—filtering, recontextualization, refusal training, moral education, and tag-annotated pretraining—directly into the pretraining data pipeline. The SafeBeam decoding algorithm operationalizes the pretraining-time tag association at inference, extending the refusal-token steering idea to a beam-search setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central thesis—that 'alignment is not unlearning' and that safety must be architecturally embedded during pretraining rather than patched via instruction tuning—challenges a widely adopted practice in the field and is supported by a clear 4× ASR gap between pretraining-time and fine-tuning-time harmfulness-tag annotation. The counterintuitive finding that training exclusively on safe (score-0) data *increases* vulnerability (38.8% → 43.8% ASR) reframes the data-curation problem from removal to contextualized exposure. Key open questions include whether the framework scales beyond 1.7B parameters, whether SafeBeam can be replaced by purely pretraining-time mechanisms for adversarial robustness, and how to further reduce the modest over-refusal tradeoff without sacrificing safety.
