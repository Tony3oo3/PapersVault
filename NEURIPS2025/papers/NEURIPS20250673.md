## Problem

AI-based chip placement algorithms are typically evaluated on intermediate surrogate metrics (e.g., MacroHPWL, HPWL) that are computationally cheap but often misalign with the final end-to-end PPA of the chip design. Existing public benchmarks use overly simplified formats (e.g., Bookshelf) or lack the design kits needed to run a full open-source EDA flow, making it impossible to measure whether placement improvements actually translate into better timing, power, and area in the final silicon.

## Input / Output

The benchmark starts from Verilog source code and runs a complete OpenROAD flow (logic synthesis → floorplanning → placement → CTS → routing) on the NanGate45 open-source technology library, producing intermediate LEF/DEF files at each stage. Placement algorithms receive Bookshelf-format netlists (converted from the pre-placement DEF), output macro positions that are written back into DEF files, and the remaining flow then yields final PPA metrics (WNS, TNS, NVP, Power, Area) alongside intermediate metrics (MacroHPWL, Regularity, HPWL, Congestion, Wirelength). The 20 designs span CPUs, GPUs, and NPUs with cell counts from ~27K to ~427K and macro counts from 10 to 136.

## Method

ChiPBench contributes three coupled components: (1) a dataset generation pipeline that uses OpenROAD to synthesize 20 Verilog designs end-to-end, with a specialized partition-and-harden procedure that automatically converts standard-cell modules into macro blocks (with corresponding LEF/LIB definitions) to create diverse macro-rich designs where open-source repositories lack them; (2) a unified end-to-end evaluation workflow that converts dataset files to Bookshelf format for algorithm input, runs the placement algorithm, converts results back to DEF, and executes the full downstream OpenROAD flow (global placement, legalization, CTS, routing, sign-off STA) to obtain final PPA; and (3) a systematic evaluation of six AI-based macro placement algorithms (SA, WireMask-EA, AutoDMP, MaskPlace, ChiPFormer, MaskRegulate) against DREAMPlace and the Hier-RTLMP baseline, followed by Pearson correlation analysis between intermediate and final PPA metrics. The key empirical finding is that algorithms excelling on MacroHPWL (e.g., WireMask-EA, ChiPFormer) often produce the worst timing performance due to central macro clustering that increases routing congestion and wirelength, while regularity-aware methods (MaskRegulate) better preserve timing by biasing macros toward boundaries.

## Results

Across 20 circuits, all six AI-based macro placement algorithms underperform the traditional Hier-RTLMP baseline on final PPA metrics, despite several achieving superior intermediate scores. WireMask-EA attains the best MacroHPWL (0.844× baseline) but suffers severe timing degradation (WNS 1.556×, TNS 11.007×). MaskRegulate, which optimizes Regularity, achieves the best PPA among AI methods (WNS 1.156×, TNS 2.213×, NVP 1.216×, Power 1.001×, Area 1.000×) yet still lags the baseline. ChiPFormer shows the worst timing performance (WNS 2.616×, TNS 7.624×) despite a competitive MacroHPWL of 0.901×, illustrating a clear disconnect between intermediate and end-to-end metrics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks six AI-based placement algorithms (WireMask-EA, SA, AutoDMP, MaskPlace, ChiPFormer, MaskRegulate) against two traditional methods (DREAMPlace and Hier-RTLMP) on 20 circuits. No AI-based method beats the Hier-RTLMP baseline on any PPA metric; MaskRegulate comes closest, matching the baseline on Area (1.000×) and nearly matching on Power (1.001×), while still degrading WNS by 15.6% and TNS by 121.3%. WireMask-EA achieves the best MacroHPWL (0.844×) but its timing collapses (TNS 11.007×), demonstrating overfitting to a single intermediate metric. AutoDMP produces the best HPWL (0.892×) and congestion (0.941×) yet still degrades WNS by 28.2%. ChiPFormer, despite a competitive MacroHPWL (0.901×), exhibits the worst WNS (2.616×) and TNS (7.624×) among all methods. The traditional DREAMPlace also underperforms the baseline on timing (WNS 2.030×, TNS 7.463×), suggesting that the hierarchical dataflow-aware strategy in Hier-RTLMP captures physical realism that neither gradient-based nor RL-based approaches replicate.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the standard evaluation paradigm in AI-based chip placement, where algorithms are assessed on intermediate surrogate metrics (MacroHPWL, HPWL, congestion) rather than final design PPA. Prior EDA benchmarks (ISPD2005, ICCAD2004, ISPD2015, ICCAD2015) provided simplified Bookshelf or partial LEF/DEF data insufficient for end-to-end evaluation with open-source tools like OpenROAD. ChiPBench carves new ground by generating 20 complete circuits from Verilog source through a fully open-source pipeline (NanGate45 library), preserving all physical implementation data needed to run the entire post-placement flow. The paper repurposes eight existing placement algorithms (SA, WireMask-EA, AutoDMP, MaskPlace, ChiPFormer, MaskRegulate, DREAMPlace, Hier-RTLMP) but re-evaluates them under a fundamentally different criterion: end-to-end PPA rather than stage-local metrics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that algorithms achieving the best MacroHPWL (WireMask-EA, ChiPFormer) simultaneously produce the worst timing performance (WNS, TNS)—challenges a core working assumption in the AI-EDA community that optimizing standard intermediate metrics translates to good final designs. This opens critical questions about how to design proxy metrics that better capture physical realism (regularity, dataflow, congestion interactions) and whether multi-fidelity or surrogate-model approaches can bridge the gap between cheap intermediate signals and expensive end-to-end PPA. Limitations include coverage of only 20 circuits at a single technology node (NanGate45), evaluation focused primarily on macro placement, and reliance on OpenROAD whose maturity lags commercial tools. The benchmark's open-source, reproducible nature positions it as a potential standard for the AI-EDA community, though its impact depends on whether the community adopts end-to-end evaluation as the primary criterion.
