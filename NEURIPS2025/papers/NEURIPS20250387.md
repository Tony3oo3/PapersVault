## Problem

While incoherence processing (rotation-based Gaussianization) enables theoretically grounded quantization of LLM weights, existing sophisticated quantizers like TCQ are limited to a few integer bitwidths (2, 3, 4 bits) and batch size 1, creating a large gap from the information-theoretic optimum that requires fine-grained fractional-bit quantizers approaching the Gaussian distortion-rate bound. No practical quantizer suite bridges this gap with optimized kernels supporting broad fractional bitwidths and larger batch sizes.

## Input / Output

The method consumes pre-trained LLM weight matrices (post incoherence processing, i.e., rotated along the input dimension with per-output-channel scaling) and a resource constraint expressed as either a total memory budget in bits or a latency budget in tokens/second. It produces per-layer quantizer assignments at fractional bitwidths with 0.25-bit granularity (via half-TCQ) or 0.5-bit granularity (via VQ/TCQ), along with a layer-fusion configuration specifying which linear layers sharing the same input should be merged into a single fused kernel call. In data-free mode, no calibration data is needed; in data-aware mode, a Hessian approximation from calibration tokens guides block-wise quantization.

## Method

The paper first derives a closed-form optimal fractional bit allocation (Theorem 3.1) under the Gaussian source coding assumption, showing that the optimal bitwidth for layer l scales as (1/2ln2)·ln(a_l/(d_in·d_out)) + C, where a_l is a sensitivity coefficient and C is set by the memory constraint. To realize this in practice, Q-Palette provides three quantizer families—NUQ (k-means LUT scalar), 2D VQ (k-means codebook over vector pairs), and TCQ (bitshift trellis with Viterbi decoding)—each with optimized Tensor Core and CUDA Core CUDA kernels supporting fractional bitwidths from 1.5 to 5.0 bits and batch sizes up to 8. A novel half-TCQ variant mixes two TCQ bitwidths within a single layer (e.g., 2.5 and 3.0 bits on two row-halves) to achieve 0.25-bit granularity. The MSQ framework formulates quantizer selection as a multiple-choice knapsack problem solved via SCIP, and the fusion-aware extension introduces binary variables per (fusible layer group, quantizer) pair to jointly optimize quantization and layer fusion as a single ILP, reducing kernel launches and memory accesses for fused projections like QKV.

## Results

On LLaMA 3.1-8B (data-free), Ours-MSQ-Mem achieves WikiText2 perplexity of 6.10 at 3.25 bits and 5.81 at 4.00 bits, outperforming the strongest baseline HIGGS-MSQ (6.39 and 5.91 respectively) and approaching FP16 (5.61). In the data-aware setting on LLaMA 2-7B, Ours-MSQ-Mem achieves 6.47 WikiText2 perplexity at 2 bits (vs. QTIP's 6.84) while delivering 4.4× higher throughput at batch size 8 (1684 vs. 386 tokens/s). The paper also demonstrates that a 2.875-bit MSQ model matches the perplexity of a 3.25-bit HIGGS-MSQ model, yielding a 1.13× higher compression ratio.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against QTIP (TCQ, NeurIPS 2024), Ours-MSQ-Mem consistently achieves lower WikiText2 perplexity at matched bitwidths (e.g., 6.28 vs. 6.81 at 3 bits on LLaMA 3.1-8B) and substantially higher throughput at batch size 8 in the data-aware setting (1684 vs. 386 tokens/s on LLaMA 2-7B at 2 bits, a 4.4× speedup). Against HIGGS-MSQ (ACL 2025), the strongest mixed-scheme baseline, Ours-MSQ-Mem improves perplexity by 0.29 at 3.25 bits (6.10 vs. 6.39) and by 0.10 at 4.00 bits (5.81 vs. 5.91) on LLaMA 3.1-8B, while also achieving higher zero-shot accuracy. Against NormalFloat (QLoRA/FLUTE) and HQQ, the simpler scalar-quantization baselines, the gap is larger: at 3.25 bits, Ours-MSQ-Mem achieves 6.10 vs. 7.70 (NF) and 8.29 (HQQ) perplexity. In the data-aware LLaMA 2-13B setting, Ours-MSQ-Mem matches QTIP's accuracy (67.0 vs. 67.0 at 3 bits) while delivering 4.8× higher throughput at batch size 8 (738 vs. 153 tokens/s). The paper also shows that Q-Palette's broader fractional-bitwidth support (TCQ-ALL) reduces the bit-allocation gap from 0.0129 to 0.0023 compared to integer-only TCQ-2,3,4, directly translating to lower surrogate objective values.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) incoherence processing (QuIP, QuIP#, QTIP), which rotates LLM weights into near-Gaussian distributions to enable sophisticated Gaussian quantizers; (2) rate-distortion theory, which provides the fundamental distortion-rate bound for Gaussian sources; and (3) mixed-scheme quantization (HAWQ, HIGGS), which formulates layer-wise bit allocation as a combinatorial optimization problem. The key departure is twofold: theoretically, the paper derives a closed-form optimal fractional-bit allocation (Theorem 3.1) showing that fine-grained quantizers approaching the Gaussian distortion bound are *necessary* (not merely beneficial) for near-optimal performance; practically, it extends QTIP's TCQ kernels from integer bitwidths at batch size 1 to fractional bitwidths (0.25-bit granularity via half-TCQ) at batch sizes up to 8, and introduces layer fusion as a joint optimization dimension in MSQ, drawing from compiler-level fusion research (TASO, IOS).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Q-Palette provides a practical, open-source suite of fractional-bit quantizers with optimized CUDA kernels that directly addresses the gap between rate-distortion theory and deployable quantization, making sophisticated TCQ quantizers accessible beyond the single-batch, integer-bitwidth regime that prior work (QTIP) was limited to. The fusion-aware MSQ formulation is a genuinely new optimization dimension that captures latency savings from layer fusion, yielding up to 36% inference speedup over NormalFloat baselines. Open questions include whether the Gaussian assumption (post-rotation) remains tight at very aggressive bitwidths (<2 bits), whether the linearity theorem surrogate degrades for non-LLM architectures, and whether the MCKP formulation scales efficiently to models with thousands of fusible groups. The paper's challenge to the working assumption that TCQ is computationally prohibitive beyond batch size 1 is well-supported by the kernel engineering and latency benchmarks.
