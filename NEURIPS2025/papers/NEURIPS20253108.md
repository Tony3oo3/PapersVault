## Problem

Theoretical work on supervised transfer learning has established statistical guarantees for weighted or constrained risk minimization, but has largely ignored the computational cost of solving these objectives. Choosing the bias parameter via cross-validation requires many optimization passes, and constrained formulations demand expensive projection steps at each iteration. This paper asks whether an efficient SGD procedure can adaptively balance source and target sampling while preserving provable transfer guarantees, without prior knowledge of source quality.

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes labeled (x, y) pairs from both a source sample S_P (n_P points) and a target sample S_Q (n_Q points), where x ∈ R^d and y ∈ R. At each SGD step it draws a single sample from either S_P or S_Q according to a Bernoulli rate controlled by a running Lagrangian multiplier λ_t, and simultaneously maintains a parallel SGD estimate θ_{Q,t} of the target ERM. The output is the ℓ_2 projection of the time-averaged iterate onto the constraint set {θ : R̂_Q(θ) − R̂_Q(θ_{Q,T}) ≤ 6ε_Q − ε_0}, yielding a single parameter vector θ̂_PQ ∈ R^d.

## Method

The core mechanism is a mixed-sample SGD that alternates between source and target data at an adaptive rate: at step t, a Bernoulli(1/(1+λ_t)) draw decides the sampling source, and the gradient step is scaled by (1+λ_t) so that the effective objective is R̂_P(θ) + λ_t R̂_Q(θ). The multiplier λ_t is updated via a projected stochastic gradient step on the constraint violation ℓ(θ_t; x_t, y_t) − ℓ(θ_{Q,t}; x_t, y_t) − 6ε_Q, causing it to grow when the current iterate's target risk exceeds the running target-ERM baseline and shrink otherwise. This adaptively tracks a sequence of constrained convex programs min R̂_P(θ) s.t. R̂_Q(θ) ≤ R̂_Q(θ_{Q,t}) + slack, where θ_{Q,t} converges to the target ERM in parallel. The key analytical contribution is showing convergence to the limiting CP solution at O(1/√T) rate despite unbounded constraint sets (e.g., low-rank Σ_Q), by tracking iterate deviation from the constraint set rather than relying on bounded-set projections. The resulting guarantee E_Q(θ̂_PQ) ≲ min{ε_Q, λ_max(Σ_P^{-1}Σ_Q)·ε_P + E_Q(θ_P*)} is adaptive: the method automatically biases toward whichever of source or target data is more predictive.

## Results

The main theoretical guarantee (Theorem 1) shows that Mixed-Sample SGD achieves target excess risk E_Q(θ_PQ) ≲ min{ε_Q, λ_max(Σ_P^{-1}Σ_Q)·ε_P + E_Q(θ_P^*)}, where ε_Q = O(d/n_Q) and ε_P = O(d/n_P), meaning the method automatically adapts to whichever of source or target data is more informative. On the School dataset, Mixed-Sample SGD achieves Q-risk nearly identical to the ideal projected SGD (PSGD) baseline while running significantly faster. On the Berkeley Yearbook dataset (512-dim ResNet18 features), the method consistently outperforms source ERM, target ERM, and HTL, with target ERM failing entirely (error > 4000) due to only 100 target samples. On CIFAR-10 Dog vs Cat (logistic loss, n_Q=50), the method slightly outperforms HTL while maintaining an adaptive rate as n_P increases from 100 to 1500.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against HTL (Kuzborskij & Orabona, ICML 2013), Mixed-Sample SGD is comparable or slightly better on most tasks: on the School dataset both achieve similar Q-risk, but on Berkeley Yearbook HTL struggles to find a proper bias parameter via cross-validation and fails to adapt when source data is more helpful, while Mixed-Sample SGD consistently outperforms it. On CIFAR-10 with increasing target samples (n_Q up to 1500), HTL slightly outperforms Mixed-Sample SGD in excess risk, but its runtime grows dramatically with n_Q while Mixed-Sample SGD's runtime remains stable. Against PSGD (Mahdavi et al., NeurIPS 2012), which solves the same constrained objective (2) via expensive CVXPY projection steps at every iteration, Mixed-Sample SGD achieves nearly identical Q-risk on the School dataset but with significantly shorter runtime, since it avoids per-iteration projections and instead uses a single final projection. On the CIC-IDS-2017 malware detection task, Mixed-Sample SGD achieves better overall accuracy and more stable Type-II error than both HTL and source/target ERM, with runtime significantly less than HTL due to avoiding cross-validation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges a gap between statistical transfer learning theory and computational efficiency. Prior STL work (Hanneke & Kpotufe 2019, 2024; Kuzborskij & Orabona 2013, 2017) established that constrained or penalized objectives yield adaptive transfer guarantees, but left open how to solve them efficiently without expensive cross-validation or projection steps. The paper adapts the SGD-for-constrained-programs framework of Mahdavi et al. (2012) to the transfer learning setting, departing from that work by handling unbounded and evolving constraint sets (driven by a parallel SGD estimating the target ERM), which classical bounded-set analyses cannot accommodate.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The main practical contribution is an SGD procedure that automatically adapts its source/target sampling ratio without any hyperparameter tuning or cross-validation, achieving the better of source-only or target-only rates. This is important for settings where the source data quality is unknown a priori and repeated optimization passes are infeasible. Limitations include the restriction to convex losses (linear regression in the main text), the need to specify ε_Q (related to target sample size), and the fact that the final projection step, while single, still requires a solver like CVXPY. Open questions include extending to non-convex losses (e.g., deep networks), understanding sensitivity to the ε_Q choice in practice, and whether the adaptivity guarantee holds under distribution shift beyond the covariate-shift assumptions used here.
