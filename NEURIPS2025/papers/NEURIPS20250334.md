## Problem

Prior work observed that LLM hidden activations resemble English even for non-English inputs, leading to the hypothesis that LLMs 'think in English' and are inherently constrained by their English capability. However, newer results show non-English performance sometimes surpassing English, creating a contradiction. The paper asks whether LLMs develop a language-agnostic parameter space that supports abstract thought transcending any specific linguistic system, and how this space evolves across model generations.

## Input / Output

The method consumes LLM parameter matrices (treating each row or column as a neuron) and 1,000 sentences per language sampled from the OSCAR corpus to compute neuron activation scores via parallel deactivation. It outputs three key quantities: the Shared Neuron Ratio (|N_shared| / mean |N_exclusive^ℓ|), the Language Agnostic Score (log(1 + mean_ℓ Imp^ℓ) where Imp^ℓ is the per-neuron perplexity impact of shared vs. exclusive deactivation), and a trained model where only a targeted subset of neurons is updated using 100K documents per language from Culturax, MADLAD, and Wikipedia. Evaluation is on MMMLU and MGSM across six typologically diverse languages (Zh, En, Th, Sw, Fr, De).

## Method

The paper identifies language-related neurons by deactivating each neuron (zeroing its parameters) and measuring the L2 change in output embedding, using a parallel layer-wise proxy for efficiency. Neurons are partitioned into Language-Shared (activated across all six languages) and Language-Exclusive (specific to one language) sets. The key metric, the Language Agnostic Score, compares the per-neuron perplexity increase from deactivating shared versus exclusive neurons; a score above log(2) indicates shared neurons are disproportionately critical, signaling language-agnostic function. Across 20 models from LLaMA, Qwen, and Gemma families, the paper shows both the shared neuron ratio and the language agnostic score increase monotonically with model generation (Pearson R=0.92), evidencing progressive emergence of abstract thought. Motivated by this, the paper proposes a score-guided training strategy: for low-score models, train all language-related neurons; for medium-score models, train shared neurons; for high-score models, train only exclusive neurons—validated on Llama-3.2-1B, Llama-3.2-3B, and Llama-3.1-8B with up to 5.7-point MGSM gains.

## Results

Neuron-targeted training yields substantial multilingual gains: on MGSM (abstract reasoning), tuning language-shared neurons in Llama-3.2-3B (medium language-agnostic score) improves average performance by +5.7 points, while tuning language-exclusive neurons in Llama-3.1-8B (high language-agnostic score) gives +4.0 points. On MMMLU (knowledge + reasoning), the best gain is +1.7 points (Llama-3.1-8B, exclusive neurons), suggesting the method primarily enhances reasoning rather than factual recall. Random neuron tuning consistently underperforms or matches the untuned baseline (e.g., −0.6 avg on MGSM for Llama-3.1-8B), confirming that targeted neuron selection is critical.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four neuron-targeted strategies (Shared, Exclusive, Random) against an untuned baseline (None) across three Llama models with varying language-agnostic scores. The optimal strategy shifts with the model's developmental stage: for Llama-3.1-8B (high language-agnostic), exclusive neuron tuning dominates (+4.0 MGSM vs. +0.3 for shared), while for Llama-3.2-3B (medium), shared neuron tuning wins (+5.7 vs. +3.6 for exclusive). For Llama-3.2-1B (low), both strategies help comparably (+3.1 shared, +4.2 exclusive), consistent with the hypothesis that under-trained models benefit from training any language-related neurons. Random neuron tuning consistently fails to match targeted strategies, with negative or near-zero gains on MGSM for the high and medium models, validating that the identified neuron subsets are functionally meaningful. The paper also reports (in Appendix E.3) that neuron-targeted tuning outperforms LoRA as a parameter-efficient baseline, though specific LoRA numbers are deferred to the supplement.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the mechanistic interpretability line of work on language-specific neurons (Tang et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024b) by introducing a shared-versus-exclusive neuron decomposition and a new metric—the Language Agnostic Score—that quantifies whether shared neurons have evolved beyond superficial overlap into functionally critical, language-agnostic components. It departs from the 'LLMs think in English' narrative (Wendler et al., 2024; Schut et al., 2025a) by proposing a third option: a compact, language-agnostic parameter space that supports abstract thought independent of any specific linguistic system. The parallel neuron detection methodology is inherited from Zhao et al. (2024b) and Wang et al. (2025a), while the practical training strategy connects to the broader multilingual enhancement literature (continual pretraining, adapter merging) but operates at a finer, neuron-level granularity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a mechanistic account of why successive LLM generations improve multilingual performance—shared neurons grow both in proportion and in functional criticality—offering practitioners a concrete diagnostic (the Language Agnostic Score) to determine which neurons to target for multilingual fine-tuning. The practical finding that training strategy should shift from shared to exclusive neurons as models mature is actionable and validated across three Llama variants. Key limitations include evaluation restricted to models ≤8B parameters and only six languages, leaving open whether the language-agnostic neuron phenomenon persists or transforms at frontier scale, and whether the correlational evidence across model generations (rather than longitudinal training of a single model) fully establishes causality.
