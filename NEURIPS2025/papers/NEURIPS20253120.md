## Problem

Existing large-scale 3D scene reconstruction methods based on 3D Gaussian Splatting rely on a divide-and-conquer paradigm that partitions scenes into independent blocks, leading to two fundamental issues: (1) complex, scene-specific parameter tuning for block sizes and image assignment thresholds, and (2) loss of global scene consistency, causing illumination mismatches and geometric discontinuities at block boundaries. This paper asks whether a large-scale scene can be optimized holistically in the same manner as a small-scale one, without partitioning.

## Input / Output

The method takes thousands of high-resolution drone-captured RGB images (downsampled 4×) along with COLMAP-estimated camera poses and an initial sparse point cloud as input. It produces a 3D Gaussian representation consisting of coarse Gaussians plus view-dependent decoded Gaussians, which are rendered via differentiable rasterization at real-time speed (≥30 FPS) on a single 24 GB RTX 3090 GPU. Unlike divide-and-conquer methods that output a merged set of independently optimized blocks, MixGS outputs a unified Gaussian set where global structure and local details are jointly optimized.

## Method

MixGS replaces explicit per-block Gaussian densification with an implicit view-aware feature pipeline. For each training view, Gaussians within the camera frustum are extracted; their 3D positions are encoded via multi-resolution hash encoding, and rotation, scale, and camera pose are concatenated as auxiliary features that modulate the spatial features through a learned attention gate. A multi-head MLP decodes these fused features into decoded Gaussians (opacity, color, rotation, scale), whose positions are refined by a learnable offset pool that predicts residual displacements rather than absolute coordinates. The decoded Gaussians are then unioned with the original view-frustum Gaussians to form mixed Gaussians for rendering, where the originals preserve coarse global structure and the decoded ones compensate for missing fine details. Training follows a three-stage schedule—coarse Gaussian optimization, implicit feature learning with frozen Gaussians, then joint fine-tuning—progressively balancing global and local fidelity while keeping peak VRAM under 24 GB.

## Results

On the four main large-scale scenes (Building, Rubble, Residence, Sci-Art), MixGS achieves the highest SSIM across all scenes (0.771–0.856) and delivers the best or highly competitive PSNR and LPIPS. Notably, on the challenging Residence scene, MixGS reaches 23.39 dB PSNR, enabling 3DGS-based methods to outperform NeRF-based baselines. On the additional MatrixCity and Campus datasets, MixGS achieves the best PSNR (28.63 and 24.28 dB), SSIM (0.857 and 0.715), and LPIPS (0.210 and 0.315) simultaneously. Rendering speed reaches ≥30 FPS on all scenes using a single RTX 3090 (24 GB), matching or exceeding methods that rely on A100 GPUs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against NeRF-based baselines (Mega-NeRF, Switch-NeRF, GP-NeRF), MixGS consistently outperforms on SSIM and LPIPS across all four main scenes, while NeRF methods retain a slight PSNR edge only on the inherently blurry Sci-Art dataset. Among 3DGS-based divide-and-conquer methods, MixGS surpasses DOGS (28.63 vs. 28.58 PSNR on MatrixCity; 24.28 vs. 24.01 on Campus), VastGaussian (28.63 vs. 28.33 on MatrixCity), and CityGaussian (28.63 vs. 27.32 on MatrixCity) on all three metrics. On the main four scenes, MixGS achieves the highest SSIM in every case, with PSNR competitive or best (e.g., 26.66 dB on Rubble, 23.39 dB on Residence). In rendering speed, MixGS reaches 33.5–53.9 FPS on a single RTX 3090, comparable to CityGaussian (37.4–64.6 FPS) and 3DGS (45.0–72.2 FPS) which run on A100 40 GB GPUs. The key practical advantage is that MixGS trains on a single 24 GB consumer GPU with tightly bounded memory (~2–3 GB), whereas DOGS and VastGaussian require five RTX 6000 (48 GB) GPUs.

## Relation to Prior Work

MixGS builds directly on 3D Gaussian Splatting's explicit Gaussian parameterization and differentiable rasterization pipeline, and on multi-resolution hash encoding (Instant-NGP) for spatial feature extraction. It departs from the dominant divide-and-conquer paradigm (Mega-NeRF, CityGaussian, VastGaussian, DOGS) that partitions large scenes into independently optimized blocks, instead treating the entire scene as a single holistic optimization problem. The key architectural departure is replacing explicit Gaussian densification with implicit high-dimensional feature encoding via hash tables, combined with a novel mixing operation that unions original (coarse) and decoded (detail) Gaussians for rendering — a design that echoes Scaffold-GS's use of sparse voxel grids to encode Gaussian attributes but applied at the per-view level with camera-pose conditioning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is enabling large-scale scene reconstruction training on a single 24GB consumer GPU, removing the multi-GPU dependency that has been standard in the field. The holistic formulation eliminates the need for scene-specific partitioning thresholds and block-boundary post-processing, which are recurring pain points in divide-and-conquer pipelines. However, the training time penalty is substantial (14–17 hours on one RTX 3090 vs. 2–4 hours on five RTX 6000s for competing methods), and the quality gains over strong baselines like DOGS are modest (typically <1 dB PSNR). Open questions include whether the single-GPU constraint can be relaxed via distributed training without sacrificing the holistic optimization property, and whether the implicit densification strategy scales to even larger scenes (e.g., city-level) where even the coarse Gaussian set becomes memory-prohibitive.
