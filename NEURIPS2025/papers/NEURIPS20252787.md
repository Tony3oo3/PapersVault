## Problem

Connected Autonomous Vehicles promise to reduce urban congestion through collective, data-driven routing, yet no standardized benchmark exists to evaluate whether RL-based routing strategies can actually outperform human drivers or simpler heuristics in realistic, large-scale traffic networks. Existing MARL studies for route choice rely on simplified macroscopic traffic models, homogeneous agent populations, or toy networks, leaving open whether current algorithms can handle the non-stationarity, exponentially growing action spaces, and multi-agent credit assignment challenges of real urban routing.

## Input / Output

Each experiment instance pairs an OpenStreetMap-derived road network (converted to SUMO XML format) with a realistic AM-peak trip demand pattern (OD pairs and departure times from synthetic population data for 28 Île-de-France subregions or the InTAS Ingolstadt scenario), plus configurable CAV fleet share (0–100%) and reward profile (selfish, cooperative, altruistic, etc.). The environment is a microscopic SUMO simulation structured as an Agent-Environment Cycle game where agents act sequentially by departure time, and each episode represents a full day of commute with a human learning model updating route-choice expectations. Outputs include per-group travel times (t_CAV, t_HDV), cumulative training cost (excess travel time during CAV learning relative to the pre-CAV baseline), mean speed and mileage deltas, and a win rate measuring how often CAVs beat the pre-CAV human travel time.

## Method

URB is a benchmarking framework rather than a new algorithm: it formalizes the mixed CAV-human routing problem as an AEC game, provides 29 real-world network-demand pairs, and ships with TorchRL implementations of four MARL algorithms (IQL, IPPO, MAPPO, QMIX) spanning independent learning and CTDE paradigms, alongside three baselines (All-or-Nothing, Random, and a trained human model). The training pipeline follows a four-phase protocol—human stabilization (200 episodes), CAV mutation, CAV training (6,000 episodes), and policy testing (100 episodes)—with a modular JSON configuration scheme for networks, tasks, algorithms, and hyperparameters. The key empirical finding is that despite lengthy training (up to ~150 hours for Ingolstadt), state-of-the-art MARL algorithms rarely outperform the human baseline, with only QMIX on the smallest network (St. Arnoult) achieving a marginal 80% win rate, and performance degrading sharply with network size and demand level, as confirmed by a sensitivity analysis showing travel-time improvement collapsing from −5% to −79% when demand is doubled.

## Results

Across three real-world networks (St. Arnoult, Provins, Ingolstadt) with 40% CAV penetration, state-of-the-art MARL algorithms (IPPO, IQL, MAPPO, QMIX) consistently fail to match the pre-CAV human travel time baseline (t^pre = 3.15, 2.80, 4.21 min respectively). Only QMIX on the smallest network (St. Arnoult) achieves a non-zero win rate (80%), with t_CAV = 3.21 min vs. human t^pre = 3.15 min; on the medium and large networks, QMIX's t_CAV (3.14 and 4.87 min) is worse than the random baseline (3.04 and 4.81 min). Training costs (c_ALL) are substantial across all instances (0.61–2.76 min per agent per episode), and all MARL methods degrade network-level performance (negative ΔV, positive ΔL). The AON baseline matches or exceeds all MARL algorithms on t_test in every network.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the HUMAN baseline (trained day-to-day route-choice model), all four MARL algorithms lose on t_test in every network; only QMIX on St. Arnoult achieves a partial win (80% WR, t_CAV = 3.21 vs. t^pre = 3.15), while on Provins and Ingolstadt even QMIX's t_CAV (3.14, 4.87) exceeds the human baseline by 12% and 16% respectively. Against the AON baseline (deterministic shortest free-flow route), AON matches or beats every MARL algorithm on t_test in all three networks (e.g., AON t_test = 3.15 vs. best MARL 3.24 on St. Arnoult; 2.81 vs. 2.90 on Provins; 4.29 vs. 4.41 on Ingolstadt) and achieves 100% WR on the two smaller networks. Against the RANDOM baseline, IPPO and IQL marginally outperform random on St. Arnoult (t_CAV 3.33/3.53 vs. 3.58) but QMIX on Provins (t_CAV = 3.14) and Ingolstadt (t_CAV = 4.87) is actually worse than random (3.04 and 4.81), indicating that centralized training does not rescue performance in congested settings. The sensitivity analysis (Table 2) further shows that performance degrades sharply with demand: IQL's CAV travel time worsens from −5.5% (half demand) to −79.3% (double demand) relative to t^pre, and granting global observations improves IQL from −12.1% to −3.5%, confirming that locality of information and non-stationarity are the primary bottlenecks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends RouteRL [3] (same first author, SoftwareX 2025), a simulation framework for mixed urban route choice, into a full benchmarking environment with 29 real-world traffic networks, standardized baselines, domain-specific metrics, and a modular configuration scheme. It draws on the classic traffic assignment problem and Wardrop/user equilibrium literature for the human behavioral model, and on the MARL algorithm landscape (IQL, IPPO, MAPPO, QMIX) for the CAV routing policies. The key conceptual departure from prior MARL routing studies (e.g., [63], [53], [61]) is the use of a microscopic traffic simulator (SUMO) with heterogeneous mixed-autonomy populations, realistic demand patterns, and partial observations, rather than simplified macroscopic models or homogeneous agent populations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding is a negative result: state-of-the-art MARL algorithms, despite thousands of training episodes, largely fail to outperform human drivers or even the All-or-Nothing baseline in realistic mixed-autonomy urban routing, with performance degrading sharply as network size and demand increase. This challenges the prevailing optimism that RL can straightforwardly solve large-scale collective routing and highlights fundamental scalability, non-stationarity, and credit-assignment barriers. The benchmark itself is the first standardized evaluation ground for this problem, and the authors note that even minor implementation adjustments (Appendix G.2) yield notable gains, suggesting the field needs both methodological advances and careful engineering. Open questions include whether fundamentally different architectures (e.g., mean-field, graph-based, or meta-RL) are needed, and how to incorporate social metrics (emissions, equity) into the optimization objective.
