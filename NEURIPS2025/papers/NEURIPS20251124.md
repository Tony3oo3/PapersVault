## Problem

Sparse attention in transformers has been studied exclusively as a computational efficiency mechanism, always treated as an approximation of full attention. This paper asks whether sparse attention can actually improve learning convergence and generalization, not just speed up inference, and identifies that the answer depends critically on whether the sparsity pattern is input-dependent or input-agnostic.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251124/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes fixed-length token sequences X = [v_1, ..., v_L] from a vocabulary of size D and produces a class label (or output vector in R^Y) via a readout linear layer applied to a token projection (last-token or mean-pooling) of the final transformer block output. All experiments use encoder-only architectures trained from scratch on structured language benchmarks (LRA tasks like ListOps, Even Pairs, Missing Duplicates; NNCH tasks) with cross-entropy loss, and a preliminary NLP next-token prediction evaluation on Penn Treebank.

## Method

The paper's core insight is that the input-stability of the softmax in the attention mechanism governs the Lipschitz constant of the learning objective, which in turn determines SGD convergence rate and algorithmic stability (hence generalization). They define 'semantic dispersion' (the range of unmasked query-key dot-products per query) as the key quantity controlling softmax stability: smaller dispersion yields a smaller Lipschitz constant. Input-dependent heavy-hitter (top-k) attention naturally reduces this dispersion by masking all but the highest-scoring keys, creating a 'semantic separation' gap between attended and unattended keys. In contrast, input-agnostic patterns (banded, block-local, strided) do not systematically reduce dispersion. Empirically, top-k attention converges 1.4×–8.8× faster than full attention across tasks and hyperparameter settings, while input-agnostic sparse attention shows no such benefit, confirming the theoretical prediction.

## Results

On the LRA and NNCH benchmarks, input-dependent top-k attention (k=5) converges 1.37× faster than full attention on ListOps (121 vs. 167 epochs to 95% training accuracy) and 8.83× faster on Even Pairs (6 vs. 53 epochs). Top-k attention also achieves equal or better final holdout accuracy: on ListOps, full attention drops from 35.1±0.6% to 28.9±1.4% while top-k drops only from 36.3±0.3% to 31.3±0.9%. Input-agnostic sparse attention (banded, block-local) converges slower than full attention and struggles with expressivity unless augmented with global tokens, showing no convergence or generalization advantage.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against full attention (Vaswani et al. [1]), top-k attention wins decisively on convergence speed (1.37×–8.83× fewer epochs to 95% training accuracy) while matching or exceeding final holdout accuracy across all three LRA tasks, with tighter inter-quartile ranges indicating lower variance. Against input-agnostic banded attention (Parmar et al. [20]) and block-local attention (Qiu et al. [21]), top-k wins on both convergence and expressivity: the input-agnostic variants converge slower than full attention and fail on tasks like Even Pairs and Missing Duplicates unless global tokens are added. Even when augmented with global tokens (Ainslie et al. [13]), the input-agnostic variants still do not match top-k's convergence speed or generalization. The Gupta et al. [15] top-k formulation is the specific input-dependent mechanism studied here; the paper's contribution is demonstrating its learning-theoretic advantages (faster convergence, better generalization) rather than proposing a new sparsity pattern. These advantages are robust across MLP activations (ReLU, GELU, Mish), number of transformer blocks (6–22), number of heads (1–8), and optimizer choices (SGD with varying LR/decay, Adam).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reframes sparse attention research, which has been studied almost exclusively through the lens of computational efficiency, by asking whether sparsity can improve learnability and generalization. It builds on the softmax stability bounds from Li et al. (ICML 2023) and the algorithmic stability-to-generalization connection from Hardt et al. (ICML 2016), extending them to characterize how the sparsity pattern affects the Lipschitz constant of the training objective. The key conceptual departure is introducing 'semantic dispersion' and 'semantic separation' as the mechanistic explanation for why input-dependent heavy-hitter sparsity (top-k) helps while input-agnostic sparsity (banded, block-local, strided) does not, drawing an analogy to sensory gating in cognitive science.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that top-k attention converges up to 8.83× faster than full attention while matching or exceeding its generalization provides a principled, theoretically grounded argument for adopting input-dependent sparse attention in large-scale transformer training — a potential significant efficiency win if it holds at scale. However, all empirical validation is on small structured-language benchmarks (LRA, NNCH) with encoder-only architectures trained from scratch, leaving the central open question of whether these benefits persist in large-scale LLM pretraining. The theoretical framework also clarifies that input-agnostic sparsity patterns offer no learning advantage over full attention, which has practical implications for architecture design choices.
