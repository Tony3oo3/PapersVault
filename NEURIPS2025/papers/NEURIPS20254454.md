## Problem

Strategic classification research has been almost exclusively focused on linear classifiers, where strategic behavior is one-dimensional and the effective model class remains unchanged. However, real-world applications (loan approval, admissions, hiring) routinely deploy non-linear models, and it is unclear how non-linearity reshapes strategic behavior, classifier expressivity, and model class complexity. This paper asks: what qualitatively different phenomena arise when users strategically respond to non-linear decision boundaries, and what are the implications for learnability and attainable accuracy?

## Input / Output

The analysis takes continuous user features x ∈ R^d, a deployed classifier h (parameterized by a score function f with decision boundary f(x)=0), and a strategic cost parameter α governing the ℓ2-norm modification budget. It produces the effective classifier h_Δ(x) = h(Δ_h(x)), characterizes which decision boundaries are impossible under strategic behavior, and derives VC dimension bounds for the induced model class H_Δ. The experiments use 2D bounded inputs (|x|_∞ ≤ 100) with degree-k polynomial classifiers and class-conditional Gaussian data, reporting effective polynomial degree k_Δ and maximum strategic accuracy as functions of α and class separation μ.

## Method

The paper takes a bottom-up approach analyzing three nested mappings: (i) individual boundary points z → ∇_h(z), identifying four cases (one-to-one, wipeout, expansion, collision); (ii) entire classifiers h → h_Δ, where the curvature transformation κ_Δ = κ/(1+ακ) bounds positive curvature at 1/α and causes negative curvature to grow unboundedly, leading to four types of impossible effective classifiers; and (iii) model classes H → H_Δ, where the non-bijective mapping means VC dimension can either increase (e.g., Θ(d·VC(H)) for ball-based classes) or decrease (from ∞ to 0 for classes with small negative regions). The key theoretical results show that universal approximators (neural nets, RBF kernels) lose universality, that piecewise-linear classes (ReLU networks) maintain learnability with VC bounded by O(dm·log(m) + ν_p·k·log(k)), and that maximum strategic accuracy can drop to the majority class rate even when standard accuracy is 1. Empirical validation uses random polynomial classifiers to measure expressivity changes and synthetic Gaussian data to quantify approximation gaps.

## Results

The paper's main contribution is theoretical: it shows that strategic behavior with non-linear classifiers can reduce VC dimension from ∞ to 0 (Thm. 1), increase it for scaling-closed classes (Thm. 2), and for piecewise-linear classifiers (e.g., ReLU networks) bounds the effective VC as O(dm·log(m) + ν_p·k·log(k)) (Thm. 3). A key impossibility result (Cor. 2) proves that universal approximators (neural nets, RBF kernels, polynomial thresholds) lose universality under strategic behavior. Experimentally, on synthetic class-conditional Gaussian data, the maximum strategic accuracy of the unrestricted effective class H_Δ* drops from 1 (standard) toward the linear baseline as class separation μ decreases and cost parameter α increases; for well-separated data (large μ), non-linear strategic classifiers still significantly outperform the linear strategic baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing strategic classification methods; instead, it contrasts the strategic and non-strategic settings and different model class families. In the approximation experiment (Sec. 6.2), the unrestricted effective class H_Δ* is compared against two benchmarks: the standard unrestricted class H* (which always achieves accuracy 1) and the linear effective class H_Δ^lin = H^lin. As data becomes harder (smaller μ), H_Δ* diverges from the H* upper bound, with the gap widening for larger α, while H_Δ* still substantially outperforms H_Δ^lin for small μ, confirming that non-linearity retains value even in the strategic regime. In the expressivity experiment (Sec. 6.1), random polynomial classifiers of degree k are compared to the degree k_Δ needed to approximate their effective h_Δ: for small α, k_Δ ≈ k (minimal impact), but for moderate α, k_Δ > k (strategic behavior increases complexity via collision artifacts), while for very large α and high k, k_Δ drops below k due to indirect wipeout removing much of the original boundary.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the strategic classification framework introduced by Hardt et al. [16] and Brückner et al. [3] from the predominantly linear regime to non-linear classifiers, building on the offset-curve machinery from computational geometry (Farouki & Neff [12]) to analyze how decision boundaries transform under strategic user responses. It departs from prior PAC analyses of strategic learning (Sundaram et al. [33], Zhang & Conitzer [36], Cohen et al. [6]) by tackling continuous cost functions rather than finite manipulation graphs, and by providing a bottom-up, three-level analysis (points → classifiers → model classes) that reveals qualitative differences absent in the linear case. The paper positions itself as a foundational understanding step, explicitly noting that existing optimization methods (e.g., Levanon & Rosenfeld [25]) rely on the linear projection structure and do not extend to non-linear settings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding that universal approximators (neural networks, RBF kernels, gradient boosting) lose universality under strategic behavior is a fundamental limitation that reshapes expectations for what is achievable in strategic learning, with concrete implications for model selection, interpolation thresholds, and attainable accuracy. The paper leaves open the general upper bound on VC increase (only bounding it for piecewise-linear classes), the optimization problem for non-linear strategic learning, and the question of how well the ℓ₂ cost model captures real human strategic behavior. Empirical validation is limited to synthetic data, so the practical magnitude of accuracy gaps and expressivity shifts on real-world tasks remains to be established.
