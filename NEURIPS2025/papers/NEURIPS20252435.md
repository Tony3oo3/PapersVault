## Problem

Training recurrent and state-space models in a way that could be physically realized in non-dissipative dynamical systems requires a forward-only, Jacobian-free credit assignment algorithm. Existing forward-mode AD methods like RTRL explicitly compute state-Jacobians and scale cubically with model size, while zeroth-order alternatives introduce prohibitive variance or bias. The paper asks whether one can provably recover exact backward-mode gradients using only forward passes of a Hamiltonian system, without any explicit Jacobian computation.

## Input / Output

The model consumes multivariate time-series sequences of varying length (from ~400 to ~50k timesteps, with 2–6 input channels) and produces either a class label (six UEA classification datasets with 2–5 classes) or a continuous scalar prediction (heart rate from PPG signals in PPG-DaLiA). Sequences are ingested step-by-step through a stack of Hamiltonian Recurrent Units that integrate a separable Hamiltonian via the Leapfrog scheme, so the input is processed causally one timestep at a time rather than in parallel as in Transformers.

## Method

RHEL (Recurrent Hamiltonian Echo Learning) computes loss gradients as finite differences of physical trajectories: after a free forward pass, the system's momenta are flipped (exploiting time-reversal symmetry of Hamiltonian dynamics) and the echo trajectory is nudged by ±ε toward the target at each step. The symmetric difference between the +ε and −ε echo trajectories, divided by 2ε, yields the gradient with no explicit Jacobian and no variance, requiring only three forward passes regardless of model depth. The paper proves this equals the continuous adjoint state method (Theorem 3.1) and, in discrete time with Leapfrog-integrated separable Hamiltonians (Hamiltonian Recurrent Units), equals BPTT exactly (Theorem 3.2). A chaining procedure (Theorem 3.3) extends RHEL to hierarchical stacks of HRUs called Hamiltonian SSMs (HSSMs), where each layer's input-gradient estimate nudges the echo of the layer below, enabling end-to-end training of multi-level architectures on sequences up to ~50k steps.

## Results

On six UEA time-series classification datasets (sequence lengths 405–17,984), RHEL-trained linear HSSMs achieve 62.9±4.9% average test accuracy versus 63.4±3.9% for BPTT, and nonlinear HSSMs achieve 57.8±4.0% versus 58.8±4.3%. On the long-range PPG-DaLiA regression task (sequence length ~50k), RHEL matches BPTT on the linear HSSM (9.5±1.0 vs 9.1±1.1 MSE×10⁻²) but shows a small gap on the nonlinear HSSM (8.4±0.5 vs 7.8±0.5). Static gradient comparison confirms near-perfect cosine similarity and norm ratio alignment between RHEL and BPTT parameter gradients across all layers of both model types.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole algorithmic baseline is Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT), the standard backward-mode AD for RNNs, applied to the same linear and nonlinear HSSM architectures. On classification (Table 1), RHEL matches BPTT within one standard deviation on average for both model types (62.9 vs 63.4 for linear; 57.8 vs 58.8 for nonlinear), with per-dataset differences generally within noise. On the long-range PPG-DaLiA regression task (Table 2), RHEL slightly underperforms BPTT: the gap is small and within one std. dev. for the linear HSSM (9.5 vs 9.1 MSE×10⁻²) but is statistically significant for the nonlinear HSSM (8.4 vs 7.8). The authors attribute this residual gap to finite-nudging approximation bias and numerical precision effects. Overall, the paper's central empirical claim—that RHEL, a forward-only algorithm requiring only three forward passes and no explicit Jacobian computation, is on par with BPTT in final model performance—is supported across all seven datasets and both model families.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Hamiltonian Echo Backprop (HEB, Lopez-Pastor & Marquardt 2023) from static, small-scale problems (XOR, MNIST) to sequential data with per-timestep losses, discrete-time symplectic integration (HRUs), and hierarchical stacks (HSSMs), while proving formal equivalence with both the continuous adjoint state method and BPTT. It departs from HEB by treating model parameters as static (rather than dynamical variables with learnable initial states) and by using standard ML tools rather than dedicated physics formalism. The work also positions itself against forward-mode AD (RTRL) and zeroth-order optimization, arguing that emulating backward-mode AD forward in time via time-reversal symmetry breaking avoids the Jacobian computation and variance/bias tradeoffs of those approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The clean equivalence proofs (RHEL ≡ BPTT ≡ continuous ASM) provide a rigorous bridge between physics-inspired learning and standard ML, potentially enabling self-learning analog hardware for sequence modeling that avoids the memory and compute costs of digital backpropagation. Key limitations remain: RHEL is restricted to non-dissipative Hamiltonian systems (limiting expressivity), still requires explicit Hamiltonian parameter gradients (not fully black-box), and is not yet online. The small but consistent performance gap with BPTT, particularly for nonlinear models on regression, suggests the finite-nudging approximation bias is non-trivial in practice. Whether this framework can be realized in actual photonic, superconducting, or spintronic circuits—and whether it offers meaningful energy savings over GPU-based BPTT—remains an open empirical question.
