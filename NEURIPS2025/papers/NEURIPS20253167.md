## Problem

Existing federated learning methods for mitigating data heterogeneity exhibit limited robustness: a method that performs well under one specific Dirichlet partition of non-IID data often degrades significantly under a different partition with the same heterogeneity parameter. This paper asks how robust these methods truly are across diverse heterogeneity scenarios and identifies that sharing statistical information from other clients is a promising but underexploited mechanism for achieving distribution-agnostic robustness.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253167/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

Each client receives local RGB image-label pairs (32×32 for CIFAR-10/SVHN, 32×32 for CIFAR-100) partitioned via Dirichlet(α=0.1), plus a shared surrogate dataset generated by sampling from class-specific Gaussian distributions (identical across all clients). Additionally, each client consumes the non-self gradient — a normalized direction vector computed from the average parameter updates of other participating clients in the previous round. The output is a global ResNet-18 (CIFAR-10/SVHN) or ResNet-50 (CIFAR-100) model producing 10- or 100-way class predictions, evaluated on top-1 accuracy, communication rounds to reach target accuracy, and speedup over FedAvg.

## Method

FedGPS integrates two orthogonal mechanisms for statistical rectification. First, a static goal-oriented objective modifies each client's loss (Eq. 6) to include a two-stage surrogate distribution alignment: the local feature distribution is aligned with a local surrogate distribution (via the local feature extractor), which is in turn aligned with a global surrogate distribution (aggregated class-wise prototypes at the server). Theorem 4.4 bounds the Wasserstein-1 distance between the true global and local distributions by ε₁+ε₂+κ, providing a privacy-preserving generalization guarantee. Second, a dynamic path-oriented gradient rectification (Eq. 7) adjusts the local update direction at every SGD step by adding a scaled, normalized non-self gradient from other clients, effectively giving each client a global perspective on the optimization path. The synergy of these static (distribution-level) and dynamic (gradient-level) components yields consistent SOTA performance across all tested heterogeneity scenarios, client counts (K=10 and K=100), and sampling rates (50% and 10%).

## Results

FedGPS achieves state-of-the-art top-1 accuracy across all three benchmark datasets and five heterogeneous scenarios. On CIFAR-10 (K=10, 50% sampling), it reaches 90.31% best accuracy in the easiest scenario and 82.04% in the hardest, outperforming the best baseline (VHL) by 1.2–1.5% per scenario. In the more challenging cross-device setting (CIFAR-10, K=100, 10% sampling), FedGPS achieves a mean accuracy of 77.04%±1.19, surpassing the next-best method (FedLESAM, 74.98%±1.88) by over 2%. On CIFAR-100, FedGPS attains 70.69%±0.64 mean accuracy versus 70.16%±0.91 for VHL, and on SVHN it reaches 95.08%±0.45 versus 94.89% for VHL.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FedGPS consistently outperforms all 13 baselines across every heterogeneous scenario and dataset. The closest competitors are VHL and FedLESAM: on CIFAR-10 (K=10), FedGPS beats VHL by 1.2–1.5% and FedLESAM by 1.5–7.9% depending on scenario difficulty. In the cross-device setting (K=100, 10% sampling), the gap widens—FedGPS surpasses FedLESAM by 2.1–5.1% and VHL by 5.6–13.9%, while several baselines (FedASAM, FedExp, FedDisco) fail to converge or fall below FedAvg. On SVHN, gains are modest (~1–2% over VHL) because vanilla FedAvg already approaches centralized performance. On CIFAR-100, FedGPS leads by a smaller but consistent margin (~0.5% over VHL) while exhibiting notably lower variance (±0.64 vs. ±0.91), underscoring its robustness advantage across diverse data partitions.

## Relation to Prior Work

FedGPS builds on two distinct lines of federated learning research: (1) distribution-alignment methods that share statistical information to bridge local-global gaps (FedProto's class prototypes, CCVR's logits calibration, VHL's virtual homogeneity data), and (2) optimization-level corrections to local update directions (SCAFFOLD's control variates, FedProx's proximal term). The key departure is a two-pronged strategy: a static goal-oriented objective that uses privacy-free Gaussian surrogate distributions in a two-stage Wasserstein alignment (formalized in Theorem 4.4), and a dynamic gradient rectification that repurposes the 'predict other clients' aggregated influence' insight from backdoor attack literature [25] for benign optimization. Unlike VHL, which still suffers residual distribution shift from virtual data, FedGPS's surrogate approach avoids raw-data-derived information entirely, and unlike prior single-mechanism methods, it coordinates both distribution and parameter spaces simultaneously.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's most valuable contribution is its comprehensive empirical finding (1100+ experiments across 3 datasets, 5 heterogeneity scenarios, and two client-scale settings) that no single existing FL method is robustly superior across diverse data partitions — a practical concern for real-world deployment where the heterogeneity structure is unknown a priori. FedGPS addresses this by achieving consistent SOTA across all tested scenarios, but its reliance on per-class Gaussian surrogate data introduces additional computational overhead, and it does not yet handle feature-level heterogeneity (only label distribution shift). Open questions include whether the surrogate alignment scales to high-dimensional or non-Gaussian feature spaces, whether the gradient rectification remains effective under extreme client sampling rates, and whether the framework extends to non-convex or multi-task federated settings.
