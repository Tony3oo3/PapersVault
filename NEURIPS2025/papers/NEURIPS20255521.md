## Problem

The conventional assumption in scientific machine learning is that a neural emulator trained on data from a low-fidelity numerical solver can at best reproduce that solver's accuracy. This paper challenges that assumption by identifying 'emulator superiority,' where the emulator, trained purely on low-fidelity data, actually outperforms its training solver when evaluated against a higher-fidelity reference, due to the interplay between the emulator's inductive biases, the training objective, and the structured nature of numerical errors.

## Input / Output

The emulator takes a discretized PDE state vector u_h^[t] (nodal values on a fixed grid of N points) and predicts the next state u_h^[t+1] in a single autoregressive step. Training data consists of consecutive state pairs generated by rolling out a low-fidelity solver (e.g., implicit first-order upwind, truncated Picard iteration for Burgers) from various initial conditions. Evaluation involves multi-step autoregressive rollouts compared against a high-fidelity reference (analytical Fourier solution for linear PDEs, or a fully converged nonlinear solver for Burgers), with the key distinction that the test initial conditions or time horizons may differ from training, probing state-space and temporal generalization.

## Method

The core theoretical contribution uses Fourier analysis to show that a minimal two-parameter linear cross-correlation ansatz, trained via closed-form least-squares on data from an implicit scheme at a single mode ψ, achieves lower error than that implicit scheme at other modes φ when measured against an analytical reference—demonstrating state-space superiority in closed form for advection, diffusion, and Poisson equations. The mechanism is that the emulator's simple functional form (mimicking a forward-in-time central-in-space scheme) has systematically different magnitude and phase error characteristics in Fourier space compared to the training solver, and these differences favor the emulator in regions where the solver's numerical errors are most pronounced. Empirically, the paper extends to nonlinear architectures (ConvNets, Dilated ResNets, FNOs, UNets, Transformers) trained with standard one-step MSE supervision on advection and Burgers equations, demonstrating both state-space superiority (ξ^[1]<1) and autoregressive superiority (ξ^[t]<1 for t≥2) where the emulator's multi-step error accumulation is more favorable than the training solver's, even when trained on data from a relatively accurate explicit scheme within its stability limit.

## Results

The paper demonstrates that a simple two-parameter linear ansatz trained on implicit first-order upwind data achieves a superiority ratio ξ^[1] < 1 (i.e., lower error than the training solver) when tested at Fourier modes φ > ψ (forward superiority) for the advection equation at γ₁ = -3.0. For nonlinear emulators on advection, nearly all tested architectures (ConvNet, Dilated ResNet, FNO, UNet, Transformer) achieve autoregressive superiority with ξ^[t] < 1 after a few rollout steps, even when trained on the relatively accurate explicit scheme within its stability limit (γ₁ = -0.9). For the Burgers equation, a UNet trained only on smooth initial frames from a nonlinearly truncated implicit solver learns a shock propagation angle that more closely matches a fully converged high-fidelity reference, demonstrating superiority over its own training data for a finite number of time steps.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between neural emulators and their own training numerical solvers (explicit first-order upwind, implicit first-order upwind, Richardson iteration), evaluated against a higher-fidelity analytical or converged reference. The emulators consistently outperform their training solver in specific regimes: for linear advection, the linear ansatz beats the implicit baseline for φ > ψ (forward superiority), while for Poisson it beats the Richardson iteration for φ < ψ (backward superiority). Among nonlinear architectures, the local ConvNet uniquely achieves state-space superiority (generalizing from single-mode training to multi-mode testing), whereas FNOs and Transformers with global receptive fields show limited extrapolation in this setting. All architectures achieve autoregressive superiority (ξ^[t] < 1 for t > 1) on advection, indicating more favorable multi-step error accumulation than the training solver. Notably, superiority is also observed when training on the explicit scheme (which is more accurate than the implicit one), suggesting the effect is not solely due to the training solver being very inaccurate.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the standard neural PDE emulator literature (FNOs, neural operators, autoregressive surrogates) by identifying a phenomenon—'emulator superiority'—that is orthogonal to prior approaches that improve emulators by mixing in high-fidelity data (Lu et al., 2022), adding physics-informed losses (Li et al., 2021b), or learning from high-fidelity references to produce improved coarse solvers (Bar-Sinai et al., 2019). The key distinction is that superiority here arises purely from the training pipeline's structure: a one-step MSE objective combined with the emulator's inductive biases (e.g., local convolutional structure) and multi-step autoregressive evaluation, with no external high-fidelity signal. The closest conceptual analogue is 'amplification' in particle physics (Butter et al., 2021), where generative models recover high-fidelity distributions from noisy data, but this paper provides a mechanistic Fourier-space explanation for deterministic PDE solvers rather than a statistical one.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that emulators can implicitly correct structured numerical errors challenges the field's default assumption that a surrogate is bounded by its training data's fidelity, with direct implications for how emulators are benchmarked: if the reference solver itself contains artifacts, a 'better' emulator may be unfairly penalized. The practical relevance is significant for compute-constrained settings where low-fidelity solvers are the only available data source, and for Model Predictive Control where emulators operate within specific regimes. Open questions include precisely characterizing the operational boundaries of superiority, understanding how it scales to multi-physics and 3D problems, and whether unrolled training or physics-informed objectives amplify or suppress the effect. The phenomenon is regime-dependent rather than universal, so its practical exploitation requires careful identification of the relevant operating conditions.
