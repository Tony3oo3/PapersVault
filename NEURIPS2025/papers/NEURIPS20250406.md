## Problem

Existing guardrail benchmarks rely on ad hoc risk taxonomies that lack principled grounding in standardized safety policies (government regulations, platform conduct guidelines, industry ethical standards), and they overlook domain-specific risks where the same risk category carries vastly different implications across contexts (e.g., privacy violation in social media vs. human resources). This limits their alignment with real-world operational requirements and their ability to reveal domain-specific blind spots in guardrail models.

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes text in four interaction formats—declarative statements, user questions/instructions, multi-turn conversations, and adversarial text with appended optimized suffixes—across eight safety-critical domains (social media, HR, finance, law, education, code generation, cybersecurity, and general regulation). Guardrail models produce binary safe/unsafe moderation labels, which are scored against fine-grained annotations mapping each instance to one of 400+ policy-grounded risk categories and 1000+ specific safety rules, enabling rule-level failure attribution. Evaluation reports F1, Recall, and FPR rather than likelihood-based metrics, since many API-based guardrails do not expose calibrated confidence scores.

## Method

GUARDSET-X is constructed via a unified pipeline: (1) a safety policy scraping agent navigates diverse web sources to collect 150+ domain-specific policy documents; (2) a two-stage GPT-4o prompting framework extracts a two-level hierarchy of 400+ risk categories and 1000+ atomic safety rules; (3) rule-conditioned prompting of uncensored LLMs generates unsafe examples that explicitly violate specific rules, while a minimal-edit detoxification prompt produces topically relevant but policy-compliant safe counterparts; (4) both types are augmented into multiple interaction formats including multi-turn conversations where intent is gradually revealed; and (5) attack-enhanced instances are created by combining three jailbreak strategies (risk category shifting, reasoning distraction, instruction hijacking) with iterative adversarial prompt optimization via PAIR and AutoDAN. The resulting 100k+ instance dataset is used to benchmark 19 guardrail models from Meta, Google, OpenAI, NVIDIA, IBM, Microsoft, Amazon, and AllenAI, revealing findings on domain specialization, scaling stagnation, adversarial fragility, and conservative bias.

## Results

Across 19 guardrail models evaluated on GUARDSET-X (100k+ instances, 8 domains, 400+ risk categories), F1 scores range from near-zero (ShieldGemma 2B: ~5% on Social Media) to 75–90% for top performers (MDJudge 2, WildGuard, Granite Guardian 3B). The best overall models—MDJudge 2 (avg F1 ~75%), WildGuard (~75%), and Granite Guardian 3B (~77%)—still show large variance across domains, with F1 dropping below 50% on Code and Law for several top models. Under adversarial attacks (PAIR/AutoDAN-optimized suffixes), even the strongest models suffer severe degradation: Granite Guardian 5B reaches 92.8% average attack success rate, while WildGuard is the most robust at 19.8% ASR. Smaller models (LlamaGuard 3 1B, Granite Guardian 3B) outperform their larger counterparts in average F1, indicating scale alone does not guarantee better moderation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a benchmark paper evaluating 19 existing guardrail models rather than proposing a new method, so comparisons are among the evaluated models. MDJudge 2 and WildGuard lead overall, with MDJudge 2 achieving the highest F1 in 6 of 8 domains (e.g., 76.9 Finance, 77.9 Education, 89.1 Cyber) and WildGuard excelling in Social Media (75.4) and Finance (86.5). Granite Guardian 3B outperforms its 5B counterpart in average F1 (77.4 vs. 74.9), and LlamaGuard 3 1B beats 8B (48.5 vs. 42.3), demonstrating that scaling does not guarantee improvement. In adversarial robustness (Table 2), WildGuard is the clear winner with 19.8% average ASR, compared to 64.5% for LLM Guard, 67.7% for Aegis Defensive, 77.6% for MDJudge 2, and 92.8% for Granite Guardian 5B. API-based guardrails (Azure Content Safety, Bedrock Guardrail, TextMod/OmniMod) consistently underperform open-weight models, with Azure Content Safety scoring below 20% F1 in 7 of 8 domains. The LlamaGuard series shows an evolution tradeoff: LlamaGuard 4 improves long-tail coverage (Cybersecurity F1: 0.472→0.797) but degrades on common risks like Hate Speech (0.777→0.734) and General Regulation (5.7 avg F1).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior guardrail benchmarks (OpenAI Mod, GuardBench, ToxicChat, BeaverTails) that rely on ad hoc risk taxonomies or narrow domain coverage by grounding all 400+ risk categories and 1,000+ safety rules in 150+ authentic policy documents spanning eight safety-critical domains. It also extends the adversarial robustness evaluation line (PAIR, AutoDAN, JailbreakBench) by targeting guardrail models specifically rather than the underlying LLMs, and introduces detoxification prompting to generate hard benign examples at scale, going beyond the small, manually-annotated sets in XSTest and OKTest. The two-level policy hierarchy (categories → atomic rules) enables rule-level failure attribution that prior category-level benchmarks cannot support.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GUARDSET-X provides the first principled, policy-aligned evaluation framework that reveals systematic blind spots in current guardrails: domain-specific specialization, severe adversarial fragility (>90% ASR for some models), and a conservative bias toward false negatives. The finding that model evolution within a series can degrade performance on common risk categories while improving long-tail coverage has direct implications for how organizations deploy and update guardrail systems. A key limitation is the Western-centric policy sourcing, leaving non-Western regulatory frameworks unrepresented. Open questions include whether robustness-aware training can close the adversarial gap without sacrificing the already-low recall, and whether the policy-grounded pipeline generalizes to rapidly evolving regulatory landscapes.
