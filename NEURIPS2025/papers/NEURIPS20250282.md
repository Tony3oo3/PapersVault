## Problem

LLMs frequently fail to prioritize system-level instructions when they conflict with user-level directives, undermining safety-critical applications. Existing remedies—prompt formatting, segment embeddings, or full fine-tuning—lack explicit structural modeling of the attention mechanism and either yield limited gains or demand substantial compute. The paper asks how to detect which internal components are responsible for instruction-conflict sensitivity and adapt only those with minimal parameter overhead.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a token sequence structured as system instruction + user instruction + task prompt, parsed via template-aware masking (ChatML, Alpaca [INST], OpenAI <|im_start|> formats) to identify the system segment boundaries. Rather than producing a standard language-modeling loss, the training signal is an attention-based focus loss computed on the final token's attention row, encouraging it to allocate more weight to system-segment tokens. The output remains a standard text response, but the supervision operates entirely on internal attention distributions rather than output tokens.

## Method

FocalLoRA operates in two stages. First, the Conflict-Sensitive Heads Identification (CSHI) module scores every attention head across layers using a composite metric that combines element-wise L1 magnitude gap, cosine directional dissimilarity, and symmetrized row-wise KL divergence between attention matrices under conflicting versus consistent instruction inputs; the top 10% of heads by composite score are designated as focal heads. Second, the System-Aware Heads Optimization (SAHO) module attaches low-rank LoRA adapters (rank 8, alpha 16) exclusively to the query and key projections of those focal heads, while all other weights remain frozen under NF4 4-bit quantization. Training uses a novel focus loss that computes the ratio of attention mass falling on the system-instruction mask at the final generation token and minimizes its negative, thereby directly steering attention toward higher-priority directives. This targeted adaptation tunes fewer than 0.02% of total parameters (e.g., 0.0188% on Llama-8B) yet yields up to 61 percentage-point gains in system instruction compliance.

## Results

On the primary instruction-conflict benchmark (Simple format), FocalLoRA#8 on Llama-3.1-8B raises system instruction compliance from 17.62% (Template baseline) to 53.14%, a +35.52 pp gain while fine-tuning only 0.0188% of parameters. Scaling to FocalLoRA#32 pushes Llama-8B to 78.96% and Qwen2.5-14B to 80.64%. Under adversarial injection attacks (out-of-domain, Ignore strategy), FocalLoRA#32 on Llama-8B reaches 76.38% compliance, surpassing the SOTA ISE baseline (67.59%) by +8.79 pp. MMLU scores remain within ±0.5 pp of vanilla, confirming no general-capability degradation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FocalLoRA is compared against three baselines: Ordinary (plain concatenation), Template (standard role-tagged formatting), and ISE (Instructional Segment Embedding, the prior SOTA). Against the Template baseline, FocalLoRA#8 alone yields gains of +10 to +35.52 pp across all five models, with the largest absolute improvement on Llama-8B. Against ISE on the out-of-domain adversarial injection benchmark (Clean Alpaca, Ignore attack), FocalLoRA#32 on Llama-8B achieves 76.38% versus ISE's 67.59% (+8.79 pp), and on Mistral-7B (Escape attack) reaches 76.37% versus ISE's 71.13% (+5.24 pp). In the in-domain setting, FocalLoRA#32 on Qwen2.5-14B (Ignore) scores 84.10% versus ISE's 80.95% (+3.15 pp). FocalLoRA consistently outperforms ISE across all models, attack types, and domain settings, with the margin being largest under the Ignore attack where explicit override phrases most severely disrupt baseline compliance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out a distinct niche within the instruction-hierarchy literature by shifting the intervention point from prompt-level or embedding-level modifications (e.g., ISE's segment embeddings, structured role tokens) to the attention mechanism itself. It builds on the growing body of work showing that only a small subset of attention heads carries disproportionate functional weight (Michel et al., Voita et al., Perez & Ribeiro), but repurposes this insight for a specific safety task: identifying 'focal heads' whose attention distributions shift most dramatically under instruction conflict, then applying LoRA exclusively to their Q/K projections. The focus loss that directly supervises attention mass on system tokens is a new training signal not present in prior PEFT or instruction-hierarchy work, and the two-stage CSHI→SAHO pipeline is positioned as complementary to (rather than replacing) architecture-level separation or inference-time attention steering approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in the extreme parameter efficiency—tuning 0.0188% of parameters to gain 35+ percentage points in system-instruction compliance across five model families—making hierarchical alignment feasible even in resource-constrained or quantized deployments. However, the method currently depends on static template parsing to construct the system-segment mask, limiting its applicability to open-ended or multi-turn settings where role boundaries are ambiguous. Open questions include whether focal heads remain stable across domains and dialogue lengths, whether dynamic head selection at inference time could further reduce the training set, and whether the approach extends to multimodal instruction conflicts. The MMLU results (±0.5 points) reassure that the gains do not come at the cost of general capability, but the single-turn, two-party evaluation protocol leaves multi-turn and third-party (retrieved content) scenarios unaddressed.
