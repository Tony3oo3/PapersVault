## Problem

Prior work demonstrated that transformer language models progressively overfit to n-grams of increasing order during pretraining, but this finding was limited to a single architecture and did not test whether contextual semantic similarity constitutes a dissociable predictive factor beyond n-gram statistics. The paper asks whether these behavioral phases are universal across architectures (Transformer, Mamba, RWKV), training corpora (OpenWebText vs. The Pile), and scale (14M to 12B parameters), and whether the three heuristics of frequency, n-gram probability, and semantic similarity jointly account for the bulk of model behavior at every training step.

## Input / Output

The analysis consumes 1,418 model checkpoints (Pythia 14M–12B, Open-GPT2 117M/345M, and newly trained Parc models in Pythia/Mamba/RWKV at ~130–169M) and evaluates each on the NaWoCo dataset—158K decontaminated words in natural sentence contexts drawn from FineWeb, filtered to be single tokens across all model vocabularies and absent from both training corpora. For every checkpoint, the method computes the model's log-probability of each target word and compares it against n-gram log-probabilities (n = 1–5, estimated via infini-gram counts with Stupid Backoff, α = 0.4, on the model's own training corpus) and contextual semantic similarity (cosine similarity between the fastText embedding of the target word and the mean or SGPT-weighted mean of its context embeddings, using both Wikipedia-trained and Common-Crawl-trained vectors). Outputs are per-step correlation coefficients, per-step regression coefficients for the three heuristics, R² values on train and validation splits, and the identification of three behavioral phases.

## Method

Experiment 1 tracks Pearson (and Spearman) correlations between LM log-probability and each of the seven heuristics (unigram through 5-gram log-probability, plus four variants of contextual semantic similarity) across all training checkpoints, revealing consistent peaks that shift toward higher-order n-grams as training proceeds. Experiment 2 fits multiple linear regressions with z-transformed unigram log-probability, 5-gram log-probability, and contextual semantic similarity as predictors of LM log-probability, allowing the authors to dissociate each heuristic's independent contribution while controlling for the others. This regression reveals three behavioral phases: (1) a sharp rise in the unigram coefficient with concurrent increase in semantic similarity, (2) a decline in unigram and rise in 5-gram coefficients, and (3) stabilization of all coefficients. The three heuristics explain up to 98% of variance in model predictions, and the phase structure is remarkably consistent across all 1,418 model instances regardless of architecture, training corpus, or parameter count. To enable the cross-architecture comparison, the authors train and release the first publicly available checkpointed Mamba-1 and RWKV-4 models (the Parc suite), all trained in parallel on identical OpenWebText sequences with matched tokenizers.

## Results

Across 1,418 model instances (14M–12B parameters, three architectures, two training corpora), three simple heuristics—unigram log-probability, 5-gram log-probability, and contextual semantic similarity—explain up to 98% of the variance in language model log-probabilities on the NaWoCo evaluation set (R² = 0.86–0.98 depending on model and training step). All models exhibit three consistent behavioral phases: (1) a sharp rise in the unigram coefficient, (2) a shift from unigram to 5-gram dominance, and (3) stabilization of all coefficients. Cross-architecture Pearson correlations between Parc-Pythia, Parc-Mamba, and Parc-RWKV log-probabilities reach r ≥ 0.93 at every step ≥ 80, confirming that the behavioral trajectory is architecture-invariant.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper extends the n-gram overfitting finding of Chang et al. (2024), who showed that 5 seeds of a 117M GPT-2 model become differentially correlated with n-gram probabilities of increasing n during pretraining, by demonstrating the same pattern holds across three orders of magnitude of scale (14M–12B Pythia), two training corpora (The Pile vs. OpenWebText), and three architectures (Transformer, Mamba, RWKV). Unlike Chang et al., who only tracked raw correlations, this paper uses multiple linear regression to dissociate the effects of unigram, 5-gram, and semantic similarity, showing that unigram probability remains overweighted even after controlling for 5-gram probability. The semantic similarity analysis builds on Michaelov et al. (2024), who reported a Pearson r = −0.46 to −0.61 between GPT-3 surprisal and fastText-based contextual similarity; here, the regression shows that after accounting for n-gram probabilities, the similarity coefficient emerges early in training and persists throughout, with the Common-Crawl-based fastText vectors (r = 0.67–0.69 with unigram) tracking unigram more closely than Wikipedia-based vectors (r = 0.34–0.35). The paper also goes beyond Karpathy et al. (2016), who observed n-gram-like behavior in RNNs, by showing that the same phased overfitting trajectory applies to modern state-space (Mamba) and recurrent (RWKV) architectures, not just transformers.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate lines of research: (1) the finding that neural language models overfit to n-grams of increasing order over training (Karpathy et al., 2016; Chang et al., 2024), and (2) the finding that language model predictions correlate with contextual semantic similarity (Michaelov et al., 2021, 2024). Chang et al. (2024) demonstrated the n-gram overfitting trajectory for a single transformer architecture (GPT-2) trained on one dataset; this paper extends that analysis to Mamba and RWKV architectures, two training corpora, and three orders of magnitude in scale, while simultaneously introducing semantic similarity as a third dissociable heuristic via linear regression. The regression framework itself is borrowed from psycholinguistics (Lau et al., 2013; Frank and Willems, 2017), where it is used to test whether frequency, predictability, and similarity have independent effects on human processing.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The demonstration that three simple heuristics explain up to 98% of variance in LM predictions—and that the same behavioral phases (overfitting to increasing n-grams, then a shift toward higher-order statistics) appear identically across Transformer, Mamba, and RWKV architectures—suggests the autoregressive task itself, rather than architectural details, is the primary driver of training dynamics. This has implications for understanding emergence: if all architectures must 'crawl through n-gram predictions' before learning more abstract patterns, then architectural innovations may not bypass these phases. Key open questions include whether the pattern holds beyond n=5, whether contextual (rather than static) embeddings change the picture, and whether larger Mamba/RWKV models (beyond the ~130–169M scale tested here) show the same dynamics. The released NaWoCo dataset and Parc checkpointed models provide useful infrastructure for future training-dynamics research.
