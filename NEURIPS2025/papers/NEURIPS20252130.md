## Problem

Despite a decade of research, Adam remains the de facto optimizer for transformer-based language model training, yet its advantage over simpler methods like SignSGD with momentum (Signum) is not fully understood. Prior work showed Adam and Signum are closely related, but the paper demonstrates that even after extensive tuning, Signum still incurs a 25% effective compute penalty relative to Adam at 160M parameters. The missing piece is identifying precisely what mechanism in Adam—beyond signed momentum—accounts for this persistent gap.

## Input / Output

The method operates coordinate-wise on stochastic gradients g_k ∈ ℝ^d and model parameters w_k ∈ ℝ^d, producing a normalized update direction d_k that is scaled by a scheduled learning rate. The analysis reduces to the scalar case (single gradient component) for theoretical clarity. Empirically, the method is evaluated by training 160M and 410M parameter transformer LMs (12–24 layers, RoPE, RMSNorm, SwiGLU) on SlimPajama and Fineweb with sequence lengths of 512–2048 and batch sizes of 128–512, reporting validation perplexity on 100M held-out tokens.

## Method

The paper's core finding is that constraining Adam's two momentum parameters to be equal (β₁=β₂=β) yields near-optimal performance across all tested configurations, reducing Adam to a one-parameter optimizer. Under this constraint, Proposition 1 shows the Adam update direction equals m_k / √(m_k² + β·EMA_β[(m_{k-1}−g_k)²]), where the denominator's second term is an online estimator of gradient variance. Theorem 4.1 then proves this variance estimator arises exactly from a regularized maximum-likelihood / KL-regularized Gaussian variational inference problem: the two momentum buffers are the posterior mean and variance of a slowly-varying Gaussian distribution over gradients. This reframes Adam as an adaptive, SNR-modulated trust-region method—equivalent to Signum whose step size shrinks when local noise dominates signal—explaining why the variance term cannot be replaced by a fixed constant and why Adam outperforms Signum on heterogeneous transformer landscapes.

## Results

On a 160M-parameter language model trained on 3.2B SlimPajama tokens (Chinchilla-optimal, sequence length 2048, batch size 256), Adam achieves a validation perplexity of 21.86±0.21, substantially outperforming Signum (23.23±0.16), RMSprop (27.04±0.34), and SGD (53.62±5.14). Signum closes 96% of the perplexity gap between SGD and Adam but still incurs a 25% effective training slowdown relative to Adam. Across 1,500+ training runs, the paper further finds that constraining Adam's momentum parameters to β₁=β₂ yields near-optimal performance across batch sizes, sequence lengths, datasets, and model scales up to 410M parameters.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Adam consistently outperforms all simplified baselines. Against Signum (SignSGD with momentum, Bernstein et al. 2018), Adam achieves 21.86 vs. 23.23 validation perplexity—a gap of ~1.37 points that persists even after extensive tuning of momentum, clipping, and learning rates (65 Signum configurations vs. 200 Adam configurations). Against plain SignSGD (no momentum), the gap widens dramatically to 21.86 vs. 36.78, confirming that momentum is essential but insufficient to replicate Adam's behavior. Against RMSprop (Tieleman & Hinton 2012, i.e., Adam with β₁=0), Adam achieves 21.86 vs. 27.04, showing that the first-moment (mean) tracking provides a substantial additional benefit beyond second-moment-only preconditioning. The paper also notes that even at shorter sequence lengths (512) where the Signum–Adam gap narrows, Adam retains a clear ~0.7 perplexity advantage, and this gap is robust to dataset choice (Fineweb), weight decay removal, and scale-up to 410M parameters.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work connecting Adam to SignSGD-with-momentum (Signum), initiated by Balles and Hennig (2018) and reinforced by Kunstner et al. (2023) and Bernstein and Newhouse (2024). It departs from prior work in two key ways: (1) it identifies β₁=β₂ as the empirically near-optimal constraint that makes Adam effectively a one-parameter optimizer, and (2) it provides a precise Gaussian variational inference interpretation of Adam's two momentum buffers as online mean and variance estimators—something Balles and Hennig's SNR heuristic could only suggest informally. The heterogeneous Hessian landscape analysis from Zhang et al. (2024a) motivates the toy quadratic experiments in §5.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical recommendation to adopt β₁=β₂ (e.g., 0.95) as the default for Adam in LM training is immediately actionable and already emerging in recent large-scale work, but this paper provides the most comprehensive empirical validation (1,500+ models across datasets, batch sizes, sequence lengths, and scales up to 410M). The variational inference interpretation offers a principled lens for future optimizer design, yet the paper explicitly leaves open why the specific quotient form m/√(m²+σ²) is optimal rather than other SNR-regularized variants. Limitations include the relatively small parameter scale (≤410M), grid-based hyperparameter search, and the fact that the theory does not yet explain the full optimization dynamics, only the statistical estimation step.
