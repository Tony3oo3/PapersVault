## Problem

When Alice trains an open-weight language model and Bob produces text or a derivative model from it, Alice has no principled way to prove provenance: existing approaches either require modifying the model (canaries, backdoor triggers), rely on private held-out sets that Bob can evade, or use heuristic output-similarity metrics that produce false positives on independently trained models. The paper formulates model and text provenance as a statistical independence testing problem and asks whether the randomized training order itself can serve as a detectable signal.

## Input / Output

In the query setting, Alice supplies her ordered training transcript (a subsample of training examples with their positions) and queries Bob's model for token log-likelihoods on those examples; an independent reference model (e.g., a same-family model trained on a different shuffle) is used to control for inherent text difficulty. In the observational setting, Alice only has Bob's generated text (a collection of short continuations, typically 128–32 tokens each) and must either index n-grams from partitioned training batches or retrain small copies of her model on reshuffled data. The output is an exact p-value (Spearman correlation under a t-distribution in the query setting; permutation-based in the partitioned observational setting) or an approximate p-value from a z-score (shuffle-based observational setting), with the null hypothesis being that Bob's artifact is independent of Alice's training run.

## Method

The core insight is palimpsestic memorization: language models assign higher likelihood to training examples seen later in training, so the training order is inscribed into the model like layers on a palimpsest. In the query setting, the test statistic is the Spearman rank correlation between Bob's model's log-likelihoods on Alice's training examples (optionally normalized by a reference model's likelihoods) and the examples' training positions; under the random-shuffle assumption, this correlation is exactly zero under the null, yielding provably exact p-values via the t-distribution. In the observational setting, two complementary approaches are used: (1) partitioning the transcript into contiguous batches, building n-gram indices per batch, and correlating the count of n-gram matches in Bob's text with batch order; and (2) retraining k copies of Alice's model from an intermediate checkpoint on independent reshuffles of the last 1–10% of training data, then computing a z-score of Bob's text likelihood under the original-order model relative to the reshuffled copies. The theoretical guarantee (Theorem 1) shows that under Assumption A1 (randomly shuffled transcript) and independence, the permutation-based p-value is exactly uniform, providing provable Type-I error control regardless of training data composition.

## Results

In the query setting, the palimpsestic membership test achieves p-values ≤ 10⁻⁸ for 34 of 40 Pythia and OLMo fine-tuned models (1B–12B parameters), with OLMo fine-tunes reaching p < 10⁻¹³ in 9 of 12 cases; the four exceptions are all 1.4B models with severely degraded pretraining loss (4.5–5.6 vs. 3.0±0.1 for the base). Non-derivative models (OLMo, Llama-3-8B) correctly yield p-values > 0.1 across all transcripts. In the observational setting, the shuffling-based test (φ_obs^shuff) achieves p < 10⁻³ from as few as 320 tokens of Bob's text (retraining on just the last 2% of Alice's pretraining data), while the partitioning-based test (φ_obs^part) requires several hundred thousand tokens to reach comparable significance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary empirical comparison is against Nikolic et al. [17] ("Model Provenance Testing for LLMs"), a similarity-based method that compares token-match rates between two models against reference models. In Appendix C, the authors demonstrate a critical failure mode: Nikolic et al.'s test reports a p-value of 9.6×10⁻⁶ (indicating non-independence) for pythia-1.4b and pythia-1.4b-deduped, which are in fact independently trained on different data orders—a false positive. In contrast, the palimpsestic test correctly assigns p-values > 0.1 to this pair (Table 4). The paper also contrasts its approach qualitatively with canary-based fingerprinting [3], dataset inference [4], and inference-time watermarks [23–26], noting that those methods fail one or more of the three desiderata (effectiveness, transparency, noninvasiveness) or are inapplicable to the observational setting. No direct head-to-head numerical comparison is run against these other methods, as they require model modification or private test sets that are incompatible with the paper's noninvasive, transparent framework.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior model provenance methods (fingerprinting via canaries/backdoors, dataset inference with private test sets, and heuristic output-similarity comparisons) by reframing provenance verification as a statistical independence test that requires no model modification and no private data. It repurposes the memorization literature's observation that language models assign higher likelihood to recently seen training data into a test statistic (Spearman correlation with training order), yielding exact p-values under the assumption of shuffled training data. In the observational setting, where no prior effective method existed, the paper introduces two novel approaches: n-gram partitioning and retraining on reshuffled data to create a reference distribution for Bob's text likelihood.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first method to provide provably exact p-values for model provenance while being both noninvasive and transparent, filling a gap where prior approaches sacrificed one of these properties. The observational setting (text-only attribution) is particularly important for real-world scenarios like detecting social media bots, though it currently requires hundreds of thousands of tokens for the partitioning approach or retraining for the shuffle approach. Key open questions include reducing computational cost, enabling third-party verification without full training data disclosure, and understanding the privacy and copyright implications of the palimpsestic memorization effects the paper characterizes.
