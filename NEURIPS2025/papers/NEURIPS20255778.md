## Problem

Existing web-agent and search benchmarks rely on short-horizon tasks with predefined, time-invariant answers (often a single string), making them ill-suited for evaluating modern agentic search systems that produce lengthy, time-varying, citation-backed reports after hundreds of actions across dozens of websites. There is no benchmark or evaluation methodology that can rigorously and automatically assess both the correctness and source attribution of such complex, open-ended answers.

## Input / Output

Each task-specific judge agent consumes the agent's answer text (including inline URL citations), the original task description, and the fetched content of every cited webpage (extracted text plus rendered screenshots for non-textual context). It outputs a binary score (0 or 1) at each leaf node of the rubric tree, which are then aggregated bottom-up via a gate-then-average rule to produce a continuous Partial Completion score and a binary Success Rate per task. Rubric trees average 50 nodes (up to 603) across 4–6 layers, with each leaf conforming to a single binary judgment.

## Method

The core contribution is an Agent-as-a-Judge framework built on tree-structured rubrics that exploit the generation-verification asymmetry: although answers vary across agents and times, the evaluation criteria are known a priori and can be encoded as a task-specific rubric. Each rubric decomposes the task into hierarchical nodes—critical nodes act as hard gates that zero out their parent on failure, while non-critical nodes allow partial credit via averaging; sequential nodes short-circuit downstream evaluation on early failure. At the operational level, a judge agent is a Python-scripted agentic workflow that interleaves an LLM-based Extractor (parsing structured information such as item names, prices, and URLs from the answer) and an LLM-based Verifier (checking extracted claims against webpage text and screenshots), plus deterministic tool calls. To scale rubric and script creation, the authors build an LLM-based code-generation pipeline (using Claude-3.7-Sonnet) with iterative self-debug and self-reflection, followed by a two-stage human refinement process. Human evaluation on 720 leaf-node verifications confirms 99.03% accuracy of the automated judge agents, substantially exceeding the <90% accuracy typical of prior LLM-as-a-Judge approaches for simpler web tasks.

## Results

On Mind2Web 2 (130 long-horizon, time-varying web search tasks), the best-performing system, OpenAI Deep Research, achieves a Partial Completion score of 0.54 and a Success Rate of 0.28, reaching 50–70% of human performance (0.79 PC, 0.54 SR) while spending less than half the time (8.4 min vs. 18.4 min). Deep Research systems collectively outperform search-augmented LLMs (ChatGPT Search, Perplexity Pro Search) and the web agent OpenAI Operator by 0.18–0.28 in Partial Completion. The large gap between Partial Completion and Success Rate across all systems (e.g., 0.54 vs. 0.28 for OpenAI Deep Research) indicates that agents frequently make partial progress but struggle to fully satisfy all task criteria.

## Baselines & Comparisons

OpenAI Deep Research (0.54 PC) leads all evaluated systems, outperforming the second-best Grok DeeperSearch (0.52) by a narrow margin and Gemini Deep Research (0.45) by 0.09. Among Deep Research systems, test-time scaling is evident: Grok DeeperSearch (0.52) beats Grok DeepSearch (0.40) by 0.12, and Perplexity Deep Research (0.42) beats Perplexity Pro Search (0.28) by 0.14, confirming that longer inference horizons yield substantial gains. Search-augmented LLMs (ChatGPT Search at 0.26, Perplexity Pro Search at 0.28) and the web agent OpenAI Operator (0.26) form a clear lower tier, trailing the best Deep Research systems by 0.26–0.28 in Partial Completion. HF Open Deep Research (0.26), despite being the only open-source system evaluated, underperforms due to frequent system-level errors (e.g., failing to generate valid tool-invocation code). Notably, OpenAI Deep Research also outperforms humans on certain detail-intensive tasks, and all systems show degraded performance on the 57 explicitly time-varying tasks, with Operator and humans being the relative exceptions due to their live-browsing capabilities.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm (Zheng et al., 2023) into a multi-step Agent-as-a-Judge framework (Zhuge et al., 2024), where a task-specific agentic workflow interleaves LLM-based extraction, verification, and tool calls rather than relying on a single LLM call. It adapts the tree-structured rubric design from PaperBench (Starace et al., 2025) but largely automates rubric generation via an LLM-based code pipeline with self-debugging, whereas PaperBench relies on manually authored rubrics. Conceptually, the paper departs from prior web-agent benchmarks (Mind2Web, WebVoyager, WebArena) and search benchmarks (BrowseComp, GAIA) that restrict evaluation to short-horizon or time-invariant single-answer tasks, instead tackling long-horizon, time-varying tasks with comprehensive, citation-backed answers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Mind2Web 2 is the first benchmark to systematically evaluate agentic search on realistic long-horizon, time-varying tasks with attribution verification, filling a critical gap as Deep Research systems become the dominant paradigm for web-scale information gathering. The Agent-as-a-Judge framework achieves 99% evaluation accuracy (vs. <90% for prior automated methods), providing a scalable and reliable evaluation methodology that could be adopted beyond this specific benchmark. Key open questions remain: how to evaluate tasks requiring multi-page cross-verification, how to assess source credibility rather than mere attribution, and whether the 130-task scale provides sufficient statistical power for fine-grained system comparisons. The finding that the best system (OpenAI Deep Research) reaches only 50–70% of human performance while halving task time suggests substantial headroom but also highlights persistent struggles with real-time web interaction.
