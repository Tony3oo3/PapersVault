## Problem

Chance constrained programming (CCP) is generally intractable because the feasible set is nonconvex even for linear constraints, and evaluating the probability constraint requires high-dimensional integration. Existing approaches either demand exact knowledge of the uncertainty distribution (convex approximation) or reduce to computationally intractable binary integer programs (SAA). This paper asks whether a general, sample-based framework can solve CCP efficiently when only samples from the unknown distribution are available.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a set of samples {h^(ℓ)} from the unknown uncertainty distribution, the differentiable objective f(x), the constraint function g(x,h), and a risk level ρ ∈ (0,1). Training data is not drawn directly from the feasible set but generated by solving a family of deterministic restricted problems with increasing constraint tightness z_i, yielding pairs (x^(i), ρ^(i)) where ρ^(i) is an empirical violation rate. The output is a sample x_0* from the product distribution Q_β(x|ρ) ∝ r(x|ρ)·B_β(x), which asymptotically concentrates at the CCP optimum as β→∞; in practice, a single DDIM-based reverse pass with T'=100 steps produces the solution.

## Method

GGDOpt reformulates CCP as sampling from the product of an unknown data distribution (encoding the nonconvex constraint) and a known Boltzmann distribution (encoding the objective). A three-stage pipeline is used: (1) feasible training points are generated by solving deterministic restricted problems and estimating empirical violation rates; (2) a U-Net score estimator is trained with classifier-free guidance to approximate the conditional score ∇_x log p_t(x|ρ), where ρ is randomly dropped with probability p_uncond; (3) the reverse SDE is augmented with a closed-form gradient guidance term G_t derived from the product-distribution structure, requiring only first- and second-order derivatives of f (no backpropagation through the score network). The second-order guidance involves inverting a regularized Hessian (∇²f + (1/βσ²)I) and uses Tweedie's formula for the posterior mean, yielding faster convergence and lower variance than the first-order variant. Theoretical analysis shows that as the diffusion time T→∞ and inverse temperature β→∞, generated samples concentrate at the global minimizer, with a finite-T, finite-β error bound decomposed into a time-length error and a temperature error.

## Results

On the linear chance constrained problem (n=8), GGDOpt with second-order guidance achieves a mean objective value of −0.6491 (std 0.0056), closely approaching the SOC_CVX ground truth of −0.6586, while running in 0.051 s versus 0.321 s for CVX (~6× speedup) and 0.628 s for SAA_PDCA (~80% overhead reduction). On the robust waveform design problem (N_t=16, K=3), GGDOpt with second-order guidance achieves the best function value (0.1237 at ρ=0.20) with a runtime of ~0.068 s, roughly 18× faster than the Bernstein-type inequality baseline (~1.26 s) and Sphere Bounding (~1.39 s). The gradient guidance is critical: without it, GGDOpt degrades to a mean objective of 0.3481 with high variance (std 0.5486), far from the optimum.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GGDOpt with gradient guidance clearly outperforms all SAA-based baselines on both solution quality and stability: its mean objective (−0.6491) is closer to the CVX ground truth (−0.6586) than SAA_PDCA (−0.6389), SAA_MIP (−0.6281), or SAA_CVaR (−0.5893), while its standard deviation (0.0056) is 3–6× lower than the SAA methods (0.0157–0.0314). SAA_SNSCO is highly unstable (std 3.40) and produces a positive mean objective, making it unreliable. In terms of runtime, GGDOpt (~0.05 s) is ~12× faster than SAA_MIP (15.45 s), ~12× faster than SAA_PDCA (0.63 s), and ~6× faster than SOC_CVX (0.32 s), achieving the paper's claimed ~80% overhead reduction relative to SAA_PDCA. On the waveform design task, GGDOpt second-order guidance achieves the best function value (0.1237 at ρ=0.20) versus Bernstein-type (0.1244) and Sphere Bounding (0.1357), while running ~18× faster than both baselines. The ablation showing GGDOpt without guidance (mean 0.3481, std 0.5486) confirms that the gradient guidance term is essential for the method to approach the optimum.

## Relation to Prior Work

GGDOpt sits at the intersection of two research threads: (1) the long line of work on solving chance constrained programming via convex approximation (requiring exact distribution knowledge) or sample average approximation (reformulating as intractable integer programs), and (2) the recent wave of diffusion-model-based optimization methods (Kong et al. 2024, Krishnamoorthy et al. 2023, Li et al. 2024) that treat optimization as sampling from a target distribution. The paper's key conceptual departure is reformulating CCP as sampling from the product of an unknown constraint-defined distribution and a known Boltzmann distribution, then using classifier-free guidance (Ho and Salimans 2022) to learn the conditional score of the unknown component while injecting closed-form first- and second-order gradient guidance for the Boltzmann component. Unlike Guo et al. (2024), whose gradient guidance requires backpropagation through the score network, GGDOpt derives guidance terms in closed form from the product-distribution structure, avoiding that computational overhead.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first diffusion-based framework for CCP that operates purely from samples without requiring parametric distribution assumptions, which is practically important in settings where the uncertainty distribution is intractable or unknown. The theoretical contribution—showing asymptotic concentration around the global minimizer and providing a non-asymptotic error bound decomposed into time-length and inverse-temperature components—gives practitioners confidence in the method's behavior. Open questions include how the approach scales to very high-dimensional decision variables (the experiments use n=8 and Nt=16), how it handles multiple coupled chance constraints with different risk levels simultaneously, and whether the ~80% runtime advantage persists when the restricted problem (13) itself becomes expensive to solve. The empirical evidence, while solid, is limited to two problem families and a small number of dimensions, leaving the method's robustness to more complex nonconvex geometries untested.
