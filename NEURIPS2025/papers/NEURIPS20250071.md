## Problem

Despite the empirical success of CLIP-based post-hoc OOD detection methods like MCM and NegLabel, there has been no formal theoretical justification for why they work. This paper asks: how can we theoretically characterize the effectiveness of these methods, and can we improve upon them with a principled decomposition of the underlying estimation task?

## Input / Output

The method takes a 224×224 RGB image, a set of K in-distribution class labels (ImageNet-1K), and L negative labels mined from WordNet (grouped into T=10 subsets). At test time, the image is randomly cropped (scale λ=0.55) to produce a sub-view, and both the full image and sub-view are encoded by CLIP ViT-B/16. The output is a scalar OOD score S_ours(x;θ) that, compared against a threshold calibrated at 95% ID recall, yields a binary ID/OOD decision. The final score is averaged over V=2 random crops to reduce variance.

## Method

The paper first establishes an information-theoretical density-based framework in which the ID density is modeled as an energy-based model whose energy function is the point-wise mutual information (PMI) between the input image and each ID label. It then proves that MCM and NegLabel are stochastic Monte Carlo estimators of this PMI, with the estimation upper bound determined by the number of sampled labels. The key algorithmic contribution is a divide-and-conquer decomposition of PMI via the chain rule: W(x,y) = W(x̃,y) + W(x,y|x̃), where x̃ is a random crop of x. This splits the estimation into a sub-view PMI term (estimated with the same NegLabel-style grouped negative labels) and a conditional PMI term (estimated using a blended feature rᵀ[βz̃+(1−β)z]/κ). The decomposition provably raises the estimation upper bound from log(K+L/T) to log(K+L) without requiring additional negative labels, reducing underestimation bias while avoiding the performance degradation observed with excessive negatives.

## Results

On ImageNet-1K with four OOD datasets (iNaturalist, SUN, Places365, Textures), the proposed method (Ours Median) achieves an average AUROC of 95.07% and FPR95 of 21.20%, outperforming the strongest zero-shot baseline NegLabel (94.21% AUROC, 25.40% FPR95) by 0.86% AUROC and 4.20% FPR95. The Ours (Mean) variant further improves to 96.00% AUROC and 22.36% FPR95. On the domain-generalizable setting (ImageNet-S, ImageNet-A as ID), the method maintains its advantage, e.g., achieving 95.52% AUROC and 25.11% FPR95 on ImageNet-S versus NegLabel's 93.59% and 27.42%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all 18 baselines on average across the four OOD datasets. Against the closest zero-shot competitor NegLabel, Ours (Median) improves average FPR95 from 25.40 to 21.20 (−4.20) and AUROC from 94.21 to 95.07 (+0.86), with the largest per-dataset gain on Places (FPR95: 35.59→26.92). Against MCM, the improvement is dramatic: FPR95 drops from 43.93 to 21.20 and AUROC rises from 90.82 to 95.07. Among training-based methods, Ours (Median) surpasses LAPT (94.68 AUROC / 23.40 FPR95) and NegPro (94.81 / 23.01) on average, and also beats CLIPN (93.10 / 31.10), LSN (92.96 / 30.22), and LoCoOp (93.52 / 28.66). The only baseline that approaches Ours on a single dataset is NegLabel on iNaturalist (FPR95 1.91 vs 1.04), where both methods are near-saturated. In the domain-generalizable setting (Table 2), Ours consistently beats both MCM and NegLabel on ImageNet-S and ImageNet-A, confirming robustness to domain shift.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper provides the first information-theoretic unification of CLIP-based post-hoc OOD detection, showing that MCM and NegLabel—despite their seemingly different scoring functions—are both stochastic Monte Carlo estimators of point-wise mutual information (PMI) between the input image and ID labels, framed within an energy-based density model. The theoretical scaffolding draws on the energy-based OOD detection framework of Liu et al. and the variational PMI estimation machinery of Poole et al., while the algorithmic contribution (decomposing PMI via the chain rule into unconditional and conditional terms) is a divide-and-conquer departure from prior methods that treat PMI estimation as a monolithic task. The method inherits NegLabel's negative-label mining and grouping strategy but augments it with a random-cropping sub-view to instantiate the conditional PMI term.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theoretical framework gives practitioners a principled lens for designing future CLIP-based OOD detectors: the estimation upper bound (log N nats) directly quantifies how many labels are needed to avoid underestimation bias, and the PMI decomposition shows that splitting the estimation into sub-tasks can provably raise this bound without requiring proportionally more negative labels. However, the empirical gains over NegLabel are modest (~3% FPR95 on ImageNet-1K), the analysis relies on the strong assumption that CLIP's similarity score approximates the true PMI up to a constant, and the method is only validated on a single ID dataset (ImageNet-1K) with one CLIP architecture (ViT-B/16). Open questions include whether the PMI decomposition generalizes to other VLMs, whether the random-cropping sub-view is optimal versus other transformations, and whether the theoretical guarantees hold under distribution shift in the ID data.
