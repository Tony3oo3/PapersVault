## Problem

Backpropagation through time in RNNs suffers from vanishing and exploding gradients over long temporal horizons, while existing alternatives like generalized teacher forcing impose overly strong constraints on neural activity that limit exploration and can destabilize learning. The paper asks how feedback can be used to guide RNN dynamics during learning in a way that preserves temporal credit assignment without the excessive activity constraints of teacher forcing.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251840/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The RNN (50-neuron leaky recurrent network) receives time-varying input sequences of varying dimensionality (1–3 channels) and produces a lower-dimensional linear readout (N_y < N) over a response window. Forcing is applied only during the response period, leaving activity unconstrained during stimulus and delay phases. Tasks include binary pulse sequences (delayed XOR), a constant analog frequency value (sine generation), and sparse left/right signal counts (evidence integration), with difficulty controlled by delay length or frequency range.

## Method

Error forcing (EF) replaces the fixed teacher state of generalized teacher forcing with an orthogonal projection of the current network state onto the zero-error manifold: the target is r_t* = r_t + g(W_φ⁺ e_t), where g denotes a stop-gradient and e_t is the output error. Because the correction lies in the row space of the readout, activity in the readout null-space is left free, allowing the network to explore the full zero-error manifold rather than being pinned to a single minimum-norm point. The stop-gradient prevents the Jacobian damping that TF induces (Eq. 7), so performance gains arise from minimally guiding activity toward an optimal regime rather than from stabilizing gradient magnitudes. A Bayesian interpretation frames the error corrections as MAP-filtered latent variables in a Gaussian state-space model, connecting EF to variational EM and explaining why training-time forcing generalizes to unforced test-time dynamics. For biological plausibility, BPTT is replaced by RFLO and the pseudoinverse feedback weights can be approximated by random-fixed or random-learned projections.

## Results

On the delayed XOR task, EF-BPTT maintains a high convergence rate (proportion of networks reaching MSE < 0.1) across the full range of delay lengths, while standard BPTT degrades sharply and TF-BPTT is outperformed at very long delays. On sine wave generation, BPTT MSE increases substantially with mean frequency, TF destabilizes learning entirely (very high test MSE across all frequencies), and EF-BPTT systematically improves over BPTT. On evidence integration, EF-BPTT consistently outperforms BPTT, with more modest gains over TF-BPTT. Under the bio-plausible RFLO setting, EF-RFLO still improves over both RFLO and TF-RFLO on delayed XOR, though gains are smaller than with BPTT.

## Baselines & Comparisons

EF-BPTT outperforms vanilla BPTT across all three tasks (delayed XOR, sine generation, evidence integration), with the largest gains on delayed XOR at long delays where BPTT's vanishing gradients cause sharp performance collapse. Against TF-BPTT (generalized teacher forcing), EF-BPTT wins on delayed XOR at very long delays and on sine generation where TF actually destabilizes learning; on evidence integration the gap between EF and TF is more modest. EF-BPTT is also notably more robust to the forcing strength parameter α than TF-BPTT, suggesting the orthogonal projection direction matters more than the exact forcing magnitude. Under the bio-plausible RFLO credit assignment, EF-RFLO still beats both RFLO and TF-RFLO on delayed XOR, though the advantage shrinks relative to the BPTT setting; on evidence integration all three (RFLO, TF-RFLO, EF-RFLO) perform comparably. The paper also shows that replacing the pseudoinverse feedback with random fixed projections severely degrades EF performance, confirming that the specific orthogonal projection geometry is essential.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the generalized teacher forcing framework (Hess et al., 2023) by replacing the fixed minimum-norm teacher state with an orthogonal projection of the current network state onto the zero-error manifold, yielding a minimally invasive correction. The key conceptual departure is that EF's benefits arise from the *direction* of the correction (toward the manifold) rather than from Jacobian dampening, which is the mechanism by which TF stabilizes gradients. The authors further ground EF in variational EM (Neal & Hinton, 1998) and the reparameterization trick, showing that the error corrections correspond to greedy MAP filtering in a Gaussian state-space model, and they combine EF with RFLO (Murray, 2019) to obtain a biologically plausible variant that avoids BPTT.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

EF provides a theoretically principled (Bayesian) justification for why training-time activity forcing generalizes to unforced test-time dynamics, resolving a known train-test mismatch in teacher forcing. The finding that TF can actively destabilize learning (sine wave task) and that directionality of feedback matters more than its magnitude challenges the prevailing view that forcing works primarily through gradient damping. Open questions include resolving the weight-transport problem for forcing synapses, extending to nonlinear/multilayer decoders, and adapting EF to reinforcement learning settings where the 'target' is a critic's expected reward rather than a supervised label.
