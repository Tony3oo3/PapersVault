## Problem

Data integration methods for causal inference typically assume that outcome measures are identical across datasets, an assumption that frequently fails in practice. When studies measure related but distinct outcomes (e.g., SOWS vs. COWS for opioid withdrawal severity) and neither study records both measures on the same individuals, it is unclear whether and when borrowing strength from auxiliary data improves estimation precision. This paper provides the first formal quantification of the efficiency–bias tradeoff in such settings.

## Input / Output

The method consumes two tabular datasets: a primary study with covariates X, binary treatment T, and outcome Y, and an auxiliary study with the same X and T but a different outcome W, where Y and W are never jointly observed for any individual. A structural linking equation ν_Y^t(x) = α(x)ν_W^t(x) + β(x) connects the two outcomes, with α and β either fully known, partially known (β known, α unknown), or entirely unknown. The output is a CATE or ATE estimate with 95% confidence intervals, accompanied by semiparametric efficiency bounds that characterize the best achievable variance under each knowledge scenario and a misspecification bias formula quantifying the cost of an incorrect α.

## Method

The paper introduces a partially linear outcome-link assumption and derives efficient influence functions (EIFs) under three nested knowledge scenarios about the linking functions α(X) and β(X). Under the strongest assumption (both known), the EIF combines scores from both studies, yielding a strictly smaller asymptotic variance than the primary-data-only estimator; however, Theorem 5 shows that misspecifying α introduces a bias proportional to E[(α_mis − α*)/α* · θ(X) | S=1]. Under weaker assumptions (α unknown), the EIF for θ(X) reduces to the primary-data-only score, proving no asymptotic efficiency gain, but a two-stage estimator that first fits μ_W(X) on auxiliary data and then regresses Y on α(X)μ_W(X)+β(X) can achieve finite-sample MSE improvements when α(X) is simpler than μ_Y(X) (i.e., ω_α ≪ ω) and the auxiliary sample is large relative to the primary sample. The framework is validated through simulations varying n, covariate dimension p, and study-size ratio, and applied to the XBOT and POAT opioid-use-disorder trials, where the known-α estimator yields a statistically significant treatment effect that reverses the primary-data-only conclusion—illustrating both the promise and the fragility of the approach.

## Results

In the MOUD case study (XBOT + POAT, n≈540 primary), the three estimators yield: θ̂₀ (primary only) = −0.18 (95% CI width 1.52), θ̂_b (auxiliary, unknown α) = 0.42 (95% CI width 0.65), and θ̂_a (auxiliary, known α=0.61) = −1.08 (95% CI width 1.41). Only θ̂_a achieves statistical significance, indicating BUP-NX is superior to XR-NTX in reducing withdrawal severity. The theoretical results show that asymptotic efficiency gains are possible only under the fully-known link assumption A.5(a); under A.5(b) or A.5(c), the semiparametric efficiency bound equals the primary-data-only bound, though finite-sample MSE improvements are possible when α(X) is simpler than μ_Y(X) and n₁ ≫ n₀.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three estimators rather than external baselines: (1) θ̂₀ using only the primary XBOT data, (2) θ̂_b incorporating auxiliary POAT data under the partially-known link A.5(b), and (3) θ̂_a incorporating auxiliary data under the fully-known link A.5(a). On precision (CI width), θ̂_b achieves the narrowest interval (0.65), but its point estimate (0.42) contradicts the direction suggested by the other two estimators. θ̂_a is the only estimator yielding a statistically significant result (−1.08), but this hinges entirely on the correctness of the assumed α=0.61 derived from published clinical thresholds. The sensitivity analysis reveals that for α > 0.75, the significance of θ̂_a disappears, exposing the fragility of the conclusion. In simulation (Appendix A), θ̂_a dominates θ̂₀ and θ̂_b in MSE especially at high dimensionality p and large auxiliary-to-primary sample ratios, while θ̂_b shows finite-sample MSE advantages over θ̂₀ only at small n that vanish asymptotically—confirming the theoretical prediction of no asymptotic gain when α is unknown.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the data-integration-for-causal-inference literature (generalization, transportability, efficiency boosting) by addressing a specific gap: integrating studies whose outcome measures differ and where neither study records both outcomes on the same individuals. Existing approaches—meta-analysis, data harmonization, and surrogate-outcome methods (Athey et al., 2019; Ghassami et al., 2022)—all require either co-measurement of both outcomes or strong equivalence assumptions. The paper builds on the Robinson (1988) semiparametric regression framework and the Tsiatis (2006)/Newey (1990) efficient influence function machinery to derive efficiency bounds under three graded linking assumptions, providing the first formal quantification of when disparate-outcome integration helps or hurts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central cautionary finding—that asymptotic efficiency gains are possible only under the strongest (fully known linking function) assumption, while misspecification of that assumption introduces bias—challenges the default intuition that pooling more data always improves causal estimates. The case study vividly illustrates the risk: a statistically significant treatment-effect difference emerges solely from an untestable linear mapping between SOWS and COWS scales. Open questions include relaxing the structural linking assumption, incorporating a third 'bridge' dataset where both outcomes are co-measured, and extending the framework to settings with no shared treatment arm across studies.
