## Problem

Prior multi-distribution learning algorithms achieve optimal sample complexity only with many adaptive rounds (often polynomial in 1/ε), while fully non-adaptive approaches incur a k-fold sample overhead. The quantitative tradeoff between the number of adaptive rounds and sample complexity in the intermediate regime was largely unexplored, leaving open whether a small or even constant number of rounds could recover near-optimal sample efficiency.

## Input / Output

The algorithm receives sequential sample access to k unknown distributions over X×{0,1}, a hypothesis class H of VC dimension d, a tunable round budget r, and target error ε. It outputs a single predictor (a majority-vote classifier over r weak learners) that minimizes worst-case error across all k distributions. Unusually, the number of samples drawn from each distribution in each round is chosen adaptively based on prior-round information, and the round complexity r is an explicit design parameter trading off against sample complexity.

## Method

For realizable MDL, the paper applies a novel variant of AdaBoost that maximizes a margin defined over the k distributions: each round learns a predictor on a weighted mixture, then updates mixture weights via thresholded empirical error, yielding a sample complexity of dk^{Θ(1/r)}/ε with a matching lower bound. For agnostic MDL, the paper introduces the OODS framework—optimizing a concave function over the simplex with restricted first-order oracle access governed by an expanding trust region—and solves it via LazyHedge, a Hedge variant that only draws new samples when the current weight vector exits the observable region (a box or ellipsoid defined by a cap vector). The ellipsoid version of LazyHedge achieves Õ(√k) rounds with near-optimal Õ((d+k)/ε²) sample complexity, while poly(k) lower bounds in OODS show that sub-polynomial rounds would require fundamentally new techniques beyond the standard ERM-plus-Hedge paradigm.

## Results

For realizable MDL, the paper establishes a tight sample-adaptivity tradeoff: an r-round algorithm achieves sample complexity O(k^{2/r} log(k) · d/ε + k log(k) log(k/δ)/ε), nearly matching the lower bound Ω(dk^{1/r}/(r log²k)). In particular, 3 rounds suffice for Õ(d√k/ε) sample complexity, while log k rounds recover the optimal Õ((d+k)/ε). For agnostic MDL, the LazyHedge algorithm achieves the optimal sample complexity Õ((d+k)/ε²) in Õ(√k) rounds (ellipsoid version), improving over the O(log(k)/ε²) rounds of prior work. The OODS framework yields nearly tight round complexity bounds: Õ(√(k/s)) upper and Ω(√(k/s)) lower in the ellipsoid setting for ε ≤ O(1/k).

## Baselines & Comparisons

In the realizable setting, prior algorithms [BHPQ17, CZZ18, NZ18] all require O(log k) rounds to achieve the optimal Õ((d+k)/ε) sample complexity. This paper's Theorem 1 shows that fewer rounds are possible at the cost of higher sample complexity: with only r = 3 rounds, the sample complexity degrades to Õ(d√k/ε), which is a significant improvement over the Ω(dk/ε) lower bound for fully non-adaptive (r=1) sampling. The lower bound (Theorem 2) confirms that log k rounds are essentially necessary for the optimal sample complexity. In the agnostic setting, the key comparison is with [ZZC+24], which achieves the same optimal sample complexity Õ((d+k)/ε²) but requires O(log(k)/ε²) rounds. This paper's LazyHedge algorithm reduces the round complexity to Õ(√k) (ellipsoid version) or O(k log k) (box version), which is strictly better when ε ≪ 1/k^{1/4}. Compared to [HJZ22] (which requires Õ((log|H|+k)/ε²) rounds) and [AHZ23] (O(log(k)/ε²) rounds), the improvement is substantial for small ε. The OODS lower bounds (Theorem 9) show that any algorithm following the same game-dynamics/ERM paradigm as [ZZC+24] faces a poly(k) round barrier, suggesting that further improvements require fundamentally new techniques.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the multi-distribution learning (MDL) framework established by Blum et al. [BHPQ17] and Haghtalab et al. [HJZ22], which achieved optimal sample complexities but required many adaptive rounds (often polynomial in 1/ε). The key departure is formalizing the sample-adaptivity tradeoff as a first-class question: prior work treated round complexity as an implementation detail, whereas this paper characterizes exactly what sample complexity is achievable with r rounds. For the realizable case, the authors apply AdaBoost in a novel margin-maximization way over distributions; for the agnostic case, they introduce the OODS (Optimization via On-Demand Sampling) framework and a LazyHedge algorithm that modifies the Hedge-based approach of Zhang et al. [ZZC+24] to draw samples lazily, reducing rounds from O(log k/ε²) to Õ(√k).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The tight dk^{Θ(1/r)}/ε characterization for realizable MDL gives practitioners a clear knob: even 3 adaptive rounds yield a √k improvement over fully non-adaptive sampling, while ~log k rounds recover the optimal bound. The OODS lower bounds (poly(k) rounds necessary) are a meaningful negative result, showing that the current game-dynamics/Hedge paradigm cannot achieve sub-polynomial rounds for agnostic MDL without fundamentally new techniques. The open question of whether polylog(k/ε) rounds suffice for agnostic MDL with optimal sample complexity remains unresolved, and the authors suggest that algorithms like Peng's [Pen24] that construct refined hypothesis classes may circumvent the OODS lower bounds.
