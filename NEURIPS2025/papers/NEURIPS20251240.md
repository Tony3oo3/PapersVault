## Problem

Frontier LLMs with long Chain-of-Thought reasoning achieve strong scores on Olympiad-level math benchmarks, yet they rely on a narrow set of learned strategies and fail on problems requiring novel insights. Existing math datasets are either too heterogeneous to isolate specific reasoning skills or too narrow in scope, making it impossible to diagnose exactly where and why models fail to generalize. OMEGA addresses this by constructing controlled train-test pairs that independently probe three axes of out-of-distribution generalization—exploratory, compositional, and transformative—inspired by Boden's typology of creativity.

## Input / Output

Input consists of programmatically generated math problems drawn from 40 templated generators across six domains (arithmetic, algebra, combinatorics, number theory, geometry, logic & puzzles), each parameterized by a complexity vector θ that controls a task-specific metric (e.g., polygon vertex count, matrix dimension, number of composed functions). Problems are calibrated to AIME-level difficulty and verified by construction via symbolic, numerical, or graphical methods (e.g., OpenCV polygon counting, grid search for function intersections). Output is exact-match accuracy on the final answer, evaluated on held-out sets of 100 samples per complexity level for frontier models and 20–50 samples per compositional/transformative setting for RL experiments.

## Method

OMEGA constructs matched training-test pairs from template-driven problem families, where each template encodes a single-scope reasoning strategy with meaningful parameter variation. For exploratory generalization, the model trains on low-complexity instances (δ ≤ δ₀) of one template and is tested on higher-complexity instances of the same family; for compositional generalization, it trains on two separate skill templates and is tested on problems requiring their synergistic fusion; for transformative generalization, it trains on conventional solutions and is tested on problems where the familiar tactic fails, demanding a qualitatively different strategy. RL fine-tuning uses the GRPO algorithm on 1,000 training problems (complexity levels 1–2) applied to Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, with evaluation on in-distribution and out-of-distribution test sets. The framework enables infinite-scale, reproducible problem synthesis with precise control over diversity, complexity, and the specific reasoning strategies required, allowing causal attribution of model failures to particular generalization gaps.

## Results

On OMEGA, all four frontier models (DeepSeek-R1, Claude-3.7-Sonnet, OpenAI-o3-mini, OpenAI-o4-mini) show consistent performance degradation from near-ceiling accuracy at low complexity to near-zero at high complexity, despite substantial inference-time compute. RL (GRPO) fine-tuning of Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct yields strong in-distribution gains (e.g., Zebra Logic: 30%→91% ID, 83% OOD) but generalization plateaus: in Arithmetic GCD, level-5 accuracy remains at 3% regardless of training up to level 1–4. Compositional generalization shows negligible improvement (<6 pp) on combined-skill tasks despite individual skills improving substantially (e.g., polygon rotation 13%→69%), and transformative OOD accuracy remains near 0% after RL, with one setting (matrix rank) actually deteriorating by 30 pp.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates four frontier reasoning LLMs—DeepSeek-R1, Claude-3.7-Sonnet, OpenAI-o3-mini, and OpenAI-o4-mini—on OMEGA and finds all four exhibit the same qualitative failure mode: accuracy drops from near-ceiling at low complexity to near-zero at high complexity, with no model maintaining above 50% past moderate difficulty. This contrasts with their strong reported performance on existing Olympiad-level benchmarks (AIME, MATH500), suggesting those benchmarks do not stress the specific generalization axes OMEGA probes. The RL fine-tuning experiments (GRPO on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct) are compared against the untrained base model: RL wins decisively on in-distribution tasks across all three generalization axes (e.g., +56 pp on matrix rank ID) but loses or ties on the hardest OOD problems, where accuracy stays at or near the base model's level. For compositional generalization, RL improves each individual skill (polygon rotation +56 pp, pattern matching +10 pp) yet the composed task gains remain under 6 pp, indicating the model cannot integrate separately learned strategies. For transformative generalization, RL provides no meaningful OOD improvement (often 0%), and in the matrix-rank setting actually degrades OOD accuracy by 30 pp, suggesting RL can entrench suboptimal strategies rather than encourage novel reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by repurposing Margaret Boden's 1998 typology of creativity (strong, weak, and autonomous creativity) into a concrete three-axis evaluation framework—exploratory, compositional, and transformative generalization—for mathematical reasoning. While prior benchmarks (GSM-Symbolic, GSM-Plus, GSM-Infinite) probe OOD robustness through numerical perturbation, and compositional generalization work (Keysers et al., Dziri et al.) tests skill recombination in controlled settings, none systematically isolate the specific question of whether RL-trained LLMs can perform creative strategic shifts. The template-based, programmatically generated problem construction draws on the synthetic-data tradition (GSM-Infinite, DeepMind Mathematics suite) but adds fine-grained complexity control and solution verification by construction, enabling causal analysis of generalization failures that existing coarse or narrow benchmarks cannot support.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

OMEGA provides the first controlled diagnostic for distinguishing mechanical proficiency from genuine mathematical creativity in LLMs, identifying that RL fine-tuning reliably improves exploratory generalization but plateaus on compositional and transformative tasks—a gap that targeted data augmentation alone may not close. The finding that RL can actually *degrade* OOD performance (e.g., matrix rank dropping 30 pp) raises important questions about whether current RL objectives inadvertently reinforce suboptimal patterns. Open questions include whether larger models or different training paradigms (e.g., explicit strategy diversity in pretraining) can bridge the transformative gap, and whether the observed limitations are specific to 7B-scale models or reflect a fundamental architectural constraint.
