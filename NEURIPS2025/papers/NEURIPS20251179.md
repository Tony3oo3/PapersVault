## Problem

Despite widespread observation of emergence in large language models, the mechanics driving sudden capability acquisition during training remain poorly understood, and how data distribution properties influence emergence timing lacks a theoretical account. This paper identifies sparse attention learning as a unifying mechanism behind emergence and asks: can we predict when and how fast sparse attention patterns form, and how do sequence length, input dimensionality, and repetition modulate this process?

## Input / Output

The linear regression task takes a sequence of T i.i.d. d-dimensional tokens (each ~ N(0, 1/d)) and requires predicting y* = W*x_T, with data distribution controlled by sequence length T, dimension d, in-context burstiness B (number of times x_T repeats in context), and cross-sample repetition probability p. The associative recall task takes N_pairs key-value token pairs followed by a query token from a vocabulary of N_tokens one-hot encoded tokens, and requires outputting the matching value. The primary scientific output is the plateau length (time to escape the initial loss plateau) as a function of these data parameters, characterized by power-law exponents.

## Method

The paper introduces a minimal attention model (y = W Σ softmax(a)_t x_t) whose gradient flow dynamics reduce to two coupled scalar ODEs (w: projection of W on W*, and Δa: attention score difference) under assumptions of uniform attention initialization and W initialized at zero. Linearizing around initialization yields a closed-form escape time T_ε = (√d T / 2) ln(ε√dT), revealing that feedforward weights must align with the target before attention can learn, creating a positive feedback loop that produces the sharp phase transition. In-context repetition (B) effectively reduces sequence length to T/B, while cross-sample repetition (p) makes the input covariance anisotropic, accelerating learning in the repeated direction and thereby pulling attention toward relevant tokens earlier. These theoretical predictions are validated on full 2-layer 4-head Transformers trained with Adam, and extended to the in-context associative recall task where the induction head circuit emerges, confirming power-law scaling with exponents T_plateau ∝ N_tokens^0.79 · N_pairs^2.25.

## Results

The paper derives that the initial plateau duration (emergence time) in the toy model scales as T_ε = (√d·T/2)·ln(ε√d·T), which is confirmed empirically with R²=0.999. In-context repetition (burstiness B) reduces the plateau as T_plateau ∝ T^0.99·B^(−0.99)·d^0.49 (R²=0.999), while cross-sample repetition (probability p) yields T_plateau ∝ (√d·T/√(p²d+(1−p)²))^1.02 (R²=0.992). On the in-context associative recall task, emergence time follows T_plateau = 0.55·N_tokens^0.79·N_pairs^2.25 (R²=0.982), and repetition accelerates emergence by a factor of 4 (in-context) and 2 (cross-sample) on test data without repetition.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing methods or baselines in the traditional sense; it is a theoretical analysis validated by empirical experiments. The primary 'comparison' is between the theoretically derived scaling laws (from linearizing gradient-flow ODEs around initialization) and the empirically measured plateau lengths in both the toy model and full 2-layer 4-head Transformers trained with Adam. The theory matches empirical results with R² ≥ 0.98 across all settings. A secondary comparison is between different data distribution configurations: in-context repetition (burstiness) provides a 4× speedup in emergence on the associative recall task, while cross-sample repetition provides a 2× speedup, with the former being more effective due to its stronger interaction with sequence length. The paper also notes that replacing Adam with SGD substantially slows emergence and increases the dependency on task difficulty, highlighting the optimizer as a critical factor not captured by the gradient-flow theory.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the single-location regression task of Marion et al. [27] by embedding it in an attention-based model and analyzing the gradient flow dynamics to characterize when sparse attention emerges. The theoretical toolkit draws directly on the ODE linearization and positive-feedback-loop analysis of deep linear networks (Saxe et al. [29]), while the empirical validation leverages the well-studied induction head circuit (Olsson et al. [12]) and the data-distribution framework for in-context learning emergence (Chan et al. [13]). Unlike prior emergence studies that are largely empirical or focus on loss-based progress metrics, this work provides a mechanistic, closed-form prediction of emergence timing as a power law of sequence length, dimensionality, and repetition parameters.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a unifying theoretical lens—sparse attention learning—as a potential common mechanism behind diverse emergence phenomena (induction heads, factual recall, in-context learning), and quantitatively predicts emergence timing with high accuracy (R² ≈ 0.999). The finding that both in-context and cross-sample repetition accelerate emergence, while simultaneously introducing overfitting risks, has practical implications for training data curation and curriculum design. Key open questions remain: how much of large-scale emergence is attributable to sparse attention versus other circuit-level mechanisms, whether training-based and size-based emergence are fundamentally distinct, and whether the proposed active-learning strategy (dynamically adjusting data diversity) scales to realistic pretraining settings.
