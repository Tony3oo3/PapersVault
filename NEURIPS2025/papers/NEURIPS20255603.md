## Problem

Geometry optimization of periodic crystal structures is a critical bottleneck in crystal structure prediction workflows, where thousands of local optimizations must be performed. Classical methods (BFGS, FIRE, conjugate gradient) require many steps or multiple energy evaluations per step, and no prior work has applied multi-agent reinforcement learning to this periodic setting. MACS addresses this gap by formulating crystal structure optimization as a multi-agent coordination problem and learning a policy that optimizes structures significantly faster with fewer energy calculations.

## Input / Output

The method takes an initial pseudo-random crystal structure (atoms placed in a fixed unit cell, generated by AIRSS with minimum interatomic distance of 1 Å) and uses CHGNet as the energy/force oracle. At each step, each atom-agent receives a 204-dimensional local observation built from its own and 12 nearest neighbors' features (covalent radius, gradient vector, displacement history, relative positions), and outputs a 3D displacement vector scaled by the local gradient norm (capped at 0.4 Å). The output is a locally optimized structure where all atomic forces fall below 0.05 eV/Å, achieved within a maximum of 1000 steps; the unit cell parameters remain fixed throughout optimization.

## Method

MACS models geometry optimization as a partially observable Markov game where each atom is an independent agent acting simultaneously to minimize local forces. The key architectural choices are: (1) local observations over k=12 nearest neighbors (including periodic images) with force-related features (gradient vector, log-gradient norm) and history features (previous displacement, gradient change); (2) gradient-norm-scaled actions where the displacement is c_i^t · u with c_i^t = min(|g_i^t|, 0.4), guiding step magnitude adaptively; and (3) a log-gradient reward R = log|g^t| − log|g^{t+1}| that avoids skewing toward early large-force reductions. Training uses independent Soft Actor-Critic with shared policy and twin Q-networks across all agents, a 10M replay buffer, and discount factor γ=0.995. The policy is trained on six compositions (~40 atoms each) and demonstrates zero-shot transfer to unseen compositions and structures up to 2× the training size, outperforming all six classical baselines in wall-clock time and energy calculation count while maintaining the lowest failure rate (0.36%).

## Results

Across all 24 test sets (9 compositions, 3 sizes each), MACS achieves a mean optimization time of 99 s and 182 energy calculations, which are 34% and 28% lower than the best baseline (BFGSLS: 152 s, 253 energy calculations). MACS attains the lowest failure rate (P_F = 0.36%), tied with BFGSLS, while BFGSLS requires only 5% fewer optimization steps (171 vs. 182). On the three unseen compositions (Sr₂TiO₄, Sr₃Ti₂O₇, Sr₄Ti₃O₁₀), MACS outperforms all baselines in both T_mean and C_mean, confirming zero-shot transferability to new chemistries and larger structures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MACS outperforms all six baselines on mean optimization time (T_mean) and mean energy calculations (C_mean) in nearly every test set, with the sole exception of Ca₃Al₂Si₃O₁₂ where BFGS is marginally faster. Against the strongest baseline BFGSLS, MACS is 34% faster (99 s vs. 152 s) and uses 28% fewer energy calculations (182 vs. 253), though BFGSLS requires 5% fewer optimization steps (171 vs. 182) because MACS performs exactly one energy evaluation per step while BFGSLS performs multiple. MACS ties BFGSLS on failure rate (0.36%) but dramatically outperforms MDMin (46.19%), CG (18.22%), and FIRE (9.64%) on reliability. On the three unseen compositions used for zero-shot transfer evaluation, MACS maintains its lead over all baselines in both T_mean and C_mean, with no degradation in failure rate, demonstrating that the learned policy generalizes to new elemental combinations and larger unit cells without retraining.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the learning-to-optimize (L2O) paradigm to periodic crystal structure optimization by formulating it as a partially observable Markov game (POMG) solved with independent Soft Actor-Critic (SAC). Prior MARL work on geometry optimization, such as MolOpt [36], targeted finite non-periodic molecules, while graph-based L2O approaches [2, 34] addressed atomic clusters; MACS is the first to apply MARL to the periodic setting where atoms interact across unit cell boundaries. The method inherits its RL backbone from SAC [21] and its energy/force evaluations from the CHGNet machine-learning interatomic potential [12], positioning it at the intersection of multi-agent RL and computational materials science.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Geometry optimization is a critical bottleneck in crystal structure prediction workflows that require thousands of local relaxations; MACS's 34% speedup and 28% reduction in energy calculations over the best classical baseline (BFGSLS), combined with a near-zero failure rate (0.36% vs. 46% for MDMin), could substantially accelerate CSP campaigns. The demonstrated zero-shot transferability to unseen compositions and larger structures suggests a single trained policy could serve as a general-purpose optimizer, reducing the need for method selection per material class. Open questions include whether the approach extends to unit cell optimization, whether richer atomic descriptors (beyond covalent radius) improve performance on complex multi-element systems like Ca3Al2Si3O12 where MACS was outperformed by BFGS, and whether the train-with-CHGNet-run-with-DFT workflow preserves the learned policy's effectiveness.
