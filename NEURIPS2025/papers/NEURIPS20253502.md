## Problem

Recent reports claim frontier LLMs outperform elite humans in competitive programming, but existing benchmarks (LiveCodeBench, CodeELO, SWE-Bench) suffer from data contamination, weak test suites, unbalanced difficulty, and reliance on noisy crowd-sourced tags, making it impossible to disentangle genuine algorithmic reasoning from memorization or tool augmentation. The paper asks whether LLMs truly reason at grandmaster level or merely excel at implementation, and where the remaining gap to human experts lies.

## Input / Output

Each problem is a competitive programming statement (from Codeforces, ICPC, or IOI) captured in real time during live contests before any solutions appear online, with full immutable hidden test sets. Models produce a single C++ solution per problem (pass@1), which is graded against the contest's official test suite. Beyond the binary accept/reject verdict, the benchmark outputs a Bayesian MAP Elo-equivalent rating per model and per algorithmic tag, expert-assigned cognitive-focus labels (knowledge-, logic-, or observation-heavy), and for a subset of 125 failed o3-mini submissions, a line-by-line root-cause diagnosis (idea-level vs. implementation-level error, 'Fails Sample' flag, verdict type).

## Method

LiveCodeBench Pro assembles 584 problems from Codeforces, ICPC, and IOI via a real-time collection pipeline that captures each problem at the moment of contest release, eliminating data-leakage pathways. A team of Olympiad medalists annotates every problem with refined algorithmic tags (correcting noisy Codeforces crowd-sourced tags) and a three-way cognitive-focus taxonomy (knowledge-heavy, logic-heavy, observation-heavy) through triple-blind adjudication. Model skill is quantified via Bayesian maximum-a-posteriori Elo estimation that treats each model as a virtual player against human contestants, yielding difficulty-aware, human-comparable ratings rather than raw pass rates. The paper then conducts a line-by-line comparison of 125 failed o3-mini submissions against 125 human submissions of similar rating to diagnose failure modes, directly compares reasoning models (DeepSeek R1, Claude 3.7 Sonnet Max Reasoning) against their non-reasoning counterparts to isolate the effect of chain-of-thought, and quantifies the contribution of pass@k and terminal/tool access to the gap between the paper's 2116 Elo and OpenAI's reported 2719.

## Results

On LiveCodeBench Pro (584 problems from Codeforces, ICPC, and IOI), the best model o4-mini-high achieves 53.5% pass@1 on Medium-difficulty problems, 83.1% on Easy, and 0% on Hard, corresponding to a Bayesian Elo rating of 2116 (1.5th percentile among human Codeforces contestants). No evaluated model solves any Hard-tier problem. Reasoning models substantially outperform their non-reasoning counterparts: DeepSeek R1 (Elo 1442) beats DeepSeek V3 (Elo 557) by ~885 points, and Claude 3.7 Sonnet with Max Reasoning (Elo 992) beats its non-reasoning variant (Elo 804) by ~188 points. The largest reasoning gains appear in combinatorics (~1400 Elo for DeepSeek) and knowledge-heavy categories (~700 Elo on segment trees), while observation-heavy categories (game theory, greedy, ad-hoc) see minimal or even negative improvement from reasoning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a benchmark paper, LiveCodeBench Pro evaluates 20 frontier LLMs rather than proposing a new method. The top performer, o4-mini-high (Elo 2116), leads Gemini 2.5 Pro (1992) by ~124 Elo points and o3-mini (1777) by ~339 points on Medium-difficulty problems, though all three achieve 0% on Hard. Reasoning models consistently dominate their non-reasoning counterparts: DeepSeek R1 (1442) outperforms DeepSeek V3 (557) by 885 Elo points, and Claude 3.7 Sonnet with Max Reasoning (992) beats its non-reasoning variant (804) by 188 points. However, the paper's key finding is that even the best model's 2116 Elo falls far short of the 2719 reported by OpenAI with terminal access and pass@k, with the gap attributed to tool augmentation (~400 points) and multiple attempts (~540 points). On observation-heavy problem categories (game theory, greedy, ad-hoc, constructive), all models collapse below Elo 1500 regardless of reasoning capability, revealing a persistent weakness that no current model overcomes. The paper also shows that pass@10 improves o4-mini-medium from Elo 1793 to 2334, but the converged rating still trails the tool-augmented 2719, confirming that implementation precision and tool access—not superior reasoning—drive the highest reported scores.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the competitive programming benchmark lineage from LiveCodeBench and CodeELO by addressing their key limitations: static problem archives vulnerable to contamination, noisy crowd-sourced tags, and reliance on raw pass rates that ignore problem difficulty. It introduces real-time problem collection from Codeforces, ICPC, and IOI contests, a cognitive-focus taxonomy (knowledge/logic/observation-heavy) annotated by Olympiad medalists, and Bayesian MAP Elo estimation borrowed from prior work [55] to produce difficulty-aware, human-comparable ratings. The line-by-line failure diagnosis by expert annotators and the controlled reasoning-vs-non-reasoning comparison represent a methodological departure from prior benchmarks that report only aggregate scores.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark provides the first fine-grained, contamination-controlled diagnostic of where frontier LLMs genuinely fail in algorithmic reasoning, revealing that 0% pass@1 on hard problems persists across all 20+ models tested and that observation-heavy problems (game theory, greedy, ad-hoc) remain a blind spot even for reasoning models. The finding that chain-of-thought reasoning yields minimal gains on observation-heavy categories raises an open question about whether current test-time scaling methods are fundamentally limited for insight-driven problems or whether an emergent threshold exists. Limitations include failure analysis restricted to o3-mini, reliance on third-party judges rather than in-house test generation, and pass@1 as the primary metric, all of which the authors flag for future work.
