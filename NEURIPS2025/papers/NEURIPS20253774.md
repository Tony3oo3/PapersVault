## Problem

MRI reconstruction models degrade under distribution shifts from different scanners, protocols, or patient populations, and existing test-time adaptation (TTA) methods cannot simultaneously achieve strong adaptation, robustness to limited data, and fast convergence. Batch-based self-supervised TTA risks over-smoothing by learning mean/median representations across slices, while single-slice TTA requires repeated per-slice optimisation that is computationally prohibitive.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253774/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes undersampled multi-coil MRI k-space measurements (4× acceleration via 1D Cartesian masks with 8% auto-calibration lines, sensitivity maps estimated by ESPiRiT) together with a pre-trained reconstruction network (U-Net or VarNet). It operates in two passes: first over all slices of a single patient (batch size 2, 25 epochs) to learn a patient-level prior, then per-slice (up to 1000 steps with early stopping on 5% held-out k-space) to refine individual reconstructions. The output is a full-resolution complex-valued MRI image per slice, evaluated via SSIM, PSNR, and LPIPS against fully-sampled references.

## Method

D2SA is a dual-stage TTA framework built on implicit neural representations. In Stage 1, a SIREN-based MR-INR with a 128-dim Gaussian-initialised latent code and random Fourier positional encoding maps each slice's geometric coordinates to a joint output [x̂, α, β], where α (variance) and β (mean) are spatially-varying affine parameters that modulate the pre-trained network's penultimate feature maps, enabling patient-specific distribution shift correction with only ~1408 extra parameters. Stage 2 freezes the CNN convolutional layers and the learned latent code, then trains a learnable Anisotropic Diffusion module that replaces standard convolutions with five merged difference-convolution kernels (vanilla, central, angular, horizontal, vertical) to compute gradient-aware diffusion coefficients, preserving edges while suppressing noise. A linear-affine theoretical analysis (Proposition 1 / Theorem 1) shows that under a subspace-plus-noise test distribution, the optimal self-supervised estimator is exactly an affine map with α* = 1/(1+s²) and β* = μ_Q, providing a principled justification for the learned modulation. The framework is plug-and-play across SSL losses (FINE, NR2N, SSDU) and backbones (U-Net, VarNet), reducing adaptation time by 3–7× over single-slice baselines while matching or exceeding their reconstruction quality.

## Results

Across five domain shifts (anatomy, dataset, modality, acceleration, sampling), D2SA (MR-INR+SST) consistently achieves the best or near-best SSIM and PSNR among all TTA methods for both U-Net and VarNet backbones. For example, on the acceleration shift with VarNet, FINE+MR-INR+SST reaches 28.64 dB PSNR and 0.829 SSIM, surpassing DIP-TTT (28.25 dB / 0.815) while reducing adaptation time from 137.2 to 44.5 min/patient (3.1× speedup). On the anatomy shift, NR2N+MR-INR+SST achieves 27.81 dB PSNR in 18.7 min versus DIP-TTT's 27.67 dB in 52.5 min. The method also substantially outperforms single-stage self-supervised baselines: FINE alone scores only 21.39 dB on the acceleration shift, a 7.25 dB gap closed by the dual-stage pipeline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DIP-TTT (single-slice Deep Image Prior with early stopping), D2SA matches or slightly exceeds PSNR/SSIM on most shifts (e.g., 27.81 vs 27.67 dB on anatomy; 28.89 vs 28.25 dB on acceleration) while cutting adaptation time by 3–7× (18.7 vs 52.5 min; 43.1 vs 137.2 min). Compared to the single-stage self-supervised baselines FINE, NR2N, and SSDU, the dual-stage MR-INR+SST pipeline yields large gains of 3–7 dB PSNR, most pronounced on the acceleration and sampling shifts where domain mismatch is greatest. Adding MR-INR alone (Stage 1) already recovers most of the gap to DIP-TTT (e.g., FINE 21.39 → FINE+MR-INR 25.30 dB on acceleration), confirming that patient-level distribution modelling is the dominant contributor. Against ZS-SSL (zero-shot self-supervised single-slice training), D2SA achieves higher SSIM/PSNR on four of five shifts and is roughly 5× faster (e.g., 17–25 min vs 100–167 min/patient). The only setting where a baseline is competitive is the sampling shift with U-Net, where DIP-TTT reaches 28.98 dB PSNR versus 29.05 dB for FINE+MR-INR+SST, a negligible 0.07 dB difference at 2× the runtime.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the implicit neural representation (INR) paradigm from DeepSDF and Functa, repurposing latent-code-conditioned SIREN networks to model patient-level distribution shifts in MRI rather than 3D shapes. It extends the single-slice test-time training strategy of DIP-TTT by adding a learnable anisotropic diffusion module (inspired by DEA-Net's difference-based convolutions) that preserves structural detail while freezing most convolutional layers. The key departure from prior TTA work (FINE, NR2N, SSDU) is the explicit two-stage decomposition: a patient-wise INR stage that learns mean/variance shifts via affine feature modulation, followed by a slice-wise refinement stage, rather than treating all slices uniformly in a single batch-based self-supervised loop.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dual-stage design offers a practical path toward clinically deployable TTA for MRI by reducing per-patient adaptation time by roughly 3–4× compared to single-slice methods like DIP-TTT while matching or exceeding reconstruction quality across five domain shifts. The theoretical analysis (Proposition 1 / Theorem 1) provides a clean linear interpretation of why affine mean/variance adaptation is optimal under distribution shift, lending principled grounding to the design. Key limitations include evaluation only on 2D slices from public datasets (no real clinical undersampled data), no 3D spatial modeling, and dependence on a pre-trained source-domain network. Open questions include extending the framework to 3D volumetric adaptation, integrating stronger generative priors (e.g., diffusion models), and developing online/incremental strategies for continuous patient streams.
