## Problem

Supervised instruction fine-tuning (SFT) with chat templates consistently degrades a pretrained LLM's context awareness — its ability to retrieve and interpret information from user-provided context — even at short context lengths. This loss is not a catastrophic forgetting of retrieval capability but rather a role-based attention bias introduced by chat templates, where the model learns to attend less to user tokens and more to its own assistant tokens. The paper identifies this bias as originating from the sparsity of context-dependent examples in standard instruction datasets.

## Input / Output

During training, the method consumes standard instruction-response conversation pairs from datasets like ShareGPT, UltraChat-200K, or WizardLM-70K, with a special [IND] token appended to user instructions identified as context-dependent (via an attention-based score threshold β=0.6). At inference, the user optionally prepends [IND] to their query to signal that the response should rely more heavily on the provided context. The model produces a standard text response; evaluation uses containment scoring for QA tasks (SQuAD, QuAC, DROP) and keyword recall for Needle-in-a-Haystack, with generation truncated at 100 tokens for QA and 50 tokens for NIH.

## Method

The paper first demonstrates via Needle-in-a-Haystack evaluations that instruction-fine-tuned models show degraded context retrieval specifically when chat templates are applied, and traces this to a measurable shift in attention allocation: user tokens receive less attention while assistant tokens receive more. To quantify which training examples drive this bias, the authors define a context-dependency score s_M(Y_m) that averages, over response tokens, the maximum attention head's total attention to all prior user instruction tokens, computed on a single middle layer of a seed model. Examples exceeding a threshold β are marked with a new [IND] special token in the user instruction. The model is then fine-tuned on this modified dataset, learning to conditionally increase attention to user tokens when [IND] is present. This conditional SFT approach selectively boosts the critical retrieval head's attention to user context without the indiscriminate side effects of post-hoc attention steering, which the paper shows can impair complex reasoning tasks like DROP.

## Results

The proposed context-dependency indicator method consistently improves context-awareness metrics over vanilla instruction fine-tuning across all three pretrained models (TinyLlama-1.1B, Llama-2-7B, Llama-3-8B) and three SFT datasets. The most dramatic gain is on NIH recall for Llama-2-7B fine-tuned on ShareGPT, where recall jumps from 0.3378 (vanilla) to 0.7007 (+Indicator), and on WizardLM-70K from 0.7375 to 0.9254. SQuAD and QuAC containment scores also improve by 1–3 points across settings (e.g., SQuAD 76.78→79.09, QuAC 23.60→24.20 for Llama-2-7B/ShareGPT), while DROP and MT-Bench scores remain comparable, confirming that general instruction-following is preserved.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between vanilla SFT and the proposed +Indicator SFT on the same pretrained models and datasets. The +Indicator method wins on all three context-dependent benchmarks (NIH, SQuAD, QuAC) in nearly every model/dataset combination, with the largest NIH gains on Llama-2-7B (e.g., +0.36 recall on ShareGPT, +0.19 on WizardLM-70K). On complex-skill tasks (DROP, MT-Bench), the two methods are roughly comparable, with +Indicator sometimes slightly ahead (e.g., DROP 34.44 vs. 34.07 on Llama-2-7B/WizardLM-70K) and sometimes slightly behind (e.g., MT-Bench 5.74 vs. 6.49 on Llama-2-7B/ShareGPT). Against the official Llama-2-7B-Chat reference, the +Indicator Llama-2-7B/WizardLM-70K model achieves higher QuAC (25.30 vs. 22.20) but lower NIH (0.9254 vs. 0.8264*—note different evaluation settings), lower DROP (34.44 vs. 44.22), and lower MT-Bench (6.2250 vs. 6.9375). Against Llama-3-8B-Instruct, the +Indicator Llama-3-8B/WizardLM-70K model ties on NIH (1.0), trails slightly on SQuAD (86.28 vs. 86.96) and QuAC (26.40 vs. 27.40), but leads on DROP (50.22 vs. 46.54), while MT-Bench is lower (6.85 vs. 8.075), reflecting the gap between open- and closed-source training data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on side effects of instruction fine-tuning (catastrophic forgetting, alignment tax) by identifying a specific and previously uncharacterized failure mode: role-based attention bias introduced by chat templates that degrades context retrieval. It builds conceptually on attention steering (Zhang et al., ICLR 2024) but departs from it by replacing inference-time intervention with a training-time conditional SFT strategy using a context-dependency indicator token. The work also distinguishes itself from positional bias studies (Liu et al., 2024; Hsieh et al., 2024) by showing that the relevant bias is not positional but role-based, tied to the user/assistant markers in chat templates rather than token position in the sequence.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Context awareness underpins retrieval-augmented generation, in-context learning, and contextual QA—among the most important real-world LLM applications—so understanding why standard instruction fine-tuning silently degrades it has broad practical implications. The finding that the degradation is tied to chat templates (ubiquitous in deployed systems) rather than to weight changes per se suggests that the issue is addressable without retraining from scratch. Key limitations include validation only on models up to 8B parameters, the need for an explicit indicator token at inference time, and the risk that the context-dependent subset encodes unintended stylistic biases. Open questions include whether the effect persists at larger scales, whether the context-dependency signal can be disentangled more cleanly, and how the method performs on RAG-style evaluations with longer, more diverse contexts.
