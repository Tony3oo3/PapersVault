## Problem

Learning fair specialized representations for classification typically relies on adversarial training (unstable, with fairness guarantees tied to the specific adversary) or normalizing flows (computationally expensive, requiring the sensitive attribute at inference and separate models per group). No existing approach offers a simple, non-adversarial mechanism that provides strong, provable fairness guarantees while maintaining high predictive accuracy and not requiring sensitive attributes during deployment.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250610/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a tabular feature vector X ∈ R^d, a binary target label Y, and a sensitive attribute S (binary or multi-class) during training. It produces a latent representation Z (of configurable dimension, often 1–3) and a class prediction Ŷ via a logistic regression head. Critically, the sensitive attribute S is used only to compute the fairness penalty during training; at inference, the encoder and classifier operate solely on X without access to S, satisfying privacy constraints that methods like Fair Normalizing Flows violate.

## Method

The paper proposes two complementary penalties. FmCF (Fairness matching Characteristic Function) matches each group-conditional distribution P_{Z|S=s} to a standard Gaussian target using the Characteristic Function Distance, estimated via Monte Carlo sampling of evaluation points t from a weighting kernel; this avoids adversarial min-max games and normalizing flow architectures, allowing any encoder (e.g., a simple MLP) to be used. FmSS (Fairness matching Sufficient Statistics) exploits the Maclaurin expansion of the characteristic function to truncate the matching to the first two moments (mean and variance), replacing the Monte Carlo CFD with a closed-form KL divergence between N(0,1) and N(μ_s, σ_s²). The key theoretical insight (Theorems 5.2–5.3) is that when E[Z|S=s] ≈ E[Z] for all s, the optimal logistic regression coefficient for predicting S from Z vanishes, yielding a provable ε-fairness bound against the entire family of LR classifiers. The total training loss is the standard classification cross-entropy plus a weighted fairness penalty (α·L_CF or α·L_KL), optimized end-to-end with Adam.

## Results

On the Adult dataset (the primary fairness benchmark), FmCF achieves 85.01% task accuracy with 54.92% adversarial accuracy under LR classifiers (g∈G) and 56.64% under deep MLPs (g∉G), while FmSS achieves 85.02% accuracy with 51.70% and 57.47% adversarial accuracy respectively. On the German Credit dataset, FmCF reaches 74.60% accuracy with 69.00% adversarial accuracy (at random-guess level), outperforming all baselines on both metrics. Across all four FNF benchmark datasets (Adult, Compas, Crime, Health), the proposed methods consistently match or exceed FNF in accuracy while achieving lower adversarial accuracy, without requiring the sensitive attribute at inference time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against adversarial methods (AdvForgetting, MaxEnt-ARL, LAFTR), FmCF/FmSS achieve substantially lower adversarial accuracy (54.92–57.47% vs. 66.68–85.18%), indicating far less sensitive information leakage, while maintaining within ~1% task accuracy. Compared to FNF [2] (the prior state-of-the-art), FmCF achieves lower adversarial accuracy (56.64% vs. 59.56% on Adult, g∉G) and higher task accuracy (85.01% vs. 84.43%), with the critical advantage of not requiring the sensitive attribute at inference. On the German Credit dataset, FmCF dominates all baselines (VFAE, CIAFL, IRwAL) on both accuracy (74.60% vs. 69.50–72.00%) and fairness (69.00% adversarial accuracy, at random level, vs. 69.80–81.10%). In the broader Adult comparisons (Tables 3a/3b), FmCF/FmSS outperform VFAE, CIAFL, IRwAL, CSN, FRTrain, and WassDB on accuracy (85.0% vs. 72.7–84.2%) while achieving the lowest adversarial accuracy (67.8–67.9% vs. 77.6–88.8%), confirming consistent superiority across evaluation protocols.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the characteristic function distance (CFD) formulation for distribution matching in ML (Ansari et al., CVPR 2020) and the ε-fairness / statistical-distance framework of Madras et al. (ICML 2018), repurposing both for fair representation learning. It departs from the dominant adversarial-learning paradigm (LAFTR, AdvForgetting, MaxEnt-ARL) by replacing the unstable min-max game with a direct, differentiable CFD penalty that matches each group-conditional distribution P_{Z|S} to a common Gaussian target. It also contrasts with Fair Normalizing Flows (Balunović et al., ICLR 2022), the current SOTA, by eliminating the need for sensitive attributes at inference, avoiding per-group flow training, and relaxing the full distributional match to a moment-based KL penalty (FmSS) that yields provable fairness guarantees against logistic-regression classifiers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a practically attractive middle ground: stronger fairness guarantees than adversarial methods (certifiable bounds via LR convexity) without the architectural and inference-time constraints of normalizing flows, all with a simple MLP encoder and no sensitive-attribute access at test time. Key open questions include whether CFD remains effective in high-dimensional settings (the authors acknowledge it degrades), whether the moment-matching relaxation extends beyond classification to regression, and whether the current low-dimensional tabular benchmarks adequately reflect real-world complexity. The finding that 1–3 dimensional latent spaces suffice for competitive performance is intriguing but unexplored in terms of why this works and whether it generalizes.
