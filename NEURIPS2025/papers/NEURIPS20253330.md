## Problem

No watermarking scheme exists for bitwise autoregressive image generative models such as Infinity and Instella, which operate over a practically infinite token space via binary bit prediction. Existing diffusion-model watermarks lack radioactivity (they do not persist when used to train downstream models), and token-level watermarks adapted from LLMs are suboptimal for image models because re-encoding introduces large token discrepancies that degrade the watermark signal. This gap leaves model owners unable to reliably detect and filter their own generated content from future training data, leaving them vulnerable to model collapse.

## Input / Output

For generation, the method takes a text prompt and produces a 1024×1024 RGB image with the watermark embedded during the autoregressive bit-prediction process, adding at most ~11% overhead to generation time. For detection, the model owner re-encodes a suspect image using their private encoder and quantizer to recover the bit representation, then counts occurrences of green-list bit sequences (length n=2) across all scales to compute a z-score via a one-sided z-test; verification takes under 0.5 seconds per image. The watermark is private: only the model owner with access to the encoder, quantizer, and green/red lists can verify it.

## Method

BitMark embeds a watermark by softly biasing the logit of the next predicted bit during autoregressive generation: all 2^n bit sequences are partitioned into a fixed green list G={01,10} and red list R={00,11}, and whenever the current (n−1)-bit prefix plus the candidate bit would complete a green-list sequence, a small constant δ is added to that bit's logit before sampling. This design operates at the bit level rather than the token level because re-encoding discrepancies are dramatically smaller for bits (~77% overlap) than for tokens (~2.4% overlap) in Infinity, making the watermark signal far more stable across the encode-decode-encode cycle. The bias preferentially flips high-entropy bits where the probability difference between 0 and 1 is small, preserving image quality (FID change < 2 for δ=2) while shifting the green fraction from the natural 50% to ~57%. Detection re-encodes the image, counts green-list sequences C, and rejects the null hypothesis of no watermark when z = (C − γT)/√(Tγ(1−γ)) exceeds a threshold, achieving 100% TPR@1%FPR on clean images and 89–91.6% under strong reconstruction attacks. The watermark is radioactive: models fine-tuned on BitMark-watermarked images reproduce detectable bit patterns even cross-modality (e.g., Infinity→Stable Diffusion 2.1 at 98.9% TPR@1%FPR), with statistical significance (p < 10⁻⁶¹) even when only 5% of training data is watermarked.

## Results

BitMark (Infinity-2B, δ=2) achieves 100% TPR@1%FPR on clean images and maintains ≥98.8% across most conventional attacks (noise, blur, color, crop, JPEG), with the notable exception of rotation (20.1% TPR@1%FPR without correction; 98.3% after rotation correction). Against the CtrlRegen+ reconstruction attack, BitMark achieves 91.6% TPR@1%FPR, far exceeding all baselines. Image quality is preserved: FID actually improves from 33.36 (no watermark) to 31.05 (δ=2), and generation overhead is only ~11% (2.58s vs 2.32s per image). BitMark also exhibits strong radioactivity: 100% TPR@1%FPR when fine-tuning Infinity-2B on watermarked outputs, and 98.9% when transferring to Stable Diffusion 2.1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BitMark decisively outperforms all three postprocessing baselines on the critical reconstruction attack: CtrlRegen+ yields 91.6% TPR@1%FPR for BitMark versus only 1.6% (RivaGAN), 44.2% (StegaStamp), and 1.1% (TrustMark). Each baseline has a fatal weakness on at least one conventional attack — RivaGAN drops to 0% on horizontal/vertical flipping, StegaStamp collapses to 1% on cropping, and TrustMark falls to 2.2% on color jitter and 1.6% on cropping — whereas BitMark maintains ≥98.8% on all conventional attacks except rotation. On the SD2.1-VAE reconstruction attack, BitMark achieves 100% TPR@1%FPR, matching StegaStamp and TrustMark but far exceeding RivaGAN (98.5%). The key advantage of BitMark is its in-generation embedding at the bit level, which makes it inherently resistant to post-hoc removal strategies that exploit pixel-space or latent-space transformations. The only area where BitMark trails baselines is rotation (20.1% vs 96.7% for RivaGAN), though this is mitigated to 98.3% with a standard rotation-correction preprocessing step.

## Relation to Prior Work

BitMark adapts the green/red list logit-biasing mechanism from KGW [16] and Unigram-Watermark [46]—originally designed for LLM token-level watermarking—to the bit level of autoregressive image generation. It departs from diffusion model watermarks (Stable Signature, Tree-Ring, PRC, Recipe) that operate at the noise, latent, or decoding stage, and from post-processing watermarks (RivaGAN, StegaStamp, TrustMark) that are applied after generation. The key conceptual departure is the bit-level design, motivated by the empirical finding that bit overlap after re-encoding (77%) vastly exceeds token overlap (2.4%), making token-level watermarks inherently fragile for image autoregressive models. The radioactivity property draws on the LLM watermarking literature [30, 31] and the radioactive data concept [28], extending these ideas to the image domain where prior work [7] had shown diffusion watermarks fail to be radioactive.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

BitMark addresses a concrete and growing threat—model collapse from iterative training on self-generated content—for the emerging class of bitwise autoregressive image models (Infinity, Instella) that are achieving state-of-the-art quality. The demonstration that a bit-level watermark can be radioactive even across modalities (Infinity → Stable Diffusion 2.1) challenges the prior finding that image watermarks cannot persist through retraining, and suggests that subtle, distributed perturbations at the representation level are the right design principle. Limitations include rotation sensitivity (degrading to 56.8% TPR at ±50°), the need for δ tuning to balance quality and strength, and the question of whether 1% FPR remains acceptable at trillion-sample scale. Open questions include whether the approach transfers to other bitwise model architectures and whether the radioactivity signal remains detectable after multiple training generations.
