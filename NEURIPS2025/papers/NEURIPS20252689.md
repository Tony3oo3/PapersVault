## Problem

Existing image restoration methods are typically tailored to specific degradation scenarios and struggle to generalize across the three major settings—single-degradation, all-in-one, and composite degradation—while also remaining computationally expensive for practical deployment. The paper addresses this by seeking a single efficient architecture that achieves state-of-the-art performance across all three settings without relying on task-specific degradation priors.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252689/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model accepts a single degraded RGB image (3-channel) of arbitrary resolution, which may suffer from one or multiple simultaneous degradations (haze, rain, snow, blur, low-light, noise). It outputs a restored image via an image-level residual connection: the network predicts a residual that is added to the degraded input. For all-in-one and composite degradation settings, no explicit degradation label or type indicator is provided at inference time; the model must handle unknown or mixed corruption purely from the input signal.

## Method

BioIR adopts a plain U-shaped encoder-decoder-refinement architecture whose core building block (BioBlock) replaces self-attention with a BioModule inspired by foveal-peripheral interactions in human vision. The BioModule splits features into two branches: the Peripheral-to-Foveal (P2F) module computes pixel-to-region affinity maps between each pixel and pooled contextual representations, then generates pixel-specific convolution kernels to inject long-range context into local regions; the Foveal-to-Peripheral (F2P) module propagates fine-grained spatial details via a static-to-dynamic two-stage strategy combining element-wise modulation with dynamic convolution. The two branch outputs are recalibrated through element-wise multiplication to enable high-order cross-branch interactions. Three model variants (Tiny/Base/Large) are scaled by adjusting channel widths and BioBlock counts, and the network is trained end-to-end with dual-domain L1 loss under supervised or multi-task learning depending on the setting.

## Results

On the CDD11 composite degradation benchmark (11 categories, up to 3 simultaneous degradations), BioIR-B achieves 30.54 dB average PSNR, surpassing the second-best MoCE-IR-S (29.05 dB) by 1.02 dB with only 7M parameters. Under the five-task all-in-one setting, BioIR-L reaches 30.99 dB average PSNR, outperforming MoCE-IR (30.58 dB) and AdaIR (30.20 dB) while using fewer parameters (16M vs. 25M and 29M). On single-degradation tasks, BioIR-T achieves 40.50 dB on SOTS-Indoor dehazing (vs. MaIR's 39.45 dB with 39% fewer parameters) and 26.22 dB on LOLv2-s low-light enhancement (vs. MambaLLIE's 25.87 dB). The tiny variant (1.32M params, 16.65G FLOPs) consistently matches or exceeds much larger models across all three restoration settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BioIR achieves state-of-the-art results across all three restoration settings without relying on explicit degradation priors. On CDD11 composite degradation, BioIR-B (30.54 dB avg) outperforms MoCE-IR-S (29.05 dB) by 1.02 dB and OneRestore (28.47 dB) by 2.07 dB, while the tiny BioIR-T (29.52 dB, 1M params) already surpasses most competitors including PromptIR (25.90 dB) and WGWSNet (26.96 dB). In the five-task all-in-one setting, BioIR-L (30.99 dB) beats MoCE-IR (30.58 dB) by 0.41 dB and AdaIR (30.20 dB) by 0.79 dB, with fewer parameters than both. On single-degradation tasks, BioIR-T achieves 40.50 dB on SOTS-Indoor dehazing, surpassing the Mamba-based MaIR (39.45 dB) by 1.05 dB while using only 39% of its parameters, and 35.62 dB on Haze4K, exceeding MBTF V2 (34.92 dB) by 0.70 dB. On LOLBlur composite degradation, BioIR-T (27.70 dB) outperforms the specialized DarkIR-L (27.30 dB) by 0.40 dB with only 10% of its parameters. The only category where a competitor leads is GoPro deblurring in the five-task setting, where MoCE-IR (30.05 dB) slightly edges BioIR-L (29.61 dB), attributed to MoCE-IR's specialized motion deblurring backbone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new architectural ground by drawing on the foveal-peripheral interaction model of human vision to design a two-branch module (P2F and F2P) that replaces self-attention or Mamba-based scanning in restoration networks. Conceptually, it sits at the convergence of three prior threads: (i) general-purpose U-shaped restoration architectures (Restormer, NAFNet, ConvIR), (ii) all-in-one and composite-degradation frameworks that handle multiple corruption types (PromptIR, AdaIR, OneRestore, MoCE-IR), and (iii) efficiency-oriented designs that use dynamic convolution or frequency-domain operations to avoid quadratic attention costs. The key departure is that BioIR achieves universality across single-degradation, all-in-one, and composite settings without any explicit degradation-aware priors, relying instead on the large-field contextual signals from the peripheral branch as implicit guidance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: a ~1M-parameter model (BioIR-T) matches or exceeds methods with 6–83M parameters on the CDD11 composite-degradation benchmark, and the base variant outperforms the prior best by over 1 dB while using fewer parameters, making real-time deployment on resource-constrained devices more feasible. The finding that no explicit degradation priors are needed for competitive all-in-one performance challenges the prevailing assumption in the all-in-one literature that task-specific conditioning is essential. Open questions include whether the bio-inspired two-branch design generalizes to even more degradation categories or to video restoration, and whether the conceptual framing (foveal vs. peripheral) offers actionable design principles beyond the specific dynamic-convolution mechanisms used here.
