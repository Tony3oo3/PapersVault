## Problem

Existing whole-body humanoid control methods treat upper and lower body as a single policy, neglecting their distinct roles: the lower body must provide robust locomotion while the upper body executes precise motion imitation. This conflation leads to computationally expensive training, poor convergence, and frequent robot falls in real-world deployment because motion tracking is prioritized over balance maintenance.

## Input / Output

The lower-body policy consumes proprioception (21 joint positions/velocities, base angular velocity, projected gravity, previous action), a 3D velocity command (vx, vy, yaw), and a 2D phase parameter, producing 12 DoF joint position targets fed to a PD controller. The upper-body policy takes the same proprioception plus a 9D reference joint vector from retargeted AMASS motions, outputting 9 DoF targets (3 shoulder + 1 elbow per arm + waist yaw). The foundation model additionally ingests a natural-language description (e.g., 'moving backward slowly and wave the left hand') concatenated with historical state-action pairs, autoregressively predicting the next 21-DoF action at each step.

## Method

ALMI formulates upper-lower body coordination as a two-player zero-sum Markov game where each body is simultaneously agent and adversary: the lower body maximizes locomotion reward while the upper body minimizes it (and vice versa for motion tracking). A key practical simplification replaces the inner-loop parameter-space optimization with command-space adversarial sampling—adversarial motions are drawn from a difficulty-sorted AMASS list with an adaptive motion scale α_s and window index α_d (dual curriculum), and adversarial velocity commands are sampled from an expanding range. This yields a single paired policy (π^l, π^u) trained via three rounds of iterative PPO updates, with a theoretical guarantee of ε-approximate Nash equilibrium under two-timescale independent policy gradients. The converged policies generate the ALMI-X dataset (80K+ MuJoCo trajectories with language annotations), which is used to train a Transformer-decoder foundation model that autoregressively predicts next actions from text commands and interaction history, enabling language-guided whole-body control.

## Results

On the CMU MoCap dataset (1122 motion clips) evaluated in Isaac Gym, ALMI achieves the lowest linear velocity error (E_vel = 0.2202 m/s) and upper-body joint position error (E_jpe_upper = 0.2116 m) under the hardest difficulty setting (1.3 m/s linear, 0.6 rad/s angular, terrain level 6, with external pushes), while maintaining a 97.23% survival rate. Under easy settings, ALMI reaches a perfect 100% survival rate with E_vel = 0.1135 m/s and E_kpe_upper = 0.0460 m. On the Unitree G1 platform, ALMI achieves E_vel = 0.1396 m/s and 94.84% survival, substantially outperforming both Exbody2 (E_vel = 0.4015, survival 88.48%) and OmniH2O (E_vel = 0.1615, survival 38.82%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ALMI consistently outperforms Exbody [6] across all difficulty levels and all metrics: on the hardest setting, ALMI reduces linear velocity error by 54% (0.2202 vs. 0.4838 m/s), upper-body joint position error by 44% (0.2116 vs. 0.3758 m), and improves survival rate by 9.5 percentage points (97.23% vs. 87.78%). Against the single-policy ablation ALMI (whole), the adversarial split-policy design yields substantially better upper-body tracking (E_jpe_upper: 0.2116 vs. 0.7022 m) and lower-body action smoothness (E_action_lower: 0.0175 vs. 2.9317 rad), confirming that separate adversarial policies are critical for coordination. On the Unitree G1 platform, ALMI surpasses both imitation-based whole-body methods: it achieves 65% lower velocity error than Exbody2 (0.1396 vs. 0.4015 m/s) and 6.6× higher survival than OmniH2O (94.84% vs. 38.82%), demonstrating that adversarial training for robustness generalizes across robot platforms and outperforms pure motion-imitation approaches that lack explicit velocity-command tracking.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm of learning a single unified whole-body policy (as in ExBody, ExBody2, OmniH2O) by decomposing control into separate upper-body and lower-body policies trained adversarially as a two-player zero-sum Markov game. It builds on PHC for motion retargeting from AMASS and on the broader RL-based humanoid locomotion literature, but introduces the key conceptual shift of treating upper-body motion as an adversarial disturbance to locomotion (and vice versa), with a dual curriculum mechanism to manage difficulty. The foundation model component connects to the VLA line of work (RT-1, Octo, OpenVLA) but targets whole-body humanoid control rather than arm manipulation, and the ALMI-X dataset is the first large-scale whole-body control dataset with language annotations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The adversarial decomposition addresses a practical failure mode of unified whole-body policies—prioritizing tracking over balance and causing frequent falls in deployment—by providing a theoretically grounded (Nash equilibrium) mechanism for coordinated upper-lower body control. The ALMI-X dataset (80K+ trajectories with text commands) is a significant community resource for training foundation models for humanoid control. However, the foundation model remains exploratory and degrades when scaling to the full dataset's diversity, and the adversarial framework performs suboptimally on highly dynamic whole-body tasks like dancing, leaving open the question of whether this decomposition generalizes beyond loco-manipulation to expressive full-body performance.
