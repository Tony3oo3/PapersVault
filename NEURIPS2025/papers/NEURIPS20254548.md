## Problem

Standard ATE estimation requires observing both treatment and control groups with known treatment assignment, but many practical settings (recommendation systems, health records, marketing campaigns) only provide a confirmed treatment group and an unlabeled group whose treatment status is unknown. This paper tackles the gap of deriving semiparametric efficiency bounds and constructing estimators that attain them for ATE estimation under this positive-and-unlabeled (PU) data structure, where the treatment indicator is fundamentally unobservable for the unlabeled units.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254548/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

In the censoring setting, the method consumes a single dataset of (X, O, Y) triples where O=1 flags confirmed treated units and O=0 flags the unknown mixture; in the case-control setting, it takes two separate datasets: a treated group (X_T, Y(1)) of size m and an unknown group (X, Y_U) of size l. The output is a point estimate of the ATE τ₀ = E[Y(1)−Y(0)] together with asymptotic confidence intervals whose coverage is validated empirically. A key structural constraint is that the true treatment indicator D is never observed directly—only the product O = D·Õ is available—making the problem strictly more informative than standard missing-treatment settings where both D=1 and D=0 labels can appear among observed units.

## Method

The paper derives the efficient influence function for ATE under both the censoring and case-control DGPs, yielding a closed-form efficiency bound V^cens (or V^cc) that decomposes into outcome variance, unknown-group variance, and heterogeneity terms. The proposed estimator is a sample average of the efficient influence function with plug-in nuisance estimates (μ_T, ν, π, g), making it an augmented IPW / doubly robust estimator. Crucially, the double-robustness structure links the observation probability π₀ with the conditional outcome models (μ_T, ν), but does NOT extend to the censoring propensity score g₀, because the treatment indicator is unobservable; consequently, √n-consistency requires g₀ to be known or estimated from an independent auxiliary dataset at a faster-than-parametric rate. Cross-fitting is employed to control nuisance estimator complexity, and the paper proves that the resulting estimator achieves the semiparametric efficiency bound, distinguishing it from the IPW and direct-method alternatives which are shown to be strictly less efficient.

## Results

In the censoring setting (n=3000, τ₀=3), the proposed efficient estimator achieves MSE 0.01 and bias 0.00 with a 93% coverage ratio when the true propensity score is known, compared to MSE 0.06 (bias −0.06) for IPW and MSE 0.01 (bias 0.03) for the DM estimator. In the case-control setting (m=1000, l=2000), the efficient estimator achieves MSE 0.00 and bias 0.00 with 95% coverage under the true propensity score, while IPW suffers severe degradation (MSE 10.85, bias 1.44) under model misspecification of the estimated propensity score. The efficient estimator consistently attains the lowest MSE and near-nominal coverage across both settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three estimators it proposes: the IPW estimator, the DM (direct method / plug-in) estimator, and the semiparametric efficient estimator. In the censoring setting with the true propensity score, the efficient estimator (MSE 0.01, bias 0.00, coverage 0.93) dominates both IPW (MSE 0.06, bias −0.06) and DM (MSE 0.01, bias 0.03, but only 9% coverage), confirming the theoretical efficiency bound. In the case-control setting with a misspecified propensity score model, the gap widens dramatically: IPW collapses to MSE 10.85 with bias 1.44, while the efficient estimator maintains MSE 0.06 and bias 0.07, demonstrating the practical value of the doubly robust structure. With the true propensity score in the case-control setting, all three methods perform well (MSE ≤ 0.03), but the efficient estimator still achieves the lowest MSE (0.00) and the most accurate coverage (95%). The authors note that all three estimators are their own proposals and that the goal is not to benchmark against external methods but to validate the theoretical efficiency claims.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the doubly robust / augmented IPW framework (Bang & Robins, 2005) to a data structure borrowed from PU learning, where only confirmed treated units and a mixed (unlabeled) group are observed. It draws on PU learning methods (Elkan & Noto, 2008; du Plessis et al., 2015) for propensity score estimation and on cross-fitting (Chernozhukov et al., 2018) to control nuisance estimator complexity, but departs from standard double machine learning in a key way: the Neyman-orthogonal (doubly robust) structure links the observation probability to the outcome model, not the propensity score, so the propensity score estimation error cannot be absorbed by cross-fitting alone. The paper also positions itself relative to missing-treatment ATE estimation (Molinari, 2010; Kennedy, 2020; Kuzmanovic et al., 2023), noting that its setting is weaker (only D=1 is observable when the label is present) and more aligned with implicit-feedback applications.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first semiparametric efficiency bounds and corresponding efficient estimators for ATE under the PUATE data structure, giving practitioners a principled benchmark and an optimal estimator for a practically common scenario (recommendation systems, marketing, health records with implicit feedback). A notable limitation is that √n-consistency requires the censoring/case-control propensity score to be known or estimated from a separate auxiliary dataset, since the standard DML machinery cannot handle its estimation error; this restricts applicability when no auxiliary data exist. Open directions include extending to CATE estimation, relaxing the SCAR assumption, and integrating with semi-supervised frameworks where additional unlabeled covariates are available.
