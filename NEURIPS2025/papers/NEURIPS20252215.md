## Problem

Prompting is the primary method through which nearly every major LLM capability (in-context learning, chain-of-thought, constitutional AI) was discovered, yet it is culturally dismissed as unscientific 'hackery' or 'dark magic' in the ML community. The paper argues this dismissal is a category error: if LLMs are treated as complex, opaque organisms, then probing them through their native interface (language) is behavioral science, not a workaround. The gap is the absence of a principled framework that positions prompting as a legitimate, complementary form of scientific inquiry alongside mechanistic interpretability.

## Input / Output

The paper's analytical framework treats LLMs as organisms probed through structured natural language prompts that manipulate the model's conditional distribution. Beyond surface text, the 'output' explicitly includes the full token probability distribution, which the authors argue constitutes objective mechanistic evidence on par with weight-level analysis. The unusual aspect is that the input is not a fixed evaluation protocol but an open-ended exploratory intervention: the researcher designs linguistic probes to elicit a priori unlikely but a posteriori predictable behaviors, making the prompt itself the experimental instrument.

## Method

The core contribution is a multi-level philosophical framework grounded in Marr's levels of analysis, positioning prompting at the computational level (what the model does and why) and mechanistic interpretability at the implementation level (how weights and circuits realize functions), with both converging at the algorithmic level. The paper provides a five-dimensional practical comparison—scalability, scope, when MI excels, understanding, and scientific narrative—showing the two approaches are complementary rather than competing. It then systematically rebuts six common dismissals (lack of mathematical formalism, no model training, prompt hacking, brittleness, non-generalization, and manual effort), arguing that prompt sensitivity reflects the model inferring pragmatic context rather than being a defect. Finally, it proposes a bidirectional workflow where prompting discovers behavioral anomalies, mechanistic interpretability reverse-engineers the implementing circuits, and mechanistic insights generate new prompting experiments, mirroring successful paradigms in neuroscience.

## Results

This is a position paper with no quantitative experiments or benchmark results. The paper's central empirical claim is qualitative: virtually every major LLM capability breakthrough (in-context learning, chain-of-thought, constitutional AI, DSPy) originated from prompting experiments rather than architectural or optimization innovations. The authors argue that prompting and mechanistic interpretability are complementary at different levels of analysis (computational vs. implementation, per Marr's framework), and that prompting's perceived inferiority stems from cultural bias in the ML community toward optimization over exploration.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not run formal experiments or compare methods on benchmarks. Instead, it provides a conceptual comparison between prompting and mechanistic interpretability across five dimensions: scalability (prompting is lightweight and deployable at scale; mechanistic interpretability struggles with billions of parameters), scope (prompting has broad expressive power via language; mechanistic methods require precise hypotheses about internals), when mechanistic interpretability excels (precise algorithmic reverse-engineering, sub-token processing, small-model circuit tracing), understanding (prompting generates accessible behavioral evidence; mechanistic explanations prioritize faithfulness but can be opaque), and scientific narrative (prompting surfaces behavioral anomalies that guide mechanistic follow-up, as with induction heads). The authors position the two as complementary rather than competing, analogous to behavioral psychology and cognitive neuroscience in studying human cognition.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper does not build on a specific method but rather repositions prompting within a scientific-inquiry framework by drawing on Marr's levels of analysis (Marr, 1982; Griffiths et al., 2024) and the complex-systems perspective on LLMs (Holtzman et al., 2023). It explicitly parallels Olah et al.'s (2020) argument for mechanistic interpretability as empirical science, and invokes Jonas & Kording's (2017) 'neuroscientist and microprocessor' analogy to argue that probing a system through its native interface is a valid scientific strategy. The paper carves new conceptual ground by treating prompting not as a workaround but as a first-class discovery method, while simultaneously acknowledging mechanistic interpretability's complementary strengths at the implementation level.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a persistent cultural bias in the ML community that privileges optimization-based and weight-level analysis over behavioral probing, arguing this bias has caused the field to underinvest in systematic prompting research despite prompting being the source of most major capability discoveries (ICL, CoT, Constitutional AI). Open questions it raises include: can we define 'control primitives' in prompt space, what makes single-prompt strategies more effective than multi-turn ones, and how can prompting and mechanistic interpretability be integrated into a bidirectional discovery loop? The main limitation is that, as a position paper, it offers no empirical validation of its proposed frameworks or future directions, leaving the practical rigor of 'prompting as science' to be demonstrated by follow-up work.
