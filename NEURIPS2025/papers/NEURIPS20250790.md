## Problem

Regression trees approximate functions with piecewise-constant values, so their formal gradient is zero almost everywhere, yet they are among the most widely used regression tools. This means the rich ecosystem of gradient-based interpretability and sensitivity analysis methods developed for differentiable models (neural networks, Gaussian processes) has no natural counterpart for tree-based models. The paper asks whether the tree structure itself implicitly encodes gradient information that can be extracted and exploited.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a fully fitted regression tree (leaf means, split variables, thresholds, and axis-aligned node bounds) and an evaluation point x ∈ [0,1]^P; for integrated gradients it additionally requires a reference point x*. The gradient estimate is obtained in a single top-down traversal of the tree, visiting only O(K) nodes along the path to the leaf containing x. The active subspace matrix output is a P×P symmetric matrix computed by summing outer products of per-node gradient estimates weighted by node volumes at the penultimate depth, requiring no Monte Carlo sampling.

## Method

The core mechanism is a finite-difference-like quantity γ_i = 2(μ̂_r^i − μ̂_l^i)/(u_σi^i − l_σi^i) computed at each internal node i, where μ̂_r and μ̂_l are the mean responses in the right and left children and the denominator is the node width along the split variable σ_i. Algorithm 1 (TBGE) accumulates these partial-derivative estimates along the root-to-leaf path: at each depth the gradient vector is inherited from the parent and updated only in the split direction, so the gradient at a point is the vector stored at its containing leaf. For integro-differential quantities (integrated gradients, active subspace matrix), two estimators are provided: a Monte Carlo version that samples points and evaluates TBGE, and a partition-based version that sums h(G^i)·μ([l^i, u^i]) over penultimate-depth nodes, which is exact (no MC error) when node volumes are computable. Under assumptions of uniform inputs, cycling splits, and a bounded Hessian, the TBGE converges to the true gradient at rate O_P(P/log N) when tree depth S(N) = P log log N, and the partition-based estimator for the active subspace matrix is shown consistent via the Dominated Convergence Theorem.

## Results

On the predictive rotation study (Table 1), TBAS rotations match or beat identity, PCA, and random rotations across 8 benchmark datasets for both regression trees and random forests, with the largest gains on Kin40k (RMSE 0.856 vs 0.963 for identity, depth-4 tree) and Keggu (RMSE 0.194 vs 0.35 for identity). In low-dimensional active subspace estimation (Fig. 5), TBAS occupies the majority of the Pareto front (subspace error vs. execution time) across dimensions 2–4, outperforming GP, PRA, and DASM at comparable or lower compute cost. In high-dimensional sparse settings (Fig. 6, dimensions 10/50/100 with only 3 nonzero coordinates), TBAS achieves substantially lower subspace estimation error than DASM at a fraction of the computational cost.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares TBAS against three gradient-free active subspace estimators—Gaussian Process (GP; Wycoff et al. 2021), Polynomial Ridge Approximation (PRA; Hokanson & Constantine 2018), and Deep Active Subspace Method (DASM; Tripathy & Bilionis 2019)—as well as against identity, PCA, and random rotations for predictive performance. In the low-dimensional active subspace estimation study (Fig. 5, dims 2–4), TBAS dominates the Pareto front of subspace error vs. execution time, while GP and PRA are only competitive at very small sample sizes (≤150) before their compute costs explode; DASM is consistently outperformed. In the high-dimensional sparse setting (Fig. 6, dims 10/50/100 with 3 active coordinates), TBAS achieves markedly lower subspace error than DASM at a fraction of the runtime, and GP/PRA are computationally infeasible. For the predictive rotation task (Table 1), TBAS matches or beats identity, PCA, and random rotations on all 8 datasets for depth-8 regression trees and random forests, with the most dramatic gains on Keggu (RMSE 0.078 vs 0.121–0.124) and Kin40k (0.586 vs 0.836–0.872) at depth 8, and on Keggu/Kin40k at depth 4 (0.194 vs 0.349–0.350; 0.856 vs 0.950–0.964). The one setting where TBAS underperforms is the classification variant (Appendix C.5), where Brier scores are less favorable, suggesting special considerations are needed for classification trees.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate literatures: gradient-based model interpretability (Integrated Gradients, Active Subspaces) developed for differentiable models like neural networks and Gaussian processes, and tree-based regression methods whose piecewise-constant structure formally has zero gradient almost everywhere. Prior gradient estimation in trees (Chaudhuri et al. 1995; Loh 2011) assumed differentiable leaf models, whereas this work extracts finite-difference-like gradient estimates directly from the value differences between sibling nodes in constant-leaf trees. The paper positions its contribution as enabling existing gradient-based sensitivity analysis and interpretability tools (Active Subspace Method, Integrated Gradients) to be deployed on the most prevalent form of regression tree, rather than introducing new interpretability criteria.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical implication is that tree-based models—already the workhorse of tabular data science—can now leverage the entire ecosystem of gradient-based interpretability and dimension reduction tools without requiring a separate differentiable surrogate. However, the convergence rate of O_P(P/log N) is slow, meaning the gradient estimates are best suited for coarse sensitivity analysis rather than precise gradient recovery, and the theoretical analysis assumes unsupervised (variable-cycling) splits rather than CART. Open questions include whether combining gradient estimates across depths (rather than overwriting) could improve the rate, how the method behaves for classification with categorical inputs, and whether physics-informed learning constraints can be imposed on tree-based models using these estimates.
