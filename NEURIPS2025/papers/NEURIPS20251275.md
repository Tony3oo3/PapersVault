## Problem

Standard retraining of foundation models across multiple tasks minimizes the aggregated loss without accounting for the downstream parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) procedure, providing no guarantee that the recovered parameters are low-rank adaptable to unseen tasks. While empirical works have demonstrated benefits of PEFT-based meta-learning over standard retraining, no theoretical guarantees establishing when and why this gap arises had been proven. This paper provides the first provable performance separation showing standard retraining is fundamentally suboptimal for producing LoRA-adaptable parameters.

## Input / Output

The method takes T retraining tasks, each consisting of input-output pairs (x, y) ∈ R^d × R^d generated from a shared matrix A* plus a task-specific rank-k perturbation, along with the pretrained model's base weight matrices. It outputs base weights Â that are explicitly designed so that a rank-k (or rank-3k for T=2) LoRA adapter QV^T suffices to adapt to any new task drawn from the same distribution. The retraining phase uses large per-task sample sizes (n_t = N), while the fine-tuning phase operates on a small number of test samples (n_{T+1} = n), reflecting the practical low-resource adaptation setting.

## Method

The proposed LoRA-ML objective replaces standard retraining's aggregated loss with a meta-learning loss that minimizes, over base weights A, the sum of per-task losses after applying task-specific symmetric low-rank adapters U_t U_t^T: L_Meta(A) = Σ_t min_{U_t} ||A* + U_t*U_t*^T − A − U_t U_t^T||_F^2. This explicitly bakes the LoRA fine-tuning mechanism into retraining, promoting base weights that are easily adapted via low-rank perturbations. The key theoretical results show that standard retraining recovers A* + (1/T)Σ U_t*U_t*^T, which is min{d, k(T+1)}-rank away from the test task (worsening with T), while any global minimum of LoRA-ML is at most rank-2k from A*, and with T ≥ 3 the ground truth A* is the unique global minimum up to orthogonal symmetry. For T = 2, every second-order stationary point is globally optimal, enabling efficient optimization via perturbed gradient descent. The approach is validated on synthetic linear regression, CIFAR-10 vision classification with MLP-Mixer, and ConvAI2 dialogue tasks with RoBERTa, using both LoRA and last-layer fine-tuning.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 (binary classification, 4 retraining tasks), LoRA-ML achieves 86.09% mean accuracy, outperforming standard retraining + LoRA (85.29%) and Reptile + LoRA (81.30%). On ConvAI2 (10 retraining tasks, 10 test tasks, RoBERTa-base), LoRA-ML achieves 47.4% average accuracy versus 35.0% for SR+LoRA and 28.4% for Reptile+LoRA — a 12.4-point and 19.0-point gap respectively. The same pattern holds for last-layer fine-tuning: PEFT-ML outperforms both baselines on both datasets, with the largest margin on ConvAI2 (40.2% vs. 24.5% vs. 19.4%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares PEFT-ML against two baselines: standard retraining (SR) and Reptile [13]. On ConvAI2, LoRA-ML (47.4% avg) beats SR+LoRA (35.0%) by 12.4 points and Reptile+LoRA (28.4%) by 19.0 points; the gap widens further for last-layer fine-tuning (40.2% vs. 24.5% vs. 19.4%). On CIFAR-10, LoRA-ML (86.09%) edges out SR+LoRA (85.29%) by 0.8 points and Reptile+LoRA (81.30%) by 4.8 points. Notably, standard retraining outperforms Reptile on both datasets, which the authors attribute to Reptile's assumption of gradient-based adaptation being misaligned with the PEFT methods used at test time. PEFT-ML wins on every individual test task in Table 2 except T1 (where SR+LoRA scores 59 vs. 57), confirming consistent gains across diverse tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the meta-learning literature (MAML, Reptile) by explicitly incorporating a specific PEFT mechanism (LoRA) into the retraining objective, rather than treating adaptation as a generic gradient step. It departs from prior theoretical analyses of gradient-based meta-learning for linear models (Collins et al. 2022, Saunshi et al. 2020), which studied shared-subspace recovery, by instead modeling tasks as low-rank perturbations of a common matrix—a structurally different problem where only three tasks suffice for exact recovery. Several concurrent works (Hou et al. 2022, Bansal et al. 2022, Gheini et al. 2023) proposed PEFT-based meta-learning objectives with empirical gains over standard retraining, but this paper provides the first theoretical proof that such meta-learning is strictly superior to standard retraining in any setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes the first provable performance gap between standard retraining and PEFT-based meta-learning, resolving a question that prior empirical work could only motivate. The counterintuitive finding that standard retraining degrades with more tasks (the recovered parameters become harder to low-rank adapt) challenges the common assumption that aggregating more task data always improves the base model. Key limitations include the restriction to linear models and symmetric adapters in the theory, with no analysis for transformers or general (non-symmetric) LoRA factors. Open questions include extending the landscape analysis to T ≥ 3 (where spurious local minima can exist in adversarial cases) and to deeper architectures.
