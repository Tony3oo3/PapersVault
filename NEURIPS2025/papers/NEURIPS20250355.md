## Problem

LLMs trained on human preference data may learn either fundamental human values (e.g., moral principles) or merely superficial surface-level patterns (e.g., writing style), but no existing benchmark systematically distinguishes between these two forms of generalization. This distinction is critical for AI alignment: systems that capture only shallow correlations risk unpredictable or harmful behavior when deployed in novel contexts where the surface patterns no longer co-occur with the underlying values.

## Input / Output

Each test prompt presents N in-context training examples (N ∈ {4, 20, 40}) where a user consistently prefers options pairing (value₁, preference₁) over (value₂, preference₂), followed by a single test question with swapped pairings: (value₁, preference₂) vs. (value₂, preference₁). The model must respond with exactly 'Option A' or 'Option B'; failed extractions are treated as missing data. The aggregate output is the Deep Value Generalization Rate (DVGR), the proportion of 12K test trials where the model selected the value-aligned option, with chance defined as 0.5.

## Method

The core contribution is a 'confound-then-deconfound' experimental design: during the in-context training phase, deep values (6 prima facie duties + 5 Schwartz social values) are perfectly correlated with shallow preferences (20 human-validated, morally neutral dichotomies like formal/informal address), making both signals equally predictive of the user's choice. The test phase breaks this correlation by swapping the shallow preferences, forcing the model to reveal which signal it actually generalizes. Contexts are grounded in 8 ecologically valid domains derived from Y Combinator AI startup tags and O*NET occupational work activities, with natural-language choice completions validated by two separate crowdworker studies (91% and 98% accuracy). Across 9 models (GPT-4o/4.1 family, Gemini-2.0, Llama-3), the average DVGR is 0.30—below chance for every model—with larger models performing slightly worse than smaller ones, and explicit instructions to prioritize deep values raising DVGR only to 0.33.

## Results

Across 9 models and 104,725 trials, the overall Deep Value Generalization Rate (DVGR) is 0.30, with every model performing significantly below the 0.5 chance level. Individual model DVGRs range from 0.24 (gpt-4.1) to 0.40 (gemini-2.0-flash). Explicitly instructing models to generalize the deep value raises the pooled DVGR to 0.33 (still below chance), while chain-of-thought prompting lowers it to 0.25. Smaller models show a slightly higher DVGR than their larger counterparts (mean absolute difference = 0.07), and models from the same developer show 76.8% agreement versus 72.2% for cross-developer pairs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a benchmark paper, so comparisons are structured around model size, prompt strategy, and developer identity rather than competing methods. On model size, smaller models consistently show slightly higher DVGRs than their larger counterparts (e.g., gpt-4.1-nano at 0.35 vs. gpt-4.1 at 0.24; llama-3-8b at 0.37 vs. llama-3-70b at 0.24), though effect sizes are small (mean absolute difference = 0.07) and all differences are statistically significant due to the large N. On prompt strategy, explicit instruction to prioritize deep values improves DVGR for 7 of 9 models (pooled 0.30 → 0.33), while chain-of-thought prompting decreases DVGR for 7 of 9 models (pooled 0.30 → 0.25), suggesting CoT amplifies reliance on shallow surface features. On developer identity, same-developer model pairs show 76.8% agreement on test questions versus 72.2% for cross-developer pairs (3.6 pp difference, 95% CI [0.4, 6.8], p = 0.04), indicating developer-specific value priors. No model, regardless of size or developer, achieves DVGR at or above chance (0.5), confirming a systematic tendency to generalize shallow preferences over deep values.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by combining the 'confound-then-deconfound' experimental design (analogous to proxy-vs-true-reward separation in reward hacking literature) with the philosophical distinction between deep values (second-order desires, prima facie duties, Schwartz basic values) and shallow preferences (first-order, morally neutral attributes). While prior work on reward hacking (AI Safety Gridworlds, reward tampering) and LLM generalization (ARC, concept induction, verbal analogies) each touches on related ideas, none provides a systematic, interpretable metric for whether preference-trained LLMs generalize underlying values versus surface correlations. The paper also draws on the LLM evaluation benchmark tradition (RewardBench, RM-Bench, ValueBench) but targets a fundamentally different property: the depth of what models extract from preference data rather than reward model accuracy or value identification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that all nine tested models generalize deep values below chance (DVGR = 0.30) raises a concrete alignment concern: systems acting on users' behalf may be tracking superficial patterns rather than internalizing the values that actually guide human choices, with potential for consequential failures in high-stakes domains. The result that scale does not help (larger models perform slightly worse) and that chain-of-thought reasoning actively worsens performance challenges two common assumptions in the field. Key open questions include whether fine-tuning can improve DVGR, whether the artificial perfect-confound design overstates the problem relative to naturalistic partial confounds, and whether future model generations will show improvement—making DVGR a useful longitudinal tracking metric.
