## Problem

Prior work has identified that Transformers learn different in-context learning strategies (memorizing vs. generalizing) under varying training conditions, but no unified account explains why these transitions occur. The paper asks: given that memorization almost always yields better in-distribution performance, why does a model first learn a generalizing solution, and why does it eventually abandon it? This gap is addressed by framing ICL strategy selection as a rational adaptation governed by a loss-complexity tradeoff.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a context token sequence from one of three task settings (Balls & Urns, Linear Regression, or Binary Classification) along with two scalar conditions: number of training steps N and task diversity D. It produces a next-token prediction as a posterior-weighted interpolation between a memorizing predictor (discrete prior over seen tasks) and a generalizing predictor (continuous prior over the true task distribution), with the interpolation weights given by posterior probabilities computed in closed form. Crucially, the model requires no access to Transformer weights—only three free parameters (α, β, γ) are fitted to a subset of (N, D) conditions, and predictions are then made for all other conditions.

## Method

The paper proposes a hierarchical Bayesian framework in which pretraining is modeled as a posterior update over candidate predictors, and inference-time ICL behavior is a posterior-weighted average of their predictions. Two computational constraints ground the model: (A1) loss scales as a power law with dataset size, making the effective evidence accumulate sublinearly as N^(1-α), and (A2) a simplicity bias encoded via Kolmogorov complexity, giving a universal prior p(Q) ∝ 2^{-K(Q)^β}. Under these constraints, the log-posterior odds between the memorizing and generalizing predictors reduce to η(N,D) = γN^(1-α)ΔL(D) − ΔK(D)^β, where ΔL is the loss difference and ΔK is the complexity difference. The sigmoid of η yields the interpolation weight, producing a closed-form predictive model that almost perfectly matches Transformer next-token predictions (mean R² = 0.97 in regression, Spearman ρ = 0.97 in Balls & Urns) across 72 experimental maps without weight access. The framework also yields novel predictions, including a superlinear scaling of the transience timescale N* with task diversity and a rapid crossover in model behavior near the posterior-odds boundary.

## Results

The hierarchical Bayesian model, with only 3 free parameters (α, β, γ) fitted across 11 training runs, achieves near-perfect prediction of Transformer next-token predictions: mean R² of 0.97 ± 0.004 (SE) in Linear Regression, mean agreement of 0.92 ± 0.007 in Classification, and mean Spearman rank correlation of 0.97 ± 0.001 in Balls & Urns. The model's posterior probabilities for the memorizing predictor correlate at 0.99, 0.98, and 0.99 with empirical relative distance values in Linear Regression, Classification, and Balls & Urns respectively. These results are replicated across 72 different maps varying MLP width, context length, and task dimensionality, with the model also capturing novel predictions such as super-linear scaling of transience time with task diversity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against competing ICL methods; instead, it validates its theoretical model against empirical Transformer behavior as the ground truth. The model's predictions are compared directly to next-token outputs of GPT-NeoX Transformers trained from scratch across 11 runs and 72 (N, D) maps, achieving the high correlations reported above without access to model weights. Qualitatively, the paper positions itself against prior single-predictor Bayesian accounts (Xie et al. [35], Raventós et al. [25], Panwar et al. [62]), arguing that a mixture of memorizing and generalizing predictors is required to capture the full phenomenology, and against recent normative accounts of pretraining (Carroll et al. [30], Elmoznino et al. [74]), which also identify a loss–complexity tradeoff but lack the quantitative, predictive functional form provided here. The framework also subsumes the Linear Interpolation of Algorithms (LIA) approach of Park et al. [26], which requires fitting N×D interpolation weights per setting, whereas the present model needs only 3 parameters.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies the memorizing-vs-generalizing predictor distinction identified across disparate ICL settings (Raventós et al.'s dMMSE vs. ridge estimator in linear regression, Park et al.'s retrieval vs. inference in Markov modeling, Singh et al.'s in-weights vs. in-context learning in classification) under a single hierarchical Bayesian framework. It departs from prior Bayesian accounts of ICL that treat the model as implementing a single predictor at inference time, instead framing pretraining as a posterior update over strategies and inference as a posterior-weighted average. The loss-complexity tradeoff it formalizes via Kolmogorov complexity and power-law scaling converges with independent findings by Carroll et al. and Elmoznino et al., but provides a quantitative, closed-form predictive model (3 free parameters) where those works offer only qualitative or architecture-dependent analyses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a unifying explanatory lens for ICL phenomenology (task diversity thresholds, transient generalization) grounded in a loss-complexity tradeoff, and yields novel quantitative predictions such as superlinear scaling of transience time with task diversity. Its main limitation is scope: validation is restricted to three toy settings with at most three predictors, and the Kolmogorov complexity estimate via lossless compression is a crude proxy for Transformer implementation cost. Open questions include whether the framework extends to the richer, multi-strategy dynamics of real LLM pretraining, how in-context posterior updates differ from parametric learning dynamics, and how to connect this top-down normative account to bottom-up mechanistic circuits like induction heads.
