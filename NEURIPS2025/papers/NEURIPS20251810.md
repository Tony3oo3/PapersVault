## Problem

Despite rapid progress in multimodal large language models and video generation, no standardized benchmark exists to assess whether these models can truly understand fine-grained cinematographic techniques (shot scale, angle, composition, camera movement, lighting, color, focal length) or generate cinema-quality camera movements. Existing movie understanding datasets focus on high-level narrative semantics or address only isolated cinematographic aspects, leaving a critical gap in evaluating the visual-language core of cinematic storytelling.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251810/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For the understanding task, the benchmark provides 610 image QA pairs and 128 video QA pairs drawn from 600+ high-resolution stills and 120 short clips sourced from IMDb's Top 250 films, alongside 100 image descriptions (~176 words) and 128 video descriptions (~168 words) for open-ended evaluation. For the generation task, video generation models receive the first frame, last frame (when applicable), and a textual prompt containing camera-movement cues, and must produce a video clip whose camera trajectory matches the original. MLLMs that lack native video input are evaluated via multi-image frame sampling at varying frame rates (1–8 fps or fixed frame counts of 4–64).

## Method

CineTechBench constructs a seven-dimension cinematographic taxonomy (scale, angle, composition, movement, lighting, color, focal length) by collecting keywords from film-education websites, organizing them hierarchically with GPT-4o, and refining through iterative feedback from professional cinematographers. Data are manually assembled from IMDb Top 250 films and the Movieclips YouTube channel, then annotated by trained annotators with ambiguous cases escalated to expert cinematographers; GPT-4o generates QA pairs and descriptions guided by the taxonomy, all manually reviewed for accuracy. Understanding is evaluated via per-dimension QA accuracy and description quality measured by BLEU/METEOR/ROUGE plus CAPability-style HR/AP/AR/F1 scores (automated by GPT-4.1-nano against ground-truth annotations). Camera-movement generation is evaluated by estimating trajectories with MonST3R and computing rotation error, relative/absolute translation error, CamMC (joint 3×4 matrix L2 distance), and CLIP frame similarity, with results further stratified by translation speed and angular velocity to expose failure modes on high-rotation shots.

## Results

On the static cinematographic QA task (Table 1), GPT-4o achieves the highest overall accuracy of 70.16%, followed closely by Gemini-2.5-Pro (69.67%), while open-source models lag by ~15 percentage points (best: InternVL3 at 55.25%). For camera movement understanding (Table 2), Gemini-2.5-Pro leads at 56.69% overall, with rotation remaining the hardest subcategory across all models. On description generation (Table 3), Gemini-2.5-Pro achieves the best F1 of 52.27, and on camera movement generation (Table 4), Kling v1.6 with first+last frame+text conditioning achieves the lowest rotation error (21.68°) and relative translation error (48.49).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among 15+ MLLMs evaluated, GPT-4o and Gemini-2.5-Pro dominate the static understanding task, with GPT-4o leading on scale (75.00%) and color (93.33%) while Gemini-2.5-Pro leads on angle (83.33%). Open-source models trail by roughly 15 points; InternVL3 (55.25%) is the strongest open-source model, surpassing even Gemini-2.0-Flash on description F1 (47.86 vs. 44.43). For camera movement QA, Gemini-2.5-Pro (56.69%) outperforms GPT-4o (50.00%), and all models struggle with rotation (best: 45.16% by Gemini-2.5-Pro). On the generation side, Kling v1.6 (FLT condition) achieves the best rotation error (21.68°) and relative translation error (48.49), outperforming open-source Wan2.1-FLF (27.80°, 48.31°) and FramePack-FLF2V (23.88°, 58.10°). Notably, all video generation models fail on high-rotation clips, with errors increasing sharply as angular velocity rises, and only Gen4turbo correctly reproduces a counter-clockwise roll in the qualitative example.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on CameraBench's [29] five-type camera movement taxonomy (Translation, Rotation, Zoom, Static, Combined) and adapts the CAPability benchmark's [32] HR/AP/AR/F1 metric suite for evaluating description quality. It departs from prior movie understanding datasets (MovieNet, MovieShots, MotionSet) that each address isolated cinematographic facets by unifying seven core dimensions under a single expert-annotated framework. The generation evaluation—reconstructing camera trajectories via MonST3R and comparing against ground-truth paths—has no direct precedent in existing video generation benchmarks (VBench, DEVIL, SCINE), which focus on general visual quality rather than cinematographic technique fidelity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that even frontier MLLMs (GPT-4o, Gemini-2.5-Pro) plateau around 70% on static cinematographic understanding and ~57% on camera movement, with a telling pattern of high hit rates (~85–89%) but low F1 scores (~43–52%) in description generation—suggesting models invoke cinematographic vocabulary without robust spatial or dynamic reasoning. For generation, the complete failure of models to reproduce intense camera rotations (e.g., Wan2.1 omits roll entirely; Kling inverts direction) highlights a concrete gap for video generation research. The identified root cause—cinematographic terms appearing in only ~0.05% of pre-training captions—points toward targeted data augmentation as a practical path forward, while the benchmark itself provides the first standardized testbed for tracking progress in cinematic AI.
