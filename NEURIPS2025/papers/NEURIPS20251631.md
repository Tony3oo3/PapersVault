## Problem

Determining optimal language proportions for multilingual LLM pretraining is intractable because cross-lingual interactions couple all languages' performance, and optimal ratios shift with training scale. Existing approaches rely on heuristic trial-and-error or reuse ratios from prior models without systematic justification, leaving a gap for a principled, scalable method that can predict and optimize multilingual data allocation without exhaustive retraining.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251631/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a multilingual corpus (Fineweb-2), a total token budget D, a set of m target languages, and per-language importance weights ω_i. It requires only 3×m×2 targeted training runs (monolingual plus two multilingual proportions at two token budgets per language) to fit all scaling-law parameters, then outputs a single optimal proportion vector r* on the probability simplex. The intermediate output—the cross-lingual interaction-aware ratios r̃_i—provides an interpretable measure of each language's effective data allocation, where r̃_i − r_i quantifies the net cross-lingual transfer benefit or interference.

## Method

CLIMB introduces a cross-lingual interaction-aware language ratio r̃_i that maps a language's multilingual validation loss back to an equivalent monolingual token budget via the Chinchilla scaling law, thereby absorbing all inter-language dependencies into a single scalar. The ratio is modeled parametrically as r̃_i = r_i + (Σ_{j≠i}(b_{ji}+k_{ji}/D)·r_j)(1−e^{−η_i r_i}), where the transfer coefficients decay linearly with data scale and the exponential term captures diminishing transfer at high self-proportions. The optimal allocation is then found in two stages: first, a closed-form Lagrange-multiplier solution equalizes marginal loss-reduction rates to fix the optimal direction p_i in r̃-space; second, a Trust-Region Interior-Point solver maximizes the aggregate r̃ magnitude along that direction subject to the simplex constraint, with a soft penalty maintaining directional fidelity. This decomposition converts an intractable bi-level non-convex problem into a tractable two-step procedure requiring no gradient access to the LLM.

## Results

On 18 multilingual benchmarks with a 1.2B model trained on 1T tokens, CLIMB achieves the best or near-best score among all data allocation methods on 16 out of 18 tasks, with headline gains of +2.60% on XNLI (41.65 vs. 39.05 for Isolated) and +1.85% average improvement over the strongest baseline. For scaling-law prediction, CLIMB attains R² of 0.978–0.992 and Huber loss of 0.205–0.518×10⁻³ across 2- to 16-language settings, substantially outperforming the MSL baseline (R² 0.823–0.854, Huber 1.94–2.20×10⁻³). At 7B scale, CLIMB-derived allocations outperform baselines by an average of 3.4% across benchmarks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the MSL scaling-law baseline (He et al., 2024), CLIMB achieves substantially better predictive accuracy: R² of 0.978–0.992 vs. 0.823–0.854, and Huber loss roughly 5–10× lower (0.205–0.518 vs. 1.94–2.20 ×10⁻³) across all language counts from 2 to 16. Among open-source multilingual LLMs, CLIMB-trained 1.2B models are competitive with LLaMA-3.2-1B and Gemma-3-1B-pt on most benchmarks (e.g., XNLI 41.65 vs. 41.29 and 36.33; FLORES 50.43 vs. 44.14 and 46.55), though Qwen3-1.7B remains stronger overall due to its larger pretraining corpus. Compared to XGLM-1.7B, CLIMB outperforms on nearly every benchmark (e.g., FLORES 50.43 vs. 21.80, MultiBLiMP 65.54 vs. 68.10 is the sole exception where XGLM leads). Against UniMax (Chung et al., 2023), CLIMB wins on 16 of 18 benchmarks, with the largest margins on XWinograd (+2.54), MultiBLiMP (+3.30), and FLORES (+1.31), while UniMax edges ahead only on XGPQA (27.10 vs. 27.03). Among the heuristic allocation baselines (Uniform, Isolated, Natural, Temp), CLIMB consistently leads, with the most pronounced advantage on MultiBLiMP (+2.36 over Natural) and XWinograd (+2.50 over Natural).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the monolingual scaling-law framework of Hoffmann et al. (Chinchilla) to multilingual pretraining by introducing a cross-lingual interaction-aware language ratio that explicitly models how one language's effective data allocation is modulated by co-trained languages. It departs from the MSL baseline [28], which assumes language-family independence, and from data-mixing methods like DoReMi and UniMax that operate primarily in monolingual settings. The two-step optimization (marginal-benefit balancing for direction, then constrained magnitude maximization) is a novel decomposition that makes the otherwise intractable joint optimization over language proportions computationally feasible.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CLIMB provides the first principled, scaling-law-based framework for multilingual data allocation that accounts for cross-lingual interactions, replacing the heuristic trial-and-error or ratio-reuse practices common in current multilingual LLM training. The finding that optimal language proportions shift systematically with data scale (simpler languages favored at small budgets, linguistically complex languages at large budgets) has direct practical implications for curriculum design. Key limitations include validation primarily at 1.2B scale (with 7B as a secondary check), no explicit incorporation of model size N into the allocation, and no modeling of transfer to/from languages absent from the training set—open questions the authors flag for future work.
