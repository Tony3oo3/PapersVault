## Problem

Existing AIGV evaluation metrics are either off-the-shelf models with heuristic rules or specialized evaluators trained on human annotations for a fixed set of aspects, making them difficult to scale to finer-grained and more comprehensive evaluations. The paper investigates whether pretrained MLLMs can serve as a unified, zero-shot evaluator for any AIGV aspect by simply modifying the evaluation prompt, eliminating the need for aspect-specific training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250564/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The evaluator takes a short AI-generated video (~5 seconds, 360p–1080p) sampled into 12–16 frames, the original text-to-video prompt, and a textual evaluation guideline specifying the target aspect (e.g., motion naturalness, structural correctness). For single video rating, it outputs a continuous score in [0,1] derived from the MLLM's token generation probabilities; for video pair comparison, it outputs a four-way categorical choice from {V₁ better, V₂ better, same good, same bad}. The same model and pipeline handle all 15 evaluation aspects by swapping only the guideline text.

## Method

The core mechanism is zero-shot prompting of pretrained MLLMs with no fine-tuning. For single video rating, the score is computed as P(t_pos)/(P(t_pos)+P(t_neg)), where t_pos and t_neg are predefined scoring tokens (default: yes/no), conditioned on the video frames, T2V prompt, and aspect-specific guideline. For video pair comparison, both videos are fed as interleaved vision-language input and the MLLM is prompted to select from the four preference options. The paper introduces UVE-Bench (4,042 pairwise annotations across 15 aspects from 8 SOTA VGMs) to benchmark this approach, and conducts ablations on prompting strategy (detailed vs. simplified aspect descriptions), scoring tokens (yes/no vs. good/bad vs. direct score generation), frame count, and a single-to-pairwise adaptation strategy that independently rates each video before converting to a four-way choice—showing this adaptation particularly benefits smaller (7B) models with weaker native pairwise comparison ability.

## Results

On UVE-Bench single video rating, the best MLLM (Seed1.5-VL, 20B active) achieves an average A^single score of 80.0 across 15 evaluation aspects, followed by InternVL-2.5-78B-MPO and GPT-4o at 78.2, and Qwen2-VL-72B at 75.4. MLLMs show particular strength in Dynamic Degree (e.g., Qwen2-VL-72B scores 92.6 on Subject Motion) and Video-Text Alignment (95.0 on Appearance Alignment), but struggle with Temporal Quality subaspects (e.g., Motion Naturalness at 48.9 for Qwen2-VL-72B) and Structural Correctness (60.2). In video pair comparison, the best MLLM (Gemini 2.5-Flash) reaches only 54.6% average accuracy versus 88.0% for human evaluators, revealing a >30-point gap.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The unified MLLM evaluators consistently outperform or match all five specialized baselines across the aspects they cover. On Technical Quality, InternVL-2.5-78B (84.0) surpasses DOVER (69.2) by 14.8 points; on Aesthetic Quality, Seed1.5-VL (88.8) beats DOVER (80.3) by 8.5 points. On Overall Alignment, Qwen2-VL-72B (89.0) exceeds UMTScore (66.4) by 22.6 points and VIDEOCON-PHYSICS (68.7) by 20.3 points. On Motion Naturalness, InternVL-2.5-78B (61.8) outperforms VIDEOCON-PHYSICS (53.4) by 8.4 points. The one notable exception is Overall Dynamic Degree, where VBench (87.8) slightly edges out Qwen2-VL-72B (86.5), though VBench relies on hand-crafted heuristics rather than a learned model. Overall, the paper demonstrates that a single prompted MLLM can replace multiple aspect-specific evaluators while achieving equal or superior accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the two dominant paradigms in AIGV evaluation—off-the-shelf models with heuristic rules (VBench, EvalCrafter) and human-annotated specialized evaluators (VideoScore, LGVQ, AIGV-Assessor)—by repurposing pretrained MLLMs as zero-shot, prompt-driven unified evaluators across 15 aspects. Conceptually, it extends the image-quality-assessment line of work (Q-Bench, Q-Align) that showed MLLM token probabilities can serve as quality signals, but generalizes it from single images to video and from a few fixed aspects to an open, aspect-agnostic formulation. The benchmark itself (UVE-Bench) fills a gap left by prior datasets that cover only 2–5 aspects, use outdated VGMs, or provide only absolute ratings rather than pairwise preferences.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical, scalable path to AIGV evaluation that avoids the cost of per-aspect human annotation and model retraining, which is critical as VGMs evolve rapidly. However, the persistent 30+ point gap between SOTA MLLMs and humans in pairwise comparison—especially on temporal aspects like motion naturalness and appearance consistency—highlights that current MLLMs cannot yet replace human evaluators for fine-grained video quality assessment. Open questions include whether improved video encoders (capturing richer temporal dynamics), automatic curation of quality-graded video pairs for training, or incorporating visual generative losses into MLLM training can close this gap. The benchmark itself will likely become a standard reference for tracking progress in unified AIGV evaluation.
