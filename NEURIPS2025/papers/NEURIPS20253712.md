## Problem

Current RL post-training methods for VLMs, particularly GRPO, always generate full-length reasoning traces before answering, incurring substantial token overhead even for trivially easy questions. No prior work has studied whether a model should reason at all ('when to think') as a distinct, trainable skill separate from reasoning capability itself ('how to think'). This paper identifies that ~52% of answers remain correct without any explicit reasoning, yet models default to conservative full-thought generation even when prompted to skip.

## Input / Output

The model receives an RGB image and a text query (e.g., a counting question, geometry problem, or GUI navigation instruction) and produces a structured response in the format `<think>T<answer>S</answer>`, where T is either a full reasoning trace or an empty delimiter (`\n\n`), and S is the final answer (a number for counting/math, or a JSON action dict with type, value, and normalized [x,y] coordinates for agent tasks). The critical design choice is that the empty-thought format is a first-class output option, not a failure mode, and the model learns to select between the two modes adaptively.

## Method

TON (Think-or-Not) is a two-stage training framework. Stage 1 is SFT with 'thought dropout': reasoning traces in the training data are randomly replaced with empty `\n\n` at a fixed probability (default 50%), teaching the model that skipping reasoning is a valid output format. Thought data is generated via a 'reverse thinking' prompt where the base model itself produces reasoning given the image, question, and ground-truth answer, avoiding dependence on external APIs. Stage 2 is standard GRPO where the model freely explores the think-or-not decision; the key mechanism is that empty-thought samples introduce significant variation in the latent response distribution π_θ(o_i|v,q), enhancing the diversity of the importance-ratio terms α and β in the clipped objective, rather than relying on advantage-space diversity as in DAPO. Reward design uses binary discrete matching for categorical/numeric answers and tolerance-based continuous matching for spatial coordinates, summed with a shared format reward. The SFT stage is essential—prompting alone fails because models never spontaneously produce skip-thought outputs, leaving the RL reward signal at zero.

## Results

On CLEVR (3B), TON achieves 98.5% accuracy with only 28 average completion tokens, compared to 93.5% and 227 tokens for vanilla GRPO. On GeoQA, TON improves accuracy by 14 points (3B: 51% vs 37%) and 17 points (7B: 61% vs 44%) over GRPO while reducing completion length by 65–90%. On the AITZ agent benchmark, TON matches GRPO's step-level accuracy (0.75 type / 0.59 exact for 3B) while cutting task-level output length from 3.7K to 0.9K tokens (75% reduction). Training time is also reduced, e.g., 57 min vs 1h44m on CLEVR-3B.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is vanilla GRPO (from DeepSeekMath [5]), which always generates full reasoning traces before answering. TON wins on both accuracy and efficiency across every benchmark: on CLEVR-3B it gains +5.0 accuracy while cutting completion length by 87%; on GeoQA it gains +14 (3B) and +17 (7B) accuracy with 65–90% length reduction. On the AITZ agent task, TON matches GRPO's step-level type and exact accuracy for the 3B model (0.75/0.59 vs 0.74/0.59) and substantially outperforms it for the 7B model (0.69/0.45 vs 0.65/0.23 exact), while reducing task-level output length by ~75%. On the OOD Super-CLEVR benchmark, TON also surpasses GRPO (62.79% vs 51.9%), suggesting the selective-reasoning policy generalizes better than always-thinking. The paper also shows that simply prompting the model to skip reasoning (hybrid prompt) fails to produce any skip-thought outputs, confirming that the SFT thought-dropout stage is essential and cannot be replaced by prompt engineering alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on GRPO (DeepSeekMath) as its RL backbone, extending it with a binary 'think-or-not' decision that prior GRPO variants (DAPO, etc.) do not consider. It departs from the broader chain-of-thought and reasoning-intensive RL literature, which focuses on *how* or *how long* to reason, by instead asking *whether* to reason at all. The 'thought dropout' SFT stage is a minimal intervention that injects the skip-thought format as prior knowledge, distinguishing TON from prompt-based or reward-penalty approaches to controlling reasoning length (e.g., long2short, token-budget-aware policies).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 90% completion-length reduction with equal or improved accuracy (up to +17% on GeoQA) offers a practical efficiency win for deploying VLMs in latency-sensitive or cost-constrained settings, and the 'free-lunch' effect—where shorter reasoning outperforms longer trajectories—challenges the assumption that more thinking is always better. However, the evaluation is confined to 3B/7B models and relatively structured benchmarks (synthetic counting, middle-school geometry, GUI navigation), leaving open whether the approach transfers to open-ended, multi-step reasoning at scale. The finding that prompting alone fails to induce skip behavior (requiring explicit SFT format training) is a useful practical lesson, but the method's applicability to proprietary or much larger models remains untested.
