## Problem

LLMs still struggle with complex multi-step logical reasoning, and existing synthetic data generation methods suffer from two key limitations: they produce simplistic reasoning patterns with limited rule types and shallow depth, and they instantiate logical symbols with entities that lack contextual or conceptual relevance, weakening model generalization. This paper addresses both gaps by generating logic trees with greater structural complexity and grounding them in coherent real-world scenarios.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253802/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a randomly generated root formula and a library of 190 first-order and propositional logic rules as symbolic inputs, then uses an LLM (e.g., GPT-4) to instantiate the resulting trees into natural language. The output is a set of 13.8k training instances, each structured as a premise set (natural language statements from leaf nodes), a conclusion with a True/False/Uncertain answer label, and a step-by-step natural language reasoning chain. Each of the 5,000 generated logic trees (depths 2–15) is instantiated into 3 thematically diverse scenarios, and 8.73% of samples are filtered out by an automatic logical-consistency check before training.

## Method

LogicTree constructs symbolic reasoning trees via iterative backward deduction: starting from a root formula, it repeatedly selects a leaf node, identifies applicable logic rules by comparing the AST operator skeleton of the node's formula against each rule's conclusion (structural pattern matching rather than exact formula equality), and expands the leaf into child premise nodes. This mechanism, combined with 190 rules spanning both propositional and first-order logic, yields trees with depths up to 15 and mixed rule types that prior template-based methods cannot produce. A two-stage LLM prompting strategy then instantiates each tree: first, the LLM assigns contextually coherent real-world entities to all leaf-node atomic formulas to form a unified scenario; second, it sequentially translates each intermediate node into a natural language reasoning step within that scenario, guided by the correct symbolic skeleton so the LLM only performs translation rather than autonomous reasoning. Finally, a verification step prompts the LLM to re-extract logical expressions from the instantiated text and performs exact string comparison against the original formulas, filtering inconsistent samples.

## Results

LogicTree achieves an average accuracy of 56.3% on Llama-3.1-8B across five logical reasoning benchmarks (LogicBench, LogiQA2.0, FOLIO, BBH-Logic, AGIEval), representing a 9.4% absolute improvement over the vanilla model (46.9%) and a 5.5% gain over the strongest baseline (LogicAsker, 52.0%). Gains are consistent across model families and scales: +4.5% on Qwen2.5-7B, +3.1% on Mistral-7B-v0.3, and +2.9% on Llama-3.1-70B. The largest per-benchmark improvement is +13.9% on BBH-Logic (Llama-3.1-8B), and LogicTree also achieves notable gains on the particularly challenging AGIEval-AR benchmark (+5.8% on Llama-3.1-8B).

## Baselines & Comparisons

LogicTree consistently outperforms all three baselines across every model backbone and benchmark. Compared to PARARULE (template-based deduction with random entities), LogicTree gains +5.5% avg on Llama-3.1-8B and +4.4% on Mistral-7B-v0.3, with the largest per-benchmark margin of +10.8% on BBH-Logic (Llama-3.1-8B). Against LogicAsker (atomic rule evaluation with targeted samples), LogicTree leads by +4.3% avg on Llama-3.1-8B and +2.8% on Llama-3.1-70B, showing that complex multi-step reasoning trees provide substantially more benefit than atomic rule practice. Versus FLD×2 (systematic rule combination with WorldNet instantiation), LogicTree achieves +5.6% avg on Llama-3.1-8B and +1.7% on Qwen2.5-7B, confirming that LLM-based realistic scenario instantiation surpasses dictionary-based entity substitution. Notably, on the hardest benchmark AGIEval-AR, LogicTree's advantage over the best baseline is most pronounced (+3.4% over LogicAsker on Llama-3.1-8B), suggesting that instantiated multi-step reasoning is particularly valuable for complex analytical reasoning tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of synthetic logical reasoning data generation (PARARULE, FLD×2, LogicAsker) by replacing template-based translation and random entity substitution with a backward-deduction procedure that constructs complex first-order logic trees via structural pattern matching (AST isomorphism), followed by a two-stage LLM instantiation that produces contextually coherent real-world scenarios. The key conceptual departure is that rule applicability is determined by structural pattern matching rather than exact formula identity, enabling the integration of propositional and first-order logic rules within a single tree (190 rules, up to 15 steps) in a way prior concatenation-based methods could not. The two-stage LLM prompting strategy (scenario instantiation then step-by-step reasoning translation) positions the LLM as a translator guided by a correct symbolic skeleton rather than an autonomous reasoner, distinguishing it from prompt-based reasoning methods like SymbCoT or Logic-of-Thought.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 9.4% average improvement on Llama-3.1-8B, with particularly large gains on multi-step tasks (d4, d5 on Multi-LogiEval) where baselines degrade sharply, suggests that complexity and contextual grounding in synthetic data are critical for scaling reasoning depth. The ablation finding that a single instantiation scenario can perform worse than no instantiation at all highlights a non-obvious overfitting risk to specific fact relationships, reinforcing the need for scenario diversity. A key limitation is that LogicTree cannot integrate other reasoning paradigms (commonsense, analogical) into the generated data, and the approach relies on LLMs for instantiation, introducing a dependency on the quality of the instantiation model. Open questions include whether the structural pattern matching mechanism can be extended to non-monotonic or probabilistic logics, and whether the 13.8k-instance dataset is sufficient for larger models or whether scaling the tree depth/breadth further yields diminishing returns.
