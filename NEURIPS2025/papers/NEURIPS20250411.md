## Problem

Prior comparisons of vision-language models (VLMs) and their base language models (LMs) on text-only tasks have yielded inconsistent or marginal differences, and no work has specifically examined whether VL training alters a model's taxonomic knowledge. This paper asks a targeted question: does VL training fundamentally restructure the underlying taxonomic knowledge in LMs (H1), or does it merely improve the model's ability to deploy that (largely unchanged) knowledge in task contexts that require it (H2)?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250411/cropped_page4_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

Models receive purely text-based inputs: programmatically generated scene descriptions (converted from GQA scene graphs via hand-crafted templates) paired with yes/no questions where a target concept is replaced by a hypernym (positive) or a non-hypernym concept (negative). Outputs are constrained yes/no token predictions, scored with three taxonomic-sensitivity metrics—Overall Accuracy, Conditional Accuracy, and Hierarchical Consistency—that jointly evaluate correctness on both positive and negative samples and condition higher-level performance on lower-level success. Representational analyses additionally yield RSA Spearman correlations between model spaces and WordNet, layerwise odds ratios from logistic regressions on contextualized cosine-similarity differences, and PCA projections of last-layer hidden states with SVM separability scores.

## Method

The paper constructs two datasets from GQA and WordNet: TaxonomiGQA (a text-only QA benchmark where scene-graph objects are substituted with hypernyms or non-hypernyms, yielding 148K instances across 126 hypernym chains) and TAXOMPS (1,380 direct taxonomic-judgment questions of the form 'Is it true that a C1 is a C2?'). Seven VLM-LM minimal pairs are compared behaviorally on both datasets, then subjected to three representational probes: (1) RSA on Park et al.'s hierarchically-transformed unembedding spaces to test whether taxonomic knowledge geometry differs, (2) static-embedding cosine-similarity contrasts between hypernym-hyponym and hypernym-non-hyponym pairs, and (3) contextualized-representation analyses (layerwise logistic regression on similarity differences and PCA of full-question hidden states with SVM separability) to test whether deployment differs. The key finding is a dissociation: VLMs and LMs show near-identical taxonomic knowledge (RSA ≥ 0.95, comparable TAXOMPS accuracy) yet VLMs exhibit substantially better linear separability of taxonomic vs. non-taxonomic question representations and a stronger positive association between contextualized hypernym-hyponym similarity and correct answers, supporting H2 over H1. A preliminary linear mixed-effects analysis further links VLM performance gains to visual similarity between members of hypernym-hyponym categories, modulated by the visual cohesion of the hypernym.

## Results

On TaxonomiGQA (a text-only QA task requiring taxonomic reasoning), 6 out of 7 VLM-LM minimal pairs show the VLM outperforming its LM counterpart, with the Qwen2.5-Instruct vs. Qwen2.5-VL-Instruct pair showing the largest gap, especially on stricter metrics (Conditional Accuracy and Hierarchical Consistency). On TAXOMPS (direct taxonomic membership judgments), most pairs perform similarly and well, indicating VL training does not alter basic taxonomic knowledge. Representational analyses confirm this: RSA scores between VLM and LM unembedding spaces are all ≥ 0.95, and embedding similarity differences (Δ) between hypernym-hyponym and negative pairs show no significant VLM advantage. A preliminary visual-similarity analysis finds that hypernym-hyponym visual similarity significantly predicts VLM Conditional Accuracy (b = 0.52, SE = 0.19, p < .01) but not LM accuracy (b = 0.23, p = 0.18).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares seven VLM-LM minimal pairs on two tasks: TaxonomiGQA (taxonomic QA) and TAXOMPS (direct taxonomic judgment). On TaxonomiGQA, VLMs win in 6 of 7 pairs (Llama-3.1→MLlama-3.2, Llama-3.1-I→MLlama-3.2-I, Mistral→Llava-Next, Qwen2→Molmo-D, Qwen2-I→Llava-OV, Qwen2.5-I→Qwen2.5-VL-I), with the Qwen2.5 pair showing the most salient advantage on Conditional Accuracy and Hierarchical Consistency. The sole exception is Vicuna vs. Llava-1.5, where the LM outperforms the VLM. On TAXOMPS, all pairs except Llama-3.1 vs. MLlama-3.2 perform comparably and at high accuracy, confirming that VL training does not change underlying taxonomic membership knowledge. On the supplementary Rodriguez et al. property-inheritance dataset, VLMs outperform LMs in 5 of 7 pairs (e.g., Qwen2 vs. Molmo-D: 0.60 vs. 0.81; Vicuna vs. Llava-1.5: 0.50 vs. 0.74), further supporting the deployment-over-knowledge interpretation. Representational analyses (RSA, embedding Δ, PCA separability) consistently show VLMs and LMs share nearly identical taxonomic structure (RSA ≥ 0.95) but differ in how taxonomic relations are deployed in task contexts, with VLM question representations being more linearly separable for hypernym vs. non-hypernym substitutions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two research threads: (1) comparisons of VLMs and their base LMs on text-only tasks, which have yielded mixed or negative results (FLAVA, Molmo vs. Qwen, Iki & Aizawa), and (2) evaluation of taxonomic knowledge in language models (Misra et al.'s COMPS, Rodriguez et al.'s property inheritance, Park et al.'s hierarchical unembedding geometry). The key departure is the explicit minimal-pair design—comparing 7 VLM-LM pairs that share the same base LM—applied specifically to taxonomic reasoning, a domain where prior VLM-LM comparisons had not been conducted. The representational analyses build directly on Park et al.'s method for extracting hierarchical structure from unembedding spaces and on the linear representation hypothesis framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The clean separation between taxonomic knowledge possession (unchanged by VL training) and its deployment (improved by VL training) has practical implications for post-training strategies: if a model's weakness is deployment rather than knowledge, additional encyclopedic data won't help, but diverse contextual supervision will. The preliminary finding that visual similarity between hypernym-hyponym members predicts VLM advantage opens a concrete research direction for understanding *why* vision helps. Key limitations include the absence of causal evidence linking representations to behavior, the confound of additional text data in VL training, and the restriction to linearly separable taxonomic distinctions.
