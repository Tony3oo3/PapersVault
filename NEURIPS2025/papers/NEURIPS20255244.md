## Problem

Equivariant neural network theory has been restricted to static (time-independent) transformations and feed-forward architectures, leaving a gap for sequence models that must handle time-parameterized symmetries such as visual motion. The paper proves that even group-equivariant RNNs (G-RNNs) are generally not equivariant with respect to such flows: their hidden states fail to transform in a geometrically structured way for moving stimuli, breaking the equivariance guarantee that static group equivariance provides.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255244/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes grayscale image sequences (28×28 for Flowing MNIST with two simultaneously moving digits, 32×32 for KTH action clips with 16 timesteps) where objects undergo translation or rotation flows at discrete velocities. For next-step prediction, it outputs the next 10 frames (extended to 70 for length generalization); for action recognition, it outputs one of 6 class labels. The hidden state is lifted to a V×G product space (flow-generator index × static group element), and a max-pool over the V dimension precedes the output decoder.

## Method

The FERNN lifts the RNN hidden state to a product space V×G, where V is a discrete set of flow generators (vector fields) and G is the static symmetry group (e.g., SE(2) or Z²). The key architectural change is a recurrence relation that applies the instantaneous one-step flow element ψ₁(ν) at each ν-position of the lifted hidden state, effectively creating a bank of G-RNNs that each flow autonomously according to their own vector field. When an input arrives undergoing a specific flow with generator ν̂, the construction shifts the V-dimension by ν̂ and applies a corresponding G-dimension transformation, so the model processes the moving input as if it were stationary in the matching channel. The input is lifted via a trivial copy of the group-lifting convolution across all ν values, and the flow convolution operates over V×G with weight sharing. The paper proves by induction that this construction is exactly flow equivariant (Theorem 4.1), and in practice truncates V to a finite grid while optionally disabling V-mixing to limit boundary errors.

## Results

On Flowing MNIST next-step prediction, FERNN-V₂ᵀ achieves a test MSE of 1.5e-4, over 50× lower than the G-RNN baseline (8.1e-3), and FERNN-V₄ᴿ reaches 6.1e-4 vs. 4.0e-3 for G-RNN on rotation flows. On Moving KTH action recognition, FERNN-V₂ᵀ achieves 71.6% test accuracy, outperforming G-RNN (66.5%), G-RNN+ (63.9%), and 3D-CNN (62.6%) with matched parameter counts (~242K). Additionally, FERNNs exhibit near-perfect length generalization (predicting 70 steps from 10-step training) and zero-shot velocity generalization to unseen flow generators, where G-RNNs fail entirely.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FERNNs dramatically outperform G-RNNs on both tasks. On Flowing MNIST translation flows, FERNN-V₂ᵀ reduces MSE from 8.1e-3 to 1.5e-4 (a >50× improvement), and even partial equivariance (FERNN-V₁ᵀ) already achieves 5.3e-4. On Moving KTH, FERNN-V₂ᵀ (71.6%) beats G-RNN (66.5%) by 5.1 points, G-RNN+ (63.9%) by 7.7 points, and 3D-CNN (62.6%) by 9.0 points, all with comparable parameter counts (~242K). The G-RNN+ ablation (which adds the lifted hidden state and max-pooling but replaces the flow action with a learned convolution) confirms that the gains come specifically from the flow-equivariant recurrence, not merely from increased capacity. On length generalization, FERNNs maintain near-constant prediction error up to 70 steps while G-RNNs diverge rapidly beyond the 10-step training horizon. On velocity generalization, FERNNs trained on low velocities (V₁ᵀ) generalize nearly perfectly to higher test velocities (V₂ᵀ), whereas G-RNNs fail to predict out-of-distribution flows at all.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the group equivariant CNN framework (Cohen and Welling, 2016) from static spatial symmetries to time-parameterized symmetries (one-parameter Lie subgroups, or 'flows') in recurrent sequence models. The key conceptual departure is showing that group-equivariant RNNs, despite being equivariant to static transformations, are provably NOT equivariant to motion — a gap that prior equivariant sequence modeling work (e.g., Azari and Erdoğmuş 2022, Nguyen et al. 2023) entirely missed because it only considered constant group elements applied uniformly across timesteps. The FERNN construction mirrors the G-CNN lifting strategy but lifts the hidden state to a product space V×G (flow generators × group elements), with an additional per-step flow action ψ₁(ν) that makes each hidden channel autonomously track its own velocity. The paper also positions itself relative to Lorentz-equivariant networks (which treat spacetime as a 4D block rather than causal sequence processing), equivariant observer theory (hand-crafted, untrained), and biologically inspired models like the Topographic VAE and Neural Wave Machine (which bake in a single velocity rather than a full set of flows).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first formal definition and constructive proof of flow equivariance for sequence models, establishing that autonomous traveling-wave-like dynamics in the hidden state are *necessary* (not merely useful) for structured representation of moving stimuli — a claim with direct resonance to observed traveling waves in visual cortex. The practical limitations are significant: the framework is restricted to constant-velocity flows, scales linearly in the number of flow generators |V|, and incurs truncation errors at the boundaries of the discrete V grid. A steerable or gauge-equivariant analog (analogous to Cohen and Welling 2016b for static groups) would be needed to make this practically scalable, and it remains open whether the construction transfers cleanly to modern sequence architectures like state-space models or transformers. The neuroscience connection — that cortical lateral connections align with Lie-group integral curves — suggests this formalism may capture a genuine computational principle in biological motion processing, but empirical validation in that direction is entirely future work.
