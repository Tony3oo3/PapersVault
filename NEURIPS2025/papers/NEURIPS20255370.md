## Problem

Despite LLMs achieving near-human performance on math and coding, physics reasoning remains severely underdeveloped due to two compounding gaps: (1) a lack of high-quality, large-scale physics training data with balanced difficulty and subject coverage, and (2) evaluation frameworks borrowed from mathematics that systematically misjudge physics answers due to unit conversion, numerical simplification, and approximation. Existing physics benchmarks are small, lack training/test splits, and cover only narrow difficulty ranges, making it impossible to both improve and fairly assess models' physical reasoning.

## Input / Output

For evaluation, the framework consumes a physics problem (bilingual EN/ZH, ~122 tokens average), the model's free-form answer (with LaTeX in \boxed{}), and the ground truth answer, then outputs a binary Correct/Incorrect judgment. The rule-based stage handles symbolic equivalence and predefined unit conversions, while the model-based stage (physics-xVerify, an 8B LoRA-tuned verifier) handles cases the rules miss. For training, the dataset provides 14,568 physics problems with detailed reasoning paths (generated by QwQ-32B via 8-round rejection sampling on 4,000 samples) as SFT targets, producing fine-tuned models that improve on both physics and math benchmarks.

## Method

The core contribution is a dual-track system: a 16,568-sample bilingual physics dataset (8,284 problems from 100+ textbooks, OCR-extracted and expert-verified, spanning 5 domains and 4 difficulty levels) and a Rule+Model evaluation framework. The evaluation first applies math-verify with predefined unit-conversion rules; if the rule judges the answer incorrect, a fine-tuned physics-xVerify (xVerify-8B-I with LoRA rank 16, trained on 918 physics/math equivalent-answer pairs distilled by GPT-4o and human-verified) performs a second check. Only when both rule and model agree the answer is wrong is it marked incorrect, achieving 95.92% accuracy on a human-annotated test set versus 83.34% for the unmodified xVerify. For training, the paper demonstrates that SFT on the PHYSICS dataset with QwQ-32B-generated reasoning paths yields consistent gains across physics benchmarks (e.g., +12.55% on PHYSICS for Qwen2.5-14B) and transfers to math (AIME-2025) and general-domain tasks (GPQA-Diamond, MMLU-Pro).

## Results

On the PHYSICS test set (2,000 problems), the best model o3 (high) achieves 58.90% Hybrid Accuracy, followed by DeepSeek-R1 at 55.30% and QwQ-32B at 53.30%, while most other models remain below 50%. The proposed Rule+Model evaluation framework (physics-xVerify) achieves 95.92% judgment accuracy on a human-annotated evaluation test set, a 12.58% improvement over the best prior method (xVerify at 83.34%). After SFT on the PHYSICS training data, Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct improves from 27.65% to 40.20% on PHYSICS and from 44.93% to 60.35% on GPQA-Physics, outperforming alternative reasoning-enhancement methods (SimpleRL, General-Reasoner, Absolute-Zero) on most physics benchmarks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

For the evaluation framework, physics-xVerify (95.92%) substantially outperforms xVerify (83.34%), Omni-Judge (67.39%), GPT-4o (58.58%), and rule-only baselines (Omni-Math-Rule 34.20%, Rule-Verifier 38.62%), with the 12.58% gain over xVerify coming at negligible additional time cost (4 min 37 s vs. 4 min 28 s). Among evaluated LLMs, o3 (high) leads at 58.90% Hybrid Acc, but even this top model falls far short of its ~90% on AIME-2025, and all models score below 60% on PHYSICS. In the training comparison (Table 9), PHYSICS SFT on Qwen2.5-14B-base achieves 35.13% on PHYSICS and 52.28% on GPQA-Physics, outperforming SimpleRL (31.77%/44.77%), General-Reasoner (30.39%/46.37%), and Absolute-Zero (24.94%/42.40%) on both physics benchmarks, while also matching or exceeding them on MATH-500 (83.22% vs. 81.50%/80.75%/78.87%). On AIME-2025, PHYSICS-14B (18.33%) also leads over all baselines, suggesting positive transfer from physics to advanced math reasoning. Cross-benchmark results (Table 18) confirm that PHYSICS provides a more discriminative evaluation: o3 scores 58.90% on PHYSICS but 85.02% on GPQA, highlighting that existing benchmarks understate the difficulty of broad-spectrum physics reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on two key components from the mathematical reasoning evaluation literature: math-verify (Kydliček, 2024) as the base rule-based verifier, and xVerify-8B-I (Chen, 2025) as the base model-based verifier, which is fine-tuned via LoRA into a physics-specific judge (physics-xVerify). Conceptually, it departs from prior physics benchmarks (GPQA, OlympiadBench, UGPhysics, PHYBench) by providing the first dataset with both a large-scale training split (14,568 samples with reasoning paths) and a balanced test split spanning four difficulty levels and five physics subfields, whereas existing datasets are evaluation-only, narrow in difficulty, or imbalanced in subject coverage. The hybrid Rule+Model evaluation framework is a direct extension of math-oriented verification pipelines, adapted to handle physics-specific challenges like unit conversion and numerical simplification that cause systematic misjudgments in prior frameworks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dataset addresses a clear gap: physics is foundational to real-world understanding yet has received far less attention than math or coding in LLM development, and no prior resource combined large-scale training data with a comprehensive, balanced evaluation benchmark. The finding that even o3 (58.90%) and DeepSeek-R1 (55.30%) score well below their math performance (~90% on AIME) quantifies a substantial and previously underappreciated capability gap. Open questions include whether the ~60% Knowledge Deficit error rate identified in the error analysis can be addressed through targeted physics pre-training or retrieval-augmented approaches, and whether the observed physics-to-math transfer (AIME gains from physics SFT) generalizes to other STEM domains. The evaluation framework's 12.58% accuracy improvement over prior methods also raises the question of how many other domain-specific evaluation biases (beyond units and simplification) remain unaddressed.
