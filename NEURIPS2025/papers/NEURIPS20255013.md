## Problem

While hierarchical learning, explicit subtask planning, and dynamic-programming-based stitching can enable compositional generalization in sequential decision-making, these approaches are often unstable or data-inefficient in real-world robotics. The paper asks whether simply structuring the representation space through a temporal alignment objective—without any explicit planning, hierarchical decomposition, or reinforcement learning—can enable a robot to compose previously learned subtasks into novel long-horizon behaviors specified by language or goal images.

## Input / Output

The policy takes a current RGB observation plus either a goal image or a natural language instruction as conditioning, and outputs a continuous 7-DoF action for a WidowX250 arm at 5 Hz, parameterized as an isotropic Gaussian with fixed variance. During training, future states are sampled at a geometrically-distributed horizon k ~ Geom(1−γ) to form temporal alignment pairs, and language annotations are augmented with 5 rephrased variants per trajectory. At inference, the policy is conditioned on ψ(g) for goal images or ξ(ℓ) for language, with a maximum of 200 timesteps per episode.

## Method

TRA learns three encoders—φ for current states, ψ for future/goal states, and ξ for language via a pretrained CLIP-style encoder—into a shared representation space. The core contribution is a symmetric InfoNCE temporal alignment loss that pulls φ(s_t) toward ψ(s_{t+k}) for geometrically-sampled future horizons, which indirectly aligns intermediate waypoint representations with final goal representations so that a standard behavioral cloning policy can 'stitch' together in-distribution subtask behaviors to reach out-of-distribution composed goals. Crucially, this temporal alignment is purely auxiliary: it is never used for policy extraction or test-time inference, only as a regularizer on the same representations consumed by the BC objective. The paper provides a theoretical bound (Theorem 1) showing that under time-isotropy assumptions, the compositional out-of-distribution imitation error is bounded by the in-distribution error plus a term that vanishes as the horizon ratio α grows, and empirically demonstrates >40% improvement over baselines across 13 real-world tasks and competitive performance on OGBench simulation benchmarks.

## Results

On real-world robot manipulation (BridgeDataV2), TRA achieves >40% average improvement over the best baseline across 13 compositionally-OOD tasks in 4 evaluation scenes. Specifically, TRA completes 88.9% of task-concatenation tasks (Set B), 83.3% of semantic-generalization tasks (Set C), and 60% of dependency tasks (Set D) with language instructions, versus best baselines of 30%, 43.3%, and 33.3% respectively. TRA outperforms the offline RL baseline AWR by 45% on instruction following and 25% on goal reaching. On OGBench simulation, TRA achieves 60.7% on antmaze-medium-stitch and 46.1% on humanoidmaze-medium-stitch, outperforming all non-RL baselines on compositional (stitch) environments.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TRA substantially outperforms GRIF [27] (its direct predecessor without temporal alignment) on all compositional task sets: e.g., 0.90 vs 0.20 on 'spoons on plates' and 0.80 vs 0.00 on 'corn+banana+sushi in bowl' (language), with GRIF achieving near-zero success on most multi-step tasks. Against GCBC/LCBC [13], TRA wins by large margins on Sets B–D (e.g., 88.9% vs 30% on Set B language tasks), while both methods perform comparably on the simpler one-step Set A tasks. TRA beats the generalist Octo policy [74] on compositional tasks (60% vs 33.3% on Set D language), though Octo occasionally matches TRA on individual one-step tasks. Against the offline RL baseline AWR [76], TRA outperforms by 45% on instruction following and 25% on goal reaching overall, with AWR frequently stopping after one subtask. On OGBench, TRA outperforms CRL [78] and all other non-RL baselines (GCBC, GCIQL, GCIVL) on the five stitch environments, but traditional offline RL methods (QRL, CRL) still outperform TRA on non-stitch environments like antmaze-large-navigate (82.8% vs 35.4%) and cube-single-noisy (99.3% vs 9.2%). The paper also shows that adding AWR on top of TRA provides no additional benefit and marginally decreases performance, confirming that temporal alignment alone is sufficient for compositional generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

TRA directly extends GRIF (Myers et al., CoRL 2023), which aligns goal-image and language representations via a CLIP-style contrastive loss, by adding a temporal alignment objective that aligns current-state and future-state representations in the same shared space. This departs from the broader line of compositional generalization work that relies on explicit hierarchical planning, VLM-based task decomposition, or offline RL 'stitching' (e.g., AWR, CRL), which the authors argue are unstable or data-inefficient in real-world robotics. The key conceptual distinction from prior contrastive representation methods (VIP, R3M, GRIF) is that TRA's temporal alignment is used purely as an auxiliary loss to structure the representation space, without extracting a value function or using the learned representations for policy extraction at test time.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that a simple time-contrastive alignment loss enables compositional generalization without any RL, planning, or hierarchical structure—challenges the prevailing assumption that stitching long-horizon behaviors requires a learned value function or explicit subtask decomposition. This opens a path toward more data-efficient and stable compositional robot learning, particularly relevant as the field scales toward VLM/VLA-based policies. Limitations include the Gaussian policy's inability to handle multimodal behaviors, degradation when BC cannot solve the base task at all, and evaluation restricted to a single tabletop manipulation platform with small trial counts (5–10 per task). Open questions include whether the approach transfers to more complex architectures (diffusion policies, transformers) and how it interacts with safety constraints in human-robot interaction.
