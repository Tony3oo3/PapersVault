## Problem

Instruction fine-tuning data for LLMs is expensive to produce, and existing augmentation methods (prompt templates, self-chat, lexical noise) lack authentic interaction structure, producing data that mismatches real-world instructional scenarios. No prior work has leveraged multi-agent simulated teaching with pedagogically grounded scenarios to enrich problem-solving datasets with diverse reasoning traces.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254904/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes existing instruction QA datasets (10K from Orca-Math-200K, 10K from ProcQA, 9K from OpenHermes 2.5) and produces BOOST-QA, a 19K-sample augmented dataset in ShareGPT multi-turn dialogue format. Each augmented sample concatenates multi-agent utterances (teacher explanations, student errors, corrections, debate turns, or analogous problem solutions) into a single training sequence, with correctness verified at 95.9% by a Qwen2.5-32B judge.

## Method

MASTER (MACLASS) simulates three pedagogical scenarios using LLMs of deliberately different sizes and temperatures as agents: (1) Error Correction, where a Qwen2.5-0.5B student (temp 0.8) generates flawed solutions that a Qwen2.5-14B teacher (temp 0.2) diagnoses and a corrected student resolves; (2) Debate, where two 7B students (temp 0.6) and one 14B summarizer (temp 0.2) discuss a problem over multiple rounds; (3) Analogical Reasoning, where allMiniLM-L6-v2 embeddings retrieve semantically similar questions via cosine similarity, and two rounds of teacher-student dialogue on the paired questions are concatenated. The key insight is that varying cognitive levels inject structured noise into the gradient, smoothing the loss landscape and encouraging the model to learn error-avoidance patterns. The resulting ShareGPT-format data is used for LoRA fine-tuning (rank-adapted, 2 epochs, lr 1e-4) of 7B base models (LLaMA-3, Qwen2.5, Mistral).

## Results

On LLaMA3-8B-base, BOOST-QA achieves an average score of 51.26 across seven benchmarks, outperforming the original data (45.98), TAGCOS (47.93), CoT-fine (41.11), RandomAug (38.77), and SpellingAug (29.21). The most dramatic gain is on MMLU-PRO-MATH, where BOOST-QA reaches 27.39 versus 14.58 (RandomAug) and 14.36 (CoT-fine), more than doubling the next-best baseline. On HumanEval (Pass@1), BOOST-QA scores 50.61, surpassing TAGCOS (42.68) by over 8 points. The ablation study (Table 4) confirms that all three pedagogical scenarios (Error Correction, Debate, Analogical Reasoning) are necessary: any single or pair of scenarios underperforms the original data, while the full combination yields the best results.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BOOST-QA (MASTER) achieves the highest average score (51.26) among all methods, beating TAGCOS (47.93) by 3.33 points and the original data (45.98) by 5.28 points. The advantage is most pronounced on MMLU-PRO-MATH (27.39 vs. 14.58 for RandomAug, the next best) and HumanEval (50.61 vs. 42.68 for TAGCOS), indicating that multi-agent simulated teaching particularly strengthens complex mathematical reasoning and code generation. However, TAGCOS outperforms BOOST-QA on MATH (26.50 vs. 23.90) and SCI-Q (84.00 vs. 80.10), suggesting that gradient-based data selection retains an edge on certain factual and science-reasoning tasks. Both RandomAug and SpellingAug (simple lexical perturbations) consistently underperform the original data across most benchmarks, with SpellingAug causing severe degradation (e.g., MMLU drops from 48.13 to 24.21). CoT-fine also trails the original data on average (41.11 vs. 45.98), indicating that simply aggregating chain-of-thought annotations without structured pedagogical interaction is insufficient for robust improvement.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the teacher-student metaphor from knowledge distillation and the multi-agent LLM interaction paradigm (Generative Agents, social simulation) into a data augmentation pipeline for instruction fine-tuning. Unlike prior augmentation methods (EDA, WizardLM, Self-Instruct, CoT Collection) that rely on predefined prompts or self-dialogue without structured interaction, MASTER introduces pedagogically grounded multi-agent protocols—error correction, debate, and analogical reasoning—as controlled conversational mechanisms. It also departs from prior multi-agent work, which has focused on social dynamics or goal-aligned collaboration, by using agent interactions specifically to enrich existing QA datasets rather than to simulate human behavior or solve tasks directly.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a principled way to transform any instruction dataset into richer multi-turn interaction data, and the ablation revealing that individual pedagogical scenarios actually degrade performance (all three are needed jointly) is a useful design insight. However, the evidence is limited: experiments use only 19K samples, models under 8B parameters, no error bars, and results are inconsistent across base models (e.g., TAGCOS outperforms BOOST-QA on MATH and SCI-Q; Mistral-7B drops on MBPP and SCI-Q). Open questions include whether the approach scales to larger instruction-tuned models, whether purpose-trained teacher/student agents would improve data quality, and whether gains extend to commonsense and reading-comprehension tasks where the paper itself notes limited improvement.
