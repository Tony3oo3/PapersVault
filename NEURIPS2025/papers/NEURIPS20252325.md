## Problem

When prediction algorithms serve user populations that self-select based on prediction quality, repeated retraining creates a feedback loop that reshapes both the model and the user base over time. The long-term evolutionary outcomes—which groups dominate, coexist, or are driven to extinction—were not formally characterized, leaving system designers without tools to anticipate or steer these population-level consequences of learning.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a population state vector p on the (K−1)-simplex (encoding group proportions), K fixed group-specific data distributions D_k, and a learning algorithm A, and produces a K-dimensional fitness vector F(p) of expected per-group accuracies under a classifier trained on the mixture D_p. The output is not a single prediction but a population game whose equilibria and induced dynamics (e.g., replicator flow) determine long-term group survival. The unusual aspect is that the input state p is itself the output of the previous dynamical step, closing the loop between learning and population evolution.

## Method

The paper defines evolutionary prediction games, a class of population games where each group's evolutionary fitness equals its expected marginal accuracy under a classifier retrained on the current mixture distribution. The key theoretical tool is a convexity argument: the overall accuracy acc_p(h_p) is a pointwise maximum of linear functions (one per group), making it a potential function whose local extrema correspond to Nash equilibria. This yields Theorem 1 (oracle classifiers induce competitive exclusion with stable single-group equilibria) and Theorem 2 (surrogate losses like hinge loss can create stable mutualistic coexistence by introducing complementary biases). A stabilization mechanism (Proposition 2) reweights training examples as if the population were at the reflected state 2p*−p, inverting the natural repulsion from unstable mixed equilibria into attraction.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with horizontal-flip groups, the evolutionary prediction game exhibits an unstable mutualistic coexistence equilibrium at 93.5±0.1% overall accuracy, exceeding the two stable single-group dominance equilibria at 92.6±0.1%; the proposed stabilization method (Prop. 2) successfully converts this into a stable coexistence. On MNIST with complementary label noise, an overparameterized CNN achieves a stable coexistence equilibrium at 80.4±0.2% test accuracy (vs. an 84% theoretical upper bound imposed by the noise), with 98.7±0.1% training accuracy confirming interpolation. On ACSIncome (3-state income classification), replicator dynamics drive the NY group to near-extinction (≤1% proportion by t≈316), reducing apparent accuracy disparity from ~2% to ~0.2%—illustrating how static fairness metrics can mask prior group elimination.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper is primarily theoretical and does not benchmark against competing methods in the traditional sense; the three experiments serve as empirical validations of the paper's own theoretical predictions (Theorem 1, Theorem 2, Proposition 2) rather than comparisons against external baselines. The CIFAR-10 experiment confirms the oracle-classifier prediction (Theorem 1) that retraining induces competitive exclusion with unstable mixed equilibria, and then demonstrates that the proposed reweighting stabilization (Prop. 2) can flip the unstable coexistence into a stable one without sacrificing accuracy. The MNIST experiment validates the finite-data/interpolation mechanism (Appendix D.7) by showing that complementary label noise across groups naturally produces stable mutualism in an overparameterized network. The ACSIncome experiment illustrates the fairness limitation (Prop. 1) by showing that a system can appear fair at equilibrium only because a group has been driven out, a phenomenon not captured by static fairness audits. No external baseline methods (e.g., standard fairness-constrained training, performative prediction algorithms) are directly compared.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper introduces evolutionary prediction games, a novel formalization that bridges evolutionary game theory (population games, replicator dynamics) and performative prediction. It builds on Sandholm's population game framework by defining fitness as prediction accuracy, and on the performative prediction literature (Perdomo et al.) by providing a low-dimensional structural assumption—user self-selection via group mixtures—that makes the otherwise infinite-dimensional stateful performative setting tractable. The paper also extends the long-term fairness literature (Hashimoto et al., Liu et al.) by adding a counterfactual dimension: fairness measured at equilibrium may mask groups that were historically driven to extinction. Unlike prior performative prediction work that seeks general convergence guarantees, this paper characterizes the *type* of equilibria (competitive exclusion vs. coexistence) and the mechanisms that select between them.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that idealized oracle retraining drives competitive exclusion while practical learning imperfections (surrogate losses, finite data, overparameterization) can enable stable mutualistic coexistence—reframes how ML practitioners should think about repeated training in social settings. The fairness implication is particularly important: a system can appear to satisfy accuracy equality precisely because disadvantaged groups have been eliminated, making static fairness audits potentially misleading. Key open questions include whether results extend to overlapping or fluid group memberships, exogenous user inflows, non-accuracy-maximizing objectives, and dynamics-aware learners that explicitly account for population feedback. The framework also raises the question of whether 'conservation' interventions (analogous to ecological conservation) should be a standard design consideration for ML systems serving heterogeneous populations.
