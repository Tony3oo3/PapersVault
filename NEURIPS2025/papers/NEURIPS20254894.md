## Problem

Hypothesis testing in regulatory settings (e.g., FDA drug approval) is typically analyzed under the assumption that agents passively submit fixed evidence, ignoring that firms strategically decide whether to participate and how many samples to collect based on the p-value threshold. Prior work by Bates et al. modeled this as a Stackelberg game but restricted to binary effectiveness and fixed trial costs, and focused only on Type I errors. This paper asks: how do strategic participation and sample-size choices reshape the principal's error landscape, and what p-value threshold minimizes a general combination of Type I and Type II errors?

## Input / Output

The model takes as input a public effectiveness distribution q over [0,1], a baseline effectiveness μ_b, revenue R, fixed cost c₀, per-sample cost c, and a p-value threshold α chosen by the principal. The agent's true effectiveness μ₀ is private (drawn from q). Outputs include the agent's optimal sample size n_{μ₀}(α) and participation decision, the participation threshold μ_τ(α), the critical p-value α̂ (where μ_τ(α̂)=μ_b), and the resulting false positive and false negative rates as functions of α. The pass probability is computed via a normal approximation to the binomial, and all key quantities are computable in O(log n_max) or O(log²(1/ε)·log n_max) time via binary search over the sample and belief spaces.

## Method

The paper formulates a Stackelberg game in which the principal commits to a p-value threshold α, and each agent with private effectiveness μ₀~q best-responds by choosing whether to participate and how many samples n to collect, maximizing expected revenue R·Pass(α,μ₀,n) minus cost c₀+cn. The key technical contribution is a piecewise monotonicity characterization: a critical p-value α̂ (defined by μ_τ(α̂)=μ_b) partitions the α-space into two regimes where the overall false positive is identically zero for α<α̂ and non-decreasing for α≥α̂, while the overall false negative is non-increasing on both sides. This is established through three lemmas: (i) the agent's optimal n is found by partitioning the utility into at most three convex/concave intervals via a quadratic in √n, solved by binary search; (ii) the participation threshold μ_τ(α) is monotone decreasing in α; and (iii) the pass probability for effective agents (μ₀≥μ_b) is non-decreasing in α even under strategic sample-size adjustment, proven via the Implicit Function Theorem decomposing the total derivative into direct and indirect effects. The principal's optimal α* is shown to satisfy α*≥α̂, and α̂ is computable in O(log²(1/ε)·log n_max) time. Empirical validation uses public cost/revenue data for oncology, cardiovascular, and vaccine drugs to show that the FDA's standard α=0.05 aligns with median revenue thresholds in these markets.

## Results

The paper's central theoretical result (Theorem 4.3) shows that the principal's optimal p-value α* satisfies α* ≥ α̂, where α̂ is the critical p-value at which the participation threshold equals baseline effectiveness (μ_τ(α̂) = μ_b). The false positive rate is exactly 0 for all α < α̂ and non-decreasing for α ≥ α̂ (Theorem 4.1), while the false negative rate is non-increasing on both sides of α̂ (Theorem 4.2). In the empirical drug-approval validation, the FDA's standard p-value of 0.05 aligns closely with the median revenue threshold across oncology, cardiovascular, and vaccine categories; for oncology drugs with ~$5B revenue, the critical threshold α̂ falls between 0.1 and 0.2, suggesting the current 0.05 standard is relatively strict and may discourage effective but lower-revenue drugs (e.g., orphan drugs) from participating.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing methods in the traditional ML benchmark sense; it is a theoretical contribution that extends the Stackelberg-game framework of Bates et al. [11] by relaxing three key assumptions: (i) agents strategically choose sample size n rather than using a fixed trial, (ii) a marginal per-sample cost c is introduced alongside the fixed cost c₀, and (iii) the principal minimizes an arbitrary weighted combination of Type I and Type II errors rather than only Type I. The empirical validation serves as the primary 'comparison': the model's predicted critical p-value α̂ is plotted against revenue for three drug categories (oncology, cardiovascular, vaccine) using publicly available cost and revenue data (Table 2), and the FDA's in-practice threshold of α = 0.05 is shown to sit near the median-revenue point in each category, supporting the model's economic realism. The paper also contrasts its Stackelberg-game approach with the contract-theoretic formulation of Bates et al. [14] and the Bayesian Decision Analysis of Isakov et al. [26], noting that its leader-follower structure more naturally captures the regulatory setting where the principal commits to a threshold before agents decide to participate.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the strategic hypothesis testing framework initiated by Bates et al. (2023), who modeled agent responses to regulatory approval thresholds as a Stackelberg game with binary effectiveness and fixed trial costs. The key departures are: (1) agents now strategically choose sample sizes with marginal per-sample costs rather than committing to a fixed trial, and (2) the principal minimizes an arbitrary weighted combination of Type I and Type II errors rather than only false positives. Related work by Shi et al. (2024) studies risk-sensitive agents under general concave utilities, while Bates et al. (2022) approaches the same agent-principal interaction through contract theory rather than Stackelberg equilibrium. The paper also connects to the p-hacking literature by showing that a well-chosen p-value threshold can economically deter participation of ineffective products, reducing the incentive to manipulate data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean, interpretable characterization: the critical p-value α̂ (where the participation threshold equals baseline effectiveness) weakly dominates all lower thresholds, is computable in polylog time, and is agnostic to the effectiveness distribution and loss weights. Empirically, the model predicts that the FDA's standard α=0.05 aligns with median revenue thresholds across oncology, cardiovascular, and vaccine categories, lending credibility to the strategic framing. Key limitations include the assumption that all cost/revenue parameters are publicly known, the normal approximation to the binomial, and the restriction to Bernoulli outcomes. Open questions include how the analysis extends to continuous-valued outcomes, multi-stage trials, or settings where the principal does not know the agent's cost structure, and whether the piecewise monotonic structure persists under more general utility functions.
