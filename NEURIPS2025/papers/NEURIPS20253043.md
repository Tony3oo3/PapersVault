## Problem

GRPO-style RL post-training methods exhibit a distribution-sharpening bias: they reinforce what the model already knows but fail to unlock new reasoning capabilities when the model initially generates no correct solutions. On hard tasks (e.g., MATH level-5), the advantage term vanishes, the KL term dominates, and the model regresses. The paper asks how to obtain effective positive training samples that enable learning in these low-success-rate regimes without relying on costly expert chain-of-thought annotations.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text question as input and produces a chain-of-thought reasoning trace followed by a final answer, evaluated by a verifiable correctness reward. During training, the model is additionally conditioned on the ground-truth answer to generate self-explanations, which serve as positive samples paired against the model's own (typically incorrect) self-generated CoTs. Training is performed on MATH and GSM8K benchmarks using Qwen2.5 and LLaMA-3.2 instruct models (1B–8B scale).

## Method

ExPO identifies two properties of ideal positive samples—being in-distribution under the current policy and providing a positive learning signal (increasing the likelihood of the correct answer relative to other CoTs)—and shows that self-explanations generated by conditioning the model on the ground-truth answer satisfy both, unlike expert CoTs (out-of-distribution) or the model's own incorrect CoTs (no positive signal). The framework is modular: ExP-DPO uses self-explanations as the preferred response in DPO pairs (with an online variant that regenerates explanations as the policy evolves to avoid distributional drift), while ExP-GRPO adds an ExP-SFT term β·log π_θ(c̃, a*|q) to the standard GRPO objective, injecting in-distribution supervision when all sampled responses are incorrect. A theoretical analysis via gradient alignment and a Jensen's-inequality-based lemma formalizes why self-explanations outperform both expert CoTs and standard CoTs as positive samples. Experiments on MATH and GSM8K show ExP-GRPO substantially outperforms GRPO and GRPO+SFT-on-expert-CoT, with the largest gains on the hardest difficulty levels.

## Results

On the MATH test set (Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, pass@4), ExP-GRPO achieves 76% accuracy on Level-4 and 23% on Level-5 questions, compared to 39% and 2% for standard GRPO and 65% and 12% for GRPO with expert CoT SFT. On easier levels (1–3), all methods perform comparably (86–96%), confirming that ExPO's gains are concentrated on the hardest problems. Across model scales (1B–8B, Table 5), ExP-GRPO consistently outperforms both baselines, e.g., 83.2% vs. 79.8% (GRPO SFT-GT-CoT) and 78.1% (GRPO) on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against standard GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), ExP-GRPO wins on every difficulty level, with the most dramatic gains on the hardest questions: +37 percentage points on Level 4 (76% vs. 39%) and +21 points on Level 5 (23% vs. 2%), where GRPO essentially fails to learn. Against GRPO augmented with supervised fine-tuning on expert CoT (GRPO SFT-GT-CoT), ExP-GRPO still leads by +11 pp on Level 4 and +11 pp on Level 5, demonstrating that self-generated explanations provide a stronger learning signal than expert-written chain-of-thought. In the DPO setting (Table 3), ExP-DPO with self-explanation outperforms DPO trained on expert CoT on MATH (60.4% vs. 49.3% online, Qwen2.5-3B) and GSM8K (85.4% vs. 76.3%), though the gap narrows or reverses on LLaMA-3.2-3B for MATH (50.2% vs. 53.6%), suggesting model-dependent sensitivity to in-distribution alignment.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the STaR self-explanation idea (conditioning on the correct answer to generate rationales) from SFT into the RL post-training setting, providing a principled theoretical justification for why it works. It builds directly on GRPO and DPO as base algorithms, adding an in-distribution positive sample term to address their distribution-sharpening limitation on hard problems. The key departure from prior work is the demonstration that expert CoTs—typically assumed to be the gold standard for positive samples—are actually inferior to self-generated explanations for RL training because they violate the in-distribution property, a finding grounded in gradient-alignment analysis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical bottleneck in RL post-training: when a model's initial success rate on a problem is near zero, standard GRPO/DPO methods receive no effective gradient signal and can even regress. ExPO provides a simple, modular fix that works across both reward-based and contrastive frameworks, and the finding that self-explanations outperform expert CoTs challenges the common assumption that higher-quality demonstrations are always better for training. Limitations include evaluation restricted to math reasoning on models up to 8B, a fixed β hyperparameter, and reliance on verifiable rewards. Open questions include whether the in-distribution advantage of self-explanations persists at larger scales, whether the approach generalizes to non-verifiable or multi-step reasoning tasks, and whether annealing β (as suggested) is necessary to prevent over-reliance on imperfect self-generated guidance.
