## Problem

The rapid adoption of LLMs has turned survey paper writing from a labor-intensive synthesis task into a low-effort, high-volume output, flooding preprint platforms with redundant, hallucinated, or superficially comprehensive manuscripts that overwhelm researchers and erode trust in the scientific record. No community-level norms, detection infrastructure, or alternative publication models currently exist to address this 'survey paper DDoS attack.'

## Input / Output

The analysis operates on 10,063 arXiv CS survey papers (2020–2024) identified by title keywords ('survey,' 'review,' 'overview,' 'taxonomy'), scored by an open-source AI-content detector and cross-validated with two independent detectors (DeTeCtive, MAGE). Author metadata is examined for abnormal submission patterns (e.g., >3 surveys per month with <2 collaborators), and citation lists are compared via Jaccard similarity. Outputs span quantitative trend curves, quality-audit metrics, a set of policy recommendations, and a proposed 'Dynamic Live Surveys' framework combining LLM-driven ingestion with expert curation.

## Method

The paper combines three empirical analyses—annual submission volume tracking, AI-content scoring with two independent detectors, and abnormal-author detection—on the 10,063-paper corpus to establish a post-2022 spike correlated with ChatGPT's release. Quality concerns are probed through heuristic detection metrics: scanning for GPT-generated phrases, measuring citation-list Jaccard overlap across surveys on the same topic (finding 60–70% shared references), and computing lexical entropy to flag repetitive, low-diversity text. Beyond detection, the paper proposes a 'Dynamic Live Surveys' architecture in which LLM-based ingestion agents continuously monitor preprint archives and benchmark leaderboards, while domain experts refine taxonomies and resolve conflicts through a version-controlled, branch-and-merge workflow analogous to software development, with periodic citable snapshots for formal attribution.

## Results

The paper analyzes 10,063 CS survey papers on arXiv (2020–2024) and documents a post-2022 spike in both volume and AI-generated content. Key quantitative findings include: papers with high AI-generated content scores roughly doubled from ~3.6% (late 2022) to ~6.2% (end of 2023); citation overlap among same-topic surveys jumped from <40% (pre-2022) to >60% (post-2022), with Jaccard indices rising from <0.3 to >0.5; and semantic similarity of survey abstracts increased from 0.6033 (2022) to 0.8367 (2023), stabilizing at 0.7986 (2024). Among 10 recent ML-topic surveys, any two share on average 60–70% of references, far above the <1% random baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper without a proposed method to benchmark against baselines; the `compares_to` list is empty. The empirical comparisons it does make are temporal (pre-2022 vs. post-2022) and qualitative (AI-generated vs. human-written surveys). The post-2022 cohort shows consistently higher citation overlap (>60% vs. <40%), higher Jaccard similarity (>0.5 vs. <0.3), and higher abstract semantic similarity (0.84 vs. 0.60), all pointing toward homogenized, AI-assisted content. The paper also contrasts AI-generated surveys with human-written ones on structural quality, noting that AI surveys tend to have disordered outlines, lack novel taxonomies, and contain fabricated or irrelevant citations, whereas human surveys provide deeper critical synthesis. No head-to-head method comparison is presented.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper does not extend a specific methodological lineage but rather synthesizes findings from AI-content detection (Kobak et al., DeTeCtive, MAGE), automated survey generation (AutoSurvey, SurveyForge, SurveyAgent), and editorial policy literature to frame a new problem: the 'survey paper DDoS attack.' It repurposes the DDoS analogy from network security to describe the flooding of preprint servers with low-quality AI-generated surveys, and introduces the 'Dynamic Live Surveys' paradigm by transplanting software version-control concepts (branching, merging, contributor metadata) into academic knowledge curation—a framing absent from prior survey-writing or publishing-policy work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a timely and growing threat to scholarly trust: the unchecked proliferation of AI-generated surveys that dilute the literature, distort citation networks ('literature poisoning'), and burden reviewers. The Dynamic Live Surveys proposal is a genuinely novel alternative paradigm, but it remains entirely speculative—no prototype, community trial, or incentive-mechanism analysis is provided. Open questions include whether version-controlled, continuously updated surveys can satisfy tenure and citation requirements, how to prevent the same 'DDoS' problem from migrating into the live-survey format, and whether the proposed policy levers (disclosure mandates, senior-reviewer thresholds, redundancy limits) are enforceable across the decentralized arXiv ecosystem.
