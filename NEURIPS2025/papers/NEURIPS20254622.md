## Problem

Despite extensive architectural work on long-context modeling, there is no principled information-theoretic framework that specifies what a model's internal state must store to capture the multi-token long-range dependencies in natural language. Conventional two-point mutual information, while easier to measure, fails to characterize the full inter-segment dependencies needed for accurate autoregressive prediction, leaving a gap between observed scaling behaviors and actionable architectural requirements.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes token sequences from natural language corpora (PG19, WIKIPEDIA) and model architecture specifications (history state dimensionality, sequence length). It produces power-law scaling exponents for bipartite mutual information (measured via LLMs as density estimators with direct and vCLUB estimators) and derives the L²M condition, which lower-bounds the required history state growth rate as a function of sequence length. A key practical detail is that LLMs model q(w_i | w_{1:i-1}, w_BOS) rather than q(w_i | w_{1:i-1}), requiring n-gram bias corrections for the first two tokens of the Y segment to avoid systematic estimation error.

## Method

The paper establishes that bipartite mutual information I(X_{1:ℓ}; Y_{1:L-ℓ}) between adjacent text segments follows a power-law (sub-volume law) scaling I^BP ~ L^β in natural language, verified empirically using LLaMA 3.1 405B and DeepSeek V3 as density estimators across multiple datasets. It then proves (Theorem 5.2, under discreteness, almost-orthogonal-direction, or Lipschitz assumptions) that a model's capacity to express bipartite MI is upper-bounded by C·dim(z) + log(M), where z is the history state. From this bound, the L²M condition follows: for MI-capability across all sequence lengths, dim(z) must grow at least as fast as L^β. The framework is validated by training GPT2, Mamba, and Mamba2 on synthetic sub-volume-law Gaussian data and PG19, showing that transformers (with linearly growing KV caches) naturally satisfy the single-model L²M condition while SSMs with fixed recurrent states require model size to scale with sequence length, with KL-divergence collapsing onto a single curve as a function of I^BP/dim(z).

## Results

The paper establishes that bipartite mutual information in natural language follows a power-law (sub-volume law) scaling I^BP ~ L^β, verified using LLaMA 3.1 405B on PG19 and WIKIPEDIA datasets across thousands of tokens. On synthetic sub-volume-law Gaussian data, GPT2 maintains consistent per-token KL divergence across sequence lengths (64–16,384), while Mamba and Mamba2 models show increasing KL divergence at longer contexts unless model size is substantially increased. Crucially, KL divergence for Mamba models of varying sizes (50M–1.4B parameters) and sequence lengths collapses onto a single curve when plotted against the ratio I^BP/dim(z), directly confirming the L²M condition. On PG19 (8192-token sequences), Mamba outperforms GPT2 at early token positions but its NLL plateaus at later positions unless model size increases, while GPT2's NLL continues to improve throughout.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares transformer (GPT2) and state-space model (Mamba, Mamba2) architectures on both synthetic and natural language data. On the synthetic Gaussian test, GPT2 maintains stable KL divergence across all sequence lengths because its key-value cache grows linearly with sequence length, naturally satisfying the L²M condition. Mamba [Gu et al., ICLR 2024] and Mamba2 [Dao & Gu, 2024], which use fixed-size recurrent states, exhibit progressively worse KL divergence at longer sequences and require substantially larger model sizes to match GPT2's performance at 4096 tokens. On PG19, Mamba initially outperforms comparable-size GPT2 at early positions but its advantage reverses at later positions due to its fixed history state capacity; the gap narrows only when Mamba's model size (and thus recurrent state dimension) is increased. The paper explicitly notes this does not imply Mamba's architectural inferiority—its linear computational complexity can make larger Mamba models practically more efficient than smaller transformers—but demonstrates that fixed-state architectures fundamentally cannot satisfy the single-model L²M condition without scaling model size with sequence length.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the long-standing study of long-range dependencies in natural language—previously characterized primarily through two-point mutual information decay (Ebeling & Pöschel, 1994) and the Hilberg/sub-volume conjecture (Debowski, 2015)—by shifting the central measure to bipartite mutual information and providing the first rigorous empirical verification of its power-law scaling using LLMs as density estimators. It departs from the neural scaling laws literature (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022), which studies performance at fixed context length, by asking a complementary question: what determines whether an architecture can maintain performance as context length increases. The framework also builds on the predictive information concept (Bialek & Tishby, 1999) and the universal prediction results for transformers (Basu et al., 2023), but derives a concrete, quantitative architectural requirement (the L²M condition) that prior work had not formalized.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The L²M condition provides a principled, information-theoretic lower bound on history state growth that explains why transformers (linearly growing KV cache) naturally handle long contexts while SSMs/RNNs (fixed recurrent state) degrade, and suggests transformers over-provision their state relative to the measured sublinear L^β requirement—pointing toward a concrete target for more efficient architectures. The framework is necessary but not sufficient for effective language modeling, and its extension to hybrid architectures, diffusion-based models, and non-linguistic sequential domains (proteins, code) remains open. A key limitation is that the measured exponent β likely underestimates the true value, meaning the actual architectural requirements may be even more stringent than reported.
