## Problem

Imitation learning for end-to-end driving produces trajectories that appear human-like but can be unsafe, as symmetric losses penalize all deviations equally regardless of safety impact. Existing score-based methods (e.g., Hydra-MDP, WOTE) address safety by regressing per-anchor rule-driven scores, but they decouple score prediction from direct policy distribution optimization, yielding suboptimal driving performance. No prior method directly optimizes the policy distribution while explicitly aligning it with safety-oriented trajectory preferences.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a concatenated front-view image (1024×256 from three forward-facing cameras), a 64×64 BEV LiDAR feature map, and an ego state vector (speed, acceleration, navigation command), all processed by a Transfuser backbone with ResNet-34. The output is a probability distribution over N=8192 discrete anchor trajectories (each consisting of T=8 future waypoints of (x, y, θ)), where anchors are obtained via k-means clustering on human driving data; the final predicted trajectory is the argmax anchor. This discrete anchor-vocabulary formulation (inherited from VADv2) replaces continuous trajectory regression with a categorical policy over a fixed vocabulary.

## Method

DriveDPO is a two-stage framework. Stage 1 (Unified Policy Distillation) fuses human imitation similarity (softmax of negative Euclidean distance to the human trajectory) and rule-based PDMS scores (from NAVSIM forward simulation) into a single target distribution via log transformation and weighted sum (w₁=0.1, w₂=1.0), then trains the policy by minimizing KL divergence to this unified target—directly optimizing the full policy distribution rather than independent per-anchor score heads. Stage 2 (Safety DPO) applies iterative Direct Preference Optimization: K=1024 candidate trajectories are sampled from the current policy, the highest-scoring trajectory is chosen as the preferred sample, and the rejected sample is selected via imitation-based criteria (closest to the human trajectory while having PDMS below a safety threshold τ=0.3), explicitly teaching the policy to suppress human-like but unsafe trajectories. The architecture uses a Transfuser perception backbone, an anchor tokenizer with NeRF-style Fourier positional encoding (L=10 frequency bands), and a cross-attention Transformer decoder where anchor tokens attend to scene tokens before a softmax policy head produces the final distribution.

## Results

On the NAVSIM benchmark (Navtest split, ResNet-34 backbone), DriveDPO achieves a new state-of-the-art PDMS of 90.0, surpassing the best imitation-learning method DiffusionDrive (88.1) by 1.9 and the best score-based method WOTE (88.0) by 2.0. The unified policy distillation stage alone (without DPO) already reaches 88.8 PDMS, while the Safety DPO fine-tuning adds further gains in NC (+0.6), DAC (+0.8), and TTC (+1.2). On the closed-loop Bench2Drive benchmark, DriveDPO achieves a Driving Score of 62.02 and Success Rate of 30.62%, outperforming the strongest baseline TCP (59.90 DS, 30.00% SR).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DriveDPO outperforms all 14 baselines on NAVSIM PDMS. Against the strongest imitation-learning method DiffusionDrive (88.1), it gains +1.9 PDMS, and against the strongest score-based method WOTE (88.0), it gains +2.0 PDMS. It also surpasses PDM-closed (89.1), which uses privileged ground-truth perception, by 0.9 PDMS. Among score-based methods, Hydra-MDP (86.5) and Hydra-MDP++ (86.6) trail by 3.5 and 3.4 PDMS respectively, while ARTEMIS (87.0) and GoalFlow (85.7) are further behind. On Bench2Drive, DriveDPO leads all baselines in Driving Score (62.02 vs. TCP's 59.90) and Success Rate (30.62% vs. 30.00%), while also achieving the highest Driving Efficiency (166.80 vs. VAD's 157.94). The ablation confirms that the unified policy distillation stage alone already beats all baselines (88.8), and the Safety DPO stage provides an additional +1.2 PDMS improvement.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of end-to-end driving policy learning and preference optimization from LLM alignment. It builds on VADv2's anchor vocabulary to discretize the action space, and on the iterative DPO framework (Wei et al., ICML 2024) to perform trajectory-level preference alignment. The key departure from prior work is twofold: (1) unlike score-based methods (Hydra-MDP, WOTE) that regress independent per-anchor scores, DriveDPO fuses imitation similarity and rule-based safety into a single unified policy distribution supervised via KL divergence; (2) unlike pure imitation learning (Transfuser, DiffusionDrive), it explicitly optimizes the policy to suppress human-like-but-unsafe trajectories through DPO fine-tuning with carefully constructed preference pairs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a concrete and underexplored safety gap in end-to-end driving: imitation learning cannot distinguish safe from unsafe trajectories that are geometrically similar to human demonstrations. Achieving 90.0 PDMS (SOTA) with clear gains in collision avoidance (NC) and time-to-collision (TTC) demonstrates that preference-based refinement is a viable path toward safer driving policies. However, the method's reliance on a high-fidelity simulator (NAVSIM) for rule-based scoring and on the predefined PDMS composite metric limits generalizability; open questions include whether more expressive or learned safety evaluators can replace simulator-based scoring, and whether preference structures can be mined self-supervisedly from historical data without simulator access.
