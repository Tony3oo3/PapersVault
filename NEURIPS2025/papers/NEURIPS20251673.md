## Problem

Deep reinforcement learning models are black boxes whose internal decision-making structure is opaque, and prior attempts to visualize their feature spaces relied on t-SNE applied to fixed-scene Atari games, requiring extensive manual annotation and producing unstable, seed-sensitive results. The paper asks whether DRL agents intrinsically organize their feature space by semantic similarity (grouping states that elicit comparable agent behaviors) and proposes a stable, automated, end-to-end method to reveal and exploit this structure without external constraints like bisimulation or contrastive learning.

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB game-state frames from Procgen environments (Ninja, Jumper, FruitBot, CoinRun) that are first encoded by a CNN feature extractor into high-dimensional vectors (e.g., 512-d for Atari-scale features). The FDR network then compresses these to a 2-D embedding, and a vector quantizer with 8 codebook entries assigns each state a discrete cluster code. The code is broadcast to the original feature dimension and added element-wise to the state feature before the PPO policy and value heads, so the agent's action output is conditioned on its semantic cluster. The primary analytical outputs are the 2-D FDR scatter plot, per-cluster mean images, and episode-level cluster-transition sequences used for policy dissection.

## Method

The core contribution is a Semantic Clustering Module (SCM) integrated into PPO (IMPALA architecture) with a joint loss L_total = L_PPO + λ_ctrl(w_FDR·L_FDR + w_VQ-VAE·L′_VQ-VAE). The FDR loss minimizes KL divergence between Student's-t-distribution affinity matrices computed in the high-dimensional and 2-D FDR spaces, preserving pairwise neighborhood geometry (proven in Theorem 1). The modified VQ-VAE loss retains only the codebook-pull term ‖sg[g(f(s))] − e_k‖², which the paper proves (Theorem 2) is algebraically identical to an online k-means centroid update, so the 8 codebook entries converge to cluster centroids without any reconstruction or commitment penalty. An adaptive control factor λ_ctrl = min(s_mean / (0.8·s_highest), 1) ramps the SCM's influence as the policy matures, letting DRL shape the feature space first before sharpening cluster boundaries. The stop-gradient on the FDR feature in L′_VQ-VAE prevents direct attraction toward centroids; instead, joint optimization with L_FDR densifies the 2-D space, and this densification propagates back into the high-dimensional features through the shared encoder, yielding tighter intra-cluster distances and more separable boundaries than t-SNE or a stop-gradient ablation.

## Results

The proposed FDR + VQ-VAE semantic clustering module achieves the best clustering quality across all three standard metrics (Silhouette, Davies-Bouldin Index, Calinski-Harabasz) compared to five dimensionality-reduction baselines (UMAP, Isomap, PCA, MDS, t-SNE) on Procgen games (Ninja, Jumper, FruitBot), with Calinski-Harabasz scores an order of magnitude larger than any baseline. Human evaluation by 15 annotators yields mean Likert scores above 4.0/5.0 for all three interpretability statements across all three games (e.g., 4.50 for 'skills aid understanding' on Jumper, 4.30 for 'consistent skill display' on Ninja). The module has minimal impact on DRL performance: SPPO curves closely track baseline PPO across three seeds on 'easy' difficulty environments.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is t-SNE (van der Maaten & Hinton, 2008), the standard tool used in prior DRL feature-space analyses (Mnih et al. 2015; Zahavy et al. 2016). The proposed FDR method decisively outperforms t-SNE on stability: t-SNE visualizations change dramatically when the number of states is halved or the random seed is altered, while the FDR mapping remains unchanged under both perturbations. On quantitative clustering metrics (Appendix E, averaged over 9 runs across Ninja, Jumper, and FruitBot), FDR achieves the highest Silhouette score, the lowest Davies-Bouldin Index, and a Calinski-Harabasz score roughly an order of magnitude larger than UMAP, Isomap, PCA, MDS, and t-SNE. In the stop-gradient ablation (Appendix C.2), removing the semantic clustering module's influence on the feature space yields fuzzier, less separable clusters and higher cluster-transition probability (e.g., 0.1520 vs. 0.1081 on FruitBot), confirming that the module sharpens an intrinsic clustering tendency rather than creating it from scratch. Overall, the method wins on every clustering-quality and stability metric tested, while matching baseline PPO performance on the underlying RL task.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes VQ-VAE's discrete codebook mechanism as an online k-means-style clustering module integrated into the DRL training loop, departing from VQ-VAE's original generative-modeling purpose. It directly addresses the limitations of prior DRL semantic analyses by Mnih et al. (2015) and Zahavy et al. (2016), which relied on unstable t-SNE visualizations of fixed-scene Atari games and required manual feature annotation. Conceptually, the paper positions itself against external-constraint approaches (bisimulation, contrastive learning) by arguing that semantic clustering emerges intrinsically from standard RL training, and replaces t-SNE's non-convex optimization with a stable, differentiable FDR network trained jointly with the policy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The stop-gradient ablation providing evidence that semantic clustering is an intrinsic property of DRL (not an artifact of the proposed module) is a useful conceptual contribution for the interpretability community, suggesting that RL agents naturally organize their feature spaces by behavioral semantics. The practical risk finding in Jumper—where the agent relies on radar/distance signals rather than visual carrot appearance—illustrates how semantic clustering analysis can expose failure modes in safety-critical settings. Key open questions include: whether the approach generalizes beyond Procgen to continuous-control or real-world domains, how to adaptively select the number of clusters, and whether automated behavior summarization (e.g., via VLMs) can replace the current manual annotation step.
