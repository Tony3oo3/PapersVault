## Problem

Computing hitting times between two vertices in a graph is a fundamental random-walk quantity with applications in centrality, recommendation, and epidemiology, but exact computation requires O(n³) time and prior approximation methods are inherently global, requiring access to the entire graph. This makes them impractical for massive networks with millions or billions of nodes. The paper addresses the gap of designing local algorithms that estimate hitting times using only short random walks centered around the query vertices, without ever accessing the full graph.

## Input / Output

The algorithms take an undirected graph G accessible via adjacency and degree queries, two query vertices u and v, and problem-specific parameters (mixing time t_mix for Algorithm 1, spectral gap λ for Algorithm 3, and target accuracy ε). They output an additive ε-approximation to H_G(u,v) with high probability (1−1/n or 9/10), and can be composed to yield an ε-approximation to the effective resistance R_eff(u,v). Crucially, the input is consumed only through local random-walk simulation from u and v—no global matrix operations or full-graph traversal are needed.

## Method

The primary algorithm (Algorithm 1) exploits the key insight that the infinite series defining H_G(u,v) can be truncated at the meeting time T of two independent random walks from u and v, because after meeting the walks share the same distribution and subsequent terms cancel by memorylessness. The meeting time is bounded in probability via a Markov Chain Chernoff bound applied on the Kronecker product graph G×G, yielding a runtime of Õ(t_mix³ / (‖π‖₂⁶ · π(v)² · ε²) · log³(n)). A second algorithm (Algorithm 3) extends Peng et al.'s spectral cutoff technique to hitting times, requiring a non-trivial adaptation to handle the inherent asymmetry H_G(u,v) ≠ H_G(v,u) that effective resistance does not face. The paper further establishes tight lower bounds via a barbell-graph construction showing that naive walk sampling is sample-optimal, and reveals a connection between hitting time estimation and sublinear-time distribution testing for local truncation without knowing the spectral gap.

## Results

On real-world graphs (Football, Facebook, Twitter), the Meeting Time Algorithm (Algorithm 1) achieves relative estimation error of 0.002–0.016 (uniform sampling) with standard deviation ≤ 0.009, substantially outperforming the Walk Sampling baseline (0.024–0.027) and the Spectral Cutoff Algorithm (0.022–1.061). The Cutoff Algorithm degrades severely on Football (error ≈ 1.0) and on Facebook/Twitter under PageRank-proportional pair sampling (error up to 0.253), while the Meeting Time Algorithm remains stable across all sampling strategies. The theoretical runtime of Algorithm 1 is Õ(t_mix³ / (‖π‖₂⁶ · π(v)² · ε²) · log³(n/(π(u)π(v)))).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its Meeting Time Algorithm (Algorithm 1) against two baselines: the Spectral Cutoff Algorithm (Algorithm 3, extending Peng et al. 2021) and a naive Walk Sampling algorithm. The Meeting Time Algorithm dominates on every dataset and sampling strategy: on Twitter it achieves 0.005 relative error (uniform) versus 0.024 for Sampling and 0.086 for Cutoff; on Facebook it reaches 0.012 versus 0.027 and 0.147; on Football it reaches 0.012 versus 0.025 and a catastrophic 0.994. The Cutoff Algorithm's poor performance on Football is attributed to sensitivity to the spectral gap hyperparameter λ, which is difficult to estimate locally. The Walk Sampling baseline, while unbiased, consistently requires more samples and exhibits higher variance than the Meeting Time Algorithm. The paper also notes that for Twitter, the true hitting time could not be computed via a linear system, so errors are reported against 100 iterations of the (unbiased) Meeting Time Algorithm as a proxy ground truth.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the local-algorithm framework of Peng et al. (KDD 2021) for effective resistance to the asymmetric hitting time problem, requiring non-trivial modifications to the spectral cutoff technique to handle directionality. It also builds on the Laplacian-based hitting time identity of Cohen et al. (FOCS 2016) as a foundational algebraic tool. The key conceptual departure is the meeting-time-based estimator (Algorithm 1), which exploits the cancellation structure of the infinite hitting-time sum and bounds meeting times via the Kronecker product graph and Markov Chain Chernoff bounds—a technique not present in prior hitting time or effective resistance literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Hitting times are a fundamental building block in recommender systems, network centrality, and epidemiological modeling, yet prior exact or approximate methods required O(n³) global computation, making them infeasible for billion-node graphs. This paper provides the first local (sublinear) algorithms with matching upper and lower bounds, establishing both practical scalability and inherent hardness of the problem. Open questions include universal lower bounds beyond the barbell construction, and whether the property-testing-based local truncation (Section 4.1) can be made fully practical without global spectral parameters. The main limitation is that all guarantees degrade with large mixing time, which is unavoidable but constrains applicability to slowly-mixing chains.
