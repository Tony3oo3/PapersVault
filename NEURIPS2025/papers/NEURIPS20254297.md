## Problem

Handwritten Mathematical Expression Recognition (HMER) has seen only marginal progress in recent years (3% improvement from CoMER to SSAN on CROHME) because prior methods rely on isolated, model-specific architectural modifications that are difficult to integrate or scale. Open-source VLMs, despite strong general capabilities, have not been effectively adapted to HMER, and the field lacks a unified framework that combines multiple complementary enhancement strategies within a single trainable model.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254297/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a handwritten (or printed) mathematical expression image paired with a task-specific text instruction prompt as input, and produces a LaTeX-formatted expression as output. Depending on the active task, the output format varies: direct LaTeX (Vanilla HMER), a tab-indented depth-first AST serialization followed by LaTeX (Tree-CoT), an error-tagged expression with <error_start>/<error_end> and <deleted> markers plus a correction log (EDL), or a symbol-frequency count string prepended to the LaTeX (Symbol Counting). Training images span five datasets with diverse writing styles, resolutions, and conditions, totaling ~392K unique images expanded to ~1.6M multi-task samples.

## Method

Uni-MuMER performs full-parameter fine-tuning of Qwen2.5-VL-3B (ViT encoder + transformer decoder) for HMER without any architectural modification, injecting domain knowledge purely through data-driven multi-task training. Three auxiliary tasks are integrated alongside vanilla recognition: (1) Tree-CoT, which serializes the expression's Abstract Syntax Tree via depth-first traversal with tab-based indentation to make 2D spatial structure explicit in the text output; (2) Error-Driven Learning, where an error corpus is generated via cross-fold training with multiple sampling, and the model learns both to detect errors (tagging with <error_start>/<error_end>/<deleted>) and to correct them (emitting REPLACE/INSERT/DELETE operations); and (3) Symbol Counting, which prepends a per-symbol frequency string to the LaTeX target to reduce omissions and hallucinations in long or repetitive expressions. All four tasks are trained jointly in a single epoch with mixed-precision (batch 512, 8×A800), and the model is evaluated using both exact-match ExpRate and the visual-equivalence-aware ExpRate@CDM metric.

## Results

On the CROHME benchmark (average of 2014/2016/2019 test sets), Uni-MuMER† achieves 79.74% ExpRate and 82.86% ExpRate@CDM, surpassing the best lightweight specialized model SSAN^a (63.43% ExpRate) by 16.31% and the strongest zero-shot VLM Gemini 2.5-flash (55.32% ExpRate) by 24.42%. On HME100K, Uni-MuMER† reaches 72.66% ExpRate and 74.30% @CDM, outperforming TAMER (69.50% ExpRate) and HiE-VL† (64.20% ExpRate). The ablation confirms all three auxiliary tasks contribute: Tree-CoT adds +2.21%, EDL adds +1.66%, and SC adds +1.22% over the Vanilla baseline, with the full combination yielding +4.65%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Uni-MuMER† decisively outperforms all 16 baselines on CROHME. Against closed-source zero-shot VLMs, it leads Gemini 2.5-flash (55.32% ExpRate) by 24.42%, GPT-4o (48.81%) by 30.93%, and Doubao-1.5-pro (48.79%) by 30.95%. Among open-source VLMs, even the largest Qwen2.5-VL-72B (56.40%) trails by 23.34%. Versus specialized HMER models, the gap is most striking against SSAN^a (63.43%, +16.31%) and PosFormer^a (62.91%, +16.83%); Uni-MuMER† also surpasses the previous VLM-based SOTA HiE-VL† (71.13%) by 8.61% and UniMERNet^a† (67.07%) by 12.67%. On HME100K, Uni-MuMER† (72.66% Exp) beats TAMER (69.50%) by 3.16% and HiE-VL† (64.20%) by 8.46%. Notably, the paper shows that feeding the same 386K external images to the lightweight CoMER model causes performance to *degrade* (46.18% vs. 58.16% without external data), underscoring that the VLM backbone is essential for leveraging large-scale diverse data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant HMER paradigm of designing specialized architectures (tree decoders, spatial-attention modules, coverage mechanisms) by instead performing full-parameter fine-tuning of a generalist VLM (Qwen2.5-VL-3B) without any architectural modification. It unifies previously fragmented auxiliary tasks—tree-structured decoding (TreeDecoder, TAMER), symbol counting (CAN), and error correction (RLFN)—into a single multi-task training framework expressed entirely in text. The closest prior VLM-based HMER methods (VLPG, HiE-VL) still require non-trivial architectural adapters or non-end-to-end training, whereas Uni-MuMER's contribution is purely in data-driven task design and prompt engineering on an off-the-shelf backbone.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a 3B VLM with carefully designed multi-task fine-tuning can surpass both the best lightweight specialized models (SSAN) and much larger closed-source VLMs (Gemini 2.5-flash) by wide margins, suggesting that task-specific data engineering may be more impactful than architectural innovation for domain-adapting VLMs. The leave-one-out experiments reveal strong cross-dataset generalization, but also expose a gap: performance drops noticeably when the target domain is very different (e.g., printed expressions without Im2LaTeXv2 data). Open questions include optimal task-mixture and domain-mixture ratios, whether the approach scales to larger VLMs, and whether the EDL self-correction loop can be extended to iterative inference-time refinement (R1-style self-correction).
