## Problem

Learning rules such as gradient descent, momentum, natural gradient, and Adam are typically adopted empirically rather than derived from first principles, leaving it unclear why some rules outperform others and under what assumptions a given rule is optimal. The paper addresses this gap by asking: can a single normative objective, combined with varying structural assumptions, systematically recover the full family of well-known learning rules?

## Input / Output

The framework takes as input a loss function L(θ), a parameter space metric G(θ) (e.g., Fisher information), a drift term f(θ) encoding partial controllability, and a Bayesian belief model over local gradient and curvature estimates that evolves via Ornstein-Uhlenbeck dynamics. It produces a continuous-time optimal trajectory {θ_t} from which discrete parameter updates θ_{Δt} − θ_0 are extracted; the specific closed-form update (gradient descent, Newton, ballistic, Adam-like) depends on the regime of the temporal discounting rate γ and the step size Δt relative to characteristic time scales.

## Method

The paper formulates learning as a continuous-time optimal control problem whose objective (Eq. 1) sums a kinetic-energy term penalizing parameter velocity (weighted by G and shifted by drift f) and a potential-energy term proportional to the loss, both temporally discounted by e^{−γt}. Optimal learning rules are derived by solving the resulting Euler-Lagrange equations under different assumptions: short-horizon (large γ) yields gradient descent, longer-horizon (small γ) yields momentum and a novel 'ballistic' rule with Hessian square-root normalization, non-Euclidean G yields natural gradient descent, and a drift term f yields non-gradient rules. Crucially, modeling the loss landscape as partially observable and maintaining Bayesian beliefs over gradient/curvature dynamics (via Ornstein-Uhlenbeck priors) naturally produces Adam-like adaptive learning rates with the characteristic V_t^{−1/2} scaling, identifying the square root as a consequence of the ballistic (highly inertial) regime rather than an ad hoc design choice. The framework is validated empirically by implementing the ballistic rule on MNIST and CIFAR-10, where it matches or exceeds SGD and approaches Adam performance.

## Results

The paper's empirical evaluation is illustrative rather than exhaustive, focusing on demonstrating that the derived 'ballistic' rule (the γ≈0 limit of the multi-step objective) is competitive in practice. On MNIST (2-hidden-layer MLP) and CIFAR-10 (4-layer CNN), the ballistic rule—implemented via an Adam-like running-average approximation of the Hessian square root—generally outperforms SGD and performs similarly to or slightly worse than Adam on both train loss and test accuracy. No specific final accuracy numbers are reported in the text; results are presented as training curves (Fig. 2e, f). Hyperparameter settings transfer well from Adam (learning rates ~1e-3 to 1e-4; β₂=0.999 for MNIST, β₂=0.9 for CIFAR-10).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the derived ballistic rule against two baselines: standard SGD and Adam. On both MNIST (MLP) and CIFAR-10 (CNN), the ballistic rule consistently outperforms SGD in both training loss and test accuracy, confirming that the Hessian-square-root preconditioning provides a meaningful improvement over unconditioned gradient steps. Against Adam, the ballistic rule performs 'similarly to or worse than' Adam, which is expected since the heuristic implementation of the ballistic rule is deliberately close to Adam's update formula (running average of uncentered gradient variances, diagonal approximation). The comparison is qualitative (training curves) rather than a head-to-head numerical benchmark, and the authors frame the empirical results as a sanity check on the theoretical derivation rather than a competitive evaluation. The primary contribution remains the normative derivation showing that Adam's square-root normalization is optimal under the ballistic (frictionless, long-horizon) regime, rather than a bug to be corrected.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the variational/optimal-control perspective on optimization (Wibisono et al., 2016) by formulating learning rules as solutions to a continuous-time optimal control problem where the objective is a sum of kinetic and potential costs, rather than the classical-mechanics-style difference. It also builds on Amari's natural gradient (1998) but departs from the line of work by Khan & Rue (2023) and Shoji et al. (2024) that treats natural gradient optimality as a given; here, natural gradient, momentum, and Adam all emerge as special cases of a single multi-step planning objective under different assumptions about geometry, discounting, and partial observability. The concurrent work of Orvieto & Gower (2025) similarly links Adam to loss-landscape shape inference, but this paper connects the square-root preconditioning specifically to the frictionless (ballistic) planning regime.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a principled starting point for designing new learning rules and for understanding which features of existing rules (e.g., Adam's square root, momentum, natural gradient preconditioning) are essential versus incidental. It clarifies that natural gradient and second-order methods play fundamentally different roles (ambient geometry vs. loss curvature), challenging the common conflation. Key limitations include the absence of computational-cost considerations, the reliance on local quadratic approximations, and limited empirical validation on small-scale tasks (MNIST MLP, CIFAR-10 CNN), leaving open whether the derived rules scale to modern deep learning. The framework also does not yet connect its assumptions to specific empirical regimes, so its practical guidance for choosing among optimizers remains qualitative.
