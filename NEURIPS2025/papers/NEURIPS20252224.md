## Problem

LM activations entangle the latent concepts that mediate model behavior, making it difficult to identify which factors drive specific behaviors. Existing interpretability methods (linear probes, contrastive activation addition, distributed interchange interventions) require external supervision—concept labels, contrastive prompts, or fully specified causal models—which biases discovery toward predefined, easily annotatable features. This paper asks whether behavior-relevant mediating concepts can be discovered from LM activations without such supervision while retaining formal identifiability guarantees.

## Input / Output

The method consumes LM activations g(x) (typically final-layer token embeddings) and coarse-grained discrete behavior labels c, which are derived by clustering next-token probabilities or expert annotation rather than requiring per-example concept labels. It produces a low-dimensional disentangled latent representation ẑ = τ(g(x)) via a feedforward bottleneck whose dimensionality d is set to the number of true mediating concepts, along with 1-sparse perturbation vectors δ̂_kl that identify which single concept dimension differentiates each pair of behavior groups. The output latents are guaranteed to align with true mediating concepts up to permutation and scaling under the stated assumptions.

## Method

The core contribution is a constrained contrastive learning objective grounded in two assumptions: (1) behavior is log-linearly related to latent concepts, and (2) every concept dimension is a 1-sparse causal differentiating concept for at least one pair of behavior groups, meaning changing only that concept suffices to flip behavior between two groups. The training objective combines a categorical cross-entropy prediction loss (enforcing the log-linear assumption) with a contrastive loss that, for each pair of behavior groups C_k and C_l, searches for example pairs whose latent representations differ by exactly one sparse perturbation δ̂_kl. The learner jointly optimizes the bottleneck encoder τ and the perturbation vectors under a span constraint ensuring all concept dimensions are covered. The paper proves identifiability up to permutation and scaling under both assumptions, and establishes via induction that the correctly aligned (disentangled) solution achieves a strictly lower contrastive loss than any entangled solution, since axis-aligned axes identify strictly more valid 1-sparse match-pairs across behavior groups.

## Results

On synthetic data (Table 1), the proposed method achieves near-perfect disentanglement (DCI-D ≥ 0.89) and completeness (DCI-C ≥ 0.89) across all mixing functions and latent dimensions (d=2, d=3), with MCC scores above 0.86, while baselines drop to DCI-D as low as 0.0. On semi-synthetic data with real text (Table 2), the method achieves MCC 0.81–0.85 and DCI-D 0.80–0.91 across DistilBERT-base, Qwen2-7B, and Llama-3.1-8B, substantially outperforming the autoencoding baseline (MCC 0.06–0.32, DCI-D 0.0–0.18) and the autoencoding+prediction baseline (MCC 0.32–0.69, DCI-D 0.05–0.18). In the refusal-behavior case study, the learned latent space is axis-aligned with dimension 1 encoding harmfulness and dimension 2 encoding topic, whereas baselines produce entangled, non-axis-aligned representations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two internal baselines (autoencoding and autoencoding+prediction) and sparse autoencoders (LlamaScope, 32k and 128k features). On semi-synthetic data, the proposed method dominates both baselines by a wide margin: on Llama-3.1-8B, it achieves MCC 0.81 vs. 0.06 (autoencoding) and 0.54 (autoenc+pred), and DCI-D 0.86 vs. 0.00 and 0.05 respectively. Against LlamaScope sparse autoencoders on Llama-3.1-8B, the proposed method achieves MCC 0.81 and DCI-D 0.86, while the best SAE configuration (ceiling analysis selecting the most predictive feature per ground-truth factor) reaches only DCI-D 0.76 and MCC 0.51. When restricted to the top-2 most informative SAE features for predicting behavior, disentanglement collapses to 0.08±0.12, indicating SAEs do not isolate the true causal factors into a small number of distinct features. The authors attribute this gap to SAEs learning fine-grained features that decompose higher-level concepts (e.g., splitting 'Einstein' into 'scientist' + 'Germany' + 'famous person'), making them less suitable for behavior-targeted interpretation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the causal representation learning literature (Ahuja et al., 2022a,b) by introducing 'causal differentiating concepts'—factors whose sparse changes are necessary and sufficient to alter model behavior—and proving identifiability under a 1-sparsity assumption. It departs from Geiger et al. (2024b), which requires a fully specified high-level causal model to supervise alignment between concepts and LM activations, by replacing that strong requirement with a weaker sparsity condition enforced through a constrained contrastive objective. Compared to sparse autoencoders, which recover behavior-agnostic features requiring post-hoc analysis to identify behavior-relevant ones, this method is explicitly behavior-targeted, learning only the latent dimensions needed to predict a given behavior class.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, supervision-free route to interpreting *why* an LM behaves a certain way on a specific task, with theoretical identifiability guarantees that prior unsupervised methods (SAEs, vanilla contrastive learning) lack. The refusal-behavior case study demonstrates practical utility: the method recovers interpretable 'harmfulness' and 'topic' axes without any labels for those factors. Key limitations include the 1-sparse assumption (only preliminary evidence for p-sparse extensions), the need to specify latent dimensionality a priori, and evaluation on relatively small models (≤8B). Open questions include whether the approach scales to more complex, multi-step behaviors and whether the discovered concepts can be used directly for steering or safety interventions.
