## Problem

Outdoor walking assistance for people with Blindness or Low Vision (BLV) remains challenging due to the lack of holistic scene understanding that balances hazard awareness with landmark-based navigation. Existing visual assistive datasets are predominantly single-view, indoor-focused, or lack explicit annotation of safety-critical corner cases (e.g., uneven ground, dangerous road crossings, high obstacles) and BLV-specific navigational landmarks, leaving a gap in both data and benchmarking for multi-modal, multi-view outdoor navigation.

## Input / Output

The model receives synchronized multi-view equirectangular panoramic RGB images (from walker, guide dog, and drone perspectives) along with a text question; depth and semantic segmentation maps are pre-converted into clockwise spatial description strings in a BLV-friendly format before being fed to the VLM. The output is a free-form text answer scored on a 1–5 scale by GPT-4o-mini against GPT-4o-generated ground-truth answers, normalized to 0–100. The 69,391 VQA triplets span 9 categories (weather/action, existence, counting, attribute, spatial, description, view comparison, risk assessment, navigational landmarks) across three difficulty levels, with questions averaging 7–10 words and answers ranging from 4 to 31 words depending on difficulty.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a scalable dataset-and-benchmark pipeline rather than a new architecture. Data is collected in the CARLA simulator across 120 manually controlled trajectories (7 scenarios × 5 weather conditions), capturing synchronized frames from three sensor groups (walker 4-direction, dog 4-direction, drone 6-direction) at 2 fps, which are collated into cubemaps and converted to equirectangular panoramas via py360convert, yielding 559K panoramic images. VQA pairs are generated by GPT-4o using the multi-view RGB images plus translated spatial/semantic strings and contextual metadata (corner cases, landmarks, weather, action), followed by keyword-based automatic filtering and manual quality validation. For benchmarking, six open-source VLMs (7–8B) are evaluated under zero-shot, 3-shot, and LoRA-fine-tuned settings, with InternVL2-8B fine-tuned on mmWalkVQA showing a 13.86% average score improvement over its zero-shot baseline. Evaluation uses GPT-4o-mini as an LLM-as-a-judge with a unified 1–5 scoring prompt, validated against human ratings (Spearman's ρ = 0.864 average across five models), and cross-dataset generalization is demonstrated on EgoTextVQA.

## Results

On mmWalkVQA, the finetuned InternVL2-8B achieves the highest average normalized score of 55.21, improving by 13.86 points over its zero-shot counterpart (41.35). Among zero-shot baselines, InternVL2-8B leads at 41.35, followed by LLaVA-NEXT-7B (39.84) and Qwen2VL-7B (37.91); among 3-shot baselines, LLaVA-NEXT-7B achieves the best average of 43.71. The hardest categories—M1 (Spatial, best zero-shot: 24.71) and H1 (Risk Assessment, best zero-shot: 54.30)—reveal that even state-of-the-art VLMs struggle with spatial reasoning and hazard identification. On the cross-dataset EgoTextVQA benchmark, the mmWalk-finetuned model improves the overall score from 18.5/1.4 (original frame input) to 21.55/1.92, demonstrating generalization to real-world outdoor VQA.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The finetuned InternVL2-8B (55.21 avg) substantially outperforms all zero-shot and 3-shot baselines, with the largest gap over LLaVA-Onevision-7B (21.87 zero-shot) and Chameleon-7B (21.14 zero-shot). Among zero-shot models, InternVL2-8B leads by 1.51 points over LLaVA-NEXT-7B (39.84) and 3.44 over Qwen2VL-7B (37.91), primarily due to its superior M3 (View Comparison) score of 53.26 versus 28.98 and 14.95 respectively. In the 3-shot setting, LLaVA-NEXT-7B (43.71) edges out Qwen2VL-7B (41.89) and Janus-Pro-7B (37.75), though Janus-Pro shows the strongest H1 (Risk Assessment) score at 54.33. Notably, Janus-Pro-7B's 3-shot score actually decreases slightly (−0.07) from zero-shot, suggesting it mimics example answers rather than reasoning from images. On the cross-dataset EgoTextVQA evaluation, the mmWalk-finetuned InternVL2-8B improves the overall score from 18.5/1.4 (original frame input) to 21.55/1.92, with the largest gains in Description (+14.9) and Intention Reasoning (+8.6) categories, confirming that training on mmWalkVQA transfers to real-world outdoor VQA.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of BLV-oriented VQA datasets (VizWiz, GuideDog, SideGuide) by introducing the first benchmark that simultaneously combines multi-view (walker, guide dog, drone), multi-modal (RGB, depth, semantic), panoramic equirectangular imagery, sequential trajectories, and BLV-specific corner cases and navigational landmarks. It repurposes the CARLA autonomous-driving simulator for pedestrian-scale data collection and leverages GPT-4o to generate a scalable VQA pipeline with BLV-friendly clockwise spatial descriptions. The empirical evaluation builds on the LLM-as-a-judge paradigm and benchmarks a range of open-source VLMs (InternVL2, Qwen2VL, LLaVA-Next, Janus-Pro, Chameleon, LLaVA-OneVision) under zero-shot, 3-shot, and fine-tuned settings, positioning the work at the intersection of assistive technology and vision-language model evaluation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

mmWalkVQA fills a clear gap: no prior dataset combines all of multi-view, panoramic, spatial/depth, egocentric, BLV-specific, and sequential-trajectory features (Table 4), making it a natural benchmark for the growing field of VLM-based walking assistance. The consistent poor performance of even SOTA models on risk assessment (H1) and spatial reasoning (M1) highlights a critical safety gap for assistive deployment, while the cross-dataset transfer to EgoTextVQA suggests the dataset's utility beyond its own domain. Key limitations include the fully synthetic (CARLA) provenance, which may not capture real-world BLV experience diversity, and the reliance on LLM-as-a-judge for evaluation. Open questions include whether real-world data collection can close the sim-to-real gap, whether additional modalities (IMU, sequential frame dynamics) unlock new task formulations, and whether the VQA framing adequately captures the continuous, real-time nature of walking assistance.
