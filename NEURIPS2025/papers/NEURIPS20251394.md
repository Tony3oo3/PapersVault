## Problem

Recent analyses suggest that base LLMs already possess latent reasoning capabilities that RL post-training merely surfaces by biasing generation toward high-reward traces, raising the question of what alternative mechanisms could elicit reasoning without additional training. While cognitive prompting (Kramer & Baumann, 2024) translated cognitive architecture principles into structured prompts, it lacks the modularity that cognitive science identifies as critical for reducing interference between reasoning operations. This paper asks whether implementing cognitive operations as discrete, callable tools within an agentic framework can elicit reasoning more effectively than monolithic prompting or RL.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251394/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a math problem as a plain text query (e.g., from AIME 2024, MATH500, or AMC) and produces a final answer in the format 'ANSWER: <value>' preceded by a reasoning trace that interleaves free-form reasoning with Python-style tool calls. Each tool call triggers a separate LLM invocation in a sandboxed context, whose structured output (e.g., a problem decomposition, analogous examples, a verification report, or a backtracking plan) is appended to the main conversation history before the LLM continues reasoning. The LLM autonomously decides which tool to invoke and when, with no enforced ordering or fixed number of calls.

## Method

The paper implements four cognitive tools—understand question, recall related, examine answer, and backtracking—plus a code-execution tool, each encapsulated as a dedicated prompt template within a standard agentic tool-calling loop. The main LLM reasons freely until it emits a tool call; generation is then paused, the tool's prompt is executed by the same LLM in an isolated context (receiving only the tool-specific inputs from its schema), and the structured output is fed back into the main reasoning context. The key design principle is modularity: each tool operates in a sandboxed context window, reducing interference from the full reasoning history and allowing the LLM to focus on a single cognitive operation. No fine-tuning or RL is involved; the entire approach is a prompting and orchestration strategy applied to off-the-shelf instruct models. On AIME 2024, this raises GPT-4.1's pass@1 from 32% to 53%, surpassing o1-preview (44.6%), and consistently outperforms both the plain baseline and monolithic cognitive prompting across open-weight models (Qwen2.5, Llama3.1/3.3).

## Results

On AIME 2024, cognitive tools yield the largest absolute gains: Qwen2.5-32B Instruct improves from 17.2% to 32.1% pass@1, and Llama3.3-70B Instruct from 13.1% to 29.8%. On the closed-model comparison, GPT-4.1 with cognitive tools reaches 53% on AIME 2024, surpassing o1-preview (44.6%) without any additional training. Across MATH500 and AMC, cognitive tools consistently improve pass@1 by 4–11 percentage points over the plain baseline for all tested open-weight models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against cognitive prompting (Kramer & Baumann, 2024), the modular cognitive tools approach consistently wins: on Smolbenchmark, cognitive tools achieve 80.0% on Llama3.3-70B versus 66.0% for cognitive prompting, and on AIME 2024 with GPT-4.1, cognitive tools reach 53% versus 42% for cognitive prompting. Against Chain-of-Thought (Wei et al., 2023), cognitive tools produce substantially larger gains on the hardest benchmark: Qwen2.5-32B jumps from 15.4% (CoT) to 32.1% (cognitive tools) on AIME 2024, and Llama3.3-70B from 18.1% to 29.8%. Against the RL-trained reasoning model o1-preview (OpenAI, 2024), GPT-4.1 augmented with cognitive tools (53% on AIME 2024) surpasses o1-preview (44.6%) without any additional training, suggesting that structured modular prompting can match or exceed RL-elicited reasoning on this benchmark. The code-equipped baseline is competitive on MATH500 and AMC but lags cognitive tools on AIME 2024, where the reasoning-oriented tools (understand question, backtracking, examine answer) provide the largest marginal benefit.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the cognitive prompting approach of Kramer and Baumann (2024) by introducing modularity: instead of a monolithic prompt that prescribes a fixed sequence of cognitive stages, each operation (understand, recall, examine, backtrack) is encapsulated as a discrete tool within an agentic tool-calling framework, inspired by the ACT-R cognitive architecture's principle of orchestrated modular operations. It also positions itself against the RL-based reasoning elicitation paradigm (o1, DeepSeek-R1) by demonstrating that structured modular prompting alone can surface latent reasoning capabilities in base models, aligning with recent analyses by Liu et al. and Yue et al. that RL primarily biases generation toward already-present reasoning traces rather than instilling new capabilities.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that GPT-4.1 augmented with cognitive tools surpasses o1-preview on AIME 2024 (53% vs. 44.6%) without any training provides concrete evidence that modular structured prompting is a viable alternative or complement to RL for reasoning elicitation, with implications for interpretability and accessibility. However, the method relies on manually designed tools, has been evaluated primarily on mathematical reasoning, and the headline comparison rests on a 30-problem benchmark, leaving open whether the approach generalizes to other domains, model families, or scales. Future work on automated discovery of cognitive operations and integration with RL could clarify the boundary between what prompting can elicit versus what requires post-training.
