## Problem

Gene regulatory network inference (GRNI) methods have addressed spurious dependencies from latent confounders, but selection bias—where only cells meeting survival or inclusion criteria are observed—remains largely unexplored. This selection-induced dependence is pervasive in single-cell gene expression data and, when conflated with true regulatory interactions, leads to flawed causal interpretation and misguided intervention recommendations. The fundamental question the paper tackles is whether dependencies arising from regulation, confounding, and selection can be distinguished from each other.

## Input / Output

The method consumes observational single-cell gene expression data D₀ and single-gene perturbation data {D_k} obtained via CRISPR-based hard (knockout) or soft (knockup/knockdown) interventions, with perturbation indicators I_k incorporated as exogenous variables in an augmented DAG. It outputs three components: a PAG-style equivalence class G over the N observed genes representing regulatory relationships, a set of latent confounder pairs L, and a set of selection pairs S. The CI tests use KCI with α=0.05, and the skeleton is recovered via FGES from observational data, making the method fully nonparametric with no functional-form assumptions.

## Method

The core insight is that selection-induced dependencies are symmetric under perturbation (perturbing either gene in a selection pair shifts the other's marginal distribution), whereas regulatory relationships are asymmetric and latent confounders produce no perturbation effect on the other variable. GISL exploits this by computing four conditional independence patterns between perturbation indicators I and observed variables X for each gene pair—specifically testing I_X ⊥ Y | S, I_X ⊥ Y | X,S, I_Y ⊥ X | S, and I_Y ⊥ X | Y,S—and matching the resulting pattern to one of four structural templates (regulatory, confounded, selection, or combined). The algorithm first recovers the skeleton via FGES on observational data, then classifies each edge by its CI pattern, and finally corrects for d-separated paths by re-testing with conditioning on non-endpoint subsets. Identifiability is established up to equivalence classes of the four CI patterns under Markov and faithfulness assumptions, with the presence or absence of selection and confounding being fully identifiable.

## Results

On synthetic data, GISL achieves 94.7% accuracy and 94.9% F1 in identifying regulatory relationships, compared to 51.6% (PIDC) and 43.4% (PPCOR) for computational baselines (Table 6). For selection bias detection, GISL reaches ~75–80% accuracy on synthetic data (Table 1) and 77.7%, 80.2%, and 74.8% Z-score-verified accuracy on the real-world Dixit, Adamson, and Norman datasets, respectively. Against the closest causal-discovery baseline CDIS, GISL improves regulatory F1 from 63.8% to 80.0% and selection-bias precision from 34.1% to 80.8% (Table 7).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GISL substantially outperforms all computational GRNI baselines: against PIDC it gains +43.1 pp accuracy and +39.8 pp F1, and against PPCOR it gains +51.3 pp accuracy and +45.2 pp F1 (Table 6), because these methods cannot distinguish selection-induced spurious dependencies from true regulation. Among interventional causal-discovery baselines (GIES, IGSP, UT-IGSP, JCI-GSP; Figure 4), GISL achieves the highest DAG F1 and, most critically, the highest precision, since it correctly removes edges that are merely selection artifacts. Against CDIS [92], the only other method explicitly modeling selection, GISL improves regulatory F1 from 63.8% to 80.0% and selection-bias detection precision from 34.1% to 80.8% (Table 7); the gap is attributed to CDIS assuming selection is a one-time pre-intervention event, whereas GISL models persistent biological survival constraints. For PAG-level comparison, GISL refines FCI/ICD outputs (e.g., converting undirected ○–○ edges to directed → edges) by exploiting perturbation-specific CI patterns, yielding a more precise equivalence class (Figure 9).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the augmented DAG framework for representing interventions (Yang et al. 2018; Korb et al. 2004) and the FCI/PAG machinery for causal discovery under latent confounders (Zhang 2008; Spirtes et al. 1995), but departs from both by introducing selection bias as a third, distinct source of spurious dependency alongside regulation and confounding. Prior causal discovery methods that handle selection (FCI, ICD, CDIS) either rely on parametric assumptions, model selection as a one-time pre-intervention event, or are limited to outcome-dependent selection structures; GISL instead treats selection as a persistent biological constraint (survival criteria) that operates both before and after perturbation, and exploits the perturbation-symmetry property of selection-induced dependencies as a nonparametric distinguishing principle. The algorithmic pipeline combines FGES for skeleton recovery with KCI-based conditional independence pattern matching over the augmented DAG, positioning it within the constraint-based interventional causal discovery family while extending its scope to jointly recover regulatory edges, confounder pairs, and selection pairs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of selection bias as a pervasive, previously unaddressed confound in GRNI is the paper's most significant contribution: it reframes a class of spurious gene-gene dependencies that existing methods silently misattribute to regulation or latent confounding, potentially leading to misguided intervention recommendations. The perturbation-symmetry diagnostic is elegant and broadly applicable beyond GRNI to any setting where persistent selection constraints interact with interventional data. Key limitations include the restriction to acyclic graphs, the indistinguishability of pure selection (C&S) from selection alone in CI patterns (limiting recall for regulatory edges), and reliance on Z-scores as a proxy ground truth for real-data validation rather than direct biological confirmation. Open questions include extending the framework to cyclic regulatory motifs, handling multi-gene simultaneous perturbations, and scaling CI-based detection to the full transcriptome without the current skeleton-reduction bottleneck.
