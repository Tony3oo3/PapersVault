## Problem

Existing governance frameworks for lethal autonomous weapon systems (LAWS) were designed for rule-based, non-adaptive systems and fail to account for the novel risks introduced by modern AI—opacity, post-deployment drift, brittleness under domain shift, and unpredictable escalation. These AI-powered systems are already deployed worldwide, yet no binding standards distinguish them from conventional LAWS, and the AI research community remains largely excluded from military AI policy discussions.

## Input / Output

The paper takes as its analytical basis a survey of publicly documented military AI systems (Table 2, spanning air, land, sea, and command domains from 2019–2025) and a review of existing policy instruments (DoD Directive 3000.09, U.K. 2024 guidance, UN CCW discussions, REAIM declarations). It produces a two-criterion behavior-based rubric for classifying AI-LAWS and five concrete policy recommendations, structured as a position paper with no computational pipeline or empirical evaluation.

## Method

The core contribution is a behavior-based oversight rubric that triggers enhanced regulation when a system meets two criteria simultaneously: (1) use of AI/ML methods (e.g., neural networks) that are necessary for system function and pose AI-specific risks such as misidentification, drift, or degradation in unfamiliar environments, and (2) involvement in semi- or fully autonomous targeting and force application, with or without human oversight. This rubric is deliberately grounded in observable system behavior rather than labels like 'fully autonomous' or 'general intelligence,' making it tractable for policymakers and creating a concrete entry point for AI researchers to contribute to failure-mode analysis. The paper then derives five policy recommendations from this definition: a binding ban on AI control over nuclear launch, an international consortium for AI-LAWS validation standards, a categorical ban on AI systems that command human soldiers, clarification of civilian AI infrastructure status under international humanitarian law, and institutional-level civil-military boundary declarations. The authors argue that all three existing oversight narratives—Meaningful Human Control, AGI existential risk, and Appropriate Human Judgment—remain underdefined and insufficient without the technical validation mechanisms (task-specific benchmarks, domain-shift stress testing, post-deployment monitoring) that only AI researchers can specify.

## Results

This is a position paper with no quantitative experiments or benchmark results. Its primary contributions are (1) a two-criteria behavioral rubric for identifying AI-LAWS (use of AI/ML essential to function + involvement in semi- or fully autonomous targeting/force application) and (2) five policy recommendations: banning AI control over nuclear weapons, developing international validation standards, banning AI 'generals' that command human soldiers, clarifying legal status of civilian AI infrastructure under IHL, and establishing institutional civil-military boundaries. The paper catalogs 28+ deployed or in-development AI-enabled military systems across air, land, sea, and command domains (Table 2) and identifies five structural risk categories (Table 1).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare methods or models in the traditional sense. Instead, it positions its proposed AI-LAWS rubric against three existing oversight narratives: (1) the humanitarian 'Meaningful Human Control' (MHC) framework, which the authors argue lacks measurable criteria for fast-moving or distributed contexts; (2) the existential-risk/AGI framing, which they consider too far-future to address currently deployed systems; and (3) the 'Appropriate Human Judgment' (AHJ) model embedded in U.S. DoD Directive 3000.09, which they find insufficient because it does not define metrics for opaque, adaptive, or continually learning systems. The authors argue their behavior-based criteria are more tractable than existing definitional thresholds (e.g., 'full autonomy' or 'superhuman learning') that exclude most deployed and near-term systems from oversight. No empirical head-to-head comparison is performed.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out a new regulatory category—AI-LAWS—by applying well-known AI failure modes (brittleness, post-deployment drift, opacity) to the existing LAWS governance landscape, arguing that rule-based autonomous weapons frameworks (UN CCW, DoD Directive 3000.09, UK 2024 guidance) were never designed for adaptive, learning-based systems. It departs from three established policy narratives (Meaningful Human Control, AGI existential risk, and Appropriate Human Judgment) by proposing a behavior-based two-criterion rubric that identifies high-risk systems based on how they function rather than how they are labeled. The paper also synthesizes civil-military fusion literature and AI safety research to argue that the unusually small gap between civilian and military AI applications creates regulatory challenges absent from prior arms-control debates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

AI-LAWS are already deployed in active conflicts (Ukraine, Iran-Israel), yet no binding international standards govern their validation, drift monitoring, or escalation risks, and the AI research community remains largely excluded from the regulatory process. The paper's open questions—how to define sufficient oversight for adaptive models, how to validate systems under domain shift without disclosing sensitive details, and how to protect civilian AI infrastructure under international humanitarian law—remain unresolved and will require sustained technical-policy collaboration. A key limitation is that the two-criterion rubric, while more tractable than prior definitions, still lacks operational metrics for what constitutes 'sufficient' validation, leaving implementation to future standards bodies.
