## Problem

Test-time adaptation methods that rely on model-generated pseudo-labels are unreliable under distribution shift because predictions are often driven by non-causal (spurious) features whose correlations with the target change across domains. While DeYO identifies this issue, it does not explicitly remove non-causal components from representations, requiring many iterative adaptation steps to suppress their influence. TACT addresses this gap by actively trimming non-causal features from both test representations and class prototypes, enabling reliable predictions from the very first adaptation step.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250227/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes unlabeled test samples (RGB images, Mel spectrograms, or text comments) along with n augmented variants generated by domain-specific augmentations designed to preserve causal content while varying non-causal attributes (e.g., stain color jitter for histopathology, demographic sentence insertion for text, AutoAugment/RandomAugment for general images). It produces a causally trimmed d-dimensional representation, a set of trimmed class prototypes maintained via a running moving average across batches, and a final class label via softmax over dot-product logits. The number of augmentations n (searched over 2^1 to 2^8) and the number of removed principal components m (searched over 1–16) are the two key hyperparameters controlling estimation quality and trimming aggressiveness.

## Method

TACT identifies non-causal directions by applying targeted data augmentations that hold causal features constant while varying non-causal ones, then computing PCA over the resulting set of representations to extract the top-m principal components as the non-causal subspace. It then performs causal trimming: both the test sample's representation z and each class prototype q_j are projected onto the identified non-causal directions and the projections are subtracted, yielding trimmed representations that retain only components along the remaining (presumed causal) directions. A moving average of the trimmed prototypes is maintained across adaptation batches to smooth out sample-specific noise in the estimated non-causal directions. The final prediction is made by computing softmax over dot products between the trimmed test representation and the moving-averaged trimmed prototypes. The paper provides three propositions establishing conditions under which TACT corrects wrong predictions, preserves causal information in the trimmed representation, and maintains already-correct predictions.

## Results

TACT (backpropagation-free) achieves 31.14% macro F1 on Birdcalls, 70.17% accuracy on Camelyon17, 71.80% worst-group accuracy on CivilComments, 43.59% accuracy on ImageNet-R, and 63.33% accuracy on ImageNet-V2, outperforming all backpropagation-free baselines on every dataset. The TACT-adapt variant, which uses TACT's predictions as pseudo-labels for gradient-based updates, achieves the best overall performance: 31.25% on Birdcalls, 83.70% on Camelyon17, 71.98% on CivilComments, 48.81% on ImageNet-R, and 63.44% on ImageNet-V2. The largest single-dataset gain is on CivilComments, where TACT improves worst-group accuracy from 55.38% (no TTA) to 71.80%, a 16.4-point increase driven by removing spurious demographic correlations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among backpropagation-free methods, TACT dominates all three baselines (T3A, LAME, FOA) on every dataset, with the most dramatic margin on CivilComments where TACT reaches 71.80% worst-group accuracy versus 56.43% for T3A and 56.24% for LAME. Among backpropagation-based methods, TACT-adapt outperforms all seven baselines (SHOT, Tent, SAR, DeYO, TAST, TSD, PASLE) on all five datasets. The closest BP-based competitor is SHOT, which TACT-adapt surpasses by 4.4 points on Birdcalls (31.25 vs 26.82), 3.4 points on Camelyon17 (83.70 vs 80.28), and 58.1 points on CivilComments (71.98 vs 13.93); on ImageNet-R the gap is marginal (48.81 vs 48.79). DeYO, the only prior method that explicitly addresses non-causal features, is outperformed by TACT-adapt on Birdcalls (31.25 vs 23.29), Camelyon17 (83.70 vs 69.64), and ImageNet-R (48.81 vs 46.87), confirming that actively trimming non-causal directions is more effective than DeYO's confidence-reweighted entropy minimization approach.

## Relation to Prior Work

TACT carves new ground at the intersection of causal inference and test-time adaptation. While DeYO [29] identified that non-causal features make entropy an unreliable confidence signal and used causal interventions to select trustworthy predictions, it never explicitly removes non-causal components from representations. TACT repurposes the linear representation hypothesis (semantic concepts as vector directions) and classical PCA to identify and subtract non-causal directions, drawing on targeted augmentation strategies from the OOD-robustness literature [9, 18, 31, 32] to generate the augmented samples needed for variance analysis. The prototype-based classifier framework is standard in TTA (T3A, AdaContrast), but applying causal trimming to both input representations and class prototypes simultaneously is a novel combination.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method's backpropagation-free nature makes it lightweight and immediately applicable, and its cross-modality validation (image, audio, text) suggests the causal-trimming principle is broadly applicable. However, a critical limitation is the need for domain knowledge to select augmentations that vary non-causal features while preserving causal ones; the authors explicitly flag this as the key open problem for future work. Additionally, the PCA orthogonality constraint may fail when causal and non-causal features are not linearly separable in representation space, and the modest gains on ImageNet-R/V2 (1–2%) suggest the approach is most impactful when spurious correlations are strong and well-characterized.
