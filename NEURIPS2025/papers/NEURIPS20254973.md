## Problem

Existing algorithmic replicability results for online learning (e.g., Esfandiari et al. 2022) only guarantee that two runs on iid-sampled input sequences produce identical action sequences, but real-world online settings often involve time-varying or adversarially chosen input distributions. This paper asks whether online learning algorithms can simultaneously achieve sub-linear regret and remain replicable when the input distributions are chosen obliviously by an adversary at each time step, a strictly stronger requirement that had not been addressed.

## Input / Output

The algorithm receives a sequence of cost vectors c_t ∈ R^n one at a time, where each c_t is drawn from a distribution D_t selected obliviously by an adversary (D_t may be a point-mass, recovering the standard adversarial setting). The algorithm is parameterized by a block size B and a grid spacing ε, and at each time step t it outputs an action a_t (a point in R^n for online linear optimization, or an expert index in [n] for the experts problem). The key output guarantee is that two independent runs on two sequences drawn from the same product distribution D_1 × … × D_T, using the same internal randomness, produce the exact same action sequence with probability at least 1−ρ, while incurring sub-linear regret against the best fixed action in hindsight.

## Method

The core technique combines two mechanisms: (1) partitioning the time horizon into fixed-size blocks of length B and updating the action only at block boundaries (transition points), and (2) rounding the cumulative cost vector at each transition point to the nearest point on a randomly-offset grid of spacing 1/ε (inspired by Follow the Lazy Leader). The blocking ensures that the switching threshold between two close trajectories falls within the same block, while the grid rounding ensures that cumulative cost vectors within O(√(nT)) in ℓ₁ distance (which holds with high probability by McDiarmid's concentration) map to the same grid point and hence select the same action. For the experts problem, geometric noise is added to each expert's cumulative cost, and the memorylessness property of geometric random variables is exploited to show that two trajectories close in ℓ_∞ distance select the same best expert with high probability. A general conversion framework is also provided that wraps any internal online learner with this blocking-and-rounding procedure, bounding the external algorithm's regret in terms of the internal one's regret, at the cost of a slightly worse bound than the problem-specific algorithms.

## Results

The paper establishes that adversarially ρ-replicable online learning with sublinear regret is achievable. For online linear optimization, FLLB(ε,B) achieves regret Õ(DT^{5/6}n^{1/6}ρ^{-1/3}) (Theorem 3.1). For the experts problem, FTPLB*(ε,B) achieves regret Õ(T^{5/6}ln^{5/6}(n)ρ^{-1/3}) (Theorem 4.1). In the iid-replicability setting, a near-optimal algorithm achieves worst-case regret O(√T·log(n)/ρ) (Theorem 1.4), which matches the lower bound Ω(√T/ρ) up to logarithmic factors. The adversarial lower bound is Ω(√T·log(n)/ρ) (Theorem 1.5), leaving a gap with the T^{5/6} upper bound.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper demonstrates that the standard Follow the Perturbed Leader (FTPL) and Follow the Lazy Leader (FLL) algorithms of Kalai and Vempala (2005) are NOT adversarially replicable (Example 3.1 shows two trajectories from the same product distribution can switch actions at different times). The proposed modifications—blocking time steps combined with grid rounding (FLLB) or geometric noise (FTPLB*)—restore adversarial ρ-replicability at the cost of a ρ^{-1/3} factor in the regret exponent. The general conversion framework (Theorem 5.1) applies to any internal algorithm with linear cost but yields a worse T^{7/8} regret (vs. T^{5/6} for the specialized algorithms), illustrating a versatility–efficiency trade-off. In the iid-replicability setting, the upper bound O(√T·log(n)/ρ) is near-optimal, matching the lower bound Ω(√T/ρ) up to polylog factors. For adversarial replicability, a gap remains between the upper bound Õ(T^{5/6}ln^{5/6}(n)ρ^{-1/3}) and the lower bound Ω(√T·log(n)/ρ), which the authors identify as an open problem.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the algorithmic replicability framework of Impagliazzo et al. (STOC 2022) and Ghazi et al. (NeurIPS 2021) from batch learning to the online setting, and further generalizes the iid-replicability notion studied by Esfandiari et al. (2022) for multi-armed bandits to adversarial (time-varying distribution) inputs. The core algorithmic techniques build directly on Kalai and Vempala's (JCSS 2005) Follow the Perturbed Leader (FTPL) and Follow the Lazy Leader (FLL) algorithms, augmenting them with two new ingredients: blocking time steps into fixed-size blocks and rounding cumulative cost vectors to a random grid. The paper also introduces a general conversion framework that wraps any regret-minimizing online learner into an adversarially replicable one, provided the cost function is linear in the input.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work establishes that sub-linear regret and adversarial replicability are simultaneously achievable in online learning, resolving a natural question about whether the two properties are fundamentally in tension. The lower bounds (Ω(√T/ρ) for iid, Ω(√(T log n)/ρ) for adversarial) quantify the irreducible regret cost of replicability and reveal a gap between the iid and adversarial settings that remains open. Key open questions include closing the gap between upper and lower bounds in the adversarial setting, extending to bandit/partial-information settings, and determining whether O(√T) regret is achievable for fixed ρ in the experts problem. The general conversion framework, while yielding worse regret than problem-specific algorithms, provides a reusable template for other online learning problems.
