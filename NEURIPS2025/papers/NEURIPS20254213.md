## Problem

Existing hallucination detectors that exploit LLM internal representations rely on predetermined token positions (first, last, or before-last token), but hallucinated content is sparse and its location varies across free-form generations of differing lengths. This makes detection performance unstable and prone to missing the tokens where hallucinated information is actually concentrated.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254213/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes per-token hidden-state representations extracted from a single LLM layer (selected via a validation set), optionally scaled by a predictive uncertainty score (token-level probability, sentence-level perplexity, or semantic entropy across multiple generations). It outputs a hallucination score for every token in the sequence and a bag-level binary prediction; the number of salient tokens selected (k) is set dynamically as ⌊0.1 × sequence_length⌋ + 1, so the method adapts to variable-length generations without fixed positional assumptions.

## Method

HaMI reformulates hallucination detection as a Multiple Instance Learning problem: each generated sequence is a bag of token instances, with positive bags (hallucinated) containing only a few positive instances and negative bags (correct) containing all-negative instances. A 2-layer MLP detector assigns a hallucination score to every token, and the MIL loss selects the top-k highest-scoring tokens from both positive and negative bags, then maximises the L2 margin between their mean scores to jointly optimise token selection and detection end-to-end. A smoothness loss penalises large score differences between adjacent tokens, exploiting the sequential nature of generation. An uncertainty augmentation module further scales each token representation by (1 + λ·P_uncertainty), where P_uncertainty can be token-level probability, sentence-level perplexity, or semantic entropy, enriching the internal states with truthfulness signals before they enter the detector.

## Results

HaMI achieves the best AUROC across all four QA benchmarks (Trivia QA, SQuAD, NQ, BioASQ) and three LLMs (LLaMA-3.1-8B, Mistral-Nemo-Instruct 12B, LLaMA-3.3-Instruct-70B). On LLaMA-3.1-8B, HaMI reaches 0.897/0.826/0.820/0.836 AUROC, outperforming the strongest internal-representation baseline SAPLMA (0.835/0.769/0.781/0.821) by 5–8% and the strongest multi-sampling baseline MARS-SE (0.824/0.780/0.777/0.744) by 7–12%. The basic variant HaMI* (without uncertainty augmentation) already surpasses all non-external-LLM baselines, and the full HaMI gains an additional 8.1–11.9% AUROC over MARS-SE across the three models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HaMI outperforms all nine baselines on every dataset and LLM configuration. Against the strongest internal-representation baseline SAPLMA [5], HaMI gains 5.5–8.7% AUROC (e.g., 0.897 vs. 0.835 on Trivia QA with LLaMA-3.1-8B), with the gap widening for the larger 70B model. Among uncertainty-based methods, HaMI surpasses MARS-SE [6] by 8.1–11.9% and Semantic Entropy [17] by 7.4–11.5%, despite not requiring an external LLM for entailment evaluation. The basic HaMI* variant already beats all non-external-LLM baselines (Perplexity [42], CCS [9], SAPLMA [5], HaloScope [14], CED [29], LLM-Check [45]) by 2–20+ points, confirming that the MIL-based adaptive token selection alone provides a substantial advantage over fixed-token probing. The weakest baselines are HaloScope [14] and CCS [9], which rely on semi-supervised or unsupervised probing and lag HaMI by 15–25% AUROC on several datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

HaMI sits at the intersection of two established lines of hallucination detection research: internal-representation probing (SAPLMA, CCS, HaloScope, LLM-Check) and uncertainty-based scoring (Semantic Entropy, MARS, Perplexity). The key departure is from prior internal-representation methods that rely on predetermined token positions (first, last, or before-last token), which the paper shows are fragile under varying generation lengths and sparse hallucination distributions. By reformulating detection as a Multiple Instance Learning problem over token-level representations, HaMI introduces the first end-to-end joint optimisation of token selection and hallucination scoring, while also augmenting internal states with multi-level predictive uncertainty (token-level, sentence-level perplexity, and semantic consistency).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Hallucination detection is a prerequisite for safe LLM deployment in high-stakes domains, and the demonstrated robustness to generation length and cross-dataset generalisation (≤4.5% performance drop) makes HaMI practically deployable across diverse QA settings. However, the method requires white-box access to internal representations, limiting it to open-source LLMs, and the uncertainty integration strategy (simple scalar multiplication) is acknowledged as a starting point rather than an optimal fusion. Open questions include whether the MIL formulation transfers to non-QA tasks (e.g., summarisation, code generation) and whether more sophisticated learnable aggregation could further close the gap to oracle token-level supervision.
