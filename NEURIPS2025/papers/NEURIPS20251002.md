## Problem

Current review criteria for AI/ML for social impact (AISI) research disproportionately reward projects that simultaneously achieve deployment and novel ML methodology, creating incentives that undermine the sustainability of the broader research ecosystem. This narrow framing forecloses scientifically valuable contributions that advance practice without novel methods or that develop methods without immediate deployment, and it treats deployment as a 'finish line' without demanding rigorous causal evaluation of whether deployed systems actually improved participant outcomes.

## Input / Output

The paper takes as its analytical subject the current landscape of AISI review guidelines (AAAI, IJCAI), publication practices, and deployment evaluation approaches, and produces a structured set of recommendations for authors, reviewers, and publication venues. The output is organized around three contribution categories (non-method, non-deployment, deployment) and three deployment evaluation types (pilot tests, RCTs, non-randomized deployments), with specific criteria and best practices articulated for each.

## Method

The paper proposes a three-part framework for restructuring AISI evaluation standards. First, it delineates non-method contributions where researchers improve partners' use of existing ML tools, articulating scientific questions about how ML enters organizational decision processes and what problem formulations are appropriate. Second, it identifies non-deployment methodological contributions through criteria such as changing practitioners' minds about estimation strategies, designing for long-term maintainability, and establishing single-variable benchmarks that measure marginal gains of complex ML over simple predictions. Third, it imports causal inference best practices from econometrics and medical trials for deployment evaluation: preregistration, power analysis, outcome validity justification, and preregistered heterogeneity detection for RCTs; and event study designs (interrupted time series, differences-in-differences, synthetic control) for non-randomized before-after comparisons, arguing that field experiments of algorithmic systems are fundamentally causal inference problems rather than ML evaluation problems.

## Results

This is a position paper and does not report quantitative experimental results. The authors' central argument is structural: current AISI review criteria over-index on the conjunction of deployment + methodological novelty, and they propose three concrete reforms (recognizing non-method contributions, recognizing non-deployment methodological contributions, and raising rigor standards for deployment evaluations). The paper illustrates its evaluation recommendations with a qualitative figure (Figure 1) showing how naive before-after comparisons in non-randomized deployments can be confounded by pre-existing trends, motivating interrupted-time-series and event-study designs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not compare methods against baselines or report head-to-head empirical comparisons. Instead, it contrasts the current de facto review standard (deployment + novel ML method as the 'ideal project') with three alternative contribution types it argues should be recognized: (1) non-method contributions where researchers improve a partner's use of existing ML tools, (2) non-deployment methodological contributions with plausible social impact, and (3) deployed systems evaluated with higher causal-inference rigor (preregistration, power analysis, event-study designs). The paper positions its recommendations against existing venue guidelines (AAAI AISI track, IJCAI Multi-Year Track) and against best practices from economics and medicine (RCT design, preregistration, differences-in-differences), arguing that ML venues should adopt analogous standards for field experiments.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper builds on the AI-for-social-impact literature (Tambe et al. 2022, Tomašev et al. 2020, Rolnick et al. 2024) that established the field's vision of partner-driven, deployment-oriented research, but departs from the implicit 'ideal project' framing (novel method + deployment) that has permeated review guidelines at AAAI and IJCAI. It repurposes causal inference and econometrics best practices—preregistration, power analysis, event study designs (interrupted time series, differences-in-differences, synthetic control)—from fields like economics and medicine to address the underdeveloped evaluation standards for ML deployments. The paper also draws on prior work on ML reproducibility (Beam et al. 2020, Pineau et al. 2021) but argues that deployment evaluation is a fundamentally different task closer to causal inference than to computational reproducibility.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a structural incentive problem: the conflation of 'impact' with 'deployment of novel methods' distorts research choices, disadvantages partner organizations whose needs don't align with methodological novelty, and suppresses rigorous post-deployment evaluation. Its concrete recommendations—preregistration requirements, event study designs for non-randomized deployments, normalization of single-variable baselines, and finer-grained contribution criteria—provide actionable standards that venues could adopt. Open questions include whether major venues will implement these changes, how to balance rigor demands with the resource constraints of nonprofit and government partners, and whether the proposed taxonomy of contributions (non-method, non-deployment, deployment) will gain traction in hiring and tenure evaluations.
