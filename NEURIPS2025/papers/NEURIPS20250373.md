## Problem

Standard gradient descent with fixed or Polyak stepsizes treats the stepsize as a scalar, ignoring local curvature information that is readily available for certain problem classes (e.g., sums of squares of absolutely convex functions, L_p regression). While second-order methods exploit curvature, they require expensive Hessian computation. The paper asks whether one can design matrix-valued stepsizes that leverage this free local curvature information while retaining the simplicity and convergence guarantees of first-order methods.

## Input / Output

The methods require a convex function f satisfying Assumption 2.1 with a known curvature mapping C: R^d → S_+^d and smoothness constant L_C, the gradient ∇f(x_k) at each iterate, and the optimal value f*. The curvature matrix is typically diagonal (e.g., for L_p norms or sums of squares of absolutely convex functions), making per-iteration cost O(d) beyond gradient evaluation. LCD2 additionally requires solving a scalar root-finding subproblem (via Newton's method, ~5 iterations) to determine the stepsize parameter β_k, while LCD3 avoids this by using a C-induced norm projection with a closed-form solution.

## Method

The paper introduces three algorithms under a new assumption (Assumption 2.1) that generalizes convexity and L-smoothness by allowing a point-dependent curvature matrix C(x) in both the lower and upper quadratic bounds on f. LCD1 minimizes the upper bound, yielding the update x_{k+1} = x_k − [C(x_k) + L_C·I]^{-1}∇f(x_k), which reduces to GD with stepsize 1/L when C ≡ 0. LCD2 projects the current iterate onto a localization set {x : M_C^low(x; x_k) ≤ f*} using the Euclidean norm; the projection has an implicit parametric form where the scalar parameter 1/β_k is the unique root of a convex, monotonically decreasing function solvable by Newton's method. LCD3 replaces the Euclidean projection with the C(x_k)-induced norm, producing a closed-form stepsize γ_k = 1 − √(1 − 2(f(x_k)−f_*)/‖∇f(x_k)‖_{C^{-1}}²) applied to C(x_k)^{-1}∇f(x_k). Convergence theorems for LCD1 and LCD2 establish O(1/k) rates matching their scalar counterparts, and the curvature mapping is shown to be readily computable (often diagonal) for Huber loss squared, L_p norms, L_p regression, and sums of squares of absolutely convex functions.

## Results

The paper evaluates LCD1, LCD2, and LCD3 on logistic regression with L2 and L3 regularization over the a2a and mushrooms datasets (LibSVM), measuring convergence of f(x_k) − f* versus iterations and wall-clock time. LCD2 consistently outperforms GD with Polyak stepsize across all regularization strengths (λ = L·10⁻⁴, L/3·10⁻³, L·10⁻³), with the gap widening as λ increases because the curvature matrix C(x) captures more regularizer information. In the L2-regularized setting, LCD2 has a closed-form solution with the same per-iteration cost as Polyak, while in the L3-regularized setting (where the objective is no longer L-smooth), LCD2 requires ~5 rounds of an O(d) projection subroutine per step. LCD3 shows promising convergence but becomes unstable at larger λ values, and LCD1 matches other methods initially but slows due to its fixed stepsize.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GD with constant stepsize, all three LCD methods converge faster, with LCD2 and LCD3 showing the clearest advantage due to their adaptivity. Against GD with Polyak stepsize, LCD2 consistently achieves lower function values at every iteration on both a2a and mushrooms; the improvement grows with the regularization weight λ because the localization set L_C(x_k) becomes a strict subset of the half-space used by Polyak. Against Barzilai-Borwein (BB) and Malitsky-Mishchenko (MM) adaptive stepsizes, LCD2 and LCD3 outperform both baselines in the L2-regularized logistic regression experiments (Figures 1–2). In the L3-regularized setting (Figures 3–4), LCD2 again leads Polyak in both iteration and time plots, while LCD3 produces competitive results at small λ but degrades in stability as λ increases. All methods have comparable per-iteration computational cost since gradient evaluation (O(nd)) dominates, making LCD2's additional O(d) projection overhead negligible in practice.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the classical gradient descent and Polyak stepsize frameworks by introducing a new assumption (Assumption 2.1) that allows a curvature matrix C(x) to vary with the current iterate, yielding matrix-valued stepsizes without requiring Hessian computation. It departs from relative smoothness/convexity frameworks (Bauschke et al. 2017, Qu et al. 2016) where the metric matrix is fixed, and from SP2/SP2+ (Li et al. 2022) which use Hessian-based second-order information but lack stability guarantees. The key conceptual departure is that the curvature is 'local' in the sense of depending on the iterate's position, enabling the localization set to shrink adaptively and produce steps that are provably closer to the optimum than standard Polyak steps.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, theoretically grounded way to exploit readily available local curvature (e.g., from Lp norms, Huber loss, or sums of squares of absolutely convex functions) to improve first-order methods, bridging the gap between cheap gradient methods and expensive Newton/quasi-Newton approaches. However, the analysis is restricted to the deterministic setting, LCD3 lacks convergence guarantees, and the assumption's full potential remains unexplored. The most natural open question is extending these results to stochastic optimization, which would be essential for practical deep learning applications where the methods are most needed.
