## Problem

Large theoretical solvation datasets (e.g., CombiSolv-QM with ~1M samples) are noisy, redundant, and imbalanced, while high-quality experimental datasets are small and limited in coverage, making direct transfer learning costly and poorly calibrated. Existing core-subset selection methods from vision/NLP either overemphasize rare samples or require expensive per-epoch re-evaluation, and conventional merged-graph GNNs cannot distinguish the directional nature of solute-in-solvent versus solvent-in-solute interactions.

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes solute and solvent SMILES strings (converted to 2D molecular graphs via RDKit) together with PubChem/GPT-4o text descriptions, which are jointly encoded by MolCA into concatenated graph+text embeddings for the CSIE selection stage. The AMGNN stage operates on a merged molecular graph whose nodes carry RDKit-derived atomic features (species, valence, partial charge, etc.), bond features (type, conjugation, ring size), and a global molecular descriptor vector. The final output is a scalar dissolution free energy ΔG_solv predicted by an MLP head, trained with MSE loss.

## Method

CSIE computes per-sample information gain as the average cosine distance to HNSW neighbors, then iteratively refines the core subset via an EM-inspired loop: the E-step extracts the top-k% highest-gain samples as an unbiased anchor set, and the M-step re-inserts them into the HNSW structure before randomly inserting the remainder and recomputing gains, guaranteeing monotonically non-decreasing total gain and submodular diminishing-returns properties. AMGNN constructs an asymmetric merged graph with 2×N^a×N^b bidirectional directed edges between solute and solvent atoms, each carrying learnable weights, so that message passing from solute→solvent and solvent→solute follows distinct pathways. A binary mask vector randomly drops intermolecular edges during node updates to prevent over-smoothing, while four learnable β parameters gate the update magnitude to preserve original atomic information. The model is first pretrained on the CSIE-selected core subset of the theoretical dataset, then fine-tuned on the small experimental set via transfer learning.

## Results

On the primary CombiSolv-Exp test set with random split, AMGNN trained on the CSIE-selected 1% core subset (QM-mini) from the 1M-sample theoretical CombiSolv-QM dataset achieves MAE 0.191 (±0.011) and RMSE 0.299 (±0.010), outperforming the SOTA baseline MMGNN (MAE 0.200, RMSE 0.317) by 4.5% and 5.6% respectively. Under solvent-split evaluation, AMGNN+CSIE reduces MAE for water from 1.921 (direct training) to 1.421, and for solute-split on oxygen-containing molecules from 1.860 to 1.251. Ablation on CombiSolv-Exp shows that removing CSIE degrades 20%-trainset MAE from 0.777 to 1.486, while removing the asymmetric edge design increases it to 0.833.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AMGNN+CSIE achieves the best MAE and RMSE across all 12 baselines in both direct-training and transfer-learning settings on CombiSolv-Exp. In the transfer setting (QM-mini→CombiSolv-Exp), AMGNN (MAE 0.191) beats the closest competitor MMGNN (0.200) by 4.5%, CGIB (0.211) by 9.5%, and CIGIN (0.219) by 12.8%; weaker baselines like GAT (0.853) and D-MPNN (0.402) are far behind. The CSIE framework itself provides consistent gains for every model: for example, D-MPNN improves from MAE 0.456 to 0.402, and GAT from 0.970 to 0.853, confirming that the data-selection benefit is model-agnostic. On the four independent experimental datasets (FreeSolv, CompSol, Abraham, MNSol), AMGNN achieves the lowest MAE in all four (0.531, 0.159, 0.188, 0.352) and the lowest RMSE on Abraham and MNSol, with statistically significant improvements (p < 0.05) over the second-best model on every dataset. In the cross-domain band-gap prediction task (NREL-MatDB), CSIE-selected 80% core subset pretraining yields MAE 0.4583, outperforming direct training (0.5784) by 0.12 eV and the next-best selection method Margin (0.5112) by 0.05 eV.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes core subset selection—a paradigm well-developed in computer vision and NLP (Sorscher et al., Sener et al.)—for molecular chemistry, grounding the information-gain computation in HNSW graph insertion dynamics rather than loss-based or embedding-distance heuristics. The iterative refinement borrows the EM algorithm structure to mitigate insertion-order bias, and the submodular framing provides a theoretical guarantee of diminishing returns. On the modeling side, AMGNN directly extends the merged-graph architecture of MMGNN (Du et al., IJCAI 2024) by replacing undirected intermolecular edges with learnable bidirectional directed edges, a minimal but principled modification to capture the asymmetry of dissolution. The pipeline also depends on MolCA for multimodal molecular embeddings, positioning the work at the intersection of graph neural networks, information-theoretic data selection, and transfer learning from quantum-chemical to experimental data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in demonstrating that a 1% core subset of a 1-million-sample theoretical dataset can match or exceed the transfer performance of the full dataset, dramatically reducing training cost and deployment burden for chemists with limited experimental data. The asymmetric graph design addresses a genuine structural blind spot in prior merged-graph models (solute-in-solvent vs. solvent-in-solute ambiguity), and the CSIE framework generalizes beyond solvation to band-gap prediction, suggesting broader applicability. However, the validation is concentrated on solvation free energy with a small supplementary band-gap experiment (52 test samples), and the reliance on MolCA embeddings for information-gain estimation introduces a dependency on a pre-trained multimodal model that may not transfer to all chemical domains. Open questions include whether the HNSW-based information gain can be computed without external embedding models, and whether the asymmetric edge design extends to multi-component or reaction systems beyond binary solute-solvent pairs.
