## Problem

Existing AI safety and digital regulation frameworks (EU AI Act, DSA, Online Safety Act) target specific features like infinite scroll or protect specific groups like children, but none address the unique risk posed by real-time generative AI that synthesizes novel, hyper-personalized content on-the-fly to maximize engagement. The paper argues that as generative models reach real-time inference latency and are coupled with RL-driven personalization, they will create a new class of addictive digital content ('digital heroin') that current regulations cannot anticipate or constrain.

## Input / Output

The systems under scrutiny take text prompts, real-time user interaction signals (clicks, swipe velocity, gaze tracking, facial expressions), and compact user preference embeddings as input, and produce personalized text, images, video, and audio generated on-the-fly at near-real-time latency. Unlike traditional recommendation systems that select from a fixed content library, these systems synthesize entirely new content for each user, with the generation loop closing in seconds as the model updates its internal state after every micro-interaction, enabling continuous adaptation to the user's current emotional and attentional state.

## Method

This position paper synthesizes interdisciplinary evidence—neuroscientific studies of dopamine-driven reward pathways, behavioral psychology of variable reinforcement, and technical benchmarks on generative AI inference speed and cost (e.g., HunyuanVideo render times dropping from 5.9 min on A100 to 22 s on GB200)—to argue that real-time hyper-personalized generative AI meets the core criteria for fostering behavioral addiction. The central contribution is a proposed 'Designated Addictive System' (DAS) regulatory overlay, modeled on tiered risk frameworks like the EU AI Act, which would mandate pre-market safety cases, annual third-party addiction audits, design-friction requirements (session caps, break reminders, one-tap de-personalization controls), age-based protections with privacy-preserving age assurance, and a Pigouvian 'attention tax' on sessions exceeding 60 minutes. The paper further outlines practical guidelines for the ML community including addiction-risk benchmarks with simulated vulnerable users, multi-objective RL with well-being constraints and safety bounds, interpretability methods for detecting exploitation of micro-signals like fatigue or hesitancy, and standardized red-teaming protocols covering teen-risk and neurodivergent user personas.

## Results

This is a position paper with no experimental results or method comparisons. The key quantitative evidence cited includes: GPU rendering cost for a 1-second 720p HunyuanVideo clip dropping from $0.27 (A100, 354 s) to $0.06 (GB200, 22 s), a 16× speedup and 4.5× cost reduction; TikTok users averaging ~95 min/day; Instagram Reels generating 22% more interaction than standard posts; adolescent depression risk rising 13% per additional hour of daily social-media use; and digital interruptions reducing workplace productivity by ~28%. The paper's central claim is qualitative: real-time generative AI coupled with engagement-maximizing RL will create addiction dynamics analogous to substance abuse, warranting regulation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not propose a new method and therefore contains no baseline comparisons or head-to-head method evaluations. The only quantitative comparison (Table 1) tracks hardware-generation progress (A100 → H100 → GB200) for a fixed HunyuanVideo workload, illustrating the trajectory toward real-time, near-zero-cost video generation rather than comparing competing algorithms. The paper's argumentative 'comparisons' are analogical: it maps real-time generative AI addiction risk onto well-studied substance-addiction frameworks (dopamine surges, variable-ratio reinforcement, neuroadaptation) and onto prior public-health crises (tobacco, opioids, khat), arguing that the 24/7, hyper-personalized nature of generative feeds makes the potential harm more pervasive than any of those precedents.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes three largely separate literatures—neuroscience of behavioral addiction, the rapid progress in real-time generative video/image models (HunyuanVideo, Veo, Sora, SF-V, T2V-Turbo), and reinforcement-learning-based personalization (Monolith, Personalized RLHF, LoRA adapters)—to argue that their convergence creates a qualitatively new regulatory problem. It departs from prior social-media addiction research by emphasizing that the feedback loop is no longer bounded by human content-production speed, and from existing AI governance frameworks (EU AI Act, UK Online Safety Act) by proposing a distinct 'Designated Addictive System' category that targets real-time generative personalization specifically. The paper does not introduce a new algorithm or benchmark; rather, it repurposes the substance-regulation playbook (age gates, excise taxes, liability) for a novel technological context.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's main contribution is a concrete, multi-layered policy proposal (DAS labeling, design-friction mandates, attention tax, addiction-risk red-teaming protocols) that could shape how the ML community and regulators approach the next generation of generative platforms. Its key limitation is that the central causal claim—that real-time generative personalization will produce addiction effects 'analogous to substance abuse'—remains speculative, as no clinical or longitudinal study has yet validated this specific mechanism; the argument extrapolates from social-media addiction data and hardware cost curves. Open questions include whether the 'digital heroin' framing is empirically justified or rhetorically overblown, how cross-border enforcement would work in practice, and whether the proposed regulatory overhead would stifle beneficial applications (personalized tutoring, creative tools) that share the same technical architecture.
