## Problem

Computing the fairness-performance Pareto front for fair representations has required training complex, high-dimensional representation models (GANs, VAEs, normalizing flows) via non-convex optimization prone to local minima, making it difficult to verify whether a given method's trade-off curve is near-optimal. No prior approach could compute the true Pareto front in a model-independent way, and existing reductions in fair classification relied critically on the special structure of accuracy loss, precluding extension to general concave performance measures.

## Input / Output

The method consumes tabular data with features X ∈ R^d, a binary sensitive attribute A, and a target variable Y. Crucially, the original high-dimensional feature space is never discretized; instead, calibrated per-group probability estimators map each sample to the probability simplex Δ_Y, which is then discretized into L bins (trivially L points on [0,1] for binary Y). The output is the complete Pareto front curve: for every fairness threshold γ ∈ [0,1], the minimum achievable prediction error under the total-variation fairness constraint, represented as a continuous curve rather than isolated operating points.

## Method

The approach rests on three theoretical results that decouple Pareto front computation from representation learning. First, a Factorization Lemma shows the data can be mapped to the small space Δ_Y × A via the Bayes optimal classifier without losing any information relevant to the trade-off. Second, an Invertibility Theorem proves that optimal representations can be restricted to a canonical form where each representation point has at most one parent per sensitive group, bounding the representation space to S_0 × S_1 × [k] for a small constant k. Third, these properties yield MIFPO, a concave minimization problem with linear constraints over the transition probabilities, solved via the DCCP (Disciplined Convex-Concave Programming) framework. The full pipeline requires only training simple calibrated classifiers (XGBoost + isotonic regression) per group, discretizing the probability simplex, and solving the resulting compact discrete optimization—no neural network representation learning is involved.

## Results

The paper evaluates MIFPO on three standard fairness benchmarks (Health, ACSIncome-CA, ACSIncome-US) by plotting the full fairness-accuracy Pareto front (statistical parity distance vs. prediction accuracy). MIFPO's Pareto front is shown as a continuous curve and consistently achieves performance equal to or superior to all five baseline methods across almost all operating points, effectively serving as an upper bound on the achievable fairness-performance trade-off. The method uses missclassification error as the performance metric and total variation distance as the fairness measure, with k=5 representation points and L bins for discretization. All experiments complete in under a day on a standard desktop, and results are deterministic (std ≤ 1e-5 across runs).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MIFPO is compared against five state-of-the-art fair representation methods: CVIB (Moyer et al., 2019), FCRL (Gupta et al., 2021), FNF (Balunović et al., 2022b), sIPM (Kim et al., 2023), and Fare (Jovanović et al., 2023). Across all three datasets (Health, ACSIncome-CA, ACSIncome-US), MIFPO's Pareto front dominates or matches every baseline at nearly all fairness levels, confirming its role as a theoretical upper bound. The baselines are represented as individual points (each corresponding to a different hyperparameter configuration), while MIFPO traces the complete optimal curve, providing a methodological advantage in characterizing the full trade-off rather than isolated operating points. The paper also compares MIFPO against fair-classification methods in supplementary experiments, where it similarly serves as an upper bound. No baseline is reported to outperform MIFPO at any operating point on the main benchmarks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper generalizes the Bayes-optimal-classifier-based reduction from Xian et al. (2023) and Wang et al. (2023), which were restricted to fair classification with accuracy loss, to the broader setting of fair representations with arbitrary concave performance measures. The key theoretical departures are the Factorization Lemma (reducing the representation space to Δ_Y) and the Invertibility Theorem (showing optimal representations can be taken in a canonical finite form), which together enable the MIFPO formulation as a compact concave minimization problem solvable via DCCP. This stands in contrast to the dominant paradigm in fair representation learning (GANs, VAEs, normalizing flows, adversarial training) that requires training complex high-dimensional models with non-convex optimization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The model-independent Pareto front computation provides a principled upper bound against which any fair representation or classification method can be benchmarked, addressing a practical gap where it was previously unclear whether a given method's fairness-performance curve was near-optimal. The ability to work with any concave loss (not just accuracy) broadens applicability beyond prior reduction-based approaches. Key limitations include restriction to binary sensitive attributes and binary targets, discretization of the simplex Δ_Y, and evaluation on only three tabular datasets; extending to multi-valued attributes and multi-label targets would increase problem size and remains open. The paper also raises the fundamental question of how scalable Pareto estimation can be made given access to the Bayes classifier, in terms of |Y| and |A|.
