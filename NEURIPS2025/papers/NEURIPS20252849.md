## Problem

Language models are entropy miscalibrated: their per-step entropy over generations grows with sequence length due to error accumulation, while log loss on human text remains roughly flat. Standard mitigations like top-k/top-p truncation reduce entropy but sacrifice diversity, which is critical for test-time scaling and synthetic data generation. The paper asks two questions: does miscalibration improve automatically with model scale, and is it theoretically possible to calibrate without the diversity tradeoff?

## Input / Output

The empirical evaluation takes a pre-trained autoregressive language model (Qwen2.5, Llama 3, Llama 2, or Pythia, ranging from 0.5B to 72B parameters) and a 128-token context prompt, then generates up to 1024 tokens to measure per-step entropy versus per-token log loss on 5000 examples. The theoretical calibration procedure (Algorithm 1) takes a model p̂, generation length T, vocabulary V, a black-box future entropy fitting algorithm, and produces per-step adjustment parameters α₁,…,α_T along with future entropy predictors f̂₂,…,f̂_{T+1}, yielding a calibrated model whose per-step entropy matches its log loss within O(δ+ε).

## Method

The paper contributes three linked results. First, a simplified theoretical setting models error accumulation as the model generating a singleton token (seen only once in training) from a power-law distribution, showing the per-step derailing probability scales as m^{1/α−1} where α is the power-law exponent — near-zero scaling for text (α≈1) and moderate scaling for code (α≈1.5). Second, empirical measurements across four model families confirm these predictions: fitted scaling exponents are around −0.05 to −0.13 for text datasets and −0.3 for code, meaning larger models accumulate error at nearly the same rate as smaller ones. Third, the paper proves a polynomial-time calibration procedure that adjusts each candidate token's probability via a future-entropy-weighted reweighting (a first-order approximation of globally normalized temperature scaling, also derivable in the MaxEnt RL framework), showing that under an assumption of tractable future entropy regression, one can calibrate entropy to match log loss while strictly preserving or improving log loss — establishing that the diversity tradeoff is not theoretically inevitable.

## Results

The paper's headline finding is that entropy miscalibration in language models improves very slowly with scale: fitted scaling exponents for calibration error vs. model size are approximately 0.0 for WikiText and WritingPrompts (older model families) and −0.13 (newer families), meaning a 10× reduction in calibration error would require a 10^10× increase in data. For CodeContests, the exponent is −0.2 to −0.35, indicating moderate improvement with scale. These empirical exponents closely match theoretical predictions derived from the power-law exponent of each dataset (0.918 for WikiText, 1.114 for WritingPrompts, 1.5 for CodeContests). Additionally, the paper proves that a future-entropy-based calibration procedure can reduce entropy calibration error to O(δ+ε) per step while preserving (or improving) log loss, showing the quality-diversity tradeoff is not theoretically inevitable.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against competing calibration methods in a head-to-head fashion; instead, it compares theoretical scaling predictions against empirical measurements across four model families (Qwen2.5, Llama 3, Llama 2, Pythia) and three datasets. The empirical scaling exponents align closely with the theoretical predictions from the singleton-mass analysis, with heavier-tailed text distributions (α≈1) yielding near-zero scaling and the less heavy-tailed code distribution (α≈1.5) yielding moderate scaling. Among mitigation strategies, the paper compares temperature reduction (0.95, 0.9, 0.85, 0.8) against instruction tuning: both reduce entropy and increase log loss (i.e., calibrate at the cost of diversity), but instruction tuning has a larger and more size-dependent effect, with the largest models ending up overconfident (negative calibration error). The theoretical future-entropy scaling procedure is shown to dominate both truncation and instruction tuning in principle, as it calibrates entropy while preserving log loss, though it is not practically implementable.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the entropy calibration framework introduced by Braverman et al. (2020), who first formalized the problem and showed that globally normalized temperature scaling can calibrate entropy while preserving log loss, but is intractable to compute. The authors draw on classic results on singleton mass in power-law distributions (Good, 1953; Karlin, 1967) to derive a scaling law for miscalibration, and connect their proposed future entropy adjustment to both a first-order approximation of global temperature scaling and the MaxEnt RL framework (Ziebart et al., 2008). The empirical study builds on the error accumulation / teacher forcing literature and the distribution truncation methods (top-k, top-p, min-p) that have become standard practice, positioning entropy calibration as a quantitative formalization of these well-known phenomena.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that entropy miscalibration barely improves with scale for text (scaling exponent ≈ 0) is practically significant: it explains why even 70B+ models still require similar truncation as smaller models, and suggests that simply scaling up will not resolve error accumulation for heavy-tailed data. The theoretical proof that calibration without a diversity tradeoff is possible (under a black-box fitting assumption) is important for synthetic data generation and test-time scaling, where diversity is critical. However, the proposed procedure is explicitly impractical, the power-law-to-scaling-exponent relationship is validated on only three datasets, and the assumption that one can fit accurate future entropy predictors remains untested empirically, leaving a substantial gap between theory and deployable practice.
