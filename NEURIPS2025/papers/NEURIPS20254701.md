## Problem

Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) based on Mixture-of-Experts architectures suffer from cognitive inefficiencies such as overthinking and underthinking, yet existing remedies—preference optimization, decoding penalties, and prompt engineering—require additional training, labeled data, or complex heuristics. This paper asks whether the sparse expert routing in MoE models reveals a small set of 'cognitive experts' whose selective amplification at inference time can improve reasoning depth and accuracy without any additional training.

## Input / Output

The method takes a standard text reasoning prompt (e.g., an AIME math problem or GPQA science question) and the MoE model's per-token expert routing weights as input. A one-time identification step requires a single forward pass over a small domain-specific dataset (30–198 problems) to compute nPMI scores between thinking-marker tokens and each expert. At inference, the output is the model's usual chain-of-thought text and final answer, but with the routing weights of the top-2 cognitive experts scaled by a multiplier β (default 64) before the weighted sum in each MoE layer, without renormalization of the remaining expert weights.

## Method

The core mechanism is a two-stage, training-free inference-time intervention. First, cognitive experts are identified by computing normalized Pointwise Mutual Information (nPMI) between each expert's activation and a set of thinking-marker tokens (think, /think, 'Alternatively'), with a signed weighting scheme (+1 for think initiation, −1 for termination and alternative-switching) that prioritizes experts specialized in initiating rather than merely terminating reasoning. Second, during generation, the gating weights of the top-l (typically l=2) identified experts are multiplied by a steering factor β whenever those experts are selected by the router, while all other expert weights remain unchanged and no renormalization is applied. The key insight is that a very small number of experts (as few as two, e.g., layer-39/expert-182 and layer-29/expert-126 in DeepSeek-R1) are consistently co-activated with thinking tokens across domains, and moderately amplifying their contribution (β=4–128) steers the model toward deeper, more committed reasoning—improving AIME accuracy from 73.3% to 83.3% while reducing average token count—whereas excessive amplification (β≥256) collapses generation into repetitive noise.

## Results

On AIME24, RICE (reinforcing two cognitive experts with multiplier 64) boosts DeepSeek-R1 from 73.3% to 83.3% accuracy while reducing average token usage from 9,219 to 8,317. On the unseen AIME25 benchmark, accuracy improves from 63.3% to 73.3% for DeepSeek-R1 and from 66.7% to 73.3% for Qwen3-235B. Across GPQA Diamond domains (math, physics, chemistry, biology), the average accuracy rises from 73.4% to 75.6%, with the largest gain in math (+10.0 pp). On ArenaHard (instruction-following), RICE maintains or slightly improves performance (91.0% → 92.0–94.0%), confirming no degradation of general capabilities.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RICE outperforms the strongest baseline TIP_t [4] (a decoding-constraint method that penalizes  tokens) by 3.3 pp on AIME24 (83.3% vs. 80.0%) and by 2.0 pp in average accuracy across AIME24+25 (78.7% vs. 76.7%). Both prompt-engineering variants (Prompt_before and Prompt_after) trail RICE by 6.6 pp on AIME24 and by 3.7–7.0 pp in average accuracy. On AIME25, RICE ties TIP_t and Prompt_before at 73.3%, while Prompt_after lags at 66.7%. Notably, RICE achieves its AIME24 gain while using ~10% fewer tokens than the baseline, whereas the prompt and decoding-constraint baselines do not report comparable efficiency improvements. The paper also shows that reinforcing two randomly selected experts yields no consistent benefit (70.0–76.7% across multipliers), confirming that the nPMI-identified cognitive experts are specifically responsible for the observed gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of MoE expert-specialization research and inference-time reasoning steering. It builds on the observation that DeepSeek-R1's routing achieves semantic specialization beyond token-dependent patterns [23, 24], and on the overthinking/underthinking literature [3, 4] that motivates inference-time interventions. However, it departs from prior steering methods (prompting, decoding penalties like TIP [4], steering vectors [50–53]) and from heavier MoE-specific approaches (gradient-based expert re-mixing [58], SAE-based interpretability [37]) by proposing a single-forward-pass nPMI metric to identify a tiny set of 'cognitive experts' and a scalar weight multiplier as the sole intervention—no training, no auxiliary models, no complex heuristics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical appeal is strong: identifying two experts via one forward pass and multiplying their routing weights by a constant is trivially deployable on any MoE LRM, making it a low-barrier tool for practitioners who cannot afford retraining or SAE training. The cross-domain transferability of the same two experts (math → physics → chemistry) suggests a genuinely shared cognitive mechanism, which could inform future interpretability work. Key limitations temper the impact: evaluation is restricted to two models and small benchmarks (AIME has 30 problems per year, no error bars reported), the nPMI metric is a coarse statistical proxy that may miss higher-order expert interactions, and the method's behavior on non-MoE architectures or on tasks without explicit think tokens remains untested.
