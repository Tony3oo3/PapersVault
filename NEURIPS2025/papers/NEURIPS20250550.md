## Problem

Vanilla softmax policy gradient can require exponentially many gradient steps in the effective horizon (1−γ)^(−1) to converge, as demonstrated by explicit lower-bound MDP constructions. The paper asks how much of the MDP's Markovian structure can be exploited directly within a policy gradient framework to eliminate this exponential dependence, and whether a principled integration of dynamic programming with gradient-based policy search can provably circumvent the worst-case committal behavior of standard PG.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250550/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes a finite tabular MDP (state space S, action space A, transition kernel p, bounded reward r, discount γ∈(0,1)), an initial state distribution μ over S, and a differentiable policy parametrization class (tabular softmax in the main analysis). It outputs a stationary policy π̂* approximating the optimal policy, obtained as the first element of a stored sequence of per-epoch policies. The horizon H is not fixed a priori but grows dynamically; convergence is triggered when the relative value improvement between consecutive iterations falls below a user-specified threshold ε.

## Method

DynPG decomposes the infinite-horizon MDP into a sequence of contextual bandit problems by incrementally extending the training horizon: at iteration h, a new decision epoch is prepended to the current horizon, and the policy for that epoch is optimized via gradient ascent while all subsequent actions are sampled from previously trained (and therefore fixed) policies. This design reduces gradient-estimation variance because rewards-to-go are computed under stationary future policies, and it yields a more benign optimization landscape per epoch (a contextual bandit rather than a full MDP). The convergence analysis employs a four-layer error decomposition—approximation, truncation, accumulated optimization, and stationary-policy error—and under the tabular softmax parametrization with exact gradients, establishes a global convergence rate of O((1−γ)^(−4) ε^(−1) log((1−γ)^(−2) ε^(−1))) gradient steps for the overall error, which is polynomial in the effective horizon and thus breaks the exponential lower bound for vanilla PG.

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 4.8) establishes that softmax DynPG requires O((1-γ)^(-4)ε^(-1)log((1-γ)^(-2)ε^(-1))) gradient steps for the overall error and O((1-γ)^(-3)ε^(-1)log((1-γ)^(-1)ε^(-1))) for the value function error, both polynomial in the effective horizon (1-γ)^(-1). In the numerical experiment (Figure 2), DynPG achieves a success probability near 1.0 for reaching ε=0.01 suboptimality, while vanilla PG plateaus at approximately 0.8 under the same interaction budget. The key distinction is that DynPG's convergence rate has no unknown model-dependent constant C(γ), in contrast to vanilla PG whose rate includes C(γ) that can be exponential in (1-γ)^(-1).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares DynPG against two key baselines from the softmax policy gradient literature. Against the upper bound of Mei et al. [16], DynPG improves the γ-dependence from O(C(γ)(1-γ)^(-6)ε^(-1)) to O((1-γ)^(-4)ε^(-1)log((1-γ)^(-2)ε^(-1))), eliminating the unknown model-dependent constant C(γ) that can be arbitrarily large. Against the exponential lower bound of Li et al. [15], which shows vanilla PG requires |S|^(2^Ω((1-γ)^(-1))) steps, DynPG provably breaks this barrier by scaling only polynomially in (1-γ)^(-1). In the empirical comparison (Figure 2), DynPG outperforms vanilla PG on the committal-behavior MDP: after ~400 interactions both methods perform similarly, but vanilla PG stagnates at ~0.8 success probability while DynPG continues to improve toward 1.0. The authors note that DynPG is not universally superior—on simpler environments where vanilla PG does not exhibit committal behavior, the more complex DynPG algorithm would not be expected to outperform it.

## Relation to Prior Work

DynPG extends the Policy Search by Dynamic Programming (PSDP) lineage [Bagnell et al., Kakade] by replacing deterministic policy search with stochastic parameterized policies optimized via policy gradient, and extends Klein et al.'s finite-horizon dynamic policy gradient to the infinite-horizon discounted setting. The convergence analysis adapts the gradient domination framework of Mei et al. [16] for softmax PG but with an improved smoothness constant, while the algorithmic design explicitly contrasts with Li et al.'s [15] exponential lower bound construction for vanilla PG. Unlike actor-critic methods that use DP only indirectly through a learned value function, DynPG applies the Bellman operator directly to shift trained policies forward in time, reducing each iteration to a contextual bandit problem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key result—that DynPG's convergence rate is polynomial in the effective horizon (1-γ)^(-1) rather than exponential—provides the first theoretical guarantee that a PG-based method can provably avoid the worst-case exponential blowup identified by Li et al. This opens a principled path toward hybrid DP-PG algorithms that inherit the model-free flexibility of gradient methods while exploiting MDP structure. However, the analysis is restricted to tabular softmax policies with exact gradients, the empirical validation is a single toy MDP, and the DynAC variant (which addresses memory concerns for large problems) lacks convergence guarantees. Open questions include whether the log-factor in the rate is tight, whether the polynomial dependence extends to function-approximation settings, and how DynPG interacts with modern deep RL algorithms like PPO or TRPO.
