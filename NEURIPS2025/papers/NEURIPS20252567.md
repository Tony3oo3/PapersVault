## Problem

Whether vision-language models exhibit the bouba-kiki effect—the robust human tendency to associate round-sounding pseudowords with round shapes and sharp-sounding ones with jagged shapes—remains unresolved due to conflicting prior evidence. Existing computational tests (e.g., embedding-similarity scores) do not closely mirror the controlled image-pair matching paradigms used in human experiments, making direct human-model comparison impossible and leaving open whether apparent associations reflect true cross-modal grounding or merely language-specific statistical regularities.

## Input / Output

The method takes two types of visual input: individual shape images (curved or jagged, sourced from prior human studies or generated by connecting random points with curved vs. straight lines) and concatenated image pairs (two shapes side-by-side in a single image for Grad-CAM analysis). Textual input consists of pseudoword labels from four sources (classic bouba-kiki/maluma-takete pairs, English sharp/curved adjectives as a baseline, 162 two-syllable pseudowords built from sonorant/plosive consonants and rounded/non-rounded vowels, and three-syllable pseudowords from Alper and Averbuch-Elor), each embedded in one of ten sentence-level prompts. Outputs are per-label probability scores (with the argmax label treated as the model's preference) and Grad-CAM attention heatmaps, from which the proportion of congruent shape-word matches is extracted and analyzed via Bayesian regression.

## Method

The paper employs two complementary evaluation methods on frozen CLIP variants (ResNet-50 and ViT), both closely modelled after human bouba-kiki experiments. First, a probability-based method computes CLIP's likelihood for every candidate label given each image and selects the highest-probability label as the model's choice, testing whether both labels in a pair are matched congruently (i.e., bouba→round AND kiki→jagged) across 10 prompts and multiple image pairs. Second, a novel Grad-CAM-based method concatenates each image pair into a single image, computes attention maps from the last attention block (ViT) or final convolutional layer (ResNet) by taking gradients of the cosine-similarity target score with respect to attention weights, sums attention over each shape's spatial region, and selects the shape receiving more total attention—directly simulating the Cwiek et al. (2022) paradigm where humans choose between two shapes after hearing one word. All analyses use Bayesian regression (brms, 4×4000 iterations) to test whether congruent-match proportions exceed the 25% chance level, with fixed effects for model, prompt, and image/label pair. The key finding is that neither model consistently exceeds chance for pseudowords, and Grad-CAM reveals attention concentrated on shape centres and background rather than on the curved or jagged edges that would be necessary for a genuine bouba-kiki association.

## Results

Neither CLIP variant (ResNet-50 or ViT) exhibits a reliable bouba-kiki effect: on the Grad-CAM shape-word matching task, both models perform at or near the 25% chance level for the classic bouba/kiki and maluma/takete pairs, far below the robust human performance reported by Cwiek et al. (2022). In the probability-based evaluation, pseudoword matching is mostly at chance across all prompts and label sets, while the English adjective baseline reaches only ~80% correct matching. ResNet shows a general (non-discriminative) preference for round shapes across all word types, and ViT can distinguish shapes for English adjectives but collapses to chance for all pseudoword sets. Attention heatmaps confirm that models focus on shape centres and background rather than on the curved or jagged edges that drive the human effect.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is against human performance on the identical bouba-kiki and maluma-takete matching task as reported by Cwiek et al. (2022), where English-speaking participants show a strong, consistent cross-modal association well above the 25% chance level (Figure 3, green line). Both CLIP variants fall markedly short: their Grad-CAM-based matching accuracy hovers around 25–52% depending on the quantification method, with no reliable above-chance signal for the classic word pairs. The English adjective baseline (~80% correct) confirms that the models can in principle distinguish curved from jagged shapes, ruling out a perceptual limitation and isolating the failure to the cross-modal (sound-symbolic) association. The paper also contrasts its findings with Alper and Averbuch-Elor (2023), who reported strong bouba-kiki evidence in CLIP embedding spaces; the authors argue that embedding-similarity scores capture language-specific regularities rather than true sensory mappings, a claim supported by Iida and Funakura (2024) who found no effect in Japanese VLMs despite Japanese being rich in sound symbolism.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the probability-based evaluation of the bouba-kiki effect in VLMs introduced by Verhoef et al. (2024) by testing a more comprehensive set of pseudowords (including newly constructed two-syllable items and Alper-style three-syllable items) across ten prompts, and by adding Grad-CAM as a novel interpretability lens on visual attention in shape-word matching. It deliberately departs from the embedding-similarity methodology of Alper and Averbuch-Elor (2023), arguing that geometric/phonetic scores in CLIP's embedding space capture language-specific regularities rather than true sensory mappings—a concern corroborated by Iida and Funakura's (2024) null results in Japanese VLMs. Instead, the paper mirrors psycholinguistic paradigms (paired minimal-difference images, congruency scoring) to enable direct quantitative comparison with human data from Cwiek et al. (2022).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Because CLIP (especially its ViT variant) serves as the frozen visual backbone in many state-of-the-art VLMs (BLIP2, LLaVA, Molmo, InternVL), the finding that it lacks consistent bouba-kiki associations raises questions about whether downstream alignment layers can recover human-like cross-modal intuitions without explicit fine-tuning. The Grad-CAM analysis adds a mechanistic dimension: models attend to shape centers and low-informative backgrounds rather than the curved/jagged edges that drive human associations, suggesting the contrastive training objective and cosine-similarity alignment do not promote phonetic-to-visual feature binding. Open questions include whether audio-visual models or models with embodied interaction data would show stronger effects, and whether the null result generalizes beyond CLIP to other architectures. The paper also highlights that individual modalities remain separable (confirmed via t-SNE), localizing the failure to cross-modal integration rather than unimodal representation.
