## Problem

Vision transformer computation scales quadratically with image resolution, making high-resolution recognition prohibitively expensive. Existing token reduction methods waste computation by processing all tokens before pruning, while token selection methods require complex, expensive per-task optimization. The paper addresses how to learn which high-resolution patches to process—without ever computing all of them—using only self-supervised supervision that transfers across tasks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252910/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a high-resolution image (e.g., 994×994 or 1008×1008 px) and a downsampled low-resolution version (e.g., 154×154 px). The selector processes only the low-res input to produce a 2D importance map over the full high-res patch grid; the extractor then receives just the top-k selected high-res patches (k can be as low as ~10% of total patches) plus the selector's low-res global tokens. The output is a full-resolution representation—a class token and spatially interpolated patch tokens—compatible with any standard task head (classification, segmentation, etc.).

## Method

LookWhere factorizes adaptive computation into a lightweight selector (truncated 3-layer ViT on low-res input) that predicts where to look, and a full-capacity extractor (ViT-B) that processes only the top-k selected high-res patches to predict what to see. The two are jointly pretrained via 'what-where distillation' from a frozen DINOv2 teacher: the selector is trained with KL divergence on the teacher's last-layer patch-token attention map, while the extractor is trained with MSE on the teacher's class and patch token representations. Crucially, the extractor never sees the full high-res input during pretraining or inference—it reconstructs the complete representation from sparse patches plus the selector's global tokens, effectively performing masked reconstruction of the teacher's features. For downstream tasks, only the extractor and a linear task head are finetuned; the selector remains frozen and generalizes across diverse tasks and resolutions without per-task retraining.

## Results

On ImageNet-1K classification, LookWhere (k=128) achieves 80.3%/83.0% top-1 accuracy (ViT-S/ViT-B) at 9.5/3.2 K im/s, the best accuracy-speed trade-off among all adaptive computation methods, with 1.36× faster throughput than the second-fastest method (DTEM). On high-resolution Traffic Signs (994×994 px), LookWhere (k=504) reaches 94.6% accuracy while reducing FLOPs by 34× and inference time by 6× compared to IPS. On ADE20K segmentation, LookWhere (k=512) achieves 40.6% mIoU at 2.0 K im/s, outperforming DTEM (r=0.5) at 38.9% mIoU and 0.7 K im/s.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DINOv2 (its own teacher), LookWhere matches or exceeds accuracy on high-resolution tasks (94.6% vs. 94.0% on Traffic Signs, 97.3% vs. 97.6% on Billiards) while processing only 10% of patches, yielding 17× fewer FLOPs and 25× faster inference on Traffic Signs. Among token reduction methods, LookWhere outperforms PiToMe (80.3% vs. 79.8% on ImageNet ViT-S), DTEM (80.3% vs. 79.4%), and ATC (80.3% vs. 79.9%) on accuracy, while also being significantly faster than ATC (9.5 vs. 0.4 K im/s) due to avoiding clustering operations. Among token selection methods, LookWhere rivals IPS in accuracy on Traffic Signs (94.6% vs. 96.3%) but with 34× fewer FLOPs and 6× less inference time; it substantially outperforms DPS (94.6% vs. 83.6% on Traffic Signs, 89.0% vs. 85.4% on Birds) at comparable or lower FLOPs. On ADE20K segmentation, LookWhere (k=512) beats DTEM (r=0.5) by 1.7 mIoU at 2.9× higher throughput (2.0 vs. 0.7 K im/s). LTRP achieves 82.8% on ImageNet ViT-B with 18.3 G FLOPs, while LookWhere reaches 83.0% at 14.8 G FLOPs and 3.2 K im/s.

## Relation to Prior Work

LookWhere sits at the intersection of self-supervised representation learning (DINOv2) and adaptive computation for ViTs, departing from both token reduction methods (PiToMe, ATC, DTEM) that must process all tokens before merging, and prior token selection methods (DPS, IPS, PatchDrop) that require complex per-task optimization with multi-stage training or reinforcement learning. Its key conceptual departure is the what-where distillation: jointly distilling a self-supervised teacher's attention map (where to compute) and token representations (what to compute) into a paired low-res selector and high-res extractor, so that neither component ever sees the full high-resolution input. This is the first use of self-supervised attention maps as direct supervision for adaptive token prioritization, and the first to pretrain a general selector-extractor pair for cross-task transfer without per-task selector training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 34× FLOPs reduction and 6× speedup on high-resolution sparse recognition (Traffic Signs) while matching or exceeding teacher accuracy is a practically significant efficiency win for domains where high-res inputs are standard (remote sensing, medical imaging, autonomous driving). The selector's ability to generalize across diverse tasks—traffic signs, fine-grained birds, billiard reasoning, medical X-rays, aerial imagery—without any task-specific finetuning suggests a reusable, domain-level pretraining recipe. Open questions include whether task-specific selector finetuning would further improve accuracy, whether the framework extends to temporal (video) or spectral (multi-band) inputs, and whether the observed adversarial robustness gains over DINOv2 are meaningful under stronger attack settings.
