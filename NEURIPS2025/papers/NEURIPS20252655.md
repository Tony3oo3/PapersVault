## Problem

Conditional diffusion models require abundant data to train from scratch, making them impractical in small-data regimes where transfer learning is the dominant paradigm. Despite widespread empirical success in fine-tuning pre-trained CDMs (e.g., freezing condition encoders), no theoretical guarantees existed for whether or how transfer learning improves sample efficiency. This paper provides the first statistical theory showing that a well-learned low-dimensional condition representation from source tasks substantially reduces the sample complexity of target tasks.

## Input / Output

The method takes K source distributions with n (x, y) pairs each and a target distribution with m ≪ n pairs, where the high-dimensional condition y ∈ [0,1]^{D_y} is assumed to contain a shared low-dimensional representation h_*(y) ∈ [0,1]^{d_y} (d_y ≤ D_y) that is a sufficient statistic for x. The output is a fine-tuned score network producing samples from the target conditional distribution P(x|y) via a reverse-time SDE, accompanied by explicit total-variation distance bounds. The condition encoder h is learned during pre-training and frozen during fine-tuning, so only the score network f is trained on the m target samples.

## Method

The approach follows a two-phase transfer learning procedure: (1) pre-training jointly optimizes K task-specific score networks and a shared representation map h over nK source samples via empirical risk minimization of the denoising score matching loss; (2) fine-tuning trains only the target score network f^0 on m samples with h held fixed. The key theoretical contribution is a novel lemma (Lemma 3.1) proving Lipschitz continuity of the forward-process score in both x and w under assumptions imposed only on the initial data distribution (sub-Gaussian tail, Lipschitz score of the original density), rather than on the entire diffusion trajectory as required by prior work. This enables local Rademacher complexity analysis for the score matching loss, yielding generalization bounds under both task-diversity (Theorem 3.4) and meta-learning (Theorem 3.6) settings. Combined with a deep ReLU approximation theorem (Theorem 4.1) that handles unbounded domains while preserving Lipschitz constraints, the paper derives end-to-end TV distance bounds of Õ((m∧n)^{-1/(d_x+d_y+9)} + (nK)^{-1/(D_y+2)}), improving over the naive rate Õ(m^{-1/(d_x+D_y+9)}) by replacing the full condition dimension D_y with the intrinsic feature dimension d_y in the target-task term.

## Results

On the synthetic conditioned diffusion task (out-of-domain, β₀=15), fine-tuning from pre-trained source tasks achieves MSE 6.14 at m=10 samples, compared to 24.41 for training from scratch — a ~4× improvement. The gap narrows as m increases, with fine-tuning reaching MSE 0.45 at m=100 versus 1.23 for train-from-scratch. On the real-data MNIST image restoration task, fine-tuning consistently outperforms train-from-scratch across all sample sizes, e.g., MSE 0.3799 vs 0.4409 at m=10 and 0.2268 vs 0.2344 at m=100.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's sole empirical baseline is training the conditional diffusion model from scratch (without any pre-trained representation), which serves as the naive single-task approach. Fine-tuning with a pre-trained condition encoder wins decisively in the few-shot regime: at m=10 on the out-of-domain synthetic task, fine-tuning achieves MSE 6.14 versus 24.41 for train-from-scratch (a 4× reduction), and at m=20 the gap is 2.65 vs 20.62 (~8×). On the in-domain setting (β₀=5.5), the advantage is smaller but still present at small m (14.47 vs 21.99 at m=10), with the two methods converging as m grows to 100 (0.91 vs 1.04). On the MNIST image restoration task, fine-tuning wins at every sample size but with a more modest margin (e.g., 0.3799 vs 0.4409 at m=10), consistent with the theoretical prediction that the benefit of transfer learning diminishes as target-task data becomes abundant. The paper notes a slight anomaly at m=50, β₀=5.5 where fine-tuning (1.9) is marginally worse than train-from-scratch (1.77), attributed to run-to-run variance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the transfer learning theory of Tripuraneni et al. (2020) — which established task-diversity-based sample complexity bounds for supervised learning — to the setting of conditional diffusion models, where the 'label' is replaced by a high-dimensional condition and the loss is denoising score matching. It also builds on the neural network approximation theory of Chen et al. (2023b) for diffusion models, adapting their Lipschitz-constrained ReLU constructions to handle the unbounded data domain. The closest prior work, Yang et al. (2024), studies transfer learning for *unconditional* diffusion models with a single source task and a linear encoder; this paper generalizes to *conditional* models with multiple source tasks and nonlinear shared representations, which is the paradigm actually used in practice (e.g., frozen text encoders in text-to-image systems).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first theoretical justification for why freezing the condition encoder during fine-tuning of CDMs is sample-efficient, a practice that underpins systems like Stable Diffusion and DreamBooth. The key open question is whether the task-diversity assumption (Definition 3.1) can be verified or relaxed in practice, since it is difficult to check empirically. The authors also note that their analysis covers only ERM and does not account for parameter-efficient fine-tuning (LoRA) or the optimization dynamics of fine-tuning from a pre-trained checkpoint, both of which are central to real-world workflows. Extending the theory to settings where the condition encoder is not the dominant parameter component (e.g., diffusion transformers where the backbone is much larger) is an important direction.
