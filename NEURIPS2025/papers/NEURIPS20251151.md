## Problem

Existing node-level membership inference attacks against graph neural networks are exclusively classifier-based, and statistic-based methods that achieve state-of-the-art performance on i.i.d. data have not been extended to the graph setting where message-passing introduces structural dependencies. Additionally, the theoretical connection between the Bayes-optimal membership inference framework and practical hypothesis-testing attacks like LiRA and RMIA remained unclear, leaving their design choices without a principled justification.

## Input / Output

The adversary receives black-box query access to a trained GNN (or i.i.d. model), a target node v, the full graph G = (X, Y, A) with node features, labels, and adjacency matrix, plus a set of K shadow models trained on random subgraphs. G-BASE additionally requires M sampled membership configurations over all non-target nodes, and its loss signal S_L includes a neighborhood-dependent term ΔL_{N_L(v)} that measures how the target node's inclusion or exclusion shifts the losses of its L-hop neighbors. The output is a calibrated membership probability P(m_v=1|θ,G) ∈ [0,1] that is thresholded at τ to yield a binary member/non-member prediction, with ROC curves obtained by sweeping τ over [0,1].

## Method

The paper derives the Bayes-optimal decision rule for node-level MIA on GNNs (Theorem 1), showing that the optimal posterior depends on a loss-based signal S_L that includes both the target node's own loss and a neighborhood correction term ΔL_{N_L(v)} capturing how the target's presence alters its L-hop neighbors' losses—a structural dependency absent in the i.i.d. case. G-BASE makes this tractable by (i) setting the prior likelihood ratio Λ=1, (ii) approximating the expectation over unknown membership configurations via M samples drawn using model-independent Bernoulli sampling, 0-hop MIA sampling, or Gibbs sampling, and (iii) replacing the intractable model-distribution integral with a log-sum-exp over K shadow models. For i.i.d. data, BASE reduces to a single sigmoid of the target loss minus the log-sum-exp of shadow-model losses plus log prior odds, requiring only one query per model. Theorem 2 proves BASE is equivalent to RMIA (γ=1) up to a monotone transformation, providing a Bayes-optimal justification for RMIA while showing BASE achieves the same performance with far fewer model queries.

## Results

On the six graph datasets, G-BASE (Gibbs sampling, K=8 online) achieves the highest AUC on the four largest datasets: Pubmed (64.10%), Flickr (57.88%), Amazon-Photo (56.53%), and Github (57.47%), outperforming RMIA/BASE by 4–6 AUC points on Pubmed and Flickr. BASE matches RMIA exactly (as predicted by Theorem 2) across all datasets, e.g., 82.45% AUC on Cora and 56.23% on Flickr, while requiring only a single model query per target sample versus RMIA's additional queries over the population set. On the smaller Cora and Citeseer datasets, LiRA achieves competitive AUC (82.35% and 85.89%) but BASE/G-BASE deliver substantially higher TPR at 0.1% FPR (e.g., 4.64% vs 1.02% on Cora). MLP-classifier baselines perform at or near random (≈50% AUC) on all datasets with more than 5,000 nodes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LiRA [11], BASE and G-BASE achieve comparable or superior AUC on all datasets, with the gap widening on larger graphs: on Pubmed, G-BASE (Gibbs) reaches 64.10% AUC versus LiRA's 52.77%, and on Flickr, 57.88% versus 51.63%. At the practically critical 0.1% FPR, the advantage is even more pronounced (e.g., TPR of 1.85% vs 0.12% on Pubmed). Against RMIA [13], BASE produces numerically identical scores (e.g., 82.45% AUC on Cora, 56.23% on Flickr), confirming the theoretical equivalence established in Theorem 2; however, BASE requires only one query of the target and shadow models per target sample, whereas RMIA additionally queries models over a population set Z, making BASE substantially more query-efficient. Against the MLP-classifier node-level attacks from [15–17], both BASE and G-BASE dominate across all datasets and model architectures; the MLP baselines degrade to near-random performance (≈50% AUC) on datasets with more than 5,000 nodes (Flickr, Amazon-Photo, Github), while G-BASE maintains 56–64% AUC. G-BASE's advantage over the 0-hop attacks (BASE, RMIA, LiRA) is most visible on Pubmed (+6.7 AUC over RMIA) and Flickr (+1.7 AUC), where exploiting L-hop neighborhood structure via the ΔL term in the loss signal provides meaningful additional membership signal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Bayes-optimal membership inference framework of Sablayrolles et al. [1] from the i.i.d. setting to graph-structured data, deriving a closed-form posterior for node-level MIAs against GNNs that accounts for L-hop neighborhood dependencies via a loss-difference term ΔL. It also bridges to the hypothesis-testing lineage of LiRA [11] and RMIA [13] by proving that the proposed BASE attack is equivalent to RMIA (at γ=1) up to a monotone transformation, thereby providing a principled Bayesian justification for RMIA. For graph data, the paper departs from the exclusively classifier-based node-level attacks in prior work [15–19] by introducing the first statistic-based (G-BASE) approach, leveraging the success of loss-based methods on i.i.d. data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The equivalence theorem between BASE and RMIA gives the field a theoretical anchor for one of the strongest practical MIAs, while the G-BASE attack opens a new direction for graph privacy auditing that prior classifier-based methods could not match on larger datasets. A key limitation is G-BASE's computational cost: the neighborhood-dependent loss signal prevents parallelization across target nodes, introducing an N×M overhead that becomes prohibitive at scale. Open questions include whether the Bayes-optimal framework extends to edge-level or graph-level attacks, whether more efficient sampling strategies can close the gap with 0-hop attacks, and whether the approach generalizes to transductive GNN settings where the member/non-member distinction is less clean.
