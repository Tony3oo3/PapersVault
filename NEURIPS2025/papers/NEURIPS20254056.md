## Problem

Existing activation steering methods for LLMs—vector addition and directional ablation—are constrained to a 2D subspace defined by the activation and feature direction, making them sensitive to layer-specific activation norms and prone to unintended interference with unrelated features. Vector addition requires brittle per-layer coefficient tuning, while orthogonalization removes a feature entirely with no mechanism for partial suppression. The paper asks whether steering can be reformulated as a continuous, norm-preserving geometric operation that unifies these techniques while offering finer-grained control.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a text prompt processed through a decoder-only transformer LLM and intervenes at every RMSNorm layer (two per transformer block: before Attention and before MLP) by rotating the normalized activation vector within a precomputed 2D steering plane. The sole control parameter is a target angle θ ∈ [0°, 360°] that determines the behavioral expression (e.g., refusal vs. compliance). The output is the steered activation vector, which propagates through the remaining layers to produce modified text generation. The steering plane and rotation matrix are precomputed once per model and shared across all intervention points, adding less than 4% inference overhead when integrated into vLLM.

## Method

Angular Steering formulates behavior control as a rotation of the activation vector within a fixed 2D subspace P spanned by a feature direction d_feat (extracted via Difference-in-Means on contrastive harmful/harmless datasets) and a second axis d_PC0 (the first principal component of all layer-wise candidate directions). The rotation is implemented by projecting the activation onto P, rotating the projected component to the target angle θ using a 2D rotation matrix, and recombining with the unchanged (d_model − 2)-dimensional orthogonal complement, yielding a norm-preserving transformation. An Adaptive variant adds a binary mask that applies the rotation only to activations positively aligned with d_feat, reducing interference with non-targeted features. The paper proves that activation addition (α-scaled vector sum) and directional ablation (orthogonal projection) are special cases of this rotation—corresponding to angles below 180° and exactly 90° respectively—thereby unifying prior methods under a single geometric framework with a single bounded hyperparameter (θ) replacing per-layer unbounded coefficients.

## Results

Across six models (3B–14B), Adaptive Angular Steering (AAS) achieves near-perfect refusal control: substring matching drops to 0.0000 (from ~0.97–1.00 baseline) and LLAMAGUARD3 harmfulness scores reach 0.99–1.00, matching or slightly exceeding Activation Addition. On general language modeling (TINYBENCHMARKS), AAS preserves or even exceeds baseline accuracy across the full 360° steering circle for 5 of 6 models, with perplexity remaining close to the non-steered baseline. The non-adaptive variant causes coherence breakdowns on 3B models, while the adaptive mask keeps perplexity stable (mean diff < 0.9 vs. > 2.4 for Span(h, d_feat) steering).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Activation Addition [51], AAS achieves identical HARMBENCH and substring-matching scores across all six models, while marginally outperforming it on LLAMAGUARD3 (e.g., 1.0000 vs. 0.9808 on QWEN2.5-7B). The practical advantage is that AAS requires a single bounded rotation angle (0°–360°) rather than layerwise unbounded coefficients, making tuning far simpler. Against Directional Ablation [1], AAS substantially outperforms on every metric: for example, on QWEN2.5-7B, AAS reaches 0.8750 HARMBENCH vs. 0.3942 for Ablation, and 1.0000 LLAMAGUARD3 vs. 0.5288. Ablation also fails to reduce substring matching below 0.0577–1.0000, whereas AAS drives it to 0.0000. In robustness comparisons (Table 3), steering within the proposed fixed plane Span(d_PC0, d_feat) yields mean perplexity close to baseline (e.g., 2.12 vs. 2.56 for QWEN2.5-7B) with mean angle-to-angle difference of 0.06, versus 4.82 mean perplexity and 2.40 mean difference when using Span(h, d_feat) as in prior work—confirming that the fixed-plane design avoids interference with unrelated features.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reframes activation steering as a geometric rotation in a fixed 2D subspace, unifying prior methods—activation addition (Turner et al., 2024) and directional ablation (Arditi et al., 2024)—as restricted special cases (sub-180° and 90° rotations, respectively). It builds on the Difference-in-Means direction extraction (Belrose, 2023) and the superposition hypothesis, but departs from prior work by using a fixed, layer-independent steering plane (spanned by the feature direction and its first PCA component) rather than the per-activation plane Span{h, d_feat}, and by operating on normalized activations to exploit the directional emphasis of RMSNorm. The adaptive variant adds conditional masking based on feature alignment, extending the conditional steering line (Lee et al., 2024) from addition-based to rotation-based operations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The unified rotation framework gives practitioners a single bounded parameter (angle) replacing per-layer unbounded coefficients, simplifying tuning and improving stability across model families and sizes. The finding that steering on a fixed plane preserves general benchmark performance (sometimes exceeding baseline) while the per-activation plane causes significant degradation is practically important for deployment. A key limitation is the heuristic selection of the steering plane, which may not generalize optimally to diverse behaviors or architectures; systematic identification of effective subspaces remains open. The perplexity observation that harmful generations retain lower perplexity than refusal responses offers a glimpse into safety alignment as a surface-level shortcut rather than deep capability removal.
