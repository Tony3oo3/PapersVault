## Problem

While LLMs are extensively benchmarked on reasoning and perception, their capacity for open-ended exploration—particularly empowerment-driven discovery—remains largely uncharacterized. Prior work on LLM exploration is confined to narrow bandit tasks, leaving unclear whether and why LLMs can match human-level exploration in combinatorial, open-ended environments. This paper asks whether LLMs can explore as effectively as humans, what strategies they deploy, and what internal mechanisms explain their success or failure.

## Input / Output

Each LLM trial receives a prompt containing the game rules, the current element inventory, and the full trial history, and must output a valid element+element combination. For the SAE analysis, per-trial element embeddings are extracted from every transformer layer of LLaMA3.1-70B (and 8B) and fed into layer-wise autoencoders. The regression pipeline encodes each element as chosen/not-chosen and predicts this label from trial number, uncertainty (a novelty-based count), and empowerment (a neural-network-predicted future-combination potential), yielding per-model and per-temperature weight estimates; the SAE pipeline yields per-layer correlation scores and beta weights linking latent neurons to uncertainty, empowerment, and choice variables.

## Method

The study combines three complementary analyses on the Little Alchemy 2 combinatorial game. First, generalized linear mixed-effects models (GLMMs) with random slopes quantify how much each model's choices are driven by uncertainty versus empowerment, across four sampling temperatures and five models plus a 29,493-player human benchmark. Second, Sparse Autoencoders trained on per-layer element embeddings identify which transformer blocks most strongly encode uncertainty, empowerment, and the final choice; targeted ablation of the most-correlated neurons establishes causal control over the corresponding strategy. Third, a sentence-level annotation scheme (seven reasoning labels) applied to GPT-4o CoT traces and DeepSeek-R1 native reasoning tokens reveals that reasoning models allocate substantially more tokens to iterative combination analysis, past-trial review, and outcome prediction, whereas traditional LLMs progress rapidly to a final choice. The central mechanistic finding is a temporal mismatch: uncertainty and choice signals peak in the earliest layers (e.g., layer 1–2 in LLaMA3.1-70B) while empowerment signals emerge only in middle layers (e.g., layer 72), so the model commits to a decision before empowerment information is processed—'thinking too fast' for effective exploration.

## Results

On Little Alchemy 2 (500 trials), o1 discovers 177 elements and DeepSeek-R1 discovers 85, both significantly outperforming the human benchmark of 42 elements (o1: t=9.71, p<0.001; DeepSeek-R1: t=3.40, p<0.027). Traditional LLMs underperform humans: GPT-4o discovers 35 (t=-5.48, p<0.001), LLaMA3.1-70B discovers 25 (t=-6.39, p<0.001), and LLaMA3.1-8B discovers only 9 (t=-28.12, p<0.001). Regression analysis reveals that traditional LLMs rely almost exclusively on uncertainty-driven strategies (near-zero empowerment weights), while o1 uniquely balances both uncertainty and empowerment like humans. SAE analysis in LLaMA3.1-70B shows uncertainty is represented at layer 2 (r=0.73) and choices peak at layer 1 (β=1.08), while empowerment only emerges at layer 72 (r=0.55), explaining premature decision-making.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks five LLMs (plus two Qwen variants) against 29,493 human players on Little Alchemy 2. o1 (177 elements) and DeepSeek-R1 (85) significantly outperform the human average of 42 elements, with o1's advantage driven by the highest empowerment weight (0.72 vs. human 0.38). GPT-4o (35 elements) falls below humans by 7 elements (t=-5.48, p<0.001) and shows a negative empowerment weight (-0.17), indicating it actively avoids empowerment-driven choices. LLaMA3.1-70B (25) and LLaMA3.1-8B (9) perform progressively worse, with the 8B model discovering fewer than a quarter of what humans find. Among the Qwen family, the reasoning-enhanced QwQ-32B (48 elements, empowerment=0.089) outperforms both humans and its non-reasoning counterpart Qwen-2.5-32B (14 elements, empowerment=-0.018), confirming that RL-trained reasoning—not model scale—drives effective exploration. Higher sampling temperatures (0→1) moderately improve all traditional LLMs (β=0.124, p<0.001) by increasing uncertainty-driven exploration, but none close the gap to humans without empowerment integration.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Little Alchemy 2 exploration paradigm from Brandle et al. [6]—originally designed to study human empowerment-driven exploration—to LLMs, moving beyond the narrow bandit-task evaluations that have dominated prior LLM exploration research [21, 24]. Methodologically, it combines behavioral regression (GLMMs separating uncertainty-driven from empowerment strategies) with Sparse Autoencoder interpretability, building on the SAE-based mechanistic analysis of LLM cognition pioneered by Demircan et al. [12] for TD-learning and Bricken et al. [7] for monosemanticity. The paper also positions itself against the growing LLM-agent literature in open-ended environments (Minecraft, text games) by focusing on the *why* behind exploration failure rather than merely optimizing task performance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The SAE finding that uncertainty and choice signals are encoded in early transformer layers (1–2) while empowerment signals emerge only in mid-to-late layers (72) provides a concrete mechanistic account of why traditional LLMs make premature, short-sighted exploration decisions—a 'thinking too fast' problem that prompt engineering and activation steering could not fix. The contrast with reasoning models (o1, DeepSeek-R1, QwQ-32B) that explicitly allocate more test-time compute to deliberation suggests that extended reasoning, not scale alone, is the key to human-like exploration. Open questions remain: whether the temporal mismatch is architecture-specific (only tested in LLaMA), whether training with explicit exploratory objectives could rewire layer-wise processing, and how these findings generalize beyond combinatorial discovery to other open-ended domains like scientific hypothesis generation.
