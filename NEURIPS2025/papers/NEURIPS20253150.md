## Problem

OOD generalization benchmarks consistently show a strong positive correlation between in-distribution and OOD accuracy (accuracy-on-the-line), which has been interpreted as evidence that spurious correlations are rare in practice. This paper shows that the positive correlation is an artifact of aggregating heterogeneous OOD examples: large, semantically coherent subsets exist where higher ID accuracy actually predicts lower OOD accuracy (accuracy-on-the-inverse-line), and these failure modes are invisible under standard aggregate evaluation.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a diverse population of up to 4,200 trained models (spanning architectures from AlexNet to ViTs, with varied pretraining and hyperparameters), their held-out ID test accuracies (probit-transformed), and a binary N×d matrix recording correct/incorrect predictions on each OOD example. It produces a binary selection vector of fixed size S identifying a subset of the OOD set whose per-model accuracy vector is negatively correlated with the ID accuracy vector. The selected subsets are released as first-class evaluation targets for the benchmarks studied.

## Method

OODSelect formulates subset discovery as a constrained optimization: minimize the Pearson correlation between ID accuracies and the selected OOD accuracies, subject to a fixed subset size S. The binary constraint is relaxed to a continuous [0,1] domain via a sigmoid parameterization, and the size constraint is enforced through a squared exact-penalty term with a tunable λ. Although the objective is non-convex and non-submodular, it is Lipschitz-continuous, enabling stable gradient-based optimization with Adam and cosine annealing of both the learning rate and λ. A key design choice is minimizing correlation rather than maximizing error, which distinguishes spurious-correlation failures (where model ranking inverts) from general difficulty (where all models fail uniformly). The method requires thousands of diverse models per dataset—orders of magnitude more than prior accuracy-on-the-line studies—to ensure correlation estimates are stable, with a formal bound showing the change in Pearson R from adding one model is O(C/√m).

## Results

Across seven domain generalization benchmarks, OODSelect identifies large OOD subsets (up to 75% of the full OOD set for CXR, 50% for WILDS-CivilComments) where the ID–OOD Pearson correlation flips from strongly positive (AoTL, R = 0.80–0.99 on the full set) to strongly negative (AoTIL, R = −0.30 to −0.99). For example, on CXR the full OOD set yields R = 0.85, while a 55,000-example OODSelect subset (75% of data) achieves R = −0.46, and a 10,000-example subset reaches R = −0.99. On WILDS-CivilComments, a 25,000-example subset (50%) achieves R = −0.96 versus R = 0.98 for the full set. Random selection consistently preserves the positive correlation (R ≈ 0.84–0.99), and the most-misclassified examples yield near-zero correlation, confirming that OODSelect isolates spurious-correlation failures rather than general difficulty.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares OODSelect against three baselines: random selection, most-misclassified (hard) examples, and CLIP latent-space distance selection. Random selection consistently preserves the strong positive ID–OOD correlation of the full OOD set (e.g., R = 0.84 on CXR vs. 0.85 full; R = 0.98 on CivilComments vs. 0.98 full), confirming that the negative correlations are not an artifact of sub-sampling. The most-misclassified examples yield near-zero correlation at small subset sizes (R ≈ 0.00 for N ≤ 100 across all datasets) and become positive at larger sizes, never reaching the strong negative values OODSelect achieves (e.g., R = −0.99 for CXR at N = 10,000 vs. R = −0.37 for Hard at the same size). CLIP distance-based selection, which greedily picks the most visually dissimilar OOD examples, often produces positive correlations (R = 0.52 on PACS at N = 10) and consistently fails to capture the negative correlations identified by OODSelect (e.g., R = −0.92 on VLCS at N = 10 for OODSelect vs. positive for CLIP). These comparisons demonstrate that OODSelect uniquely identifies subsets where spurious correlations drive systematic generalization failures, a signal invisible to both difficulty-based and visual-dissimilarity-based selection.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly challenges the interpretation of the accuracy-on-the-line (AoTL) phenomenon established by Miller et al. (2021) and Taori et al. (2020), which was widely taken as evidence that spurious correlations are rare in practice. It builds on Teney et al. (2023), who showed that with more diverse model populations some datasets exhibit non-positive ID-OOD correlations, and on Salaudeen et al. (2025a), who argued theoretically that benchmarks with AoTL are misspecified for studying spurious correlations. The key departure is methodological: rather than proposing a new domain generalization algorithm, the paper introduces OODSelect, a gradient-based subset selection procedure that reveals AoTIL (accuracy-on-the-inverse-line) hidden within standard DomainBed and WILDS splits, using orders of magnitude more models (thousands vs. tens) than prior AoTL studies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that up to 75% of a standard OOD benchmark (CXR) can exhibit near-perfect negative ID-OOD correlation (R = -0.99) fundamentally reframes how the community should interpret aggregate OOD evaluation: the reassuring AoTL pattern may be an artifact of averaging over heterogeneous examples, masking systematic failures on semantically coherent subpopulations. This has direct implications for the consequential validity of distribution shift benchmarks and suggests that future OOD methods should be evaluated on disaggregated subsets, not just aggregate accuracy. Key open questions include whether the identified subsets generalize across model families, how to design training methods that improve worst-case subset performance without sacrificing average accuracy, and whether the spurious features driving AoTIL are expressible in human-interpretable terms (the paper shows they often are not).
