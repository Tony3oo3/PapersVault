## Problem

Activation engineering has proven effective for mitigating hallucinations in LLMs and ImageLLMs, yet its applicability to VideoLLMs remains entirely unexplored. The paper asks whether activation engineering can transfer to the video domain and, if so, what internal factors determine which modules and datasets are effective for hallucination suppression.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252006/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a video clip and a text question as input and produces a text response, operating entirely at inference time without any LLM fine-tuning. During inference, the video is fed in parallel to both the VideoLLM's visual encoder and a lightweight temporal variation classifier; the classifier's prediction (temporal-invariant vs. temporal-variant) selects which pre-computed offset vector set is injected into the LLM's attention heads. The offset vectors are computed once per model from frame-downsampled (hallucination-inducing) versus full-frame (normal) prompts, so no additional runtime overhead is introduced beyond the classifier's forward pass.

## Method

The key empirical finding is that hallucination sensitivity of internal modules (attention heads or layers) in VideoLLMs correlates with the temporal variation characteristic of the task (temporal-invariant vs. temporal-variant) rather than the task type (e.g., binary QA vs. multiple choice). Guided by this, the authors build an automated pipeline that partitions ShareGPT4Video into two high-quality datasets—one for each temporal variation class—using frame-count filtering and GPT-4o-based semantic verification. A lightweight binary classifier is then trained to predict the temporal variation of any given (video, question) pair. At inference, the classifier routes the input to the corresponding pre-computed offset vector set (derived from the difference between normal and frame-downsampled activation vectors at the final token position), which is injected into the top-K most hallucination-sensitive attention heads identified via per-head binary classification accuracy. This temporal-aware routing ensures that each sample receives offset vectors computed from modules genuinely sensitive to its type of hallucination, yielding consistent gains across VideoLLaMA2, Video-LLaVA, and Qwen2.5-VL on VidHalluc and EventHallusion without any LLM retraining.

## Results

On the VidHalluc benchmark, the proposed temporal-aware activation engineering achieves the best Overall score across all three VideoLLMs: 72.49 on VideoLLaMA2 (vs. 68.46 baseline), 68.57 on Qwen2.5-VL (vs. 64.22 baseline), and 56.30 on Video-LLaVA (vs. 50.75 baseline). On EventHallusion, the method yields up to +24.21% improvement over the baseline (e.g., Video-LLaVA Overall rises from 46.70 to 70.91). On the VideoMMMU capability benchmark, performance remains comparable to the baseline (within ±0.2%), confirming that hallucination mitigation does not degrade general video understanding.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against four inference-time hallucination mitigation methods: TCD (contrastive decoding for video), DINO-HEAL (attention re-weighting via DINO), CGD (CLIP-guided decoding from the image domain), and VTI (latent space steering from the image domain). On VidHalluc Overall, the proposed method outperforms TCD by up to 6.15%, DINO-HEAL by up to 4.5%, CGD by up to 3.88%, and VTI by up to 2.16% across the three models. The largest gains appear on the temporal-variant subtasks (STH, TSH), where the method surpasses TCD by up to 19.39% and DINO-HEAL by up to 14.69%, reflecting the core insight that temporal variation drives hallucination sensitivity. On EventHallusion, the method beats TCD on Qwen2.5-VL (69.19 vs. 63.81) and Video-LLaVA (70.91 vs. 58.68), though TCD edges ahead on VideoLLaMA2 (61.37 vs. 59.17). On the VideoMMMU capability benchmark, all methods including the proposed one remain within ±0.2% of the baseline, confirming no degradation of general video understanding.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the activation engineering lineage from ITI (Li et al., NeurIPS 2023) for LLMs and VTI (Liu et al., ICLR 2025) for image-domain MLLMs to the video domain, where it had not been systematically explored. It adopts the frame-downsampling strategy from TCD (Zhang et al., 2024) to construct hallucination-inducing prompts, but departs from prior work by discovering that hallucination-sensitive modules are determined by temporal variation characteristics rather than task type, and by designing a temporal-aware module selection pipeline. Unlike contrastive decoding (TCD, CGD) or auxiliary-signal methods (DINO-HEAL), the approach operates purely through inference-time activation manipulation without retraining.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that temporal variation, not task type, governs which internal modules are hallucination-sensitive provides a principled lens for understanding VideoLLM hallucination mechanisms and could guide future interpretability and mitigation work. The train-free, model-agnostic nature of the method makes it practically deployable across diverse VideoLLMs without costly retraining. Open questions include whether the two-category (temporal-invariant vs. temporal-variant) taxonomy is sufficient for all video types, whether the GPT-4o-based data filtering introduces bias, and whether the approach generalizes to longer or more complex temporal structures beyond the tested benchmarks.
