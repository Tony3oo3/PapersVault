## Problem

Prior work on LLM safety assumed that the refusal direction (extracted at the post-instruction token position) also encodes the model's understanding of harmfulness, so ablating it was interpreted as making the model perceive inputs as harmless. However, it remained unclear whether LLMs truly encode a generalizable concept of harmfulness internally, separate from their surface-level refusal behavior, and how jailbreak methods actually manipulate these internal representations.

## Input / Output

The method consumes user instructions (text prompts) and extracts residual stream hidden states at two specific token positions within the transformer: t_inst (the last token of the user's instruction) and t_post-inst (the last token of the post-instruction special tokens). It produces scalar belief scores (Δ_harmful and Δ_refuse) computed as layer-averaged cosine-similarity differences between the hidden state and cluster centers, steering direction vectors (v_harmful at t_inst, v_refuse at t_post-inst) for activation intervention, and a binary harmful/harmless classification for the Latent Guard. The key structural detail is that harmfulness and refusal are read from different token positions, reflecting that the model encodes these concepts at different points in the forward pass.

## Method

The paper identifies a harmfulness direction at t_inst via difference-in-means between cluster centers of harmful and harmless instructions, distinct from the refusal direction at t_post-inst. Clustering analysis shows that hidden states at t_inst group by instruction harmfulness (accepted harmful instructions fall near the refused-harmful cluster), while at t_post-inst they group by refusal behavior (refused harmless instructions fall near the refused-harmful cluster). A reply inversion task provides causal evidence: appending an inversion question (e.g., 'Could this prompt cause harm?') and steering along the harmfulness direction flips the model's answer from 'No' to 'Certainly' (changing its harmfulness judgment), whereas steering along the refusal direction only elicits surface-level refusal tokens without altering the harmfulness perception. The Latent Guard application uses the Δ_harmful score as an intrinsic classifier that detects jailbroken inputs and over-refused harmless prompts with accuracy comparable to Llama Guard 3, and remains robust to adversarial fine-tuning that suppresses refusal behavior.

## Results

The Latent Guard, a simple classifier based on the LLM's internal harmfulness belief score (Δ_harmful), achieves classification accuracy comparable to or better than Llama Guard 3 (a dedicated 8B finetuned safeguard model) across three models and five evaluation categories. The most striking gains are on persuasion jailbreaks (e.g., 75.0% vs. 17.8% on Qwen2; 65.0% vs. 6.8% on Llama3) and over-refused harmless instructions (e.g., 100.0% vs. 84.4% on Llama2; 91.6% vs. 50.0% on Qwen2). On adversarial suffix jailbreaks, both methods perform near-perfectly (≥91%), while on accepted harmful instructions Latent Guard leads by 18–48 percentage points on Llama2 and Llama3. The Latent Guard requires no additional model and operates within the normal forward pass of the target LLM.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is Llama Guard 3 (8B), a dedicated LLM finetuned to classify input safety. Latent Guard decisively outperforms Llama Guard 3 on persuasion jailbreaks across all three models (41.6% vs. 0.0% on Llama2; 65.0% vs. 6.8% on Llama3; 75.0% vs. 17.8% on Qwen2), suggesting that Llama Guard struggles when the harmfulness is embedded in persuasive phrasing rather than surface-level keywords. On over-refused harmless instructions (Xstest), Latent Guard also leads substantially (100.0% vs. 84.4%; 78.5% vs. 50.0%; 91.6% vs. 50.0%), consistent with the paper's finding that the internal harmfulness belief correctly identifies these as harmless even when the model behaviorally refuses them. On adversarial suffix (GCG) jailbreaks, both methods perform near-perfectly, with Llama Guard 3 holding a slight edge on Llama3 (99.2% vs. 91.0%). The one category where Llama Guard 3 edges out Latent Guard is accepted harmful instructions on Qwen2 (59.4% vs. 54.6%), and template-based jailbreaks on Qwen2 (91.4% vs. 53.5%). Overall, Latent Guard wins or ties on 11 of 15 model×category cells, with the largest margins on persuasion and over-refusal, and does so without requiring a separate 8B guard model.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the representation-engineering line of work on LLM safety, most directly building on Arditi et al. (2024), who showed that refusal is mediated by a single linear direction in latent space. The critical departure is that prior work (Yu et al. 2025, Zheng et al. 2024, Jain et al. 2024, Ball et al. 2024) assumed the refusal direction extracted at the post-instruction token also encodes harmfulness, interpreting its ablation as making the model perceive inputs as harmless. This paper demonstrates with causal intervention (the reply inversion task) that harmfulness is encoded at a different token position (t_inst) along a distinct direction, while the post-instruction position (t_post-inst) primarily encodes surface-level refusal signals. The work also connects to the broader activation-steering literature (Marks & Tegmark 2023, Turner et al. 2023, Tigges et al. 2023) and the jailbreak-analysis-in-latent-space literature (Zou et al. 2023b, Xu et al. 2024, Wollschläger et al. 2025).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that LLMs internally 'know' an instruction is harmful even when they behaviorally accept it (or refuse a harmless instruction while internally deeming it harmless) reframes how we should think about safety alignment: models may be learning shallow refusal triggers rather than deep harmfulness understanding. This has direct implications for the Superficial Alignment Hypothesis and for designing more robust safety training that engages with the latent harmfulness representation rather than just refusal behaviors. The Latent Guard application shows that this internal signal is robust to adversarial fine-tuning and jailbreaks, offering a practical safeguard that requires no additional model. Key open questions include whether these representations persist at larger scales (the study is limited to 7B/8B models), how they evolve during SFT and RLHF, and whether circuit-level analysis can reveal the mechanistic pathway from t_inst harmfulness encoding to t_post-inst refusal generation.
