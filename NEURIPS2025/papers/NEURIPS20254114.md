## Problem

Scaling laws are typically fit on narrow families of models with fixed hyperparameters and limited width-depth ratios, yet practitioners routinely apply these prescriptions to entirely different training setups. This paper asks how sensitive scaling law prescriptions are to the experimental design choices—model shape, learning rate schedule, cooldown, and fitting procedure—that are often treated as incidental, and whether a single 'compute-optimal' tokens-per-parameter ratio can be meaningfully defined across diverse architectures.

## Input / Output

The method consumes over 4,000 transformer checkpoints (50M–2B parameters, 11 widths from 256–3072, 18 depths from 3–80) with logged validation log-perplexity on a fixed 100M-token held-out set, alongside a specialized FLOP count that accounts for architectural asymmetry (unlike the 6×N rule of thumb). Outputs include compute-optimal tokens-per-parameter prescriptions, fitted power-law coefficients (A, B, α, β), benchmark error/accuracy predictions via sigmoidal and exponential mappings, and the full open-source checkpoint suite with training metrics. A key structural detail is that the 2D width-depth exploration means some models are never Pareto-optimal, which fundamentally changes how the lower envelope must be constructed compared to prior 1D (parameter-count-only) analyses.

## Method

The paper trains 22 Gemma-inspired transformer models across a 2D width-depth space on 350B tokens of Dolma 1.7, using a unified learning-rate scaling rule (lr_eff = lr_base / (width × √depth)) and scalable parameter initialization to enable hyperparameter transfer across shapes. The core technical contribution is a convex-hull fitting method for Approach 1: rather than binning FLOPs and selecting per-bin minima (which fails when the model space is 2D and the Pareto frontier is sparsely populated), the authors fit a lower convex hull to the loss-vs-FLOPs/GPU-hours scatter, using only the hull vertices to regress the scaling law. This is shown to be substantially more robust to model-selection choices than both the original Chinchilla binning and the parametric power-law fit (Approach 3, fitted via L-BFGS with Huber loss). The paper then systematically ablates cooldown schedule, learning rate, token-count range, and embedding inclusion to quantify how each design decision shifts the fitted slope, and extends the analysis to wall-clock GPU hours where wider models become Pareto-optimal under tensor-parallel-only training.

## Results

The paper's central quantitative finding is that scaling law slopes (the exponent in parameters = constant × compute^exponent) are highly sensitive to fitting design choices. With embeddings included, the base Approach 1 slope is 0.4579 (vs. Hoffmann's 0.5126) and Approach 3 is 0.6965; without embeddings, Approach 3 yields 0.7141 (vs. Kaplan's 0.7300). The most dramatic sensitivity is to the token range used: restricting to >120B tokens shifts the Approach 1 slope by +0.34 (to 0.7987) and the no-embeddings Approach 1 slope by +0.40 (to 0.9666). The novel convex hull fitting method reduces variance compared to Hoffmann's 250-bin approach, and the paper demonstrates that Approach 3 (power-law fitting) is substantially more robust to model selection than Approach 1 (envelope fitting).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its fitted scaling laws against two canonical baselines: Hoffmann et al. (Chinchilla, 2022) with a slope of 0.5126 (embeddings included) and Kaplan et al. (2020) with a slope of 0.7300 (embeddings excluded). The Gemstones' Approach 3 (power-law fitting) with embeddings excluded yields a slope of 0.7141, closely matching Kaplan's 0.7300, while Approach 1 without embeddings gives only 0.5689—far from Kaplan—showing that the fitting method itself is a major source of discrepancy. Against Hoffmann's Chinchilla prescription, the Gemstones' Approach 1 base slope (0.4579) is lower, but the gap widens or narrows dramatically depending on token range, cooldown inclusion, and learning rate: the >120B-token subset pushes the slope to 0.7987, overshooting Hoffmann by 0.29. The paper's key comparative finding is that Hoffmann's binning-based Approach 1 is far more sensitive to model selection (slope changes up to 0.34) than the parametric Approach 3 (slope changes ≤0.06), and that the novel convex hull method reduces this sensitivity by excluding sub-optimal points from the fit. The 'Chinchilla Reduced Sampling' experiment—matching Hoffmann's 5-model selection—shows that even on Hoffmann's own data, reduced sampling increases the slope by 0.10, confirming that the fragility is not unique to the Gemstones' diverse architecture set.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the scaling law fitting framework established by Kaplan et al. (2020) and Hoffmann et al. (2022, Chinchilla) from a 1D exploration (varying parameter count at roughly fixed aspect ratio) to a 2D exploration that simultaneously varies model width, depth, learning rate, and cooldown schedule. It builds directly on Chinchilla's Approach 1 and 3 fitting procedures and their compute-optimal prescription (Equation 4/6), but introduces a convex hull fitting method to handle the sparser data that arises when models are no longer densely packed along a single parameter-count axis. For benchmark prediction, it adapts the error-prediction function from Gadre et al. (2024) and the sigmoidal accuracy-prediction form from Bhagia et al. (2024), while the architectural design draws on Gemma, Llama, and Pythia conventions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central practical finding—that scaling law slopes can shift by 0.3+ depending on seemingly innocuous choices like token-count filtering, cooldown inclusion, or embedding parameterization—challenges the assumption that a single fitted law generalizes across practitioner setups. The 4000+ open-source checkpoints (10T+ tokens, 22 architectures spanning 50M–2B parameters) fill a gap left by the takedown of the Pile dataset and enable reproducible, multi-faceted scaling studies that prior single-family model collections could not support. Key open questions include whether the FLOPs-vs-GPU-hours tradeoff between deep and wide models persists under pipeline parallelism, how the findings extend to other hyperparameters held constant here (expansion factor, vocabulary size, batch size), and whether the convex hull method generalizes to even sparser or higher-dimensional model families.
