## Problem

While shaping is a well-established protocol for teaching complex sequential tasks to animals, the neural dynamics underlying how schemas are represented and progressively formed through shaping remain poorly understood. Specifically, it is unclear what computational structures encode the learned schema and how these structures evolve across the stages of primitive, sequence, and schema learning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250243/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For the primary odor-sequence task, inputs are 96-dimensional one-hot odor vectors (active entry = 20) presented for 5 timesteps with variable inter-stimulus intervals (5–10 steps) and Gaussian noise (σ=1); the output layer is 18 units during primitive learning (2-d reward + 16-d odor identity) but collapses to 2 units (reward only) during sequence and schema learning. For the keyword spotting extension, inputs are MFCC features from TIMIT utterances and the output is a 4-way word class label. The output targets follow ramping dynamics that transition to target values at stimulus onset and hold until the next stimulus.

## Method

The paper trains a tanh-activated RNN (96 input, N recurrent, K output units) via BPTM with Adam across a three-stage shaping protocol: (1) odor-reward association, (2) linking odors into six-position sequences with random transitions, and (3) successive exposure to novel odor sets sharing the same sequence structure. The key mechanistic finding is that the learned schema is implemented as a sequence attractor — a chain of locally stable fixed points (one per sequence position) connected by low-dimensional manifolds, with the principal eigenvector of each fixed point's Jacobian aligned with the sequential transition direction. Analysis via PCA, optimization-based fixed point identification, and Jacobian eigenvalue spectra reveals that shaping drives a progressive attractor evolution: 16 isolated discrete attractors (primitive stage) are reorganized into sequence-specific attractor trajectories (sequence stage), which are then compressed into a unified abstract attractor shared across structurally similar sequences (schema stage). This shaped attractor organization is shown to be critical for generalization, as unshaped networks exhibit premature dimensionality collapse and fail to form stable, separated attractor trajectories for critical sequence positions.

## Results

On the odor-sequence task, the shaped RNN achieves 99.89±0.03% reward prediction accuracy versus 93.20±2.4% for the unshaped RNN, with the largest gap at positions P4/P5 of S2a/S2b where shaped networks reach near-perfect accuracy while unshaped networks plateau at only 25% after Problem 1. The shaped RNN's representational dissimilarity matrix correlates with rat OFC data at >90% Pearson correlation, and its top-3 principal components explain ~80% of hidden-state variance, with progressive dimensionality compression across problems. On the TIMIT keyword-spotting task, shaping reduces the number of epochs needed to reach 90% test accuracy compared to direct training, and PCA of hidden states reveals a tree-like structure matching the phoneme hierarchy with 7 leaf attractors and 1 root attractor.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between RNNs trained with the three-stage shaping protocol (task primitive → task sequence → task schema) and RNNs trained directly on the full odor-sequence problems without shaping. Shaped RNNs outperform unshaped RNNs on every measured axis: overall accuracy (99.89% vs. 93.20%), the critical P4/P5 discrimination where reward contingencies are reversed (near-perfect vs. 25% after Problem 1), and representational alignment with biological OFC data (>90% RDM correlation vs. markedly lower). Mechanistically, shaped networks build discrete attractors first, then link them into sequence attractors, and finally compress them into abstract schemas; unshaped networks instead undergo premature dimensionality collapse (93% vs. 82.5% top-3 PC variance) and fail to form stable separate attractor trajectories for the critical positions, leading to poor generalization. On the keyword-spotting task, the same shaping framework reduces training epochs to 90% accuracy and produces interpretable phoneme-level attractor structure, confirming the approach generalizes beyond the odor-sequence paradigm.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Zhou et al.'s (Nature 2021) odor-sequence task in rats' orbitofrontal cortex, adopting its three-stage shaping protocol (primitive → sequence → schema) as the experimental template, and on Driscoll et al.'s (Nature Neuroscience 2024) framework of attractor dynamics in recurrent networks, using their optimization-based fixed-point analysis to identify and track attractor states. Conceptually, it bridges two previously disconnected literatures: the neuroscience of schema learning (which has characterized what schemas look like in OFC but not how they form dynamically) and the ML curriculum learning literature (which optimizes performance but lacks a mechanistic account of how abstract knowledge is acquired). The paper departs from prior attractor-dynamics work by showing that attractor structures are not static but evolve through a specific three-stage trajectory—discrete point attractors, linked sequence attractors, and finally compressed abstract attractors—driven by the shaping curriculum.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first explicit dynamical mechanism linking shaping to schema formation, showing that sequence attractors serve as reusable templates for generalization and that shaping fundamentally alters the learning trajectory (progressive compression vs. premature collapse in unshaped networks). This could inspire new curriculum learning algorithms that explicitly scaffold abstract knowledge acquisition rather than merely ordering training examples by difficulty. Key limitations include reliance on BPTT (not biologically plausible) and modeling only the PFC without the hippocampus-PFC interaction that is thought to underlie complementary learning timescales in the brain. Open questions include whether the attractor-based shaping framework generalizes to more complex, multi-modal tasks and whether biologically grounded learning rules (dopamine + Hebbian plasticity) can reproduce the same attractor evolution.
