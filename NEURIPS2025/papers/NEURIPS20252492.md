## Problem

Existing Theory of Mind benchmarks evaluate static, text-based scenarios from a single perspective, creating a significant gap relative to real-world social interactions where agents must continuously infer others' states, goals, and behaviors from multimodal, dynamic, embodied contexts. No prior benchmark simultaneously evaluates multi-perspective ToM (first-person real-time state inference and third-person holistic goal/behavior inference) across diverse social relationships (collaboration and covert obstruction) in an open-world embodied setting.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252492/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

First-person evaluation provides serialized game screenshots (captured every 4 seconds, 1–14 images per input, 363 total) alongside dialogue transcripts, action commands, system feedback, and crafting rules, requiring the model to select one of three options about current resource states at a specific time point. Third-person evaluation provides a complete spectator-mode video (1:34–8:14, avg 263 s, 1080×2044, 30 fps) with subtitles showing all agents' dialogue and system feedback, or uniformly sampled frames for models without video input, requiring selection among three options about the final goal or specific agent behaviors. The first-person design deliberately uses discrete screenshots rather than video to isolate 'point-in-time' belief states, while the third-person design uses full video to simulate an external observer's holistic understanding.

## Method

The paper builds the SOMI embodied interaction environment, a three-tiered asynchronous architecture (Multi-agents → AAct WebSocket middleware → SoMi-Mindcraft) that enables three GPT-4o-based agents to autonomously control Minecraft characters, negotiate division of labor, and execute crafting tasks with hierarchical sub-goals (e.g., collect logs → craft planks → build crafting table → craft final item). Social dynamics include 20 purely cooperative tasks and 15 tasks where one agent covertly obstructs the other two using irrelevant speech or delayed actions, while the obstructed agents remain unaware. From 35 task recordings, the authors construct 1,225 expert-annotated three-option multiple-choice questions split across first-person state inference (1,050 questions probing self-ToM and others' ToM at 3–18 time points per task) and third-person goal/behavior inference (175 questions). Evaluation compares 21 human subjects against eight state-of-the-art LVLMs (GPT-4o, Gemini 1.5 Pro, Gemini 2.0 Flash, InternVL2.5 78B, Qwen2.5-VL, LLaVA 1.6 13B, VideoLLaMA 3 7B, LLaVA-Video 7B) under both vanilla and Chain-of-Thought prompting, with majority voting over three option permutations and five runs each to mitigate choice-order bias.

## Results

On the first-person state inference evaluation (1050 questions), humans achieve 90.0% accuracy while the best LVLM (GPT-4o with CoT) reaches only 59.5%, yielding a 40.1% average gap. On the third-person goal and behavior inference evaluation (175 questions), humans score 93.3% versus a best LVLM (GPT-4o with CoT) at 82.9%, a 26.4% average gap. Without CoT, the strongest first-person performer is InternVL2.5 78B (45.7%), while in the third-person setting Gemini 2.0 Flash and Qwen2.5-VL lead at 78.3%. Notably, CoT improves most models in first-person evaluation but degrades performance for several models (e.g., InternVL2.5 78B, VideoLLaMA 3) in the third-person setting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4o shows the largest CoT gain in first-person evaluation (+23.9%, from 35.6% to 59.5%) and also leads in third-person with CoT (82.9%), but is the weakest without CoT in first-person. Gemini 2.0 Flash and Qwen2.5-VL tie for best third-person accuracy without CoT (78.3%), with Qwen2.5-VL achieving 100% on goal inference; both show modest CoT improvements. Gemini 1.5 Pro is the strongest first-person model without CoT among the top tier (43.7%) and benefits from CoT to 57.0%, but its third-person CoT performance actually drops (75.4% → 71.4%). InternVL2.5 78B is the best first-person model without CoT (45.7%) but is the only top model whose CoT performance decreases (to 44.6%), likely due to insufficient step-by-step reasoning output. The smaller 7B models (VideoLLaMA 3, LLaVA-Video) perform substantially worse in third-person evaluation (37.7% and 50.3% respectively), with VideoLLaMA 3 showing the largest degradation under CoT (37.7% → 36.0%). LLaVA 1.6 13B is the weakest overall (31.6% first-person) and frequently fails to follow the multiple-choice format (5.3% of cases).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the ToM benchmarking literature by combining three dimensions that prior work has treated separately: multi-perspective evaluation (first-person state inference during interaction AND third-person goal/behavior inference after the fact), embodied multi-agent interaction in an open-world game (Minecraft), and diverse social dynamics (collaboration vs. covert obstruction). Prior multimodal ToM benchmarks (MMToM-QA, MuMA-ToM, Phase) evaluate single-perspective, single-relationship scenarios with short videos or static images, while text-based benchmarks (FANToM, ToMBench, NegotiationToM) lack any embodied or visual grounding. The SOMI environment itself builds on the Minecraft platform used by Mindcraft and MineDojo, but introduces a three-tiered asynchronous architecture enabling autonomous multi-agent interaction without human intervention, which prior Minecraft-based frameworks did not support.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The substantial performance gaps (40.1% in first-person, 26.4% in third-person) between humans and state-of-the-art LVLMs highlight that current models lack reliable state-tracking, resource accounting, and hierarchical goal understanding in dynamic multi-agent settings—capabilities essential for real-world embodied social AI. The finding that CoT prompting consistently improves first-person state inference but often degrades third-person goal/behavior inference for most models (except GPT-4o, Gemini 2.0 Flash, Qwen2.5-VL) suggests that explicit reasoning chains are only beneficial when the model's intrinsic reasoning capacity matches the task's demands, a nuance with broader implications for prompting strategies. Open questions include whether the benchmark's deliberately calibrated difficulty (simplified to match current model capabilities) will remain informative as models improve, and how social context (personality, history, relationship depth) affects ToM reasoning—directions the authors flag for future work.
