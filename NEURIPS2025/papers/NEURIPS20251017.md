## Problem

Modern LMs are trained with deterministic BPE tokenizers that map each string to a single canonical token sequence, yet the same vocabulary admits many alternative (non-canonical) segmentations. It has been widely assumed that models are tightly coupled to their training-time tokenization, but it remains unclear whether instruction-tuned LMs can actually process text presented in entirely unseen tokenization schemes at inference time, and whether such schemes could be exploited to improve performance.

## Input / Output

The input is a standard text prompt re-encoded using a non-canonical tokenization drawn from the same tokenizer vocabulary: either a uniformly random finer-grained split of each canonical token, a full character-level decomposition, or right-aligned three-digit grouping for numbers. No model weights are modified; only the token IDs fed to the model change. The output is the model's free-form generation or a benchmark accuracy score, evaluated across 20 standard benchmarks (MMLU, GSM8K, ARC, etc.) plus four constructed tasks (character counting, acronym generation, code description, large-number arithmetic). Notably, models consistently emit canonically tokenized output regardless of the input tokenization scheme.

## Method

The paper is an empirical study with three components. First, it measures robustness by re-tokenizing inputs for Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct, LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct, and OLMo-2-7B-Instruct and evaluating across 20 benchmarks, finding that the strongest model retains 93.4% of canonical performance under random tokenization and 90.8% under character-level tokenization, with retention correlating with overall model strength and decreasing as tokenization granularity increases (verified via BPE-dropout with varying p). Second, it identifies settings where non-canonical tokenization helps: character-level segmentation yields +14.3% on code description and +6.9% on character counting, while right-aligned digit grouping (e.g., ["1","000","000"] instead of ["100","000","0"]) improves 10-digit arithmetic by +33.7%, all without any fine-tuning. Third, it traces the source of robustness through the training pipeline using OLMo-2 and TULU-3 model families, showing that base models produce incoherent output on non-canonical inputs while SFT alone restores fluency; ablations reveal that the critical factor is the separation of instruction and response into distinct dialogue turns demarcated by special template tokens, not the loss masking or gradient computation. The authors explain this via a 'misspelling perception' hypothesis: both base and instruct models recognize the semantics of non-canonical tokenizations (scoring >90% on a word-repeat task) but misidentify them as misspellings (at chance on a misspelling-detection task); base models mimic the perceived errors and degenerate, while instruct models, trained to produce fluent responses regardless of input style, override this misperception.

## Results

Across 20 benchmarks, QWEN-2.5-7B-INSTRUCT retains 93.4% of its canonical performance under random non-canonical tokenization and 90.8% under character-level tokenization; LLAMA-3.1-8B-INSTRUCT retains 87.7% and 79.4%, respectively; OLMO-2-7B-INSTRUCT retains 73.1% and 62.0%. Stronger models are consistently more robust, and robustness decreases monotonically as tokenization granularity increases (Kendall's τ p=0.003). On constructed tasks where orthographic access matters, non-canonical tokenization *improves* performance: character-level segmentation yields +14.3% on code description and +33.7% on 10-digit arithmetic (via right-aligned digit grouping) for LLAMA-3.1-8B-INSTRUCT, with no finetuning required.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external methods; its central comparisons are internal. Across the three model families, robustness to non-canonical tokenization tracks absolute model strength: QWEN-2.5-7B (93.4% overall retention) > LLAMA-3.1-8B (87.7%) > OLMO-2-7B (73.1%) under random tokenization, with the same ordering under character-level tokenization (90.8% > 79.4% > 62.0%). Short-answer benchmarks consistently show better retention than multiple-choice benchmarks across all models, likely because free-form generation allows the model to condition on its own correctly-tokenized output. In the improvement experiments (§3), the canonical BPE tokenization is the implicit baseline: character-level tokenization outperforms it by +6.99 to +14.3 points on string/code tasks, and right-aligned digit grouping outperforms the default left-to-right grouping by +33.7 points on 10-digit arithmetic. Ablations in §4.2 show that removing the chat-template turn boundary during SFT collapses robustness (spelling score drops from 0.786 to 0.0698), identifying the instruction/response separation as the critical factor rather than the gradient masking or template format itself.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on tokenization robustness (BPE-dropout, StochasTok, CUTE) by shifting the question from training-time regularization to inference-time behavior: can a model trained with one deterministic BPE tokenization process entirely different token sequences of the same text? It also connects to prior observations that LMs maintain an implicit vocabulary and can compose non-canonical token sequences, but provides the first systematic generative evaluation across 20 benchmarks and multiple model families. The finding that robustness emerges specifically from SFT's turn structure (instruction/response separation) departs from prior work that attributed tokenization sensitivity to pretraining alone, and the inference-time tokenization intervention builds on arithmetic-tokenization findings by Singh & Strouse but generalizes to code and string manipulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication—that one can swap tokenization schemes at inference time for free performance gains (up to +33% on arithmetic, +14% on code) without any finetuning—opens a new axis of inference-time control that is broadly applicable to any BPE-based LM. The mechanistic finding that robustness requires the SFT turn structure (not just gradient exposure to varied tokenizations) suggests a specific architectural property of instruction-tuned models that may generalize to other forms of input perturbation. Open questions include whether automatically discovering the optimal tokenization per input is feasible, whether the effect holds for coarser (superword) tokenizations, and whether the 7–8B models studied here represent the floor or whether smaller models lose this robustness entirely.
