## Problem

Several closely related submodular and supermodular ratio problems—Densest Subgraph, Densest Supermodular Set, Submodular Function Minimization, Heavy Nodes in a Small Neighborhood, and Minimum Norm Point—are treated disparately across the literature with distinct algorithms and analyses, despite deep structural connections. The paper asks whether a unified perspective can bridge these gaps, enabling algorithmic cross-over where methods designed for one problem class can be repurposed to solve others, and whether simple combinatorial heuristics like SUPERGREEDY++ have a principled explanation for their empirical success beyond their original setting.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a normalized submodular or supermodular set function f: 2^V → ℝ via a value oracle, which in practice is instantiated by graph structures (undirected graphs for DSG, weighted bipartite graphs for HNSN, graphs with anchor sets for ADS) or general set functions. The output is either a combinatorial subset S ⊆ V (the optimal or approximate ratio set) or a continuous vector x* in the base polytope B(f) (the minimum norm point), with the two representations being interconvertible via thresholding. All reductions between the five equivalent problems run in O(n) or O(n log n) oracle calls, making the cross-over practically efficient.

## Method

The core contribution is a chain of strongly polynomial reductions proving that MNP, SFM, DSS, USSS, and UDSS are all equivalent, with the Minimum Norm Point in the base polytope B(f) serving as the unifying hub: thresholding the MNP solution at any λ recovers the minimizer of f(S) − λ|S| (Lemma 11), and the dense decomposition of f falls out as a byproduct. The paper then shows that three seemingly different algorithms—SUPERGREEDY++ (iterative peeling), Frank-Wolfe (conditional gradient), and Fujishige-Wolfe's FW-MNP (affine-hull refinement)—are all implicitly solving MNP, making them universal solvers for all five problem classes. The convergence proof for SUPERGREEDY++ on MNP uses Jaggi's Frank-Wolfe framework with an approximate LMO, where the PEELWEIGHTED subroutine provides a descent direction with error decaying as O(1/t), yielding a Õ((diam²(B(f)) + n·Σf(u|V−u)²)/ε²) iteration bound. A new flow-based algorithm for HNSN is constructed by embedding Dinkelbach's density-improvement iteration into a min-cut subproblem, achieving up to 595× speedups over prior state-of-the-art baselines.

## Results

On the HNSN problem (a special case of USSS), FW-MNP (Fujishige-Wolfe Minimum Norm Point) and FW (Frank-Wolfe) consistently outperform all six baselines from Ling et al. [42], achieving speedups ranging from 5× to 595× (with the 595× speedup on the YooChoose dataset). On the Minimum s-t Cut problem, SUPERGREEDY++ is consistently the fastest iterative method, finding the exact minimum cut in 16 of 24 instances and achieving solutions within 1.000023× the optimum in the remaining 8 cases after at most 500 iterations. Across over 400 experiments spanning seven problem types (DSG, DSS, USSS, UDSS, SFM, MNP, HNSN) and datasets with up to ~100 million edges, general-purpose convex optimization and combinatorial methods frequently outperform problem-specific state-of-the-art heuristics by orders of magnitude.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the six HNSN baselines from Ling et al. [42] (ContractDecompose, Greedy Approximation, Greedy, Fast Greedy, LP via Gurobi, and GreedRatio from Bai et al. [5]), FW-MNP and FW achieve speedups of 5× to 595× while reaching the same optimal density; notably, GreedRatio [5] terminated at a suboptimal solution on YooChoose and was excluded from runtime comparisons. The new flow-based algorithm (based on the density-improvement framework of Huang et al. [34] and Hochbaum [33]) ranks third, confirming that the iterative density-improvement strategy—not the specific flow solver—drives the speedup, as standard push-relabel solvers match Hochbaum's PseudoFlow performance. SUPERGREEDY++ (IP) ranks fourth on HNSN but dominates on Minimum s-t Cut, outperforming both FW-MNP and FW by large margins and converging to the exact cut faster than Edmonds-Karp in most instances. GREEDY++ [9] is referenced as a specialized DSG baseline but is not the focus of the main comparisons. Overall, the paper's key finding is that universal solvers (FW-MNP, FW, SUPERGREEDY++) beat or match all problem-specific baselines across every problem class tested, with the best-performing method varying by problem structure (FW-MNP excels on HNSN/USSS, while SUPERGREEDY++ excels on SFM/min-cut).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies five previously treated-disparately problems (MNP, SFM, DSS, USSS, UDSS) by proving they are equivalent under strongly polynomial reductions rooted in Fujishige's classical base polytope theory and the minimum norm point characterization. It extends Chekuri et al.'s SUPERGREEDY++ (originally for DSS) and Fujishige-Wolfe's MNP algorithm (originally for SFM) by showing both are, under the hood, solving the same MNP problem, thereby making them universal solvers across all five problem classes. The empirical framework builds on recent density-improvement flow methods (Huang et al., Hochbaum) and the HNSN formulation of Ling et al., adapting Dinkelbach's classical fractional programming iteration to new settings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The equivalence result enables algorithmic cross-over: a practitioner solving HNSN (a USSS instance) can now apply FW-MNP and achieve 595× speedups over task-specific baselines, while SUPERGREEDY++ becomes a practical min s-t cut solver. Key open questions remain: when does each universal solver dominate (the identity of the best method varies by instance), whether multiplicative approximation guarantees can be established for unrestricted (non-monotone, negative) settings, and whether the pseudo-polynomial runtime of SUPERGREEDY++ on SFM can be improved to strongly polynomial. The paper also leaves open a systematic exploration of decomposable submodular function minimization (DSFM) techniques and their interplay with these universal solvers.
