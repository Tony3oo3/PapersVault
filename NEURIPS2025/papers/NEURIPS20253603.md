## Problem

Current evaluations of Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) rely on established math and coding benchmarks that suffer from data contamination, offer no controlled manipulation of problem complexity, and measure only final answer accuracy while ignoring the structure and quality of intermediate reasoning traces. This leaves fundamental questions unanswered: how do frontier LRMs' reasoning capabilities scale with compositional complexity, how do they compare to non-reasoning counterparts under equivalent inference compute, and what patterns emerge inside their thinking processes as problems grow harder?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253603/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

Models receive a puzzle problem description with a controllable size parameter N (disk count, checker count, actor-agent pairs, or block count) plus explicit initial and goal state configurations, and must output a complete sequence of moves in a prescribed format. A custom puzzle simulator validates every individual move against the puzzle's constraints and, critically, extracts all intermediate candidate solutions explored within the reasoning trace, enabling step-by-step analysis of the thinking process rather than just final-answer correctness. In a separate experiment, the model is additionally given the explicit solution algorithm and asked to merely execute the prescribed steps, isolating execution ability from solution discovery.

## Method

The paper constructs a controlled experimental framework using four algorithmic puzzle environments (Tower of Hanoi, Checker Jumping, River Crossing, Blocks World) where complexity is systematically varied by adjusting problem size while preserving the core logical structure, avoiding the data contamination and uncontrolled difficulty of standard math benchmarks. The core comparison pairs thinking and non-thinking variants of the same model backbone (Claude 3.7 Sonnet, DeepSeek R1/V3, plus o3-mini and QwQ-32B for accuracy-only experiments) under equivalent inference token budgets, measuring both pass@k upper-bound performance and per-complexity accuracy. Custom puzzle simulators validate every move in both final solutions and intermediate reasoning traces, enabling extraction and positional analysis of all candidate solutions explored during the thinking process. This reveals three distinct complexity regimes (non-thinking wins at low complexity, thinking wins at medium, both collapse at high), a counterintuitive scaling limit where thinking tokens decrease near the accuracy collapse point despite ample remaining budget, and the failure of models to benefit from explicit algorithm provision—indicating a fundamental limitation in exact sequential execution rather than solution discovery.

## Results

Across four controllable puzzle environments (Tower of Hanoi, Checker Jumping, River Crossing, Blocks World), frontier LRMs (o3-mini, DeepSeek-R1, Claude-3.7-Sonnet-Thinking) exhibit complete accuracy collapse to near-zero beyond model-specific complexity thresholds (e.g., Tower of Hanoi at N≈7–8, River Crossing at N=3 requiring only 11 moves). Counterintuitively, thinking token usage initially increases with complexity but then *decreases* near the collapse point, despite models operating well below their 64k token generation limits. On MATH-500, pass@k of thinking models is comparable to non-thinking counterparts under equivalent inference token budgets, while the gap widens on AIME24 and further on AIME25—suggesting potential data contamination effects.

**Table: Three complexity regimes for thinking vs. non-thinking models (averaged across puzzles, equivalent inference compute).**

| Complexity Regime | Non-thinking (Claude 3.7 Sonnet / DeepSeek-V3) | Thinking (Claude 3.7 Sonnet Thinking / DeepSeek-R1) | Relative Advantage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low (simple) | Higher accuracy, fewer tokens | Lower accuracy, more tokens | Non-thinking wins |
| Medium (moderate) | Moderate accuracy | Higher accuracy, more tokens | Thinking wins |
| High (complex) | Collapses to ~0% | Collapses to ~0% (slightly delayed) | Both fail |

*Note: The paper presents these results primarily as figures (Figs. 4–6) showing accuracy and thinking-token curves vs. problem size N, rather than a single numeric table. Collapse points vary by puzzle: Tower of Hanoi N≈7–8, Checker Jumping N≈4, River Crossing N=3, Blocks World N≈4 (temp=0) to N≈30 (sampling).*

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares each reasoning model against its non-thinking counterpart with the same backbone: Claude 3.7 Sonnet (thinking vs. non-thinking) and DeepSeek-R1 vs. DeepSeek-V3. At low puzzle complexity, non-thinking models achieve equal or better accuracy with fewer tokens, indicating that extended thinking is unnecessary and even counterproductive for simple problems. At medium complexity, thinking models pull ahead, confirming a genuine but bounded advantage of RL-trained long CoT. At high complexity, both model types collapse to near-zero accuracy, with thinking models only slightly delaying the collapse threshold. On established math benchmarks (MATH-500), pass@k converges between thinking and non-thinking models under matched token budgets, while the gap widens on AIME24/AIME25—potentially reflecting reduced contamination rather than superior reasoning. A striking additional comparison shows that even when the solution algorithm is explicitly provided in the prompt (reducing the task to mere execution), performance does not improve and collapse occurs at the same complexity points, suggesting a fundamental limitation in sequential step execution rather than solution discovery.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of three research threads: (1) the growing literature on RL-trained Large Reasoning Models (o1/o3, DeepSeek-R1, Claude Thinking) and their emergent behaviors like overthinking and thought-answer discrepancy; (2) prior work showing that pass@k performance of thinking and non-thinking models converges on math benchmarks (Yue et al., Ma et al.); and (3) controllable puzzle-based evaluation environments used in planning benchmarks (Valmeekam et al., Ruoss et al.). Rather than proposing a new benchmark, the paper repurposes these puzzle environments as a controlled experimental testbed to systematically vary compositional complexity and analyze intermediate reasoning traces—something established math benchmarks cannot support due to contamination and lack of fine-grained structural control. The key departure from prior work is the shift from measuring final-answer accuracy to dissecting the internal reasoning process and identifying complexity-dependent behavioral regimes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The findings challenge the prevailing narrative that RL-trained reasoning models represent a qualitative leap in generalizable reasoning, revealing instead a hard complexity ceiling beyond which all frontier LRMs collapse to near-zero accuracy. The discovery that providing the solution algorithm does not improve performance points to a fundamental limitation in symbolic execution and verification that is orthogonal to the model's ability to discover solutions. Practically, the three-regime structure (standard LLMs win at low complexity, LRMs win at medium, both collapse at high) has direct implications for when and whether to deploy expensive thinking tokens. Open questions remain about whether these limitations are specific to sequential planning puzzles or generalize to broader reasoning, and whether the observed scaling limit in thinking tokens reflects a training-time artifact or an architectural constraint.
