## Problem

Existing motor unit decomposition algorithms from high-density EMG (e.g., SCD, MUEdit, convolutive BSS) are computationally intensive—requiring up to hours of processing—and often need manual intervention, confining them to controlled research settings. This computational bottleneck prevents real-time closed-loop neuroprosthetic control, where rapid and reliable decoding of motor intent from peripheral EMG is essential for assistive applications such as FES-driven hand movement in spinal cord injury patients.

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes multichannel HD-EMG signals (64 monopolar channels from an 8×8 grid at 2048 Hz, or 75 bipolar channels from a stretchable fabric at 3000 Hz) after bandpass filtering (20–500 Hz) and FES artifact removal. Input is windowed into non-overlapping bins (20 ms for SPD matrix computation, 100 ms for SNN inference) and augmented with R lagged channel copies. Outputs include per-motor-unit binary spike trains, BSS forward spatial filters (mappable to the 2D electrode grid as dipoles), and MUAP waveforms via spike-triggered averaging; in the clinical application, a downstream SNN converts spike trains into 10 Hz three-class motor-intent predictions (Hand Open, Hand Close, Rest) that trigger FES patterns.

## Method

MUelim frames decomposition as second-order-statistics blind source separation: it computes cospectral SPD matrices from extend-lag-augmented data (R lagged copies of each channel, inspired by EEG augmented-covariance embeddings and Takens delay embedding) to increase effective dimensionality and improve source separability. An approximate joint diagonalization (JADOC) is then performed on randomly sampled subsets of these SPD matrices, exploiting the sparsity of motor unit activity to extract many candidate sources simultaneously rather than incrementally. A subtractive elimination loop refines each candidate via peak detection in source power, k-means-based signal/noise separation, silhouette-score thresholding (SIL > 0.85), and cosine-similarity uniqueness checks, discarding redundant or noisy sources. For the neuroprosthetic application, the resulting spike trains feed a fully-connected spiking neural network (1000×500 LIF neurons, 40% dropout, trained with SLAYER for 15 epochs) that decodes three-class motor intent in real time, enabling a spinal cord injury participant to volitionally trigger movement-specific FES patterns during task practice.

## Results

On simulated HD-EMG data (32–64 channels, 5–30 motor units), MUelim achieves 99.99±0.01% spike-timing accuracy with 0.99±0.14 false positives and 0.03±0.02 false negatives per source, matching SCD and outperforming MUEdit (98.98±0.07% accuracy, 3.06±0.20 FN). On physiological data from six healthy participants, MUelim (extension factor 4, lag 4) decomposes 18.8±4.2 motor units in 3.1±0.2 minutes during ramp contractions and 11.2±2.2 in 0.7±0.0 minutes during MVC, with a mean silhouette score of 0.92±0.01. In the clinical neuroprosthetic application, the motor-unit-based SNN decoder achieves 85% bin-wise accuracy for real-time FES control, comparable to an offline RMS-NN decoder (86%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MUelim is compared against two state-of-the-art open-source decomposition methods: SCD (Swarm Contrastive Decomposition, Grison et al. 2025) and MUEdit (Avrillon et al. 2024). Against SCD, MUelim decomposes substantially more motor units (18.8 vs. 3.2 in ramp; 11.2 vs. 5.6 in MVC) while also being faster (3.1 vs. 14.6 min for ramp), indicating SCD's lower source yield is a more significant limitation than its speed. Against MUEdit, MUelim achieves a comparable source count in the ramp paradigm (18.8 vs. 18.2) but with a dramatic 36× speed advantage (3.1 vs. 112.3 min); in MVC, MUEdit extracts slightly more sources (12.5 vs. 11.2) but takes 44× longer (30.9 vs. 0.7 min). On simulated data, all three methods identify the correct number of sources, with MUelim and SCD achieving near-identical 99.99% accuracy, while MUEdit has a higher false-negative rate (3.06 vs. 0.03 per source). In the clinical closed-loop setting, the MUelim-based SNN decoder (85% bin-wise accuracy) performs on par with an offline RMS-NN baseline (86%), confirming that the speed advantage of MUelim does not come at the cost of decoding quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

MUelim sits at the intersection of second-order-statistics blind source separation (Belouchrani et al. 1997, Pham 2001) and the convolutive BSS framework that underpins prior HD-EMG decomposition methods (Negro et al. 2016, Holobar & Zazula 2007). Its key conceptual departure is the adoption of an extend-lag data augmentation procedure borrowed from EEG covariance-matrix classification (Carrara & Papadopoulo 2024) to overcome the channel-count limitation of SOS methods, combined with a compressive-sensing-inspired strategy of randomly sampling SPD matrices for iterative joint diagonalization. The algorithm is instantiated with JADOC (de Vlaming & Slob 2021) as the diagonalization solver and positions itself as a speed-oriented alternative to the higher-order-statistics methods SCD (Grison et al. 2025) and MUEdit (Avrillon et al. 2024), which the authors identify as too slow and inconsistent for closed-loop neuroprosthetic use.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 36× speed improvement over MUEdit (minutes vs. hours) is the practical enabler that moves motor-unit decomposition from an offline research tool toward a viable real-time control signal for assistive neurotechnology, as demonstrated by the closed-loop FES control experiment with a spinal cord injury participant. However, the clinical evidence is thin—only one participant, a single session, and a 10 Hz prediction rate—leaving open questions about long-term filter stability, generalizability across injury levels and muscles, and performance on intramuscular EMG. The near-parity between the motor-unit-based SNN decoder (85%) and a standard RMS-NN decoder (86%) also raises the question of whether the added complexity of full decomposition is justified over simpler features in practical deployments, a point the authors acknowledge but do not resolve.
