## Problem

Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) are the leading tool for extracting interpretable features from LLMs, but they provide no guarantee that target concepts are present, localized, or monosemantic in the latent space. Moreover, no standardized metric exists to quantify how monosemantic a given feature representation actually is, making it impossible to systematically compare or rank SAEs by representational quality.

## Input / Output

The method consumes residual stream activations (d-dimensional vectors) extracted from a frozen transformer block (e.g., block 11 of Llama-3-8B-base) along with per-token or per-sequence concept labels for supervised conditioning. For detection, it outputs per-token concept activation values in [0,1] from the conditioned latent features; for steering, it produces modified residual streams by adding scaled decoder-column steering vectors controlled by a steering factor α ∈ [-1,1] and a balancing parameter γ that adapts to current concept presence. The FMS metric outputs a scalar monosemanticity score in [0,1] combining feature capacity, local disentanglement, and global disentanglement.

## Method

G-SAE extends a standard TopK-sparse SAE (encoder-decoder with Sigmoid activation, latent dimension 6× hidden dimension) by adding a binary cross-entropy conditioning loss on a contiguous block of latent features f[0:c], forcing each supervised concept to occupy a known, isolated latent dimension. The joint training objective is L_total = L_reconstruction + L_conditioning, where the reconstruction loss preserves general SAE fidelity while the conditioning loss enforces monosemantic localization. For detection, only the encoder projects the residual stream into the monosemantic space, leaving the stream unchanged; for steering, decoder columns serve as concept-specific steering vectors, normalized to the residual stream magnitude and modulated by γ_i (the current activation of that concept) to enable adaptive, fine-grained control. The FMS metric uses iteratively trained decision tree classifiers to decompose monosemanticity into three components: feature capacity (root-node accuracy), local disentanglement (accuracy drop after removing top-p features), and global disentanglement (cumulative accuracy gain from adding further features), combined into a single normalized score.

## Results

G-SAE nearly doubles the Feature Monosemanticity Score (FMS) over vanilla SAEs across all three domains: average FMS@1 improves from 0.27 to 0.52, and FMS@5 from 0.30 to 0.56. The gains are most pronounced for privacy (FMS@1: 0.62 vs. 0.28) and Shakespeare (0.57 vs. 0.28), while toxicity shows a more modest improvement (0.37 vs. 0.26), likely reflecting the greater conceptual complexity of toxicity. In steering, G-SAE achieves the highest SuccessRate on all four evaluation settings, reaching 0.98 on toxicity detoxification, 0.72 on Shakespearean style, 0.82 on multi-concept (T&S), and 0.53 on privacy preservation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

G-SAE outperforms all five steering baselines on SuccessRate across every dataset. Against the strongest latent-space methods (ICV and DiffVec), G-SAE gains +0.01 on toxicity (0.98 vs. 0.97), +0.06 on Shakespeare (0.72 vs. 0.66), and matches on T&S (0.82 vs. 0.82), while leading by +0.11 on privacy (0.53 vs. 0.42). Prompt-based methods (PreADD, Model Arithmetic) trail more substantially, with PreADD reaching only 0.53 on Shakespeare and 0.40 on privacy. The unmodified Llama3-8B-base is the weakest baseline, confirming that steering is necessary. In pairwise LLM-judge win-rate comparisons, G-SAE answers are reliably preferred over all baselines, indicating that the SuccessRate gains are not merely metric artifacts but reflect genuinely better output quality. The vanilla SAE (unconditioned) is consistently outperformed by G-SAE, with the largest gap on privacy (+0.06), confirming that the conditioning loss is the key differentiator.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Sparse Autoencoder (SAE) framework for LLM interpretability (Gao et al., ICLR 2025; Templeton et al., 2024) by introducing a supervised latent conditioning loss that enforces concept localization in specific latent dimensions, departing from the fully unsupervised training paradigm of prior SAEs. It also draws on the disentanglement literature (MIG, β-VAE) to formalize monosemanticity as a localized form of disentanglement, and builds on Paulo & Belrose's observation that SAEs trained on identical data learn different features, motivating the need for guaranteed concept presence. For steering, it positions itself against contrastive activation addition (DiffVec), in-context vectors (ICV), and model arithmetic, offering a monosemantic alternative that avoids the noisy, multi-forward-pass overhead of prior methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The FMS metric provides the first systematic, model-agnostic tool for quantifying monosemanticity in latent representations, filling a gap the authors explicitly identify in the interpretability literature. The finding that vanilla SAEs spread concepts across ~41 features on average (rather than isolating them in single neurons) challenges the common assumption that SAEs yield monosemantic features, with practical implications for anyone relying on SAE-based detection or steering. Key limitations include the need for labeled data at training time (shifting supervision burden from inference to training), the impracticality of supervising many concepts simultaneously, and residual activations in semantically neighboring features. Open questions include whether hierarchical latent structures (e.g., Matryoshka SAEs) or global disentanglement regularizers could further improve feature isolation without the supervision cost.
