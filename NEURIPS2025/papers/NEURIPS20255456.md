## Problem

The algorithmic fairness discourse has produced two competing narratives about decision-making ecosystems: monoculture, where homogeneous models cause systematic exclusion with no recourse, and multiplicity, where many models produce inconsistent and arbitrary outcomes. Neither narrative has been empirically tested in the context of large language models used for consequential prediction tasks, leaving it unclear which (or whether either) actually describes the landscape decision-makers will face.

## Input / Output

The evaluation takes 50 pre-trained LLMs (open-source models from 1B to 141B parameters plus commercial models such as GPT-4.1) and prompts them with natural-text encodings of tabular features from 7 binary classification tasks (6 from US Census data, 1 from BRFSS health surveys), under both zero-shot and 10-shot conditions. Each model yields a binary prediction per instance, which is aggregated across the empirical Rashomon set (models within ε=0.05 accuracy of the best) to produce pairwise agreement rates, individual recourse levels, ambiguity, and discrepancy. A complementary analysis varies four minor prompt aspects—feature order, format, connector, and granularity—across 90 prompting styles per model to measure how prompt variation alone reshapes agreement and recourse.

## Method

The paper is a large-scale empirical evaluation rather than a new architecture or training procedure. It uses the folktexts package to standardize zero-shot and 10-shot prompting in a multiple-choice format, calibrating raw model outputs via a decision threshold fitted on a 2,000-sample validation set to maximize balanced accuracy. The analytical framework centers on the empirical Rashomon set, where the authors measure pairwise agreement against two theoretical baselines (randomly located errors for strong multiplicity, and perfect consensus for strict monoculture) and introduce recourse with respect to a model set—the fraction of models accepting a given individual—as a generalization of Bommasani et al.'s systemic failure metric. They further test whether minor prompt variations from a single model can substitute for model diversity in providing recourse, and check robustness under few-shot prompting and disaggregation by model provider, variant, and demographic groups (sex, race, age).

## Results

Across 50 LLMs and 7 binary prediction tasks, the empirical landscape sits between strict monoculture and strong multiplicity. On ACSIncome, 79% of positive instances are accepted by at least half the models (substantial recourse), while 7% face no recourse and 51% enjoy full recourse; mean pairwise agreement is 88.37% versus a random-error baseline of 63.66%. Across all tasks, no-recourse rates range from 3% (BRFSS Blood Pressure) to 68% (ACSMobility), and ambiguity ranges from 0.30 (ACSEmployment) to 0.86 (ACSPublicCoverage). Even minor prompt variations (feature order, format, connector, granularity) shift accuracy by up to 3 percentage points and induce substantial predictive multiplicity within a single model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing methods but rather benchmarks observed model behavior against two theoretical extremes: strict monoculture (all models identical, agreement = 100%) and strong multiplicity (errors randomly located, agreement = aa′ + (1−a)(1−a′)). Observed pairwise agreement (mean 88.37% on ACSIncome) far exceeds the random-error baseline (mean 63.66%), confirming that models share strong inductive biases and are not independently erring. However, agreement is well below 100%, ruling out strict monoculture. On the recourse dimension, the observed distribution (7% no recourse, 51% full recourse on ACSIncome) shows a higher concentration at both extremes than the random-error Poisson-Binomial baseline would predict, yet the curve is far from the step function that strict monoculture would produce. Two tasks—ACSMobility (68% no recourse) and ACSPublicCoverage (14% no recourse, 0% full recourse)—deviate toward the monoculture extreme, driven by low predictive signal and high class imbalance. The authors note their setup (identical prompts, same dataset) likely understates real-world diversity, so actual ecosystems may be further from monoculture than measured.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate research threads—predictive multiplicity (Rashomon set, ambiguity, discrepancy from Breiman 2001 and Marx et al. 2020) and algorithmic monoculture/outcome homogenization (Kleinberg & Raghavan 2021, Bommasani et al. 2022)—into a single empirical evaluation on modern LLMs. Prior multiplicity work examined traditional models (regression, trees, small neural nets) in controlled settings, while monoculture work was largely theoretical or used early models (CLIP, RoBERTa-base). The paper generalizes Bommasani et al.'s systemic failure metric into a continuous 'recourse level' and applies both sets of measures to 50 LLMs (1B–141B parameters) under zero- and few-shot prompting, using the folktexts calibration framework from Cruz et al. 2024.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that the empirical landscape sits firmly between strict monoculture and strong multiplicity—79% of positive instances have substantial recourse, yet 7% face systematic exclusion—challenges both the monoculture alarm and the multiplicity comfort that have dominated the fairness discourse. The discovery that even minor prompt variations (feature order, formatting) induce meaningful predictive diversity is practically important for institutions deploying LLMs, as it suggests natural operational heterogeneity provides a partial safeguard. However, the paper acknowledges its setup favors monoculture (identical dataset, similar model families), so real-world ecosystems with different data encodings may be even more diverse. Open questions include how demographic disparities in recourse (noted for race and age) evolve with model scale, and whether policy interventions should target the non-negligible 'no recourse' tail rather than the broader ecosystem.
