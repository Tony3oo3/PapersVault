## Problem

Existing graph generation methods, including discrete and continuous diffusion models, approximate the joint distribution of node and edge attributes but overlook topologically invariant properties such as connected components and loops. This limitation is particularly consequential for domains like brain connectomics, where global topological structure (e.g., inter-hemisphere connectivity determining the number of connected components) is a critical biomarker and is lost when generation focuses solely on local statistics like degree and clustering coefficients.

## Input / Output

During generation, the model starts from a fully noisy graph (random categorical assignments for all nodes and edges) and iteratively denoises over T discrete diffusion steps. At each denoising step, the network receives the current noisy graph G_t and a pre-computed averaged homological feature vector μ_G' ∈ R^(L·S) (derived from persistence landscapes of all training graphs), since individual graph-to-vector matching is unavailable at inference time. The output is a binary adjacency matrix with node and edge attributes, obtained by thresholding the final probability vectors at 0.5.

## Method

TAGG builds on the discrete categorical diffusion framework of DiGress, where forward noise is imposed via transition matrices Q_t over one-hot node/edge attributes. Two topology-aware components differentiate it: (1) a Topology-aware Attention Module (TAM) that injects the persistence-landscape embedding H_{G0} as a global additive bias into the attention score, enabling the network to capture homological structure tracked across the entire filtration rather than only local edge information; and (2) a Persistence Diagram Matching (PDM) loss that computes the 1-Wasserstein distance between persistence diagrams of the original and predicted graphs (with diagonal padding for cardinality alignment), regularizing the denoiser to preserve Betti numbers. The denoising network applies TAM iteratively M times, and training minimizes a weighted combination of cross-entropy on node/edge attributes and the PDM loss.

## Results

TAGG achieves the best average MMD score across all four datasets: 0.427 on ADNI (160-node brain networks), 0.021 on ENZYMES, 0.040 on Community-small, and 0.015 on Ego-small, outperforming the strongest baseline DiGress by 37%–67% in average MMD. On the homological assessment (ADNI), TAGG attains the lowest ATOL KL-divergence (0.23), Persistence Image MSE (0.59), and Curvature Filtration score (27.96), reducing these by 30.3%, 11.9%, and 11.7% respectively over the second-best method (DiGress). TAGG also most closely matches the real brain network's Betti numbers (β̄₀=2.92 vs. 2.98; β̄₁=229.96 vs. 225.64), whereas baselines deviate substantially (e.g., GDSS: β̄₀=6.54, β̄₁=90.98).

## Baselines & Comparisons

TAGG outperforms all five baselines on the average MMD across every dataset. Against the closest discrete-diffusion competitor DiGress, TAGG reduces average MMD from 0.683→0.427 on ADNI (37% improvement), 0.093→0.021 on ENZYMES (77%), 0.076→0.040 on Community-small (47%), and 0.046→0.015 on Ego-small (67%). The continuous-diffusion baseline GDSS is particularly weak on ENZYMES (0.301 avg MMD) and ADNI (0.739), while the autoregressive GraphRNN lags most on ADNI (0.820) and Community-small (0.159). LocalPPGN achieves the best single-metric scores on some small-graph metrics (e.g., Ego-small Orbit 0.006) but is consistently outperformed on the averaged score. On the dedicated homological assessment (Table 4), TAGG leads all baselines on ATOL, PI, and CF, with the largest gap over EDP-GNN (ATOL 0.23 vs. 16.25) and a clear margin over DiGress (ATOL 0.23 vs. 0.33; CF 27.96 vs. 31.68). In Betti-number fidelity on ADNI, TAGG (β̄₀=2.92, β̄₁=229.96) nearly matches the test set (2.98, 225.64), while GDSS and LocalPPGN overestimate β̄₀ (6.54, 6.00) and EDP-GNN drastically overestimates β̄₁ (1120.64).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends discrete diffusion-based graph generation (following DiGress [43] and its predecessor [3]) by explicitly incorporating topological invariants from persistent homology into both the training objective and the denoising architecture. It departs from continuous-time score-based methods (GDSS, EDP-GNN) that lose graph sparsity under Gaussian noise, and from prior TDA-informed graph learning (filtration learning, topological GNNs) that used persistent homology for classification or link prediction rather than generation. The two core contributions — a Wasserstein-distance-based Persistence Diagram Matching loss and a Topology-aware Attention Module that injects persistence landscape embeddings as a global attention bias — bridge algebraic topology and discrete diffusion in a way not previously attempted for graph synthesis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that topological fidelity (connected components, loops) is a meaningful and measurable gap in current graph generators, particularly for structured real-world graphs like brain connectomes where inter-hemisphere connectivity determines the number of connected components. The PDM loss's transferability to unrelated baselines (Table 3) suggests it could serve as a plug-in regularizer for any graph generator. Key limitations include the ~10× training overhead from CPU-only persistent homology computation, the inability to track which individual topological features drive learning, and the reliance on a single vectorization method (persistence landscapes) whose optimality is not established. Open questions include GPU-accelerated TDA, alternative filtration functions for higher-dimensional simplices, and whether topology-aware generation extends to dynamic or attributed graphs.
