## Problem

Knowledge graph data is scarce, and existing automated extractors (OpenIE, GraphRAG) lack effective entity resolution and relation normalization, producing sparse, disconnected graphs where nearly every edge has a unique relation type. This sparsity renders extracted KGs poorly suited for downstream tasks like graph embeddings and multi-hop retrieval, and the field lacks benchmarks to measure extraction fidelity.

## Input / Output

KGGen consumes unstructured plain text of arbitrary length (tested from 100 characters to 1M+ characters) and produces a knowledge graph of subject-predicate-object triples with de-duplicated entities and consolidated relation types. Each relation is linked back to its source text chunk, enabling provenance-aware retrieval. The method is zero-shot: no task-specific training is required, and the LLM is constrained via DSPy signatures to ensure subjects and objects in triples are drawn from a pre-extracted entity list.

## Method

KGGen is a three-stage pipeline: (1) a two-step LLM extraction (using Gemini 2.0 Flash via DSPy) first identifies entities, then extracts triples constrained to those entities, ensuring consistency; (2) cross-source aggregation normalizes all entities and edges to lowercase and merges duplicates; (3) an iterative entity and edge resolution stage combines S-BERT semantic embeddings with k-means clustering (clusters of 128), retrieves top-16 candidates per item via fused BM25 and cosine similarity, and prompts an LLM to identify exact duplicates (accounting for tense, plurality, case, abbreviations) and select a canonical representative. This hybrid clustering-plus-LLM approach consolidates synonymous entities and relation types, preventing the sparse, under-specified graphs that would yield meaningless TransE/TransR embeddings, while strong prompting constraints prevent over-merging of semantically distinct but superficially similar items.

## Results

On MINE-1 (knowledge retention from short texts), KGGen achieves 66.07% average fact-recovery accuracy, substantially outperforming GraphRAG (47.80%) and OpenIE (29.84%). On MINE-2 (KG-assisted RAG over WikiQA), KGGen performs comparably to GraphRAG. In a manual triple-validity check, 98% of KGGen's extracted triples conform to proper subject-predicate-object structure, versus 0% for GraphRAG and 55% for OpenIE. On scaling, KGGen reuses each unique relation type an average of ~10 times (increasing with corpus size), while GraphRAG reuses each relation type only ~2 times regardless of corpus size.

## Baselines & Comparisons

KGGen clearly outperforms both baselines on MINE-1, scoring 66.07% versus GraphRAG's 47.80% and OpenIE's 29.84%, indicating substantially better information retention from source text. On MINE-2 (downstream RAG over WikiQA), KGGen and GraphRAG perform comparably, suggesting that GraphRAG's community-summarization strategy compensates for its sparser graph structure in retrieval. However, KGGen produces far more structurally valid knowledge graphs: 98% of its triples are proper subject-predicate-object triples, while GraphRAG scores 0% (its 'relations' are long natural-language sentences rather than concise predicates) and OpenIE scores 55%. On generalizability, KGGen's relation types are reused ~10 times on average and this ratio grows with corpus size, whereas GraphRAG's relations are nearly unique (966 relation types for 981 edges on a 1M-character corpus), limiting their utility for embedding-based downstream tasks. KGGen also processes the 1M-character test corpus in 551 seconds at $0.84 total cost, compared to GraphRAG's 2,319 seconds for the extraction phase alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

KGGen positions itself against two dominant text-to-KG extractors: OpenIE (Angeli et al., 2015), which uses dependency parsing and natural logic inference, and Microsoft's GraphRAG (Larson and Truitt, 2024), which prompts LLMs to extract entities and relations with few-shot examples. The key conceptual departure is the addition of an iterative entity and edge resolution stage—combining S-BERT embeddings, k-means clustering, fused BM25/semantic retrieval, and LLM-based de-duplication—inspired by crowd-sourcing entity resolution strategies (Wang et al., 2012). This resolution step is what distinguishes KGGen from prior LLM-based extractors that produce sparse graphs with near-unique relation types per edge. The paper also introduces MINE, the first benchmark specifically designed to measure extraction fidelity from plain text, filling a gap the authors identify in the evaluation literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The KG scarcity problem directly bottlenecks graph-based RAG, KG embedding methods (TransE, TransR), and synthetic training data generation, making a high-quality open-source extractor practically important. The MINE benchmark provides the field with a standardized way to compare extractors on information retention and downstream RAG utility, which was previously absent. However, open questions remain: the benchmark only covers corpora up to ~5M tokens (far below web-scale), the LLM-generated articles in MINE-1 may not fully capture the noise and ambiguity of real-world text, and domain-specific extraction (medicine, finance) remains unaddressed. The finding that GraphRAG produces 0% structurally valid triples while achieving comparable RAG accuracy raises an interesting question about whether traditional KG structure is actually necessary for retrieval performance.
