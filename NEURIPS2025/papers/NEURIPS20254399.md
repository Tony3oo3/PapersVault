## Problem

It is widely assumed that RL finetuning of LLMs requires updating all model parameters, since desired post-training behaviors differ substantially from pretrained capabilities. This paper asks whether RL actually updates all parameters or only a subset, and discovers that it updates only a small subnetwork (5%–30% of parameters) across all 7 major RL algorithms and 10 LLMs, without any explicit sparsity-promoting regularization or architectural constraint.

## Input / Output

The analysis takes publicly released pretrained LLM checkpoints (Llama, DeepSeek, Mistral, Qwen, Eurus families at 7B–70B scale) in bfloat16 precision and compares them against their RL-finetuned counterparts, treating two parameters as 'unchanged' when their absolute difference is ≤ 10⁻⁵. The key analytical outputs are a binary subnetwork mask (same dimensionality as the full parameter vector) identifying which parameters were updated, a scalar update sparsity metric, and a subnetwork-only finetuned model produced by re-running RL with gradients masked to only the identified subnetwork. For the controlled experiments, training data consists of preference pairs (DPO) or on-policy rollouts (PRIME/GRPO) from GSM8K, MATH, Tulu, and PRIME datasets.

## Method

The paper is an empirical study built around three core experiments. First, it measures parameter update sparsity by element-wise comparison of pre- and post-RL checkpoints across 7 algorithms (PPO, GRPO, DPO, KTO, ORPO, SimPO, PRIME) and 10 models, finding 68.5%–96% of parameters unchanged. Second, it tests Conjecture 1 via a gradient-masking procedure: after identifying the subnetwork from a full RL run, it re-runs the same RL training with gradients zeroed outside the mask, showing the resulting model is 90–94% identical in parameter values and matches or exceeds full-finetuning test accuracy. Third, it ablates potential causes of sparsity, finding that training on in-distribution data (data close to the current policy distribution) is the dominant factor, while KL regularization, gradient clipping, and prior SFT have limited impact. The updates, though sparse, are nearly full-rank across all parameter matrices, and the subnetwork shows substantial overlap across different seeds, datasets, and RL algorithms, suggesting a partially transferable structure in the pretrained model.

## Results

Across 7 RL algorithms and 10 LLMs, RL finetuning updates only 5%–30% of parameters (68.5%–96.0% sparsity), while SFT produces dense updates (only 6%–15% sparsity). Finetuning only the identified subnetwork (θ_sub) matches or outperforms full finetuning (θ_full): on DPO, θ_sub achieves 41.9 avg vs 40.3 for θ_full (+1.6); on PRIME, θ_sub reaches 72.2 overall MATH500 vs 69.8 for θ_full (+2.4), with the largest gain of +5.2 on the hardest level. Parameter overlap between θ_sub and θ_full is 94.0% (DPO) and 90.5% (PRIME) at tolerance 10⁻⁵, rising to 100% at tolerance 10⁻⁴. Updates are nearly full-rank (99.2%–99.8% of max rank) despite their sparsity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares subnetwork-only finetuning against full RL finetuning across two algorithm families. For DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023), θ_sub outperforms θ_full on 4 of 5 tasks (LSAT-AR +3.5, LSAT-LR +1.6, LogiQA +2.0, MMLU Pro Math +0.8) and ties on GPQA, yielding a +1.6 average gain. For PRIME (Cui et al., 2025), θ_sub matches θ_full on levels 1–2 and surpasses it on levels 3–5 (up to +5.2 on the hardest level), for a +2.4 overall MATH500 improvement. On the in-distribution vs. out-of-distribution axis, rejection-sampling SFT (RFT) achieves 91.2% sparsity and RAFT++ (Xiong et al., 2024) achieves 69.4%, both far sparser than out-of-distribution SFT (6.8%) or out-of-distribution DPO (6.8%–7.7%), confirming that data distribution proximity—not the specific algorithm—drives sparsity. GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) with KL regularization shows 69.8% sparsity vs. 68.8% without, indicating KL has negligible impact on the sparsity phenomenon.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a natural extension of the Lottery Ticket Hypothesis to the RL finetuning regime, but departs from LTH in three key ways: the subnetworks emerge organically during training rather than being identified via pruning, the recovery claim extends from matching performance to recovering nearly identical parameter values, and the setting is finetuning from pretrained LLMs rather than training from scratch. It also contrasts with explicit sparse-training and LoRA-based RL methods by showing that comparable parameter efficiency arises without any architectural constraint or low-rank restriction—the updates are sparse yet nearly full-rank. The paper carves new empirical ground by being the first to systematically document this sparsity phenomenon across seven RL algorithms and ten model families, and by identifying in-distribution training (rather than KL regularization or gradient clipping) as the primary causal factor.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that RL effectively updates only 5–30% of parameters offers a concrete mechanistic explanation for why RL better preserves pretrained capabilities than SFT, and opens a practical path toward more efficient RL training by explicitly freezing the inactive ~70–95% of parameters. The subnetwork's partial consistency across seeds, datasets, and algorithms suggests potential for cross-run parameter reuse in hyperparameter sweeps or algorithm transfers (e.g., reusing a DPO-identified subnetwork in PPO). Key open questions include a theoretical account of why in-distribution training induces sparsity, whether the phenomenon extends to multimodal or diffusion models, and whether the subnetwork can be identified early in training to enable dynamic sparsity-aware optimization. Limitations include single-factor-at-a-time ablations, reliance on public checkpoints for some comparisons, and the absence of a formal proof for Conjecture 1.
