## Problem

Scientific machine learning lacks a standardized common task framework with hidden test sets, leading to weak baselines, reporting bias, and p-hacking through self-reported test scores. Unlike NLP and computer vision, which have mature CTF platforms driving progress, scientific disciplines have no shared infrastructure for fair, reproducible head-to-head comparison of diverse modeling methods across the range of tasks critical to engineering and the physical sciences.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255677/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Participants receive training data matrices (10m×n for full data, M×n for limited snapshots) under four conditions—noise-free, medium/high noise, limited data, and parametric variation—and must produce prediction matrices for a truly withheld test set scored by an independent referee. The framework outputs 12 individual scores (E1–E12) spanning short-time normalized RMSE, long-time power spectral density matching (KS) or per-coordinate histogram L₁ distance (Lorenz), denoising reconstruction, and parametric interpolation/extrapolation, aggregated into a composite score and visualized as a 12-axis radar plot profile per method.

## Method

The CTF defines four test categories—forecasting, noisy data, limited data, and parametric generalization—applied to two canonical nonlinear systems: the 3D Lorenz ODE and the 1D Kuramoto-Sivashinsky PDE. Short-time performance is measured by normalized RMSE, while long-time performance uses power spectral density matching over the first 100 wavenumbers for the PDE and per-coordinate histogram L₁ distance for the ODE, reflecting the fundamental Lyapunov-time limitation that makes trajectory-level matching infeasible beyond a few Lyapunov times. The framework is deployed as a Python package (ctf4science) with a Kaggle competition platform where an independent referee scores submissions on hidden test data, preventing the p-hacking inherent in self-reported evaluations. The authors benchmark 13 methods (LSTM, DeepONet, Reservoir, KAN, ODE-LSTM, Spacetime, OptDMD, FNO, SINDy, PyKoopman, NeuralODE, Higher-Order DMD, PINN) against zero and average baselines, revealing that no single method dominates all tasks and that each method's implicit assumptions determine its suitability for specific task-dataset combinations.

## Results

On the Kuramoto-Sivashinsky (KS) dataset, PyKoopman achieves the highest composite score of 81.83, followed by LSTM (64.54) and PINN (62.77), while the zero-prediction baseline scores −39.00. On the Lorenz dataset, Higher Order DMD leads with 33.57, followed by ODE-LSTM and FNO (both 18.57), while the average-prediction baseline scores 12.26. No single method dominates across all 12 metrics on either dataset: for example, PyKoopman excels on KS long-time forecasting (E2 = 36.85) but scores −96.80 on KS noisy reconstruction (E3), while Reservoir Computing achieves strong short-time scores on Lorenz (E1 = 86.45) but weak long-time scores (E2 = 18.52). The multi-metric radar plot reveals that each method occupies a distinct region of the performance space, with strengths in noise robustness, limited-data forecasting, or parametric generalization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks 13 methods against two naive baselines (zero prediction for KS, average prediction for Lorenz) across 12 task-specific metrics. PyKoopman dominates the KS composite score (81.83 vs. LSTM's 64.54 and PINN's 62.77) but collapses on Lorenz (18.26), where Higher Order DMD leads (33.57) despite scoring near −100 on most individual Lorenz metrics—its composite is inflated by a single strong E12 score. LSTM and DeepONet/Reservoir achieve near-perfect long-time spectral scores on KS (E2 ≈ 97) but degrade sharply under noise (E3–E6) and limited data (E7–E10). SINDy and OptDMD, designed for system identification, score negative on KS short-time forecasting (E1 = −67.33) yet remain competitive on parametric generalization (E11–E12). The key finding is that no method outperforms all others across the full metric suite: Reservoir Computing is best adapted to chaotic time-series forecasting, operator-based methods (FNO, DeepONet) struggle with autoregressive temporal tasks, and physics-informed methods (PINN, SINDy) underperform when their structural assumptions mismatch the evaluation task.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper transplants the Common Task Framework (CTF) paradigm—mature in NLP, computer vision (ImageNet, CVPR challenges), and reinforcement learning (Atari, StarCraft, OpenAI Gym)—into scientific machine learning for dynamic systems. It acknowledges existing scientific ML benchmarks (The Well, CoDBench, PDEBench, PDEArena) but departs from them in two key ways: (1) it enforces a hidden test set scored by an independent referee to eliminate self-reporting bias and p-hacking, and (2) it replaces single-metric evaluation with a 12-score multi-metric system spanning short/long-time forecasting, denoising, limited-data, and parametric generalization. The canonical test systems (Lorenz ODE, Kuramoto-Sivashinsky PDE) are well-established in the literature, so the novelty lies in the evaluation protocol and scoring architecture rather than in new problem formulations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper directly addresses the reproducibility crisis in scientific ML—weak baselines, reporting bias, and inconsistent evaluations that McGreivy & Hakim (2024) documented for PDE solvers. The 12-metric radar-plot scoring reveals that no single method dominates across all tasks, which is practically important: it means method selection must be task-specific rather than one-size-fits-all. Key limitations include small dataset sizes (by design, to reflect real-world data scarcity), only three datasets at launch, and the absence of control tasks. The planned SST sea-surface-temperature competition with a true holdout set and the open-source ctf4science package position this as a living, community-driven benchmark that could become the de facto standard for evaluating scientific ML methods, analogous to ImageNet's role in computer vision.
