## Problem

Existing dueling bandits methods assume unbiased pairwise comparison feedback, yet in practical settings (e.g., human users evaluating LLM outputs), evaluators carry heterogeneous biases—ranging from spammers to adversarial attackers—that inflate regret by up to 8.44×. The core difficulty is that evaluator bias is entangled with the observed feedback and cannot be trivially decoupled by algebraic transformation without inducing unbounded regret, and the joint estimation of arm performance and bias is non-convex.

## Input / Output

At each time slot, the agent receives a binary pairwise comparison (arm i preferred over arm j, or vice versa) from a randomly arriving evaluator, along with the evaluator's identity (or known bias level in the benchmark case). The agent outputs a pair of arms to duel next, updated estimates of pairwise winning probabilities p_ij, per-evaluator bias coefficients η_m, and UCB values for each arm. The feedback is strictly binary (no ties, no real-valued scores), and the bias model assumes each evaluator m reports the ground-truth comparison with probability η_m ∈ [0,1], where η_m = 0.5 corresponds to a random spammer and η_m = 0 to an adversarial attacker.

## Method

For the known-bias case, the paper formulates arm performance estimation as a convex least-squares problem over the observed per-bias-group feedback fractions, solved via KKT conditions to yield an unbiased estimator that weights each evaluator's contribution by (2η_b − 1), automatically down-weighting spammers. A matching confidence radius is derived by applying Hoeffding's inequality to the weighted-sum structure and bounding via the triangle inequality. The resulting bias-sensitive UCB algorithm selects the two arms with the highest UCB Borda scores each round. For the unknown-bias case, the arm-performance and bias estimators form a coupled non-convex system; the paper resolves this by initializing p_ij with the bias-ignoring empirical fraction (decoupling the two), then applying two rounds of Block Coordinate Descent per time slot to alternately update p_ij and η_m, with a regularization term γ‖η − η̄‖² stabilizing the bias estimate. Regret analysis yields a sublinear bound of order O(√(B log T)/(T·Γ)) for known bias and the same order plus a bounded error term ξ(t) for unknown bias, where Γ captures the average deviation of evaluators from random spammers.

## Results

With 10 arms and 10 evaluators over 10,000 rounds, the proposed BS-UN (unknown bias) and BS-K (known bias) algorithms achieve the lowest cumulative average and weak regrets across most settings. Under the default configuration (σ²=2.0, α_B=2.0), BS-UN attains a cumulative average regret of 689 and weak regret of 161, while BS-K achieves 713 and 90 respectively. The headline regret reductions compared to the strongest original baseline (MBTW) reach up to 56.0% (average) and 40.2% (weak) for BS-UN, and up to 86.9% weak regret reduction versus RUCB. The bias-sensitive versions of baselines (e.g., DT-B) also substantially outperform their originals, with DT-B reducing average regret from 1425 to 411 (71.2%) under the same default setting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against five dueling bandits baselines: Relative Confidence (RC), Relative UCB (RUCB), Double Thompson Sampling (DT), Modified Beat The Winner (MBTW), and UCB. BS-UN outperforms all five on both average and weak regret in the majority of settings, with the largest gains against RUCB (up to 70.9% average and 86.9% weak regret reduction) and RC (up to 71.0% average and 81.6% weak reduction). Against DT, BS-UN achieves up to 68.5% average and 76.2% weak regret reduction; against UCB, up to 68.0% and 78.5%; and against MBTW, up to 56.0% and 40.2%. The bias-sensitive versions of baselines (RUCB-B, DT-B) also consistently beat their originals, confirming the estimator's transferability: DT-B reduces average regret by up to 75.9% and weak regret by up to 69.5% relative to DT. BS-K (known bias) generally matches or slightly beats BS-UN, with the gap widening under lower bias concentration (α_B=1.0), where BS-K achieves 554 vs. BS-UN's 825 in cumulative average regret.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the dueling bandits literature (Yue & Joachims 2011, Zoghi et al. 2014, Wu & Liu 2016, Wang & Shao 2023) by incorporating evaluator-specific bias into the feedback model, a setting absent from all prior dueling bandits work. It draws on the bias modeling from mobile crowdsourcing pairwise ranking (Chen et al. 2013) and the reward-biasing ideas from conventional MAB (Hung et al. 2020, Liu et al. 2020), but departs from both: the former lacks exploration-exploitation tradeoff, and the latter relies on real-valued rewards unavailable in dueling settings. The method family is UCB-based, with the key technical departure being a weighted unbiased estimator (via KKT conditions) that replaces the naive bias-decoupling approach, and a BCD-based extension for unknown bias that handles the resulting non-convex joint estimation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical motivation is strong: LLM selection based on human pairwise feedback is a growing deployment scenario where evaluator bias (expertise, demographics) is unavoidable, and the paper shows baselines suffer 8.44× regret inflation under bias. The proposed estimator is modular and can be plugged into existing dueling bandits algorithms (RC, RUCB, DT), reducing their regret by up to 75.9% without architectural changes. Key open questions include: the ξ(t) term in the unknown-bias regret bound lacks a closed form (making the guarantee somewhat loose), bias is assumed time-invariant and evaluator-only (not arm-dependent), and no real-world human evaluation experiments were conducted. Extending to time-varying or arm-dependent bias, and validating on actual LLM evaluation data, are natural next steps.
