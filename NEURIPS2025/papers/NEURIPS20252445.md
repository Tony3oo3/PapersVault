## Problem

Random splitting of image segmentation datasets produces folds with shifted class distributions, leading to biased and high-variance model evaluations, especially for small or imbalanced datasets. While stratified sampling is well-established for classification, it does not directly extend to segmentation because each image is a multi-class sample with pixel-level proportions rather than a single label, making the stratification problem fundamentally more complex.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a segmentation dataset of N images with pixel-level label masks over C classes, a target proportion vector r specifying the desired fraction of samples per fold, and the number of folds K. It outputs K disjoint subsets whose pixel-level class distributions closely match the global dataset distribution while respecting the sample-count proportions. The stratification is guided by per-class pixel counts within each image (not merely class presence), and the problem is shown to be NP-hard via reduction to multi-way partitioning.

## Method

WDES formulates stratification as a genetic-algorithm optimization where each individual encodes a full assignment of N samples to K folds, and fitness is the Label Wasserstein Distance (LWD) between each fold's cumulative class distribution and the global one. Tournament selection, segment-exchange crossover with a proportionality correction step, and pair-swap mutation drive evolution over G generations with population size M. The paper proves the expected suboptimality converges at rate O(1/MG), guaranteeing approach to the global optimum with sufficient budget. A simpler baseline, Iterative Pixel Stratification (IPS), greedily assigns samples by pixel-count demand per class in order of rarity, but its sequential, path-dependent decisions limit its ability to balance inter-class distributions. Across five benchmark datasets, WDES consistently yields lower PLD and LWD than both IPS and random splitting, and reduces cross-validation performance variance most strongly on low-entropy (imbalanced) datasets.

## Results

WDES achieves the lowest Pixel Label Distribution (PLD) and Label Wasserstein Distance (LWD) across all five datasets, e.g., reducing PLD from 955×10⁻⁵ (random) to 456×10⁻⁵ on PascalVOC and from 1110×10⁻⁵ to 377×10⁻⁵ on LoveDA. In 10-fold cross-validation with a UNet-ResNet34 model, WDES yields the lowest standard deviation in F1 (24.0×10⁻³ vs. 32.5×10⁻³ random on PascalVOC; 17.9×10⁻³ vs. 27.0×10⁻³ on EndoVis) and IoU (24.2×10⁻³ vs. 34.3×10⁻³ on PascalVOC), indicating more stable model evaluation. The advantage of WDES over random splitting is most pronounced on low-entropy datasets (PascalVOC, EndoVis) and diminishes on high-entropy datasets like Cityscapes, where random splitting becomes competitive.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares WDES against two baselines: random K-fold splitting (scikit-learn) and Iterative Pixel Stratification (IPS). WDES dominates both baselines on PLD and LWD across all five datasets, with average ranks of 1 versus 2.4 (random) and 2.6 (IPS) for PLD, and 1 versus 2.2 (random) and 2.8 (IPS) for LWD. In downstream 10-fold cross-validation, WDES achieves the lowest average rank (1.6) in standard deviation of Accuracy, F1, and IoU, compared to 2.4 for random and 2.0 for IPS. IPS, despite being designed for pixel-level balance, consistently underperforms random splitting in segmentation variance (rank 2.6 vs. 1.8 for IoU), attributed to its greedy single-pass allocation introducing path dependency. On high-entropy datasets like Cityscapes (entropy 3.17), the gap between WDES and random narrows substantially (LWD 217 vs. 304), suggesting that targeted stratification is most valuable for small, imbalanced, or low-diversity datasets where random splits are most prone to bias.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends two lines of stratification research to the segmentation setting: Iterative Stratification (IS) for multi-label data [Sechidis et al., 2011] and Evosplit, a genetic-algorithm-based splitter [Florez-Revuelta, 2021]. The key conceptual departure is that prior methods operate on binary label presence per sample, whereas segmentation requires preserving pixel-level class proportions within each fold—a fundamentally different constraint. The authors formalize this gap, show that the NP-hard multi-way partition structure of segmentation stratification differs from the multi-label case, and introduce pixel-level similarity metrics (PLD, LWD) that have no direct analogue in the multi-label literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for anyone running cross-validation on small or imbalanced segmentation datasets, where random splits can silently bias evaluation by underrepresenting rare classes in test folds. The finding that the straightforward greedy extension (IPS) underperforms even random splitting in some settings, while a global genetic search (WDES) consistently wins, suggests that local, sequential decisions are inadequate for the coupled multi-class pixel balance problem. Open questions include whether spatial structure or class co-occurrence beyond marginal distributions should be incorporated, whether the approach generalizes to instance/panoptic segmentation, and whether the computational overhead of WDES remains acceptable as dataset size grows (the paper notes random splitting becomes competitive at high entropy).
