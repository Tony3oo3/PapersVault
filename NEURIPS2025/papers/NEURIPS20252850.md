## Problem

Existing DNN fingerprinting schemes are evaluated primarily on robustness (resistance to adversarial modification), leaving the uniqueness property—whether a limited set of triggers can distinguish among a large pool of registered models—largely unexplored. When query costs make the number of triggers a bottleneck, it is unclear which triggers contribute most to model discrimination and how many are truly necessary, since trigger outputs are neither uniformly distributed nor independent across models.

## Input / Output

The method takes a set of P registered DNN models, a pool of N candidate fingerprinting triggers (of any type: noise, benign, OOD, or adversarial), and a trigger budget N̂ ≤ N. It outputs an optimized ordered subset of N̂ triggers that maximizes cumulative conditional mutual information, which is then used to compute each model's fingerprint (its output vector on the selected triggers) and to evaluate the uniqueness rate under a given adversarial tolerance ε. The approach is plug-and-play: it operates on the trigger selection layer and is agnostic to the underlying fingerprinting scheme or model architecture.

## Method

The paper models copyright tracing as a communication channel where the model identity is the source and the fingerprint is the received signal, then quantifies each trigger's contribution via its conditional mutual information I_ε(t_n | t_1:(n-1)), which accounts for adversarial modifications through a Fano's-inequality-style lower bound (Theorem 1). An efficient O(max{P²n, PC}) algorithm (Algorithm 1) computes I_0 by partitioning models into equivalence classes based on partial fingerprints, avoiding the naive O(C^n) enumeration. A greedy selection procedure (Algorithm 2) iteratively picks the trigger with the largest conditional mutual information; the objective is shown to be submodular, guaranteeing a (1−1/e) approximation to the optimal trigger set. The paper further derives the first necessary condition (Theorem 2) on the minimum number of triggers required to achieve full copyright tracing, and validates the framework across four image classification benchmarks (MNIST, FashionMNIST, CIFAR-10, ImageNet) with 600 models and five fingerprinting schemes, as well as on generative language model tracing.

## Results

Across all four datasets (MNIST, FashionMNIST, CIFAR-10, ImageNet) with 600 registered models, greedily selected triggers consistently outperform the original trigger ordering in uniqueness rate. For example, on MNIST with OOD triggers at ε=0 and N̂=5, uniqueness improves from 63.0% to 78.2%; with Adv-2 triggers it jumps from 23.2% to 58.7%. The necessary condition from Theorem 2 requires at least 4 triggers (exceeding the naive ⌈log₁₀600⌉=3), and the first 15 greedily selected triggers provide the same mutual information as all 50 triggers. Under adversarial settings (ε=10%), the advantage of greedy selection persists but absolute uniqueness rates drop substantially (e.g., MNIST OOD: 96.5%→96.5% at ε=0 vs. 1.7%→4.7% at ε=10% for N̂=15).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's method is a plug-in trigger selection strategy applied on top of five existing fingerprinting schemes (Noise, Benign, OOD, Adv-1, Adv-2), so the primary comparison is original trigger ordering vs. greedy mutual-information-based selection. Greedy selection wins in every single configuration tested (4 datasets × 5 schemes × 3 threat levels × 3 budget sizes) with no exceptions. The largest relative gains appear for Adv-2 triggers on MNIST (23.2%→58.7% at N̂=5, ε=0) and OOD triggers on ImageNet (29.5%→86.0% at N̂=5, ε=0), where the original ordering is highly suboptimal. A notable finding is that robustness-oriented rankings of fingerprinting schemes are overturned: Adv-2, previously not the most informative scheme, becomes optimal for CIFAR-10 after greedy selection, while Noise and OOD achieve perfect uniqueness on ImageNet post-selection despite Adv-1 failing. The method also generalizes to generative language models (50 LLMs), where T5-Sentinel and T5-Hidden can distinguish all models with just 5 greedily selected prompts, whereas LLMDet fails even with 10 random prompts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the established DNN fingerprinting literature (RAI2, AFA, IPGuard, MetaFinger, Intrinsic Examples) which has primarily evaluated fingerprinting schemes through the lens of robustness under adversarial modifications. It departs from this robustness-centric framing by introducing an information-theoretic perspective—specifically conditional mutual information—to quantify how much each trigger contributes to distinguishing among registered models, and by casting trigger selection as a submodular optimization problem solvable via greedy algorithms. The necessary condition on the minimum number of triggers (Theorem 2) and the lower bound relating adversarial and vanilla mutual information (Theorem 1, a Fano-inequality variant) are new theoretical contributions not present in prior fingerprinting work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical bottleneck in large-scale copyright tracing: when query costs are high, practitioners need a principled way to select a small subset of triggers that maximizes model distinguishability rather than relying on all available triggers or random sampling. The greedy selection method is model-agnostic and can be plugged into any existing fingerprinting scheme, making it broadly applicable. However, the method requires access to registered models during the selection phase, and the adversarial analysis relies on a simplified threat model where the attack is independent of the model and triggers. Open questions include whether the robustness-uniqueness trade-off observed empirically can be formally characterized, and how the approach extends to more complex generative tasks beyond classification.
