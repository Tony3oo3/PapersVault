## Problem

The outer retina performs nonlinear processing critical for contrast-invariant visual coding, but how photoreceptors, horizontal cells, and bipolar cells each contribute to this computation remains unclear. Existing computational models either omit the outer plexiform layer entirely or collapse it into linear filters, while biophysically detailed models lack the differentiability needed for task-based parameter optimization. This paper asks which specific biophysical mechanisms are necessary for contrast-invariant classification and whether horizontal cell feedback provides a measurable advantage.

## Input / Output

The model consumes 28×28 MNIST digit images with randomly sampled contrast (0.1–1.0) and luminance (0–0.1) scaling factors, converted to per-pixel photoisomerization rates (up to 40,000 P*/s) and applied to 200 spatially arranged cone photoreceptors for 50 ms. The output is a 10-way softmax over digit classes, computed from the time-averaged, rectified membrane voltage of 10 bipolar cell readout neurons (one per digit). The model is also evaluated at contrast levels outside the training range to test out-of-distribution generalization.

## Method

The paper builds a fully-differentiable Hodgkin-Huxley model of a circular patch of mouse outer retina containing 200 cone photoreceptors (each described by 26 ODEs covering the phototransduction cascade, voltage-gated ion channels, and a four-pool ribbon synapse), horizontal cells, and bipolar cell readouts, implemented in JAX/JAXLEY. A dual-constraint training strategy is used: first, 11 phototransduction parameters and 7 ribbon synapse parameters per cone are fit to whole-cell photocurrent recordings and iGluSnFR glutamate imaging data via gradient descent with MSE loss; second, the remaining 2,000–2,900 free parameters (ribbon synapse conductances and HC-to-cone ionotropic synapse conductances) are trained end-to-end via backpropagation to minimize cross-entropy on MNIST classification with contrast and luminance variation. Five architectures are compared, varying horizontal cell count (0, 1, 9, 200), connectivity pattern (full, local, one-to-one), and readout type (passive leak vs. active bipolar cell ion channels). The key finding is that bipolar cell ion channels are the dominant factor for task performance, while horizontal cells—despite adding 400–900 trainable parameters—do not substantially improve accuracy, and the biophysical model generalizes better to unseen contrast levels than a linear classifier.

## Results

On MNIST classification with contrast (0.1–1.0) and luminance (0–0.1) variation, the best biophysical architecture (200 cones, 9 locally connected horizontal cells, active bipolar cell readouts) achieves 0.64 test accuracy, compared to 0.42 for a passive readout and 0.63 without any horizontal cells. The phototransduction cascade parameters fit to patch-clamp data with MSE 0.0092 and 86% explained variance on a held-out cone, while ribbon synapse parameters fit to iGluSnFR glutamate recordings with Pearson correlations of 0.83–0.98. For out-of-distribution contrast generalization (trained at full contrast, tested at lower contrasts), the biophysical model's accuracy degrades substantially more slowly than a linear classifier, though it is outperformed by linear classifiers preceded by adaptive normalization or thresholding.

## Baselines & Comparisons

In the out-of-distribution contrast generalization experiment (Section 4.3), the biophysical model is compared against five baselines: a linear classifier, a linear classifier with adaptive normalization, a linear classifier with adaptive thresholding, a two-layer MLP, and the biophysical model at its initial (untrained) parameters. The biophysical model outperforms the plain linear classifier and the untrained biophysical model across all lower contrast levels, with accuracy degrading much more gradually as contrast drops below the training value of 1.0. However, the linear classifiers preceded by adaptive normalization or thresholding outperform the biophysical model at most contrast levels, suggesting the biophysical model performs a similar contrast-normalization computation in a biophysically grounded manner. The two-layer MLP also outperforms the biophysical model but uses far more trainable parameters and its internal computations are not interpretable. The paper notes that the similar shape of the performance curves between the biophysical model and the normalization-based baselines hints at a shared underlying computation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the JAXLEY differentiable biophysical simulation framework (Deistler et al., 2024) to enable gradient-based optimization of detailed Hodgkin-Huxley models, and extends the biophysical photoreceptor-as-input-layer approach of Idrees et al. (2024), which showed that photoreceptor adaptation improves retinal ganglion cell prediction and out-of-distribution luminance generalization. The key departure is a dual-constraint strategy: some parameters are fit to experimental recordings (photocurrents, iGluSnFR glutamate traces) while others are optimized for a visual classification task — a combination the authors note is absent from prior work. Prior biophysical retina models (Schröder et al., 2020; Behrens et al., 2022) were not differentiable or task-optimized, while task-constrained vision models (Lappalainen et al., 2024; Yamins et al., 2014) used highly simplified point neurons, leaving a gap that this paper fills with 26-equation photoreceptors, ribbon synapses, and cell-type-specific ion channels.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that the nonlinearities intrinsic to photoreceptors (phototransduction cascade, ribbon synapse dynamics, voltage-gated channels) are sufficient for contrast-invariant classification, and that horizontal cell feedback — long hypothesized to be critical for lateral contrast enhancement — does not improve performance in this setting, challenging a common assumption in retinal neuroscience. Limitations include the absence of phototransduction noise, a phenomenological HC-to-cone synapse, and evaluation only on MNIST, so the HC finding may not generalize to more complex stimuli or richer training regimes. The JAXLEY-based pipeline, however, provides a reusable template for probing the computational roles of specific biophysical mechanisms in other retinal or cortical circuits, and the authors identify natural extensions (chromatic information, noise, more detailed HC synapses) that could sharpen these conclusions.
