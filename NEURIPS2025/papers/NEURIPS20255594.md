## Problem

Vision-language models are increasingly deployed as zero-shot tools for embodied tasks, yet their ability to perform active, goal-driven exploration in realistic, unstructured 3D environments remains largely untested. Existing ObjectNav benchmarks are predominantly indoor and evaluate complex systems built on top of VLMs rather than the VLMs' own exploratory reasoning. This paper asks whether current VLMs can autonomously search for objects in large outdoor spaces and characterizes where and why they fail.

## Input / Output

At each step the VLM receives a 500×500 RGB image from a downward-facing UAV camera overlaid with a coordinate grid, a numeric altitude reading, and a text prompt describing the target object; in FS-2 an additional reference image showing the target from above is provided. The model must respond with an XML-formatted reasoning block followed by either a relative movement command <Action>(X, Y, Z)</Action> in meters or the token FOUND to signal task completion. The camera is always ground-facing, and a collision-avoidance system clamps any action that would bring the UAV within 0.5 m of an obstacle or outside the fly zone.

## Method

FlySearch is a benchmark built on Unreal Engine 5 that procedurally generates photorealistic outdoor scenarios (forest and city) with unlimited variation in time of day, vegetation density, and UAV launch altitude. It defines three standardized challenge sets of increasing difficulty: FS-1 (400 scenes, object visible from start, 10 steps), FS-Anomaly-1 (200 scenes, object is contextually out of place), and FS-2 (200 scenes, object may be occluded, 20 steps, dynamic lighting, agent may fly beyond its field of view). The evaluation pipeline couples a headless UE5 simulator (communicating via TCP/IP, built on UnrealCV) with a Python controller that handles scenario generation, VLM communication, and success-criterion checking (object center within a 90° camera cone, altitude ≤ 10 m above object). Nine VLMs are evaluated zero-shot alongside a human baseline; for fine-tuning, Qwen2.5-VL-7B is trained with GRPO using LoRA on 67,500 synthetic flight trajectories in the Forest environment, with a reward that incentivizes both reasoning length and per-step distance reduction toward the target. The key finding is that while fine-tuning substantially improves basic perception (FS-1 City from 1.5% to 27%), it does not address the fundamental lack of systematic exploration strategy that causes near-total failure on FS-2.

## Results

On FS-1 (basic object search), the best-performing VLM, Gemini 2.0 Flash, achieves 42.0% success rate, compared to 66.7% for untrained humans — a gap of ~25 percentage points. On the harder FS-2 (systematic exploration with obscured objects), the gap widens dramatically: the best VLM (Claude 3.5 Sonnet) reaches only 6.5% versus 60.8% for humans, a relative drop of ~835% compared to humans' ~9% drop from FS-1 to FS-2. Small open-weight models (<11B parameters) essentially fail, with success rates at or below 4% on FS-1. GRPO fine-tuning of Qwen2.5-VL-7B on the Forest environment boosts FS-1 City performance from 1.5% to 27.0%, but yields 0% on FS-2, indicating that systematic exploration remains an unsolved problem.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among proprietary models, Gemini 2.0 Flash leads on FS-1 (42.0% overall) with the strongest City performance (41.5%), while Claude 3.5 Sonnet edges ahead on FS-2 (6.5% vs. 6.0%) and Forest (52.0% vs. 42.5%). GPT-4o trails both by 2–3 percentage points on FS-1 but drops to 3.5% on FS-2, the lowest among proprietary models. Among open-weight models, Pixtral-Large (124B) is the clear leader at 29.8% on FS-1, outperforming Qwen2-VL-72B (17.2%) and LLaVA-OneVision-72B (9.5%) by 12–20 points, yet still lags proprietary models by ~12 points. All small open-weight models (<11B) — Phi-3.5 Vision, InternVL-2.5-8B-MPO, LLaVA-Interleave-7B, and Qwen2.5-VL-7B — achieve at most 3.8% on FS-1, with their failures largely attributed to inability to follow output formatting instructions rather than visual perception. The GRPO-fine-tuned Qwen2.5-VL-7B reaches 57.0% on FS-1 Forest (its training domain) and 27.0% on FS-1 City, but remains at 0% on FS-2, confirming that fine-tuning addresses perception and instruction-following gaps but not the fundamental lack of systematic exploration strategy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the ObjectNav (Object-Goal Navigation) task, which has been dominated by indoor environments (Habitat, AI2-THOR, Gibson) and 2D outdoor variants (OUTDOOR), to a fully 3D outdoor UAV setting with altitude control and photorealistic procedural generation via Unreal Engine 5. It deliberately departs from prior work that uses VLMs as components within larger navigation systems, instead isolating and measuring the VLM's own exploration and spatial reasoning capabilities in a zero-shot, open-ended search. The benchmark also draws on VLN literature (AerialVLN, TRAVEL, CityNav) but shifts the focus from instruction-following to autonomous object discovery without route guidance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a fundamental gap: VLMs drop ~90% in success rate from basic perception (FS-1) to multi-step systematic exploration (FS-2), while humans drop only ~9%, suggesting current architectures lack genuine exploration strategy formation. The finding that GRPO fine-tuning yields a 14× improvement on FS-1 City but zero improvement on FS-2 implies the exploration deficit is architectural rather than a data-coverage problem. Open questions include whether longer context windows, multi-agent cooperation, or fundamentally different planning modules could close this gap, and whether the benchmark's simplifications (stationary targets, single camera, limited steps) mask or exaggerate real-world UAV challenges.
