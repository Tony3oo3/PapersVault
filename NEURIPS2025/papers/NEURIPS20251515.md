## Problem

Existing AI economic simulation platforms are confined to simplified, single-domain tasks (primarily taxation) with limited agent heterogeneity, no cross-domain policy coordination, and poor scalability. This prevents AI from exploring complex, interconnected economic challenges such as multi-government policy interactions, demographic shifts, and large-scale heterogeneous agent dynamics. EconGym addresses this by providing a unified, modular testbed that composes diverse economic roles with multiple AI algorithms across 25+ tasks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251515/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Users define a task by selecting from 11 heterogeneous role types across four categories (individuals, governments, banks, firms), assigning one of six algorithm types (RL, LLM, behavior cloning, economic rules, expert heuristics, real-data replay) to each role, and specifying population size (10 to 100k agents). The platform outputs dynamic multi-agent trajectories with computed macroeconomic indicators (GDP, welfare, Gini coefficient, pension fund balance, dependency ratio, inflation, consumption, labor supply) at each timestep, enabling both economic policy analysis and AI algorithm benchmarking under identical conditions.

## Method

EconGym models each economic role type as a modular Markov game agent with role-specific observation, action, and reward spaces grounded in rigorous economic theory (Ramsey/OLG household models, nonlinear HSV tax functions, Taylor-rule monetary policy, dual-component Chinese pension formulas, and four market-structure firm models). The key architectural insight is compositional modularity: all 11 role types share a unified interaction interface, so users can freely combine any subset of roles and algorithms to construct new tasks without redesigning the environment. Six algorithm families—RL (PPO, DDPG), LLMs, behavior cloning trained on 2022 Survey of Consumer Finances data, classical economic rules (Saez tax, Taylor rule, IMF adjustment), expert heuristics, and real-data replay—can be mixed across roles within a single simulation. The platform scales to 100k agents at ~32 ms per step on a single Apple M1 Pro, with realism validated via Wasserstein Distance against empirical distributions, and supports cross-domain coordination tasks where multiple government agents interact simultaneously.

## Results

In the multi-government coordination benchmark (33 configurations), hybrid AI+economic strategies dominate: ID-28 (Saez Tax + DDPG + LLM) achieves the highest GDP of 3.06e+10 and lowest Gini coefficient of 0.32, while ID-27 (Saez Tax + DDPG + DDPG) reaches GDP 2.63e+10 with Gini 0.33. Pure AI agents underperform dramatically—LLM-only (ID-21) yields GDP 1.98e+09 with Gini 0.68, and PPO-only (ID-22) collapses to GDP 1.50e+09 with negative welfare (−8.91e+04). In the single-scenario pension task, RL agents sustain the pension fund for 165 years (vs. ~65 years for the real-data agent at retirement age 67), while LLM agents maximize per-capita utility. At N=100k agents, the average environment step time is 32.125 ms, confirming scalability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks 33 algorithmic configurations spanning RL (PPO, DDPG), LLMs (DeepSeek-V3-0324), classical economic rules (Saez Tax, Taylor rule, IMF adjustment rule), and real-data policy replay across single-, double-, and triple-government settings. Hybrid configurations combining economic priors with adaptive AI consistently outperform both pure-AI and pure-economics baselines: ID-28 (Saez Tax + DDPG + LLM) achieves GDP 3.06e+10 and Gini 0.32, surpassing the best single-government baseline (ID-2, Taylor rule: GDP 2.32e+10, Gini 0.37) by ~32% in GDP and reducing inequality by 0.05. Pure LLM agents (ID-21) and pure PPO agents (ID-22) fail catastrophically in the multi-agent setting, with GDP dropping by over an order of magnitude (1.98e+09 and 1.50e+09 vs. ~2.3e+10 for structured baselines) and welfare turning negative for PPO. In the pension-specific task, RL outperforms the real-data agent by ~100 years in fund longevity (165 vs. ~65 years) and the IMF rule-based agent, while LLMs achieve the highest per-capita utility but shorter fund sustainability. The paper also positions EconGym against prior platforms (AI Economist, EconoJax, TaxAI, EconAgent, AgentSociety) on feature dimensions—task diversity (>25 vs. 1–2), cross-domain support, agent scale (100k vs. 2–10k), and heterogeneous role types (11 vs. 2–4)—rather than on shared quantitative benchmarks.

## Relation to Prior Work

EconGym extends the line of AI-driven economic simulation platforms (AI Economist, TaxAI, EconoJax, R-MABM, ABIDES-Economist, EconAgent, AgentSociety) by unifying them under a single modular architecture grounded in classical economic theory (Ramsey, OLG, CES demand, Taylor rule, Saez tax). Prior platforms each addressed a narrow slice—single-domain taxation, market competition, or LLM-based reasoning—with limited role heterogeneity (2–4 types) and no cross-domain coordination. EconGym departs by treating economic roles as composable Markov game agents with 11 heterogeneous types, enabling users to freely combine fiscal, monetary, pension, and market policies in shared environments, and by benchmarking six algorithm families (RL, LLM, BC, rule-based, economic solvers, real-data) under identical conditions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

EconGym fills a concrete gap: no prior platform supported cross-domain multi-government coordination at 100k-agent scale with multi-algorithm benchmarking, making it a practical testbed for both AI researchers (algorithm development) and economists (reproducible policy analysis). The finding that hybrid AI+economic methods consistently outperform pure learning-based approaches in complex multi-agent settings provides a useful design principle for future economic AI. Key limitations remain: the platform covers only a core set of institutions (no local governments, insurance, international trade), agent scale is still far below population-level modeling, and government policy calibration to real-world responses is incomplete due to data scarcity. Open questions include whether the hybrid advantage persists as model complexity and agent heterogeneity grow, and whether the platform can support genuinely novel economic phenomena (crises, regime shifts) beyond the 25 curated tasks.
