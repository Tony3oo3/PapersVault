## Problem

Existing distribution compression methods such as Kernel Herding were designed for unlabelled data and target the marginal distribution P_X, but no prior approach directly compresses the family of conditional distributions P_{Y|X} of a labelled dataset. While the joint distribution P_{X,Y} can be compressed via tensor-product RKHSs, the paper argues that directly preserving conditional structure should be preferable, analogous to how direct conditional density estimation outperforms separate joint-and-marginal approaches.

## Input / Output

The method takes a labelled dataset D = {(x_i, y_i)}_{i=1}^n and produces a compressed set C = {(x_j, y_j)}_{j=1}^m with m << n such that the empirical conditional distribution ~P_{Y|X=x} approximates P_{Y|X=x} almost everywhere. The compressed set enables KCME estimation at O(m^3 + m^2 n) cost instead of O(n^3), and evaluation at O(m^2) instead of O(n^2). For discrete responses (e.g., classification), an indicator kernel replaces the Gaussian response kernel, requiring a modified optimisation strategy since standard gradient-based updates are inapplicable on the discrete response space.

## Method

The paper introduces the Average Maximum Conditional Mean Discrepancy (AMCMD), a proper metric on families of conditional distributions, and derives a closed-form plug-in estimator. The key technical insight (Lemma 4.7) is that the tower property eliminates the need to explicitly estimate the KCME when computing the compression objective, reducing cost from O(n^3) to O(n). Leveraging this, ACKH is a greedy algorithm that iteratively adds one pair at a time (analogous to Kernel Herding), while ACKIP jointly optimises all m pairs via gradient descent (analogous to MMD gradient flow), achieving O(m^3 + m^2 n) complexity versus ACKH's O(m^4 + m^3 n). Joint baselines JKH and JKIP compress the joint distribution P_{X,Y} via tensor-product kernels for comparison. Experiments show ACKIP outperforms both the greedy ACKH and the joint-compression baselines, confirming that directly targeting the conditional distribution and jointly optimising the compressed set are both beneficial.

## Results

Across all experimental settings, ACKIP (the proposed joint conditional compression method) achieves the lowest RMSE in estimating conditional expectations, outperforming both joint-distribution compression (JKH, JKIP) and the greedy conditional variant (ACKH). On the Imbalanced 4-class dataset, ACKIP at m=250 (3% of the 10,000-sample dataset) matches full-data accuracy in estimating class-conditional probabilities, while ACKH performs worse than random sampling on 3 of 4 classes. On the Superconductivity dataset (81 features), ACKIP achieves the lowest RMSE across all test functions, with ACKH in second place for all but one. In the true conditional distribution setting (m=500), ACKIP achieves the lowest AMCMD², confirming that directly targeting the conditional distribution is superior to compressing the joint distribution.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four compression algorithms (ACKIP, ACKH, JKIP, JKH) against random subsampling and full-data performance across four datasets. ACKIP consistently outperforms JKIP on RMSE, demonstrating that directly compressing the conditional distribution is preferable to compressing the joint distribution. JKIP in turn consistently outperforms JKH, showing that joint optimisation of the compressed set beats the greedy herding heuristic. ACKIP outperforms ACKH on all datasets, with the gap being most pronounced on the discrete Imbalanced dataset where ACKH degrades below random sampling on 3 of 4 classes. On the continuous Heteroscedastic dataset, ACKIP attains the lowest RMSE on 3 of 4 test functions, with all methods performing similarly on the fourth. The full-data estimate serves as an upper bound, and ACKIP at m=250 approaches it closely on the Imbalanced task.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the distribution compression literature (Kernel Herding, Gradient Flow, Kernel Thinning) from unlabelled data to labelled data, introducing both joint (JKH, JKIP) and conditional (ACKH, ACKIP) compression algorithms. The conditional compression framework is built directly on the Kernel Conditional Mean Embedding (KCME) and Maximum Conditional Mean Discrepancy (MCMD) of Park & Muandet (2020), with the new AMCMD metric generalizing the Kernel Conditional Discrepancy (KCD) and Average Maximum Mean Discrepancy (AMMD) by permitting a distinct distribution for the outer expectation. The key technical departure is a tower-property observation that reduces the AMCMD estimation cost from O(n³) to O(n), enabling linear-time conditional compression algorithms that had no prior analogue.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to directly compress conditional distributions rather than relying on indirect joint compression, and the O(n³)→O(n) reduction makes the KCME practically applicable at scales where it was previously infeasible. The empirical finding that direct conditional compression (ACKIP) consistently outperforms joint compression (JKIP) validates the paper's central hypothesis and provides practitioners with a clear recipe for accelerating KCME-based applications (conditional density estimation, likelihood-free inference, Bayesian optimisation). Open questions include the absence of formal convergence guarantees, the dependence on kernel gradients (limiting applicability to graphs or text), and whether the advantage of conditional over joint compression persists in high-dimensional or highly non-Gaussian settings.
