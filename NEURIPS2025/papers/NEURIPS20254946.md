## Problem

Training Spiking Neural Networks for RL control tasks faces two intertwined challenges: the non-differentiable spike function requires surrogate gradients whose slope settings have unclear effects on gradient magnitude and alignment in deep networks, and the stateful dynamics of SNNs demand a warm-up period for hidden-state stabilization that early, poorly-performing policies (e.g., crashing drones) cannot survive long enough to bridge. This paper systematically analyzes the surrogate gradient slope tradeoff and proposes a training framework that overcomes the warm-up barrier in unstable control environments.

## Input / Output

The deployed SNN controller consumes an 18-dimensional state vector (position, linear velocity, orientation angles, angular velocities) sampled at 100 Hz from the Crazyflie quadrotor and produces a 4-dimensional continuous motor command vector. Continuous inputs are converted to spike currents via population-based encoding, and output spikes are decoded back to motor commands through a final linear layer. Crucially, the SNN's LIF neurons maintain internal hidden state across timesteps, so the deployed policy requires no explicit observation or action history, unlike the ANN baselines which need a 32-step action history to achieve comparable performance.

## Method

The paper contributes two complementary components. First, it analyzes surrogate gradient slope k (fast-sigmoid derivative, Eq. 5) across a 4-layer SNN, showing that shallower slopes (k=1) broaden the gradient support and increase magnitude in deeper layers but drive cosine similarity with the true gradient toward zero, while steeper slopes (k=100) approximate the Dirac delta but suffer vanishing gradients. In RL, this induced gradient noise acts as implicit exploration, yielding a ×2.1 performance gain over steep slopes; an adaptive schedule (Eq. 7) that increases k as reward and its derivative grow balances exploration and fine-tuning, reducing time-to-reward by ×4.5. Second, the TD3BC+JSRL algorithm combines TD3BC's decaying behavioral-cloning regularization (λ₀=0.2, decay 0.99/epoch) with a Jump-Start framework: a privileged ANN guide policy (receiving action history) controls the drone for the first 500−N timesteps of each rollout while the spiking actor handles the remaining N steps, with N linearly increasing until the guide only covers the 50-step warm-up. The asymmetric actor-critic uses a 256-128 LIF SNN actor and a feedforward ANN critic with privileged inputs, trained on 100-step sequences with a linearly tightening reward curriculum.

## Results

On the Crazyflie quadrotor position control task with a reward curriculum, TD3BC+JSRL achieves an average reward of 400 points, while BC, TD3BC, and vanilla TD3 all fail to exceed −200 points — a 600-point gap. When deployed on the real Crazyflie, the SNN (no action history) achieves a position error of 0.04 m, outperforming the ANN with action history (0.1 m) and the ANN without action history (0.25 m, which also fails trajectory tracking). The SNN exhibits 79% activation sparsity and an estimated energy consumption of 9.7×10⁻⁵ mJ per inference, while matching the ANN's reward (446 vs. 447) without requiring explicit action history.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the three RL baselines (BC, TD3BC, TD3), TD3BC+JSRL dominates by a wide margin: it reaches 400 average reward under the curriculum while all three baselines plateau at or below −200, a 600-point gap. BC and TD3BC fail because their static offline datasets cannot adapt to the tightening reward curriculum, while vanilla TD3 cannot bridge the 50-step SNN warm-up period due to early crashes. In the real-world deployment comparison against Eschmann et al.'s ANN controllers [14], the SNN (without action history) achieves a lower position error (0.04 m) than the ANN with action history (0.10 m), though its trajectory tracking error is slightly higher (0.24 m vs. 0.21 m). Critically, the ANN without action history fails to stabilize the drone at all, whereas the SNN successfully executes circles, figure-eights, and square trajectories. The SNN also matches the ANN's simulation reward (446 vs. 447) while using 79% activation sparsity and primarily energy-efficient accumulate operations instead of multiply-accumulates.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper combines three established threads: TD3's twin-critic actor-critic framework, TD3BC's behavioral-cloning regularization for offline-to-online bridging, and Jump-Start RL's use of a privileged guide policy to bootstrap a secondary learner. It departs from prior SNN+RL work (DSQN, PopSAN, SNN-PPO) that either resets hidden states between transitions or fails to address the warm-up period problem in unstable environments. The surrogate gradient slope analysis extends the theoretical work of Gygax and Zenke by systematically characterizing how slope affects gradient magnitude and alignment across network depth, a dimension prior work left largely unexplored.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies and solves a concrete bottleneck in deploying SNNs for real-world control: early crashes prevent the long sequences needed to stabilize recurrent hidden states, creating a chicken-and-egg problem. The ablation showing complete training failure without the jump-start component (reward 32 vs. 412) underscores how critical this warm-up bridging is. However, the method's dependence on a pre-trained guiding policy limits its applicability to settings where such a policy can be obtained, and validation is confined to a single drone platform and task. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to higher-dimensional control problems, whether the surrogate gradient slope findings hold for deeper or more heterogeneous SNN architectures, and whether the oscillatory behavior observed in real-world deployment can be resolved without sacrificing the SNN's temporal advantages.
