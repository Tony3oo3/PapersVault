## Problem

In regression active learning, predictive uncertainty cannot be directly estimated because the model and training data are not independent, causing training losses to approach zero and making the expected loss gradient φ₂ intractable without access to the conditional distribution P(Y|X). Existing alternatives such as committee-based methods and MC dropout are computationally intensive and provide only heuristic proxies rather than theoretically grounded uncertainty measures. This paper asks whether typically discarded imperfect auxiliary data—distributionally shifted yet statistically related to the target—can serve as a reliable uncertainty surrogate when properly weighted.

## Input / Output

The method consumes an unlabeled target data pool drawn from P, a labeled auxiliary dataset drawn from a different distribution Q (where both P(X) and P(Y|X) may differ from Q), and an initial labeled set. Crucially, the auxiliary data is never used for model training—experiments confirm that naively including it degrades performance—instead it is consumed solely to estimate the density ratio r(x,y)=p(x,y)/q(x,y) and the weighted expected loss gradient φ̂₂. The output is a batch of N unlabeled points selected for annotation at each AL step, along with updated model parameters, where selection is driven by the auxiliary-guided gradient kernel K_grad-aux rather than the standard gradient kernel.

## Method

AGBAL decomposes sample informativeness into model sensitivity φ₁ (the parameter gradient, directly computable) and predictive uncertainty φ₂ (the expected loss gradient, intractable in regression). The key mechanism is a three-step density-ratio-weighted loss approximation: (1) estimate the joint density ratio r(x,y) between target P and auxiliary Q using LSIF or a Platt-calibrated classifier, (2) compute the model's loss gradient on auxiliary samples, and (3) weight these by r̂(x,y) to obtain φ̂₂, an estimate of the true expected loss gradient. This yields the auxiliary-guided gradient feature map φ_aux = φ̂₂·φ₁ and kernel K_grad-aux, which projects high-confidence points near the origin while preserving high-uncertainty points at greater distances. NTK-based theoretical analysis shows that φ̂₂ has variance proportional to the ridge estimator variance, confirming it as a meaningful uncertainty surrogate. The framework is plug-and-play: it replaces only the base kernel in Holzmüller et al.'s selection pipeline, leaving all downstream selection strategies (maxdet, kmeanspp, lcmd, fw, etc.) and kernel transformations unchanged.

## Results

AGBAL (auxiliary-data-guided gradient kernel) consistently outperforms BMDAL (standard gradient kernel) across all 7 datasets (2 synthetic + 5 real-world) and all 7 selection strategies, measured by AUC of MSE learning curves (lower is better). The average improvement over BMDAL ranges from 0.6% (lcmd) to 18.0% (maxdiag), with the largest gains for uncertainty-driven strategies: maxdiag (18.0%), maxdet (8.9%), and bait (6.3%). On the real-world BIKE dataset, maxdiag(ours) reduces AUC from 0.597 to 0.410, and on CT from 0.526 to 0.304. The paper also confirms that naively adding auxiliary data to training degrades performance (e.g., DIAMOND MSE increases from ~20 to ~24), validating that auxiliary data should only guide selection, not training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is BMDAL (Holzmüller et al., JMLR 2023), which uses the standard gradient kernel without auxiliary data for uncertainty estimation. AGBAL wins against BMDAL in nearly all dataset–selection-method combinations, with the most dramatic gains for uncertainty-driven strategies: maxdiag improves by 18.0% on average (e.g., BIKE AUC drops from 0.597 to 0.410, CT from 0.526 to 0.304) and maxdet by 8.9% (best on S1, S2, BIKE, and CT). Diversity-based methods show smaller but consistent gains: kmeanspp improves 4.5% (leading on BIO, DIAMOND, STOCK) and maxdist 1.8%, while lcmd (the best BMDAL strategy) sees only 0.6% improvement. Against the random baseline, AGBAL is substantially better across all settings, confirming that the auxiliary-guided kernel provides meaningful selection signal. The paper further demonstrates that directly incorporating auxiliary data into training degrades MSE across all datasets (e.g., DIAMOND MSE rises from ~20 to ~24 with N_aux=10,000), confirming that the distributional shift makes auxiliary data unsuitable for training but effective for selection guidance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the gradient kernel framework for deep batch active learning established by Holzmüller et al. (2023), which decomposed sample informativeness into a feature mapping (gradient kernel) and a selection strategy but set the expected loss gradient φ₂ ≡ 1, effectively ignoring predictive uncertainty. The key conceptual departure is repurposing distributionally shifted auxiliary data—previously discarded due to its unsuitability for direct training—through density ratio weighting to recover a meaningful φ₂ estimate. The theoretical grounding draws on NTK asymptotics and kernel ridge regression, while the density ratio estimation component borrows from the importance estimation literature (LSIF, classifier-based approaches). The paper explicitly distinguishes itself from transfer active learning by allowing both P(X) and P(Y|X) to shift, a stricter setting than standard domain adaptation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine gap in regression active learning: without labels, predictive uncertainty is inestimable, and existing proxies (MC dropout, ensembles) are computationally expensive and heuristic. By showing that auxiliary data—naturally abundant in medical, autonomous driving, and industrial settings—can serve as a theoretically grounded uncertainty surrogate when density-ratio-weighted, the work opens a practical path for AL in domains where auxiliary data exists but is too noisy or shifted for training. Limitations include the asymptotic (infinite-width) nature of the NTK analysis, the requirement that auxiliary data be available and absolutely continuous with respect to the target, and the added computational overhead of density ratio estimation. Open questions include whether the approach extends to classification, how it behaves under severe distribution shifts beyond the tested ζ range, and whether the auxiliary data requirement limits real-world applicability.
