## Problem

LLM-based agents performing tasks on behalf of users frequently leak contextually inappropriate information (e.g., medical history when booking a restaurant), even absent adversarial attacks. While LLMs possess general reasoning abilities, they have not been explicitly trained to reason about the contextual appropriateness of each piece of information they disclose, leaving a gap between their capabilities and the demands of Contextual Integrity (CI) norms.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253485/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a user task prompt (e.g., 'send a message to the restaurant manager to book a table'), a set of user attributes as key-value pairs where required and restricted information are intermixed under deliberately neutral key names (to avoid cueing the model), and context metadata specifying scenario, domain, sender, recipient, and transmission principle. The output is structured in two parts: an explicit CI reasoning trace within <think> tags where the model evaluates each attribute as necessary, helpful, optional, or inappropriate, followed by the task completion within <answer> tags that should include all required information and exclude all restricted information.

## Method

The paper proposes a two-stage approach grounded in the insight that CI is fundamentally a reasoning task. First, CI-CoT prompting instructs the model to document its contextual integrity assessment of each personal attribute before generating the response. Second, CI-RL applies GRPO (a critic-free RL algorithm) with a rule-based reward function that computes the fraction of required keywords present minus the fraction of restricted keywords present in the task completion, with a hard penalty for format violations. Training uses a synthetic dataset of ~700 examples generated by GPT-4 in three stages (scenario/domain/principle seeds → vignettes → naturalistic examples with neutral keys and string-matchable annotations), spanning ten domains and three transmission principles (Confidentiality, Proportionality, Consent). The approach is validated across six model families/sizes (Qwen2.5 1.5B–14B, Llama-3.1-8B, Mistral-7B) and shown to transfer to the human-annotated PrivacyLens benchmark, reducing privacy leakage by up to 40%.

## Results

On the synthetic CI dataset, CI-RL consistently improves Integrity (I) and Complete (C) metrics across all six models while maintaining or improving Utility (U). For example, Qwen2.5-7B-IT improves from I=46.9% to 75.0% and C=29.7% to 48.4%, while Mistral-7B-IT jumps from I=38.8% to 89.1% and C=24.5% to 73.4%. On the PrivacyLens benchmark, CI-RL reduces leakage rate (LR) by up to 40% (e.g., Qwen2.5-14B-IT: 52.9%→33.9%) while preserving or improving helpfulness scores. On ConfAIde Tier 4, the combined 'Leaks Secret or Omits Info' rate drops from 0.85 to 0.45.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B (an LRM), the instruction-tuned Llama-3.1-8B-IT +CI-RL substantially outperforms on all three metrics: I 79.7% vs 68.7%, U 79.7% vs 65.6%, C 62.5% vs 45.3%; similarly, Qwen2.5-14B-IT +CI-RL beats DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B on I (78.1% vs 75.0%) and C (50.0% vs 46.9%), though the LRM edges ahead on U (64.1% vs 60.1%). On PrivacyLens, CI-CoT reduces leakage for frontier models: Claude 3.7 Sonnet drops from LR 30.4% to 23.1% (ALR 35.9%→25.4%) while slightly improving helpfulness (2.49→2.69), and Gemini 2.5 Flash drops from LR 29.0% to 19.7% (ALR 30.8%→24.0%) at a small helpfulness cost (2.75→2.31). Gemini 2.5 Pro shows the largest absolute LR reduction (37.3%→25.3%) but still retains the highest leakage among frontier models. The open-weight models with CI-RL (e.g., Llama-3.1-8B-IT at LR 18.5%) approach or match frontier model leakage rates, though at lower helpfulness scores (1.18 vs 2.69 for Claude 3.7 Sonnet).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the RL-for-reasoning paradigm established by DeepSeek-R1 (reward-based training with structured CoT) from math and code domains to the contextual integrity (CI) setting, combining it with CI-specific chain-of-thought prompting. It departs from prior CI research in two ways: rather than evaluating CI via benchmarks (CI-Bench, ConfAIde, PrivacyLens) or enforcing it through external system-level defenses (AirGapAgent, firewall architectures), it trains the LLM policy itself to internalize CI norms via GRPO with a rule-based keyword reward. The synthetic dataset construction pipeline (seeds → vignettes → annotated examples) is novel but follows standard GPT-4-assisted data generation practices.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to explicitly use RL to instill CI reasoning in LLMs, demonstrating that ~700 synthetic examples suffice to transfer CI improvements to human-annotated benchmarks like PrivacyLens (up to 40% leakage reduction). Key limitations include the coarse keyword-matching reward, absence of statistical significance testing, and reliance on a small synthetic dataset that may not capture the full complexity of real-world CI norms. Open questions include whether the approach scales to larger models, how it compares head-to-head with inference-time guardrails, and whether evolving or user-customizable privacy policies can be incorporated without retraining.
