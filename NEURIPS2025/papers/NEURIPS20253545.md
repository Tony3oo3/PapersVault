## Problem

Existing step-level preference optimization for diffusion models relies on pixel-level reward models (PRMs) that require costly denoising and VAE decoding at every timestep, suffer severe distribution shift at high-noise timesteps, and lack explicit timestep conditioning. This paper asks whether a model can naturally assess preferences directly in the noisy latent space while remaining timestep-aware and compatible with arbitrary noise levels.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253545/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The LRM takes a noisy latent image x_t (at any timestep t ∈ [0, 950]) and a text prompt as input, and outputs a scalar preference score computed as a temperature-scaled L2-normalized dot product between projected visual and textual features. During LPO training, K noisy latent candidates are sampled from the same x_{t+1} at each step, scored by LRM, and the highest/lowest-scoring pair (if their gap exceeds a dynamic threshold) forms a training sample. The final output is a LoRA-fine-tuned diffusion model (512×512 for SD1.5, 1024×1024 for SDXL) that generates images better aligned with human preferences.

## Method

The key insight is that a pre-trained diffusion model is inherently suited as a step-level reward model: it already processes noisy latents at all timesteps, possesses text-image alignment from pre-training, and is naturally timestep-sensitive. The Latent Reward Model (LRM) repurposes the U-Net (or DiT) and text encoder of a diffusion model, extracting multi-scale visual features via average pooling and combining them with a Visual Feature Enhancement (VFE) module that injects text-conditioned signal inspired by Classifier-Free Guidance. Training uses a Bradley-Terry loss on Pick-a-Pic pairs, with a Multi-Preference Consistent Filtering (MPCF) strategy that retains only pairs where the winning image outperforms the losing one across aesthetic, CLIP, and VQA score gaps simultaneously. Latent Preference Optimization (LPO) then performs step-level DPO entirely in the noisy latent space: at each timestep, K candidates are sampled from x_{t+1}, scored by LRM, and the best/worst pair is used for optimization with a dynamic threshold tied to the DDIM standard deviation σ_t, enabling training across the full denoising range t ∈ [0, 950] without any VAE decoding.

## Results

On SD1.5, LPO achieves the best scores across all metrics: PickScore 21.69, ImageReward 0.6588, HPSv2.1 27.86, Aesthetic 5.945, and GenEval 48.39 (20 steps), outperforming the strongest baseline SPO (PickScore 21.22, GenEval 40.46) by a large margin. On SDXL, LPO reaches PickScore 22.86, ImageReward 1.2166, HPSv2.1 31.89, Aesthetic 6.360, and GenEval 59.27, slightly surpassing InterComp (which uses a higher-quality internal dataset) on most metrics. Training efficiency is dramatically improved: LPO requires only 23 A100 hours on SD1.5 (10× faster than Diffusion-DPO's 240 h) and 92 A100 hours on SDXL (28× faster than Diffusion-DPO's 2,560 h).

## Baselines & Comparisons

LPO outperforms all seven baselines on nearly every metric. Against SPO (the closest step-level competitor), LPO gains +0.47 PickScore, +0.49 ImageReward, +2.03 HPSv2.1, and +7.93 GenEval on SD1.5, and +0.16 PickScore, +0.22 ImageReward, +0.74 HPSv2.1, and +8.75 GenEval on SDXL. Against Diffusion-DPO, LPO improves GenEval by +4.60 (SD1.5) and +1.49 (SDXL) while also leading on all preference scores. Against SePPO, LPO leads by +0.44 PickScore and +3.42 GenEval on SD1.5. On SDXL, LPO slightly edges out InterComp (which uses a superior internal dataset with SD3/FLUX images) on PickScore (+0.23), HPSv2.1 (+0.37), Aesthetic (+0.344), and GenEval (+0.03), though InterComp retains a small advantage on HPSv2 (29.08 vs. 28.96) and ImageReward (1.2728 vs. 1.2166). LPO also achieves a 10–28× training speedup over Diffusion-DPO and 2.5–3.5× over SPO, making it both more effective and substantially more efficient.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the step-level preference optimization framework introduced by SPO (Liang et al., 2024), which itself builds on Diffusion-DPO's (Wallace et al., CVPR 2024) adaptation of DPO to diffusion models. The key conceptual departure is replacing the pixel-level reward model (SPM, a fine-tuned CLIP variant) with a Latent Reward Model that repurposes the diffusion model's own U-Net/DiT and text encoder to score noisy latents directly, borrowing CLIP's dot-product scoring architecture (Radford et al., ICML 2021) but operating in latent space. This eliminates the need for x̂₀,t prediction and VAE decoding at every sampling step, a requirement that plagued all prior pixel-level reward models in step-level settings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 2.5–28× training speedup makes step-level preference optimization substantially more accessible, and the insight that a diffusion model is naturally suited to reward modeling in its own latent space is likely to influence how future alignment methods are designed. Key limitations include the requirement that LRM and the target model share the same VAE encoder, the dependence on Pick-a-Pic's relatively low-quality training images, and MPCF's reliance on external VLM-based reward models for data filtering. Open questions include whether LRM can be applied to reward fine-tuning methods to avoid multi-step backpropagation, and whether step-level best-of-N selection (analogous to inference-time compute scaling in LLMs) can further improve generation quality.
