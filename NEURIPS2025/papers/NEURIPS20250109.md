## Problem

Machine learning has been widely integrated with heuristics and MIP-based exact solvers for combinatorial optimization, but its application to dynamic programming (DP) remains largely unexplored. DP suffers from state space explosion (the curse of dimensionality), and existing relaxation strategies for the Orienteering Problem with Time Windows rely on hand-crafted heuristics or iterative tightening that can be computationally expensive. This paper asks whether a learned projection function can approximate effective state space relaxations in a one-shot manner, thereby accelerating an exact DP algorithm while preserving optimality.

## Input / Output

The model receives a directed graph where each vertex carries 2D coordinates, a score, a service time, and a time window, and each directed edge carries a Euclidean travel time and a categorical tag (indicating whether the edge survives the reduction procedure, connects to the depot, or is a self-loop). Before feeding the graph to the network, an edge reduction step removes edges that provably cannot appear in any ng-set (depot/self-loop edges and edges where the 2-cycle j→i→j is infeasible under time windows). The network outputs a per-edge probability indicating the likelihood that the edge belongs to the ng-set of its source vertex; the top-n·|V| edges exceeding a 0.5 threshold are selected to initialize the ng-sets for the DP-NG solver, which then returns the optimal elementary tour.

## Method

The core contribution is DiConvNet, a directed variant of the Residual Gated Graph ConvNet that maintains separate source (p_i) and target (q_i) embeddings per vertex, updated by aggregating messages from out-neighbors and in-neighbors respectively, so that edge embeddings h_ij remain asymmetric and capture the directional structure inherent in time-windowed routing. The network is trained in a supervised fashion using labels generated by the DP-NG algorithm itself: the final ng-sets from the iterative tightening procedure serve as ground-truth positive edges, and a weighted binary cross-entropy loss (with class weights balancing the heavily impositive/negative edge ratio) is minimized over mini-batches of synthetic instances. At inference, the predicted ng-sets initialize the DP-NG algorithm, which projects the original state (S, τ, i) onto the lower-dimensional memory state (Π(P), τ, i), dramatically reducing the number of dominance-tested labels and the iterations needed to eliminate non-elementary cycles. The key insight is that the ng-set construction problem—deciding which previously visited vertices must be remembered to prevent cycles—can be framed as a binary edge classification task learnable from graph structure, replacing the iterative, heuristic-driven tightening with a single forward pass.

## Results

On the more challenging Cordeau benchmark (48–288 vertices), DP-NG-ML achieves an average 7.33× speedup over the SOTA pulse algorithm and 4.52× over the un-augmented DP-NG, with the number of DP iterations dropping from 23.9 to 1.3. On Solomon's 100-vertex benchmark, the method yields a 1.38× average speedup over pulse (winning on 21 of 29 instances) and a 2.23× speedup over DP-NG, reducing iterations from 24.4 to 2.9. The DiConvNet predictor achieves a 3.41% error rate on Solomon and 4.49% (with n̂=3) on Cordeau, substantially outperforming the Nearest and MaxScores heuristics (24–32% error).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the SOTA pulse algorithm [12], DP-NG-ML achieves a 7.33× average speedup on Cordeau (winning on all 10 instances) and a 1.38× average speedup on Solomon (winning on 21 of 29 instances, though losing on some easy instances where pulse is already near-instantaneous). Compared to the Nearest heuristic [2] for ng-set construction, DiConvNet reduces the error rate from 25.75% to 3.41% on Solomon and from 6.88% to 4.49% on Cordeau (with n̂=3), while dramatically cutting the false-negative rate from 70.36% to 6.40% on Cordeau. The MaxScores heuristic [28] is similarly outperformed, with DiConvNet achieving a 4.49% error rate versus 5.89% on Cordeau and a far lower FNR (6.40% vs. 53.99%). Against the undirected ConvNet [15], DiConvNet achieves lower error rate (3.41% vs. 5.46% on Solomon; 8.21% vs. 10.60% on Cordeau) and lower FPR, attributed to its directed edge-embedding mechanism. Finally, against the generic DiDPPy DP solver [20], the DP-NG algorithm is ~110× faster and DP-NG-ML reaches up to 814× speedup on 90-vertex Cordeau instances.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two research threads: (1) the ng-route relaxation framework from Baldacci et al. [2] and the bidirectional DP with decremental state space relaxation (DSSR) for OPTW from Righini and Salani [30], and (2) graph convolutional networks for combinatorial optimization, specifically the Residual Gated Graph ConvNet of Bresson and Laurent [5] and its TSP adaptation by Joshi et al. [15]. The key conceptual departure is that prior ML-for-COP work has focused on end-to-end solution generation or augmenting branch-and-bound/MIP solvers, whereas this paper is the first to use a learned model to approximate the state-space projection function within a dynamic programming framework. The DiConvNet architecture is a directed-graph variant of the undirected ConvNet, introducing separate source and target vertex embeddings to capture asymmetric edge structure inherent in time-windowed routing problems.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The learning-augmented DP paradigm opens a new axis for accelerating exact COP solvers beyond the well-trodden MIP/B&B augmentation path, and the authors argue it should generalize to other graph-structured COPs. However, the approach is tightly coupled to the ng-route relaxation mechanism, and the final ng-sets still nearly triple in size relative to the initial predictions, indicating substantial room for improvement. Generalization remains a concern: the model trained on 50-vertex instances shows degraded performance on harder, wider-time-window instances (Table 6), and the Solomon benchmark results are mixed (only 21/29 instances beat the pulse algorithm). Open questions include whether reinforcement learning or more expressive architectures can close the gap, and whether the paradigm extends to multi-route variants via column generation.
