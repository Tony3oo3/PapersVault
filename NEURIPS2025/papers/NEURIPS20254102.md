## Problem

Synaptic weights in biological neural circuits follow heavy-tailed (Lévy α-stable) distributions, yet nearly all theoretical analyses of recurrent neural networks assume Gaussian connectivity. Mean-field theory for infinite-width Lévy RNNs predicts that the quiescent state is always unstable—implying ubiquitous chaos with no transition—but this prediction breaks down for finite-size networks. The paper asks: where is the quiescent-to-chaotic transition in finite heavy-tailed RNNs, how does its location and sharpness depend on network size N and tail index α, and what are the dynamical consequences of the resulting extended edge-of-chaos regime?

## Input / Output

The networks are discrete-time RNNs with tanh activation, driven either autonomously (zero input) or by i.i.d. Gaussian noise (variance 0.01) at each step. Weights are drawn from a symmetric Lévy α-stable distribution with scale σ = g/N^(1/α), where the gain g serves as the sole control parameter swept across a grid. The primary outputs are the full Lyapunov spectrum (computed via QR decomposition over T=3000 steps with K=100 accumulation steps), the Kaplan-Yorke dimension D_KY, the participation ratio PR (from the empirical covariance of post-warmup hidden states), and the critical gain g* at which the maximum Lyapunov exponent first crosses zero. For the theoretical derivation, an annealed approximation redraws weights at each time step, converting the RNN into a T-layer feedforward network whose linearized dynamics reduce to a scalar multiplicative Kesten process.

## Method

The core theoretical contribution reduces the stability analysis of the quiescent fixed point to a scalar Kesten process via an annealed approximation: conditioning the perturbation evolution on the previous state shows that the conditional scale γ^(t) obeys a multiplicative recursion γ^(t+1) = γ^(t)·ξ^(t), where ξ^(t) = g(1/N Σ|z_j|^α)^(1/α) with z_j ~ L_α(1). The critical gain is then g* = exp(−⟨Ξ_{N,α}⟩), and a Laplace-transform analysis of the α-stable tail shows g* ∝ 1/(ln N)^(1/α) for α < 2, in contrast to the rapid convergence to the Gaussian mean-field value 1/√2. Empirically, the paper validates this prediction by sweeping g across quenched and annealed networks of sizes N = 100 to 10,000, computing Lyapunov spectra via the standard QR-based algorithm, and characterizing the transition's sharpness and the resulting attractor geometry. The key empirical finding is a robustness-dimensionality tradeoff: heavier tails (lower α) widen the edge-of-chaos regime in gain space but compress the Lyapunov spectrum near zero, reducing both D_KY and PR, indicating that dynamics are driven by a small number of extreme 'mega-synapse' outliers onto a lower-dimensional slow manifold.

## Results

The paper derives a closed-form prediction for the critical gain at which finite heavy-tailed RNNs transition from quiescent to chaotic dynamics: g* = exp(−⟨Ξ_{N,α}⟩), which decays logarithmically as g* ∝ 1/(ln N)^{1/α} for α < 2, in contrast to the Gaussian case (α = 2) where g* rapidly converges to the mean-field value 1/√2. Simulations confirm that heavier-tailed networks (lower α) exhibit a significantly slower rise in the maximum Lyapunov exponent near the transition, sustaining edge-of-chaos dynamics over a broader gain regime. However, this robustness comes at a cost: both the Kaplan-Yorke Lyapunov dimension (D_KY) and the participation ratio (PR) decrease monotonically with tail heaviness, indicating that heavy-tailed connectivity compresses the chaotic attractor onto a lower-dimensional slow manifold. In a reservoir-computing delayed-XOR task, heavy-tailed networks maintain strong performance across a wider gain range than Gaussian networks, providing a functional readout of the extended critical regime.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between heavy-tailed (Lévy α-stable, α < 2) and Gaussian (α = 2) RNNs of the same size N. Gaussian networks exhibit a sharp, rapid onset of chaos with a narrow edge-of-chaos window, while heavy-tailed networks show a gradual MLE increase that keeps them near the edge of chaos over a substantially wider gain range — a robustness advantage that translates to superior reservoir-computing performance across un-tuned gain values. Against the infinite-width mean-field prediction (which asserts ubiquitous chaos for any tanh-like activation in Lévy networks), the finite-size analysis reveals a well-defined quiescent-to-chaotic transition whose location shifts leftward with increasing N, with the shift being more pronounced for heavier tails. In terms of attractor geometry, Gaussian networks achieve higher D_KY and PR near the transition, meaning they support richer, higher-dimensional slow dynamics; heavy-tailed networks trade this dynamical richness for parameter robustness. The paper also notes that for α < 1 (e.g., α = 0.5), the MLE becomes non-monotonic and the network ultimately settles into a stable non-chaotic regime at large g, a behavior absent for α ≥ 1.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the mean-field theory of heavy-tailed RNNs (KuSmierz et al., 2020), which predicted ubiquitous chaos in the infinite-width limit, by showing that finite-size effects produce a sharp, predictable quiescent-to-chaotic transition whose location depends on both network size N and tail index α. It builds on the annealed approximation and Kesten process framework to derive a closed-form prediction for the critical gain (Eq. 7), and provides a mechanistic explanation for the extended critical regimes previously observed empirically by Wardak and Gong (2022) and Qu et al. (2022) without a theoretical account. The work also connects to the broader literature on edge-of-chaos dynamics in Gaussian RNNs (Sompolinsky, Bertschinger, Legenstein & Maass) by showing how the circular-law-based transition is qualitatively altered when weight entries follow Lévy α-stable distributions with unbounded eigenvalue spectra.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a tractable theoretical framework for understanding dynamics in biologically sized, heavy-tailed neural circuits, where mean-field approximations break down. The robustness-dimensionality tradeoff it identifies—extended edge-of-chaos regime at the cost of lower attractor dimensionality—offers a principled explanation for why biological circuits with heavy-tailed synapses may operate robustly near criticality while exhibiting the low-dimensional activity patterns widely observed in recordings. Key open questions include how learning algorithms interact with the broad critical regime, whether the non-monotonic MLE behavior for α < 1 reflects a genuine dynamical phase, and how spiking dynamics, Dale's law, and cell-type diversity modify these effects. The reservoir-computing proof-of-concept is preliminary, and the framework has not yet been tested on trained networks or complex temporal tasks.
