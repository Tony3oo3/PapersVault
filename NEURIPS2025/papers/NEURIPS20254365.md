## Problem

Existing Forward-Backward (FB) representation methods for zero-shot RL produce inconsistent and biased successor measure and Q-value distributions due to two compounding issues: limited model expressivity in both the F/B representations and the policy, and extrapolation errors from out-of-distribution actions during offline pretraining. Prior CQL-style regularizers (MCFB/VCFB) only partially mitigate the OOD problem, leaving significant estimation bias that degrades zero-shot generalization.

## Input / Output

The method operates on state-based (not pixel-based) inputs: a d-dimensional environment state vector and a task vector z ∈ ℝ^d (d=50) derived at inference time from a small set of reward-labeled samples or goal states via the backward representation. The output is a full multimodal action distribution over the continuous action space, generated by solving diffusion ODEs/SDEs with a task-conditioned denoiser, followed by rejection sampling over K candidates scored by the Q-function F(s,a,z)ᵀz. All experiments use state vectors of varying dimensionality (24–78 dims for locomotion, 55+ dims for manipulation) with 6–12 dimensional continuous actions.

## Method

BREEZE reformulates FB-based zero-shot RL around three coupled components. First, it replaces the unstable policy-generated Q-target in the FB Bellman loss with a task-conditioned V-function learned via expectile regression (IQL-style), converting policy optimization into a stable in-sample weighted regression problem; the optimal policy admits a closed-form KL-regularized solution π_z*(a|s) ∝ μ(a|s)·exp(α·(F(s,a,z)ᵀz − V_πz(s,z))). Second, this weighted behavior-cloning objective is realized by a task-conditioned diffusion model trained with a weighted regression loss (not guidance-based), where the task vector z modulates intermediate features via FiLM, enabling exact sampling from the multimodal optimal policy without a separate time-dependent classifier. Third, the F-network processes state-task and state-action embeddings as a length-2 sequence through self-attention blocks, while the B-network uses a multi-head transformer, providing the expressivity needed to capture complex dynamics that MLP-based FB architectures cannot.

## Results

On the ExORL benchmark (full datasets, IQM metric), BREEZE achieves the best or near-best performance across all 12 domain-dataset combinations. Headline numbers include 693±16 on Walker-RND (vs. 661±10 for FB), 84±14 on Jaco-RND (vs. 32±23 for FB), and 725±23 on Quadruped-RND (vs. 671±14 for FB). In the challenging 100k-transition small-sample regime, BREEZE's advantage widens dramatically, e.g., 525±13 on Walker-RND vs. 264±33 (FB) and 350±29 (VCFB), and 474±21 on Quadruped-RND vs. 176±123 (FB). On D4RL Kitchen, BREEZE substantially outperforms vanilla FB on long-horizon sequential manipulation tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BREEZE consistently outperforms SF-LAP [Borsa et al., ICLR 2019] by large margins across all domains and datasets (e.g., 693 vs. 516 on Walker-RND, 84 vs. 18 on Jaco-RND), confirming that successor-feature-based methods with Laplacian eigenfunction features are substantially weaker. Against vanilla FB [Touati et al., ICLR 2023], BREEZE wins in 10 of 12 settings, with the largest gaps on Jaco (84 vs. 32 on RND; 132 vs. 22 on APS) and Quadruped-PROTO (389 vs. 222). Compared to the CQL-regularized variants VCFB and MCFB [Jeen et al., NeurIPS 2024], BREEZE achieves clear wins on all Jaco tasks and most Quadruped tasks, while being competitive on Walker; the advantage is especially pronounced in the 100k small-sample regime (e.g., 525 vs. 350 on Walker-RND, 474 vs. 233 on Quadruped-RND). The closest competitor is HILP [Park et al., ICML 2024], which edges out BREEZE on PROTO-Walker (715 vs. 663) and is essentially tied on DIAYN-Walker (461 vs. 463) and DIAYN-Quadruped (670 vs. 666), but BREEZE dominates HILP on all Jaco tasks and PROTO-Quadruped (389 vs. 216), and shows lower variance in most settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

BREEZE extends the Forward-Backward (FB) representation framework (Touati et al., 2021, 2023) for zero-shot RL by addressing two identified weaknesses: OOD extrapolation during offline pretraining and insufficient model expressivity. Rather than the CQL-style explicit value constraints used by MCFB/VCFB (Jeen et al., 2024), BREEZE reformulates policy optimization as a KL-regularized in-sample problem solved via expectile regression (borrowed from IQL, Kostrikov et al., 2021) and extracts the policy through a task-conditioned diffusion model trained with weighted regression (building on Zheng et al., 2024). The architectural shift from MLPs to attention-based networks for the F and B representations is a practical departure from all prior FB implementations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete diagnostic (scale mismatch and negative successor-measure values) for why existing FB-based zero-shot methods underperform, and offers a principled fix that consistently improves robustness across data regimes. The combination of behavioral regularization, diffusion-based multimodal policy extraction, and attention-based representations forms a practical recipe for FB-based zero-shot RL that practitioners can adopt. Key limitations include the computational overhead of diffusion sampling at inference time and the fact that experiments are confined to state-based, low-dimensional control tasks; whether the approach scales to high-dimensional or image-based settings remains open.
