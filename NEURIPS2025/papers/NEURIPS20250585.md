## Problem

Existing benchmarks for LM scientific reasoning focus on narrow tasks like multiple-choice questions or simple claim verification that do not reflect the complexity of real-world translational research. In clinical genetics, variant and gene curation requires interpreting scientific publications against precise, disease- and gene-specific protocols (VCEP/SOP guidelines) to assign evidence codes, judge evidence strength, and extract structured experimental findings—a multi-step reasoning task with no existing benchmark that captures its full complexity.

## Input / Output

Each task instance pairs a full-text PubMed article with a structured query (disease, variant/gene in HGVS/HGNC nomenclature, mode of inheritance) and the relevant VCEP or SOP criteria specification, which varies per gene-disease pair and defines the discrete set of valid evidence codes or scoring rubrics. Outputs are structured: E-Score produces a hierarchical evidence code (primary/secondary/tertiary) plus a free-text explanation; E-Ver produces a binary met/not-met label; GCI extraction produces tuples of (evidence category, explanation, numeric score within SOP-defined bounds, reason for score deviation). The GCI task uses a date-based split (post-July 2024 entries for evaluation, pre-July 2024 for in-context examples) to avoid contamination.

## Method

CGBENCH aggregates expert-curated annotations from ClinGen's Evidence Repository (ERepo), VCI criteria specifications, and GCI standards of practice into three benchmarking tasks: evidence scoring (multiclass over VCEP-specific code sets), evidence verification (binary met/not-met), and experimental evidence extraction (structured multi-field output). Prompts combine chain-of-thought, role-playing as a clinical genetics expert, and full VCEP/SOP context; in-context learning uses description-only examples (omitting full paper text) to fit up to 30 demonstrations within context limits. Evaluation uses pass@5 sampling for E-Score and GCI extraction, with precision/recall for multilabel tasks and TPR/TNR/F1 for binary verification. A key methodological contribution is an LM-as-a-judge protocol for scoring free-text explanation concordance against ClinGen curator explanations, using task-aware prompting, majority vote over 20 samples at temperature 1.0, and positional bias correction, calibrated against a manually reviewed subset (0.744 F1 agreement). Eight LMs spanning closed/open-weight and reasoning/non-reasoning families are benchmarked, revealing that reasoning models excel at fine-grained code extraction but underperform on high-level evidence strength judgment, while non-reasoning models show the opposite pattern.

## Results

On the VCI Evidence Scoring task (205 samples, 13 evidence codes), reasoning models lead on fine-grained tertiary code prediction: o4-mini achieves the best Precision@5 of 0.420 and DeepSeek-R1 the best Recall@5 of 0.517, while the best non-reasoning model (GPT-4o) reaches 0.383 P@5. On VCI Evidence Verification (242 samples), GPT-4o attains the highest F1 of 0.634, with DeepSeek-R1 close behind at 0.629; o4-mini and Qwen2.5 perform near random (F1 ≈ 0.51). On GCI Evidence Extraction (336 samples), o4-mini achieves the best scoring metrics (Normalized MAE 0.186, ΔStrength 0.445) while GPT-4o leads in category-matching precision (0.493); all models extract roughly 2–3× more evidence pieces than the ground-truth average of 1.27.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks 8 LMs (3 closed non-reasoning: GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, Sonnet 3.7; 2 open non-reasoning: Qwen2.5 72B, Llama 4; 3 reasoning: DeepSeek-R1, o3-mini, o4-mini) across three curation tasks. On E-Score tertiary codes, reasoning models (o4-mini 0.420, DeepSeek-R1 0.418, o3-mini 0.402 P@5) outperform the best non-reasoning models (GPT-4o 0.383, Llama 4 0.361), but all remain well below expert-level. On E-Ver, GPT-4o (F1 0.634) and DeepSeek-R1 (F1 0.629) lead, while o4-mini (F1 0.509) and Qwen2.5 (F1 0.486) drop to near-random, revealing that reasoning models struggle with binary met/not-met judgments. On GCI extraction, GPT-4o achieves the highest category precision (0.493) and o4-mini the best scoring (Normalized MAE 0.186, ΔStrength 0.445), but DeepSeek-R1 has the worst structure adherence (61.6% vs. >96% for others). In-context prompting (up to 30-shot) improves E-Score for o4-mini and Llama 4 but yields diminishing or negative returns for GPT-4o and does not help E-Ver for reasoning models. The LM-as-judge evaluation shows that even correctly-classified explanations only achieve 48–70% agreement with ClinGen curator explanations, with GPT-4o 30-shot reaching the highest at 70.4%.

## Relation to Prior Work

CGBENCH departs from prior scientific literature interpretation benchmarks (LitQA2, PubMedQA, SciAssess, BixBench) that focus on narrow tasks like claim verification, multiple-choice questions, or general scientific comprehension, by grounding its evaluation in real-world clinical genetics curation protocols from ClinGen's VCI and GCI. The closest prior work, LitGen, uses an LSTM to classify evidence types for variants but cannot adapt to diverse VCEP-specific guidelines or handle the multi-step, protocol-driven reasoning that CGBENCH requires. The paper also builds on the LM-as-a-judge paradigm for explanation evaluation, extending it with task-aware and evidence-aware prompting variants calibrated against manual review by a clinical genetics trainee.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CGBENCH reveals that even state-of-the-art LMs struggle substantially with precise, protocol-driven scientific interpretation: the best model achieves only 0.420 Precision@5 on tertiary evidence codes, and no model correctly determines evidence strength direction more than 50% of the time (near random). The finding that reasoning models excel at fine-grained extraction but underperform on evidence-strength judgment, while non-reasoning models show the opposite pattern, suggests a fundamental tradeoff that current architectures have not resolved. Key limitations include the exclusion of supplementary data and multimodal content (tables, figures), single-paper scope (no multi-document reasoning), and reliance on an LM judge whose trustworthiness requires further validation. Open questions include whether prompt-tuning, memory-based approaches, or agentic systems can close the gap between LM and expert interpretation, and whether the observed hallucination patterns (correct classification but wrong explanation) indicate a deeper misalignment in how LMs process scientific evidence.
