## Problem

Writing formal probabilistic models in a probabilistic programming language requires expertise in both programming and statistics, making Bayesian inference inaccessible to most domain experts. While LLMs can generate such models, they are unreliable—sometimes producing good models and sometimes bad ones. This paper asks how to mathematically combine an LLM's stochastic model generation with a PPL's inference machinery to obtain a well-defined posterior that is robust to individual model failures.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250245/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a plain-language problem description (structured with PROBLEM, DATA, and GOAL blocks) and observed numerical data as input. The user specifies only the target variables to predict, not the full model structure or latent variables; different LLM-generated models may introduce different latent spaces, which are marginalized out automatically. The output is a posterior distribution over the target variables, represented as a weighted mixture of per-model posteriors, where weights are proportional to estimated marginal likelihoods rather than uniform (as in flat averaging).

## Method

The paper defines a joint distribution p(z, x, m | t) = p(m|t) · p(z, x | m) that 'glues' an LLM (defining a prior over formal model strings) to a PPL (defining a likelihood over data and targets given a model). The final posterior p(z|x,t) is obtained by marginalizing over models, yielding a Bayesian model average where each model's weight is proportional to p(m|t)·p(x|m). The practical inference recipe samples N models from the LLM (using Llama-3.3-70B with in-context examples), runs NUTS MCMC on each to approximate per-model posteriors, and estimates marginal likelihoods via importance-weighted variational bounds where the variational distribution q is a Gaussian matched to MCMC sample moments. The theoretical analysis decomposes the total error into self-normalized importance sampling variance in model space (bounded by the χ² divergence between the posterior and prior over models) and per-model approximation error, showing that the LLM prior should be broad to avoid overconfident model selection.

## Results

Across five custom problems, the LLB method (marginal-likelihood-weighted model averaging) produces posteriors that capture user intent and outperform a flat (unweighted) average of LLM-generated models. On the Rain problem, the final posterior predicts 0.793 probability of rain (vs. 0.573 for the flat average), correctly weighting a multi-day dependency model over independent-day models. On the Gold problem with 150 observations, the LLB posterior closely tracks the true density while the flat average is substantially worse. Compilation rates vary widely: 93.8% (Rain), 82.8–96.6% (Coin variants), 55.5% (Polling), 71.9% (City Temperature), and only 0.4–1.2% (Gold, requiring 16,384 candidate models).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is a 'flat average' (Eq. 7) that weights each LLM-generated model by its prior probability p(m|t) alone, rather than by the posterior weight p(m|t)·p(x|m). The LLB method clearly outperforms the flat average on the Rain problem (0.793 vs. 0.573 predicted probability), where a single high-marginal-likelihood model capturing multi-day dependencies dominates the weighted posterior but is diluted in the flat average. On the Gold problem with 150 observations, the LLB posterior is 'clearly better' than the flat average, tracking the true sigmoid density closely. However, on the Polling and City Temperature problems, the paper reports 'little benefit over a flat average,' with a single model capturing nearly all posterior weight in Polling (possibly indicating inaccurate inference) and both methods achieving identical 88% coverage on City Temperature. On the three Coin variants, the LLB method successfully differentiates posteriors based on user-stated assumptions (standard vs. looks-standard vs. bent), a capability the flat average partially captures but less sharply. The paper does not compare against other LLM-based Bayesian modeling systems (e.g., Wong et al. [33], Requeima et al. [28]) or against human-written models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a novel 'gluing' of an LLM to a probabilistic programming language, defining a joint distribution over formal models, latent variables, and data that has no direct precedent. It builds on Bayesian model averaging [16, 32] but replaces the hand-specified model prior with an LLM-defined stochastic distribution p(m|t), and on importance-weighted variational inference [3] for marginal likelihood estimation. The optimal model-weighting result (Corollary 2, Eq. 19) is explicitly acknowledged as previously derived by Kejzlar et al. [18] and Ohn & Lin [24], but the paper's contribution is showing how to approximate it via self-normalized importance sampling when p(m|t) is intractable. It departs from prior LLM-for-Bayesian-modeling work (Wong et al. [33], Capstick et al. [4], Selby et al. [29]) by not generating a single model or eliciting priors, but by treating the LLM's stochastic output as a full distribution over models and performing proper data-dependent model averaging.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work makes Bayesian inference accessible to domain experts who cannot write probabilistic programming code, reducing the barrier from 'learn a PPL + statistical distributions + formalization skills' to 'describe your problem in plain English.' The theoretical analysis (Sec. 5) provides clean error bounds showing that the LLM prior should be broad rather than overconfident, and that constant errors in marginal likelihood bounds are harmless. Key limitations include the high computational cost (independent MCMC per model), variable LLM compilation rates (1–96% across problems), and the Stan truncation issue that biases weights downward. Open questions include whether computation can be shared across similar models, whether constrained generation can eliminate invalid models, and whether the approach scales beyond the small problems tested here.
