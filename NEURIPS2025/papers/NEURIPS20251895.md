## Problem

Deep learning models like DeepGaze III achieve state-of-the-art scanpath prediction but remain opaque, while interpretable mechanistic models like SceneWalk lag far behind in accuracy. The paper addresses the absence of a systematic, principled procedure for identifying which specific cognitive mechanisms to add to a mechanistic model to close this performance gap, rather than relying on ad hoc intuition-driven model development.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251895/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Both models take a natural scene image and the sequence of previous fixation locations as input, producing a conditional probability distribution p(f_{i+1} | f_{≤i}, I) over the next fixation. The spatial baseline (which feeds into SceneWalk's activation map and serves as the lower reference) is a per-image mixture of a KDE over other subjects' fixations, a center bias, a uniform component, and a SOTA saliency prediction, with parameters fitted per image. Performance is scored as information gain in bits per fixation relative to uniform, and explainable information gain quantifies the fraction of the gap to DeepGaze that the mechanistic model closes.

## Method

The core methodology identifies 'controversial fixations' — individual fixations where DeepGaze III and SceneWalk disagree most in log-likelihood (measured via LLD and WLLD) — and uses these as entry points for targeted empirical analysis. From 72 examined controversial fixations, 92% clustered into four actionable categories, motivating three new mechanisms added to SceneWalk: (1) time-dependent temperature scaling via exponential decay of the activation/inhibition exponent γ, (2) a saccadic momentum and return bias map modulated by previous saccade length and fixation index, and (3) a cardinal attention bias with horizontal/vertical anisotropy and a decaying leftward asymmetry. These simple, interpretable additions double SceneWalk's explained variance on MIT1003 from 35% to 70%, setting a new state of the art in mechanistic scanpath prediction. The key insight is repurposing a performance-optimized DNN not as a black-box predictor but as a queryable scientific instrument for hypothesis generation and testing, enabling controlled analyses (e.g., removing saliency) that sparse empirical data alone cannot support.

## Results

On MIT1003, SceneWalk-X achieves 2.82 bits/fixation log-likelihood (LL) and 0.9343 AUC, doubling the explained variance of the original SceneWalk from 35% to 70% and closing 56% of the remaining gap to DeepGaze III (2.94 LL, 0.9386 AUC). On DAEMONS, SceneWalk-X reaches 2.88 LL and 0.9267 AUC, closing 40% of the residual gap to DeepGaze (3.18 LL, 0.9365 AUC). Consistent gains are observed on COCO Freeview (2.64 LL, 0.9275 AUC) and the Potsdam Corpus (2.42 LL, 0.9164 AUC), establishing a new state of the art in mechanistic scanpath prediction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SceneWalk-X outperforms all mechanistic baselines across every dataset. Against the spatial baseline (which ignores scanpath history), it gains 0.30 bits/fixation on MIT1003 and 1.00 bits on DAEMONS, demonstrating strong exploitation of sequential dependencies. Compared to CLE [6], SceneWalk-X leads by 0.64 LL on MIT1003 and 0.46 LL on DAEMONS; against Kadner et al. [40], it surpasses the AUC by 0.0106 on MIT1003 and 0.0461 on DAEMONS. Relative to the base perisaccadic SceneWalk [70], the three added mechanisms (temperature scaling, saccadic momentum, cardinal attention bias) each contribute roughly equally on MIT1003, with momentum and cardinal bias dominating on DAEMONS. DeepGaze III [51] remains the strongest model, but SceneWalk-X narrows the explainable-performance gap from 65% to 9% on MIT1003 (closing 56% of the residual) and from 40% to 24% on DAEMONS (closing 40%), making it the first mechanistic model to approach deep-network-level scanpath prediction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the mechanistic scanpath model SceneWalk (Engbert et al., Schwetlick et al.) and the deep-learning model DeepGaze III (Kümmerer et al.), positioning them at opposite ends of the predictability-interpretability Pareto front. Conceptually, it extends the 'controversial stimuli' idea from Golan et al. [30] and the Scientific Regret Minimization framework [2] to the scanpath domain: rather than distilling a DNN into a simpler model or performing post-hoc feature attribution, the authors use a DNN as a dense, queryable proxy for the data-generating process to identify specific fixations where a mechanistic model fails, then formalize the missing behavior as explicit, interpretable mechanisms. This departs from the typical framing where DNNs and cognitive models are treated as competing alternatives, instead repurposing the DNN as a scientific instrument for hypothesis generation within a mechanistic framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The methodology offers a principled, repeatable procedure for closing the gap between data-driven prediction and mechanistic understanding in cognitive modeling, with the potential to generalize beyond scanpath prediction to other sequential decision-making tasks. The doubling of SceneWalk's explained variance (35%→70% on MIT1003) and the finding that 92% of model disagreements cluster into just four interpretable categories validate the approach and suggest that much of the 'black-box' performance of DNNs in this domain can be captured by a small number of explicit cognitive mechanisms. Key limitations include reliance on DeepGaze as a proxy for human predictability (it may encode dataset-specific shortcuts), the manual nature of mechanism identification, and evaluation restricted to natural-scene free viewing. Open questions include automating the search over candidate mechanisms, extending to task-dependent or dynamic viewing, and confirming that the added mechanisms reflect genuine cognitive processes rather than computational proxies.
