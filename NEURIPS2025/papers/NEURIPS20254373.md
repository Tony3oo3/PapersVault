## Problem

Under network interference, the Horvitz-Thompson estimator of the average treatment effect requires a fractional exposure threshold h to define which units are 'fully treated' or 'fully controlled,' but selecting h is non-trivial: extreme values (h=0 or h=1) introduce high bias or high variance, respectively, and existing approaches either fix h by convention or rely on domain expertise. This paper asks whether h can be chosen data-adaptively to minimize the estimator's mean-squared error without assuming a specific parametric form for the interference function.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a graph adjacency matrix W (with zero diagonal), an observed outcome vector Y, and a binary treatment assignment vector z, from which it computes each unit's exposure e_i as the fraction of treated neighbors (D^{-1}Wz)_i. It outputs the Horvitz-Thompson ATE estimate evaluated at the selected threshold ĥ, along with the threshold itself. The candidate threshold set H is discrete (e.g., {0, 1/d, 2/d, …, 1} for a d-regular graph), and exposure probabilities π_i^x, π_ij^xy required by the variance estimator are computed via Monte Carlo simulation (2×10⁴ to 10⁶ trials in experiments).

## Method

AdaThresh selects the MSE-optimal threshold by first fitting a simple linear regression Y = βz + γe + c to obtain the bias slope γ̂, a first-order approximation of how the potential-outcome function ψ(·, e_i) changes with exposure, without assuming the true model is linear. For each candidate threshold h, it estimates the bias via a Horvitz-Thompson-weighted sum of (1−e_i)γ̂ over treated-exposed units and e_iγ̂ over control-exposed units, and estimates the variance using the Aronow–Samii joint-exposure-probability variance estimator. The threshold ĥ = argmin_h [Biaŝ²(h) + V̂ar(h)] is then plugged into the standard HT estimator to produce the final ATE estimate. Theoretically, the paper proves that under bounded-degree graphs, unconfoundedness, and bounded-variation interference, the probability of selecting the wrong threshold decays exponentially in n, with rates governed by the MSE gap Δ_n, the maximum degree d_max, and the treatment probability p(1−p); a corollary extends this to non-linear exposure functions with bounded linear approximation error δ.

## Results

The paper evaluates AdaThresh via RMSE (normalized by the true ATE) across varying spillover ratios γ/β on multiple graph structures. On a 1000-node 2nd-power cycle graph with unit-level Bernoulli(0.5) randomization, AdaThresh consistently achieves lower RMSE than both HT(1) and HT(0), and outperforms Lepski-HT particularly at high γ/β ratios. On the Amazon DVD product similarity network (n=1000), AdaThresh consistently outperforms the fixed-threshold baselines across all spillover ratios. On the smaller village network (n=110), AdaThresh interpolates between HT(1) and HT(0) but does not uniformly dominate HT(0), with the authors attributing this to imprecise estimation of small exposure probabilities for higher-degree nodes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares AdaThresh against three baselines: HT(1) (Horvitz-Thompson at threshold h=1, which is unbiased but suffers high variance especially for high-degree nodes), HT(0) (Horvitz-Thompson at threshold h=0, which has low variance but is biased under interference), and Lepski-HT (threshold selected via an adaptation of Lepski's method). AdaThresh wins against HT(1) across all settings by reducing variance through a lower threshold, and wins against HT(0) at moderate-to-high γ/β ratios by reducing bias through a higher threshold. Against Lepski-HT, AdaThresh outperforms at high spillover ratios (large γ/β) where the monotonicity assumptions underlying Lepski's method are violated by the network dependence structure; at low γ/β the two methods perform comparably. On the village network (n=110), AdaThresh does not consistently beat HT(0), suggesting the method benefits from larger n and smaller d_max as predicted by the theoretical bounds. On the Amazon DVD network and 1000-node cycle graphs, AdaThresh achieves the lowest RMSE across the full range of γ/β values tested, confirming the theoretical prediction that performance improves with larger n and bounded degree.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Horvitz-Thompson estimator for ATE under network interference (Aronow & Samii, 2017) by introducing a data-adaptive procedure to select the exposure threshold h that minimizes MSE, rather than relying on domain expertise or fixed choices (h=0 or h=1). Conceptually, it reframes threshold selection as an optimal bandwidth selection problem, drawing on Lepski's method (Goldenshluger & Lepski, 2011; Su et al., 2020) from nonparametric statistics, but departs from it by arguing that network-induced dependence can violate the monotonicity and decay-rate conditions Lepski's method requires. The paper also positions its threshold selection as the estimation-stage 'dual' of cluster-size selection in cluster-randomized designs (Ugander et al., 2013; Eckles et al., 2017), and uses a first-order linear regression 'bias slope' as a practical proxy for the exposure sensitivity, echoing the spillover-slope approximation in Baird et al. (2018).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method addresses a genuine practical gap: choosing the exposure threshold h has required domain expertise or arbitrary defaults, and the paper provides a principled, data-driven alternative with finite-sample concentration guarantees (Theorem 4.5, Corollary 4.6) on the probability of selecting the MSE-optimal threshold. However, empirical gains are modest — on the small village network (n=110), AdaThresh does not consistently beat the HT(0) baseline, and the authors attribute this to difficulty estimating small exposure probabilities for high-degree nodes. Open questions include extending to non-linear exposure functions (local regression is shown to help but is not fully integrated), providing inferential tools (confidence intervals), and generalizing to learned or higher-order neighborhood structures.
