## Problem

Existing physical adversarial attacks on traffic sign recognition rely on visually conspicuous stickers, trackable visible light, IR lasers vulnerable to simple filters, or acoustic signals easily blocked by physical protections. No prior attack medium combines stealth (invisible when dormant), selective triggering, and resistance to common sensor filters. This paper introduces fluorescent ink as a new attack vector that is transparent under normal conditions but emits visible-spectrum light when activated by invisible UV illumination, thereby bypassing IR filters while remaining undetectable to human observers.

## Input / Output

The method takes a traffic sign image (32×32 for most classifiers, 299×299 for Inception v3) and a UV light intensity parameter as input, and produces a set of K circle parameters—each with center coordinates, radius, RGB color, and opacity—that define the fluorescent ink pattern to apply to the physical sign. The UV trigger modulates the L channel in LAB color space over the sign region, simulating the fluorescence effect. The attack operates in a black-box setting requiring 1500 queries, and the physical deployment uses a 60 cm × 60 cm sign with a 14 cm perturbation radius at 1.5 m height, triggered by a UV lamp (40–120 W) positioned up to 8 m away.

## Method

FIPatch is a four-module framework: (1) automatic traffic sign localization via histogram equalization, Canny edge detection, and HSV color-based masking to constrain perturbations to the valid sign region; (2) fluorescence modeling that parameterizes the ink as K circles with position, radius, RGB color, and opacity, applying a per-pixel alpha-blend perturbation function and modeling UV intensity as L-channel scaling in LAB space; (3) fluorescence optimization using goal-based losses (hiding, generative, or misrecognition) combined with an area-minimization loss, solved via Particle Swarm Optimization with n-random-restarts in the black-box discrete search space; (4) robustness improvement through an extended Expectation-over-Transformation with fluorescence-specific distributions (perspective, brightness, distance, rotation, motion blur) and a transparency degradation model over 5 days. The key insight is that the emitted fluorescence falls in the visible spectrum, making it immune to IR filters while the dormant ink remains optically transparent.

## Results

In the physical world, FIPatch achieves an attack success rate (ASR) of 98.31% for generative attacks and 100% for misrecognition attacks (ResNet50) under low-light conditions (200 lux), while hiding attacks reach 87.81% (Faster R-CNN). ASR degrades with increasing ambient light, dropping to 83.39% (generative) and 25.92% (hiding) at 3000 lux. Against five popular defenses, FIPatch maintains an ASR of at least 96.72%, with the strongest defense (Defensive Dropout) reducing ASR by only 2.44% at most. Results are validated across 10 detector and classifier models on both GTSRB and CTSRD datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FIPatch is evaluated against five defense baselines: Image Smoothing (Cohen et al., 2019), Feature Compression (Jia et al., 2019), Input Randomization (Xie et al., 2017), Adversarial Training (Madry et al., 2017), and Defensive Dropout (Wang et al., 2018). All five defenses fail to meaningfully reduce the attack: ASR drops by at most 2.44% (Defensive Dropout on GoogleNet) and in some cases actually increases (e.g., Image Smoothing raises ResNet101 ASR from 99.30% to 99.46%). Defensive Dropout is the most effective defense, reducing ASR by 0.66%–2.44% across models, while Adversarial Training and Input Randomization reduce ASR by less than 1% for all models. Feature Compression offers modest gains of 0.25%–2.13% reduction. Overall, FIPatch bypasses all five defenses with a minimum ASR of 96.72% (Inception v3 under Defensive Dropout), demonstrating that current adversarial defense strategies are largely ineffective against fluorescent-ink-based physical attacks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the physical adversarial patch literature for traffic sign recognition by introducing fluorescent ink as a novel attack medium, departing from prior approaches that rely on visually conspicuous stickers (Eykholt et al., Song et al.), trackable visible-light projections (Lovisotto et al., Duan et al.), filterable infrared lasers (Sato et al.), or blockable acoustic signals (Zhu et al.). Methodologically, it builds on EOT (Athalye et al., 2018) for robustness and adapts particle swarm optimization for black-box parameter search, but the key conceptual departure is the parameterization of fluorescence-specific properties (color, transparency, UV-triggered emission) into a differentiable optimization objective. The paper positions itself as the first to systematically model and optimize fluorescent perturbations for adversarial purposes, filling a gap where prior physical AEs were either poorly stealthy or easily countered by simple physical defenses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that all five widely-used adversarial defenses (image smoothing, feature compression, input randomization, adversarial training, defensive dropout) reduce ASR by at most ~2.5% is practically alarming for autonomous driving safety, as it suggests no off-the-shelf mitigation exists for this attack class. The paper's limitations are acknowledged: experiments assess the AI component rather than the full AV decision pipeline, and effectiveness degrades significantly under bright ambient light (hiding attack ASR drops below 32% at 3000 lux). Open questions include whether multi-vehicle collaborative perception or high-definition map cross-referencing can provide meaningful defense, whether the attack generalizes to curved or non-planar sign surfaces, and whether regulatory or hardware-level countermeasures (e.g., UV-blocking coatings on signs) are feasible at scale.
