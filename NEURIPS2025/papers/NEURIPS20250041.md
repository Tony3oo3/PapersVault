## Problem

Evaluation platforms like BIG-bench and HELM routinely select 'lite' subsets of metrics for efficiency, but the term 'representative' has never been formally defined in this context. Without a precise notion of representation, practitioners cannot reason about tradeoffs between subset size and the information preserved, nor can they verify whether an existing curated subset actually captures the full metric suite. This paper fills that gap by formalizing two representation properties drawn from social choice theory.

## Input / Output

The method takes a full preference profile σ_N where each of n metrics provides a complete ranking over m alternatives, along with either a group size g (for positional representation) or an error tolerance ε (for positional proportionality). The output is a subset K ⊂ N of metrics with a formal guarantee: for every alternative and every position cutoff r, the fraction (or floor-scaled count) of metrics in K ranking that alternative in the top r is within the specified tolerance of the corresponding quantity in N. The framework also accepts an arbitrary collection of metric groups G (e.g., by language, difficulty, or expert endorsement) to preserve non-rank information alongside rank information.

## Method

The paper introduces two formal properties: positional representation (preventing under-representation at every rank cutoff, parameterized by group size g) and positional proportionality (preventing both under- and over-representation within an additive error ε at every rank cutoff). For positional representation, a polynomial-time greedy algorithm (Algorithm 1) first assigns 'colors' to blocks of g metrics that rank the same alternative highly, then iteratively selects the metric covering the most uncovered colors, achieving |K| ≤ O((n/g) log m) — tight up to a log factor against the worst-case lower bound. For positional proportionality, a probabilistic existence proof via Hoeffding's inequality shows |K| = O(ε⁻² log m) suffices, with a matching lower bound. The paper further proves that any K satisfying positional proportionality approximates any social choice scoring rule (e.g., Borda count) on σ_N within ε, and shows that finding the minimum-size K for the generalized group-based versions of both properties is NP-hard.

## Results

Across all three case studies (BIG-bench with n=141 metrics, HELM with n=34 scenarios, and Cal Hospital Compare with n=50 metrics), the paper's algorithms consistently find subsets smaller than the existing deployed subsets while guaranteeing positional representation (PR) at a smaller group size g or positional proportionality (PP) at a lower error tolerance ε. For PR, the greedy algorithm (Algorithm 1) outperforms the theoretical upper bound of O((n/g)·log(m)) in practice, though a gap remains relative to the optimal integer programming solution for small g. For PP, the integer program finds subsets that guarantee PP at a strictly lower ε than the existing subsets; for Cal Hospital Compare specifically, adding fewer than 5 metrics to the existing 12-metric subset achieves a substantially smaller ε.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its algorithms against three existing deployed subsets: BIG-bench Lite (24 of 141 JSON metrics), HELM Lite (7 of 34 Classic scenarios), and Cal Hospital Compare's Patient Safety Honor Roll (12 of 50 CMS metrics). For all three baselines, the paper's methods win: the greedy algorithm for PR finds a strictly smaller subset than the existing one while guaranteeing representation at a finer granularity (smaller g), and the integer program for PP finds a smaller subset guaranteeing proportionality at a tighter ε. The BIG-bench Lite and HELM Lite comparisons show that the existing curated subsets do not achieve the best possible tradeoff between subset size and representation/proportionality guarantees. The Cal Hospital Compare case is particularly striking for PP, where augmenting the existing 12-metric subset with fewer than 5 additional metrics yields a much smaller ε, suggesting the current hand-selected subset is suboptimal from a proportionality standpoint. The paper also notes that for small group sizes in PR, the IP-based augmentation of the existing subset can outperform the standalone greedy algorithm, offering a practical hybrid approach.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper draws on social choice theory—particularly Justified Representation (Aziz et al., 2017) and Proportional Justified Representation (Sánchez-Fernández et al., 2017)—to formalize what it means for a subset of evaluation metrics to be 'representative,' a notion that has been used informally in practice (BIG-bench Lite, HELM Lite, Cal Hospital Compare) but never rigorously defined. The algorithmic toolkit (greedy set-cover-style selection, Hoeffding-based probabilistic arguments, integer programming) is standard, but the specific problem formulation of positional representation and positional proportionality over preference profiles is new. The paper explicitly positions itself as distinct from prior work on benchmark aggregation (Colombo et al., Rofin et al.) and prompt-level selection (Perlitz et al., Polo et al.), focusing instead on selecting whole metrics while preserving rank information.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first formal framework for reasoning about tradeoffs between subset size and representativeness in evaluation, which could influence how future 'lite' benchmarks are constructed and validated. A key practical finding is that existing deployed subsets (BIG-bench Lite, HELM Lite, Cal Hospital Compare) do not satisfy the formal representation guarantees the paper defines, and smaller subsets that do exist—suggesting current practice is suboptimal. Open questions include how well a selected subset generalizes to new alternatives under distribution shift, how to handle missing data, and how to incorporate heterogeneous metric costs. The NP-hardness of the general group-based versions means practitioners will likely rely on the greedy approximation or IP-based augmentation in practice.
