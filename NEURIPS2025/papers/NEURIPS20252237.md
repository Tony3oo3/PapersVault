## Problem

Existing tokenization algorithms like BPE and UNIGRAM impose algorithmic constraints—bottom-up pairwise merges or top-down pruning—that are artifacts of their specific approaches rather than fundamental to the tokenization task itself. This paper asks whether tokenization can be formulated more generally as a covering problem, what its computational complexity is, and whether a practical algorithm can be designed that operates without these merge-sequence constraints.

## Input / Output

The method takes a corpus of distinct byte-sequence words with their frequencies, a token budget k, and a candidate token set T comprising all substrings of length ≥ 2 from the corpus words, alongside a fixed base set B of 256 byte values. It produces a vocabulary S ⊆ T of size at most k and the corresponding tokenized sequences for each word. A key structural detail is that the MIP formulation uses binary variables for every adjacent singleton pair in every word and every candidate token, encoding the constraint that each character position can be covered by at most one token and that a chosen token must cover its entire span contiguously.

## Method

The paper reformulates tokenization as a partition cover optimization: the fundamental decision is whether two adjacent singletons in a word are covered by the same token, and the objective minimizes total tokens (equivalently, maximizes total covered adjacent pairs). This formulation is shown NP-hard via a reduction from vertex cover, and its mixed-integer-program encoding naturally relaxes to the weighted maximum coverage problem, which admits a (1−1/e) greedy approximation. GREEDTOK is a polynomial-time greedy algorithm that builds S from the empty set by iteratively selecting the candidate token with the largest marginal gain in the MIP objective, subject to the contiguity and non-overlap constraints. Unlike BPE, it imposes no pairwise-merge restriction; unlike UNIGRAM, it constructs the vocabulary bottom-up rather than pruning top-down. Empirically, GREEDTOK achieves ~3% fewer tokens per word than BPE and UNIGRAM on real corpora, and pre-trained 1B-parameter transformers using GREEDTOK outperform their BPE counterparts on both benchmark accuracy and bits-per-byte compression.

## Results

On compression, GREEDTOK achieves a mean improvement of 2.88% over BPE and 3.43% over UNIGRAM in tokens-per-word across four real-world corpora (e.g., 1.742 vs 1.837 tokens/word on arXiv at k=1000). In 1B-parameter language model pre-training on DCLM Dedup, GREEDTOK (GTET, equal tokens) achieves an average benchmark score of 63.2 and 0.6989 bits/byte on Wikitext, outperforming the BPE-based model (BPEM) which scores 62.0 and 0.7066. GREEDTOK uses ~18% fewer tokens to represent the full DCLM Dedup dataset (7.35×10¹¹ vs 8.94×10¹¹ tokens). Empirically, GREEDTOK attains an objective value of at least 0.9(1−1/e) of the optimal for large k, as measured by the d_inst ratio against GREEDWMC.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against BPE, GREEDTOK consistently achieves better compression (2.5–5.1% fewer tokens/word depending on corpus and k) and also wins on downstream language model benchmarks: GTET scores 63.2 average vs BPEM's 62.0, and 0.6989 vs 0.7066 bits/byte on Wikitext, even when trained on the same number of tokens. Against UNIGRAM, GREEDTOK achieves comparable or better compression (up to 6.7% improvement on arXiv at k=1000) while avoiding UNIGRAM's known tendency to over-prioritize whole words at the expense of informative subword tokens; UNIGRAM's per-word tokenization cost of O(k·|W|) also makes it impractical for large vocabularies. Against GREEDWMC (the (1−1/e) greedy algorithm for the relaxed weighted maximum coverage problem), GREEDTOK's objective value ratio d_inst approaches 1 as k grows, empirically confirming a ~0.9(1−1/e) approximation guarantee for practical NLP vocabulary sizes. The paper notes that BPE's bottom-up merge constraint wastes vocabulary capacity on intermediate tokens that never appear in the final encoding, which explains GREEDTOK's compression advantage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant merge-based (BPE) and path-based (UNIGRAM) formulations of tokenization by recasting the problem as a partition cover optimization, where the core question is simply whether two adjacent characters are covered by the same token. The formulation subsumes prior merge-based approaches in the sense that all BPE/UNIGRAM solutions are valid partition cover solutions, but the converse need not hold. Theoretically, the paper connects to the weighted maximum coverage problem (WMC) via a mixed-integer-program relaxation, and provides a simpler NP-hardness proof via vertex cover reduction compared to the concurrent work of Whittington et al. [WBP24] and the APX-completeness results of Kozma and Voderholzer [KV24].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Tokenization is a foundational component of all modern LLMs, and even modest compression gains translate into substantial compute savings at scale; GREEDTOK's ~3% reduction in tokens per word and its lower bits/byte in 1B-parameter pre-training suggest practical value for production systems. However, the lack of a formal approximation guarantee for GREEDTOK (only an empirical d_inst → 1 argument), the limited pre-training scale (1B parameters, single seed), and the absence of low-resource language evaluations leave important open questions. The partition cover framing also opens a path for integrating downstream NLP objectives or fairness constraints directly into the MIP, which could be a useful direction for future tokenizer design.
