## Problem

Self-Play Fine-Tuning (SPIN) enables iterative self-improvement without preference-labeled data, but it applies uniform learning signals across all tokens, ignoring that both chosen and rejected responses contain a mix of high- and low-quality tokens. As the model improves, this uniformity becomes increasingly suboptimal. The paper asks how to incorporate fine-grained token-level importance into self-play alignment without the prohibitive cost of training separate contrastive models (as in TIS-DPO) or relying on explicit reward models.

## Input / Output

The method takes an SFT dataset of prompt-response pairs, a frozen teacher model (e.g., Zephyr-7B or Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct), and an initial student model (e.g., Qwen1.5-1.8B or GPT2-1.5B). The teacher is used exclusively in a single offline forward pass per iteration to compute per-token importance weights via log-odds ratios, not for logits or hidden-state matching. The output is an iteratively refined student model trained over M self-play rounds (4 iterations × 2 epochs), where at each round the current student generates synthetic rejected responses that are paired with ground-truth responses for weighted preference optimization. A key practical detail is that the method handles tokenizer mismatches between teacher and student through word-segment-based alignment, enabling weight transfer across heterogeneous tokenization schemes.

## Method

SWIFT formulates token-level distribution matching via an Integral Probabilistic Metric (IPM) over token-based reward functions, then connects this to advantage-function maximization to derive a weighted DPO-style objective with per-token importance weights ω_t. The weights are estimated from a teacher model as ω_t = k·exp(μ·clamp(log(π_T(y_t|x,y_<t)/π_S(y_t|x,y_<t)), L, U)), which measures how much more 'reward' the teacher assigns to each token relative to the student. When tokenizers differ, weights are aggregated over shared word-level segments rather than aligned token-by-token. The final training objective (Eq. 13) minimizes a logistic loss over the difference between weighted log-likelihood ratios of chosen and rejected sequences, with a weighted sequence KL divergence term, trained via student forcing. This avoids the need for two additional contrastive LLMs (as in TIS-DPO), reducing weight computation from ~15 hours to ~3 hours per iteration while achieving superior performance.

## Results

On the HuggingFace Open LLM Leaderboard (6 benchmarks), SWIFT achieves the highest average score of 47.46, surpassing the strongest baseline TIS-DPO (45.80) by +1.66 and SPIN (43.20) by +4.26. The largest gains appear on GSM8k (37.93 vs. 30.71 for TIS-DPO, +7.22) and MMLU (44.84 vs. 41.61, +3.23). In the knowledge distillation setting (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct → GPT2-1.5B), SWIFT attains the best average ROUGE-L of 29.20, outperforming DSKD (28.90), MinED (28.71), and ULD (27.65) across four generation benchmarks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SWIFT outperforms all eight baselines in both experimental settings. In the alignment setting, SWIFT beats SPIN (its direct self-play predecessor) by +4.26 average, with the most dramatic improvement on GSM8k (+19.51) and MMLU (+4.03), indicating that token-level importance weighting particularly benefits reasoning tasks. Against the strongest preference-optimization baseline TIS-DPO (which requires training two additional contrastive LLMs), SWIFT gains +1.66 average while being significantly more computationally efficient (3.08h vs. 15.33h per iteration for weight computation). SWIFT also surpasses DPO (+4.29), IPO (+1.80), and TDPO (+4.31) on average, despite not requiring any human-labeled preference data. In the knowledge distillation setting, SWIFT edges out DSKD by +0.30 ROUGE-L and MinED by +0.49, while using less peak GPU memory (55.39 GB vs. 66.63 GB for DSKD) and comparable training time per sample to plain SFT (0.30s vs. 0.26s).

## Relation to Prior Work

SWIFT extends SPIN's self-play fine-tuning framework by replacing its uniform token-level learning signal with importance weights estimated from a stronger teacher model, drawing the weighting formula from TIS-DPO's contrastive token importance sampling but replacing the expensive two-model contrastive procedure with a single teacher forward pass. The theoretical derivation connects the IPM-based distribution matching objective to the advantage function maximization from TDPO, then recasts the resulting teacher-forcing formulation into a student-forcing (scheduled sampling) objective to obtain the final training loss. A practical contribution is the shared-surface-segment token mapping strategy that handles tokenizer mismatches between teacher and student, a problem that prior token-level methods (TIS-DPO, TDPO) implicitly assume away by requiring shared vocabularies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that token-level importance weighting can serve a dual role—improving self-play alignment quality and functioning as a lightweight knowledge distillation strategy that avoids online teacher access—offering a practical middle ground between full RLHF and plain SFT. However, the method still requires access to a stronger teacher model, and the repeated teacher forward passes across multiple self-play iterations introduce non-trivial overhead (though far less than TIS-DPO's two-model training). Open questions include how the approach scales to much larger model gaps, whether the word-segment alignment strategy degrades for languages with different tokenization characteristics, and a deeper understanding of how token weighting shapes the learning dynamics across iterations.
