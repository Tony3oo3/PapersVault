## Problem

Current generative video models rely exclusively on RGB signals, causing them to prioritize color and texture over structural and dynamical attributes. Over long sequences, small frame-wise errors accumulate and compound, producing severe temporal drift, object warping, and violations of physical constraints. The paper tackles how to maintain structural and motion consistency in long-horizon video generation by incorporating perceptual cues that are inherently more stable than raw color.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251540/cropped_page1_idx23.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a text prompt (4–6 action descriptions) and an initial image as conditioning, and jointly predicts RGB frames, depth maps, and optical flow over 20–30 second horizons at 480×832 (Wan2.1-1.3B) or 480×720 (CogVideoX-2B). Depth is synthesized via Video Depth Anything + DepthAnythingV2 and optical flow via SEA-RAFT, both encoded into the same 3D VAE latent space as RGB; the three modalities are concatenated along the channel dimension into a single joint latent that the Diffusion Transformer denoises. At inference, the model streams prediction groups sequentially, using a memory bank of prior frames (with depth kept noise-free and RGB/flow lightly noised) to guide each new group.

## Method

WorldWeaver extends a pretrained diffusion or flow-matching video model by adding depth and optical-flow channels to both input and output layers, then trains a unified objective that jointly predicts all three modalities from a single noisy joint latent. The key architectural insight is a perceptual memory bank: short-term memory retains a few fully denoised adjacent frames for fine texture, while long-term memory keeps depth frames noise-free (exploiting depth's invariance to illumination and texture drift) and applies only low-level noise (t_m=0.8) to RGB and flow channels, providing stable structural guidance without the heavy noising that prior methods require. A segmented group-wise noise schedule assigns each of G prediction groups a distinct noise level sampled from a structured interval, aligning training noise with the sequential streaming denoising used at inference and reducing both drift and computational cost. A non-causal sampling head with delayed denoising further allows the model to refine predictions using context beyond the immediate past, mitigating the train–test discrepancy inherent in teacher-forced training.

## Results

On the controlled comparison (CogVideoX-2B, DROID robotic manipulation dataset), WorldWeaver achieves the best subject consistency (90.92), background consistency (92.39), and motion smoothness (0.75) among all long-horizon methods, while maintaining a low drift score of 0.07. It converges in 30K training steps, substantially fewer than History Diffusion (70K) and Diffusion Forcing (80K). In the broader open-source comparison (Table 1), WorldWeaver achieves the lowest quality drift (0.07) and highest background consistency (90.49) among 20–30 s videos, though it trails Magi 4.5B on subject consistency (87.34 vs. 88.71) and image quality (0.59 vs. 0.62).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CausVid [74], WorldWeaver wins on every metric in the controlled setting (Table 2): subject consistency 90.92 vs. 90.43, background consistency 92.39 vs. 91.56, motion smoothness 0.75 vs. 0.67, and drift 0.07 vs. 0.19. Versus History Diffusion [60], WorldWeaver improves subject consistency by +1.14, background consistency by +0.47, and motion smoothness by +0.07, while requiring less than half the training steps (30K vs. 70K). Compared to Diffusion Forcing [10], WorldWeaver gains +2.25 in subject consistency and +1.16 in background consistency, with a comparable drift score (0.07 vs. 0.06) but at 2.7× fewer training steps (30K vs. 80K). Rolling Diffusion [58] achieves the lowest drift (0.05) and fastest convergence (20K steps), but WorldWeaver surpasses it on all consistency and motion metrics. In the open-source comparison (Table 1), WorldWeaver outperforms StreamingT2V [32] and SkyReels-V2 [11] on background consistency and drift, but trails Magi 4.5B [59] on subject consistency (87.34 vs. 88.71) and image quality (0.59 vs. 0.62), which the authors attribute to differences in training data and engineering optimizations.

## Relation to Prior Work

WorldWeaver extends the long-horizon video generation line of work (StreamingT2V, CausVid, Diffusion Forcing, History Diffusion, Rolling Diffusion) by introducing joint RGB–depth–optical-flow modeling as a unified latent representation, building on the flow-matching/diffusion-transformer backbone of Wan2.1 and CogVideoX. Its closest conceptual predecessor is VideoJAM, which also conditions on optical flow but relies on complex sampling-stage guidance; WorldWeaver instead uses a simpler joint-prediction objective and adds depth as a drift-resistant structural anchor. The memory-bank design with channel-specific noise levels (noise-free depth, low-noise RGB) departs from prior methods that apply uniform or high noise to all historical channels to prevent drift, and the group-wise segmented noise schedule replaces the per-frame random scheduling of Diffusion Forcing with a structured curriculum that better aligns training and inference.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Long-horizon video generation is a prerequisite for practical world models in robotics, autonomous driving, and interactive simulation, yet temporal drift remains the central unsolved bottleneck. The empirical finding that depth is substantially more drift-resistant than RGB (Fig. 3, Fig. 5) provides a concrete, actionable principle for stabilizing long sequences without the heavy noise injection that prior methods require, and the 30K-step convergence versus 70–80K for comparable baselines makes the approach more accessible. Key open questions remain: success rate still degrades beyond ~20 seconds, small objects can vanish even with depth cues, and whether additional perceptual signals (e.g., surface normals, semantic segmentation at scale) could further extend the stable horizon is unexplored.
