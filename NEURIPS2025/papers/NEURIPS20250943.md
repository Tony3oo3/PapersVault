## Problem

Existing distribution-modifying LLM watermarks face an inherent trade-off governed by watermark window size: smaller windows resist scrubbing (paraphrasing) attacks but are easily reverse-engineered for low-cost statistics-based spoofing, while larger windows resist spoofing but become fragile to localized edits. This paper identifies an overlooked mechanism—equivalent texture keys, where multiple tokens in the window can independently support detection—that can break this trade-off and achieve a Pareto improvement on both axes simultaneously.

## Input / Output

At generation time, the method consumes a text prompt, a secret key ξ, an integer hash function H mapping to a small hash space {1,…,d}, a watermark strength δ, and a window size h. The vocabulary V is partitioned into d uniform sub-vocabularies, each receiving its own cipher to produce a sub-green list; the final green list is the union of all sub-green lists, and δ is added to the logits of green tokens before sampling. At detection time, the z-score is computed per sub-vocabulary: a token's green/red status is verified only against the cipher of the sub-vocabulary it belongs to, so the detector reconstructs d independent sub-partitions rather than a single global partition.

## Method

SEEK (Sub-vocabulary decomposed Equivalent tExture Key) exploits hash collisions in a reduced hash space to create equivalent texture keys—multiple tokens in the watermark window that independently satisfy the Min aggregation and thus each can reconstruct the cipher for valid detection. To avoid the text-quality degradation caused by a single reduced-hash cipher (which limits green-red partition diversity to O(d)), SEEK splits the vocabulary into d disjoint sub-vocabularies and assigns each its own cipher θ_n^i: if the hash value i appears in the window's hash signature I, θ_n^i = i·ξ; otherwise a default cipher is used. The final green list is the union of all sub-green lists, yielding O(2^d) distinct partitions and preserving generation quality. Because detection is localized to the sub-vocabulary containing the candidate token, an attacker must disturb all equivalent texture keys within that sub-vocabulary to remove the watermark, while the large effective window size (h tokens across d sub-vocabularies) keeps the statistics-based spoofing sample complexity at O(|V|^h), maintaining strong spoofing resistance.

## Results

Under ChatGPT-based scrubbing on C4-RealNewsLike, SEEK (h=6) achieves TP@1% of 80.5% and TP@5% of 92.6%, outperforming KGW-Min4 (73.5%/86.5%) by +10.2%/+6.1 percentage points. Against the more aggressive DIPPER-I paraphrasing attack on C4, SEEK reaches TP@1% of 96.0% versus 82.6% for KGW-Min4. On spoofing resistance, SEEK achieves FPR@1e-3 of 0.10/0.07/0.18 on Dolly-CW/MMW-BookReports/MMW-FakeNews, compared to 0.85/0.91/0.95 for KGW-Min4, representing gains of +88.2%/+92.3%/+82.0%. Text quality is preserved: SEEK (d=12) maintains Log Diversity of 0.95/0.96/0.95 on WikiText/LFQA/PubMedQA, nearly matching unwatermarked KGW-Min (0.96/0.95/0.95).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against KGW-Min (SelfHash) [30], the primary baseline, SEEK achieves consistent wins on both scrubbing and spoofing: under ChatGPT scrubbing, SEEK6 surpasses KGW-Min4 by +10.2% TP@1% on C4 and +27.0% on LFQA, while under the stronger DIPPER-I attack the gap widens to +13.4% on C4. On statistics-based spoofing, SEEK reduces FPR@1e-3 from 0.85 to 0.10 on Dolly-CW (an 88% reduction) and from 0.91 to 0.07 on MMW-BookReports (92% reduction), and similarly outperforms KGW-Min under distillation-based spoofing (FPR@1e-2: 0.11 vs 0.38 on Dolly-CW). Against KGW-Sum and KGW-Left variants [29], SEEK dominates across all metrics; KGW-Sum4 is particularly weak on scrubbing (TP@1% of 13.9% on C4) while KGW-Left1, despite its small window, still trails SEEK by 7.7% TP@1% on C4 under ChatGPT. Compared to UNIGRAM [53], which achieves the highest scrubbing robustness (TP@1% 91.8% on C4) but is trivially spoofable due to its fixed green list, SEEK achieves 80.5% TP@1% on C4 scrubbing while maintaining strong spoofing resistance (FPR@1e-3 = 0.10), demonstrating the Pareto improvement the paper claims.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the KGW family of distribution-modifying watermarks (Kirchenbauer et al., 2023; SelfHash/KGW-Min, 2023) by identifying an overlooked mechanism—equivalent texture keys arising from hash collisions—that provides scrubbing robustness orthogonal to the watermark window size. It reinterprets UNIGRAM (Ren et al., 2023) as a degenerate special case (d=1) of this mechanism, unifying previously separate design choices under a single framework. The key departure from prior work is the sub-vocabulary decomposition in SEEK, which decouples green-list construction across disjoint sub-vocabularies to recover the O(2^d) partition diversity lost when reducing hash space cardinality, thereby resolving the quality-robustness tension that limited the simpler hash-space-reduction approach.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By demonstrating a Pareto improvement over the widely-acknowledged scrubbing-spoofing trade-off, this paper provides LLM providers with a concrete, deployable mechanism to strengthen watermark security without the previously assumed compromise. The theoretical propositions (collision probability, expected erasure cost) give practitioners principled guidance on choosing window size h and hash space d. Open questions remain: whether the equivalent texture key mechanism transfers to multi-bit or semantic-level watermarks, how it interacts with more sophisticated adaptive attacks beyond the statistics- and distillation-based spoofing studied here, and whether the sub-vocabulary decomposition introduces new attack surfaces (e.g., learning sub-vocabulary boundaries). The approach is also specific to KGW-family partitioning schemes and does not directly apply to logit-bias or token-level watermarking variants.
