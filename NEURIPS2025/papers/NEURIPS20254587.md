## Problem

Existing multimodal safety benchmarks evaluate LVLM vulnerabilities under static image-text inputs, leaving the distinct safety risks introduced by video's temporal dimension largely unexplored. Sequential frame inputs can convey harmful actions that evolve over time, creating compositional attack surfaces that single-frame evaluations cannot capture, yet no comprehensive benchmark exists to systematically assess video LVLM safety under such video-text threats.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254587/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance pairs a synthesized 10-second video with one of two query types: a harmful query containing explicit malicious intent, or a benign query that appears neutral but implicitly references harmful content shown in the video. The evaluated LVLM produces a free-text response, which is then scored by Qwen-2.5-72B using RJScore—a confidence-weighted expected toxicity value computed from the softmax-normalized logits over a 5-point ordinal scale. Attack success is determined by whether RJScore exceeds a calibrated threshold (τ=2.85), yielding a binary harmful/safe label per sample and an aggregate ASR per model and category.

## Method

The benchmark construction uses a three-stage controllable pipeline that decomposes video semantics into a subject image (what is shown) and motion text (how it moves): LLMs refine abstract harmful queries into detailed scene descriptions, T2I models (Midjourney-V6, KLING 1.5) render subject images, an LVLM (GPT-4o) infers temporally coherent motion trajectories from those images, and I2V generators (KLING 1.6, Sora, Jimeng P2.0 Pro) synthesize the final 10-second clips. For evaluation, the authors propose RJScore, which instead of using the judge model's final discrete prediction, computes the expected ordinal toxicity score as the weighted sum over the softmax-normalized token probabilities for scores 1–5, thereby incorporating the model's internal confidence. The decision threshold is calibrated via 5-fold cross-validation against human annotations from 40 expert annotators, achieving 91% agreement. The benchmark spans 2,264 video-text pairs across 13 primary and 48 fine-grained unsafe categories, and is used to evaluate 24 state-of-the-art video LVLMs (7 proprietary, 17 open-source) under both harmful and benign query conditions.

## Results

Across 24 video LVLMs, benign-query video-text pairs achieve an average attack success rate (ASR) of 67.2%, which is 28.1% higher than harmful-query ASR, revealing that implicit video-referential threats are far more effective than explicit textual malice. Video inputs (Frame>1) yield an average 8.6% higher ASR than single-image inputs (Frame=1), with Qwen2.5-VL-72B showing a near-linear increase from 66.9% (1 frame) to 77.2% (64 frames) on benign queries. Larger models within the same series are not safer: Qwen2.5-VL-7B/32B/72B achieves 68.7%/73.2%/74.0% ASR on benign queries, and GPT-4o (43.3%) is less safe than GPT-4o-mini (35.9%). The proposed RJScore metric with Qwen-2.5-72B as judge reaches 91.0% agreement with human annotations at threshold τ=2.85, outperforming GPT-4o (88.2%) and Llama Guard 3 (79.5%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against four existing image-text safety benchmarks, Video-SafetyBench (benign queries) consistently yields the highest ASR: Qwen2.5-VL-72B scores 74.0% vs. 66.9% (MM-SafetyBench), 40.8% (Figstep), 36.0% (JailbreakV), and 11.6% (HADES), confirming that temporal video-referential attacks are substantially harder to defend against than static image-text attacks. Among the 24 evaluated LVLMs, proprietary models are markedly safer than open-source ones: Claude 3.5 Sonnet achieves the lowest benign-query ASR (19.9%), followed by Claude 3.7 Sonnet (23.8%) and GPT-4o-mini (35.9%), while most open-source models exceed 65% (e.g., MiniCPM-o-2.6 at 86.5%, Qwen2-VL-72B at 83.3%). Scaling within a series does not improve safety: Qwen2.5-VL-7B/32B/72B shows increasing ASR (68.7%→73.2%→74.0%), and GPT-4o (43.3%) is less safe than GPT-4o-mini (35.9%). For the judge model comparison, RJScore (91.0% human agreement) outperforms GPT-4o (88.2%), Qwen-2.5-72B alone (88.4%), Llama Guard 3 (79.5%), HarmBench (77.1%), Rule-based (76.5%), and Beaver-Dam (69.6%), with the largest gap over Beaver-Dam by 21.4 percentage points. On the temporal dimension, increasing sampled frames from 1 to 64 raises benign-query ASR by up to 10.3% (Qwen2.5-VL-72B: 66.9%→77.2%) and harmful-query ASR by up to 20.0% (LLaVA-Video-72B: 52.0%→72.0%), demonstrating that video inputs amplify safety risks beyond what single images pose.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the multimodal safety evaluation line of work (FigStep, MM-SafetyBench, HADES, JailbreakV, VLSBench) from static image-text pairs to video-text compositional attacks, explicitly identifying the temporal dimension as an underexplored attack surface. It repurposes existing T2I and I2V generation tools (Midjourney, KLING, Sora) within a novel three-stage controllable pipeline that decomposes video semantics into subject images and motion text, a design motivated by the limited text-video alignment of current generative models. The RJScore metric builds on LLM-as-judge paradigms (GPT-4o, Qwen-2.5) but introduces token-level logit confidence and 5-fold cross-validated threshold calibration to handle borderline harmful outputs, departing from prior binary or single-score judge approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As video LVLMs are increasingly deployed in consumer and enterprise settings, this benchmark provides the first systematic evidence that temporal video inputs introduce distinct safety risks beyond what image-text evaluations capture, with benign video-referential queries proving more effective than explicit harmful text. The finding that model scaling does not improve safety alignment (and may worsen it) challenges a common deployment assumption and suggests that safety must be explicitly engineered rather than assumed to emerge from scale. Open questions include whether findings generalize to longer or real-world (non-synthetic) videos, how to design effective temporal safety alignment mechanisms, and whether the 48-category taxonomy adequately captures emerging video-specific risks.
