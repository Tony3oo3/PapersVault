## Problem

Truncation by death renders the population-level value function of dynamic treatment regimes (DTRs) ill-defined, since counterfactual outcomes for deceased patients are not merely missing but inherently undefined. Existing approaches either misapply censoring corrections or restrict the endpoint to survival itself, neglecting clinically important quality-of-life outcomes. Prior principal stratification methods for the always-survivor stratum are limited to single-decision settings and do not handle the time-varying confounding and multi-stage decision sequences inherent in DTRs.

## Input / Output

The method consumes longitudinal tabular patient records with K decision points (K=2 in the main analysis), where each trajectory includes baseline covariates X₁, intermediate covariates X₂, binary treatment assignments A₁ and A₂, survival indicators S₁ and S₂, censoring indicators C₁ and C₂, and a final continuous outcome Y (e.g., negative SOFA score). Data are subject to monotone censoring and truncation by death, meaning variables after a death or dropout event are unobserved. The method outputs a point estimate of the always-survivor value function V_AS(π) for a given policy π, and when used for policy learning, produces a linear two-stage treatment rule π_β = (1{h₁ᵀβ₁ > 0}, 1{h₂ᵀβ₂ > 0}) that assigns stage-wise binary treatment decisions.

## Method

The paper extends principal stratification to multi-stage DTRs by defining the always-survivor stratum U=1111 (survival under all treatment combinations) and showing that the always-survivor value V_AS(π) = E[Y^π | U=1111] is identifiable under monotonicity, principal ignorability, and modified sequential randomization assumptions. It then derives the efficient influence function (EIF) of V_AS(π) via Gâteaux differentiation of the numerator and denominator of the identification formula, yielding a semiparametric efficiency bound. From the EIF, the authors construct a multiply robust (MR) estimator that remains consistent if any of five distinct combinations of nuisance models (propensity scores, survival probabilities, outcome regressions, and their conditional means) are correctly specified, and achieves the efficiency bound when all are correct. Policy learning proceeds by maximizing the MR value estimate over a class of linear policies using a differential evolution algorithm, with cross-fitting ensuring valid asymptotic normality. The approach generalizes to K≥3 stages with a product-form identification formula and corresponding EIF.

## Results

In the off-policy evaluation simulation (Table 4), the MR estimator achieves small bias (−0.20 to 0.22 at n=2000; −0.07 to 0.18 at n=5000) across all five consistent model-specification scenarios (M1–M5), while the intentionally misspecified scenario M6 shows large bias (−1.74 at n=2000). The MR estimator's standard error (0.63 at n=2000, 0.37 at n=5000 in M1) sits between the outcome-regression estimator (0.475/0.280) and the IPW estimator (2.250/1.346), confirming comparable efficiency to OR with greater robustness. For off-policy learning, the MR estimator achieves a Percentage of Correct Decisions on Always-Survivors (PCD-AS) of 0.992 (n=2000) and 0.994 (n=5000), with 95% CI coverage of 94.8% and 96% respectively. On MIMIC-III (high-risk sepsis, SOFA > 8), the MR-learned policy is closer to the true optimum than the AIPW-learned policy, with age and weight as the dominant covariates driving baseline treatment assignment.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the standard AIPW estimator (Eq. 4) that treats death as a censoring event, which is the conventional approach in the DTR literature (Zhang et al., 2013). In the off-policy learning simulation, the MR estimator outperforms AIPW on PCD-AS: 0.992 vs. 0.987 at n=2000 and 0.994 vs. 0.988 at n=5000, with lower variability (SD 0.007/0.004 vs. 0.008/0.007). On the MIMIC-III sepsis application, the MR-learned policy achieves a test-set value closer to the benchmark true optimum than the AIPW-learned policy (Figure 3). In terms of estimation efficiency, the MR estimator's standard error is comparable to the outcome-regression estimator (0.63 vs. 0.475 at n=2000) while being substantially tighter than the pure IPW estimator (2.250), and it retains consistency under five distinct partial-misspecification scenarios where the AIPW baseline would fail. The key advantage is robustness: the MR estimator remains consistent if any one of five nuisance-model combinations is correct, whereas the AIPW approach requires correct specification of the combined censoring-death model.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the principal stratification framework for truncation by death from single-decision settings (Chu et al., 2023; Grossi et al., 2025) to multi-stage dynamic treatment regimes, navigating time-varying confounding and correlated variables across decision points. It builds on the multiply robust estimation paradigm of Jiang et al. (2022) and the original principal stratification of Imbens and Angrist (1994), while addressing a gap in the DTR literature (Robins, 1997; Murphy, 2003; Zhang et al., 2013) where traditional value functions become ill-defined when death precludes outcome observation. The key methodological departure is deriving the efficient influence function for the always-survivor value function in a multi-stage setting, yielding a multiply robust estimator that generalizes AIPW to the truncation-by-death context.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Truncation by death affects a substantial fraction of critical care patients (e.g., 15% in MIMIC-III sepsis cohorts), rendering standard DTR evaluation inapplicable and forcing clinicians to either inappropriately treat death as censoring or restrict endpoints to survival alone, potentially neglecting quality-of-life outcomes. The multiply robust property (consistency under five distinct model specification scenarios) is practically valuable in clinical settings where nuisance model misspecification is likely. Open questions include scaling the identification and EIF derivation to K≥3 decision points, verifying the monotonicity and principal ignorability assumptions in real data, and resolving the active debate over whether policies learned from MIMIC-III data are clinically transportable given missing data and diagnostic uncertainty.
