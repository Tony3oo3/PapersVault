## Problem

Existing post-hoc concept-removal methods (e.g., LEACE, SAL) eliminate sensitive attributes from neural network embeddings but simultaneously degrade task-relevant information, especially when the protected concept is correlated with the target label. Prior approaches that attempt to preserve task information sacrifice linear guardedness, leaving the concept partially recoverable. The paper asks whether one can provably remove a concept while exactly preserving its covariance with a task label, without relaxing the linear-guardedness guarantee.

## Input / Output

The method consumes last-layer embeddings from a frozen or fine-tuned model (e.g., the [CLS] token from BERT, mean-pooled tokens from Multilingual E5, or hidden states from Llama 2/3), together with a concept label z to erase (binary, one-hot, or continuous) and a task label y to preserve. It outputs a single oblique projection matrix P (and an offset b for non-centered data) such that the transformed embeddings r(x)=Px+b have zero covariance with z (linear guardedness) while exactly retaining the original covariance with y. The projection is applied once in closed form—no iterative training is required—and can be applied to any layer of a frozen model.

## Method

SPLINCE constructs an oblique projection that simultaneously satisfies a kernel constraint (PΣ_{x,z}=0, ensuring no linear classifier can recover z) and a range constraint (PΣ_{x,y}=Σ_{x,y}, preserving task-relevant covariance). The closed-form solution P* = W⁺ V(UᵀV)⁻¹ Uᵀ W uses a whitening matrix W=(Σ_{x,x}^{1/2})⁺, where U spans the orthogonal complement of colsp(WΣ_{x,z}) and V spans colsp(WΣ_{x,y}) plus a residual subspace U⁻; this is the unique minimum-distortion projector under both constraints. The key insight is that LEACE's free choice of projection range is partially repurposed to keep task-relevant directions in the range, while the remaining freedom is used to minimize embedding distortion via whitening/unwhitening. A complementary theorem shows that when a linear classifier is re-fitted without regularization, all same-kernel projections (SPLINCE, LEACE, SAL) yield identical predictions, so SPLINCE's advantage materializes specifically when regularization is applied or when the last layer is frozen (e.g., in language-modeling interventions).

## Results

On the profession dataset (Llama2-7B, Llama2-13B, Llama3-8B), all three projections reduce stereotypical reliance (exp(β_stereo) drops from ~360–400 to ~79–88), but SPLINCE uniquely preserves or increases factual gender information: exp(β_fact) reaches 2427, 3324, and 1343 respectively, versus 1214, 1632, and 768 for LEACE and 590, 481, and 350 for SAL. On the Bias in Bios and Multilingual Text Detoxification classification tasks, SPLINCE outperforms LEACE and SAL in both overall and worst-group accuracy as the task–concept correlation increases. On Winobias, SPLINCE improves anti-stereotypical coreference accuracy more than the other projections for 2 out of 3 Llama models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SPLINCE is compared against LEACE and SAL, the two other projections that guarantee linear guardedness and differ only in their choice of range. On the profession dataset, SPLINCE matches or slightly beats both baselines in removing stereotypical cues (exp(β_stereo) ≈ 79–82 vs. 80–88 for LEACE/SAL), but substantially outperforms them in preserving factual gender information: exp(β_fact) is roughly 2× higher than LEACE (e.g., 2427 vs. 1214 on Llama2-7B) and 4–5× higher than SAL (e.g., 2427 vs. 590). On the classification benchmarks (Bias in Bios, Multilingual Text Detoxification), SPLINCE achieves higher overall and worst-group accuracy than both LEACE and SAL, with the gap widening as the task–concept correlation increases. On Winobias, SPLINCE yields the best anti-stereotypical coreference accuracy for 2 of 3 Llama models, while LEACE and SAL perform more similarly to each other. The paper also proves (Theorem 2) that when a linear classifier is re-fitted without regularization, all three methods produce identical predictions, so SPLINCE's advantage manifests specifically when the last layer is frozen or regularized.

## Relation to Prior Work

SPLINCE is a direct extension of LEACE (Belrose et al., 2023), adding a range constraint that forces the projection to preserve the covariance between embeddings and a task-relevant label, while retaining LEACE's kernel constraint for linear guardedness. It also relates to SAL (Shao et al., 2023) and INLP (Ravfogel et al., 2020) as other linear concept-removal methods, but unlike prior approaches that preserve task information by removing a *different* (smaller) subspace and thereby sacrificing linear guardedness (Dev et al., 2021; Holstege et al., 2024; Bareeva et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2024), SPLINCE maintains the full linear-guardedness guarantee. The key conceptual departure is reframing the problem as an oblique projection with two simultaneous geometric constraints (kernel for concept removal, range for task preservation) rather than a single-constraint minimization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SPLINCE provides a principled, closed-form solution to a well-known practical failure mode of concept-removal methods: collateral damage to task-relevant information. The theoretical result (Theorem 2) that all linear-guardedness-preserving projections are equivalent under unregularized re-fitting is a useful clarification but also delineates precisely when SPLINCE's advantage manifests—namely, with regularization or frozen last layers (the language-modeling setting). Open questions include whether preserving covariance is the optimal proxy for task performance, how to adapt the objective for middle-layer interventions, and why the method underperforms on vision classification tasks relative to NLP. The paper also notes the method can be distortive and that multimodal alignment of covariance subspaces remains unaddressed.
