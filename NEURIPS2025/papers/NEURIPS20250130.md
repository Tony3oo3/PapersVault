## Problem

Evaluating Causal Representation Learning (CRL) methods is fundamentally difficult because real-world datasets lack annotated ground-truth causal variables and structures, while existing synthetic benchmarks are too simplistic to reflect real visual complexity. This creates a dilemma between realism and evaluative precision that hampers systematic comparison of CRL approaches. CausalVerse addresses this gap by providing high-fidelity simulated visual data with complete, configurable access to the underlying causal generating processes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250130/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark supplies RGB images at 1024×1024 or 1920×1080 resolution and video sequences (3–32 seconds, diverse frame rates) across 24 scenes in four domains, totaling ~200k images and 300M+ frames. CRL methods consume these visual inputs—optionally augmented with domain labels, multi-view captures, or temporal sequences—and must recover latent causal variables (ranging from 3 to 129 per scene, mixing categorical and continuous types) along with their structural relationships. Evaluation is performed via component-wise MCC, block-wise R², and an over-complete MCC variant that handles the case where the learned representation dimension exceeds the true latent dimension.

## Method

CausalVerse is constructed through a hierarchical domain–scene–instance pipeline: four domains (static image generation, dynamic physical simulation, robotic manipulation, traffic analysis) are each decomposed into curated scenes with predefined causal graphs, and instances are generated by sampling latent variables according to those graphs and rendering via domain-specific engines (Blender/BlenderProc for static scenes, Bullet Physics and RoboSuite/Habitat-Lab for physical and robotic dynamics, Unreal Engine 4 for traffic). The key differentiator is full configurability of the causal structure—users can modify domain labels, temporal dependencies, and intervention histories to either satisfy or deliberately violate the assumptions of specific CRL methods (e.g., sufficient domain variation for Sufficient Change, sparsity for Mechanism Sparsity). The empirical evaluation trains nine representative CRL methods (Sufficient Change, Mechanism Sparsity, Multiview, Contrastive Learning, IDOL, CaRiNG, TDRL, TCL, iVAE) under both assumption-satisfied and assumption-violated conditions, using a shared VAE architecture for fair comparison, and reports MCC and R² against ground-truth latents to quantify the gap between theoretical identifiability guarantees and practical performance on high-dimensional visual data.

## Results

On the three static image scenes (Ball on the Slope, Cylinder Spring, Light Refraction), the supervised upper bound achieves near-perfect MCC (0.9916 avg) and R² (0.9891 avg), while the best unsupervised CRL method, Sufficient Change, reaches only 0.5768 avg MCC and 0.9131 avg R². On the two temporal video scenes (Fall Simple, Robotics Study), all five temporal CRL methods exhibit low MCC values (0.17–0.25), with IDOL achieving the highest MCC (0.2527 on Fall Simple, 0.2500 on Robotics Study) and R² (0.5901, 0.6503). The key finding is a substantial gap between the supervised ceiling and current unsupervised CRL performance, particularly in component-wise identifiability (MCC), even under unmet assumptions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates nine CRL baselines across static and temporal settings. Among static image methods, Sufficient Change [6] achieves the highest average MCC (0.5768), outperforming Contrastive Learning (0.4323), Multiview (0.3998), and Mechanism Sparsity (0.2560), because it is explicitly designed for component-wise identifiability. However, for block-wise R², Contrastive Learning (0.9734) and Sufficient Change (0.9131) both perform well, while Mechanism Sparsity lags significantly (0.3216) due to violated sparsity assumptions. In the temporal setting, IDOL [24] leads with the highest MCC on both video scenes (0.2527 on Fall Simple, 0.2500 on Robotics Study), followed by CaRiNG [8] (0.2280, 0.2225) and TDRL [1] (0.2003, 0.2440), while TCL [43] and iVAE [44] trail behind. All temporal methods remain well below the supervised ceiling, with MCC values capped around 0.25, indicating that temporal causal representation learning under realistic visual complexity remains an open challenge. The supervised upper bound (MCC ≈ 0.99, R² ≈ 0.99) confirms that the encoder architecture is not the bottleneck—the gap is in the unsupervised identification principle itself.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a response to the evaluation bottleneck in Causal Representation Learning (CRL), where prior benchmarks (Pendulum, CAUSAL3D, Causal3DIdent, CausalCircuit, Ball/Cloth) trade realism for ground-truth access by using low-dimensional, static, single-domain synthetic scenes. CausalVerse departs from this lineage by leveraging high-fidelity rendering engines (Unreal Engine 4, Blender, Bullet Physics, RoboSuite) to generate visually complex data across four domains while retaining full causal ground truth and configurable simulation parameters. The empirical evaluation draws on the full spectrum of existing CRL identifiability strategies—sufficient change, mechanism sparsity, multi-view alignment, contrastive learning, and temporal disentanglement—treating them as the methods to be benchmarked rather than proposing a new algorithmic contribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CausalVerse addresses a concrete and widely acknowledged gap: the inability to rigorously evaluate CRL methods on data that is both visually realistic and causally annotated. The empirical results quantify the substantial theory-to-practice gap (all unsupervised CRL methods achieve MCC < 0.6 even on simulated data, versus ~0.99 for supervised upper bounds), and the configurable simulation pipeline enables stress-testing under violated assumptions—a capability no prior benchmark offered. Key limitations include the residual sim-to-real gap, handcrafted causal structures that may not capture real-world ambiguity, and the absence of natural sensor noise. Open questions include whether methods that exploit the configurable structure (e.g., sufficient domain variation) can close the identifiability gap, and whether findings transfer to genuinely real-world data.
