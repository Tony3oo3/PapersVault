## Problem

Existing neural audio codecs face a fundamental trade-off: acoustic codecs achieve high-fidelity reconstruction but lack semantic representations and rely on multi-codebook designs, while semantic codecs capture content but discard acoustic detail. Single-codebook designs simplify downstream modeling but struggle to balance compression with both reconstruction quality and effective representations at low bitrates. FocalCodec addresses this by compressing speech into a single binary codebook at ultra-low bitrates (0.16–0.65 kbps) while preserving sufficient semantic and acoustic information for downstream tasks.

## Input / Output

The codec takes 16 kHz speech waveforms and produces a sequence of discrete binary tokens from a single implicit codebook of size 8192 (13-dimensional sign vectors on the unit hypersphere), at token rates of 50, 25, or 12.5 Hz. At inference, the decoder reconstructs the waveform from the dequantized features via inverse STFT. The encoder is a frozen WavLM-large (first 6 layers), so the learned components operate on pre-extracted continuous representations rather than raw waveforms, and the quantizer requires no explicit codebook lookup—quantization is a simple sign operation followed by normalization.

## Method

FocalCodec uses a compressor–quantizer–decompressor sandwich between a frozen WavLM encoder and a Vocos decoder. The compressor and decompressor employ focal modulation blocks (replacing self-attention) with Snake activations, enabling fine-to-coarse context aggregation in linear time with inductive biases suited to speech periodicity. Quantization uses Binary Spherical Quantization (BSQ), the first application of binary quantization in speech, which defines an implicit codebook of 2^L entries via sign projection onto the unit hypersphere, encouraging high codebook utilization without lookup tables. Training is decoupled into two stages: the compressor-quantizer-decompressor is first trained to reconstruct frozen encoder features (L2 + entropy loss), then the decoder is trained separately with adversarial, Mel-spectrogram reconstruction, and feature-matching losses. This decoupling preserves both semantic and acoustic information in the tokens without requiring distillation losses, supervised fine-tuning, or multi-codebook disentanglement.

## Results

On clean LibriSpeech test-clean, FocalCodec@50 (0.65 kbps) achieves UTMOS 4.05, dWER 2.18%, and speaker similarity 97.4%, outperforming the previous state-of-the-art BigCodec (1.04 kbps) on dWER (2.18% vs. 2.55%) at a 38% lower bitrate. At the ultra-low 0.16 kbps, FocalCodec@12.5 still achieves UTMOS 4.22 and dWER 7.94%, remaining competitive with EnCodec (1.50 kbps, dWER 8.08%). On voice conversion (VCTK), FocalCodec variants achieve the highest speaker similarity (92.2–92.6%) while maintaining dWER of 21.27–29.93%, surpassing all multi-codebook hybrids like SpeechTokenizer (dWER 20.32%, Sim 81.2%) and Mimi (dWER 110%, Sim 89.7%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FocalCodec@50 (0.65 kbps) beats the current SOTA BigCodec (1.04 kbps) on dWER (2.18% vs. 2.55%) and code usage (100% vs. 100%) while using 38% less bitrate, and also outperforms Stable Codec (0.70 kbps) on dWER (2.18% vs. 4.97%). Against acoustic codecs, FocalCodec dominates: EnCodec (1.50 kbps) scores UTMOS 1.58 and dWER 8.08%, while DAC (1.00 kbps) scores UTMOS 1.29 and dWER 20.04%, both far below FocalCodec@50. Among hybrid codecs, FocalCodec@50 achieves the lowest dWER, beating SpeechTokenizer (5.14%), Mimi (5.73%), SemantiCodec (8.97%), and WavTokenizer (11.55%) at equal or lower bitrates. On voice conversion, FocalCodec achieves the highest speaker similarity (92.2–92.6%) across all three variants, outperforming SpeechTokenizer (81.2%), Mimi (89.7%), and BigCodec (68.9%), while maintaining competitive dWER (21.27% for @50). Even at the extreme 0.16 kbps, FocalCodec@12.5 matches or exceeds EnCodec (1.50 kbps) on dWER (7.94% vs. 8.08%) and UTMOS (4.22 vs. 1.58), demonstrating that the single binary codebook design preserves sufficient information at ultra-low bitrates.

## Relation to Prior Work

FocalCodec sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) VQ-VAE-based neural audio codecs (EnCodec, DAC, SoundStream), (2) self-supervised speech representation learning (WavLM, HuBERT) used in semantic and hybrid codecs, and (3) focal modulation networks originally developed for image/video processing. It departs from prior hybrid codecs (SpeechTokenizer, SemantiCodec, Mimi, Stable Codec) by replacing multi-codebook RVQ designs with a single binary spherical quantizer, and from prior single-codebook acoustic codecs by incorporating a frozen WavLM encoder to retain semantic information. The decoupled two-stage training (compressor-quantizer-decompressor trained to reconstruct WavLM features, then a separate Vocos decoder trained on those features) is a key architectural departure from end-to-end VQ-VAE training, and the use of focal modulation as the compression backbone replaces the self-attention or convolutional blocks typical in prior codecs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The single binary codebook design (8192 codes from 13-dimensional latents) eliminates the multi-codebook flattening complexity that plagues downstream LLM-based speech models, making FocalCodec a practical tokenizer for speech language modeling at ultra-low bitrates (0.16–0.65 kbps). However, the model is significantly undertrained (~460 hours of English speech) compared to competitors (WavTokenizer: 8k hours, Stable Codec: 105k hours, Mimi: 7M hours), so the true ceiling of the architecture remains unclear. Open questions include whether the focal modulation advantage over self-attention persists at scale, whether the decoupled training strategy can be extended to multilingual or noisy training data, and whether the TTS finding that lower-bitrate variants outperform higher-bitrate ones in autoregressive generation generalizes beyond the limited 460-hour training set.
