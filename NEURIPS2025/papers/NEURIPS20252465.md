## Problem

Existing LVM-based gait recognition methods rely heavily on human-designed gait priors (feature smoothing, language guidance, geometry-driven denoising) while treating all LVM layers equally, underutilizing the rich and distinct representations that intermediate layers inherently provide. This paper asks whether LVM's multi-layer structure alone—without elaborate gait priors—can unlock substantially better gait recognition performance.

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB walking video frames resized to LVM-specific resolutions (448×224 for DINOv2, 224×224 for CLIP, 512×256 for SAM), sampled at 30 frames per sequence. It produces a set of 12 gait representations (one per uniformly sampled LVM layer), each a GaitBase embedding derived from a 16×64×32 projected feature map. At inference, ensemble testing averages Euclidean distances across all 12 layer-specific scores to yield a single similarity value per probe-gallery pair.

## Method

BiggerGait uniformly samples 12 intermediate layers from a frozen LVM backbone and processes each independently: the deepest layer feeds a 1×1-convolution auto-encoder that generates a human silhouette mask (the sole gait prior), which is then applied to all layer features to suppress background noise. Each masked feature passes through a lightweight linear projection (two 1×1 convs, two BNs, GELU; 16 output channels), is bilinearly upsampled to 16×64×32, and is extracted by a dedicated GaitBase module. Training combines triplet loss, cross-entropy loss, and the auto-encoder reconstruction loss. The key empirical finding is that middle layers consistently outperform the final layer, layer contributions are task-dependent (shallow layers favor appearance-heavy domains, deep layers favor semantic-heavy ones), and fusing all layers via ensemble testing yields 7–10% average gains. An optional grouping variant (BiggerGait*) concatenates features within J=6 depth groups and shares P=2 gait encoders, cutting ~109M parameters and ~24G FLOPs for DINOv2-S while preserving near-identical accuracy.

## Results

Trained on CCPG, DINOv2-S-based BiggerGait achieves 89.8% Rank-1 on CCPG full-clothing change (CL), surpassing BigGait (82.6%) by +7.2% and DeepGaitV2 (78.6%) by +11.2%. In cross-domain evaluation, BiggerGait delivers dramatic gains: 70.9% CL on SUSTech1K (vs. BigGait's 43.7%, +27.2%) and 93.0% NM on CASIA-B* (vs. BigGait's 77.4%, +15.6%). The overall average across all four test datasets reaches 67.0% for DINOv2-S BiggerGait, a +14.0% improvement over BigGait (53.0%). The grouping variant BiggerGait* (J=6, P=2) retains most of these gains while cutting FLOPs from 45.7G to 29.8G for DINOv2-S.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BiggerGait (DINOv2-S) outperforms all silhouette-based baselines by large margins: +18.2% CL over GaitBase (71.6%), +11.2% over DeepGaitV2 (78.6%), and +5.9% over the strongest silhouette method MultiGait++ (83.9%). Against the closest RGB-based LVM competitor BigGait (also DINOv2-S), BiggerGait gains +7.2% on CCPG CL and a striking +27.2% on SUSTech1K CL (70.9% vs. 43.7%), confirming that layer-wise feature fusion is the key driver rather than the backbone. On CCGR_MINI (trained on CCGR_MINI), BiggerGait slightly trails BigGait by 2.3% R-1 (85.7% vs. 88.0%), which the authors attribute to CCGR_MINI's preference for deeper semantic layers; however, BiggerGait still surpasses all silhouette-based methods by over 50% on this dataset. The grouping variant BiggerGait* (J=6, P=2) loses only 0.5–1.0% within-domain accuracy while reducing FLOPs by ~35%, making it competitive with BigGait's 12.7G FLOPs at 29.8G. CLIP-S-based BiggerGait shows the strongest cross-domain transfer (15.2% CCGR R-1, 91.5% CASIA-B NM), while SAM-S-based BiggerGait excels in within-domain CCPG (86.8% CL), revealing that LVM pretraining strategy shapes domain adaptation behavior.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends BigGait (CVPR 2024), which first demonstrated that large vision models (LVMs) can extract gait features from raw RGB, by shifting the focus from guiding LVMs with elaborate gait priors (geometric constraints, denoising, diversity losses) to exploiting the LVMs' own layer-wise representations. It also draws on the broader layer-wise analysis literature from LLMs (Skean et al., Sun et al.) and vision (Head2Toe, Perception Encoder), which showed intermediate layers often outperform final layers, and validates this insight in the significantly more demanding fine-grained gait recognition setting. The per-layer gait extractor is GaitBase from OpenGait, and the only human-designed prior retained is a lightweight silhouette mask generated by an auto-encoder on the final LVM layer.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges the prevailing assumption in LVM-based gait recognition that heavy, task-specific priors are necessary, showing that a single silhouette mask plus ensemble of intermediate LVM layers achieves state-of-the-art across four benchmarks. This simplifies the design space and suggests that future gait methods should focus on how to best exploit LVM representations rather than on engineering additional gait constraints. Key open questions remain: temporal modeling is entirely absent (features are per-frame), and predicting which layers are optimal for a new unseen dataset is unresolved, as the best layer shifts with training data and task type (83.3% of the time). The grouping strategy (BiggerGait*) offers a practical path for GPU-limited deployment but sacrifices some accuracy.
