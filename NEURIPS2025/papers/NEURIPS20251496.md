## Problem

Existing theoretical analyses of physics-informed machine learning assume complete prior knowledge of governing differential equations and are largely restricted to linear operators, leaving a gap for the hybrid, nonlinear settings that dominate practical applications. This paper asks what quantity actually governs generalization when physical constraints are incomplete and the underlying equations are nonlinear, and shows that the answer is the dimension of an affine variety rather than the number of model parameters.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251496/cropped_page7_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes noisy point observations (x_i, y_i), a differential operator D (which may be nonlinear), a fixed basis B of d scalar functions, and a finite collection T of trial-function/measure pairs that encode the physical constraint in a unified residual form. The output is a weight vector w constrained to the affine variety V(D, B, T) ∩ B_2(R), yielding a function approximation f_w, together with a high-probability minimax risk bound whose leading term scales as √(d_V log(d_V d)/n) where d_V is the intrinsic dimension of the variety. The unified residual form is notable because choosing Dirac measures recovers the pointwise collocation residuals of PINNs, while choosing Borel measures recovers the weak-form constraints of finite element methods, so a single framework covers both.

## Method

The paper introduces a Unified Residual Form that expresses the differential equation constraint as ⟨D[f], ψ_k⟩_{μ_k} = 0 for a finite set of trial functions and measures, unifying collocation and variational approaches. When the hypothesis class is linear over a fixed basis, the admissible weight vectors form an affine variety V ⊂ R^d defined by K polynomial equations, and the key theoretical result (Theorem 3.5) shows that the minimax risk is governed by the intrinsic dimension d_V of this variety and a topological complexity parameter β, rather than the ambient dimension d. The proof proceeds via Dudley's entropy integral applied to a sub-Gaussian process over the variety, with the covering number controlled by a (β, d_V)-regularity condition whose β is bounded by the Petrovskii-Oleinik-Milnor inequality. For linear operators the variety reduces to a linear subspace with d_V = d − rank(D), recovering and unifying the effective-dimension analysis of prior PI-kernel work. A practical numerical method approximates d_V by sampling points on the variety and computing the maximum rank of the Jacobian of the defining polynomials, with complexity O(N·min(K,d)·K·d) for the nonlinear case.

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 3.5) establishes that the minimax risk of physics-informed linear regression scales as O(√(d_V log(d_V·d)/n)) where d_V is the affine variety dimension, rather than O(√(d/n)) for standard ridge regression. Experimentally, on the discrete Bernoulli equation (Table 1), PILR achieves test MSE of 0.012–0.013 across all settings (d=100 or 200, linear or nonlinear), while RR degrades to 0.48–0.72 as d increases—a roughly 40× improvement. On the discrete diffusion equation (Table 2), PILR achieves 0.22–1.13 MSE versus RR's 1.11–2.21, with the gap widening as spatial resolution increases (d_V=10 vs d=4010).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is standard Ridge Regression (RR), which performs unconstrained linear regression over the same basis functions without physics constraints. PILR consistently and substantially outperforms RR across all experimental settings. On the 1D Bernoulli equation (Table 1), PILR achieves roughly 40× lower MSE (0.012 vs 0.48–0.72), and critically, its performance remains flat as the number of parameters d doubles from 100 to 200, while RR's MSE worsens. On the 2D diffusion equation (Table 2), PILR achieves approximately 2× lower MSE (0.22–1.13 vs 1.11–2.21), with the advantage growing as spatial resolution increases (d_V=10 vs d=4010). The harmonic oscillator and diffusion experiments (Figs. 1–2) further confirm that PILR's test MSE is independent of the number of basis functions d (since d_V=2 or d_V=2·min(d_x,d_t)+1 remains fixed), whereas RR degrades monotonically with d. This validates the core theoretical claim: generalization is governed by the intrinsic affine variety dimension d_V, not the ambient parameter count d.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the physics-informed kernel framework of Doumèche et al. [15,16], which analyzed generalization of linear PDE-constrained estimators via effective dimension, by replacing the full eigenvalue spectrum analysis with the intrinsic dimension of an affine variety defined by the PDE constraints. It also builds directly on the covering-number bounds for real algebraic varieties from Zhang and Kileel [42], adapting their geometric slicing argument to the PIML setting. The Unified Residual Form unifies the collocation (PINN) and variational (FEM) constraint formulations under a single measure-theoretic framework, bridging two previously separate lines of PIML theory (Shin [38] for strong-form convergence, Arnone et al. [5] for weak-form convergence).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, geometry-based explanation for why physics-informed constraints improve generalization: the governing quantity is the affine variety dimension d_V, not the ambient parameter count d, which unifies analysis across linear and nonlinear PDEs. A practical numerical method for estimating d_V (via tangent-space rank sampling) makes the theory actionable for model selection. Key limitations include the restriction to linear hypothesis classes over fixed bases (not neural networks), the need for the basis to be well-specified (Section 5.4 shows approximation error dominates when it is not), and the (β, d_V)-regularity assumption that may be hard to verify in practice. Open questions include extending the framework to deep architectures, optimizing basis/trial function selection, and handling unknown PDE parameters in the augmented variety.
