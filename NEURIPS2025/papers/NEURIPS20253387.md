## Problem

LLM benchmarks increasingly inform real-world deployment and safety decisions, yet users lack a structured means to assess whether a given benchmark is reliable enough to trust. Prior work identified benchmark deficiencies qualitatively but did not quantify risk in terms of likelihood and severity, leaving no principled way to triage which failure modes matter most or to compare benchmarks on reliability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253387/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes publicly available benchmark documentation (research papers, project websites, blog posts) and produces a per-benchmark reliability score normalized to 0–100 across five dimensions (comprehensiveness, intelligibility, consistency, correctness, longevity), alongside a catalog of 57 identified failure modes and 196 associated mitigations. Each failure mode carries an expert-assigned severity in [0,1] and an initial likelihood of 1.0; the final dimension score is the sum of risk reductions (severity × likelihood) achieved by adopted mitigations, so a score of 0 means no known risk is mitigated and 100 means all identified risk is fully addressed.

## Method

BenchRisk adapts the NIST SP 800-30 information-security risk-assessment process to benchmark reliability, replacing 'threats' with reliability-engineering 'failure modes' so that no adversarial actor is presumed. The authors iteratively processed 26 benchmarks, at each round extracting new failure modes and candidate mitigations from documentation, then scored each failure mode's severity (catastrophic → marginally degraded, per DoD MIL-STD-882E levels) and each mitigation's reduction in likelihood and severity via expert judgment with primary/secondary reviewer agreement. Mitigations stack multiplicatively: if two mitigations each reduce likelihood by 0.5, the resulting likelihood is 0.25, and the dimension score accumulates the absolute risk reduction |post-mitigation risk − pre-mitigation risk| across all failure modes in that dimension. Inter-rater reliability was validated on the BBQ benchmark with five independent raters, yielding a Fleiss' κ of 0.53 (moderate agreement), with disagreements traced primarily to reviewer error or subjective severity judgments. The framework is released as an open-source, community-driven tool where benchmark authors can self-score, submit new failure modes, and contest or confirm mitigations through a GitHub issue-based consensus process.

## Results

BenchRisk scores 26 LLM benchmarks across five reliability dimensions (Longevity, Correctness, Comprehensiveness, Consistency, Intelligibility), normalized to 0–100. AILuminate achieves the highest mean score (74) and the highest Longevity score (75), while Wordcraft scores the lowest mean (13) with a minimum dimension score of 0. All 26 benchmarks exhibit significant risk in at least one dimension, with Longevity being the weakest dimension overall (most benchmarks score 0–11). Inter-rater reliability among five independent scorers on the BBQ benchmark yielded a Fleiss' kappa of 0.53 (moderate agreement), with 117 of 150 mitigations scored consistently.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper introduces a new meta-evaluation framework rather than comparing against competing methods, so there are no traditional baselines. The closest comparison is with BetterBench (Reuel et al., 2024), a prior benchmark-quality assessment tool. The authors find that BenchRisk and BetterBench measure largely independent properties: BetterBench's design score and BenchRisk's correctness score show no significant correlation (Figure 5, right), because BetterBench does not model adversarial SUT developers, less-qualified end-users, or safety-critical coverage needs. On the inter-rater reliability front, five independent scorers achieved a Fleiss' kappa of 0.53 on the BBQ benchmark, with 117/150 mitigations scored identically; disagreements were attributed to reviewer error or subjective judgment on ambiguous mitigations. A time-to-saturation analysis of seven benchmarks with human baselines suggests that higher BenchRisk Longevity scores correlate with slower performance saturation, though the sample size is too small for firm conclusions. The authors note that BenchRisk scores are expected to be superseded by benchmark self-scores, which can incorporate non-public operational details unavailable to external assessors.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the NIST information security risk management framework (SP 800-30) to the domain of LLM benchmark reliability, replacing 'threats' with reliability-engineering 'failure modes' and scoring them by severity and likelihood. It builds directly on BetterBench (Reuel et al., 2024), which assessed benchmark quality for scientific replication, but deliberately reframes the question from 'is this benchmark well-designed for researchers?' to 'can a user rely on this benchmark to make a real-world deployment decision?' The reliability engineering lineage (Rausand & Hoyland, 2004) provides the formal structure for failure mode analysis, while the paper's iterative, community-driven scoring process departs from both BetterBench's static checklist and traditional statistical validity frameworks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that all 26 scored benchmarks exhibit significant unmitigated risk in at least one of five dimensions (comprehensiveness, intelligibility, consistency, correctness, longevity) provides concrete evidence that current benchmarking practice is insufficient for the safety and regulatory decisions it is increasingly asked to support. The demonstrated independence between BetterBench design scores and BenchRisk reliability scores suggests that a well-documented, scientifically rigorous benchmark can still be unreliable for decision-making, opening a distinct research axis. Key open questions include whether the community-driven consensus scoring process will converge, whether benchmark self-scores will substantially differ from external assessments, and whether the framework generalizes beyond LLMs to multimodal or non-foundation-model evaluations.
