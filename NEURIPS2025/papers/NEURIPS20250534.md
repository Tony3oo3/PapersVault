## Problem

Representational drift—gradual change in neural representations despite stable task performance—has been observed in biological and artificial systems, but a systematic characterization of its sources across architectures and learning rules is missing. In particular, it is unclear how the structure of the data distribution, specifically the statistics of task-irrelevant stimuli that the network learns to ignore, contributes to long-term drift of task-relevant representations.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a network at its converged solution (on the solution manifold), the input data covariance decomposed into task-relevant and task-irrelevant subspaces (with eigenvalues λ_∥ and λ_⊥), the learning rule (SGD, Oja's rule, or Similarity Matching), and the learning rate η. It produces pairwise angular diffusion coefficients D_sr for each pair of output representations, total drift rates, and the geometric structure of drift (anisotropic for learning noise vs. isotropic for synaptic noise). The key structural constraint is that the solution manifold has a rotational degeneracy (Stiefel manifold for Oja/SM, coordinated weight rotations for autoencoders), and drift is characterized as diffusion along this tangent space.

## Method

The paper develops a unified theoretical framework that decomposes online learning dynamics near the solution manifold into fast normal-space fluctuations (Ornstein-Uhlenbeck, covariance ∝ η) and slow tangential-space diffusion. The central insight is that the sample update at the solution contains a multiplicative term x_∥ x_⊥^T, meaning both task-relevant and task-irrelevant components must be simultaneously present to drive a perturbation; this makes task-irrelevant stimuli an effective noise source even though they are suppressed at the output. Because the tangential projection of the update vanishes on the manifold, the resulting diffusion is second-order in fluctuations, yielding drift rates scaling as η³ (not η). The framework is applied analytically to four architectures—multi-dimensional Oja's network, Similarity Matching, a linear autoencoder with bottleneck, and a supervised two-layer network—producing closed-form pairwise diffusion coefficients that all depend on the variance and dimension of the task-irrelevant subspace. A key qualitative prediction is that learning-induced drift is anisotropic (direction-dependent, governed by the data spectrum), in contrast to the isotropic drift produced by additive Gaussian synaptic noise, and that the drift rate exhibits a non-monotonic dependence on output dimension (rising then falling to zero), which is confirmed on MNIST data.

## Results

The paper derives closed-form expressions for pairwise angular diffusion coefficients in four architectures (Oja, Similarity Matching, Autoencoder, supervised two-layer). For Gaussian data with task-irrelevant variance λ⊥ ≪ 1, the total drift rate scales as D_y ≈ η³λ⊥²/8 · (m−1)(n−m) for Oja and SM (Eq. 11), and D_h ≈ η³λ⊥²/32 · (p−1)(n−p) for the autoencoder (Eq. 12), confirming that drift increases quadratically with task-irrelevant variance and linearly with its dimension. On MNIST (projected to n=20 PCs), the drift rate as a function of output/bottleneck dimension shows a characteristic non-monotonic curve: it rises to a maximum and then decreases to zero at m=n, matching theoretical predictions. Theoretical drift rates match simulation measurements across all tested learning rates, variances, and dimensions (Figure 3).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between drift induced by intrinsic learning noise (from task-irrelevant stimuli) and drift induced by additive Gaussian synaptic noise. Learning-induced drift is anisotropic (direction-dependent, as seen in Eq. 7 where drift rate differs along different principal axes), while synaptic-induced drift is isotropic with a uniform pairwise coefficient D_sr = ησ_syn²/4. The dimension-dependency also differs qualitatively: learning noise produces a non-monotonic drift-vs-output-dimension curve (peaking then vanishing at m=n), whereas synaptic noise yields a monotonically increasing function of output dimension (Figure 6b). Synaptic noise dominates the total drift only when its strength exceeds σ_syn* ~ ηλ⊥√(n−m); below this threshold, the drift plateaus at a level set entirely by task-irrelevant data statistics. Across all four architectures studied (Oja, Similarity Matching, Autoencoder, supervised two-layer), the qualitative dependence on task-irrelevant variance and dimension is consistent, though the exact spectral dependence differs per architecture.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the SDE-based framework for post-convergence drift developed by Li et al. [24] and Pashakhanloo & Koulakov [25] (who studied SGD-induced drift in a two-layer autoencoder) to a broader set of architectures and learning rules, including Hebbian-based Oja's rule and Similarity Matching networks. The key conceptual departure is identifying task-irrelevant stimuli—data the network has learned to suppress—as a specific, data-dependent source of learning noise that drives tangential diffusion on the solution manifold. Prior work on representational drift in computational models [9–16] treated noise sources more generically; this paper isolates the contribution of the task-irrelevant subspace and shows it produces qualitatively different drift geometry (anisotropic) compared to additive Gaussian synaptic noise (isotropic).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides testable, architecture-specific predictions (drift rate scaling with task-irrelevant variance and dimension, anisotropic vs. isotropic geometry, non-monotonic dimension dependence) that could help reverse-engineer the dominant noise source in biological systems, where drift has been observed but its origin remains debated. Limitations include the restriction of theoretical derivations to linear networks and small learning rates, and the nonlinear result is demonstrated in a single ring-position task rather than systematically. Open questions include extending the theory to nonlinear activations, studying cases where task-irrelevant stimuli appear in separate contexts, and quantifying the interplay when multiple noise sources (synaptic, sampling, intrinsic) coexist in biological circuits.
