## Problem

RL agents trained in conventional benchmarks rely on externally defined, task-specific reward functions that are brittle in dynamic, non-stationary real-world settings. No existing environment systematically places internal physiological state regulation (homeostasis) at the core of agent behavior, leaving a gap in studying how autonomous, internally driven goals emerge without handcrafted supervision. EVAAA addresses this by providing a biologically inspired 3D benchmark where a single unified reward grounded in internal state dynamics replaces all task-specific reward engineering.

## Input / Output

The agent consumes a multimodal egocentric observation vector at each timestep: a 64×64 monocular RGB frame, a 10-dimensional distance-weighted olfactory vector aggregated from nearby resources, a 3×3 thermal sensor grid sampling an invisible 100×100 temperature field, a 10-dimensional collision raycast vector (100 rays over 360°), and four scalar essential variables (satiation, hydration, temperature, damage) that passively decay between steps. The agent outputs one of five discrete actions (none, eat, move forward, turn left, turn right), and performance is measured as survival steps per episode in training and binary task success in testbeds, with the reward at each step being the negative normalized Euclidean distance of all four EVs from their homeostatic setpoints.

## Method

EVAAA is a Unity ML-Agents simulation where the reward is a single function of internal state dynamics—r_t = −√Σ((EV_i − μ_i)/σ_i)²—rather than any task-specific signal, so goals emerge autonomously from the agent's physiological needs. The environment is organized into a two-tiered architecture: a four-level naturalistic training curriculum (basic foraging → obstacle mapping → terrain exploration → dynamic predator threats with day/night cycles) that progressively increases complexity, and a suite of unseen controlled testbeds (two-resource choice, collision avoidance, risk-taking, Y-maze, goal manipulation, multi-goal planning, predator avoidance) that isolate specific decision-making processes for zero- and few-shot generalization evaluation. Four essential variables evolve via linear decay plus discrete event-driven changes from resource interaction, and an episode terminates if any EV exits its viable range. The empirical study benchmarks three representative algorithms—DQN, PPO, and DreamerV3—both with and without curriculum learning, and contextualizes agent performance against human gameplay data from eight participants.

## Results

In the naturalistic training environment, DreamerV3 consistently achieves the highest survival steps across all four levels, while PPO and DQN demonstrate successful survival only in Level 1-1 and fail to scale to more complex levels. DQN converges faster than DreamerV3 in Level 1-1 but fails to generalize beyond Level 1-2. In the unseen experimental testbeds, human participants (n=8) achieve near-ceiling success rates across all tasks, substantially outperforming all RL agents. Among RL algorithms, DreamerV3 performs best on test tasks, but increasing training difficulty does not yield monotonic gains in generalization—tasks like two-resource choice and collision avoidance show no consistent improvement with higher training complexity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks three RL algorithms—DreamerV3 (model-based), DQN (value-based), and PPO (policy-based)—on both the naturalistic training curriculum and unseen testbeds. DreamerV3 outperforms both DQN and PPO on survival steps across all training levels, benefiting from its world-model planning and memory under partial observability. DQN converges faster than DreamerV3 in the simplest level (Level 1-1) but collapses entirely beyond Level 1-2, indicating poor generalization. PPO similarly fails to scale past Level 1-1. On the experimental testbeds, DreamerV3 achieves the highest success rates among RL agents but still falls well short of human performance, which reaches near-ceiling levels on all tasks. Curriculum training (progressive levels) outperforms direct training on complex tasks for all three algorithms, though the gap is most pronounced for DreamerV3.

## Relation to Prior Work

EVAAA extends the homeostatic RL line initiated by Keramati & Gutkin (who defined reward as reduction in deviation from a set point) and Yoshida (who implemented embodied survival agents in 3D) into a comprehensive, multi-level benchmark. It departs from existing RL environments (MuJoCo, DeepMind Lab, ThreeDWorld) that rely on handcrafted task-specific rewards, and from survival benchmarks (Avalon, Crafter, MineRL) that either abstract physiology into a single scalar or lack fully embodied multimodal sensing. The key conceptual move is unifying four essential variables (satiation, hydration, temperature, damage) as both reward sources and modulatory signals within a single egocentric, multimodal 3D agent, rather than treating internal states as auxiliary features.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

EVAAA fills a concrete gap: no prior benchmark systematically evaluates how RL agents generalize homeostatic regulation from a progressive survival curriculum to novel, controlled cognitive tasks (risk-taking, multi-goal planning, goal manipulation under volatile internal states). The finding that increasing training difficulty does not monotonically improve generalization—and that agents trained on easy levels can adopt sub-optimal self-terminating policies that transfer to harder tasks—raises important open questions about curriculum design and the conditions under which internally driven control becomes robust. Limitations include fixed (non-adaptive) set points, a simple action space, and the absence of higher-order cognitive tasks (planning, inference, social reasoning), leaving the full scope of biologically inspired autonomy unexplored.
