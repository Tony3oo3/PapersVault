## Problem

LLM-as-a-judge evaluation is vulnerable to adversarial attacks that inflate scores, but existing methods modify responses post hoc (appending or substituting text), making them detectable via perplexity analysis or human inspection. This paper asks whether one can instead adversarially tune upstream textual preambles that steer a frozen candidate-LLM's generation toward higher judge scores, producing responses that are virtually indistinguishable from unattacked outputs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253388/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The preamble generator receives a fixed instruction prompt concatenated with a question and outputs a textual preamble (capped at 512 tokens). This preamble is prepended to the question and fed to a frozen candidate-LLM, which produces the final response evaluated by a frozen judge-LLM on a 1–10 scale. Notably, the preamble generator's training reward is computed solely on the candidate-LLM's response and the judge's score; the generator never directly observes its own preamble in the reward signal, making the optimisation indirect.

## Method

The paper introduces RLRE (Reinforcement Learning for Reverse Engineering), which pipelines a trainable preamble generator with a frozen candidate-LLM and a frozen judge-LLM. Training uses an online adaptation of Contrastive Policy Gradient (CoPG): for each question, two preambles are sampled, each prepended to the question to elicit a candidate response, and the judge-LLM scores both responses. The loss is the squared difference between the two rewards, regularised by a KL-divergence term against a reference model with a deliberately low coefficient (β=0.03) that prioritises reward over preamble fluency. Because the candidate-LLM remains frozen, its output retains its original style and perplexity profile, rendering the attack undetectable by existing PPL-based filters or human inspection. The approach is the first to apply CoPG in an online (rather than offline) setting and demonstrates transferability across unseen candidate-LLMs, judge-LLMs, and benchmarks.

## Results

On MT-Bench (1–10 scale, Command R+ judge), the adversarial preamble generator consistently outperforms all baselines across three candidate-LLMs. For Command R7B, preambles raise the overall score from 7.29 (no attack) to 7.93, beating the strongest baseline (refinement-aware bias, 7.61) by 0.32 points. For Command R, the overall score increases from 7.83 to 8.18 (+0.35 over no attack), and for Llama 3.1 70B Instruct from 8.06 to 8.22 (+0.16). On Arena-Hard, preamble injection yields +3.5 points with the Command R+ judge and +1.2 with GPT-4.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two families of prior attacks: (1) four bias-exploiting attacks from Ye et al. [38] (verbosity, bandwagon, authority, refinement-aware) and (2) the universal adversarial phrase from Raina et al. [32]. The preamble-based method wins on every candidate-LLM and every turn. Against the strongest Ye et al. baseline (refinement-aware bias), preambles improve Command R7B's overall score by +0.32 (7.93 vs. 7.61), Command R by +0.23 (8.18 vs. 7.95), and Llama 3.1 70B by +0.09 (8.22 vs. 8.13). Against Raina et al.'s universal attack, the gains are +0.52, +0.26, and +0.05 respectively. Notably, the preamble attack also transfers: preambles trained with one candidate-LLM still outperform all baselines when pipelined with a different candidate (Table 3a), and preambles trained with Command R+ as judge still boost scores under GPT-3.5, GPT-4o-mini, and Claude Haiku judges (Table 3b). Crucially, unlike the universal attack (FNR = 0.04, i.e. almost always detected via perplexity), the preamble attack is virtually undetectable (FNR = 0.90) and is flagged by human annotators at a rate (14%) comparable to the non-attacked model (12%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on Contrastive Policy Gradient (CoPG) [Flet-Berliac et al., EMNLP 2024] as its RL optimisation backbone, extending CoPG from the offline setting to online learning for the first time. It departs from prior adversarial attacks on LLM-as-a-judge (Raina et al. 2024, Shi et al. 2024, Ye et al. 2025, Zheng et al. 2025), which all intervene post hoc on the candidate response by appending or substituting text. The key conceptual shift is attacking the input side: rather than modifying the output, the paper trains a small preamble generator whose outputs are injected upstream into a frozen candidate-LLM, shaping the response indirectly and leaving the generated text itself unaltered.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that this upstream-preamble attack is virtually undetectable by both perplexity-based filters (FNR ≈ 0.90) and human inspection (14% vs. 12% for non-attacked) raises serious concerns about the reliability of LLM-as-a-judge, which underpins many widely used benchmarks. The observation that non-fluent, even character-string preambles (Llama pipeline) raise scores as effectively as natural-language ones challenges the assumption that conditioning tokens must reside on the natural-language manifold. Open questions include whether jury-of-judges evaluation or other robustness mechanisms can mitigate this class of attack, and whether RLRE generalises to other reward signals (e.g., toxicity reduction, bias mitigation) beyond adversarial score inflation.
