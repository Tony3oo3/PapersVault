## Problem

Existing structural causal bandits identify optimal intervention sets (POMISs) under the assumption of stationary reward distributions, but real-world sequential decision-making environments exhibit evolving causal mechanisms that shift reward distributions over time. Prior non-stationary bandit approaches model these shifts purely statistically, ignoring the underlying causal structure, while applying stationary POMIS to non-stationary settings yields myopic strategies that fail to account for how interventions at earlier time steps propagate to influence future rewards. This paper is the first to study non-stationary multi-armed bandits through a structural causal model lens, addressing the gap of identifying temporally-aware, non-myopic intervention strategies.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input a rolled-out causal diagram G with identical topology across time slices, transition edges encoding information propagation between consecutive time steps, a collection of time-indexed variable sets V_t, time-indexed reward variables Y_t, and a time horizon T. It produces a set of valid intervention sequences, where each time step t is assigned a POMIS+ set — the union of the current-time POMIS (optimizing immediate reward Y_t) and additional variables W_t from the same time slice that serve as predetermined values for the next temporal model (optimizing future reward Y_{t+1}). The algorithm operates under a first-order time-slice Markov assumption, restricting information propagation to adjacent time slices only.

## Method

The paper introduces the NS-SCM-MAB framework, which models non-stationarity as information propagation across time via transition edges in a rolled-out causal graph, formalized through temporal models M_t that condition on predetermined values from prior time steps. The key theoretical contribution is Theorem 5.1 (Existence of Optimal Partial Assignment), which guarantees that a partial assignment to preceding variables can strictly improve expected reward at a future time step, motivating the definition of POMIS+ — an extension of POMIS that incorporates variables contributing to both immediate and long-term reward maximization. Graphically, POMIS+ is characterized by two novel constructs: IB+ (Interventional Border for subsequent time steps, computed over the expanded window G[V_t ∪ V_{t+1}]) and QIB (Qualified Interventional Border, ensuring the future-support variables do not block causal influence on the current reward). A recursive backward-search algorithm (Alg. 1) enumerates all valid POMIS+ intervention sequences by iteratively computing MUCT/IB pairs, propagating IB+ and QIB maps through time, and taking Cartesian products at the base case, with proven soundness and completeness (Theorem 7.1) and time complexity O(2^{2|V|} · T).

## Results

Across three non-stationary causal bandit tasks, POMIS+ substantially outperforms the myopic POMIS baseline in both cumulative regret (CR) and optimal arm selection probability (OAP) at step 100K. On Task 1, POMIS+ achieves CR of 1.9K ± 29 (Thompson Sampling) and 4.8K ± 43 (KL-UCB) with OAP of 98.5% and 97.0%, respectively, while the myopic POMIS baseline exceeds 34K/37K CR with near-zero OAP. On Task 2, POMIS+ reaches CR of 1.1K ± 32 (TS) and 2.8K ± 44 (KL-UCB) with OAP of 96.5% and 90.0%, versus 11.8K/14.1K CR for the baseline. On Task 3 (which violates the time-slice Markov assumption), the gap narrows but POMIS+ still leads with CR of 3.7K ± 80 (TS) vs. 5.1K ± 89 and OAP of 87.0% vs. 80.0%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the myopic POMIS strategy from Lee and Bareinboim (2018), which identifies possibly-optimal minimal intervention sets independently at each time step without considering temporal information propagation. POMIS+ decisively wins on Tasks 1 and 2: on Task 1, the myopic baseline's CR exceeds 34K (TS) and 37K (KL-UCB) with OAP near zero, while POMIS+ achieves CR below 5K and OAP above 97%. On Task 2, POMIS+ reduces CR by roughly 10× (1.1K vs. 11.8K for TS) and lifts OAP from near-zero to 96.5%. On Task 3, which intentionally violates the time-slice Markov assumption by introducing long-range dependencies via dynamic unobserved confounders, the advantage narrows: POMIS+ still outperforms the baseline on both CR (3.7K vs. 5.1K for TS) and OAP (87.0% vs. 80.0% for TS), but the gap is substantially smaller, suggesting that the first-order Markov structure is important for the full benefit of POMIS+.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the stationary Structural Causal Bandit (SCM-MAB) framework of Lee and Bareinboim [2018]—which uses do-calculus, MUCT, and Interventional Border to identify Possibly-Optimal Minimal Intervention Sets (POMISs)—to a non-stationary setting where reward distributions shift over time due to information propagation across temporal models. It departs from conventional non-stationary bandit approaches (bounded-change, recharging-payoff models) by grounding non-stationarity in explicit causal structure rather than statistical drift, and introduces POMIS+, IB+, and QIB as temporally-extended graphical constructs. The SCM formalism and do-calculus machinery from Pearl [2009] underpin the entire framework, with the key novelty being the temporal model abstraction that treats each time slice as a conditional SCM whose parameters are determined by prior interventions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first formulation of non-stationary multi-armed bandits through a causal lens, providing a principled, graph-based method for identifying non-myopic intervention sequences with theoretical guarantees (existence of optimal partial assignments, soundness and completeness of the enumeration algorithm). Key limitations include the requirement of full causal graph knowledge, the time-slice Markov assumption (first-order temporal dependencies only), and exponential time complexity O(2^(2|V|)·T) that restricts scalability. Open questions include extending to partially observed or uncertain temporal structures, exploiting modular/repeated causal patterns for efficiency, and validating the framework on larger, real-world sequential decision problems in healthcare or resource allocation.
