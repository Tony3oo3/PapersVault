## Problem

Traditional fairness methods impose constraints or regularization at the prediction level, treating fairness as an external correction rather than addressing bias encoded in data representations. When the target Y and sensitive attribute Z share overlapping subspaces, removing all Z-related information also discards valuable predictive signal, causing substantial utility loss. This paper asks how to decompose the representation space finely enough to selectively remove only the sensitive components while preserving target-relevant directions, and how prediction error and fairness gaps evolve as shared information is gradually reintroduced.

## Input / Output

The method takes a tabular feature vector X ∈ R^p, a sensitive attribute Z (binary in all experiments), and a target label Y ∈ R^K. It produces a sequence of fair projection matrices P^(m) for m = 0, …, ŝ, where m controls how many shared Y–Z directions are retained; the reduced representation Ξ^(m) = P^(m)X is then fed into any standard supervised model. The user selects the final m* via a validation criterion (≥95% of original accuracy while minimizing demographic parity), making the shared dimension s a discrete tuning parameter analogous to a regularization coefficient.

## Method

The core mechanism is a three-way subspace decomposition of R^p into (i) directions informative about Y but orthogonal to Z, (ii) directions shared by both Y and Z, and (iii) directions unrelated to either, all estimated via sufficient dimension reduction (SDR). The shared subspace is identified by solving a generalized eigenvalue problem M_Y Σ M_Z ν = λ Σ ν, whose leading eigenvectors span the intersection S_{Y|X} ∩ S_{Z|X}; the Y-only directions are obtained by applying SDR to (Y, Q_z X) where Q_z projects onto the Z-orthogonal complement. A sequence of partially fair projections P^(m) = B̃B̃^T + Φ_m Φ_m^T is then constructed, where m indexes how many shared directions are included (m=0 is fully Z-independent, m=s recovers the full Y central subspace). The paper provides theoretical guarantees that prediction error Δ(m) is non-increasing in m while distance covariance between the predictor and Z is zero at m=0, and uses influence functions to derive asymptotic normality of both the fair estimator and the resulting predictor, quantifying the bias-variance trade-off introduced by the projection.

## Results

On the Adult dataset, SFP achieves the highest accuracy (76.88%) among all fairness-aware methods while also attaining the lowest Demographic Parity gap (3.78%), outperforming the next-best accuracy method (AdvDebias, 76.49%) and the next-best DP method (FairMixup, 3.93%). On the Bank dataset, SFP dominates with 89.66% accuracy (vs. 72.51% for the runner-up RLACE) and the lowest DP gap (0.51). SFP is competitive on TPR gap but trails RLACE on MCDP (10.52 vs. 7.45 on Adult; 10.57 vs. 8.98 on Bank), indicating a slight trade-off on this distributional fairness metric.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SFP clearly outperforms all seven baselines on accuracy: on Adult it reaches 76.88% vs. 76.49% (AdvDebias) and 75.01% (DRAlign); on Bank it reaches 89.66% vs. 72.51% (RLACE), a 17-point margin. On Demographic Parity, SFP achieves the lowest gap on both datasets (3.78% on Adult, 0.51% on Bank), beating FairMixup (3.93%) and INLP (0.93%) respectively. On TPR gap, SFP is competitive but not the best: SUP edges it out slightly (4.37 vs. 4.43 on Adult; 2.51 vs. 2.33 on Bank). On MCDP, RLACE is the strongest baseline (7.45 on Adult, 8.98 on Bank), while SFP trails at 10.52 and 10.57 respectively. Overall, SFP's key advantage is preserving near-full predictive utility while simultaneously achieving the best demographic parity, whereas representation-erasure methods (INLP, RLACE, SUP) sacrifice 4–17 accuracy points to gain marginal MCDP improvements.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the sufficient projection debiasing framework of Shi et al. (2024, SUP), which removes all Z-related directions from representations, by introducing a finer three-way subspace decomposition: Y-relevant but Z-orthogonal, shared between Y and Z, and unrelated. The intersection subspace estimation leverages Luo's (2022) dimension reduction for interaction between two response variables, while the asymptotic analysis builds on Kim et al. (2020)'s post-dimension-reduction inference theory. Unlike in-processing fairness methods (AdvDebias, FairMixup, DRAlign) that impose constraints at the prediction level, or representation-level erasure methods (INLP, RLACE) that remove all sensitive information indiscriminately, this work provides a graduated, interpretable mechanism for controlling how much shared information is retained.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a model-agnostic, single-integer tuning parameter (m) that gives practitioners transparent control over the fairness-utility trade-off, with theoretical guarantees that prediction error is monotonically non-increasing and distance covariance is zero at m=0. The influence function analysis providing asymptotic normality for fair estimators is a useful addition for statistical inference in post-SDR settings. Key limitations include reliance on the linear SDR assumption (nonlinear extension is left as future work), evaluation on only two tabular datasets with linear downstream models, and the fact that the method requires access to the sensitive attribute Z during the projection estimation phase. Open questions include whether the approach extends to deep representation learning and whether the linear SDR assumption holds in high-dimensional, nonlinear real-world settings.
