## Problem

Existing LLM scaling laws either assume a single law generalizes across all model families (ignoring family-specific training differences) or require training multiple models per family to fit family-specific parameters, which is prohibitively expensive. The paper asks whether the correlation structure among benchmarks can be exploited to build a scaling law that is both family-aware and parameter-efficient, requiring only one observed model per family.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251318/cropped_page5_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes publicly available benchmark scores (12 benchmarks from Open LLM Leaderboard v1/v2) paired with model size (parameters) and training token counts for each model, plus a family identifier. It outputs predicted benchmark scores for hypothetical larger models, interpretable latent skill levels (e.g., Reasoning, Knowledge, Instruction Following), compute-optimal parameter/token allocations under a FLOPs budget, and downstream task predictions (HumanEval, EQ Bench, AgentBench). A key constraint is that only one model per family is needed at training time to estimate the family-specific efficiency intercept, while skill slopes are shared across all families.

## Method

Sloth models each benchmark's logit as a linear combination of d << J low-dimensional latent skills plus a bias, where the loading matrix Λ encodes inter-benchmark correlations (analogous to factor analysis). Each skill follows a translog production function of log(s) and log(t) with a family-specific intercept (absorbing data quality, post-training, etc.) but shared slope coefficients across families, plus a log(s)·log(t) interaction term. The link function σ_j is optionally learned per benchmark via a two-layer monotonic neural network (non-negative weights, sigmoid output). The model is fitted by minimizing Huber loss (δ=0.01) via Adam with decaying learning rate, and identifiability is established up to an orthogonal rotation of the skill space; Geomin oblique rotation is then applied to make the loading matrix interpretable. This factor structure reduces the parameter count from O(J·f) to O(d·f + J·d), making the model more parameter-efficient than family-specific baselines when the number of families is moderate.

## Results

On the Open LLM Leaderboard v1 (12 benchmarks), Sloth with d=4 latent skills achieves the lowest average MAE of 4.1 percentage points, outperforming the FLOPs (shared intercept) baseline (7.8), the FLOPs baseline (4.9), the Size and Tokens baseline (4.4), and the best PCA+FLOPs variant (6.0 at d=2). The best Sloth variant (d=3) achieves 4.2 average MAE, and even the simplest Sloth(d=1) reaches 5.6, beating all PCA+FLOPs variants. On the MAPE metric (Figure 10), Sloth(d=4) achieves 11.3% average MAPE on v1 benchmarks, again the best among all methods tested.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Sloth(d=4) achieves the best average MAE (4.1 pp) across all 12 Open LLM Leaderboard v1 benchmarks, beating the FLOPs (shared intercept) baseline from Owen [2024] by 3.7 pp (7.8→4.1) and the FLOPs baseline by 0.8 pp (4.9→4.1). Against the PCA+FLOPs baselines adapted from Ruan et al. [2024], Sloth(d=4) improves average MAE by 1.9 pp over the best PCA variant (d=2, 6.0 pp) and by 2.3 pp over PCA+FLOPs(d=4) (6.4 pp). Notably, Sloth(d=4) also outperforms the 'Size and Tokens' baseline (4.4 pp), which uses the same translog input structure but without the latent skill factorization (i.e., d=J), confirming that the low-dimensional skill structure provides a beneficial inductive bias. On individual benchmarks, Sloth(d=4) achieves the lowest MAE on MMLU (5.3), ARC (2.7), HellaSwag (3.6), Winogrande (2.6), and GSM8K (5.9), while the FLOPs baseline retains a slight edge on TruthfulQA (9.0 vs. 4.2 for Sloth(d=4) — actually Sloth wins here too). The gains are most pronounced on GSM8K (12.4→5.9) and MMLU (9.4→5.3) for the shared-intercept FLOPs baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two research threads: (1) observational scaling laws for LLM benchmark performance (Owen 2024; Ruan et al. 2024), which model scores as functions of FLOPs but are either too restrictive (assuming FLOPs-only dependence, no family effects) or too flexible (family-specific slopes requiring many models per family); and (2) latent skill / factor analysis approaches for LLMs (Ilić 2023; Burnell et al. 2023; Kipnis et al. 2024), which identify low-dimensional capability structures but do not connect them to compute resources. Sloth unifies these by modeling benchmark scores as linear combinations of a small number of latent skills (via a factor-loading matrix Λ), where each skill follows a translog production function of log(parameters) and log(tokens) with family-specific intercepts but shared slopes. The trainable monotonic neural network link function draws on single-index model literature (Bietti et al. 2022), and the identifiability analysis mirrors standard exploratory factor analysis (Chen et al. 2019).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Sloth offers a practical tool for LLM practitioners: given one model from a new family, it can predict that family's performance on 12 benchmarks at larger scales without additional training, while simultaneously revealing which skills (reasoning, knowledge, instruction following) drive performance and how they respond differently to parameters vs. tokens. The compute-optimal allocation results (Table 2) are directly actionable for training decisions. Key limitations include the requirement of at least one model per family, the lack of identifiability guarantees for the full model with trainable link functions, and the risk that the flexible neural network σ_j interpolates well but extrapolates poorly for out-of-distribution model sizes. Open questions include whether the three-skill structure is stable across future model generations and whether the framework extends to multimodal or agentic benchmarks.
