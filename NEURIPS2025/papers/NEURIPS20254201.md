## Problem

Traditional score-only reward models for multi-modal RLHF suffer from low accuracy, weak out-of-distribution generalization, and poor interpretability, creating a bottleneck for aligning MLLMs with human preferences. While generative reward models (GRMs) can leverage MLLMs' reasoning to discriminate pair-wise responses, their pair-wise paradigm does not readily translate into the point-wise scores required for RL optimization. This paper addresses how to bridge the gap between pair-wise generative reasoning and learnable point-wise rewards in multi-modal alignment.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254201/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The GRM takes a text prompt, an associated image, and a pair of candidate responses as input, and outputs predicted preference principles, free-form reasoning traces, and two scalar scores (one per response) in a structured boxed format. For RL optimization, the system generates n candidate responses per prompt and the GRM performs all C(n,2) pair-wise comparisons within the group; each response's final reward is the average of its scores across both its position as first and second argument in every pair. Notably, the GRM produces fine-grained continuous scores rather than being constrained to integer values, and the reward signal for GRM training is binary (+1/−1) based solely on whether the preferred response receives the higher score.

## Method

The framework is a two-stage pipeline. Stage 1 trains a multi-modal GRM via RL (extending SPCT to vision), where the GRM autonomously reasons about the underlying human preference principles behind each response pair and outputs pair-wise scores; the RL reward is +1 if the preferred response scores higher, −1 otherwise. A key finding is that in the multi-modal setting, letting the GRM explore principles autonomously generalizes better than selecting from a predefined reference set. Stage 2 performs RL optimization (GRPO) using grouped comparison: for each prompt, n candidate responses are generated, the GRM scores all pair-wise combinations, and each response's final reward is the mean of its scores across all pairs (averaged over both argument positions). This grouped comparison converts the GRM's pair-wise outputs into more precise point-wise rewards, and the combination of GRM+RL with grouped comparison yields near-linear RL performance gains as n increases, a scaling property absent when either component is removed.

## Results

Generative RLHF-V (GRLHF-V) improves 4 MLLMs (Qwen2-VL-2B/7B, Qwen2.5-VL-3B/7B-Instruct) across 7 benchmarks by an average of 18.1%, compared to only 5.3% for baseline RLHF with score-only RMs. The RL-trained GRM achieves a 20.4% average accuracy improvement on out-of-distribution preference discrimination tasks. On the MLLM-as-a-Judge Score task, GRM+RL with grouped comparison reaches a Pearson correlation of 0.43 with human expert scores, approaching GPT-4o's 0.48. The framework also demonstrates near-linear performance gains as the number of candidate responses n increases in GRPO.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DPO with score-only RM [Rafailov et al., NeurIPS 2023], GRLHF-V consistently wins on all 7 benchmarks for every model tested; for Qwen2-VL-7B, gains range from +5.3 (MIA-Bench: 62.31 vs. 57.01) to +10.4 (MM-Safety: 91.96 vs. 81.59). Against the social-choice aggregation methods from [Conitzer et al., 2024] (Borda Count, Instant Runoff, Ranked Pairs) and Spectral Ranking [Vigna, 2016], grouped comparison outperforms all alternatives in the RL fine-tuning setting (Table 4b): on MIA-Bench, GRLHF-V with grouped comparison scores 62.31 versus 55.76 (Borda), 57.89 (Instant Runoff), 59.94 (Ranked Pairs), and 57.63 (Spectral Ranking); the authors attribute this to grouped comparison providing finer-grained scoring signals suitable for RL, whereas ranking-based methods only yield coarse ordinal rewards. In the batch-ranking evaluation (Table 4a), Spectral Ranking actually achieves the lowest edit distance (0.5301) among all methods, slightly beating grouped comparison (0.4451), but this advantage does not transfer to the RL optimization loop. The paper also compares against score-only RMs trained with Bradley-Terry loss and GRMs trained via SFT; GRLHF-V outperforms both, with the gap being especially large for pretrained (non-instruct) models where score-only RMs fail to discriminate out-of-distribution responses.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Self-Principled Critique Tuning (SPCT) [Liu et al., 2025] from text-only LLMs to the multi-modal vision-language setting, training a generative reward model (GRM) via RL to reason about human preference principles and output pair-wise scores. It departs from SPCT in a key finding: in the multi-modal scenario, allowing the GRM to autonomously explore principles (rather than selecting from a predefined reference set) yields superior out-of-distribution generalization. The grouped comparison mechanism for converting pair-wise GRM scores into point-wise RL rewards is a novel bridge that prior GRM work (e.g., LLaVA-Critic, generative verifiers) had not addressed, as those methods were limited to Best-of-N selection or offline DPO rather than online RL optimization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 18.1% average improvement over baseline RLHF (5.3%) across 7 benchmarks and 4 MLLM sizes demonstrates that RL-trained GRMs with grouped comparison are a practically viable path to multi-modal alignment, and the near-linear scaling with candidate response count opens a new post-training scaling axis. However, the computational overhead of GRM reasoning plus O(n²) pairwise comparisons per group is significant, and the reward hacking case study (self-praise behavior exploited by MLLM-as-judge evaluators) highlights a critical vulnerability that limits the method's robustness under overtraining. Open questions include whether the grouped comparison can be further improved via structured social-choice aggregation, how to mitigate the identified reward hacking, and whether the autonomous-principle-exploration finding generalizes beyond the Qwen-VL and LLaVA architectures tested.
