## Problem

Prior video similarity work either computes a single uninterpretable global score from spatio-temporal embeddings or generates free-form natural language descriptions of differences, but neither captures how humans actually compare videos—by selectively attending to specific semantic aspects like action, location, or subjects. This paper identifies the missing middle ground: structured, quantitative, per-concept similarity scores that are both interpretable and composable, and provides a benchmark to measure how well current models align with human judgments on such concept-conditioned comparisons.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255168/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The task takes a pair of trimmed video clips (averaging 28.2 s, sourced from FineVideo) and a natural-language concept prompt (e.g., 'main action', 'location') as input, and produces a single similarity score on a 1-to-5 Likert scale for that concept. For most LMMs the videos are supplied as concatenated frame sequences (16 frames per video by default); Gemini receives full video. In the retrieval variant, an anchor video is scored against a fixed set of four candidate videos per concept, yielding a ranked list. The concept set is open-ended—users can introduce arbitrary semantic dimensions—and individual per-concept scores can be aggregated into a weighted global score via non-negative weights summing to one.

## Method

ConViS formalizes video similarity as s(V₁, V₂ | Cᵢ) ∈ ℝ, conditioning the comparison on a natural-language concept Cᵢ so that only details relevant to that concept are attended to. ConViS-Bench comprises 610 video pairs from 543 videos spanning 16 domains, each annotated by an average of 6.2 human raters (147 total, recruited via Prolific) with per-concept 1–5 Likert scores across five concepts (main action, main subjects, main objects, location, order of actions) plus free-form similarity and difference tags. The evaluation benchmarks ten state-of-the-art LMMs (LLaVA-OV, Qwen2.5-VL, InternVL, mPLUG-Owl3, Gemini-2.0-Flash) using Spearman's ρ and Kendall's τ correlations with human judgments, and additionally probes global representation methods (DINOv2, VideoMAE, CLIPScore, VQAScore, Sentence-BERT captioning) to reveal which concepts they implicitly prioritize. A concept-conditioned retrieval experiment (R@1, P@1, F1@1 over 532 partial orderings) tests whether LMMs can rank candidate videos by concept-specific similarity, and a frame-ablation study quantifies how much temporal context each model actually relies on.

## Results

On ConViS-Bench, LLaVA-OV-7B achieves the highest overall alignment with human judgment, reaching 58.94/47.72 (Spearman ρ / Kendall τ) on Location and 58.64/48.17 on Main Objects. The Order of Actions concept is consistently the most challenging across all models, with even the best performer (LLaVA-Video-7B) reaching only 41.25/34.05. Among global representation methods (Table 3), DINOv2 leads on Location (57.4/41.7), while VQAScore (LLaVA-OV) attains the highest average correlation overall. In concept-conditioned retrieval (Table 4), LLaVA-OV-7B achieves the best F1@1 of 69.3 on Location, well above the random baseline of 28.4.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LLaVA-OV-7B outperforms all other LMMs on four of five concepts, with particularly strong margins on Main Objects (+17.0 ρ over Gemini-2.0-Flash) and Location (+16.8 ρ over Gemini). Gemini-2.0-Flash is the strongest closed-source model but trails LLaVA-OV-7B by 6–20 ρ points across concepts. Surprisingly, InternVL models (which were pre-trained on FineVideo, the source of ConViS-Bench videos) perform poorly, with InternVL2.5-4B scoring below 15 ρ on most concepts, indicating that exposure to the data does not confer concept-level similarity judgment ability. Smaller models (LLaVA-OV-0.5B, Qwen2.5-VL-3B) show near-zero or negative correlations, confirming a clear scaling trend. For global representations (Table 3), DINOv2 dominates on Location (57.4 ρ) while text-to-text methods (mPLUG-Owl3 + SBERT) lead on Main Action (52.1 ρ) and Order of Actions (49.9 ρ), and VQAScore (LLaVA-OV) achieves the best average across all concepts, suggesting that cross-modal alignment via VQA is the most balanced global strategy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from two established lines of video pair comparison: (1) global similarity metrics that compute a single uninterpretable score from spatio-temporal embeddings (VideoMAE, DINOv2, self-supervised video similarity), and (2) natural-language video differencing that generates free-form text descriptions of differences within narrow domains (VidDiffBench for action skills, StepDiff for cooking). ConViS carves new ground by introducing a structured, per-concept quantitative formulation—computing a 1-to-5 similarity score conditioned on a specific semantic concept (action, subjects, objects, location, order of actions)—bridging the gap between uninterpretable global scores and unstructured text. The concept set is grounded in cognitive science findings about how humans encode events along semantic and temporal features, and the evaluation leverages LMMs as prompted judges rather than training new models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ConViS-Bench is the first benchmark to provide per-concept human-annotated similarity scores across 16 diverse video domains, enabling diagnosis of *which* semantic dimensions a model fails on rather than just *whether* it fails. The key open question it surfaces is why all current LMMs struggle most with temporal structure (order of actions) and spatial context (location), and whether pre-training on the exact source videos (as with InternVL/FineVideo) provides any benefit—it does not, suggesting memorization is insufficient for concept-conditioned reasoning. Limitations include the small scale (610 pairs), only five concepts, and absence of statistical significance testing, leaving open whether findings generalize to larger, more diverse collections or to domain-specific concept taxonomies.
