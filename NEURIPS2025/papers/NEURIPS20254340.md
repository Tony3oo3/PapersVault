## Problem

Convolutional filters learned end-to-end in deep networks lack the normalization that classical filters (Gaussian blur, Difference-of-Gaussian) rely on for consistency and interpretability. This unnormalized state causes filter responses to distort under atmospheric transfer functions—affine gain-bias changes in illumination—leading to severe accuracy drops (up to 97%) on corrupted inputs. No existing mechanism enforces atmosphere-equivariance at the filter level, and standard remedies like data augmentation or instance normalization either require anticipating domain shifts or operate on activations rather than the filters themselves.

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10, 224×224 for ImageNet) or 5-channel multi-spectral patches (32×32×5 for LEGUS astronomy data) and produces class labels via standard classification heads. The key operational detail is that the normalization is applied to filter weights at the convolutional layer level, making it data-agnostic: the same normalized filter produces structurally consistent responses regardless of the input's global gain or bias. At test time, inputs may carry arbitrary atmospheric corruptions (constant, linear, or blob-shaped gain-bias fields) without requiring any modification to the inference pipeline.

## Method

The core contribution is filter normalization: each convolutional filter's weights are decomposed into positive (w⁺) and negative (w⁻) parts, each independently normalized to unit L1 norm, followed by learnable scaling (a) and shifting (b) parameters analogous to batch normalization. This decomposition is grounded in the theoretical result that any linear filter is a weighted sum of a normalized differencing filter and a normalized averaging filter (Eq. 9), with the positive weight ratio r(w) controlling the blend. Normalized averaging filters become equivariant to both gain and offset, while normalized differencing filters become invariant to offset and equivariant to gain, yielding atmosphere-equivariance and co-domain symmetry at the convolutional layer. Unlike instance or batch normalization, which adjust activations, this method corrects the filter's response to atmospheric corruptions directly at the weight level, allowing standard batch normalization to still handle covariate shift during training. The approach adds only 0.08% parameters and 0.06 ms inference overhead to a ResNet-34, and is applicable to any convolution-based architecture including ViTs with convolutional components.

## Results

On ImageNet-1k, R34N (22M params) achieves 73.2% top-1 accuracy on the clean set (D), matching vanilla R34 (73.3%), while maintaining 72.8% on constant corruption (D_C), 71.7% on linear (D_L), 68.1% on blob (D_B), and 67.0% on strong-shift (D_S) — a maximum drop of only 5.2%. In stark contrast, vanilla R34 collapses to 2.1% on D_S, and even CLIP-L/14 (428M params, trained on LAION-1B) drops to 49.1%. On CIFAR-10, R20N achieves 91.5% on D and 89.5% on D_S, versus 38.1% for vanilla R20 on D_S. The method adds only 0.08% parameters and 0.06 ms inference overhead per image.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against vanilla ResNets, R34N matches clean accuracy (73.2% vs 73.3%) but dominates on all corrupted sets — e.g., 67.0% vs 2.1% on D_S and 68.1% vs 36.8% on D_B. Against ViTs, R34N (22M) outperforms ViT-L (304M) on D_S (67.0% vs 1.1%) and D_B (68.1% vs 41.8%), while ViT-L only leads on clean D (79.3% vs 73.2%). Against CLIP, R34N surpasses CLIP-L/14 (428M) by 17.9 points on D_S (67.0% vs 49.1%) and CLIP-B/32 (151M) by 52.7 points on D_S (67.0% vs 14.3%), despite being trained on far less data. Against norm-equivariant networks [15], R20N outperforms R20E on every corrupted CIFAR-10 benchmark (e.g., 89.5% vs 32.3% on D_S) while also matching clean accuracy (91.5% vs 88.6%). Against instance normalization, R44N achieves 90.2% on D_S versus 85.4% for R44+IN, while maintaining 93.2% on D (vs 90.5% for R44+IN). Against data augmentation, R20N without any augmentation (89.5% on D_S) outperforms R20 with gain-bias augmentation (76.9% on D_S), showing that architectural normalization is more effective than distribution-matching augmentations under large distribution shifts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper draws a direct line from classical image processing (normalized Gaussian, DoG, and derivative filters) to deep learning, arguing that the normalization discipline that makes classical filters interpretable and artifact-free has been lost in end-to-end learned convolutions. It departs from prior equivariant-network work (e.g., Herbreteau et al. [15], which replaces convolutions with affine-constrained layers and sort-pooling activations) by instead normalizing the filter weights themselves and adding learnable scale/shift, a data-agnostic modification that composes with standard BatchNorm. It also distinguishes itself from activation-level normalization (IN, BN, GN) by operating on the kernel rather than the feature map, and from data-augmentation strategies by providing inherent robustness without anticipating domain shifts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The technique is a near-zero-cost drop-in (0.08% extra parameters, 0.06 ms inference overhead) applicable to any convolutional architecture, making it immediately adoptable for robustness-critical deployments in autonomous driving, medical imaging, and remote sensing. A key open question is whether the gain-bias equivariance generalizes to more complex atmospheric or sensor degradations (the paper's ImageNet-C results are mixed, outperforming on brightness but underperforming on snow). The finding that filter normalization promotes feature diversity and regularizes training suggests broader benefits beyond atmospheric robustness, but this remains underexplored. Whether the approach composes cleanly with other robustness techniques (e.g., adversarial training, test-time augmentation) is also unaddressed.
