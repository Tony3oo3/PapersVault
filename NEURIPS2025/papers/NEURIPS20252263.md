## Problem

LoRA fine-tuning of LLMs in few-shot regimes produces overconfident predictions, and existing uncertainty-aware LoRA methods (BLoB, Laplace-LoRA) model only epistemic (parameter) uncertainty while ignoring aleatoric (input-dependent) uncertainty. This neglect of how individual input characteristics affect predictive confidence leads to poorly calibrated uncertainty estimates, particularly when training data is scarce.

## Input / Output

The method consumes text questions with multiple-choice or binary answer options (e.g., Winogrande, ARC, OBQA, BoolQ) and frozen LLaMA2-7B weights, applying LoRA adapters (rank r=8) to query, value, and output projection layers. Beyond the predicted class label, it outputs a sample-specific calibrated probability distribution obtained by Monte Carlo sampling from an input-conditioned posterior over a small r×r matrix, yielding per-sample uncertainty estimates quantified via ECE and NLL. The contextual modules operate in the low-dimensional r-dimensional space (r=8), so the additional per-layer overhead is O(r⁴) compared to O(d²) for the main model.

## Method

C-LoRA inserts a small r×r matrix E between the standard LoRA factors B and A (h = (W₀ + BEA)x), reducing the stochastic parameter count to O(r²) independent of the frozen weight dimension d. Each layer's E is made input-dependent: a lightweight two-layer MLP (the contextual module) takes the low-dimensional representation z^l = A^l x^{l-1} as input and outputs the mean and variance of a Gaussian posterior q(E_x^l | x^{l-1}), enabling per-sample heteroscedastic uncertainty. Training uses an amortized VI objective (ELBO) where B and A are updated via the expected NLL term alone (supervised, no KL), while the contextual module parameters are updated via the reparameterization trick with both NLL and per-sample KL terms. At inference, uncertainty is estimated by drawing M samples of E_x from the input-conditioned posterior and averaging predictions, or by using the posterior mean directly (M=0) for a deterministic but still calibrated estimate.

## Results

On six commonsense reasoning benchmarks with LLaMA2-7B, C-LoRA (M=10) achieves the lowest or second-lowest ECE across all datasets (e.g., 6.86% on WG-S, 8.83% on ARC-C, 3.71% on WG-M, 1.62% on BoolQ) and among the lowest NLL values (e.g., 0.63 on WG-S, 0.88 on ARC-C, 0.48 on ARC-E). Accuracy remains competitive, typically within 1–2% of the best methods (e.g., 69.02% on ARC-C, 81.13% on OBQA for C-LoRA M=0). Under distribution shift (fine-tuned on OBQA, tested on Chem/Phy), C-LoRA (M=10) achieves the best ECE (12.49% on Chem, 18.16% on Phy) and NLL (1.31 on Chem, 1.42 on Phy), outperforming all baselines including BLoB and Deep Ensemble.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against BLoB (Wang et al., NeurIPS 2024), the closest Bayesian LoRA baseline, C-LoRA (M=10) reduces ECE by 2–5 points on most datasets (e.g., 6.86% vs. 11.23% on WG-S, 8.83% vs. 10.77% on ARC-C, 3.71% vs. 4.52% on WG-M) while matching or slightly exceeding NLL (0.88 vs. 0.88 on ARC-C). Against Deep Ensemble (Lakshminarayanan et al., NeurIPS 2017), C-LoRA achieves substantially lower ECE (e.g., 6.86% vs. 28.72% on WG-S, 4.27% vs. 11.87% on ARC-E) and lower NLL (0.63 vs. 2.44 on WG-S), though Deep Ensemble retains a 1–3% accuracy advantage. Against Monte-Carlo Dropout (Gal & Ghahramani, ICML 2016), C-LoRA dominates on both ECE and NLL across all six datasets, with ECE improvements of 15–22 points. Against Laplace-LoRA (Yang et al., ICLR 2024), C-LoRA achieves far more consistent calibration (ECE 3.71–8.83% vs. 10.80–45.85%) and lower NLL on 5 of 6 datasets, while Laplace-LoRA occasionally matches on NLL for individual tasks (e.g., 0.62 on WG-S). Overall, C-LoRA trades 1–3% accuracy for 2–20 point ECE reductions, making it the strongest uncertainty-aware PEFT method in few-shot regimes.

## Relation to Prior Work

C-LoRA extends the Bayesian LoRA line of work initiated by BLoB (Wang et al., NeurIPS 2024), which applies mean-field variational inference to LoRA adapters but treats the variational posterior as input-independent, capturing only epistemic uncertainty. The key departure is making the posterior data-dependent through lightweight contextual modules (small MLPs) that produce per-sample mean and variance for a low-rank intermediate matrix E, thereby modeling aleatoric uncertainty. The architectural choice of inserting E between B and A (a lightweight factorization) reduces the stochastic parameter count to O(r²) independent of the frozen weight dimension d, and the feature-reuse strategy for the contextual module follows the contextual dropout framework of Fan et al. (ICLR 2021).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

C-LoRA addresses a practical gap in few-shot LLM fine-tuning where models become overconfident and miscalibrated, offering sample-level uncertainty estimates at minimal computational overhead (O(r⁴) per layer vs. O(d²) for the main model). The substantial ECE reductions (often 2–3× lower than BLoB and Deep Ensemble) and the clear ablation showing the contextual module is critical make this a useful tool for safety-critical deployments. However, experiments are limited to LLaMA2-7B and six commonsense-reasoning benchmarks, scaling to larger models remains untested, and the Gaussian variational family is inherently mode-seeking, potentially underestimating posterior uncertainty. Open questions include whether the aleatoric/epistemic decomposition holds theoretically, and how the method behaves in multi-modal or multi-task settings.
