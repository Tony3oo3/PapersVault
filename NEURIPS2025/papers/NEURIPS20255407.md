## Problem

Betting-based confidence interval methods achieve state-of-the-art results in the time-uniform (anytime) setting but are either sub-optimal or rely on heuristics without finite-time guarantees in the fixed-horizon regime. No betting-based algorithm was known to guarantee the optimal O(√(σ² log(1/δ)/n)) confidence interval width, leaving a gap between what is theoretically achievable and what existing methods deliver for small-to-moderate sample sizes.

## Input / Output

The method consumes n i.i.d. samples from a bounded random variable on [0,1] along with a confidence level δ (and an auxiliary parameter α controlling the probability of the optimality guarantee). It produces a two-sided (1−δ) confidence interval [l_n, u_n] for the mean by discretizing [0,1] into grid points and running a sequential betting test at each candidate mean m, rejecting m if the accumulated wealth reaches 1/δ. The interval is the set of non-rejected candidates, and the algorithm naturally yields a confidence sequence as a by-product when samples arrive sequentially.

## Method

The core contribution is the STaR (Sequential Target-Recalculating) betting principle: at each round t, the bet size is computed from the current wealth W_t and remaining rounds n−t rather than being fixed in advance, specifically as ℓ_t = √(2·log(1/(W_t·δ)) / ((n−t+1)·v_t)) where v_t is an online estimate of E[(X−m)²]. This is applied within a test-process framework where the log-wealth is approximated via a second-order Taylor expansion, and the optimal constant bet ℓ* = S/V is replaced by its target-recalculating version that only cares about reaching log(1/δ) total log-wealth. The second-moment estimator v_t = (Σ(X_i−m)²)/(t−1) + 10·log(8/α)·n/(t−1)² is constructed to have small relative error uniformly over all m and t via a time-uniform Bennett-type inequality. The resulting STaR-Bets algorithm (Algorithm 4) is proven to reject all m below the sample mean minus σ√((2+c)·log(1/δ)/n) with probability at least 1−α, matching the Bernstein-optimal rate up to a 1+o(1) factor, and strictly improves upon standard Hoeffding and Bernstein betting by never losing already-achieved wealth and by escalating bets aggressively in the final rounds.

## Results

The paper's main theoretical result (Theorem 8) proves that Bets (Algorithm 3) rejects every mean candidate m below the sample mean minus σ√((2+c)·log(1/δ)/n) with probability at least 1−α, establishing that the confidence interval width is optimal up to a 1+o(1) factor diminishing with n (with n₀ ∈ O(c⁻⁴)). Empirically, on Bernoulli and Beta distributions with n ranging from 8 to 256, STaR-Bets produces the shortest confidence intervals among all betting-based competitors, with coverage empirically indistinguishable from the nominal 1−δ level. The ★-technique strictly tightens Hoeffding and Bernstein intervals without any loss, and STaR-Bets matches the performance of the T-test (which lacks coverage guarantees) as n grows.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Hedged-CI [23] (the strongest prior betting-based method), STaR-Bets is consistently and significantly shorter across all sample sizes and distributions, with the gap most pronounced at small n (e.g., n=8–32) where Hedged-CI's additive variance-estimation penalty is largest. Against Clopper-Pearson [5], STaR-Bets produces shorter intervals in most settings, particularly at small sample sizes where the discrete CDF inversion of Clopper-Pearson is wasteful. Against Randomized Clopper-Pearson [22] (the optimal binomial CI), STaR-Bets is mildly worse in the very low-sample regime (n≈30) but becomes competitive or slightly better as n increases to 1000. Against Hoeffding [10] and Empirical Bernstein [12], the ★-technique provides a strict, provable improvement (Proposition 4, Corollary 5): the ★-version rejects a superset of hypotheses, yielding narrower intervals, with the gap visible in Figure 1 where vanilla Hoeffding/Bernstein lose previously-achieved wealth while the ★-versions stop betting upon reaching the target. Against Phan et al. [16] (state-of-the-art for n=10–50), STaR-Bets is consistently stronger and also scales to larger n where Phan's method degrades. The T-test produces slightly shorter intervals than STaR-Bets on some Beta distributions but with coverage well below the claimed 1−δ, violating the fundamental guarantee that STaR-Bets maintains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the betting-based confidence interval framework of Shafer et al. (2011) and Cover (1974) from the time-uniform setting to the fixed-horizon regime, where prior methods were either sub-optimal or relied on heuristics without finite-sample guarantees. The key conceptual departure is the STaR (Sequential Target-Recalculating) principle: instead of choosing a fixed betting strategy in advance (as in Hoeffding, Bernstein, or Hedged-CI of Waudby-Smith and Ramdas 2023), the algorithm recalculates its bet at each step based on the remaining rounds and the remaining wealth multiplier needed. This connects to the universal portfolio literature (Cover and Ordentlich) and the regret-based betting of Orabona and Jun (2024), but explicitly argues that minimizing regret against a fixed strategy is the wrong objective for finite-sample CIs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first betting-based algorithm with a proven optimal O(√(σ² log(1/δ)/n)) confidence interval width, closing a gap that had persisted since the time-uniform literature achieved state-of-the-art results. The STaR technique is a general, drop-in improvement that strictly tightens any existing betting-based CI (Hoeffding, Bernstein, Bennett), making it broadly applicable. However, the combined STaR-Bets algorithm (Algorithm 4) lacks a proven optimality guarantee—only the base Bets algorithm (Algorithm 3) has the theoretical result—and the authors explicitly note that quantifying how much STaR helps beyond 'never hurts' remains an open theoretical question. The practical impact is most relevant in settings where sampling is expensive and tight finite-sample intervals are needed.
