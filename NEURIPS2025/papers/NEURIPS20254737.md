## Problem

GP inference requires solving an n×n linear system whose cost scales quadratically, and existing large-scale methods force a trade-off: preconditioned conjugate gradient (PCG) handles ill-conditioning but becomes prohibitively expensive beyond n~10⁶, while stochastic dual descent (SDD) scales further but lacks convergence guarantees, is sensitive to stepsize tuning, and degrades under ill-conditioning. No prior method simultaneously addresses both the conditioning and scalability challenges of GP inference.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a feature matrix X ∈ ℝ^{n×d}, a target vector y ∈ ℝ^n, a kernel function k, and a likelihood variance λ > 0, and produces the solution W to the linear system (K + λI)W = Y where K = k(X,X), from which the GP posterior mean m_n(·) = k(·,X)W is computed. Posterior samples are generated via pathwise conditioning with random features (costing O(q|X_*|) per sample), avoiding the O(|X_*|³) matrix square root of standard sampling. All experiments run in single precision on NVIDIA A6000 GPUs, and the method scales to n > 3×10⁸ samples using 4 GPUs with blocksize b = 1.65×10⁵.

## Method

ADASAP extends the sketch-and-project (SAP) framework with four key ingredients: (i) subspace preconditioning that preconditions the subspace gradient by the inverse of the subspace Hessian (K_BB + λI), incorporating second-order information at O(bn + b³) per-iteration cost; (ii) approximate preconditioning via a rank-r randomized Nyström approximation of K_BB, reducing the per-iteration cost from O(b³) to O(br) and enabling much larger blocksizes; (iii) distributed matrix-matrix products (ColDistMatMat and RowDistMatMat) that partition kernel evaluations across multiple GPUs for near-linear speedup; and (iv) Nesterov acceleration with default parameters μ = λ and ν = n/b. The theoretical contribution uses determinantal point process (DPP) sampling theory to prove that tail-averaged SAP achieves a two-phase convergence along the top-ℓ spectral basis functions: a condition-number-free sublinear rate O(φ(b,ℓ)/t) in the early phase (where φ(b,ℓ) = O(1) under polynomial spectral decay), followed by an asymptotic linear rate (1 − 1/(2φ(b,n)))^{t/2}. This yields the first efficient, condition-number-free time complexity bound for estimating the GP posterior mean along dominant spectral directions.

## Results

On six large-scale GP regression benchmarks (n ranging from 3.6×10⁵ to 1.84×10⁶), ADASAP achieves the lowest test RMSE and mean NLL across all datasets. The advantage is most dramatic on houseelec (n=1.84×10⁶), where ADASAP attains RMSE 0.027 versus 0.268 for SDD-1, 0.119 for SDD-10, and 1.278 for PCG. On the NYC Taxi dataset with n=3.31×10⁸ samples—the first full GP inference at this scale—ADASAP reaches RMSE 0.50, matching SDD-10's 0.52 in 45% less wall-clock time (a 14-hour saving), while PCG runs out of memory and SDD-100 diverges. On a large-scale Bayesian optimization task, ADASAP yields 10.42% and 13.86% improvement over random search (lengthscales 2.0 and 3.0), compared to 0.00% for SDD-10/SDD-100 and 0.13%/5.54% for PCG.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SDD (Lin et al., ICLR 2024), ADASAP wins on all six benchmark datasets for both RMSE and NLL, with the gap widening as dataset size grows: on houseelec, ADASAP's RMSE of 0.027 is roughly 10× better than SDD-1 (0.268) and 4.4× better than SDD-10 (0.119). SDD is highly sensitive to its stepsize hyperparameter—SDD-100 diverges on every dataset, and SDD-10 diverges on malonaldehyde—whereas ADASAP uses automatic per-iteration stepsizes with no tuning. On the 3.31×10⁸-sample taxi dataset, ADASAP matches SDD-10's final RMSE (0.50 vs 0.52) while finishing 45% faster (saving ~14 hours). Against PCG (Wang et al., NeurIPS 2019, with Nyström preconditioning), ADASAP ties on the two smallest datasets (yolanda, song) but dramatically outperforms it on larger ones: on houseelec, ADASAP achieves RMSE 0.027 versus PCG's 1.278, and on the taxi dataset PCG simply runs out of memory (requiring ~130 GB for the sketch). On the Bayesian optimization task, ADASAP's 10.42%/13.86% improvement over random search substantially exceeds PCG's 0.13%/5.54% and SDD-1's 6.50%/11.17%, while SDD-10 and SDD-100 provide zero improvement due to instability.

## Relation to Prior Work

ADASAP extends the sketch-and-project (SAP) framework of Gower and Richtárik [2015] to GP inference, building most directly on Rathore et al. [2025] (approximate SAP for kernel ridge regression) while adding tail averaging, multi-right-hand-side support, and distributed computation. It departs from the two dominant large-scale GP inference paradigms—preconditioned conjugate gradient (PCG, Wang et al. 2019) and stochastic dual descent (SDD, Lin et al. 2024)—by combining PCG's robustness to ill-conditioning (via subspace preconditioning) with SDD's per-iteration cost and scalability (via block sampling and distributed matrix products). The theoretical contribution draws on determinantal point process (DPP) sampling theory (Dereziński and Yang 2024, Dereziński et al. 2024) to establish a two-phase convergence rate that is condition-number-free in the early phase, a guarantee absent from prior stochastic GP inference analyses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The condition-number-free convergence guarantee along top spectral basis functions is the first of its kind for stochastic GP inference, providing a principled explanation for why SAP-style methods outperform SGD/SDD on ill-conditioned kernels. Practically, ADASAP is the first method to perform full GP inference on a dataset exceeding 3×10^8 samples, and its default hyperparameters eliminate the stepsize sensitivity that plagues SDD. Key open questions include extending the theoretical analysis from exact SAP to ADASAP (which uses approximate Nyström preconditioning, Nesterov acceleration, and uniform sampling rather than DPP sampling), and investigating whether the approach benefits from lower-precision arithmetic (float16). The acknowledged theory-practice gap between the DPP-sampled SAP analyzed in the paper and the uniform-sampled ADASAP used in experiments remains a significant limitation.
