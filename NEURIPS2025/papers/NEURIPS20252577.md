## Problem

Existing synthetic data methods for LLM reasoning training rely on rephrasing or slightly modifying seed questions, which limits data volume growth to modest multiples, produces outputs highly similar to seeds, and incurs prohibitive costs from closed-source model APIs. The paper tackles how to achieve orders-of-magnitude data expansion with genuinely novel question diversity while keeping synthesis costs low and quality high.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252577/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes 7.5K seed question-solution pairs from the MATH training set, from which up to 5 key concepts (theorems, formulas, properties) are extracted per problem and filtered via dual LLM+embedding deduplication into ~10K qualified concepts. These concepts form a co-occurrence graph whose edges encode explicit (same-problem) and implicit (multi-hop) relationships; the graph's concept combinations—paired or grouped by hop distance and community structure—are fed as prompts to a generator. The output is GRIP-MATH, a 2.1M question-solution pair dataset where each sample is difficulty-rated, and solutions are generated by size-matched math models (7B for low/medium, 72B for high difficulty). Notably, the synthesis prompt deliberately excludes any seed example to prevent the generator from mimicking original phrasing.

## Method

GRIP constructs a Key Concepts Relationship Graph (KCRG) where nodes are extracted key concepts and edges encode co-occurrence (explicit, one-hop) or proximity (implicit, two-hop and three-hop) in the seed data. The core insight is that the number of valid non-co-occurring concept pairs vastly exceeds co-occurring ones, so combining concepts that never appeared together in the seed yields truly novel problems outside the original distribution. Three-hop combinations are restricted to hub concepts (high-degree nodes) to preserve semantic coherence, and community-based combinations (fully connected 3–4 node subgraphs) produce integrative multi-concept problems. Synthesis is performed by Qwen2.5-32B using prompts that specify the target concept pair without including any seed text, and quality is enforced by a multi-model voting scheme: three open-source math LLMs (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen7B, Qwen2.5-Math-Instruct-7B, DeepSeek-Math-RL) jointly score problems (weighted average ≥ 0.85) and solutions (unanimous 1-vote veto), achieving 95.7% agreement with human annotation—surpassing GPT-4.1 at a fraction of the cost.

## Results

On mathematical reasoning benchmarks, GRIP-trained models achieve substantial gains over their base models: Mistral-7B improves from 28.0 to 57.3 AVG (nearly 30 points), Qwen2-7B from 66.5 to 70.2, and LLaMA3-8B from 31.6 to 53.2. GRIP-Mistral-7B (57.3 AVG) outperforms the best prior data synthesis method MathScale (51.2) by 6.1 points and MAmmoTH2 (52.2) by 5.1 points. GRIP-Qwen2-7B (70.2 AVG) approaches the proprietary Qwen2-Math-7B (72.2) and slightly surpasses DeepSeekMath-RL-7B (69.3), using only 7.5K seed examples and open-source models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GRIP-Mistral-7B (57.3 AVG) decisively outperforms all prior open-source data synthesis methods on the same base model: MathScale (51.2, +6.1), MAmmoTH2 (52.2, +5.1), MetaMath (40.3, +17.0), WizardMath (38.0, +19.3), MathCoder-CL (38.8, +18.5), and MAmmoTH (39.0, +18.3). On Qwen1.5-7B, GRIP (61.9) also surpasses MathScale (56.9) by 5.0 points. GRIP-Qwen2-7B (70.2) slightly trails the proprietary Qwen2-Math-7B (72.2) by 2.0 points but edges out DeepSeekMath-RL-7B (69.3) by 0.9 points, despite using only 7.5K seed examples and open-source synthesis models. GRIP-Qwen2-1.5B (58.6) approaches Qwen2-Math-1.5B (59.9) within 1.3 points. On the scientific reasoning benchmarks (Table 3), GRIP-trained models consistently improve over their base models (e.g., Qwen2-7B: 56.0→57.7 AVG; Mistral-7B: 45.2→51.9 AVG), demonstrating cross-domain generalization without any out-of-domain training data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm of seed-data-centric synthesis (MetaMath, WizardMath, MathCoder, XwinMath) that rephrases or slightly modifies existing questions, and from knowledge-point-driven methods (MathScale, XwinMath) that rely solely on explicit co-occurrence relationships. GRIP introduces a graph-structured intermediate representation (KCRG) that captures both explicit and implicit (two-hop, three-hop, community) relationships among extracted key concepts, enabling the generation of concept combinations that never co-occurred in the seed data. It also replaces the expensive closed-source models (GPT-4, GPT-3.5) used by prior methods with open-source LLMs (Qwen2.5-32B for synthesis, multi-model ensemble for evaluation), fundamentally changing the cost structure of large-scale data synthesis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GRIP-MATH (2.1M samples) provides a freely available, high-quality mathematical reasoning dataset that achieves a 280× expansion ratio at roughly 2% of the per-sample cost of GPT-3.5-based methods, making large-scale synthetic data generation accessible without proprietary model access. The 71.8% novelty rate and demonstrated cross-domain generalization to scientific reasoning benchmarks suggest that implicit concept relationships are an underexploited source of diversity. The primary open question is whether the graph-based concept-combination paradigm generalizes beyond mathematics to domains with less structured knowledge hierarchies (commonsense reasoning, procedural tasks), which the authors acknowledge but do not yet validate.
