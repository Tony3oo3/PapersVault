## Problem

Existing adaptive graph learning in STGNNs suffers from two coupled limitations: quadratic complexity in the number of nodes that hinders scalability, and a ReLU activation in the standard A=Softmax(ReLU(E₁E₂ᵀ)) formulation that provably amplifies negative-similarity (noisy) edges while suppressing positive ones. Linear-complexity approximations (e.g., BigST, GSNet) alleviate the scalability issue but introduce a low-rank bottleneck (rank ≤ d_G ≪ N) that severely limits representational capacity.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a historical spatiotemporal tensor X ∈ ℝ^{T×N×f} (T=12 for traffic, T=24 for other domains) projected to a node-level representation Z⁽⁰⁾ ∈ ℝ^{N×d} via a linear map plus a learnable spatiotemporal position embedding P ∈ ℝ^{N×d}. It produces a flattened prediction Ŷ ∈ ℝ^{N×(T_P·f)} that is reshaped to T_P×N×f. The architecture uses L=3 MAGE blocks with d=128, graph-embedding dimension d_G=32, K_G=16 candidate experts, and K=4 activated experts per node, all trained end-to-end with Adam and L₁ loss.

## Method

MAGE replaces the quadratic Softmax(E₁E₂ᵀ) similarity with a kernel-based factorization Softmax(E₁)·Softmax(E₂ᵀ)H, reducing per-layer cost to O(Nd_G²) (linear in N) while provably eliminating ReLU-induced edge noise. To counteract the resulting rank collapse (rank ≤ d_G), K experts each generate an independent adaptive graph via a differential formulation A⁽ᵏ⁾ = Softmax(E₁⁽ᵏ⁾)Softmax(E₂⁽ᵏ⁾ᵀ) − λ·Softmax(E₃⁽ᵏ⁾)Softmax(E₄⁽ᵏ⁾ᵀ), raising the representation rank to min(d, K·d_G). A sparse-yet-balanced routing mechanism selects top-K experts per node from K_G candidates using sigmoid affinity scores, with a load-balancing priority modulator β_k updated via sign-gradient descent to enforce uniform expert utilization. The paper further proves a 'You Only Convolve Once' (YOCO) equivalence: a single MAGE graph convolution is mathematically equivalent to multiple convolutional steps under conventional full-rank graphs, explaining why the linear formulation retains strong expressive power.

## Results

MAGE achieves state-of-the-art performance on 94% (48/51) of metrics across 17 real-world spatiotemporal datasets spanning traffic, energy, meteorology, and mobile communication domains. On the SD traffic dataset (716 nodes), MAGE attains MAE 16.29, RMSE 28.04, and MAPE 10.87, outperforming the strongest baseline D²STGNN (MAE 17.13) by 5.0%. On the large-scale GBA dataset (2352 nodes), MAGE achieves MAE 19.58 vs. PatchSTG's 19.75, while also reducing training memory to 10,220 MB (vs. 39,102 MB for D²STGNN) and training speed to 57 s/epoch (vs. 5,527 s/epoch for D²STGNN). The method maintains linear complexity in the number of nodes, enabling it to handle all 17 datasets without out-of-memory issues that affect several baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MAGE outperforms all 14 baselines on every reported metric across the 10 main datasets. The closest competitors are PatchSTG (Transformer-based, MAE 17.46 on SD vs. MAGE's 16.29, a 6.7% gap) and D²STGNN (MAE 17.13 on SD, 5.0% gap), both of which suffer from quadratic complexity and fail on large-scale datasets like GLA and CA. Among linear-complexity baselines, BigST (MAE 17.68 on SD) and GSNet (MAE 18.75) trail MAGE by 8.5% and 14.9% respectively, confirming the low-rank bottleneck identified theoretically. On non-traffic domains, MAGE leads STID on Electricity (MAE 172.1 vs. 174.9), STAEformer on UrbanEV (4.95 vs. 5.01), and STWave on Beijing Weibo (0.7988 vs. 0.8308). Static-graph methods (STGCN) and random-projection methods (RPMixer) are consistently the weakest, with RPMixer underperforming MAGE by over 60% MAE on SD (26.01 vs. 16.29). The paper reports SOTA on 48 out of 51 total metrics (94%), with the 3 non-SOTA cases occurring on KnowAir where D²STGNN's MAE (15.39) is within 0.2% of MAGE's (15.36).

## Relation to Prior Work

MAGE extends the adaptive graph learning paradigm introduced by AGCRN and GWNet (A = Softmax(ReLU(E₁E₂ᵀ))) by identifying and removing the ReLU component through a theoretical edge-noise amplification analysis. For linear complexity, it builds on BigST's random-feature approach and GSNet's low-rank factorization, but departs from both by using a kernel-based decomposition (Softmax(E₁)·Softmax(E₂ᵀ)) that preserves the multiplicative union law. The sparse-yet-balanced mixture-of-experts mechanism, inspired by auxiliary-loss-free load balancing in MoE literature [32], is introduced specifically to overcome the low-rank bottleneck inherent in any single linear approximation, a problem not addressed by prior efficient STGNNs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled explanation for why a ubiquitous component (ReLU) in adaptive graph learning is counterproductive, which could influence how future STGNNs are designed. The combination of kernel linearization and MoE offers a practical path to scaling STGNNs to large node counts (thousands of sensors) without the quadratic memory blowup that limits D²STGNN and STAEformer. Open questions include whether the Gaussian-distribution assumptions in the noise-amplification theorem hold for trained embeddings, whether the MoE rank-recovery argument generalizes to non-softmax similarity functions, and whether the approach transfers to other graph-based tasks beyond spatiotemporal forecasting.
