## Problem

Existing dictionary-learning-based XAI methods (SAEs, NMF, KMeans) fail to reveal meaningful differences between two model representations, especially when those differences are subtle. They produce indistinguishable explanations for both models because they discover overlapping concepts and rely on maximum-activation sampling that misses the nuanced, lower-coefficient images where representational divergence actually lives. This makes it impossible to use standard XAI tools to answer the fundamental scientific question of how two models differ in what they represent.

## Input / Output

RDX takes two embedding matrices A ∈ R^{n×d_A} and B ∈ R^{n×d_B} computed by two different models on the same n data items (images in this work), with no requirement that d_A = d_B. It produces a set of m 'difference explanation grids,' each a small set of 9–25 images that are considered similar by one model but not the other, along with the intermediate affinity matrix and spectral cluster assignments. The method is entirely unsupervised and requires no training; it operates purely on the pre-computed embeddings and can be applied to any model that produces a fixed-dimensional embedding per input.

## Method

RDX constructs a locally biased difference matrix by first converting pairwise Euclidean distances in each embedding to rank-based (scale-invariant) distances, then computing G_{A,B}^{ij} = tanh(γ·(D̄_A^{ij} − D̄_B^{ij}) / min(D̄_A^{ij}, D̄_B^{ij})), which emphasizes differences in nearby embeddings while suppressing differences in distant ones. This difference matrix is converted to a symmetric affinity matrix F = exp(−β·G), on which spectral clustering (solving a relaxed normalized-cut problem) identifies groups of items that are close in one representation but far in the other. Within each cluster, a k-neighborhood affinity (KNA) score selects the most 'disagreement-dense' sub-region to form a visual explanation grid. An optional CKA-based alignment step (learning a linear transform to maximize centered kernel alignment) can be applied before comparison to filter out differences that are merely due to representational rotation or scaling, surfacing more fundamental representational distinctions.

## Results

The primary evaluation metric is the Binary Success Rate (BSR), which measures how frequently pairs of items within an explanation grid are closer in one representation than the other. RDX consistently achieves the highest BSR across all experiments, outperforming all seven dictionary-learning baselines (TKSAE, SAE, NMF, PCA, KMeans, CNMF, NLMCD) on both 'known' difference experiments (MNIST, CUB PCBM) and 'unknown' difference experiments (DINO vs. DINOv2, CLIP vs. CLIP-iNat). When a representational alignment step is applied, baseline BSR scores degrade noticeably while RDX maintains strong performance. On SemanticLens metrics, RDX is best on Redundancy and comparable to other methods on Clarity and Polysemanticity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RDX outperforms all seven baselines on BSR across every experiment. NMF [33] and KMeans [36] are the most clearly defeated: they generate indistinguishable explanation grids for both representations being compared, failing to surface any representational difference even when one is known to exist (e.g., the 3% accuracy gap between M_S and M_E on MNIST-[3,5,8]). SAE [41] and TKSAE [17,38] produce explanations that are not only unfocused but can be actively misleading (e.g., SAE suggests the weaker model has a better-separated representation). PCA [16] and CNMF [11] similarly fail to isolate differences, producing generic concept grids unrelated to the known modification. NLMCD [65], the only baseline explicitly designed for model comparison, also trails RDX on BSR, and its performance degrades further under the alignment step. The gap between RDX and baselines is most pronounced in the 'unknown' difference setting (DINO/DINOv2, CLIP/CLIP-iNat), where RDX maintains high BSR with or without alignment while all baselines drop.

## Relation to Prior Work

RDX departs from the dominant dictionary-learning paradigm for XAI (SAE, NMF, PCA, KMeans), which analyzes each representation independently and then attempts to compare the resulting concepts. Instead, it constructs a pairwise difference matrix that simultaneously encodes information from both representations, then applies spectral clustering [67] to isolate clusters where the two models disagree. For representational alignment, it leverages CKA [53] to learn a transformation before comparison. The approach is conceptually closest to DiSC [57] (differential spectral clustering), but differs by operating on input-level similarity structures rather than shared feature spaces, and by producing visual explanation grids rather than feature-level scores.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Model comparison is fundamental to understanding how architectural and training choices affect learned representations, yet standard XAI tools produce indistinguishable explanations for models with known behavioral differences. RDX fills this gap with a training-free, model-agnostic method that requires only two embedding matrices as input. Key limitations include O(n²) memory scaling (tested up to ~5000 points), reliance on Euclidean distances that may not capture manifold geometry, and the discretization of concepts into image grids that cannot represent continuous properties like 'roundness.' Open questions include linking discovered representational differences to downstream task performance, scaling to larger datasets, and extending beyond vision to text and multimodal representations.
