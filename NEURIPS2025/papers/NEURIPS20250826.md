## Problem

Diffusion models trained on Boltzmann-distributed molecular data can generate correct equilibrium samples via denoising, but the learned score at t=0 — which should correspond to physical forces — often produces inconsistent distributions when used for Langevin simulation. This sampling-simulation mismatch (p(x) ≠ p₀(x)) worsens with system size and is traced to violations of the Fokker-Planck equation at small diffusion timesteps, where the model fails to capture the correct evolution of the data distribution.

## Input / Output

The model takes coarse-grained molecular configurations (bead positions), atom-type or residue-identity features, and a diffusion time t as input, and outputs a conservative score vector (gradient of a learned energy). For iid sampling, it starts from Gaussian noise and iteratively denoises through the full t∈(0,1) range; for simulation, only the t≈0 score is used as a force estimator in Langevin dynamics. Coarse-graining resolutions range from 5 atoms (alanine dipeptide) to one bead per residue (Chignolin: 10, BBA: 28) or up to 10 atoms per dipeptide, with the transferable model additionally encoding atom identity, residue index, and amino acid type.

## Method

The core contribution is a Fokker-Planck regularization term added to the standard denoising score-matching loss, which penalizes deviations from the log-density Fokker-Planck equation and enforces consistency between the learned energy landscape and the denoising distribution. To avoid expensive higher-order derivatives, the residual is evaluated in a weak formulation using Gaussian perturbations (Hutchinson-style trace estimation) and finite differences for the time derivative. The score is parameterized conservatively as the gradient of a scalar energy from a graph transformer (pairwise distances for translation invariance, random rotations for equivariance), making the forces physically meaningful and simulation-stable. A time-based Mixture-of-Experts partitions the diffusion timeline into subintervals, applying Fokker-Planck regularization and conservative parameterization only to the small-t expert while using simpler unconstrained models for larger t, reducing over-regularization and cutting inference time by over 50%.

## Results

On the three protein systems (Alanine Dipeptide, Chignolin, BBA), the proposed Fokker-Planck-regularized model ('Ours') achieves simulation PMF errors of 0.091, 0.038, and 0.254 respectively, compared to 0.206, 1.438, and 1.624 for the Two For One baseline — a 37× improvement on Chignolin and 6.4× on BBA. For iid sampling, both methods recover the reference distribution well (PMF ≈ 0.03–0.10), confirming that the inconsistency is specific to the simulation regime. On the transferable dipeptide benchmark, the full 'Both' model (MoE + Fokker-Planck) achieves a simulation PMF of 0.203±0.104, outperforming Two For One (0.741±0.319) by 3.7× and the unregularized Diffusion model (6.515±3.175) by 32×, while matching the best iid PMF (0.199±0.127).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against Two For One (Arts et al., 2023), the closest prior energy-based diffusion model for coarse-grained MD, and Transferable BG (Klein and Noe, 2024), a state-of-the-art Boltzmann generator. Against Two For One, the proposed method wins decisively on simulation quality: on Chignolin, sim PMF drops from 1.438 to 0.038 (37×), and on BBA from 1.624 to 0.254 (6.4×), while iid sampling quality remains comparable (0.035 vs 0.027 on Chignolin). The Two For One method's reliance on evaluating at a finite diffusion time t≫0 introduces substantial noise that degrades structural observables (e.g., bond-length distributions), a failure mode absent in the proposed approach. Against Transferable BG on the dipeptide benchmark, the proposed 'Both' model achieves a lower iid PMF (0.199 vs 0.230) and, critically, supports consistent Langevin simulation (PMF 0.203) where Transferable BG cannot perform simulation at all. The unregularized Diffusion baseline shows that without Fokker-Planck regularization, simulation PMF explodes to 6.515 on dipeptides, confirming that the inconsistency is a fundamental property of standard diffusion training rather than an architectural artifact.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the energy-based diffusion model framework of Arts et al. (2023) ('Two for One'), which first proposed using the score at t=0 as a force estimator for coarse-grained MD but suffered from sampling-simulation inconsistency. The key departure is identifying Fokker-Planck equation violations at small diffusion timesteps as the mechanistic source of this inconsistency and introducing a targeted regularization to correct it, building on the FP-inspired loss of Lai et al. (2023) but repurposing it to enforce energy-based consistency rather than improve iid sample quality. The mixture-of-experts design, inspired by image-domain work (Balaji et al., 2023; Ganjdanesh et al., 2025), allows the FP regularization to be applied selectively to small-t experts while keeping large-t models unconstrained, and the transferable dipeptide model builds on the Boltzmann generator framework of Klein and Noe (2024).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides a principled fix for a fundamental limitation of energy-based diffusion models in molecular simulation: the learned score at t=0 no longer corresponds to the true Boltzmann forces, making simulation unreliable. The practical impact is significant for coarse-grained settings where force labels are unavailable, enabling a single model to serve both as a Boltzmann sampler and a force field for Langevin dynamics. However, the authors note the results are primarily empirical, the FP residual introduces non-trivial computational overhead (multiple forward passes per training step), and perfect alignment between diffusion sampling and Langevin simulation may be fundamentally unattainable without limiting model expressivity. Open questions include scaling to larger systems, whether fine-tuning existing pretrained diffusion models with the FP regularization can correct deviations post-hoc, and whether the approach generalizes beyond the tested biomolecular families.
