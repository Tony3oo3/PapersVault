## Problem

Video aesthetic assessment lags significantly behind image aesthetic assessment due to the scarcity of large-scale, standardized datasets with rich multi-dimensional annotations and the difficulty of capturing temporal dynamics. Existing video aesthetic datasets are limited in scale (160–6,000 videos), rely on sparse annotation types (binary labels or single scores), and lack professional annotation protocols with detailed scoring criteria, making it impossible to train robust, generalizable assessment models.

## Input / Output

The model consumes 12 uniformly sampled video frames at 1 fps (224×224 resolution) from 5–20 second clips, paired with 32-token text sequences comprising both free-form language comments and structured aesthetic tags (e.g., 'symmetric composition', 'top lighting'). It produces a continuous overall aesthetic score on a 1–10 scale plus 10 attribute-specific scores spanning general dimensions (composition, shot size, lighting, visual tone, color, depth of field) and character-specific dimensions (expression, movement, costume, makeup). The underlying VADB dataset contains 10,490 videos each annotated by at least 13 professionals with an average of 22 language comments and 7 objective tags across four video categories.

## Method

VADB-Net employs a two-stage training strategy built on CLIP ViT-B/32. In the pre-training stage, a video encoder with 3D convolution initialization extracts spatiotemporal features from frame sequences, while a dual-text-encoder architecture processes natural language comments and structured aesthetic tags separately; a Dynamic Fusion Module then adaptively combines the two text representations via a learnable attention weight α (initialized at 0.7), and a symmetric contrastive loss with a learnable temperature aligns the fused text and video feature spaces over 226,940 samples. In the fine-tuning stage, the video encoder is frozen and a lightweight three-layer MLP (512→512→256→1) is trained with MSE loss to regress aesthetic scores. The key architectural insight is leveraging both unstructured language comments and structured technical tags as complementary text supervision signals for CLIP-based video pretraining, enabling the model to capture nuanced aesthetic reasoning that neither modality alone provides.

## Results

On the VADB validation set, VADB-Net achieves an overall SRCC of 0.93, PLCC of 0.93, RMSE of 0.54, and KRCC of 0.77, outperforming all three video quality assessment baselines. Across the 10 attribute-specific dimensions, VADB-Net maintains SRCC ≥ 0.87 and PLCC ≥ 0.84 in every case, with the strongest performance on Composition (SRCC 0.93, PLCC 0.94) and Shot Size (SRCC 0.93, PLCC 0.93). In the cross-dataset evaluation on DIVIDE-3k, VADB-Net achieves the best PLCC (0.46) and ties for best KRCC (0.29), though its RMSE (3.74) is substantially higher than the baselines due to scoring-criteria mismatch between datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VADB-Net outperforms all three baselines on the primary VADB evaluation. Against FastVQA (the strongest baseline), VADB-Net improves SRCC from 0.92 to 0.93, reduces RMSE from 0.65 to 0.54, and raises KRCC from 0.76 to 0.77, while matching its PLCC of 0.93. Against SimpleVQA, the gains are larger: SRCC improves by 0.08, RMSE drops by 0.21, and KRCC rises by 0.12. Against ModularBVQA, VADB-Net leads on SRCC (+0.04), RMSE (−0.11), and KRCC (+0.06). The advantage is most pronounced on character-specific attributes (Movement, Makeup, Expression), where VADB-Net's SRCC reaches 0.87–0.91 versus 0.56–0.87 for the baselines, reflecting the benefit of its dual-text-encoder pretraining on rich aesthetic annotations. In the cross-dataset test on DIVIDE-3k, VADB-Net achieves the best PLCC (0.46 vs. 0.44 for ModularBVQA) but suffers a much higher RMSE (3.74 vs. 0.52–0.69), which the authors attribute to fundamental differences in scoring criteria between the two datasets rather than model weakness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the CLIP4Clip video-text contrastive pretraining framework by replacing the single text encoder with a dual-encoder architecture (natural language comments + structured aesthetic tags) fused via a learnable attention mechanism, and by initializing the ViT-B/32 visual backbone with 3D convolutions for spatiotemporal feature extraction. Conceptually, it departs from prior video aesthetic datasets (Telefonica, VAQ700, AVAQ6000, DIVIDE-3k) which relied on crowd-sourced or single-annotator binary/ordinal ratings, instead grounding annotation in a systematic film-and-television aesthetics taxonomy developed by 37 professionals from the Beijing Film Academy. The assessment model itself builds on the broader trend of applying CLIP-based representations to aesthetic tasks (AesCLIP, AesExpert) but is the first to do so for video with multi-dimensional attribute scoring.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

VADB fills a critical gap as the largest professionally annotated video aesthetic dataset, providing the multi-dimensional scoring (11 dimensions), language comments, and objective tags that prior datasets lacked, enabling more nuanced model training and evaluation. However, the cross-dataset experiment (trained on VADB, tested on DIVIDE-3k) reveals severely limited generalization—VADB-Net's RMSE of 3.74 versus 0.52 for ModularBVQA suggests the model is tightly coupled to VADB's specific scoring criteria and cultural context. Open questions include whether expert-driven annotations from a single institution (Beijing Film Academy) can capture diverse aesthetic preferences, how the framework extends to AIGC-generated video, and whether the moderate inter-annotator agreement (Krippendorff's Alpha 0.54–0.66) reflects inherent subjectivity or insufficient standardization.
