## Problem

Existing dataset distillation methods synthesize images for training randomly initialized models from scratch, but modern vision pipelines increasingly rely on pre-trained self-supervised feature extractors with linear probes on top. This paper addresses the unexplored problem of distilling tiny synthetic datasets that are optimal for training linear classifiers in the embedding space of a pre-trained backbone, rather than for end-to-end model training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252019/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a real labeled image dataset (e.g., ImageNet-1k with 1.3M images) and a frozen pre-trained self-supervised ViT-B feature extractor (CLIP, DINO-v2, EVA-02, or MoCo-v3) at 224×224 resolution. It produces a synthetic dataset of exactly one image per class, stored internally as a multi-scale pyramid (1×1 through 256×256) that is rendered into a single 256×256 image via bilinear upsampling and summation. The distilled images are then used to train a randomly initialized linear probe to convergence, yielding a classifier competitive with one trained on the full real dataset.

## Method

Linear Gradient Matching optimizes synthetic images by matching the gradients they induce in a randomly initialized linear classifier (sampled fresh each step) to those induced by real images, after both are passed through the frozen feature extractor. The meta loss is the cosine distance between the vectorized gradients of the cross-entropy classification loss with respect to the linear probe weights, back-propagated through the inner gradient computation to update the synthetic images. To prevent overfitting to the distilling model and enable cross-model transfer, the method employs three key regularizers: a progressive multi-scale pyramid representation (replacing naive pixel optimization), color decorrelation via a fixed linear channel transform, and multiple rounds of differentiable augmentations (horizontal flip, random resized crop, Gaussian noise) applied to concatenated copies of each synthetic image at every iteration. This combination yields realistic-looking distilled images that generalize across different pre-trained backbones, enabling, for example, a DINO-distilled dataset to train a competitive CLIP linear probe.

## Results

On ImageNet-100 with one synthetic image per class, Linear Gradient Matching achieves 87.2% average linear-probe accuracy across four self-supervised backbones (CLIP, DINO-v2, EVA-02, MoCo-v3), compared to 92.8% for the full dataset. On the harder ImageNet-1k, the method reaches 67.9% average accuracy (75.0% with DINO-v2), closing the gap to the full-dataset benchmark (80.0%) to just 12 points. The method also excels on fine-grained datasets: on Stanford Dogs it reaches 69.9% average vs. 80.1% full-dataset, and on CUB-200-2011 it reaches 66.2% vs. 73.8% full-dataset, with larger margins over real-image baselines than on ImageNet.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against three real-image baselines: Neighbors (real image closest in embedding space to the distilled image), Centroids (real image closest to the class-mean embedding), and Random (random real image per class). Linear Gradient Matching outperforms all three baselines across every backbone and every dataset tested. On ImageNet-100, the average margin over the strongest baseline (Centroids) is +6.6 points (87.2 vs. 80.6), and over Random it is +22.9 points. On ImageNet-1k, the margins are even larger: +8.2 points over Centroids (67.9 vs. 59.7) and +28.3 points over Random (67.9 vs. 39.6). The advantage is most pronounced on fine-grained datasets (Stanford Dogs, CUB-200-2011), where the gap over Centroids widens to ~11–10 points, suggesting that a single real image is especially insufficient to capture the discriminative features needed for closely related classes. The only setting where the method does not clearly dominate is the adversarial Spawrious dataset, where the distilled images inherit the distilling model's spurious-correlation biases, narrowing or eliminating the advantage over baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reframes the dataset distillation task (Wang et al. 2018) for the modern paradigm of pre-trained self-supervised backbones with linear probes, rather than the original setting of training randomly initialized models from scratch. The optimization objective, Linear Gradient Matching, is a direct simplification of prior gradient-matching (Zhao et al. 2020) and trajectory-matching (Cazenavette et al. 2022) methods, restricting gradient comparison to only the linear classifier rather than the full network. The regularization components—pyramid representation (Fort & Whitaker 2025), color decorrelation (Olah et al. 2017), and differentiable augmentations (Zhao & Bilen 2021)—are borrowed from adjacent image-optimization and feature-visualization literature and adapted to the distillation setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As the field shifts toward frozen pre-trained backbones with lightweight downstream heads, the original dataset distillation task (training from scratch) becomes less practically relevant; this paper addresses the gap by showing that a single synthetic image per class can recover most of the full-dataset linear-probe performance (e.g., 75% vs. 83% on ImageNet-1k with DINO-v2). The distilled images double as interpretability probes, revealing model biases (e.g., MoCo-v3's reliance on spurious backgrounds) and quantifying cross-model alignment under the Platonic Representation Hypothesis. Key limitations include restriction to linear probes (no fine-tuning), significant memory/compute overhead from bi-level optimization, and the need to load large real batches at each step; open questions include whether the approach extends to non-linear heads or multi-task settings.
