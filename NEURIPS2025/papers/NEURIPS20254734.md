## Problem

Kernel ridge regression under covariate shift requires importance-weighted ERM to correct for the train-test distribution mismatch, but the full Gram matrix inversion costs O(n³) time and O(n²) memory, making it infeasible for large datasets. While random projection methods like Nyström approximation have been shown to preserve statistical accuracy in standard settings, their theoretical guarantees under covariate shift—especially with potentially unbounded importance weights—had not been established. This paper closes that gap by proving that Nyström approximation with ALS sampling achieves the same optimal excess risk rates as full W-KRR while drastically reducing computational costs.

## Input / Output

The method consumes n training pairs (x_i, y_i) drawn from the training distribution ρ^tr, a bounded kernel K: X×X→R defining the RKHS, importance weights w(x_i) (or estimated weights v(x_i) via RuLSIF), and a regularization parameter λ. It selects m ≪ n Nyström centers via approximate leverage score sampling and produces a predictor f̂(x) = Σ_{i=1}^m c̃_i K(x̃_i, x) that approximates the regression function g*(x) under the test distribution ρ^te. The output is a real-valued prediction, and the key practical constraint is that m can be chosen well below n (e.g., m ≈ √n·log n in the standard setting) without degrading the convergence rate.

## Method

The core algorithm restricts the hypothesis space to a random m-dimensional subspace H_m = span{K_x̃_1, …, K_x̃_m} of the RKHS, where centers are drawn via ALS sampling using T-approximate leverage scores, and solves the importance-weighted regularized ERM problem on this subspace. The resulting Nyström W-KRR estimator has the representer form f̂(x) = Σ c̃_i K(x̃_i, x) with coefficients computed from an m×m system rather than the full n×n Gram matrix, reducing time from O(n³) to O(m³ + m²n) and memory from O(n²) to O(mn). The main theoretical contribution (Theorem 1) establishes that under a source condition (smoothness parameter r), a capacity condition (effective dimension N(λ) ≤ Qλ^{-γ}), and moment bounds on the IW function (parameterized by q ∈ [0,1]), choosing λ ∝ (||w||_∞/n)^{1/(2r+γ)} and m ≳ (n/||w||_∞)^{γ/(2r+γ)} log n yields the optimal excess risk rate O((||w||_∞/n)^{2r/(2r+γ)}) for bounded weights, and O(n^{-2r/(2r+1)}) for unbounded weights—matching the best-known full-model rates. The proofs require new technical arguments to handle the interaction between the train-test distribution mismatch and the Nyström approximation, particularly when the IW function is unbounded.

## Results

On four real-world wearable-sensor datasets (HAR70+, HARChildren, HHAR, WISDM), the proposed Nyström W-KRR method achieves essentially identical MSE to full importance-weighted KRR (W-KRR) while reducing training time by 1–2 orders of magnitude: e.g., 89 s vs. 1785 s on HAR70+ (n=20000), 8 s vs. 809 s on HARChildren (n=15000), 6.5 s vs. 1034 s on HHAR (n=15500), and 9.9 s vs. 3364 s on WISDM (n=25000). MSE values are within 0.1–0.3 of the full W-KRR across all datasets (e.g., 5.1 vs. 5.0 on HAR70+, 4.7 vs. 4.8 on WISDM). The unweighted KRR baseline is consistently worse in MSE (e.g., 10 vs. 5.0 on HAR70+, 26.6 vs. 13.5 on HARChildren), confirming the necessity of importance-weighting under covariate shift.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares two baselines: (1) standard KRR (Caponnetto & De Vito, 2007) without importance weighting, and (2) full importance-weighted KRR (W-KRR, following Shimodaira, 2000) that inverts the full n×n Gram matrix. Against unweighted KRR, the proposed Nyström W-KRR wins decisively on MSE across all four datasets (e.g., 5.1 vs. 10 on HAR70+, 13.2 vs. 26.6 on HARChildren, 1.8 vs. 3.7 on HHAR, 4.7 vs. 7.8 on WISDM), confirming that importance weighting is essential under covariate shift. Against full W-KRR, Nyström W-KRR ties on MSE (differences ≤ 0.3, within one standard deviation) while achieving 15–340× speedups in training time (e.g., 89 s vs. 1785 s on HAR70+, 9.9 s vs. 3364 s on WISDM) and 10–25× speedups in prediction time. The number of Nyström centers m required to match full W-KRR accuracy is modest (1100–1800 out of n = 15000–25000), validating the theoretical prediction that m ≪ n suffices. In the simulation study (Figure 1), Nyström W-KRR with optimally chosen m matches full W-KRR error while using only m = 1000 centers out of n = 3000, further confirming the computational savings without statistical degradation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate research threads: importance-weighted kernel ridge regression under covariate shift (Shimodaira 2000; Ma et al. 2023; Gogolashvili et al. 2023) and Nyström/random-projection approximation for computational efficiency (Rudi et al. 2015; Williams & Seeger 2000). The closest prior work is Myleiko & Solodky (2024), who analyzed Nyström subsampling under covariate shift with uniform sampling and known bounded weights; this paper generalizes to ALS (leverage-score) sampling and extends the analysis to potentially unbounded importance weights via a moment-boundedness assumption. The theoretical proofs build on the empirical effective dimension machinery of Rudi et al. (2015) and the concentration inequalities for unbounded weights from Gogolashvili et al. (2023), but require new technical arguments to handle the interaction between the distribution mismatch and the random subspace projection.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that the O(n³) → O(m³ + m²n) computational savings of Nyström approximation come at zero statistical cost under covariate shift, which is practically significant for kernel methods on datasets with n > 10⁵ where full Gram matrix inversion is infeasible. A key open question is the role of the constants in the learning bounds: the paper notes empirically that covariate shift can be either benign or severely adversarial depending on its alignment with the source condition, but a refined analysis of these constants is left for future work. The assumption that all moments of the weight function are bounded (Assumption 3) is stronger than the second-moment condition in Ma et al. (2023), and the fully misspecified case (where the regression function's projection is not in the RKHS) remains unaddressed.
