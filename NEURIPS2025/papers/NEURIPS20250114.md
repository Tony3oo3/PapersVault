## Problem

Existing motion-controlled image-to-video generation models treat the first-frame boundary as an immovable wall, making it impossible to generate objects exiting the visible region or new identities entering the scene — a fundamental limitation for replicating the cinematic Frame In / Frame Out technique. No training dataset, architecture, or evaluation protocol exists for this unbounded, border-crossing generation task.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250114/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a first-frame image, a text prompt, a canvas-expansion bounding box (specifying top-left and bottom-right pixel offsets that enlarge the generation region beyond the first frame), a spatiotemporal pixel-aligned motion trajectory (colored per-object point tracks, not optical flow), and an optional identity reference image. It outputs a 49-frame, 12 FPS video at 384×480 canvas resolution; the user-visible output is cropped back to the original first-frame region, while the full canvas is used internally for loss computation and motion conditioning. The identity reference is consumed as an extra frame appended to the video sequence rather than as a side-channel condition.

## Method

Built on CogVideoX-I2V (5B-parameter Diffusion Transformer), the architecture introduces an unbounded canvas by zero-padding the first frame and re-anchoring the absolute/RoPE coordinate system so the canvas top-left is the origin, allowing motion trajectories to extend beyond the original frame. Motion conditioning is injected via channel-wise concatenation of VAE-encoded trajectory images (with a zero-initialized projector initialized from pre-trained weights, à la Marigold), avoiding the cost of ControlNet or cross-attention branches. The identity reference is encoded by the same 3D VAE, noise-augmented, and frame-wise concatenated as an additional token block that is discarded after each forward pass. A key design choice is the full-field loss: the denoising objective is computed over the entire canvas rather than only the first-frame region, which the authors found necessary for stable training. Training proceeds in two stages — first adapting the model to motion conditioning and modified position encodings, then jointly training Frame In and Frame Out with the unbounded canvas and ID reference — using a semi-automatically curated dataset (~72K clips from OpenVid-1M, VidGen-1M, and WebVid) built with OneFormer panoptic segmentation, CoTracker3 cycle tracking, and SAM2-based ID extraction.

## Results

On the Frame Out benchmark (189 cases), the proposed Stage 2 model achieves FID 32.02, FVD 318.38, LPIPS 0.268, Trajectory Error 17.85, VSeg MAE 0.0229, and VLM accuracy 0.735, outperforming the best baseline (DragAnything) by 34% on FID, 48% on FVD, and 57% on trajectory error. On the Frame In benchmark (183 cases), the method achieves FID 30.84, FVD 227.30, LPIPS 0.218, Trajectory Error 10.37, VSeg MAE 0.0112, and VLM accuracy 0.863, substantially surpassing the closest elements-to-video baseline (Phantom + motion prompt) by 58% on FID and 66% on FVD. A human study with 450 pairwise comparisons confirms 73–100% win rates over all five baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the three motion-controllable I2V baselines on Frame Out, the proposed method wins on every metric: DragAnything [69] (FID 48.73, Traj. Err. 41.24), Image Conductor [32] (FID 99.29, Traj. Err. 42.72), and ToRA [87] (FID 57.83, Traj. Err. 40.72) all fail to move objects fully outside the first-frame boundary, whereas the unbounded canvas formulation reduces trajectory error by 50–58% and halves VSeg MAE. On Frame In, the two elements-to-video baselines—SkyReels-A2 [12] (FID 74.00, VLM 0.448) and Phantom [35] (FID 69.55, VLM 0.415)—cannot accept explicit motion trajectory conditioning, so the authors add LLM-generated motion description prompts as a fair compromise; even with this boost, both remain far behind the proposed method on FID (30.84 vs. 61.20/72.84), FVD (227.30 vs. 550.26/671.05), and VLM accuracy (0.863 vs. 0.535/0.540). The only metric where the proposed method is slightly behind is Relative DINO (1.62 vs. Phantom+prompt's 1.39), which the authors attribute to the model faithfully following motion guidance that occasionally moves the identity outside the canvas, reducing frame-wise similarity. The human study (Table 6) corroborates these quantitative gains with 73.3–100% win rates across all five baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the CogVideoX-I2V video Diffusion Transformer and extends the motion-control paradigm established by MotionCtrl, DragAnything, and ToRA, which all treat the first-frame boundary as a hard spatial constraint on trajectory conditioning. It also draws on identity-preserving video generation (Concat-ID, Phantom, SkyReels-A2) for the Frame In identity-reference mechanism. The key conceptual departure is the 'unbounded canvas' formulation: by zero-padding the first frame into a larger region and re-anchoring position encodings to the canvas origin, the model accepts motion trajectories and identity references that extend beyond the visible frame, a conditioning regime absent from all prior controllable I2V methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Frame In/Frame Out is a fundamental cinematic operation (character entrance/exit) that no prior controllable video generation system could express, making this the first end-to-end solution with a dedicated dataset, architecture, and evaluation protocol. The unbounded-canvas conditioning strategy is a generalizable idea that could extend to other out-of-frame effects (camera reveals, multi-scene transitions). However, the evaluation is limited to a small benchmark (~185 cases per task), the model is a 5B-parameter architecture, only one identity reference is supported, and no error bars or multi-seed results are reported, leaving open questions about robustness at scale and with multiple simultaneous identities.
