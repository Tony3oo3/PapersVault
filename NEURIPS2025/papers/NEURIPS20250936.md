## Problem

Standard MSE loss in time series forecasting fails to accurately capture seasonality or trend within the forecasting horizon, even when decomposition modules are applied in the forward propagation. The inductive bias of separating trend and seasonality is not enforced on the prediction side, leading to poor seasonal predictions, poor trend predictions, or both simultaneously.

## Input / Output

DBLoss is a drop-in loss function that operates on the prediction Ŷ and ground truth Y within the forecasting horizon (F future timestamps across N channels). It requires no architectural changes to the backbone model—only the training loss is replaced. The EMA decomposition is applied independently to both Ŷ and Y, producing four component tensors (Ŷ_S, Ŷ_T, Y_S, Y_T) from which two separate losses are computed and combined.

## Method

DBLoss decomposes both the model prediction and the ground truth into seasonal and trend components using exponential moving averages (EMA) within the forecasting horizon, then computes a weighted sum of component-specific losses: an L2 norm for the seasonal component and an L1 norm for the trend component. A scale alignment mechanism multiplies the trend loss by stopgrad(L_S / (L_T + ε)) to prevent scale differences from causing one component to dominate optimization, while the stopgrad operation blocks gradient flow through the alignment ratio to avoid interference between the two loss terms. The total loss is L = β·L_S + (1−β)·L_T_aligned, where β balances seasonal versus trend contributions. A theoretical analysis shows that MSE introduces a cross-term 2·(T_t − T̂_t)(S_t − Ŝ_t) that couples the gradient updates of the two components, whereas DBLoss explicitly decouples them, enabling independent optimization of trend and seasonality.

## Results

DBLoss consistently reduces MSE and MAE across all four backbone models (iTransformer, Amplifier, PatchTST, DLinear) on eight real-world datasets. The most dramatic improvement is DLinear on ETTh2, where average MSE drops from 0.470 to 0.409 (−13%) and MAE from 0.468 to 0.424. On PatchTST, ETTh1 average MSE improves from 0.419 to 0.402, and on iTransformer, ETTh1 average MSE improves from 0.439 to 0.423. Gains are smaller on datasets with weaker seasonality (e.g., Electricity, Weather) but remain positive in the majority of settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the standard MSE loss, DBLoss wins on the majority of model–dataset–horizon combinations, with the largest gains on DLinear (e.g., ETTh2 avg MSE 0.470→0.409) and PatchTST (e.g., ETTh1 avg MSE 0.419→0.402). In the direct loss-function comparison (Table 2, DLinear backbone), DBLoss achieves the lowest average MSE on ETTh2 (0.409 vs. TILDE-Q 0.418, FreDF 0.413, PSLoss 0.417) and Traffic (0.417 vs. TILDE-Q 0.431, FreDF 0.419, PSLoss 0.419), while tying PSLoss on ETTm1 (both 0.351). TILDE-Q and FreDF show larger improvements on ETTh2 but degrade on Traffic, whereas DBLoss maintains consistent gains across all three datasets. The paper also demonstrates that DBLoss improves zero-shot generalization (Table 3) and 5% few-shot fine-tuning of foundation models (Table 4), outperforming MSE in nearly all transfer settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the EMA decomposition technique introduced in xPatch (Stitsyuk & Choi, AAAI 2025) but repurposes it from a forward-propagation component into a loss-function mechanism. It positions itself against three families of prior time-series loss functions—shape-based (TILDE-Q, Soft-DTW), dependency-based (FreDF), and patch-based (PSLoss)—by introducing a fourth category: decomposition-based losses that explicitly separate seasonal and trend errors in the forecasting horizon. The key conceptual departure from prior work is that while models like DLinear, TimesNet, and TimeMixer apply seasonal-trend decomposition during forward propagation to extract representations, DBLoss applies the same decomposition to both the prediction and ground truth at the loss level, arguing that the inductive bias is not fully realized in the output unless the loss function itself enforces component-wise alignment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DBLoss offers a genuinely plug-and-play improvement: replacing MSE with DBLoss requires no architectural changes and yields consistent (if modest) gains across eight backbone models and eight datasets, including foundation models in few-shot settings. The theoretical analysis identifying the MSE cross-term as a source of trend-seasonal coupling during gradient propagation provides a clear mechanistic explanation for why the decomposition helps. However, the paper acknowledges that optimal hyperparameters (α for EMA smoothing, β for seasonal-trend weighting) vary across datasets and architectures with no principled selection rule, leaving an open question about automated or adaptive parameter tuning. The consistent observation that DBLoss produces higher training loss but lower test loss suggests it acts as a mild regularizer, a property worth further investigation.
