## Problem

Existing offline imitation learning guarantees require the expert policy to belong to a known, tractable policy class (expert realizability), an assumption that is often unrealistic for complex or nonstationary experts (e.g., human-written text). Moreover, sample complexity bounds scale with the log-covering number of this expert class, and misspecification can render learning computationally intractable. This paper asks whether structural assumptions on the MDP itself (rather than on the expert) can guarantee matching expert performance without any expert realizability requirement.

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes a finite dataset of state-action pairs {X_E^i, A_E^i} drawn i.i.d. from the expert's occupancy measure μ^π_E, along with either a known d-dimensional feature mapping φ: X×A → R^d (linear case) or a function class Q ⊂ R^{X×A} with bounded covering number (general case). It outputs a stationary policy π^out whose normalized expected return is within O(ε) of the expert's, with no access to the MDP's reward, transitions, or the expert policy itself. In the linear setting, the algorithm only ever operates on d-dimensional vectors, making it computationally lightweight regardless of the state or action space size.

## Method

SPOIL (Saddle-Point Offline Imitation Learning) exploits the identity ρ^π_E − ρ^π = L(π; Q^π) ≤ sup_{Q∈Q} L(π; Q), where L(π; Q) = E_{(X,A)~μ^π_E}[Q(X,A) − Q(X,π)], to cast imitation learning as the saddle-point problem min_π sup_Q L(π; Q). The algorithm alternates two updates: the actor (primal) performs a mirror-descent / exponential-weights policy update π_{k+1}(a|x) ∝ π_k(a|x) exp(η Q_k(x,a)), while the critic (dual) plays the best response Q_k ∈ argmax_{Q∈Q} L̂(π_k; Q). In the linear Q^π-realizable setting, the critic update reduces to a closed-form projection of an empirical feature-gap vector onto a Euclidean ball, and the actor update requires storing only a single d-dimensional accumulated vector. This primal-side optimization (as opposed to IQ-Learn's dual-side approach) is what enables a clean regret decomposition: the total error splits into a sublinear online-learning regret term (controlled via the performance-difference lemma and per-state mirror descent) and a uniform estimation error term (controlled via covering-number concentration over the algorithm's own policy class, which is far smaller than the full policy space). The result is an O(d/((1−γ)^4 ε^2)) sample complexity for linear MDPs and O(log N_{ε'}(Q)/((1−γ)^8 ε^4)) for general Q^π-realizable MDPs, both independent of any expert policy class complexity.

## Results

The main theoretical contribution establishes that SPOIL requires Õ(d/((1−γ)⁴ε²)) expert samples for linear Q^π-realizable MDPs (Theorem 1) and Õ(log N_{ε′}(Q,‖·‖∞)/((1−γ)⁸ε⁴)) for general Q^π-realizable MDPs (Theorem 2), without any expert realizability assumption. Empirically, on a large linear MDP (|X|=500, A=1000, d=7), SPOIL successfully imitates both a simple linear expert and a complex three-layer neural network expert, while behavior cloning degrades significantly on the latter. On continuous-state benchmarks (CartPole-v1, Acrobot-v1, LunarLander-v2) with subsampled expert trajectories, SPOIL achieves performance comparable to IQ-Learn and consistently outperforms BC and P²IL.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SPOIL is compared against four baselines across two experimental settings. Against IQ-Learn (Garg et al., 2021), SPOIL achieves comparable performance on all three continuous-state benchmarks (CartPole-v1, Acrobot-v1, LunarLander-v2), with both methods tracking similar learning curves as the number of expert trajectories increases. Against behavior cloning (BC; Pomerleau, 1991; Foster et al., 2024), SPOIL clearly outperforms BC in the continuous-state experiments and dramatically outperforms it in the linear MDP experiment when the expert is a complex neural network policy — BC struggles with the high complexity of the neural network policy class even when the realizability condition is perfectly satisfied. Against P²IL (Viano et al., 2022), SPOIL also shows consistent improvements on the continuous-state benchmarks. Theoretically, SPOIL's key advantage over all BC-based methods is that its sample complexity depends only on the environment's feature dimension d (or the covering number of Q), not on the complexity of the expert policy class, making it robust to arbitrarily complex or misspecified experts.

## Relation to Prior Work

SPOIL departs from the dominant line of offline imitation learning work (Rajaraman et al., Foster et al., Rohatgi et al.) that requires the expert to belong to a known, tractable policy class (expert realizability), instead leveraging structural assumptions on the MDP itself (Q^π-realizability). Algorithmically, it shares the same saddle-point objective as IQ-Learn (Garg et al., 2021) and AdVIL (Swamy et al., 2021), but differs in optimizing the primal (policy) via mirror descent with best-response critic updates, rather than the dual or simultaneous gradient methods. It also generalizes classical apprenticeship learning (Abbeel & Ng, 2004; Syed & Schapire, 2007) by removing the need for known transitions and linear rewards, while extending beyond tabular MDPs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first result showing that structural MDP assumptions alone can guarantee matching expert performance in the offline setting without any expert realizability assumption, with sample complexity depending only on environment complexity (feature dimension d) rather than expert policy class size. The O(ε^{-2}) rate in the linear case is clean, but the O(ε^{-4}) degradation in the general function approximation case remains an open question the authors flag as improvable. The theoretical grounding of IQ-Learn's objective (a widely used practical method) is a notable contribution, and the empirical demonstration that BC fails with complex experts even under perfect realizability highlights a practical limitation of the dominant approach. Future directions include improving the general-case rate, handling misspecification, and extending to continuous action spaces.
