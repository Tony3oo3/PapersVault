## Problem

Error Consistency (EC), the standard metric for quantifying behavioral alignment between classifiers (e.g., DNNs vs. humans), has historically been reported as a single point estimate without confidence intervals. Because EC is inherently noisy—individual trials can substantially swing the score, especially at the small trial counts typical of human experiments—valid benchmarking conclusions are problematic without uncertainty quantification. The paper demonstrates that many previously reported differences between deep vision models in influential benchmarks (Model-vs-Human, Brain-Score) are statistically insignificant once CIs are computed.

## Input / Output

For bootstrapping existing data, the method consumes two binary sequences of length N (each entry encoding whether a classifier was correct on a given trial) and produces a 95% percentile confidence interval around the EC value plus p-values from significance tests. For experiment planning without data, it takes target marginal distributions (i.e., classifier accuracies), a target EC, and a trial count N, then simulates the full distribution of EC estimates to predict CI widths before any data is collected. The significance tests support both a basic null (independence, κ=0) and an advanced null (equal EC between two candidate classifiers relative to a fixed reference observer).

## Method

The paper contributes two complementary techniques. First, a bootstrapping procedure resamples N trial pairs with replacement M=10,000 times, recomputes EC on each resample, and reports the central 95% of the resulting distribution as a confidence interval—applied hierarchically through the full aggregation pipeline of benchmarks like Model-vs-Human. Second, the paper proves that Cohen's κ (and thus EC) decomposes into a copy probability p_copy multiplied by a marginal-mismatch factor f (Propositions 1 and 2), yielding a generative model: given target accuracies and EC, one inverts for p_copy and the underlying marginals, then simulates trial sequences to predict CI widths a priori. This copy model also underpins the significance tests: the basic test samples from independent binomial observers under H₀:κ=0, while the advanced test bootstraps EC differences between two candidates and exchanges their responses with probability 0.5 to construct the null distribution. Applied to Model-vs-Human and Brain-Score, the methodology reveals that while the human-machine gap is real, most pairwise differences among the 52 DNNs are not statistically distinguishable.

## Results

Re-analyzing the Model-vs-Human benchmark with bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals, the best model (CLIP-trained ViT) achieves an EC of 0.279 to humans, while the second-best (BiTM-ResnetV2-101x1) reaches 0.251; this 0.028 gap is the only statistically significant difference between neighboring models (p < 1×10⁻¹⁰). VGG-19's confidence interval overlaps with more than 70% of all 52 models, and no other pairwise neighbor differences survive significance testing. Ranking stability across 10,000 bootstraps yields a mean Kendall's τ of 0.88, confirming the broad human–machine gap but showing that fine-grained model ordering is unreliable. Simulations show that at N = 400 trials (typical in the literature), CIs still span ~0.2 κ units, motivating the paper's recommendation of ≥1,000 trials per classifier at ~75% accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new classifier or benchmark metric to compare against baselines; instead, it applies its bootstrapping and copy-probability methodology to re-examine two existing benchmarks. On Model-vs-Human (Geirhos et al., 2021), the headline conclusion—that humans are more consistent among themselves than any DNN is to humans—remains robust (Kendall's τ = 0.88 across bootstraps). However, the fine-grained ranking of 52 DNNs is largely unresolved: only the CLIP ViT vs. BiTM-ResnetV2-101x1 gap is significant, and the CIs of mid-range models (e.g., VGG-19) overlap with the majority of the field. On Brain-Score, the top-30 model ranking is similarly unstable, with any of the top ten models potentially being second-best given the width of the CIs. The paper's practical takeaway is that previously reported EC differences between deep vision models (typically 0.01–0.05) fall within the noise floor of the current experimental protocol, and that the human–machine gap (~0.1–0.2 κ) is the only reliably detectable effect at the available sample sizes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Error Consistency (EC) metric introduced by Geirhos et al. (2020), which itself builds on Cohen's kappa (1960) to quantify trial-by-trial behavioral agreement between classifiers. The key departure is twofold: first, it applies bootstrapping (Efron & Tibshirani, 1994) to EC to obtain confidence intervals and significance tests—something prior EC applications (Model-vs-Human, Brain-Score, LLM comparisons) had not done. Second, it introduces a generative 'copy model' (Propositions 1–2) that algebraically decomposes Cohen's kappa into a copy probability and a marginal-mismatch factor, giving EC an interpretable probabilistic meaning and enabling simulation-based power analysis before data collection.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central practical finding is that many published differences between deep vision models in influential benchmarks (Model-vs-Human, Brain-Score) are statistically insignificant once confidence intervals are computed—e.g., VGG-19's rank could plausibly range from 8th to 45th. While the broad human–machine gap survives scrutiny, the fine-grained model rankings that drive Brain-Score scores are unreliable with current human data volumes. The paper provides concrete experimental design guidance (≥1000 trials, ~75% accuracy target, uniform human marginals) and a Python package, but leaves open whether analytic CI solutions (à la Gwet, Fleiss) could replace bootstrapping, and whether other benchmark metrics beyond EC suffer the same underpowered-comparison problem.
