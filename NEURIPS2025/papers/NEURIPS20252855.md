## Problem

Diffusion-based video super-resolution methods require dozens of sampling steps and often introduce auxiliary modules (e.g., ControlNet, temporal layers), making inference prohibitively slow for real-world use. While single-step acceleration has been explored for image SR and video generation, it remains unaddressed for VSR due to the prohibitive training cost of multi-network distillation on multi-frame data and the instability of adversarial training, which conflicts with VSR's high-fidelity requirements.

## Input / Output

The method takes a low-resolution video, bilinearly upsamples it to the target resolution, and encodes it via a frozen 3D causal VAE into a latent representation that serves as the noised input at a fixed timestep (t=399). A single Transformer denoising step (v-prediction) produces the HR latent, which is decoded back to pixel space. All experiments use a 4× scaling factor; training operates at 320×640 with 25-frame clips, while evaluation uses 720×1280 videos of 33 frames. An empty pre-encoded text prompt is used as conditioning, and no optical flow or ControlNet conditioning is required.

## Method

DOVE fine-tunes the Transformer denoiser of CogVideoX (a pretrained text-to-video diffusion model) for one-step VSR, preserving the VAE and adding no auxiliary modules. The key training innovation is a two-stage latent-pixel strategy: Stage 1 minimizes MSE between predicted and HR latents in the compressed latent space on 25-frame video clips (10K iterations), enabling efficient long-sequence training; Stage 2 refines in pixel space using a mixed image/video schedule (80% images, 20% video) with MSE, DISTS perceptual loss, and a frame-difference loss for temporal consistency, where videos are processed frame-by-frame through the VAE to avoid multi-frame memory spikes. A curated dataset HQ-VSR (2,055 videos) is constructed from OpenVid-1M via a four-step pipeline—metadata filtering, scene segmentation, multi-metric quality scoring (CLIP-IQA, FasterVQA, DOVER), and motion-area-based cropping—to provide high-quality, detail-rich training data tailored for VSR.

## Results

DOVE achieves state-of-the-art or near-state-of-the-art performance across all six evaluation datasets. On UDM10, it attains PSNR 26.48, LPIPS 0.2696, CLIP-IQA 0.5107, and DOVER 0.7809, all best among compared methods. On the real-world VideoLQ benchmark (no HR reference), DOVE scores DOVER 0.7435 and FasterVQA 0.7764, outperforming all multi-step diffusion baselines. Critically, DOVE achieves these results with a single inference step (14.90 s on a 33-frame 720p video on A100), compared to 121–425 s for competing diffusion-based methods, yielding up to 28× speedup over MGLD-VSR.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DOVE outperforms all seven baselines on the majority of metrics across all six datasets. Against the strongest non-diffusion baseline RealBasicVSR, DOVE gains +2.35 dB PSNR on UDM10 (26.48 vs 24.13) and +0.23 DOVER on RealVSR (0.7867 vs 0.7636). Among diffusion-based VSR methods, DOVE surpasses MGLD-VSR (the closest competitor) by +2.25 dB PSNR on UDM10 and +0.034 DOVER on VideoLQ, while being 28× faster (14.90 s vs 425.23 s). DOVE also beats STAR and VEnhancer by large margins on perceptual metrics (e.g., CLIP-IQA 0.5107 vs 0.2417/0.2852 on UDM10) and video quality (DOVER 0.7809 vs 0.4830/0.4576). The only notable losses are on temporal consistency (E*): VEnhancer achieves 1.03 on UDM10 and 0.95 on YouHQ40 vs DOVE's 1.77 and 2.05, and on CLIP-IQA for YouHQ40 where Upscale-A-Video edges ahead (0.5258 vs 0.4985). On MVSR4x, MGLD-VSR marginally leads on PSNR (22.77 vs 22.42), but DOVE still wins on all perceptual and video-quality metrics.

## Relation to Prior Work

DOVE extends the line of diffusion-based VSR methods (MGLD-VSR, Upscale-A-Video, STAR, VEnhancer) that adapt pretrained text-to-image or text-to-video models with auxiliary modules (ControlNet, temporal layers, optical flow) and multi-step sampling. It departs from this lineage by removing all auxiliary components and reducing inference to a single denoising step, drawing on one-step acceleration techniques (DMD, VSD, rectified flow, adversarial post-training) while deliberately avoiding their multi-network distillation or adversarial losses in favor of a simpler two-stage regression strategy (latent-space MSE followed by pixel-space MSE + DISTS + frame-difference loss). The video processing pipeline and HQ-VSR dataset also build on prior data curation practices (scene detection, quality scoring, optical-flow-based motion filtering) but reorganize them specifically for VSR's fidelity and detail requirements.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 28× speedup over MGLD-VSR while matching or exceeding quality across six benchmarks makes diffusion-based VSR practically deployable for the first time, removing the primary barrier to real-world adoption. The finding that a straightforward regression-based two-stage training suffices for one-step VSR—without the instability of adversarial training or the multi-network overhead of distillation—offers a practical recipe that could transfer to other restoration tasks. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes beyond 4× scaling, whether it works with other pretrained video backbones, and how it handles extreme or out-of-distribution degradations. The HQ-VSR dataset (2,055 videos) also provides a useful, if small, high-quality resource for the community.
