## Problem

Modern Transformers have moved away from ReLU activations, eliminating the natural activation sparsity that made earlier models efficient. Prior attempts to re-introduce sparsity—reverting to ReLU variants, applying top-k masking, or adding sparse predictors—either degrade model quality, increase parameter count, or introduce costly sorting operations that slow training by up to 10× on TPUs. The paper asks whether high activation sparsity can be re-introduced in both FFN and attention of state-of-the-art gated-activation Transformers while maintaining quality, parameter count, and standard single-stage training.

## Input / Output

The model takes a standard text token sequence and produces next-token logits for autoregressive generation, identical in interface to Gemma-2 2B. Internally, the input embedding vector q ∈ R^{d_model} is split into two sub-vectors q[:r] and q[r:] (with r ≈ d_model/2), where the first part serves as a low-rank predictor for selecting which FFN neurons or attention keys to activate. The sparsity is enforced per-token: only 8% of FFN neurons (k=1106 out of d_ff=13824) and at most 256 attended tokens are computed per token, reducing FLOPs to ~40% of the dense baseline.

## Method

Spark Transformer unifies FFN and attention as key-value lookup tables and introduces sparsity via a statistical top-k operator applied to a low-cost predictor built from repurposed input dimensions (no extra parameters). In Spark FFN, the input is split so that K₁ᵀq[:r] acts as a predictor; statistical top-k selects the top-k entries, and the remaining computation (K₂ᵀq[r:] and multiplication with V) is performed sparsely. Spark Attention applies the same principle to key vectors, using top-k with −∞ masking followed by softmax and softplus. The key technical tool, statistical top-k, estimates a threshold as mean(x) + std(x)·Q(1−k/d) under a Gaussian assumption and applies soft-thresholding, achieving O(d) complexity (vs. O(d log d) for sorting) while remaining differentiable almost everywhere. A theoretical guarantee (Theorem 3.1) bounds the relative error in the number of selected entries, and the full model is pretrained from scratch with the Gemma-2 recipe, achieving near-quality neutrality with 2.5× FLOPs reduction and up to 1.79× decode speedup on CPU.

## Results

Spark Transformer (Gemma-2 2B recipe) achieves 92% FFN activation sparsity (only 8% neurons active) and limits attention to at most 256 tokens per query, yielding a 2.5× overall FLOPs reduction (FFN FLOPs reduced by 72%, attention dot-product FLOPs by 75%). On CPU (4-core), prefill speed improves from 28 ms/token to 15 ms/token (1.86×) and decode from 141 ms/token to 86 ms/token (1.64×); on NVIDIA T4 GPU, decode speedup reaches 1.40×. Downstream benchmark evaluation (Figure 1b) confirms near-quality neutrality relative to standard Gemma-2, with a −0.9% quality loss in Table D.1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the standard Gemma-2 2B baseline, Spark Transformer matches downstream benchmark performance (Figure 1b) while reducing FLOPs to 40% and delivering 1.64–1.86× CPU decode/prefill speedups. Compared to ReLUification [64] and ReLU² [92], Spark Transformer achieves substantially greater FLOPs reduction (−72% vs −62%/−42%) with less quality loss (−0.9% vs −2%/−1.9%), and avoids the need to switch activation functions. Versus ProSparse [81], Spark Transformer provides 13 percentage points more FLOPs reduction (−72% vs −59%) at a comparable quality cost (−0.9% vs −1.1%) and without requiring a 2-layer FFN predictor. Against HiRE [89], Spark Transformer achieves 12 percentage points more FLOPs reduction (−72% vs −60%) with nearly identical quality loss (−0.9% vs −0.8%), while requiring zero additional training cost (HiRE needs +100% training). Compared to CATS [49], Spark Transformer more than doubles the FLOPs reduction (−72% vs −33%) with less quality degradation. Finally, the proposed statistical top-k operator introduces negligible training overhead, whereas the standard JAX top-k (jax.lax.approx_max_k [14]) causes >10× training slowdown even at 50% recall (Figure 6).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the lazy-neuron phenomenon (Li et al., 2022), which revealed natural activation sparsity in ReLU-based Transformers, and the key-value memory interpretation of FFNs (Geva et al., 2021). It departs from prior sparsity-enforcement methods (ReLUification, ProSparse, HiRE, CATS) that either revert to ReLU, require finetuning, or add extra predictor parameters, by introducing a statistical top-k operator based on Gaussian quantile estimation and repurposing existing input dimensions as a zero-cost predictor. The soft-thresholding operator from compressed sensing (Beck & Teboulle, 2009) is repurposed to make the top-k selection differentiable, and the approach unifies FFN and attention sparsity under a single architectural framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Spark Transformer achieves a 2.5× FLOPs reduction (92% FFN sparsity + 256-token attention cap) with only −0.9% quality loss, translating to 1.79× CPU and 1.40× GPU decode speedups — a practically significant efficiency gain for deploying high-quality models on constrained hardware. The requirement for full pretraining from scratch (rather than finetuning an existing model) is a notable limitation that restricts immediate adoption for already-deployed architectures. Open questions include whether the Gaussian assumption underlying statistical top-k holds for other model families and scales, and whether the demonstrated synergies with speculative decoding and quantization compound multiplicatively in practice.
