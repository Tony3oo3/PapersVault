## Problem

Conventional visuomotor policies predict actions forward from the current observation, a myopic objective that accumulates compounding errors over long horizons and degrades sharply under spatial distribution shifts. Existing mitigations such as action chunking and goal-conditioned behavioral cloning address symptoms rather than the root cause: the forward prediction paradigm never explicitly anchors local actions to the final task goal. This paper asks whether reversing the generation direction—starting from a goal and reasoning backward—can structurally eliminate this failure mode.

## Input / Output

The model consumes four 128×128 RGB views (front, left/right shoulder, wrist) plus a proprioceptive gripper state, encoded by a ResNet-18 backbone and a linear projection respectively. It outputs a variable-length sequence of 8-dimensional continuous end-effector poses (3 position + 4 quaternion + 1 gripper), generated autoregressively in reverse from a predicted keyframe action until a distance-based stopping criterion confirms the trajectory has reached the current state. In the real-world setting, a single 640×480 camera resized to 224×224 is used, and the policy runs at 10 Hz while a PD controller executes at 1000 Hz.

## Method

CoA factors the trajectory distribution in reverse: p(a_{1:T}|O) = p(a_T|O) · p(a_{T-1}|a_T,O) · … · p(a_1|a_{2:T},O), where a_T is a keyframe action (detected via gripper-state-change or zero-velocity heuristics) that encodes the task-specific goal. A 4-layer Transformer encoder processes visual and proprioceptive tokens, and a 7-layer decoder generates action tokens autoregressively from a learned SOS token, with the first predicted token being the keyframe and subsequent tokens reasoning backward. Four supporting designs make this stable: (1) continuous action tokens regularized by a latent consistency loss that aligns predicted latents with the encoder's output, preventing error amplification; (2) multi-token prediction with 5 parallel heads that jointly predict short action chunks during training to enforce local coherence; (3) a distance-based dynamic stop that terminates decoding when the predicted action is close to the current end-effector pose; and (4) a reverse temporal ensemble that aligns multiple backward rollouts at the keyframe anchor to reduce variance during closed-loop execution. Training uses imitation learning on 100 demonstrations per task with a combined action-space regression loss and latent-space consistency loss, and the architecture is otherwise matched to ACT to isolate the paradigm shift.

## Results

On the RLBench-60 benchmark (60 tasks, 100 demos each), CoA achieves an average success rate of 0.756, outperforming ACT (0.488) by 16.3% and Diffusion Policy (0.416) by 23.2%, winning on 81.7% and 80.0% of tasks respectively. On the RLBench-10 subset, CoA scores 0.756 average success rate versus 0.488 (ACT), 0.416 (DP), and 0.644 (Octo). In real-world kitchen manipulation (8 tasks, Fetch robot), CoA achieves 0.613 average success rate, surpassing ACT (0.463) by 15% and DP (0.363) by 25%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CoA decisively outperforms ACT (Zhao et al., RSS 2023) across the board: 0.756 vs. 0.488 average on RLBench-10, with a 16.3% average gain over 60 tasks, and 0.613 vs. 0.463 in real-world settings. Against Diffusion Policy (Chi et al., RSS 2023), the margin is even larger at 23.2% average improvement on RLBench-60, with particularly dramatic gaps on tasks like Pick Up Cup (0.80 vs. 0.00) and Push Button (0.76 vs. 0.12). CoA also beats the finetuned generalist policy Octo (Ghosh et al., 2023) on RLBench-10 (0.756 vs. 0.644), though Octo is competitive on a few individual tasks (Open Box: 0.96 vs. 0.76). On RLBench-18, CoA (37.33 avg) substantially outperforms image-based policies (ACT: 24.44, DP: 17.33) but still trails 3D-based hierarchical methods like RVT-2 (81.4) and 3D Diffuser Actor (81.3), which leverage point clouds and motion planners. CoA loses to ACT and DP on a small number of tasks (e.g., Sweep Dust, Press Switch, Reach Target), but these are typically simpler or already near-saturated tasks where all methods perform well.

## Relation to Prior Work

CoA departs from the dominant forward-prediction paradigm in visuomotor policy learning (ACT, Diffusion Policy) by reversing the autoregressive generation direction: instead of predicting next-step actions from the current observation, it generates an entire trajectory backward from a keyframe action that encodes the task goal. The architecture inherits the Transformer encoder-decoder structure and temporal ensembling concept from ACT, the keyframe detection heuristic from C2F-ARM, and the multi-token prediction regularization from NLP literature. Unlike hierarchical 3D-based methods (PerAct, RVT-2) that rely on point clouds and motion planners in open-loop, CoA operates end-to-end on RGB inputs with closed-loop execution, and unlike CoT-style VLA agents that introduce intermediate semantic representations, it models reasoning directly in action space.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The backward autoregressive formulation addresses the root cause of compounding errors in forward-prediction policies rather than mitigating symptoms through action chunking, yielding a 16–23% improvement over ACT and DP across 60 RLBench tasks with the same base architecture. The spatial generalization analysis—showing CoA's advantage grows with object placement variance—suggests the global-to-local structure provides a principled mechanism for robustness under distribution shift. A key limitation is the reliance on hand-crafted keyframe heuristics for trajectory segmentation, which may not generalize to diverse task types; learning keyframe structures in an unsupervised manner is identified as an important open direction. The substantial remaining gap versus 3D-based hierarchical methods on RLBench-18 (37 vs. 81 average) also signals that RGB-only end-to-end policies still face challenges on precision-critical tasks.
