## Problem

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models trained on offline expert demonstrations lack the ability to learn from failures encountered during real-world deployment. Existing refinement approaches—behavior cloning fails to exploit failure trajectories, while reinforcement learning is unstable for large-scale VLA models—leaving a gap in how to stably and effectively fine-tune VLA policies from sub-optimal human-intervention data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251235/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB observations paired with language instructions, along with interaction trajectories annotated with binary desirability labels (desirable policy actions, undesirable failure actions, and human-corrected actions) collected during human-robot collaboration deployment. It outputs refined discrete action token sequences that decode into continuous robotic actions (e.g., end-effector poses at 20 Hz). A key constraint is that the preference signal is binary per action rather than paired, reflecting the irreversibility of physical interaction where matched positive-negative samples under identical conditions cannot be obtained.

## Method

APO adapts KTO's prospect-theory-based preference alignment to VLA models by replacing paired preference data with binary desirability signals derived from human-robot interaction, using a KL-divergence penalty term z₀ to anchor the updated policy to a reference model. The central technical contribution is an adaptive reweighting mechanism: for each sample, the L1 regression loss between the decoded continuous action and ground truth is computed and batch-normalized, then used to modulate the importance weights λ_D and λ_U in the utility function—increasing the weight of high-error desirable samples and samples proximate to failure actions among undesirable data. This bridges the mismatch between discrete token classification probabilities and continuous action regression errors inherent in autoregressive VLA models. The full pipeline alternates between a deployment phase (human-assisted interaction rollout with real-time correction via SpaceMouse) and an optimization phase (balanced sampling of 50% expert, 25% human-intervention, and 25% failure actions for preference fine-tuning with LoRA), enabling iterative improvement across multiple deployment-optimization loops.

## Results

On four RoboMimic manipulation tasks (Coffee_D0, StackThree_D0, ThreePieceAssembly_D0, Square_D0), APO achieves a mean success rate of 48.0%, outperforming the base OpenVLA policy (40.5%) by 7.5 percentage points and the strongest baseline KTO (43.5%) by 4.5 points. APO's largest gain is on Coffee_D0 (60% vs. 54% for TPO and 48% for KTO). In disruption scenarios (position, background, texture), APO reaches 28.0% mean success rate versus 24.0% for KTO and 21.3% for the base policy. In real-world fine-grained insertion, APO achieves 85% in-distribution and 55% under position/texture disruption, compared to 65%/25% for the base policy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

APO outperforms all five baselines on mean success rate across the four RoboMimic tasks. The two behavior-cloning baselines (Dagger at 39.0% and Sirius at 39.5%) actually underperform the unmodified base policy (40.5%), confirming that standard BC fine-tuning on interaction data causes distribution shift without a regularization mechanism. DPO (37.0%) is the weakest overall, as its reliance on synthetically perturbed negative actions fails to capture real failure modes. TPO/GRaPE (41.5%) shows unstable per-task performance (e.g., 18% on Square_D0) due to random trajectory sampling, while KTO (43.5%) is the strongest baseline thanks to its KL-regularized prospect-theoretic objective. APO's adaptive reweighting provides an additional 4.5-point gain over KTO by dynamically modulating sample weights based on continuous-action L1 error, concentrating gradient updates on failure-prone actions. In disruption and real-world settings, APO consistently leads by 4–10 points over the best competing method.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the KTO (prospect-theoretic preference optimization) framework from LLM alignment to the VLA model setting, specifically addressing two challenges absent in the LLM context: irreversible physical actions that prevent paired positive-negative sampling, and the mismatch between discrete action-token probabilities and continuous action regression errors. It departs from prior human-robot interactive learning methods (DAGGER, Sirius) that rely on behavior cloning objectives, and from RL-based approaches (RLIF, HIL-SERL) that struggle with gradient stability at VLA scale, by introducing an adaptive reweighting mechanism that modulates sample importance based on decoded continuous-action L1 loss. The human-robot collaboration deployment framework for collecting intervention trajectories follows the general paradigm of interactive imitation learning but is specifically structured to produce binary desirability labels rather than reward signals.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

APO provides a practical, stable optimization pathway for iteratively refining VLA models from sub-optimal interaction data during deployment, addressing a critical gap where current VLA models cannot learn from their own failures. The finding that standard behavior cloning degrades below the base model when fine-tuned on mixed expert+interaction data underscores the distribution-shift problem at VLA scale and validates the need for preference-based regularization. Key limitations include evaluation only on autoregressive VLA models (OpenVLA, π0-FAST) with modest absolute gains (~7% in simulation), and the method's dependence on a human operator for real-time intervention, which limits full autonomy. Open questions include generalization to diffusion-based VLA policies, reducing the human intervention ratio over extended deployment, and scaling to more complex multi-task or multi-embodiment settings.
