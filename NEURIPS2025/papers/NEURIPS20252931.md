## Problem

Neural population dynamics are nonlinear and vary over time, trials, and experimental conditions, yet classical linear dynamical system (LDS) models assume time-invariant linear dynamics, while modern nonlinear alternatives (RNNs, LFADS, transformers) sacrifice interpretability and require large datasets. The paper asks whether one can retain the tractable inference and transparent interpretability of linear models while capturing the condition-dependent nonlinear structure that real neural circuits exhibit.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252931/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes per-trial neural firing-rate trajectories (N neurons × T timesteps) together with observed experimental conditions u_t (e.g., continuous head direction θ, or 2D vectors combining reach angle and task phase) that may vary within a trial or remain constant. It produces latent state trajectories x_t, condition-dependent parameter functions A(u), b(u), C(u), d(u), m(u), reconstructed firing rates, a composite dynamics flow field obtained by marginalizing over u, and closed-form fixed points x*(u) = (I − A(u))⁻¹b(u). Because conditions are observed rather than latent, the model can interpolate to entirely unseen condition values, enabling prediction with as few as one trial per condition.

## Method

CLDS parametrizes a family of linear dynamical systems indexed by an observed covariate u_t, where each entry of the dynamics, bias, emission, and initial-condition matrices is a function of u governed by a Gaussian Process prior approximated via a finite Fourier feature expansion. Inference proceeds with an EM algorithm: the E-step performs exact Kalman smoothing to obtain latent state moments, and the M-step reduces to solving a Sylvester equation in an expanded Kronecker feature space, yielding closed-form updates with monotonic marginal log-likelihood guarantees. The GP prior's length-scale hyperparameter κ controls the trade-off between a time-invariant LDS (κ→∞) and independent per-condition dynamics (κ→0), and the model can be viewed as a first-order Taylor linearization of a fully nonlinear system in (x, u) around E[x|u], with explicit upper bounds on the approximation error in terms of the Hessian of the true dynamics and the conditional variance of x given u.

## Results

On the synthetic head-direction ring attractor, CLDS achieves a co-smoothing R² of 0.86 for held-out single-neuron reconstruction and recovers the true dynamics matrix A(θ), bias b(θ), and eigenvalues with high fidelity. On real mouse ADn recordings, the model recovers empirical tuning curves almost exactly and identifies a ring attractor structure with HD-dependent fixed points. On the macaque PMd reaching task, CLDS outperforms all baselines in co-smoothing R², with the largest advantage at 1 training trial per condition (the most data-limited regime). The model also captures nonlinear ring-attractor dynamics from a non-CLDS generative model (SPDE-based), demonstrating robustness to model mismatch.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares CLDS against three baselines on the macaque PMd reaching task (Fig. 4e): a standard LDS, gpSLDS (with rSLDS as a special case), and LFADS. CLDS achieves the highest co-smoothing R² across all data regimes, with the largest margin over baselines at 1 training trial per reach angle. gpSLDS sometimes approaches CLDS performance when more trials per condition are available, but consistently underperforms in the severely data-limited (1-trial) setting. LFADS shows progressively better performance as more data is added but has not yet plateaued and underperforms CLDS in all low-data regimes tested. The standard LDS baseline, which uses only additive inputs of the form Bu_t, is substantially outperformed by CLDS, confirming that the nonlinear condition-dependent parameterization is critical. The key advantage of CLDS is its data efficiency: the GP prior over parameters enables statistical power sharing across nearby conditions, allowing accurate inference even with a single trial per condition.

## Relation to Prior Work

CLDS sits at the intersection of classical linear dynamical system (LDS) modeling and modern nonlinear approaches to neural dynamics. It extends GPFA by allowing the latent dynamics parameters (not just the latent trajectories) to vary as a function of observed experimental covariates, and generalizes Wishart process models from single-time-step condition-dependent noise correlations to multi-step condition-dependent dynamics. The key departure from switching LDS models (rSLDS, gpSLDS) is that the 'switching' between dynamical regimes is governed by an observed covariate vector rather than a discrete latent process, which enables closed-form EM inference via Kalman smoothing and a Sylvester equation in an expanded Fourier-feature space. The theoretical connection to SLDS is made precise by showing that a one-dimensional SLDS induces a specific GP kernel over time, positioning CLDS as a continuous-covariate generalization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in data efficiency: by placing a smooth GP prior over condition-dependent parameters, CLDS can share statistical power across nearby conditions and make accurate predictions with as few as one trial per condition, a regime where fully nonlinear models like LFADS underperform. This is directly relevant to neural recording experiments that are often trial-limited. Key limitations include the requirement for observed covariates (performance degrades during forecasting or with partial observations), the assumption of conditional linearity (which may fail for cognitive tasks with long internal deliberation periods), and the current restriction to Gaussian observation noise. Open directions include extending to Poisson/COM-Poisson spike-count likelihoods, handling partially observed covariates, and leveraging the Wishart process connection to estimate across-time noise correlations in trial-limited settings.
