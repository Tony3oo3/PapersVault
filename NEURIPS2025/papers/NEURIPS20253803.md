## Problem

Existing deformable 3D Gaussian Splatting methods rely entirely on gradient-based optimization to recover all 3D motion, which struggles to converge on real-world datasets with complex, large-scale motion due to the high degrees of freedom involved. The paper identifies that 3D motion observability is inherently anisotropic: components projecting onto the camera image plane are directly measurable via optical flow, while components orthogonal to the image plane must be inferred from additional viewpoints, yet prior methods treat all motion components uniformly.

## Input / Output

The method consumes multi-view video frames (Neu3DV at 1352×1014 with 15–20 cameras; CMU-Panoptic at 640×360 with 31 cameras), per-frame 2D object masks (from SAM-track or dataset-provided), dense optical flow (DIS by default), and the 3D Gaussian representation from the previous frame or an initial COLMAP-based reconstruction. It outputs per-frame updated 3D Gaussian attributes (position, rotation, scale, opacity, SH coefficients) and a compact set of H3D control points (0.2k–2.5k per object, each with 9 attributes), which together enable real-time novel-view rendering. The streaming pipeline operates in a Group-of-Scenes (GOS-N) structure where one keyframe is followed by N−1 non-keyframes, with keyframes receiving full optimization and non-keyframes updating only control point parameters.

## Method

The core contribution is H3D control points, a heterogeneous motion representation that decouples each control point's 3D translation and rotation into observable and learnable components. In a local ray coordinate system aligned with the camera-to-point direction, the 2D translation (V_x, V_y) and 1D rotation (ω_z) are directly computed by back-projecting optical flow over a small pixel neighborhood, while the 1D depth translation (V̂_z) and 2D in-plane rotations (ω̂_x, ω̂_y) remain learnable parameters optimized via gradient descent. Each moving Gaussian receives its motion by inverse-distance-weighted interpolation from its K=3 nearest control points, and the streaming pipeline chains 3D segmentation (multi-view mask voting), H3D control point generation (uniform grid sampling per camera), object-wise motion manipulation, and keyframe-based residual compensation to prevent error accumulation. This structural decoupling eliminates the need for complex loss regularizations, allowing convergence within 100 iterations and 2 seconds per frame on a single RTX 4070 GPU using only the standard L1 + D-SSIM rendering loss.

## Results

On the Neu3DV dataset (6 sequences, 15–20 cameras), H3D-DGS achieves an average PSNR of 30.91 dB, SSIM of 0.9437, and LPIPS of 0.0941, outperforming all five baselines on every metric. Training time is dramatically reduced to 1.89 hours versus 57.35 h for Dynamic-GS and 6.88 h for 4D-GS, with per-frame processing at ~2 s on a single RTX 4070. On CMU-Panoptic (3 sequences, 31 cameras), the method improves PSNR over Dynamic-GS on softball (27.48 vs. 26.93) and boxes (27.88 vs. 27.79), though it trails on basketball (27.72 vs. 28.54).

## Baselines & Comparisons

H3D-DGS outperforms all five baselines on the Neu3DV average metrics. Against the closest competitor 4D-GS (global implicit deformation field), it gains +0.42 dB PSNR, +0.0036 SSIM, and −0.0057 LPIPS while training 3.6× faster (1.89 h vs. 6.88 h). Against Dynamic-GS (per-Gaussian motion residuals), the PSNR gap is +3.26 dB with a 30× speedup in training. MA-GS, which also uses optical flow but via gradient-based implicit fields, is surpassed by +2.15 dB PSNR. SP-GS (superpoint clustering) lags by +3.88 dB PSNR. On CMU-Panoptic, where only Dynamic-GS is compared, H3D-DGS wins on softball and boxes but loses on basketball (27.72 vs. 28.54 PSNR), likely due to the large-scale ball motion challenging the local control-point interpolation. SC-GS is excluded from the Neu3DV average because it failed to converge on most sequences, reporting results only on sear_steak and coffee_martini where it still trails H3D-DGS by ~1.6 dB PSNR.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the deformable 3D Gaussian Splatting line of work (Dynamic-GS, 4D-GS, MA-GS, SP-GS, SC-GS), which typically learns all motion parameters via gradient-based optimization over implicit neural fields or dense per-Gaussian residuals. The key departure is the introduction of H3D control points that structurally decouple 3D motion into an observable component (recovered directly from optical flow back-projection) and an unobservable component (learned via gradients), drawing on classical graphics control-point ideas (ED-graphs) and the observation that optical flow is the 2D projection of scene flow. The streaming pipeline with keyframe-based residual compensation extends the NeRFPlayer/3DGStream paradigm to the Gaussian splatting setting with a compact, object-wise motion representation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 100× convergence speedup (100 iterations vs. ~10k) and 2-second-per-frame processing on a single RTX 4070 make streaming 4D reconstruction practically viable for near-real-time applications, a regime where prior deformable 3DGS methods were infeasible. The compact control-point representation (0.2% of Gaussian count) also enables user-driven motion manipulation, opening a path toward editable dynamic scenes. Open questions include whether the method extends to monocular video, how to mitigate background jitter from the incremental streaming design, and whether the initial static reconstruction bottleneck can be decoupled from the motion pipeline.
