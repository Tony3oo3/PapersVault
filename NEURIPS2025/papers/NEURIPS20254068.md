## Problem

Robotic manipulation requires task-relevant tabletop scenes for policy training, but existing scene construction methods rely on labor-intensive manual design or purely randomized layouts that lack plausibility and task alignment. The core difficulty is the substantial representational gap between high-level natural language task instructions and structured 3D spatial configurations, compounded by the absence of large-scale datasets featuring realistic layouts with complex inter-object relations such as stacking and containment.

## Input / Output

The method takes a natural language manipulation task instruction (e.g., 'Organize fruit from bowl into empty bowl and place tray near lamp'), a rectangular tabletop surface size in centimeters, and optionally a no-placement area (e.g., a sink region for bathroom vanities). It produces a structured 3D scene layout specifying each object's position, axis-aligned bounding box size, rotation angle, and textual description, along with a symbolic scene graph encoding inter-object spatial relations. The layout is then resolved into a complete 3D tabletop scene by retrieving matching assets from a 12,000+ object database using a weighted combination of SBERT text similarity (α=0.9) and cosine size similarity (β=0.1).

## Method

MesaTask fine-tunes Qwen3-8B with a two-stage procedure: supervised fine-tuning on 50,000 task-scene pairs derived from the MesaTask-10K dataset, followed by Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) on 10,000 positive-negative layout pairs. The key architectural insight is a Spatial Reasoning Chain that decomposes generation into a structured chain of thought: (1) object list completion from task-relevant objects, (2) inter-object relation inference conditioned on task instructions and co-occurrence patterns, (3) scene graph construction with nodes enriched by coarse 3×3 grid positions and 8-direction orientations, and (4) final 3D layout output. DPO negative examples are generated via three targeted corruptions—geometric perturbation to induce collisions, scene graph relation replacement for semantic misalignment, and removal of task-critical objects—each addressing a specific SFT failure mode. The supporting dataset MesaTask-10K (~10,700 scenes, 200+ object classes, 6 table types) is built by generating scene images with FLUX, recovering coarse 3D layouts via depth estimation and asset retrieval, then refining layouts manually in Blender with physics validation in IsaacSim.

## Results

MesaTask achieves a FID of 40.3 on the MesaTask-10K test set, substantially outperforming GPT-4o (74.4), Holodeck-table (91.3), and I-Design-table (96.0). On the proposed GPT-Score protocol, MesaTask scores 8.25/10 on average (with 7.22 on Consistency-with-Task and 7.53 on Layout Coherence & Realism), compared to 7.09 for GPT-4o, 5.42 for Holodeck-table, and 6.87 for I-Design-table. In a 127-participant user study, MesaTask receives the highest preference score of 6.12/10, nearly doubling the next-best baseline (GPT-4o at 4.21). The full model achieves a 99.1% success rate in producing parseable scene layouts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MesaTask decisively outperforms all three baselines on every reported metric. Against GPT-4o (zero-shot), MesaTask reduces FID from 74.4 to 40.3 and improves the GPT-Score average from 7.09 to 8.25, with the largest gap on Consistency-with-Task (7.22 vs. 5.30), indicating far better task-scene alignment. Against Holodeck-table, MesaTask wins on FID (40.3 vs. 91.3), GPT-Score (8.25 vs. 5.42), and user preference (6.12 vs. 2.29); Holodeck-table's optimization-based placement produces overly simplistic and symmetrical layouts that fail to capture complex inter-object relations like stacking and containment. Against I-Design-table, MesaTask achieves a much higher success rate (99.1% vs. 56.5%), lower FID (40.3 vs. 96.0), and a substantially better user-study score (6.12 vs. 1.73), as I-Design's backtracking placement struggles with the dense, small-scale object arrangements typical of tabletop scenes. The ablation rows further show that the spatial reasoning chain contributes ~1.2 FID points and ~0.1 GPT-Score points over the no-reasoning variant, while DPO adds an additional ~0.4 user-study points by reducing collisions and improving task-object fidelity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of LLM-based scene generation (LayoutGPT, Holodeck, I-Design) and tabletop scene datasets (TO-Scene, SetItUp), but departs from both in key ways. Unlike prior LLM-based generators that operate zero-shot on indoor scenes or use multi-stage prompting, MesaTask introduces a structured Spatial Reasoning Chain (object completion → interrelation inference → scene graph → 3D layout) trained via SFT and DPO on a purpose-built dataset. The dataset construction pipeline—text-to-image generation (FLUX) → depth estimation (Depth Anything V2) → 3D asset retrieval → human refinement in Blender → physics simulation (IsaacSim)—repurposes components from single-image 3D reconstruction and 3D asset curation (Objaverse, PartNet-Mobility) into a novel workflow for creating task-relevant tabletop scenes with rich inter-object relations like stacking and containment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MesaTask-10K is the first large-scale dataset specifically designed for task-oriented tabletop scene generation, filling a gap that hinders training of robot manipulation policies on diverse, physically plausible scenes. The spatial reasoning chain provides a reusable decomposition that could transfer to other structured 3D generation tasks, and the DPO-based quality refinement for 3D layouts is a practical recipe applicable beyond tabletops. Key limitations include reliance on a fixed 3D asset database (capping object diversity), labor-intensive human annotation for layout refinement, and evaluation via GPT-4o scoring that may share blind spots with the generation pipeline. Open questions include whether the approach scales to full-room scenes, whether 3D object generation can replace retrieval, and how generated scenes actually improve downstream robot policy learning.
