## Problem

State-of-the-art test-time adaptation (TTA) methods incur substantial per-batch latency from backpropagation, data augmentation, and model ensembling, making them impractical for latency-sensitive edge deployments such as autonomous driving or real-time health monitoring. Naively reducing adaptation frequency (sparse TTA) to cut latency causes severe accuracy degradation because only a small fraction of the data stream is used for updates. The paper addresses how to maintain competitive accuracy under very low adaptation rates (as low as 1%) while drastically reducing computational overhead on resource-constrained hardware.

## Input / Output

SNAP consumes unlabeled RGB test images in batches of 16 (32×32×3 for CIFAR, 224×224×3 for ImageNet) alongside a pre-trained backbone (ResNet-18/50 or ViT-Base) and produces per-sample class predictions at every inference step. Model parameter updates occur only every k batches (controlled by the adaptation rate), while a fixed-size memory buffer (equal to batch size) continuously stores representative samples and their early-layer feature statistics. On non-adaptation batches, the model still performs inference with corrected normalization statistics derived from the memory, so no backpropagation is triggered.

## Method

SNAP introduces two plug-in components that integrate with existing TTA algorithms (Tent, CoTTA, EATA, SAR, RoTTA). First, Class and Domain Representative Memory (CnDRM) maintains a compact buffer of samples selected by two criteria: class representativeness (high pseudo-label confidence with prediction-balanced selection across classes) and domain representativeness (minimum Wasserstein-2 distance to a momentum-tracked domain centroid computed from early-layer batch-normalization statistics). When the buffer overflows, the sample farthest from the domain centroid within the most overrepresented class is evicted. Second, Inference-only Batch-aware Memory Normalization (IoBMN) corrects normalization statistics at every inference step by applying a soft-shrinkage adjustment that blends the memory's adapted statistics with the current batch's statistics, weighted by their sampling variances. This dual mechanism allows the model to remain aligned with the evolving target distribution between sparse update events without any backpropagation, reducing latency by up to 93% while keeping accuracy drops below 3.3% across adaptation rates from 1% to 50%.

## Results

On CIFAR100-C (ResNet18, AR=0.1), SNAP maintains or exceeds full-adaptation accuracy for 3 of 5 baselines: Tent+SNAP reaches 55.84% (vs. 55.76% original), CoTTA+SNAP 50.52% (vs. 49.39%), and EATA+SNAP 52.35% (vs. 50.74%), while SAR and RoTTA drop by 2.18% and 0.22% respectively. On ImageNet-C (ResNet50), SAR+SNAP surpasses the original by +2.65% (41.99% vs. 39.34%) and CoTTA+SNAP by +1.47% (30.19% vs. 28.72%), while Tent, EATA, and RoTTA see drops of 0.50%, 4.32%, and 1.36%. Across all settings, the maximum accuracy drop from full adaptation is 4.32% (EATA on ImageNet-C), and latency is reduced by up to 93.12% (CoTTA on Raspberry Pi 4: 74.77 s → 4.95 s per batch). Naive STTA (no SNAP) suffers much larger drops, e.g., CoTTA loses 13.53% on CIFAR100-C and 5.95% for EATA on ImageNet-C.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SNAP is integrated with five SOTA TTA baselines (Tent, CoTTA, EATA, SAR, RoTTA) and evaluated at adaptation rates from 0.01 to 0.5. CoTTA benefits most from SNAP: at AR=0.1 it gains +1.13% on CIFAR100-C and +1.47% on ImageNet-C over its full-adaptation accuracy, while naive STTA causes a catastrophic 13.53% drop on CIFAR100-C. SAR shows the largest ImageNet-C improvement (+2.65%) but a modest 2.18% drop on CIFAR100-C. Tent and RoTTA are essentially matched to their full-adaptation counterparts (within 0.5% and 1.4% respectively). EATA is the only baseline where SNAP underperforms the original on ImageNet-C (−4.32%), though it still exceeds naive STTA by 1.63%. In all cases, SNAP reduces per-batch latency by 26–93% depending on the method and device, with the largest gains for compute-heavy methods like CoTTA (93.12% on Raspberry Pi 4) and RoTTA (75.62% on Raspberry Pi 4).

## Relation to Prior Work

SNAP positions itself as a lightweight, plug-in module that integrates with existing backpropagation-based TTA methods (Tent, CoTTA, EATA, SAR, RoTTA) rather than replacing them, addressing the underexplored latency bottleneck on edge devices. Its sampling strategy (CnDRM) repurposes ideas from data-efficient deep learning—specifically Moderate Coreset's centroid-based selection and confidence-based filtering—into the unsupervised TTA setting where ground-truth labels are unavailable. The IoBMN component extends test-time normalization work (Nado et al., Schneider et al.) by incorporating memory-based statistics with soft-shrinkage correction, bridging the gap between adaptation-time and inference-time normalization. The paper also draws on Wasserstein distance from domain generalization literature to quantify domain representativeness of individual samples.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SNAP addresses a practical gap that has been largely overlooked: TTA latency on resource-constrained edge devices, where even lightweight methods incur multi-second per-batch overheads. The demonstrated 93% latency reduction with under 3.3% accuracy loss across five SOTA methods and three benchmarks makes TTA viable for latency-sensitive applications like autonomous driving and real-time health monitoring. Key limitations include the fixed adaptation rate and confidence threshold, and the inability to distribute backpropagation across batches due to PyTorch constraints. Open questions include whether dynamically adjusting the adaptation rate based on detected distribution shift magnitude could further improve responsiveness, and whether the sparse-update paradigm extends to non-visual modalities or larger foundation models.
