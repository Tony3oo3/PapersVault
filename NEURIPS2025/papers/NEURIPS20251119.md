## Problem

Current LLM training data markets operate under exploitative pricing that systematically undervalues human annotators' labor, driving high-quality sellers out of the market and degrading long-term data supply. No formal economic framework existed to model the strategic buyer-seller interactions in LLM data markets or to demonstrate that such exploitative pricing is ultimately self-defeating for buyers as well. This paper asks: can a pricing mechanism grounded in data valuation simultaneously maximize seller profit, sustain market participation, and optimize buyer utility over the long term?

## Input / Output

The framework consumes per-sample data valuation scores (computed via InflIP, DataInf, or BM25 against a representative validation set), buyer budget constraints, and LLM model parameters (Pythia-1b/410m, Llama 3.2-Instruct-1b) to produce optimal per-dataset prices and binary purchasing decisions. A key structural constraint is the information-transparency assumption: both buyers and sellers observe the same valuation scores, which serve as common signals for pricing and purchasing. The output is a Stackelberg equilibrium price vector where each seller's price equals one of the buyers' Maximum Willingness to Pay values, along with the corresponding buyer selection vectors that maximize net utility under budget.

## Method

The paper models the LLM data market as a non-cooperative Stackelberg game where sellers (leaders) set prices anticipating buyer demand, and buyers (followers) select datasets to maximize net utility (utility gain minus acquisition cost). The core pricing mechanism defines each buyer's Maximum Willingness to Pay (MWP) as the minimum of the marginal utility gain from adding a dataset and the buyer's remaining budget surplus; the seller's optimal price is proven to be one of the buyers' MWP values (Lemma 2). The paper then proves via a Bellman-equation analysis over an infinite horizon that any exploitative pricing (price below MWP) is strictly suboptimal after a finite threshold, because declining seller participation (modeled by a multiplicative participation probability) erodes future data supply—a formalization of the 'market for lemons' dynamic. Data valuation scores from influence-function methods (InflIP, DataInf) or lexical similarity (BM25) provide the per-sample utility estimates that drive the pricing, and the framework is validated through 100-step market simulations across three NLP tasks (MathQA, MedQA, PIQA) with LoRA fine-tuning, showing fairshare pricing yields higher performance-per-dollar for buyers and sustained seller participation compared to exploitative, random, or reduced pricing baselines.

## Results

Under fairshare pricing, sellers achieve the highest cumulative profit over 100 time steps, while exploitative pricing (fixed at 10% of average utility) triggers rapid seller exit and data supply collapse. For the high-budget buyer, fairshare maximizes long-term cumulative utility; for the low-budget buyer, fairshare trades short-term utility for sustained long-term data availability. In the data valuation ablation, InflIP delivers the best performance-per-dollar tradeoff across MathQA, MedQA, and PIQA, outperforming constant, random, and BM25 baselines. The Spearman correlation between InflIP and the one-step-training oracle is 0.54 on MathQA and 0.42 on PIQA, confirming reasonable valuation accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four pricing strategies (fairshare, reduced at 50% of fairshare, random, and exploitative at 10% of average utility) across three LLMs (Pythia-1b, Pythia-410m, Llama 3.2-Instruct-1b) on MedQA, MathQA, and PIQA. Fairshare consistently outperforms all alternatives in long-term seller profit and buyer cumulative utility, particularly for well-funded buyers; exploitative pricing initially benefits buyers but causes irreversible seller exit within ~20–30 time steps, after which even high-budget buyers face data shortages. Among data valuation methods, InflIP and DataInf (influence-based) both outperform BM25 (lexical similarity) and the constant/random baselines in model performance per dollar spent, with InflIP offering the strongest cost-efficiency balance. The framework is shown to be method-agnostic: fairshare pricing yields mutually beneficial outcomes regardless of which valuation method is used, though the quality of the valuation score affects the magnitude of buyer gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper integrates three previously separate research threads: Stackelberg game-theoretic pricing models (from industrial organization and supply chain literature), LLM-specific data valuation methods (InflIP, DataInf, BM25), and the emerging data market design literature. Prior data market work (auctions, personalized pricing) was conducted on classic ML models and assumed myopic buyers with fixed seller participation, while prior data valuation work focused on selection without connecting to pricing incentives. The paper's key departure is formalizing the LLM data market as a non-cooperative Stackelberg game where data valuation scores serve as the shared signal linking seller pricing to buyer purchasing decisions, and proving that the seller's profit-maximizing price (MWP) coincides with the buyer's long-run utility-maximizing price.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first formal economic framework showing that exploitative data pricing is provably suboptimal for both buyers and sellers in LLM training markets, connecting the well-known 'market for lemons' mechanism to the concrete context of AI data labor. This offers actionable guidance for platform designers and policymakers seeking to structure fair data procurement. Key limitations include the strong information-transparency assumption (all parties observe true data values), a simplified linear participation-decay model, and empirical validation on small open-source models (≤1B) with LoRA fine-tuning on relatively narrow tasks. Open questions include extending to incomplete-information (Bayesian) settings, pretraining-scale data markets, and multi-round strategic interactions beyond the one-shot Stackelberg structure.
