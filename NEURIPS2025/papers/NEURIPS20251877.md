## Problem

Deep neural networks trained with SGD exhibit a range of emergent phenomena—universal representation alignment across independently trained models, progressive sharpening and flattening of the loss landscape, and systematic gradient balance across layers—that cannot be explained by explicit loss minimization alone. While the notion of 'implicit bias' is widely acknowledged, its precise mathematical mechanism has remained elusive. This paper asks: what is the exact force arising from stochasticity and discrete-time updates that drives these universal behaviors?

## Input / Output

The framework takes as input the per-sample loss function, a learning rate (scalar η or general symmetric matrix Λ), weight decay γ, and minibatch sampling distribution. It produces an entropic loss F_{η,γ} that augments the standard empirical risk with an entropy term S(θ) proportional to the expected squared gradient norm. The key outputs are closed-form balance equations (equipartition theorems) constraining gradient second-moments across layers and neurons, a proof of perfect representation alignment for deep linear networks, and analytic expressions for the sharpness of the converged solution as a function of label-noise covariance. The method is architecture-agnostic in principle but is instantiated for ReLU networks, polynomial networks, self-attention layers, and deep linear networks.

## Method

The paper derives an entropic loss φ_η via a 'renormalization' argument: running n steps of gradient descent on φ_η with learning rate η/n reproduces one step of SGD on the original loss, and taking n→∞ yields a loss whose gradient flow exactly matches discrete-time SGD (Theorem 1). The leading-order correction is S(θ) = (1/4)E_B‖√Λ E_{x∈B}∇ℓ(x,θ)‖², an effective entropy that penalizes gradient fluctuation. The central result (Theorem 2) shows this entropy term breaks all continuous parameter symmetries except norm-preserving (rotational) ones, while discrete orthogonal symmetries are preserved (Theorem 3). A Master Balance Theorem (Theorem 4) then implies that at any local minimum, gradients along positive and negative eigendirections of the symmetry generator must balance, yielding layer-wise and neuron-wise equipartition theorems (Theorems 5–7). Applied to deep linear networks, the framework proves the Platonic Representation Hypothesis: at the global minimum of the entropic loss, every hidden layer of two independently trained networks is perfectly aligned up to a rotation and scaling, independent of initialization (Theorem 8). The sharpness paradox is resolved by showing that label-noise imbalance, combined with the entropic force, can drive the solution to arbitrarily sharp minima even though SGD has no inherent preference for flatness (Theorem 10).

## Results

The paper is primarily theoretical, with empirical validation rather than benchmark comparisons. Key quantitative findings include: (1) ResNet18 on CIFAR-10 reaches 98% training / 88% test accuracy, with entropy (gradient norm) spiking at learning-rate drops as predicted by the entropic loss; (2) independently trained 6-layer networks on MNIST achieve near-perfect representation alignment (cosine similarity ≈ 1) between every pair of hidden layers, even when inputs are randomly transformed; (3) ViT-B (86M) and ViT-H (300M) pretrained on ImageNet show strong CKA alignment between corresponding MLP and self-attention layers; (4) layer and neuron gradient balance (Theorems 5–6) approaches zero during training and correlates with entropy decrease, not loss decrease; (5) the predicted sharpness formula (Eq. 16) matches empirical ηλ_max values, with label-noise imbalance driving solutions toward the edge-of-stability boundary (ηλ_max ≈ 2).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper without standard method-vs-method baseline comparisons. Instead, the empirical experiments serve to validate the paper's own theorems against observed training dynamics. The entropic loss predictions are compared against (a) the actual gradient balance in trained ReLU, linear, and self-attention networks (Theorems 5–7 verified: imbalance metrics approach zero and track entropy, not loss); (b) the universal representation alignment observed in independently trained networks (Theorem 8 verified: alignment ≈ 1 for SGD with no weight decay, but disappears under large weight decay per Theorem 9); and (c) the edge-of-stability sharpness boundary (Theorem 10 verified: ηλ_max stays below 2, and increasing label-noise imbalance monotonically increases sharpness). The paper also contrasts its entropic-force explanation against the prior 'modified loss' framework [22–24], showing that the first-order entropic term (gradient fluctuation penalty) is the key symmetry-breaking mechanism, whereas prior work focused on the second-order (flatness-seeking) term.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate lines of work: the 'modified loss' / effective landscape literature (Barrett & Dherin 2020; Smith et al. 2021; Dandi et al. 2022), which showed that discrete-time SGD implicitly optimizes a perturbed loss, and the parameter symmetry literature (Liu et al. 2024; Tanaka & Kunin 2021; Zhao et al. 2022), which showed that continuous symmetries in the loss yield conservation laws that make SGD initialization-dependent. The key conceptual departure is identifying the modified loss's gradient-fluctuation term as an *entropic force* that systematically breaks continuous symmetries while preserving discrete ones, thereby reframing symmetry-breaking as a thermodynamic effect rather than a purely dynamical one. For the Platonic Representation Hypothesis (Huh et al. 2024), prior work only hypothesized positive cross-model alignment; this paper provides the first proof of *perfect* alignment under specific conditions, inverting the explanatory burden so that misalignment becomes the phenomenon to be explained.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a single formalism (entropic loss + symmetry breaking) that simultaneously explains gradient balance across layers, universal representation alignment, and the sharpness paradox (edge of stability vs. flatness-seeking), which were previously treated as separate puzzles. The counterintuitive finding that weight decay *destroys* universal representation (Theorem 9) has practical implications for training protocols, and the data-imbalance mechanism for progressive sharpening (Theorem 10) suggests that label noise structure in language models may drive sharpness. Major limitations include the restriction to explicit (exact) symmetries, first-order entropic corrections, and scalar learning rates; the authors note that extending to approximate symmetries and higher-order effects is an important open direction, and the main proofs are for deep linear networks with ViT experiments limited to pretrained checkpoints.
