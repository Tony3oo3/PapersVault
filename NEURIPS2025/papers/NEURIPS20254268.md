## Problem

Standard hierarchical clustering produces a single tree of nested partitions, limiting users to one clustering perspective per data fit. Switching between paradigms (e.g., from density-based to center-based) requires re-running costly fitting procedures. This paper asks whether a single ultrametric representation can yield multiple, equally meaningful cluster hierarchies at negligible additional cost.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254268/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a point dataset with pairwise distances and first fits an ultrametric (DC tree, Cover tree, KD tree, or HST-DPO), represented as an LCA-tree where leaf-to-leaf distances are given by the value at their lowest common ancestor. It then produces the full set of optimal center-based clustering solutions for all k ∈ [1, n] simultaneously, organized as a cluster hierarchy (a rooted tree of nested partitions). Any flat partition can be extracted in O(n) time via elbow, stability, thresholding, or the proposed Median-of-Elbows method. The ultrametric fitting is the computational bottleneck (seconds to minutes), while hierarchy construction and partition extraction take only milliseconds.

## Method

The core result proves that all center-based clustering objectives (k-center, k-median, k-means) reduce to sorting in any relaxed ultrametric, yielding optimal solutions for all k in Sort(n) time. For k-center, the farthest-first traversal algorithm becomes exactly optimal under the strong triangle inequality, and its execution reduces to sorting the O(n) unique LCA-distances from largest to smallest. For (k,z)-clustering (z=1 for k-median, z=2 for k-means), the paper shows that the cost-decreases of adding each optimal center themselves satisfy the strong triangle inequality, enabling a reduction to k-center in a derived relaxed ultrametric. The resulting optimal partitions are provably hierarchical (each P_k is obtained by splitting one cluster from P_{k-1}), and the elbow plot is guaranteed convex. The SHiP framework packages this as a three-step pipeline: fit an ultrametric, choose a center-based hierarchy on it, and extract a partition—enabling rapid exploration of many clustering perspectives from a single data fit.

## Results

On the DC tree ultrametric, the SHiP framework's best hierarchy/partition combination (varying by dataset) achieves ARI values that match or exceed Euclidean k-means with ground-truth k on 9 of 13 datasets, e.g., 99.3 on Boxes (vs. 93.5 for k-means), 75.4 on PenDigits (vs. 55.3), and 81.2 on COIL20 (vs. 58.2). However, on image datasets like USPS (33.7 vs. 52.3) and MNIST (46.0 vs. 52.7 for Ward), the DC tree combinations fall short of Ward clustering. Cover tree combinations consistently underperform Euclidean k-means across all datasets (e.g., 2.6 vs. 93.5 on Boxes), revealing that HST-based ultrametrics fail to preserve underlying cluster structure.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares SHiP (DC tree and Cover tree variants) against five baselines: Euclidean k-means (with GT k), SCAR (spectral clustering, GT k), Ward agglomerative clustering (GT k), AMD-DBSCAN, and DPC. On the DC tree, SHiP's best combination beats or matches Euclidean k-means on 9/13 datasets (e.g., +5.8 ARI on Boxes, +20.1 on PenDigits, +23.0 on COIL20) but loses to Ward on USPS (33.7 vs. 63.9) and MNIST (46.0 vs. 52.7). SCAR is consistently the weakest competitor, often scoring near or below 0 ARI, while AMD-DBSCAN and DPC show highly variable performance (e.g., DPC scores 71.5 on lactate but 0.0 on USPS and MNIST). Cover tree combinations are uniformly inferior to Euclidean k-means across all 13 datasets, with gaps as large as 90.9 ARI on Boxes (2.6 vs. 93.5), challenging the assumption that HSTs are well-suited for preserving cluster structure. The key practical advantage is that SHiP's DC tree hierarchies are computed in milliseconds after the initial ultrametric fit, enabling rapid exploration of multiple clustering perspectives without re-running expensive algorithms.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies and generalizes two prior lines of work: Beer et al.'s optimal k-center algorithm in the dc-dist ultrametric (O(n²) time) and Cohen-Addad et al.'s optimal k-median algorithm in 2-HST ultrametrics (O(n log²(Δ+n)) time). The key conceptual departure is showing that *all* center-based objectives (k-center, k-median, k-means) in *any* relaxed ultrametric reduce to sorting, via the observation that farthest-first traversal becomes exact under the strong triangle inequality. The SHiP framework then repurposes the dc-dist (already computed during HDBSCAN/DBSCAN execution) as a universal substrate from which multiple novel cluster hierarchies can be extracted for free, rather than treating hierarchical clustering as a single fixed dendrogram.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Sort(n) time bound (proven tight) means that once an ultrametric is fitted, an essentially unlimited menu of optimal center-based hierarchies becomes available at negligible cost, fundamentally changing the exploratory clustering workflow. A notable negative finding is that HSTs (Cover trees, KD trees, HST-DPOs) consistently fail to preserve underlying cluster structure regardless of hierarchy or partitioning method, challenging their assumed suitability for accelerating precision-sensitive ML tasks. The main open limitation is that the initial ultrametric fitting remains the computational bottleneck, and the clustering quality is heavily dependent on which ultrametric is chosen; developing fast, high-fidelity ultrametric fitting algorithms is identified as the key future direction.
