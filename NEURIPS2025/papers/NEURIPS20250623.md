## Problem

When multiple observations of the same instance are available (e.g., several images of one plant in citizen science platforms like Pl@ntNet), naively aggregating classifier scores before applying conformal prediction breaks exchangeability and yields impractically large prediction sets. Simultaneously, enforcing class-conditional coverage requires sufficient calibration points per class, which is infeasible for the highly imbalanced class distributions typical of citizen science data. The paper addresses both issues by performing conformal prediction at the individual-observation level and aggregating the resulting p-values rather than the raw scores.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pre-trained classifier (e.g., ResNet-50 with softmax output), a labeled calibration set of n points, and m observations (images) of a single test instance. It outputs a prediction set C_α ⊆ [K] with a class-conditional coverage guarantee P[y ∈ C_α | Y_{n+1}=y] ≥ 1−α for every class y. The score function is 1 minus the softmax probability, and ties in scores (common with neural network outputs) are handled via a randomization step that draws i.i.d. uniform variables to break ties, yielding a randomized p-value vector whose exact distribution is known.

## Method

The key theoretical contribution is Theorem 3.5, which shows that under exchangeability of scores within the true class, the ordered vector of class-conditional conformal p-values is uniformly distributed over the set of non-decreasing discrete trajectories A_{n_y,m}, with each marginal n_y·p_{(j)} following a Beta-Binomial(n_y, j, m−j+1) distribution. Building on this, the paper proposes a general p-value aggregation framework: one defines a score function v on the p-value vector, calibrates it by Monte Carlo sampling from U(A_{n_y,m}) (via a Pólya-urn simulation), and retains labels whose v-score exceeds the empirical α-quantile. Concrete score functions include a quantile envelope (min over j of the Beta-Binomial CDF), an ℓ^q-area norm (generalizing the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test), and an ℓ^q distance to the asymptotic identity vector (j/(m+1)). The paper also refines majority voting by replacing the fixed m/2 threshold with the α-quantile of the exact Beta-Binomial distribution of the number of sets containing the true label, yielding smaller prediction sets while preserving the class-conditional guarantee.

## Results

On the synthetic Gaussian mixture dataset (K=10, d=6, base classifier top-1 accuracy 0.49), the ℓ₂-envelope p-value aggregation method reduces the average prediction set size from 6.6 (single observation) to 3.6 at m=20, while maintaining marginal coverage above 1−α=0.9 and class-conditional coverage close to 0.9. On the LifeCLEF Plant Identification Task 2015 (K=688 species, ResNet-50 with 43% top-1 accuracy), all p-value aggregation methods achieve valid coverage ≥0.9 and produce prediction sets smaller than 100 classes even at m=1 (with temperature=20), whereas the majority vote baseline with raw softmax outputs (Temp=1) predicts nearly all 688 classes. The Beta-Binomial refinement of majority voting improves over the original Gasparin–Ramdas methods but remains outperformed by the p-value aggregation approaches in set size.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the majority voting framework of Gasparin and Ramdas (2024), which aggregates class-conditional conformal sets by retaining labels appearing in at least half the sets (Majority C^M) or in all prefix subsets (Exchangeable Majority C^Me). The proposed p-value aggregation methods (Quantile, Wilcoxon/ℓ₁-Area, ℓ₂-Area, and ℓ₂-envelope) decisively outperform both majority vote variants on prediction set size across all values of m in both the synthetic and LifeCLEF experiments, while maintaining the required class-conditional coverage of 1−α. On LifeCLEF with default temperature (Temp=1), the majority vote baseline is particularly degraded—predicting nearly all classes—due to score ties from softmax saturation, whereas p-value methods remain robust and produce sets under 100 classes. The paper's Beta-Binomial refinement (C^BB) and its Binomial approximation (C^Bin) improve upon the original majority vote by replacing the fixed m/2 threshold with a distribution-aware quantile, yielding smaller sets, but still trail the p-value aggregation methods. The Binomial approximation introduces a small coverage loss of order (m−1)min(1,αm)/(n_y+1), which is negligible for small m relative to the per-class calibration size but becomes noticeable for large m in classes with few calibration examples.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends split conformal prediction (Vovk et al., 2005) to the multi-input setting by performing class-conditional conformal prediction on each individual observation and then aggregating the resulting p-values, rather than aggregating scores first (which breaks exchangeability). The theoretical core builds on Gazin et al. (2023)'s characterization of the joint distribution of conformal p-values, extending it to the class-conditional case and explicitly handling score ties via randomization. The aggregation framework is positioned as a refinement of the majority-voting approach of Gasparin and Ramdas (2024), replacing the fixed m/2 threshold with Beta-Binomial quantiles and introducing more informative p-value-based score functions (quantile envelopes, ℓq-norm distances).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method addresses a practical gap in citizen science platforms (Pl@ntNet, iNaturalist, eBird) where multiple images of the same species are available but standard conformal prediction either ignores the multi-input structure or naively aggregates scores, producing uninformative prediction sets. A key open question is the exchangeability assumption: the paper demonstrates that truly correlated multi-inputs (as in LifeCLEF's jointly-submitted images) violate this condition, causing p-value-based methods to lose validity while majority-voting methods remain more robust. Future work on handling dependent multi-inputs, adapting to long-tailed class distributions, and extending beyond the fixed-m setting would broaden applicability.
