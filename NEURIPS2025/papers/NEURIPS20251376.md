## Problem

LLMs process entire input contexts with equal computational priority regardless of relevance, wasting computation on irrelevant tokens while suffering from the 'lost-in-the-middle' effect. Existing context compression methods (LLMLingua family, RAG) impose static, predefined compression rates that are content-agnostic, risking information loss or over-compression. This paper asks whether a model's own internal representations can signal when sufficient information has been gathered, enabling dynamic, content-adaptive early termination without external heuristics.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text context (up to 40K tokens) and a query, partitioning the context into non-overlapping left-to-right chunks (default 10% of total tokens each). At each cumulative step, the model's attention head activations are fed to a lightweight ensemble classifier that outputs a binary sufficiency signal (sufficient/insufficient). Processing terminates at the first chunk where the classifier confidence exceeds a threshold τ, and the cached KV activations from all processed chunks are reused for final answer generation. For larger models (14B+), the classifier can be replaced by a self-prompting meta-query appended to each cumulative context.

## Method

The key insight is that specific attention heads, primarily in middle transformer layers, inherently encode context sufficiency signals detectable via linear probes. The method first probes all attention heads across all layers with lightweight linear classifiers (p_θ(x) = σ(⟨θ, x⟩)) trained on cumulative context chunks labeled sufficient/insufficient, then selects the top-k=5 heads by validation F1. On these selected heads, an ensemble of 8 lightweight classifiers per head is trained via StratifiedKFold (n=5), with the top 4 by cross-validation AUC forming the final weighted ensemble. During inference, the context is processed incrementally left-to-right in 10% chunks with KV cache reuse (A(C_i) = f_model(C_i \ C_{i-1}, A_cache^{i-1}), avoiding redundant computation), and the ensemble classifier checks sufficiency at each step until confidence exceeds τ. Notably, the paper discovers an emergent scaling phenomenon: while 1B–8B models require the probing-based classifier, 14B+ models develop intrinsic self-assessment capability through simple prompting, achieving competitive sufficiency detection without any trained probe.

## Results

On the short-form QA benchmark (6 datasets, 4 model families from 1B to 70B), the proposed dynamic context cutoff method achieves 39.2% average accuracy (29.0% multi-hop, 49.4% single-hop) while processing only 1.33× fewer tokens than full context, representing a 3.4% absolute accuracy improvement over the full-context baseline (37.9%). On the long-form benchmark (~40K tokens), the method achieves 30.8% average accuracy with 1.27× token reduction, again outperforming full context (30.3%). For sufficiency classification, probing attention heads achieves F1=91.1 on the 70B model, substantially outperforming supervised fine-tuning (79.5) and self-prompting (83.1).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all five baselines on overall accuracy across the four model families. Against the strongest static compression baseline LLMLingua2 (37.7%), the method gains +1.5% absolute accuracy while achieving a higher token reduction (1.33× vs. 1.25×). RAG baselines (BM25 at 31.9%, SBERT at 33.1%) suffer the largest accuracy drops, particularly on multi-hop tasks where they lose 6-7% relative to full context. The LLMLingua family (34.4–35.0%) is competitive on single-hop but degrades on multi-hop reasoning. Among dynamic methods, the fine-tuned classifier (FT, 27.7%) underperforms significantly due to misaligned sufficiency signals across models, while self-prompting (35.8%) shows emergent capability at 14B+ but struggles at 1B (F1=52.6). The proposed probing-based approach maintains consistent high performance across all model sizes (F1: 87.2–91.1), making it the most reliable sufficiency detector regardless of scale.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the interpretability line of work on latent knowledge in model activations (probing for hallucination, reasoning correctness, long-context understanding) by repurposing linear probes on attention heads for a new purpose: detecting when a model has processed sufficient context. It departs from the dominant context-compression paradigm (LLMLingua family, RAG) that imposes static, pre-defined compression rates, instead proposing a dynamic, content-adaptive cutoff driven by the model's own internal representations. The method also connects to the 'lost-in-the-middle' literature by showing that truncating redundant context can improve rather than degrade accuracy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical value is a lightweight, model-agnostic mechanism (a few-MB ensemble of linear probes) that reduces token processing by ~33% while slightly improving accuracy, offering a drop-in efficiency gain for long-context QA pipelines. The emergent finding that 14B+ models develop intrinsic self-assessment via simple prompting—while 1B models cannot—suggests sufficiency awareness is a scaling property, which has implications for how we design inference strategies across model sizes. Key open questions include whether the approach generalizes beyond factoid QA to tasks requiring holistic context (summarization, creative writing), whether sufficiency thresholds are truly model-specific or task-specific, and whether adaptive threshold selection can replace validation-based hyperparameter tuning.
