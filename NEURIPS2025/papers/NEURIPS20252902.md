## Problem

Existing adversarial training for LLMs faces a fundamental trade-off: discrete attacks (e.g., GCG suffixes, PAP rephrasings) capture real-world vulnerabilities but are computationally expensive to generate, while continuous embedding-space perturbations (e.g., CAT) are fast but fail to cover the full spectrum of discrete attack strategies. As a result, models trained with only one type of perturbation remain highly vulnerable to the other, with prior defenses achieving ALO-ASR above 50% across diverse attack suites.

## Input / Output

The method consumes harmful text prompts from HarmBench (40 non-copyright samples) along with their PAP-generated rephrasings (drawn from 40 persuasion strategies) as discrete adversarial seeds. Continuous L2 perturbations are then applied in the token embedding space on top of these seeds. The output is a LoRA-adapted LLM (7B–32B parameters) that produces refusal or safe text responses when evaluated against a suite of attacks (PAP, TAP, PAIR, AutoDAN, GCG, HumanJailbreaks) and utility benchmarks (ARC, MMLU, MT-Bench, XSTest, Harmless).

## Method

MIXAT defines a unified perturbation space N_MIXAT(x) = R(x) + B²(0, ε), where continuous L2-ball perturbations are centered on discrete adversarial rephrasings rather than the original benign prompt, effectively shifting the continuous search into a more adversarial region of embedding space. A mixing parameter α controls the batch-wise probability of applying continuous attacks on top of PAP rephrasings (P_{C+D}=α) versus on top of clean prompts (P_C=1−α), with α=0.5 as default. Training uses LoRA adapters with the standard combined loss (away from harmful responses, toward safe responses, plus utility loss) and dynamically generates adversarial samples throughout training. The key distinction from the DUALAT variant—which simply alternates between discrete and continuous losses—is that MIXAT composes the two perturbation types into a single attack, yielding broader coverage of the adversarial embedding space and substantially lower ALO-ASR (<20%) at training costs comparable to continuous-only methods.

## Results

MIXAT achieves the lowest At Least One Attack Success Rate (ALO-ASR) across all evaluated models: 15.0% on Zephyr-7B, 25.0% on Llama3-8B, 15.0% on Qwen2.5-14B, and 7.5% on Qwen2.5-32B, compared to >50% for all prior defenses. The MIXAT+GCG variant further reduces ALO-ASR to 7.5% (Zephyr-7B) and 5.0% (Qwen2.5-14B). Utility is well-preserved, with MMLU scores within 1–2 points of the base model (e.g., 55.8% vs. 56.2% on Zephyr-7B) and XSTest compliance at 74.0% (Zephyr-7B). Training cost is comparable to CAT: ~4 hours on 2×A100 for Zephyr-7B vs. ~6h40m for CAT, and ~$11.2 vs. ~$20.0.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against R2D2 [5] (GCG-based discrete AT), MIXAT achieves a dramatically lower ALO-ASR (15.0% vs. 77.5% on Zephyr-7B; 25.0% vs. not reported on Llama3-8B), though R2D2 retains an edge on GCG-specific attacks (0.0% vs. 12.5% on Zephyr-7B). Against CAT [7] (continuous AT), MIXAT reduces ALO-ASR from 70.0% to 15.0% on Zephyr-7B and from 82.5% to 25.0% on Llama3-8B, while matching or exceeding CAT on all jailbreak attacks (PAP, TAP, PAIR, AutoDAN all at 0.0% for MIXAT vs. 25–70% for CAT). Against LAT [9] (latent-space AT), MIXAT substantially outperforms on ALO-ASR (15.0% vs. 97.5% for LAT-KL and 52.5% for LAT-SFT on Zephyr-7B), though LAT-SFT preserves higher XSTest compliance (38.4% vs. 74.0% for MIXAT, but LAT-SFT severely degrades MMLU to 22.9%). Against prompt-based defenses RPO [21] and PAT [20] on Llama3-8B, MIXAT achieves 25.0% ALO-ASR versus 100.0% and 72.5–82.5% respectively, while maintaining competitive utility (MMLU 59.1% vs. 54.6% for RPO). The DUALAT ablation (separately combining discrete and continuous losses) confirms that MIXAT's approach of applying continuous perturbations on top of discrete seeds is strictly superior, achieving 15.0% vs. 22.5% ALO-ASR on Zephyr-7B at the same α=0.5 mixing ratio.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on two lines of adversarial training for LLMs: discrete methods (R2D2, PAP-AT) that train on concrete adversarial prompts but are computationally expensive, and continuous methods (CAT, LAT) that perturb embeddings or hidden states for efficiency but fail to capture discrete attack vulnerabilities. The key conceptual departure is MIXAT's perturbation space, which applies continuous PGD perturbations *on top of* discrete adversarial seeds (PAP rephrasings) rather than treating the two as separate training objectives (DUALAT). This 'centering' of the continuous L2 ball around discrete adversarial examples is the paper's core design insight, distinguishing it from prior work that either uses one attack type or naively alternates between them.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ALO-ASR metric and the finding that MIXAT reduces worst-case vulnerability from >50% to <20% across diverse attacks provide a practical, efficient baseline for LLM safety practitioners. The paper also exposes evaluation blind spots (chat template inconsistency, quantization effects, temperature sensitivity) that affect how prior defenses are assessed, which is valuable for the broader community. However, the authors acknowledge that adversarial training alone cannot fully resolve harmful request generation, that GCG remains a residual vulnerability, and that current evaluation datasets are insufficient for holistic robustness claims—leaving open the question of whether a truly universal defense is achievable through training alone.
