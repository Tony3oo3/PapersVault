## Problem

Traditional cooperative bargaining solutions such as Nash and Kalai-Smorodinsky require the mediator to access agents' utility values and gradient magnitudes, which are unavailable or incomparable in settings like human-AI interactions, privacy-sensitive applications, or when utilities are scaled by unknown non-affine transformations. The paper proves that no algorithm using only direction oracles (normalized gradients) can recover NBS or KSBS for all bargaining games, establishing a fundamental limitation of existing solution concepts in this information-constrained setting.

## Input / Output

The mediator receives, for each agent, a direction oracle returning the normalized negative cost gradient (a unit vector in the shared decision space) and a precomputed preferred state x*,i; it never observes utility values or gradient magnitudes. Direction oracles can be constructed from a minimal comparison oracle where agents respond only 'yes', 'no', or 'indifferent' to proposed state changes, using existing zeroth-order estimation methods (e.g., Sign-OPT). The output is a single bargaining solution state x† in the shared state space S, guaranteed to be Pareto stationary, symmetric, and invariant to strictly increasing monotonic non-affine transformations of individual agent costs.

## Method

DiBS (Direction-based Bargaining Solution) iteratively updates the state as x_{k+1} = x_k − α_k Σ_i ||x_k − x*,i||_2 · ∇ℓ^i(x_k)/||∇ℓ^i(x_k)||_2, weighting each agent's most preferred direction by its Euclidean distance to that agent's preferred state. The key insight is that this distance-based weighting in the shared state space replaces the cost-space reasoning (e.g., NBS's 1/(d^i − ℓ^i(x)) scaling) that is inaccessible without utility values, while preserving fairness: agents farther from their ideal state receive proportionally more influence. The continuous-time analog is shown to be a globally asymptotically stable dynamical system via a Lyapunov function V(x) = h(x)ᵀh(x) and LaSalle's invariance theorem, under strong convexity and smoothness of agent costs. Discrete-time convergence follows from standard stochastic approximation results with square-summable step sizes. The solution inherits invariance to monotonic non-affine transformations because normalized gradients are unchanged by such transformations, and it satisfies the symmetry and (under uniqueness of the fixed point) independence-of-irrelevant-alternatives axioms.

## Results

In the multi-agent formation assignment experiment (10 agents, nonconvex costs), DiBS produces qualitatively similar balanced formations to NBS and KSBS in the original setting, but critically remains invariant when a monotone nonaffine transformation (sign(l)·l²) is applied to odd agents' costs, whereas NBS and KSBS produce skewed outcomes favoring the transformed agents. In the mediated portfolio management experiment (N=10 investors, n=5/10/20/50 stocks, 100 random scenarios), DiBS with comparison-based direction oracle estimation (Sign-OPT) achieves a median relative error below 1 even when the number of comparisons per agent per iteration is significantly lower than the state dimension, indicating consistent improvement over the initial state toward the exact-direction DiBS solution. Accuracy improves monotonically with more comparisons (1 → 10,000), and higher-dimensional problems require more comparisons for equivalent accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares DiBS against two classical bargaining solutions: NBS (Nash, 1950) and KSBS (Kalai & Smorodinsky, 1975). In the unscaled formation assignment setting, all three methods reach reasonable, balanced solutions that respect agents' preferences, with NBS and KSBS slightly prioritizing clustering of same-parity agents while DiBS slightly prioritizes minimizing distances to the center. However, under a monotone nonaffine transformation applied to odd agents' costs, NBS and KSBS both produce qualitatively different, unfair solutions that disproportionately favor the odd agents, while DiBS retains a fair outcome identical to the unscaled case — this is the paper's central empirical demonstration. In the portfolio management experiment, the comparison is internal (DiBS with exact direction oracles vs. DiBS with Sign-OPT comparison-based estimation), showing that the comparison-based variant degrades gracefully: the median relative error stays under 1 across all tested dimensions and comparison budgets, confirming practical viability when only binary preference queries are available. The paper does not report a single scalar performance metric where DiBS 'wins' over NBS/KSBS; rather, the comparison establishes that DiBS is the only method among the three that satisfies invariance to nonaffine monotonic transformations, which is the core theoretical and practical contribution.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the classical cooperative bargaining literature (Nash 1950, Kalai-Smorodinsky 1975), which assumes the mediator has full access to agents' utility values and gradients, by formulating bargaining under a strictly weaker information model: only normalized utility gradients (direction oracles) are available. It builds conceptually on zeroth-order and comparison-based optimization (Sign-OPT, SignSGD, dueling convex optimization) for practical oracle implementation, and distinguishes itself from two prior direction-based bargaining works [7, 17] that target self-interested (non-cooperative) agents rather than cooperative settings. The paper also draws on nonlinear dynamical systems theory (LaSalle's invariance theorem, Brouwer's fixed point theorem) to establish convergence, repurposing tools from control theory for a bargaining context.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DiBS addresses a practically important gap: in human-AI mediation, privacy-sensitive settings, and scenarios with incomparable or nonaffinely-scaled utilities, the mediator cannot rely on explicit utility values, yet existing solutions (NBS, KSBS) break down under nonaffine transformations. The invariance guarantee to monotonic nonaffine transformations is the key differentiator, making DiBS robust to utility exaggeration or imperfect reward modeling. However, the convergence proof requires strong convexity of agent costs, which is restrictive for many real-world nonconvex settings (as the formation experiment itself uses nonconvex costs), and only asymptotic guarantees are provided. Open questions include relaxing strong convexity, obtaining non-asymptotic rates, and scaling to large learning-based multi-agent settings.
