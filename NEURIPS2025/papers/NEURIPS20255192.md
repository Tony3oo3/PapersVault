## Problem

While prior empirical work (Bell & Lawrence, Li & Hiratani) suggested that placing dissimilar tasks adjacently improves continual learning performance, the field lacked a rigorous theoretical framework to formalize, analyze, and characterize such similarity-guided orderings. This paper asks whether greedy dissimilarity-maximizing orderings can provably outperform random ones, and under what conditions they might fail.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255192/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a collection of T jointly-realizable linear regression tasks (X_m ∈ R^{n_m×d}, y_m ∈ R^{n_m}) and the current parameter vector w_{t-1}, and at each step selects the next task by evaluating a greedy criterion (residual norm or projection distance) over all remaining tasks. Each selected task is trained to convergence via (S)GD before the next selection, making the per-step cost a full task solve rather than a single gradient step. In the CIFAR-100 experiments, the input is a frozen ResNet-20 embedding of 50 binary classification tasks, and the output is the final linear probe weights after 50 sequential task trainings.

## Method

The paper formalizes two greedy orderings adapted from the Kaczmarz and projection-methods literature: Maximum Distance (MD), which selects the task whose orthogonal projection P_m = I − X_m^+ X_m removes the largest component of (w_{t-1} − w_★), and Maximum Residual (MR), which selects the task with the largest current loss ‖X_m w_{t-1} − y_m‖². Under the joint-realizability assumption, each task update reduces to an orthogonal projection onto the task's solution subspace, giving a clean geometric interpretation where greedy selection maximizes the angle between consecutive solution subspaces. The key analytical results are: (i) for rank-(d−1) tasks, MD achieves a 1/eT loss bound matching the best random-ordering rate and a square-root approximation to the optimal distance; (ii) for general-rank tasks, single-pass greedy orderings can be adversarially forced into Ω(1) loss (catastrophic forgetting), while greedy with repetition recovers a dimensionality-independent O(1/k^{1/3}) bound; (iii) a hybrid scheme that switches from greedy to random once projection decrements fall below a threshold inherits random-ordering guarantees while retaining early greedy speedups.

## Results

On synthetic regression (T=50, r=10, d=100, isotropic Gaussian data), greedy MD and MR orderings consistently achieve lower average loss than random ordering across all iterations, with the gap widening as more tasks are learned. On CIFAR-100 domain-incremental binary classification (T=50 tasks, ResNet-20 linear probe), greedy orderings outperform random in average test accuracy, approaching the joint-training baseline. Analytically, for high-rank tasks (r=d−1), greedy MD achieves a loss bound of 1/(eT), matching the best known random-ordering rate; for general rank, greedy with repetition achieves O(1/k^{1/3}), while single-pass greedy can suffer Ω(1) loss on adversarial constructions (loss ≥ 1/8 − 1/(4d) for d ≥ 30).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against random orderings (Evron et al. [35], Attia et al. [10]), the paper reveals a nuanced picture. For high-rank tasks (r=d−1), greedy MD matches the O((d−r)/T) random rate, achieving 1/(eT). However, for general-rank tasks, a clear separation emerges: random orderings guarantee O(1/√k) convergence regardless of task structure, while single-pass greedy orderings can fail catastrophically with Ω(1) loss on adversarial task collections (Theorem 5.2: loss ≥ 1/8 − 1/(4d)). Greedy with repetition recovers a dimensionality-independent O(1/k^{1/3}) bound, which is slower than random's O(1/√k) but avoids the single-pass failure mode. Empirically on both synthetic regression and CIFAR-100, greedy orderings outperform random in practice, suggesting the adversarial worst case is rare in natural data. The proposed hybrid scheme (greedy early, random late) inherits the O(1/√T) random bound while retaining greedy's faster early convergence.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper formalizes similarity-guided task orderings in continual linear regression by adapting greedy selection rules (Maximum Distance, Maximum Residual) from the Kaczmarz and projection methods literature [75, 73] to the continual learning setting. It builds directly on the projection-based analytical framework of Evron et al. [33, 34], which established the geometric interpretation of continual learning as sequential orthogonal projections. The work extends the empirical observations of Bell and Lawrence [14] and Li and Hiratani [62]—who found that dissimilar adjacent tasks improve performance—into a rigorous theoretical treatment with provable guarantees and, crucially, adversarial counterexamples.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals a fundamental tension: while greedy orderings empirically converge faster than random ones, single-pass greedy orderings can provably fail catastrophically (Ω(1) loss) on adversarial task collections, whereas random orderings always converge at O(1/√k). This negative result, combined with the O(1/k^{1/3}) bound for greedy with repetition and the proposed hybrid scheme, provides practitioners with a nuanced decision framework for task ordering. Open questions include extending the analysis to nonlinear models, class- and task-incremental settings, and whether probabilistic greedy rules (inspired by randomized Kaczmarz) can achieve tighter bounds while retaining greedy's empirical speed.
