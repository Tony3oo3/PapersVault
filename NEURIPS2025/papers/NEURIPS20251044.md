## Problem

Diffusion-based inverse algorithms require 100–1000 denoising steps for acceptable quality, and naively reducing steps degrades performance. While high-order ODE solvers accelerate unconditional diffusion sampling, they fail to generalize to inverse problems because (1) many inverse algorithms lack explicit ODE/SDE formulations, and (2) the approximations and heuristics prevalent in these algorithms introduce estimation errors that undermine analytical solver coefficients.

## Input / Output

The method takes a degraded observation (e.g., blurred, masked, or downsampled image), the corresponding observation function A, a pre-trained diffusion model, and a small set of learned linear combination coefficients (one per timestep per previous step). It outputs a restored clean image at the original resolution. LLE is trained on only 50 diffusion-generated samples in minutes on a single GPU, and at inference it wraps around any existing inverse algorithm conforming to the proposed canonical form without modifying the underlying network or algorithm logic.

## Method

The paper first establishes a canonical form that decomposes any diffusion-based inverse algorithm into three modules—Sampler (denoising estimate), Corrector (observation-consistency refinement), and Noiser (re-noising for the next step)—and proves via ODE analysis that the evolution of the clean-signal estimate between steps is a linear combination of the current corrected estimate and all previous step results. Building on this, LLE learns a set of linear combination coefficients per timestep that optimally blends the current corrected estimate with previous-step results, trained against ground-truth reconstructions using a combined MSE + LPIPS loss. For linear inverse problems, the coefficients are further decoupled into separate sets for the range-space and null-space components of the observation matrix, exploiting the fact that observations constrain these subspaces differently. The approach is universal: it requires no architectural changes, no additional neural networks, and can be plugged into any algorithm conforming to the canonical form, with the entire training completing in minutes on 50 samples.

## Results

On CelebA-HQ linear tasks with 3 steps, LLE yields the largest gains for weaker baselines: IIGDM improves by +3.74 dB PSNR on 4×SR (21.93→25.67) and +3.01 dB on CS50% (16.32→19.33) at 5 steps, while DDNM's inpainting PSNR jumps from 16.64 to 24.38 (+7.74 dB) at 3 steps. For stronger baselines like DDRM and DDNM on deblurring, gains are modest (≈0.1–0.3 dB PSNR). On nonlinear deblurring (Table 2), LLE consistently improves all five algorithms (DPS, RED-diff, DiffPIR, ReSample, DAPS) by 0.1–1.5 dB PSNR across both noiseless and noisy settings on FFHQ and CelebA-HQ.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LLE is evaluated as a plug-in enhancement on nine existing inverse algorithms (DDNM, DDRM, IIGDM, DMPS, DPS, DiffPIR, ReSample, RED-diff, DAPS), comparing each algorithm with and without LLE under identical step budgets. LLE wins on virtually every metric across all algorithms and tasks, with the most dramatic gains occurring for weaker or heuristic-based methods: IIGDM improves by up to +3.74 dB PSNR (4×SR, 5 steps) and DDNM's inpainting PSNR surges by +7.74 dB at 3 steps (16.64→24.38). For already-strong baselines like DDRM on deblurring, gains are smaller (≈0.01–0.3 dB), consistent with the paper's observation that LLE approximates the original trajectory when it is already near-optimal. On the nonlinear deblurring task (Table 2), LLE improves all five tested algorithms by 0.1–1.5 dB PSNR, with the largest gain for DPS at 3 steps (+2.97 dB on CelebA-HQ noiseless). The decoupled-coefficient variant (Section 4.3) further adds 0.2–1.8 dB over the single-coefficient LLE on linear tasks (Table 3), confirming the benefit of separating range-space and null-space components. No comparison shows LLE degrading performance relative to the unenhanced baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the high-order ODE solver literature for diffusion sampling (DPM-Solver, UniPC) and the optimal linear subspace search method of Duan et al. (2023), but addresses the key gap that analytical high-order solvers fail for inverse problems due to heterogeneous formulations and heuristic approximations. It introduces a canonical form (Sampler–Corrector–Noiser) that unifies nine diverse inverse algorithms (DDRM, DDNM, DPS, DiffPIR, ReSample, RED-diff, DAPS, IIGDM, DMPS) under a single modular framework, and shows via Proposition 1 that the evolution of the clean-signal estimate at each step admits a linear combination structure. LLE then learns the combination coefficients in a data-driven manner, positioning itself as complementary to existing few-step methods like shortcut sampling, DDC, and conjugate integrators, all of which can be embedded within the same canonical form.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: LLE requires only 50 diffusion-generated samples and minutes of training on a single GPU, making it trivially integrable into any existing inverse algorithm without retraining the diffusion model. The canonical form provides a reusable organizational language for the field, potentially enabling systematic comparison and hybrid design of inverse solvers. Key limitations include the restriction to a linear subspace spanned by previous steps (nonlinear extensions are left open), and the method's gains diminish when the base algorithm is already near-optimal. Open directions include combining LLE with learnable timestep schedules, extending to nonlinear search, and applying the framework to latent-space or consistency-model-based inverse solvers.
