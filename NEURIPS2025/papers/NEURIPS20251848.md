## Problem

Randomized selection mechanisms for peer review and grant funding (e.g., randomize-above-threshold, Swiss NSF lottery) are ad hoc and violate natural axioms such as ex post validity and stability. The paper shows that in fully Bayesian settings deterministic selection always maximizes expected utility, so randomization is only justified under Knightian uncertainty where no reliable probabilistic model exists. The gap is a principled, axiomatic framework for randomized top-k selection when uncertainty is represented by quality intervals rather than probability distributions.

## Input / Output

The method takes a set of n quality intervals [ℓ_i, u_i] (one per proposal) and a budget k, where overlapping intervals indicate insufficient evidence to distinguish proposals and non-overlapping intervals encode strict dominance. It outputs a vector of marginal selection probabilities p ∈ [0,1]^n with ‖p‖₁ = k, from which exactly k proposals are sampled via systematic sampling. The intervals need not come from any specific probabilistic model and can arise from imputation bounds, model ensembling, expert input, or multi-criteria aggregation.

## Method

MERIT formulates selection as a maximin optimization: maximize the worst-case expected number of true top-k proposals selected over all rankings consistent with the interval order, solved as a linear program with exponentially many constraints. A polynomial-time separation oracle (Algorithm 1) checks feasibility by, for each of the k+1 intervals with the largest lower bounds, constructing the worst-case top-k set that includes all higher-ranked intervals and excludes the current one, running in O(n·max{k, log n}). In practice, a cutting plane algorithm (Algorithm 2) initializes the LP with pruning (removing intervals strictly above/below k others), symmetry constraints (equal A,B counts share probabilities), and monotonicity constraints along chains of the (A,B) partial order, converging in O(k^{w+1}) iterations where w is the chain-cover width. A post-processing step (Algorithm 3) enforces ex post validity by transferring probability mass from dominated to dominating intervals, provably without decreasing the objective value.

## Results

Under the Swiss NSF's linear miscalibration model, MERIT achieves expected utility comparable to the Swiss NSF randomized method and deterministic model-based selection across all three datasets (Swiss NSF 2020, NeurIPS 2024, ICLR 2025). Under the paper's worst-case objective over interval-consistent rankings, MERIT substantially outperforms all baselines, achieving up to 0.19 higher worst-case utility than the Swiss NSF method, with the largest gaps on wide-interval settings (NeurIPS LOO, NeurIPS Gaussian, ICLR Subjectivity). Computationally, MERIT completes in under 5 minutes on a standard laptop even for 11,520 ICLR proposals, with the cutting plane algorithm converging in under 30 iterations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares MERIT against three baselines: the Swiss NSF Bayesian ranking-and-lottery method [21], deterministic top-k selection, and randomize-above-threshold. Under the Swiss NSF's linear miscalibration model (the setting the Swiss NSF method is designed for), MERIT matches the Swiss NSF method and deterministic model-based selection in expected utility, while deterministic mean-based selection performs poorly. Under the paper's own worst-case objective, MERIT outperforms the Swiss NSF method by up to 0.19 in worst-case utility, with the gap being largest when intervals are wide (NeurIPS LOO, NeurIPS Gaussian). In the axiomatic comparison, MERIT satisfies reversal symmetry and avoids maximal instability, while both the Swiss NSF method and randomize-above-threshold violate both properties. All three randomized methods (including MERIT) violate monotonicity in budget, and the paper proves this violation is inherent—ex ante optimality and monotonicity in budget cannot be simultaneously satisfied.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the Swiss NSF's Bayesian ranking-and-lottery framework [21] by replacing fully specified probabilistic models with Knightian uncertainty intervals, arguing (and proving) that randomization is unnecessary under the former. Conceptually, it repurposes distributionally robust optimization [41] and the ellipsoid/separation-oracle machinery from combinatorial optimization to solve a maximin top-k selection problem over interval orders, while drawing on randomized social choice [10] for an axiomatic comparison framework. The polynomial-time algorithm is notable because the minimum-weight k-ideal problem on general partial orders is NP-hard [15], yet the interval-order structure admits an efficient separation oracle. The paper does not build on a single prior method but synthesizes ideas from robust optimization, game theory (Stackelberg equilibrium), and social choice to carve out a new formulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This provides the first principled, axiatically-grounded alternative to the ad-hoc randomize-above-threshold and Swiss NSF lotteries now deployed by multiple funding agencies, with a practical algorithm scaling to 11,000+ candidates in under five minutes. The proof that ex ante optimality and monotonicity in budget are simultaneously unsatisfiable is a useful negative result that constrains future mechanism design. Key open questions include extending to variable-cost candidates, general partial orders beyond interval orders, richer positional utility functions, and analyzing equilibrium effects on reviewer and applicant incentives. A limitation is that MERIT exploits only interval ordering, so it may be suboptimal when well-calibrated probabilistic models are available.
