## Problem

Spectral feature learning for nonparametric instrumental variable (NPIV) regression has shown strong empirical performance, but the conditions under which it succeeds or fails remained unclear. This paper derives a generalization error bound for a two-stage least squares estimator with spectral features, revealing that performance is governed by two factors—spectral alignment of the structural function with the top eigenspaces of the conditional expectation operator, and the rate of singular value decay—leading to a three-regime taxonomy (good, bad, ugly) that explains both successes and failure modes.

## Input / Output

The method consumes two separate datasets: an unlabeled collection of (X, Z) pairs used to train spectral features via a contrastive loss, and a labeled collection of (X, Z, Y) triples used for the downstream 2SLS estimation. In the dSprites application, X is a 64×64 RGB image, Z is a 3-dimensional vector (x-position, orientation, scale), and Y is a scalar outcome. Beyond the structural function estimate h₀(X), the method outputs a diagnostic of the problem's regime by estimating the alignment coefficients ⟨v_i, h₀⟩ and the singular value decay profile from data, enabling practitioners to predict whether spectral methods will be effective.

## Method

The paper shows that the spectral contrastive loss from Sun et al. [36] is exactly an Eckart-Young-Mirsky objective that minimizes the Hilbert-Schmidt distance between the learned rank-d operator and the true conditional expectation operator T, thereby targeting its leading eigenspaces. The key theoretical result (Corollary 1) bounds the L₂ generalization error of Sieve 2SLS with spectral features as O_p(‖(Π_{X,d})_⊥ h₀‖ + √(d/(nσ_d²))), decomposing error into a spectral alignment term and an ill-posedness term. Theorem 3 extends this to learned features, showing that if the learned operator approximates the true rank-d truncation within operator-norm error ε̂_d, the estimator retains strong guarantees with an additional penalty of √2·ε̂_d·‖h₀‖/σ_d. A practical data-driven procedure estimates the alignment coefficients via ⟨v_i, h₀⟩ = E[Y·u_i(Z)]/σ_i, allowing empirical diagnosis of which regime a given problem occupies.

## Results

The paper's primary contribution is a theoretical generalization bound (Corollary 1): ‖ĥ − h₀‖_{L²} = O_p(‖(Π_{X,d})_⊥ h₀‖ + √(d/(nσ_d²))), which decomposes error into spectral alignment and ill-posedness terms. Synthetic experiments (Figure 1) confirm the predicted taxonomy: MSE decreases monotonically as singular value decay slows (larger c_σ) and as h₀ concentrates on top singular functions (smaller c_α), for both oracle and learned spectral features. On the dSprites benchmark, the alignment diagnostic from Section 5 confirms the structural function is spanned by the leading singular functions of T, placing it firmly in the 'good' regime and explaining why spectral methods outperform alternatives there.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against competing IV methods (e.g., DeepIV, kernel IV, saddle-point estimators) in its experiments; instead, it validates its theoretical 'good-bad-ugly' taxonomy through controlled synthetic settings. The key empirical comparison is between oracle spectral features (exact singular functions) and learned features (50-dimensional neural network features trained via the contrastive loss, Eq. 8): both exhibit the same qualitative MSE trends across the (c_σ, c_α) grid, with learned features showing a small but consistent gap from the oracle, consistent with the additional √2·ε̂_d·‖h₀‖/σ_d penalty in Theorem 3. Figure 2 further illustrates that when h₀ is orthogonal to the singular functions ('very ugly' regime), all spectral methods fail completely regardless of sample size, while partial alignment yields imperfect but non-trivial recovery. The dSprites application serves as a real-world diagnostic rather than a head-to-head comparison, confirming the 'good' regime prediction that motivated the spectral approach in [36].

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper provides the missing theoretical foundation for the spectral contrastive learning approach of Sun et al. [36], showing that their contrastive loss is an Eckart-Young-Mirsky approximation of the conditional expectation operator T, and that the learned features explicitly target its leading eigenspaces. It specializes the generalization bound of Chen & Christensen [6] for Sieve 2SLS to the case of spectral features, leveraging the result of Blundell et al. [3] that top singular subspaces minimize the sieve measure of ill-posedness. The paper departs from the saddle-point optimization line of work (Dikkala et al., Bennett et al.) by staying within the 2SLS framework and focusing on the feature design question rather than the estimation objective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The good-bad-ugly taxonomy gives practitioners a concrete diagnostic: before investing in spectral feature learning, one can estimate spectral alignment and singular value decay from data to predict whether the method will succeed, degrade, or fundamentally fail. The 'ugly' regime—where misalignment causes failure regardless of sample size or instrument strength—challenges the implicit assumption that stronger instruments always improve estimation. Key open questions include precisely controlling the operator approximation error ε̂_d from finite-sample contrastive learning, extending the analysis to non-compact or non-injective operators, and developing remedies for the ugly regime (e.g., adaptive feature selection beyond the top-d eigenspaces).
