## Problem

Existing adversarial transfer attacks struggle with cross-architecture transferability (e.g., CNN to ViT) due to significant structural differences in how these models extract features. Prior alignment methods like Model Alignment (MA) only constrain final prediction logits, ignoring spatial feature mismatches and adversarial feature distributions that are critical for cross-architecture attacks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250893/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a clean RGB image (ImageNet validation, 1000 images), a surrogate model (e.g., ResNet-50), and a witness model of a different architecture (e.g., ViT-B) as inputs. It produces a fine-tuned surrogate model (1 epoch of SGD, momentum 0.9, no extra data) that, when used with standard attacks (MI, NI, GI, DI, TI, SSA) under an l∞ budget of ε=16/255, generates adversarial examples with substantially improved cross-architecture transferability. The alignment phase requires ~10 hours on an RTX 3090 (batch size 64), but the subsequent attack phase incurs no additional memory or compute beyond the base attack.

## Method

SAA fine-tunes the surrogate model via a composite loss with two components. Spatial-aware alignment minimizes KL divergence between global logits (surrogate vs. witness) and cross-entropy loss on local features, where the witness model's per-position pseudo-labels (from last conv layer for CNNs or patch token embeddings for ViTs) supervise the surrogate's corresponding spatial positions. Adversarial-aware alignment introduces a self-adversarial strategy: adversarial examples are iteratively generated by maximizing KL divergence between the surrogate's output on the perturbed input and the witness's output on the clean input, then the same spatial alignment is applied to these adversarial examples. The final loss L_SAA = L_SA + κ·L_AA (with γ=0.2, ω=0.02, κ=0.02) is optimized for one epoch, causing the surrogate to concentrate on features shared with the witness model, so that perturbations crafted on the surrogate exploit these common features and transfer effectively across architectures.

## Results

On ImageNet (1,000-image subset), SAA achieves up to 91.4% average attack success rate (Avg. ASR) when Swin-B is the surrogate and DN121 the witness, compared to 42.5% for the unaligned MI baseline. For the critical cross-architecture CNN→ViT transfer, SAA improves Avg. ASR by 25.5–39.1% over Model Alignment (MA); e.g., Res50 surrogate with ViT-B witness jumps from 53.5% (MA) to 74.0% (SAA). When combined with the strongest multi-attack ensemble (SSA-DI-TI-MI), SAA reaches 88.2% Avg. ASR, nearly closing the gap to white-box attacks. On ViT-specific attacks, PatchOut-SAA more than doubles transferability (43.3% → 78.7% Avg. ASR).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SAA consistently outperforms the unaligned MI baseline and the prior alignment method MA across all 16 surrogate–witness combinations. Against MA, SAA gains 11.9–13.1% Avg. ASR for Res50 surrogate (e.g., 75.8% vs 63.9% with DN121 witness) and 22.9–30.5% for ViT/Swin surrogates (e.g., Swin-B surrogate with Res50 witness: 85.7% vs 55.4%). The advantage is most pronounced in cross-architecture settings: CNN→ViT transfer improves by 25.5–39.1% over MA, and ViT→CNN transfer improves by up to 45% (ViT-B→Res50: 80.6% vs 51.9%). When stacked on top of existing transfer attacks (Table 3), SAA adds 21.7% on GI and 8.4% on SSA, and the full SSA-DI-TI-MI-SAA ensemble reaches 88.2% Avg. ASR. On ViT-specific attacks (Table 4), SAA lifts PatchOut from 43.3% to 78.7%, PNA from 60.3% to 83.3%, and TGR from 69.6% to 85.1% Avg. ASR, confirming that SAA is complementary to architecture-specific modifications rather than a replacement.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Model Alignment (MA, ECCV 2024), which aligns surrogate and witness models via KL divergence on final logits, by adding two missing dimensions: spatial-aware alignment (matching local feature maps at corresponding positions using pseudo-labels from the witness model) and adversarial-aware alignment (a self-adversarial strategy that generates adversarial examples supervised by the witness model's global features). Unlike prior transfer attacks that modify the optimization procedure (MI, NI, DI, TI, SSA) or the model's forward/backward pass (SGM, LinBP, BPA, PNA, PatchOut, TGR), SAA operates at the model level—fine-tuning the surrogate once so that all subsequent attacks benefit without any architectural or propagation changes. The paper also positions itself against the broader CNN-to-ViT transferability gap identified by Wei et al. (AAAI 2022), arguing that the root cause is misalignment of spatial and adversarial feature representations rather than a deficiency in any single attack technique.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Cross-architecture adversarial transferability (especially CNN→ViT) remains a significant blind spot in evaluating real-world model robustness, and SAA's 25–39% improvement over MA in this setting, combined with its plug-and-play compatibility with existing attacks (reaching 88.2% ASR with SSA-DI-TI-MI), makes it a practically useful tool for red-teaming. The empirical finding that ViT-as-surrogate with CNN-as-witness yields the strongest cross-architecture transfer provides actionable guidance for practitioners selecting surrogate models. However, the method lacks theoretical justification, incurs ~10 hours of alignment training cost, and the evaluation is limited to a 1,000-image ImageNet subset, leaving open whether the gains persist on larger or more diverse benchmarks and whether the spatial alignment mechanism generalizes to non-classification tasks.
