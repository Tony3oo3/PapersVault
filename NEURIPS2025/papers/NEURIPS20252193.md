## Problem

Existing interpretability methods for neural networks focus on individual neurons or low-dimensional projections, which are limited in expressiveness and fail to capture the distributed, population-level dynamics that actually drive computation. The paper asks whether the high-dimensional raw activity of neural populations contains structured, recurring patterns that mirror regularities in the training data (the Reflection Hypothesis), and whether these patterns can be extracted as interpretable units using the cognitive principle of chunking.

## Input / Output

The methods consume precomputed hidden-state sequences S_h = (h^1, ..., h^n) of dimension d (e.g., d=4096 for LLaMA-3-8B) recorded across all layers as the model processes a tokenized text prompt, plus optionally known concept labels (e.g., word positions) for the supervised PA method. Outputs include a dictionary of recurring population-level chunks defined as closed balls B(h̄_C, Δ) in the embedding space, a binary chunk-detection function f_chunk for classifying whether an unknown activation belongs to a concept, and causal interventions (grafting a chunk prototype or freezing a subpopulation to zero) that produce measurably altered text generation. DSC additionally produces a symbolic string representation of the trajectory and a parse length that reflects hierarchical structure in the input.

## Method

The paper proposes three architecturally agnostic chunk-extraction methods that complement each other based on dimensionality and label availability. Discrete Sequence Chunking (DSC) applies to small d by clustering each neuron's activations individually, converting the population trajectory into a sequence of discrete symbol strings, and iteratively merging the most frequent adjacent chunk pairs above a frequency threshold until convergence, yielding a vocabulary of recurring population trajectories. Population Averaging (PA) applies to large d with known concept positions by averaging hidden states at all occurrences of a concept, identifying the neural subpopulation C(s) whose activities fluctuate within a tolerance around the mean, and computing a maximal deviation radius Δ that defines the chunk as a ball in R^d; the optimal tolerance is selected by maximizing TPR while minimizing FPR. Unsupervised Chunk Discovery (UCD) applies when labels are absent by learning a dictionary matrix D ∈ R^{K×d} that minimizes a negative max-cosine-similarity loss, encouraging each embedding to align with its nearest dictionary entry. The key distinguishing insight is that these chunks are bounded, compact subspaces (balls) rather than linear subspaces or individual neuron directions, and their causal role is validated by showing that grafting a chunk prototype into the network's hidden state at a target layer reliably shifts generated text toward the grafted concept, while freezing the corresponding subpopulation suppresses it.

## Results

Population Averaging (PA) extracts neural subpopulation chunks that predict the presence of target concepts (e.g., 'cheese', 'cake', 'cheesecake') with high true positive rates and low false positive rates across all 32 layers of LLaMA-3-8B, outperforming a pretrained sparse autoencoder (SAE) on the same task. Causal grafting of extracted chunks into LLaMA-3's hidden states increases target concept occurrence from ~11–15% (no grafting) to ~45–56% across six TREC question categories, with early-layer grafting (layers 1–9) being most effective. For specific concepts, grafting 'cheese' raises its occurrence from 0% to 90% and 'cake' from 1% to 83% in generated text (N=100). The method generalizes across architectures (LLaMA-3, T5, RWKV, Mamba) and to abstract concepts such as narrative schemas and part-of-speech tags.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary quantitative comparison is between Population Averaging (PA) and a pretrained sparse autoencoder (SAE, EleutherAI Sparsify) on LLaMA-3-8B for detecting the presence of target words ('cheese', 'cake', 'cheesecake') in hidden states. PA-extracted population chunks achieve higher true positive rates and lower false positive rates than the most active SAE concept-related neurons used as decoders, across all layers (Figure 4). PA also has a significant computational advantage: it requires no gradient computation or training, operating as a post-hoc analysis on precomputed activations, whereas SAEs require multiple forward/backward passes over many epochs (orders of magnitude more compute). The paper also contrasts neural chunk grafting with input-level perturbation in RNNs, showing that grafting hidden-state chunks reliably induces compositional reuse while simply changing the input does not (Figure 13). Compared to representation engineering, PA assigns a single bounded chunk per concept rather than a linear direction with a free magnitude hyperparameter, making interventions more direct and interpretable.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Wu et al.'s (2022) hierarchical chunking algorithm for discrete sequence compression, repurposing it to segment neural population trajectories into interpretable entities. It departs from the dominant interpretability paradigm—individual monosemantic neurons, sparse autoencoders, and linear representation engineering—by operating at the population level and treating recurring activity patterns (chunks) as the fundamental interpretable units, grounded in the cognitive science of perceptual chunking. The 'Reflection Hypothesis' provides a theoretical bridge between data regularities and internal network dynamics, positioning this work as a complement to rather than a replacement for SAE-based or direction-based methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a lightweight, architecturally agnostic interpretability toolkit (PA requires no training; UCD runs on a single GPU) that could become a practical first-pass diagnostic for understanding LLM internals, especially given its demonstrated superiority over SAE concept neurons on decoding accuracy. Key limitations include PA's dependence on known concept labels and its reduced effectiveness for context-sensitive or functional words (prepositions, conjunctions), and the LLM experiments are confined to models ≤8B parameters, leaving open whether the Reflection Hypothesis and chunk structure persist at production scale. Future work is needed to formalize the theoretical foundations, characterize the learning dynamics that give rise to chunks, and extend the framework to multi-concept interactions and hierarchical composition.
