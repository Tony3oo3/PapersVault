## Problem

Shuffled regression aims to learn a regression function when the correspondence between input features and target responses is unknown, a setting arising in anonymized clinical data, multi-device measurements, and image registration. Existing methods rely almost exclusively on linear models (or a single recent neural-network approach), leaving the well-known benefits of Gaussian processes—nonparametric flexibility and principled uncertainty quantification—unexploited in this problem.

## Input / Output

The method consumes shuffled data organized as M pairs of input-sets (L points each) and response-sets (L values each), where the within-set pairing is unknown; a single test input point is then queried. It produces a full predictive Gaussian (mean and variance) at the test point, the recovered permutation matrix giving the input–output correspondence, and optimized kernel hyperparameters. Unlike standard regression, the training data never provides explicit (x, y) pairs—only grouped sets—and the correspondence must be inferred jointly with the function.

## Method

GPSR places a zero-mean GP prior on the regression function and models shuffling via block-diagonal permutation matrices applied to latent function values before adding Gaussian noise. The key theoretical result is that the GPSR marginal likelihood and predictive distribution are exactly equivalent to those of standard GPR evaluated on 'pseudo-coupled data' (responses inversely permuted by the current permutation estimate), enabling GPR to serve as a drop-in subroutine. Inference alternates between optimizing kernel hyperparameters via GPR on pseudo-coupled data and updating the permutation matrix by solving a quadratic assignment problem (QAP) with simulated annealing. For scalability, the sparse-spectrum variant SS-GPSR re-expresses the model in weight space as Bayesian Shuffled Linear Regression with random Fourier features, reducing the correspondence update from NP-hard QAP to a closed-form sorting operation in O(L log L) per group while lowering memory from O(N²) to O(NH).

## Results

On five UCI benchmark datasets (Airfoil, Concrete, Housing-1D, Housing, MPG) with shuffled set sizes L=2–16, GPSR and SS-GPSR achieve the lowest test MSE in nearly all settings, outperforming both the linear baseline SLR and the neural-network baseline SDR. For example, on Airfoil (L=2), GPSR achieves 9.05±0.99 MSE versus 19.57±1.73 for SDR and 23.66±1.39 for SLR, approaching the oracle GPR bound of 6.42±0.47. On MPG (L=2), SS-GPSR achieves 7.60±1.29 MSE, beating SDR (8.96±1.55) and SLR (12.31±1.30) and approaching the oracle DR bound of 7.69±1.00. Performance degrades for all methods as L increases to 16, but GPSR/SS-GPSR remain competitive or best in most cases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPSR and SS-GPSR consistently outperform the linear shuffled regression baseline (SLR [4]) across all five datasets and all set sizes, with margins ranging from ~2–10 MSE points on Airfoil to ~50–90 MSE points on Concrete (e.g., GPSR 45.05 vs. SLR 139.79 on Concrete L=2). Against the state-of-the-art neural-network baseline SDR [12], GPSR or SS-GPSR achieves the best MSE in 19 out of 20 dataset–L combinations (the sole exception being Housing L=16, where SDR's 39.11 edges out GPSR's 41.25 and SS-GPSR's 46.88). The advantage over SDR is most pronounced at small L: on Concrete L=2, GPSR (45.05) nearly halves SDR's error (101.62), and on Airfoil L=2, GPSR (9.05) is more than 2× better than SDR (19.57). SS-GPSR tends to match or slightly exceed GPSR at larger L (e.g., MPG L=16: 10.65 vs. 18.43), while GPSR is typically stronger at small L. Both proposed methods approach the oracle coupled-data baselines (GPR, DR) at small L, confirming that the GP prior and evidence-based inference recover most of the information lost to shuffling.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the shuffled regression literature, which has been dominated by linear models (SLR) and, more recently, neural-network-based methods (SDR), by introducing the first Gaussian process formulation (GPSR). The key conceptual move is proving that GPSR's marginal likelihood and predictive distribution are exactly equivalent to those of standard GPR applied to 'pseudo-coupled data' (inputs paired with inversely-permuted responses), which allows GPR to serve as a drop-in subroutine for hyperparameter optimization. For scalability, the paper further develops a weight-space variant (BSLR) using random Fourier features, showing that the correspondence-update step reduces from an NP-hard quadratic assignment problem to a simple sorting operation—a structural simplification not previously identified in the shuffled regression literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By bringing GPs into shuffled regression, this work provides nonparametric flexibility and principled uncertainty quantification that neither SLR nor SDR offer, which is valuable in privacy-sensitive or multi-device data collection scenarios. However, the practical scope remains limited: experiments are confined to small UCI tabular datasets (≤5 input dimensions), the full GPSR algorithm requires solving QAP via simulated annealing, and SS-GPSR is restricted to shift-invariant kernels. Open questions include whether the approach scales to higher-dimensional or image/point-cloud data, whether deep kernel learning or fully Bayesian treatments of the permutation can close the remaining gap to oracle GPR, and how the method performs when the number of pairs M is large.
