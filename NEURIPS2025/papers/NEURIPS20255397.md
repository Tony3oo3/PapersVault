## Problem

Existing autoregressive mesh generation methods construct meshes face-by-face in lexicographic or traversal order, which fails to capture geometry in a manner consistent with human coarse-to-fine perception and prevents intuitive control over generation quality. Unlike 2D images where next-scale prediction (e.g., VAR) naturally defines a progressive generation order, the analogous 'next-level-of-detail' concept for 3D meshes has been largely unexplored due to the irregular connectivity and topology management challenges.

## Input / Output

The model is unconditional, receiving no external conditioning signal. It outputs a 3D simplicial complex (generalizing triangular meshes to include isolated points and edges) that is built up from a single point through successive vertex-split operations. A key property is that generation can be early-stopped at any autoregressive step to yield a mesh at a user-specified level of detail, with the number of generated vertices being exact rather than approximate. The tokenized sequence uses BPE compression over a 16,384-token vocabulary, reducing sequence length by 2–3×.

## Method

The pipeline has three stages. First, a generalized simplification algorithm (GSlim), adapted from Garland & Zhou's quadric-based simplification to operate on simplicial complexes, progressively collapses edges (including virtual edges from Delaunay tetrahedralization to enable topological changes) until the mesh reduces to a single point. Second, the simplification sequence is reversed into a Progressive Simplicial Complex (PSC): a 1D sequence of vertex-split operations, each encoded as a JSON-like configuration specifying the split vertex index, midpoint flag, positional offset, and topological labels for all adjacent simplices (constrained by four enumerated rules). Third, a 12-layer transformer (width 768, bf16 Flash Attention) is trained autoregressively on BPE-compressed token sequences, with a constrained decoding step that uses a hardcoded validity function φ evaluated via depth-first search to guarantee every predicted operation is topologically consistent with previously generated tokens.

## Results

On ShapeNet unconditional generation, ARMesh achieves the best MMD (2.44 Chair, 1.94 Table, 0.75 Bench, 1.54 Lamp), FID (1.54, 2.05, 2.63, 6.60), and KID (0.0001, 0.0002, 0.0001, 0.0005) across all four categories, substantially outperforming MeshGPT (FID 18.46, 6.24, 8.72, 19.91). ARMesh also achieves the highest COV on Bench (56.29) and Lamp (70.68), though it trails MeshGPT on COV for Chair (36.67 vs. 43.28) and Table (32.94 vs. 45.68). In the tokenization comparison (Table 4, bench category), ARMesh at only 10% of AR steps achieves COV 41.07 and MMD 1.67, far exceeding EdgeRunner (COV 9.43, MMD 10.32) and BPT (COV 15.15, MMD 3.02) at the same budget, and even surpasses their 100% results.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ARMesh decisively outperforms all baselines on MMD, FID, and KID across all four ShapeNet categories, with FID improvements of 10–100× over MeshGPT (e.g., 1.54 vs. 18.46 on Chair). Against MeshGPT, the closest direct AR competitor, ARMesh wins on MMD (2.44 vs. 3.29 Chair; 0.75 vs. 1.44 Bench) and FID/KID by large margins, but loses on COV for Chair (36.67 vs. 43.28) and Table (32.94 vs. 45.68), suggesting slightly less surface coverage on those categories. ARMesh dominates on COV for Bench (56.29 vs. 55.23) and Lamp (70.68 vs. 53.88), where the progressive LOD structure captures more of the target geometry. In the partial-generation comparison (Table 4), ARMesh at 10% AR steps (COV 41.07, MMD 1.67) already exceeds EdgeRunner and BPT at 100% steps (COV 54.90/56.35, MMD 0.81/0.76), demonstrating that intermediate LODs are consistently good approximations rather than incomplete meshes. GET3D and GET3D* use far more vertices (13,725–13,767) yet achieve worse FID (78–94) than ARMesh, while AtlasNet and BSPNet are outperformed on every metric by a wide margin. DMesh, compared in the single-mesh encoding experiment (Figure 11), requires significantly more training steps to reach comparable reconstruction quality at different LODs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts two decades-old mesh processing techniques — Garland & Zhou's quadric-based simplification in any dimension (2005) and Popović & Hoppe's progressive simplicial complexes (1997) — into a novel autoregressive generation pipeline. It departs from the dominant face-by-face lexicographic/traversal ordering used by PolyGen, MeshGPT, EdgeRunner, and BPT, instead drawing an analogy to VAR's next-scale prediction in 2D to define a 'next-level-of-detail' ordering for 3D meshes. The generalization from triangular meshes to simplicial complexes is what enables topological changes during generation, a capability absent from prior AR mesh generators that assume fixed or incrementally-built topology.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The LOD-ordered generation paradigm provides a practical quality-time knob (early stopping) that was unavailable in prior face-by-face AR mesh generators, and the intermediate results at partial steps are consistently good approximations of the full mesh rather than incomplete fragments. The GSlim algorithm's ability to handle non-manifold meshes and reduce any topology to a single point removes a preprocessing bottleneck for real-world datasets. Key open questions include scaling to conditional generation (text/image), reducing the linear generation complexity via parallel vertex splits, and deriving a differentiable PSC for gradient-based fitting — all acknowledged by the authors as future work.
