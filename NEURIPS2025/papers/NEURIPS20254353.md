## Problem

Existing multi-kernel aggregation methods for kernel two-sample and independence tests assume that combining more kernels always increases test power, but the paper shows that redundant or weak kernels can actually dilute performance. The core challenge is a trade-off: kernels must be both individually powerful and mutually diverse, yet no prior method explicitly optimizes for both properties simultaneously while maintaining valid Type-I error control.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254353/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a data-split: a training set W_tr and a testing set W_te (each of size n), where for two-sample testing each set contains paired observations (x_i, y_i) from distributions P and Q, and for independence testing each set contains quadruples (x_i, x_{i+n}, y_i, y_{i+n}). Kernels are learned on W_tr via gradient optimization, and the final output is a binary reject/accept decision for H_0, with the critical threshold τ̂_α estimated via B wild-bootstrap iterations using Rademacher multipliers. The data-splitting is essential: kernels and the signum vector F_tr are fixed before seeing W_te, ensuring non-asymptotic Type-I error control.

## Method

DUAL constructs a multivariate U-statistic vector across c learned kernels and aggregates them via a Mahalanobis-distance statistic T_n^K(W) = n²(U_n^K)^T Σ̂_H0^{-1} U_n^K, where Σ̂_H0 is the second-order covariance matrix estimated from null-hypothesis resampled data, explicitly penalizing redundant kernels. Kernels are learned on training data by gradient ascent on T_n^K(W_tr), initialized with distinct bandwidths, so that the optimization simultaneously maximizes individual kernel signal and minimizes inter-kernel covariance. At test time, a selection-inference step computes signum vectors F_tr and F_te of the Schur-decomposed statistic; only kernel components whose signs align between training and testing are retained in the final test statistic T = n²‖F ⊙ L̂_H0^{-1} U_n^K(W_te)‖². The wild bootstrap replicates this selection procedure B times to calibrate the threshold, and the paper proves non-asymptotic Type-I error ≤ α under H_0 and power → 1 under any fixed H_1 (Assumption 1: at least one kernel's sign matches the population direction).

## Results

The paper reports test power (rejection rate under H₁) averaged over 1,000 repetitions with Type-I error controlled at α = 0.05 across six benchmarks. MMD-DUAL and HSIC-DUAL consistently achieve the highest test power on all benchmarks — BLOB, MNIST, and ImageNet for two-sample testing, and Higgs, MNIST, and CIFAR-10 for independence testing — outperforming all baselines at every sample size tested. The power advantage is most pronounced at moderate sample sizes, where the combination of learned kernel diversity and selection inference provides the greatest gain over fixed-kernel or simple aggregation approaches.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MMD-DUAL outperforms all four two-sample baselines (AutoTST, MMD-Agg, MMD-D, and MMD-FUSE) on BLOB, MNIST, and ImageNet, achieving the highest rejection rate under the alternative at every sample size. For independence testing, HSIC-DUAL surpasses FSIC, HSIC-Agg, and HSIC-O on Higgs, MNIST, and CIFAR-10 with the same consistent power advantage. The paper notes that the gap over MMD-Agg and HSIC-Agg (simple aggregation without diversity or selection) is particularly large, confirming that both the covariance-based diversity measure and the signum-vector selection inference contribute independently to the power gain. MMD-FUSE, the most recent competitor that also learns kernels, is outperformed by MMD-DUAL, suggesting that explicit diversity modeling via the Mahalanobis-form statistic provides an advantage over FUSE's fusion strategy. All methods are calibrated to the same α = 0.05 significance level, so the power differences are not due to differing Type-I error rates.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Mahalanobis-distance-based aggregated U-statistic of Chatterjee & Bhattacharya (Biometrika 2025), which normalizes multiple kernel statistics by their null-hypothesis covariance, and on the signum-vector selection technique of Zhou et al. (NeurIPS 2023) for identifying locally significant differences. The key departure is identifying that naive kernel aggregation can degrade power due to redundancy, and coupling a learned diverse kernel pool (optimized via gradient ascent on the aggregated statistic) with a post-selection inference step that filters kernels based on training/testing sign alignment. The wild bootstrap framework for threshold calibration follows Schrab et al. (NeurIPS 2022), with the exchangeability argument extended to the selection-inference setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that indiscriminately adding kernels to an aggregation can reduce test power challenges a common assumption in the kernel testing literature and motivates more principled kernel selection. The DUAL framework provides a general recipe—diversity-aware aggregation plus selection inference—that could be adapted to other U-statistic-based tests (goodness-of-fit, conditional independence) and to adaptive multiple testing settings, as the authors note. A limitation is the data-splitting requirement, which halves effective sample size, and the method's reliance on the covariance matrix being strictly positive-definite. Open questions include whether the diversity measure generalizes beyond second-order (co)variance and how the approach behaves under distribution shift or non-i.i.d. sampling.
