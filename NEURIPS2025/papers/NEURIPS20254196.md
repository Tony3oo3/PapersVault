## Problem

Existing conversational response generation methods either produce only facial reactions without verbal feedback, generate only speech without visual responses, or operate offline by waiting for the full speaker input before responding. No prior work addresses the challenge of simultaneously and incrementally generating synchronized verbal (audio) and non-verbal (facial) listener responses in an online dyadic setting, where the model must reason over partial speaker input and produce temporally aligned multimodal output on the fly.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254196/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

At each time step, the model consumes the speaker's streaming facial features (extracted from 1024×1024 frontal-face video), audio, and word-level transcript, along with the listener's previously generated facial features, accumulated conversation history, and a static instruction prompt. The model autoregressively produces the listener's next facial coefficient vector (rendered to a 2D frame via a pretrained visual renderer given a fixed portrait image), a discrete audio-codec token (decoded to waveform by Spark-TTS), and a text token, all temporally aligned at the same frame index. The online setting means the model processes inputs sequentially and generates outputs frame-by-frame rather than over a complete utterance.

## Method

OmniResponse is a Multimodal LLM built on Phi-3.5 mini-instruct (3.8B parameters) fine-tuned with LoRA (rank 64), augmented with two key modules. Chrono-Text Markup inserts two special tokens—[PAUSE] for silent intervals and [LASTING] for continued speech—between every text token, making the textual sequence the same length as the visual frame sequence and providing explicit temporal anchoring for autoregressive alignment. TempoVoice is a controllable online TTS module that fuses the listener's voiceprint (via Spark-TTS global tokenizer) with generated text hidden states, then uses a cross-attention Transformer decoder with zero-initialized placeholder tokens to produce discrete audio semantic tokens in lockstep with visual and text tokens. The training objective is a weighted sum of next-token text loss, L2 facial-coefficient reconstruction loss, and discrete audio-token cross-entropy loss, all optimized jointly under a causal omni-attention mechanism that enforces temporal ordering across modalities while keeping static instruction tokens globally accessible.

## Results

On the ResponseNet test set, OmniResponse achieves METEOR 0.141, BERTScore_F1 0.806, ROUGE-L 0.081, and Distinct-2 0.882 for text quality; LSE-D 9.56 and UTMOSv2 1.41 for audio; and FD 15.46 and FVD 314.94 for video. These are the best scores among all online multimodal baselines (LSTM and Audio-visual LLM) across every evaluated metric. In a user study with 49 participants, OmniResponse received a minimum A/B preference of 67.3% (vs. LSTM) and a maximum of 95.9% (vs. Audio-visual LLM) on audio-visual synchronization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the two direct online multimodal baselines, OmniResponse dominates: it outperforms the Audio-visual LLM on every metric (e.g., METEOR 0.141 vs. 0.030, FD 15.46 vs. 580.86, FVD 314.94 vs. 681.55) and the LSTM baseline on all text and audio metrics (METEOR 0.141 vs. 0.042, UTMOSv2 1.41 vs. 1.21), though LSTM achieves a lower FD (6.51) due to producing repetitive static faces. Compared to single-modality systems, OmniResponse's text quality (METEOR 0.141, BERTScore 0.806) is competitive with strong offline LLMs like GPT-o1 (0.189, 0.822) and Gemini-2.5-Flash (0.175, 0.824), while also generating synchronized audio and video that those text-only models cannot produce. Against the online speech model Moshi, OmniResponse achieves higher METEOR (0.141 vs. 0.120) and Distinct-2 (0.882 vs. 0.499), though Moshi reports a higher UTMOSv2 (2.21 vs. 1.41) for audio naturalness. For facial reaction generation, OmniResponse improves FVD over ReactFace (314.94 vs. 340.28) and ViCo (314.94 vs. 325.65), and substantially reduces FD (15.46 vs. 32.72 and 57.13), confirming that the text-as-intermediate-modality strategy yields more natural and temporally coherent non-verbal responses.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of facial reaction generation (ReactFace, ViCo, REACT2024), spoken dialogue models (Moshi, dGSLM, AudioPALM), and autoregressive multimodal generation, but departs from all three threads by requiring simultaneous online generation of both verbal and non-verbal listener responses. Prior FRG work produces only facial reactions, prior spoken dialogue systems handle only audio/text, and talking-head methods assume audio is given rather than generated. The key conceptual departure is introducing text as an intermediate modality to decouple the difficult audio-visual synchronization problem, operationalized through Chrono-Text Markup (temporal token markers) and TempoVoice (controllable online TTS), rather than attempting direct joint audio-visual generation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

OMCRG and ResponseNet establish a new benchmark for a task that is arguably more realistic than offline or single-modality alternatives, and the 14+ hour dyadic dataset with separated audio channels and word-level annotations fills a clear gap identified in Table 1. The text-as-bridge insight is practically useful: it suggests that for future multimodal generation systems, routing through a semantically rich intermediate modality may be more tractable than direct cross-modal alignment. Open questions include scalability to longer conversations, robustness to noisy or overlapping speech, handling of emotionally rich or fast-paced interactions, and whether the approach generalizes beyond the English-language, frontal-camera setting of ResponseNet.
