## Problem

Existing causal representation learning (CRL) aims to perfectly recover latent variables and their causal graph, decoupled from any downstream objective. This paper identifies that perfect recovery can be excessive for a given task: one only needs the latent structure at the coarsest level sufficient to identify the optimal intervention for a reward function. The challenge is to design an adaptive exploration algorithm that learns just enough of the latent causal system to optimize a downstream reward over an exponential space of interventions, without incurring the exponential regret that a vanilla bandit approach would.

## Input / Output

The method consumes d-dimensional observations X = G·Z (where G is an unknown full-column-rank linear transform and d ≥ n) along with a scalar reward U(Z) = θ^T Z + ε_U at each time step, and selects interventions from the 2^n space of node subsets. It outputs the optimal intervention a* maximizing expected reward, estimated latent variables Ẑ_t = H_t X_t (recovered up to scaling and, under soft interventions, mixing with parents), and an estimated causal graph Ĝ_t (transitive closure under soft, exact under hard interventions). The performance metric is cumulative regret R_T over horizon T, with the key constraint that all probability distributions, graph topology, and transformation are unknown a priori.

## Method

RO-CRL operates in two stages: a forced exploration phase collecting T₀ samples under the observational model and each single-node atomic intervention, followed by an adaptive UCB-based selection phase. The CRL step exploits precision matrix differences R_i = Θ_{i} − Θ_∅ to recover the inverse transform H_t row-by-row via principal eigenvectors, then estimates latent variables and the graph; under hard interventions, an additional MMSE-based refinement step removes parent-mixing to achieve recovery up to scaling only. The UCB step estimates SEM weight matrices B, B* and reward parameter θ via weighted ridge regression on the estimated latent variables (with weights that down-weight outlier samples), constructs confidence ellipsoids, and selects the intervention maximizing the upper confidence bound on expected utility. An under-sampling rule forces continued exploration of single-node interventions to keep CRL estimates accurate over time. The key insight is that the 2^n interventional distributions share two noise sources and are parameterized by B, B*, and θ, so learning them reduces to estimating a small number of matrix entries, breaking the exponential dependence on n.

## Results

The paper establishes an upper regret bound of $\tilde{O}(d^{1/3} n^{1/3} u^{2/3} T^{2/3} + u\sqrt{T})$ and a nearly matching lower bound of $\Omega(d^{1/3} n^{1/3} p^{2/3} T^{2/3} + p\sqrt{T})$ for soft interventions, where $u$ measures total graph uncertainty and $p$ counts causal paths. For hard interventions, the upper bound tightens to $\tilde{O}(d^{1/2} n^{1/2} u_H^{2/3} T^{2/3} + u_H\sqrt{T})$ with perfect graph recovery. In the causal bandit special case ($\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{I}$), the bounds simplify to $\tilde{\Theta}(d_{\mathcal{G}}^{L}\sqrt{T})$, improving upon prior results by eliminating a gap in $d$. Empirically, RO-CRL achieves cumulative regret of 4832 (soft) and 5382 (hard) at $n=5$, substantially outperforming vanilla UCB (16320 and 15690 respectively).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares RO-CRL against two baselines: vanilla UCB (treating each of the $2^n$ interventions as a separate bandit arm) and a modified RO-CRL that performs CRL for a fixed fraction of the sample budget before proceeding without accounting for latent recovery error. RO-CRL achieves cumulative regret of 4832 (soft) and 5382 (hard) at $n=5$, outperforming vanilla UCB by roughly 3.4× (16320 soft, 15690 hard) and the modified RO-CRL by about 2× (9726 soft, 10132 hard). The gap between RO-CRL and the modified version is expected to grow with longer horizons since the modified version ignores accumulating CRL estimation error. Additionally, in the causal bandit setting (where the latent-to-observation transform is identity), the paper's bounds improve upon the state-of-the-art from [24] by eliminating a gap in the observation dimension $d$, yielding matching upper and lower bounds at $\tilde{\Theta}(d_{\mathcal{G}}^{L}\sqrt{T})$.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the linear CRL identifiability framework of Squires et al. (ICML 2023) and the finite-sample PAC recovery guarantees of Acartürk et al. (NeurIPS 2024), but departs from both by reframing the CRL objective: rather than recovering latent variables and the causal graph perfectly (or to a fixed accuracy), it learns them only to the coarsest level sufficient for optimizing a downstream reward over interventions. It also subsumes the causal bandit literature (Varıcı et al., JMLR 2023; Yan et al., NeurIPS 2024) as a special case (identity transformation), extending those settings to handle unknown latent variables and unknown graph topology simultaneously, and improving the resulting regret bounds by eliminating a dimension-dependent gap present in prior work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key conceptual contribution—formalizing that perfect CRL is often excessive and that one should learn only the coarsest representation needed for a downstream task—provides a principled stopping criterion for representation learning that prior CRL work lacked. The almost-matching upper and lower regret bounds (differing only in a graph-connectivity factor) give tight characterizations of the fundamental sample complexity of reward-oriented CRL. However, all results are restricted to linear SEMs, linear transformations, and linear utility functions, and the UCB computation remains intractable on general causal graphs (requiring the non-negative weight restriction in experiments). Open questions include extending to non-linear models, handling the computational bottleneck of UCB on arbitrary DAGs, and understanding robustness when the linear assumptions are violated in practice.
