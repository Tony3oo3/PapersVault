## Problem

Despite a decade of research into tactile-based RL, the utility of tactile feedback remains inconsistent, with conflicting evidence across studies about whether it improves manipulation performance. The authors identify that tactile data's inherent sparsity and non-smoothness cause learning instabilities, leading agents to prematurely converge to suboptimal proprioceptive-only policies that ignore the tactile channel. This paper addresses the gap by developing self-supervised learning objectives that force the observation encoder to extract task-critical information from sparse binary contacts, and by introducing the RoTO benchmark to standardise evaluation.

## Input / Output

The agent receives a k-timestep history (k=16 for Find, k=4 for Bounce/Baoding) of proprioceptive readings (joint angles, joint velocities, last action, and optionally end-effector pose) concatenated with N_sensors binary contact signals (2 for the Franka gripper, 17 for the Shadow Hand links). The binary tactile format is a deliberate design choice to maximise real-world scalability and avoid sim-to-real challenges associated with continuous force measurements. The policy outputs joint position actions (9-dim for Franka, 20-dim for the underactuated Shadow Hand) executed at 60 Hz within a 120 Hz physics simulation.

## Method

The approach augments PPO with four self-supervised auxiliary objectives—tactile reconstruction (TR), full reconstruction (FR), forward dynamics (FD), and tactile forward dynamics (TFD)—that train a shared MLP encoder (input→1024→512→256) to produce task-relevant latent representations feeding both the policy and value networks. The most effective objective, FD, uses a BYOL-style architecture where a forward model autoregressively predicts future latent states over n timesteps, compared against an EMA-updated target encoder (τ=0.01) through a nonlinear projection head, encouraging the representation to encode object positions and velocities. A separated auxiliary memory storing N_rollouts worth of data (rather than the single on-policy rollout) stabilises auxiliary gradient updates and yields substantial gains on temporally complex tasks like Baoding. The total loss combines PPO (clip variant) with the auxiliary loss, using separate optimisers at different learning rates for the RL and SSL components, with gradient clipping applied only to the RL update.

## Results

On the RoTO benchmark, the best self-supervised agent (Forward Dynamics) finds the object 36% faster than RL-only (1.4s vs 1.9s in Find), achieves 79 bounces in 10s vs 69 for RL-only in Bounce (best seed: 88, exceeding the human Guinness World Record of ~59), and completes 17 Baoding rotations vs 5 for RL-only (best seed: 25, vs the fastest human demonstration of 13). The Forward Dynamics (FD) objective is the most effective SSL method overall, while Tactile Reconstruction (TR) is competitive in Baoding due to tighter performance variance. Adding a separated auxiliary memory to FD yields a substantial improvement in Baoding (17 vs ~12 rotations) but only minimal gains in Find and Bounce.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four RL-only baselines (proprioceptive-only, proprioceptive+tactile, and a variant without last-action) against four SSL objectives (TR, FR, FD, TFD) and a separated-memory variant. In Find, tactile sensing offers only marginal gains over proprioception alone, and all SSL methods improve over RL-only, with FD achieving the fastest search time (1.4s vs 1.9s). In Bounce, adding tactile information improves sample efficiency and final returns over proprioception-only, and FD further boosts performance to 79 bounces (vs 69 RL-only), with the best seed reaching 88 — surpassing the human world record. In Baoding, the gap is most dramatic: proprioception-only agents fail entirely, while proprioceptive+tactile RL achieves only ~5 rotations with high variance; FD with auxiliary memory reaches 17 rotations (3.4× improvement) and the best seed achieves 25, far exceeding the fastest human demonstration (13). Among SSL objectives, FD dominates in Find and Bounce, while TR is competitive in Baoding due to lower variance; TFD is the worst in Bounce and FR shows no consistent advantage, suggesting that combining proprioceptive and tactile reconstruction causes negative interference in contact-rich tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the auxiliary-task RL framework (Jaderberg et al., 2017) to the tactile modality, adapting BYOL-style forward dynamics (EMA target encoder, projection head) and reconstruction objectives to sparse binary contact signals. It departs from the dominant tactile RL paradigm that relies on pixel-based tactile sensors, privileged state information, or teacher-student distillation, arguing instead that the simplest possible tactile signal (binary on/off contacts) combined with self-supervised representation learning is sufficient for dexterous manipulation. The separated auxiliary memory mechanism is a novel modification to on-policy RL training, and the multi-step forward dynamics objective applied to binary tactile in a model-free setting is, to the authors' knowledge, the first such study.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The RoTO benchmark (Find, Bounce, Baoding) provides a much-needed standardised testbed for tactile manipulation research, addressing the field's lack of hardware/data convergence that has hindered systematic progress. The practical finding that 17 binary contacts suffice for superhuman simulated dexterity has direct implications for sensor design, suggesting the field can avoid the cost and sim-to-real complexity of pixel-based tactile. Key open questions include sim-to-real transfer (acknowledged as the primary limitation), whether the SSL benefits generalise beyond these three Isaac Lab tasks, and the precise mechanism by which forward dynamics encodes object state — the mutual information analysis is suggestive but not fully conclusive.
