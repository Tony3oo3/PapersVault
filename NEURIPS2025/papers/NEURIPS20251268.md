## Problem

Best-of-N (BoN) selection with imperfect reward models produces a stochastic output distribution where the correct answer is often the most probable outcome but rarely has probability near 1. Existing methods either sample from this distribution (BoN) or ignore the reward signal entirely (Self-consistency), leaving the 'mode is correct but probability < 1' structure unexploited. The paper asks whether one can reliably recover the mode of BoN's output distribution without an oracle.

## Input / Output

Given N LLM-generated outputs for a question and their corresponding reward model scores, MoB produces a single final answer. No additional LLM generation is required beyond the N outputs already produced for BoN; all MoB computation (bootstrapping, majority voting) runs on CPU with negligible overhead. The adaptive subsample-size procedure eliminates the need for any user-specified hyperparameter, and a closed-form O(N log N) computation replaces the need for an explicit bootstrap sample count B.

## Method

MoB estimates BoN's output distribution π_m via m-out-of-n bootstrapping: it draws B subsets of size m < N with replacement from the N generated outputs, selects the highest-reward output in each subset, and performs majority voting over the resulting answers. The use of m < N (rather than m = N) is critical because BoN selects an extreme (maximum reward), a known failure case for standard bootstrapping; m-out-of-n bootstrapping remedies this, and the paper proves consistency of the estimate under mild tail conditions (Theorem 1). An adaptive procedure, inspired by Bickel and Sakov (2008), selects m by minimizing the ℓ₁ distance between estimates at m and qm for a fixed q = 0.75, requiring only O(log N) distribution evaluations. The paper also derives a closed-form expression for the bootstrapped distribution by sorting outputs by reward, reducing computation to O(N log N) and effectively operating as if B = ∞.

## Results

On MATH500 (N=128), MoB-Adaptive achieves 69.95% accuracy with Qwen2.5-3B + GRM, improving over BoN (63.95%) by +6.00 points, SC (66.40%) by +3.55 points, and WBoN (67.45%) by +2.50 points. Across all 30 experimental setups (5 benchmarks × 3 base models × 2 reward models), MoB outperforms BoN in 25 out of 30 configurations, with the largest gains on MATH500 (up to +11.35 points with Llama3.1-8B + ArmoRM) and MMLU-Pro-Chem (up to +7.70 points). The method adds only negligible CPU overhead since the bootstrap estimation uses a closed-form O(N log N) computation rather than explicit resampling.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MoB consistently outperforms BoN across most benchmarks: on MATH500 it gains +6.00 points (69.95 vs 63.95), on MMLU-Pro-Math +3.20 points (69.30 vs 66.10), and on MMLU-Pro-Chem +7.45 points (56.45 vs 49.00) with Qwen2.5-3B + GRM. The only exception is CommonsenseQA, where MoB-Adaptive (77.40) is marginally below BoN (77.70) by 0.30 points, a difference within the standard error. Against Self-Consistency, MoB wins on all five benchmarks in this setup, with the largest margin on MMLU-Pro-Chem (+3.95 points). Against Weighted BoN, MoB dominates on MATH500 (+2.50), MMLU-Pro-Math (+4.95), and MMLU-Pro-Chem (+3.35), while WBoN performs anomalously poorly on CSQA (54.90 vs MoB's 77.40). Across the full 30-setup evaluation, MoB wins 25/30 against BoN, with the largest single improvement being +11.35 points on MATH500 with Llama3.1-8B + ArmoRM.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits within the sample-and-marginalize family of inference-time scaling methods (Wang et al., 2022), which includes Self-consistency (SC), Best-of-N (BoN), and Weighted BoN. Its core conceptual move is to transplant the majority-voting principle from SC (vote over the base model's output distribution) to the BoN setting (vote over BoN's output distribution), using m-out-of-n bootstrapping (Bickel et al., 2011; Bickel and Sakov, 2008) to estimate that distribution without additional LLM calls. The adaptive subsample size selection directly follows the optimality framework of Bickel and Sakov (2008) for choosing m in m-out-of-n bootstrap, and the closed-form computation of the bootstrapped BoN distribution (Appendix B) avoids the need for explicit resampling. Unlike regularized BoN variants (Jinnai et al., 2024; Ichihara et al., 2025) that modify the reward scoring, MoB leaves the reward model untouched and only changes the final selection step.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MoB offers a drop-in, zero-extra-LLM-call improvement over BoN that requires only CPU computation, making it practically attractive for any pipeline already using reward-model-based selection. The theoretical consistency guarantee (Theorem 1) and the hyperparameter-free adaptive m selection strengthen its appeal as a default replacement. However, the method is limited to tasks with discrete final answers, and the improvements, while consistent (25/30 setups), are moderate (typically 2–8 percentage points), with some setups (e.g., CSQA with certain models) showing no gain or slight degradation. Open questions include whether the MoB selection signal can enable early stopping in parallel generation, and whether the approach extends to open-ended or continuous-output tasks where a 'mode' is not well-defined.
