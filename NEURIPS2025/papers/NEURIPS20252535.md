## Problem

Fully adaptive bandit algorithms (UCB, Thompson Sampling) explore at every time step, which is costly or unethical in safety-critical domains, while purely greedy policies fail without strong contextual diversity assumptions. The literature lacks a rigorous analysis of whether and how infrequent exploration suffices for near-optimal regret in linear bandits with fixed arm features, leaving a substantial gap between these two extremes.

## Input / Output

INFEX takes as input a base fully-adaptive linear bandit algorithm (e.g., LinUCB or LinTS), a predetermined exploration schedule T_e ⊂ ℕ specifying which time steps trigger exploration, and a finite set of K arm feature vectors in the d-dimensional unit ball. At each step it outputs an arm selection; at scheduled steps the base algorithm chooses the arm, while at all other steps a ridge-regression-based greedy rule selects the arm with the highest estimated reward. The framework is modular: any algorithm with polylogarithmic regret can serve as the base, and the schedule can be periodic, logarithmically growing, or sublinear.

## Method

INFEX interleaves a base exploratory policy with greedy selections: at scheduled time steps it executes the base algorithm (e.g., LinUCB or LinTS), and at all other steps it computes a ridge estimator θ̂_t = V_t^{-1} Σ X_i Y_i and selects argmax_x x^⊤ θ̂_t. The key analytical insight is that the estimation error for the optimal arm decreases as the number of optimal selections grows, and the base algorithm guarantees at least a quarter of its selections are optimal after a threshold τ_Alg. The main theorem shows that if the cumulative exploration count f(t) = ω(log t), the total regret decomposes into the base algorithm's regret evaluated at f(T) plus a T-independent constant plus a term matching LinUCB's instance-dependent bound O((log T + d log log T + d log(d/Δ))²/Δ). A lower bound (Theorem 3) establishes that the ω(log t) threshold is necessary via an information-theoretic two-instance argument. The framework also yields a new instance-dependent regret bound for LinTS and reduces per-step computational cost by replacing expensive confidence-set or posterior-sampling computations with O(d² + dK) greedy updates.

## Results

Theoretically, INFEX achieves instance-dependent regret of O((log T + d log log T + d log(d/Δ))²/Δ) — matching the LinUCB bound — provided the exploration frequency f(t) = ω(log t). Empirically (d=10, T=10000), INFEX with LinTS as the base algorithm and m=5, 20, or 100 (i.e., 80–99% greedy steps) all reduce cumulative regret below the fully-adaptive LinTS baseline, while also cutting computation time. For LinUCB, m=5 similarly improves regret over the base algorithm. The ω(log t) threshold is shown to be necessary: below it, regret scales as Ω(T^{1−ε}) for some problem instances (Theorem 3).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the fully-adaptive LinTS baseline, INFEX with m=5, 20, or 100 all reduce cumulative regret (Figure 1), with the largest gains at m=5 and m=20; this is notable because the base LinTS already explores at every step. Against the fully-adaptive LinUCB baseline, INFEX with m=5 also improves regret, while m=20 and m=100 are roughly on par. Against the purely greedy policy, INFEX consistently achieves lower regret across all K values (10, 100, 1000), confirming that even infrequent exploration is necessary in fixed-arm linear bandits. Against OLSBandit (Goldenshluger & Zeevi, 2013), INFEX is substantially more efficient because OLSBandit spends Ω(d² log T) steps on forced sampling, whereas INFEX limits exploration to a sparse schedule. Against ε-greedy (ε_t = t^{-1/3}), INFEX achieves polylogarithmic regret while ε-greedy is theoretically lower-bounded at Ω(T^{2/3}), making it fundamentally suboptimal. All INFEX variants also reduce wall-clock computation time relative to their fully-adaptive counterparts, since the expensive UCB/TS updates are performed only every m steps.

## Relation to Prior Work

INFEX positions itself in the gap between fully adaptive exploratory methods (LinUCB, LinTS) that balance exploration and exploitation at every step, and purely greedy policies that succeed only under strong contextual diversity assumptions. It builds directly on LinUCB and LinTS as base exploratory algorithms, incorporating their confidence-set and posterior-sampling machinery, while introducing a modular schedule that replaces most exploratory steps with cheap greedy selections via the ridge estimator. The framework also connects to earlier scheduled-exploration approaches (ETC, forced-sampling, ε-greedy) but departs from them by maintaining adaptivity through the base algorithm rather than committing to a fixed exploration phase, and by providing the first rigorous analysis of how exploration frequency affects instance-dependent regret in linear bandits.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that ω(log T) exploration suffices for polylogarithmic regret—and that this threshold is necessary—resolves a fundamental open question about whether continuous exploration is required, with direct practical implications for safety-critical and costly domains where frequent experimentation is undesirable. The new instance-dependent regret bound for LinTS (Theorem 2) is a standalone contribution of independent interest. Open questions remain: the minimax regret threshold for infrequent exploration is conjectured to be higher than ω(log T), the analysis assumes a fixed arm set and positive gap, and extension to time-varying or contextual settings is left for future work. The computational efficiency gains (reducing expensive posterior sampling or confidence computations to infrequent intervals) make the framework attractive for large-scale or real-time deployment.
