## Problem

Binary preference feedback reveals which option was chosen but not how strongly, making reward model learning sample-inefficient—especially when one option consistently dominates. Response time is a free, implicit signal of preference strength (faster decisions indicate stronger preferences), yet prior work exploiting it was limited to active linear bandit settings with carefully designed queries. This paper asks how to robustly integrate response-time information into general, passive preference learning with provable convergence guarantees for both linear and non-parametric reward classes.

## Input / Output

The method consumes triples of (query pair X = (X¹, X²), binary preference Y ∈ {+1, −1}, response time T) drawn i.i.d. from an unknown distribution, where the query pairs can be images, text, or other modalities (experiments use CLIP 1024-d embeddings of text-image pairs from Pick-a-Pic). The EZ-diffusion model parameters—barrier height a and non-decision time t_nd—are treated as known constants (a = 1 in the main text), with the reward difference r(X) := r(X¹) − r(X²) as the target. The output is a reward function estimate r̂(·) (linear parameter θ̂ or a neural network) that can be plugged into downstream policy optimization or DPO-style objectives.

## Method

The core contribution is a Neyman-orthogonal loss L^ortho(r; r̄, t) = E[(Y − (T − t(X))r̄(X) − r(X)t(X))²] that jointly leverages binary choices and response times while being first-order insensitive to errors in the nuisance pair (r̄, t), where r̄ is a preliminary reward estimate and t approximates E[T|X]. The key identity from the EZ-diffusion model is r(X) = E[Y|X]/E[T|X], but the naive squared-error loss built on this is highly sensitive to nuisance estimation; the orthogonal construction cancels the first-order cross-term via the debiasing residual (T − t(X)). A two-stage meta-algorithm (with cross-fitting or data-splitting) first fits nuisance functions—r̄ via logistic regression and t via a neural network or the plug-in t(X) = tanh(r̄(X))/r̄(X)—then minimizes the orthogonal loss for the final reward estimate. For linear rewards, the asymptotic variance of the orthogonal estimator scales polynomially (via tanh(x)/x) with reward magnitude, versus exponential scaling (via σ(x)σ(−x)) for the standard log-loss estimator. For general reward classes (RKHS balls, neural networks), finite-sample bounds show nuisance errors enter only as second-order terms, recovering oracle rates as if true expected response times were known a priori.

## Results

Across all three experimental settings (linear rewards, neural-network rewards, and the Pick-a-Pic text-to-image task), the proposed Neyman-orthogonal loss consistently achieves lower MSE and policy regret than both the preference-only log-loss and the non-orthogonal squared-error loss. On the Pick-a-Pic benchmark (M=10,000 evaluation queries), the orthogonal loss yields significantly lower MSE and cumulative regret across all training-set sizes N. For linear rewards, the estimation error under log-loss grows rapidly with the true parameter norm ‖θ₀‖, while the orthogonal loss maintains substantially lower error, consistent with the theoretical prediction of polynomial (vs. exponential) scaling in reward magnitude.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three loss functions: (1) the standard log-loss (Bradley-Terry, preference-only), (2) a non-orthogonal squared-error loss that naively incorporates response time, and (3) the proposed Neyman-orthogonal loss. The orthogonal loss wins on every metric in every setting: on the Pick-a-Pic text-to-image task it achieves consistently lower MSE and cumulative regret than both baselines across all training sizes; on synthetic linear rewards it dramatically outperforms log-loss as the parameter norm grows (where log-loss error explodes); and on neural-network rewards it beats both alternatives in MSE and policy regret. The non-orthogonal loss improves over log-loss but remains clearly inferior to the orthogonal loss, confirming that the Neyman-orthogonalization step is essential for robustness to nuisance estimation error. The paper also notes that substituting the orthogonal loss into the sequential-elimination algorithm of Li et al. [LZR+24] improves best-arm identification probability, though this is reported qualitatively in the appendix rather than as a head-to-head table.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes two previously separate research threads: the Neyman-orthogonal loss framework from orthogonal statistical learning (Foster & Syrgkanis, 2023) and the EZ-diffusion model from cognitive psychology (Wagenmakers et al., 2007), which links response times to preference strength. It extends the most direct prior work—Li et al. (2024), who showed response times improve active preference-based linear bandits—by moving to passive i.i.d. supervised settings, general (nonlinear) reward classes, and establishing oracle-rate guarantees via debiased estimation techniques from Chernozhukov et al. (2018). The paper also departs from GP-based response-time integration (Shvartsman et al., 2024) by avoiding variational approximations entirely, making the approach scalable to high-dimensional deep reward models used in RLHF pipelines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The exponential-to-polynomial improvement in variance scaling with reward magnitude is a clean theoretical result with direct implications for sample efficiency in RLHF and preference-based learning, where reward magnitudes can be large. However, all experiments use synthetically generated response times from the EZ model rather than real human data, and the framework assumes a homogeneous reward function—both limitations that could substantially affect practical performance. Open questions include whether the gains persist with noisy, population-dependent real response times, how the method extends to online bandit settings with regret guarantees, and whether the orthogonal loss can be adapted to DPO-style direct policy optimization without an explicit reward model.
