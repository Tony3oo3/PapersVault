## Problem

Machine unlearning was originally motivated by the right to be forgotten under GDPR, and emerging AI regulations like the EU AI Act appear to create new compliance use cases for MU. However, recent work questions whether MU can actually fulfill these regulatory roles, and no systematic assessment exists of where MU's state of the art falls short of what AI regulation actually demands. This position paper argues that MU's regulatory potential will only be realized if researchers proactively close the identified technical gaps.

## Input / Output

The analysis takes as input the full text of the EU AI Act (particularly Articles 9, 15, 53, and 55) and the current machine unlearning literature, and produces a structured catalog of six compliance use cases—accuracy, bias, confidentiality attacks, data poisoning, generative AI risk, and copyright—each annotated with the specific AIA provisions, an assessment of SOTA MU readiness, and a list of open technical problems. The scope is restricted to ML techniques that explicitly modify model parameters (deletion/retraining, fine-tuning, parameter editing), deliberately excluding output-suppression approaches like guardrailing, which the authors argue merit separate regulatory evaluation.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a systematic 'state of the union' analysis that maps each of six potential MU applications to specific AIA provisions, then scrutinizes whether current SOTA MU can technically support each application with special attention to auditability—the ability of regulators to inspect and verify unlearning efficacy. For each use case, the authors assess whether existing MU methods (retraining, fine-tuning, gradient ascent, parameter editing) meet the verification requirements unique to regulatory contexts, and flag concrete open problems such as identifying forget sets at scale, providing formal forgetting guarantees, and managing utility-privacy trade-offs (including the 'onion effect' and 'Streisand effect'). The paper also presents alternative views, arguing that for several use cases, non-MU approaches (differential privacy, RLHF, dataset governance, robust training) may be more appropriate, and positions MU best as a reactive complement rather than a primary compliance tool.

## Results

This is a position paper with no quantitative experiments or results tables. Its primary contribution is a qualitative catalog of six potential applications of machine unlearning to EU AI Act compliance (accuracy, bias, confidentiality attacks, data poisoning, generative AI risk, and copyright), each accompanied by an assessment of whether current SOTA MU can support the use case. The authors conclude that in most cases, significant technical gaps exist between MU's current capabilities and what would be needed for regulatory compliance, with auditability and verification being a recurring cross-cutting challenge.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark MU against baselines quantitatively. Instead, it qualitatively positions MU relative to alternative compliance strategies for each of the six use cases. For accuracy, full retraining or data pipeline improvements are noted as simpler and more reliable, with MU serving as a fallback. For bias, pre-/in-/post-processing debiasing methods are considered more holistic and verifiable than MU's subtractive approach. For confidentiality, differential privacy (DP) and access control are framed as stronger proactive safeguards, though the authors note DP struggles with the utility-privacy trade-off and is overly broad for targeted forget-sets. For data poisoning, robust/certified training is preferred as a proactive defense, with MU as reactive remediation. For generative AI risk, RLHF and guardrailing are considered more effective at constraining behavior, with MU addressing residual representations. For copyright, dataset governance and output filtering are preferred preventive tools, with MU as a post-hoc corrective. The authors argue MU is not a silver bullet but could complement these alternatives, particularly where targeted, post-deployment intervention is needed.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper repurposes the machine unlearning (MU) literature—originally motivated by the GDPR's right to be forgotten (Ginart et al. 2019; Cao & Yang 2015; Bourtoule et al. 2021)—toward a new regulatory context: the EU AI Act. It engages critically with recent works that question MU's viability in generative settings (Cooper et al. 2024; Shumailov et al. 2024) and positions MU as a post-processing technique within the broader debiasing taxonomy (Mehrabi et al. 2021). Rather than proposing a new algorithm, the paper carves new ground by systematically mapping six AIA compliance requirements to specific MU capabilities and, for each, cataloging the technical gaps between state-of-the-art and regulatory need, with particular emphasis on auditability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central finding is that the gaps between MU's current capabilities and AI regulation compliance needs are substantial across most use cases: identifying forget sets at scale, providing formal guarantees for auditability, handling conceptual (non-discrete) forget targets, and managing the subtractive-only nature of MU (it cannot remedy data deficits). The auditability gap is especially critical—regulators need verifiable proofs of forgetting, yet most approximate MU methods rely on empirical proxies rather than formal guarantees. The paper issues a concrete call to action for the ML community to solve these open problems, making it a useful roadmap for researchers at the intersection of MU and AI governance, though its impact depends on whether the identified gaps are actually closed.
