## Problem

Theoretical analyses of in-context learning in Transformers have largely relied on simplified architectures that omit MLPs, single homogeneous data sources, and linear tasks, leaving open how nonlinear MLPs and heterogeneous data mixing shape ICL behavior. This paper addresses two gaps: (i) characterizing the role of nonlinear MLP heads in ICL under realistic multi-source data, and (ii) understanding how data mixture ratios and source properties (covariance structure, noise level) govern ICL performance and feature learning.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a context of ℓ input-label pairs (each input a d-dimensional vector, each label a scalar) plus a new query input, which are stacked into an embedding matrix Z ∈ R^{(d+1)×(ℓ+1)} with a zero placeholder for the unknown label. The output is a scalar regression prediction for the query. In the real-world multilingual sentiment experiment, inputs are 64-dimensional PCA-reduced embeddings from the multilingual-e5-small model applied to review texts, and labels are demeaned/scaled star ratings in [-1,1]. The ICL error is defined as the average squared prediction error across S data sources.

## Method

The architecture is a single-block Transformer with linear attention followed by a two-layer nonlinear MLP head, trained in two stages: one gradient descent step on the first-layer matrix F (with step size η scaling as o(d²)) followed by ridge regression on the second-layer vector w. The key theoretical result (Theorem 4.12) proves that under high-dimensional asymptotics (d, ℓ, n, k → ∞ with fixed ratios), this Transformer is asymptotically equivalent in ICL error to a finite-degree polynomial model whose coefficients are determined by the Hermite expansion of the activation function. The proof proceeds by showing the attention output vec(H_Z) has a low-rank spiked covariance structure, establishing a conditional Gaussian equivalence for the MLP feature map, and then leveraging Hermite polynomial orthogonality to identify the equivalent polynomial degree. The data model allows S heterogeneous sources with distinct input/task covariance matrices and noise variances, enabling precise characterization of how structured covariances and low noise in training sources improve ICL, and how feature learning (via the gradient step on F) only benefits ICL when task vectors exhibit non-isotropic covariance.

## Results

The paper's main theoretical result (Theorem 4.12) establishes that a Transformer with a two-layer nonlinear MLP head is asymptotically equivalent to a finite-degree polynomial model in terms of ICL error, under high-dimensional scaling (d, ℓ, n, k → ∞ with fixed ratios). Empirically, the nonlinear MLP Transformer consistently outperforms the linear Transformer baseline on nonlinear (ReLU) regression tasks across varying sample sizes, context lengths, and hidden dimensions (Figure 1), and its ICL error curves closely track the polynomial surrogate even at moderate dimensions (d=80). Data mixing experiments (Figure 2) show that increasing the proportion of a structured (spiked-covariance) source or reducing its noise variance monotonically decreases ICL error. In the real-world multilingual sentiment analysis experiment (Figure 3c), the Transformer's ICL error again aligns with the polynomial surrogate, and increasing the step size η reduces error, confirming effective feature learning on real text embeddings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three models: (1) a linear Transformer with linear attention only (Eq. 4), (2) a Transformer with a two-layer nonlinear MLP head trained via one gradient step on the first layer and ridge regression on the second (Eq. 5), and (3) the theoretically derived equivalent polynomial model (Eq. 13). The nonlinear MLP Transformer outperforms the linear Transformer across all synthetic settings (Figures 1–3), confirming that the trained first layer enables meaningful feature learning on nonlinear tasks. The nonlinear MLP Transformer and the polynomial surrogate produce nearly identical ICL error curves in every experiment, empirically validating the asymptotic equivalence theorem. On the real-world multilingual sentiment analysis task (English vs. German reviews from the Multilingual Amazon Reviews Corpus), the same alignment holds, and a higher proportion of English data (the stronger source for the multilingual-e5-small embedding) yields lower ICL error, consistent with the theoretical prediction that higher-quality sources improve performance. No external baselines beyond the linear Transformer are compared; the paper's contribution is the theoretical equivalence and the characterization of data-mixing effects rather than a benchmark comparison against other ICL methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the linear-attention ICL framework of Zhang et al. [47] and Lu et al. [27] by incorporating a nonlinear two-layer MLP head, and bridges the Gaussian universality results for supervised two-layer networks (Hu & Lu [21], Demir & Doğan [13], Moniri et al. [30]) into the in-context learning setting. The two-stage training procedure (one gradient step on the first layer, ridge regression on the second) follows Ba et al. [4] and Damian et al. [9], while the multi-source data mixing setup departs from the single-homogeneous-source assumption in all prior ICL theory. Recent MLP-in-Transformer ICL studies [23, 24, 32] either use nonstandard architectures (MLP before attention), restrict activation types, or omit attention training; this work addresses those gaps by analyzing the canonical post-attention MLP with trained linear attention under heterogeneous data sources.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The polynomial equivalence result provides a tractable analytical lens for understanding how nonlinear MLPs shape ICL, and the identification of structured task covariance (rather than input covariance) as the key driver of feature learning offers a concrete criterion for data curation in multi-source pretraining. Limitations are significant: the analysis is restricted to a single attention block, linear attention, a specific two-phase training schedule, and scaling assumptions (η = o(d²), joint proportional limits) that may not hold in practice. Open questions include extending to multi-layer Transformers, softmax attention, end-to-end training, and characterizing the polynomial degree needed for different task families—directions that would substantially broaden the practical relevance of the framework.
