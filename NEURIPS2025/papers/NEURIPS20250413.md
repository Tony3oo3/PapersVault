## Problem

Existing diffusion-based OOD sample generation methods (e.g., Dream-OOD, NCIS, BOOD) rely on perturbing text-conditioned embeddings in a latent space, which causes semantic instability—small embedding shifts produce unpredictable and disproportionate image changes—and limited shift diversity, capturing primarily semantic variation while neglecting covariate and domain shifts. This makes it difficult to generate the diverse, informative OOD samples needed to train robust detectors via Outlier Exposure.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pretrained ID classifier (ResNet-34) and Stable Diffusion v1.5 as off-the-shelf components, with class label descriptions (e.g., 'A high-quality image of the <class>') serving as conditional text prompts to anchor generation near the ID class. The diffusion model operates at 512×512 or 128×128 resolution, and a differentiable bilinear downsampling layer bridges the resolution gap to the classifier's input size (224×224 for ImageNet, 32×32 for CIFAR), enabling end-to-end gradient flow. At training time it produces ~23,000 synthetic OOD images (12,500 image-level + 10,500 feature-level for ImageNet-100); at test time it outputs a unified OOD score that adaptively fuses pixel-level free energy and feature-space k-NN distance.

## Method

GOOD directly guides the denoising trajectory of a diffusion model in pixel space using gradients from an off-the-shelf ID classifier, eliminating the embedding-alignment and perturbation steps of prior methods. It introduces two complementary guidance signals: image-level guidance (GOOD_img) that follows the gradient of the classifier's free energy to push samples toward low-likelihood regions in pixel space, and feature-level guidance (GOOD_feat) that follows the gradient of k-NN distance in the classifier's normalized feature space to promote sampling in feature-sparse areas. Both are integrated into the TFG (Training-Free Guidance) framework, which applies variance guidance (a second-order signal on the noisy sample x_t) and mean guidance (a first-order signal on the clean estimate x_{0|t}) with time-dependent increasing step schedules. A balanced sampling strategy draws from a 5×5 grid of (ρ, μ) guidance-strength combinations, producing OOD samples spanning subtle boundary-adjacent shifts to semantically abstract outliers. At inference, a unified OOD score adaptively weights the energy and k-NN signals using the KL divergence between test-set and ID-validation score distributions, emphasizing whichever signal is more discriminative for the current OOD scenario.

## Results

On ImageNet-100, GOOD achieves an average FPR95 of 17.68% and AUROC of 96.30%, substantially outperforming the next-best diffusion-based method NCIS (FPR95 33.89%, AUROC 93.32%) and all post-hoc scoring baselines. On CIFAR-100, GOOD with a 128×128 Stable Diffusion model reaches FPR95 19.50% and AUROC 94.68%, improving over FodFOM (FPR95 34.45%, AUROC 93.52%) and Dream-OOD (FPR95 40.31%, AUROC 90.15%). The method maintains competitive ID accuracy (87.16% on ImageNet-100) while achieving the largest FPR95 reduction among all compared approaches.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GOOD outperforms all diffusion-based synthesis baselines by a wide margin on ImageNet-100: it reduces average FPR95 from 33.89% (NCIS, the closest competitor) to 17.68%, and from 38.76% (Dream-OOD) to 17.68%, while also improving AUROC from 93.32% to 96.30%. Against post-hoc scoring methods, GOOD surpasses the strongest baselines—ReAct (FPR95 41.17%) and kNN (FPR95 41.38%)—by over 23 percentage points in FPR95. The sole exception is the Textures dataset, where NPOS (FPR95 8.98%) outperforms GOOD (19.51%), which the authors attribute to Stable Diffusion's limited ability to generate texture-like OOD images. On CIFAR-100, GOOD (128×128 SD) achieves FPR95 19.50% versus 34.45% for FodFOM and 40.31% for Dream-OOD, with particularly dramatic gains on ISUN (0.07% FPR95) and SVHN (6.65% FPR95). The ablation (Table 3) confirms that both image-level and feature-level guidance are necessary: removing either raises FPR95 from 17.70% to over 32%, and the unified OOD score provides the single largest improvement over using energy score alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

GOOD builds directly on the TFG (Training-Free Guidance) framework [71], which provides a unified formalism for steering diffusion sampling with arbitrary differentiable target functions, and repurposes it for a fundamentally different objective: generating OOD samples rather than enforcing semantic or stylistic alignment. The two guidance signals are drawn from established OOD scoring literature—free energy [46] for pixel-level likelihood and k-NN feature distance [61] for latent-space sparsity—while the Outlier Exposure training loss follows VOS [16]. The key departure from prior diffusion-based OOD synthesis methods (Dream-OOD, NCIS, BOOD, FodFoM) is the elimination of the two-stage embedding-alignment-and-perturbation pipeline: GOOD guides the denoising trajectory directly in pixel space via classifier gradients, avoiding the semantic instability and limited shift diversity that the paper attributes to latent-space perturbations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By removing the expensive and semantically fragile embedding-alignment step, GOOD offers a simpler, more scalable pipeline for synthetic OOD generation that is model-agnostic (works with any pretrained classifier) and requires no retraining of the diffusion model. The unified OOD score with KL-divergence-adaptive weighting is a practical detection-time contribution that addresses the well-known weakness of single-metric OOD scoring across heterogeneous shift types. A key open question is how the method scales to more powerful generative models (e.g., SDXL, video diffusion) and whether the reliance on the diffusion model's learned distribution caps the diversity of generated outliers, as the authors acknowledge. The finding that a 128×128 generator outperforms 512×512 on CIFAR-100 also raises practical questions about optimal generator resolution for low-resolution ID datasets.
