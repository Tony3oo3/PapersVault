## Problem

Extending Q-learning to continuous-time stochastic control causes the Q-function to collapse into an action-independent value function, losing its ability to distinguish between actions—a critical property for decision-making. Existing work either relies on time discretization (which introduces approximation error and breaks continuous-time structure) or uses first-order approximations like little q-learning that sacrifice full action dependence. The paper asks: how can one design a principled, discretization-free Q-learning framework for stochastic continuous-time environments that preserves the action-evaluation capability of the Q-function?

## Input / Output

The method takes a state vector x ∈ ℝⁿ and interacts with a continuous-time environment simulator that, given (x, a), returns a next state x' and reward r over a small interval Δt. Actions are not chosen directly but sampled from a diffusion process: starting from a^{T} ~ N(0, I), the score function Ψ^v is applied iteratively to denoise down to a^{0}, yielding an action drawn from a Boltzmann distribution π(a|x) ∝ exp(Q(x,a)/λ). The learned outputs are the Q-function parameters θ (a quadratic form in (x,a)) and the score function parameters v (a linear function of (x,a)), which together define both the value estimate and the policy.

## Method

The core insight is a martingale characterization: Q(·,·,·;Ψ) is the correct Q-function for score Ψ if and only if Q(s,X_s,a_s;Ψ) + ∫[r − (λ/2)‖Ψ‖²]du is a martingale under the joint state-action filtration. This yields an HJB equation whose optimal score satisfies Ψ* = λ⁻¹∇ₐQ, and a Score Improvement Theorem proving that aligning Ψ with ∇ₐQ guarantees monotonic Q-value improvement. The CQSM algorithm is an actor-critic procedure: the critic updates θ via a temporal-difference-style rule derived from the martingale orthogonality condition (analogous to MSTDE in discrete RL), while the actor updates v by minimizing ‖Ψ^v − λ⁻¹∇ₐQ‖², i.e., matching the diffusion score to the Q-function's action gradient. Actions are sampled from the stationary distribution of the action SDE, which under σ_a = √2 I_d takes the closed-form Boltzmann shape exp(Q/λ)/Z, giving a soft optimal policy shaped directly by Q-values rather than by auxiliary Hamiltonians.

## Results

The paper evaluates CQSM on a 1D Linear-Quadratic stochastic control problem (A=−1, B=C=0, D=1, M=N=P′=2, R=P=1, β=1, λ=0.1) with a single state trajectory of length T=10⁵. The primary metric is the running average reward over the learning process, reported across three discretization step sizes (Δt=0.01, 0.1, 1). CQSM's learned Q-function and score parameters converge toward their closed-form theoretical optima (e.g., θ*=[−0.5905, −0.2307, −0.4614, −0.3562, −0.1512, 0.1731], v*=[1.5291, −1.5119, −3.5624]), though parameters tied to ∇ₐQ (θ₂, θ₃, v₂) exhibit residual bias due to the dual estimation of Q and its gradient. Each experiment takes approximately 352 seconds on an Intel Core i5-10500 CPU with 32 GB RAM.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CQSM is compared against two continuous-time RL baselines: (1) the policy gradient method of Jia & Zhou (JMLR 2022, Algorithm 3) and (2) the little q-learning method of Jia & Zhou (JMLR 2023, Algorithm 4). Across all three step sizes (Δt=0.01, 0.1, 1), CQSM consistently achieves higher running average rewards in the early-to-mid stages of training, converging to good reward levels faster than both baselines. However, after a sufficiently long training horizon, all three methods stabilize to similar average reward levels, indicating that CQSM's advantage is primarily in convergence speed rather than asymptotic performance. The paper does not report a single scalar summary metric (e.g., final reward or area-under-curve); the comparison is qualitative from the reward trajectories in Figure 3. Notably, CQSM is the only method that preserves full action-dependence of the Q-function without time discretization, whereas little q-learning relies on first-order Q approximations and policy gradient operates on the state-only value function.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of continuous-time reinforcement learning (CT-RL) and diffusion-based policy learning. It builds directly on the CT-RL framework of Wang, Zariphopoulou, and Zhou [52] and the policy evaluation/gradient methods of Jia and Zhou [23, 24], as well as the little q-learning approach of Jia and Zhou [25]. The key departure is from Psenka et al. [35], who also connect diffusion policy scores to Q-function gradients but only determine the update direction (not magnitude) and rely on time discretization. CQSM instead derives a full martingale characterization of the continuous-time Q-function and a score improvement theorem that specifies both direction and magnitude of the policy update, operating without any time or action-space discretization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a recognized gap in CT-RL: standard Q-functions collapse to action-independent value functions in continuous time, losing their decision-making utility. The martingale-based characterization and the score improvement theorem provide a theoretically grounded path to preserve action dependence, and the LQ closed-form solution validates the framework. However, empirical validation is limited to a single 1D LQ problem, the chosen test function is acknowledged as theoretically insufficient to guarantee the full martingale condition, and no convergence rate is established. Open questions include extending to mean-variance objectives (which introduce time inconsistency), optimizing the diffusion coefficient σ_a for better exploration, and scaling to higher-dimensional or nonlinear environments.
