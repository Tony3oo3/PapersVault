## Problem

Existing PPI prediction benchmarks evaluate models on isolated pairwise accuracy, overlooking whether predictions collectively reconstruct biologically meaningful PPI networks. This gap leaves it unclear whether current models can recover the topological structure (sparsity, community organization) and functional semantics (pathway coherence, essential protein centrality) of real interactomes, which are critical for downstream applications like disease mechanism analysis and drug target identification.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253131/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Models consume protein amino acid sequences (and optionally 3D structures for structure-based methods) to produce binary interaction labels for each protein pair, which are then aggregated into a reconstructed PPI network. The benchmark evaluates these reconstructed graphs against ground-truth networks across four species (Human, Arath, Ecoli, Yeast; 21,484 proteins, 186,818 interactions) using five topology metrics (graph similarity, relative density, degree distribution MMD, clustering coefficient MMD, spectral MMD) and three function-oriented metrics (pathway precision/recall/connectivity, GO functional alignment/consistency ratio, essential protein P@100/distribution overlap). Test subgraphs are sampled via BFS, DFS, and random walk at sizes 20–200 nodes to probe sensitivity to network scale and traversal topology.

## Method

PRING is a graph-level benchmark rather than a new prediction model. It curates a gold-standard multi-species PPI dataset by integrating high-confidence interactions from STRING (score > 0.7), UniProt/Swiss-Prot, Reactome, and IntAct, then applies MMSeqs2 sequence clustering (≤40% identity) and cross-species functional ID deduplication to minimize redundancy, followed by a leakage-free protein-disjoint train/test split. The evaluation suite comprises two topology-oriented tasks (intra-species and cross-species network construction) and three function-oriented tasks (protein complex pathway prediction from 235 Reactome pathways, GO enrichment via Louvain community detection and g:Profiler, and essential protein justification using CCDS/UniProt annotations). Eleven baselines spanning four model categories—sequence similarity (SPRINT), naive sequence (PIPR, D-SCRIPT, TopsyTurvy), PLM-based (PPITrans, TUnA, PLM-interact 35M/650M), and structure-based (Struct2Graph, TAGPPI, RF2-Lite)—are trained from scratch on the PRING training set and evaluated on all five tasks, revealing that even the best PLM-based models achieve graph similarity below 0.5 and fail to preserve functional modularity or essential/non-essential protein separation.

## Results

On the intra-species (Human) topology task, PLM-interact (650M) achieves the best Graph Similarity of 0.491 (RW sampling) and 0.396 (BFS), while all models remain below 0.5 GS, indicating poor topological fidelity. On function-oriented tasks, PLM-interact (650M) ranks first overall with P@100 = 0.77 and Distribution Overlap = 0.440 for essential protein identification, and FA = 0.368 for GO enrichment. Cross-species generalization degrades substantially: average GS drops by 15.2% (Arath), 25.3% (Yeast), and 35.2% (Ecoli) relative to Human. All 11 baselines exhibit over-prediction (RD > 1 in most settings), with the best RD of 0.514 (RF2-Lite, RW) still deviating significantly from the ideal value of 1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PLM-based models (PLM-interact 650M, PLM-interact 35M, PPITrans, TUnA) occupy the top four positions across both topology and function tasks, with PLM-interact (650M) achieving the best overall rank (1) and PLM-interact (35M) second (2). On the topology task (Table 2), PLM-interact (650M) leads with GS = 0.491 (RW), outperforming PPITrans (0.449) and TUnA (0.450) on RW, and SPRINT (0.227) by a wide margin on BFS. Naive sequence-based models (PIPR, D-SCRIPT, Topsy-Turvy) and structure-based models (Struct2Graph, TAGPPI) consistently rank in the bottom half, with PIPR achieving the lowest GS (0.0749 on DFS) and Struct2Graph the worst overall function rank (10). Notably, the non-deep-learning SPRINT method ranks third on topology (GS = 0.227 BFS), outperforming several deep learning baselines, suggesting that simple sequence similarity preserves basic network structure better than some learned representations. On function tasks, PLM-interact (35M) achieves the highest P@100 (0.80) for essential protein identification, while PLM-interact (650M) achieves the best FA (0.368) and CR (0.844) for GO enrichment, though all models score below 0.4 on FA, indicating weak functional modularity preservation across the board.

## Relation to Prior Work

PRING extends the PPI prediction evaluation paradigm from pairwise binary classification to graph-level network reconstruction, building on the leakage-free splitting protocol introduced by Bernett et al. (HIPPIE, 2024) and the multi-species evaluation framework of D-SCRIPT (2021). While prior benchmarks (GUO, PAN, D-SCRIPT, HIPPIE) assess models solely on per-pair accuracy, PRING repurposes the same prediction outputs into reconstructed networks and evaluates them with topology-aware metrics (graph similarity, relative density, degree distribution, clustering coefficient, spectral) and function-oriented tasks (pathway prediction, GO enrichment, essential protein justification). The paper does not propose a new prediction architecture but rather repositions the evaluation question: can models that score well on pairwise F1 actually recover the structural and functional organization of real interactomes?

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PRING reveals a significant gap between pairwise classification performance and network-level biological utility: the best model achieves graph similarity below 0.5, nearly all models fail to preserve functional modularity (FA < 0.4), and reconstructed networks cannot reliably separate essential from non-essential proteins. This challenges the field's reliance on standard classification metrics and suggests that current PPI models, despite impressive benchmark scores, are not yet ready for downstream applications like pathway reconstruction or drug target identification. Open questions include how to design architectures that simultaneously capture sparse topology and functional semantics, how to improve cross-species transfer to evolutionarily distant organisms, and whether the positive-to-negative training ratio should be adapted to reflect the inherent sparsity of real interactomes.
