## Problem

Kernel-based ODE solvers, particularly LS-SVMs, suffer from O(an³) computational complexity (a=1 for linear, a=27 for nonlinear ODEs) that becomes prohibitive for fine-grained temporal discretizations or long-time simulations. While the Nyström method has been widely used to accelerate kernel machines in supervised learning, no prior work has systematically integrated it into the LS-SVM ODE-solving framework to break this cubic bottleneck while preserving regularization benefits.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a p-th order ODE specification (coefficients f_k(t) or nonlinear function f, forcing term r(t)), p initial or boundary conditions, a temporal grid of n points, and three hyperparameters: RBF kernel bandwidth σ², regularization γ, and the number of Nyström landmark points m (m ≪ n, typically m=50). It outputs a closed-form primal model ŷ(t) = ωᵀφ(t) + b where ω ∈ R^m and φ(t) is an explicit m-dimensional feature vector, yielding solution values at all n time points. Unlike dual-form LS-SVMs that store n kernel coefficients, the primal representation requires only m weights, enabling O(1) prediction cost per new time point.

## Method

The core innovation is applying the Nyström approximation in the primal domain rather than the dual, constructing an explicit finite-dimensional feature map φ(t) ∈ R^m (m ≪ n) and its p-th order derivatives via Mercer's theorem and analytic kernel derivatives (e.g., ∇₁⁰K, ∇₂⁰K for RBF). This bypasses the kernel trick entirely: the ODE constraints are written directly in terms of ωᵀφ⁽ᵖ⁾(tᵢ), and the KKT system is solved in primal form. For linear ODEs, this yields a single (m+p)×(m+p) linear system (Eq. 10), reducing complexity from O((n+p)³) to O((m+p)³). For nonlinear ODEs, the primal system (Eq. 11) has dimension (m+n+p) and is solved by Newton's method, reducing per-iteration cost from O((3n+p-2)³) to O((m+n+p)³). A convergence theorem establishes quadratic Newton convergence governed by the product β(σ²,γ)·L(σ²,γ), linking kernel smoothness and regularization to solver stability.

## Results

Across 16 benchmark ODEs, NLS-SVMs achieves comparable solution accuracy to classical LS-SVMs with <0.13% relative difference in MAE, RMSE, and ‖y-ŷ‖∞, while delivering 10–3355× speedup depending on the number of time steps. Against PINNs, NLS-SVMs reduces RMSE by up to 72% (Problem 4: 72.0% RMSE reduction) and runs 49–6426× faster. The method scales to n=5×10⁴ time steps (100 s horizon) with m=50 landmark features in 17.5–460 s, where both LS-SVMs and PINNs fail due to memory constraints.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against classical LS-SVMs [Mehrkanoon 2012], NLS-SVMs matches accuracy within <0.13% relative error across all 16 problems while achieving 10–3355× speedup (e.g., 370× on Problem 4, 58× on Problem 16), confirming that the Nyström approximation preserves solution fidelity. Against PINNs [Raissi 2019], NLS-SVMs dominates on both accuracy and speed: it reduces RMSE by up to 72% (Problem 4) and runs 49–6426× faster, with the largest gap on Problem 16 where PINN fails entirely. Against RK4 [Dormand 1980], NLS-SVMs achieves 18.7× lower MAE on the stiff ODE (Problem 3: 2.06×10⁻⁸ vs 3.87×10⁻⁷) and 17.9× lower MAE on the nonlinear ODE (Problem 6: 8.89×10⁻⁴ vs 1.59×10⁻²). Against EAB [Hairer 1993], NLS-SVMs is critical on the singular ODE (Problem 11), where EAB produces an error ~5 million times larger (MAE 1.19×10⁻² vs 2.36×10⁻⁹), effectively rescuing a simulation that EAB cannot complete accurately.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Nyström method—classically applied in the dual formulation of kernel machines for low-rank matrix approximation (Williams & Seeger, 2000)—into the primal formulation of LS-SVM-based ODE solvers (Mehrkanoon et al., 2012). The key conceptual departure is that by constructing an explicit finite-dimensional feature map φ(t) ∈ R^m and its high-order derivatives via Nyström, the method bypasses the kernel trick entirely, which is essential because ODE operators require direct evaluation of p-th order derivatives of the solution. Prior Nyström acceleration work in supervised learning (Rudi et al., 2015; Drineas & Mahoney, 2016) targets O(m²n) complexity for classification/regression but has not been systematically translated to the ODE context where the O(an³) bottleneck arises from solving dense linear/nonlinear systems of size (n+p) or (3n+p-2).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method enables kernel-based ODE solvers to scale to n=10⁴ time steps (where classical LS-SVMs and PINNs fail on 32 GB RAM), opening the door to long-time simulations in multi-physics, biological network modeling, and real-time control. A key limitation acknowledged by the authors is that for nonlinear ODEs, the per-Newton-iteration complexity remains O(n³) (reduced only in the constant factor from 27n³ to (m+n+p)³), so the full bottleneck is not broken for the nonlinear case. The paper also notes the framework is readily extendable to PDEs by reformulating differential operators, but this remains unvalidated. Open questions include whether the O(n³) nonlinear barrier can be further reduced (e.g., via iterative solvers or further low-rank structure exploitation) and whether the accuracy guarantees hold for higher-dimensional or multi-dimensional ODE systems.
