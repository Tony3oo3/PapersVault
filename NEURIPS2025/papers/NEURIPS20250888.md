## Problem

While transformers have been shown to approximate stationary RL algorithms such as LinUCB and Thompson Sampling in in-context learning, their behavior in non-stationary environments—where reward distributions drift or shift abruptly—remained entirely unexplored. This paper asks whether a transformer trained via supervised in-context learning can achieve near-optimal dynamic regret when the environment changes over time, and provides the first theoretical guarantee showing it can match the minimax-optimal rate.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250888/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The transformer receives the full interaction history as an embedded matrix H ∈ R^{d×(2t−1)} of alternating state and (action, reward) token embeddings, with the current state appended at the final position. To implement multi-scale window scheduling, the input is structurally augmented by duplicating the trajectory n+1 times with auxiliary order-index entries, forming a non-continuous input of dimension D = ((d+5)(n+1)+5) that the transformer processes sequentially. The output is a probability distribution over A actions obtained by applying a learned linear projection and softmax to the last position of the transformer output.

## Method

The core insight is that a transformer can internally implement the MASTER algorithm's two key non-stationarity-handling operations: a stochastic window scheduler (WS) that restricts attention to recent data across multiple time scales, and a restart mechanism (RM) that resets auxiliary memory when stationarity tests detect environmental change. The window scheduler is approximated by a transformer block that uses self-attention to extract learned randomness for stochastic multi-scale instance selection, while the restart mechanism is implemented by a two-layer MLP that zeros out counts and rewards upon test failure. The transformer is trained via supervised pretraining on offline trajectories augmented with base-algorithm actions, and the resulting regret bound decomposes into a generalization term (from finite training data), an approximation term (from transformer capacity), and the intrinsic algorithmic regret of MASTER. Under sufficient data (N ≥ CT³ log T) and model capacity (ε ≤ T⁻³), the transformer achieves the minimax-optimal dynamic regret rate of Õ(min{√(JT), Δ^{1/3}T^{2/3} + √T}).

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 1, Corollary 2) shows that a transformer trained via supervised in-context learning achieves dynamic regret of Õ(min{√(JT), Δ^{1/3}T^{2/3} + √T}) in non-stationary bandits, matching the minimax optimal rate of Besbes et al. (2014). This improves upon the O(T) regret of LinUCB and the O(Δ^{1/4}T^{3/4}) regret of Thompson Sampling in non-stationary settings. In experiments on linear bandits (d=32, A=10), the transformer (GPT-2, L=16, M=16 heads) achieves cumulative regret comparable to or lower than MASTER+LinUCB and MASTER+TS in both low and moderate high non-stationarity environments.

**Table 1: Upper bounds on dynamic regret of transformers in the non-stationary setup.**

| Method | Dynamic Regret |
|---|---|
| Lin et al. (2024) – LinUCB | O(T) |
| Lin et al. (2024) – Thompson Sampling | O(Δ^{1/4} T^{3/4}) |
| **Ours (Transformer)** | **O(min{√(JT), Δ^{1/3} T^{2/3} + √T})** |

*T is the time budget; Δ and J represent the amount and number of changes in the environment, respectively. The bound for Ours matches the optimal rate derived in Besbes et al. (2014).*

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the transformer against four baselines: LinUCB, Thompson Sampling (TS), and their MASTER-wrapped expert variants (MASTER+LinUCB, MASTER+TS) from Wei and Luo (2021). In the low non-stationarity setting (1,000 rounds with two reward spikes), the transformer matches or slightly outperforms both MASTER+LinUCB and MASTER+TS in cumulative regret and suboptimality. In the high non-stationarity setting with b=0.018 (unseen during training), the transformer clearly outperforms the expert algorithms, demonstrating generalization to unseen non-stationary patterns. However, at the more extreme b=0.025, the transformer no longer outperforms MASTER+LinUCB or MASTER+TS, likely due to limited coverage of highly non-stationary scenarios in the training data. Theoretically, the transformer's regret bound O(min{√(JT), Δ^{1/3}T^{2/3}+√T}) strictly improves upon LinUCB's O(T) and TS's O(Δ^{1/4}T^{3/4}) in non-stationary environments.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two established lines of work: (1) Lin et al. (2024), who proved that transformers trained via supervised pretraining can approximate stationary RL algorithms (LinUCB, Thompson Sampling) in an in-context learning setup, and (2) Wei and Luo (2021), who introduced the MASTER algorithm achieving minimax-optimal dynamic regret in non-stationary environments without prior knowledge of change parameters. The key departure is extending the in-context RL theory from stationary to non-stationary settings by showing transformers can internally implement the window scheduler and restart mechanism that MASTER relies on. The proof technique—treating the transformer's hidden state as maintaining multiple hypothesis policies and using learned randomness in self-attention to sample among them—is novel relative to both parent lines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first theoretical guarantee that a transformer-based in-context learner can achieve minimax-optimal dynamic regret in non-stationary environments, closing a gap where prior in-context RL analyses assumed a fixed reward distribution. The identification of specific architectural requirements (depth, attention heads, ReLU activations) needed for the transformer to 'forget' outdated information and implement restart-style strategies provides actionable design guidance. However, the empirical validation is limited to linear bandits with only two base algorithms (LinUCB, TS), and the proof relies on a non-continuous transformer variant for simplicity. Open questions include whether the results extend to tabular MDPs, how the degree of non-stationarity in training data affects generalization, and whether a cleaner proof exists for standard (continuous) transformers.
