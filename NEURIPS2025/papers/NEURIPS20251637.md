## Problem

Existing benchmarks for class-imbalanced learning on tabular data are fragmented across narrow domains (finance, healthcare, education), evaluate only a handful of algorithms within a single paradigm, and lack a unified software package for reproducible comparison. This makes it difficult to draw general conclusions about which CIL strategies work under varying imbalance levels and data conditions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251637/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each dataset provides tabular features (numerical and categorical) with binary or multi-class labels, preprocessed via standardization for numerical columns and ordinal/one-hot encoding for categorical ones. The benchmark uses 5-fold stratified splits preserving the original imbalance ratio, with decision trees as the base classifier and ensemble size fixed at 100. Evaluation reports AUPRC, macro-F1, and balanced accuracy averaged across all folds, with per-dataset rankings computed over all 30 methods (29 CIL + base).

## Method

CLIMB is a benchmark built on an open-source Python package providing unified scikit-learn-style implementations of 29 CIL algorithms spanning six categories: undersampling, cleaning, oversampling, undersample ensembles, oversample ensembles, and cost-sensitive (ensemble) methods. The 73 datasets are curated from OpenML under strict criteria (natural imbalance with IR > 2, non-trivial difficulty, no missing values, i.i.d., documented), spanning imbalance ratios from 2.1 to 577.9 across diverse domains. The benchmarking protocol employs Optuna-based hyperparameter search (100 trials per method-dataset pair with 5-fold internal validation) to ensure fair, near-optimal configurations. Key empirical findings include: naive rebalancing often degrades performance, ensemble methods (especially undersample ensembles like SPE and Balance Cascade) consistently outperform single-model approaches, and data quality issues (10% label noise or 30% missing features) cause performance drops comparable to a 500% increase in imbalance ratio.

## Results

Across 73 real-world tabular datasets and 29 CIL algorithms, the benchmark reveals that naive rebalancing (undersampling, oversampling, cost-sensitive reweighting) often degrades AUPRC relative to a no-balancing baseline, especially under extreme imbalance (IR > 50), where Random Undersampling drops AUPRC from 42.6 to 18.9. In contrast, undersample ensembles consistently achieve the best performance: Self-paced Ensemble (SPE) reaches 59.3 AUPRC on low-imbalance (IR < 5) datasets and 57.5 on extreme-imbalance (IR > 50) datasets, compared to 51.0 and 42.6 for the base model. Among oversample ensembles, SMOTEBagging (58.9) and OverBagging (58.4) are competitive on low-imbalance data but degrade under extreme imbalance. Robustness experiments show that 10% label noise or 30% missing features cause a performance drop comparable to increasing the imbalance ratio by 500%, suggesting data quality is at least as critical as class imbalance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The benchmark compares 29 CIL algorithms across six method families. Undersample ensembles (SPE, Balance Cascade, BRF, EasyEnsemble, RUSBoost, UnderBagging) dominate: SPE achieves the best average AUPRC rank (2.7–4.5) across all four imbalance groups, outperforming all standalone resampling, cleaning, and cost-sensitive methods by 8–25 AUPRC points. Standalone undersampling methods (RUS, Cluster Centroids, IHT, NearMiss) consistently underperform the no-balancing baseline, with IHT dropping to 13.5 AUPRC on extreme-imbalance data versus 42.6 for the base. Cleaning methods (Tomek Links, ENN, RENN, AllKNN, OSS, NCR) provide modest, stable gains (+1 to +3 AUPRC) and are the safest non-ensemble option. Oversampling methods (SMOTE, Borderline-SMOTE, SVM-SMOTE, ADASYN) show negligible or negative effects, particularly under high imbalance where synthetic samples introduce noise. Oversample ensembles (OverBagging, SMOTEBagging) are competitive with undersample ensembles on low-to-medium imbalance but degrade sharply under extreme imbalance (e.g., SMOTEBagging drops to 56.0 vs. SPE's 57.5, while RUSBoost falls to 35.5). Cost-sensitive methods (CS, AdaCost, AdaUBoost, AsymBoost) yield only marginal improvements over the base, confirming that reweighting alone is insufficient. In pairwise win-ratio analysis, SPE wins against all other 28 methods on >59% of datasets by AUPRC, making it the most consistently superior CIL approach in the benchmark.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper does not build on a single prior method but rather unifies and extends the evaluation landscape for class-imbalanced learning (CIL) on tabular data. Prior empirical studies (Zhu et al. 2018, Xiao et al. 2021, Khushi et al. 2021, Kim and Hwang 2022, Wongvorachan et al. 2023) were restricted to specific domains, limited algorithm counts (4–21), and typically a single learning paradigm. CLIMB departs from this fragmented landscape by providing a unified, open-source benchmark spanning 73 real-world datasets, 29 algorithms across all major CIL paradigms (resampling, cleaning, cost-sensitive, and their ensemble variants), and a rigorous multi-fold protocol with hyperparameter search, while adopting the scikit-learn API design for compatibility and extensibility.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CLIMB fills a critical gap by offering the first comprehensive, reproducible, and extensible benchmark for CIL on tabular data, with a well-documented Python package that lowers the barrier for fair algorithm comparison. The finding that data quality issues (10% label noise, 30% missing features) degrade performance comparably to a 500% increase in imbalance ratio reframes practitioner priorities toward data cleaning alongside rebalancing. Open questions remain around combined effects of imbalance with other data quality challenges, the potential of deep learning architectures combined with CIL paradigms, and AutoML systems that dynamically compose CIL modules—directions the authors explicitly flag for future work.
