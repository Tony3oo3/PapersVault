## Problem

Both lateral inhibition (LI) and spike frequency adaptation (SFA) are known to sparsify neuronal responses in the fly olfactory circuit, yet it remains unclear whether these two mechanisms play truly redundant roles or occupy distinct functional niches during odor discrimination learning. The paper asks how these putatively redundant sparsification modules interact under varying noise levels that simulate complex natural environments, and whether their combined recruitment is necessary for optimal learning.

## Input / Output

Each odor sample is a 50-dimensional ORN firing-rate vector (prototype drawn uniformly from [0,1] plus zero-mean Gaussian or Ornstein-Uhlenbeck noise, clipped to non-negativity), delivered as continuous current drive to LIF neurons over a 30 ms stimulus window following a 10 ms baseline. The output is not a spike count but the time-averaged membrane potential of each MBON readout unit, converted to a class probability vector via softmax; this membrane-potential readout is chosen because it varies more smoothly than discrete spike trains and yields a more stable gradient signal for BPTT.

## Method

The model is a four-layer spiking network (50 ORNs → 50 PNs → 2,000 KCs → MBONs) built in snnTorch, with sparse PN→KC connectivity (each KC samples ~6 PNs at fixed weight 0.3) and fully connected KC→MBON readout. Two sparsification mechanisms are implemented: LI as an inhibitory current onto PNs driven by exponentially decaying LN spike traces (5 ms time constant), and SFA as a spike-triggered inhibitory adaptation current (50 ms time constant) in PNs, LNs, and KCs. Only KC→MBON weights are learned via BPTT with an arctan surrogate gradient for the Heaviside spike nonlinearity, optimized with Adam and L2 decay over 100 epochs. The key finding is a noise-regime-dependent division of labor: LI boosts discrimination at low and medium noise but its benefit reverses at high noise, while SFA consistently improves accuracy across all noise levels; when both mechanisms are active simultaneously, their benefits are additive, demonstrating that the apparent redundancy is in fact a complementary, noise-adaptive strategy.

## Results

In noise-free conditions (1,000 odor classes), the LI model achieves 98.30% discrimination accuracy, outperforming the SFA model (93.50%) and the Baseline (91.70%). Under high noise (N.I. = 0.30), the ranking reverses: SFA reaches 69.34% while LI drops to 53.82% (below the Baseline's 59.03%), demonstrating that LI's benefit diminishes and reverses as noise increases. The combined Full model (LI + SFA) achieves the highest accuracy in low- and medium-noise regimes, with benefits of the two mechanisms being approximately additive. Across 5,000 classes at N.I. = 0.30, SFA still leads (30.38%) while LI falls to 16.55% versus Baseline at 20.26%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three circuit configurations—Baseline (no sparsification), LI (lateral inhibition via local interneurons), and SFA (spike-frequency adaptation currents)—on a noisy odor discrimination task. In noise-free and low-noise settings (N.I. ≤ 0.15 for 1,000 classes), LI consistently outperforms both SFA and Baseline by 6.8–18.5 percentage points, while SFA provides a smaller but still significant gain of 1.8–3.7 points over Baseline. At high noise (N.I. ≥ 0.20), SFA becomes the dominant mechanism, surpassing Baseline by 9.7–11.6 points, whereas LI actually degrades performance below Baseline (e.g., 53.82% vs. 59.03% at N.I. = 0.30). The Full model combining both LI and SFA achieves the highest accuracy in low- and medium-noise conditions, confirming additive benefits, but under the highest noise only SFA alone remains optimal. These findings are robust to noise type (Gaussian vs. Ornstein-Uhlenbeck) and hyperparameter variations (seed, learning rate, batch size), with accuracy shifts under 3% across all tested perturbations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the computational modeling of the Drosophila olfactory circuit (ORN→PN→KC→MBON) established by Kee et al. (2015) and Assisi et al. (2020), and builds on the well-characterized roles of lateral inhibition (Wilson & Laurent 2005; Olsen & Wilson 2008) and spike-frequency adaptation (Farkhooi et al. 2013; Benda 2021) in shaping olfactory representations. While prior work has documented each mechanism's contribution to sparsification, contrast enhancement, and decorrelation in isolation, this paper is the first to systematically compare their *complementary* contributions to discrimination *learning* across a continuum of noise intensities. The odor dataset follows Wang et al. (2021), and the broader motivation connects to the FlyLoRA architecture (Zou et al. 2025), which draws on the same circuit's computational principles for parameter-efficient AI design.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that LI's benefit reverses at high noise while SFA remains consistently beneficial reframes biological redundancy as noise-regime-specific optimization rather than true redundancy, with direct implications for designing robust, biologically-inspired classifiers and neuromorphic hardware. Key limitations include the use of artificial (uniformly sampled) odors, biologically implausible BPTT training, and fixed input-layer weights, all of which constrain generalizability to naturalistic olfactory scenes. Open questions include whether the noise-dependent recruitment pattern holds with naturalistic odor statistics, biologically plausible plasticity rules, and whether the additive combination of LI and SFA generalizes to other sensory circuits or larger-scale SNN architectures.
