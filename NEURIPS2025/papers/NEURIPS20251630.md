## Problem

Existing RL methods for pursuit-evasion games require recomputation or computationally intensive fine-tuning when the underlying graph structure changes, and their training relies on specific opponent behavior patterns (e.g., evader following shortest paths to exits), limiting applicability to no-exit scenarios and making policies exploitable. The paper asks whether a general RL framework can train PEG policies with robust zero-shot performance across unseen graph structures without any fine-tuning.

## Input / Output

The method takes a graph's adjacency matrix and the current positions of m pursuers and 1 evader, converting them into a state feature of normalized shortest-path distances from every node to all m+1 agents (and exits, if present), computed via the Floyd algorithm. The output is a per-agent action distribution over valid neighbor nodes, generated sequentially by querying a shared policy network m times (one per pursuer) through a pointer network, which is equivalent to sampling from the joint policy under sequential decision-making decomposition. The same architecture serves both pursuer and evader sides by swapping the roles of the equilibrium guidance and adversary.

## Method

The EPG framework trains a generalized policy via cross-graph reinforcement learning (SAC-based) where each graph in a diverse training corpus is paired with its own equilibrium policy serving as an unexploitable adversary, and the equilibrium pursuer policy provides KL-divergence guidance to the RL loss to counter sparse-reward exploration difficulties. For no-exit graphs, a novel dynamic programming algorithm computes pure-strategy Nash equilibrium in near-optimal O~(|S|) time via a queue-based state expansion that solves the Bellman minimax equation, with a grouping mechanism extending it to many pursuers by decomposing into 2- or 3-pursuer sub-teams. For multi-exit graphs, a bipartite graph matching heuristic approximates equilibrium by allocating pursuers to exits based on shortest-path reachability. The policy is represented by a sequence model with a masked self-attention encoder (using the graph adjacency matrix as mask), an unmasked decoder, and a pointer network head, where the shortest-path distance feature replaces low-level node indicators to enable cross-graph transfer with a fixed number of attention layers.

## Results

In 2-pursuer no-exit PEGs, the EPG-trained RL pursuer achieves pursuit success rates of 0.60–1.00 against the optimal DP evader across 10 unseen real-world graphs (Table 1), while a shortest-path strategy (SPS) drops to 0.02–0.81. The RL evader maintains evasion timesteps within ~20% of the DP-DP baseline (e.g., 11.88 vs 12.29 on Grid Map). In the 6-pursuer setting (Table 5), the RL pursuer achieves 0.85–1.00 success rates. In multi-exit PEGs (5 pursuers, 8 exits), EPG's zero-shot performance reaches 0.637 (Grid Map) and 0.652 (Scotland-Yard Map), matching or exceeding the fine-tuned results of state-of-the-art methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Grasper [17] and MT-PSRO [18] in the 5-pursuer, 8-exit multi-exit setting, EPG's zero-shot performance (0.637 on Grid Map, 0.652 on Scotland-Yard Map) significantly outperforms both methods' zero-shot baselines (their starting points before fine-tuning) and matches or exceeds their results after 10 minutes of PSRO fine-tuning; only Grasper on Scotland-Yard Map reaches EPG's zero-shot level after fine-tuning. Against the shortest-path strategy (SPS) baseline in no-exit PEGs, EPG's RL pursuer is dramatically superior on complex real-world graphs (e.g., 0.62 vs 0.02 on Walk of Fame, 0.66 vs 0.04 on Sagrada Familia), while SPS only matches EPG on the simple Grid Map. Against the optimal DP oracle, EPG's RL policies approach but do not fully match DP performance (e.g., 0.60–0.99 vs 1.00 pursuit success rate), which is expected since DP is the exact equilibrium; however, in graphs where pursuit is not globally guaranteed (e.g., Scale-Free Graph), the mixed-strategy RL pursuer actually outperforms the pure-strategy DP pursuer (0.44 vs 0.10 success rate). Against best-responding (BR) policies trained specifically on test graphs, EPG's RL policies show no significant vulnerability, with BR achieving no advantage over the DP opponent.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) framework for the RL training loop, extends the state-expansion idea from sequential PEGs (Vieira et al., 2008) to simultaneous-move Markov PEGs via a novel DP algorithm, and borrows attention-based encoder-decoder architectures from robot exploration work (Cao et al., 2023). Its key conceptual departure from prior PEG methods like Grasper and MT-PSRO is the use of per-graph equilibrium policies as both the adversarial opponent and the policy guidance signal during cross-graph RL training, rather than relying on fixed behavioral assumptions (e.g., evader always follows shortest path to exits). The shortest-path-distance feature for cross-graph state representation and the grouping mechanism for multi-pursuer scalability are also novel contributions not present in prior graph-based PEG work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The EPG framework eliminates the need for per-graph fine-tuning when the underlying graph structure changes, which is critical for real-time deployment in dynamic urban environments where traffic patterns, construction, or emergencies alter the graph. The near-optimal DP algorithm for simultaneous Markov PEGs provides a theoretically grounded equilibrium oracle that was previously unavailable (prior methods were either exponential or O(|S|²)). A key open question the authors identify is whether perfect-information equilibrium oracles remain beneficial under partially observable or imperfect-information settings, which would be necessary for many real-world deployments. The framework's applicability to both pursuer and evader sides in both no-exit and multi-exit scenarios makes it a general-purpose tool, though its current scope is limited to deterministic, finite-graph PEGs.
