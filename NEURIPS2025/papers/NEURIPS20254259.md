## Problem

Existing vision-language models cannot perceive tactile attributes such as hardness, elasticity, and friction that are invisible to cameras, while prior tactile learning approaches either handle only static attributes or lack the ability to ground haptic perceptions in natural language reasoning. The temporal dynamics of tactile interactions—how surfaces respond to pressing, sliding, and rotation—remain largely unexplored in the context of cross-modal language understanding.

## Input / Output

The model consumes visuo-tactile video sequences (320×320 pixels at 20 FPS) captured by GelSight Mini, DIGIT, or Tac3D sensors, where each video encodes three sequential interactions (pressing, rotation, sliding) over an object surface, alongside a free-form text prompt. The video is tokenized via a ViT-base encoder with temporal embeddings and inserted into the LLM's input sequence using special <video_start>/<video>/<video_end> anchor tokens. The model produces natural-language responses covering four tactile attributes (hardness, protrusion, elasticity, friction) at three ordinal levels each, as well as higher-order reasoning outputs for comparative analysis, surface optimality identification, and scenario-based decision making.

## Method

VTV-LLM couples a VideoMAE-adapted ViT-base encoder with a Qwen 2.5 LLM through a two-layer visual projector, trained via a three-stage paradigm. Stage 1 (VTV enhancement) replaces VideoMAE's tube masking with an optical-flow-guided strategy: a Gaussian mixture model generates a continuous-valued keyframe mask, RAFT computes bidirectional optical flow across the sequence, and backward warping propagates the mask temporally to prevent the information leakage that standard tube masking suffers under the large deformations typical of tactile contact. A joint reconstruction (MSE) and attribute classification (cross-entropy) loss trains the encoder. Stage 2 (VTV-text alignment) trains only the V-Projector and T-Projector with the LLM frozen to establish cross-modal correspondence. Stage 3 (text prompt finetuning) jointly fine-tunes the projectors and LLM with parameter-efficient techniques on 10,000 newly generated question-answer pairs, enabling coherent tactile reasoning in natural language.

## Results

On the VTV150K test set (600 question-answer pairs over novel objects), VTV-LLM-7B achieves an average accuracy of 60.4% across nine tactile reasoning tasks, substantially outperforming all seven baselines. The largest margins appear in combined-attribute assessment (35.6% vs. 4.3% for the best baseline, Gemini-2.5-Pro-Exp) and individual attribute tasks such as hardness (73.9% vs. 36.2%) and protrusion (75.0% vs. 34.7%). Among high-level reasoning tasks, VTV-LLM-7B scores 71.3% on surface feature distinction (SFD) and 64.0% on tactile scenario analysis (TSA), compared to 42.6% and 40.0% respectively for the strongest proprietary model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VTV-LLM-7B wins decisively against all seven baselines on every reported task, with an average accuracy of 60.4% versus 29.5% for the strongest proprietary model (Gemini-2.5-Pro-Exp) and 25.0% for the best open-source video VLM (LLaVA-Video-Qwen2-7B). The gap is most dramatic on the combined-attribute task, where VTV-LLM-7B scores 35.6% while all baselines remain below 5%, suggesting that jointly reasoning over multiple tactile attributes is nearly intractable without dedicated visuo-tactile training. Among proprietary models, GPT-4o (28.0%) and Gemini-2.5-Pro-Exp (29.5%) are closely matched, both trailing VTV-LLM-7B by roughly 31 percentage points on average. Among open-source baselines, VideoLLaMA3-7B (12.6%) and InternVL2.5-VL-8B (17.1%) perform worst, indicating that general-purpose video LLMs struggle substantially with tactile-specific reasoning. The ablation in Table 2 further confirms that the optical-flow-guided masking and attribute classifier in the VTV encoder contribute an additional ~18 points over a standard VideoMAE backbone trained on the same data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: visuo-tactile sensing (GelSight, DIGIT, Tac3D), self-supervised video representation learning (VideoMAE, Sparsh, AnyTouch), and multi-modal large language models (LLaVA, Qwen-VL). It departs from prior tactile representation learning, which focused on static attribute classification or cross-sensor transfer without language grounding, and from prior MLLMs that process natural images/videos but cannot interpret the distinct deformation and pressure patterns in visuo-tactile data. The key methodological departure is the optical flow-guided Gaussian masking strategy, which adapts VideoMAE's tube masking to handle the significant inter-frame motion inherent in tactile interactions where standard tube masking would leak information through temporally adjacent frames.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work establishes the first language-grounded framework for reasoning about visuo-tactile video, opening a path toward embodied agents that can articulate tactile perceptions in natural language for human-machine interaction. The VTV150K dataset, spanning three sensor types and four attributes, provides a structured benchmark that the tactile sensing community has lacked. However, the evaluation is confined to a self-constructed 600-sample test set with only four discrete attributes and 100 objects, leaving open questions about generalization to continuous attribute scales, additional sensor types, and real-time deployment. The manual data collection protocol also limits scalability, and the fixed three-level annotation scheme may not capture the nuance of real-world tactile perception.
