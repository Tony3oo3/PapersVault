## Problem

Data poisoning attacks are increasingly designed to be harmless and purposeful (e.g., dataset ownership verification, protecting artists' IP from generative AI), yet poisoning generators lack a provable, unified mechanism to declare the presence of poisoning in their datasets. Existing detection methods are attack-specific, heuristic, and offer no verifiable claim of poisoning, creating disputes when poisoned data is inadvertently misused. This paper asks: can watermarking provide provable guarantees that a dataset has been poisoned while preserving the poisoning's utility?

## Input / Output

The method operates on RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 64×64 for Tiny-ImageNet) and takes as input a clean data sample x ∈ [0,1]^d, a poisoning perturbation δ^p bounded by ε_p in ℓ∞ norm, a watermark perturbation δ^w bounded by ε_w over q selected dimensions, and a secret d-dimensional detection key ζ. The output is a watermarked poisoned sample x + δ^p + δ^w, and detection is performed by computing the inner product ζ^T x and comparing it against a threshold to classify the sample as watermarked or clean. In poisoning-concurrent mode, the d dimensions are partitioned into a watermarking set W (|W| = q) and a poisoning set P = [d] \ W, so the two perturbations act on disjoint coordinates.

## Method

The paper proposes two watermarking schemes: post-poisoning watermarking (a third party embeds a watermark after the poison is applied, with total perturbation ε_w + ε_p) and poisoning-concurrent watermarking (the generator simultaneously crafts both poison and watermark on disjoint dimensions, with total perturbation max{ε_w, ε_p}). Detection uses a simple inner-product test ζ^T x against a threshold, where ζ is a random ±1 key. The core theoretical contribution proves that post-poisoning watermarking achieves detectability with q = Θ(√d/ε_w) dimensions, while poisoning-concurrent watermarking requires only q = Θ(1/ε_w²), using McDiarmid's inequality for concentration bounds. For poisoning utility, the paper shows via Xavier-normalized NTK-style analysis that post-poisoning watermarking preserves the poisoning objective with no additional length constraint, whereas poisoning-concurrent watermarking requires q = O(√d/ε_p) to prevent the watermark from overwhelming the restricted poisoning dimensions. The universal (single-key) version extends finite-sample guarantees to the full distribution when N = Ω(d), and experiments on Narcissus, AdvSc, UE, and AP attacks across six architectures confirm the theoretical thresholds.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with ResNet-18, both watermarking schemes achieve perfect detection (AUROC = 1.0) at q = 3000 for all four attacks. Poisoning-concurrent watermarking reaches AUROC = 1.0 at substantially shorter lengths (e.g., q = 1500–2000) compared to post-poisoning (q = 2000–3000), confirming the theoretical advantage of Θ(1/ε_w²) over Θ(√d/ε_w). For post-poisoning watermarking, poisoning utility is well preserved: Narcissus ASR remains above 90% and UE accuracy stays below 12% even at q = 3000. In contrast, poisoning-concurrent watermarking degrades poisoning efficacy at large q: Narcissus ASR drops from 95% (baseline) to 9.75% at q = 3000, and UE accuracy recovers to 91.79%, consistent with the O(√d/ε_p) upper bound on q.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates its two watermarking schemes on four existing poisoning attacks: Narcissus [91] and AdvSc [88] (clean-label backdoor) and UE [37] and AP [22] (availability). Poisoning-concurrent watermarking consistently achieves higher AUROC than post-poisoning at the same q — e.g., at q = 100, Narcissus post-poisoning yields AUROC 0.552 while poisoning-concurrent reaches 0.929, and UE post-poisoning gives 0.584 vs. 0.820 for poisoning-concurrent — validating the theoretical prediction that Ω(1/ε_w²) < Ω(√d/ε_w). However, post-poisoning watermarking preserves poisoning utility far better at large q: at q = 3000, Narcissus ASR under post-poisoning remains 90.02% versus only 9.75% under poisoning-concurrent, and UE accuracy stays at 9.99% (post-poisoning) versus 91.79% (poisoning-concurrent), confirming the O(√d/ε_p) upper bound on q for poisoning-concurrent. For AdvSc, post-poisoning ASR degrades more gradually (95.53% → 74.82% at q = 3000) compared to poisoning-concurrent (95.53% → 9.97%), likely because AdvSc relies on a spatial shortcut in the top-left quarter, implicitly reducing effective poison dimensions. Overall, post-poisoning is preferable when a third party handles watermarking (larger q is safe), while poisoning-concurrent is more practical for self-watermarking but requires keeping q moderate to avoid destroying the poison.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two well-established lines of work: data poisoning attacks (backdoor and availability) and watermarking for copyright/content provenance. While prior work has used backdoor patterns as implicit dataset watermarks for ownership verification (Li et al., 2022, 2023), and watermarking has been extensively studied for LLMs and generative images, no prior work provides a unified, provable framework for embedding a *separate* watermark into a poisoned dataset to certify the existence of poisoning while preserving attack utility. The paper repurposes the inner-product detection mechanism from dataset watermarking and combines it with NTK-style generalization bounds to derive explicit watermarking-length thresholds for two distinct deployment scenarios (post-poisoning vs. poisoning-concurrent).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As 'harmless' data poisoning (unlearnable examples, NightShade, Glaze) becomes a mainstream tool for IP protection, the absence of a verifiable, dispute-resistant mechanism for poisoners to declare their actions creates real-world liability and trust gaps. This paper provides the first provable conditions under which a watermark is simultaneously detectable and non-destructive to the poisoning, giving practitioners concrete design guidelines (e.g., use poisoning-concurrent with moderate q for self-embedding, or post-poisoning with larger q for third-party certification). Key open questions remain: only sufficient (not necessary) length conditions are established, robustness to practical defenses (augmentation, diffusion purification, key leakage) is discussed but not fully characterized, and the theory assumes i.i.d. sampling and Xavier-normalized networks, which may not hold for all real-world pipelines.
