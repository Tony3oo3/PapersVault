## Problem

Disaggregated evaluation of model performance across subgroups is the dominant practice for assessing algorithmic fairness, but the paper shows that equal performance across subgroups is an unreliable fairness criterion when data reflect real-world disparities, since even Bayes-optimal predictors do not generally attain equal performance across subgroups. Furthermore, when data are misrepresentative of the target population due to selection bias, both disaggregated evaluation and standard conditional independence tests may be invalid without explicit assumptions about the bias mechanism. The paper addresses the gap of providing a principled causal framework for interpreting when observed performance differences reflect genuine model failure versus expected consequences of distributional heterogeneity.

## Input / Output

The framework operates on tabular data with covariates X, a binary label Y, and a categorical subgroup indicator A, and consumes model scores R from either subgroup-agnostic (Z=X) or subgroup-aware (Z={X,A}) predictors. The key output is the statistic T_a, defined as the difference between unweighted subgroup performance and a weighted population estimate M_a computed with weights proportional to P(A=a|V) for a chosen control variable V∈{X,Y,R}. T_a is interpreted as a conditional independence test for {R,Y}⊥A|V, so its value (zero or non-zero) is mapped to specific causal and fairness implications depending on the model type and control variable, as formalized in Supplementary Table B3.

## Method

The paper constructs a taxonomy of prototypical data generating processes using causal DAGs (covariate shift, outcome shift, label shift, presentation shift, complex shift, and selection-augmented variants) to characterize when performance metrics are stable across subgroups for Bayes-optimal and arbitrary models. The central theoretical result is that {R,Y}⊥A holds in none of the settings, but {R,Y}⊥A|V holds for specific V under specific graphs (e.g., V=X under covariate shift, V=Y under label shift), meaning performance differences can be attributed to identifiable distributional confounders. The practical contribution is a controlled evaluation procedure that computes T_a via importance weighting with weights w∝P(A=a|V), estimated via cross-fitting to avoid overfitting, which operationalizes the conditional independence test and allows practitioners to falsify causal assumptions about the data generating process. The approach is validated through simulations matching each DAG setting and experiments on ACS PUMS data (income and public coverage prediction stratified by race/ethnicity), confirming that subgroup-aware prediction improves performance when Y⊥̸A|X and that controlled evaluation correctly identifies the source of performance differences.

## Results

The paper's central theoretical result (Table 2) shows that {R,Y} ⊥ A (unconditional equal performance) never holds across any of the six causal settings, meaning equal performance across subgroups should not be expected even for Bayes-optimal models. Control for X explains performance differences only under covariate shift (subgroup-agnostic), control for Y only under label shift (subgroup-agnostic), and control for R (sufficiency) holds for subgroup Bayes-optimal models in all settings. Empirically, on ACS PUMS, the 'ACSPublicCoverage' task shows stronger evidence against Y⊥A|X than 'ACSIncome': subgroup-aware prediction outperforms subgroup-agnostic for nearly all race/ethnicity subgroups on 'ACSPublicCoverage' while improvements are more minor on 'ACSIncome', and sufficiency violation is observed for nearly all subgroups on 'ACSPublicCoverage' versus only the 'Other' subgroup on 'ACSIncome'.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary empirical comparison is against the weighted evaluation approach of Cai et al. [16], which maps data to a shared overlap region between subgroup distributions to avoid extreme weights. The authors apply both their proposed weighting scheme (w ∝ P(A=a|V)) and the Cai et al. approach to the same simulation and ACS PUMS experiments, finding that the results are 'largely qualitatively consistent' with each other, confirming that the core conditional-independence-test interpretation is robust to the specific weighting formulation. The paper also compares subgroup-agnostic (Z=X) versus subgroup-aware (Z={X,A}) prediction, showing that subgroup-aware models improve log-loss and AUC-ROC in all settings where Y≰A|X (i.e., all except covariate shift), with the exception of the separable-subgroup case where the two approaches behave identically. On ACS PUMS, stratified prediction (separate models per subgroup) mitigates sufficiency violation for the underrepresented 'Other' subgroup on 'ACSIncome' in a way that a single model with A as a covariate does not, suggesting that relative underrepresentation can cause suboptimality even for subgroup-aware models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies three previously somewhat separate threads: causal graphical models for distribution shift (Mhasawade et al. [29], Veitch et al. [30], Schrouff et al. [31]), weighted evaluation under distribution shift (Cai et al. [16]), and the documented incompatibilities among fairness criteria (Liu et al. [37], Kleinberg et al. [38], Chouldechova [39]). It departs from prior work by providing a systematic causal DAG framework that explains *why* performance differences across subgroups arise (as a consequence of confounding from distributional heterogeneity) rather than merely documenting that they do, and by recasting weighted evaluation procedures as conditional independence tests with explicit causal interpretations. The selection bias analysis extends Bareinboim and Pearl's [25] framework to the fairness evaluation context.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges the widespread practice of treating performance differences across subgroups as direct evidence of unfairness, showing that even Bayes-optimal models exhibit such differences when data reflect real-world disparities. The controlled evaluation framework (interpreting weighted comparisons as conditional independence tests) gives practitioners a concrete tool to disambiguate distributional confounding from genuine model failure, but its practical utility is limited by the need for an oracle weight model P(A|V) and the restriction to binary outcomes. Open questions include the impact of estimation error in the weight model, extension to continuous or multi-class settings, and the unresolved normative questions about whether predicting disparate outcomes across populations is itself justified.
