## Problem

The widely-cited claim that ImageNet-trained CNNs are inherently texture-biased rests on the cue-conflict experiment of Geirhos et al., which frames feature reliance as a binary shape-versus-texture choice, entntangles multiple cues in the synthesized stimuli, and introduces spatial and interface biases that systematically favor texture for CNNs and shape for humans. This paper asks whether the observed texture bias is a genuine architectural inductive bias or an artifact of the E protocol's design limitations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252033/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes standard 224×224 RGB images and applies targeted suppression transformations with fixed hyperparameters (e.g., patch-shuffle grid size 3 or 6 for global/local shape, bilateral filter with d=12/σc=170/σs=75 for texture, grayscale for color). It produces a per-model, per-feature relative-accuracy score defined as accuracy under suppression divided by baseline accuracy on the original image, yielding a four-dimensional performance-degradation profile (global shape, local shape, texture, color) that is directly comparable across architectures, humans, and domains after normalizing chance-level to 0 and baseline to 1.

## Method

The core contribution is a domain-agnostic suppression-based E framework that quantifies feature reliance by measuring accuracy degradation under targeted removal of individual visual cues, rather than inferring reliance from forced-choice preferences on cue-conflict hybrids. Six transformations (two per feature type) are validated against quantitative metrics—local variance, high-frequency Egi, edgedge-SSIM, and gradient correlation—across 800 ImageNet images to confirm each suppresses its target while preserving the others. The framework is applied to compare 20 human participants against 12 architectures (ResNet50 standard/sota, ConvNeXt/V2, EfficientcientNet/V2, MobileNetV3, ConvMixer, ViT, DeiT, Swin, CLIP ViT) on a curated 16-class ImageNet subset, then extendeded to medical imaging (MedMNIST-v2) and remote sensing (UCMerced, RSD46, DeepGlobe, PatternNet, AID) with continuous suppression-strength curves. The key empiricalirical finding is that CNNs are not texture-biased but predominantly rely on local shape—a sensitivity substantially mitigated by modern training recipes and most closely aligned with humans in CLIP ViT—while domain semantics systematically shift reliance: CV models prioritize shape, MI models emphasizephasize color, and RS models show strong texture dependence.

## Results

On the curated ImageNet16 dataset, ResNet50-standard retains 79.5% accuracy under texture suppression but drops to only 27.6% under local shape suppression (Patch Shuffle, grid=6), revealing a pronounced local-shape reliance rather than a texture bias. Humans show a similar profile (97.9% texture, 76.3% local shape) but with substantially higher robustness to local shape disruption. Modern training strategies substantially mitigate the local-shape vulnerability: ResNet50-sota recovers to 61.8%, ConvNeXtV2 to 64.7%, and CLIP ViT reaches 75.8%—nearly matching the human value of 76.3%. Across domains, CV models prioritize shape, medical imaging models emphasize color, and remote sensing models exhibit the strongest texture reliance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is not a single method beating baselines but rather characterizing how different architectures and training regimes differ in feature reliance. ResNet50-standard (trained from scratch with basic augmentations) shows the most extreme local-shape vulnerability at 27.6% retained accuracy, while the same architecture trained with a modern recipe (ResNet50-sota) recovers to 61.8%, demonstrating that training strategy—not architecture—drives the reliance pattern. ConvNeXtV2 further improves to 64.7%, and CLIP ViT (75.8%) most closely matches human performance (76.3%), suggesting vision-language supervision yields the most human-aligned representations. ViT (63.6%) performs comparably to ResNet50-sota, challenging the narrative that transformers are inherently more shape-oriented than CNNs. MobileNetV3 (21.7%) and EfficientNet (24.0%) retain the strongest local-shape dependence among all tested models, indicating that capacity limitations or inductive biases in compact architectures persist even with standard training. Across all architectures, texture suppression causes minimal degradation (76–95% retained), directly contradicting the texture-bias hypothesis.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly re-examines the texture bias hypothesis formalized by Geirhos et al. (2019) through their cue-conflict protocol, identifying specific methodological flaws (lack of feature isolation, spatially unbalanced texture signals, and shape-biased human response interfaces) that may have produced the observed CNN–human divergence. Rather than building on the cue-conflict paradigm, it departs from it entirely, proposing a suppression-based evaluation framework that measures reliance through targeted feature removal rather than forced-choice preference. The work also connects to the line of research showing that training objectives and augmentations (Hermann et al., 2020) matter more than architecture for feature reliance, and to recent findings on vision-language models (CLIP) and transformers exhibiting more human-aligned representations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By reframing the texture bias narrative as a local shape reliance that is mitigable through modern training, the paper challenges a widely cited conclusion that has shaped robustness and interpretability research for over five years. The domain-agnostic suppression framework offers a practical, generalizable tool for characterizing feature reliance across CV, medical imaging, and remote sensing, revealing that reliance patterns are domain-dependent rather than architecture-fixed. Open questions remain about the relative contributions of architectural inductive biases versus training regimes, whether suppression artifacts (e.g., block structures from Patch Shuffle) confound the measurements, and whether the small human study (20 participants, 16 categories) generalizes to broader perceptual settings.
