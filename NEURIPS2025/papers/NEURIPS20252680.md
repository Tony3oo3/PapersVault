## Problem

Adversarial attacks on LLMs are evaluated purely empirically—plotting success rate against query count—without a principled framework to quantify how much information each response leaks or how close an attack is to its fundamental limit. This leaves defenders without guidance on the transparency-security trade-off (e.g., whether to expose thinking processes or logits) and attackers without a yardstick for optimality.

## Input / Output

The method consumes the observable signal Z from a single model query, which can be answer tokens alone, tokens plus token-level logits, tokens plus chain-of-thought thinking-process tokens, or all three combined, along with a target property T that ranges over a binary success flag, a K-ary hidden string, or a continuous value. It outputs the per-query mutual information I(Z;T) in bits, estimated as the maximum of three variational lower bounds (MINE, NWJ, InfoNCE) using a RoBERTa-based critic network, from which the minimum query budget N_min(ε) = log(1/ε)/I(Z;T) is derived. The binary attack-success label is generated by applying a fixed predicate T = g(Y) to the raw model reply.

## Method

The paper casts the attacker–model dialogue as an information channel and proves that the minimum number of adaptive queries to achieve error ε scales as Θ(log(1/ε)/I(Z;T)), where I(Z;T) is the per-query mutual information between the observable signal and the target property. The lower bound follows from Fano's inequality (extended to K-ary and continuous targets) applied to sequential adaptive queries, while a matching upper bound is achieved via a sequential probability ratio test (SPRT) grounded in Wald's identity, making the bound asymptotically tight. Empirically, seven LLMs are evaluated across three attack scenarios (system-prompt leakage, jailbreak, relearning) using three attack algorithms (paraphrase rewriting, GCG, PAIR) under four disclosure regimes, with I(Z;T) estimated via three variational lower bounds implemented in a RoBERTa critic. Log-log regression of measured query counts against estimated mutual information yields slopes indistinguishable from −1 for adaptive attacks, confirming the predicted inverse law N ∝ 1/I, while non-adaptive attacks and decoding-hyperparameter sweeps (temperature, nucleus threshold) further validate the framework's predictions.

## Results

Across all seven LLMs (GPT-4, DeepSeek-R1, three OLMo-2 variants, two Llama-4 checkpoints), adaptive attacks (GCG, PAIR) yield log-log regression slopes indistinguishable from the theoretical value of −1 (slopes range from −0.97 to −1.03, all with p > 0.05), confirming the predicted inverse law N ∝ 1/I(Z;T). In contrast, non-adaptive paraphrase attacks deviate strongly (slopes from −0.32 to +0.26, all p < 10⁻³), showing they fail to exploit per-query leakage. Practically, exposing answer tokens alone requires ~1,000 queries for a successful jailbreak; adding logits reduces this to ~100; and revealing the full thinking process trims it to a few dozen, representing a one-to-two-order-of-magnitude drop per additional signal.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against competing attack methods in the traditional sense; instead, it validates its information-theoretic bound N_min = log(1/ε)/I(Z;T) against empirical query counts under two attack paradigms and four disclosure regimes. Adaptive attacks (GCG and PAIR) align almost perfectly with the theoretical slope of −1 across all seven models and three tasks, with no data point falling below the information-theoretic lower bound in Figure 1. Non-adaptive paraphrase rewriting, by contrast, requires far more queries and shows slopes that are statistically indistinguishable from zero (or even positive), confirming that fixed query policies cannot exploit input-level variation in leakage. Among disclosure regimes, each additional signal (logits, thinking-process tokens) shifts the operating point down-right on the log-log plot, with the combined Tok+TP+logit regime reducing the query budget by roughly one order of magnitude compared to tokens alone. The temperature and nucleus-sampling ablation (Figure 2) further confirms that the −1 slope is preserved across decoding hyperparameters, with lower entropy shifting the cloud upward in parallel rather than changing the slope.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends classical Shannon-type lower bounds on estimation risk (Xu & Raginsky 2016; Esposito et al. 2023) from a single-observation, static setting to the sequential, adaptive query regime of LLM adversarial attacks. The achievability side draws directly on Wald's sequential probability ratio test, while the problem framing borrows from twenty-question-game and active-learning query-complexity results, but departs from those by allowing the per-query observation Z to be a high-entropy, multi-component signal (answer tokens, logits, chain-of-thought traces) rather than a binary or low-dimensional parameter. The mutual information estimation pipeline (MINE, NWJ, InfoNCE with a RoBERTa critic) is standard, and the attack algorithms (GCG, PAIR, paraphrase) are reused from prior adversarial-attack literature; the novelty lies in unifying all of these under a single bits-per-query metric and proving tight lower/upper bounds.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first principled, model-agnostic yardstick for the transparency–security trade-off in LLM deployment: defenders can convert disclosure knobs (which signals to expose) and rate limits into a measurable safety margin in bits per query, and attackers gain a ceiling against which to benchmark algorithmic progress. The clean empirical validation across seven models and three attack families (slopes indistinguishable from −1) is reassuring, but the mutual information is estimated via variational lower bounds that may underestimate true leakage, and the bounds assume conditionally i.i.d. observations given T, which may not hold under strongly adaptive, multi-turn interactions. Open questions include whether the framework extends to multi-agent or federated settings, whether the SPRT achievability is practically realizable under API rate limits and latency, and how the bounds behave when the target property T is itself non-stationary (e.g., a model being fine-tuned in real time).
