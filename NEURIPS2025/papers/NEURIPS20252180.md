## Problem

Multimodal in-context learning (ICL) remains largely unexplored in the medical domain, where clinicians routinely adapt to specialized tasks from a few relevant prior cases. While MLLMs have shown progress on medical VQA, no benchmark has systematically evaluated their ability to learn multimodal medical tasks from expert-designed in-context demonstrations, and prior few-shot medical evaluations rely on randomly selected examples rather than purpose-built task demonstrations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252180/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each problem presents a multimodal query (a medical image from one of 13 imaging modalities plus a free-text question) alongside 2–19 expert-curated in-context examples, each an image-question-answer triplet serving as a task demonstration. The model produces either a free-text answer (open-ended) or selects from a closed set of answer choices derived from the in-context example answers (closed-ended MCQA). The benchmark spans 6 medical specialties and includes both common and uncommon clinical presentations, with answer formats ranging from binary yes/no to free-text diagnoses to numerical values.

## Method

SMMILE is an expert-driven benchmark in which 11 medical specialists (9 physicians, 2 medical students) curated 111 problems (517 question-image-answer triplets) through a guided web-based pipeline, with each in-context example designed as a relevant task demonstration rather than a randomly retrieved sample. An augmented variant, SMMILE++ (1038 problems), is constructed by permuting in-context example order to isolate positional effects. The evaluation framework benchmarks 15 MLLMs (open-source 0.5B–90B and closed-source GPT-4o, Claude 3.7 Sonnet) using four complementary metrics—exact match, LLM-as-a-Judge (Llama3.3-70B), human expert ratings, and MCQA accuracy—with bootstrap resampling (N=1000) for confidence intervals. Fine-grained analyses include injecting one noisy example (random or specialty-matched) to quantify sensitivity to example quality, and controlling placement of answer-matching examples to reveal recency bias across all evaluated models.

## Results

On SMMILE (111 problems), GPT-4o achieves the best open-ended ICL score of 49.88% (LLM-as-a-Judge) and 58.85% closed-ended MCQA accuracy, while Qwen2.5-VL-72B leads on the augmented SMMILE++ (1038 problems) with 53.80% ICL LLM-as-a-Judge and 63.22% MCQA. The average ICL improvement over zero-shot is only 8.0% on SMMILE and 9.4% on SMMILE++, and 7 out of 15 evaluated models perform worse than a Random baseline (27.86%). A single irrelevant in-context example degrades performance by up to 9.5%, while placing the most relevant example last yields up to 71% absolute improvement due to recency bias.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4o is the overall leader on SMMILE, achieving 49.88% ICL LLM-as-a-Judge (vs. 32.56% zero-shot) and 58.85% MCQA, outperforming all other models including Claude 3.7 Sonnet (36.17% ICL, 42.01% MCQA) and Llama-3.2-Vision-90B (40.66% ICL, 30.30% MCQA). On the larger SMMILE++ benchmark, Qwen2.5-VL-72B takes the lead with 53.80% ICL LLM-as-a-Judge and 63.22% MCQA, surpassing GPT-4o (46.45% ICL, 55.76% MCQA). Domain-specific medical models fail to consistently outperform size-matched general-purpose models: LLaVA-Med-7B suffers severe ICL degradation (21.65% → 10.19%), while MedGemma-4B (36.86% ICL) roughly matches Qwen2.5-VL-3B (33.58% ICL). Seven of fifteen models (including all LLaVA variants and LLaVA-Med-7B) score below the Random baseline of 27.86% in the open-ended ICL setting, indicating that in-context examples actively harm rather than help these models. The text-only Llama 3.3 70B baseline achieves 38.62% MCQA ICL accuracy, suggesting that multiple-choice tasks rely less on visual grounding than open-ended generation, where the same model scores only 16.53%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the multimodal in-context learning (ICL) paradigm—pioneered by Flamingo and evaluated in general-domain benchmarks like VL-ICL Bench—to the medical domain, where no prior benchmark has systematically assessed ICL capabilities. It departs from earlier medical few-shot evaluations (Med-Flamingo, VQA-RAD, PathVQA) that relied on randomly retrieved examples by instead using expert-designed task demonstrations, aligning more closely with how clinicians actually reason from a small set of relevant prior cases. The benchmark also builds on the LLM-as-a-Judge evaluation paradigm and bootstrap-based uncertainty quantification, applying them to a multimodal medical setting for the first time.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SMMILE is the first benchmark to expose how poorly current MLLMs perform at multimodal ICL in high-stakes medical settings, with even the best models (GPT-4o, Qwen2.5-VL-72B) answering only ~50% of questions correctly. The recency bias finding (up to 71% improvement by reordering examples) and noise sensitivity (a single irrelevant example degrading performance by ~9.5%) have direct practical implications for how retrieval-augmented and few-shot medical AI systems should be designed. Key limitations include the small problem count (111), image-only modality, and focus on diagnostic tasks; open questions include whether these biases persist at larger scales, whether domain-specific fine-tuning can be made ICL-robust, and how to extend the benchmark to volumetric, temporal, and genomic modalities.
