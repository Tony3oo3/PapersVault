## Problem

Existing conformal risk control methods calibrate blackbox models under a fixed, static data-generating distribution, but in sociotechnical settings (credit scoring, content moderation, power generation), predictions influence the very outcomes they aim to predict, creating a cyclical dependency between the decision threshold and the data distribution. This paper initiates the study of statistically rigorous, finite-sample risk control under performativity, a setting that had been entirely unexplored in the risk control literature.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained blackbox model f and iteratively consumes calibration samples drawn from D(λ̂_{t-1}) — the distribution induced by the previously deployed threshold — along with user-specified parameters (risk level α, failure probability δ, safety parameter τ, sample size n). It produces a calibrated threshold λ̂ such that the performative risk R(λ̂) = E_{(x,y)~D(λ̂)}[ℓ(y, T_λ(x))] is (α, δ)-controlled, with the additional guarantee that every intermediate iterate λ̂_t along the trajectory is also risk-controlled. The key distinction from standard risk control is that the calibration data distribution itself shifts as the threshold changes, so the algorithm must simultaneously account for generalization error and performative distribution shift at each step.

## Method

The core contribution is an iterative calibration procedure (Algorithm 1) that starts at a safe threshold λ_safe and progressively decreases λ, at each step computing a composite value V = empirical loss + confidence width + performativity guard τ(λ̂_{t-1} − λ), where the guard term accounts for distribution shift bounded via a (γ,p,g)-sensitivity assumption on the Wasserstein distance between loss distributions at different thresholds. The confidence width is derived from concentration inequalities (Hoeffding, Bernstein, Hoeffding-Bentkus, or CLT) and the safety parameter τ ≥ γ ensures the performative error is dominated by the guard. The algorithm terminates when progress falls below a threshold Δλ, guaranteeing that the final λ̂_T achieves tightness R(λ̂_T) ≥ α − Δα while maintaining R(λ̂_t) ≤ α for all t with probability 1 − δ. The framework extends to quantile-based risk measures (CVaR, VaR) by constructing CDF confidence bounds and applying a Hölder-inequality-based sensitivity bound on the quantile risk.

## Results

On the Kaggle credit scoring dataset (n=2000, α=0.3, Δα=0.082, δ=0.1), PRC with τ≥γ (γ≤1.38) achieves (α,δ)-performative-risk-controlled Type II error across 1,000 trajectories, while the non-performative algorithm (τ=0) exceeds the risk threshold. For the 90%-CVaR setting (n=10,000, α=0.25, Δα=0.12, p=0.064, γ≤0.205), PRC with τ≥2.05 maintains quantile risk control, with each trajectory's final R(λ̂_T) falling inside the confidence bounds. In the power-plant demand forecasting experiment (N=4032, α=300, Δα=85, β=0.2, τ=3204.43), the performative algorithm carefully reduces λ_t to maintain risk control, whereas the non-performative algorithm (τ=0) exceeds the risk threshold on the first iteration.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external baseline methods; instead, the key comparison is between PRC with and without performativity accounting (τ=0 vs. τ≥γ). With τ=0, the algorithm ignores distribution shift and its iterates can exceed the risk threshold α, as clearly shown in Fig. 2 (top-left) and Fig. 11. With τ≥γ, PRC guarantees R(λ̂_t)≤α for all iterations t with probability ≥1−δ, and the final λ̂_T achieves tightness R(λ̂_T)≥α−Δα. Increasing τ beyond γ makes updates more conservative (smaller per-iteration Δλ), resulting in longer trajectories but tighter safety margins. Even for some τ<γ values (e.g., τ=0.5, 0.8, 1.0 in the expected-risk experiment), the final risk is empirically still controlled, though the theoretical guarantee only holds for τ≥γ. The failure probability (proportion of 1,000 trajectories where α−Δα < R(λ̂_T) or R(λ̂_T) > α) is essentially zero for τ=1 and τ=2, well below the nominal δ=0.1.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate research threads: conformal risk control (Angelopoulos et al. [4], Bates et al. [5], Angelopoulos et al. [6]), which calibrates blackbox models to achieve finite-sample risk guarantees under a fixed distribution, and performative prediction (Perdomo et al. [7]), which models how deployed predictions shift the underlying data distribution. The key departure is that prior risk control methods assume a static, unknown distribution, while prior performative prediction work focuses on finding optimal or stable model parameters rather than providing distribution-free risk guarantees. The technical machinery adapts the UCB calibration of Bates et al. [5] and the (γ,p,g)-sensitivity assumption of Perdomo et al. [7] into an iterative procedure that accounts for both generalization error and performative distribution shift at each step, with quantile-based extensions drawing on Snell et al. [17].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first framework to provide statistically rigorous, finite-sample risk control in settings where predictions influence the outcome they predict—a gap the authors explicitly identify as absent from the literature. The 'safe at anytime' property (every intermediate threshold is risk-controlled) is particularly relevant for policy deployment where risk must be monitored as usage scales incrementally. Key limitations include sample efficiency (the iterative process requires fresh samples each round), the need to specify a safety parameter τ ≥ γ that is typically unknown in practice, and the resulting conservatism of the bounds. An open question the authors raise is whether relaxing 'safe at anytime' to 'safe on average' could improve sample efficiency without sacrificing practical safety.
