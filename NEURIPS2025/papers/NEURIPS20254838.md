## Problem

Standard variational inference relies on gradient-based optimisation (SGD, natural gradient descent) that requires either the reparameterisation trick or log-derivative gradient estimators, both of which fail when the target is discrete, intractable, non-differentiable, or likelihood-free. Moreover, naive natural gradient descent requires costly Fisher information matrix inversions that scale cubically with the number of parameters. This paper introduces a gradient-free, Monte Carlo-based scheme that avoids both the gradient requirement and the explicit FIM inversion.

## Input / Output

The method requires only pointwise evaluations of the unnormalised log-target f = log π at Monte Carlo samples drawn from the current variational approximation—no gradients, no automatic differentiation, and no reparameterisation are needed. The variational family must be an exponential family (Gaussian, Bernoulli product, etc.), specified via its sufficient statistic s and initial natural parameters η₀. The output is the updated natural parameter vector η defining the approximate posterior; for Gaussian families this translates directly to mean and covariance (or diagonal variance in the mean-field case).

## Method

LSVI recognises that the first-order optimality condition for KL minimisation within an exponential family is equivalent to an ordinary least squares regression of f(X) on s(X) under the current variational distribution q_η. Each iteration performs a relaxed fixed-point step η_{t+1} = ε_t · OLS(η_t) + (1−ε_t)·η_t, which the paper proves is exactly a biased stochastic natural gradient descent step (equivalently, mirror descent in the moment-parameter space). For Gaussian variational families, a reparametrisation of the regression onto uncorrelated Hermite-polynomial features t(Z) with Z ~ N(0,I) eliminates the FIM inversion entirely: the OLS coefficients are simply Monte Carlo averages of t(Z)f(μ+CZ), and a closed-form back-mapping recovers the natural parameters. This yields O(d³) per-iteration cost for full-covariance Gaussians (dominated by the Cholesky factorisation) and O(d) for mean-field Gaussians, with convergence rates of O(k^{-1}) + O(N^{-1}) under standard smoothness and relative convexity assumptions.

## Results

On the Pima logistic regression benchmark (d=9, full-covariance), LSVI (Algorithm 1) converges essentially in one step, while LSVI-FC (Algorithm 3) converges in fewer than 100 iterations, both matching or outperforming NGD and GMMVI in KL divergence with lower variance. On the larger Census-Income dataset (d=48, mean-field, subsampling), LSVI-MF completes in ~13 s versus ~70 s for NGD (MF), a 5× speedup. For the toad's displacement BSL problem, full-covariance LSVI matches the MCMC posterior at roughly 27% of the CPU cost (72.9 s vs. 268.1 s), and for variable selection (d=92, Bernoulli product family), LSVI recovers posterior inclusion probabilities close to the SMC benchmark in 60.8 s versus 290.7 s for SMC.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against **ADVI** (Kucukelbir et al. 2017, via PyMC3/Blackjax), LSVI is consistently faster: on Pima, LSVI-FC runs in 3.4 s vs. 5.9 s for PyMC ADVI; on Sonar, 5.1 s vs. 9.6 s; on MNIST, 10.4 s vs. 19.5 s for Blackjax. Convergence in KL divergence is comparable, with LSVI exhibiting lower iteration-to-iteration noise. Against **NGD** (Amari 1998, with MC-estimated FIM and JAX autodiff gradients), LSVI matches or beats convergence quality with less variance, and the speed advantage grows with dimension: on Census-Income (d=48), LSVI-MF finishes in 12.9 s versus 70.0 s for NGD (MF), a ~5.4× speedup, while using less peak memory (3.30 GB vs. 3.33 GB). Against **GMMVI** (Arenz et al. 2018), a gradient-free method for Gaussian mixtures, LSVI (Algorithm 1) achieves similar KL convergence on Pima but with noticeably lower variance across 100 repetitions, and uses less memory (1.28 GB vs. 4.57 GB). Against **SMC** (Schäfer & Chopin 2013) on the variable selection task, LSVI (Bernoulli product family) recovers posterior inclusion probabilities that are visually close to the SMC benchmark at ~4.8× lower runtime (60.8 s vs. 290.7 s); the authors note this is the first application of VI to variable selection with a Bernoulli product family. For the BSL toad problem, full-covariance LSVI matches the MCMC posterior (31% acceptance rate, chain length 10⁴) at roughly 27% of the CPU cost, demonstrating that gradient-free VI is viable when log-π is noisy and non-differentiable.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Salimans and Knowles' [22] observation that the fixed-point equation for exponential-family VI is an OLS regression, and on the well-established NGD/mirror-descent equivalence in the variational inference literature (Khan et al. [12, 18, 20]). The key departure is a reparameterization of the regression for Gaussian families—using a Cholesky-based change of variables that makes the design matrix orthonormal—thereby eliminating the costly FIM inversion that plagues naive NGD implementations. The convergence analysis extends the stochastic mirror descent framework of Hanzely and Richtárik [30] to handle the bias introduced by Monte Carlo estimation of the FIM, yielding explicit rates in both the number of iterations and samples.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LSVI fills a practical gap for variational inference in settings where the reparameterization trick is unavailable: discrete exponential families (e.g., Bernoulli products for variable selection), non-differentiable or intractable log-targets, and likelihood-free inference (Bayesian synthetic likelihood). The O(d) mean-field and O(d³) full-covariance complexity matches the cost of standard matrix operations, making it competitive with gradient-based methods even when gradients are accessible. Limitations include restriction to exponential families (no mixtures), reliance on smoothness/convexity assumptions that rarely hold globally in practice, and the fact that the convergence rates are conditioned on high-probability events. Open questions include extending the framework to mixture families and tree-structured discrete dependencies.
