## Problem

Standard representational similarity measures (CCA, CKA) between neural recordings and computational models are systematically biased when only a limited subset of neurons is recorded, yet the magnitude and mechanism of this sampling-induced underestimation have not been rigorously characterized. Existing bounds (e.g., Pospisil & Pillow) are tight only under a white Wishart assumption and become uninformative for the power-law spectra ubiquitous in neural data. This paper provides the first eigencomponent-level theoretical framework, grounded in Random Matrix Theory, that explains how finite neuron sampling distorts similarity through eigenvector delocalization and offers a principled correction.

## Input / Output

The method consumes sample eigenvalues {λ_i} from N recorded neurons (N < P stimuli), the sample cross-overlap matrix M ∈ R^{P×P}, and a power-law spectral prior (single exponent γ). It produces the inferred population cross-overlap M̃, the corresponding population CKA/CCA, and MLE-based confidence intervals for each M̃_ja entry. A key practical constraint is that only the first ~√N leading eigenvectors can be reliably resolved; the method explicitly truncates to these localized components rather than attempting full matrix inversion, which is ill-posed when P ≫ N.

## Method

The framework decomposes the problem into a forward analysis and a backward inference. In the forward direction, the paper shows via RMT that the sample cross-overlap decouples as M = Q^x M̃ Q^y (Eq. 7), where the self-overlap matrices Q are computed analytically from the resolvent of the sample Gram matrix (Eq. 8–9), revealing that eigenvector delocalization—quantified by the BBP phase transition—drives the systematic underestimation. In the backward direction, Algorithm 1 first estimates the population eigenvalue decay rate γ from sample eigenvalues under a power-law ansatz, then computes Q in closed form, and finally solves a constrained least-squares problem (min ‖M − Q·M̃‖_F s.t. M̃_ij ∈ [0,1]) to recover the population overlap. For power-law spectra, the paper derives a universal scaling law: the number of reliably resolvable eigenvectors grows as (1+γ)/√8 · √N, providing a concrete rule of thumb for experimental design. The approach is validated on ResNet18/CIFAR-10 activations and primate V2 cortex recordings, where it corrects sample CKA/CCA upward and can even reverse model rankings that are artifacts of limited sampling.

## Results

The paper's central quantitative finding is that for power-law population spectra (λ̃_i ∝ i^{-(1+γ)}), the number of well-localized eigenvectors scales as i*(N) ≈ (1+γ)/√8 × √N, providing a concrete rule of thumb for how many principal components can be resolved from N recorded neurons. On synthetic data with a known population Gram matrix, the forward theory (RMT-based eigencomponent analysis) matches empirical CKA and CCA across all tested neuron counts N, and the backward denoising algorithm recovers population similarity close to ground truth even at N ≈ 20 neurons, where the naive sample similarity is substantially underestimated. On real primate V2 cortex recordings (N = 20 out of 103 neurons), the inferred population CKA and CCA are consistently higher than the raw sample-based estimates across multiple computational models, confirming that limited sampling systematically underestimates model–brain correspondence.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against a fixed set of competing methods in the traditional sense; instead, it compares its RMT-based denoising framework against (i) the naive sample-based CKA/CCA (the standard practice in neuroscience), (ii) moment-based unbiased estimators [22, 28, 14], and (iii) the tight bounds from Pospisil & Pillow [41]. Against the naive sample-based measure, the denoising method consistently yields higher and more accurate population similarity estimates, particularly at low N where eigenvector delocalization is most severe. Compared to moment-based estimators, the proposed approach provides an interpretable eigencomponent-level explanation for the bias (eigenvector delocalization) rather than a black-box correction, though it requires sufficiently large datasets for asymptotic approximations to hold. Relative to the bounds in [41], the RMT framework is tighter for realistic power-law spectra (where the white-Wishart assumption underlying those bounds breaks down), yielding practical rather than vacuous predictions. The paper also demonstrates a qualitative advantage: sampling can invert the ranking of two models (Fig. 4), an effect that neither moment-based estimators nor the loose bounds of [41] capture, while the eigencomponent analysis explains it directly through differential delocalization of higher-index eigenvectors.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the eigencomponent decomposition of CCA and CKA from Kornblith et al. [29] and the random-matrix-theory overlap formulas of Bun et al. [7] and Ledoit & Péché [32], which together provide the mathematical machinery for expressing similarity measures in terms of eigenvector overlaps. It departs from prior sampling-bias analyses (moment-based estimators [28,14], tight bounds under white Wishart assumptions [41]) by targeting the power-law spectra ubiquitous in neural data, where those earlier tools become too loose or lack interpretability. The backward-inference (denoising) step—using a population eigenvalue prior to invert the forward relationship M = Q M̃—is a new contribution not present in the cited RMT literature, which typically addresses only the forward problem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that finite neuron sampling can reverse the ranking of two computational models relative to the brain is a practical warning for model-selection pipelines that rely on CKA/CCA with modest recording sizes (e.g., N=20 out of 103 neurons in V2). The √N scaling law for the number of resolvable eigenvectors under power-law spectra gives neuroscientists a concrete rule of thumb for experimental design. Key limitations include the Gaussian random-projection assumption, the reliance on a single power-law exponent (rather than broken power-laws), and the exclusion of additive trial-to-trial noise; extending the framework to nonlinear, dynamic, or multi-population spectral structures remains open.
