## Problem

RL-enhanced LLM reasoning has been predominantly studied in Math and Code, leaving it unclear whether reasoning gains transfer across diverse domains and how to build models with balanced multi-domain competence. A major obstacle is the absence of a high-quality, multi-domain RL dataset with reliable verifiable rewards spanning beyond STEM competition problems.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254479/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

Each training and evaluation sample is a natural-language reasoning prompt from one of six domains (Math, Code, Science, Logic, Simulation, Tabular). The model produces a free-form reasoning chain followed by a structured final answer (e.g., \boxed{}, <answer> tag, or JSON block); for Code tasks the output is an executable function or script. Rewards are binary (0/1) and computed via rule-based matching, sandboxed execution against unit tests, or a 1.5B model-based entailment verifier for open-ended Science answers.

## Method

The paper introduces GURU, a 92K-example RL dataset constructed through a five-stage pipeline: multi-source data collection, conservative substring-based deduplication, domain-specific reward design (rule-based, execution-based, or model-based), heuristic filtering for noise and format issues, and difficulty filtering using pass-rate statistics from a weak (Qwen2.5-7B) and strong (Qwen3-30B) model over 16 runs. Using GURU, the authors conduct a controlled cross-domain transfer study with Qwen2.5-7B trained via GRPO on single-domain (3K) and mixed-domain (18K) subsets, revealing that Math, Code, and Science benefit most from cross-domain RL while Logic, Simulation, and Tabular rely primarily on in-domain training, and that mixed-domain training matches or exceeds single-domain performance. They then train GURU-7B and GURU-32B from Qwen2.5 base models using GRPO on the full 92K dataset (2 epochs, batch 512, 16 rollouts per prompt, 4k/8k token limits), achieving state-of-the-art results among open RL-trained models on a 17-benchmark, six-domain evaluation suite.

## Results

On a 17-benchmark, six-domain evaluation suite, GURU-7B achieves an average score of 41.17%, outperforming the strongest open RL baseline (Open-Reasoner-Zero-7B at 33.90%) by 7.3 percentage points. GURU-32B reaches 52.68%, surpassing Open-Reasoner-Zero-32B (46.95%) by 7.8 points. The largest individual gains appear in Logic (ARC-AGI: 7.00 vs 0.00 for 7B; 18.50 vs 13.00 for 32B) and Code (LiveCodeBench: 16.49 vs 5.73 for 7B), while GURU-32B trails ORZ-32B on the hardest math tasks (AIME24: 32.29 vs 47.50).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GURU-7B (41.17 avg) decisively outperforms all three 7B baselines: Open-Reasoner-Zero-7B (33.90, +7.3 pts), SimpleRL-Qwen-2.5-7B (33.04, +8.1 pts), and GeneralReasoner-7B (31.30, +9.9 pts). The advantage is most pronounced in Logic (ARC-AGI 7.00 vs 0.00/1.34/0.90) and Code (LiveCodeBench 16.49 vs 5.73/7.21/8.24), where prior open RL efforts were weakest. At 32B scale, GURU-32B (52.68) beats ORZ-32B (46.95, +7.8 pts) and SimpleRL-32B (44.88, +7.8 pts), but ORZ-32B retains a significant edge on the hardest math benchmarks (AIME24: 47.50 vs 32.29; MATH500: 89.80 vs 84.00) and slightly on Science (GPQA: 53.53 vs 52.02; SuperGPQA: 46.05 vs 43.60). GURU-32B dominates on Code, Logic, Simulation, Tabular, and generalization tasks (IFEval 58.04 vs 38.26; LiveBench 34.03 vs 28.78), confirming that multi-domain RL training closes the gap in underrepresented domains while single-domain-focused baselines still lead on their home turf (math). Notably, GURU-7B also outperforms ORZ-7B on IFEval (35.81 vs 32.72) and LiveBench (18.57 vs 12.64), suggesting that cross-domain RL confers broader generalization even at smaller scale.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the RLVR (Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards) paradigm pioneered by OpenAI-o1 and DeepSeek-R1, and the open-source follow-ups (Open-Reasoner-Zero, DeepScaler, SimpleRL, DeepCoder) that have largely confined RL training to Math and Code. Rather than proposing a new RL algorithm, GURU repurposes GRPO on Qwen2.5 base models and shifts the contribution to data: a 92K-sample, six-domain corpus with domain-specific verifiers, plus a controlled cross-domain transfer study that prior single-domain efforts did not attempt. The data pipeline (deduplication, heuristic filtering, difficulty-aware filtering using weak/strong model pass rates) builds on standard curation ideas but applies them systematically across heterogeneous domains including Logic, Simulation, and Tabular, which had no prior open RLVR coverage.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The GURU dataset and its 17-benchmark evaluation suite fill a concrete gap: prior open RL reasoning resources were siloed in Math/Code, leaving Logic, Simulation, and Tabular without verifiable training data. The cross-domain transfer findings—particularly that Math/Code/Science benefit from cross-domain RL (attributed to pretraining exposure) while Logic/Simulation/Tabular do not, and that difficulty-filtering improves in-domain but degrades easier cross-domain tasks—provide actionable guidance for multi-domain RL training design. Open questions remain: whether the uniform-mix strategy holds as domain count grows, whether results generalize beyond Qwen2.5, and whether the 32B model's underperformance on AIME24 (32.3 vs. ORZ-32B's 47.5) reflects a fundamental limitation of multi-domain RL at scale or a data-mixing issue.
