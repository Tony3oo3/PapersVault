## Problem

Subject-driven image generation methods lack reliable metrics that can both quantify and spatially localize visual inconsistencies of a subject across generated images. Existing global feature-based metrics (CLIP, DINO) miss fine-grained appearance details, while VLM-based approaches provide only holistic scores without identifying where inconsistencies occur. No prior method leverages the visual (appearance-level) information already encoded in diffusion model backbones for this purpose.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250112/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pair of RGB images depicting the same subject in different poses or contexts and processes them through a frozen Stable Diffusion 2.1 UNet backbone, extracting multi-layer decoder features at 48×48 spatial resolution projected to 384 dimensions. It outputs a scalar VSM score (the fraction of semantically matched regions that are also visually consistent, thresholded at T_v=0.6) and a per-pixel spatial heatmap highlighting inconsistent regions, enabling both quantitative ranking and targeted post-processing of specific subject parts.

## Method

The core contribution is a contrastive architecture that disentangles semantic and visual features from a frozen diffusion backbone using two parallel ResNet-based aggregation networks (Ψ_s, Ψ_v) with trainable per-layer scalar weights. Training data is generated by an automated pipeline that segments subjects with Grounded-SAM, computes semantic correspondences via CleanDIFT, selects localized regions with SAM (using skewness of the similarity distribution to reject ambiguous flat-texture matches), and creates controlled visual inconsistencies via diffusion inpainting. The semantic branch is trained with a standard contrastive loss over all correspondences, while the visual branch uses a split objective: a negative-similarity loss pushes features apart at inpainted (inconsistent) points and a positive-similarity loss pulls them together at consistent points. At inference, the VSM metric thresholds semantic similarity to identify confident matches, then measures what fraction of those matches also exceed a visual similarity threshold, yielding both a global consistency score and a spatial map of inconsistent regions.

## Results

On the controlled inconsistency benchmark, VSM achieves a Pearson correlation of 0.448 and Spearman correlation of 0.582 with the oracle, substantially outperforming CLIP (Pearson −0.053), DINO (Pearson 0.087), and ChatGPT-4o VLM (Pearson 0.072). On the realistic subject-driven generation setting (evaluating Diptych, DSD-Diffusion, and EasyControl), VSM maintains a Pearson correlation of 0.405 and Spearman of 0.369, again far exceeding all baselines (best baseline: DINO at Pearson 0.164). The VSM metric also enables spatial localization of inconsistent regions, a capability none of the compared baselines provide.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VSM decisively outperforms all three baselines in both evaluation settings. CLIP image-to-image similarity is essentially uncorrelated with the oracle (Pearson −0.053 in controlled, 0.156 in subject-driven), confirming that global semantic embeddings cannot capture fine-grained visual inconsistencies. DINO performs marginally better than CLIP (Pearson 0.087 vs. −0.053 in controlled) but remains far below VSM, with a gap of roughly 0.36 in Pearson correlation. The ChatGPT-4o VLM baseline, prompted following the DreamBench++ protocol, shows the weakest performance in the subject-driven setting (Pearson 0.079) and tends to assign scores in a narrow 75–95 range regardless of actual visual discrepancies, as evidenced by the KDE distributions in Figure 7. VSM's advantage is most pronounced in the controlled setting (Spearman 0.582 vs. best baseline 0.120 for DINO), where ground-truth inconsistency regions are known, and remains substantial in the realistic setting (Spearman 0.369 vs. 0.146 for DINO). Additionally, VSM uniquely provides spatial localization of inconsistent regions via per-point visual similarity scores, a capability entirely absent from the global-scalar baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the semantic correspondence paradigm built on diffusion model backbones (CleanDIFT, Diffusion Hyperfeatures, Emergent Correspondence) by arguing that these backbones also encode appearance-level visual features that can be disentangled from semantic ones. Where prior work leveraged diffusion features exclusively for semantic matching (structurally similar points), this paper introduces a contrastive architecture trained on an automatically generated dataset of visually consistent/inconsistent pairs to separate the two feature types. The evaluation side departs from global feature-similarity metrics (CLIP, DINO) and VLM-based scoring by introducing a per-point visual correspondence mechanism that enables spatial localization of inconsistencies, a capability absent in all prior subject-driven generation evaluation approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first framework that simultaneously quantifies and localizes visual inconsistencies in subject-driven generation, opening the door to targeted post-processing correction of specific inconsistent regions rather than global re-generation. Key limitations include partial disentanglement (visual features may still carry semantic information), dependence on the quality of the reference dataset (Subjects200k), and spatial resolution constraints of diffusion features that limit detection of fine-grained inconsistencies. Open questions include whether full visual-semantic separation is achievable, whether the approach generalizes beyond SD 2.1 to DiT-based or autoregressive backbones, and whether the metric can be extended to style and color-level inconsistencies.
