## Problem

Most ML diagnostic models assume complete data availability and treat diagnosis as a static classification task, failing to reflect the sequential, resource-aware test selection that clinicians actually perform. There is a gap for generalizable frameworks that can reason dynamically under uncertainty and cost constraints without requiring task-specific training data or full modality availability.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a natural-language clinical vignette (demographics, symptoms, and known lab results converted from tabular form because LLMs struggle with structured tables) plus a list of available diagnostic tests. At each iteration it outputs a recommended next test, a disease probability in [0,1], and intermediate transparency artifacts (sampled test-outcome distributions, per-test KL divergences). The process is iterative and clinician-in-the-loop: the system recommends rather than decides, and a KL-based stopping criterion halts acquisition when no remaining test is expected to meaningfully shift the posterior belief.

## Method

ACTMED couples Bayesian Experimental Design with LLMs acting as zero-shot generative surrogates: for each candidate test, the LLM is prompted to sample M plausible outcomes conditioned on the current patient context, approximating P(U_t | K_t) without mechanistic models or fine-tuning. The expected information gain of each test is computed as the Monte Carlo average of KL divergence between the posterior and prior Bernoulli disease-probability distributions, and the selected test maximizes the normalized utility F = I(U)/c(U), trading off information gain against test cost. A principled stopping rule compares the expected KL shift of each remaining test against a target posterior defined by a confidence-margin hyperparameter γ, terminating acquisition when no test can justify further belief movement. The key architectural insight is shifting the probabilistic decision logic out of the LLM and into the explicit BED framework: the LLM only generates samples and probability estimates in natural language, while KL-based selection and stopping are handled analytically, compensating for LLMs' known weaknesses in Bayesian reasoning.

## Results

Under a 3-test-per-patient constraint, ACTMED (KL-based, GPT-4o) achieves 83.9%±1.3% accuracy on Hepatitis C, 68.2%±1.2% on Diabetes, and 99.9%±0.3% on CKD, consistently outperforming all baselines including the full-information LLM classifier on the two harder tasks. The KL-based stopping criterion (γ=0.7) reduces the average number of tests to under 2 across most datasets while maintaining or improving accuracy. In a clinician-in-the-loop evaluation, 94.5%±1.4% of ACTMED's test selections were judged clinically reasonable by five experienced evaluators across 450 decisions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ACTMED is compared against four baselines using the same LLM backbone: (1) LLM classifier with all features, (2) random 3-feature selection, (3) global best fixed subset (predefined 3 features), and (4) implicit LLM feature selection. On Hepatitis C, ACTMED (GPT-4o: 83.9%) surpasses the full-information LLM classifier and all other baselines, with the largest gap over implicit selection (~7 percentage points). On Diabetes, ACTMED (68.2%) again outperforms the full-information baseline and all feature-selection baselines, with the implicit method showing a strong bias toward globally optimal features rather than patient-specific choices. On CKD, all methods achieve near-perfect accuracy (>97%), making it a less discriminative task. The KL-based stopping criterion further reduces the average number of tests to ~1.1–2.3 (depending on γ and dataset) while maintaining accuracy, compared to the fixed 3-test budget used by all baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

ACTMED extends the Bayesian Experimental Design (BED) framework—previously applied in engineering, physics, and neuroscience—to sequential clinical test selection, using LLMs as generative surrogate models that simulate plausible test outcomes without mechanistic equations or task-specific fine-tuning. This builds directly on the LLM-as-surrogate-for-active-learning paradigm introduced by Astorga et al. (NeurIPS 2024) for partially observable cost-aware settings, and on earlier work showing LLMs can approximate structured decision-making tasks like Bayesian optimization. The paper departs from prior clinical ML approaches that assume full data availability or treat diagnosis as static classification, and from RL-based diagnostic agents that require substantial task-specific training, by operating entirely at inference time with a KL-divergence-based acquisition criterion.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ACTMED offers a transparent, clinician-in-the-loop framework for resource-aware sequential diagnosis that outperforms both naive LLM feature selection and full-information baselines on several datasets, with 94.5% of test selections judged clinically reasonable by experts. However, the evaluation is constrained by small sample sizes (~1,000 patients total), binary classification only, and reliance on high-capacity LLMs (GPT-4o) for physiologically coherent surrogate sampling; simpler models fail to produce accurate samples. Open questions include extending to multi-label comorbidities, incorporating free-text and imaging data, scaling to larger heterogeneous cohorts, and reducing the number of LLM queries needed per decision step to make the framework viable in resource-limited settings.
