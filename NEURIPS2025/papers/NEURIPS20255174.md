## Problem

Hyperdimensional computing (HDC) models operate on high-dimensional binary vectors but struggle to match neural network accuracy due to limited representational capacity and the difficulty of training expressive classifiers directly in the discrete, high-dimensional binary space. This paper asks whether one can construct binary neural networks that provably match the accuracy of their floating-point counterparts without any training in the binary domain, bridging the gap between HDC and deep learning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255174/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method accepts standard classification inputs—grayscale 28×28 images (MNIST), RGB 32×32 images (CIFAR-10), or 56-dimensional sensor vectors (HAR-WSS)—and outputs a class label. Unusually, the input is first mapped into a high-dimensional binary hypercube via a random sign projection φ(x) = sign(Gx) with N ≫ p dimensions, and all subsequent network operations (weight multiplications, activations) occur entirely in this binary Hamming space. The G-Net is trained on the original real-valued data, and the binary EHD G-Net is obtained by a one-time random sign embedding of the trained weights, requiring no retraining.

## Method

The core mechanism is grounded in Grothendieck's identity, which shows that inner products on the unit sphere are approximately preserved under random sign embedding up to a known (2/π)arcsin distortion. G-Nets are floating-point networks with ℓ2-normalized inputs, row-normalized weight matrices, and a composite activation σ = τ ∘ (2/π)arcsin that absorbs this distortion, so that each layer's real-valued computation is faithfully replicated by the binary operation sign(WGᵀ)sign(G·) in the embedded space. Three activation variants are defined: ASU (identity τ), RASU (ReLU τ, yielding non-negative integer outputs), and TASU (tanh-approximated sign τ, yielding fully binary outputs). The hyperdimension N per layer controls the approximation error, with non-asymptotic concentration-of-measure bounds showing N = O(ε⁻²n log n) suffices for ε-discrepancy per layer, and the network-level error scales linearly in depth. The framework extends to convolutional, pooling, and batch-normalization layers, and Rademacher (±1) matrices can replace Gaussian matrices for hardware efficiency at a small additional √(n/p) discrepancy cost.

## Results

On MNIST, the Rademacher RASU G-Net (EHD G-Net) exceeds 99% test accuracy, and on CIFAR-10 it surpasses 81% test accuracy, rivaling real-valued convolutional neural networks. The TASU variant achieves 98.4% on MNIST. Across all three datasets (MNIST, CIFAR-10, HAR-WSS), the EHD G-Net outperforms all five prior HDC baselines by a significant margin, with the CIFAR-10 improvement reaching approximately 30% relative accuracy gain over the best prior HDC method. Performance asymptotically approaches the floating-point G-Net baseline as the hyperdimension N increases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the Rademacher RASU EHD G-Net against five prior HDC methods: Classic HDC [8], HoloGN [23], Laplace HDC [26], OnlineHD [10], and RFF-HDC [37]. The EHD G-Net wins decisively on all three datasets (MNIST, CIFAR-10, HAR-WSS), achieving >99% on MNIST and >81% on CIFAR-10, while the best prior HDC baselines fall substantially short—particularly on CIFAR-10, where the paper reports an approximately 30% relative accuracy improvement over prior HDC models. The gap narrows as the hyperdimension N increases for the baselines, but the EHD G-Net maintains its lead across the entire range of N tested. Among the G-Net variants, RASU consistently outperforms TASU (e.g., >99% vs. 98.4% on MNIST), and Gaussian embedding yields slightly better accuracy than Rademacher embedding, though the difference is small. The key practical advantage is that the EHD G-Net achieves these results without any training in the binary domain—only a compact floating-point G-Net is trained in the primal space, then converted via a random sign embedding.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of hyperdimensional computing (HDC) and binary neural networks (BNNs), but departs from both in a distinctive way. Rather than training BNNs via straight-through estimators or quantization heuristics, or relying on HDC's typically simple centroid/online classifiers, the authors train a floating-point G-Net (with arcsine-based activations derived from Grothendieck's identity) and convert it to a binary EHD G-Net via a random sign embedding—without any training in the binary space. The 'bundle embedding' framing, where both data and model weights are coherently mapped into the hyperdimensional Hamming space, is a new conceptual contribution that unifies the HDC embedding and inference stages into a single trainable architecture. The theoretical analysis draws on concentration of measure and random matrix theory, extending Grothendieck's identity to Rademacher embeddings under sparsity conditions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first non-asymptotic, layer-wise, and network-level guarantees linking the hyperdimension N to the accuracy gap between a G-Net and its binary EHD counterpart, addressing a key open question in HDC theory about how far these models can be pushed. The ~30% accuracy improvement over prior HDC methods on CIFAR-10 is meaningful within the HDC subfield, though the baselines (classic HDC, OnlineHD, RFF-HDC) are relatively weak compared to modern CNNs. Limitations include the high dimensionality of the resulting binary models (inheriting HDC's memory footprint), the cascade analysis being restricted to ASU activations, and a non-vanishing √(n/p) error term for Rademacher embeddings. Open directions include fine-tuning EHD G-Nets directly, extending the Rademacher theory from the hypersphere to the hypercube, and studying adversarial robustness of the randomized binary constructions.
