## Problem

Cooperative MARL policies are typically tuned for maximum cooperation in ideal simulations, yet their behavior under real-world uncertainties—observation noise, action deviations, environmental shifts—remains poorly understood. The literature conflates robustness (maintaining performance under perturbation) with resilience (recovering from a disrupted state), and the role of hyperparameters in shaping these properties is largely unexplored, despite evidence that hyperparameters can dominate algorithm choice in determining performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251468/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The evaluation framework takes per-agent state-vector observations and joint continuous/discrete actions as inputs, then injects 13 distinct uncertainty perturbations (Gaussian noise, greedy PGD-based attacks, learned optimal attacks, random/greedy/optimal policy deviations, and environmental parameter shifts) at either all-agent (ε=0.1) or single-agent (ε=0.2) scope. Robustness is measured as the expected discounted return under continuous uncertainty, while resilience is measured as the expected return from a perturbed state s_u with no further perturbations applied—capturing recovery rather than sustained performance. All 18 tasks across 4 real-world environments (DexHand, Quads, Traffic, Voltage) are evaluated with 5 random seeds, yielding 82,620 total experiments.

## Method

The paper conducts a systematic one-at-a-time hyperparameter sweep (15 hyperparameters, 34 model variants) across three actor-critic MARL backbones (MADDPG, MAPPO, HAPPO) on 18 tasks in 4 real-world environments, using a two-phase evaluation protocol: cooperative models are first trained, then frozen and evaluated under 13 uncertainty types for robustness, and separately re-initialized from the perturbed state for resilience. Statistical analysis via Pearson correlation, two-way ANOVA, and paired t-tests reveals that hyperparameters outperform algorithm choice in 9 of 18 tasks (p<0.001), and that the cooperation–robustness/resilience correlation weakens linearly with attack severity (r=0.85, p<.001). Counterintuitively, standard practices like parameter sharing, GAE, and PopArt degrade robustness and resilience, while early stopping, critic-dominant learning rates, and Leaky ReLU consistently improve all three metrics. The optimized hyperparameter set yields average gains of 52.60% (cooperation), 34.78% (robustness), and 60.34% (resilience), and generalizes to the robust MARL method ERNIE across all backbones with even larger improvements (89.43%, 65.83%, 82.96% respectively).

## Results

Across 82,620 experiments (5 seeds × 27 uncertainty settings × 18 tasks × 34 hyperparameter configurations), the paper finds that the Pearson correlation between cooperation and robustness (r=0.85, p<.001) and between cooperation and resilience (r=0.76, p<.001) weakens linearly as attack severity increases. Hyperparameter optimization alone yields average improvements of 52.60% in cooperation, 34.78% in robustness, and 60.34% in resilience across all three MARL backbones. When the same optimized hyperparameters are transferred to the robust MARL method ERNIE, improvements reach 89.43% (cooperation), 65.83% (robustness), and 82.96% (resilience). Two-way ANOVA confirms hyperparameters dominate algorithm choice in 9 of 18 tasks (p<0.001).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates three MARL backbones—MADDPG, MAPPO, and HAPPO—plus the robust method ERNIE across 18 tasks and 13 uncertainty types. No algorithm dominates overall: MADDPG is significantly more robust to action uncertainties (due to persistent exploration noise), while MAPPO and HAPPO perform better under observation uncertainties (thanks to centralized critics improving sample efficiency). When MADDPG is excluded, MAPPO and HAPPO show strong linearity with robustness under action uncertainty (r≥0.69, p<.05), confirming the algorithm-specific sensitivity. For ERNIE, applying the paper's optimized hyperparameters (early stopping, high critic LR, Leaky ReLU, no GAE/PopArt) on top of default settings yields 89.43% cooperation, 65.83% robustness, and 82.96% resilience improvements, demonstrating that hyperparameter tuning generalizes to robust MARL methods. The paper also validates findings on MPE, SMAC, and Multi-Agent MuJoCo, noting that GAE and PopArt are effective in MuJoCo (dense rewards) but harmful in the real-world environments (sparse, variable rewards).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the robust MARL evaluation literature by formally separating robustness (maintaining performance under persistent uncertainty) from resilience (recovering after a discrete disruption), a distinction well-established in control theory [Zhu & Başar, 2024] but largely conflated in prior MARL work. It builds on the hyperparameter-importance line of research (Engstrom et al. for single-agent RL; MAPPO for MARL) but redirects it toward trustworthy deployment rather than cooperative performance alone. Methodologically, it departs from prior robustness benchmarks (RRLS, Robust Gymnasium, EIR-MAPPO) by evaluating across four real-world environments with diverse control modes and by systematically varying 15 hyperparameters across 82,620 experiments rather than testing a single algorithm under a fixed configuration.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that widely adopted practices—GAE, PopArt, and parameter sharing—can degrade robustness and resilience challenges default configurations in mainstream MARL codebases and provides practitioners with concrete, statistically validated guidance for trustworthy deployment. The released benchmark (adv_marl_benchmark) lowers the barrier for evaluating robustness and resilience in new MARL settings. Key limitations include the restriction to policy-gradient backbones (MADDPG, MAPPO, HAPPO) due to continuous-control requirements, and the GAE/PopArt findings may be environment-specific (effective in MuJoCo's dense-reward setting but harmful in sparse-reward real-world tasks). Open questions include whether these hyperparameter recommendations transfer to value-based MARL methods, whether the robustness/resilience distinction yields different algorithmic design principles, and how to jointly optimize for all three properties during training rather than post-hoc model selection.
