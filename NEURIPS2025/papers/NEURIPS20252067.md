## Problem

Reinforcement learning in continuous state-action spaces for scientific computing suffers from poor sample efficiency, lack of pathwise physical consistency, and weak theoretical guarantees. Standard model-free RL cannot encode physical laws or structural priors, while model-based RL requires explicit reward access or mid-trajectory re-planning capabilities that are unavailable when cost functionals are only accessible via expensive simulation. This paper seeks a fundamentally different formulation that embeds physics-informed inductive biases without explicit constraints.

## Input / Output

The method takes an initial state x₀ sampled from a continuous distribution ρ₀ (e.g., control points on a 2D surface boundary, coarse-grid function values, or molecular dihedral angles) and interacts with a black-box environment B that returns only scalar score evaluations g along the generated trajectory—no closed-form reward, no gradients, no mid-trajectory resets. The output is a neural network G_θ structured as Id + Δt·S∇g_θ (where S is the canonical symplectic matrix), which when iteratively applied produces a trajectory x₀, G(x₀), G²(x₀), … reaching a low-energy final configuration. The extended state x = (s, p) couples the physical state with an adjoint variable, doubling the effective dimensionality.

## Method

The core reformulation applies Pontryagin's Maximum Principle to the continuous-time control objective, yielding a differential dual (Hamiltonian) system where the policy is a symplectic gradient flow ẋ = S∇h(x) over the extended state-adjoint space. Instead of learning a value function, dfPO learns a score surrogate g_θ ≈ h (the reduced Hamiltonian) via supervised regression on on-policy samples, and the policy is recovered as G_θ = Id + Δt·S∇g_θ through automatic differentiation—this symplectic structure implicitly encodes physics priors. The algorithm proceeds stage-wise (Dijkstra-like): at each stage k, it collects N_k on-policy trajectories, trains g_θ_k to approximate both the true score g and the previous stage's score g_θ_{k-1} (preventing policy drift), and updates the operator. This locality enables pointwise convergence guarantees at each timestep—a property unavailable in standard RL—and yields a regret bound of O(K^{5/6}) under restricted hypothesis spaces, with the overall time complexity scaling as O(H²ε^{-6}).

## Results

On three scientific computing tasks, dfPO achieves the lowest final evaluation cost F(s) across all settings: 6.32 on Surface Modeling, 6.06 on Grid-based Modeling, and 53.34 on Molecular Dynamics (lower is better). The most dramatic margin is on Molecular Dynamics, where dfPO (53.34) outperforms the best baseline DDPG (68.20) by ~22%, and TRPO/PPO (1842.30) by over 34×. On Surface Modeling and Grid-based Modeling, dfPO leads the nearest competitors (CrossQ at 6.42 and S-TRPO at 6.48) by modest but consistent margins. All experiments use limited-sample conditions (100k steps for tasks 1–2, 5k steps for task 3) with 200 test episodes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

dfPO outperforms all 12 baselines (6 standard + 6 reward-shaped variants) on every task. Against TRPO, dfPO wins by a large margin on Molecular Dynamics (53.34 vs. 1842.28, a 34× gap) and by smaller margins on Surface (6.32 vs. 6.48) and Grid (6.06 vs. 7.10). PPO is the weakest baseline overall, failing to converge on Molecular Dynamics (1842.31) and performing poorly on Surface (20.61), likely due to its simplified trust-region update lacking physics-aligned inductive bias. SAC produces smooth learning curves but plateaus well above optimal values (7.41 on Surface, 1361.31 on Mol. Dyn.), attributed to entropy-regularization bias. DDPG is the strongest single baseline on Molecular Dynamics (68.20 reshaped) but still trails dfPO by ~22%. CrossQ and TQC are competitive on Surface Modeling (6.42 and 6.67 respectively) but degrade sharply on Molecular Dynamics (923.90 and 76.87). Reward-shaped variants (using r(s,a) = β⁻ᵗ(½‖a‖² − F(s))) consistently improve over their standard counterparts yet remain below dfPO, confirming that the differential structure itself—not just reward shaping—drives the performance gain.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the continuous-time optimal control lineage (Pontryagin's Maximum Principle, Hamiltonian mechanics) and the recent wave of continuous-time RL formulations (Wang et al. 2020, Jia & Zhou 2023, Zhao et al. 2023), but departs by using the continuous-time view solely as a derivation tool: it constructs the differential dual via PMP, then discretizes the dual to obtain a symplectic gradient-flow operator over the extended state-adjoint space. Unlike TRPO/PPO/SAC which optimize global value or Q-functions, dfPO learns a local score function g whose gradient defines the policy operator pointwise, making it structurally closer to a score-based generative model than to a standard policy-gradient method. The authors conjecture their g-function aligns with the Hamiltonian-based q-function of Jia & Zhou (2023), positioning dfPO as an iterative refinement procedure within that framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The pointwise convergence guarantee (Theorem 3.2) is a genuinely stronger property than the cumulative regret bounds available in standard RL, showing the learned policy is near-optimal at each timestep rather than only in aggregate. For scientific computing where simulation rollouts are expensive and physical consistency is critical, the implicit symplectic bias offers a principled alternative to ad-hoc reward shaping. However, the empirical validation is limited to three narrow tasks with small models (≤0.66 MB) and very short horizons (6–20 steps), and the surface-modeling gap over the best baseline (CrossQ) is marginal (6.32 vs 6.42). Open questions include whether the O(K^{5/6}) regret bound tightens with stronger smoothness assumptions, whether adaptive discretization can reduce the H²ε⁻⁶ training cost, and whether the framework extends to stochastic or partially-observed dynamics.
