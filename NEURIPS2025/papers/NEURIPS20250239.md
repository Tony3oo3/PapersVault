## Problem

Standard conditional mutual information (CMI) generalization bounds, long believed to be robust where mutual information bounds fail, were recently shown to become vacuous for carefully constructed stochastic convex optimization instances where the hypothesis dimension grows as Ω(n⁴ log n). This raised questions about the fundamental utility of information-theoretic bounds for generalization analysis. The paper demonstrates that these failures are not inherent to the CMI framework but stem from the uncontrolled dimensionality of the hypothesis space, and can be resolved by incorporating stochastic projection and lossy compression.

## Input / Output

The method takes a learning algorithm A: Z^n → W with W ∈ R^D, a random projection matrix Θ ∈ R^{D×d} (d ≪ D, chosen independently of the data), and a loss function ℓ: Z × W → R. It produces a compressed hypothesis Ŵ ∈ R^d via the Markov chain (S_n, Θ, W) → Θ^T W → Ŵ, along with a generalization error upper bound of the form inf over quantization kernels and projection distributions of E[√(2Δℓ/n) · CMI^Θ] + ε. The distortion criterion requires that the generalization gap between the original model W and the projected-back model ΘŴ is at most ε in expectation, and the bound's Δℓ term captures the squared loss difference between training and ghost samples evaluated at ΘŴ.

## Method

The core technique augments the CMI super-sample framework with two operations: (1) a stochastic Johnson-Lindenstrauss projection that maps the D-dimensional hypothesis W into a d-dimensional subspace Θ^T W, and (2) a lossy quantization step that adds independent noise to the projected model, yielding Ŵ with the Markov constraint P_{Ŵ|S_n,Θ,W} = P_{Ŵ|Θ^T W}. The key insight is that the CMI is computed on the low-dimensional compressed model Ŵ rather than the full W, so the dimension-dependent CMI term no longer blows up even when D = Ω(n⁴ log n); for the CLB counter-example, d = 1 suffices. The distortion from projecting back is controlled by the noise injection (analogous to lossy source coding) and bounded via concentration inequalities, yielding an additive ε term. The resulting Theorem 1 gives O(LR/√n) generalization bounds for the counter-examples where standard CMI bounds yield Θ(LR) with no n-decay, and O(ε) at optimal sample complexity. The same projection-quantization machinery is then applied to the memorization problem, showing that for any algorithm that memorizes training data, an auxiliary algorithm ΘŴ exists that no adversary can trace while maintaining arbitrarily close generalization error.

## Results

The paper's main quantitative contribution is a new CMI-based generalization bound (Theorem 1) that, when applied to the counter-examples of Attias et al. [2024] and Livni [2023], yields gen(μ, A) ≤ 8LR/√n for the CLB class and gen(μ, A) ≤ 8L_c R/√n for the CSL class, in sharp contrast to the classic CMI bound which gives Θ(LR) (independent of n). For ε-learner algorithms at optimal sample complexity N(ε,δ) = Θ((LR/ε)²), the new bound reduces to O(ε), accurately capturing the excess error. For generalized linear stochastic (non-convex) optimization problems, the bound decays as O(1/n^{1/4}), slower than the convex O(1/√n) rate but still non-vacuous.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not run empirical experiments; all comparisons are between theoretical bounds applied to the same counter-example problem instances. The classic CMI bound of Steinke and Zakynthinou [12] (Eq. 1) and its individual-sample variant [55, 56] both yield Θ(LR) for the CLB and CSL counter-examples of Attias et al. [2024], meaning the bound does not decay with the training set size n and fails to capture the actual O(ε) excess error of optimal algorithms. The new bound of Theorem 1, which incorporates stochastic projection (Johnson-Lindenstrauss) and lossy compression (quantization), reduces the bound to O(LR/√n) for the same instances, matching the correct rate. For the Livni [2023] counter-example where the single-datum MI bound becomes vacuous, the new CMI-based bound remains informative at O(1/√n). The improvement is achieved with a one-dimensional projection (d=1) for the convex cases, though d = Θ(√n) is needed for the broader generalized linear non-convex class.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the conditional mutual information (CMI) generalization framework of Steinke and Zakynthinou (2020) by incorporating stochastic projection (Johnson-Lindenstrauss) and lossy compression (quantization) as auxiliary tools, analogous to how Nadjahi et al. (2024) adapted slicing to the mutual information setting. The distortion criterion and rate-distortion formulation follow Sefidgaran et al. (2022), while the paper's primary motivation is to resolve specific counter-examples from Attias et al. (2024) and Livni (2023) that demonstrated classic CMI bounds become vacuous in over-parameterized stochastic convex optimization. The memorization analysis builds on the recall-game framework of Attias et al. (2024) but reaches a qualitatively different conclusion about the necessity of memorization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a constructive resolution to a recently raised concern that information-theoretic generalization bounds may be fundamentally inadequate for over-parameterized learning, showing the CMI framework can be repaired rather than abandoned. The result that memorization is not necessary for good generalization (Theorem 6) is a meaningful conceptual contribution to the ongoing debate about what drives generalization in machine learning. However, the analysis is confined to stochastic convex optimization and generalized linear problems; extending these techniques to non-convex deep learning settings remains an open question, and the bounds, while non-vacuous, are still worst-case and may be loose in practice.
