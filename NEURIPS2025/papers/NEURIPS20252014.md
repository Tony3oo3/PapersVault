## Problem

While LLMs achieve human-level language performance, it remains unclear which specific computational units (e.g., individual MLP neurons) encode hierarchical syntactic structures, and whether their processing mechanisms resemble those in the human brain. Prior work on LLM syntax (structural probes, attention analyses) and brain syntax (HFT, MEG) uses incompatible methodologies, precluding direct cross-system comparison of how syntactic hierarchies are represented.

## Input / Output

The method consumes two types of signals: (1) MLP neuron activations extracted from six LLMs (GPT-2, Gemma, Gemma 2, Llama 2, Llama 3.1, GLM-4) on concatenated Chinese/English four-syllable/word corpora, sampled on an explicit 4 Hz clock so that phrase (2 Hz) and sentence (1 Hz) boundaries fall within the Nyquist band; and (2) sEEG recordings from 26 native Chinese speakers listening to matched sentence and phrase corpora, analyzed via inter-trial phase coherence. Outputs include per-neuron and per-channel frequency spectra, binary labels identifying sentence-selective, phrase-selective, and dual-selective units, Structure Representational Dissimilarity Matrices (SRDMs) built from cosine similarities across six experimental conditions, and scalar model-brain alignment scores S(m,b) and model-region scores S(m,b_r) computed via Spearman correlation between SRDMs.

## Method

HFTP imposes a hierarchical frequency tagging scheme (4 Hz syllable/word → 2 Hz phrase → 1 Hz sentence) on both LLM activations and brain signals, then applies a unified permutation-based probe: 1000 randomizations of the structured input generate a null distribution, and units whose real FFT/ITPC amplitude at 1 or 2 Hz falls outside the 95% CI are flagged as syntactically significant. A word-order-randomized control isolates syntactic from purely lexical effects, and z-score deviation between experimental and control conditions further classifies neurons/channels as sentence-selective, phrase-selective, or dual-selective. For cross-system alignment, the method constructs SRDMs per computational unit (cosine similarity across six conditions in frequency space), then applies RSA (Spearman correlation) between model-layer SRDMs and brain-channel SRDMs, aggregating over the top-100 channels per layer to yield global and region-specific alignment scores. An orthogonal predictive-encoding analysis (ridge regression from layer-averaged model FFT coefficients to channel ITPC spectra, with 5-fold CV) confirms the RSA findings without relying on representational geometry.

## Results

Using SRDM-RSA alignment on the sentence corpus, GPT-2 achieves the highest overall model-brain similarity S(m,b) = 0.654 (left hemisphere), followed by Llama 2 (0.645), Gemma 2 (0.644), GLM-4 (0.630), Gemma (0.582), and Llama 3.1 (0.514). Left-hemisphere alignment consistently exceeds right-hemisphere alignment across all models (e.g., GPT-2: 0.654 L vs. 0.442 R). One-way ANOVAs confirm significant between-model differences at both sentence level (F = 59.74, p < 0.001) and phrase level (F = 12.10, p < 0.001), with a strongly left-lateralized effect concentrated in core language cortex (STG, MTG, IFG).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Rather than comparing against external baselines, the paper benchmarks six LLMs (GPT-2, Gemma, Gemma 2, Llama 2, Llama 3.1, GLM-4) against human sEEG data using a unified HFTP probe. GPT-2 achieves the highest brain alignment (S(m,b) = 0.654 L, sentence), while Llama 3.1 shows the lowest (0.514 L), a 21.5% drop from its predecessor Llama 2 (0.645 L). The model-upgrade comparison reveals divergent trends: Gemma 2 improves over Gemma (0.644 vs. 0.582 L, +10.7%), whereas Llama 3.1 degrades relative to Llama 2 (0.514 vs. 0.645 L, −20.3%), attributed to Llama 3.1's larger pretraining corpus emphasizing code and multilingual text that dilutes language-specific syntactic regularities. All models show stronger alignment with left-hemisphere language cortex (IFG, STG, A1) than with the right hemisphere, and the predictive-encoding control analysis (Tables 3–4) reproduces the same inter-model ordering, confirming method robustness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Ding et al.'s (2016) hierarchical frequency tagging (HFT) paradigm—originally designed for cortical electrophysiology—into the LLM domain by applying FFT-based frequency analysis to individual MLP neuron activations, creating a unified probe (HFTP) that operates on both systems. It departs from prior model-brain alignment work (Sun et al. 2020, Schrimpf et al. 2021, Caucheteux et al. 2021, Oota et al. 2023) that typically compares embedding-level representations using heterogeneous metrics, by instead targeting discrete computational units (neurons/channels) and isolating syntactic-specific frequency signatures (1 Hz sentence, 2 Hz phrase) with a shared permutation-test criterion. The structural probe lineage (Hewitt & Manning 2019, Linzen et al. 2016) informs the LLM side, while the ITPC-based sEEG analysis follows the HFT neurophysiology tradition (Ding et al. 2016, Sheng et al. 2019, Keitel et al. 2018).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

HFTP provides the first unified, neuron-wise tool for detecting and aligning syntactic structure representations across LLMs and the human brain, addressing a methodological gap where prior alignment studies used incompatible representation choices and metrics. The counterintuitive finding that Llama 3.1 shows substantially less brain alignment than Llama 2 (while Gemma 2 improves over Gemma) raises a critical open question: whether behavioral improvements in newer LLMs are driven by human-like or non-human-like computational mechanisms, with implications for how we interpret 'progress' in language modeling. Key limitations include Chinese-only sEEG data (26 participants), evaluation of only six models, and the speculative attribution of the Llama 3.1 drop to training-data composition rather than architectural change, leaving the causal mechanism unresolved.
