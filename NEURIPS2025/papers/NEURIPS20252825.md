## Problem

Existing theoretical analyses of transformer in-context learning focus almost exclusively on i.i.d. linear regression tasks, leaving open how transformers generalize when in-context examples are structured trajectories from a shared Markovian process. This paper asks whether a single-layer linear self-attention (LSA) transformer can actually realize the optimal solution for Markovian dynamical functions, and what the fundamental representational and computational limits are.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252825/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a prompt matrix Z_0 ∈ {0,1}^{(d+1)×(n+1)} where the first n columns are full binary Markov chain trajectories (length d+1) sharing a common transition kernel, and the last column is a query sequence with its final token masked to zero. The output is a scalar prediction for the query's next binary state. Theoretically, the paper also produces a closed-form global minimizer X* in an enlarged reparameterized space (dimension dm where m=(d+1)(d+2)/2) and characterizes the multilayer forward pass as iterated preconditioned updates on two groups of objectives R_1 and R_2.

## Method

The paper introduces a reparameterization φ(b,A)→X that maps the bilinear LSA parameters into a strictly convex objective f̃(X) in an enlarged space, yielding a closed-form global minimizer X*=H^{-1}b for length-2 Markov chains (Theorems 3.3–3.4). It then proves that inverting this reparameterization—recovering (b,A) from X*—is NP-hard via reduction from bilinear separability, establishing a fundamental realizability gap for 1-layer LSA on structured inputs (Theorem 3.5). For multilayer LSA, the paper shows (Theorem 4.1) that the forward pass within a specific parameter subspace is equivalent to preconditioned gradient descent on two multi-objective functions: R_1 captures standard squared-loss alignment plus cross-state interaction terms, while R_2 emphasizes the last parameter w_d with cubic or quadratic penalties depending on whether x_{j,d}=0. Empirically, a 10-layer LSA trained on 100 Markov chains of length 5 initially approaches the Pareto front but degrades at greater depths, and GPT-2 experiments confirm that deeper architectures progressively sharpen attention toward structurally similar sequences.

## Results

The paper is primarily theoretical, with key results being: (1) a closed-form global minimizer for 1-layer LSA on length-2 Markov chains (Theorems 3.3–3.4), (2) an NP-hardness proof for recovering transformer parameters from the reparameterized optimum (Theorem 3.5), and (3) a multi-objective optimization interpretation of multilayer LSA forward passes (Theorem 4.1). Empirically, 1-layer LSA achieves only ~0.55 accuracy on |S|=3 Markovian prompts (near random), while 3-layer LSA reaches 0.98. GPT-2 models (small and standard configurations) match or exceed SVM and logistic regression baselines on next-token prediction, while the reparameterized model's accuracy advantage over 1-layer LSA grows with sequence dimension d.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares GPT-2-based transformers (tiny: 64-dim/3-blocks, small: 128-dim/6-blocks, standard: 256-dim/12-blocks) against four classical baselines—logistic regression, linear regression, 3-Nearest Neighbors, and SVM—on next-token prediction for Markovian prompts (1280 prompts, 90% CI via 1000 bootstraps). The small and standard GPT-2 configurations achieve accuracy comparable to SVM and logistic regression and clearly outperform 3-NN, while the tiny configuration is slightly worse than its larger counterparts. Additionally, the analytically computed reparameterized linear model (least-squares upper bound) consistently outperforms the 1-layer LSA, with the accuracy gap widening as the Markov chain dimension d increases from 1 to 10, empirically confirming the NP-hardness-based realizability gap. For LSA depth ablation, 1-layer performs near random (~0.55 for |S|=3), 2-layer improves substantially, and 3-layer reaches 0.98, demonstrating that the multi-objective optimization interpretation (Theorem 4.1) requires sufficient depth to approximate the squared-loss minimization effectively.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two prior research lines: (1) ICL for linear regression with i.i.d. Gaussian inputs, where transformers are shown to implement (preconditioned) gradient descent [Ahn et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2024], and (2) transformers trained on Markovian data for single-sequence next-token prediction [Makkuva et al. 2024; Rajaraman et al. 2024; Nichani et al. 2024]. The key departure is that prior ICL work assumes i.i.d. inputs with sparse optimal solutions, while prior Markovian work restricts attention to local tokens within a single sequence. Here, each in-context example is a full Markov chain trajectory and attention operates across sequences, introducing dense temporal dependencies that fundamentally alter the loss landscape and parameter structure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The NP-hardness result (Theorem 3.5) reveals a fundamental representational gap: even when the global optimum is analytically characterized in an enlarged parameter space, recovering the corresponding 1-layer LSA parameters is computationally intractable due to dense bilinear coupling induced by Markovian dynamics. This challenges the implicit assumption that characterizing the optimal solution in a convex reparameterization guarantees architectural realizability. The multi-objective optimization interpretation for multilayer LSA (Theorem 4.1) extends the gradient-descent narrative beyond squared loss, offering a mechanistic explanation for why depth helps. Open questions include extending the analysis to higher-order Markov chains, nonlinear (softmax) attention, MLP blocks, and non-binary state spaces, all of which are explicitly noted as limitations.
