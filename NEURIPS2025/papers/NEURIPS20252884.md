## Problem

Decentralized federated learning (DFL) eliminates the central server, but its peer-to-peer structure has not been analyzed for insider collusion threats. Existing poisoning attacks assume external adversaries causing indiscriminate damage to all clients, leaving unexplored the scenario where a subset of legitimate participants selectively manipulates only the models they share with non-collaborators to gain a competitive performance advantage while still benefiting from the collaboration.

## Input / Output

Each selfish client receives the pre-aggregation local models from all non-selfish clients (as vectors of model parameters), its own pre-aggregation model, the aggregation rule in use, and a trade-off hyperparameter λ. The output is a distinct crafted shared model for each (selfish client j, non-selfish client i) pair, computed dimension-wise so that the kth parameter of the crafted model depends on the kth parameters of all received models. The crafted models are not uniform across recipients: each non-selfish client i receives a tailored set of shared models designed to steer its specific post-aggregation model to a per-client optimal target.

## Method

SelfishAttack formulates the attack as minimizing a weighted sum of two losses per non-selfish client: keeping its post-aggregation model close to its pre-aggregation model (utility) and driving it away from the model it would obtain without selfish clients (competitive advantage). Because the objective is non-differentiable with respect to shared models under most aggregation rules, the authors reparameterize over the post-aggregation model and solve a bounded quadratic program independently for each parameter dimension k, yielding a closed-form optimal target w_i*[k]. For FedAvg, m−1 selfish clients set their kth values to w_i*[k] and the last is solved algebraically; for Median, an edge case with even total clients is handled by adjusting one value to force the median exactly to w_i*[k]; for Trimmed-mean, selfish models are split into extreme values that get trimmed and tuned values that steer the remaining average. A loss-convergence-based timing mechanism (triggered when the decrease in average training loss over I rounds falls below ε·max_gap) determines when selfish clients begin sending crafted models rather than honest ones.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with 6 selfish and 14 non-selfish clients, SelfishAttack achieves accuracy gaps (MTAS − MTANS) of 15.0%, 11.9%, and 11.3% under FedAvg, Median, and Trimmed-mean, respectively, while maintaining selfish-client accuracy (MTAS) of 47.5%–59.7%. On FEMNIST, the gap reaches 12.8% under Median, and on Sent140 it reaches 15.2% under FedAvg. SelfishAttack also satisfies the utility goal: selfish clients' MTAS under SelfishAttack exceeds their accuracy when training only among themselves (Two Coalitions) in most settings, e.g., 54.3% vs. 50.5% on CIFAR-10 under Median.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SelfishAttack dominates both poisoning baselines across all three datasets and all three coordinate-wise aggregation rules. Against TrimAttack (Fang et al., USENIX Security 2020), SelfishAttack achieves a 3.5× larger gap on CIFAR-10 under FedAvg (15.0% vs. 4.3%) and a 10.8× larger gap under Median (11.9% vs. 1.1%); TrimAttack also severely degrades selfish clients' own accuracy (MTAS drops to 20.7% under FedAvg on CIFAR-10), whereas SelfishAttack preserves MTAS at 47.5%. Against GaussianAttack (Blanchard et al., NeurIPS 2017), the gap is negligible under robust rules (0.1% under Median on CIFAR-10) and GaussianAttack collapses MTAS to 9.8% under FedAvg, while SelfishAttack maintains 47.5%. The key structural advantage is that SelfishAttack preserves useful information from non-selfish clients in the aggregation (utility goal), so selfish clients' models remain strong, whereas both poisoning baselines indiscriminately corrupt the shared models, hurting all parties and yielding near-zero or negative gaps.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the established line of model poisoning attacks in federated learning (e.g., Gaussian attack, TrimAttack), which assume external adversaries seeking to indiscriminately degrade model performance. SelfishAttack instead introduces an internal, collusion-based threat where a subset of legitimate participants selectively manipulates only the models shared with non-selfish peers to widen a performance gap in their favor while still benefiting from collaboration. Theoretically, the paper builds on Byzantine-robust aggregation rules (Median, Trimmed-mean, Krum, FLTrust, FLAME, etc.) as attack targets but formulates the attack as a per-dimension bounded quadratic program with closed-form optimal solutions for coordinate-wise rules—a formulation absent from prior poisoning attack literature that typically relies on heuristic or gradient-based model crafting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals a previously unacknowledged vulnerability class in DFL: the absence of a central authority makes it difficult to detect subtle, targeted collusion that selectively degrades only non-participating clients. This has direct practical implications for competitive collaborative settings (hospitals, banks) where clients cannot agree on a central coordinator. Key open questions include designing defenses against SelfishAttack, extending optimality guarantees to non-coordinate-wise aggregation rules (Krum, FLAME), and adapting the attack to dynamic or unknown aggregation rules. The paper also notes that the attack requires knowledge of the aggregation rule and a colluding subset, limiting its immediate applicability but motivating future work on more adaptive variants.
