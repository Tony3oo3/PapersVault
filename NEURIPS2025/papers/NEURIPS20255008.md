## Problem

Existing methods for comparing deep network representations to brain activity either capture task-irrelevant geometric structure (RSA, CKA) or are confounded by accuracy differences and decision biases (Cohen's Kappa, error consistency). This leaves the field without a principled, task-relevant, accuracy-agnostic measure of how similarly two observers solve a classification problem on a trial-by-trial basis, making it difficult to determine whether more accurate or robust networks actually become more brain-like.

## Input / Output

The method takes high-dimensional neural representations (time-binned spike counts from 100 neurons in monkey V4/IT, or penultimate-layer activations from CNNs/ViT with dimensions ranging from 10³ to 10⁷) paired with class labels, and produces a single noise-corrected DVC correlation score per observer pair. Before decoding, representations are reduced via PCA to a common dimensionality (default 25 components) to stabilize LDA in the high-dimensional, few-sample regime. The DVC is computed per class pair and averaged across all pairs, yielding one scalar per observer pair.

## Method

For each pair of image classes, the method applies Linear Discriminant Analysis to the (PCA-reduced) neural representation to find the optimal decision axis, then projects each image's representation onto this axis to obtain a scalar decision variable (DV). The DVC between two observers is the Pearson correlation of their DVs across images, averaged over all class pairs. A split-half normalization procedure corrects for measurement noise by dividing the cross-observer correlation by the geometric mean of within-observer split-half reliabilities, yielding an unbiased estimate of the true underlying correlation. Unlike RSA, DVC is invariant to task-irrelevant representational structure; unlike Cohen's Kappa, it is invariant to decision criterion shifts and accuracy differences, isolating pure trial-by-trial consistency of the decision strategy.

## Results

Monkey-monkey DVC is high (0.57 for V4+IT combined; 0.63 for V4 alone), indicating strong trial-by-trial consistency across brains. Model-monkey DVC is consistently lower (0.29 ± 0.05 for 43 ImageNet-1k models) and shows a strong negative correlation with ImageNet-1k top-1 accuracy (Pearson r = −0.70, p = 2.28e-07). Adversarially trained models (0.27 ± 0.02) and data-rich models (0.24 ± 0.05) do not improve—and slightly worsen—DVC to the brain, while model-model DVC within the same architecture family is significantly higher than across families (p = 1.33e-56).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Cohen's Kappa (Geirhos et al. [13]), the paper shows that when both metrics use the same optimal linear decoder (5-fold cross-validated logistic regression), DVC and Cohen's Kappa are strongly correlated (Fig. 5b,c), yielding model-monkey Kappa of 0.13 ± 0.04 and model-model Kappa of 0.23 ± 0.07—consistent with DVC. However, the original [13] results (very low model-human, very high model-model Kappa) are shown to be artifacts of a biased probability-aggregation decoder and large accuracy gaps between observers; DVC is invariant to these decision biases while Cohen's Kappa is not (Fig. 5g). Against RSA (Kriegeskorte et al. [15]), Appendix E demonstrates that RSA is positively correlated with DVC on the real data and also reproduces the negative accuracy-alignment trend, but a controlled simulation (Fig. A.8e) shows RSA is confounded by task-irrelevant shared variance (it plateaus as irrelevant correlations grow), whereas DVC remains stable because it isolates only the task-relevant decision axis. Thus DVC wins on specificity to task-relevant dimensions, while RSA captures broader geometric structure; the two are complementary rather than directly competing.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper generalizes the decision variable correlation (DVC) metric from Sebastian & Geisler (2018), which was defined for binary behavioral choice data, to high-dimensional neural representations of both brains and deep networks. It positions itself as a task-relevant alternative to representation-level methods (RSA, CKA, CCA) that are blind to which dimensions matter for a specific task, and to behavior-level methods (Cohen's Kappa, I2n) that are confounded by accuracy differences and decoder bias. The paper explicitly departs from the prevailing hypothesis that increasing model accuracy, adversarial robustness, or training data scale improves brain-model alignment, showing instead a consistent negative relationship.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that DVC decreases with ImageNet-1k accuracy and is not rescued by adversarial training or larger datasets challenges a central assumption in the brain-model alignment literature and suggests that current training objectives may be driving representations away from biological ones in task-relevant dimensions. The paper also clarifies that prior Cohen's Kappa-based conclusions (e.g., model-model >> human-human consistency) were substantially inflated by decoder bias and accuracy mismatches, providing a more nuanced picture. Key limitations include reliance on only two macaque subjects and one primary neural dataset, and the method assumes optimal linear readouts, which may not capture nonlinear decision strategies. Open questions include whether ecologically motivated training or explicit noise modeling can close the model-brain gap, and how DVC at the behavioral and neural levels can be jointly decomposed.
