## Problem

State-of-the-art NMS latency attacks (Daedalus, Overload, Phantom Sponge, Beyond Phantom Sponge) have been accepted to top venues claiming they can dramatically slow object detection pipelines, yet prior evaluations tested only in narrow, non-deployment settings. This paper asks whether these attacks survive realistic deployment conditions—GPU execution, model quantization, format conversion, cross-version transfer, domain shift, and existing defenses—by systematically evaluating their practical impact.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251289/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

EVADE consumes 114 adversarial datasets (4 attacks × 5 YOLO versions × 3 datasets × 2 splits, plus genuine baselines) and evaluates them across 7 hardware platforms, 3 export formats (ONNX, CoreML, OpenVINO), 2 quantization schemes (FP16, INT8 PTQ), and 3 defenses (PDM-Pure, JPEG, max-detection threshold). The core output metric is the average number of bounding boxes surviving NMS (hardware-agnostic proxy for attack success), supplemented by measured NMS inference time in milliseconds and a binary risk classification (harmful/harmless) per attack-component pair. The 300-box maximum detection cap and per-model proposal caps (25k for YOLOv5, 8k for YOLOv8) are critical constraints that bound the achievable latency increase.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is the EVADE (Evaluate and Validate Adversarial Digital Examples) framework, a multi-axis evaluation protocol that tests each attack independently along seven deployment dimensions: hardware (GPU vs. CPU across 7 platforms), model family (YOLO v3/v5/v8/v11/v12), model size (nano through XL), export format, quantization, domain shift (COCO→BDD-100k→nuImages), and defenses. Rather than proposing a new attack or defense, EVADE reuses the four published attacks with their original hyperparameters and measures transferability by applying adversarial examples generated on one model to all others. The key empirical finding is that NMS latency attacks are effectively neutralized in practice: GPU-based NMS processes even 30k boxes in under 100 ms, attacks show zero transferability across model versions or sizes, universal patches fail under any domain shift, and all three defenses (active PDM-Pure purification, passive JPEG compression, and the simple max-detection=10 cap) reduce bounding box counts to near-genuine levels, rendering every attack 'harmless' in the final EVADE scorecard.

## Results

Using the EVADE framework, all four state-of-the-art NMS latency attacks (Daedalus, Overload, Phantom Sponge, Beyond Phantom Sponge) are rated 'harmless' under realistic deployment conditions. Overload achieves the highest score at 2/6 evaluations passed (model format and quantization transferability), while Daedalus, Phantom Sponge, and Beyond Phantom Sponge each pass only 1/6 or 1/7. Critically, no attack succeeds when NMS runs on GPU, none transfer across YOLO model versions (v3→v12) or sizes (nano→XL), universal patches fail under domain shift (COCO→BDD-100k→nuImages), and all attacks are neutralized by three tested defenses (PDM-Pure, JPEG compression, max-detection threshold of 10).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates four NMS latency attacks as baselines against realistic deployment conditions. Daedalus (Wang et al., IEEE Trans. Cybernetics 2021) passes only 1/6 criteria (model format transfer) and fails on GPU hardware, cross-version transfer (e.g., v8n→v11n drops from 247 to 7 boxes), cross-size transfer, and all three defenses. Overload (Chen et al., CVPR 2024) is the strongest attack, passing 2/6 (format and INT8 quantization transfer), but still fails on GPU, model family (v8n→v12n: 300→6 boxes), model size, and defenses (though it shows mild JPEG resistance at 55 boxes vs. 5–8 for others). Phantom Sponge (Shapira et al., WACV 2023) and Beyond Phantom Sponge (Schoof et al., 2024) both pass only 1/7 (format transfer) and additionally fail the domain-shift test: a universal patch optimized on COCO yields only 5–7 boxes on BDD-100k and nuImages (vs. 224 and 152 on COCO). All four attacks are fully neutralized by the max-detection threshold of 10, reducing bounding boxes to 5–8 regardless of attack.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a critical, deployment-realistic evaluation of the NMS latency attack literature (Daedalus, Overload, Phantom Sponge, Beyond Phantom Sponge), which has been accepted at top venues but tested only under idealized conditions (non-quantized, single model, no defenses, CPU-only). It departs from prior adversarial evaluation frameworks (ART, RobustBench) by introducing component-level deployment criteria—hardware platform, quantization, model export format, domain shift, and defenses—that prior NMS attack papers systematically omitted. The EVADE framework repurposes the general adversarial evaluation paradigm into a structured, multi-axis assessment designed to test attack practicality rather than merely attack existence.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that all four SOTA NMS latency attacks are ineffective under realistic deployment conditions challenges the perceived threat model for object detection systems and suggests the field has been overestimating the practical risk of these attacks. Open questions include whether new attack formulations could overcome the identified weaknesses (e.g., generating higher-confidence fake boxes, targeting NMS-free architectures like DETR or YOLOv10), and whether EVADE generalizes to other attack types and model families beyond YOLO. The paper also notes that even when defenses neutralize the latency impact, residual fake bounding boxes may degrade downstream tasks such as multi-object tracking, leaving a secondary risk unquantified.
