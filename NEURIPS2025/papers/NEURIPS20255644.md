## Problem

Existing multi-fidelity hyperparameter optimization methods assume a sufficiently large budget and aim to maximize asymptotic validation performance, ignoring the trade-off between computational cost and performance that matters to users with limited cloud credits or allocated compute time. This paper introduces cost-sensitive multi-fidelity HPO, where the goal is to maximize a user-defined utility function (balancing cost against performance) and automatically stop the optimization process near the utility maximum rather than at a fixed budget.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255644/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a discrete pool of hyperparameter configurations, a PFN-based learning-curve extrapolator (a 12-layer Transformer trained via transfer learning with LC mixup), a utility function U(b, ỹ_b) = ỹ_b − α(b/B)^c with user-specified penalty α and exponent c, a total budget B, and threshold hyperparameters (β, γ). It outputs the best hyperparameter configuration trained up to a specific epoch (not necessarily the final epoch T) and the budget b* at which the BO loop terminates via the adaptive stopping criterion. The utility function itself can be estimated from pairwise user preference data using a Bradley-Terry model, requiring only ~30 comparisons to recover simple functional forms.

## Method

CFBO introduces three coupled components. First, a utility-based Expected Improvement acquisition function that extrapolates remaining learning curves via the PFN, computes cumulative best performance at each future epoch, and selects both the configuration and the optimal increment Δt that maximizes expected utility improvement over the most recent (not maximum) utility value, since spent budget is irreversible. Second, an adaptive stopping criterion that compares an estimated normalized regret against a BetaCDF-shaped threshold δ_b = BetaCDF(p_b; β, β)^γ, where p_b is the probability of future utility improvement, smoothly interpolating between a fixed-threshold regret test and a pure probability-of-improvement test. Third, a transfer learning strategy using two-level LC mixup (dataset-level interpolation between benchmark LC matrices and configuration-level interpolation between individual learning curves) to generate infinite synthetic training examples for the PFN, improving early-stage extrapolation accuracy and preventing premature stopping.

## Results

On the real-world RoboFlow100 object detection benchmark, CFBO achieves the lowest normalized regret across all cost-sensitivity settings: 1.3±0.1 (α=0), 3.6±0.3 (α=2⁻⁶), and 5.7±0.3 (α=2⁻⁴), with corresponding ranks of 3.3, 2.9, and 1.4 out of 9 methods. On the PD1 benchmark (from ablation), the full CFBO pipeline reduces normalized regret from 6.0±0.6 (no components) to 0.9±0.0 at α=2⁻⁴, a ~6.7× improvement. Wall-clock time per BO step is 1.52s (LCBench), 0.78s (TaskSet), and 0.23s (PD1), only ~2–3× slower than ifBO while delivering substantially better utility trade-offs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CFBO outperforms all 8 baselines on the RoboFlow100 real-world benchmark across every cost-sensitivity level. The advantage grows with stronger cost penalties: at α=2⁻⁴, CFBO achieves 5.7±0.3 normalized regret versus 9.6±0.0 for the next-best transfer-learning baseline FSBO (a 41% reduction) and 9.8±0.5 for ifBO. Even the non-transfer variant CFBO-NT surpasses all non-transfer baselines (Random, BOHB, DEHB, DyHPO, DPL, ifBO) and beats FSBO in most PD1 settings. On conventional multi-fidelity HPO (α=0), CFBO is competitive with the strongest baselines (FSBO at 2.1, ifBO at 2.3) while achieving the best rank (3.3 vs. 3.9). The ablation on PD1 confirms that the adaptive stopping threshold contributes the largest single gain in cost-sensitive settings, reducing regret from 8.5 to 1.7 at α=2⁻², while the utility-based acquisition function provides the second-largest improvement (from 11.7 to 8.5 at the same setting).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends freeze-thaw Bayesian optimization (Swersky et al. 2014; ifBO, Rakotoarison et al. 2024) by replacing the standard performance-maximization objective with a user-defined utility function that captures the cost-performance trade-off. The acquisition function generalizes expected improvement from validation performance to expected improvement in utility, and the stopping criterion introduces an adaptive threshold that smoothly interpolates between fixed-threshold regret and pure probability-of-improvement criteria. For the surrogate, it builds on Prior-Data Fitted Networks (Müller et al. 2021; LC-PFN, Adriaensen et al. 2023) but adds a novel LC-mixup transfer learning strategy to improve sample efficiency, departing from ifBO's purely synthetic-prior training. The utility estimation via the Bradley-Terry model borrows from preference learning (Bai et al. 2022) but applies it to a new domain: eliciting a user's cost-performance trade-off rather than ranking model outputs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The utility-based formulation is practically relevant for cloud-computing and HPC users who face hard budget constraints and want automatic early stopping rather than running HPO to a fixed budget. The consistent outperformance across three benchmarks and diverse utility forms (linear, quadratic, square-root) with varying penalty strengths suggests the approach is robust to how users define their preferences. Key limitations include the restriction to pool-based (discrete) HPO, the non-differentiability of the MC-based acquisition (preventing gradient-based continuous optimization), and the single-task assumption that ignores parallel or sequential multi-task budget allocation. Open directions include extending to continuous hyperparameter spaces, integrating with context-optimization approaches like Tune-Tables, and handling multi-task or future-budget-aware settings.
