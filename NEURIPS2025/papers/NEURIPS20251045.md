## Problem

Most neural PDE solvers operate within a Newtonian framework, missing the conservation-law structure that Hamiltonian mechanics naturally encodes. While Hamiltonian Neural Networks have shown promise for discrete particle systems, extending them to infinite-dimensional PDEs requires approximating functionals (function-to-scalar maps) and computing their functional derivatives—capabilities that standard architectures lack. This paper bridges that gap by showing how kernel-integral parameterizations can serve as learnable Hamiltonian functionals whose gradients are well-behaved functional derivatives.

## Input / Output

The model receives a discretized PDE state u(x_i) at n grid points along with their spatial coordinates x_i, and produces a scalar Hamiltonian value H_θ[u] computed as a Riemann sum over all grid points. The functional derivative δH_θ/δu is then extracted via automatic differentiation with respect to the input function values, yielding a vector of the same length as the input grid. This derivative is multiplied by the (analytically known) symplectic operator J to obtain du/dt, which is integrated with a 2nd-order Adams-Bashforth scheme to produce the next state. Unlike standard neural operators that predict u^{t+1} directly, HNS predicts the instantaneous rate of change and relies on an external ODE integrator for temporal evolution.

## Method

The Hamiltonian functional is parameterized as an Integral Kernel Functional (IKF): H_θ[u] = ∫ κ_θ(x, u(x)) u(x) dx, approximated by a Riemann sum where the kernel κ_θ is a neural field (SIREN layers with FiLM conditioning on local or global input information). The Riesz representation theorem guarantees that a single kernel can approximate any linear functional, and the nonlinear extension via conditional kernels (where κ depends on u(x) or the full state vector u) empirically captures nonlinear Hamiltonians. Training is supervised on the functional derivative δH/δu (or equivalently du/dt) computed from ground-truth PDE data via finite differences, with the network's autograd output fitted to these labels. At inference, the forward pass computes H_θ[u], a backward pass extracts δH_θ/δu, and the symplectic operator J (implemented via finite differences) converts this to du/dt for ODE integration. The architecture shares weights across all spatial points, making it parameter-efficient, and the kernel's structure ensures that the learned gradient is a meaningful functional derivative rather than an arbitrary vector.

## Results

On 1D Advection, HNS achieves a rollout error of 0.0039±0.0008, outperforming FNO (0.83±0.17) and U-Net (0.40±0.29) by over two orders of magnitude, and even the derivative-predicting variants FNO(du/dt) (0.048) and U-Net(du/dt) (0.057) by ~12×. On 1D KdV, HNS reaches a correlation time of 151.1±3.0, exceeding FNO (68.0), U-Net (134.0), and their derivative variants (77.6, 113.3). On 2D Shallow Water Equations, HNS achieves rollout errors of 0.026 (Sines) and 0.021 (Pulse), matching U-Net on in-distribution Sines (0.010) but halving its error on out-of-distribution Pulse ICs (0.042). HNS also conserves the Hamiltonian with relative L2 errors of 0.0002 (Adv), 1.32 (KdV), 0.0015 (SWE-Sines), and 0.0003 (SWE-Pulse), far below all baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HNS decisively outperforms FNO [13] on both 1D PDEs: rollout error drops from 0.83 to 0.0039 on Advection (~213×) and correlation time increases from 68.0 to 151.1 on KdV (~2.2×). Against U-Net [25], HNS wins on both 1D tasks (0.0039 vs 0.40 rollout error on Adv; 151.1 vs 134.0 correlation time on KdV) but loses on in-distribution 2D SWE Sines (0.026 vs 0.010 rollout error), while winning on out-of-distribution Pulse ICs (0.021 vs 0.042). PINO [33] (physics-informed FNO) achieves 0.053/0.114 rollout error on Sines/Pulse, roughly 2× worse than HNS on both. Transolver [34] (attention-based) is the weakest baseline at 0.084/0.122, with HNS achieving ~3× lower error. The derivative-predicting variants of FNO and U-Net narrow the gap substantially on 1D Advection (0.048/0.057 vs HNS 0.0039) but still trail HNS by over 10×, and their Hamiltonian conservation is poor (errors of 6.75e8 and 5.76e6 on KdV vs HNS's 1.32).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Hamiltonian Neural Networks (HNNs) from finite-dimensional particle systems to infinite-dimensional PDE systems by representing the Hamiltonian as an integral kernel functional (IKF) parameterized by a neural field. The IKF framework builds directly on prior work in neural integral functionals [2], while the Hamiltonian structure generalizes the discrete HNN formulation [35] to functionals and their variational derivatives. Conceptually, the paper positions the IKF as a special case of neural operators (evaluated at a single output point), bridging the neural operator and neural field literatures, and grounds the linear functional approximation in the Riesz representation theorem. The key departure from prior HNN work is the use of automatic differentiation through the kernel integral to implicitly learn functional derivatives, enabling gradient-domain training for PDEs rather than next-state prediction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that embedding Hamiltonian structure as an inductive bias in neural PDE solvers yields improved energy conservation, longer temporal extrapolation, and better generalization to unseen initial conditions—properties that are difficult to achieve with standard next-state prediction architectures. A notable finding is that gradient-domain learning with conventional architectures (e.g., U-Net) actually degrades energy conservation, suggesting that the integral kernel functional architecture is specifically what enables meaningful functional derivatives. Key limitations include: the theory for nonlinear functionals remains underdeveloped, inference incurs overhead from backpropagation and quadrature, the method is restricted to Hamiltonian (conservative) systems and cannot handle dissipation, and scalability to high-dimensional or large-grid problems is a concern. Open questions include extending the framework to non-conservative PDEs, improving the theoretical guarantees for nonlinear functionals, and reducing inference cost.
