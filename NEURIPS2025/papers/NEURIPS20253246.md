## Problem

Gradient-based coreset selection methods (e.g., Craig, Crest) produce subsets whose induced loss landscapes are misaligned with the full dataset's landscape, a problem that becomes catastrophic under label noise or small subset budgets. Existing remedies that explicitly match Hessians are computationally prohibitive and still brittle to noisy curvature estimates. The paper asks whether one can achieve loss-landscape alignment through a cheaper, sampling-based mechanism that avoids direct second-order computation.

## Input / Output

At each training epoch, the method consumes the full training dataset, the current model weights (with Gaussian perturbations δ ~ N(0, σI) applied only to batch normalization layer parameters), and a subsampling budget R. It outputs a shadow coreset S_t of size P·m (where P is the number of independent subsampling rounds and m is the per-round selection size), which is then used for standard mini-batch SGD updates. The perturbation σ is selected via cross-validation from {0.1, 0.01, 0.001}, and 4 Monte Carlo samples are used per forward pass, adding at most one extra layer's memory overhead.

## Method

The core mechanism replaces the deterministic gradient-matching criterion of Craig (comparing ∇l_i(w_t) vs. ∇l_j(w_t)) with a posterior-sampling expectation: the coreset is selected to minimize E_δ[‖∇l_j(w_t+δ) − ∇l_i(w_t+δ)‖] over δ ~ N(0, σI), effectively averaging gradient information across a local neighborhood in weight space. This Monte Carlo smoothing provably bounds the Hessian difference between the coreset and full dataset (‖E‖ = O(√ε)) and the Newton-step discrepancy, without any explicit Hessian computation (Theorem 3.2). Computationally, the sampling is restricted to batch normalization layers rather than all weights, motivated by BN's role in controlling sharpness and by the observation that full-model perturbation is prohibitively expensive. The algorithm (Algorithm 1) performs P independent subsampling rounds per epoch to build a shadow dataset, then trains via standard mini-batch SGD on that shadow set, yielding a convergence rate of O(1/√(MRT)) under multiplicative noise—an improvement of O(1/√M) over prior coreset SGD bounds.

## Results

On large-scale benchmarks (SNLI, TinyImageNet, ImageNet-1k), the proposed posterior-sampling coreset method consistently outperforms both Random sampling and Crest (second-order coreset) across corruption levels from 0% to 50%. The most dramatic gain is on SNLI at 50% label corruption, where the method achieves 60.62% accuracy versus 53.16% for Random and 51.04% for Crest — a 7.5-point absolute improvement over the next best method. On ImageNet-1k at 0% corruption, Crest marginally edges out the proposed method (71.36% vs. 70.91%), but Crest requires 2× the training time (42h vs. 20h) and 3× the memory. At higher corruption (60–90% on CIFAR-10/100), the method maintains a clear advantage, e.g., 39.11% vs. 25.28% (Random) and 33.06% (Crest) on CIFAR-100 at 60% corruption.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against **Craig** (Mirzasoleiman et al., 2020), the proposed method produces substantially better gradient estimation (lower average gradient error, Figure 2 Left) and a loss landscape that more closely matches the full-data landscape (MSE 0.0662 vs. 0.0703 on MNIST/LeNet, Figure 1 Right); Craig is not included in the large-scale Table 1 due to OOM on TinyImageNet. Against **Crest** (Yang et al., 2023), the proposed method wins on 11 of 12 settings in Table 1, with the sole exception being ImageNet-1k at 0% corruption where Crest leads by 0.45 points but at 2× training time and 3× memory; the gap widens sharply under corruption (e.g., 7.5 points on SNLI at 50%). Against **Random sampling** (Okanovic et al., 2023), the method consistently outperforms across all datasets and corruption levels, with the advantage growing as corruption increases (e.g., 8.07 points on TinyImageNet at 50% corruption). Against **Glister** (Killamsetty et al., 2021b), comparisons appear in Figure 4 on MNIST/EMNIST/CIFAR-10/CIFAR-100 where the proposed method again leads, particularly at high corruption ratios where Glister and other gradient-matching baselines degrade sharply or fail to converge.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the gradient-matching coreset selection lineage (Craig, Grad-Match, Crest) by replacing deterministic gradient or Hessian comparisons with a stochastic posterior-sampling criterion. It draws on the random weight perturbation / randomized smoothing literature (Bisla et al., Duchi et al., Liu et al.) but repurposes it for subset selection rather than optimization, arguing that marginalizing over a local Gaussian posterior in weight space implicitly captures curvature information without explicit Hessian computation. The specific restriction of sampling to batch normalization layers departs from prior work that either perturbs all weights or only the final layer, motivated by recent findings on BN's role in controlling sharpness.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a concrete and previously underappreciated failure mode—loss landscape misalignment between coreset-induced and full-data surfaces—showing it degrades catastrophically under label noise (20–50% corruption). The theoretical contribution (provable Hessian alignment and an improved O(1/√M) convergence rate for multiplicative noise) gives the method a principled foundation beyond empirical tuning. However, the generalization guarantee is limited to vision and NLP tasks; the authors explicitly note that sound/video modalities are out of scope. An open question is whether the BN-layer-specific sampling choice can be theoretically justified rather than remaining empirically motivated.
