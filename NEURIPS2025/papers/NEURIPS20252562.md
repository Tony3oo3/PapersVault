## Problem

Most test-time adaptation (TTA) methods for vision-language models rely on backpropagation or iterative optimization, limiting scalability and real-time deployment, while simultaneously lacking explicit modeling of class-conditional feature distributions. This distributional gap leads to unstable decision boundaries and poor robustness under distribution shifts, since existing approaches define boundaries solely through text-based prototypes or similarity-score adjustments without capturing intra-class variation or inter-class confusion.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252562/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes unlabeled test images encoded by a frozen CLIP ViT-B/16 image encoder and class-name text prompts encoded by the CLIP text encoder, producing a K-way class probability distribution and argmax label for each image. In the online setting, samples arrive sequentially (batch size 1) and the model maintains fixed-size per-class knowledge banks (capacity L=16) of high-confidence features; in the transductive setting, the full test set is available at once (L=6). No source data, ground-truth labels, or gradient updates are required at any point.

## Method

ADAPT reframes TTA as a Gaussian Discriminant Analysis (GDA) inference problem, modeling each class-conditional feature distribution as a multivariate Gaussian with a shared covariance matrix estimated via a Bayesian ridge shrinkage estimator for numerical stability in high dimensions. Class means are initialized from CLIP text prototypes and progressively refined using high-confidence samples stored in fixed-size knowledge banks, with the current test sample excluded from mean updates to prevent error propagation. A regularized objective combines online negative log-likelihood, a CLIP-prior KL divergence term anchoring GDA parameters to pretrained semantics, and a knowledge-bank consistency term weighted by cosine similarity to historical samples. Minimizing this objective yields a closed-form, one-pass label estimator (Eq. 8) that fuses CLIP zero-shot logits, GDA discriminant scores, and memory-guided consistency—requiring no backpropagation, no iterative sub-optimization, and no task-specific hyperparameter tuning beyond the bank size L and prior strength β.

## Results

On the natural distribution shift task (Table 2), ADAPT achieves 66.53% average top-1 accuracy in the online setting, outperforming all backpropagation-free baselines (best: AWT at 65.81%) and all gradient-based methods (best: DPE at 65.93%). In the transductive setting, ADAPT further improves to 67.09%, surpassing the strongest transductive baseline Frolic (66.00%). On corruption robustness (ImageNet-C), ADAPT reaches 28.56% (online) and 30.29% (transductive) average accuracy, and on fine-grained categorization it achieves 70.76% (online) and 72.43% (transductive), outperforming all compared methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ADAPT outperforms all 26 compared baselines across the three evaluation tasks. In the online natural distribution shift setting, ADAPT (66.53% avg) beats the strongest backpropagation-free methods—AWT (65.81%), Dota (65.76%), and BCA (65.57%)—by 0.7–0.9%, and also surpasses the best gradient-based method DPE (65.93%) by 0.6%. On ImageNet-A specifically, ADAPT gains +2.19% over DPE and +2.18% over BCA, showing the largest advantage under severe domain shift. In the transductive setting, ADAPT (67.09%) exceeds Frolic (66.00%) by 1.09% and TransCLIP (61.36%) by 5.73%, despite not requiring full target-set access for its core estimation. On corruption robustness (ImageNet-C), ADAPT leads all online baselines by 0.22% over TDA (28.56% vs. 28.34%) and all transductive baselines by 3.22% over TransCLIP (30.29% vs. 27.07%). On fine-grained categorization, ADAPT (70.76% online) surpasses the best BP-free method TCA (68.69%) by 2.07% and the best BP-based method DMN (70.30%) by 0.46%, while in the transductive setting it edges out Frolic (72.43% vs. 71.10%) by 1.33%.

## Relation to Prior Work

ADAPT reframes test-time adaptation of VLMs as a Gaussian Discriminant Analysis (GDA) inference problem, building on the classical probabilistic classifier of Hastie & Tibshirani (1996) and the Bayesian ridge covariance estimator of Kubokawa & Srivastava (2008). It departs from the dominant prompt-tuning and adapter-tuning paradigms (TPT, DiffTPT, DPE) that require backpropagation, and from similarity-based training-free methods (TDA, DMN) that lack explicit class-conditional distribution modeling. By combining the distribution-aware modeling of transductive methods (TransCLIP, Frolic, StatA) with a fixed-size knowledge bank for online operation, ADAPT unifies both settings under a single closed-form, optimization-free framework that requires neither source data nor iterative updates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: ADAPT achieves SOTA across distribution shift, corruption, and fine-grained benchmarks while running 4× faster than iterative baselines with under 1 GB memory, making real-time and streaming deployment feasible. The shared-covariance Gaussian assumption, while effective, is a clear simplification that may fail on multi-modal or highly skewed class-conditional distributions; the authors acknowledge this and suggest Gaussian mixtures or non-parametric estimators as future directions. An open question is whether the knowledge bank's fixed-size, confidence-based selection strategy remains reliable under severe or non-stationary distribution drift, and whether the method extends naturally to open-vocabulary or multi-label settings beyond closed-set classification.
