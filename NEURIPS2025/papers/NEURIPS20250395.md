## Problem

Conventional GNNs propagate information via graph heat diffusion, which causes over-smoothing and information dilution in deep architectures, limiting their capacity for flexible reasoning on complex graph structures. Meanwhile, neuroscience has established that neural oscillatory synchronization (governed by Kuramoto-type dynamics) enables the brain to coordinate distributed activity while preserving discriminative information. This paper asks whether the mechanistic principles of brain rhythm synchronization can be transplanted into graph learning to replace diffusion-based message passing with a synchronization-driven regime that inherently resists over-smoothing.

## Input / Output

For brain rhythm identification (BRICK), the input is a time series of BOLD fMRI signals per brain region (e.g., 116 or 246 regions over 175–405 time points) combined with a structural connectivity matrix; the geometric scattering transform converts scalar BOLD values into multi-scale vector representations on a unit hypersphere before synchronization. For graph learning (BIG-NOS), the input is standard node feature embeddings and an adjacency matrix, and the output is node or graph class labels. In both settings, the model also produces a learned control pattern Y that encodes task-specific modulation of oscillator coupling, providing an interpretable spatial attention field distinct from standard graph attention weights.

## Method

The core contribution is a unified Kuramoto-based dynamical system where each node (brain region or graph vertex) is a coupled oscillator on a unit hypersphere, evolving via a vector-valued ODE that combines intrinsic frequency, neighbor coupling, and a task-adaptive feedback control signal derived from attending memory. The synchronization step proceeds in three stages per iteration: (1) aggregate weighted neighbor states plus the control signal, (2) project the result onto the tangent space of the hypersphere at the current oscillator state to enforce manifold constraints, and (3) perform forward Euler integration followed by renormalization. A trainable reweighting matrix S adaptively modulates coupling strengths, and the entire dynamics are shown to be a gradient flow of a Lyapunov energy functional that balances pairwise synchronization against task-driven alignment. Unlike diffusion-based GNNs that converge to indistinguishable node embeddings, the oscillatory dynamics produce partial synchronization that preserves high-frequency discriminative information, enabling stable performance even at 128 layers on Cora and Citeseer.

## Results

On the aggregate graph benchmarks (Table 3), BIG-NOS achieves the highest total average accuracy of 71.70%, substantially outperforming the next-best baseline GCNII (67.74%) by nearly 4 points, and leading on heterophilic graphs (69.18% vs. 62.01% for GCNII). On the large-scale ogbn-arxiv dataset, BIG-NOS reaches 0.86 accuracy, ranking second on the OGB leaderboard behind only BiGTex (0.89) and surpassing all standard GNN baselines (0.69–0.71). On brain data, BRICK consistently achieves the best cognitive-state decoding accuracy across HCP-Aging, HCP-Young Adults, and HCP-WM with statistically significant improvement (p < 0.001) over all seven GNN baselines. BIG-NOS also demonstrates robustness to depth, maintaining high classification accuracy up to 128 layers on Cora and Citeseer, whereas conventional GNNs degrade sharply.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BIG-NOS decisively outperforms all ten baselines on heterophilic graphs, achieving 69.18% average accuracy versus the next-best GCNII at 62.01% (a 7.2-point margin), with particularly large gains on Actor, Squirrel, and Chameleon where conventional message-passing GNNs (GCN, GAT, GraphSAGE) score below 45%. On homophilic graphs (Cora, Citeseer, Pubmed), BIG-NOS (76.76%) is competitive but trails GCNII (79.20%), GraphCON (78.61%), and GRAND (78.32%), indicating that diffusion-based and residual architectures retain an edge when neighborhood features are already aligned. On the large-scale ogbn-arxiv benchmark, BIG-NOS (0.86) surpasses all standard baselines (GCN 0.70, GIN 0.71, GAT 0.70, GCNII 0.71, GraphSAGE 0.71, SAN 0.69) and the OGB leaderboard entry SimTeG+TAPE+RevGAT (0.78), though it falls short of the top leaderboard model BiGTex (0.89). The Kuramoto-based KGNN baseline (51.23% heterophilic, 58.14% total) is notably weaker than BIG-NOS, suggesting that the attending-memory control mechanism and manifold-constrained dynamics provide a meaningful advantage over a plain Kuramoto formulation. On graph classification, BIG-NOS achieves the best ENZYMES accuracy (60.00 ± 4.28) and competitive PROTEINS accuracy (75.02 ± 2.61) with a minimal batch size of 1, outperforming all listed GNN baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Kuramoto model from its traditional role as a forward simulator of brain dynamics (Cabral et al., Capouskova et al.) into a learnable computational module for representation learning, and further generalizes it to arbitrary graph data. It builds on prior Kuramoto-based GNNs (KGNN [46], GraphCON [48]) but departs by introducing a task-driven 'attending memory' control term and geometric scattering transform for multi-frequency decomposition, moving beyond simple phase-coupling message passing. The heat-diffusion-to-oscillatory-synchronization framing positions BIG-NOS as a principled alternative to the standard graph convolution/message-passing paradigm, while the BRICK component draws on control theory and manifold-constrained dynamics to ground the model in neurobiological mechanism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a biologically interpretable alternative to diffusion-based GNNs that demonstrably resists over-smoothing up to 128 layers and achieves notably strong results on heterophilic graphs (~7 points over the next best baseline). However, the iterative solver introduces per-batch computational overhead that limits practical scalability, and the ogbn-arxiv result (0.86) trails the current leaderboard leader (0.89). Open questions include whether more efficient numerical solvers can close the efficiency gap, whether the oscillatory mechanism generalizes to other modalities (e.g., temporal graphs, multimodal data), and whether the neuroscience-inspired interpretability translates into actionable clinical insights beyond benchmark accuracy.
