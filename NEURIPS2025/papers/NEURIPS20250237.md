## Problem

Existing symbolic regression methods suffer from two structural deficiencies: expression tree representations fail to capture mathematical equivalences and break permutation invariance, inflating the search space with redundant states; and expression-level constraints (e.g., constant fitting, physical-law verification) are only applied as post-hoc reward penalties rather than as sampling-time priors, leading to sparse rewards and inefficient exploration in constrained scientific problems.

## Input / Output

The method consumes tabular input-output pairs (x_i, y_i) along with a predefined operator dictionary (grammar) that includes arithmetic operations, functions, a const operator for constant insertion, and a trans operator for sub-expression transplantation. It produces a closed-form symbolic expression f(x) represented internally as a directed acyclic graph with node features (operator type, arity) and typed edge features (operand relations such as base/exponent or +/− for unified sum/product nodes), with all constants optimized via BFGS nonlinear regression at evaluation time.

## Method

GSR encodes expressions as Expression Graphs (EGs), a DAG representation built by applying a handcrafted Term Rewriting System (TRS) to normalize expressions into canonical forms—unifying commutative/associative operations into single node types (Σ, Π) and aligning commutative operands—then converting to a shared-substructure DAG that provably yields a unique representation per equivalence class (Theorem 3.1). A Relational Graph Convolutional Network (R-GCN) with edge-type-dependent message passing encodes each EG into a prior probability matrix P over the operator dictionary and a scalar predicted reward r, thereby embedding expression-level constraint priors directly into the search. A hybrid neural-guided MCTS (hnMCTS) drives expression construction via an adaptive ε-UCB policy that stochastically switches between vanilla UCB (random rollouts) and PUCB (neural-prior-guided) selection, providing a warm-start phase and provable convergence even when the neural prior assigns zero probability to the optimal action (Theorem 3.2). The R-GCN is trained end-to-end by minimizing a combined reward-prediction and policy-matching loss over completed trajectories, with the ε threshold and complexity bound dynamically annealed during training.

## Results

On the copper interatomic potential energy task, GSR achieves the best test MAE of 2.63 meV/atom (train: 2.41) with complexity 24, outperforming MCTS (3.65 meV/atom, complexity 31), GP2 (2.70, complexity 28), GP1 (3.53, complexity 21), and CGCNN (3.09). On the transfer dataset (50% volumetric compression), GSR achieves the lowest transfer MAE of 34.15 meV/atom, compared to MCTS (36.31), GP2 (41.63), GP1 (43.32), and CGCNN (44.89). On the SRBench black-box dataset, GSR consistently achieves higher R² scores than most of the 21 baselines, and on the Feynman dataset it recovers a larger number of ground-truth expressions than the 14 baselines, with training times comparable to the pretrained TPSR method.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the genetic programming baselines from Hernandez et al. [16], GSR achieves a lower test MAE (2.63 vs. 2.70 for GP2) with lower complexity (24 vs. 28 nodes) and, critically, satisfies the electrostatic repulsion constraint f(r→0)=∞ that both GP1 and GP2 violate, making GSR's expression physically meaningful at short interatomic distances. Compared to CGCNN [30], GSR achieves a better test MAE (2.63 vs. 3.09 meV/atom) and a substantially better transfer MAE (34.15 vs. 44.89), indicating less overfitting and stronger generalization to shorter bond lengths. Against the vanilla MCTS baseline, GSR reduces test MAE from 3.65 to 2.63 meV/atom and transfer MAE from 36.31 to 34.15, demonstrating that the expression-level constraint priors encoded via R-GCN mitigate reward sparsity during random rollouts. On the SRBench black-box and Feynman benchmarks, GSR outperforms TPSR [10], uDSR [9], and DGSR [6] in R² score and ground-truth recovery rate, respectively, while matching TPSR's training time without requiring any pretraining or large auxiliary datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the MCTS-based symbolic regression line (Sun et al. 2023; Kamienny et al. 2023) and the neural-guided MCTS framework from AlphaGo (Silver et al. 2017), but departs from both in two key ways. First, it replaces the standard expression tree (ET) representation with expression graphs (EGs) constructed via a term-rewriting system (TRS) and DAG structure, which provably captures mathematical equivalences and preserves permutation invariance — a property that ET-based methods (DSR, TPSR, uDSR) fundamentally lack. Second, it introduces a hybrid neural-guided MCTS (hnMCTS) with an adaptive ε-UCB policy that blends vanilla MCTS random rollouts with R-GCN-guided PUCB selection, enabling expression-level constraint priors to be incorporated directly during sampling rather than only as post-hoc reward penalties.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses two structural weaknesses in SR that limit its applicability to real-world scientific problems: redundant state spaces that slow convergence, and sparse rewards from constraints that can only be evaluated on complete expressions. The EG representation with its uniqueness theorem and the hnMCTS convergence proof (which relaxes the P(s,a*)>0 requirement of pure neural-guided MCTS) provide clean theoretical grounding. The interatomic potential application demonstrates that physics-constrained SR can produce transferable, interpretable models that outperform both black-box GNNs and unconstrained GP methods. However, the TRS is handcrafted and limited to basic algebraic equivalences, leaving higher-order symmetries and domain-specific invariances unaddressed; extending to learned or automated rewriting rules remains an open challenge.
