## Problem

The successor representation (SR), a widely used proto-representation in RL, captures only transition dynamics and is reward-agnostic, limiting its ability to guide agents away from low-reward regions. The default representation (DR), a reward-aware analogue from the linearly solvable MDP literature, had been applied mainly in neuroscience but lacked efficient learning algorithms, eigenspectrum characterization, and a function-approximation extension. This paper provides the missing theoretical foundation and demonstrates empirical advantages of the DR over the SR in reward shaping, option discovery, exploration, and transfer.

## Input / Output

The method operates on tabular MDPs with a default policy π_d that assigns non-zero probability to all state-action pairs, negative rewards on all non-terminal states, and a KL-divergence penalty λ for deviating from π_d (γ=1 in the linearly solvable formulation). The DR matrix Z(s,s') encodes the expected reward-weighted visitation between states (Eq. 7: a sum over trajectories of P(τ)·exp(r(τ)/λ)), contrasting with the SR's transition-count weighting. Outputs include the full DR matrix, its eigenvectors for shaping/exploration, the optimal value function and policy via linear equations, and default features ζ(s) that enable policy computation under new terminal rewards without access to transition dynamics.

## Method

The paper derives a dynamic programming update Z_{k+1} = R^{-1} + R^{-1}P^{π_d}Z_k (Theorem 4.1) and proves convergence via a Neumann series argument, then converts it into a sample-based TD rule Z(s,j) ← Z(s,j) + α[Y − Z(s,j)] where the bootstrapping target Y = exp(r/λ)(1_{s=j} + Z(s',j)) for non-terminal states. Theorem 3.1 establishes that when rewards are constant, the SR and DR share eigenvectors with a monotone eigenvalue mapping, explaining when the two representations are equivalent. For function approximation, the paper defines default features ζ(s) = (Z_NN P_NT^{π_d} Φ)_s by decomposing terminal rewards as φ(s)ᵀw, yielding a TD update ζ(s) ← ζ(s) + α(exp(r/λ)ζ(s') − ζ(s)) that propagates features from terminal states through the reward and transition dynamics. A state-action extension (Eq. 8) generalizes the DR to action-dependent rewards, enabling direct optimal policy retrieval via π*(a|s) ∝ π_d(a|s)exp(q*(s,a)/λ) without requiring transition dynamics.

## Results

In count-based exploration, Sarsa+DR achieves 2,964 (±252) total undiscounted return on RiverSwim and 3,518 (±4,571) on SixArms, outperforming Sarsa+SR (1,206 and 1,066 respectively) by 2.5× and 3.3×. In reward shaping across four grid environments, DR-pot (potential-based shaping with the DR's top eigenvector) consistently achieves the highest undiscounted return, significantly outperforming SR-pot, SR-prior, and no-shaping baselines. In option discovery, RACE (DR-based eigenoptions) achieves higher average reward per step than CEO (SR-based) while maintaining comparable state visitation, and RACE+Q substantially outperforms CEO+Q in cumulative return.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the SR-based reward shaping baseline (SR-prior, Wang et al. ICML 2021), DR-pot achieves significantly higher undiscounted return in all four grid environments because the DR's top eigenvector encodes low-reward region locations, guiding the agent along the optimal path rather than the shortest sub-optimal one. In option discovery, RACE (DR-based) outperforms CEO (SR-based, Machado et al. JMLR 2023) in average reward per step and in combined RACE+Q vs. CEO+Q return, as CEO cannot distinguish high-reward from low-reward paths and learns sub-optimal routes first. In count-based exploration, Sarsa+DR beats Sarsa+SR (Machado et al. AAAI 2020) by 2.5× on RiverSwim (2,964 vs. 1,206) and 3.3× on SixArms (3,518 vs. 1,066), with the DR's norm serving as a more informative density model. In transfer learning, Default Features (DFs) outperform Successor Features (SFs, Barreto et al. NeurIPS 2017) because DFs directly compute the optimal policy under new terminal reward configurations, whereas SFs can only recover a policy as good as those used to compute them.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper takes the Default Representation (DR), originally introduced by Piray and Daw in a neuroscience context within the linearly solvable MDP framework, and provides the theoretical and algorithmic foundation that was previously missing: dynamic programming and TD learning methods with convergence guarantees, eigenspectrum characterization, and a function-approximation extension via default features. It positions the DR as a reward-aware generalization of the Successor Representation (SR), proving that the two share eigenvectors when rewards are constant (Theorem 3.1) while diverging meaningfully when rewards vary. The default features concept directly parallels successor features but incorporates non-terminal reward dynamics, and the state-action extension builds on Ringstrom et al.'s generalization of linearly solvable MDPs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The DR offers a principled way to incorporate reward structure into proto-representations, yielding qualitatively different (risk-aware) behavior in reward shaping, option discovery, and exploration compared to the reward-agnostic SR. However, the practical scope is currently narrow: all experiments are tabular and on small grid worlds, the DR suffers numerical instability from exponentiating negative rewards, and it performs identically to the SR when non-terminal rewards are constant. Key open questions include whether the DR scales to high-dimensional settings via neural networks, whether the flexibility tradeoff (transfer only when terminal rewards change, vs. any rewards for SR/SFs) is acceptable in practice, and how the DR's risk-aversion interacts with exploration in more complex environments.
