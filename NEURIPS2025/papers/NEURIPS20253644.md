## Problem

Standard temporal contrastive learning (CRL) fails in combinatorial reasoning domains because it overfits to instance-specific context (e.g., wall layouts in Sokoban) rather than capturing the temporal structure needed for planning. This causes trajectory representations to collapse into isolated clusters, rendering them useless as heuristics for search or greedy planning. The paper asks whether representations can be learned that provably discard such spurious context and instead encode the temporal dynamics that enable efficient combinatorial reasoning.

## Input / Output

The method takes discrete, structured state observations (grid configurations, cube face colorings, tile arrangements) and a goal state as input, operating under the assumption of known, deterministic transition dynamics. It produces k-dimensional state embeddings (64-dim in practice) whose pairwise distances serve as a planning heuristic. At inference time, the agent either greedily selects the neighbor minimizing representation distance to the goal (no search) or uses these distances as a heuristic within Best-First Search, yielding an action sequence that solves the puzzle.

## Method

CRTR modifies the negative sampling scheme of standard contrastive RL: instead of drawing all negatives from different trajectories, it repeats trajectory IDs within a batch (controlled by a 'repetition factor'), so that some negative pairs are two states from the same trajectory at different time points. This forces the encoder to distinguish temporally distant states sharing the same static context, preventing reliance on spurious features like wall layouts. Theoretically, the resulting InfoNCE objective is a lower bound on the conditional mutual information I(X; X⁺ | C), which decomposes to I(X; X⁺) − I(X⁺; C), effectively performing context removal without explicit adversarial training. Practically, the change is only a few lines of code relative to CRL (Algorithm 1), and the method requires no a priori knowledge of what constitutes context. The learned representations are then used as a metric space for planning, where greedy nearest-neighbor selection in embedding space can solve puzzles like Rubik's Cube without any explicit search.

## Results

CRTR achieves the highest or near-highest success rate across all five combinatorial domains (Sokoban, Rubik's Cube, 15-Puzzle, Lights Out, Digit Jumper) when used with BestFS, with the largest gains over CRL in Sokoban and Digit Jumper. Without any search, CRTR solves nearly all instances on 4 of 5 tasks within 6000 greedy steps, including 100% of Rubik's Cube configurations (average solution length 448.7 moves vs. 1830.3 for CRL). On 15-Puzzle, CRTR achieves an average no-search solution length of 82.4 moves, compared to 119.5 for CRL and 1054.3 for the supervised baseline. In A* search on Rubik's Cube (6000-node budget), CRTR outperforms CRL at every α value, e.g., 63% success rate vs. 54% at α=0 and 33% vs. 30% at α=400.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CRL [21], CRTR wins decisively on all five domains: it achieves substantially higher success rates with BestFS (Fig. 2), shorter no-search solution lengths (448.7 vs. 1830.3 on Rubik's Cube; 82.4 vs. 119.5 on 15-Puzzle), and higher A* success rates at every α value on Rubik's Cube (e.g., 63% vs. 54% at α=0). Against the supervised baseline [14], CRTR is strictly superior on Rubik's Cube (supervised achieves 0% success rate at all search budgets) and Lights Out, while being competitive on the remaining domains. Against DeepCubeA [1], CRTR is competitive on Rubik's Cube but is the only method that can solve arbitrary cube states without relying on an external search algorithm, achieving 100% solve rate with greedy planning alone. The paper also reports that CRTR fails on Sokoban without search (due to dead-ends) and does not generalize to Sudoku or 3-SAT in low-data regimes, where GNN-based methods reach ~90%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Contrastive Reinforcement Learning (CRL) by Eysenbach et al. [21], which uses InfoNCE to learn goal-conditioned state representations, and on temporal contrastive learning (CPC, TCN) for sequential data. The key departure is identifying a specific failure mode—overfitting to instance-specific context (e.g., wall layouts in Sokoban) that causes representations to collapse into per-trajectory clusters—and proposing in-trajectory negative sampling as a minimal fix. The theoretical analysis connects the method to conditional mutual information maximization (CCE [42]) and adversarial feature learning, while the practical algorithm requires only a one-line code change to standard CRL training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that appropriately learned metric representations can eliminate the need for explicit search in combinatorial reasoning, with the striking result that CRTR solves arbitrary Rubik's Cube states via greedy action selection alone (~400 moves, no search). However, the approach is limited to deterministic, fully observable, goal-conditioned settings—it fails on Sudoku (unknown goal) and is untested in stochastic or partially observable environments. Open questions include whether the method's data efficiency (requiring millions of gradient updates) can be improved, whether it extends to stochastic dynamics, and whether the emergent algorithmic behaviors (e.g., block-building) can be made more reliable or interpretable.
