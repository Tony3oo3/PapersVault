## Problem

Latent action models (LAMs) are widely used to infer action-relevant changes from unlabeled video, but it remains unclear whether the learned latents capture true control signals or merely compress all frame-to-frame differences, including exogenous noise. This paper provides the first rigorous theoretical analysis of what LAMs actually learn, identifying conditions under which latents faithfully represent actions versus noise, and revealing previously undocumented failure modes related to over-parameterization and deterministic data-collection policies.

## Input / Output

The linear LAM takes a pair of consecutive observation vectors (o, o') ∈ R^{d_o} × R^{d_o} (e.g., features from a pre-trained image encoder) and produces a low-dimensional latent z ∈ R^{d_z} with d_z ≪ d_o, plus a reconstruction ô' of the next observation. The transition model is additive: o' = o + q + ε, where q = Xa is the controllable change (action effect matrix X ∈ R^{d_o×d_a}, d_a ≪ d_o) and ε is exogenous noise. A small optional subset of samples carries action labels a for auxiliary prediction. The bottleneck is a continuous low-rank projection rather than vector quantization, which is what makes the analysis tractable.

## Method

The paper replaces the deep IDM and FDM networks in standard LAMs with linear layers (z = Co + Do', ô' = Ao + Bz), preserving the reconstruction objective ‖ô' − o'‖² while making the optimization analytically solvable. The central result (Proposition 4.1) shows that, when observations are uncorrelated with frame changes, the linear LAM loss reduces exactly to PCA on the covariance of q + ε, so the latent captures the top d_z principal components of the signal-plus-noise mixture. The paper then identifies an over-parameterization issue where o-information can leak into z, and proves (Proposition 4.3) that applying different random augmentations to the IDM and FDM inputs forces A = I and C + D = 0, cleanly removing observation information from the latent. A second key result (Proposition 4.4) proves that adding a small auxiliary action-prediction head (even with only 1% labeled data) biases the encoder D to be orthogonal to the noise subspace, so the latent encodes q while suppressing ε. The analysis also shows that deterministic data-collection policies let the FDM absorb action effects directly, starving the latent of action information, and that higher policy randomness improves latent quality.

## Results

On a synthetic 4×4 grid-world with a realistic CNN-based LAM (VQ bottleneck, UNet FDM), the paper validates its linear LAM theory through ablations. Under low noise with a uniform policy, the baseline LAM achieves a controllable loss of 0.781±0.079 and stochastic loss of 0.739±0.047. Data augmentation reduces controllable loss to 0.415±0.020 (a 47% improvement), while just 1% action-labeled data further reduces it to 0.295±0.011 (a 62% improvement over baseline). Switching from a uniform to a correlated (95% deterministic) policy degrades controllable loss to 1.997±0.022, confirming the theoretical prediction that deterministic data collection harms latent quality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical analysis paper without direct method-to-method baselines; instead, it validates its linear LAM theory against a more realistic CNN-based LAM on a controlled synthetic dataset. The key comparisons are ablation-style: (1) increasing noise from none to high degrades action information (controllable loss 0.624→1.046) while increasing noise capture (stochastic loss 0.739→0.607), confirming the PCA interpretation that latents encode whatever has the largest variance. (2) A correlated (deterministic) policy nearly triples controllable loss (0.781→1.997) compared to a uniform policy, validating the theoretical finding that FDM can absorb deterministic policy structure, leaving latent capacity for noise. (3) Data augmentation reduces controllable loss by 47% (0.781→0.415) and increases stochastic loss (0.739→0.898), confirming Proposition 4.3 that augmentation forces A=I and C+D=0, removing observation information from the latent. (4) Just 1% action labels reduce controllable loss by 62% (0.781→0.295) and push stochastic loss to 0.986, confirming Proposition 4.4 that auxiliary action prediction biases the encoder perpendicular to noise.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper provides the first rigorous theoretical analysis of Latent Action Models (LAMs), building on the modern LAM template established by LAPO (Schmidt and Jiang, 2023) and followed by Genie, LAPA, IGOR, Moto, and Go-1. By replacing deep networks with linear layers, the authors reduce LAM training to PCA on the mixture of controllable changes and exogenous noise (via the Eckart-Young-Mirsky theorem), a connection that had not been formally established despite the architectural similarity to linear autoencoders. The analysis also formalizes and justifies two practical techniques already appearing in the LAM literature—data augmentation (Chen et al., 2024b; Sun et al., 2024) and auxiliary action prediction (Nikulin et al., 2025)—by showing precisely what problem each resolves (over-parameterization and noise capture, respectively).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LAMs are increasingly central to embodied AI pre-training pipelines (Genie, LAPA, Go-1, GR001T N1), yet their learnability has been assumed rather than proven; this paper identifies concrete conditions under which latents capture true actions versus noise, and reveals a previously undocumented risk that deterministic data-collection policies (common in teleoperated robotics datasets) degrade latent quality. The practical guidance—prefer stochastic policies, apply data augmentation, and use even 1% action labels—is immediately actionable for teams building LAM-based systems. Key limitations include the reliance on a linear, additive-noise model for the main analysis, and the 'beyond linear' validation being conducted on 4×4 synthetic images rather than realistic video, leaving open whether the PCA-equivalence and policy-randomness findings fully transfer to high-dimensional, nonlinear settings.
