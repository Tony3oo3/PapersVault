## Problem

Classical zeroth-order (derivative-free) convex optimization requires O(d) function evaluations per gradient estimate, making it polynomially worse in dimension than first-order methods. Previous quantum applications of gradient estimation to convex optimization achieved only quadratic speedups in d, failing to match the dimension-independent rates of classical gradient descent. This paper asks whether quantum (sub)gradient estimation can close this gap entirely, using only noisy function evaluations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252834/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The algorithm's sole oracle access is a θ-approximate binary oracle U_f^(θ) that returns function values with additive error bounded by θ, where θ must scale as O(ε^5/d^4.5) (Euclidean) or O(ε^5/d^5) (non-Euclidean) for the nonsmooth case, and O(ε^4/d^3) for the smooth case. For white-box applications (SDP, LP, zero-sum games), the input additionally includes constraint matrices A_i, objective matrix C, and right-hand-side vector b, with the output being an ε-optimal dual solution satisfying feasibility and objective gap constraints. The per-iteration cost for SDP evaluation is O((mns+n^ω)log(1/ε)) via eigendecomposition, while LP evaluation drops to O(m√n) via quantum amplitude amplification.

## Method

The core mechanism is to estimate (sub)gradients using quantum phase estimation (Jordan's algorithm and its suppressed-bias variant from van Apeldoorn et al.) from O(log(d/ρ)) noisy function evaluations, then feed these estimates into classical iterative schemes: quantum projected subgradient method (QPSM) for ℓ₂, quantum mirror descent (QMD) for ℓ_p, and quantum mirror prox (QMP) for smooth ℓ_p problems. The key technical contribution is a robust convergence analysis that accommodates the bias and variance inherent in quantum gradient estimation, showing that averaging over iterates absorbs the per-step errors without degrading the overall rate. For the smooth setting, a suppressed-bias gradient estimator (Theorem 4.1) controls both E[‖k−∇f‖²] and ‖E[k]−∇f‖ simultaneously, enabling O~(LR²/ε) query complexity. For white-box SDP/LP solving, the dual SDP is reformulated as a nonsmooth eigenvalue minimization in ℓ₁-space, to which QMD applies directly, yielding O~((mns+n^ω)(r_p r_d/ε)²) gate complexity for SDPs and O~(m√n(r_p r_d/ε)²) for LPs.

## Results

The headline result is that zeroth-order quantum (sub)gradient methods, using only a noisy function evaluation oracle, achieve query complexities of O~((GR/ε)²) for nonsmooth convex optimization and O~(LR²/ε) for L-smooth optimization, exactly matching the first-order query complexities of classical projected subgradient, gradient descent, mirror descent, and mirror prox methods (up to polylogarithmic factors in d). Since classical zeroth-order gradient estimation requires Ω(d) function queries, this constitutes an exponential separation in dimension d between quantum and classical zeroth-order convex optimization. For the μ-PL condition, the quantum gradient descent achieves O~(L/μ) queries, again matching the classical first-order rate. In the white-box setting, the quantum mirror descent solver achieves O~((mns+n^ω)(r_p r_d/ε)²) for SDPs and O~(m√n(r_p r_d/ε)²) for LPs, and O~(m√n·ε^{-2}) for zero-sum games.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The core comparison is between quantum zeroth-order methods and classical first-order methods: the quantum algorithms match the classical first-order query complexities exactly (up to polylog factors), while classical zeroth-order methods would require an additional factor of d per gradient estimate, yielding an exponential gap in d. For SDP solving, the quantum mirror descent solver achieves O~((mns+n^ω)(r_p r_d/ε)²), providing a quadratic speedup in (r_p r_d/ε) over the classical matrix-multiplicative weights method of Arora and Kale [6] (which has (r_p r_d/ε)⁴ dependence), though the state-of-the-art quantum QMWU of van Apeldoorn and Gilyen [73] has superior m,n dependence (√ms vs. mns). For LPs, the O~(m√n(r_p r_d/ε)²) complexity compares favorably to the quantum IPM of Apers and Gribling [4] (O~(√mn^9.5)) in regimes where m ≫ n, but the classical IPM [23,77] at O~((m+n)^ω) remains competitive for well-conditioned instances. For zero-sum games, the O~(m√n·ε^{-2}) algorithm outperforms the best prior quantum methods [11,30] (O~(√(m+n)·ε^{-2.5}+ε^{-3})) in a specific parameter regime identified in Figure 1, where m is large relative to n and ε is not too small, but loses when ε^{-3} dominates or when m ≈ n.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the quantum gradient estimation framework of Jordan (2005) and Gilyen et al. (2019), and the quantum subgradient estimation of Chakrabarti et al. (2020) and van Apeldoorn et al. (2020), to show that zeroth-order quantum (sub)gradient methods can match the dimension-independent query complexities of classical first-order methods. It departs from prior quantum optimization work in two key ways: (1) previous applications of quantum gradient estimation to convex optimization [20, 75] achieved only quadratic speedups in dimension by targeting high-precision regimes, whereas this paper targets the low-precision regime where gradient methods are typically competitive; and (2) it extends the analysis from Euclidean to general ℓ_p-normed spaces via quantum mirror descent, dual averaging, and mirror prox. The white-box applications leverage the classical connection between SDP and eigenvalue optimization (Helmberg & Rendl, 2000) and the suppressed-bias gradient estimation of van Apeldoorn et al. (2023).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes the first dimension-independent query complexities (up to polylog factors) for zeroth-order convex optimization, demonstrating an exponential quantum-classical separation in the derivative-free setting — a regime where prior work had shown no quantum speedup when gradients are available (q≥1). The resulting quantum SDP, LP, and zero-sum game solvers achieve state-of-the-art complexity in certain parameter regimes (e.g., quadratic improvement in r_p·r_d/ε over classical MWU for dense SDPs). Key limitations include stringent oracle precision requirements (polylogarithmic in 1/ε), the absence of experimental validation due to hardware constraints, and the failure to match Nesterov's accelerated O(1/√ε) rates. Open questions include whether zeroth-order quantum methods can achieve accelerated convergence and whether precision requirements can be relaxed to make the algorithms practically competitive.
