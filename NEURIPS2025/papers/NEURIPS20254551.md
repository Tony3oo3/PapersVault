## Problem

Existing context-dependent choice models face a fundamental trade-off: featureless models capture halo effects but ignore rich alternative features, while feature-based neural models either restrict interactions to first-order terms or entangle all interaction orders through deeply nested nonlinearities, making it impossible to identify which order of interaction drives a given choice. No prior framework simultaneously handles features, captures arbitrary higher-order interactions, and provides principled, order-by-order interpretability of context effects.

## Input / Output

The model takes a choice set S of up to J alternatives, each described by a feature vector x_j ∈ R^{d_x}, arranged as a padded feature matrix X_S ∈ R^{d×J} with zero vectors for missing slots and a binary mask for numerical stability. It outputs per-alternative utility values u_j(X_S) on the extended reals R ∪ {−∞}, which are passed through softmax to yield choice probabilities. Beyond prediction, the model supports post-hoc extraction of relative context effect coefficients α_jk(T) for any subset T, enabling order-specific interpretation of how particular combinations of alternatives shift relative utility.

## Method

DeepHalo parameterizes the Batsell–Polking inclusion-exclusion decomposition of utility as a stack of residual layers, where layer l explicitly adds l-th-order interaction effects while preserving all lower-order terms via a skip connection. Each layer computes a permutation-equivariant context summary Z̄^l by symmetrically aggregating (averaging) the previous layer's representations across all alternatives, then modulates each alternative's frozen base embedding z_j^0 with this summary through H head-specific nonlinear transformations φ_h^l, yielding z_j^l = z_j^{l−1} + (1/H)Σ_h Z̄_h^l · φ_h^l(z_j^0). This design ensures that the l-th layer captures interactions up to order l, giving practitioners direct control over model complexity and interpretability. In the featureless (one-hot) specialization, the architecture reduces to a residual network with polynomial activations that provably serves as a universal approximator of context-dependent choice functions, requiring at most ⌈log₂(J−1)⌉ layers with quadratic activations to span all interaction orders. Training is standard supervised learning minimizing negative log-likelihood (or MSE on synthetic data) with Adam optimizer.

## Results

On the feature-based LPMC dataset, DeepHalo achieves a test NLL of 0.6430, outperforming the strongest baseline TCNet (0.6487) and FateNet (0.6774). On the larger Expedia dataset (275K transactions, up to 38 alternatives per choice set), DeepHalo reaches a test NLL of 2.5263, again beating TCNet (2.5340) and FateNet (2.5515). In the featureless setting, DeepHalo also achieves the lowest NLL across all three datasets (Hotel: 0.7483, SFOshop: 1.5263, SFOwork: 0.8066), surpassing CMNL which captures only first-order interactions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DeepHalo outperforms all eight baselines on both feature-based datasets. Against context-independent models (MNL, MLP, TasteNet, RUMnet), the gains are substantial: on LPMC, DeepHalo's test NLL of 0.6430 is roughly 0.04–0.22 lower than these baselines, confirming that context effects are critical for choice prediction. Among context-dependent baselines, DeepHalo beats FateNet (Pfannschmidt et al., 2022) by 0.0344 NLL on LPMC and 0.0252 on Expedia, and beats TCNet (Wang et al., 2023) by 0.0057 on LPMC and 0.0077 on Expedia. The gap over TCNet is smaller on Expedia, where the larger choice sets (up to 38 hotels) and richer features make the task harder for all models. On the featureless datasets, DeepHalo consistently beats CMNL (first-order only) by 0.008–0.042 NLL, demonstrating the value of modeling higher-order interactions. Notably, on SFOshop the NLL of DeepHalo constrained to second-order interactions (1.5339) is nearly identical to full-order DeepHalo (1.5331), suggesting real-world human choices are well-captured by low-order context effects.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Batsell-Polking (1985) inclusion-exclusion decomposition of context-dependent utility into a neural architecture that explicitly controls interaction order through a residual, layer-by-layer construction. It departs from prior neural choice models (TCNet, ResLogit, FateNet) that entangle all interaction orders through deeply nested nonlinearities or attention mechanisms, and from Pfannschmidt et al.'s FETA/FATE architectures by providing a principled decomposition where each layer captures a specific order of interaction. The permutation-equivariance framework from Deep Sets is adapted to maintain set-order invariance while enabling feature-based generalization beyond the classic one-hot (featureless) setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap in context-dependent choice modeling: existing methods either sacrifice interpretability (deep neural models) or expressiveness (first-order-only models like CMNL). The finding that second-order interactions nearly match full-order performance on real-world data (SFOshop: 1.5339 vs. 1.5331 NLL) suggests that human choice behavior may be largely low-order, which has implications for model complexity in deployment. Open questions include whether the specific residual parameterization is optimal, how the approach scales to very large choice sets (the polynomial-activation variant helps but is not fully explored empirically), and whether the interpretability gains translate to actionable insights in downstream applications like assortment optimization or LLM reward alignment.
