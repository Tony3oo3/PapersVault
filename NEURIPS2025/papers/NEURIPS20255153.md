## Problem

When a user asks a role-played deceased character about post-death events, the character's temporal knowledge boundary clashes with the question's temporal reference, creating what the authors term 'concept incongruence.' No prior work systematically defines this phenomenon, quantifies how models behave under such boundary clashes, or investigates whether the resulting inconsistencies stem from internal representation failures.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255153/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a role-play system prompt assigning one of 100 historical figures (died 1890–1993) and a time-based question (U.S. president by ordinal or by year, with the year varied ±30 from the character's death year). Because role-play responses contain character-specific expressions that defeat string matching, outputs are scored by GPT-4o-mini as a judge into three behavioral labels (abstain, answer, correct/incorrect) with >95% agreement to human annotators. The final outputs are aggregate behavioral metrics (abstention rate, answer rate, conditional accuracy) and probe-derived internal representation metrics (linear probe test accuracy, Spearman correlation, RMSE on temporal predictions).

## Method

The paper introduces three behavioral metrics—abstention rate, answer rate, and conditional accuracy—to quantify model behavior under temporal concept incongruence in role-play, then uses two families of probing experiments to locate the source of failures. First, linear classifiers trained on final-token hidden states test whether the model encodes a reliable dead/alive distinction and a precise death year; results show ROLE-PLAY prompts degrade the dead/alive probe to ~85% accuracy (vs. ~100% in NON-ROLE-PLAY) and no year-specific probe achieves reliable separation. Second, linear ridge-regression probes (following Gurnee & Tegmark's temporal representation framework) are trained on U.S. president and artwork-release-date questions to measure how role-play shifts the model's internal timeline; the key signature is high Spearman correlation paired with substantially increased RMSE, indicating role-play adds a systematic offset to temporal representations rather than reordering them. A restricted prompt that explicitly provides the death year and instructs the model to check temporal boundaries improves abstention by 66–75% but worsens the accuracy drop, confirming a fundamental tension between character immersion and factual temporal alignment.

## Results

In the NON-ROLE-PLAY setting, all models achieve 100% accuracy on U.S. president questions. Under ROLE-PLAY, conditional accuracy drops to 92.0% for Llama-3.1-8B, 92.0% for Gemma-2-9B, 99.6% for Claude-3.7-Sonnet, and 91.8% for GPT-4.1-nano. After-death abstention rates are far below the expected 100%: Llama abstains only 18.7% of the time, Claude 9.6%, Gemma 1.9%, and GPT 0.4%. Probing experiments reveal that Llama's temporal representation shifts under role-play (Spearman correlation drops from 0.996 to 0.974; RMSE increases from 2.6 to 10.8 years), and on generalized artwork-date questions, Llama's accuracy plummets from 85.0% to 38.6% (a 46.4% drop).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is each model's ROLE-PLAY behavior against its own NON-ROLE-PLAY baseline (100% accuracy, 0% abstention). Llama-3.1-8B suffers the largest behavioral deviation: an 81.3% deviation from expected abstention and an 8% accuracy drop (92.0% vs. 100%), with a 46.4% accuracy collapse on generalized temporal questions (85.0% → 38.6%). Gemma-2-9B shows a similar 8% accuracy drop on president questions (92.0%) and an 11.2% drop on artwork dates (93.0% → 81.8%), but rarely abstains (1.9% after death), approximating the 'always answer' behavior pattern. Claude-3.7-Sonnet is the most faithful to the expected behavior, with only a minimal accuracy drop (99.6%) and a 90.4% deviation from expected abstention (9.6% after death). GPT-4.1-nano never abstains (0.4% after death) and shows an 8.2% accuracy drop (91.8%), similar to Gemma. Across all models, the ROLE-PLAY setting consistently degrades temporal representation quality (higher RMSE, lower Spearman correlation) relative to NON-ROLE-PLAY, confirming that role-playing shifts internal temporal encodings rather than merely gating knowledge access.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on Gurnee & Tegmark's [12] finding that language models encode calendar time in linearly accessible representations, adapting their ridge-regression probing methodology to test whether role-playing preserves or distorts those temporal encodings. It positions itself within the role-playing LLM literature [28, 33, 42] and the knowledge-boundary evaluation work [2, 47], but departs by introducing 'concept incongruence' as a unifying three-level framework (prompt-level, prompt-vs-representation, representation-vs-representation) that reframes role-playing knowledge violations as a specific instance of I-B incongruence. The accuracy-drop finding connects to prior observations that personas degrade reasoning [11, 52], but the paper goes further by identifying the mechanism—shifts in internal temporal representations—rather than merely documenting the performance gap.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic quantification of how role-playing disrupts a model's internal temporal representations, revealing a fundamental trade-off: explicit specification improves abstention behavior but deepens the accuracy drop, suggesting the model cannot simultaneously optimize character immersion and factual precision. The three-level concept incongruence taxonomy offers a useful lens for diagnosing other under-specified behaviors (alignment faking, jailbreaking) beyond the time-and-death setting studied here. Key open questions include whether the 'correct' behavior is abstention or generous role-play, how to detect concept incongruence at inference time, and whether cross-modal incongruence (text-image-audio) exhibits analogous representation clashes. Limitations include the narrow focus on U.S. president questions and a single incongruence type (I-B), leaving the broader space of concept incongruence largely unexplored.
