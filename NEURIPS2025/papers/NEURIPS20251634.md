## Problem

Reinforcement learning for LLM reasoning wastes significant compute on questions that are too easy (model always succeeds) or too hard (model always fails), since these produce zero advantage and no gradient signal. No prior work provides a theoretical guarantee connecting question difficulty to expected policy improvement, nor a practical algorithm to algorithmically select optimally difficult questions during training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251634/cropped_page5_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

At each training step, LILO takes a candidate pool of |D| questions (typically 4× the batch size) and the current model parameters θ. It rolls out N_learnability = 8 attempts per candidate question to estimate empirical success rate p̂, computes learnability as p̂(1−p̂), and returns the top-|B| questions by learnability. These selected questions are then used for the actual training update with N_train rollouts (e.g., ~500 for VinePPO), so the 8 learnability-estimation samples are reused as part of the training batch, adding only ~4% overhead in large-N_train settings.

## Method

The paper proves Theorem 3.1, showing that for any advantage-based policy gradient algorithm (RLOO, GRPO, PPO, VinePPO), the expected policy improvement from one gradient step is upper bounded by the variance of the terminal reward (i.e., learnability) times a gradient-norm factor. Building on this, LILO implements a rejection-sampling curriculum: at every step it estimates learnability over a candidate pool, selects the highest-learnability questions, and trains only on those. For binary rewards, learnability equals p(1−p), which peaks at p = 0.5 — the 'frontier' where the model sometimes succeeds and sometimes fails. The method is implemented in fewer than 20 lines of code added to existing training libraries (OatLLM, VinePPO), and across three algorithms, three datasets, and two base models, it improves final test accuracy by 1.5–2.7 percentage points while reaching baseline-best performance in 1.5–3.3× fewer training steps.

## Results

LILO consistently improves final test accuracy across all three RL algorithms and three datasets: PPO on MATH improves from 19.1% to 21.8%, PPO on GSM8K from 51.1% to 53.2%, VinePPO on MATH from 22.8% to 24.9%, VinePPO on GSM8K from 53.2% to 55.9%, and GRPO on ORZ57K from 35.5% to 37.1%. The headline speed-up is up to 3.3× fewer training steps to reach the baseline's best test accuracy (VinePPO on GSM8K), with a typical range of 1.5×–3.3× across configurations. On out-of-distribution benchmarks (CollegeMath, OlympiadBench), LILO also improves generalisation, e.g. VinePPO+LILO reaches 28.8% on CollegeMath vs. 26.9% for VinePPO alone.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares LILO against three policy-gradient baselines, each evaluated with and without the learnability-prioritised data selection. Against PPO [Schulman et al., 2017], LILO wins on both MATH (+2.7 pp, 2.5× fewer steps) and GSM8K (+2.1 pp, 1.9× fewer steps). Against VinePPO [Kazemnejad et al., 2024], LILO again wins on both datasets: MATH (+2.1 pp, 3.2× fewer steps) and GSM8K (+2.7 pp, 3.3× fewer steps), with the largest absolute and relative gains in the paper. Against GRPO [Ramesh et al., 2024] on the larger ORZ57K dataset, LILO achieves a more modest +1.6 pp improvement with 1.5× fewer steps, and on the individual sub-benchmarks it improves 4 out of 5 (MATH, AMC, Minerva, OlympiadBench) while AIME remains roughly unchanged. In all five algorithm–dataset combinations, LILO strictly dominates the corresponding baseline in both final accuracy and training efficiency, with no configuration where the baseline outperforms the LILO variant.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges the unsupervised environment design (UED) and learnability literature (SFL, Florensa et al., Tzannetos et al.) with LLM reinforcement learning by proving that expected policy improvement is upper-bounded by the variance of terminal reward (learnability) for standard advantage-based policy gradient algorithms. It departs from SFL, which mixes high-learnability and randomly sampled levels, by training exclusively on high-learnability questions, and from prior ACL work by providing a theoretically grounded selection criterion rather than heuristics. The practical method (LILO) is a minimal rejection-sampling wrapper that can be added to existing training libraries in under 20 lines, positioning it as a drop-in enhancement to PPO, GRPO, and VinePPO rather than a new algorithm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 3× training speedup with a <20-line code addition is practically significant for the extremely compute-intensive RL post-training of reasoning LLMs, and the theoretical result gives practitioners a principled, model-adaptive criterion for data selection. However, the paper honestly surfaces important limitations: learnability does not close the train-test generalisation gap, finding learnable questions becomes progressively harder as the model improves, and the method fails to further improve already heavily post-trained models (Oat-Zero-1.5B). Open questions include whether learnability can guide the generation of new training data at the correct difficulty, whether it extends to non-binary rewards, and how to build fully open-ended curricula for LLM agents that autonomously expand the frontier of knowledge.
