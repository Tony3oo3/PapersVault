## Problem

While AI policy design depends on both rigorous evidence and scientific consensus, the field has mature mechanisms for evidence generation (academic research, industry reports) and a nascent mechanism for evidence synthesis (the International Scientific Report on AI), yet no established process exists for forming scientific consensus on AI policy. This paper identifies that void as a critical bottleneck preventing evidence-based AI policy from reaching its full potential.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251197/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The paper takes as its analytical framework the three-stage research-to-policy pipeline (evidence generation → evidence synthesis → scientific consensus) and the IPCC's multi-step assessment process as a structural template. It produces three concrete pilot proposals for NeurIPS—a standing year-round working group, a dedicated single-year conference track, and structured debates paired with pre/post-conference community surveys—along with two worked examples (evaluation selection and compute threshold design) illustrating where consensus would most directly unblock policy.

## Method

The argument rests on two pillars: NeurIPS uniquely satisfies both internal legitimacy (unparalleled convening power with 16,000+ attendees spanning disciplines and sectors) and external credibility (premier-venue reputation) required for a consensus process to be meaningful, and the IPCC provides a proven template for structured consensus through inclusive expert nomination, reasoned agreement without voting, calibrated uncertainty language, and a line-edited Summary for Policymakers. The paper translates these IPCC lessons into three pilots operating at different temporal granularities—year-round working group, submission-cycle track, and conference-event debates/surveys—so they can be implemented synergistically. It then grounds the proposal with two concrete topics where scientific expertise is the binding constraint and current literature reveals clear expert disagreement: which evaluations constitute state-of-the-art for regulatory purposes, and how to design compute or capability thresholds that reliably proxy for model risk.

## Results

This is a position paper with no empirical experiments or quantitative results. The paper's main contribution is a qualitative argument that NeurIPS should catalyze scientific consensus on AI policy, drawing on the IPCC process as a template. It proposes three concrete pilots: (i) a standing working group for year-round consensus formation, (ii) a dedicated NeurIPS track for consensus-oriented scholarship, and (iii) conference debates paired with pre/post-conference community surveys. Two substantive topics are identified as initial targets: evaluation selection (which evaluations constitute 'state of the art') and threshold design (e.g., compute thresholds for regulatory scoping).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare methods empirically but instead evaluates alternative institutions for consensus formation. It argues NeurIPS Pareto-dominates other AI conferences (FAccT, ICML, CVPR, ACL) on both legitimacy (broadest attendance across subareas) and credibility (highest prestige). The International Scientific Report on AI (led by Bengio, 96 experts, 30 nations) is positioned as the best evidence-synthesis mechanism but is too resource-intensive (months of work, dedicated admin staff) to also handle consensus formation. The AI summit series (UK 2023, Seoul 2024, Paris 2025) is deemed unsuitable due to entanglement with geopolitics, illustrated by the shift from Harris's 2023 'full spectrum of AI risk' framing to Vance's 2025 'AI opportunity' framing. The IASEAI is acknowledged as potentially promising but lacking NeurIPS's track record. The paper concludes no existing mechanism fills the consensus-formation void, making NeurIPS the strongest candidate.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper carves new ground by identifying a 'complete void' in scientific consensus formation for AI policy, distinguishing it from the existing mechanisms for evidence generation (academic publications, company reports) and evidence synthesis (the International Scientific Report on AI led by Bengio et al., 2025). It repurposes the IPCC's multi-step consensus process—legitimacy through inclusive expert selection, credibility through independent review, and impact through the Summary for Policymakers—as a template for AI policy, while acknowledging the IPCC's known limitation of potentially downplaying extreme risks. The paper also situates itself relative to the AI summit series (UK 2023, Seoul 2024, Paris 2025) and prior multi-author AI policy papers (Brundage et al., 2018; Bengio et al., 2024; Kapoor et al., 2024), arguing these address evidence synthesis or smaller-coalition consensus but not large-scale, structured consensus formation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a genuine structural gap in the AI policy ecosystem: while evidence is abundant (25,000+ NeurIPS submissions) and synthesis mechanisms exist, no process exists to distill expert agreement on technically grounded policy questions like evaluation selection and threshold design. The proposed pilots (standing working group, dedicated track, conference debates with pre/post surveys) are concrete and low-cost, making them actionable for NeurIPS leadership. Key open questions include whether the AI community is too fragmented on fundamental issues (e.g., AGI timelines, near-term vs. long-term risk prioritization) to achieve meaningful consensus, how to manage industry entanglement and external coercion, and whether a consensus-oriented process will inadvertently suppress important minority views on extreme risks—a tension the authors acknowledge by drawing on IPCC critiques.
