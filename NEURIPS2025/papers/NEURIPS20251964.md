## Problem

Pretrained vision-language models like CLIP degrade significantly under common image corruptions, yet existing test-time adaptation methods for VLMs largely overlook the quality of image embeddings themselves and lack theoretical understanding of why corruptions cause accuracy drops. This paper identifies a previously uncharacterized phenomenon—variance collapse, where both intra-class and inter-class embedding variances shrink as corruption severity increases—and provides a theoretical explanation linking this collapse to the visual encoder encoding corruption-related patterns that dilute class-discriminative features.

## Input / Output

Mint consumes corrupted RGB images in a streaming, online fashion with batch sizes as small as 1, alongside fixed text class descriptions (e.g., 'a photo of a {class}'). It produces a class label per image, updated LayerNorm parameters in the visual encoder, and dynamically adjusted text embeddings that blend the original text embeddings with cumulative image embedding means. A key design constraint is that the method must operate without access to the full test set or ground-truth labels, relying instead on pseudo-labels from the model's own predictions and information accumulated across previously seen batches.

## Method

Mint maximizes pseudo-label-based inter-class variance (PL-inter) on the fly by updating only LayerNorm parameters via a single gradient ascent step per batch. To handle the noise and bias inherent in small-batch estimation, it employs two accumulators: a mean accumulator that maintains running averages of global and per-class image embeddings (enabling stable PL-inter estimation even at batch size 1), and a gradient accumulator that averages update directions across batches to reduce gradient noise. The PL-inter objective is reparameterized as PL-total minus PL-intra variance, so the gradient direction for each sample is approximately the difference between its cumulative class mean and the global mean. Additionally, the cumulative class means are used to refine text embeddings via a Bayesian-style interpolation controlled by a prior-strength hyperparameter K_prior, which shifts weight from original text embeddings toward adapted image means as more samples are observed. After each batch, the image encoder and optimizer are reset to initial values while the accumulators persist, allowing cross-batch information to guide subsequent adaptation.

## Results

Mint achieves average accuracy of 71.0% on CIFAR-10-C (ViT-B/32), 44.1% on CIFAR-100-C (ViT-B/16), and 47.0% on ImageNet-C (ViT-L/14), all at corruption severity 5. These represent absolute gains of +12.0%, +8.3%, and +7.4% over the unadapted CLIP baselines (59.0%, 35.8%, 39.6% respectively). Mint also demonstrates strong robustness to batch size, maintaining near-peak performance even at batch size 1 (e.g., 70.5% on CIFAR-10-C vs. 71.0% at batch size 20). In terms of efficiency, Mint processes 10,000 images in 1m07s on CIFAR-100-C, roughly 47× faster than WATT-S (50m20s) while achieving higher accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Mint outperforms all nine baselines across all three model–dataset combinations. Against the strongest baseline WATT-S (NeurIPS'24), Mint gains +3.9% on CIFAR-10-C (71.0 vs. 67.1), +2.2% on CIFAR-100-C (44.1 vs. 41.9), and +3.1% on ImageNet-C (47.0 vs. 43.9). The second-strongest baseline TPS (WACV'25) is surpassed by +4.6%, +5.5%, and +3.9% respectively. Training-free and augmentation-based methods (VTE, Zero, Ensemble) trail Mint by 5–12 points, confirming that updating the image encoder's LayerNorm parameters is critical. Notably, CLIPArTT (WACV'25), which also adapts the image encoder, is outperformed by +6.6% on CIFAR-10-C and +6.3% on ImageNet-C, suggesting that Mint's cross-batch information sharing via accumulators provides a meaningful advantage over per-batch adaptation. Mint also runs ~47× faster than WATT-S (1m07s vs. 50m20s for 10,000 images on CIFAR-100-C), making it the only method that combines top accuracy with near-real-time efficiency.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the disentangled latent representation framework of Wiles et al. (ICLR 2022) to provide a theoretical account of why CLIP's image embeddings degrade under corruption, identifying a phenomenon they term 'variance collapse.' It departs from prior TTA-for-VLM methods that focus on text prompt tuning (TPT, TPS) or memory-based prediction adjustment (TDA, DMN-ZS) by instead targeting the image encoder's LayerNorm parameters to maximize pseudo-label inter-class variance, drawing on Fisher score and contrastive learning objectives. Unlike recent normalization-layer adaptation methods (WATT-S, CLIPArTT) that require large batches to align image-image or text-text similarities, Mint uses cumulative mean and gradient accumulators to operate effectively at batch size 1, filling a gap in online/streaming TTA settings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The variance collapse diagnosis (0.98 correlation between GT-inter variance and accuracy) gives practitioners a concrete, measurable signal for understanding and monitoring CLIP robustness failures, and the theoretical result that maximizing pseudo-label inter-class variance provably suppresses corruption-related features provides a principled justification for the adaptation objective. The method's striking efficiency (1m07s vs. 50m20s for WATT-S on CIFAR-100-C while achieving higher accuracy) makes it practical for deployment scenarios where compute is constrained. However, the analysis is limited to classification tasks and natural corruptions; extending the variance collapse framework to adversarial perturbations, dense prediction tasks, or non-CLIP architectures remains open, and the theoretical guarantees rely on idealized assumptions (binary classification, Rademacher noise, infinite sample size) that may not hold in practice.
