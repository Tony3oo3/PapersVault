## Problem

High-residual adaptive sampling methods for PINNs (e.g., R3, L∞) concentrate collocation points on the largest residuals while neglecting medium- and low-residual regions, yet no prior work provides a theoretical account of how this concentration interacts with model complexity to destabilize training. The paper shows that under heavy-tailed feature norms that emerge in deeper networks, high-residual sampling inflates the maximum Hessian eigenvalue far beyond the uniform-sampling case, imposing a much tighter upper bound on the learning rate and causing divergence at learning rates where uniform sampling remains stable.

## Input / Output

The method takes a PDE specification (nonlinear operator N_x, initial condition h(x), boundary condition g(x,t)) over a spatio-temporal domain Ω = X × [0,T], together with a fixed-size initial population of N_pde collocation points (1,000 in experiments). It outputs the trained PINN f_θ approximating the solution u(x,t) and, at each training step, an updated collocation population whose points have been displaced by Langevin dynamics rather than resampled from an estimated distribution. The residual exponent is fixed at k=2, and the Langevin step size τ and concentration parameter β are the only method-specific hyperparameters.

## Method

LAS replaces distribution-estimation-based resampling with iterative Langevin dynamics: at each inner iteration, every collocation point is updated as x_n^{l+1} = x_n^l + (τ/2)∇_x|R_θ(x_n^l)|² + β√τ z_n^l, where the gradient term pulls points toward high-residual regions and the Gaussian noise term prevents over-concentration. The stationary distribution of this Markov chain is p_∞(x) ∝ exp(|R_θ(x)|²/β²), which naturally weights high-residual areas while the noise injection biases the sampler toward flatter residual surfaces (Theorem 4.2), a property linked to more stable training dynamics analogous to SGD's preference for flat minima. Theoretically, the paper proves that under heavy-tailed feature norms (validated empirically via Pareto tail indices and Hill estimators) and N ∝ D scaling, the Hessian eigenvalue under high-residual sampling scales as D^{2/α} while the uniform-sampling eigenvalue scales as D^{2/α−1}, establishing a strictly tighter learning-rate bound (Theorem 3.1). In practice, a single Langevin step (l_L = 1) without re-initialization per epoch suffices, keeping computational cost comparable to R3 and RAD while avoiding the MCI instability that plagues RAD in dimensions ≥ 4.

## Results

On the 1D Allen-Cahn equation with 10 hidden layers, LAS achieves a relative L² error of 0.62±0.27, outperforming Random-R (11.49), RAD (1.36), R3 (34.47), and L∞ (10.95). In high-dimensional DF-Heat PDEs, LAS is the only method that produces valid solutions at 6D (3.49±0.20) and 8D (6.92±0.31), while all baselines fail with 100% relative error. At 4D, LAS achieves 1.72±0.23, beating R3 (2.15), L∞ (2.46), RAD (5.72), and Random-R (7.73). Across all 1D benchmark PDEs (Burgers', Convection, Allen-Cahn, KdV, Schrödinger), LAS consistently achieves the best or second-best relative L² error.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LAS outperforms all three baselines (RAD, R3, L∞) and Random-R across the majority of evaluated PDEs, with the most dramatic wins in high-dimensional settings. On the 1D Allen-Cahn equation at 10 layers, LAS (0.62) beats RAD (1.36) by 2.2×, R3 (34.47) by 55×, and L∞ (10.95) by 17.7×. In 4D DF-Heat, LAS (1.72) is the only method below 2.0, while R3 (2.15) and L∞ (2.46) are close but still inferior, and RAD (5.72) and Random-R (7.73) degrade substantially. At 6D and 8D, LAS is the sole method producing valid solutions (3.49 and 6.92 respectively), whereas RAD, R3, L∞, and Random-R all fail completely (100% error). On the 1D Schrödinger equation, LAS achieves the best 8-layer result (0.08) and is competitive at 10 layers (0.09 vs. Random-R's 0.11). The key differentiator is that LAS avoids the Monte Carlo integration instability that plagues RAD and R3 in high dimensions, and the noise injection that distinguishes it from L∞ prevents the sharp loss landscapes that cause L∞ to fail at higher model complexity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the adaptive sampling lineage for PINNs that began with importance sampling (Nabian et al. [31]) and was formalized through distribution-based methods like RAD (Wu et al. [47]) and high-residual methods like R3 (Daw et al. [10]) and L-infinity adversarial training (Wang et al. [43]). Its key conceptual departure is twofold: (1) it provides the first theoretical analysis linking sampling concentration to Hessian eigenvalue bounds and learning-rate stability, showing that extreme high-residual focus tightens the stability constraint as model complexity grows; and (2) it replaces both Monte Carlo integration–based resampling and pure gradient ascent with Langevin dynamics, injecting stochastic noise into the collocation-point update to favor flatter residual regions. This positions LAS as a principled middle ground between uniform sampling (β→∞) and single-point concentration (β→0).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theoretical result that high-residual sampling imposes a learning-rate upper bound that degrades with model depth challenges the prevailing heuristic in the PINN community of always concentrating on the largest residuals. Practically, the method's ability to produce stable solutions in 6D and 8D PDEs where all MCI-based and gradient-only baselines completely fail (100% relative L² error) suggests a path toward scalable PINN training for high-dimensional problems. Open questions include: whether the heavy-tailed feature-norm assumption (Assumption 3.2) holds universally across architectures and PDE classes, how to select the Langevin step size τ and concentration β without expensive tuning, and whether the single-iteration (l_L=1) setting that keeps compute comparable to R3/RAD remains sufficient for very stiff or multi-scale PDEs.
