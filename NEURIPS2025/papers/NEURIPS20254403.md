## Problem

Existing human-in-the-loop imitation learning methods treat the training data as a static, growing dataset and imitate the mixed agent-plus-human behavior distribution, which fails to adapt to the evolving quality of novice trajectories as the policy improves and risks learning suboptimal actions caused by human intervention latency. This paper addresses both issues by formulating learning from human intervention as an online non-convex dynamic regret minimization problem and deriving an unbiased objective that faithfully targets only the human expert policy.

## Input / Output

The method consumes sensory state vectors (MetaDrive) or bird's-eye-view RGB images (CARLA) as observations, paired with a mixture of agent-generated novice trajectories and human intervention trajectories collected in real time during training. It outputs a continuous driving policy (acceleration, braking, steering) extracted via weighted behavior cloning, where the weights are derived from a value function optimized against a proxy reward that up-weights expert-consistent states and down-weights novice-only deviations. The training loop is fully online: at each round, a sampled policy interacts with the environment, a human expert intervenes as needed, and all K ensemble learners are updated on their respective rolling windows.

## Method

FaithDaIL combines two core components. First, a faithful off-policy imitation objective reformulates the KL divergence from the agent policy to the expert policy by inserting the behavior distribution as an intermediate term, yielding a tractable Bellman-flow optimization with a proxy reward r̃(s,a) = −log[d^B(s,a)/d^H(s,a)] that acts as an importance weight to focus learning on expert-consistent behavior while still leveraging novice data for coverage. Second, the algorithm employs the FTPL-D+ ensemble framework for dynamic regret minimization: K novice policies each operate on a different rolling-window length τ_k = 2^{k−1} with exponential perturbation, and a Hedge meta-algorithm adaptively reweights the ensemble based on per-learner imitation losses, enabling the system to track the shifting data distribution as the agent's policy improves. This is, to the authors' knowledge, the first formulation of HIL imitation learning as online non-convex optimization with dynamic regret, distinguishing it from prior methods that minimize static regret over accumulated historical data.

## Results

On MetaDrive-Keyboard, FaithDaIL achieves the highest episodic return (354.35±3.43) and success rate (0.91±0.04) among all HIL methods, with the lowest episodic safety cost (1.47±0.28), while using the least human intervention data (4.8K steps, only 16% of total). On CARLA-Wheel, it attains the best route completion (0.95±0.02) and success rate (0.91±0.03) with 4.2K human data steps. Notably, FaithDaIL approaches the human expert's performance (return 374.73, success 0.98 in MetaDrive; route completion 0.99, success 1.0 in CARLA) while requiring far fewer human interventions than all competing HIL baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FaithDaIL outperforms all HIL baselines on both benchmarks. Against the strongest HIL competitor PVP, FaithDaIL improves MetaDrive success rate from 0.85 to 0.91 and safety cost from 2.51 to 1.47, while using 31% less human data (4.8K vs. 7.0K); in CARLA, it raises success rate from 0.73 to 0.91 and route completion from 0.92 to 0.95. IWR and HG-DAgger lag substantially behind, with success rates of 0.75 and 0.59 in MetaDrive respectively, due to their inability to handle early suboptimal trajectories and limited human data. HACO underperforms most severely (0.26 success in MetaDrive, 0.40 in CARLA), likely due to inaccurate value estimation in its off-policy RL formulation. Standard RL baselines (PPO, TD3) require 1M training steps and still trail FaithDaIL in success rate (0.76/0.58 vs. 0.91 in MetaDrive), while in CARLA they fail entirely (0.00 success). Behavior cloning on 30K expert demonstrations performs poorly (0.12 success in MetaDrive), confirming that HIL with agent-generated data is far more sample-efficient than pure expert cloning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of off-policy distribution-matching imitation learning (DICE/ODICE family) and online non-convex learning (FTPL-D+), applying both to the human-in-the-loop (HIL) setting. Prior HIL methods (HG-DAgger, IWR, HACO, PVP) either minimize static regret over accumulated data or imitate the mixed behavior distribution directly, which conflates expert and novice actions. FaithDaIL departs by (1) reformulating HIL learning as dynamic regret minimization to track the evolving novice policy distribution, and (2) introducing a proxy reward from the behavior-to-expert distribution ratio that yields an unbiased imitation objective targeting only the expert policy, thereby avoiding error propagation from intervention-latency deviations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is meaningful for safety-critical HIL deployment: FaithDaIL achieves the best safety and success metrics while requiring the least human intervention data (4.8K steps, ~16% of total), reducing the costly human supervision burden. However, the evaluation is limited to two driving simulators with only three human participants, and the paper acknowledges the absence of theoretical guarantees on imitation capability under dynamic regret. Open questions include generalization to physical platforms (robotic arms), robustness to diverse or suboptimal human intervention styles, and whether the FTPL-D+ ensemble overhead is justified in settings where the policy distribution shifts slowly.
