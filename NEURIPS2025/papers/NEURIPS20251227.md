## Problem

Many proposed generalization bounds for contemporary learning algorithms (e.g., deep neural networks) are empirically vacuous, yet the precise conditions under which tight bounds can or cannot exist for a given algorithm-distribution pair remained unresolved. While Gastpar et al. (2024) showed distribution-free bounds fail in the under-sampled regime, the general question of which algorithm-distribution collections admit tight generalization bounds was open.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a deterministic learning rule A mapping m labeled examples to a hypothesis, a collection of population distributions D, and a sample size m, and produces conditions (necessary, sufficient, or both) for the existence of a tight generalization bound. The key quantitative output is an estimability characterization: whether there exists a function E(S) of the training set that predicts the population loss L_D(A(S)) within precision ε with confidence δ. The necessary-and-sufficient condition (Proposition 5.2) reduces to checking whether the conditional variance E[var(L_D(A(S)) | S)] is small, a quantity that can be estimated empirically from a single training run.

## Method

The paper establishes three complementary results. First, two inestimability theorems show that algorithms with inductive bias toward nearly-orthogonal function classes (including subsets of VC classes when m ≤ O(√d)) cannot have tight bounds, using the Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma to construct such classes and a technical lemma relating the number of interpolating hypotheses to estimation error. Second, a positive result (Theorem 4.3) proves that loss-stable or hypothesis-stable algorithms are uniformly estimable, with the estimator being the empirical loss on a held-out subset of the training data; this identifies the 'correct' notion of stability for estimability, distinct from leave-one-out stability. Third, Proposition 5.2 gives a clean necessary-and-sufficient condition: ℓ₂-estimability holds if and only if the expected conditional variance of the population loss given the training set is bounded. Preliminary experiments on one-hidden-layer perceptrons trained with SGD on MNIST, FashionMNIST, and CIFAR-10 confirm that neural networks exhibit substantial hypothesis stability, supporting the practical applicability of the positive result.

## Results

The paper's main theoretical contributions are: (1) Theorems 3.1 and 3.2 show that any algorithm with an inductive bias toward a VC class of dimension d (with m ≤ √d/10) or toward a set of 2^m+1 nearly-orthogonal functions is not (1/4−o(1), ~1/6, m)-estimable on average; (2) Theorem 4.3 shows that (α,β,m,k)-loss-stable or hypothesis-stable algorithms are (α₀+α₁, β₀+β₁, m)-uniformly estimable; (3) Proposition 5.2 gives a necessary and sufficient condition: A is (ε,m)-estimable in ℓ₂ iff E[var(L_D(A(S)) | S)] ≤ ε. Empirically, one-hidden-layer perceptrons (512 neurons, SGD) trained on MNIST, FashionMNIST, CIFAR-10, and CIFAR-10 with random labels show hypothesis agreement (stability) consistently exceeding test accuracy, with agreement ranging from 99% (MNIST) down to 21–38% (CIFAR-10 random labels), and increasing with network width.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is primarily a theory paper, so it does not benchmark against competing generalization-bound methods in the usual sense. Instead, it positions its stability-based estimability guarantee (Theorem 4.3) against the more common leave-one-out stability of Bousquet & Elisseeff (2002), showing that the memorization algorithm (Example 1.6) is estimable and stable under the paper's definitions but fails the leave-one-out criterion. Compared to the prior inestimability results of Gastpar et al. (2024, ICLR 2024), which required abstract total-variation conditions or exact algebraic structure (parity functions), Theorems 3.1 and 3.2 give concrete combinatorial/geometric conditions (VC dimension, near-orthogonality) that are easier to verify. Relative to Nagarajan & Kolter (2019) and Bartlett & Long (2021), which preclude tight bounds only for specific distributions (Gaussian, per-sample) and specific SGD variants, the present results apply to arbitrary distributions and a broad class of algorithms. The empirical section validates that the stability condition in Theorem 4.3 is empirically satisfied by simple neural networks, with agreement exceeding test accuracy across all four datasets, suggesting the stability-based bound would be non-vacuous in practice.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends the estimability framework introduced by Gastpar et al. (2024, ICLR), which first formalized the question of when tight generalization bounds exist for specific (algorithm, distribution) pairs. While Gastpar et al. provided an abstract total-variation condition and results for exactly-orthogonal parity functions, this paper replaces those with concrete combinatorial and geometric conditions (VC dimension, nearly-orthogonal function classes) and adds the first positive results showing that loss/hypothesis stability guarantees estimability. The technical toolkit also expands, incorporating the Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma and linear programming duality to prove inestimability for broader function classes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that any finite VC class admits inestimable algorithms is a meaningful negative finding: it implies that for any neural network architecture, some training algorithms will be fundamentally incapable of admitting tight generalization bounds, even under realizability. The stability-based positive result (Theorem 4.3) provides a practical pathway — if an algorithm is loss-stable, tight bounds exist — and the preliminary experiments suggest neural networks trained with SGD are indeed quite stable. Open questions include whether inestimability results extend to larger sample sizes (beyond m ≤ O(√d)), whether simple necessary-and-sufficient conditions exist for all-distribution estimability, and how the framework extends to randomized algorithms.
