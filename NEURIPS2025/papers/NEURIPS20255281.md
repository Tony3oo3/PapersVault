## Problem

Existing domain reweighting algorithms for LLM pretraining operate in a multi-source-single-target paradigm, either optimizing for one specific task (causing overfitting) or averaging across tasks (failing under dominant or conflicting gradients). No principled method existed to simultaneously calibrate pretraining data mixtures for robust performance across multiple heterogeneous target tasks while adapting to their evolving learning difficulties during training.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pretraining corpus partitioned into K source domains (e.g., 7 SlimPajama collections or 20 ClimbLab semantic clusters) and N target task validation sets (e.g., 6 reasoning benchmarks or 8 low-resource languages), along with the current model parameters. It produces two coupled weight vectors updated periodically every ΔT steps: domain weights α ∈ Δ^K governing the sampling distribution over source domains, and task weights z ∈ Δ^N encoding the relative priority of each target task. The RoI metric normalizes one-step loss improvement by current loss, making progress assessment scale-invariant across tasks with different intrinsic difficulty levels.

## Method

GRAPE formulates a regularized minimax optimization where the inner maximization applies Group DRO to assign higher task weights z to tasks exhibiting the lowest Rate-of-Improvement (RoI), and the outer minimization optimizes domain weights α to maximize expected loss reduction under that worst-case task distribution. Solving this via Bregman-divergence-regularized mirror descent yields multiplicative update rules: task weights increase exponentially for tasks whose normalized gradients (∇log l_n) have low alignment with the current update direction, while domain weights increase for domains whose training gradients align well with the prioritized struggling tasks. This creates a negative feedback loop that continuously redirects training focus toward underperforming tasks by upweighting relevant source domains, implementing a dynamic curriculum without requiring auxiliary proxy models or complex gradient surgery. Theoretical analysis establishes O(1/T) convergence to a Pareto-optimal neighborhood and monotonic variance reduction of task performances under standard smoothness assumptions.

## Results

On 125M models trained on ClimbLab (1.6B tokens), GRAPE achieves 44.16% average 5-shot accuracy across 6 reasoning benchmarks (ARC-E, ARC-C, SciQ, PIQA, LogiQA, HellaSwag), outperforming the uniform baseline (40.89%) by 3.27 points and DoGE (42.45%) by 1.71 points. On 0.7B models (7.8B tokens), GRAPE reaches 49.56% average accuracy versus 48.11% for uniform and 48.72% for DoGE, while using approximately 40% fewer training tokens to match the uniform baseline's performance. In the multilingual setting on Wiki-40B, GRAPE accelerates low-resource language modeling by at least 60% in log-perplexity across all 8 target languages compared to both uniform sampling and DoGE.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GRAPE consistently outperforms all four baselines on the 125M ClimbLab setting: it beats DoGE (44.16% vs 42.45% avg), RegMix (44.16% vs 41.02%), CRISP (44.16% vs 41.97%), and ClimbMix (44.16% vs 41.98%), with the largest margin over RegMix (+3.14 pts). On the 0.7B scale, GRAPE surpasses DoGE (49.56% vs 48.72%) and uniform (49.56% vs 48.11%), improving on all 6 individual tasks above the uniform baseline (vs only 3 for DoGE), and achieves comparable accuracy to uniform with ~40% fewer tokens. DoGE, which uses uniform task weights, shows notable degradation on LogiQA (25.96% vs 28.57% uniform) at 0.7B scale, illustrating the sub-optimality of average task weighting under conflicting gradients. RegMix and CRISP, as offline reweighting methods, exhibit strong topical biases (over-concentrating on specific domains like healthcare or code), leading to uneven multi-task performance. In the multilingual Wiki-40B experiment, DoGE's single-target gradient alignment degrades significantly under 8 simultaneous target languages, while GRAPE's adaptive task reweighting maintains consistent gains across all low-resource languages.

## Relation to Prior Work

GRAPE extends the domain reweighting line of work (DoGE, RegMix, CRISP, ClimbMix) from the multi-source-single-target or average-target paradigm to an explicit multi-source-multi-target setting by formulating the problem as a Group DRO minimax game. The Rate-of-Improvement metric is adapted from FAMO's equal-loss-decrease-rate principle, while the Bregman-regularized multiplicative updates follow the online DRO framework of Qi et al. and Namkoong & Duchi. Unlike gradient-surgery MTL methods (PCGrad, CAGrad, MGDA) that require storing N task gradients and scale poorly to foundation models, GRAPE operates at the domain level with only O(N+K) gradient computations per update step, making it tractable for LLM pretraining.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GRAPE fills a concrete gap in adaptive pretraining: no prior method jointly optimizes data mixture and task priority for robust multi-target performance, and the paper demonstrates that uniform task weighting (as in DoGE) is suboptimal when tasks exhibit conflicting or heterogeneous learning dynamics. The dynamic curriculum revealed by task weight evolution (e.g., reading comprehension → physical/commonsense reasoning; Ukrainian→Russian, Romanian→Italian in multilingual settings) offers interpretable insight into how models acquire skills. Key limitations include evaluation only at 125M–0.7B scale, convergence guarantees relying on strong convexity that does not hold in practice, and sensitivity to domain granularity and hyperparameters (μ_α, μ_z, ΔT). Open questions include how the optimal curriculum shifts with model scale, whether sample-level DRO can replace task-level prioritization, and whether the framework transfers to larger-scale pretraining where gradient estimation costs become prohibitive.
