## Problem

A foundational assumption in feature-based interpretability methods (SAEs, logit lens, linear probes) is that transformer layers build additively upon each other's contributions to the residual stream. This paper shows that assumption is insufficient: adjacent layers systematically and selectively reverse portions of each other's contributions, meaning that analyzing each layer's output in isolation can misrepresent its true function and that extracted features may be immediately corrected downstream.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254643/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis takes token sequences (primarily ~100K tokens from WikiText, plus 79K generated tokens from 100 prompts) processed through open-weight LLMs (Llama 3.1 8B with d_m=4096, OLMo 1B/7B, Gemma 2 2B, Mistral, Qwen2) and extracts per-layer marginal contributions c_i,t. Outputs include: (1) a d_m × d_m cosine similarity matrix M averaged across tokens, revealing off-diagonal negative stripes for adjacent layers; (2) per-token TLCM activation counts (number of adjacent layer pairs with cossim < -0.1); (3) symmetrized Jacobian eigendecompositions of layer i+1 identifying which eigenvector directions are corrected (negative eigenvalues) versus promoted (positive eigenvalues); and (4) linear response curves mapping perturbation scale α to correction strength.

## Method

The paper discovers TLCM by computing cosine similarity between marginal contributions of all layer pairs, revealing that adjacent layers (i, i+1) have mean cossim ≈ -0.2 while non-adjacent layers do not. Causal intervention experiments perturb layer i's contribution by α·a_t and show layer i+1 responds with near-linearly scaled correction, establishing direct causal dependence. The key mechanistic tool is the symmetrized Jacobian J̄ = (J + J^T)/2 of layer i+1: its negative-eigenvalue eigenvectors span the corrected subspace (with eigenvalue magnitude dictating correction strength, peaking near λ = -1 for full reversal), while positive-eigenvalue eigenvectors are promoted (peaking near λ = 0.5). This bimodal eigenvalue distribution proves TLCM is selective rather than uniform. The authors synthesize findings into the propose-and-reject hypothesis: a layer proposes multiple candidate features, and the next layer uses attention to gather context and selectively rejects inappropriate ones, explaining why TLCM fires most on contextually-dependent tokens (numbers, punctuation, dates) and why it emerges during pretraining rather than being architectural.

## Results

Adjacent transformer layers in 5 of 7 open-weight model families (Llama 3, OLMo, Mistral, Gemma, Qwen2) exhibit a mean cosine similarity of ≈ −0.2 between their residual stream contributions, defining the Transformer Layer Correction Mechanism (TLCM, threshold: cossim < −0.1). Under a random-normal null hypothesis, a single TLCM event is a 6σ occurrence in 4096-dim space; under an empirical null it is still ~3σ, confirming the effect is not chance. Jacobian eigendecomposition reveals a bimodal eigenvalue spectrum for TLCM-active layers: one mode near λ = −1 (full reversal) and another near λ = +0.5 (active promotion), with ~3000 of 4096 eigenvalues negative. Causal interventions show a near-linear adaptive relationship: scaling the prior layer's contribution by α elicits a proportionally scaled correction from the next layer, with correction capacity diminishing only beyond 2× amplification.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a discovery/empirical-study paper rather than a method-comparison paper, so there are no traditional baselines to beat. The key comparative findings are: (1) TLCM is present in Llama 3, OLMo, Mistral, Gemma, and Qwen2 but absent in GPT-2 and Phi-3/Phi-4, which both use dropout blocks after sublayers, suggesting an architectural dependency. (2) Across training checkpoints of OLMo 1B/2B/7B, TLCM first appears at ~21% through pretraining (step 135,500, ~0.5T tokens) and strengthens through the remaining training, ruling out initialization or post-training (SFT/RL) as the source. (3) Token-level analysis on 79K generated tokens shows statistically significant variation: punctuation/number tokens (e.g., '\n' at 12.41 ± 0.1, '202' at 16.66 ± 0.16 mean activations) receive ~50% more corrections than content words (e.g., 'sustainability' at 7.63 ± 0.46), while BOS tokens receive 0–1 corrections. (4) Jacobian analysis contrasts TLCM-active layer pairs (bimodal eigenvalue distribution with modes at λ ≈ −1 and λ ≈ +0.5) against non-TLCM pairs (single peak at λ ≈ 0), demonstrating that correction is selective rather than uniform attenuation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the foundational assumption in feature-based interpretability (SAEs, logit lens, linear probes) that transformer layers build additively upon each other's residual stream contributions. It is conceptually adjacent to self-repair work (Hydra effect, copy suppression) but distinguishes TLCM as a pervasive, always-on mechanism occurring during normal inference rather than a compensatory response to ablation. The paper repurposes existing tools—cosine similarity matrices, causal interventions, and Jacobian eigendecomposition—to characterize a previously unreported phenomenon, and positions its findings as explaining three known puzzles in SAE-based interpretability (low feature specificity, high amplification thresholds for steering, and CLTs outperforming SAEs).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By demonstrating that adjacent layers systematically and adaptively correct each other's contributions, the paper challenges a core assumption underlying much of the interpretability toolkit and offers a unified explanation for why SAE features lack specificity, why steering requires extreme amplification, and why cross-layer transcoders outperform SAEs. The propose-and-reject hypothesis provides a testable conceptual framework for enrichment, though the authors explicitly note they do not directly verify it. Open questions include the architectural cause (RMSNorm blindness is implicated but insufficient), why TLCM is absent in GPT-2 and Phi models, and whether Jacobian-informed interventions can enable more effective model steering at lower magnitudes.
