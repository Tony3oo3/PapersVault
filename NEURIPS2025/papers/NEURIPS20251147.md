## Problem

Reproducing large-scale self-supervised pretraining (e.g., DINOv2) demands prohibitive compute—hundreds of epochs on billions of images with multi-GPU clusters—putting state-of-the-art SSL out of reach for most labs. Moreover, robustness to common corruptions in these models emerges only at extreme scale, with no explicit mechanism to build it in at smaller budgets. The paper asks whether a structured data curriculum can simultaneously accelerate convergence and strengthen corruption robustness without resorting to larger models or datasets.

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes standard RGB images cropped per DINOv2's protocol (global crops at 224×224, local crops at 96×96). During the first 75% of training epochs, all crops are bicubic-downsampled by half (to 112×112 and 48×48 respectively) before entering the ViT; in the final 25%, full-resolution crops are used alongside Gaussian noise patching. The output is a frozen ViT backbone (ViT-S/16 or ViT-B/16) whose patch and [CLS] embeddings serve as visual features for linear probing, instance recognition, and semantic segmentation.

## Method

FastDINOv2 introduces a two-stage frequency-based curriculum on top of DINOv2's teacher-student ViT training. Stage 1 (75% of epochs) trains exclusively on bicubic-downsampled images, exploiting the fact that natural images concentrate information in low frequencies and that ViT self-attention acts as a low-pass filter, thereby reducing per-token FLOPs by 75% and accelerating convergence. Stage 2 (remaining 25%) switches to full-resolution inputs and applies Gaussian noise patching—replacing a random square region with N(1, scale²) noise—to counteract the high-frequency feature bias that the low-resolution curriculum inadvertently induces. The key insight is that the curriculum's high-frequency bias and the noise patching's low-frequency bias are complementary: their combination achieves spectral balance, yielding improved robustness across both low- and high-frequency corruption categories while reducing total training time by 1.6× and FLOPs by 2.25× on ImageNet-1K with a ViT-B/16 backbone.

## Results

On ImageNet-1K with a ViT-B/16 backbone, FastDINOv2 reduces pre-training time from 16.64 to 10.32 days (1.6×) and FLOPs from 493.76 to 219.92 GFLOPs (2.25×) while achieving 76.2% linear probing accuracy (vs. 77.8% for DINOv2) and 56.7% ImageNet-C corruption accuracy (vs. 56.5%). On the smaller ImageNet-100 setup, the 112-224 curriculum matches DINOv2's 250-epoch accuracy (78.44% vs. 78.6%) in just 200 epochs with a 1.44× training-time speedup. Combining the curriculum with Gaussian noise patching boosts ImageNet-100-C corruption accuracy from 46.84% to 52.88% (+6.04%) while retaining 78.4% clean accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the standard DINOv2 pre-training protocol. FastDINOv2 clearly wins on efficiency: it cuts training time by 1.6× (10.32 vs. 16.64 days) and FLOPs by 2.25× (219.92 vs. 493.76 GFLOPs) on ImageNet-1K, and by 1.44× on ImageNet-100 (13.9 h vs. 24 h). On clean ImageNet-1K linear probing, DINOv2 retains a small edge (77.8% vs. 76.2%), but FastDINOv2 slightly edges out DINOv2 on ImageNet-C corruption robustness (56.7% vs. 56.5%). On the ImageNet-100-C benchmark, FastDINOv2 (curriculum + Gaussian noise patching) substantially outperforms DINOv2 on corruption accuracy (52.88% vs. 46.84%, +6.04%), with the largest gains on high-frequency noise corruptions (e.g., Gaussian noise: 57.51% vs. 32.11%). On instance recognition (Oxford/Paris), FastDINOv2 wins on Oxford Easy (+3.73% mAP) and Medium (+1.22%) but trails slightly on Oxford Hard (−0.57%); on ADE20K semantic segmentation the two methods are essentially tied (mIoU 19.16 vs. 19.2).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper revisits the coarse-to-fine curriculum learning paradigm introduced for ViT pretraining by RECLIP and EfficientTrain++, but applies it specifically within DINOv2's self-distillation framework and, critically, analyzes its previously unexamined effect on model robustness. While prior curriculum approaches focused solely on convergence speed, this work connects the frequency-domain robustness literature (Yin et al., 2019) to curriculum design, discovering that low-frequency training induces a high-frequency feature bias. The Gaussian noise patching augmentation (Lopes et al., 2019) is repurposed as a complementary, low-frequency-biased intervention to counterbalance this effect, creating a spectral balance not explored in prior augmentation or curriculum work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical value is clear: a 1.6× reduction in pretraining time and 2.25× reduction in FLOPs makes DINOv2-scale self-supervised pretraining accessible to labs without massive GPU clusters, while the low-resolution first stage also cuts peak memory from 33.5 GB to 9.47 GB. Conceptually, the paper argues that robustness need not be an emergent by-product of extreme scale but can be deliberately engineered through curriculum and augmentation design. However, the absolute robustness gain on ImageNet-C is modest (56.7% vs. 56.5%), clean accuracy drops slightly (76.2% vs. 77.8%), and the fixed 75/25 transition schedule is acknowledged as a heuristic that may not generalize across hyperparameter settings. Open questions include adaptive scheduling, integration with adversarial training, and whether the frequency-bias mechanism transfers to other SSL paradigms beyond DINOv2.
