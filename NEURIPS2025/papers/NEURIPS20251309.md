## Problem

Specialized chemical LLMs are fragmented across narrow task formats, and while model merging offers a data-free route to unify them, the chemical domain presents two challenges absent in NLP: (1) large parameter disparities from task-specific fine-tuning that amplify interference and noise, and (2) a highly imbalanced expert distribution where some tasks are over-represented while others (e.g., retrosynthesis) have only one or no capable model. Existing merging methods (Task Arithmetic, TIES, DARE) fail to resolve these inconsistencies, producing merged models that underperform even the best individual expert.

## Input / Output

The method requires a shared pretrained base model (LLaMA-3-8B-Instruct) and N fine-tuned expert models of identical architecture, plus a small validation set (ChemBench dev + 100 Mol-Instructions samples) used solely to rank experts by capability. All merging is performed on weight-space task vectors (differences between expert and current merged weights), requiring no access to original training data. The output is a single merged model evaluated on both predictive tasks (9 multiple-choice chemistry questions on ChemBench) and generative tasks (retrosynthesis and forward reaction prediction on Mol-Instructions, scored by round-trip accuracy, SELFIES BLEU, validity, and exact match).

## Method

CMM replaces simultaneous multi-expert merging with a progressive curriculum: experts are ranked in ascending order of validation performance and merged one at a time, with each step computing the task vector as the difference between the expert's weights and the *current* (already partially merged) model rather than the original base. The recursive composition θ_k = θ_{k-1} + β_k·(θ_ft^{(k)} − θ_{k-1}) causes earlier-merged weaker experts to be dynamically attenuated by the cumulative product of subsequent (1−β_i) terms, naturally shifting emphasis toward stronger experts merged later. Adaptive coefficients β_k follow an increasing schedule (linearly from 0.3 to 0.6) to further amplify the contribution of high-performing experts. The framework is composable with sparsification (DARE_CMM variant) to drop a fraction of task-vector entries and rescale the remainder, reducing parameter interference without sacrificing the curriculum structure.

## Results

On ChemBench (9 multiple-choice chemistry tasks), CMM achieves an average accuracy of 61.43%, surpassing the best individual expert (KALE-LM-Chem-1.5, 57.07%) by 7.6% and the best merging baseline (Task Arithmetic, 51.85%) by 18.5%. On Mol-Instructions (retrosynthesis and forward reaction prediction), CMM attains a round-trip accuracy of 0.35, matching the top expert (Molinst-Molecule-8B, 0.37) while all other baselines drop below 0.10. Across both benchmarks combined, CMM achieves an overall average of 56.62, outperforming the best SOTA merging method (TA, 43.88) by 29.03%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CMM decisively outperforms all five SOTA merging baselines on the unified overall metric: TA (43.88), AF (42.26), SCE (32.84), TIES (34.69), and DARE_TA (30.86), with the largest margin over TA at +29.03%. On ChemBench specifically, CMM (61.43) beats TA (51.85) by 9.58 points and AF (51.54) by 9.89 points, while TIES, DARE_TA, and SCE all fall below 41%. On Mol-Instructions (the expert-sparse generative benchmark), CMM (0.35 RTA) is the only method that approaches the sole competent expert (Molinst-Molecule-8B, 0.37); DARE_TA is the closest baseline at 0.33, while TA, TIES, SCE, and AF all collapse to ≤0.08. Notably, all baseline merging methods underperform even the best single expert (KALE-LM-Chem-1.5, 47.97 overall), whereas CMM exceeds it by 18.03%, demonstrating that progressive capability-aware merging resolves the parameter interference and fine-tuning noise that simultaneous merging strategies cannot handle.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Task Arithmetic (Ilharco et al., ICLR 2023) for task-vector extraction and linear combination, and on DARE (Yu et al., ICML 2024) for sparsification-based interference reduction, but departs from all prior merging methods (TA, TIES, DARE, SCE, AF) by introducing a curriculum-based sequential ordering: weaker experts are merged first, stronger ones later, with progressively increasing weights. The curriculum learning principle from Bengio et al. (2009) is repurposed from training-data scheduling to model-merging sequence design. The paper positions itself as the first systematic application of model merging to the chemical LLM domain, where greater parameter disparities and imbalanced task coverage (expert-abundant vs. expert-sparse) create challenges not present in the more homogeneous NLP merging literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CMM addresses a practical bottleneck in chemical AI: organizations with multiple specialized fine-tuned models cannot retrain on combined data due to privacy constraints, making training-free merging essential. The finding that all standard merging baselines underperform even the best individual expert in this domain (e.g., TA at 51.85 vs. KALE-LM-Chem at 57.07) highlights that naive merging is actively harmful under high model disparity, and that ordering and weighting are not merely tuning details but critical design choices. Open questions include whether the curriculum ordering generalizes to domains with more than six experts (where prior work shows saturation), whether cross-architecture merging is possible, and whether a theoretical account of why progressive merging reduces parameter interference can be formalized.
