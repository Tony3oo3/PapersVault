## Problem

Despite widespread use of reflective thinking in LLM reasoning (e.g., DeepSeek-R1, OpenAI-o1), it remains unclear how self-verification actually contributes to empirical improvements, especially given that LLMs detect only a limited fraction of errors in their own CoTs. The paper asks two specific questions: what role self-verifying reflection plays in training and execution of reasoning models, and how reflective reasoning evolves under RL with verifiable outcome rewards. The complexity of natural language and prohibitive LLM training costs make it difficult to draw clear theoretical and experimental conclusions.

## Input / Output

For integer multiplication, the input is a pair of non-negative integers (x, y) and the model produces a sequence of intermediate states of the form [x_t × y_t + z_t] (mathematically equal to x×y) until one multiplicand reaches zero, at which point the accumulated sum z_t is the answer. For Sudoku, the input is a 9×9 grid with blank cells (represented as 0) and the model fills cells step-by-step until no blanks remain. In both tasks, each reasoning step is augmented with verification labels (binary ✓/× or detailed per-element labels), and the model can reject and resample incorrect steps or trace back to earlier states. Difficulty is stratified by digit count (multiplication) or number of blanks (Sudoku) into ID-Easy, ID-Hard, and OOD-Hard levels.

## Method

The paper introduces a minimalistic reflective reasoning framework built on the Markov Thought Process (MTP), where a self-verifying policy π̃ = {π, V} simultaneously plans reasoning steps and discriminatively verifies them. Two reflective execution variants are defined: the Reflective MTP (RMTP), which instantly rejects steps flagged as incorrect and resamples, and the Reflective Trace-Back Search (RTBS), which propagates negative labels backward to recursively reject parent steps after m failed attempts, enabling depth-first exploration. Theoretically, the paper proves (Theorem 1) that RMTP improves accuracy over vanilla MTP if and only if the sum of false-positive and false-negative verification errors is at most 1, and that RTBS further outperforms RMTP for sufficiently large problems when negative states are more easily discriminated (f > α) and the search width m exceeds 1/(1−α). Experimentally, tiny causal-attention transformers (1M–16M parameters) are trained from scratch through a four-stage pipeline—pretraining, non-reflective SFT, reflective SFT (learning to predict verification labels from an expert rule-based verifier), and GRPO-based RL fine-tuning with binary outcome rewards—demonstrating that self-verification acts as a regularizer during training and that RL incentivizes frequent reflection only when the planning policy has sufficient exploratory capacity.

## Results

Tiny transformers (1M–16M parameters) with self-verifying reflection achieve remarkable LLM-level performance: the best 4M Sudoku model matches OpenAI o3-mini, and the best 16M integer-multiplication model outperforms DeepSeek-R1 on in-distribution difficulties. Reflective SFT with binary verification brings the largest gains for smaller models (1M, 4M) on ID-Easy and ID-Hard multiplication, while detailed verification benefits the 16M model on OOD-Hard problems. After GRPO fine-tuning, in-distribution accuracy improves substantially, but out-of-distribution performance remains largely unchanged, and verification errors shift from false negatives to false positives rather than decreasing overall.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks its tiny transformers against two strong LLM baselines: OpenAI o3-mini and DeepSeek-R1. On Sudoku, the best 4M-parameter model with self-verifying reflection matches o3-mini's accuracy, demonstrating that a model with roughly 1/1000th the parameters can reach comparable performance on this task. On integer multiplication, the best 16M model outperforms DeepSeek-R1 on in-distribution (ID) difficulties, though the gap narrows or reverses on out-of-distribution (OOD) problems. These comparisons are made at the task level (accuracy on ID-Easy, ID-Hard, and OOD-Hard splits) rather than on a shared benchmark suite, so the comparisons are indicative rather than exhaustive. The paper also shows that RL fine-tuning (GRPO) improves ID performance for both multiplication and Sudoku but does not close the OOD gap, suggesting the gains are distributional rather than reflective of new generalizable reasoning skills.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper formalizes self-verifying reflection within the Markov thought process (MTP) framework of Wang [37], introducing two concrete reflective execution mechanisms (RMTP and RTBS) that extend the base MTP with discriminative verification labels. It departs from the dominant LLM-centric study of reflection (e.g., DeepSeek-R1 [5], Self-Refine [19]) by stripping away natural language entirely, using tiny 1M–16M transformers on structured tasks (integer multiplication, Sudoku) to achieve analytic clarity. The training pipeline mirrors standard LLM-reasoner pipelines (pretraining → SFT → RLVR with GRPO), but the key novelty is the theoretical characterization of when reflection provably helps, building on the RLVR paradigm [15] while providing the first formal conditions (Theorem 1) linking verification error rates to reasoning accuracy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical guarantee that self-verifying reflection improves CoT reasoning accuracy under a simple condition (e₋ + e₊ ≤ 1), demystifying why reflection helps even with weak verifiers. The RL finding that fine-tuning induces an error-type trade-off (shifting from false negatives to false positives) rather than genuinely improving verification challenges the common narrative that RLVR strengthens the verifier. However, all results are from tiny models on highly structured, non-linguistic tasks, so the transfer to natural-language LLMs remains an open question. The paper also highlights that RL's in-distribution gains come from shallow statistical patterns rather than generalizable problem-solving skills, raising important questions about the limits of RLVR for reasoning.
