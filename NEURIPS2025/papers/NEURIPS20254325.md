## Problem

Constraint-based causal discovery methods (e.g., PC, k-PC) rely on discrete conditional independence tests that become unreliable under small sample sizes, while differentiable score-based methods (e.g., NOTEARS, DAGMA) optimize continuous objectives but forgo explicit CI constraint enforcement. No existing framework makes d-separation itself differentiable, preventing gradient-based optimization of the very constraints that define constraint-based causal discovery.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254325/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes an observational dataset of n samples and d variables, from which raw p-values of 0th- and 1st-order conditional independence tests (Fisher-z or Chi-squared) are computed and used directly as soft CI labels without thresholding. It outputs a weighted adjacency matrix W ∈ [0,1]^{d×d} (parameterized as σ(θ) for θ ∈ R^{d×d}) representing a DAG, with the final structure selected from top-k candidates via a CI-statement-based DAG selection score. The method restricts attention to conditioning sets of cardinality ≤ 1, making it robust to the statistical unreliability of higher-order CI tests.

## Method

The core contribution is a percolation-based differentiable d-separation score: d-separation is first recast as First-Order Logic formulae over graph reachability, then relaxed via LogLTN operators (product t-norm for AND, LogMeanExp approximation of max t-conorm for OR) in log-space to yield continuously differentiable functions over W ∈ [0,1]^{d×d}. The critical distinction from diffusion-based relaxations (e.g., matrix powers) is that the product t-norm correctly captures positive correlation among paths sharing edges, providing principled lower bounds on expected d-separation/d-connection over Bern(W) graph distributions (Theorem 3.8). Training uses a multi-task objective combining 'true positive' losses (maximizing d-separation scores where data shows independence), 'true negative' losses (maximizing d-connection scores where data shows dependence), and a log-determinant acyclicity regularizer from DAGMA, with PCGrad resolving gradient conflicts and Discrete Langevin Proposal enabling exploration of the weight space.

## Results

On the real-world Sachs protein signaling dataset (11 variables, 853 samples), DAGPA achieves CI-MCC scores of 0.75–0.98, substantially outperforming all baselines which cluster between 0.3–0.65. On synthetic binary data with d=10, r=2, DAGPA demonstrates superior robustness in the low-sample regime (n=100), performing on-par with DAGMA-Nonlinear while clearly outperforming constraint-based methods (PC, k-PC) and most score-based methods. In the high-sample regime (n=10,000), DAGPA maintains competitive performance alongside constraint-based approaches, confirming that the differentiable d-separation framework does not sacrifice accuracy when data is abundant.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DAGPA decisively outperforms all seven baselines on the Sachs dataset, achieving CI-MCC of 0.75–0.98 versus 0.30–0.65 for every competitor—a gap of roughly 0.15–0.68 in CI-MCC. Among constraint-based methods, both PC and k-PC struggle in the low-sample synthetic setting (n=100) due to unreliable high-order CI tests, while DAGPA's soft p-value weighting provides clear robustness advantages. Among score-based and continuous-optimization methods, NOTEARS (linear and nonlinear) and DAGMA (linear) underperform in the low-sample regime, likely due to overfitting to limited data. DAGMA-Nonlinear is the closest competitor, achieving on-par performance with DAGPA at n=100, but DAGPA still leads on the real-world Sachs benchmark. In the high-sample regime (n=10,000), DAGPA converges to competitive performance with constraint-based methods, indicating the differentiable relaxation does not introduce a fundamental accuracy ceiling.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out a 'third avenue' between constraint-based causal discovery (PC, k-PC, LOCI) and score-based differentiable methods (NOTEARS, DAGMA) by introducing differentiable d-separation scores grounded in percolation theory. It builds on NOTEARS/DAGMA for continuous DAG parameterization and log-determinant acyclicity, on LogLTN for differentiable fuzzy logic operators (product t-norm, max t-conorm in log-space), and on k-PC/LOCI for restricting to low-order conditioning sets. The key conceptual departure is recognizing that d-separation is a percolation measure (accounting for path dependencies) rather than a diffusion measure (matrix powers assuming path independence), and formalizing this via first-order logic formulae that admit soft-logic relaxation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The differentiable d-separation framework is of independent interest and could be integrated into existing gradient-based causal discovery methods, potentially combining CI constraint signals with likelihood-based signals. However, the current instantiation (DAGPA) is limited to low-order conditioning sets (|Z| ≤ 1), assumes no latent confounders, and uses a simple p-value heuristic for dependence strength. Open questions include extending to higher-order CI statements, handling latent confounders, improving scalability, and validating the large performance gap on Sachs across more real-world datasets. The framework's measurement-agnostic design (swappable CI tests) is a practical advantage for robustness across preprocessing pipelines.
