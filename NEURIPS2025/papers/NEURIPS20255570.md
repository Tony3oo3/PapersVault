## Problem

Existing test-time scaling methods for LLMs, such as best-of-N sampling with imperfect verifiers and majority voting, can fail to drive success probability toward 100% even when a single LLM call already solves a problem with moderate probability. This paper addresses the gap by proposing algorithms with provable inference scaling laws—formal guarantees that failure probability decays to zero as test-time compute grows—under clearly stated and minimal assumptions about the LLM.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single text problem (e.g., a multiple-choice question or math word problem) as input and outputs one selected solution. Both the generation and aggregation stages are executed by a black-box LLM (or an ensemble of LLMs) with no external verifier or reward model required. For problems with short final answers, each candidate solution is required to include a chain-of-thought reasoning trace to facilitate pairwise comparisons in the aggregation stage.

## Method

The paper proposes two two-stage algorithms. The knockout-style algorithm first generates N candidate solutions in parallel, then aggregates them via a knockout tournament where each pair of candidates is compared K times and the majority-voted winner advances; the final-round winner is the output. The league-style algorithm also generates N candidates but evaluates each by its average win rate against K randomly sampled opponents (or all N−1 opponents in a round-robin variant), selecting the candidate with the highest win rate. The key theoretical contribution is proving that under minimal assumptions (non-zero probability of generating a correct solution, and better-than-random pairwise comparison ability), the knockout algorithm's failure probability decays exponentially (when both N and K scale) or by a power law (when only N scales), while the league algorithm's failure probability decays exponentially under a more robust assumption about average win-rate separation. Both algorithms require only a black-box LLM and support fully parallel execution, with total LLM calls bounded by (K+1)×N for the knockout variant.

## Results

On GPQA, the knockout-style algorithm (KO) with the Mixed backend (Llama3.1 + Qwen2.5) improves accuracy from 45% to 55% as N scales to 64, while QwQ-32B improves from 60% to 72% on GPQA-diamond as N scales to 16. On the filtered subset of problems approximately satisfying Assumption 2.1 (0 < p̂_gen < 1 and p̂_comp > 0.5), KO (Mixed) achieves a substantial accuracy gain from 55% to 80% with increasing N, closely matching the theoretical prediction of convergence to 100%. The league-style algorithm (LG) with Mixed improves from 45% to 53% on GPQA as N grows to 16, and shows a 25% accuracy increase on the filtered subset satisfying Assumption 3.1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is Majority Voting (MV), a widely adopted test-time scaling method. The knockout-style algorithm (KO) consistently achieves higher accuracy than MV for all LLM backends except Llama3.1 when given the same number N of initial candidates on GPQA and GPQA-diamond. Importantly, even accounting for the fact that KO requires (K+1)×N LLM calls (5N for Llama3.1/Qwen2.5/Mixed, 3N for GPT-4o/QwQ-32B) versus only N calls for MV, KO retains a clear advantage: KO at N=8 (40 calls) outperforms MV at N=64 (64 calls) for Mixed, and KO at N=4 (12 calls) outperforms MV at N=16 (16 calls) for QwQ-32B. The paper also notes that MV's accuracy is expected to plateau at a lower level than KO as N grows further, consistent with the theoretical failure mode where a single incorrect answer mode with slightly higher probability dominates. The league-style algorithm (LG) shows comparable but slightly different performance to KO given the same initial candidates, with neither consistently dominating the other across all settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes tournament and league structures (previously explored in LLM alignment and LLM-as-a-judge contexts) into a two-stage test-time scaling framework, but its core contribution is theoretical: providing the first provable scaling laws for test-time compute that require only a black-box LLM and no external verifier or reward model. It departs from best-of-N sampling (which needs a verifier and can degrade with imperfect ones) and majority voting (which can converge to zero success probability under certain conditions) by showing that pairwise comparison-based aggregation achieves guaranteed convergence to 100% accuracy under minimal assumptions (non-zero generation probability and better-than-random pairwise discrimination). The paper explicitly positions its novelty as the rigorous theoretical analysis—identifying sufficient conditions and deriving quantitative bounds on compute/sample complexity—rather than the algorithmic structures themselves.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides a principled, assumption-explicit framework for understanding when and why test-time compute scaling works, filling a gap left by prior methods that either require perfect verifiers or lack formal guarantees. The practical implication is significant: practitioners can now deploy simple tournament/league aggregation with a black-box LLM and have theoretical confidence in scaling behavior, without engineering a domain-specific verifier. Key open questions include determining the minimal N and K needed for a target success probability in practice (the theorems give upper bounds but not tight ones), extending the framework to agentic workflows with multiple sub-problems, and developing anytime variants for online settings where compute is adaptive. A notable limitation is that the assumptions, while minimal, can fail on knowledge-heavy tasks where the LLM simply lacks the relevant information, in which case no amount of test-time compute helps.
