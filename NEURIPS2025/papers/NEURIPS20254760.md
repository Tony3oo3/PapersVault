## Problem

Conventional ML privacy research equates verbatim memorization with privacy loss, yet for instruction-tuned LLMs the realistic threat is non-adversarial chat extraction, which prior work largely overlooks. This paper asks whether the degree of verbatim memorization actually predicts how much private information leaks through a chat interface, and what factors (model quality, data augmentation, attribute uniqueness) govern that leakage.

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes two types of text: (1) synthetic biographies (~74M tokens, 550k records with 8 PII attributes up to 10 tokens each) for unsupervised fine-tuning, and (2) 80k reformulated question-answer pairs for instruction tuning. Evaluation produces extraction accuracy per attribute under two protocols: verbatim extraction via greedy next-token prediction given the exact training prefix, and chat extraction via random decoding (temperature=1.0, top-p=0.9) in response to natural-language questions about held-out individuals. All extraction is measured on the 'tail' set (least-repeated biographies) to isolate generalization effects.

## Method

The paper fine-tunes Llama-1/2/3 models through a two-stage pipeline: unsupervised fine-tuning on BIOs-augment (synthetic biographies with varied sentence formulations and 1–10× repetition buckets) followed by 10 epochs of instruction tuning on QA pairs from the most-repeated ('head') individuals. The key experimental contribution is a controlled ablation study comparing verbatim vs. chat extraction across model quality (scratch, Llama1-7B, Llama2-7B/13B, Llama3-8B), data augmentation (varied vs. fixed templates), instruction-tuning data source (head vs. novel individuals), and a targeted unlearning procedure that drives ID-token loss to a random-guessing target. The central finding is that chat extractability is governed by the model's knowledge-manipulation capacity and the contextual diversity of training data rather than by verbatim memorization rate, and that instruction tuning itself suppresses verbatim extraction while leaving the underlying encoding recoverable through realignment.

## Results

After 12 epochs of unsupervised fine-tuning on BIOs-augment, all models achieve ~100% verbatim extraction for all attributes (including 8-digit IDs), yet chat extraction plateaus well below 100%, with unique attributes (ID, ID Seq) showing significantly lower chat extractability than shared-value attributes (College, Degree). Model quality, not size, drives chat extraction: Llama3-8B (MMLU 69.4) outperforms Llama2-13B (MMLU 54.8), which outperforms Llama2-7B (MMLU 45.3), while a from-scratch 7B model fails entirely at chat extraction despite achieving 100% verbatim extraction. Instruction tuning eliminates verbatim extraction for most attributes, but retraining for next-token prediction restores it, confirming the information remains encoded. Unlearning verbatim memorization of IDs (targeting loss≈2) systematically reduces their chat extractability while leaving held-out IDs unaffected.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical study rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so the 'baselines' are alternative model configurations and training setups. The key comparison is across five models (Scratch, Llama1-7B, Llama2-7B, Llama2-13B, Llama3-8B) all post-trained identically on BIOs-augment: all reach 100% verbatim extraction, but chat extraction strictly follows model quality (MMLU score) rather than parameter count, with Llama3-8B (69.4 MMLU) beating the larger Llama2-13B (54.8 MMLU). A second comparison contrasts augmented vs. non-augmented unsupervised fine-tuning data: without varied formulations, chat extraction drops uniformly across all attributes, showing that repetition alone (which boosts verbatim memorization) is insufficient for chat extractability. A third comparison shows that instruction tuning on head-set individuals is essential—replacing it with a disjoint hold-out set causes most tail attributes to become unextractable via chat. Finally, the unlearning experiment on Llama3-8B-BIOs demonstrates that driving ID token loss to ~2 (near random-guessing) reduces chat extractability of unlearned IDs while held-out IDs remain unaffected, confirming that removing verbatim encoding is sufficient to block chat extraction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the knowledge storage and extraction framework of Zhu & Li (2023) and the synthetic biography construction methodology of Allen-Zhu & Li (2023) by introducing a critical distinction: verbatim memorization (the focus of prior LLM privacy work à la Carlini et al.) versus chat extraction after instruction tuning. While the memorization literature (Carlini et al. 2019, 2021b, 2022; Nasr et al. 2023) has thoroughly characterized how training data can be recovered via prefix prompting, this paper argues that the more realistic threat model—non-adversarial chat queries against an instruction-tuned model—has been largely overlooked and behaves very differently. The paper also engages with the unlearning literature (Maini et al. 2024) to test whether removing verbatim encoding eliminates higher-level chat extractability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges the foundational assumption in ML privacy that memorization directly implies privacy loss, showing that the relationship is mediated by the model's ability to manipulate and retrieve knowledge through chat. This has practical implications for LLM practitioners who fine-tune on domain-specific data: a model with high verbatim memorization may still be relatively safe against casual chat queries, while a smaller but higher-quality model may leak more. Key limitations include the entirely synthetic setting, restriction to attributes ≤10 tokens, US-centric English data, and absence of error bars, leaving open whether these trends hold for production-scale models, longer sequences, and diverse linguistic contexts. The finding that instruction tuning degrades verbatim extraction (and can be reversed by retraining) also raises open questions about the stability of privacy guarantees across training pipelines.
