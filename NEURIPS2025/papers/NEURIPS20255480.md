## Problem

Existing multi-modal benchmarks such as MMLU and MMMU evaluate models' ability to interpret multi-modal inputs and produce text-only reasoning, but they neglect a critical dimension: whether models can generate multi-modal outputs (drawings, auxiliary lines, traced paths) as part of their visual reasoning process. This paper asks whether current foundation models, including the latest omni-models and reasoning models, can actually draw or modify images to support their reasoning, a capability that is fundamental to human problem-solving but largely untested in the literature.

## Input / Output

Each of the 803 questions accepts a text prompt and, in 763 of 803 cases, an accompanying image as input; the remaining 40 are text-only. The expected output is dual-modality: a textual answer (multiple-choice or open-ended) plus, critically, a multi-modal output such as a drawn geometric figure, constructed auxiliary lines, a traced maze path, or a completed graph. Unlike prior benchmarks where the image is purely an input to be interpreted, here the model must actively produce or modify visual content as an intermediate step in its reasoning chain before arriving at the final answer.

## Method

RBench-V is a hand-curated benchmark of 803 questions spanning four domains—math (176), physics (157), counting (195), and games (275)—each selected under the strict criterion that its solution requires generating new visual content or modifying an existing image during the reasoning process. The evaluation protocol is zero-shot, using a unified LLM-as-a-Judge framework (GPT-4o) to score both multiple-choice and open-ended responses, reporting Top-1 accuracy. To validate that the benchmark genuinely demands multi-modal outputs, the authors conduct a controlled comparison against MMLU and MMMU where both human experts and models rate whether drawing is required during the thinking process; RBench-V wins 92–94% of the time. A 'w/o math' ablation is reported to control for algebraic shortcuts, since models often convert geometry problems into coordinate-based algebra rather than performing genuine visual reasoning. Human expert scores (82.3% overall) are established by recruiting senior undergraduates across relevant majors to provide a human-level reference.

## Results

On RBench-V (803 questions across math, physics, counting, and games), the best-performing model, OpenAI o3, achieves only 25.8% top-1 accuracy, far below the human expert score of 82.3%. The best open-source model (Qwen2.5VL-72B) scores just 10.6%, while the best non-thinking closed-source model (GPT-4o) reaches 14.1%. Excluding math questions (where models exploit algebraic shortcuts), o3's accuracy drops to 19.5% versus 81.7% for humans, widening the gap to 62.2 percentage points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

OpenAI o3 leads all 18 evaluated models by a wide margin, scoring 25.8% overall versus 20.9% for o4-mini and 20.2% for Gemini 2.5 Pro, a gap of roughly 5–6 percentage points over the next-best models. Among open-source models, Qwen2.5VL-72B (10.6%) and Llama4-Scout (9.5%) are the strongest, but both trail o3 by more than 15 points, confirming a substantial open-vs-closed gap. Scaling within a model family yields diminishing returns: Qwen2.5VL improves only from 8.3% (7B) to 10.6% (72B), and InternVL-3 from 8.0% (14B) to 10.0% (38B), suggesting that parameter scaling alone cannot close the multi-modal output reasoning gap. Long text-only CoT provides marginal gains (Doubao-1.5-vision-pro 15.6% → Doubao-1.5-thinking-pro-m 17.1%), while o3's dedicated visual reasoning capability delivers the largest single-model jump. All models remain far below the 82.3% human expert baseline, with the gap widening to 62.2 points when math questions (susceptible to algebraic shortcuts) are excluded.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by identifying a gap in the evaluation landscape: existing multi-modal benchmarks (MMLU, MMMU) assess understanding of multi-modal inputs but require only text outputs, while RBench-V specifically demands that models produce visual outputs (drawings, auxiliary lines, traced paths) as part of their reasoning chain. Conceptually, it extends the multi-modal chain-of-thought (M-CoT) paradigm from a methodological direction to an evaluation direction, asking whether models can actually *generate* visual content to support reasoning rather than merely *consume* it. The paper does not build on a specific prior method but repurposes the well-studied domains of geometry, physics, counting, and games under a new evaluation criterion: vision-indispensable multi-modal output.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 56.5-point gap between the best model (o3 at 25.8%) and human experts (82.3%) reveals that current foundation models fundamentally struggle to generate and integrate visual content during reasoning, a capability that is central to human problem-solving. Two findings carry broad implications: (1) scaling model size (e.g., Qwen2.5VL 7B→72B) yields negligible gains, suggesting next-token prediction may be inherently inadequate for precise multi-modal generation; and (2) models exploit 'multi-modal reasoning shortcuts' (converting geometry to algebra) that inflate scores without demonstrating genuine visual reasoning. Open questions include whether M-CoT or agent-based frameworks can close the gap, whether the benchmark generalizes beyond text+image to video/audio outputs, and how to design training objectives that incentivize true visual reasoning over textual reformulation.
