## Problem

Despite the empirical success of Mixture-of-Experts networks in modern LLMs, the theoretical foundations explaining their ability to efficiently model complex tasks remain poorly understood. This paper addresses the gap by providing rigorous approximation guarantees for MoEs on two canonical structural priors: functions supported on low-dimensional manifolds and piecewise functions with compositional sparsity, showing how MoEs overcome the curse of dimensionality that plagues dense networks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252307/cropped_page6_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

For the shallow MoE setting, the input is a point x on a compact d-dimensional smooth manifold M ⊂ R^D (d ≪ D), and the output is a scalar f(x) approximated to within Õ(m^{-κ/d}) error. For the deep MoE setting, the input is a point x = (x_1, …, x_L) on a product manifold M = M_1 × … × M_L, and the output is a vector of L subfunction values (f_{1,i_1}(x_1), …, f_{L,i_L}(x_L)) that jointly encode E^L distinct piecewise tasks. A key constraint is that the target function may be highly smooth within each local region but only low-order continuous at region boundaries, making direct global approximation inefficient.

## Method

The core construction decomposes approximation into localized subproblems plus a routing problem. For shallow MoEs (Theorem 4.8), a depth-2 network with E experts per layer works as follows: Layer 1 acts as a dense network approximating partition-of-unity functions ρ_i, and Layer 2's linear gating uses these to exactly route each input to the expert responsible for its manifold chart U_i; each expert then approximates the low-dimensional composition f|_{U_i} ∘ φ_i^{-1} and the smooth chart map φ_i, yielding an error rate governed by intrinsic dimension d rather than ambient D. For deep MoEs (Theorem 5.2), a depth-2L network stacks L such routing-and-approximation blocks, where the l-th block handles the l-th coordinate x_l and its E subregions, so that the hierarchical composition of L blocks realizes E^L distinct piecewise tasks. The key architectural insight is that depth enables hierarchical composition of subtasks while expert count enables per-subtask specialization, and that nonlinear gating (or a preceding dense layer) is essential for correct input-to-expert assignment.

## Results

Theoretically, a depth-2 MoE with E experts per layer approximates functions on a d-dimensional manifold in R^D at rate Õ(m^{-κ(f|_{U_i})/d ∧ 1/2}), governed by intrinsic dimension d rather than ambient dimension D (Theorem 4.8). For deep MoEs, a depth-2L network with E experts per layer approximates piecewise functions with E^L pieces at rate max_{l,i} Õ(m^{-κ(f_{l,i})/d_l ∧ 1/2}) (Theorem 5.2). Experimentally, the 1-4-MoE maintains test error ~3.4e-4 across input dimensions D=16 to 128 (no curse of dimensionality), and the 2-3-MoE achieves 2.59e-6 test error at width m=128 on the 9-piece compositional task, while the comparable 1-6-MoE plateaus at 4.60e-5.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external MoE or dense-network baselines in a standard benchmark sense; instead, it provides theoretical rate comparisons and controlled architectural ablations. Theoretically (Table 1), shallow MoE networks achieve approximation rate Õ(m^{-κ/d ∧ 1/2}) governed by intrinsic manifold dimension d, versus Õ(m^{-κ/D ∧ 1/2}) for shallow dense networks governed by ambient dimension D, yielding a substantial improvement when d ≪ D. Experimentally, the 2-3-MoE (depth 2, 3 experts per layer) decisively outperforms the 1-6-MoE (depth 1, 6 experts) on the 9-piece compositional task: at m=128, the deeper model reaches 2.59e-6 while the shallower model degrades to 4.60e-5, a ~18× gap. This demonstrates that depth enables hierarchical composition of subtasks (E^L pieces) that cannot be replicated by simply increasing the number of experts at a fixed depth. The 1-4-MoE experiment further confirms that test error remains stable (~3.2–3.4e-4) as ambient dimension D grows from 16 to 128, validating the theoretical curse-of-dimensionality avoidance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the classical ReLU approximation theory (Mao & Zhou 2023; Yang & Zhou 2024) and the dense-network results on manifold approximation (Shaham et al. 2018; Chen et al. 2019) and compositional sparsity (Mhaskar & Poggio 2016; Poggio 2023) to the Mixture-of-Experts setting. While prior MoE theory focused on training dynamics (Chen et al. 2022), sparsity equivalence (Baykal et al. 2022), or routing benefits (Dikkala et al. 2023), this work is the first to systematically characterize the expressive power of both shallow and deep MoEs for structured functions. The key conceptual departure is showing that the gating mechanism and expert networks play fundamentally different roles—routing vs. local approximation—that together enable MoEs to exploit low-dimensionality and sparsity in ways dense networks cannot match computationally.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This provides the first clean theoretical justification for why MoEs (now central to LLMs like DeepSeek and Qwen) efficiently handle diverse tasks: they decompose structured problems into localized subproblems with exponential compositional capacity (E^L pieces at depth O(L)). The practical architectural suggestions—nonlinear gating to reduce depth, alternating MoE-dense layers, and autoencoding experts for parameter efficiency—offer concrete design guidance. Key open questions remain: whether SGD can actually find these structured solutions (training dynamics with non-differentiable Top-K routing and load-balancing losses), and whether the results extend to other sparsity structures (group, graph, temporal) beyond compositional sparsity.
