## Problem

Offline black-box optimization methods—whether forward surrogate modeling, inverse mapping, or learned search policies—are fundamentally limited by the scarcity of high-value examples in the offline dataset, causing surrogate models to overfit or fail to identify promising regions. This paper addresses the data bottleneck by asking: can the low-to-high transformation needed for optimization be learned from synthetic functions that resemble the unknown oracle, rather than from the offline data alone?

## Input / Output

The method consumes a static offline dataset of input-output pairs (x_i, y_i) with no new oracle queries, plus the top 128 highest-scoring designs from that dataset as simulation starting points. It produces 128 candidate input vectors (continuous or discrete) that are then scored by the oracle; performance is reported at the 50th, 80th, and 100th percentiles of these 128 scores. Notably, the method never directly models the objective function—instead it learns a distributional transformation, and the synthetic training data is generated entirely from closed-form GP posterior means rather than from the offline observations themselves.

## Method

ROOT reframes offline optimization as distributional translation: it learns a probabilistic bridge (a generalization of the Brownian Bridge Diffusion Model) that maps an implicit low-value input distribution to a high-value one. The bridge is trained on paired (low-value, high-value) samples generated from 800 synthetic functions, each being the posterior mean of a GP fitted to the offline data with a randomly sampled RBF kernel (lengthscale and variance drawn uniformly around a base value); low- and high-value points are obtained by 100 steps of gradient descent/ascent from 1024 offline starting points per function. The noise-prediction network is a 4-layer MLP (1024 units, Swish activation) trained with classifier-free guidance on the output scores y_T and y_0, using a dropout probability of 0.15 to prevent over-reliance on the guidance signal. At inference, the backward process runs 200 denoising steps from each of the 128 best offline designs, with a classifier-free guidance weight β = −1.5 and a target score scaled by α = 0.8 times the best observed value, yielding high-value candidates without ever querying the oracle during training.

## Results

On the Design-Bench benchmark (100th percentile of 128 candidates), ROOT achieves a mean rank of 1.25/22, setting new SOTA on D'Kitty (0.972±0.005), TF-Bind-8 (0.986±0.007), and TF-Bind-10 (0.685±0.053). On Ant, CMA-ES reports a higher 100th-percentile score (1.561) but with a standard deviation of 0.896 (≈64× ROOT's 0.014) and collapses near zero at the 80th/50th percentiles. On the three RNA-binding tasks, ROOT ranks first on all benchmarks, outperforming the prior best (Boot-Gen) by margins of 0.043, 0.074, and 0.136 with roughly half the variance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ROOT outperforms all 21 baselines on D'Kitty (0.972 vs. next-best PGS at 0.966) and TF-Bind-10 (0.685 vs. CMA-ES at 0.664), and matches or exceeds the best discrete-task baselines on TF-Bind-8 (0.986 vs. GA at 0.985). On Ant, CMA-ES reports a higher 100th-percentile score (1.561 vs. 0.965) but with a standard deviation of 0.896 (≈64× ROOT's 0.014) and collapses to 0.007 at the 80th percentile, indicating it produces only a single outlier design. On the RNA-binding tasks, ROOT surpasses the prior state-of-the-art Boot-Gen by 0.043 (RNA-A), 0.074 (RNA-B), and 0.136 (RNA-C) while halving the standard deviation. In the few-shot experimental design setting (1% labeled data), ROOT outperforms ExPT by 0.002 on Ant and 0.021 on TF-Bind-8. Under poor offline data coverage (only the 10% worst designs), ROOT maintains 0.915 on TF-Bind-8 and 0.861 on Ant, consistently outperforming GA, COMs, REINFORCE, and LTR across all coverage levels.

## Relation to Prior Work

ROOT generalizes the Brownian Bridge Diffusion Model (Li et al., 2023) and DDPM (Ho et al., 2020) by replacing the data-to-noise translation with a low-value-to-high-value design translation, and incorporates classifier-free guidance (Ho & Salimans, 2022) to condition on output scores. Conceptually, it departs from the three dominant families in offline optimization—forward modeling (COMs, ICT), inverse modeling (MINs, DDOM), and search policies (BONET, PGS)—by reframing the problem as distributional translation rather than surrogate fitting or trajectory learning. The synthetic data generation via multiple GP posteriors draws inspiration from ExPT (Nguyen et al., 2023) but repurposes it to train a meta probabilistic bridge rather than a surrogate, addressing the data bottleneck from a fundamentally different angle.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The distributional translation perspective shifts the optimization focus from modeling the unknown objective function to learning design-to-design transitions, which sidesteps the OOD extrapolation problem that plagues surrogate-based methods. The consistent SOTA across diverse tasks (DNA sequences, robot morphology, RNA design) with notably low variance suggests the approach is robust rather than task-specific. Open questions include how different bridge parameterizations (beyond Brownian bridge) affect learning, whether the framework scales to higher-dimensional continuous spaces, and whether the GP-based synthetic data generation can be replaced by more expressive function families. The paper also leaves the choice of bridge design as an open problem, noting that different mean/kernel specifications define distinct transport plans with potentially different learning behaviors.
