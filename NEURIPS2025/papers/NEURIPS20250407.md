## Problem

Existing RLHF algorithms with theoretical guarantees either rely on optimistic exploration that is computationally intractable in general MDPs (requiring maximization of exploration bonuses over state distributions) or demand a human preference query at every single round. This paper tackles the open problem of designing tractable RLHF algorithms that actively select informative trajectory comparisons to reduce human feedback workload while maintaining regret or PAC-style guarantees in general MDPs with trajectory-level preferences.

## Input / Output

The learner operates in an infinite-horizon MDP with a linear reward model r(s,a) = ⟨θ*, φ(s,a)⟩ and queries a Bradley-Terry preference oracle that returns binary labels for trajectory pairs. At each round, the learner selects two policies, executes them to obtain full trajectories, and optionally queries the oracle; a PAC-RL oracle (e.g., PPO) maps a sampled reward parameter to an ε-optimal policy. The output is a near-optimal policy with high-probability regret or suboptimality bounds, plus a constrained maximum-likelihood estimate of θ* obtained by minimizing the logistic loss over collected preference pairs.

## Method

The core mechanism replaces intractable optimistic exploration with randomized exploration: the reward parameter is sampled from a Gaussian centered at the MLE estimate (or at zero for preference-free exploration), and the RL oracle produces a policy for the sampled reward, yielding a constant probability of optimism via anti-concentration arguments. Algorithm 1 (RPO-Regret) performs online preference learning by comparing each new policy against the previous one, while Algorithm 2 (RPO-Explore) defers all preference queries to a single batch at the end, achieving PAC-style suboptimality of Õ(√(κd³/T)). Algorithm 3 (LRPO-OD-Regret) further reduces query complexity by combining lazy updates—collecting trajectory pairs without querying the oracle until the design matrix determinant grows by a multiplicative factor (1+C), limiting the number of batches to O(log T)—with greedy D-optimal design to select the most informative comparisons from each batch, exploiting the submodularity of log det for a (1−1/e) approximation guarantee and enabling parallelization of preference queries within a batch.

## Results

In the 6×6 gridworld, RPO-Regret (Algorithm 1) achieves considerably lower cumulative regret with less variance than an entropy-based exploration baseline across 20 runs. On the Isaac-Cartpole-v0 continuous control task, all three algorithms (RPO-Regret, LRPO-Regret, and LRPO-OD-Regret) exhibit sublinear regret that flattens after a few hundred RLHF rounds, reaching performance competitive with PPO trained on ground-truth rewards. LRPO-OD-Regret (Algorithm 3 with D-optimal design) matches the regret of RPO-Regret while substantially reducing the number of preference queries issued, demonstrating that optimal design selects informative comparisons in practice despite worst-case lower bounds of Ω(T) queries.

## Baselines & Comparisons

In the tabular gridworld, RPO-Regret clearly outperforms an entropy-regularized exploration baseline in both median regret and variance, confirming that entropy exploration alone does not guarantee low regret in MDPs. In the Isaac-Cartpole-v0 continuous control setting, the paper compares three of its own algorithms: RPO-Regret (online, queries every round), LRPO-Regret (lazy updates, no optimal design), and LRPO-OD-Regret (lazy updates + D-optimal design). All three achieve comparable sublinear regret, with LRPO-OD-Regret showing slightly higher initial regret but quickly converging to the same level. The key practical advantage is that LRPO-OD-Regret issues significantly fewer preference queries than RPO-Regret, as the optimal design subroutine selects only the most informative trajectory pairs from each batch. The paper also implicitly compares against reward-based RL (PPO with the true reward), showing that preference-based learning with a small number of queries can match reward-based performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes three distinct threads: randomized exploration from linear Thompson sampling (Abeille & Lazaric, 2017), lazy updates from linear bandits (Abbasi-Yadkori et al., 2011), and D-optimal experimental design from statistics, applying them jointly to preference-based RL in general MDPs. It departs from the dominant optimistic-exploration paradigm in RLHF (Pacchiano et al., 2021; Zhan et al., 2024), which is computationally intractable in trajectory-level settings, by showing that sampling reward parameters from a confidence ellipsoid achieves comparable regret bounds without requiring intractable maximization over policies. The suboptimality analysis for Algorithm 2 builds on Das et al. (2024)'s contextual bandit framework, extended here to full RL with trajectory-level feedback under a PAC-RL oracle assumption.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a critical practical bottleneck in RLHF: the need for a human (or LLM) to provide a preference label at every single round. By combining lazy updates (enabling parallel query collection) with D-optimal design (selecting only informative comparisons), Algorithm 3 substantially reduces the number of preference queries while preserving worst-case regret guarantees. Key limitations include reliance on the Bradley-Terry model (which may not capture real human feedback), the need for an RL oracle call at every step, and experimental validation limited to simple gridworlds and one continuous control task with synthetic preferences. Open questions include whether richer preference models, reward-free oracles, or real human/LLM feedback change the practical picture, and whether the theoretical worst-case lower bound of Ω(T) queries can be circumvented in broader settings.
