## Problem

Prior scalable-oversight work on AI debate assumes neutral judges and ignores how strong prior beliefs and cognitive biases impair human evaluation of controversial claims. No systematic study has compared debate against single-advisor consultancy for judges with divergent beliefs, nor tested whether persona-conditioned LLM judges can outperform their human counterparts on cognitively challenging factuality tasks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255666/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

Each trial presents a binary true/false factuality claim (from CheckCOVID or Climate-Fever) alongside a curated pool of reference sources supporting both the correct and incorrect positions, ensuring comparable persuasive material on each side. Judges first record an initial judgment and 0–100 confidence rating, then interact with the AI protocol over three structured rounds, and finally produce a revised judgment, confidence rating, and free-text justification. The paper reports accuracy against ground truth, Brier calibration scores, belief-transition rates (correct→incorrect, incorrect→correct), and truth-aligned confidence shifts.

## Method

The paper implements two GPT-4o (t=0.2) intervention protocols: (1) Debate, where two agents argue opposing positions in a strict three-round turn-taking format with the judge able to interject questions, and (2) Consultancy, where a single agent argues one assigned position (randomly correct 50% of the time for COVID-19; 92.5% correct for climate change) over three rounds. Study I recruits human judges with mainstream or skeptical prior beliefs via Prolific and measures accuracy, calibration, and belief-reversal rates under both protocols. Study II assigns GPT-4o judges human-like personas (demographics + COVID-19 beliefs) in public-personalized (debaters see the persona) and private-personalized settings, and compares them against non-personalized GPT-4o and Qwen-2.5-7B judges. The key mechanism is that adversarial debate forces exposure to both sides' evidence and counterarguments, which the paper shows reduces harmful belief reversals, improves confidence calibration (Brier 0.231 vs 0.335), and equalizes performance across belief groups, while persona-conditioned LLM judges reach 78.5% accuracy—exceeding both human (70.1%) and default LLM (69.8%) judges.

## Results

On COVID-19 factuality claims, debate achieves 70.1% final accuracy (up from 63.2% initial) versus consultancy's 60.0% (down from 61.4%), a 10.0% overall advantage (p<0.01). The gap is largest for judges with mainstream beliefs (+15.2%, p<0.01) and smaller for skeptical judges (+4.7%, p=0.2). On climate change claims, debate maintains a 3.8% advantage (72.1% vs 68.3%, p<0.01) even when consultants argue for the correct answer 92.5% of the time. Persona-based LLM judges in public personalized settings reach 78.5% accuracy with debate, surpassing both human judges (70.1%) and default LLM judges without personas (69.8%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is single-advisor consultancy (Michael et al., 2023), where one GPT-4o agent argues a randomly assigned position (correct 50% of the time for COVID-19; correct 92.5% of the time for climate change). Debate wins on every primary metric: overall accuracy (+10.0% on COVID-19, +3.8% on climate change), truth-aligned confidence shift (+14.6/+15.3 vs +6.9/−12.6 for skeptical/mainstream priors), and Brier calibration score (0.231 vs 0.335). The advantage is most pronounced for mainstream-belief judges (+15.2% accuracy), while for skeptical judges the accuracy gap (+4.7%) is not statistically significant (p=0.2), though debate still reduces harmful belief reversals. Notably, debate's edge persists even under the stronger climate-change consultancy baseline where the consultant is correct 92.5% of the time, suggesting the adversarial structure itself—rather than mere access to correct information—drives the improvement. In the LLM-judge study, persona-conditioned GPT-4o judges (78.5% with public personalization) outperform both unconditioned GPT-4o (69.8%) and Qwen-2.5-7B (70.7%), indicating that simulating human belief profiles adds meaningful oversight value beyond default model behavior.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the scalable oversight debate framework established by Kenton et al. [30], Khan et al. [31], and Michael et al. [36], which demonstrated that adversarial AI debate helps neutral judges identify truth. The key departure is introducing the dimension of judge prior beliefs and cognitive biases: prior work assumed judges were unbiased evaluators, whereas this paper systematically tests whether debate remains effective when judges hold strong mainstream or skeptical priors on controversial topics. The consultancy baseline (single AI advisor) is drawn directly from Michael et al. [36], and the persona-based LLM judge setup is a novel experimental addition that emulates human demographic profiles to study automated oversight.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a single AI advisor can actively *worsen* judgment for mainstream believers (accuracy drops from 63.4% to 55.9%) while adversarial debate consistently improves it provides concrete evidence for why debate's competitive structure matters for oversight design. Persona-based LLM judges outperforming human judges (78.5% vs 70.1%) suggests a practical path toward automated scalable oversight, though this is demonstrated only with GPT-4o on two domains. Key open questions include whether these results hold in-the-wild (vs. controlled Prolific experiments), whether they generalize beyond U.S. English-speaking populations, and whether the persona-LLM-judge advantage persists with different base models or more adversarial debaters.
