## Problem

Current visual-spatial intelligence (VSI) evaluation in multimodal LLMs relies on VQA-style textual prompts that conflate linguistic proficiency with genuine spatial reasoning, obscure geometric structure, and invite linguistic shortcuts. No existing benchmark provides a structured, geometry-explicit representation of scene layout that can separate spatial capability from language priors, particularly in safety-critical domains like autonomous driving where precise object localization and relational reasoning are essential.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251077/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a driving scene image with annotated vehicle bounding boxes and a task prompt as input; for ICL, it additionally consumes k image-SIG pairs (where each SIG is a 10×10 grid encoded as a JSON mapping object labels to [row, col] coordinates) as few-shot context. Outputs include the predicted 10×10 SIG grid, spatial relation paragraphs (sentences with directional and proximal prepositional phrases between object pairs), and for the gaze task, a predicted attention map. Evaluation produces three metric families: MLSM (precision, recall, F1, association accuracy across multiple distance thresholds), SRGS (graph similarity and weighted similarity scores), and SRD (MAE, MSE, and accuracy on a circular 8-direction and linear 5-proximity scale).

## Method

The core contribution is the Spatial Intelligence Grid (SIG), a 10×10 discrete grid that maps each object (ego-vehicle, vehicles labeled by color+type+order, traffic signs, traffic lights) to a grid cell, from which a directed Spatial Relation Graph (SRG) with direction+distance edges and a Spatial Relation Paragraph (SRP) with prepositional phrases are derived. Three novel evaluation metrics are built on this structure: MLSM performs multi-threshold bipartite matching (inspired by MOTA/HOTA) with attribute-weighted cost functions; SRGS computes graph edit distance over the SRG with separate node and edge substitution/insertion/deletion costs; and SRD measures semantic distance on a circular 8-direction and linear 5-proximity scale. For human-like VSI, raw gaze attention maps are projected from image space to SIG space via a homographic transformation solved by SVD, and the resulting attention weights scale node/edge edit costs and semantic distances to penalize errors on highly attended objects more severely. The ICL protocol feeds image-SIG pairs as few-shot context to MLLMs, demonstrating that SIG-based context yields larger, more stable, and more comprehensive VSI gains than VQA-style context across GPT-4o and Gemini-2.5-Pro. SIGBench (1,423 frames from the FHWA driving dataset) provides ground-truth SIG, SRP, and gaze attention annotations to support both grid-based and human-like VSI evaluation.

## Results

On SIGBench-tiny (90 frames), 3-shot ICL-SIG consistently improves VSI over zero-shot for both GPT-4o and Gemini-2.5-Pro: GPT-4o gains +0.070 in MLSM-F1 (0.479 vs 0.462), +0.010 in SRGS-S (0.337 vs 0.327), and +0.090 in SRD-Proximal Acc (0.436 vs 0.346); Gemini-2.5-Pro gains +0.069 in MLSM-F1 (0.565 vs 0.496), +0.081 in SRGS-S (0.305 vs 0.224), and +0.054 in SRD-Proximal Acc (0.493 vs 0.439). In zero-shot evaluation on the full SIGBench (1,423 frames), Gemini-2.5-Pro leads in MLSM (F1=0.496) and SRD-Directional (Acc=0.295), while GPT-4o leads in SRGS (S=0.327). Cross-domain experiments on SIG-COCO and SIG-ARKitScenes confirm ICL-SIG generalizes beyond driving, with GPT-4o improving MLSM-F1 from 0.771→0.849 (COCO) and 0.727→0.823 (ARKitScenes).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is ICL-SIG (proposed) vs. ICL-MC (VQA-style multiple-choice) vs. zero-shot, evaluated on GPT-4o and Gemini-2.5-Pro. ICL-SIG wins on nearly every VSI metric for both models, with the largest gains in SRGS-S (+0.081 for Gemini-2.5-Pro) and SRD-Proximal Acc (+0.090 for GPT-4o). ICL-MC is notably unstable: for Gemini-2.5-Pro it actually degrades SRGS-S from 0.224 (Z-S) to 0.185, and for GPT-4o it slightly worsens SRGS-S (0.327→0.324), whereas ICL-SIG improves it in both cases. Among the five MLLM families evaluated in zero-shot (GPT, Gemini, Claude, InternVL, Qwen-VL), Gemini-2.5-Pro achieves the best MLSM and SRD-Directional scores, GPT-4o the best SRGS, and Claude-3.7-Sonnet the best SRD-Proximal performance; open-source models (InternVL, Qwen-VL) trail proprietary ones by a wide margin on all grid-based VSI metrics. In the human-like VSI tasks (Table 6), model rankings closely mirror the grid-based results, indicating that current MLLMs have not yet learned to prioritize objects by human gaze attention.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant VQA-style paradigm for evaluating visual-spatial intelligence (VSI) in MLLMs, arguing that text-only scoring conflates linguistic proficiency with genuine spatial reasoning. Conceptually, it repurposes the grid-based decomposition tradition from Renaissance art (Dürer, da Vinci) as an analogy for a structured machine representation, and adapts graph edit distance and bipartite matching (from tracking metrics like MOTA/HOTA) into a suite of spatial evaluation metrics (MLSM, SRGS, SRD). The human-like VSI component extends gaze/saliency prediction literature by projecting attention maps onto the SIG via homographic transformation, creating an attention-weighted evaluation channel absent from prior AD VQA benchmarks (NuScenes-QA, NuPlanQA, NuScenes-SpatialQA).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SIG provides a principled, domain-agnostic data schema that separates spatial capability from language priors, and the finding that SIG-based ICL yields larger, more stable, and more comprehensive VSI gains than VQA-style ICL (sometimes the latter degrades below zero-shot) offers a concrete, low-cost recipe for improving MLLM spatial reasoning without fine-tuning. SIGBench, with its 1.4K annotated driving frames and human gaze traces, fills a gap in benchmarks that incorporate both structured spatial representation and human attention. Key open questions include extending SIG to temporal/tracking settings, exploring SIG-based fine-tuning or RLHF, and whether the grid abstraction generalizes to 3D or multi-camera scenarios beyond the single-frame, single-camera pilot studied here.
