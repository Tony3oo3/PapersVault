## Problem

Prior physics-informed machine learning has focused primarily on architectural inductive biases such as equivariance, leaving the loss function as a generic, physically meaningless objective (e.g., MSE or cross-entropy). This paper asks whether embedding physical principles directly into the loss function—specifically, using energy-based losses derived from thermal equilibrium—yields better training signals and models that more faithfully reflect physically valid configurations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255470/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework is architecture-agnostic and applies across three task families: (1) diffusion-based molecular generation, where a noisy latent sample and timestep are consumed by an EGNN/GNN/JODO network to produce 3D atomic positions and atom types; (2) spin ground-state prediction, where a CNN takes the full coupling matrix J_ij of an Ising Hamiltonian on a 16×16 lattice and outputs a binary spin configuration; and (3) shape prediction, where a radius scalar is mapped to N 2D polygon vertices. In all cases the loss operates on pairwise distances (or local spin fields) rather than raw coordinates, making it invariant to rigid motions and label permutations without requiring explicit alignment procedures.

## Method

The core derivation starts from the reverse KL divergence between a deterministic model prediction and a Boltzmann distribution p(ŷ|y) = exp(−E(ŷ,y)/T)/Z(y,T) centered on each data sample, yielding a loss proportional to the energy difference E(ŷ,y)/T. For atomistic systems, E is a quadratic pair potential over pairwise distances—E(ŷ,y) = Σ_ij ½ k_ij(y)(‖y_i−y_j‖−‖ŷ_i−ŷ_j‖)²—which is a second-order Taylor approximation of standard interaction potentials (Morse, Lennard-Jones) and is naturally E(d)-invariant. For discrete spin systems, the energy is a local-field linear form that captures the cost of flipping each spin. The paper proves that this distance-based loss in a diffusion model recovers the correct score up to rigid-motion kernel directions at low noise, and that rigidity theory allows replacing the O(N²) pairwise terms with O(N) sparse rigid-graph edges without changing global optima.

## Results

On QM9 with GDM-aug, the energy loss achieves 89.8% molecule stability and 97.7% validity, outperforming MSE (83.7%, 93.6%) and Kabsch-aligned MSE (82.3%, 90.8%). On the larger GEOM-Drugs dataset, the energy loss yields a dramatic 24.6% molecule stability versus only 0.8% for MSE. For spin ground-state prediction on 16×16 spin glasses, the local-field energy loss achieves a test energy of 45.6, substantially lower than cross-entropy (58.8) and margin loss (49.87). The sparse rigid-graph variant of the energy loss matches the full energy loss performance while reducing computation to O(N) operations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Kabsch-aligned MSE baseline (Klein et al., 2023), the energy loss wins on every metric: +7.5 pp molecule stability, +6.9 pp validity, and +1.5 pp atom stability on QM9, without requiring the expensive Kabsch alignment procedure at training time. Against the equivariant EDM baseline (Hoogeboom et al., 2022) trained with MSE, the energy loss with a non-equivariant GDM architecture achieves higher molecule stability (89.8% vs. 82.4%) and validity (97.7% vs. 93.0%), showing that loss-level physics invariance can outperform architecture-level equivariance at negligible extra cost. Against JODO (Huang et al., 2023), the energy loss improves all alignment metrics (bond MMD 0.0928 vs. 0.1218, angle MMD 0.0046 vs. 0.0110, dihedral MMD 4.95e-4 vs. 5.91e-4) and molecule stability (96.6% vs. 92.8%), though FCD is slightly worse (1.495 vs. 0.854) for the exponential-coefficient variant. On spin ground-state prediction, the local-field energy loss reduces test energy by 22% over cross-entropy and 8% over margin loss, though it remains far from the true-energy minimization baseline (14.6), which does not use the data as a classification target.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm of embedding physics into neural network architectures (equivariance, symmetry-aware layers) and instead embeds physical principles directly into the loss function. The derivation proceeds from the reverse KL divergence with a Boltzmann distribution centered on each data sample, yielding loss functions as energy differences; this recasts MSE as a physically meaningless quadratic potential and motivates distance-based pair potentials (second-order Taylor approximations of Morse and Lennard-Jones potentials) as principled alternatives. For diffusion models, the paper builds on the noise-prediction / score-matching formulation (Ho et al. 2020; Kingma and Gao 2024) and the reparameterization as data-prediction regression, showing that replacing MSE with the distance-based energy loss still recovers the correct score up to rigid motions. The invariance property is achieved by construction (the loss depends only on pairwise distances), avoiding the expensive Kabsch alignment or minimization procedures required by prior invariant losses (Klein et al. 2023).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a general, architecture-agnostic recipe for designing physically grounded loss functions that are invariant to system symmetries without costly alignment, which is practically valuable for molecular generation, spin systems, and potentially crystals and proteins. A notable practical finding is that a non-equivariant architecture (GDM) trained with the energy loss outperforms an equivariant architecture (EDM) trained with MSE, suggesting loss-function design can complement or even outweigh architectural equivariance. Open questions include whether the correct score is recovered at higher noise levels (the current analysis is valid only for small σ_t), whether richer surrogate energies capturing torsional angles improve results, and how the approach scales to very large systems (the sparse rigid-graph construction addresses this partially). The ad hoc nature of coefficient choices (exponential vs. inverse-squared decay) and the need for task-specific ablation remain limitations.
