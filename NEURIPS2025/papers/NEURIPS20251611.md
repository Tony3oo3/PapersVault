## Problem

While computer vision has made progress in estimating individual physical parameters (mass, friction, Young's modulus) from video, recovering dense, omni-directional force fields—such as wind distributed throughout 3D space—remains largely unexplored. Forces are far more complex than scalar parameters because they are vectors that can vary across space and time, and the time integration in physics simulators causes gradient instability that makes direct backpropagation intractable.

## Input / Output

The method takes a monocular video clip of a single moving/deforming object as input. The first frame is consumed by a metric-depth model (UniDepth) for 3D Gaussian initialization, a VLM (GPT-4o) for material property estimation, and a grounded segmentation model (Grounded SAM) for object masking. CoTracker provides sparse 2D keypoint trajectories across all frames. The output is a spatio-temporal force field encoded as a causal tri-plane (Eulerian grid), which can be queried at any (x, t) to retrieve a 3D force vector, and is then used to re-simulate or generate novel object motions under the same force field.

## Method

The framework is an end-to-end differentiable inverse-graphics pipeline with three coupled components: (1) objects are represented as 3D Gaussians serving as Lagrangian MPM particles, with physical properties (mass, Young's modulus, Poisson ratio) assigned per-Gaussian via VLM commonsense; (2) forces are encoded as a causal tri-plane that disentangles spatial (tri-plane feature map) and temporal (recursively-initialized MLP) dimensions, capturing spatial continuity and temporal causality; (3) a differentiable MPM simulator propagates Gaussian states forward using both external forces (sampled from the tri-plane at particle positions) and internal constitutive forces. The key optimization insight is a 4D sparse tracking objective: instead of photometric or dense flow losses that produce vanishing gradients, the method tracks a small set of 3D keypoints via CoTracker + as-rigid-as-possible re-projection, then controls per-Gaussian target positions through fixed barycentric interpolation from those keypoints, drastically reducing the prediction space and stabilizing backpropagation through the time-integrated simulator.

## Results

On synthetic force-recovery scenarios spanning elastic, elastoplastic, and viscoplastic materials, the proposed causal tri-plane representation achieves the lowest force magnitude error (5.14% for elastic, 21.44% for viscoplastic) and direction error (4.38° for elastic, 7.50° for viscoplastic) among all compared representations. In the overall force-recovery table (Table 1), the method attains PSNR values of 25.92–41.35 and SSIM ≥ 0.94 across six objects, with magnitude errors ranging from 14.38% to 33.31% and direction errors from 7.02° to 23.40°. The VLM-based material-property estimation achieves a perfect F1 of 1.0 on material-type classification and a 4.65% MAPE on density across 17 everyday objects.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the proposed causal tri-plane force representation against two baselines: point contact forces and K-Planes [Fridovich-Keil et al., CVPR 2023]. On elastic materials, the causal tri-plane dominates decisively, reducing magnitude error from 95.91% (Point) and 18.03% (K-Planes) to 5.14%, and direction error from 76.48° and 39.83° to 4.38°. On viscoplastic materials, the advantage is equally pronounced: magnitude error drops from 98.30% (Point) and 89.04% (K-Planes) to 21.44%, and direction error from 92.02° and 33.09° to 7.50°. On elastoplastic materials, the causal tri-plane still achieves the best PSNR (39.93 vs. 31.40 and 30.14) and lowest magnitude error (75.06% vs. 98.36% and 87.81%), though point contact forces yield a lower direction error (26.80° vs. 45.50°), and K-Planes marginally outperforms on PSNR for viscoplastic (41.73 vs. 39.00). Overall, the causal tri-plane's recursive temporal dependency and spatial continuity modeling provide the most accurate force recovery, particularly in magnitude and direction, which are the metrics most directly tied to physical plausibility.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the differentiable inverse-graphics lineage (Galileo, Physics 101, GradSim) from estimating a handful of scalar physical parameters (mass, friction, Young's modulus) to recovering dense, spatio-temporal force fields — a fundamentally higher-dimensional and more ill-posed problem. It repurposes 3D Gaussian Splatting as Lagrangian particles and the tri-plane representation (Chan et al.) as a spatial force encoder, adding a causal temporal MLP to capture force evolution, and couples both to a differentiable MPM simulator. The sparse-tracking objective with barycentric interpolation departs from prior photometric or dense-flow losses by reducing the optimization target to a small set of tracked keypoints, sidestepping the gradient instability that plagues backpropagation through long MPM rollouts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Recovering distributed force fields (e.g., wind) from natural videos opens a path to physics-grounded video generation and editing that prior methods — which require manually specified forces or only learn velocity fields — cannot support. The demonstrated ability to swap objects, change mass, or alter boundary conditions under a recovered force field is a concrete step toward controllable, physically consistent video synthesis. Key limitations constrain near-term impact: the framework handles only small or bending-only deformations (no fluids, no large plastic deformation), composites foreground over a static background, and still exhibits 14–33% magnitude and up to 23° direction errors on synthetic benchmarks. Open questions include extending to multi-object interactions, fluid–solid coupling, and whether the VLM-based property assignment remains reliable for uncommon or composite materials.
