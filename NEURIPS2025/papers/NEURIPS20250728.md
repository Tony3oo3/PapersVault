## Problem

Modern artificial neural networks abstract away the precise timing and interplay of individual neuron interactions, deviating from fundamental biological computation principles. This paper asks whether reintroducing neuron-level temporal dynamics and neural synchronization as a core representational mechanism can unlock capabilities—sequential reasoning, adaptive computation, interpretable internal processes—that current architectures lack.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250728/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The CTM is task-agnostic in architecture but task-specific in I/O: for mazes it takes 39×39 or 99×99 grid images (deliberately without positional encoding) and outputs a 100-step action sequence; for ImageNet it takes 224×224 RGB images and produces class probabilities at each of 50 internal ticks; for parity it takes a 64-length binary sequence and outputs cumulative parity at every position. Unusually, the model generates a full output at every internal tick, and the training loss dynamically selects which ticks to optimize (the minimum-loss tick and the maximum-certainty tick per sample), so the effective computation time varies per input without any explicit halting module.

## Method

The CTM introduces two core mechanisms. First, each of D neurons owns a privately parameterized Neuron-Level Model (NLM), a small MLP that processes a rolling history of M pre-activations to produce that neuron's post-activation, generating rich, diverse temporal dynamics across the population. Second, neural synchronization—defined as the inner product of post-activation histories over time—forms a D×D matrix from which selected neuron pairs are projected to serve simultaneously as the prediction output and the cross-attention query, making temporal correlation patterns the model's latent representation. A U-Net-style synapse model shares information across neurons each tick, and a novel loss function that averages the cross-entropy at the minimum-loss tick and the maximum-certainty tick per sample yields native adaptive computation time as an emergent property, without dedicated halting components.

## Results

On ImageNet-1K, the CTM (50 internal ticks, ResNet-152 backbone) achieves 72.47% top-1 and 89.89% top-5 accuracy on uncropped data. On 2D maze navigation (39×39 mazes, up to 100 steps), the CTM significantly outperforms LSTM (1–3 layers) and feed-forward baselines, and generalizes to 99×99 mazes via sequential re-application. On the cumulative parity task (64-length binary sequences), CTMs with 75 and 100 internal ticks achieve perfect accuracy in some seeded runs, while parameter-matched LSTMs struggle with stability. On CIFAR-10, the CTM demonstrates the best test accuracy and the best calibration among CTM, FF, and LSTM baselines, with calibration comparable to or better than human baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the CTM against LSTM (1, 2, and 3 layers) and feed-forward (FF) baselines across multiple tasks. On 2D maze navigation, the CTM substantially outperforms all baselines in both trainability and accuracy, while LSTMs suffer from training instability (particularly beyond one layer or with more than 50 ticks) and the FF model overfits. On the parity task, parameter-matched LSTMs (e.g., 5.72M params at T=50 vs. 5.72M for CTM) show unstable training and lower accuracy, whereas CTMs with 75–100 ticks reach perfect accuracy in some runs. On CIFAR-10, the CTM achieves the best test accuracy and calibration, outperforming both the FF baseline (which overfits) and the LSTM baseline (which is less stable and requires a lower learning rate). The paper explicitly states it is not targeting state-of-the-art results, so no comparison against other modern architectures (e.g., Transformers, Perceivers) is provided; the focus is on demonstrating the CTM's unique capabilities (adaptive compute, interpretable attention, emergent dynamics) rather than benchmarking against the broader literature.

## Relation to Prior Work

The CTM carves new ground at the intersection of several research threads: adaptive computation time (ACT, PonderNet, Universal Transformers), biologically inspired temporal dynamics (Liquid Time-Constant Networks, Spiking Neural Networks, Liquid State Machines), and neural synchronization (Reichert & Serre's complex-valued neurons, control-theoretic synchrony). Its two core departures are (1) privately-parameterized neuron-level models that process rolling histories of pre-activations, serving as a mid-level abstraction between standard activation functions and full biophysical neuron models, and (2) using neural synchronization (inner products of post-activation histories) directly as the latent representation for attention queries and outputs, rather than treating synchrony as an emergent or post-hoc property. Unlike prior recurrent architectures that rely on a single hidden state or explicit halting modules, the CTM decouples an internal 'thought' timeline from data dimensions and lets adaptive computation emerge from the unfolding of its own neural dynamics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The CTM demonstrates that reintroducing neuron-level temporal dynamics and synchronization as a first-class representation can unlock sequential reasoning, native adaptive computation, and interpretable internal strategies (e.g., 'looking around' images, scanning sequences) without explicit mechanisms for any of these behaviors. This suggests a path toward more biologically plausible architectures that may bridge the gap between current AI's rigidity and human-like flexible cognition. However, the work is explicitly preliminary: baselines are limited to LSTMs and feed-forward networks, hyperparameter search is minimal, and the sequential internal ticks increase training cost. Key open questions include whether the architecture scales competitively with modern transformer-based systems, how the NLMs and synchronization interact at larger widths and depths, and whether the emergent behaviors (traveling waves, adaptive halting) persist under stronger training regimes.
