## Problem

The Double Descent phenomenon—where test error peaks at the interpolation threshold before descending again—has been well established for in-distribution generalization, but its role in out-of-distribution (OOD) detection remains entirely unexplored. This paper asks whether overparameterization, which is known to benefit ID generalization, also enhances post-hoc OOD detection, and whether the characteristic U-shaped risk curve reappears when measuring OOD detection performance as a function of model complexity.

## Input / Output

The framework operates on a pretrained image classifier (CNN, ResNet-18/34, ViT, or Swin) trained on CIFAR-10 or CIFAR-100 (32×32 RGB) with 20% randomly swapped labels to make the double descent effect visible. Post-hoc scoring functions (logit-based, feature-based, or hybrid) are applied to the frozen model's outputs to produce a continuous OOD confidence score per image, which is then thresholded to yield an ID/OOD flag. Model width (hidden dimension k, ranging 1–128) is the swept variable, with depth held constant to isolate the effect of effective model complexity.

## Method

The paper combines a theoretical analysis with extensive empirical validation. Theoretically, it extends Belkin et al.'s (2020) Random Matrix Theory framework from regression to binary classification and OOD data, deriving upper and lower bounds on the expected OOD risk for least-squares classifiers on Gaussian covariate models; the bounds diverge to infinity at the interpolation threshold (p/n = 1), formally establishing a double descent curve for OOD risk. Empirically, it sweeps model width across four architecture families (4-block CNN, ResNet-18/34, ViT, Swin) under 20% label noise, evaluating seven post-hoc OOD detection methods (MSP, Energy, ReAct, MaxLogit, Mahalanobis, NECO, ViM, ASH) against six OOD benchmarks, confirming the double descent pattern in AUC. To explain why the overparameterized regime does not always win, the paper introduces a Neural Collapse-based criterion (the NC1_u/NC1_o ratio) that predicts when simpler models achieve better class separation and thus superior OOD detection.

## Results

The paper confirms that post-hoc OOD detection exhibits a double descent curve with respect to model width, with AUC peaking (worst) near the interpolation threshold and recovering in the overparameterized regime. Across architectures, the NC1_{u/o} ratio (ratio of Neural Collapse intra/inter-class covariance in under- vs. overparameterized regimes) predicts whether overparameterization helps: CNN (NC1_{u/o}=0.88) performs best in the underparameterized regime (e.g., Softmax AUC 76.09 vs. 75.08 on SUN), while ResNet (1.96), Swin (1.70), and ViT (2.32) improve with overparameterization (e.g., Swin Softmax AUC jumps from 58.82 to 67.91 on SUN). NECO achieves the highest AUC in the overparameterized regime for ResNet (75.60 on SUN), Swin (73.29 on SUN), and ViT (75.89 on SUN), outperforming logit-based methods by 3–9 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates seven post-hoc OOD detection methods (Softmax/MSP, MaxLogit, Energy, Energy+ReAct, NECO, ViM, ASH-P) across four architectures (CNN, ResNet-18, Swin, ViT) to study how model width affects detection AUC. Logit-based methods (Softmax, MaxLogit, Energy, Energy+ReAct) show the clearest double descent curves, with AUC dropping sharply near the interpolation threshold and recovering in the overparameterized regime. Feature-based methods (Mahalanobis, Residual) do not always exhibit double descent, suggesting their performance depends more on representation geometry than model capacity. NECO (the authors' Neural Collapse-based method) consistently achieves the highest AUC in the overparameterized regime for ResNet, Swin, and ViT (e.g., 75.89 on SUN for ViT vs. 64.87 for Softmax), but underperforms in the underparameterized regime for CNN. The key finding is that NC1_{u/o} > 1 (improved class separation with more capacity) predicts when overparameterization benefits OOD detection, while NC1_{u/o} < 1 (as for CNN) indicates the underparameterized regime is preferable.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two well-developed threads: the double descent phenomenon (Belkin et al., 2019; Nakkiran et al., 2021), which has been extensively characterized for in-distribution generalization via Random Matrix Theory and empirical studies, and post-hoc OOD detection (MSP, Energy, Mahalanobis, NECO, ViM, ASH), which has been studied as a function of model quality but not systematically as a function of model complexity. The theoretical analysis extends Belkin et al. (2020)'s RMT framework from regression to classification and OOD risk, while the empirical section follows Nakkiran et al. (2021)'s width-sweep protocol across CNN, ResNet, ViT, and Swin architectures. The paper departs from prior double descent work by showing the phenomenon transfers to OOD detection, and from prior OOD detection work by introducing the Neural Collapse framework (Papyan et al., 2020; Ammar et al., 2024) as a diagnostic for when overparameterization helps or hurts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that overparameterization does not consistently improve OOD detection challenges the common assumption that 'bigger is better' for safety-critical deployment, and the NC1-based criterion offers a practical, post-hoc way to identify the optimal complexity regime without exhaustive width sweeps. However, the theoretical guarantees are limited to linear classifiers on Gaussian data, and the empirical results are confined to CIFAR-scale datasets and post-hoc methods, leaving open whether the phenomenon persists at ImageNet scale, with training-based OOD methods, or under different shift types (covariate vs. semantic). The depth-wise double descent results (Appendix F) are a promising but underdeveloped direction that could reshape how practitioners choose model depth for OOD-sensitive applications.
