## Problem

Protein-ligand binding affinity prediction is bottlenecked by the scarcity of experimentally measured labels (PDBbind contains fewer than 20K complexes), while vast numbers of unlabeled 3D complexes exist in the PDB. Self-supervised graph contrastive learning could exploit this unlabeled data, but no comprehensive dataset provides well-defined positive/negative complex pairs, and standard graph augmentation (node/edge perturbation) produces physically unrealistic conformations that violate biochemical constraints.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251952/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes a 3D protein-ligand complex as a graph where nodes are atoms (with 3D spatial coordinates and non-spatial features like atom type) and edges represent covalent bonds within the ligand or distance-threshold (5 Å) interactions within the protein and between protein-ligand. The output is a single continuous binding affinity score (in pKd units). DecoyDB provides 61,104 ground-truth complexes (X-ray resolution ≤ 2.5 Å, ligand MW in (50, 700)) and 5,353,307 computationally generated decoys, each annotated with its RMSD from the native pose; a 2 Å RMSD threshold separates positive (near-native) from negative (suboptimal) pairs.

## Method

The paper introduces DecoyDB, constructed by filtering PDB entries at ≤ 2.5 Å resolution and generating 100 decoy poses per ligand via AutoDock Vina (exhaustiveness 8, 5 Å grid padding), yielding a diverse RMSD range (0.03–25.56 Å). On top of this dataset, a customized two-category InfoNCE contrastive loss is designed: negative samples are drawn both from same-complex decoys with RMSD > 2 Å (weighted by a continuous β = α·RMSD/d_max to push more deviated poses further) and from entirely different complexes (β = 1), so the model learns graded negativeness rather than binary contrast. A denoising score matching (DSM) regularization term is added, where Gaussian noise is injected into ligand coordinates and the encoder is trained to recover the clean structure, encoding the intuition that the native pose corresponds to a local energy minimum. The total pretraining objective L = L₁ + μL₂ is optimized for 20 epochs on DecoyDB, after which a regression head is attached and the model is fine-tuned on PDBbind affinity labels, improving accuracy, sample efficiency, and generalizability across GIGN, EGNN, and SchNet backbones.

## Results

On the PDBbind core set 2016, GIGN+OURS achieves the best RMSE of 1.189 (±0.031) and Pearson R of 0.838 (±0.011), outperforming the best base model GIGN (RMSE 1.262, R 0.811) by 5.8% in RMSE and 3.3% in R. On the PDBbind core set 2013, GIGN+OURS reaches RMSE 1.377 (±0.039) and R 0.813 (±0.013), improving over GIGN (RMSE 1.421, R 0.786). The pretraining framework also improves EGNN from RMSE 1.334 to 1.267 on PDBbind 2016, and on the leakage-proof LP-PDBbind split, pretraining reduces RMSE from 1.496 to 1.371 (an 8.4% improvement), demonstrating enhanced generalizability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GIGN+OURS achieves the best performance across all evaluated settings, reducing RMSE from 1.421 to 1.377 (2013) and 1.262 to 1.189 (2016) over the GIGN base model, with paired t-tests confirming statistical significance (p < 0.001 for 2016). Compared to general GCL augmentations (edge perturbation and node dropping) applied to GIGN, OURS yields a further 3.1–5.8% RMSE reduction, indicating that structure-aware decoy-based contrastive learning substantially outperforms random graph perturbations. Against the closest pretraining competitor ConBAP (which uses binary decoy negatives on Redocked2020), EGNN+OURS improves RMSE from 1.300 to 1.267 on PDBbind 2016 (p = 0.045), attributable to the two-category continuous-RMSD weighting and DSM regularization. On the leakage-proof LP-PDBbind split, pretraining delivers a much larger gain (RMSE 1.496 → 1.371, −8.4%) than on a random split (1.294 → 1.269, −1.9%), confirming that DecoyDB's broader PDB-derived coverage (beyond PDBbind) is critical for generalizability. DTA-based methods (DeepDTA, GraphDTA) and CNN-based methods (Pafnucy, OnionNet) remain 10–25% worse in RMSE than GNN-based approaches, underscoring the importance of explicit 3D interaction graph representations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends graph contrastive learning (GCL) to protein-ligand binding affinity prediction by constructing a purpose-built dataset (DecoyDB) of computationally generated decoy complexes, departing from prior GCL methods that rely on node/edge perturbation which can violate biochemical constraints. It builds directly on the denoising score matching (DSM) formulation of Zaidi et al. (2023) for the regularization term, and positions itself against ConBAP (Luo et al., 2024), which used binary decoy negatives derived solely from PDBbind (introducing pretraining/fine-tuning leakage) and lacked continuous negativeness weighting. The two-category InfoNCE loss with RMSD-scaled β weights represents a novel loss design that distinguishes near-native decoys from far-from-native ones and from entirely different complexes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DecoyDB addresses a fundamental bottleneck in structure-based drug discovery: the scarcity of labeled binding affinity data (PDBbind has <20K complexes) versus the abundance of unlabeled 3D structures in PDB. By filtering from the full PDB rather than PDBbind, the dataset avoids the leakage problem that plagued Redocked2020, enabling genuine generalizability evaluation. Open questions include how the choice of docking tool and its parameterization affects pretraining quality, whether the framework extends to binding pose estimation or virtual screening, and whether the 2 Å RMSD cutoff for positive/negative classification is optimal across different protein families. The paper does not explicitly discuss limitations of the dataset (e.g., bias toward X-ray structures, exclusion of certain ligand classes).
