## Problem

While differential privacy provides formal upper bounds on privacy loss and membership inference attacks (MIAs) provide practical lower bounds, the quantitative relationship between dataset properties (specifically examples per class) and MIA vulnerability in fine-tuned models remained uncharacterized. Prior work observed that more data reduces vulnerability, but no one had derived the rate of decrease or analyzed how the most vulnerable individual samples behave as dataset size grows, making it impossible to predict what dataset size is needed for adequate protection.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained feature extractor (ViT-Base-16 or ResNet-50) fine-tuned with a linear Head or FiLM layer on a VTAB subset dataset with S shots per class and C classes, along with M=256 shadow models trained on sampled subsets of the same distribution. It produces per-sample and aggregate membership predictions via LiRA or RMIA likelihood-ratio scores, vulnerability quantified as TPR at fixed FPR (ranging from 0.1 down to 0.001), and regression coefficients (β_S, β_C, β_0) that parameterize the power-law. The theoretical analysis operates on a simplified model where data points are vectors on a high-dimensional unit sphere classified by inner product with estimated class means, making LiRA provably optimal by the Neyman-Pearson lemma.

## Method

The paper introduces a simplified model of fine-tuning where class means are sampled orthogonally on a high-dimensional sphere, data points are Gaussian around these means, and classification uses inner products with the empirical mean. Within this model, they prove that per-example LiRA vulnerability follows a power-law log(TPR−FPR) ≈ −½ log S + constant, with the exponent −½ arising from the 1/√S scaling of the mean shift between IN and OUT score distributions. They extend this to average-case vulnerability via the law of unconscious statistician and derive an analogous result for RMIA's pairwise likelihood-ratio formulation. Empirically, they fit a linear regression log₁₀(TPR−FPR) = β_S log₁₀(S) + β_C log₁₀(C) + β_0 across 12+ VTAB datasets and 6 seeds, achieving R²=0.930 at FPR=0.001, and validate generalization to ResNet-50 backbones, FiLM fine-tuning, and from-scratch training. They further analyze worst-case individual vulnerability (finding a slower decay slope of ≈−0.27 for the maximum) and extrapolate the power-law to estimate the dataset size needed to match formal DP bounds, concluding that tens of thousands of examples per class are required even at FPR=0.001.

## Results

The paper establishes a power-law relationship between MIA vulnerability and dataset size: log(TPR − FPR) ≈ −0.5·log(S) + constant, confirmed both theoretically (Theorem 2) and empirically with R² = 0.930 for the regression model at FPR = 0.001. The fitted coefficient β_S is approximately −0.5 across FPR values, matching the theoretical prediction. For worst-case (maximum individual) vulnerability, the decrease is slower with a slope of −0.2695, and extrapolation to match DP guarantees (δ = 10⁻⁵) requires up to 5.5 × 10⁹ examples per class at ε = 0.25, FPR = 0.1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LiRA and RMIA are the two primary attacks compared. Both exhibit the predicted power-law in vulnerability vs. shots S, but RMIA is notably more unstable than LiRA, especially at lower FPR values (Figure 3). The regression model trained on ViT-B (Head) data generalizes well to ResNet-50 (Head) with test R² = 0.790, confirming the power-law is architecture-robust. However, for from-scratch training (using Carlini et al. 2022 data), the model underestimates vulnerability, suggesting that fine-tuning is inherently less vulnerable than training from scratch. The empirical β_S ≈ −0.5 closely matches the theoretical prediction of −0.5 from the simplified model, while the worst-case slope of −0.2695 is substantially flatter, indicating that the most vulnerable individual samples require far more data to protect than the average case.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the LiRA (Carlini et al., 2022) and RMIA (Zarifzadeh et al., 2024) attack frameworks, extending them from empirical evaluation tools to objects of theoretical analysis. Prior work has noted qualitatively that MIA vulnerability decreases with more training data and increases with more classes, but this paper is the first to derive a closed-form power-law (exponent −1/2) relating shots per class to vulnerability at fixed FPR, using a simplified linear-classifier model where LiRA is provably optimal by the Neyman-Pearson lemma. It also extends the analysis to worst-case individual sample vulnerability, a direction recently highlighted by Guépin et al. (2024) and Meeus et al. (2024), and connects the empirical power-law to formal DP bounds via the Kairouz et al. (2015) translation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical takeaway is that while average-case MIA vulnerability drops as S^{-1/2}, the worst-case (most vulnerable sample) vulnerability decays much more slowly (empirically ~S^{-0.27}), implying that protecting even a single adversarially chosen sample to match meaningful DP guarantees would require billions of examples per class—far beyond practical dataset sizes. This sharply delineates the regime where simply collecting more data is insufficient and formal DP mechanisms become necessary. Open questions include whether the power-law holds for from-scratch training (where the paper's regression model underestimates vulnerability), how pre-training data properties interact with fine-tuning vulnerability, and whether the simplified Gaussian/linear model captures the behavior of deeper architectures.
