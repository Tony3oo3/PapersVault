## Problem

It is widely assumed that explicit process-level supervision via Process Reward Models (PRMs) is necessary to develop models capable of evaluating intermediate reasoning steps. This paper challenges that assumption by asking whether pure RL training—using only rule-based outcome rewards—can implicitly endow models with strong PRM capabilities, and whether existing PRMs actually improve performance of already-strong RL-trained reasoning models.

## Input / Output

For PRM evaluation, the model receives a problem-solution pair from PROCESSBENCH (GSM8K, MATH, OlympiadBench, OmniMath) and outputs a step-level correctness judgment scored as error rate, correct rate, and F1. For the Self-PRM reranking experiments, the model first generates k sampled solutions (k ∈ {8, 16, 32, 64}) to a math problem from AIME24, AIME25, or CNMO24, then scores each candidate using its own internal reward signal to select the best solution. The Self-REF variant additionally feeds the model's own generated solution as a reference signal into the PRM prompt to assess whether self-generated traces improve process-level judgment.

## Method

The paper conducts a systematic empirical study with three components. First, it evaluates PRM capabilities of RL-trained models (DeepSeek-R1, QwQ-32B) versus discriminative PRM baselines on PROCESSBENCH, finding that RL-trained models (F1 ≈ 83.5–83.7) substantially outperform all PRM-fine-tuned models (F1 ≤ 78.3), and chi-square tests confirm a statistically significant correlation between problem-solving accuracy and process-judgment F1. Second, it tracks both accuracy and PROCESSBENCH F1 during RL training of Qwen2.5-7B-Base with DAPO, showing that process-judgment gains emerge earlier and more steadily than accuracy gains, indicating co-evolution of the two competencies. Third, it introduces Self-PRM, where each model reranks its own k sampled solutions using internally derived reward scores (Best-of-N with Self-PRM), which consistently outperforms both majority voting and external PRM reranking (Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-72B) at larger k, though fine-grained analysis reveals precision below 10% on the hardest problems where the model overestimates the correctness of its own flawed solutions.

## Results

On PROCESSBENCH, RL-trained models DeepSeek-R1 (avg F1 = 83.5) and QwQ-32B (avg F1 = 83.7) surpass all discriminative PRM baselines, with the best PRM-trained model (Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-72B) reaching only 78.3 F1. On AIME24, Self-PRM achieves 90.0% at k=32/64 for QwQ-32B, outperforming majority voting (86.7%) and BoN with external PRM (86.7%). External PRM (Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-72B) provides no measurable gain over majority voting across all three benchmarks (AIME24, AIME25, CNMO24) for both QwQ-32B and DeepSeek-R1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On PROCESSBENCH, DeepSeek-R1 (F1 83.5) and QwQ-32B (F1 83.7) outperform all discriminative PRM baselines: Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-72B (78.3), RetrievalPRM-7B (65.8), Skywork-PRM-7B (42.1), Math-Shepherd-PRM-7B (31.5), and RLHFlow-PRM-Mistral-8B (28.4). Among proprietary models, o1-mini (F1 87.9) slightly edges out the RL-trained models, while GPT-4o-0806 (F1 61.9) is substantially weaker. In the reranking experiments (Table 4), BoN with external PRM (Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-72B) ties or underperforms majority voting across all k values and benchmarks — e.g., on AIME25 with QwQ-32B both plateau at 76.7% regardless of k. Self-PRM consistently beats both majority voting and external PRM at k≥16, gaining +3.3 points over majority voting on AIME24 (90.0 vs 86.7) and +6.6 points on CNMO24 (88.9 vs 83.3) for QwQ-32B at k=16. However, Self-PRM remains below the Pass@k upper bound (e.g., 90.0 vs 93.3 on AIME24 at k=32), and fine-grained analysis reveals precision as low as 3.6% on the hardest problems, indicating that reward alignment remains imperfect even in the strongest models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself against the growing PRM literature (PRM800K, Math-Shepherd, Skywork-PRM, GenPRM, RetrievalPRM) by questioning whether explicit process supervision is necessary at all. It builds on the RL-trained reasoning model lineage (DeepSeek-R1, QwQ-32B, Kimi 1.5, OpenReasonerZero) and the PROCESSBENCH benchmark, but carves new ground by being the first systematic empirical study of the intrinsic co-evolution between problem-solving accuracy and process-judgment capability during pure RL training. The Self-PRM framework repurposes the model's own internal reward signal as a process reward, departing from both externally trained discriminative PRMs and generative PRM prompting approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that pure RL implicitly induces PRM capability challenges a widely held assumption in the reasoning-LLM community and has direct practical implications: teams investing in PRM annotation pipelines may find that continued RL scaling is a more efficient path. However, the Self-PRM precision collapse on hard problems (<10% on CNMO24) reveals that reward alignment remains imperfect, and the entire study is confined to mathematical reasoning with verifiable answers. Open questions include whether the co-evolution mechanism generalizes to open-ended or multi-modal reasoning, whether the precision gap closes with further RL scaling, and what the underlying representational mechanism is that couples solving and judging.
