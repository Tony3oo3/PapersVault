## Problem

Learning latent representations for solutions of constrained optimization problems (COPs) with unknown cost functions is difficult because standard generative models like VAEs cannot reliably produce feasible structured outputs (e.g., valid paths or Hamiltonian cycles), and existing inverse optimization methods assume a single underlying cost function, making them unable to capture diverse agent behaviors without explicit labels. The discrete nature of feasible solution spaces also renders gradients with respect to model parameters zero almost everywhere, blocking end-to-end learning through solvers.

## Input / Output

The model takes as input a binary edge-usage vector representing an observed path or cycle in a fixed graph, optionally paired with problem requirements (start and target node indices for path planning, or a null requirement for Hamiltonian cycles). At inference, given a sampled latent vector and a (possibly unseen) start-target pair, it outputs a guaranteed-feasible path or cycle by decoding the latent vector into edge costs and running a COP solver (Dijkstra or TSP). It can also produce a full path distribution via KDE in latent space, per-path outlier scores based on quantile distances to the inferred distribution, and denoised versions of noisy observed paths.

## Method

IO-LVM is a VAE-like architecture where the decoder is replaced by a composition of a neural network mapping the latent vector to an unconstrained cost space and a black-box COP solver that maps costs plus problem requirements to a feasible solution, thereby guaranteeing output validity. Training uses a modified ELBO in which the reconstruction term is a Fenchel-Young loss computed with additive Gaussian perturbation on the cost vector; the gradient through the non-differentiable solver is estimated as x − ω(y + ε), yielding an unbiased estimator that requires only one solver call per sample per epoch (via linearity of expectation rather than Monte Carlo). A key design choice, justified by Proposition 1, is that when all feasible solutions have equal length (exactly for Hamiltonian cycles, approximately for real-world paths), the perturbation induces equal expected cost and variance across solutions, preventing the noise from biasing gradients toward particular paths. The encoder uses amortized inference with the reparameterization trick, and the decoder applies a Softplus activation to ensure positive edge costs, enabling the model to learn a distribution over cost functions rather than a single one.

## Results

On path distribution prediction (Table 3), IO-LVM achieves the best Jensen-Shannon divergence and RMSE across all three datasets: D_JS = 0.054±0.003 / RMSE = 0.151±0.034 (Single S&T), D_JS = 0.141±0.010 / RMSE = 0.96±0.11 (Ship), and D_JS = 0.289±0.008 / RMSE = 1.56±0.10 (Taxi). On full Hamiltonian cycle reconstruction (Table 1), IO-LVM(k=10) reaches 91.0% exact match on burma14(3feats.) and 77.1% on bayg29(3feats.), substantially outperforming VAE(k=10) at 78.4% and 46.0% respectively. The VAE baseline fails to converge (NC) on the sparse Ship dataset, while IO-LVM still achieves D_JS = 0.141.

## Baselines & Comparisons

IO-LVM outperforms the Perturbed Optimizer (PO) [7] on all three datasets for both D_JS and RMSE, with the largest gap on the Taxi dataset (D_JS 0.289 vs. 0.439; RMSE 1.56 vs. 2.61), indicating that modeling a latent distribution over costs captures path diversity better than a single-point cost estimate. Against the VAE baseline [18], IO-LVM wins on every metric where the VAE converges, and the VAE fails entirely (no convergence) on the sparse Ship dataset, where only ~2,500 paths exist relative to a 2,513-node graph. The naive Bayesian Optimization (BO) baseline, which tunes a single perturbation variance, is dominated by a wide margin across all settings (e.g., D_JS 0.777 vs. 0.054 on Single S&T). On TSPLIB full-cycle reconstruction (Table 1), IO-LVM(k=10) achieves 91.0% exact match on burma14(3feats.) versus 78.4% for VAE(k=10), and 31.9% on the hardest setting (bayg29, 50 features) versus 3.4% for VAE(k=10), confirming that the solver-in-the-loop decoding guarantees feasible outputs that VAEs cannot match.

## Relation to Prior Work

IO-LVM sits at the intersection of variational autoencoders and inverse optimization, adapting the VAE's amortized inference and ELBO framework to learn a distribution over COP cost functions rather than a single cost. The gradient estimation through the non-differentiable solver is inherited from the perturbed optimizer framework (Berthet et al., 2020) and the Fenchel-Young loss formalism (Blondel et al., 2020), but the key departure is the latent-space formulation: prior inverse optimization and inverse RL methods recover a single or context-conditioned cost, whereas IO-LVM learns a full posterior over costs, enabling unsupervised multi-agent modeling. The solver-in-the-loop decoding step distinguishes it from standard VAEs, which the paper shows produce infeasible structured outputs on route problems.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ability to learn a distribution over cost functions without agent labels addresses a practical gap in real-world route data where multiple agents with heterogeneous preferences coexist. The solver-in-the-loop guarantee of feasibility is a strong practical advantage over VAE-based approaches, and the interpretable latent space (capturing unobserved factors like ship width or directional cost biases) opens doors to analysis tasks like denoising and outlier detection. Limitations include the dependence on fast solvers (scaling bottleneck), the additive perturbation assumption requiring roughly equal-length feasible solutions (Proposition 1), and the restriction to problems with available black-box solvers. Open questions include whether alternative perturbation schemes can handle highly variable path lengths, and how the approach scales to much larger graphs or more complex COPs beyond shortest path and TSP.
