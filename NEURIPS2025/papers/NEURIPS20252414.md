## Problem

Gradient boosting and most modern ML methods are designed for scalar or Euclidean outputs, relying on algebraic operations (addition, subtraction, scalar multiplication) that do not exist in general metric spaces. Complex-structured data such as probability distributions, networks, SPD matrices, and compositional data reside in non-Euclidean geodesic spaces, leaving a gap in how to apply boosting-style ensemble learning to these outputs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252414/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a multivariate Euclidean predictor vector X ∈ R^p (with mixed continuous and categorical features) and produces a prediction in a geodesic metric space: a probability distribution in the 2-Wasserstein space, a graph Laplacian matrix for network outputs, a symmetric positive-definite matrix, or a point on the positive orthant of a unit sphere for compositional data. A key requirement is that the output space must admit a geodesic transport map T that extends any geodesic from an arbitrary starting point, which is specified per space (e.g., optimal transport maps for Wasserstein space, linear translation for Frobenius-metric matrix spaces, spherical rotation for compositional data).

## Method

FGBoost replaces the Euclidean residual (Y − F) with the geodesic γ connecting the current prediction to the observed response, and constructs the ensemble by sequentially 'adding' geodesics via a geodesic transport map T that extends each new geodesic from the current prediction point. At each boosting iteration, a decision tree is fitted to pseudo-residuals (the geodesics from current predictions to observations), with leaf values computed as Fréchet means that decouple into separate Fréchet mean problems over starting and ending points under the product metric d_G on the space of geodesics. The loss function is the squared d_G distance between the true geodesic (from reference point Y_0 to observation) and the ensemble's predicted geodesic. Theoretical guarantees (existence, uniqueness, and consistency of the solution) are established under the assumption that the output space is a Hadamard space, using empirical process theory to show uniform convergence of the empirical risk to its population counterpart.

## Results

FGBoost achieves the lowest mean squared prediction error (MSPE) across all simulated and real-world settings. On simulated distributional data at n=2000, FGBoost reaches MSPE 0.015, compared to 0.026 for the next-best method (FRF) and 0.036 for GFR. On simulated network data at n=2000, FGBoost achieves 4.996 versus 8.308 (FRF) and 13.459 (GFR). On real-world data, FGBoost attains MSPE 20.35 on human mortality distributions and 8.30 on NYC taxi networks, outperforming all five baselines in both cases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FGBoost outperforms all five baselines on every dataset and sample size evaluated. The closest competitor is Fréchet Random Forest (FRF, Capitaine et al. 2024), which is the only method that approaches FGBoost at small sample sizes (e.g., 0.048 vs 0.034 on distribution data at n=100), but FGBoost pulls ahead decisively as n grows (0.015 vs 0.026 at n=2000). Global Fréchet Regression (GFR, Petersen & Müller 2019) is a strong linear baseline but plateaus at larger n (0.036 on distributions, 13.459 on networks at n=2000), reflecting its inability to capture non-linear predictor–output relationships. Sufficient dimension reduction (SDR, Zhang et al. 2024) and single index Fréchet regression (IFR, Bhattacharjee & Müller 2023) perform worst among the structured methods, with IFR particularly struggling on network data (MSPE 16–17 across all n). Random forest weighted local linear Fréchet regression (RFWLLFR, Qiu et al. 2024) is consistently outperformed, suggesting that FGBoost's geodesic-based ensemble construction captures the output geometry more effectively than local linear smoothing. On real-world data, FGBoost reduces MSPE by 35% over GFR on human mortality (20.35 vs 31.41) and by 30% on taxi networks (8.30 vs 11.80).

## Relation to Prior Work

FGBoost extends Friedman's gradient boosting framework [19] to geodesic metric spaces by replacing Euclidean residuals with geodesics and introducing a geodesic transport map to make geodesic addition well-defined. It builds on the Fréchet regression paradigm of Petersen and Müller [47], which established regression for metric space-valued outputs, but departs from all prior non-Euclidean regression methods (GFR, SDR, IFR, FRF, RFWLLFR) that rely on restrictive structural assumptions such as linearity, single-index form, or low-dimensional manifold constraints. The paper also develops a novel SHAP extension for non-Euclidean outputs, replacing the standard prediction-difference with a metric distance. To the authors' knowledge, this is the first boosting framework that operates intrinsically in general geodesic spaces without embedding into Euclidean space.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FGBoost provides a principled, geometry-respecting alternative to ad-hoc Euclidean embeddings for regression with complex-structured outputs (distributions, networks, SPD matrices, compositional data), with theoretical guarantees (consistency) under the Hadamard space assumption. The geodesic transport map requirement (Assumption 1) is a key limitation: the method applies only to spaces where such maps are well-defined, and extending beyond Hadamard spaces or to non-Euclidean predictors remains open. The consistent and substantial performance gains over all baselines across four distinct data types suggest the geometric structure is genuinely informative rather than incidental, but the method's practical adoption will depend on the availability of transport maps for new domains and computational cost for high-dimensional outputs.
