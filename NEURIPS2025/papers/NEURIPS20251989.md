## Problem

While covariate shift (changing input distribution) is well-studied, concept shift—where the input-label relationship itself changes at test time—remains largely uncharacterized in terms of its effects on generalization behavior. The paper asks: how does the degree of concept shift alter the data dependence of prediction risk, and can more training data actually hurt generalization even in optimally regularized, correctly specified models?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251989/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The theoretical model takes P-dimensional Gaussian feature vectors with covariance Σ and noisy linear responses y = βᵀx + ξ, producing a deterministic prediction risk in the thermodynamic limit decomposed into bias and variance. For transformer experiments, the input is a sequence of in-context (x_i, y_i) pairs followed by a query point x_n in P=32 dimensions, and the output is the predicted response ŷ_n; concept shift is introduced by changing the coefficient vector β̃ for the query's true response while keeping in-context examples drawn from the original β. Classification experiments use 28×28 grayscale MNIST/FashionMNIST images with PCA-based feature shuffling to simulate concept shift, outputting test accuracy.

## Method

The paper derives an exact closed-form expression for ridge regression prediction risk under concept shift in the thermodynamic limit (N,P→∞, P/N→γ) using random matrix theory and the Stieltjes transform of the empirical covariance. The key structural insight is that concept shift enters only through the bias term (via the mismatch β−β̃), leaving the variance term completely unaffected, which enables clean separation of effects. For isotropic features, the analysis reveals a sharp phase transition at κ cos θ = 1/2 (where κ is the scaling factor and cos θ the coefficient alignment between training and test coefficients) that separates a weak-shift regime where more data improves generalization from a strong-shift regime where more data hurts. For anisotropic features, the paper shows that concept shift on low-variance features produces risk maxima at large N, while shift on high-variance features produces maxima at intermediate N, due to the different sample complexities of learning each scale. The theory is validated by training nanoGPT transformers (8 layers, 128-dim embeddings) to perform in-context linear regression, showing quantitative agreement between transformer ICL risk and the thermodynamic-limit predictions, and by classification experiments on MNIST/FashionMNIST with PCA-based feature shuffling.

## Results

The paper derives an exact thermodynamic-limit expression for ridge regression prediction risk under concept shift, revealing a sharp phase transition at κcosθ = 1/2 that separates weak (more data helps) from strong (more data hurts) concept shift regimes. For isotropic features with in-distribution optimal regularization (λ = γ/SNR), the risk at N=0 is κ²‖β‖² while the risk at N→∞ is (1−2κcosθ+κ²)‖β‖², making the transition independent of SNR. Transformer in-context learning experiments (P=32, SNR=128, nanoGPT with 8 layers) show strong quantitative agreement with the thermodynamic-limit theory, confirming that longer context degrades performance under strong concept shift. MNIST and FashionMNIST classification experiments (multinomial logistic regression, 2000 epochs) demonstrate qualitative agreement: when concept shift is strong and few PCA features remain robust, increasing training data size N reduces test accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is primarily a theoretical paper that does not benchmark against competing methods; instead, it validates its analytical predictions against empirical experiments. The key 'comparison' is between the thermodynamic-limit theory (optimally tuned ridge regression) and transformer-based in-context learning on finite-dimensional data (P=32), showing strong quantitative agreement in both isotropic and anisotropic settings. For anisotropic features, transformers pretrained on anisotropic data closely track the structured ridge regression curve (effectively implementing feature whitening with effective κ²=(1+κ₊²)/2), while transformers pretrained on isotropic data exhibit more pronounced nonmonotonic risk peaks than the isotropic ridge theory predicts. The paper explicitly distinguishes its nonmonotonicity from double descent (absent under optimal regularization) and from model misspecification effects, positioning concept shift as a fundamentally distinct mechanism for data-dependent performance degradation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the high-dimensional ridge regression analysis framework of Dobriban & Wager [17] and Hastie et al. [18] to the concept shift setting, building directly on the thermodynamic-limit trace formulas of Patil et al. [30] for out-of-distribution prediction. It deliberately departs from the dominant covariate shift literature by isolating the case where the input-label relationship changes at test time, and from double descent work by using optimally tuned regularization to eliminate that confound. The transformer experiments connect to the in-context learning literature (Garg et al. [31], Akyürek et al. [32], Raventós et al. [33]) by testing whether the analytical predictions survive in a learned, data-driven algorithm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first exact analytical characterization of when and why more training data degrades generalization under concept shift, identifying a sharp phase transition (κcosθ = 1/2) that cleanly separates regimes where data helps versus hurts. This has direct practical implications for in-context learning (longer context can be detrimental) and for understanding why models overfit to spurious training-time relationships. Key limitations include restriction to linear models in the thermodynamic limit, discrete (not gradual) shifts, and the absence of a mitigation algorithm; extending to nonlinear systems and continuous drift remains open.
