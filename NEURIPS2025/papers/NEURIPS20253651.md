## Problem

The standard reward hypothesis in RL assumes a single scalar reward suffices to capture an agent's preferences, but certain preference structures—such as strict safety-first priorities—cannot be represented by any scalar function. While Hausner's 1954 theorem showed that dropping the Continuity axiom from von Neumann–Morgenstern expected utility yields lexicographically ordered vector utilities, it neither specifies the dimensionality of those vectors nor extends to sequential decision-making. This paper identifies a simple, interpretable condition (a 'safety-first' axiom) that forces a 2-dimensional lexicographic reward and develops the full axiomatic framework for lexicographic MDPs.

## Input / Output

The framework takes as input a preference relation over distributions on sequences of abstract events (E*) in an MDP, structured by axioms: Completeness, Transitivity, Independence, Memorylessness, and optionally a Safety-First axiom or Temporal γ-Indifference. The output is a d-dimensional (typically 2-dimensional) linear lexicographic utility function u: Δ(E*) → R^d satisfying a recursive equation u(e·τ) = r(e) + Γ(e)u(τ), where r: E → R^d are per-event reward vectors and Γ: E → L_+^{d×d} ∪ {0} are transition-dependent lower-triangular reward multipliers. In the 2D safety-first case, the first component encodes probability of safety minus 1 and the second encodes expected utility conditional on safety, making the utility of any single trajectory directly evaluable without aggregation.

## Method

The paper extends Hausner's lexicographic expected utility theorem to sequential settings by introducing a Memorylessness axiom (preferences are unaffected by prepending an event, or the event is terminal), yielding a general d-dimensional recursive utility structure (Theorem 3) and a discounted variant under Temporal γ-Indifference (Theorem 4). It then introduces a Safety-First axiom stating that any infinitesimal probability of a critically unsafe outcome dominates any amount of safe utility, which provably violates Continuity and forces a 2-dimensional lexicographic representation (Theorem 5). The sequential extension (Theorem 6) produces an explicit piecewise recursive equation where unsafe events reset the utility to (−1, 0) and safe events update both dimensions via a structured 2×2 lower-triangular multiplier. A key result (Theorem 7) proves the Fundamental Theorem of MDPs carries over: a stationary deterministic uniformly optimal policy exists and is characterized by lexicographic greediness with respect to Q*, in sharp contrast to CMDPs where such policies may not exist and randomization may be required.

## Results

This is a purely theoretical paper with no experimental results or quantitative benchmarks. The main contributions are axiomatic characterizations: (1) a 2-dimensional lexicographic utility function (Theorem 5) that arises from a simple 'Safety First' axiom, where the first dimension encodes probability of safety and the second encodes expected utility given safety; (2) a sequential extension (Theorem 6) yielding a recursive utility equation with event-dependent reward multipliers; and (3) a Fundamental Theorem of LMDPs (Theorem 7) proving that stationary deterministic uniformly optimal policies exist, mirroring the scalar MDP case. The paper also shows that CMDPs cannot in general be reformulated as LMDPs (Section 8) because CMDPs violate the Independence axiom.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper contains no experimental baselines or quantitative comparisons, as it is a theoretical/axiomatic contribution. The closest analogue to a 'comparison' is the conceptual contrast between LMDPs and CMDPs (Sections 7–8). The key distinction is that LMDPs preserve the Independence axiom (violating only Continuity), which guarantees the existence of stationary deterministic uniformly optimal policies, whereas CMDPs violate both Independence and Continuity, so optimal policies may require randomization and may depend on the starting state distribution. Additionally, LMDPs allow per-run evaluation of a single trajectory (assigning a utility vector to each event sequence), while CMDPs only constrain expected values, making individual runs unevaluable. The paper also contrasts lexicographic optimization with penalty and constrained approaches in Appendix B, showing that lexicographic minimization yields the lowest-risk solution where penalty methods trade off risk for cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Hausner's (1954) lexicographic expected utility theorem—which generalizes vNM expected utility by dropping the Continuity axiom—to the sequential decision-making setting, building directly on the memorylessness axiom of Shakerinava & Ravanbakhsh (2022) and the temporal γ-indifference axiom of Bowling et al. (2023). Unlike prior lexicographic RL work (Gabor et al., 1998; Skalse et al., 2022; Wray et al., 2015) that proposes algorithms for a given lexicographic objective, this paper provides the axiomatic justification for *when* lexicographic objectives are necessary, introducing a simple 'Safety First' condition that pins the utility dimensionality to 2. The paper also draws a sharp contrast with the CMDP framework, showing that LMDPs violate only Continuity while CMDPs violate both Continuity and Independence, which explains why LMDPs retain fundamental MDP policy properties that CMDPs lose.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, axiomatic answer to the question of when scalar rewards are fundamentally insufficient, which is directly relevant to AI safety settings where safety must take strict, non-compensatory precedence over other objectives. The result that LMDPs admit stationary, deterministic, uniformly optimal policies (unlike CMDPs) is practically significant because it means standard MDP solution techniques (value iteration, policy iteration) carry over with only lexicographic comparisons replacing scalar ones. Key limitations include the absence of new algorithmic methods (the paper relies on prior work for algorithms), the restriction to settings where objectives genuinely cannot be traded off, and the focus on environment-independent reward specification. Open questions include developing efficient algorithms for LMDPs (the Bellman equation is not a contraction, requiring slack-based approximations) and exploring concrete AI safety applications such as maintaining safety guardrails for superintelligent agents.
