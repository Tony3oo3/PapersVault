## Problem

Existing adversarial attacks on stereo depth estimation (e.g., Stereoscopic, Stereopagnosia) rely on full-image perturbations that are impractical in the physical world, and when naively deployed as localized patches they fail to mislead modern stereo models. The paper identifies that simple texture repetition within a patch does not generate sufficient matching ambiguity, and discovers that introducing regular spatial intervals (grid structure) among repeated textures dramatically enhances both digital and physical attack effectiveness.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250072/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a stereo image pair (left and right, e.g., 1242×375 for KITTI or 1758×800 for DrivingStereo) along with a user-specified physical patch size (0.891 m × 1.26 m) and ground-truth distance (5 m), and produces a printable adversarial patch whose texture elements are tiled in a 5×4 grid with optimized interval spacing. The attack objective is to drive the predicted disparity in the patch region to zero (infinite depth), making the patch 'disappear' from the depth map despite being physically close.

## Method

DepthVanish jointly optimizes a single texture element E that encodes both the adversarial texture content and the interval structure, then tiles E in a regular grid to form the final patch. The optimization minimizes a composite loss: (1) regional MSE between the attacked and clean disparity predictions over the patch region, (2) an entropy regularization that pushes E toward binary values to form crisp interval boundaries, and (3) a total variation loss that encourages spatially coherent regions within each texture tile. Gradients are averaged across all k×k grid positions where E is repeated, so a single optimized element propagates its effect across the entire patch. This joint texture-and-structure optimization outperforms a grid-based baseline (which uses fixed intervals and optimizes only the texture content) and achieves near-complete 'disappearance' of the patch in depth predictions across PSMNet, DeepPruner, AANet, RAFT-Stereo, and STTR, with strong transferability to the Intel RealSense D435i in physical deployment.

## Results

On KITTI-scene, DepthVanish achieves 89.31% D1-error and 66.01 EPE against RAFT-Stereo, 92.38% D1 and 69.25 EPE against STTR, and 97.07% D1 against DeepPruner, indicating near-complete patch 'disappearance' in predicted depth. The grid-based variant (without joint structure optimization) already reaches 40.09% D1 on RAFT-Stereo and 67.24 EPE, far exceeding both baselines. Physical deployment on the Intel RealSense D435i camera confirms consistent attack transferability across models, viewing angles, and distances.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DepthVanish decisively outperforms both existing baselines across all five target models. Against RAFT-Stereo, DepthVanish achieves 89.31% D1-error versus only 5.79% (Stereoscopic [1]) and 4.18% (Stereopagnosia [31])—a roughly 15× improvement. On STTR, the gap is even wider: 92.38% D1 for DepthVanish versus 4.58% and 3.02% for the two baselines. The EPE metric tells a similar story, with DepthVanish reaching 66.01 on RAFT-Stereo compared to 3.58 and 2.09 for the baselines. The authors note the comparison is not perfectly fair since Stereoscopic and Stereopagnosia were designed for full-image digital attacks rather than localized physical patches, which partly explains their near-zero effectiveness under the patch deployment protocol. The intermediate Grid-based variant (fixed intervals, optimized texture only) already surpasses both baselines on EPE across all models, confirming that the interval structure itself is the key driver of attack effectiveness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of adversarial attacks on stereo depth estimation initiated by Stereoscopic [1] and Stereopagnosia [31], which optimized repetitive textures as full-image digital perturbations. The key conceptual departure is the discovery that naively tiling such textures into a localized patch fails in physical deployment, and that introducing regular spatial intervals (grid structure) among the repeated texture elements is what enables effective physical patch attacks. Methodologically, the paper builds on the tiling-based optimization of [1] but adds a joint optimization of texture content and interval structure via entropy and total variation regularization, moving from a purely digital full-image paradigm to a physically deployable patch paradigm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first adversarial patch attack demonstrated to be effective in both digital and physical settings against modern stereo depth estimators (RAFT-Stereo, STTR) and a commercial RGB-D camera (Intel RealSense D435i), exposing a concrete safety vulnerability in autonomous driving and robotics pipelines. The 'disappearance' attack objective—making a nearby object appear at infinite depth—is particularly dangerous for collision avoidance. However, the physical evaluation relies on manually captured stereo pairs with imprecise alignment, and the paper does not explore defenses; open questions include whether structured-interval patches generalize to multi-camera or event-based stereo systems, and whether adversarial training can mitigate this specific structural vulnerability.
