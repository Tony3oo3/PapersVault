## Problem

Existing time series forecasting models apply a single uniform loss (e.g., MSE) across the entire output sequence, treating seasonal and trend components identically despite their fundamentally different error sensitivities. Seasonal oscillations are highly sensitive to phase and amplitude distortions, while trend components tolerate local errors but suffer from long-term drift under overly strict constraints. This mismatch between representation-level decomposition and supervision-level uniformity limits forecasting accuracy and long-term stability.

## Input / Output

The framework is a model-agnostic loss plug-in that operates on any forecasting backbone's output: given a multivariate input window of length L=96 across C channels, the backbone produces H-step predictions (H ∈ {96, 192, 336, 720}). Before loss computation, both the prediction and ground truth sequences are decomposed into seasonal and trend components via a shared moving-average filter (kernel size 25, or a learnable multi-kernel variant). The loss is computed separately on each decomposed component rather than on the raw sequence, making the framework compatible with any architecture that outputs a full forecast sequence.

## Method

PMLF assigns structurally distinct loss functions to each decomposed component, grounded in physical analogies from molecular dynamics. Seasonal components are penalized with a quadratic loss (equivalent to MSE on the seasonal residual), reflecting the harmonic potential energy profile of molecular vibrations where small displacements produce rapid energy increases. Trend components are penalized with a logarithmic loss, log(1 + |error|), reflecting the sublinear energy accumulation of structural drift under sustained external forces where restoring resistance saturates. A softmax-based dynamic weighting mechanism adaptively balances the two losses during training by computing weights from exponentially scaled, gradient-detached loss values, preventing the slowly accumulating trend loss from dominating gradient updates and suppressing high-frequency seasonal corrections.

## Results

PMLF achieves the best MSE and MAE across all three datasets (ETTh2, ETTm2, Weather) in the loss-function comparison (Table 2), e.g., on ETTh2 at horizon 720 it reaches MSE 0.403 / MAE 0.423 versus 0.446 / 0.455 for standard MSE. Across the five SOTA backbones in Table 1, PMLF yields improvements in 93.75% (60/64) of iTransformer comparisons, with MAE gains typically exceeding MSE gains, reflecting the trend-aware logarithmic penalty. Robustness runs (5 seeds) show standard deviations below 0.005 on all reported metrics, and the dynamic-weighting hyperparameter β is insensitive over the range 0.2–5 (max deviation < 2.5%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PMLF is evaluated as a model-agnostic loss plug-in on seven architectures spanning Transformer (iTransformer, TimeXer, PatchTST), state-space (S-Mamba), MLP (Amplifier, TimeMixer), and CNN (TimesNet) families. Against the standard MSE baseline, PMLF improves both MSE and MAE in 93.75% of iTransformer comparisons (60/64) and shows consistent gains across all other backbones and all eight datasets. In the head-to-head loss comparison (Table 2), PMLF outperforms every competing objective—Soft-DTW, Huber, TILDE-Q, and FreDF—on all three datasets and all four horizons for both MSE and MAE; the largest margin is on ETTh2 at H=720, where PMLF reduces MSE by 4.3% (0.403 vs. 0.422 for FreDF) and MAE by 3.0% (0.423 vs. 0.436). MAE improvements are systematically larger than MSE improvements, confirming that the logarithmic trend loss contributes most to long-horizon stability. Ablation (Table 3) shows that removing either the seasonal quadratic loss or the trend logarithmic loss degrades performance substantially (e.g., ETTh2 H=336 MSE jumps from 0.385 to 0.670 without L_s), and fixing the softmax weights instead of using dynamic balancing also hurts accuracy, validating both structural components of the framework.

## Relation to Prior Work

PMLF sits at the intersection of two established lines of work: multiscale decomposition in forecasting (Autoformer, TimeMixer) and structure-aware loss design (Soft-DTW, TILDE-Q, FreDF). Rather than introducing a new architecture, it repurposes the seasonal-trend decomposition already present in many backbones and applies it at the loss level, assigning a quadratic penalty to seasonal errors and a logarithmic penalty to trend errors. The physics analogy (harmonic molecular vibration vs. structural drift under sustained force) provides a conceptual justification for this asymmetric supervision, but the resulting loss functions are essentially MSE and a log-robust variant, making the contribution primarily one of principled loss composition and dynamic balancing rather than novel mathematical machinery.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a model-agnostic, plug-and-play loss modification can yield consistent (if modest) improvements across diverse architectures and datasets, suggesting that the mismatch between uniform supervision and heterogeneous temporal structure is a real and exploitable gap. However, the physics framing, while elegant, is somewhat post-hoc: the quadratic loss for seasonal components reduces to standard MSE, and the logarithmic trend loss is a known robust-loss variant, so the core insight is really 'use different loss shapes for different components with adaptive weighting.' The stated limitation—that PMLF assumes separable multiscale structure and may underperform on single-scale or regime-shift signals—leaves open the question of how to detect when decomposition is appropriate and how to extend the framework to non-stationary or abrupt-change settings.
