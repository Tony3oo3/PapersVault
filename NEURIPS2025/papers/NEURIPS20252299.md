## Problem

Standard evaluations of language model reasoning emphasize correctness of the final answer while overlooking efficiency—how well a model avoids unnecessary inference steps when much of the available information is irrelevant. The paper asks whether current LMs can identify and ignore distracting axioms, and quantifies how far their generated proofs deviate from the shortest possible proof in a formal logic program.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252299/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The input is a natural language GSM-style math word problem whose axioms are drawn from a verbalized logic program, augmented with 1 to 3 irrelevant distractor axioms (or trees of axioms) that vary in agent/entity overlap with the goal theorem. The LM produces a natural language proof, which is parsed back into a sequence of grounded atoms via hand-written templates (each atom maps to a disjoint set of surface strings ending in a period). The output is both the final goal theorem answer and an efficiency score defined as the ratio of shortest-proof vertices to the LM's proof vertices, with a secondary metric restricting to non-axiom theorems to separate genuine detours from mere restatement of irrelevant premises.

## Method

The paper formalizes deductive reasoning as shortest-path search in a directed hypergraph induced by a typed logic program with built-in arithmetic and set-theoretic predicates, where each inference rule becomes a hyperedge from premise atoms to a conclusion atom. To bridge formal proofs and natural language, the authors introduce verbalized logic programs: a verbalizer maps each grounded atom to a disjoint set of surface strings, enabling linear-time parsing of LM output back into a proof forest. Efficiency is measured as |P*|/|P|, the ratio of the shortest (A,h_g)-proof size to the LM's proof size, which disentangles inefficiency from unnecessary deduction steps versus verbosity in verbalization. Empirically, the authors construct 500 GSM problems with injected irrelevant axioms varying in structural complexity (single axiom, single tree, multiple trees) and semantic overlap (none, entity, agent, both), then evaluate four LMs via 5-shot in-context learning with DFS-ordered proof demonstrations, reporting accuracy, efficiency, and the proportion of correct intermediate theorems recovered.

## Results

On base GSM problems, DeepSeek-R1 achieves near-perfect accuracy (99.4%), while Llama-3.1-8B reaches 65.0% and Qwen2.5-Math-7B only 52.8%. Introducing irrelevant axioms degrades accuracy across all models: Llama-3.1-8B drops to 41.0% with multiple irrelevant trees, and Qwen2.5-Math-7B falls to 23.5%. On proof efficiency (ratio of shortest-proof size to generated-proof size), Llama-3.1-8B scores 60.2% and Qwen2.5-Math-7B scores 43.7% under no-overlap non-ground queries, dropping to 23.6% and 25.3% respectively when both agent and entity overlap with the goal—indicating that over half of the theorems the models generate are irrelevant to the proof.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates four LMs—Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct, QwQ-32B, and DeepSeek-R1—on the same verbalized logic program problems. DeepSeek-R1 is nearly saturated at ~98–99% accuracy across all conditions, showing minimal sensitivity to irrelevant axioms. Llama-3.1-8B and Qwen2.5-Math-7B suffer substantial accuracy drops (14–29 percentage points) when irrelevant axioms are added, and their efficiency scores (43–60%) reveal that they generate many theorems not needed for the shortest proof. QwQ-32B is an outlier: its control-problem accuracy (76.2% for single-axiom, 65.4% for multi-tree) exceeds its base accuracy (63.4%), suggesting it benefits from longer, all-relevant inputs, yet it still drops sharply under irrelevance (to 50.1% for multi-tree). Across all models, accuracy on irrelevant-axiom problems is consistently lower than on same-length control problems, confirming that the degradation is caused by irrelevance rather than input length. On the efficiency metric, Llama-3.1-8B outperforms Qwen2.5-Math-7B by roughly 15–20 percentage points across all overlap conditions, and both models show the largest efficiency drops when irrelevant axioms share agents or entities with the goal query.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the logic programming tradition (Kowalski, 1974) and the GSM8k math word problem benchmark, adapting the logic program structure from Opedal et al. (2023, 2025) to formalize deductive reasoning over grade school arithmetic. It departs from prior work on irrelevant information in reasoning (Shi et al., 2023; Mirzadeh et al., 2025), which typically appends a single irrelevant statement, by systematically varying the number and structural complexity of irrelevant axioms and controlling for agent/entity overlap. The key methodological departure is the introduction of 'verbalized logic programs,' which map natural-language proofs onto formal proof forests, enabling an efficiency metric that disentangles unnecessary inference steps from mere verbal verbosity—something token-count-based efficiency measures (Arora & Zanette, 2025; Han et al., 2025) conflate.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that LMs produce proofs containing a large fraction of irrelevant theorems even when they arrive at the correct answer (efficiency scores as low as ~34%) reframes the efficiency problem: it is not merely verbosity but genuine logical detours. The observation that lexical overlap between the query and irrelevant axioms systematically degrades both accuracy and efficiency suggests LMs rely on surface-level heuristics rather than goal-directed logical search, a concern for deploying LMs in settings with noisy or adversarial context. Limitations include the restriction to GSM-style problems, the fact that only two of four models could be fully analyzed for proof efficiency due to formatting compliance, and the somewhat artificial nature of the verbalization templates. Open questions include whether the framework generalizes to other reasoning domains (e.g., natural language inference, planning) and whether training with efficiency-aware rewards could reduce irrelevant inference.
