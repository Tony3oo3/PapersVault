## Problem

Despite CLIP's success in cross-modal retrieval, a persistent modality gap limits fine-grained discrimination between semantically similar items. While off-the-shelf MLLMs exhibit strong inherent cross-modal alignment (quantified via a unified Wasserstein-distance metric), converting them into retrievers with standard contrastive losses diminishes this alignment capability. No prior method systematically transfers an MLLM's fine-grained alignment priors into the embedding space of a retrieval model.

## Input / Output

The policy model (Qwen2-VL-2B or 7B, fine-tuned with LoRA) takes RGB images (capped at 384×384) and text queries, producing normalized embeddings via bidirectional attention and mean-pooling over final hidden states. An off-the-shelf MLLM (e.g., Qwen2-VL-7B) serves as a frozen reward model that scores image-text alignment by computing softmax over 'Yes'/'No' token logits from a relevance prompt. The final output is a retrieval ranking over a gallery, evaluated via Recall@1 on general benchmarks and binary correct/incorrect matching scores on fine-grained benchmarks (Winoground, NaturalBench, MMVP, BiVLC).

## Method

MAPLE operates in two stages. First, preference data construction: offline, DINOv2 embeddings with SemDeDup clustering identify hard negatives, and a large MLLM (Qwen2.5-VL-72B) generates comparative captions highlighting inter-image differences; online, a frozen MLLM computes alignment scores for each anchor-candidate pair, which are sorted to form pairwise and listwise preference structures. Second, the Relative Preference Alignment (RPA) loss adapts DPO to embedding learning by replacing log-probabilities with scaled dot-product similarity scores, eliminating the reference model via a uniform prior. The listwise variant (inspired by PRO) applies a weighted softmax over each suffix of the MLLM's ranked list, with weights proportional to the average preference margin, while the pairwise variant uses margin-weighted sigmoid losses. The total objective combines RPA with a standard contrastive regularizer (λ-weighted) to prevent feature collapse, and an expanded negative pool from hard negatives implicitly enlarges the effective batch size without extra compute.

## Results

MAPLE(Qwen2-VL-7B) achieves 75.5/60.3 R@1 on COCO (Text/Image) and 94.3/86.1 on Flickr30K, outperforming all CLIP-based and MLLM-based baselines. The gains are most pronounced on fine-grained retrieval: MAPLE reaches 56.0/32.7 on Winoground and 76.1/76.8 on NaturalBench, surpassing the strongest prior method (VladVA-LLaVA-1.5-7B at 40.5/17.5 on Winoground) by 15.5 and 15.2 points respectively. Even the smaller MAPLE(Qwen2-VL-2B) variant (43.0/22.5 on Winoground) exceeds all 7B+ baselines on fine-grained tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MAPLE(Qwen2-VL-7B) outperforms all eight baselines across every reported benchmark. Against the strongest CLIP-based model (SigLIPv2, 2B params), MAPLE gains +2.7/+4.2 on COCO and a dramatic +16.2/+15.7 on Winoground, confirming that the modality gap in dual-encoder architectures severely limits fine-grained discrimination. Against the closest MLLM-based competitor VladVA(LLaVA-1.5-7B), MAPLE improves Winoground by +15.5/+15.2 and COCO Image by +1.3, while matching Flickr30K Text (94.3). The 2B MAPLE variant already surpasses VladVA-7B on Winoground (43.0 vs 40.5 Text), suggesting the preference-alignment strategy is more effective than simply scaling the MLLM backbone. On NaturalBench, MAPLE(7B) leads the next-best method (SigLIPv2 at 65.5/68.7) by +10.6/+8.1, and on MMVP/BiVLC (Table 3) it achieves 45.9/43.7 and 89.4/75.5 respectively, again the best among all compared methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) CLIP-style contrastive retrieval, which suffers from a well-documented modality gap; (2) MLLM-based retrievers (E5-V, VladVA, MM-Embed) that partially close this gap through unified architectures but still rely on coarse contrastive alignment; and (3) DPO from LLM alignment, which the authors adapt to the embedding setting via a novel Relative Preference Alignment (RPA) loss. The key conceptual departure from prior MLLM-based retrievers is treating an off-the-shelf MLLM not as the retrieval model itself but as a frozen reward model whose yes/no logits generate fine-grained preference signals, which are then distilled into a separate trainable MLLM-based retriever. The unified Wasserstein-distance metric for comparing modality gap across logit-based and embedding-based architectures is a new analytical tool that prior work (which relied on mean-embedding distances) did not provide.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that off-the-shelf MLLMs exhibit substantially stronger inherent cross-modal alignment than CLIP (as quantified by the Wasserstein metric) reframes the modality gap problem: the gap is not an irreducible property of contrastive training but can be actively corrected by transferring alignment priors from generative models. The RPA loss offers a practical, reference-model-free adaptation of DPO to embedding spaces that could be broadly applicable beyond retrieval. However, the approach inherits potential biases from the reward MLLM, requires significant compute for online scoring during training, and has not been validated on more complex tasks like composed or multi-hop retrieval. Whether preference-guided alignment can replace or complement contrastive pretraining at scale remains an open question.
