## Problem

Existing LLM knowledge editing benchmarks are confined to narrow knowledge domains, evaluate only a limited subset of generality and locality criteria, and are constructed independently per criterion without a unified framework. This makes it impossible to assess how editors handle complex, multi-criteria ripple effects (e.g., a sample that simultaneously involves multi-hop reasoning, relation reversal, and subject aliasing) across diverse open-domain knowledge.

## Input / Output

Each of the 311K UNIEDIT entries consists of three linked components: an editing triple (s, r, o) drawn from Wikidata, a generality sample (a multi-hop QA chain of up to 4 hops that must contain the full edit triple), and a locality sample (a multi-hop chain that excludes or partially overlaps the edit triple, classified into six cross-feature types such as subject-crossed, relation-crossed, object-crossed, etc.). The cloze target is a node in the chain, producing either a single-chain prompt (endpoint) or a double-chain prompt (intermediate node). Final prompts are natural-language sentences generated by DeepSeek-V3 from the structured subgraphs, covering 25 domains across five sectors.

## Method

The core contribution is the Neighborhood Multi-hop Chain Sampling (NMCS) algorithm, which unifies and extends all prior evaluation criteria under a single subgraph-sampling procedure. Given an initial triple, NMCS expands both sides to build an acyclic chain of up to 4 hops, then selects a node as the prediction target and extends from both sides to form valid multi-hop QA chains, while preventing intermediate hops from being multi-valued to maintain prompt clarity. For generality, sampling starts from the edit triple itself; for locality, it starts from a triple sharing only the subject, relation, object, or nothing with the edit triple, thereby systematically covering all six locality cross-feature types. The data pipeline begins with 29.9M cleaned Wikidata entities and 2,500 properties, uses domain-specific keyword retrieval via Elasticsearch with sequential weighted sampling (with similarity-based decay) to select 30K head entities per domain, and finally converts sampled subgraphs into grammatically diverse natural-language prompts using DeepSeek-V3 with automated and human quality checks.

## Results

On UNIEDIT, SERAC achieves the highest average editing score across all three backbones (88.77 on GPT2-XL, 89.02 on GPT-J, 88.96 on LLaMa-3.1), followed closely by IKE (86.58, 87.72, 87.95). Fine-Tuning reaches perfect reliability (100%) but only moderate generality (49.46–69.00%), while L&E methods like ROME and AlphaEdit show severe generality deficits (35.84–55.10%) despite high locality scores (>95%). GRACE achieves near-perfect locality (99.97–99.99%) but the worst generality among high-reliability editors (28.00–51.89%), revealing a strong locality–generality trade-off. Pre-edit LLMs score only 29.69–43.68% on reliability, confirming the long-tail nature of open-domain knowledge.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SERAC (edit-training-based) leads on average score across all three backbones, outperforming IKE by ~2 points and FT by ~1–9 points, primarily due to superior generality (78.79–83.66% vs. 76.46–89.52% for IKE and 49.46–69.00% for FT). Among L&E methods, ROME and AlphaEdit achieve high reliability (>92%) but their generality scores (35.84–55.10%) are roughly 20–30 points below SERAC, confirming that direct backpropagation through edit statements fails to propagate knowledge to related queries. GRACE achieves the best locality (99.97–99.99%) but the worst generality among high-reliability editors (28.00–51.89%), illustrating that its token-based linear distance retrieval prevents interference at the cost of generalization. T-Patcher underperforms all other editors on LLaMa-3.1 (68.71 avg.), suggesting architecture-specific fragility. IKE's in-context learning approach yields the best generality on LLaMa-3.1 (89.52%) but at the cost of the lowest locality (80.79%), highlighting the generality–locality trade-off inherent in prior-weighting strategies.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies the fragmented landscape of knowledge editing benchmarks (ZSRE, CounterFact, MQuAKE, BAKE, RippleEdit, ReCoE, EVOKE, etc.), each of which covers only a narrow subset of evaluation criteria (rephrase, multi-hop, relation reversal, subject alias, 1-N forgetfulness, etc.) within limited knowledge domains. The key methodological departure is the Neighborhood Multi-hop Chain Sampling (NMCS) algorithm, which samples multi-hop chain subgraphs from Wikidata's knowledge graph to simultaneously instantiate multiple evaluation criteria within a single sample, rather than constructing separate datasets per criterion. The paper positions itself as an empirical evaluation study, applying existing editors (ROME, SERAC, AlphaEdit, T-Patcher, GRACE, IKE) as test subjects rather than proposing a new editing method.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

UNIEDIT provides the first open-domain, unified evaluation framework for knowledge editing, with 311K entries spanning 25 domains and all major generality/locality criteria, making it a likely standard benchmark for future editing research. The finding that L&E methods (ROME, AlphaEdit) drop to 35–55% generality on complex multi-criteria samples—despite near-perfect reliability—highlights a significant gap between reported and real-world editing robustness. Key limitations include English-only coverage, single-modality focus, and the reliance on DeepSeek-V3 for natural language generation, which may introduce systematic biases. Open questions include whether the NMCS framework extends to multimodal editing, non-English settings, and whether the domain-bias findings (worse performance in Social/Applied Sciences) can be addressed through targeted training data.
