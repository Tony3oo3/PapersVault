## Problem

Standard causal inference methods estimate average treatment effects but fail on rare, high-impact events (e.g., extreme climate disasters) where tail data is scarce and outcomes may be unbounded. Conversely, extreme value theory characterizes tail behavior but cannot accommodate counterfactual treatments or covariate adjustment. No existing framework bridges these two strands to quantify how a policy intervention shifts the tail average of an outcome distribution.

## Input / Output

The method consumes i.i.d. observations (X_i, D_i, Y_i, U_i) where X is a bounded covariate, D is binary treatment, Y is the outcome, and U is a multivariate regularly varying extreme variable (e.g., wind speed, rainfall, storm surge). A data-driven threshold t is selected via the adaptive Hill estimator, and a scaling exponent α̂_n (estimated heuristically by regressing log|Y| on log||U||) controls the polynomial normalization. The output is a single scalar NETE estimate θ̂_{n,t} = η̂_{n,t} · μ̂_n, where η̂ captures the spectral-measure treatment effect and μ̂ = 1/(1 − α̂_n γ̂_n) is the Pareto moment correction, accompanied by a non-asymptotic error bound that decomposes into nuisance, variance, EVI-estimation, and bias components.

## Method

The key insight is a spectral-magnitude decomposition: under multivariate regular variation, the norm ||U|| and direction U/||U|| become asymptotically independent, so NETE factors into (i) the expected treatment effect on the spectral measure (direction) and (ii) the α-th moment of the limiting Pareto norm. The spectral factor is estimated via sample-split IPW or doubly-robust estimators that regress the normalized pseudo-outcome Y/||U||^α̂ on (X, D, U/||U||) and the propensity D on X, while the Pareto moment is estimated by an adaptive Hill estimator with a data-driven threshold. The non-asymptotic analysis requires a Pareto-type structural assumption (U = AZ with Z approximately multivariate Pareto) to invoke Wasserstein-distance bounds on the spectral measure and concentration inequalities for the Hill estimator, yielding rates of O(n^{-s} log(1/δ)) or O(n^{-1/(2+max{β,1})} log(1/δ)) depending on the tail-regularity parameter s.

## Results

On the semi-synthetic wavesurge dataset, EVT-DR estimates range from 0.13 to 0.43 across four (α₁, α₂) configurations, closely tracking the test-set ground-truth values of 0.13–0.20. In contrast, the naive IPW and DR baselines overshoot the true NETE by roughly one to two orders of magnitude (estimates of 7.91–41.93). In the synthetic experiments (Figures 1–2), EVT-DR achieves the lowest MSE across most configurations of α, β, d_z, and d_u, and the method remains robust even when the Pareto assumption (Assumption 3.7) is violated by using a Pareto mixture.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its EVT-IPW and EVT-DR estimators against two naive baselines (Naive-IPW and Naive-DR) that apply standard causal inference estimators to extreme samples without leveraging the regularly varying tail structure. The naive methods consistently overshoot the true NETE by a factor of 40–300× (e.g., Naive-DR estimates 41.93 vs. a ground truth of 0.13 for (α₁,α₂)=(2,2)), because they ignore the Pareto scaling of the tail. EVT-DR is the most accurate estimator in 3 of 4 configurations, deviating by at most 0.3 from the test-set estimate, while EVT-IPW is competitive (within 0.05–0.31). In the synthetic experiments, EVT-DR achieves the smallest MSE in most settings, and both EVT-based methods substantially outperform the naive baselines across all tested configurations of α, β, and dimensionality. The advantage is most pronounced when the tail is heavier (smaller β) or the dimensionality is higher, where the naive estimators suffer from exploding variance due to unbounded ‖U‖.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously disconnected literatures: causal inference (IPW, doubly robust estimation, double machine learning à la Chernozhukov et al. 2018) and multivariate extreme value theory (regular variation, spectral measures, Hill estimation à la Boucheron & Thomas 2015 and Zhang et al. 2023). It departs from prior extreme treatment effect work (Chernozhukov & Du 2006, Zhang 2018, Deuber et al. 2024) which targets extreme quantile treatment effects using univariate EVT, by instead defining a normalized tail-conditional ATE (NETE) built on multivariate regular variation. The identification formula leverages the asymptotic independence of norm and direction in regularly varying vectors, a property from classical EVT that had not previously been exploited for causal estimation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper opens a principled path for causal inference in settings where the outcome of interest is only observable in rare, high-impact events (disasters, financial crises), where standard ATE estimators fail due to data scarcity and unbounded outcomes. Key limitations include the strong Pareto-type Assumption 3.7 needed for non-asymptotic bounds, the independence assumption (U ⊥ D,X) required for propensity-based estimation, and the heuristic estimation of the scaling exponent α. Open questions include theoretically grounded estimation of α, relaxing Assumption 3.7 to general second-order regular variation, and validating the framework on genuine observational causal problems rather than semi-synthetic data.
