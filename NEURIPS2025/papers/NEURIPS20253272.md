## Problem

Standard fairness auditing compares point estimates of metrics like statistical parity against fixed thresholds, treating a subgroup of 10 identically to one of 10,000 and ignoring sampling uncertainty. This brittleness is amplified in intersectional settings, where joint sensitive attributes produce many small subgroups whose estimates carry excessively wide confidence intervals, making it impossible to distinguish genuine discrimination from noise. No existing framework provides a unified, size-adaptive statistical test that remains valid from the most common to the rarest intersectional groups.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a subgroup definition S (an intersection of protected attribute values), the classifier's binary predictions on a held-out test set, a user-chosen significance level α, and a minimum cell-count threshold ñ (default 30). It outputs a binary fairness decision (reject or fail to reject H₀: SP(S)=0), a (1−α) confidence or credible interval, a p-value or posterior tail probability, and a point estimate of the statistical parity gap. A key structural feature is the adaptive routing: if every contingency cell (n_{0,S}, n_{1,S}, n_{0,S̄}, n_{1,S̄}) meets ñ, the large-sample Wald path is taken; otherwise the Bayesian path is used, so the same interface yields calibrated inference regardless of subgroup sparsity.

## Method

The framework (SAFT) combines two complementary tests under a single decision procedure. For sufficiently large subgroups, the authors prove a Central Limit Theorem for the plug-in statistical parity estimator (Theorem 1), yielding an analytic asymptotic variance σ²(S) = VᵀΣ₄V and hence closed-form Wald confidence intervals and a p-value with guaranteed type-I error at level α. For the long tail of small intersectional groups where asymptotics fail, they place a Dirichlet prior over the four joint outcome probabilities (f(x)∈{0,1} × x∈{S,S̄}), obtain a conjugate Dirichlet posterior, and draw K Monte Carlo samples to construct empirical-quantile credible intervals for SP(S); the null is rejected when 0 falls outside the (1−α) credible interval. A general delta-method CLT (Theorem 2) extends both tests to any continuously differentiable fairness metric expressible as a function of categorical probabilities (e.g., Equal Opportunity, Disparate Impact). The authors further derive resolution-limit curves that, for each global negative-prediction rate and group size, chart the minimum fraction of negative outcomes in S required to achieve statistical power, explicitly delineating a 'no-power' zone where no observed disparity can be certified as significant.

## Results

The SAFT framework is validated on four datasets (Adult, COMPAS, German Credit, Student Performance) using an XGBoost classifier (mean test accuracies: 0.870, 0.714, 0.763, 0.768 respectively over 20 splits). The key finding is that fixed-threshold δ-SP (θ=0.1) produces false positives on small intersectional groups (where the 95% CI includes 0) and false negatives on large groups (where the CI excludes 0 but the point estimate falls below threshold). The number of detected fairness violations stabilises at approximately 1,097 (on Adult) once the minimum cell count ñ reaches 20, confirming the Bayesian-to-Wald switch at ñ=30 is safe. Resolution-limit analysis shows that under severe class imbalance (90% negative predictions), a subgroup needs at least 35 members all predicted negative to yield a statistically significant disparity at α=0.05.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares SAFT against two threshold-based baselines: δ-SP (fixed threshold θ=0.1) and γ-SP (Kearns et al. [33], size-weighted threshold). Against δ-SP, SAFT corrects both types of error: on small intersectional groups (e.g., n_S ≈ 10 in COMPAS), δ-SP flags violations in roughly half of train-test splits while SAFT consistently fails to reject H₀ because the credible interval includes 0; conversely, on large groups (n_S > 1,000), δ-SP misses a statistically significant disparity that SAFT correctly detects. Against γ-SP, scatterplots (Figure 4) on both COMPAS and Adult show that no single horizontal threshold on the γ-SP score cleanly separates SAFT-confirmed violations from non-violations, confirming that size-weighting alone is insufficient without a proper uncertainty model. The Bayesian credible intervals also outperform bootstrap CIs in the small-sample regime: at n_S=2 the bootstrap interval [−0.204, −0.192] is spuriously narrow (mirroring the asymptotic CI), while the uniform-prior Bayesian interval [−0.190, 0.488] correctly encodes the high epistemic uncertainty. As n_S grows to 210 or 1,522, all methods (Wald, Bayesian, bootstrap) converge to nearly identical intervals, validating the asymptotic theory.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the asymptotic distribution results of Besse et al. [6,7] for fairness metric ratios to a broader class of metrics (statistical parity, equal opportunity, disparate impact) via a general delta-method theorem, and crucially connects them to the small-sample regime where those asymptotics fail. It builds on the Dirichlet-multinomial Bayesian model of Foulds et al. [22,23] for intersectional fairness but departs from their threshold-based decision rule by replacing it with a formal hypothesis test whose significance level adapts to subgroup size. The framework also contrasts with Kearns et al. [33], who reweight disparities by group probability but still require an arbitrary global threshold, and with Kim et al. [34], who use Bayesian augmentation with unlabeled data but provide no asymptotic guarantees or hypothesis tests.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The resolution-limit analysis provides practitioners with a concrete, data-driven answer to 'how small is too small' for intersectional auditing, a question that has been identified as critical but left unresolved in prior work. The practical finding that no single threshold on γ-SP can separate true violations from non-violations across varying group sizes strengthens the case for abandoning fixed-threshold auditing in favor of size-adaptive tests. Limitations include the reliance on a (weakly informative) prior for the Bayesian branch, the focus on group-level metrics (statistical parity, equal opportunity) without empirical validation for individual or counterfactual fairness, and the use of a single XGBoost model without hyperparameter tuning, which limits generalizability claims about the auditing method's sensitivity to model quality.
