## Problem

Prior LLM safety benchmarks focus on explicit harms, jailbreaks, or adversarial prompts, leaving untested whether models can systematically generalize well-established safety facts to novel, seemingly innocent user queries that implicitly contain risks. This paper asks: do frontier LLMs exhibit the compositional, rule-like generalization of safety knowledge that humans do, or do they possess only 'piecemeal safety' that fails in new contexts?

## Input / Output

During testing, the model receives only the user query (a 'Safety Naive' prompt containing an implicit risk or a 'Safety OK' prompt without one); the safety fact is withheld. During judgment, the LLM-as-a-judge receives the query, the model's response, and the relevant safety fact, producing a binary pass/fail label. Scores are aggregated at three levels: model-level (fraction of all 104 facts where every variant passes, analogous to aviation's no-single-failure standard), fact-level (fraction of variants passed per fact), and area under safety curve (averaging model-level scores across exponentially decreasing pass thresholds from 100% to 0%).

## Method

SAGE-Eval is constructed via a four-step LLM generation pipeline (GPT-4o/GPT-4o-mini) that produces base questions in four patterns, second-layer questions in five more complex patterns (yes/no reformulations, multi-part questions, long contextual narratives with hidden risks), refined prompts, and Safety OK counterparts, all validated by 144 human annotators via Prolific. Programmatic augmentations (typos, spacing errors, emotional tone shifts) expand 1,724 verified base prompts to 10,428 test scenarios across 104 safety facts in 7 domains. Evaluation employs a multi-model LLM-as-a-judge consensus: o3-mini screens all responses, and flagged cases are re-evaluated by Gemini-2.0-Flash and Gemini-1.5-Pro, with a response marked unsafe only if all judges agree—achieving 100% alignment with human labels on held-out samples. The paper further introduces a bias-corrected power-law forecasting method that extrapolates safety scores from 10 test prompts per fact to 100 or 1,000, enabling developers to estimate real-world failure rates before deployment.

## Results

On SAGE-Eval (104 safety facts, ~100 augmented prompts per fact), the best-performing model, Claude-3.7-Sonnet, achieves only 57.69% Model-level Safety Score (MLSS), while o1 scores 41.35% and GPT-4o just 24.76%. All 15 evaluated models fall below 58%, with open-source models (Llama-3-70B, Qwen2-72B) scoring below 18%. Safety performance correlates only weakly with general capability (R²=0.218, p=0.079) and training compute (R²=0.220, p=0.171), and a bias-corrected power-law forecast predicts the strongest model would drop to ~38% MLSS at 1,000 prompts per fact.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SAGE-Eval evaluates 15 frontier and open-source LLMs rather than proposing a new method, so the comparisons reveal performance patterns across model families. Within each family, newer/larger variants generally outperform older/smaller ones (e.g., Claude-3.7-Sonnet 57.69% > Claude-3.5-Sonnet 49.04% > Claude-3.5-Haiku 34.62%; o1 41.35% > o3-mini 34.62% > o1-mini 14.42%), but the gaps are inconsistent and do not track with Chatbot Arena rankings or FLOPS. Notably, reasoning models (o1, o3-mini) do not dominate: o1 (41.35%) trails Claude-3.7-Sonnet by 16 points, and GPT-4o (24.76%) underperforms even DeepSeek-V3 (25.96%). Open-source 70B-class models (Llama-3.3-70B, Qwen2-72B) cluster around 16–17%, far below their 405B counterpart (Llama-3.1-405B, also 16.35%), suggesting scale alone does not close the safety-generalization gap. For the RLHF ablation, DPO fine-tuning on OLMo-2-32B raises the Area Under Safety Curve from 0.132 (SFT) to 0.226, a 71% relative improvement, yet both remain near zero on the strict MLSS metric.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of two established research threads: systematic generalization (Fodor & Pylyshyn; Lake & Baroni; SCAN, gSCAN, COGS) and LLM safety evaluation (HarmBench, AdvBench, SALAD-Bench, SG-Bench). Prior systematic generalization benchmarks operate in synthetic domains (grid-worlds, mini-languages), while prior safety benchmarks target explicit harms, jailbreaks, or adversarial prompts. SAGE-Eval is the first to test whether models generalize well-established safety facts to *implicit* risks embedded in *naive* user queries, bridging the compositional-generalization literature with real-world safety-critical QA. Methodologically, it repurposes the LLM-as-a-judge paradigm (with multi-model consensus to eliminate false positives) and introduces a power-law forecasting framework borrowed from scaling-law literature to extrapolate deployment failure rates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that safety generalization scores correlate only weakly with both model capability (R²=0.218) and training compute (R²=0.220) challenges the prevailing assumption that scaling or capability improvements will automatically close safety gaps, and suggests that HHH alignment objectives lack an explicit 'risk awareness' competency. The power-law forecasting framework with bias correction (reducing prediction error from 32.97 to 5.86) gives developers a practical tool to estimate real-world failure rates from limited test sets. Open questions include whether more sophisticated alignment objectives (beyond DPO) can close the gap, whether the benchmark's LLM-assisted construction introduces systematic biases, and how to incorporate non-Western safety norms. The benchmark's 10,428 scenarios across 7 domains make it a likely standard for pre-deployment safety evaluation.
