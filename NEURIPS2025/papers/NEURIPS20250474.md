## Problem

Prior work on implicit bias in homogeneous neural networks characterizes converged solutions as KKT points of a minimum-norm max-margin problem, but always within a single fixed-width architecture. The structural relationship between these KKT points when a network is widened via neuron or channel splitting—i.e., whether and how solutions of a smaller network are embedded in those of a larger one—remained unexplored, leaving the role of parameter redundancy and overparameterization in shaping the solution space unclear.

## Input / Output

The method takes a homogeneous network (fully-connected or CNN) with parameter vector θ ∈ ℝ^m, a binary classification dataset of n samples with labels ±1, and non-negative splitting coefficients c_i satisfying Σc_i² = 1. It produces a linear isometric transformation T: ℝ^m → ℝ^m̃ that maps KKT points of the smaller network's max-margin problem P_Φ to those of the augmented network's P_Φ̃, and—dynamically—preserves the entire gradient flow trajectory so that η(t) = Tθ(t) at every time step. Empirically, the validation uses 2D toy datasets (100 samples from two Gaussians) with MLPs and CNNs trained via GD or SGD, measuring the trajectory error ‖η(t) − Tθ(t)‖₂.

## Method

The paper introduces a general KKT mapping theorem (Theorem 4.2) stating that a linear transformation T preserves KKT points between two constrained problems if it preserves constraints, objective subgradients, and constraint subgradients (up to positive scaling). This is specialized via an equivalence theorem (Theorem 4.5) to the condition that T preserves the network output and its Clarke subdifferential, which is then verified for neuron splitting in fully-connected networks (Theorem 4.8) and channel splitting in CNNs (Theorem 4.11), where T scales the split neuron's incoming weights by c_i and outgoing weights by c_i, yielding a linear isometry. The dynamic extension (Theorem 5.2) proves that under C¹-smooth losses, the subdifferential of the training loss transforms as T(∂°L(θ)) = ∂°L̃(Tθ), so gradient flow trajectories initiated from η(0) = Tθ(0) satisfy η(t) = Tθ(t) for all t. Consequently, the ω-limit sets of normalized parameter directions are mapped exactly (Theorem 5.4), meaning that if the narrow network's direction converges to a unique KKT-aligned limit θ̄*, the wide network's direction converges to Tθ̄*.

## Results

The paper's empirical validation confirms the theoretical trajectory preservation principle (Theorem 5.2): the trajectory error ‖η(t) − Tθ(t)‖₂ between a narrow network and its neuron-split wide counterpart remains at machine precision (~10⁻¹³) throughout training with gradient descent on 2D separable data. With SGD using identical mini-batch sequences, the error is 4.35×10⁻¹³, while with different batch sequences it degrades to 1.53×10⁻¹ (as expected, since the mapping no longer holds). For a CNN with channel splitting under GD, the trajectory error is 2.36×10⁻¹⁰, confirming the principle extends to convolutional architectures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper without direct method-to-method comparisons; the empirical experiments serve as validation of the authors' own theoretical predictions rather than benchmarks against competing approaches. The key 'comparison' is between the theoretical prediction (trajectory error should be zero) and the observed numerical error, which confirms the prediction to within floating-point precision for GD and identical-batch SGD. The only notable deviation is Exp. 4, where different SGD batch sequences break the trajectory mapping (error ~10⁻¹), which is expected since the isometric embedding T only guarantees preservation when both networks see identical data at each step. No competing embedding or width-scaling methods are benchmarked.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Embedding Principle of Zhang et al. (2021), which showed that loss landscapes of smaller networks embed into larger ones, by specifically characterizing how KKT points of the minimum-norm max-margin problem (formulated following Gunasekar et al., 2018 and Lyu and Li, 2019) map across network widths via neuron and channel splitting. While prior implicit bias work (Soudry et al., 2018; Ji and Telgarsky, 2020; Nacson et al., 2019b) characterizes converged solutions within a single fixed-width homogeneous network, this paper is the first to establish a structural correspondence between KKT points of networks of different widths and to connect this static embedding to the full gradient flow trajectory. The use of Clarke subdifferentials and the admissibility of chain rules (Davis et al., 2020; Bolte and Pauwels, 2021) provides the analytical machinery to handle non-smooth ReLU-like activations in the subgradient preservation proofs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that a simple neuron-splitting isometry preserves not just KKT points but entire gradient flow trajectories offers a concrete mechanistic explanation for why overparameterized networks can replicate the solutions of their narrower counterparts, connecting parameter redundancy to structural embedding. However, the framework is restricted to homogeneous networks (no biases, 1-homogeneous activations) and the empirical validation is limited to 2D toy datasets with small MLPs and CNNs, leaving open whether the trajectory preservation holds under stochastic optimization with different batch sequences or in realistic high-dimensional settings. Key open questions include extending the principle to non-homogeneous networks (with biases or non-1-homogeneous activations) and to architectures beyond fully-connected and convolutional layers, as well as understanding whether the embedding captures the full solution set or only a subset of KKT points.
