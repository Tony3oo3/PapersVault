## Problem

Selecting the data mixture (domain weights) for pretraining large foundation models currently relies on costly trial-and-error, with no principled method to determine optimal proportions across domains. While scaling laws predict loss as a function of model size N and training tokens D, they do not account for how the domain weight vector h interacts with scale, leaving a gap in predicting performance for unseen mixtures at larger compute budgets.

## Input / Output

The method takes a model size N, a number of training tokens D, and a k-dimensional domain weight vector h (an element of the simplex Δ_k, with each weight ≥ 0.1 in the fitting grid) as inputs, and outputs the predicted loss on a user-specified target domain. The target domain need not be one of the training domains, enabling optimization for downstream-relevant objectives (e.g., OpenHermes for LLMs). The fitted law is trained on small-scale runs (e.g., 100M–1.4B parameters, 4–50B tokens) and extrapolated to predict losses at an order of magnitude larger scale (e.g., 7–8B parameters, 150–330B tokens) with previously unseen domain weights.

## Method

The paper extends the Chinchilla additive power law by making its coefficients depend on the domain weight vector h through a weighted sum of per-domain power terms, yielding two formulations: an additive law (5+2k parameters) where the mixture term 1/Σ(C_i·h_i^γ_i) is independent of N and D, and a joint law (5+4k parameters) where the N- and D-dependent coefficients A^h and B^h also vary with h, making optimal weights scale-dependent. Fitting uses Huber loss minimization via a Basin-hopping algorithm with L-BFGS as an inner routine, which is necessary because the 37+ parameter space makes standard random-restart L-BFGS unreliable. The laws are validated across three large-scale settings—LLMs (7 SlimPajama domains, up to 7B/150B tokens), native multimodal models (3 domains: text, paired, interleaved, up to 8B/160B tokens), and large vision models (4 image-caption domains, up to 1B/330B tokens)—achieving mean relative errors below 1% on most domains. Only 10–20 small-scale training runs are needed to fit the laws accurately, after which the optimal domain weights h* are obtained by minimizing the predicted loss over the simplex using mirror descent.

## Results

The proposed scaling laws achieve consistently low Mean Relative Error (MRE) across all three modalities: for LLMs (7 domains), the additive law reaches 0.29–0.92% train MRE and 0.31–4.45% validation MRE, while the joint law improves to 0.24–0.57% train and 0.34–2.51% val. For NMMs (3 domains), MREs are as low as 0.10% (joint, train) and 0.37% (joint, val). Critically, only 10–20 small-scale training runs are needed to fit the laws, which then extrapolate to models an order of magnitude larger (e.g., from <1B to 7B/8B parameters). On downstream 7B LLM evaluations, the model trained with the OpenHermes-optimal mixture h_OH* achieves a CORE score of 58 and MMLU of 37, outperforming the uniform-weight baseline (53/30) and the standard SlimPajama distribution (52/25).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Ye et al. [68] (data mixing laws for fixed N,D), the paper's additive law achieves systematically lower train MRE (e.g., 0.07% vs. 0.08–0.17% on Wikipedia) and lower test MRE on unseen mixtures (0.18% vs. 0.14–0.31%), while Ye's M3 formula is notably worse (2.54% train). Against RegMix [40], the proposed law is substantially better on all four Pile domains (e.g., Wikipedia: 0.07% vs. 0.92% train MRE; 0.18% vs. 1.31% test MRE). Against BiMix [23], the additive law (fixed N) achieves 3–10× lower MRE (e.g., GitHub: 0.20% vs. 1.99% train; 0.26% vs. 2.26% test), which the authors attribute to BiMix's inability to model cross-domain interactions. For NMM pretraining, the optimized mixtures from both additive and joint laws consistently outperform the fixed mixtures used in MM1 [44] and Shukor et al. [56], as well as uniform and randomly sampled mixtures, with the advantage persisting when extrapolating from <1B to 8B parameter models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Chinchilla/Kaplan scaling law framework (which models loss as a function of model size N and training tokens D) by introducing domain weights h as a third input, yielding joint scaling laws over (N, D, h). Prior data-mixing laws (Ye et al., Ge et al.) model loss as a function of h only for a fixed (N, D) budget, and regression-based approaches (RegMix, DoReMi) use auxiliary models to predict mixture quality. The key conceptual departure is the joint interaction term: the paper shows that the optimal domain weights are compute-dependent (∂²L/∂N∂h ≠ 0), a phenomenon invisible to fixed-scale mixing laws. The fitting procedure also departs from standard L-BFGS grid-search by employing Basin-hopping to handle the 5+4k parameter space.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: only 10–20 small-scale runs (below 1B parameters) are needed to predict optimal data mixtures for models an order of magnitude larger, replacing costly trial-and-error with a principled optimization. Validation across three modalities (LLM, native multimodal, large vision) with consistently low MRE (<1% for most domains) strengthens confidence in broad applicability. Key limitations include the assumption of a fixed mixture throughout training (no curriculum), no data repetition, and focus on pretraining loss rather than direct downstream task prediction. Open questions include whether dynamic/evolving mixtures can be captured, whether the laws extend to continual pretraining and fine-tuning, and whether the compute-dependent optimal weights predicted by the joint law hold at even larger scales (100B+ parameters).
