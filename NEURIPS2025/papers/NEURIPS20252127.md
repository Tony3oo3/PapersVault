## Problem

Web navigation agents require step-level reward signals for both training and test-time search, yet no specialized process reward model (PRM) existed for this domain. Prior work relied on prompted MLLMs (e.g., GPT-4o) as evaluators, which are prohibitively expensive (~$14,000 for tree search on WebArena), slow (40 hours on one A100), and empirically less accurate than trained reward models. This paper introduces the first PRM purpose-built for web navigation trajectories.

## Input / Output

At inference, WEB-SHEPHERD receives a user instruction, a generated checklist of natural-language subgoals, the current browser observation (accessibility tree text and optionally a rendered screenshot), and a candidate action. It autoregressively produces a natural-language feedback explanation followed by per-checklist-item judgments (Yes / In Progress / No). The final continuous reward is computed via a verbalizer that aggregates LM-head logits over token variants for each label, weighting 'In Progress' at 0.5, then averaging across all K checklist items. The model operates on Qwen2.5-3B/8B (text-only) or Qwen2.5-VL-3B (multimodal) backbones fine-tuned with LoRA (rank 16, 3 epochs).

## Method

WEB-SHEPHERD is a two-step generative process reward model: it first generates a task-specific checklist decomposing the instruction into coarse-grained subgoals, then evaluates each candidate action against that checklist using next-token prediction as the training objective (rather than Bradley-Terry loss, which the authors show generalizes worse out-of-domain). Training uses the WEBPRM COLLECTION—40K step-level preference pairs with annotated checklists spanning 50 websites and three difficulty levels—where each instance pairs a chosen expert action with up to four rule-filtered rejected actions. At inference, a verbalizer maps semantic labels to sets of token variants (e.g., 'Yes', 'Done', 'Completed' for the positive class) and sums their logits to obtain soft probabilities, yielding a continuous reward without requiring discrete output parsing. The checklist-based decomposition is the key design choice: ablations show that removing either the checklist or the feedback component causes substantial performance drops, and the approach achieves 85% trajectory accuracy on WEBREWARDBENCH versus 5–15% for prompted GPT-4o baselines.

## Results

On WEBREWARDBENCH (WebArena test set), WEB-SHEPHERD (8B) achieves 97.8 MRR and 85.0% trajectory accuracy, substantially outperforming GPT-4o (69.7 MRR, 15.0% Acc.traj), GPT-4o-mini (60.0 MRR, 15.0% Acc.traj), and Qwen-2.5-VL-72B (52.3 MRR, 5.0% Acc.traj). On WebArena-lite with GPT-4o-mini as the policy, reward-guided trajectory search with WEB-SHEPHERD (8B) achieves 34.55% success rate, a +10.9 point improvement over the 23.64% baseline without trajectory search. In terms of cost, WEB-SHEPHERD (3B) costs approximately $4.67 per 1,000 instances, roughly 10× cheaper than GPT-4o-mini ($43.57) and 100× cheaper than GPT-4o ($435.74).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares WEB-SHEPHERD against three prompted MLLM baselines: GPT-4o-mini, GPT-4o, and Qwen-2.5-VL-72B. On WEBREWARDBENCH (WebArena test), WEB-SHEPHERD (8B) achieves 97.8 MRR and 85.0% trajectory accuracy, outperforming GPT-4o (69.7 MRR, 15.0% Acc.traj) by 28.1 MRR points and 70 Acc.traj points, GPT-4o-mini (60.0 MRR, 15.0%) by 37.8 MRR points, and Qwen-2.5-VL-72B (52.3 MRR, 5.0%) by 45.5 MRR points. The gap is especially stark on trajectory accuracy, where all MLLM baselines score at or below 15% while WEB-SHEPHERD (8B) reaches 85%. On the downstream WebArena-lite task, using GPT-4o-mini as a prompted PRM actually degrades performance (15.75% SR vs. 23.64% baseline), whereas WEB-SHEPHERD (8B) improves it to 34.55%. Additionally, WEB-SHEPHERD (3B) costs ~$4.67 per 1,000 instances versus $43.57 for GPT-4o-mini and $435.74 for GPT-4o, making it 10–100× more cost-effective.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the process reward model (PRM) paradigm from mathematical reasoning (Math-Shepherd, Lightman et al.) to web navigation, where step-level credit assignment is critical because actions are irreversible (e.g., a booked ticket cannot be refunded). It adopts the generative reward modeling framework (next-token prediction with verbalizer-based soft probabilities) from Mahan et al. and Zhang et al., but departs from prior web agent work (Pan et al., Koh et al.) that relies on prompted MLLMs as evaluators, arguing that domain-specific training is necessary for both performance and cost efficiency. The checklist-based decomposition of instructions into subgoals is a novel mechanism for structuring the reward signal in a partially observable, long-horizon setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work fills a clear gap by providing the first PRM, training dataset (40K step-level pairs), and meta-evaluation benchmark for web navigation, enabling the community to develop and test reward models without running expensive online agents. The 10× cost reduction versus GPT-4o-mini makes reward-guided search practically deployable, and the checklist mechanism offers interpretability that black-box reward scores lack. Open questions include integration with reinforcement learning (explicitly deferred due to compute), extension to coordinate-based actions and multimodal instructions, and whether the approach generalizes beyond the Qwen-based 3B–8B scale to larger foundation models.
