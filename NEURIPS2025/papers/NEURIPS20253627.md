## Problem

Existing benchmarks for long-context vision-language models (LCVLMs) suffer from four critical gaps: they cover only one or two task types (typically NIAH or long-document VQA), restrict image types to either natural or synthetic, lack a unified cross-modal token-counting scheme, and do not provide multiple standardized context lengths per example. As a result, it remains unclear how well current LCVLMs perform across diverse real-world long-context vision-language applications and how robust they are to input length variation.

## Input / Output

Each benchmark example is delivered as an interleaved image-text sequence at one of five standardized lengths (8K, 16K, 32K, 64K, 128K tokens), where image tokens are counted as 14×14 patches after a 2×2 pixel unshuffle and text tokens use the Llama2 tokenizer. The model receives a task-specific question prompt appended to the context and produces a free-form answer, which is scored via substring exact match (SubEM), accuracy, or LLM-based evaluation depending on the task category. The benchmark comprises 13,331 examples across 13 datasets spanning both natural images (photographs, scenes, food, species) and synthetic images (PDF documents, web pages, slides).

## Method

MMLONGBENCH unifies five long-context vision-language task categories—Visual RAG, Needle-in-a-Haystack, Many-Shot In-Context Learning, Summarization, and Long-Document VQA—under a single cross-modal tokenization scheme and five standardized input lengths, enabling controlled analysis of performance as context grows. For VRAG, gold Wikipedia passages are embedded among distractors; for NIAH, target images or text needles are placed within long visual-textual haystacks; for ICL, natural-language labels are replaced with class IDs to force on-the-fly learning from hundreds of exemplars; for summarization and DocVQA, PDF documents are truncated or padded to target lengths. The authors evaluate 46 closed-source and open-source LCVLMs using greedy decoding on 100 randomly sampled examples per dataset, then perform cross-category Spearman correlation analysis and error analysis (comparing PDF vs. OCR pipelines, and image vs. text-only inputs) to diagnose bottlenecks. Key findings include that no single NIAH task reliably predicts overall long-context ability, that OCR and cross-modality retrieval remain major failure points, and that reasoning-enhanced models (e.g., Gemini thinking variants) consistently outperform their base counterparts.

## Results

Across 46 evaluated LCVLMs at 128K tokens, even the strongest model (Gemini-2.5-Pro) achieves only ~82.7 SubEM on VRAG and 65.3 on summarization, while GPT-4o averages 62.9 across all tasks. Open-source models lag significantly: Qwen2.5-VL-72B scores 50.3 on VRAG and 28.5 on summarization at 128K. On the challenging VH-Multi NIAH task, even GPT-4o drops to 50.7% accuracy at 128K (near random-guess level of 50%), and most models remain below 60% even at 8K. Long-document VQA emerges as the most reliable single-task proxy for overall long-context ability, showing the highest average Spearman correlation (≥0.85) with other categories.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Gemini-2.5-Pro is the strongest LCVLM overall, outperforming the best open-source models by ~20 absolute points on VRAG, summarization, and DocVQA (e.g., 82.7 vs. 64.6 SubEM on VRAG at 128K), though the gap narrows to ~14 points on ICL where Qwen2-VL-72B is competitive. GPT-4o (67.3 VRAG, 42.4 Summ at 128K) and Claude-3.7-Sonnet (68.8 VRAG, 37.5 Summ) remain strong but are surpassed by Gemini-2.5-Pro on most tasks; Claude-3.7-Sonnet is additionally limited to 100 images, causing N/A at longer lengths. Among open-source models, Qwen2.5-VL-32B (64.6 VRAG) outperforms Gemini-2.0-Flash (60.3) on VRAG, and Ovis2-34B (41.6 Summ) nearly matches GPT-4o (42.4) on summarization, showing open-source models are competitive in specific niches. The thinking variant Gemini-2.0-Flash-T consistently improves over its base on all tasks, with the largest gains on summarization (+25.3%) and DocVQA (+10.1%), confirming that enhanced reasoning boosts long-context performance. Critically, NIAH tasks (VH, MM-NIAH) show weak Spearman correlation (ρ < 0.8) with other task categories, meaning strong NIAH scores do not predict overall long-context ability, whereas long-document VQA achieves the highest cross-category correlation and is recommended as a more reliable single-task proxy.

## Relation to Prior Work

MMLONGBENCH unifies and extends several existing long-context evaluation efforts—Visual Haystack and MM-NIAH for retrieval, MMLongBench-Doc/LongDocURL/SlideVQA for document VQA, InfoSeek/ViQuAE for visual RAG, GovReport/Multi-LexSum for summarization, and image classification datasets for many-shot ICL—into a single framework with standardized cross-modal token counting (14×14 patches + 2×2 unshuffle) and five fixed input lengths (8K–128K). Its evaluation methodology directly follows HELMET's multi-length protocol, but adapts it to the vision-language setting where prior benchmarks either counted only images or only text tokens. The key departure from prior work is the simultaneous coverage of five task categories, both natural and synthetic image types, and rigorous length control, none of which any single prior benchmark achieved.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that NIAH performance does not reliably predict overall long-context ability across diverse tasks challenges the field's default diagnostic tool and suggests model developers should evaluate on multiple task categories rather than relying on a single retrieval-style probe. OCR accuracy and cross-modal information retrieval emerge as persistent bottlenecks even for frontier models like GPT-4o (62.9 average at 128K), indicating substantial headroom for improvement in vision encoders and cross-modal attention. The consistent advantage of 'thinking' model variants (e.g., Gemini-2.0-Flash-T gaining 25.3% on summarization) points toward reasoning as a lever for long-context robustness. Open questions include whether position extrapolation methods (YaRN, V2PE) can close the gap for open-source models, and how to design training objectives that simultaneously improve OCR fidelity and cross-modal retrieval in long contexts.
