## Problem

While prior interpretability work has examined information flow in text-output VLMs, the internal mechanisms of native multimodal models—trained from scratch to jointly generate both images and text—remain largely unexplored. This paper asks how visual information is transferred to the textual domain in these models, what structural and causal mechanisms mediate cross-modal communication, and how these differ from non-native architectures built on pre-trained LLM backbones.

## Input / Output

The models consume RGB images tokenized into 1024 discrete VQ-GAN tokens followed by a text instruction prompt (e.g., 'This animal is a _' for classification, 'Provide a one-sentence caption...' for captioning), with a special [EOI] token marking the image-to-text boundary. Outputs are autoregressive text: single-word class labels, VQA answers, or one-sentence captions. The interpretability analysis operates on residual stream representations and attention matrices at each layer to trace how visual semantics propagate, rather than on final outputs alone.

## Method

The paper combines geometric, attentional, and causal interpretability tools to characterize image-text communication across six VLMs (Chameleon-7B/34B, Emu3-8B, LLaVA-7B, Pixtral-12B, Janus-1.3B, VILA-U-7B). Geometric analysis (cosine similarity, Advanced Density Peaks clustering) reveals that native multimodal models maintain a pronounced modality gap—image and text embeddings remain nearly orthogonal throughout all layers—while non-native models increasingly mix modalities in deeper layers. Cross-modal attention quantification shows that in native models the [EOI] token captures 30–50% of all text-on-image attention, whereas in non-native models attention is distributed across internal image tokens. Neighborhood overlap probing confirms that [EOI] in native models encodes the richest visual semantics (exceeding 0.4 overlap with ImageNet labels), while in non-native models semantic content remains spread across many tokens. Causal interventions—attention knockout of text→[EOI] and activation patching of [EOI] representations—demonstrate that ablating this single token causes a >90% performance drop in native models but has negligible effect in non-native models, while patching [EOI] from a target class steers predictions in ~90% of cases. The paper further proposes a masked fine-tuning strategy that forces the model to redistribute visual-to-text communication away from [EOI], progressively recovering ablation-robust performance.

## Results

In native multimodal models, ablating the [EOI] token causes a dramatic performance collapse: Chameleon-7B drops from 0.51 to 0.25 on VQAv2 and from 0.34 to 0.04 on Flickr30k, while Emu3 falls from 0.63 to 0.33 on MS-COCO. Strikingly, blocking all text-on-image attention produces smaller declines than ablating the single [EOI] token in native models (e.g., Chameleon-7B: 0.40 vs. 0.25 on VQAv2), confirming [EOI] as the dominant communication channel. In contrast, non-native models like LLaVA are completely unaffected by [EOI] ablation (0.80→0.80 on VQAv2) but collapse to near-zero when all image-token attention is removed (0.80→0.00), revealing a distributed communication pattern. Activation patching at [EOI] in Chameleon achieves ~90% class-transfer success, while the same intervention in LLaVA has no measurable effect.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares six VLMs across two architectural families. Native multimodal models (Chameleon-7B/34B, Emu3-8B), trained from scratch with VQ-GAN tokenization, exhibit a pronounced narrow gate: the [EOI] token receives 40–50% of text-on-image attention in Chameleon and 30–40% in Emu3, and ablating it causes >90% performance drops on captioning tasks. Non-native models (LLaVA-7B, Pixtral-12B, Janus-1.3B, VILA-U-7B), built on pre-trained LLM backbones with CLIP/SigLIP encoders, show no such bottleneck: [EOI] ablation leaves performance essentially unchanged (e.g., LLaVA VQAv2 stays at 0.80), while removing all image-token attention destroys performance entirely. Geometrically, native models maintain near-orthogonal image-text representations (cosine similarity <0.10) with perfect cluster homogeneity, whereas non-native models show increasing modality mixing (cosine similarity rising to 0.5 in LLaVA) and mixed-modality clusters in late layers. The paper further demonstrates that masked [EOI] fine-tuning can redistribute the narrow gate, recovering ablation-robust performance in native models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the mechanistic interpretability toolkit (attention knockout, activation patching, neighborhood overlap) previously applied to text-output VLMs to the emerging class of native multimodal models trained from scratch for joint image and text generation. While prior work (Neo et al., Basu et al., Wen et al., Trager et al.) characterized information flow in LLaVA-style architectures, this study is the first to systematically compare native multimodal VLMs (Chameleon, Emu3) against non-native ones, revealing a qualitatively different communication regime. The modality-separation analysis builds on density-peak clustering and cosine-similarity methods from the representation-geometry literature, while the causal interventions follow the activation-patching and attention-knockout frameworks established for language models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of a single-token bottleneck ([EOI]) for all visual-to-text communication in native multimodal models has dual practical implications: it provides a precise causal handle for steering image semantics with minimal intervention, but also exposes a single point of failure that makes these models vulnerable to targeted perturbations. The proposed masked fine-tuning strategy offers a concrete path toward more robust, distributed communication, though it remains to be validated at scale and across tasks beyond the four benchmarks tested. Key open questions include whether the narrow gate generalizes to text-to-image communication, to models with continuous or diffusion-based image encoders, and to multimodal settings beyond vision and language.
