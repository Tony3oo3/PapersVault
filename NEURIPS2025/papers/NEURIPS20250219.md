## Problem

Existing evaluation measures for time series segmentation—such as F1, Covering, and ARI—either focus solely on change point localization, treat all segmentation errors equally regardless of their temporal position or type, or provide no interpretable diagnostic of where and how a method fails. This limits practitioners' ability to distinguish minor boundary misalignments from severe isolated misclassifications and hinders targeted model improvement.

## Input / Output

Both measures consume a ground truth state sequence R and a predicted state sequence P, each of length N over a finite discrete state set. WARI returns a single scalar score in the same range as ARI (can be negative for strongly discordant segmentations). SMS returns a scalar score in [0,1] plus a per-error-block classification into one of four types (delay, isolation, transition, missing), with user-configurable penalty weights per type that allow application-specific tuning without drastically altering the overall ranking.

## Method

WARI extends the standard Adjusted Rand Index by replacing point counts in the contingency matrix with weighted sums, where each time point's weight is w_i = 1 + α·d_i (d_i being its distance to the nearest ground-truth change point, α defaulting to 0.1). This makes interior misclassifications penalized more heavily than boundary-adjacent ones, encoding position sensitivity while preserving ARI's chance-adjusted range. SMS operates in two stages: first, the Hungarian algorithm finds the optimal bijection between predicted and real state labels that maximizes total overlap; second, error blocks in the remapped sequence are identified, classified by atomicity and context into delay, isolation, transition, or missing, and each block receives a penalty proportional to its length, error-type weight, and (for isolation/transition) normalized distance to the nearest real boundary. The final SMS score is 1 minus the total penalty divided by N, and the authors prove the score is bounded within explicit limits around the baseline 1−E/N regardless of weight choices.

## Results

Across five datasets and six segmentation methods, the proposed SMS and WARI measures reveal ranking differences relative to ARI. On multivariate datasets, Time2State achieves the highest SMS (0.70 on MoCap, 0.71 on USC-HAD, 0.80 on ActRecTut) and WARI (0.75 on MoCap, 0.81 on USC-HAD, 0.83 on ActRecTut), with statistically significant superiority over all other methods. On the univariate UCR-SEG dataset, ClaSP achieves the highest SMS (0.82) and WARI (0.65), substantially outperforming ARI (0.59) for the same method. Error-type analysis via SMS shows that neural/probabilistic methods (Time2State, E2USD, HDP-HSMM) produce predominantly isolation errors, while profile-based and clustering methods (ClaSP, TICC, PaTSS) produce more missing and delay errors.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates six segmentation methods—E2USD, Time2State, HDP-HSMM, TICC, ClaSP+kMeans, and PaTSS—under the proposed WARI/SMS measures alongside standard ARI, F1, and Covering. On multivariate datasets, Time2State is ranked first under WARI and SMS with statistically significant margins (Wilcoxon sign-rank test, α=0.05), while E2USD leads under ARI on MoCap (0.71 vs. Time2State's 0.58), illustrating how the choice of measure changes the apparent winner. On the univariate UCR-SEG dataset, ClaSP dominates under SMS (0.82) and WARI (0.65) but only achieves 0.59 ARI, showing that ARI underestimates its performance due to sensitivity to the small number of ground-truth segments. TICC and HDP-HSMM consistently rank last across all measures and datasets. A key novel finding is that SMS decomposes errors by type: neural and probabilistic methods (Time2State, E2USD, HDP-HSMM) produce more isolation errors, whereas ClaSP, TICC, and PaTSS produce more missing and delay errors—insight entirely inaccessible under ARI or F1.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground in the evaluation of time series segmentation by proposing a formal typology of four error types (delay, isolation, transition, missing) and two corresponding measures—WARI (a distance-weighted extension of ARI) and SMS (a type-aware, Hungarian-alignment-based score). It departs from the dominant practice of relying on point-based clustering metrics (ARI, NMI, AMI) or change-point metrics (F1, Covering), which the authors show fail to distinguish error position or type. Conceptually, WARI repurposes the contingency matrix framework with boundary-distance weights, while SMS introduces a novel two-stage pipeline (optimal state mapping via the Hungarian algorithm, then typed penalty scoring) that has no direct precedent in the segmentation evaluation literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The error typology and the finding that neural methods (Time2State, E2USD, HDP-HSMM) produce predominantly isolation errors while profile-based and probabilistic methods (ClaSP, TICC, PaTSS) produce more missing/delay errors offer actionable diagnostics for model selection and hyperparameter tuning that were previously inaccessible. However, SMS is not adjusted for chance and can overvalue simplistic single-segment predictions, while WARI still cannot differentiate error types (failing Property P3), so the two measures are complementary rather than a single drop-in replacement. Open questions include whether the error typology generalizes to non-discrete or continuous-state settings, how the measures behave under noisy or subjective ground-truth annotations at scale, and whether the penalty-weight sensitivity (bounded but non-zero) becomes problematic in high-stakes applications where error-type severity is genuinely asymmetric.
