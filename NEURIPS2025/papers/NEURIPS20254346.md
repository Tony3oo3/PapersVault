## Problem

Human pose estimation systems are evaluated almost exclusively on accuracy metrics (PCK, MPJPE, AP), while privacy is neither defined in measurable terms nor linked to the regulatory frameworks (GDPR, BIPA, HIPAA, AI Act) that govern real-world deployment. The paper argues that common assumptions—such as non-RGB modalities automatically guaranteeing privacy or anonymization being sufficient—fail to account for residual biometric leakage, re-identification from structured outputs, and risks introduced by multi-tier (edge-fog-cloud) deployment architectures.

## Input / Output

The proposed evaluation framework takes as input the full design specification of an HPE system: the sensing modality (RGB, depth, thermal, RF, LiDAR), the pose representation (kinematic joints, planar silhouette, volumetric mesh), the inference and training location (local vs. cloud), the storage and retention policy, and the transmission and encryption scheme. It produces a structured privacy assessment consisting of a four-level risk classification (low, medium, high, unacceptable), a numeric privacy score combining modality exposure and handling penalties, a legal-category label aligned with GDPR/AI Act provisions, and an empirical re-identification resistance score.

## Method

Rather than proposing a new pose estimation architecture, the paper introduces a multi-dimensional privacy evaluation framework grounded in existing international regulations. It defines four risk levels by mapping concrete design choices (e.g., local inference on keypoints = low; cloud inference on stored RGB video = high; biometric collection without consent = unacceptable) to specific legal provisions from GDPR, BIPA, HIPAA, PIPL, and the AI Act. On top of this qualitative classification, the paper proposes four complementary quantitative indicators: a modality scoring scheme (0–3 points based on identifiability of the input), a cumulative penalty system for risky pipeline decisions (cloud inference, unencrypted transfer, long-term storage, data reuse), a structured legal label (biometric data yes/no, AI Act risk tier, retention period, international transfer status), and an empirical re-identification resistance test using a classifier to measure whether system outputs (skeletons, meshes, depth maps) still enable identity recovery. The central argument is that these indicators should be reported alongside accuracy in benchmarks—e.g., '85% AP@0.5 under medium privacy risk'—making the performance-privacy trade-off explicit and comparable across systems.

## Results

This is a position paper and does not report quantitative experimental results or benchmark comparisons. Its primary contribution is a four-level privacy risk taxonomy (low, medium, high, unacceptable) mapped to specific HPE design choices (input modality, inference location, storage policy, transmission security) and aligned with GDPR, BIPA, HIPAA, PIPL, and the EU AI Act. The authors further propose four practical privacy indicators—modality scoring (0–3 points), a penalty system for risky data handling, legal-category labels, and re-identification resistance testing—that could be reported alongside standard accuracy metrics (PCK, MPJPE, AP) in future benchmarks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not compare its method against empirical baselines. Instead, it critiques prevailing evaluation practices in HPE, arguing that accuracy-only metrics (PCK, MPJPE, AP) are insufficient for real-world deployment. The authors challenge four common assumptions: (1) that accuracy is the sole objective, (2) that switching to non-RGB modalities guarantees privacy, (3) that privacy is too vague to measure, and (4) that not storing data equates to privacy preservation. They argue that even depth, thermal, LiDAR, and RF signals retain biometric and physiological information enabling re-identification, and that anonymization techniques (blurring, pixelation, federated learning) leave residual leakage risks. The paper calls for benchmarks that report both accuracy and a privacy risk level (e.g., '85% AP@0.5 under medium privacy risk'), but does not itself run such experiments.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper departs from the dominant HPE evaluation paradigm, which has been built almost entirely around accuracy metrics (PCK, MPJPE, AP) on RGB benchmarks like COCO and Human3.6M. Rather than proposing a new model or dataset, it repurposes existing regulatory frameworks (GDPR, BIPA, HIPAA, PIPL, AI Act) as a structured lens for assessing HPE system design choices, and synthesizes scattered findings from the re-identification and privacy-in-vision literature into a coherent argument that non-RGB modalities and anonymization techniques do not automatically guarantee privacy. It carves new ground by insisting that privacy must be a first-class, measurable evaluation axis alongside accuracy, rather than a deployment afterthought.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As HPE moves into regulated domains (healthcare, workplace monitoring, elderly care), the absence of any privacy metric in standard benchmarks means that a highly accurate model may be legally undeployable, yet this trade-off is invisible in current comparisons. The paper's proposed indicators (modality scoring, penalty systems, legal labels, re-identification resistance) are practical starting points but remain unvalidated and lack community adoption; no benchmark currently reports them. Key open questions include how to standardize a privacy score that is both interpretable and comparable across architectures, how to incentivize privacy-aware design in competitive benchmarking, and whether the proposed risk taxonomy can be operationalized into automated compliance checks for multi-tier (edge-fog-cloud) deployments.
