## Problem

Existing Best-of-Both-Worlds (BOBW) algorithms for combinatorial semi-bandits are FTRL-based and require solving optimization problems at every round to compute arm-selection probabilities, which is computationally expensive. The Follow-the-Perturbed-Leader (FTPL) policy avoids this by simply selecting the m arms with the smallest perturbed estimated losses, but it was unknown whether FTPL could achieve BOBW guarantees in the m-set semi-bandit setting (m > 1), where arm-selection probabilities have a far more complex structure than in the standard multi-armed bandit case (m = 1) handled by Honda et al. [2023].

## Input / Output

At each round t, the learner receives a loss vector ℓ_t ∈ [0,1]^d from the environment and must select exactly m arms out of d. The learner only observes losses for the selected arms (semi-bandit feedback o_t = A_t ⊙ ℓ_t), and maintains a cumulative estimated loss vector L̂_t updated via importance-weighted estimates. Because the FTPL arm-selection probabilities w_{t,i} = φ_i(η_t L̂_t) involve order-statistic integrals over Fréchet perturbations and are intractable to compute directly, the algorithm uses geometric resampling: it repeatedly redraws perturbations until arm i is selected, using the number of resamples K_{t,i} as an unbiased estimator of 1/w_{t,i}. The output is the selected m-arm subset A_t and the theoretical regret guarantees in both adversarial and stochastic regimes.

## Method

The algorithm (FTPL with geometric resampling) adds i.i.d. Fréchet(2) perturbations r_t/η_t to the cumulative estimated loss vector at each round and selects the m arms with the smallest perturbed values, using η_t = 1/√t. The key technical contribution is extending the standard FTRL/Mirror-Descent analysis framework to m-set semi-bandits, where the convex hull of the action set {a ∈ {0,1}^d : ‖a‖₁ = m} has no interior points, so ∇Φ and ∇Φ* are not mutual inverses. The stability term analysis reduces to bounding the ratio V_{i,4}(λ)/V_{i,3}(λ), which involves sums over all subsets of size < m; the paper proves a new integral inequality (Lemma 4.2) showing this ratio is O(√(m log d / (σ_i(λ)−m))) or O(m^{1/3}) depending on the rank of arm i, yielding the adversarial regret O(√(nm)(√(d log d) + m^{5/6})). The penalty term exploits the fact that the expected sum of the top-m order statistics of d i.i.d. Fréchet(2) samples is O(√(md)), and the stochastic regret O(Σ log n / Δ_i) follows from a self-bounding argument that tracks when the estimated losses correctly identify the optimal m arms.

## Results

The main theoretical contribution is a near-optimal adversarial regret bound of O(√(nm)(√(d log d) + m^{5/6})) for the m-set semi-bandit problem (Theorem 3.1), which is the first FTPL-based result approaching the FTRL-optimal O(√(nmd)) up to log(d) and m^{5/6}/√d factors. In the stochastic setting (Theorem 3.2), the algorithm achieves O(Σ_{i,Δ_i>0} log(n)/Δ_i) regret, matching the best-known stochastic bounds up to additive terms. Empirically, on d=10, m=5, n=10^7, FTPL with Fréchet perturbations is compared against five baselines in both stochastic and adversarial environments (Figure 1), showing competitive or superior regret trajectories, particularly in the adversarial regime where it avoids the expensive optimization required by FTRL-based methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares FTPL against five baselines: COMBUCB and Thompson Sampling (stochastic-specific), EXP2 and LogBarrier (adversarial-specific FTRL policies), and a hybrid FTRL BOBW algorithm [Zimmert et al., 2019]. In the stochastic setting (d=10, m=5, Δ=0.1), FTPL achieves regret comparable to COMBUCB and Thompson Sampling, confirming its logarithmic regret guarantee. In the adversarial setting (phase-based losses with Δ=0.1), FTPL outperforms EXP2 and LogBarrier in terms of regret growth rate, while remaining competitive with the hybrid FTRL BOBW algorithm. The key practical advantage highlighted is computational: FTPL avoids solving convex optimization problems at each step (required by all FTRL baselines), instead simply selecting the m arms with the smallest perturbed estimated losses, making it significantly more efficient for large d. The lower bounds in Appendix C.3 show that the log(d) and m^{5/6}/√d factors in the adversarial bound are inherent to the current analysis approach, meaning FTPL cannot match the exact O(√(nmd)) FTRL bound without fundamentally different techniques.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Honda et al. [2023] and Lee et al. [2024], who established that FTPL with Fréchet perturbations achieves Best-of-Both-Worlds guarantees in the classical multi-armed bandit setting (m=1), to the m-set combinatorial semi-bandit problem where the learner selects m arms simultaneously. It departs from prior BOBW algorithms (Tsallis-INF, hybrid FTRL) that require solving optimization problems at each round to compute arm-selection probabilities; instead, FTPL simply selects the m arms with the smallest perturbed estimated loss, avoiding this computational bottleneck. The analysis builds on the standard FTRL framework of Lattimore and Szepesvári [2020], which the authors extend to handle the case where the convex hull of the action set has no interior points—a structural difficulty absent in the m=1 case.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first FTPL algorithm to approach BOBW guarantees in the m-set semi-bandit setting (for m ≤ d/2), offering a computationally simpler alternative to FTRL-based methods that require explicit probability computation. The lower bounds demonstrating that the log(d) and m^(5/6)/d^(1/2) factors are inherent to the current analysis technique are important negative results, indicating that closing the gap to the optimal O(√(nmd)) bound requires fundamentally different methods. Key open questions include whether the ratio V_{4,i}/V_{3,i} can be bounded more tightly by analyzing the full summation directly, whether FTPL can match FTRL's BOBW guarantee in the regime m ≥ d/2, and whether the uniqueness-of-optimal-action assumption can be removed.
