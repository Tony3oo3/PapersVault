## Problem

Foundation models pretrained on noisy web-scale data inherit structural degradation that impairs downstream out-of-distribution generalization, a phenomenon called catastrophic inheritance. While this effect has been documented for natural images and general language, its impact on medical models—where annotations are scarce, domain shifts are large, and pretraining sources (e.g., Twitter pathology captions, auto-parsed radiology reports) are inherently noisy—remains unexplored. This paper asks whether pretraining label noise degrades medical downstream tasks, what structural mechanism drives the degradation, and how to mitigate it without retraining the backbone.

## Input / Output

The method consumes frozen feature vectors extracted from a pretrained backbone (ResNet-50, CLIP, PLIP, ViT-L, PubMedBERT, or GPT-2) applied to medical images (histopathology patches, dermoscopic photos, chest X-rays) or biomedical text sequences. A lightweight MLP and linear classifier head are trained on top of these frozen features, producing class labels (e.g., tumor vs. non-tumor, 7 skin-lesion categories, 14 chest-pathology findings) or token-level entity labels (gene, chemical, disease). The backbone parameters are never updated, making the approach compatible with closed-source or black-box medical foundation models.

## Method

The paper identifies that pretraining label noise causes a measurable flattening of feature and logit distributions, quantified by declining skewness and kurtosis across feature dimensions. To counteract this, the proposed SKD method inserts a lightweight MLP between the frozen backbone and classifier head, then augments the standard cross-entropy task loss with three scalar regularizers: a skewness loss that penalizes per-dimension deviation from a target asymmetry τ_s, a kurtosis loss that penalizes deviation from a target peakedness τ_k, and a disagreement loss that enlarges the margin between the ground-truth logit and the mean of incorrect-class logits. The targets τ_s and τ_k can be set to zero or computed from a clean reference model, and the method is insensitive to their exact values. No architectural changes to the backbone are required, and the added overhead is minimal (150 s vs. 20 s for plain linear probing on PanNuke), making it practical for real-world clinical deployment.

## Results

On CLIP backbones pretrained with noisy ImageNet-1K/YFCC15M, SKD achieves an average gain of 6.51% over LP on Camelyon17 (89.48% at 0% noise vs. 83.94% for LP), 7.33% on HAM10000 (55.19% vs. 49.36%), and 3.13% on NIH ChestX-ray (45.92% vs. 44.75%). On ResNet-50 backbones, SKD gains are 1.18% (Camelyon17), 3.09% (HAM10000), and 5.73% (ChestX-ray). On the real-world PLIP model, SKD reaches F1 0.959 on Kather colon and 0.956 on PanNuke, surpassing NML (0.931 and 0.948) and LP (0.899 and 0.930). Across five biomedical NER datasets with PubMedBERT, SKD improves F1 by up to 4.06% over LP (e.g., 0.9459 vs. 0.9053 on BC2GM).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SKD consistently outperforms both baselines across all six backbone–dataset combinations in Table 1. Against GCE (Zhang & Sabuncu, NeurIPS 2018), which provides only marginal gains over LP (0.16–0.94% average), SKD delivers substantially larger improvements of 1.18–7.33%, with the most dramatic gap on CLIP+HAM10000 (7.33% vs. 0.94%). Against NML (Chen et al., 2024), the prior SOTA for catastrophic inheritance, SKD wins on every setting: on CLIP+Camelyon17, SKD's average gain is 6.51% versus NML's 1.93%; on CLIP+ChestX-ray, NML actually degrades performance below LP (−6.29% average) while SKD improves it by 3.13%. On ResNet-50, NML is inconsistent (gaining 0.74% on Camelyon17 but losing 0.74% on HAM10000 and 1.07% on ChestX-ray), whereas SKD is uniformly positive. On the real-world PLIP model (Table 2), SKD surpasses NML on Kather colon (F1 0.959 vs. 0.931) and PanNuke (0.956 vs. 0.948), though NML edges out SKD slightly on DigestPath (0.979 vs. 0.976). On biomedical NER (Table 3), SKD outperforms NML on all five reported datasets, with the largest margin on BC2GM (F1 0.9459 vs. 0.9187, +2.72%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the catastrophic inheritance framework of Chen et al. [18, 19], which showed that pretraining label noise degrades OOD generalization and proposed spectral preservation (NML) as a remedy. The key departure is diagnostic and therapeutic: instead of analyzing the feature spectrum via singular values, the authors identify higher-order distributional shape (skewness and kurtosis) as the structural signature of collapse, and replace spectral regularization with two lightweight scalar regularizers on the fine-tuning loss. The work also explicitly distinguishes itself from the noisy-label learning literature [21, 22, 16], which targets noise in downstream supervised data, by isolating noise inherited from the pretraining stage under frozen-backbone transfer.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic study of how pretraining noise propagates into medical foundation models, a setting where annotations are scarce, domain shifts are large, and backbones are often frozen or closed-source, making lightweight fine-tuning the only practical lever. The finding that a simple skewness-kurtosis regularizer outperforms the more complex spectral NML approach (e.g., +6.51% vs. +1.93% on Camelyon17) suggests distributional shape is a more relevant structural signal for fine-grained clinical tasks than spectral energy. Open questions remain: the noise model is uniformly random (not structured or semantic), experiments use mid-sized backbones, and real-world medical pretraining corpora (e.g., PLIP's Twitter data) cannot be directly corrupted, so the gap between synthetic and deployed noise is unquantified.
