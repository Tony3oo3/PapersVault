## Problem

Gradient boosting is the dominant method for tabular prediction, yet principled statistical inference—confidence intervals, prediction intervals, and hypothesis tests for variable importance—remains largely unexplored. The only existing frequentist framework (Boulevard regularization) recovers at most half the true signal, does not construct proper intervals, and scales cubically in sample size, making it impractical for real datasets.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a tabular training set (X ∈ [0,1]^{n×d}, y ∈ R^n) with sub-Gaussian noise and produces, for any test point x: a point prediction, a (1−α) confidence interval for f(x), a prediction interval for y|x, a reproduction interval for an independently retrained booster, and a chi-squared test statistic for variable importance. A key practical detail is that all intervals and tests are computed in O(ns²) time via the Nyström approximation (s near-linear in effective dimension), and the prediction intervals enjoy conditional pointwise coverage—a stronger guarantee than conformal prediction's marginal coverage.

## Method

The paper proposes two algorithms that extend Boulevard regularization with dropout (BRAT-D) and parallel leave-one-out tree growth (BRAT-P). In BRAT-D, each tree in the ensemble is randomly subsampled with probability q during residual computation, and the residual sum is divided by b rather than the number of retained trees, so each new tree fits more of the unexplained signal; tuning the dropout rate p smoothly interpolates between vanilla Boulevard and a modified random forest. In BRAT-P, K trees are grown in parallel per round, each fit on residuals computed by leaving out its own column (a parallel backfitting scheme), which recovers the full signal without rescaling. Both algorithms are shown to converge almost surely to a kernel ridge regression and satisfy a central limit theorem, enabling the construction of asymptotically valid intervals and a chi-squared variable-importance test. The counterintuitive finding is that increasing dropout or parallelism improves signal recovery by up to a factor of 2 and asymptotic relative efficiency by up to a factor of 4 over vanilla Boulevard.

## Results

On nine UCI regression datasets, both BRAT-D (Algorithm 1) and BRAT-P (Algorithm 2) are competitive in test MSE with XGBoost, LightGBM, random forests, and vanilla boosting, though no single method dominates across all datasets. The key quantitative gains are in statistical efficiency: Algorithm 1 achieves up to a 4× improvement in asymptotic relative efficiency (ARE) and 2× signal recovery over vanilla Boulevard, while Algorithm 2 achieves at least 4× ARE improvement with no rescaling needed. The variable importance chi-squared test maintains correct size control while Type II error decreases rapidly with training set size, and prediction intervals attain near-nominal conditional coverage on the Friedman function after adaptive calibration.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against XGBoost and LightGBM, the proposed methods are competitive but do not consistently outperform; the authors note XGBoost is a 'consistently strong performer' and that Algorithm 2 occasionally exhibits instability on some datasets. Against vanilla Boulevard (Zhou & Hooker, 2022), both algorithms strictly improve: Algorithm 1 recovers up to 2× more signal (factor (1+λ)/(1+λq) ∈ (1,2]) and achieves up to 4× lower asymptotic variance, while Algorithm 2 requires no rescaling and achieves unbounded ARE gains as λ→0. Against random forests, the methods interpolate smoothly between boosting and RF behavior via the dropout rate p (Algorithm 1) or parallelism K (Algorithm 2), with RF being 'clearly more suited to some datasets.' Against conformal prediction (Barber et al., 2020), the proposed prediction intervals offer a stronger conditional coverage guarantee and variable-width intervals that identify 'hard' examples, whereas conformal intervals have constant width. Computationally, the Nyström-based inference runs in O(ns²) time versus O(n³) for prior Boulevard-based replication intervals, making the procedures practical at scale.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Boulevard regularization framework of Zhou and Hooker (2022), which established the first CLT for boosting via convergence to kernel ridge regression but recovered at most half the signal and provided no practical inference procedures. By incorporating random dropout (Algorithm 1) and a leave-one-out parallel tree-growing scheme (Algorithm 2), the authors improve signal recovery by up to a factor of 2 and asymptotic relative efficiency by up to a factor of 4, while retaining the KRR limit. The inference procedures (confidence, prediction, reproduction intervals, and chi-squared variable importance tests) are directly inspired by the analogous random forest inference literature of Wager et al. (2014) and Mentch and Hooker (2016a,b), and the computational tractability is achieved via the Nyström approximation of Musco and Musco (2017).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to provide a unified, practically implementable framework for frequentist statistical inference (intervals and hypothesis tests) for gradient boosting with formal CLTs, filling a gap that has persisted despite boosting's dominance in tabular prediction. The counterintuitive finding that dropout and parallelism *improve* signal recovery under regularization challenges the standard view of these techniques as purely variance-reduction tools. Open questions include relaxing the structure-value isolation and non-adaptivity assumptions, extending to classification and survival settings, and improving the convergence rate (currently minimax for 1/2-Hölder functions rather than the optimal Lipschitz rate). The methods remain competitive but do not consistently outperform XGBoost in raw MSE, limiting immediate adoption for pure prediction tasks.
