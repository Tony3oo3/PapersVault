## Problem

The optimization dynamics and sample complexity of gradient-based training for extensive-width (r ≍ d^β) two-layer quadratic neural networks with anisotropic, power-decaying second-layer coefficients (λ_j ∝ j^{−α}) remained open. Prior analyses either restricted to finite-rank targets, assumed isotropic coefficients, or required information exponent k ≥ 3 to decouple dynamics across feature directions—none of which apply to the quadratic (k = 2) extensive-width setting. This paper asks whether such networks exhibit smooth power-law risk scaling (analogous to LLM scaling laws) and establishes the precise optimization and sample complexity.

## Input / Output

The method consumes online Gaussian samples (x, y) where y is generated by a teacher network with r orthonormal signal directions and power-law decaying coefficients, along with a student width r_s and step size η. The output is not merely trained weights but sharp asymptotic risk curves: the population MSE as a function of rescaled time t·log d (or t·r·log d in the heavy-tailed regime), and alignment per teacher direction measured via subspace projection (polar decomposition) rather than individual neuron matching. The risk scaling takes the form t^{−(2α−1)/α} + r_s^{−(2α−1)} for α > 0.5, or (1 − t^{(1−2α)/α})_+ for α < 0.5, with the discrete-time version replacing t by ηt.

## Method

The analysis reduces the infinite-dimensional training dynamics to two matrix Riccati ODEs governing the weight Gram matrix and the alignment Gram matrix. A key technical ingredient is the monotonicity of the Riccati flow with respect to its initialization: by sandwiching the (generally non-diagonal) initial condition between diagonal upper and lower bounds, the coupled dynamics are bounded by decoupled scalar ODEs that converge to the same limit as d → ∞, yielding sharp alignment and risk characterizations. For the discrete-time online SGD on the Stiefel manifold, the Euler discretization of the Riccati equation loses monotonicity; the authors construct an auxiliary monotone discrete map (a rational approximation matching the Riccati update to second order) and use it to bound the true non-monotone iterates from above and below in operator norm. This operator-norm comparison framework—rather than the Frobenius-norm control used in finite-rank settings—is essential to avoid pessimistic r-dependent rates in the extensive-width regime. The algorithm itself is a two-phase procedure: Stiefel-constrained online SGD for subspace (directional) recovery, followed by a cheap fine-tuning step that fits the radial component via a closed-form least-squares solution.

## Results

The paper establishes sharp scaling laws for the prediction risk of two-layer quadratic neural networks in the extensive-width regime (r ≍ d^β). For the light-tailed regime (α > 0.5), the population gradient flow risk decays as t^(-(2α-1)/α) + r_s^(-(2α-1)), while for the heavy-tailed regime (α < 0.5), it follows (1 − t^((1-2α)/α))_+ + (1 − (r_s/r)^(1-2α))_+. The online SGD sample complexity is n ≍ dr^(1+α)·polylog(1+d/r_s) in the heavy-tailed case and n ≍ d·polylog(d) in the light-tailed case, the latter being information-theoretically optimal up to logarithmic factors. Empirically, ReLU networks trained with gradient descent exhibit power-law risk curves whose exponents closely match the theoretical quadratic prediction (e.g., ideal exponent −1 for α=1, empirical −1.01).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical analysis paper, so comparisons are to prior theoretical bounds rather than empirical baselines. In the isotropic setting (α = 0), the paper's sample complexity n ≍ dr·polylog(1+d/r_s) improves upon the recent result of Ren and Lee [RL24], which established a sufficient sample size of n ≳ d·poly(r) for a similar quadratic setting. In the light-tailed regime (α > 1/2), the required sample size n ≍ d·polylog(d) is information-theoretically optimal up to logarithmic factors, and substantially improves upon kernel methods and lazy-regime neural networks which require n ≳ d² samples to learn a quadratic target. The paper also extends the decoupling framework of Oko et al. [OSSW24] and Simsek et al. [SBH24], which assumed information exponent k ≥ 3, to the k = 2 quadratic case that was previously open. For the intermediate heavy-tailed regime α ∈ (0, 1/2), the authors conjecture the optimal sample complexity is T ≍ dr, implying their current bound is suboptimal by a factor of r^α.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the multi-index model learning literature (phase retrieval, multi-spike PCA) from the finite-rank setting to the extensive-width regime (r ≍ d^β) with anisotropic power-law second-layer coefficients, a setting where prior analyses either assumed isotropic contributions or yielded exponential dimension dependence. The technical core builds on the closed-form matrix Riccati ODE solution for quadratic networks [MBB23] and the Stiefel-manifold SGD framework [AGP24], but introduces a novel monotonicity-with-respect-to-initialization argument that overcomes the failure of decoupling at information exponent k=2 (where [OSSW24, SBH24] required k≥3). The work also connects to the neural scaling laws literature [KMH+20, HBM+22] by providing a rigorous feature-learning mechanism that produces power-law risk decay, in contrast to prior scaling law analyses that assumed lazy/random-feature training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first rigorous derivation of scaling laws for gradient-based feature learning in an extensive-width nonlinear network, validating the 'additive model hypothesis' that superposition of emergent staircase curves at different timescales yields smooth power-law decay. The sample complexity in the isotropic case (n ≍ dr·polylog) improves upon [RL24] and is information-theoretically optimal up to logs, while the heavy-tailed regime result (n ≍ dr^{1+α}) is conjectured suboptimal by a factor of r^α. Key open questions include extending the analysis to ReLU/GeLU activations (where multi-phase dynamics from higher Hermite modes complicate the picture), closing the gap in the heavy-tailed sample complexity, and understanding whether standard Euclidean SGD (rather than Stiefel-constrained) achieves the same scaling.
