## Problem

Existing emergency traffic control methods focus exclusively on traffic light signal preemption, leaving emergency vehicles (EMVs) and regular vehicles (REVs) without coordinated navigation strategies. This creates a seesaw problem where facilitating EMV passage through green waves disrupts regular traffic, and the lack of joint coordination among all three agent types (EMVs, REVs, traffic lights) prevents globally efficient traffic flow during emergencies.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254640/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At each discrete timestep, the method consumes structured observations from all agents: traffic lights report signal phase, queue lengths, lane occupancy, flow rate, and traffic pressure; vehicles report current intersection, lane, and destination. These observations are organized into a heterogeneous temporal graph with four edge types (TL-REV, TL-EMV, TL-TL, EMV-REV) that is rebuilt every step. The method outputs discrete actions (traffic light phase selection from 8 non-conflicting phases, vehicle routing as left/straight/right) plus continuous dynamic role embeddings that condition the policy and drive a role-aware intrinsic reward mechanism.

## Method

RMTC constructs a Heterogeneous Temporal Traffic Graph (HTTG) at each timestep, encoding four relation types among EMVs, REVs, and traffic lights, and processes it through a two-layer heterogeneous role convolution with type-specific weight matrices. A Dynamic Role Learning module infers per-agent role embeddings by maximizing MINE-style mutual information lower bounds between each agent's embedding and both the EMV's positional features and a GRU-encoded EMV trajectory history, while a temporal contrastive loss enforces role stability across consecutive steps. The learned role embedding is concatenated with raw observations to form the agent state, and a role-aware intrinsic reward redistributes EMV extrinsic rewards to nearby agents weighted by cosine similarity of their role embeddings to the EMV feature. Training uses PPO with shared policy networks across same-type agents, optimizing a combined extrinsic-plus-intrinsic reward, while the role encoder is updated periodically using the role loss in a decoupled schedule.

## Results

RMTC achieves the best EMV travel time across all four datasets: 112.00s (Grid 4×4), 141.00s (Avenue 4×4), 36.00s (Cologne8), and 26.00s (FengLin), with the largest improvements of 23.40% and 23.52% on Cologne8 and FengLin respectively. It also attains the lowest REV travel time on every dataset (163.62s, 491.49s, 87.90s, 218.91s), improving over the best baseline by 3.96%–7.84%. All improvements are statistically significant (p < 0.01) relative to the best-performing baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RMTC outperforms all eight baselines on both EMV and REV travel time across all four datasets. Against the strongest EMV-focused baselines, RMTC reduces EMV travel time by 4.27% over X-Light/EMVLight (117→112s) on Grid 4×4, by 9.03% over RECAL (162→141s) on Avenue 4×4, by 23.40% over RECAL/EMVLight (47→36s) on Cologne8, and by 23.52% over EMVLight (34→26s) on FengLin. For regular vehicles, RMTC beats the best REV baseline (X-Light at 170.37s on Grid, rMAPPO at 519.92s on Avenue, rMAPPO at 95.38s on Cologne8, IPPO at 230.91s on FengLin) by 3.96%, 5.46%, 7.84%, and 5.19% respectively. Rule-based methods (FixedTime, MaxPressure) perform worst overall, with MaxPressure particularly damaging to REV travel time (e.g., 768.82s on Avenue vs. RMTC's 491.49s). General-purpose RL methods (CoLight, IPPO, rMAPPO, X-Light) lack explicit EMV awareness and show large EMV delays, while EMV-specific methods (RECAL, EMVLight) sacrifice REV efficiency (e.g., RECAL inflates Avenue REV time to 757.83s). RMTC's joint optimization of vehicle routing and signal control is the key differentiator, achieving simultaneous improvements on both metrics without the seesaw trade-off seen in prior work.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the emergency traffic control line (EMVLight, RECAL) that previously coordinated only traffic lights and EMV routing, by adding regular vehicle (REV) navigation as a third jointly-optimized agent type. The role-learning mechanism draws on MINE-style mutual information estimation (Belghazi et al., 2018) and heterogeneous graph message passing, while the multi-agent optimization uses PPO with shared policy networks as in rMAPPO. Conceptually, it also connects to the role-based MARL literature (ROMA, RODE) by learning per-agent role embeddings, but applies them to the traffic domain with a temporal heterogeneous graph that captures EMV-specific spatial and trajectory relationships.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Jointly coordinating traffic signals, EMV routing, and REV navigation is a more realistic and practically deployable formulation than light-only control, and the role mechanism provides a principled way to handle the heterogeneity of agents at different distances from an EMV. However, the approach assumes vehicles strictly follow assigned routes and only activates during emergency events, limiting its applicability to mixed normal/emergency traffic. Open questions include scalability to city-wide networks, robustness to driver non-compliance, and whether the role embeddings generalize across different road topologies without retraining.
