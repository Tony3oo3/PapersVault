## Problem

Most high-performing vision-language models are closed-source, and the community's reliance on distillation from proprietary models to label training data makes it impossible to measure genuine scientific progress in model design and training. Furthermore, the paper demonstrates through scaling-law analysis that synthetic data generation plateaus on challenging video understanding tasks (fine-grained activity, spatio-temporal reasoning), revealing a critical data gap that no amount of synthetic scaling can fill.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250795/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For images, PLM accepts up to 36 dynamic tiles at 448² resolution, each compressed via 2×2 average pooling; for video, it processes 32 frames at 448² with the same spatial pooling. Region-grounded tasks (RCap, RTLoc, RDCap) take a masklet specified as a colored box overlay on video frames alongside the video and a text prompt. Outputs range from multiple-choice or open-ended text answers, to video-level captions, to structured temporal intervals (start/end timestamps), to dense sequences of (start, end, caption) tuples covering the full video duration including out-of-frame periods.

## Method

PLM pairs a pre-trained Perception Encoder (PE L/14 for 1B/3B, PE G/14 for 8B) with a Llama 3 decoder via a 2-layer MLP projector, trained in three stages: projector warmup on 1M synthetic images, large-scale midtraining on 72M open-source synthetic samples (16 tiles/frames), and supervised fine-tuning on 19M samples at higher resolution (36 tiles/32 frames). The synthetic data engine uses only open-source models (Llama 3 for captioning, PySceneDetect for video segmentation, OCR metadata) to generate ~66.1M samples, establishing power-law scaling relationships that reveal synthetic data is effective for base tasks (α ≈ −0.15) but fails for challenging video tasks (α ≈ −0.03). To close this gap, the authors collect 2.8M human-verified annotations: PLM-FGQA (2.4M fine-grained video QA pairs across 780K clips from cooking, DIY, automotive, and egocentric domains) and PLM-STC (194.2K spatio-temporal captions with SAM 2-derived masklets and timestamps, converted into RCap/RTLoc/RDCap training tasks). They also introduce PLM-VideoBench, a five-subtask evaluation suite (FGQA, SGQA, RCap, RTLoc, RDCap) probing what/where/when/how of video content, with human performance baselines and LLM-judge metrics.

## Results

On the proposed PLM-VideoBench, PLM-8B achieves an average score of 55.6, outperforming GPT-4o (51.6), Gemini 1.5 Pro (44.0), and the best open-access model InternVL2.5-8B (38.5), while human performance is 73.9. On standard video benchmarks, PLM-8B reaches 77.1 on MVBench, 68.8 on EgoSchema, 82.7 on PerceptionTest, and 58.6 on Charades-STA (temporal localization), surpassing all open-access baselines of comparable scale. On image benchmarks, PLM-8B scores 94.6 on DocVQA, 85.5 on ChartQA, and 870 on OCRBench, competitive with proprietary models like GPT-4o (92.8, 85.7, 810).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PLM-8B decisively outperforms all open-access baselines on PLM-VideoBench, achieving 55.6 average vs. InternVL2.5-8B (38.5), Qwen2.5VL-7B (34.8), and LLaVA-OV-7B (32.0), with the largest gaps on spatio-temporal tasks (RDCap: 52.8 vs. 5.7 for InternVL2.5-8B; RTLoc: 59.1 vs. 8.8). Against proprietary models, PLM-8B beats GPT-4o (51.6) and both Gemini variants (44.0, 42.5) on the overall PLM-VideoBench average, though GPT-4o retains an edge on SGQA (63.7 vs. 46.2). On standard video benchmarks, PLM-8B surpasses InternVL2.5-8B on MVBench (77.1 vs. 72.6), PerceptionTest (82.7 vs. 68.9), TemporalBench (28.3 vs. 24.3), MotionBench (61.4 vs. 53.5), and Charades-STA (58.6 vs. 14.3), while GPT-4o still leads on EgoSchema (72.2 vs. 68.8) and TemporalBench (38.5 vs. 28.3). On image benchmarks, PLM-8B is competitive with GPT-4o (e.g., DocVQA 94.6 vs. 92.8, OCRBench 870 vs. 810) and outperforms InternVL2.5-8B on most OCR and perception tasks, though it trails on MMMU (46.1 vs. 56.0) and BLINK (56.0 vs. 54.8, roughly tied). The ablation confirms that PLM's advantage on fine-grained and spatio-temporal tasks comes specifically from the human-annotated PLM-FGQA and PLM-STC data, which synthetic data alone cannot replicate.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant VLM training paradigm that relies on distillation from proprietary models (GPT-4o, Gemini) to generate training data, instead building entirely on open-source components: the Perception Encoder as vision backbone, Llama 3 as language decoder, and a data engine using only open models for synthetic annotation. It extends the Molmo line of open-data VLMs by scaling to video understanding and introducing a three-stage training pipeline (projector warmup → large-scale synthetic midtraining → human-annotated SFT) with dynamic tiling for high-resolution inputs. The spatio-temporal captioning formulation (PLM-STC) builds on SAM 2 for masklet generation and extends prior video captioning and temporal localization work by jointly associating captions with both temporal intervals and dense segmentation masks, a combination absent from prior datasets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By establishing that synthetic data scaling follows a power law with exponent −0.15 for general video QA but only −0.03 for challenging fine-grained tasks, the paper provides quantitative evidence that the field's current 'scale synthetic data' strategy has fundamental limits for the hardest video understanding capabilities. The 2.8M human-labeled video instances (nearly 8× the largest existing video QA dataset) and PLM-VideoBench create a reproducible foundation for measuring genuine scientific progress without proprietary confounds. Open questions include whether better synthetic data engines can close the gap on hard video tasks, whether the three-stage pipeline (which outperforms a merged single-stage training despite lower FLOPs) is optimal, and whether the identified data gaps generalize beyond the specific domains covered (cooking, DIY, automotive repair).
