## Problem

Current multimodal LLM benchmarks primarily assess passive visual perception (captioning, VQA, retrieval) but offer limited insight into the more active cognitive skill of mental visualization—internally constructing and manipulating visual representations to support reasoning, prediction, and extrapolation. This paper introduces Hyperphantasia, a synthetic benchmark specifically designed to probe whether MLLMs can perform this type of active visual reasoning, and reveals a substantial performance gap between state-of-the-art models and humans.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250132/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each puzzle presents a single 384×384 synthetic image (dot grids, contour samples, partial geometric edges, or partial trajectories) alongside a text prompt with four multiple-choice options. Models are instructed to reason freely but must enclose their final answer in <ANSWER> tags; temperature is set to 0 for deterministic evaluation. The four puzzle types—Seven Segments, Connect the Dots, Linear Trajectory, and Parabolic Trajectory—each span three difficulty levels (Easy/Medium/Hard) with 100 samples per level, totaling 1,200 samples. Difficulty is controlled by specific hyperparameters: number of digits, minimum dot spacing, visible edge percentage, or visible trajectory arc length.

## Method

Hyperphantasia is a procedurally generated benchmark organized into two categories: Interpolation (Seven Segments, Connect the Dots) testing inference of internal structure from partial information, and Extrapolation (Linear Trajectory, Parabolic Trajectory) testing prediction of visual structures beyond given data. The paper evaluates 13+ state-of-the-art MLLMs (proprietary and open-source) and finds that most models perform near random guessing on harder puzzles, exhibit hallucinated visual features, or resort to fixed/heuristic answers. To address these limitations, the authors apply GRPO-based reinforcement learning to Qwen-VL 2.5 7B on the extrapolation tasks, training on various difficulty and task mixtures. They show that training solely on Easy puzzles induces brittle heuristics (e.g., always selecting the rightmost point), while training on Medium or mixed Easy+Medium data enables meaningful generalization to Hard puzzles and cross-task transfer. A targeted ablation on Seven Segments—adding explicit edges to the image—reveals that most models fail even when the visual answer is directly rendered, exposing a separate visual robustness limitation beyond mental visualization.

## Results

On Hyperphantasia, the best-performing model (o4-mini) achieves a mean accuracy of 62.75% (Easy), 51.25% (Medium), and 51.25% (Hard), compared to human accuracy of 99.72%, 84.93%, and 84.22% respectively — a gap of 37–48 percentage points. Performance degrades sharply with difficulty: on Hard Parabolic Trajectory, nearly all models fall to near random-guessing (~25%), while humans still achieve 52.33%. On Seven Segments, most models score 0% across all difficulties, with only o4-mini (83–85%) and Gemini 2.5 Pro (40–51%) showing partial competence. The RL-trained Qwen-VL 2.5 7B (Parabolic Mix) reaches 44% on Hard Parabolic Trajectory, surpassing o4-mini's 33% on that specific task.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All evaluated MLLMs fall substantially short of human performance across every puzzle type and difficulty level. Among proprietary models, o4-mini leads with 62.75% mean Easy accuracy, followed by Gemini 2.5 Pro (51.25%) and Claude 3.7 Sonnet (44.25%); GPT-4o trails at 35.75% despite strong Connect the Dots performance (96% Easy). Among open-source models, LLaVA-OneVision 72B is the strongest (40.75% Easy mean), outperforming Llama 3.2 90B (35.25%), Qwen VL 2.5 32B (32.00%), Molmo 72B (29.25%), and Deepseek-VL2 (26.75%). Notably, no open-source model exceeds 50% on any single Hard-difficulty task, whereas o4-mini reaches 85% on Hard Seven Segments. The RL-trained Qwen-VL 2.5 7B (Parabolic Mix) achieves 44% on Hard Parabolic Trajectory, beating o4-mini (33%) and all other baselines on that specific task, demonstrating that targeted RL can close part of the gap on extrapolation tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by defining mental visualization as a distinct, evaluable capability separate from passive visual perception, drawing on cognitive science literature (Shepard & Metzler mental rotation, intuitive physics) to motivate the task design. It positions itself against existing VLM benchmarks (VQAv2, MMMU, MathVista, SRBench, LEGO-Puzzles) that the authors argue primarily test perception, captioning, or retrieval rather than active internal construction and extrapolation of visual structure. Methodologically, it repurposes the GRPO reinforcement learning framework for eliciting visual reasoning in a 7B VLM, a novel application of RLVR to a perceptual-spatial task rather than a linguistic one.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a stark and consistent gap between human performance (84–99% mean) and even the best MLLMs (≤51% mean), suggesting that current architectures lack a unified mechanism for visual simulation. The Seven Segments ablation—where explicitly drawing edges still yields 0% accuracy for most models—exposes a deeper visual robustness failure beyond mental visualization, pointing to a missing layer of structured visual abstraction. Open questions include whether visual thinking tokens or omni-model architectures can close this gap, and whether the RL-based improvements generalize beyond the narrow extrapolation tasks tested. The benchmark's scope (4 synthetic puzzle types) limits its coverage of the broader mental visualization space.
