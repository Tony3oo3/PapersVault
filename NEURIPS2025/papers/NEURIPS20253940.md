## Problem

While in-context learning enables LLMs to answer questions using external context, the specific mechanism by which individual attention heads process contextual information versus parametric memory during retrieval-augmented generation remains unclear. Prior work identified in-context and parametric heads but did not reveal how in-context heads further specialize into distinct functional roles (instruction comprehension vs. verbatim retrieval) or demonstrate their causal influence on answer generation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253940/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on instruction-tuned LLMs (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, Gemma-2-9B-it) consuming QA prompts composed of a question plus a context passage (oracle, counterfactual, or DPR-retrieved from Wikipedia). Beyond head categorization, the pipeline produces extractable function vectors that can be inserted into arbitrary prompts to induce specific behaviors, and a lightweight linear probe that outputs both a binary parametric-vs-contextual label for the first generated token and a token index pinpointing the source span within the context document.

## Method

The core technique is AttnLRP-based attribution: positive relevance scores are summed over each head's latent output to quantify its contribution to a target logit, and a difference score D between open-book (counterfactual context) and closed-book (no context) settings separates in-context heads (top positive D) from parametric heads (top negative D). Within in-context heads, attention-weight relevance is decomposed by token position—relevance flowing to question tokens identifies task heads encoding the intensional frame, while relevance to answer-object tokens identifies retrieval heads performing verbatim copying. Causal validation is achieved by extracting function vectors from task and parametric heads and inserting them into unrelated prompts (inducing question-answering or entity-attribute recall), and by amplifying retrieval-head attention weights in needle-in-a-haystack settings to force position-independent copying. For source tracking, each retrieval head's output at the final token position is decoded via logit lens, and a linear probe trained on these scores classifies the first answer token as parametric or contextual while a weighted aggregation of attention maps localizes the exact source token in the context.

## Results

Ablating 20 identified in-context heads reduces open-book QA recall by 13.86%–63.84%, while ablating 100 parametric heads reduces closed-book recall by 44.26%–68.66%, confirming their causal roles. Extracting function vectors (FVs) from task heads yields a 78.75-point average recall improvement over random heads in zero-shot induction, parametric FVs give a 30.41-point gain, and boosting retrieval-head attention weights produces an 83.15-point gain in verbatim copying. For source tracking, the retrieval-head probe achieves ROC AUC ≥ 94% in distinguishing contextual from parametric predictions and top-1 localization accuracy ≥ 84% across Llama 3.1, Mistral v0.3, and Gemma 2.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to the Attention Weight Recall (AWR) method of Wu et al. [83], which identifies only retrieval heads, the paper's AttnLRP-based method recovers both in-context and parametric heads. Ablating just 20 of the paper's in-context heads causes a recall drop roughly equivalent to ablating 100 AWR heads, and the performance decrease from ablating in-context heads is more drastic than ablating the same number of AWR heads, indicating that attribution-based identification is more precise than attention-score-based selection. For source tracking, the retrieval-head probe (using only forward-pass attention maps) is slightly outperformed by a full AttnLRP backward pass in top-1 localization accuracy (e.g., 97% vs. 98% on Llama 3.1, 96% vs. 99% on Mistral v0.3, 84% vs. 96% on Gemma 2), but the probe avoids the extra backward pass, making it more computationally efficient for deployment. Random-head ablation barely affects model performance, confirming that the identified heads are genuinely specialized rather than artifacts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on AttnLRP (Achtibat et al., ICML 2024) as its core attribution mechanism, extending its use from general feature attribution to the specific problem of categorizing attention heads by their functional role in retrieval-augmented generation. It departs from prior work on in-context and parametric heads (Jin et al., ACL 2024 Findings) by revealing a finer-grained specialization: in-context heads further decompose into task heads (encoding the intensional frame/instruction) and retrieval heads (performing verbatim copying), a distinction not captured by attention-weight-based methods like AWR (Wu et al., ICLR 2025). The use of function vectors (Todd et al., ICLR 2024) and logit lens decoding (nostalgebraist, 2020) are repurposed here as causal intervention tools to demonstrate head functionality, rather than as standalone interpretability techniques.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first attribution-based mechanistic map of how RAG operates at the attention-head level, enabling practitioners to selectively ablate heads to resolve knowledge conflicts between parametric memory and retrieved context. The source-tracking probe (94%+ ROC AUC, 84%+ top-1 localization) offers a lightweight, forward-pass-only method for attributing generated tokens to specific context spans, a practical step toward transparent RAG systems. Key limitations include the focus on attention heads alone (MLP interactions remain unexplored), the primary reliance on QA tasks, and the absence of error bars across runs. Open questions include whether the task/retrieval head decomposition generalizes to non-QA tasks, how these heads interact with knowledge neurons in MLP layers, and whether redundant heads can be identified and pruned.
