## Problem

Weakly Supervised Video Anomaly Detection (WSVAD) models achieve strong clean performance but collapse to near-random accuracy under adversarial perturbations. The core difficulty is that the MIL framework provides only video-level labels, so standard adversarial training produces weak perturbations that affect only the single frame selected by the max-aggregator, leaving all other frames unprotected. This paper identifies and addresses this fundamental mismatch between video-level training supervision and frame-level attack surface.

## Input / Output

The model takes a video clip partitioned into chunks of 16 frames (224×224 resolution) and processes each chunk through an X-CLIP encoder, computing dot products with frozen text embeddings for the prompts 'Normal' and 'Abnormal' to yield per-chunk anomaly scores via softmax. At inference, each chunk's score is duplicated across its constituent frames, and a fixed threshold τ=0.5 converts scores into binary normal/abnormal labels. The adversarial training phase additionally consumes synthetic pseudo-anomaly videos generated by the SRD module, which carry exact chunk-level ground-truth annotations.

## Method

FrameShield is a two-phase adversarial training pipeline. Phase 1 trains a PromptMIL model (X-CLIP encoder + text-prompt scoring + max-aggregator + BCE loss) under standard weak supervision, then uses it to generate noisy frame-level pseudo-labels for real anomaly videos. Phase 2 switches to fully supervised chunk-wise BCE loss and performs PGD-10 adversarial training (ε=0.5/255, ℓ∞) on a combined dataset of normal videos (exact labels), real anomaly videos (pseudo-labels), and synthetic pseudo-anomalies produced by Spatiotemporal Region Distortion (SRD). SRD identifies foreground objects via Grad-CAM on a pre-trained ResNet-18, applies k harsh augmentations to the largest connected component within a bounding box, and propagates the distorted region across consecutive frames along a randomly curved vector to simulate spatiotemporal motion irregularities. The theoretical contribution shows that max-based MIL aggregation restricts gradient flow to a single frame, making video-level adversarial training inherently weak, and proves that frame-level boundary-error losses (analogous to TRADES) provide a sharp upper bound on robust risk, justifying the shift to per-frame adversarial optimization.

## Results

Under PGD-1000 adversarial attacks (ε=0.5/255), FrameShield maintains 76.2–87.1% overall AUROC across five benchmarks (UCSD Ped2, ShanghaiTech, TAD, UCF Crime, MSAD), while all SOTA baselines collapse to 0–12% AUROC. On clean data, FrameShield achieves 78.9–97.1% overall AUROC, slightly below the strongest baselines (e.g., 89.5% vs. 97.2% on ShanghaiTech) but within a few points. On anomaly-segment AUROC under attack, FrameShield reaches 30.0–91.3%, compared to 0–13% for all other methods. The paper reports an average 71.0% improvement in overall AUROC over SOTA under adversarial conditions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FrameShield decisively outperforms all seven traditional WSVAD baselines (RTFM, TEVAD, MGFN, Base MIL, UMIL, UR-DMU, VAD-CLIP) under PGD-1000 attack: while these methods drop to 0–12% overall AUROC, FrameShield retains 76–87%. On clean data, FrameShield is slightly behind the strongest baselines (e.g., 89.5% vs. 97.2% for RTFM on ShanghaiTech; 85.1% vs. 92.9% for UMIL on TAD), a trade-off the authors attribute to the inherent cost of adversarial training. Against the two multimodal LLM-based methods (Holmes-VAU and LAVAD), FrameShield also wins under attack: on ShanghaiTech, FrameShield achieves 87.1% vs. 16.0% (Holmes-VAU) and 21.4% (LAVAD); on UCF Crime, 78.7% vs. 14.2% and 18.7% respectively. Even when the baselines are themselves adversarially trained (Table 17), they still achieve only 14–23% AUROC under PGD, far below FrameShield's 76–87%. On anomaly-segment AUROC (Table 2), the gap widens further: FrameShield reaches 30–91% under attack versus 0–13% for all baselines, confirming that the robustness gain is not merely an artifact of normal-frame classification.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the adversarial training paradigm (rooted in TRADES-style boundary-error regularization) to the weakly supervised video anomaly detection (WSVAD) setting, where only video-level labels are available. It departs from the dominant MIL-based WSVAD line (Sultani et al., MGFN, UMIL, UR-DMU, VAD-CLIP) by replacing video-level max-aggregation loss with frame-level chunk-wise supervised loss during adversarial training, and by introducing Spatiotemporal Region Distortion (SRD) to generate precisely labeled synthetic anomalies that mitigate pseudo-label noise. The backbone leverages X-CLIP, connecting to the recent VLM-based WSVAD trend (CLIP-TSA, VAD-CLIP, TEVAD), while the SRD module draws on data augmentation techniques (Cutout, Copy-Paste) and Grad-CAM for region identification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FrameShield is the first adversarial training pipeline purpose-built for WSVAD, filling a clear gap: prior SOTA methods collapse to near-random AUROC under modest PGD perturbations, undermining their deployment in safety-critical surveillance. The theoretical demonstration that MIL max-aggregation restricts gradient flow to a single frame (making video-level adversarial training inherently weak) provides a principled justification for frame-level training and may inform robustness design in other MIL-based tasks. Open questions include whether the clean-vs-robust trade-off can be further narrowed (e.g., via TRADES-style losses, which the authors explore but do not fully resolve), whether SRD's heuristic region selection generalizes to more diverse anomaly types, and whether the approach extends to other weakly supervised video tasks beyond anomaly detection.
