## Problem

Most gradient-based optimizers stop searching the parameter space once they reach a local minimum, relying solely on local gradient information that cannot guarantee the found minimum is the lowest or best-generalizing. This limitation is especially acute in large-batch training, where far fewer parameter updates are available and the optimizer must find a good minimum within a constrained budget, yet the learning rate is bounded by landscape geometry and cannot simply be scaled up indefinitely.

## Input / Output

The adaptor takes standard stochastic gradient estimates g_k computed from mini-batches of size |Z_k| (ranging from 128 to 50K in experiments), the current model parameters θ_k, and a small set of hyperparameters (learning rate η_k, three EMA decay factors β_1, β_2, β_3, acceleration factor α constrained by |α| < 1/(1−β_1), and weight decay λ_k). It outputs updated parameters θ_{k+1} via a layer-wise normalized step that incorporates both the modified gradient (g_k + α·a_k) and Lamb's per-layer trust ratio, producing a trained model with improved generalization. The key unusual input is the gradient difference (g_k − g_{k-1}), which serves as an acceleration signal encoding the curvature-dependent direction for escaping sharp minima.

## Method

The paper proposes a universal gradient-based optimizer adaptor 'E' that replaces the raw gradient g_k with g_k + α·a_k, where a_k = β_1·a_{k−1} + (1−β_1)(g_k − g_{k−1}) is an exponential moving average of consecutive gradient differences. The theoretical insight is that −∇‖∇f(θ_k)‖² ≈ H_k(θ_k − θ_{k−1}) ≈ g̅_k − g̅_{k−1}, meaning the gradient-difference direction naturally amplifies steps near sharp minima (large Hessian norm) while shrinking them near flat minima, enabling the optimizer to escape sharp local minima while being captured by flatter ones. Applied to Lamb with layer-wise regularization, the resulting ALTO optimizer uses negative α (e.g., α = −5, β_1 = 0.99) for large-batch training to maximize exploration range and preferentially converge to flatter minima, while positive α (e.g., α = 0.5, β_1 = 0.01) suits small-batch settings. The paper provides convergence proofs for both non-convex (O(ε⁻²) iteration complexity, improving on Lamb's O(ε⁻⁴)) and convex (O(√T) regret) settings, and demonstrates that ALTO outperforms Lamb by an average of 2.5% test accuracy across 17 CV and NLP large-batch tasks.

## Results

On large-batch training (batch size 16384), ALTO achieves 88.83% top-1 accuracy on CIFAR-10 and 57.78% on CIFAR-100 with ResNet-20, outperforming all baselines. On ImageNet with ResNet-50, ALTO beats Lamb at every batch size from 1K to 32K (e.g., 77.35% vs 76.92% at 4K). For GPT-2 (345M) pre-training with batch size 4096, ALTO achieves 78.37 test perplexity versus Lamb's 83.13, a 4.76-point improvement. ALTO also reaches 70.83% on ImageNet (batch 4086, ResNet-34), surpassing small-batch SGD (70.64% at batch 256).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ALTO consistently outperforms Lamb (its direct base optimizer) in large-batch settings: +5.27% on CIFAR-10 (88.83 vs 83.56), +1.72% on CIFAR-100 (57.78 vs 56.06), and +0.49% on ImageNet batch 4086 (70.83 vs 70.34). Against AdaBelief, ALTO gains +0.80% on CIFAR-10 large-batch and +4.84% on CIFAR-100 large-batch. On ImageNet ResNet-50 (Table 4), ALTO beats Lamb at all six batch sizes (1K–32K), with the largest margin at 4K (77.35% vs 76.92%). In GPT-2 pre-training, ALTO achieves 78.37 perplexity versus Lamb's 83.13 and Lion's 110.54, while also reaching that perplexity in roughly one-third of Lion's iterations. On small-batch (128) CIFAR-10, SGD (91.85%) and AdaBelief (91.12%) remain competitive with ALTO (91.24%), confirming that ALTO's advantage is most pronounced at larger batch sizes where gradient noise is lower and the exploration term a_k carries more informative signal.

## Relation to Prior Work

ALTO is built directly on Lamb's layerwise-adaptive structure and Adam's EMA machinery, adding a single exploration term a_k (an EMA of successive gradient differences) that nudges the optimizer to keep moving along low-loss valleys rather than settling at the first local minimum it encounters. Conceptually, this is closest to Adan (which also tracks EMA of g_k − g_{k−1}) and Lion (which uses a double-EMA of the gradient), but ALTO differs by injecting the acceleration into the gradient before the momentum/second-moment computation, using a negative α to favor exploration over exploitation, and retaining the second-moment normalization. The paper also provides convergence proofs for both convex and non-convex settings, extending the analysis framework used for Lamb and Adam under weaker hyperparameter constraints.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Large-batch training of modern LLMs and vision models is a central practical bottleneck, and ALTO's consistent ~2.5% accuracy gain over Lamb (the de-facto SOTA for this regime) across 17 CV/NLP tasks, together with a 29.7% wall-clock savings at matched accuracy, makes it a potentially drop-in upgrade for production training pipelines. The main open questions are: (i) the two extra hyperparameters (β₁, α) require task-dependent tuning and the paper acknowledges no universal optimal setting; (ii) the 'valley-following' narrative is illustrated in 2D but its validity in the high-dimensional, non-convex landscapes of real networks remains unproven; (iii) the ~2% extra GPU memory and per-iteration cost, while small, compound at the scale of trillion-parameter models where every FLOP matters. Whether the exploration mechanism provides a genuine generalization advantage beyond simply using a larger effective learning rate is not cleanly isolated by the ablations.
