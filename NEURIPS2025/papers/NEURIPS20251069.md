## Problem

Deep learning approaches to Bayesian Experiment Design suffer from 'belief explosion': as an agent progresses deeper into an adaptive experiment, the effective number of realizable posterior beliefs grows enormously, causing standard architectures (FCNs, CNNs, Transformers) to overfit and fail to generalize to unseen belief states at deeper timesteps. The paper argues that this bottleneck is exacerbated by the absence of an appropriate inductive bias and identifies domain permutation equivariance—a structural property of optimal BED policies—as a critical, previously overlooked symmetry that can dramatically tame belief diversity.

## Input / Output

In the discrete setting, the input is a complete undirected graph with M nodes (one per discretized domain point), where each node is labeled with the 2D posterior statistics (μ_t(x), σ²_t(x)) and each of the M(M−1)/2 edges is labeled with the posterior covariance Cov(f(x), f(x′)|I_t). The output is a discrete action selecting one of the M domain points. In the continuous setting (Appendix F), the input is the raw information set I_t with t−1 nodes carrying observed values and edges carrying kernel evaluations k(x_i, x_j), and the output is a continuous query point parameterized as a learned convex combination of previously observed locations.

## Method

The paper proves (Theorem 1) that a large family of BED tasks satisfy reward and transition invariance under domain permutations, implying that optimal policies are equivariant and optimal Q-values are invariant under the same permutations. This structural property is exploited by representing the posterior belief as a complete graph and using a TransformerConv GNN as the policy/critic architecture, which is permutation-equivariant by design. Training is performed via both behavior cloning (imitating a 1-step greedy expected-information-gain expert) and reinforcement learning (DDQN with nonstationary Q-values and integrated Bellman targets). The GNN achieves up to two orders of magnitude improvement in sample efficiency over FCN, CNN, and Transformer baselines, and generalizes zero-shot from small training grids to significantly larger test domains, reducing online compute by an order of magnitude at test time.

## Results

In behavior cloning, the GNN matches the 1-step greedy expert's rollout performance using only 5,000 samples on both Active Search and Bayesian Optimization tasks, while FCN, CNN, and Transformer baselines overfit and fail to generalize. In reinforcement learning, the GNN is the only architecture that successfully learns non-myopic policies in either task; all other networks train so slowly they cannot even reach myopic performance within the same budget. For generalization, a GNN trained on a 2D BO task (5,000 samples) transfers zero-shot to 3D and 5D tasks, matching the EI expert (1.49±0.06 vs 1.48±0.06 on 3D; 1.47±0.05 vs 1.48±0.05 on 5D) while reducing inference time from ~0.3 ms (EI) to ~57 ms for the full amortized policy. On the 32×32 Active Search grid, the GNN achieves 7.15±0.12 recovery in 67.5 ms, whereas the greedy EIG expert is computationally intractable (>10,000 ms).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the FCN (dense network) baseline, the GNN achieves at least an order-of-magnitude improvement in sample efficiency for behavior cloning, matching the expert at 5,000 samples while the FCN still overfits and stagnates. The CNN baseline, which provides a stronger spatial inductive bias, learns more efficiently than the FCN but remains limited by its inability to model cross-point correlations in the belief state, resulting in consistently worse test performance than the GNN. The Transformer baseline (operating on information sets) is highly data-hungry and fails to match the GNN on both AS and BO tasks at all dataset sizes tested. Against non-amortized acquisition function baselines (EI, UCB, Thompson Sampling), the GNN matches or slightly exceeds the EI expert's performance on 2D–5D BO tasks while incurring ~200× higher per-step inference time (57 ms vs 0.3 ms), but this is still orders of magnitude faster than the EIG expert (>10 s per decision) which becomes intractable at scale. On the 32×32 Active Search grid, the GNN (7.15±0.12) is the only method that produces a viable policy, as the greedy EIG search exceeds 10,000 ms per decision and is marked N.A. In the continuous BO setting (2D/4D/8D), the GNN outperforms the Transformer by a growing margin as dimension increases, approaching expert LogEI simple regret with at least 10× fewer training trajectories.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the growing line of work applying deep learning to Bayesian Experiment Design (BED), including Foster et al.'s Deep Adaptive Design (2021), Volpp et al.'s meta-learning of acquisition functions (2019), and Igoe et al.'s multi-agent active search (2022). It departs from these approaches by identifying 'belief explosion'—the combinatorial growth of reachable posterior beliefs—as the central training bottleneck, and by proving a domain-permutation equivariance property of optimal BED policies (Theorem 1) that is complementary to Foster et al.'s observation-set permutation invariance. The key methodological contribution is repurposing Graph Neural Networks (specifically TransformerConv) as an inductive bias that naturally encodes this equivariance, rather than relying on the fully-connected, CNN, or Transformer-on-information-set architectures used in prior BED+DL work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The two-orders-of-magnitude sample-efficiency gain and the ability to train on small domains and zero-shot transfer to much larger ones (e.g., 8×8 → 32×32 active search, 2D → 5D BO) make amortized BED policies practical in settings where non-amortized acquisition functions are computationally intractable. However, all experiments are on small synthetic tasks with well-specified Gaussian Process priors and exact posterior moments; extending to misspecified models, hyperpriors over kernel parameters, and real-world BED tasks remains open. The paper also leaves open whether the equivariance structure can be discovered automatically for broader task families (e.g., SIR parameter identification) and how to handle the exponential growth of belief-state representations in high-dimensional continuous domains without discretization.
