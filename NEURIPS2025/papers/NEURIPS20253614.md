## Problem

Text-to-image models generate visually compelling designs but cannot guarantee physical soundness (e.g., a generated chair may not support weight), while structural optimization produces physically valid designs but offers no mechanism to embed complex visual or aesthetic goals such as 'resembles an arch' or 'shaped like an avocado.' No prior approach jointly optimizes a text-driven visual objective and a physics-based structural objective within a single design loop.

## Input / Output

The method takes a free-form text prompt (e.g., 'a large hexagon, dark black outline'), the locations of applied forces and fixed supports, a target density scalar, and the design-space dimensions as input. The design space is a single-channel density grid (values in [0,1]) that is repeated threefold to grayscale, resized to 224×224, and passed through a frozen CLIP image encoder; the output is a 2D binary material-presence map that can be extruded along the y-axis and 3D printed into a load-bearing beam. Design spaces range from 32×32 to 512×512 elements, and symmetric problems exploit half-domain computation with mirroring.

## Method

TIDES jointly optimizes three terms—structural compliance (via a differentiable finite-element SIMP solver), material cost (MAE against a target density), and a negative CLIP cosine-similarity loss—using AdamW over a directly parameterized density grid. Two key mechanisms make the joint optimization work: (1) a Hill-function-style sigmoid (α=0.8, n=20) applied to the raw parameters pushes densities toward the binary extremes 0 and 1, counteracting CLIP's tendency to produce intermediate grayscale shading; and (2) a compliance-based mask (threshold on log-compliance at −20) zeros out pixels that do not structurally contribute before the image is shown to CLIP, preventing the optimizer from creating floating, purely aesthetic features. The CLIP visual judge uses the augmentation scheme from CLIPDraw (random crops, perspective transforms, resizes) to sample different views of the design each step, which also introduces diversity across repeated runs with the same prompt. The entire pipeline is end-to-end differentiable: gradients flow from the CLIP image encoder and the FEM compliance back through the Hill function to the design parameters, requiring no retraining of either the CLIP model or the physics solver.

## Results

In the 3-point bending experiment with 3D printed beams, the physics-alone design achieved the lowest experimental compliance of 1.0×10⁻³ N/mm, while TIDES designs ('honeycomb, many hexagons' at 1.13×10⁻³ N/mm and 'large arch' at 1.19×10⁻³ N/mm) remained within the same order of magnitude, confirming physical soundness. In simulation, the physics-alone design returned a compliance of 280.0 c, with TIDES designs at 285.71 c ('large arch') and 299.55 c ('honeycomb'). Across 30 trials on the suspended bridge and hoop problems, TIDES designs approach the upper bound of physics-alone compliance distributions, and in the hoop problem, arch and triangle prompt designs overlap in performance with physics-alone results, suggesting the visual loss can act as a mechanism for escaping local minima.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares TIDES against two internal baselines: physics-alone (standard SIMP topology optimization) and vision-alone (CLIP score maximization without physics). TIDES designs achieve compliance values within the same order of magnitude as physics-alone across all tested problems (tower, bridge, hoop, beam), while vision-alone designs exhibit compliance orders of magnitude higher and contain floating material, confirming that TIDES successfully enforces physical constraints. Against the external baseline of Zhong et al. [33] (topology optimization with text-guided stylization), TIDES produces structurally meaningful features: in the Art Deco comparison, [33] generates off-white windows painted over solid material via RGB channels, whereas TIDES generates windows as actual voids (no material present) in a binary density field. The paper also notes that [33] uses Connected Component Labeling at every optimization step (poor scaling), while TIDES uses a compliance-based mask with no additional computational overhead. In the hoop design problem, TIDES designs for 'arch' and 'triangle' prompts overlap in performance with physics-alone results, indicating the visual constraint does not necessarily degrade physical performance and may help escape local minima.

## Relation to Prior Work

TIDES sits at the intersection of CLIP-guided image generation (building on CLIPDraw's augmentation pipeline [23] and the general text-to-image optimization paradigm) and differentiable topology optimization (building on the AutoGrad SIMP solver from Hoyer et al. [8] and the modified SIMP formulation from Sigmund [17]). It departs from the closest prior work, Zhong et al. [33], which applied text-guided stylization to topology optimization using RGB channels and connected-component labeling; TIDES instead operates on a shared binary density field, introduces a Hill-function sigmoid to enforce bimodal material distribution, and replaces CCL with a physics-based compliance mask that carries no additional computational cost. Unlike physics-constrained 3D generation approaches (Atlas3D [28], DSO [29]) that focus on gravity-induced stability, TIDES handles arbitrary force and support configurations through a fully differentiable finite element solver.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical implication is that a frozen pretrained CLIP model can serve as a 'visual judge' alongside a differentiable physics simulator without any new training on physics data, lowering the barrier to text-informed structural design. The finding that text-guided designs sometimes match or overlap with physics-only performance (hoop problem) suggests visual constraints can act as a mechanism for escaping local minima in topology optimization, not merely as a trade-off. Limitations are significant: the approach is restricted to 2D (layered 3D), design spaces are small (≤512×512), hyperparameters (β weights, Hill function parameters, compliance threshold) are set heuristically, and manufacturability constraints beyond density are not modeled. Extending to 3D meshes, non-differentiable physics (soft robotics), and higher-fidelity manufacturing constraints are the natural next steps.
