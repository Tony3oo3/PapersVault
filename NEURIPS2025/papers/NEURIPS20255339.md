## Problem

Binary classification in the high-dimensional, low-sample-size (HDLSS) regime with severe class imbalance remains largely unsolved: oversampling/undersampling distort boundaries or discard too much data, cost-sensitive SVMs suffer from data pilling, and ensemble methods like random forests or SMOTE collapse as dimensionality grows. No existing method simultaneously handles extreme feature counts (p >> n), tiny minority-class samples, and the need for feature selection without overfitting.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255339/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a training set of n high-dimensional feature vectors x_i ∈ R^p (p up to 10,000+) paired with binary labels y_i ∈ {0,1}, where the minority class may constitute as little as 5% of samples. Before fitting, each binary label is jittered by adding an independent Uniform(0,1) noise to produce a nearly-continuous response Z = Y + U, which serves as the quantile regression target. The output is a binary prediction for a new point x_new, determined by whether the fitted linear score α̂ + x_new^T β̂ exceeds the threshold 1.

## Method

QuanDA establishes that the population minimizer of the cost-sensitive Bayes risk for imbalanced classification is exactly a conditional quantile of the binary label at level τ = w₁/(w₀+w₁), linking quantile regression directly to the classification objective. To make this operational, the method jitters binary labels with uniform noise (Z = Y + U) so that standard sparse quantile regression can be applied, then fits a linear model Q_Z(τ|x) = α + x^T β with an elastic-net penalty (λ₁‖β‖₁ + λ₂‖β‖₂²) to perform simultaneous feature selection and shrinkage. The quantile level τ is tuned via five-fold cross-validation over a grid centered at the estimated minority-class proportion, and 10 independent jittering replicates are averaged to stabilize the coefficient estimates. A theoretical guarantee (Theorem 3.1) shows the lasso quantile estimator achieves the near-optimal rate √(s log p / n) under standard restricted eigenvalue and sparsity conditions, allowing p to grow exponentially in n.

## Results

On simulated HDLSS data (n=400, p=10,000), QuanDA achieves AUC scores of 0.849–0.947 across all covariance structures and imbalance levels (π₀ ∈ {0.85, 0.9, 0.95}), consistently outperforming all baselines. On benchmark gene-expression datasets, QuanDA attains AUC of 0.949 on breast (42×22,283), 0.993 on leuk (72×7,128), 0.909 on LSVT (126×309), 1.000 on ovarian (253×15,154), and 0.969 on prostate (102×6,033). The advantage over competing methods grows with increasing class imbalance, with gaps widening from ~1–2 AUC points at π₀=0.85 to ~10–15 points at π₀=0.95.

## Baselines & Comparisons

QuanDA outperforms all four baselines in 17 of 18 simulation settings (the sole exception being π₀=0.85/CS2 where dsda edges it by 0.002 AUC). Against dsda, the closest competitor, QuanDA leads by 0.5–3.5 AUC points, with the gap widening as imbalance increases (e.g., 0.770 vs. 0.738 at π₀=0.95/AR5). Weighted logistic regression (glmnet) trails QuanDA by 2–11 AUC points, with the largest deficits at π₀=0.95 (e.g., 0.770 vs. 0.660 on AR5). Random forest and SMOTE perform substantially worse, typically 10–20 AUC points below QuanDA, and in several high-imbalance settings they collapse to near-chance AUC (~0.65) or predict all samples as majority class (yielding F1 and G-mean of zero). On the five benchmark gene-expression datasets, QuanDA achieves the highest or tied-highest AUC in 4 of 5 cases (tied at 1.000 on ovarian), with the clearest margin on breast cancer (0.949 vs. 0.926 for dsda and 0.891 for RF).

## Relation to Prior Work

QuanDA draws a novel bridge between two established but previously disconnected lines of work: high-dimensional sparse quantile regression (Belloni & Chernozhukov, 2011) and the cost-sensitive Bayes risk framework for imbalanced classification (Lin et al., 2002). The key insight is that the population minimizer of the weighted Bayes risk coincides exactly with a conditional quantile of the binary label, mirroring the classical least-squares-to-LDA connection (Hastie et al., 2009; Mai et al., 2012) but in the imbalanced setting. The jittering step, which adds uniform noise to discrete labels to make quantile regression well-defined, is conceptually related to Papandreou and Yuille's (2011) perturb-and-MAP random fields but serves a fundamentally different purpose. The method is explicitly distinguished from distance-based 'quantile classifiers' (Hennig & Viroli, 2016), which share the 'quantile' terminology but operate on a different principle.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

QuanDA fills a practical gap in the HDLSS regime where existing imbalanced-classification tools (SMOTE, cost-sensitive SVMs, random forests) degrade or fail entirely, offering a simple, theoretically grounded classifier that leverages off-the-shelf sparse quantile regression solvers. The theoretical guarantee (Theorem 3.1) establishes near-optimal estimation rates under standard restricted eigenvalue conditions, giving practitioners confidence in ultra-high-dimensional settings. However, the method is currently limited to binary classification with linear decision boundaries; the multi-class extension (one-vs-one) is only preliminary, and extensions to kernel or deep learning frameworks remain open. For fields like genomics and clinical diagnostics where HDLSS imbalanced data are the norm, this provides a principled alternative to ad-hoc resampling or weighting heuristics.
