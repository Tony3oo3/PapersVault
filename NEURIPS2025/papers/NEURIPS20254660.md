## Problem

Dataset Aggregation (DAgger) for real-world contact-rich manipulation faces two unresolved challenges: collecting human correction data that remains within the base policy's state-action distribution (existing take-over or offline methods introduce force discontinuities, distribution shift, and missing force information), and updating the policy effectively with a small amount of correction data (retraining is costly, finetuning is unstable, and prior residual policies require large sample counts or simulation).

## Input / Output

The Compliant Residual Policy consumes the same wrist-camera RGB images and proprioception as the base policy, plus a 6-DoF force/torque signal from an ATI sensor mounted on the end effector. It outputs five frames (0.1 s at 50 Hz) of a 15-dimensional action vector: the first 9 dimensions encode an SE(3) delta pose relative to the base policy's predicted end-effector trajectory, and the last 6 dimensions encode the expected contact wrench (force + torque). These are fed into a 500 Hz admittance controller that blends the base policy's 1 Hz position commands with the residual's high-frequency corrections and force targets.

## Method

CR-DAgger combines a Compliant Intervention Interface with a Compliant Residual Policy. The interface uses a kinesthetic handle on the end effector with an admittance controller (~1000 N/m stiffness) so a human can apply gentle delta forces while the base policy keeps running, recording both the delta action and the measured wrench at the exact correction start/end timestamps. The residual policy is a lightweight (~2 MB) network that reuses the base policy's frozen image encoder, encodes the force signal with a temporal convolution network, and decodes actions via fully-connected layers; it is trained exclusively on 50–100 real-world correction episodes. Two training strategies ensure stability: no-correction segments are included and labeled as zero residual actions, and samples immediately after correction onset are up-weighted fourfold to emphasize reactive corrective behavior. This formulation lets a position-only base policy become force-aware without retraining, and the delta-correction paradigm avoids the abrupt control-authority switching that plagues take-over methods.

## Results

On the book flipping task (base policy: 40% success), CR-DAgger with On-Policy Delta data and Compliant Residual Policy achieves 100% success rate, a 60-point improvement. On the belt assembly task (base policy with force: 43.8% success), CR-DAgger achieves approximately 94% success, a 50-point improvement. The Compliant Residual Policy outperforms the best position-only residual baseline by 45 points on book flipping and 53 points on belt assembly, demonstrating the critical role of force modality. Retraining from scratch yields only a 1.67% drop (book) or 18.8% gain (belt), while finetuning degrades performance by 30% (book) and 15.6% (belt) relative to the base policy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against HG-DAgger (take-over correction, Kelly et al. 2019), CR-DAgger's On-Policy Delta collection yields a 45-point higher success rate on book flipping (100% vs. ~55%) when paired with the Compliant Residual Policy, because take-over corrections introduce abrupt force discontinuities and distributional shifts that cause the residual policy to produce noisy, overly large corrective motions. Against the KL-regularized finetuning approach (DPoK, Fan et al. 2023), CR-DAgger substantially outperforms: finetuning with KL regularization still underperforms the base policy on both tasks (approximately 15–35% success vs. 40%/43.8% base), while CR-DAgger reaches 100% and ~94% respectively. The paper also shows that retraining from scratch with aggregated data is stable but learns corrective behaviors very slowly (only 18.8% gain on belt assembly), and plain finetuning is the worst-performing update method, degrading below the base policy on both tasks due to distribution mismatch between correction and initial training data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the DAgger framework [36] and its robotics instantiations (HG-DAgger [23], LazyDAgger [19]) by replacing take-over or offline correction with on-policy delta corrections collected through a kinesthetic teaching interface with admittance control. For policy updates, it departs from prior residual policy approaches that rely on RL in simulation [2, 51] or large-scale real-world data [5, 22], instead training a lightweight residual (frozen image encoder + temporal convolution for force + FC head) on only 50–100 real-world correction episodes. The base policy is a standard Diffusion Policy [8], and the compliance control layer draws on industrial admittance control [40] but is novel in its use as a human-robot correction interface that records both delta actions and force feedback simultaneously.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides rare, well-controlled real-world ablations showing that (a) force modality is critical for contact-rich manipulation (45–53% success-rate gain over position-only baselines), (b) on-policy delta corrections substantially outperform take-over corrections for residual training, and (c) finetuning a base policy on small correction data is actually worse than doing nothing. These findings give practitioners concrete design guidance (large-batch DAgger, dense sampling after intervention start) that is often missing from the literature. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes beyond two tasks on a single UR5 arm, whether more expressive action heads (e.g., Flow Matching) are needed for multi-modal action distributions, and what theoretical conditions govern the base-vs-residual success-rate trade-off.
