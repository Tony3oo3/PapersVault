## Problem

Current flow matching and Schrödinger bridge methods rely on zero-drift reference processes, which enforce gradient field dynamics and produce straight or energy-minimizing trajectories. This fundamentally prevents them from capturing non-gradient, periodic behaviors—such as cell cycling, ocean vortices, and fluid vortices—that are ubiquitous in natural systems. The paper asks whether one can learn such non-gradient dynamics in a simulation-free manner while still respecting population-level marginal constraints.

## Input / Output

The method takes two empirical marginal distributions (population snapshots at t=0 and t=1) and an approximate reference velocity field f_t (e.g., RNA velocity from scRNA-seq, HYCOM ocean current vectors, or finite-difference velocities from Lagrangian particle positions). When a closed-form f_t is unavailable, it is approximated via a k-NN kernel that weights the initial velocity f_0(x_0) by distance to the current path point. The output is a learned drift v_{t,θ}, a score function s_{t,θ}, and the resulting particle trajectories, all trained without simulating trajectories during optimization.

## Method

CURLY-FM solves a Schrödinger bridge problem with a non-zero drift reference process via a two-stage simulation-free algorithm. In stage one, a neural path interpolant φ_{t,η} is trained by regressing the time derivative of the curved path μ_{t,η} = tx_1 + (1−t)x_0 + t(1−t)φ_{t,η}(x_0,x_1) against the reference drift f_t, which forces the interpolant to exhibit cyclic or non-gradient behavior. In stage two, the generative drift v_{t,θ} and score s_{t,θ} are learned via a combined flow-matching and score-matching objective, where the coupling between marginals is determined by a minibatch OT plan whose cost is the stochastic kinetic-energy mismatch of the neural path. The key distinction from prior work is that the non-zero reference drift elevates the problem beyond entropic OT, enabling the learned dynamics to carry curl and periodicity while remaining simulation-free and computationally efficient.

## Results

On the human fibroblast cell cycle task, CURLY-FM achieves a cosine distance to the reference RNA-velocity field of 0.295 (d=2), 0.300 (d=10), and 0.249 (d=20), substantially lower than CFM (~1.0), OT-CFM (~0.8–1.0), and TrajectoryNet (~1.0), confirming its ability to capture non-gradient cyclic dynamics. On ocean currents, CURLY-FM achieves EMD of 0.019–0.045 across held-out timepoints, outperforming OT-CFM (0.148–0.250) and the simulation-based SBIRR (0.072–0.120) while training in 0.06 hours versus 4.67 hours for SBIRR. On the CFD Taylor-Green vortex task, CURLY-FM attains the best cosine distance (0.189) and highest Precision@25 (0.628) versus OT-CFM (0.248, 0.496). On mouse erythroid development (d=2), CURLY-FM achieves cosine distance 0.009 and L2 cost 1.663, outperforming OT-CFM (0.146, 2.704) and MFM (0.014, 1.999).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CURLY-FM decisively outperforms all zero-drift baselines (CFM, OT-CFM, MFM) on the key metric of cosine distance to the reference velocity field across every dataset, with improvements of roughly 3–7× on the cell cycle task (e.g., 0.295 vs. 1.065 for CFM at d=2). On ocean currents, CURLY-FM achieves the best EMD (0.019–0.045) by a wide margin over OT-CFM (0.148–0.250) and even the simulation-based SBIRR (0.072–0.120), while being ~78× faster in wall-clock time (0.06 h vs. 4.67 h). On the CFD task, CURLY-FM improves cosine distance from 0.248 (OT-CFM) to 0.189 and Precision@25 from 0.496 to 0.628, indicating more faithful recovery of the true particle coupling. The trade-off is that CURLY-FM's W₂ to held-out marginals is slightly higher than OT-CFM in some settings (e.g., 1.199 vs. 0.248 on cell cycle d=2), reflecting its objective's emphasis on velocity-field alignment over strict marginal matching. Against TrajectoryNet (simulation-based), CURLY-FM achieves comparable W₂ but substantially lower cosine distance (0.295 vs. 1.077 at d=2) at a fraction of the compute cost (0.06 h vs. 7.44 h). On mouse erythroid development, CURLY-FM matches or beats MFM on cosine distance (0.009 vs. 0.014 at d=2) while MFM retains a lower W₂, suggesting MFM adheres more tightly to the data manifold at the cost of velocity fidelity.

## Relation to Prior Work

CURLY-FM extends the Schrödinger bridge framework from its standard zero-drift (Brownian) reference process to a non-zero drift reference, enabling the modeling of non-gradient field dynamics that are fundamentally inaccessible to prior flow matching methods (CFM, OT-CFM) and diffusion Schrödinger bridge approaches. The two-stage simulation-free algorithm—first regressing a neural path interpolant against the reference drift, then learning the generative process as a mixture of conditional bridges—builds directly on the simulation-free score-and-flow matching paradigm of Tong et al. (2024b) and minibatch OT couplings of Fatras et al. (2020), but departs from simulation-based methods like TrajectoryNet and SBIRR that were previously the only options for incorporating reference velocity information. The key conceptual departure is elevating the entropic optimal transport problem to a new class where the solution must simultaneously match a prescribed non-zero drift and the endpoint marginals.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work opens a path toward modeling periodic and cyclical dynamics (cell cycles, ocean vortices, fluid vortices) in trajectory inference that gradient-field methods provably cannot capture, while being ~78× faster than the prior simulation-based state-of-the-art (SBIRR). A key limitation is the dependence on approximate reference velocity information (e.g., RNA-velocity, finite-difference velocities), which is inherently noisy and difficult to infer over longer timescales, constraining the method's applicability. The W2 trade-off observed in cell cycle experiments—where CURLY-FM matches the reference field far better but at the cost of worse marginal reconstruction—highlights an open tension between fidelity to the reference dynamics and endpoint accuracy. Future directions include unbalanced transport for cell birth/death, multiomics integration, and lineage-tracing verification.
