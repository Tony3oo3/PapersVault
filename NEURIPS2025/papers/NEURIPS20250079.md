## Problem

Standard Polyak adaptive step size estimates the full Lipschitz smoothness constant, which scales as O(d) in high-dimensional M-estimation (d/n → ∞), forcing overly conservative steps that break rate invariance—the iteration count to reach optimal statistical precision grows with dimension even when conditioning and achievable precision remain constant. The paper asks whether an adaptive step size can be designed that preserves rate invariance in this regime without requiring prior knowledge of the restricted smoothness constant.

## Input / Output

The algorithm takes an objective function f (e.g., logistic or linear regression loss), a sparsity level s ≥ s*, a target function value f̂ set at or above the statistical accuracy level, and a number of iterations T, producing an s-sparse parameter estimate θ_T. Unusually, f̂ is a user-specified target reflecting desired statistical precision rather than the true optimum, and a double-loop variant (Algorithm 2) adaptively refines a lower bound on f* when f̂ is unknown, requiring only a valid lower bound (e.g., 0 for non-negative losses).

## Method

Sparse Polyak modifies the classical Polyak step size by replacing the full gradient norm ‖∇f(θ_t)‖² in the denominator with the hard-thresholded gradient norm ‖HT_s(∇f(θ_t))‖², thereby estimating the restricted Lipschitz smoothness (RSS) constant rather than the full Lipschitz constant. This is critical because the full gradient norm can be √(d/s) times larger than the thresholded norm, making the unmodified Polyak step size scale as O(1/d) and break rate invariance. The resulting algorithm is Iterative Hard Thresholding (IHT) with this adaptive step size, and the paper proves linear convergence at rate 1/(80κ̄) up to statistical precision O(‖HT_s(∇f(θ̂))‖²/μ̄²), matching the guarantees of IHT with the optimal fixed step size. Under a signal-to-noise ratio condition, the method additionally guarantees exact support recovery. The theoretical framework relies on Restricted Strong Convexity and Restricted Smoothness assumptions, specialized to logistic regression, linear regression, and low-rank matrix regression.

## Results

The main theoretical guarantee (Theorem 1) establishes linear convergence of Sparse Polyak at rate (1 − 1/(80κ̄)) per iteration, reaching a final precision of O(‖HT_s(∇f(θ̂))‖²/μ̄²), matching the rate of IHT with the optimal fixed step size up to a constant factor. The key empirical finding is rate invariance: as d/n → ∞ with s·log(d)/n held constant, the number of iterations to reach near-optimal statistical precision remains unchanged for Sparse Polyak, whereas standard Polyak's step size requires an increasing number of iterations. In synthetic experiments (d ∈ {5000, 10000, 20000}, s* = 300, α = 5), the adaptive step size also converges faster in wall-clock iterations than the fixed step size 2/(3L̄), with the speedup more pronounced for logistic regression where local curvature varies.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares Sparse Polyak against two baselines. Against the unmodified standard Polyak step size (Polyak, 1969), Sparse Polyak decisively wins on the rate invariance criterion: in Figure 1, as d increases from 5000 to 20000 (with s, s*, κ̄, and log(d)/n held constant), standard Polyak requires progressively more iterations to reach optimal statistical precision, while Sparse Polyak's iteration count remains essentially flat. The root cause is that standard Polyak estimates the full Lipschitz smoothness constant L, which scales as O(d) in high dimensions, leading to overly conservative step sizes. Against IHT with the fixed step size γ = 2/(3L̄) from Jain et al. (2014), Sparse Polyak achieves the same theoretical convergence rate and final precision but converges faster in practice (Figure 2, left and center panels), because the adaptive step size can be more aggressive when local curvature is favorable. The advantage is most visible in logistic regression, where the Hessian varies significantly across the domain.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Polyak's (1969) adaptive step size to the high-dimensional sparse M-estimation setting by replacing the full gradient norm with the hard-thresholded gradient norm in the step-size formula, thereby estimating the restricted Lipschitz smoothness (RSS) constant rather than the full Lipschitz constant. The convergence framework builds directly on Jain et al. (2014), who established linear convergence of IHT under RSC/RSS with a known RSS constant, and on Hazan and Kakade (2019) for the double-loop variant that handles unknown optimal function values. The key departure from prior work is the recognition that standard adaptive step-size rules (which estimate the full Lipschitz constant, scaling as O(d)) are fundamentally mismatched to high-dimensional problems where only the restricted smoothness remains dimension-independent, a gap the paper identifies as unaddressed in the literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper resolves a practical bottleneck in high-dimensional M-estimation: practitioners cannot know the RSS constant in advance, and the natural adaptive alternative (Polyak's step size) degrades as dimension grows, destroying the rate invariance property that makes IHT attractive. The Sparse Polyak rule is a minimal, elegant modification (thresholding the gradient in the denominator) that restores rate invariance with no additional assumptions beyond those already required for IHT convergence. Limitations include the sparsity requirement s ≥ (240κ̄)²s* (which is larger than the O(κ̄²s*) needed for fixed-step IHT), the need for a target function value or lower bound, and the fact that the analysis is primarily deterministic. Open questions include whether analogous modifications are needed for other adaptive step-size rules (Barzilai-Borwein, AdaBB) and whether the approach extends to the stochastic setting without the restrictive condition-number bounds imposed by Li et al. (2024).
