## Problem

Zeroth-order (ZO) fine-tuning methods like MeZO eliminate backpropagation memory costs but introduce gradient estimation noise that degrades optimization, causing slow convergence and sensitivity to learning rate. The paper identifies that this noise disproportionately corrupts updates to large-magnitude weights while small weights remain relatively resilient, motivating a selective optimization strategy that targets only noise-resistant parameters.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250712/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes pre-trained LLM weights (LLaMA-7b/30b, OPT-13b, Mistral-7B), labeled minibatches from SuperGLUE or other NLP benchmarks, and task-specific prompts. It outputs fine-tuned LLM weights updated via sparse ZO gradient steps. A key implementation detail is that the sparse mask is computed on-the-fly during the forward pass using per-layer percentile thresholds, so only the original parameters need to reside in GPU memory—no separate mask tensor or perturbed parameter copies are stored, keeping memory at inference level (14.6 GB for LLaMA-7b).

## Method

Sparse-MeZO (S-MeZO) modifies the standard MeZO ZO gradient by applying perturbation only to a dynamically selected subset of small-magnitude parameters: at each iteration, a per-layer threshold (set at a target sparsity percentile, e.g., 80%) determines which parameters receive the Gaussian noise z, yielding the sparse perturbation ẑ = m ⊙ z and the sparse ZO gradient g_ẑ(θ) = [L(θ+εẑ) − L(θ−εẑ)]/(2ε) · ẑ. Because only a fraction d̂ of parameters are perturbed, the method tolerates larger learning rates (up to 5×10⁻⁶ vs. 1×10⁻⁶ for vanilla MeZO) and converges in O(d̂L/σ²) steps per the provided convergence theorem. The memory-efficient implementation computes the mask m^(i) layer-by-layer inside the forward pass—y^(i) = (θ_t^(i) + ε·m(θ_t)·z^(i))·x^(i) + b^(i)—and immediately releases it, avoiding the need to store the full mask or perturbed parameter copies. This design enables fine-tuning LLaMA-30b on a single A100 GPU while achieving 9% absolute accuracy gains and 3.5× speedup over MeZO on RTE.

## Results

On SuperGLUE with LLaMA-7b, Sparse-MeZO (S-MeZO) achieves an average accuracy of 80.3%, improving over vanilla MeZO (76.6%) by 3.7 points. The largest gains appear on RTE (+9.0%, from 71.7% to 80.7%) and BoolQ (+5.0%, from 75.9% to 80.9%). S-MeZO also achieves a 3.5× convergence speedup on RTE (reaching 70% accuracy in ~5,000 steps vs. ~17,500 for MeZO) while maintaining inference-level memory (14.6 GB vs. 128.2 GB for full fine-tuning). On LLaMA-30b, S-MeZO further improves RTE to 82.1% and BoolQ to 85.7%, demonstrating scalability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

S-MeZO consistently outperforms vanilla MeZO across all six SuperGLUE tasks, with the most substantial gains on RTE (+9.0%) and BoolQ (+5.0%), while SST-2 and COPA see only marginal improvements (+0.4%). Against the random-mask variant R-MeZO, S-MeZO wins on every task, confirming that magnitude-based parameter selection is critical rather than arbitrary sparsification. Compared to first-order baselines, S-MeZO (80.3% avg) still trails LoRA (82.3%) and full fine-tuning (82.9%), but narrows the gap from 6.3 points (MeZO) to 2.0 points while using only 14.6 GB of GPU memory versus 22.4 GB for LoRA. Against other ZO methods on LLaMA2-7b (Table 2), S-MeZO (80.0% avg) surpasses ZO-SGD-Cons (75.7%), ZO-SGD-Adam (77.1%), ZO-AdaMU (77.8%), and AdaZeta (77.9%), with the largest margin over ZO-SGD-Sign (66.9%). S-MeZO also significantly outperforms zero-shot (60.1%) and in-context learning (66.5%) baselines, demonstrating that even gradient-free fine-tuning substantially enhances pre-trained model capabilities on downstream tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends MeZO (Malladi et al., 2023), a memory-efficient zeroth-order optimizer that uses SPSA-based gradient estimation with random-seed reconstruction to avoid storing gradients. The key conceptual departure is the empirical finding that ZO gradient noise disproportionately corrupts updates to large-magnitude weights, motivating a sparse mask that restricts perturbation and updates to small weights only. The convergence analysis draws on the sparse-perturbation ZO theory of Ohta et al. (2020), adapting it to the LLM fine-tuning setting. The dynamic-mask implementation (computing the mask during the forward pass rather than storing it) is a practical departure from MeZO's seed-based reconstruction, which cannot handle magnitude-dependent masks that change as parameters shift.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Sparse-MeZO enables fine-tuning LLaMA-30b on a single A100 GPU at inference-level memory (14.6 GB), making zeroth-order LLM fine-tuning accessible to practitioners without multi-GPU clusters. The 9% absolute accuracy gain and 3.5× convergence speedup over MeZO on RTE, replicated across LLaMA, LLaMA2, Mistral, and OPT, suggest the sparse-masking principle is broadly applicable to ZO fine-tuning. However, a meaningful performance gap versus first-order fine-tuning (e.g., 80.3 vs. 82.9 average on SuperGLUE) remains, and the paper does not explore whether the optimal sparsity level transfers across tasks or scales with model size. Open questions include whether the small-weight preference is specific to the SPSA estimator or holds for other ZO gradient approximations, and whether the approach composes with other ZO variants (ZO-AdaMU, AdaZeta).
