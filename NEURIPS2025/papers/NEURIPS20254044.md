## Problem

While individual LM attention patterns have been studied for attribution, no prior work has systematically examined whether context aggregation patterns are shared across different LMs. This gap prevents efficient cross-model knowledge transfer: adapters fine-tuned on one LM cannot be reused on another, forcing practitioners to retrain from scratch and inflating development costs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254044/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes raw text sequences processed by any transformer LM, from which L×L attention matrices are extracted per layer and decomposed into order-level OLA maps. For TOA, stacked 1st- and 2nd-order OLA maps (resized to 50×50, outlier-zeroed, row-normalized, and lower-triangular-masked for CLMs) form a multi-channel input tensor. Outputs are task-specific: 19-way relation labels, 37-class BIO entity tags, dependency heads with 55 relation types, or 45-class POS tags per token.

## Method

The core contribution is Order-Level Attention (OLA), which decomposes the Attention Rollout product ∏(A⁽ⁱ⁾+I) into terms grouped by the number of attention traversals (order), normalizing each group to yield a comparable L×L matrix regardless of layer count or head count. The paper demonstrates that same-order OLA across architecturally distinct LMs (Qwen, Gemma, Llama, BERT, RoBERTa, Electra) exhibits high visual and quantitative similarity (OLAS), and that OLA implicitly encodes syntactic dependency structure. Building on this, TOA trains an axial-transformer-based adapter (5 layers for CLMs, 3 for MLMs) on OLA from a source LM for a downstream task, then transfers it directly to an unseen target LM with zero parameter updates—the axial architecture is chosen because attention semantics are distributed across both rows (query aggregation) and columns (key aggregation), and diagonal features are extracted to form the per-token sequence fed to task-specific heads.

## Results

For cross-model OLA similarity (Table 1, CLM), first-order OLA achieves 92–95% classification accuracy across most source–target LM pairs, substantially outperforming Attention Rollout (27–66%), IRNL (18–77%), and ALTI (22–86%). For the TOA transfer task, transferring an adapter trained on LLaMA3.2-3B to Qwen2-1.5B elevates relation-extraction accuracy from 7.69% (zero-shot) to 34.90%, and dependency-parsing UAS from 6.17% to 65.44%. Across all four NLP tasks, TOA surpasses the zero-shot baseline in every source–target pair for MLMs, and in >94% of CLM pairs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

First-order OLA consistently outperforms all three attention-analysis baselines in cross-model similarity. Against Attention Rollout [10], 1st-order OLA gains 25–87 percentage points (e.g., 94.60% vs. 66.10% for L-3b→L-8b; 52.60% vs. 27.90% for Q-1b5→Q-7b), because Rollout aggregates all orders and is dominated by attention-sink artifacts. Against IRNL [36], 1st-order OLA leads by 34–83 points in most settings (e.g., 93.10% vs. 58.10% for G-2b target), as IRNL's norm-based token-level attribution captures model-specific feature-space relationships rather than shared aggregation patterns. Against ALTI [11], 1st-order OLA wins by 29–79 points (e.g., 94.60% vs. 85.60% for L-3b→L-8b), with ALTI being the closest competitor but still trailing by a meaningful margin. For the TOA transfer task, the relevant comparison is against the zero-shot capability of the target LM: TOA trained on LLaMA3.2-3B improves Qwen2-1.5B's RE accuracy from 7.69% to 34.90% (+27.2 pp) and its DP UAS from 6.17% to 65.44% (+59.3 pp), while for MLMs the gains are even more dramatic (e.g., BERT-base NER F1 from 0.00% to 68.47%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Attention Rollout (Abnar & Zuidema, ACL 2020) by performing an order-wise decomposition of the rollout matrix, yielding Order-Level Attention (OLA) as a unified, comparable representation across models with different layer counts and head configurations. It departs from the dominant line of attention interpretability work (IRNL, ALTI, norm-based attribution) that analyzes individual models or specific heads, instead asking whether different LMs converge to shared aggregation patterns. On the application side, it extends the adapter-transfer literature (CMC, zero-shot continuous prompt transfer) by introducing a training-free mechanism that leverages OLA as a model-agnostic syntactic feature space, avoiding the tight coupling between adapters and source-model feature distributions that plagues prior approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The OLAS phenomenon — that structurally diverse LMs (Llama, Qwen, Gemma, BERT, RoBERTa, Electra) produce highly similar same-order attention maps for the same text — provides a concrete, measurable commonality that could serve as a bridge for cross-model knowledge transfer, model comparison, or even as a diagnostic for training quality. The practical TOA application demonstrates that this commonality is exploitable: an adapter trained on one model's OLA transfers to unseen models without any fine-tuning, with particularly large gains for smaller models (e.g., Qwen2-1.5B RE accuracy jumping from 7.69% to 34.90%). Limitations include that TOA underperforms zero-shot for large high-capacity CLMs on some tasks, the similarity is relative and varies across model pairs, and all experiments are English-only. Open questions include whether OLAS persists in multilingual or code-specialized models, whether higher-order OLA components encode other linguistic properties beyond syntax, and whether the phenomenon can be exploited for model compression or distillation.
