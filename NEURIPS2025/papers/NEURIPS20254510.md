## Problem

Despite the widespread belief that the greedy (exploitation-only) algorithm performs poorly in bandits, the literature lacks a general characterization of when Greedy actually succeeds (sublinear regret) versus fails (linear regret) in structured bandit problems. Prior work provided only scattered, structure-specific examples of success or failure without a unifying principle. This paper asks: what property of the problem instance determines Greedy's asymptotic behavior across arbitrary finite reward structures?

## Input / Output

The algorithm receives a known reward structure (finite function class F), a context x_t each round drawn from a fixed distribution, and T_0 rounds of warm-up data (context-arm-reward tuples with Gaussian noise). At each round it outputs an action a_t by solving a least-squares regression over F (or an MLE in the DMSO setting) and selecting the best arm or policy under the fitted model. The characterization's verdict is binary: sublinear regret if the instance is self-identifiable, or linear regret if a decoy exists, with the function-gap Γ(f*, F) controlling the multiplicative constants in both directions.

## Method

The core contribution is identifying 'self-identifiability' as a necessary and sufficient condition for Greedy's asymptotic success: a suboptimal arm is self-identifiable if fixing its expected reward uniquely identifies it as suboptimal within the reward structure, equivalently the instance admits no 'decoy' (an alternative reward function whose optimal arm is suboptimal for the true model and on which both models coincide). For the positive result, self-identifiability makes the problem intrinsically easy—any algorithm satisfying a mild non-degeneracy condition achieves sublinear regret with a bound of (K/Γ(f*))²·O(log t). For the negative result, a decoy implies that with probability at least e^{-O(K/Γ²(f_dec))}, Greedy gets permanently stuck on the decoy arm, incurring linear regret. The framework extends from vanilla bandits to contextual bandits (via a non-trivial potential argument avoiding exponential dependence on the policy space) and to DMSO with arbitrary feedback (using MLE and KL-divergence-based model gaps), and a partial characterization for infinite function classes is provided via a tunable margin parameter ε.

## Results

The paper provides a complete if-and-only-if characterization of when Greedy achieves sublinear vs. linear regret in structured bandits. For StructuredMAB (Theorem 1), under self-identifiability Greedy attains E[R(t)] ≤ T₀ + (K/Γ(f*))² · O(log t), while without it, Greedy incurs linear regret with probability at least e^{-O(K/Γ²(f_dec))}. For StructuredCB (Theorem 2), the positive bound becomes E[R(t)] ≤ T₀ + (|X|K/Γ(f*))²/p₀ · O(log t), and the negative result holds with probability X^{-O(KX/Γ²(f_dec))}. The characterization extends to the full DMSO framework (Theorem 3) and to infinite function classes via a margin parameter ε (Theorem 4), yielding E[R(t)] ≤ T₀ + (K/ε)² · O(log t) under ε-self-identifiability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines. The paper positions its characterization against prior work on Greedy in structured bandits. For positive results, prior work (Kannan et al. 2018, Bastani et al. 2021, Raghavan et al. 2023, Kim and Oh 2024) achieves near-optimal regret rates for linear contextual bandits under specific context diversity/smoothness assumptions; the present paper's positive results are more general in terms of context diversity assumptions but weaker in regret constants, and crucially add the missing negative (failure) characterization. For negative results, prior work (Banihashem et al. 2023) characterizes Greedy failure only for unstructured K-armed bandits; the present paper extends this to arbitrary finite reward structures (linear, Lipschitz, polynomial bandits) and shows failure holds for almost all problem instances in these settings. The paper also contrasts its self-identifiability notion with the Graves-Lai coefficient (Appendix B), showing the two are incomparable: Greedy can succeed when GLC > 0 and fail when GLC = 0, demonstrating that GLC does not capture per-instance Greedy behavior.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the DMSO (Decision-Making with Structured Observations) framework of Foster et al. (2021) and the regression-oracle abstraction from contextual bandits literature (Foster et al., 2018; Foster and Rakhlin, 2020) to provide a unified, general theory of the greedy algorithm in structured bandits. Prior work yielded scattered positive results (e.g., linear contextual bandits with diverse contexts: Kannan et al. 2018, Bastani et al. 2021, Raghavan et al. 2023) and negative results (unstructured K-armed bandits: Banihashem et al. 2023; dynamic pricing: Harrison et al. 2012; dynamic control: Lai and Robbins 1982), but no general characterization existed for arbitrary reward structures. This paper departs from that fragmented landscape by introducing self-identifiability and decoy as the necessary and sufficient conditions, yielding a complete if-and-only-if characterization for all finite reward structures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This resolves a long-standing open question in the bandits literature: when does exploitation-only (greedy) behavior succeed or fail in structured settings? The conceptual insight that greedy's success is not due to algorithmic cleverness but rather to the problem being intrinsically easy (any reasonable algorithm would succeed) reframes how practitioners should think about exploration-free systems in user-facing applications. Key limitations include the finite-structure assumption for the sharp characterization, the realizability assumption, and possibly large constants in the regret bounds. Open questions include extending the characterization to infinite action sets, analyzing approximate greedy algorithms (relevant to human behavioral biases), and obtaining tighter instance-dependent regret guarantees for specific structures.
