## Problem

Standard model accuracy is measured in expectation over an input distribution, providing no guarantee for any particular input a user actually queries. This paper asks whether a learned model can formally prove the correctness of its output on a per-input basis, so that a user can verify correctness (or detect error) for the specific answer in hand rather than relying on average-case statistics.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254901/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The Self-Proving model P_θ takes a text sequence x and first autoregressively generates an answer y, then enters a multi-round interactive protocol with a manually-defined verifier V: over R rounds, V sends a query q_r and P_θ responds with an answer a_r, after which V outputs accept or reject. The model plays a dual role (generator and prover), and each response is conditioned on the full interaction history, making the process stateful. The verifier is a fixed, efficient algorithm with formally proven worst-case soundness, not a learned component.

## Method

The paper defines Verifiability as the probability that V accepts the model's output over x~μ, and proves it implies correctness via a union bound with V's soundness error. Two training algorithms are developed: Transcript Learning (TL) autoregressively fits the model to honest accepting transcripts, with a gradient lemma showing it optimizes a lower bound on Verifiability; and Reinforcement Learning from Verifier Feedback (RLVF), an on-policy policy-gradient method that emulates the full verifier interaction and updates parameters only on accepting transcripts, requiring no transcript generator. A two-phase paradigm is proposed: TL first establishes a base model with non-zero Verifiability, then RLVF amplifies it to full Self-Proving. Convergence of TL is proven under convexity and Lipschitzness assumptions, yielding O(C²B_Norm²B_Lip²/ε²) sample complexity where C bounds the prover's communication.

## Results

The paper's primary contributions are theoretical: Lemma 3.2 proves that Transcript Learning (TL) computes an unbiased estimator of the gradient of a lower-bounding function A(θ) ≤ ver_V(θ), and Lemma 3.3 proves that RLVF computes an unbiased estimator of the Verifiability gradient ∇_θ ver(θ). Theorem 4.1 establishes that under convexity, Lipschitzness, and realizability assumptions, TL requires O(C² B_Norm² B_Lip² / ε²) samples to output a (1−ε)-Self-Proving model, where C bounds the prover's communication complexity. Empirical validation on a simple arithmetic capability (Appendix F) confirms the theoretical predictions, with standard errors reported.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper that does not compare against competing methods in a standard benchmark sense. The two proposed learning algorithms (TL and RLVF) are complementary rather than competing: TL requires access to accepting transcripts and provides convergence guarantees, while RLVF operates on-policy without transcript access but requires a non-zero initial Verifiability. The paper positions RLVF as an adaptation of the Policy Gradient method [Sutton et al., 1999] to the verifier-feedback setting, and the two-phase TL→RLVF paradigm as an adaptation of Nair et al. [2018] (RL with demonstrations). The paper notes that subsequent work (RLVR, Lambert et al. 2024) implemented RLVF with KL regularization and achieved state-of-the-art performance on full-scale LLMs, providing empirical validation of the theoretical framework. No direct head-to-head numerical comparison between TL and RLVF is presented in the main paper.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Interactive Proof framework (Goldwasser et al. 1985) to formalize a new learning objective: training models that prove their own output correctness to a manually-defined verifier. The RLVF algorithm is explicitly derived from Policy Gradient (Sutton et al. 1999), and the two-phase TL-then-RLVF training paradigm adapts the demonstration-based exploration method of Nair et al. (2018). The key departure from prior Prover-Verifier Game work (Anil et al. 2021, Hammond & Adam-Day 2025) is the use of a manually-defined verifier with formal worst-case soundness guarantees, rather than a learned verifier, and the explicit separation of worst-case (verifier soundness) from average-case (model verifiability) guarantees.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This paper provides the first theoretical foundations for RLVF/RLVR, a technique that has since been widely adopted for LLM post-training across domains (medical reasoning, robotics, computer vision). The formal result that verifiability implies correctness (Remark 2.5) gives practitioners a clean per-input trust guarantee absent from standard average-case accuracy metrics. Open questions include convergence analysis for RLVF (left to future work), whether a single generalist model can prove correctness to multiple verifiers, and tightening the sample complexity bounds for TL beyond the convexity and Lipschitzness assumptions required by Theorem 4.1.
