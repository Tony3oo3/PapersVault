## Problem

In multimodal late fusion, dominant modalities overshadow weak ones, and conventional remedies (confidence weighting, knowledge distillation, adaptive ensembling) force weak modalities to align their target-class predictions with the dominant modality. This over-alignment suppresses modality-specific information and can even degrade weak-modality accuracy on samples where the weak modality was originally correct (an 'over-alignment collapse'). The paper asks whether a fundamentally different guidance signal—suppressing non-target classes rather than reinforcing the target class—can stabilize the decision space while preserving complementary information.

## Input / Output

Each modality is processed independently by its own pre-trained backbone (ResNet for images/depth/video, BERT for text, ResNet-18 for audio) producing a logit vector over C classes; these logits are combined via static averaging or a dynamic fusion module (PDF, QMF, etc.) into a single fused logit vector, from which a class label is read. For the multi-LLM extension, two fine-tuned Qwen models each emit a single answer-token logit that is fused at the decision level. MNL is a training-time-only auxiliary loss: at inference the pipeline is identical to the underlying fusion method with zero additional overhead.

## Method

The core mechanism is a negative-learning loss MNL = −ȳ · P^(RDM) · log(P^(IM)), where ȳ masks out the ground-truth class (0 at y, 1 elsewhere) so the inferior modality is guided only on non-target classes by the robust dominant modality's softmax probabilities, which are detached to prevent gradient flow back into the guide. The RDM/IM roles are assigned dynamically per sample using a joint criterion (Definition 3.3): a modality must have both higher target-class confidence and a larger Unimodal Confidence Margin (UCoM) to qualify as RDM, preventing a high-confidence-but-low-margin modality from misleading a correct one. Theoretically, the paper proves (Theorem 3.1) that the late-fusion robustness radius is lower-bounded by the weighted UCoM sum over the weighted Lipschitz constants, so enlarging the inferior modality's UCoM via MNL directly tightens this bound. Training is two-stage: Stage 1 optimises only cross-entropy on all branches; Stage 2 adds λ·MNL once unimodal performance stabilises, with the total loss being fusion CE + per-modality CE + λ·MNL.

## Results

On the four primary benchmarks (MVSA, UMPC Food-101, NYU Depth V2, CREMA-D) under Gaussian noise, LF+MNL achieves the largest gains over static late fusion, improving accuracy by +2.62 to +10.57 points depending on dataset and noise level (e.g., 73.71% vs. 68.04% on CREMA-D at ε=0, and 63.01% vs. 55.16% on MVSA at ε=10). When integrated with the dynamic fusion method PDF, MNL yields smaller but consistent improvements of +0.01 to +2.37 points (e.g., 80.54% vs. 79.94% on MVSA at ε=0). Under Salt noise (Table 2), similar trends hold with LF+MNL gaining up to +6.88 points over LF on MVSA at ε=10. On the three-modality CMU-MOSEI dataset, MNL improves LF by +9.29 points at ε=10 (55.09% vs. 45.80%), confirming scalability beyond two modalities.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against static late fusion (LF), MNL delivers the largest and most consistent gains, improving accuracy by 2.6–10.6 points across all four datasets, with the most dramatic improvement on CREMA-D (+5.67 at ε=0) and MVSA under high noise (+7.85 at ε=10). Against the dynamic fusion baselines PDF and QMF, MNL provides smaller but generally positive improvements (e.g., PDF+MNL reaches 80.54% vs. PDF's 79.94% on MVSA at ε=0), though one QMF+MNL cell on Food-101 at ε=5 shows a slight −0.62 drop, which the authors attribute to a misalignment between MNL's margin-enhancement objective and dynamic fusion's tendency to down-weight the very modality being improved. Against DynMM and TMC, LF+MNL outperforms both on most dataset-noise combinations (e.g., 73.71% vs. 63.27% for DynMM and 63.63% for TMC on CREMA-D at ε=0), though DynMM and TMC remain competitive on NYU Depth V2 where modality imbalance is minimal. In the latent-fusion comparison (Table 7 on MVSA), MNL (79.50/74.03/63.01 at ε=0/5/10) outperforms Deep CCA (76.49/63.58/53.94), LFM (77.26/66.47/57.80), and SUFA (78.18/71.40/59.11) at all noise levels, confirming that the late-fusion + MNL combination is more robust than feature-level alignment approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the multimodal robustness framework of Yang et al. (ICLR 2024), which introduced the Unimodal Confidence Margin (UCoM) and a robustness lower bound for multimodal systems, by reinterpreting the role of weak modalities in late fusion. It departs from the dominant 'positive learning' paradigm—where weak modalities are forced to align with dominant ones via knowledge distillation, confidence weighting, or adaptive ensembling—by proposing a 'negative learning' strategy that guides weak modalities to suppress non-target classes rather than improve target-class predictions. The dynamic guidance mechanism, which jointly considers both target-class confidence and UCoM to determine which modality leads, is a novel departure from prior work that typically assigns fixed or confidence-only modality roles.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a clean theoretical argument (tightening the robustness lower bound via UCoM) for why suppressing non-target class uncertainty in weak modalities improves multimodal robustness, and validates this across four standard benchmarks plus multi-LLM fusion settings. However, the practical gains are modest (typically 1–7%) and the method is notably less effective when combined with dynamic fusion strategies, revealing a fundamental tension between UCoM enhancement and weight assignment that remains unresolved. Open questions include whether the approach extends to multi-label, open-set, or temporal fusion settings, and how to reconcile the UCoM-weight misalignment in dynamic fusion without sacrificing the benefits of adaptive weighting.
