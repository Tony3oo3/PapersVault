## Problem

Prior theoretical analyses of GCNN embedding stability under edge perturbations are limited to worst-case formulations, which are overly pessimistic and ignore the statistical properties of the input graph signals. This paper asks: how do the second-order statistics of node features jointly with graph topology perturbations determine the expected embedding deviation of graph filters and multilayer GCNNs, and can this distribution-aware perspective yield more effective adversarial perturbation strategies?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250716/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a graph adjacency matrix, a collection of node feature vectors (graph signals) from which the empirical second-moment matrix K is computed, fixed pre-trained GCNN weights, and an integer edge perturbation budget m. It outputs a set of m edge additions/deletions (the adversarial perturbation) and quantifies the resulting embedding perturbation as a Frobenius norm. Unusually, the analysis is entirely task-agnostic at the embedding level: no downstream loss or label is required, and the key intermediate quantity is the K-modulated filter perturbation ⟨K, E_g^T E_g⟩, which re-weights structural changes by signal correlation.

## Method

The paper introduces a probabilistic stability framework where the expected embedding perturbation E[‖E_g X‖²_F] is the central metric. For graph filters, Theorem 1 gives the exact identity E[‖E_g X‖²] = ⟨K, E_g^T E_g⟩, showing that stability is governed by the filter perturbation measured in a K-induced norm. For L-layer GCNNs, Theorem 2 derives a distribution-aware upper bound that decomposes into model-complexity factors (activation Lipschitz constants, filter spectral norms, weight norms) and two perturbation terms: a norm-based term (L−1)‖E_g‖²·Tr(K) and the K-modulated term ⟨K, E_g^T E_g⟩. Proposition 1 further decomposes the K-modulated term into self-terms and coupling terms for adjacency and Laplacian filters, revealing that intersecting edge perturbations aligned with signal correlation are most impactful. Building on this, Prob-PGD relaxes the discrete edge-selection problem and applies projected gradient descent to maximize ⟨K, E_g^T E_g⟩, yielding adversarial perturbations that consistently produce larger embedding deviations than worst-case (Wst-PGD) or random baselines across graph filters, multilayer GCNs, and downstream classification tasks.

## Results

On MUTAG, Prob-PGD reduces GCN accuracy from 70.00% (unperturbed) to 42.89% and SGC accuracy to 41.58%, far exceeding the degradation caused by Random (73.16%/72.89%) or Wst-PGD (69.21%/68.16%). On ENZYMES, Prob-PGD drops GCN accuracy to 44.25% and SGC to 40.75%, again the largest drop among all methods. On Cora, Prob-PGD reduces GIN accuracy from 74.92% to 56.67%, a 18.25-point drop versus only 3.17 and 1.16 points for Random and Wst-PGD respectively. In terms of embedding perturbation magnitude, Prob-PGD consistently produces the largest values (e.g., 96.26 vs. 28.62 for Random and 37.47 for Wst-PGD on MUTAG GIN), confirming its effectiveness as a distribution-aware attack.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares Prob-PGD against two baselines: Random (uniform random edge perturbation) and Wst-PGD (worst-case projected gradient descent from Kenlay et al. [25]). On embedding perturbation magnitude, Prob-PGD dominates both baselines across all three datasets and all three architectures (GCN, GIN, SGC), with particularly large gaps on MUTAG GIN (96.26 vs. 37.47 for Wst-PGD and 28.62 for Random) and Cora GIN (43565 vs. 16901 for Wst-PGD). On downstream accuracy degradation, Prob-PGD achieves the largest drop in 8 out of 9 dataset-architecture combinations; the sole exception is MUTAG GIN, where Wst-PGD (76.32%) and Prob-PGD (76.58%) are statistically indistinguishable. The most dramatic downstream effect is on MUTAG SGC, where Prob-PGD reduces accuracy from 68.95% to 41.58% (a 27.4-point drop), compared to only 1.0 and 6.1 points for Wst-PGD and Random respectively. On Cora GIN, Prob-PGD causes an 18.25-point accuracy drop versus 1.16 for Wst-PGD, highlighting the advantage of incorporating the signal's second-moment matrix into the attack objective.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on embedding stability of GCNNs under deterministic edge perturbations (Kenlay et al. [25], Levie et al. [29,30], Nguyen et al. [35]) by replacing the worst-case formulation with a distribution-aware probabilistic one based on expected embedding perturbation. The adversarial attack algorithm Prob-PGD builds directly on the projected gradient descent framework of Xu et al. [54], repurposing it to optimize the K-modulated filter perturbation objective rather than a task-specific loss. The structural interpretation section also provides a rigorous theoretical justification for the DICE heuristic of Waniek et al. [50], connecting community-aware perturbation strategies to the second-moment matrix structure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The distribution-aware formulation reveals that embedding stability is jointly governed by the second-moment matrix of node signals and the filter perturbation structure, offering a principled explanation for why certain edge perturbations are more impactful than others. This opens the door to data-aware adversarial defenses and more targeted robustness training, though the paper leaves adversarial training and scaling to large graphs as future work. A key limitation is that the multilayer bound loosens with depth (the (L-1) factor), and the empirical evaluation is confined to relatively small graphs (≤2708 nodes), leaving open whether the distribution-aware advantage persists at scale.
