## Problem

Standard offline RL algorithms (IQL, SAC+BC, CRL) fail to scale on complex, long-horizon tasks even with up to 1B transitions—orders of magnitude more data than typical benchmarks—saturating far below optimal performance. The paper identifies the 'curse of horizon' as the root cause: biases in TD learning targets accumulate over long horizons, and the state-to-optimal-action mapping becomes intractably complex, and neither larger models nor hyperparameter tuning can overcome this barrier.

## Input / Output

The method operates on low-dimensional state vectors (not raw images) with oracle goal representations, isolating sequential decision-making from visual representation learning. At inference, the high-level policy takes (current state, goal) and outputs a subgoal state via rejection sampling over N candidates from a flow BC policy; the low-level policy then takes (current state, subgoal) and produces a continuous action by solving a flow-matching ODE (Euler method, 10 steps). The hierarchical decomposition means the agent never directly maps the full state-goal pair to an action, instead chaining two simpler mappings.

## Method

SHARSA is a minimal hierarchical offline RL method that reduces both the value horizon (via n-step SARSA) and the policy horizon (via hierarchical flow BC decomposition). It trains high-level and low-level flow-matching policies via behavioral cloning, then learns n-step SARSA value functions (Q^h and V^h) over length-n trajectory segments using binary cross-entropy losses. High-level policy extraction uses rejection sampling: N subgoals are drawn from the trained flow BC policy and the one maximizing Q^h is selected, avoiding the ill-defined first-order gradients that reparameterized approaches suffer in state space. The low-level policy is simply the behavioral cloning flow (or a second round of rejection sampling in 'double SHARSA'). The paper's broader empirical finding is that even the simplest horizon-reduction technique—replacing 1-step TD targets with n-step returns in SAC+BC—substantially improves scaling, and that combining value and policy horizon reduction (as in SHARSA) yields the best asymptotic performance across all four benchmark tasks.

## Results

On four challenging OGBench tasks (cube-octuple, puzzle-4x5, puzzle-4x6, humanoidmaze-giant) with up to 1B transitions, standard offline RL methods (IQL, CRL, SAC+BC, Flow BC) all fail to reach 100% success rate, with many completely failing on cube-octuple even at 1B data. SHARSA, which reduces both value and policy horizons via hierarchical flow BC and n-step SARSA, is the only method achieving non-trivial performance on all four tasks and exhibits the best asymptotic scaling. On reward-based variants (Table 1), SHARSA (κ=0.7) achieves 94±2% on puzzle-4x5 vs. 24±7% for IQL and 0% for SAC+BC, and 87±3% on humanoidmaze-giant vs. 44±35% for SAC+BC. The didactic combination-lock experiment confirms that 1-step TD learning suffers compounding Q-errors that grow with horizon length (H=256 to 4096), while 64-step DQN maintains significantly lower Q-errors.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All four standard flat offline RL methods (IQL, CRL, SAC+BC, Flow BC) completely fail on cube-octuple even with 1B data, and their performance saturates well below 100% on the other three tasks. CRL, which avoids TD learning via contrastive objectives, achieves notably better asymptotic performance on humanoidmaze-giant than TD-based methods, partially supporting the bias-accumulation hypothesis. n-step SAC+BC (value horizon reduction only) substantially improves over SAC+BC on multiple tasks, confirming that reducing the value horizon alone helps. Hierarchical Flow BC (policy horizon reduction only) enables non-zero performance on cube-octuple where flat Flow BC fails entirely, but does not match SHARSA's overall performance. SHARSA (reducing both value and policy horizons) dominates all baselines: it is the only method achieving non-trivial performance on all four tasks simultaneously, and on reward-based tasks it outperforms IQL by 70+ points on puzzle-4x5 (94% vs. 24%) and SAC+BC by 87 points on humanoidmaze-giant (87% vs. 44%). HIQL, which uses a flat value function with hierarchical policy extraction, improves over flat methods but still falls short of SHARSA's full value+policy horizon reduction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a scalability study for offline RL, deliberately contrasting with prior scaling work that focused on the 'width' axis (solving more diverse but not harder tasks with larger multitask models). It builds on SARSA for value learning, flow matching for expressive policy representation, and rejection sampling (from OPAL and related hierarchical methods) for high-level policy extraction, combining these into the minimal SHARSA method. The key departure from prior work is the identification of the effective horizon—not model capacity, architecture, or hyperparameters—as the fundamental bottleneck preventing standard offline RL algorithms (IQL, SAC+BC, CRL, FQL) from scaling on long-horizon tasks, supported by a didactic combination-lock experiment isolating TD bias accumulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that 1000× more data (1B transitions) is fundamentally insufficient for standard offline RL on long-horizon tasks, and that the bottleneck is horizon-induced bias accumulation in TD learning rather than model capacity, redirects the field's scaling narrative and suggests that simply throwing more compute at existing algorithms will not work. SHARSA demonstrates that a minimal combination of SARSA, flow BC, and rejection sampling outperforms complex methods, but the paper is honest that none of the current techniques achieve 100% performance even at 1B scale, and that two-level hierarchies only mitigate rather than solve the problem. Open questions include whether TD learning can be avoided entirely (e.g., via model-based RL or shortest-path algorithms), whether hierarchies can be extended beyond two levels, and whether the curse of horizon is fundamentally unsolvable in deep RL. The paper also calls for the field to evaluate new methods on substantially larger datasets and harder tasks rather than relying on 1M–5M transition benchmarks.
