## Problem

While documentation frameworks like Model Cards and Datasheets exist for AI models and datasets, no standardized framework captures the complete evaluation context that LLM benchmarks represent — including evaluation methodologies, targeted risks, and benchmark-specific assumptions. This absence makes it difficult for practitioners to compare benchmarks, select appropriate ones for their specific needs, and correctly interpret results, increasing the risk of benchmark misuse and misinterpretation.

## Input / Output

The framework takes benchmark publications (papers, repositories) as input and produces structured BenchmarkCards in both human-readable Markdown and machine-readable JSON formats. Each card follows a fixed six-section template (Benchmark Details, Purpose & Users, Data, Methodology, Targeted Risks, Ethical & Legal Considerations) with fields spanning dataset provenance, annotation processes, metric calculation formulas, and AI Risk Atlas risk mappings. The JSON output enables programmatic integration into compliance and auditing workflows, while the Markdown version serves as a human-facing reference.

## Method

BenchmarkCards define a standardized documentation template that captures essential benchmark metadata across objectives, methodology, data characteristics, targeted risks (mapped to the IBM AI Risk Atlas taxonomy), and ethical/legal considerations. Initial cards are auto-generated from benchmark publications using gpt-4o-mini with a structured extraction prompt that enforces factual accuracy and defaults to 'N/A' for unavailable information rather than making assumptions. The framework is validated through two complementary user studies: semi-structured interviews with 10 benchmark users to assess utility and information needs, and surveys with over 25 benchmark authors to verify correctness and completeness of the auto-generated cards. The cards are distributed via a public GitHub repository and HuggingFace dataset, designed as a community-driven ecosystem where creators share documentation and the community collectively refines both individual cards and the template itself.

## Results

The paper validates BenchmarkCards through two user studies: 10 semi-structured interviews with benchmark users (6 PhD students, 2 postdocs, 1 UX designer, 1 technical-sales engineer; mean 5 years ML/AI experience) and a survey of 25+ benchmark authors. A strong majority of users reported that the standardized structure would improve their benchmark selection and understanding processes, with sections like Methodology, Validation, and Targeted Risks frequently cited as most helpful. Of the 25 author survey responses, 10 confirmed all information was correct or required only minor clarifications, and nearly all authors endorsed the initiative as valuable for the ML community. Over half of user participants independently suggested adding concrete data examples and explicit comparisons between similar benchmarks as improvements.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against quantitative baselines, as BenchmarkCards is a documentation framework validated through qualitative user studies rather than a model with measurable performance metrics. The paper positions itself against existing AI documentation frameworks—Model Cards [34], Datasheets for Datasets [23], FactSheets [3], Data Cards [44], and the CLEAR framework [12]—arguing that none of these address the specific metadata needs of benchmarks (evaluation methodologies, targeted risks, interpretation guidance). Unlike Unitxt Cards [6], which target automated execution of evaluations, BenchmarkCards are designed for human comprehension of broader benchmark characteristics. The user study findings serve as the primary validation: users reported the structured format improves clarity over sifting through disparate papers, and authors confirmed the template accurately captures their benchmark's essential elements, with no existing framework offering this combination of benchmark-specific documentation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper draws conceptual inspiration from existing AI documentation frameworks—Model Cards for models, Datasheets for Datasets, FactSheets for services, and Data Cards—but identifies that none of these address benchmarks as a distinct documentation target. The authors explicitly position BenchmarkCards as the first framework to standardize documentation for the combination of dataset, metrics, and pre/post-processing steps that constitute a benchmark, filling a gap left by dataset-level and model-level documentation. The work also builds on the IBM AI Risk Atlas as a structured risk taxonomy and leverages gpt-4o-mini for automated card generation, while distinguishing itself from execution-oriented tools like Unitxt Cards and LM Eval Harness by focusing on human-facing interpretability rather than machine-readable evaluation specifications.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

BenchmarkCards addresses a practical pain point in the rapidly proliferating LLM benchmark ecosystem: without standardized metadata, users struggle to select appropriate benchmarks and interpret results correctly, risking misuse and false assurances of model safety. The framework's potential to map benchmarks against risk taxonomies (the authors identified significant coverage gaps across 130+ benchmarks) could guide the research community toward under-explored evaluation areas. However, the contribution's impact hinges on community adoption and sustained maintenance in a decentralized ecosystem, and the validation rests on a small user study (10 participants) and author survey (25 responses), leaving open questions about scalability, long-term upkeep, and whether the framework will evolve to capture emerging LLM risks.
