## Problem

Existing RL environments for orbital dynamics rely on custom-built, simplified physics models that require extensive validation and fail to capture real-world perturbations, limiting their ability to bridge the sim-to-real gap. No standardized framework simultaneously provides industry-grade orbital propagation, multi-agent RL with partial observability, realistic thrust modeling, and interactive visualization. OrbitZoo addresses this by combining a high-fidelity astrodynamics library (Orekit) with a multi-agent RL interface (PettingZoo) into a single, extensible Python environment validated against real satellite ephemerides.

## Input / Output

The environment is configured via a JSON file specifying bodies (position, velocity, mass, uncertainties), active forces, and mission parameters; at runtime, each agent receives a partial observation (e.g., equinoctial elements, relative distances, fuel state) and outputs a continuous polar thrust command (T, θ, φ) constrained by maximum thrust, deviation angle, and azimuthal angle. For validation, publicly available Starlink ephemeris data from Space-Track serves as ground truth, with Bayesian optimization tuning drag and reflection coefficients to minimize RMSE over 16.6-hour propagations. The system also produces a real-time 3D visualization and can export high-fidelity orbital datasets independent of any RL training loop.

## Method

OrbitZoo wraps Orekit's numerical propagators in a modular Python architecture with four core classes: Body (individual propagator with covariance-based uncertainty propagation via state transition matrices), Satellite (extends Body with polar thrust parameterization, fuel mass tracking via specific impulse, and agent-specific parameters), Interface (real-time 3D rendering via play3d), and Environment (PettingZoo-compatible reset/step/reward/render loop supporting both single-agent and multi-agent POMDPs). Propagation uses variable-step integrators (e.g., Dormand-Prince) with configurable forces including Holmes-Featherstone gravity harmonics, atmospheric drag from historical weather data, solar radiation pressure with lunar occlusion, and third-body effects from all solar system bodies. Parallelism is achieved at two levels: Orekit's PropagatorsParallelizer for simultaneous multi-body propagation and PettingZoo's ParallelEnv for concurrent episode collection, yielding approximately O(n) per-step scaling. Experiments train DQN, DDPG, PPO, and Federated PPO (FedAvg) agents on Hohmann transfers, collision avoidance with POC-based rewards, GEO constellation coordination, and chase-target missions, with validation achieving a 0.16% MAPE against Starlink ephemerides.

## Results

OrbitZoo achieves a Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) of 0.16% when validated against real Starlink ephemeris data over 16.6-hour propagation (1000 steps). The RMSE across 31 satellites ranges from 24.14 m (low-RMSE group) to 1924.90 m (high-RMSE group), with a medium-RMSE group at 83.75 m. In the collision avoidance maneuver experiment, PPO with continuous actions outperforms DQN with discrete actions under realistic (full-perturbation) evaluation dynamics, demonstrating superior probability-of-collision reduction. The Hohmann transfer experiment yields near-optimal semi-major axis values matching the analytical solution, with deviations only in inclination due to thrust misalignment under perturbations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper directly replicates two prior missions to demonstrate OrbitZoo's equivalence and added flexibility. Against Kolosa (2019) [40], who used Orekit with DDPG for a low-thrust orbit change maneuver, OrbitZoo reproduces the mission using the same DDPG algorithm while adding perturbative forces (drag, SRP, third-body) and continuous thrust modeling that the original simplified setup lacked. Against Herrera (2020) [31], who implemented PPO with a 4th-order Yoshida integrator for LEO station-keeping modeling only gravitational forces and atmospheric drag, OrbitZoo replicates the station-keeping task using Orekit's variable-step Dormand-Prince integrator with a fuller set of perturbations. In both comparisons, OrbitZoo matches or exceeds the original results while operating within a standardized PettingZoo multi-agent framework, adding capabilities (partial observability, multi-agent coordination, 3D visualization) that neither baseline environment supported. The key differentiator is that OrbitZoo is the only environment in Table 1 that simultaneously provides all seven capabilities, whereas Kolosa and Herrera each cover at most five.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on two well-established components — Orekit for high-fidelity orbital propagation and PettingZoo for multi-agent RL standardization — to create a unified environment that prior work has not combined. It departs from the dominant paradigm of custom-built RL environments with simplified dynamics (CR3BP, Newtonian-only, or drag-only models) by integrating industry-standard perturbative forces (harmonic gravity, SRP, third-body effects, drag) with a POMDP multi-agent structure. The paper also extends the RL-for-orbital-dynamics line of work (Kolosa, Herrera, Casas, Kazemi, etc.) by adding continuous thrust modeling, federated learning (FedAvg), and real-time 3D visualization, none of which were simultaneously available in prior environments as shown in Table 1.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

With LEO congestion accelerating due to mega-constellations and the Kessler syndrome risk, the field needs standardized, high-fidelity benchmarks to develop and validate autonomous satellite control policies before deployment. OrbitZoo's validation against 31 real Starlink satellites (MAPE 0.16%) provides credibility that prior custom environments lacked, though the high-RMSE group (up to ~1925 m) highlights that physical parameter uncertainty remains a barrier. Open questions include scaling to thousands of agents with heterogeneous dynamics, generalizing policies across orbital regimes, and bridging the remaining sim-to-real gap for operational deployment. The paper also notes that parallel propagation only outperforms sequential for complex force models, suggesting hardware-aware scheduling will matter for large constellations.
