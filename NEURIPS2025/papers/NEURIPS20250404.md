## Problem

When neural network representations are flexibly aligned to behavioral data via linear probing, predictive accuracy may no longer identify the true data-generating model. This paper asks whether the best-fitting model under flexible evaluation is actually the right one, and demonstrates through large-scale model recovery simulations that even with millions of behavioral trials, recovery accuracy plateaus below 80%, exposing a fundamental identifiability gap in current representational alignment practices.

## Input / Output

The method consumes final-layer activations (512–2560 dimensional) from 20 diverse pretrained ANNs applied to 1,854 THINGS object images, plus 4.5M human odd-one-out triplet judgments used for initial alignment and noise-ceiling calibration. It produces model recovery accuracy across 18 dataset sizes (50 to 4.2M synthetic triplets), 20×20 confusion matrices mapping data-generating models to recovered models, and per-model representational geometry shift magnitudes. Unusually, all candidate models are re-fitted from scratch to synthetic data sampled from a single generator's calibrated probabilistic predictions, emulating the constraint that neither the ground-truth model nor its alignment parameters are known in real experiments.

## Method

The core contribution is a noise-calibrated model recovery simulation framework for discrete behavioral tasks. Each of 20 ANNs is aligned to THINGS odd-one-out via a learnable p×p transformation matrix W (fitted with a novel scalar-matrix shrinkage regularizer that avoids the performance degradation of Frobenius-norm penalties), then its softmax temperature is tuned so simulated response variability matches the empirical human noise ceiling of 67.8%. In each of 600 simulations per dataset size, one aligned model generates synthetic triplet responses, all 20 models are independently re-fitted to the synthetic training data, and the generator is recovered if it achieves the highest cross-validated test accuracy. Regression analysis identifies two causes of misidentification: alignment-induced representational geometry shifts (quantified via whitened Pearson correlation of RDMs before and after alignment) and elevated post-alignment effective dimensionality (participation ratio of PCA eigenvalues). The paper further demonstrates a sharp predictive-accuracy-versus-identifiability trade-off: increasing evaluation flexibility from zero-shot through diagonal to full p×p W improves prediction but systematically erodes the ability to discriminate among competing models.

## Results

Model recovery accuracy improves with training-set size but plateaus below 80% even at 4.2 million synthetic triplets (chance = 5% for 20 models). With an expanded 30-model set, recovery drops further to ~70%, and coarser objective-type recovery reaches only 73.7% while architecture-type recovery reaches 70.3%. Regression identifies two significant predictors of misidentification: the candidate model's alignment-induced representational geometry shift (β = 0.495, p = 0.02) and the data-generating model's post-alignment effective dimensionality (β = −0.455, p = 0.01). Under the most flexible evaluation (full p×p W), several architecturally distinct models (e.g., OpenAI CLIP RN50 at 59.6%, ViT-L/16 at 59.3%) achieve near-noise-ceiling predictive accuracy (~67.8%) with no statistically significant differences among them.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new model but instead evaluates 20 diverse pretrained ANNs (spanning CNNs, ViTs, and image-text-aligned models) as competing models of human perceptual representation. The central comparison is across evaluation flexibility levels: zero-shot (W=I) yields the lowest accuracy (range 0.37–0.54), while full p×p linear probing lifts all models substantially (range 0.51–0.60), with the top performers (OpenAI CLIP RN50, ViT-L/16, DeiT3-H) converging to within ~0.3% of each other and none significantly outperforming the others (Bonferroni-corrected sign test). This convergence under flexible evaluation is precisely what undermines identifiability: model recovery accuracy plateaus below 80% even at 4.2M triplets, and OpenAI CLIP RN50 is systematically over-attributed as the data-generating model in the confusion matrices. Controlling for transformation dimensionality (fixing all models to 500×500 W via PCA) does not improve recovery, ruling out parameter-count differences as the cause. The paper further shows that even grouping models by training objective (supervised vs. self-supervised vs. image-text) or architecture type (CNN vs. ViT) yields only ~70–74% recovery, indicating that linear probing obscures not just individual model identity but broader representational motifs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the model recovery simulation framework (Pitt & Myung; Palminteri et al.; Wilson & Collins) to the specific and realistic setting of flexible evaluation of pretrained ANNs against noise-calibrated behavioral data. It builds directly on the linear probing methodology of Muttenthaler et al. [17] and the THINGS odd-one-out paradigm of Hebart et al. [38], adopting their analytic pipeline (with a modified scalar-matrix shrinkage regularizer from [47]) as the evaluation procedure under test. The key departure from prior model recovery studies (Kornblith et al. [24], Han et al. [18], Schütt et al. [36]) is the use of noise-calibrated discrete behavioral responses rather than deterministic, noiseless ANN activations, making the identifiability question empirically grounded in the signal-to-noise constraints of real experiments.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that model recovery accuracy plateaus below 80% even with 4.2 million training triplets challenges the widespread assumption that predictive accuracy under linear probing is a valid index of representational alignment, with direct implications for how the field interprets model-brain and model-behavior comparisons. The identified causes—alignment-induced geometry shifts and elevated post-alignment effective dimensionality—point toward concrete remedies (constrained readouts, adaptive stimulus selection, richer architectural priors) but leave open the critical question of how severe this trade-off is for neural data, where responses are multivariate and continuous. The paper's scope is limited to one behavioral task and a closed model set, and the authors explicitly note that the true biological model is absent from any candidate set, making closed-set recovery a necessary but insufficient criterion.
