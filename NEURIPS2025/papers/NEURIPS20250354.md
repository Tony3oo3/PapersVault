## Problem

Prior comparisons between masked diffusion and autoregressive language models showed diffusion requiring up to 16× more compute, but these comparisons were conducted exclusively in single-epoch settings where each token is seen once. This conflates compute efficiency with sample efficiency, leaving it unclear whether diffusion's apparent disadvantage stems from needing more compute or simply more unique data. The paper asks: in data-constrained regimes where repeated passes over limited data are the norm, does diffusion's advantage emerge?

## Input / Output

Both model families consume 2048-token sequences from the C4 corpus (GPT-2 BPE vocabulary) and produce token-level predictions. The AR model uses a causal attention mask to predict each token from its left-to-right prefix, while the diffusion model samples a random masking ratio r~U(0,1), replaces tokens with [MASK] at that rate, and predicts masked tokens using full bidirectional attention. Beyond per-token predictions, the paper's primary outputs are fitted scaling-law parameters (R_D*, R_N*, A, B, α, β, E_0), a closed-form critical compute threshold C_crit(U), and downstream task accuracies across nine benchmarks.

## Method

The paper trains 200 models (100 AR, 100 masked diffusion) spanning 7M–2.5B parameters, 25M–100M unique tokens, and up to 800 epochs, using an identical GPT-2-style Transformer backbone with RoPE, SwiGLU, and RMSNorm. It extends the Muennighoff et al. data-constrained scaling law framework to masked diffusion, fitting a modified Chinchilla loss L(N,D) = A/(N')^α + B/(D')^β + E_0 where D' captures diminishing returns from repeated data via an exponential-decay term governed by R_D*. The key finding is that diffusion's R_D* ≈ 500 versus AR's R_D* ≈ 32, meaning diffusion extracts useful signal from repeated data over ~15× more epochs. From the fitted laws, the authors derive a closed-form critical compute threshold C_crit(U) ∝ U^2.174 that predicts when diffusion overtakes AR for any dataset size. A controlled ablation—training AR models with N random token-ordering permutations—confirms that diffusion's advantage arises from its implicit exposure to diverse conditional prediction tasks, functioning as a form of data augmentation absent in AR's fixed left-to-right factorization.

## Results

In data-constrained settings (100M unique tokens), masked diffusion models achieve a final validation loss of 3.55 after 500 epochs, outperforming the best AR model's 3.71 after 50 epochs — a 67% loss reduction vs. 48% for AR. The fitted data-reuse half-life R_D* is ~500 epochs for diffusion versus ~31 for AR, meaning diffusion can exploit repeated data over an order of magnitude more epochs before saturating. On downstream benchmarks (100M tokens), diffusion outperforms AR on 8 of 9 tasks, with the largest gains on SciQ (68.67% vs. 58.05%) and Lambada (15.19% vs. 10.91%). The critical compute threshold beyond which diffusion surpasses AR follows a power law: C_crit(U) = 2.12 × 10^1.956 · U^2.174.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between masked diffusion models and autoregressive (AR) models under identical architecture, data, and hyperparameters. At the Chinchilla compute-optimal point (single epoch), AR clearly wins with validation loss 7.07 vs. 10.65 for diffusion, consistent with prior reports of up to 16× compute overhead for diffusion. However, once training extends beyond ~50 epochs on repeated data, diffusion reverses the gap, reaching 3.55 vs. AR's 3.71, and AR begins to overfit while diffusion shows no signs of degradation. On downstream tasks, diffusion beats AR on 8 of 9 benchmarks (e.g., SciQ +10.6 pts, Lambada +4.3 pts, RACE +3.7 pts), with PiQA being the sole exception where flop-matched AR edges out diffusion by 0.9 pts. The paper further shows that adding 16 random token-ordering permutations to AR training narrows the gap substantially, supporting the hypothesis that diffusion's randomized masking acts as implicit data augmentation over diverse prediction orders.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the data-constrained scaling law framework of Muennighoff et al. (NeurIPS 2023), which characterized diminishing returns from repeated data for autoregressive models, to masked diffusion language models. It departs from prior diffusion-vs-AR comparisons (Nie et al., Swerdlow et al.) that were conducted exclusively in the single-epoch regime, where diffusion models appeared to require up to 16× more compute; here, the authors explicitly decouple compute scaling from data reuse to isolate sample efficiency. The architectural choices (RoPE, SwiGLU, RMSNorm) follow modern LLM practice, and the training protocol adopts Muennighoff et al.'s hyperparameters to ensure a controlled comparison.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As high-quality training data approaches exhaustion, the finding that diffusion models extract value from repeated data for ~100 epochs (vs. ~4 for AR) and show no overfitting even at 500+ epochs provides a concrete, actionable decision rule for practitioners facing data scarcity. The closed-form critical compute threshold (C_crit ∝ U^2.174) gives a predictive tool for choosing between paradigms. Open questions include whether these scaling laws hold at much larger data and model scales (current fits span only 25M–100M unique tokens and up to 2.5B parameters), how hybrid models interpolating between AR and diffusion might capture the best of both regimes, and whether the implicit-augmentation mechanism generalizes to non-linguistic sequence domains like robotics or healthcare.
