## Problem

Estimating the mean of the best arm in a multi-armed bandit (best-mean estimation) is fundamentally harder than best-arm identification because the answer is a real-valued quantity drawn from infinitely many possibilities rather than one of K discrete arms. This continuous answer space makes the characterizing optimization problem non-convex, which prevents direct application of standard pure-exploration techniques like Track-and-Stop that rely on continuity of the optimal solution. The paper addresses this gap by establishing a sharp instance-dependent lower bound and designing an algorithm that matches it asymptotically.

## Input / Output

The algorithm adaptively samples from K R-sub-Gaussian reward distributions (with known sub-Gaussian parameter R and known mean bound S) given target precision ε and confidence level δ, and stops after a random number of rounds τ. It outputs the midpoint U(τ) − ε of a confidence interval [U(τ) − 2ε, U(τ)] of length 2ε that contains the true best mean μ* with probability at least 1−δ. The regularization parameter λ is set to (R/S)², making the algorithm essentially free of tunable hyperparameters.

## Method

EllipsoidEst constructs a joint confidence ellipsoid over all K arm means using a martingale-based anytime bound (Abbasi-Yadkori et al.), and employs a novel stopping condition that checks whether the region {μ : max_i μ_i ≤ U(t) − 2ε} lies outside this ellipsoid—enabling global hypothesis testing that per-arm confidence intervals cannot provide. Sampling follows a two-phase strategy: Phase 1 pulls the arm with the highest UCB to obtain rough estimates, and Phase 2 (triggered when the estimated means are sufficiently concentrated) additionally allocates extra samples to the tentatively best arm to match the optimal allocation r_{i*}β(t,δ). The key analytical insight is that the (K+1)-dimensional non-convex characterizing optimization (P1) decomposes into a convex subproblem (P2) for fixed U, with the remaining 1D non-convex minimization confined to a narrow interval [U̲(μ), U̲(μ) ∨ Ū(μ)] solvable by grid search. This yields asymptotic optimality: E[τ]/log(1/δ) → 2R²f(μ) as δ→0, where f(μ) is the optimal value of (P1).

## Results

EllipsoidEst consistently outperforms both SE and UGapEc in sample complexity for small and moderate numbers of arms (K up to ~16), with the slowest growth rate as δ→0, confirming its asymptotic optimality (E[τ]/log(1/δ) → 2R²f(μ)). However, EllipsoidEst's sample complexity increases more rapidly with K than the baselines, which is a practical limitation. The output μ̂* = U(τ)−ε exhibits an upward bias relative to the true best mean μ*, particularly pronounced for large K, while the maximum empirical mean max_i μ̂_i(τ) is notably less biased. The regularization parameter λ=(R/S)² is shown to be nearly optimal, making the algorithm essentially free of hyperparameters.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares EllipsoidEst against two baselines adapted for best-mean estimation: Successive Elimination (SE) [Even-Dar et al. 2006; Hassidim et al. 2020] and UGapEc [Gabillon et al. 2012]. EllipsoidEst outperforms both baselines in sample complexity for small and moderate K (up to ~16 arms) and exhibits the slowest growth as δ→0, consistent with its proven asymptotic optimality. SE, while having suboptimal asymptotic sample complexity, performs reasonably well for moderate δ values especially when K is large, but shows a faster increase in sample complexity as δ→0. UGapEc achieves an asymptotically optimal order for best-arm identification and demonstrates strong empirical performance at practical confidence levels, but performs poorly when K is small or when the true means are tightly clustered. EllipsoidEst's main disadvantage is that its sample complexity grows more rapidly with K than either baseline, motivating future work on algorithms that scale more gracefully with the number of arms.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the martingale-based anytime confidence bounds from linear bandits [Abbasi-Yadkori et al., 2011] to the MAB setting, using them to construct a joint confidence ellipsoid over all arm means rather than per-arm intervals. The lower bound proof builds on the multiple-correct-answer framework of Degenne and Koolen [2019], extended here to handle infinitely many candidate estimates of the best mean. A key departure is from Track-and-Stop [Garivier & Kaufmann, 2016], whose standard machinery for pure exploration fails here because the characterizing optimization is non-convex, preventing continuity of the optimal solution. The paper also extends the worst-case O(K/ε²) log(1/δ) bounds of Osogami et al. [2024] to sharp instance-dependent bounds with matching constants.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first PAC algorithm for best-mean estimation with asymptotically optimal instance-dependent sample complexity, closing a gap that standard pure-exploration tools (Track-and-Stop) cannot address due to non-convexity. The practical motivation—efficiently evaluating AI agents and foundation models where each evaluation is costly—gives the result immediate relevance to the growing inference-time reasoning literature. However, the algorithm requires a known bound S on mean magnitudes, and experiments reveal that sample complexity grows more rapidly with K than baselines, leaving open the question of whether the K-dependence can be improved. The bias in the midpoint output (winner's curse) is acknowledged but not corrected, suggesting room for practical refinements.
