## Problem

Current LVLMs approach spatial reasoning in a text-centric manner, translating visual information into language and reasoning purely in text space, which inevitably loses spatial details and makes dynamic position tracking prohibitively complex. Existing tool-integrated reasoning methods are further constrained by black-box perception tools that fragment reasoning into disconnected invocations and prevent the model from developing self-reflective correction of its own visual operations. The paper asks whether LVLMs can instead reason directly in visual space through elementary drawing operations, mirroring how humans use mental visualization and manipulation for spatial tasks.

## Input / Output

The model receives a visual input (a single image or a video sequence uniformly sampled to 16 frames at 256×28×28 resolution during training, 448×28×28 at evaluation) together with a spatial reasoning question, and produces a T-step interleaved reasoning path where each step contains natural-language reasoning, one or more drawing operations (bounding-box or auxiliary-line annotations targeting a specific image by index), and the resulting annotated images fed back as observations. The final output is a multiple-choice or numerical answer extracted from the last reasoning step; the intermediate annotated images form an interpretable visual trace of the model's spatial analysis. Drawing operations can target any previously generated annotated image, enabling cross-frame and cross-step spatial tracking in video and multi-view settings.

## Method

ViLaSR introduces a 'drawing to reason in space' paradigm in which the LVLM (Qwen2.5-VL-7B) generates a chain of interleaved reasoning and drawing steps, using two primitive operations—bounding-box annotation and auxiliary-line drawing—each parameterized by a target image index, spatial coordinates, and a semantic label. Training proceeds in three stages: (1) cold-start supervised learning on 33k synthetic reasoning paths generated by Qwen2.5-VL-72B and filtered for correctness and format validity; (2) reflective rejection sampling that fine-tunes only on self-sampled paths that are both correct and exhibit self-correction (re-annotating the same semantic label with revised coordinates across steps); and (3) reinforcement learning via GRPO (without KL penalty) using a rule-based reward that combines answer correctness (exact match for MCQ, Mean Relative Accuracy for numerical) with format validity, plus early-termination penalties for circular or degenerate reasoning. The key design insight is that the minimal two-operation drawing set avoids dependence on specialized perception tools while the staged curriculum progressively builds basic drawing ability, self-reflective correction, and reward-optimized reasoning efficiency.

## Results

ViLaSR achieves 98.2 on MAZE (maze navigation), 63.9 on SpatialEval-Real, 45.4 on VSI-Bench (video), 37.6 on SPAR-Bench, and 30.2 on MMSI-Bench (multi-view), yielding an average improvement of 18.4% over the Qwen2.5-VL-7B backbone without reasoning. The most dramatic gain is on MAZE (+64.5 over the backbone), where iterative drawing operations enable precise step-by-step path tracking. On VSI-Bench, ViLaSR matches Gemini-1.5-Pro and Gemini-2.0-Flash (45.4) while surpassing all open-source models by at least 4.5 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On MAZE, ViLaSR (98.2) achieves a near-perfect score, far exceeding the next-best proprietary model OpenAI o4-mini (79.0) and all open-source models (best: Qwen2.5-VL-72B at 50.5), demonstrating the critical advantage of iterative visual drawing for sequential path tracking. On VSI-Bench (video), ViLaSR (45.4) ties with Gemini-1.5-Pro and Gemini-2.0-Flash while outperforming the strongest open-source baseline LLaVA-NeXT-Video-72B (40.9) by 4.5 points and SpaceR-7B (43.5) by 1.9 points. On SPAR-Bench (multi-view), ViLaSR (37.6) edges out Qwen2.5-VL-72B (37.4) and SpaceR-7B (37.1), though it trails OpenAI o4-mini (46.2). On SpatialEval-Real, ViLaSR (63.9) underperforms several larger open-source models (LLaVA-NeXT-Video-72B: 72.5; Kimi-VL: 68.9), suggesting the drawing paradigm offers less advantage for static spatial relation queries. On MMSI-Bench, ViLaSR (30.2) is competitive with Qwen2.5-VL-72B (30.7) but falls short of OpenAI o3 (41.0). Overall, ViLaSR dominates on tasks requiring sequential planning and temporal tracking, while proprietary reasoning models retain an edge on multi-view perspective transformation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant tool-integrated reasoning paradigm (CoGCOM, VisCoT, VisProg, ViperGPT) that relies on black-box perception tools like grounding and OCR, instead proposing that elementary drawing operations (bounding boxes, auxiliary lines) serve as intrinsic reasoning primitives within the model. Conceptually, it extends the ReAct action-observation loop to the visual domain and adopts GRPO from DeepSeek-R1 for the RL stage, but the key departure is making visual manipulation a learned capability rather than an external tool invocation. The three-stage training recipe (cold-start SFT → reflective rejection sampling → RL) is a novel progressive curriculum that specifically targets the self-correction gap identified in prior tool-based approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Spatial reasoning remains a fundamental weakness of LVLMs, and this paper demonstrates that equipping models with direct visual manipulation capabilities—rather than relying on external perception tools—can substantially close the gap, particularly for sequential planning and temporal tracking tasks. The finding that prompting alone (without training) actually degrades performance below the backbone is a useful cautionary result for the community. Key limitations include restriction to 2D drawing operations, exclusion of free-form answers, and evaluation on a 7B model; extending to 3D spatial reasoning and more complex visual operations remains an open direction that could broaden applicability to robotics and AR.
