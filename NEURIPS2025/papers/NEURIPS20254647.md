## Problem

Evaluating the step-by-step correctness of LLM Chain-of-Thought reasoning remains difficult because high-quality step-level supervision is expensive to annotate, becomes outdated as LLMs update rapidly, and existing process reward models reduce feedback to scalar scores rather than substantive critiques. This paper eliminates the need for manual step-level annotation by evolving a critic through adversarial self-play.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254647/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The critic receives a math problem, the partial reasoning trajectory preceding the current step, and the step to evaluate, then produces a standardized natural-language critique containing an analysis of the partial solution and a definite correct/incorrect judgment. The sneaky generator receives the same problem and partial trajectory plus a correct step, and outputs a chain-of-thought transformation (selecting from five predefined error types) followed by the subtly incorrect step. During test-time search, the critic is invoked after each single step is generated (controlled via newline delimiters), and if it flags an error the LLM regenerates that step up to five times before skipping.

## Method

SPC fine-tunes two copies of Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct into opposing roles: a sneaky generator (SFT-initialized on GPT-4-generated error transformations from PRM800K) and a critic (SFT-initialized on R1-generated critiques refined by GPT-4o into standardized format). They engage in an iterative adversarial game where the generator converts correct steps into incorrect ones and the critic attempts to detect them; a sneaky step is validated as truly incorrect only if it drops the solver's success rate from ≥75% to 0%. Rewards are +1/−1 based on game outcomes, and both models are updated via GRPO-style offline RL with KL regularization. A key design choice is an asymmetric evolution strategy: round-2 data is generated by pitting Sneaky-1 against Critic-0 (rather than Sneaky-1 vs Critic-1) to maintain a balanced win rate (~32.5% vs ~13.2%), preventing the critic from stalling on an unbalanced opponent. After two rounds of self-play, the critic's accuracy on ProcessBench improves from 70.8% to 77.7%, surpassing the same-sized distilled R1 model and all PRM baselines.

## Results

On ProcessBench, SPC progressively improves from 70.8% (Round 0) to 77.7% (Round 2) average accuracy across GSM8K, MATH, Olympiad-Bench, and Omni-MATH, surpassing the strongest baseline DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B (75.2%). On PRM800K, SPC Round 2 reaches 75.8% average accuracy with a 75.8% harmonic mean, outperforming Qwen2.5-Math-7B-PRM800K (73.6%) and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B (71.4%). On DeltaBench (long-CoT reasoning), SPC achieves 60.5% average accuracy, substantially outperforming PRM baselines that drop to 14.3% and 41.3% HarMean. For test-time search, SPC + Self-Consistency boosts Qwen2.5-32B to 85.2% on MATH500 and 23.3% on AIME2024, and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B to 94.0% on MATH500.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SPC (Round 2) outperforms all seven baseline categories across every benchmark. Against PRM baselines, SPC beats Qwen2.5-Math-7B-PRM800K by 8.0 points on ProcessBench (77.7% vs. 69.7%) and by 2.2 points on PRM800K (75.8% vs. 73.6%), while the gap widens dramatically on DeltaBench where the PRM's HarMean collapses to 41.3% versus SPC's 59.5%. Against prompting baselines, SPC surpasses GPT-4o (70.1%) by 7.6 points and Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct (69.3%) by 8.4 points on ProcessBench. The closest competitor is DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B (75.2% on ProcessBench, 71.4% on PRM800K), which SPC exceeds by 2.5 and 4.4 points respectively; notably, SPC is trained on short-CoT data yet generalizes to the long-CoT regime where R1-Distill leads. For test-time search on AIME2024, SPC + Self-Consistency achieves 23.3% with Qwen2.5-32B, outperforming Self-Consistency + Qwen2.5-Math-7B-PRM800K (16.7%) by 6.6 points, and matches the best PRM-guided result (73.3%) for DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B while exceeding it on MATH500 (94.0% vs. 91.8%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the self-play paradigm (AlphaGo/AlphaZero, and recent LLM self-play for alignment) into the domain of step-level reasoning verification, where prior work has relied on human-annotated step correctness (PRM800K) or LLM-as-a-Judge to train process reward models. It departs from existing PRMs (Math-Shepherd, Qwen2.5-Math-7B-PRM800K) in two key ways: (1) it replaces scalar step scores with natural-language critiques, and (2) it eliminates the need for human step-level annotation by generating training data through an adversarial game between a 'sneaky generator' and a 'critic.' The RL training objective directly adopts the GRPO/RLOO formulation from DeepSeekMath, using advantage estimation with baseline subtraction and KL regularization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The adversarial self-play framework offers a practical path to training step-level critics without the expensive and rapidly-outdated human annotation that has bottlenecked PRM development, and the step-by-step verification approach (abandoning incorrect steps immediately rather than scoring full solutions post-hoc) is a more natural and effective use of a critic during inference. However, the current evaluation is limited to mathematical reasoning with 7B-scale models and only two rounds of self-play, leaving open whether the approach generalizes to other domains, scales to larger models, or continues to improve with additional iterations. The modest but consistent gains (e.g., 70.8%→77.7% on ProcessBench) suggest the framework is sound but far from saturating the task.
