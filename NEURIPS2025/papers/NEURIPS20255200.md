## Problem

Whole-slide histopathological images (WSIs) are gigapixel-scale, making full high-resolution analysis computationally infeasible, yet diagnostic regions occupy only a small fraction of each slide. Existing sparse-sampling methods like RLogist close this gap but suffer from ~10–15% accuracy loss relative to full-resolution baselines, rely on generic ImageNet-pretrained features that are poorly diagnostic of pathology, and exhibit unstable RL training due to concurrent policy and classifier optimization.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255200/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes multi-resolution WSIs (5× scanning and 20× high-resolution) segmented into non-overlapping 256×256×3 RGB patches (~800 at 5×, ~12,500 at 20× per slide), with features extracted by a WSI-pretrained ViT into 384-dim embeddings. At inference, the RL agent sequentially selects 10–20% of low-resolution patches to zoom into at 20×, and the final output is a slide-level class label (binary cancer/normal or multi-class subtype). The WSI representation used by the classifier is compressed to N×d (vs. N×k×d for full-resolution methods), yielding >16× compressibility.

## Method

SASHA integrates three components: (1) HAFED, a two-stage hierarchical attention model whose first stage distills k high-resolution sub-patch features into a single d-dim vector per low-res patch, and whose second stage applies multi-branch attention (with ACMIL-style dissimilarity loss) across all N patches for slide-level classification; (2) TSU, a targeted state updater that, upon visiting a patch, updates only those other patches whose cosine similarity exceeds a threshold τ via a small MLP, avoiding the state corruption caused by global updates; and (3) a PPO-based RL agent whose policy network selects the next patch to zoom into, with reward defined as negative cross-entropy of the (frozen) HAFED classifier. The classifier is trained end-to-end with HAFED before RL training and then frozen, decoupling policy optimization from feature learning to ensure stable convergence. This design achieves near-full-resolution accuracy (e.g., 95.3% vs. 94.9% on CAMELYON16) while sampling only 20% of patches, with 4–8× faster inference and ~6% of the memory cost.

## Results

On CAMELYON16, SASHA-0.2 (sampling only 20% of patches at high resolution) achieves 95.3% accuracy, 97.9% AUC, and 93.7% F1, matching or exceeding all full-resolution (100% sampling) baselines such as CLAM_SB (94.9% acc) and DTFD (94.4% acc). On TCGA-NSCLC, SASHA-0.2 reaches 91.2% accuracy and 96.3% AUC, on par with the best 100%-sampling method DTFD (91.1% acc, 96.2% AUC). Inference time drops from ~117 s (full-resolution) to ~26 s (SASHA-0.2) and ~14 s (SASHA-0.1), yielding 4× and 8× speedups, while WSI representation compressibility improves by >16×.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the primary RL competitor RLogist [40], SASHA-0.2 wins by a large margin: 95.3% vs 86.2% accuracy on CAMELYON16 (+9.1 pp) and 91.2% vs 83.9% on TCGA-NSCLC (+7.3 pp), with similar gaps at the 10% budget. Against full-resolution MIL baselines (ABMIL, CLAM, TransMIL, DTFD, ACMIL), SASHA-0.2 matches or slightly exceeds the best performers (e.g., 95.3% vs 94.9% for CLAM_SB on CAMELYON16; 91.2% vs 91.1% for DTFD on TCGA-NSCLC) while examining only 20% of patches. Against Zoom-MIL [36], SASHA-0.2 dominates on CAMELYON16 (95.3% vs 75.9% accuracy) but ties on TCGA-NSCLC (91.2% vs 91.2%), where Zoom-MIL's fixed top-k attention strategy happens to align well with the more uniform tumor distribution. The ablation (Table 2) further shows that replacing the learned RL policy with random sampling drops accuracy to 51.6%, and replacing TSU with a global update drops it to 83.7%, confirming that both the sequential attention-based sampling and the targeted state update are critical for the observed gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

SASHA directly extends RLogist [40], the prior RL-based WSI sampling method, by replacing its generic ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-50 features with a hierarchical, multi-attention MIL feature distiller (HAFED) inspired by ACMIL [39] and ABMIL [15], and by substituting RLogist's global state update with a targeted, similarity-based update (TSU) that exploits inter-patch feature correlations. It also departs from the fixed top-k attention sampling strategy of Zoom-MIL [36] by learning an adaptive, context-aware policy via PPO. The overall architecture is a deliberate integration of three previously separate ideas—RL-based sequential observation, multi-branch attention MIL, and correlation-aware state propagation—into a single end-to-end framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: SASHA achieves near-full-resolution accuracy while sampling only 10–20% of patches, yielding up to 8× faster inference and >16× WSI compressibility, which has direct implications for clinical deployment and archival storage. The finding that SASHA-0.2 produces better-calibrated predictions (lower ECE) than full-resolution ACMIL and DTFD is a useful signal for building clinician trust and for deferral-to-specialist workflows. Key limitations remain: training still requires processing all high-resolution patches (negating the inference-time savings during training), and the observation budget directly affects calibration, leaving open the question of how to decouple training cost from inference efficiency. Whether the RL-based adaptive sampling strategy generalizes beyond histopathology to other gigapixel-scale medical imaging tasks (e.g., satellite pathology, ultra-wide-field retinal imaging) is an open direction.
