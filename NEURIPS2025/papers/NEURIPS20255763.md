## Problem

Concept-based Models (CBMs) are designed to produce interpretable concepts and reliable predictions, but it remains unclear when they can actually learn concepts and inference layers with the intended semantics. Prior work on reasoning shortcuts (RSs) only covers Neuro-Symbolic CBMs with a fixed, pre-specified inference layer, leaving the more general and practically relevant case—where the inference layer is jointly learned—entirely unanalyzed. This paper identifies and formalizes Joint Reasoning Shortcuts (JRSs), showing that maximum-likelihood training is insufficient to recover intended semantics and that existing mitigation strategies largely fail in this setting.

## Input / Output

The CBM takes as input either a pair of MNIST digit images (128×128 concatenated), a Clevr 3D scene image (128×128×3, pre-segmented into 2–4 object crops via a fine-tuned YOLOv11 detector), or a driving scene image from BDD-OIA (preprocessed via Faster R-CNN bounding boxes and a 2048-d convolutional feature extractor). Each object/digit is processed independently by a shared backbone that outputs a discrete concept vector; a separate inference layer—ranging from a linear or second-degree polynomial layer (CBNM) to a learned truth table with fuzzy or probabilistic logic (DSL, DPL*)—then maps the concept vector to a class label (digit sum, sum parity, Clevr object class, or driving action). The learned inference layer is a key output alongside the concepts, and its quality is evaluated by feeding ground-truth concept annotations (permuted via Hungarian matching) through the learned map and measuring prediction accuracy.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a theoretical framework that unifies CBMs and NeSy-CBMs as pairs of functions (α, β) mapping ground-truth concepts to predicted concepts and predicted concepts to labels, respectively. It defines 'intended semantics' (Definition 3.3) requiring both disentanglement (each learned concept corresponds to a distinct ground-truth concept up to an invertible transformation) and generalization (the inference layer correctly undoes these transformations), and formalizes JRSs as maximum-likelihood solutions that violate this condition. The key theoretical result (Theorem 3.9) proves that if the number of deterministic JRSs is zero—countable via a closed-form expression (Theorem 3.6)—then under a natural extremality assumption on the inference layer, every maximum-likelihood CBM is identifiable, i.e., possesses intended semantics. Empirically, the authors evaluate six CBM architectures (DPL, CBNM, SENN, DSL, DPL*, bears*) across four tasks of increasing complexity and test a battery of mitigation strategies (concept supervision, knowledge distillation, reconstruction, entropy maximization, contrastive learning), finding that JRSs are pervasive, concept collapse signals simplicity bias, and only contrastive learning provides consistent (though partial) improvement.

## Results

On MNIST-Add (which provably contains no JRSs), all CBMs achieve high label accuracy (F1(Y) ≥ 90%), high concept quality (F1(C) ≥ 90%), and strong inference-layer quality (F1(β) ≥ 75%), confirming the identifiability guarantee of Theorem 3.9. On MNIST-SumParity (which admits ~100k JRSs), all models still attain F1(Y) ≥ 90% in-distribution, yet concept quality collapses: F1(C) never exceeds 50% and F1(β) drops to near-chance levels (≤ 0.54). The same pattern holds on Clevr (F1(C) ≤ 0.49, F1(β) ≤ 0.53) and BDD-OIA (F1(C) ≈ 0.43, F1(β) ≈ 0.42), demonstrating that JRSs are pervasive across architectures and domains. On the biased MNIST-SumParity OOD split, only a fully supervised CBNM (100% concept + knowledge supervision) achieves acceptable OOD F1(Y) = 0.97; all other configurations drop to F1(Y) ≤ 0.40.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new CBM but analyzes six existing architectures (DPL, CBNM, SENN, DSL, DPL*, bears*) to demonstrate that JRSs are universal. On MNIST-Add (no JRSs), all methods perform comparably well (F1(Y) 90–99%, F1(C) 80–99%), with CBNM and DSL achieving the best inference-layer quality (F1(β) = 1.00). On MNIST-SumParity, DPL (which has access to ground-truth knowledge K) still suffers from concept collapse (Cls(C) = 0.36) and moderate concept quality (F1(C) = 0.43), showing that fixed prior knowledge alone does not prevent JRSs. CBNM and DSL are the most severely affected, with F1(C) dropping to 0.07–0.09 and concept collapse reaching 0.66–0.80, indicating they learn highly degenerate concept representations. SENN is a partial outlier with the best F1(C) (0.49) and zero collapse, likely due to its higher-capacity inference layer, but its F1(β) (0.53) remains near chance. bears* (an ensemble wrapping DPL*) trades off label performance for calibration, achieving the lowest F1(β) (0.36) while maintaining high F1(Y). The key takeaway is that no existing method, even with ground-truth knowledge or ensemble strategies, achieves intended semantics on tasks with JRSs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends the reasoning shortcut (RS) analysis of Marconato et al. (NeurIPS 2023), which studied shortcuts in Neuro-Symbolic CBMs with a fixed, knowledge-specified inference layer, to the more general setting where the inference layer is also learned. It introduces Joint Reasoning Shortcuts (JRSs), a strictly broader failure mode where both the concept extractor and the inference layer can simultaneously deviate from ground-truth semantics while still achieving optimal training likelihood. The identifiability framework draws on the equivalence-relation formalism from ICA and Causal Representation Learning (Khemakhem et al., Lachapelle et al.), but applies it to discrete concepts and a learned inference layer rather than continuous representations. The empirical evaluation builds on the CBNM architecture (Koh et al., ICML 2020) and the RS benchmark suite (Bortolotti et al., NeurIPS 2024), testing DPL, DSL, SENN, and BEARS variants across four tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical identifiability conditions (Theorem 3.9) for CBMs with learned inference layers, showing that zeroing deterministic JRSs guarantees recovery of intended semantics under an extremality assumption. Practically, it demonstrates that well-established mitigations for regular RSs (reconstruction, entropy maximization, knowledge distillation) largely fail to prevent JRSs, with only contrastive learning showing partial benefit on concept collapse — a sobering result for practitioners relying on these strategies. Key limitations include dependence on two standard data-generation assumptions (extrapolability, deterministic knowledge) and an extremality condition on the inference layer, and the empirical evaluation is concentrated on small synthetic tasks (MNIST variants, Clevr) plus one real-world driving dataset. Open questions include extending the theory to relax these assumptions, applying it to general neural networks and LLMs, and developing effective JRS-specific mitigations.
