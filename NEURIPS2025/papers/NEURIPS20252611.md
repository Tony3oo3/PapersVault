## Problem

Existing speculative tree decoding methods verify drafted tokens using per-token probabilities in a top-down, layer-by-layer fashion. This introduces two critical limitations: sequence-level acceptance is suboptimal because token-level decisions sacrifice global optimality, and rejecting a parent node prematurely discards all its descendants, wasting drafted candidates that could form valid subsequences.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252611/cropped_page4_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes a valid autoregressive sampling tree T rooted at prefix X₀, along with draft model distributions M_s(·|X^i) and target model distributions M_b(·|X^i) at every node. It outputs an accepted chain X^τ (a root-to-node path in T) plus one additional token Y sampled from the target distribution at the acceptance position. The tree structure is arbitrary (chain, binary tree, EAGLE sparse tree, etc.) and the algorithm is a plug-and-play replacement for any existing verification step in a speculative decoding pipeline.

## Method

Traversal Verification rethinks the verification paradigm by operating bottom-up from leaf nodes toward the root, using sequence-level acceptance rates rather than per-token probabilities. The acceptance rate at each node is defined as the cumulative product of target-to-draft probability ratios along the path from root to that node (capped at 1), so accepting a node means accepting the entire sequence from root to that node. When a leaf is rejected, the algorithm removes it, updates residual and draft distributions via a sequence-level RRSw mechanism (scaling the target distribution by the parent's acceptance probability and zeroing the rejected token's draft probability), then proceeds to sibling nodes before backtracking to the parent. The paper proves two key theoretical results: (1) the output distribution is identical to the target model's (losslessness), established via induction exploiting the algorithm's self-similar recursive structure, and (2) in the single-chain case, Traversal Verification achieves the optimal upper bound on expected acceptance length, matching Block Verification. Experiments on Llama3 and Llama2 models across six Spec-Bench tasks show 2.2%–5.7% average acceptance length improvement over token-level verification, with gains growing for deeper and larger trees.

## Results

On Llama3.2-1B-Instruct (draft) + Llama3.1-8B-Instruct (target), Traversal Verification improves average acceptance length by 2.2%–2.8% over token-level verification across Chain, Binary Tree, and EAGLE Sparse Tree structures (e.g., 3.88→3.99 for Chain, 4.63→4.73 for Binary Tree), with throughput gains of 1.7%–2.5% token/s. On the Llama-68M + Llama2-7B pair, improvements are larger: 3.8%–5.7% in acceptance length and 3.0%–4.8% in throughput. Gains grow with chain depth (up to depth 8) and tree size (up to 2⁶−1 nodes), and are more pronounced at higher sampling temperatures (2.8% at T=1.0 vs. 1.0% at T=0.2 for Chain).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is Token-level Verification (Tok.V) with Recursive Rejection Sampling without Replacement (RRSw), implemented following EAGLE [21]. Traversal Verification (Tra.V) wins on every task, tree structure, and model combination tested, with no losses or ties. On the Llama3 pair, average acceptance length improves by 2.2% (Binary Tree), 2.4% (EAGLE Sparse Tree), and 2.8% (Chain); on the Llama2 pair, improvements are larger at 3.8%–5.7%. Throughput gains are slightly smaller than acceptance-length gains (e.g., 1.7% vs. 2.2% for Binary Tree on Llama3) due to the additional computational overhead of bottom-up traversal, though the authors note this can be mitigated with optimized implementation. The advantage is most pronounced for the weaker draft model (Llama-68M) and at higher temperatures, where the gap between sequence-level and token-level acceptance probabilities is widest.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits squarely within the speculative decoding lineage, building on the foundational drafting-verification paradigm of Leviathan et al. (ICML 2023) and the tree-based RRS/RRSw verification of SpecInfer (ASPLOS 2024) and EAGLE (ICML 2024). Its key conceptual departure is inverting the verification direction: rather than the standard top-down, token-by-token acceptance used by all prior tree decoding methods, Traversal Verification proceeds bottom-up from leaf nodes and accepts entire root-to-leaf sequences based on joint probability. The single-chain optimality proof directly inherits from Block Verification (Sun et al., ICLR 2025), whose lemmas establish the upper bound that Traversal Verification matches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The provable losslessness and single-chain optimality give Traversal Verification a strong theoretical foundation, and its plug-and-play nature means it can replace the verification step in any existing speculative decoding pipeline without architectural changes. However, the practical gains are modest (2.2%–5.7% in acceptance length, 1.7%–4.8% in throughput), and the bottom-up traversal introduces additional computational overhead that narrows the throughput advantage relative to the acceptance-length advantage. Open questions include whether the gains scale meaningfully to the very deep, wide trees used in model-offloading scenarios (e.g., Sequoia's 768-node trees), and whether optimized implementations can close the gap between acceptance-length and throughput improvements. The proprietary codebase (Lenovo) also limits immediate reproducibility.
