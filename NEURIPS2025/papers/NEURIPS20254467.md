## Problem

Existing semi-supervised graph anomaly detection methods assume that all normal nodes share a similar level of homophily and that a small labeled set of normal nodes can represent the overall homophily pattern. In practice, normal nodes exhibit substantial homophily variation, causing low-homophily normal nodes to be misclassified as anomalies, especially when neighborhood aggregation produces oversmoothed representations.

## Input / Output

The method operates on an attributed graph G=(V,E,X) where only a small fraction (5–20%) of normal nodes are labeled; no anomaly labels are available. It is transductive, consuming the full graph Laplacian during both training and inference. The output is a scalar anomaly score per unlabeled node, computed as the average squared Euclidean distance from the node's embedding to two one-class hypersphere centers (one per view), with higher scores indicating greater abnormality.

## Method

RHO introduces two modules. AdaFreq learns adaptive spectral filters of the form g(λ)=1−kλ with a single learnable parameter k per layer, applied in two complementary views: a cross-channel view (one shared k across all feature channels) and a channel-wise view (a separate k per channel, implemented via Hadamard product with the Laplacian). A theoretical result (Theorem 1) shows that minimizing the one-class loss drives g(λ_m) to amplify frequency components where labeled normal nodes exhibit coherent spectral behavior and suppress inconsistent components, enabling the filter to adapt to diverse homophily levels without explicit eigen-decomposition. GNA then enforces consistency between the two views via a symmetric InfoNCE contrastive loss, treating same-node cross-view representations as positive pairs and different-node representations as negatives. The total training objective combines the one-class losses from both views with the GNA alignment loss weighted by α, and inference simply scores each node by its average distance to the two learned centers.

## Results

On eight real-world GAD datasets with 15% labeled normal nodes, RHO achieves the best AUROC on six of eight datasets, with headline results of 0.8509 on Elliptic, 0.8623 on T-Finance, 0.7129 on Photo, and 0.6255 on Tolokers. In AUPRC, RHO achieves particularly large gains on Elliptic (0.5095 vs. 0.2425 for GGAD) and T-Finance (0.4893 vs. 0.1825 for GGAD), representing up to ~110% and ~168% relative improvements respectively. The paper reports a maximum 12.19% AUROC and 30.68% AUPRC improvement over the best semi-supervised competitor GGAD across the six datasets where RHO wins.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RHO outperforms the strongest semi-supervised baseline GGAD on six of eight datasets in AUROC (e.g., 0.8509 vs. 0.7290 on Elliptic, 0.8623 vs. 0.8228 on T-Finance, 0.6255 vs. 0.5340 on Tolokers), while GGAD retains the edge on Amazon (0.9443 vs. 0.9302) and Reddit (0.6354 vs. 0.6207). In AUPRC, RHO's advantage over GGAD is even more pronounced, with gains of ~110% on Elliptic (0.5095 vs. 0.2425) and ~168% on T-Finance (0.4893 vs. 0.1825). Against spectral baselines, RHO consistently beats both the fixed GCN low-pass filter and the pre-defined BWGNN band-pass filter across all eight datasets, confirming that adaptive frequency response is critical for handling heterogeneous homophily. Among fully supervised methods adapted to the semi-supervised setting, PMP and CONSISGAD underperform RHO on most datasets (e.g., PMP: 0.8321 vs. 0.8623 AUROC on T-Finance; CONSISGAD: 0.7354 vs. 0.8509 on Elliptic), as they cannot leverage labeled anomalies. Reconstruction-based methods (DOMINANT, AnomalyDAE) and one-class methods (OCGNN) are uniformly outperformed, with OCGNN lagging by up to 56 AUROC points on Elliptic (0.2881 vs. 0.8509). RHO also achieves the best AUPRC on all eight datasets, indicating superior precision-recall tradeoff particularly important in the highly imbalanced GAD setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant assumption in semi-supervised GAD that all normal nodes share a similar level of homophily, instead showing that normal nodes exhibit substantial intra-class homophily variation. It builds on spectral graph filtering (as in BWGNN and GCN) but replaces fixed or pre-defined frequency responses with a learnable linear filter g(λ)=1−kλ, motivated by a theoretical result showing this form adaptively amplifies consistent spectral components of labeled normal nodes. The dual-view design (cross-channel and channel-wise) with contrastive alignment (GNA) draws on multi-view contrastive learning, while the one-class hypersphere objective follows OCGNN and GGAD. Unlike prior homophily-modeling GAD work that modifies neighbor selection or edge weights, RHO addresses the problem at the filter level, making it the first to learn heterogeneous normal patterns from diverse-homophily labeled nodes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The observation that normal nodes in GAD datasets have diverse homophily levels (not just a single high-homophily cluster) challenges a foundational assumption underlying most semi-supervised GAD methods and explains why they misclassify low-homophily normal nodes as anomalies. The adaptive filter approach is lightweight (one scalar per layer/channel) yet provides a principled mechanism for handling this diversity, with the theoretical guarantee that consistent spectral components are preserved. A key limitation is that RHO is transductive, sharing a constraint with most existing GAD methods, and its applicability to inductive or streaming settings remains open. The approach could also inform broader graph learning tasks where intra-class feature/spectral heterogeneity is present but unmodeled.
