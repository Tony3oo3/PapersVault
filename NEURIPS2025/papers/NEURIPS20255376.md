## Problem

Behaviorally relevant latent dynamics are preserved across recording sessions and subjects, but extracting them is difficult because different sessions record different neuron subsets, trial durations vary, and existing alignment tools either operate per-subject, discretize continuous behavior into categories, or lack explicit mechanisms to align temporal dynamics across sessions. This paper asks whether a single interpretable dynamical model can be recovered from heterogeneous multi-session recordings while preserving the natural continuity of behavior.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255376/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes high-dimensional neural activity from M sessions (varying neuron counts, e.g., ~220 neurons/session for calcium imaging at 30 Hz or 20 ms binned spike rates for electrophysiology) together with continuous behavioral signals (e.g., 1-D wheel velocity or 2-D hand velocity) at each time bin. It produces a shared D-dimensional latent embedding per time point, a single linear dynamical system (matrices A, C, Q, R) shared across all sessions, causally filtered latent states via Kalman filtering, and session-agnostic behavior decoding predictions. A key design constraint is that the LDS dimensionality equals the embedding dimensionality (n_z = D), enabling real-time causal inference while the nonlinear encoder absorbs session-specific structure.

## Method

CANDY uses session-specific MLP encoders to project heterogeneous neural recordings into a common D-dimensional embedding space, then aligns those embeddings with a Rank-N-Contrast loss adapted for continuous behavioral variables: within each minibatch, every pair of embeddings is ranked by the L1 distance of their associated behaviors, and the loss enforces that cosine-similarity distances in latent space preserve this behavioral ordering. A shared linear dynamical system (z_t = Az_{t-1} + w_t, ũ_t = Cz_t + v_t) is fitted to the aligned embeddings via a k-step-ahead neural prediction loss, with session-specific MLP decoders reconstructing the original neural activity. All components—encoders, decoders, LDS parameters, and an optional session-invariant linear behavior decoder—are trained end-to-end under a single combined objective. The critical finding is that the contrastive alignment loss is not merely a decoding aid but is necessary to uncover the preserved LDS: without it, the shared dynamics cannot be learned even when behavior supervision is present.

## Results

On the mouse wheel-turning task (13 sessions, ~3,000 neurons), CANDY achieves the highest session-agnostic behavior decoding R² on both latent embeddings and latent dynamics across all tested dimensions, with performance plateauing at only 8 latent dimensions for dynamics. The pretrained shared LDS generalizes to a held-out third animal: with just 10% of held-out session data, decoding R²≈0.7 is achieved, rising to R²≈0.8 at 50%, and the pretrained model outperforms training from scratch at every data fraction including 100%. On the macaque center-out reaching task (40 sessions), CANDY similarly outperforms all baselines on both embedding and dynamics decoding, and the pretrained LDS generalizes to two held-out sessions. CANDY also achieves a significantly higher alignment score (Pearson correlation between behavioral and embedding distance matrices) than multi-session CEBRA across all latent dimensionalities.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against multi-session CEBRA [16], CANDY wins on all three primary metrics: alignment score (significantly higher Pearson correlation between behavioral and embedding distance matrices, Fig. S2), behavior decoding R² on latent embeddings (Fig. 3D, S5B), and behavior decoding R² on latent dynamics (Fig. 3E, S5C). The key distinction is that CEBRA discretizes behavior into categories and does not model temporal dynamics, whereas CANDY uses continuous rank-based contrastive alignment plus a shared LDS. Against DFINE-supervised [22], CANDY wins decisively on cross-session alignment (DFINE produces disjoint, session-specific embedding regions with no shared structure, Fig. 3B-right) and on session-agnostic decoding R² for both embeddings and dynamics; DFINE is inherently per-session and cannot produce a shared latent space. Against Stitch-LFADS [14] and SLDS [26], CANDY achieves higher behavior decoding R² on latent dynamics across all dimensionalities (Fig. 3E), with these baselines requiring substantially more dimensions to converge. In the generalization experiment (Fig. 5), CANDY's frozen pretrained LDS outperforms a model trained from scratch on the held-out animal at every data fraction, confirming that the learned dynamics are truly preserved across subjects rather than session-specific.

## Relation to Prior Work

CANDY sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) single-session neural dynamics modeling (LDS, SLDS, DFINE, LFADS), which captures temporal structure but cannot align across sessions; (2) multi-session alignment via post-hoc CCA or session-specific encoders (Stitch-LFADS, CEBRA), which produces a shared space but either assumes fixed trial durations or discretizes behavior into categories; and (3) rank-based contrastive learning (Rank-N-Contrast, Zha et al. 2024), originally developed for regression-aware representation learning in other domains. The key departure is integrating a continuous-behavior-anchored contrastive objective directly with a shared LDS in an end-to-end framework, eliminating the need for post-hoc alignment and preserving the natural continuity of behavioral signals that CEBRA's discrete categorization discards.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a frozen linear dynamical system trained on two mice generalizes to a third mouse with only 10% of the data (R²≈0.7), providing evidence for genuinely preserved dynamical structure in striatal circuitry under a wheel-turning task. The clean ablation showing that behavior supervision alone cannot substitute for contrastive alignment—despite achieving comparable embedding-level decoding—reframes alignment as a prerequisite for dynamics recovery rather than a decoding convenience. Limitations include the linear Gaussian LDS assumption (nonlinear dynamics are only approximated via the encoder), failure to generalize to entirely unseen behavioral states (e.g., a held-out reaching direction), and validation on only two species and two tasks. Open questions include whether the framework can identify subject-distinctive dynamical strategies, scale to larger cohorts, and handle nonlinear or switching dynamics natively.
