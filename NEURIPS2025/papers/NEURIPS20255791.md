## Problem

Current visual editing models can perform simple instruction-following but lack the ability to execute reasoning-informed visual editing (RISE) — making intelligent modifications grounded in temporal, causal, spatial, or logical reasoning. No existing benchmark systematically evaluates this capability, leaving it impossible to quantitatively measure how well models handle reasoning-driven edits versus superficial instruction compliance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255791/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each test case pairs an input image (natural photos, Blender 3D renders, or stylized/synthetic layouts) with a text editing instruction that requires multi-step reasoning to execute correctly. The model produces a single edited image, which is scored on three dimensions: Instruction Reasoning, Appearance Consistency, and Visual Plausibility. For complex scenes where a text description is insufficient (particularly in Logical and Spatial tasks), the evaluation pipeline supplies a reference image to the judge model for direct visual comparison rather than relying solely on textual ground truth.

## Method

RISEBench-360 curates 360 human-annotated test cases across four reasoning categories — Temporal (85), Causal (90), Spatial (100), and Logical (85) — each decomposed into multiple subcategories (e.g., life progression, structural deformation, component assembly, puzzle solving). The evaluation framework scores outputs on three dimensions using an LMM-as-a-judge pipeline built on GPT-4.1, with task-specific prompts, in-context examples, and a binary scoring scheme for logical-reasoning appearance consistency versus a continuous 1–5 scale for natural-image tasks. A sample is counted as solved only if it achieves a perfect 5 on all three applicable dimensions, producing a strict accuracy metric. The LMM judge is validated against six human experts on 100 sampled outputs, yielding MAE of 0.4–0.7 on the 1–5 scale. Nine models spanning diffusion-based (FLUX.1-Canny), autoregressive (EMU2, OmniGen, BAGEL), hybrid (Step1X-Edit), and proprietary (GPT-image-1, Gemini-2.0-Flash series, HiDream-Edit) architectures are evaluated, with the best model achieving only 28.9% overall accuracy.

## Results

On RISEBench-360, GPT-image-1 achieves the highest overall accuracy at 28.9%, with per-category scores of 34.1% (Temporal), 32.2% (Causal), 37.0% (Spatial), and 10.6% (Logical). The Gemini-2.0-Flash series ranks second and third at 13.3% and 9.4% overall, respectively. All open-source and remaining proprietary models (BAGEL, Step1X-Edit, OmniGen, EMU2, HiDream-Edit, FLUX.1-Canny) score at or below 5.8%, with several at 0.0%. A sample is counted as solved only if it receives a perfect score of 5 on all three evaluation dimensions (Instruction Reasoning, Appearance Consistency, Visual Plausibility), making the reported accuracies a strict success rate.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-image-1 dominates all other models by a wide margin, achieving 28.9% overall accuracy versus 13.3% for the next-best model (Gemini-2.0-Flash-exp), a gap of over 15 percentage points. However, even GPT-image-1's performance drops sharply on Logical Reasoning (10.6%) compared to its Spatial (37.0%) and Temporal (34.1%) scores, identifying logical reasoning as the critical bottleneck. Among the Gemini variants, the Experimental version outperforms the Preview version overall (13.3% vs. 9.4%), largely because the Preview version suffers a significant spatial reasoning decline (23.0% → 11.0%) despite having superior image generation quality. BAGEL (5.8%) is the strongest open-source model, showing some instruction-following ability but weak visual plausibility. Step1X-Edit (1.9%), OmniGen (0.8%), and EMU2 (0.5%) all perform near zero, indicating they cannot meaningfully execute reasoning-driven edits. HiDream-Edit and FLUX.1-Canny score 0.0% across all categories, confirming they lack any reasoning-informed editing capability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of image editing and multimodal reasoning. Prior image editing work (InstructPix2Pix, MagicBrush, FLUX, Step1X-Edit) focused on following explicit instructions and preserving appearance, while unified LMMs (BAGEL, EMU2, OmniGen, Show-o) addressed the architectural unification of understanding and generation. The LMM-as-a-judge evaluation paradigm has been used in text-to-image evaluation, but no prior work systematically evaluated whether editing models can perform temporal, causal, spatial, or logical reasoning to produce correct visual modifications. RISEBench introduces this reasoning-informed editing formulation as a distinct evaluation axis, decomposing quality into instruction reasoning, appearance consistency, and visual plausibility.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a stark capability gap: even GPT-image-1 achieves only 28.9% accuracy, and open-source models score near zero, indicating that reasoning-driven visual editing is far from solved. The specific finding that frontier models handle physical-world reasoning (temporal, causal, spatial) substantially better than formal logical reasoning (10.6% vs. >30%) identifies a concrete bottleneck for future research. Key limitations include the small dataset size (360 samples), the reliance on a single LMM judge (GPT-4.1) despite human validation, and the absence of any proposed method to improve performance—making this primarily a diagnostic contribution. The benchmark is likely to become a standard reference point as reasoning-aware editing models emerge.
