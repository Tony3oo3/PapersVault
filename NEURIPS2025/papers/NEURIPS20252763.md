## Problem

In multimodal joint training, dominant modalities suppress weaker ones, causing modality imbalance where unimodal performance in the joint model falls far below its intrinsic ceiling. Existing rebalancing methods assess modality contribution only at the modality level, overlooking the fact that a modality's importance varies dynamically across individual samples (e.g., audio may dominate for one sample while text dominates for another). This coarse-grained, modality-level analysis fails to capture fine-grained, sample-specific contribution differences and lacks interpretability.

## Input / Output

The method consumes heterogeneous multimodal inputs per sample: audio as mel-spectrograms (257×299 or 257×1004), video as 3 uniformly sampled frames from 10, text sequences truncated to 128 tokens, and RGB/optical-flow/depth frames at 224×224. Beyond the final class label, the method produces a per-modality contribution score Φ(x_i^j) for every modality in every sample, computed via causal intervention (removing one modality and measuring the change in a Shannon-information-theoretic benefit function). These scores drive a dynamic, sample-level optimization schedule that selectively augments weak modalities during training.

## Method

CMoB introduces a causal-aware modality valuation framework with three coupled components. First, a benefit function B(M) grounded in Shannon's information uncertainty assigns each sample a value equal to the number of modalities only when the multimodal prediction is correct and confidence increases with added modalities. Second, a causal quantification method computes the Individual Treatment Effect (ITE) of each modality j in sample i by applying a do-intervention (removing modality j) and measuring the difference in benefit between the full and intervened sets, then adding the modality's own unimodal benefit to obtain the total contribution Φ(x_i^j). Third, a dynamic optimization strategy (Eq. 5) uses these per-sample scores to selectively enhance weak modalities: in the first half of training, weak-modality samples receive augmented loss via a monotonically increasing reweighting function; in the second half, adaptive masking (time masking for audio, spatial masking for video) is applied to strong modalities to prevent overfitting and enforce cross-modal dependency. The entire procedure operates at the sample level rather than the modality level, enabling fine-grained, interpretable rebalancing that generalizes to any number of modalities.

## Results

CMoB achieves state-of-the-art or near-state-of-the-art accuracy across five multimodal benchmarks. On CREMA-D it reaches 79.75% ACC (vs. 79.43% for MLA), on UCF-101 it attains 86.82% ACC (vs. 85.38% for MLA), on CMU-MOSEI it scores 81.24% ACC / 74.97% F1 (vs. 81.18% / 74.64% for MMPareto), and on NVGesture it achieves 84.06% ACC (vs. 83.82% for MMPareto). On KineticsSounds, CMoB (72.03% ACC) trails MMPareto (74.55% ACC) by 2.5 points, the only dataset where it does not lead. All reported numbers include standard deviations over multiple runs (e.g., ±0.27% on CREMA-D).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CMoB outperforms the strongest baselines on four of five main datasets: it edges out MLA on CREMA-D (79.75 vs. 79.43 ACC) and UCF-101 (86.82 vs. 85.38 ACC), surpasses MMPareto on CMU-MOSEI (81.24 vs. 81.18 ACC) and NVGesture (84.06 vs. 83.82 ACC). On KineticsSounds, however, MMPareto retains a 2.5-point ACC advantage (74.55 vs. 72.03), the sole dataset where CMoB does not lead. Against the simple Concat baseline, CMoB gains 14.25 points on CREMA-D, 6.40 on KineticsSounds, 5.02 on UCF-101, 2.25 on CMU-MOSEI, and 2.73 on NVGesture, confirming that modality imbalance is a pervasive issue. On the three-modality CMU-MOSEI dataset, CMoB is the only method that simultaneously exceeds the best unimodal (text, 80.58 ACC) and the Concat baseline, demonstrating its advantage in handling more than two modalities. On the additional Caltech101-20 experiment with up to five modalities, CMoB consistently outperforms both Concat and the Shapley-value-based MMCooperation by 0.2–0.7 points in accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself within the modality imbalance literature for multimodal learning, departing from gradient-based rebalancing methods (OGM, AGM, GBlending) that operate at the modality level and from sample-level approaches like Shapley-value-based MMCooperation and SMLS. Its key conceptual departure is repurposing causal inference machinery—specifically intervention (do-operator) and individual treatment effect (ITE)—from the causal learning literature to quantify per-modality, per-sample contribution, combined with a Shannon information-theoretic benefit function. The cognitive science framing (human multisensory integration as causal inference) provides motivation but the technical core is a novel synthesis of causal effect estimation with multimodal training dynamics rather than an extension of any single prior method.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The causal-aware sample-level valuation offers an interpretable alternative to gradient-magnitude heuristics for identifying weak modalities, which is practically useful for practitioners debugging multimodal pipelines. However, the empirical gains over strong baselines (MMPareto, MLA) are modest and inconsistent—CMoB underperforms MMPareto on KineticsSounds—and the benefit function's reliance on correct predictions limits its applicability in early training or noisy regimes. Open questions include whether the ITE-based valuation scales efficiently to many modalities (requiring 2^M subset evaluations in the worst case), whether the causal framing provides genuine theoretical guarantees beyond the empirical correlation it captures, and how the method behaves under distribution shift or domain generalization settings.
