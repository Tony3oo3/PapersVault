## Problem

Current automated theorem proving pipelines prompt LLMs to generate thousands of whole proofs and discard all but the one that passes the Lean compiler, leaving the compiler's rich diagnostic feedback (syntax errors, incorrect tactics, open goals with line numbers) entirely unused. This brute-force approach is wasteful in both sampling and token budgets, and it prevents the LLM from iteratively refining a nearly-correct proof the way a human mathematician would using a proof assistant.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251389/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The system takes a formal theorem statement in Lean4 and a user-chosen LLM proof generator model (model-agnostic, ranging from general-purpose LLMs like o4-mini to specialized provers like Goedel-V2-8B). It produces a fully verified Lean4 proof file with zero sorry or admit commands, confirmed by the Lean REPL compiler. Unlike fixed-budget @K sampling, Apollo's sampling cost is dynamic and governed by a user-specified recursion depth r; each unresolved sub-lemma triggers a targeted top-32 LLM call, so total samples scale with proof complexity rather than a flat multiplier.

## Method

APOLLO is a modular, model-agnostic agentic pipeline that closes the loop between LLM generation and Lean compiler feedback through five stages: (1) a Syntax Refiner applies regex-based corrections for common Lean3-to-Lean4 errors; (2) a Sorrifier parses the proof into a nested tree of sub-lemmas, compiles it via the Lean REPL, and replaces each failing block with a sorry placeholder (or removes it) until the file compiles cleanly; (3) an Auto Solver attempts to close each sorry using Lean's hint mechanism and built-in tactics (nlinarith, ring, simp, norm_num, etc.) wrapped in try; (4) for any remaining sorry blocks, Apollo extracts the local context (hypotheses, prior lemmas) and recursively re-invokes the LLM on each sub-goal, repeating the full verify-refine-solve cycle up to depth r; (5) a Proof Assembler splices all repaired sub-proofs back together and re-verifies the complete file. The key insight is that the compiler's per-line error messages pinpoint exactly which sub-lemmas failed, enabling targeted repair of fine-grained goals rather than wholesale regeneration, which dramatically reduces the sampling budget (e.g., 362 samples vs. 25,600 for Goedel-Prover-SFT) while improving accuracy.

## Results

On miniF2F-test, APOLLO establishes a new state-of-the-art accuracy of 84.9% among sub-8B-parameter models using Goedel-V2-8B with only 63 average samples and 344K tokens, matching the base model's best accuracy (84.9% at 128 samples) at half the budget. For Goedel-Prover-SFT, Apollo raises accuracy from 64.7% to 65.6% while cutting the sampling budget from 25,600 to ~362 samples (a 70× reduction). General-purpose LLMs see the largest relative gains: o4-mini jumps from 7.0% to 46.7% and o3-mini from 3.3% to 40.2% accuracy. On PutnamBench, Apollo adds one additional valid proof (11/658 vs. 10/658) while consuming nearly half the tokens.

## Baselines & Comparisons

APOLLO outperforms all tree-search baselines on miniF2F-test: Goedel-V2+Apollo reaches 84.9%, surpassing BFS-Prover (70.8%), HPv16+BFS+DC (68.4%), and IntLM2.5-SP+BFS+CG (65.9%), while requiring orders of magnitude fewer samples. Among whole-proof generation models, Apollo consistently improves each base model: Goedel-SFT gains +0.9% (64.7→65.6%) at 70× fewer samples, Kimina-Preview gains +4.2% (70.8→75.0%) at 3× fewer samples, and Goedel-V2 matches its best accuracy (84.9%) at half the sample budget (63 vs. 128). For general-purpose LLMs, the gains are dramatic: o4-mini improves from 7.0% to 46.7% and o3-mini from 3.3% to 40.2%, both far exceeding their standalone performance. Against the RMaxTS Monte Carlo Tree Simulation method [45], Apollo achieves 75.0% vs. 64.3% at a comparable ~2M token budget. Apollo also outperforms the REPL-feedback repair baseline [44] (40.2% vs. 25.4% for o3-mini at similar sample budgets), demonstrating that recursive, compiler-guided sub-lemma decomposition is more effective than single-round compiler feedback.

## Relation to Prior Work

Apollo sits at the intersection of whole-proof generation (Goedel-Prover, Kimina-Prover, GPT-f) and compiler-feedback repair (BALDUR, THOR, REPL-based repair from Yousefzadeh et al.), but departs from both by making the Lean compiler an active, recursive collaborator rather than a binary verifier or a one-shot feedback source. Where prior repair work (SED, BIFI, Ringer) addresses single-pass fixes and where tree-search methods (BFS, MCTS, Hypertree) explore proof space at high inference cost, Apollo introduces a modular agentic pipeline—Syntax Refiner → Sorrifier → Auto Solver → recursive LLM re-invocation—that decomposes failing sub-lemmas and targets the LLM only on the specific goals that remain open. The recursive decomposition strategy is conceptually related to DSP and LEGO-Prover's sketch-then-prove pipelines, but Apollo operates entirely in the formal domain without requiring an informal proof sketch as input.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The most practically significant finding is the 1–2 order-of-magnitude reduction in sampling budget (e.g., 25,600 → ~362 samples for Goedel-SFT) while simultaneously improving accuracy, which directly addresses the GPU-cost bottleneck that limits real-world deployment of LLM-based theorem provers. The jump from 3–7% to 40%+ for general-purpose models (o3-mini, o4-mini) suggests that compiler-guided repair can unlock mathematical reasoning capability that is already latent in large LLMs but wasted by syntax errors and fine-grained tactic failures. Key limitations remain: Apollo's success depends heavily on the base model producing a coherent proof skeleton, and it has not been integrated with tree-search provers where recursive lemma decomposition could potentially prune search paths. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to harder benchmarks (e.g., full IMO problems) and whether the recursion-depth parameter can be learned adaptively rather than set manually.
