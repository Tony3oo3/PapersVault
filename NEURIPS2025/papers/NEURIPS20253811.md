## Problem

Single-agent Inverse Reinforcement Learning has well-established characterizations of feasible reward sets and sample complexity bounds, but the multi-agent setting lacks analogous theoretical foundations. A critical gap is that a single observed Nash equilibrium is insufficient to identify a meaningful reward set, because equilibrium multiplicity allows the recovered reward to change the game's strategic nature (e.g., from coordination to anti-coordination), yielding a Nash Gap of order (1−γ)⁻¹. The paper asks: what constitutes a rigorous MAIRL definition, and is reward identifiability achievable in multi-agent games?

## Input / Output

The method takes a Markov game structure (finite state space, per-agent action spaces, transition kernel, discount factor) together with an observed expert equilibrium policy (either a Nash equilibrium or, in the regularized setting, a unique Quantal Response Equilibrium) and environment samples drawn from a generative model. The output is not a single reward function but a feasible reward set defined by equilibrium constraints, along with an explicit reward decomposition (Lemma 3.6) that separates policy-dependent and transition-dependent components. The sample complexity bound scales as Õ(γ²R_max²|S||A||B| / ((1−γ)⁴ε²Δ_min⁴)), where the product |A||B| reflects the joint action space and Δ_min is a lower bound on the minimum QRE action probability.

## Method

The paper first defines the feasible reward set for a single Nash equilibrium observation and proves (Proposition 3.4) that this set is non-expressive: it permits reward functions that introduce new exploitable equilibria in the original game. To resolve the equilibrium selection problem, the authors shift to entropy-regularized Markov games, which admit a unique fully mixed Quantal Response Equilibrium. In this setting, they derive an explicit reward characterization (Lemma 3.6) showing that each reward entry R(s,a,b) is determined by the QRE policy, the value function, and the transition kernel, with a recursive dependence on other actions of the opponent. An error propagation theorem (Theorem 3.7) quantifies how estimation errors in the transition model and the opponent's policy amplify into reward estimation error, with the total variation distance of the opponent's policy entering directly. For identifiability, the paper shows that in general-sum games only the average reward (marginalized over the opponent's actions) is recoverable up to a constant under rank conditions on induced transition matrices (Theorem 4.1), while full reward identification requires the additional structural assumption of linearly separable rewards R(s,a,b) = R_A(s,a) + R_B(s,b), under which identifiability holds up to an additive constant given appropriate rank conditions (Proposition 4.3).

## Results

The paper establishes that a single Nash equilibrium observation is fundamentally insufficient for MAIRL: Proposition 3.4 shows that any MAIRL algorithm selecting a non-constant reward from the feasible set can yield a policy with Nash Gap of order (1−γ)⁻¹ in the original game. For entropy-regularized (QRE) experts, Theorem 3.9 provides a sample complexity bound of Õ(γ²R_max²|S||A||B| / ((1−γ)⁴ε²Δ_min⁴)) for the uniform sampling algorithm to satisfy the optimality criterion. On identifiability, Theorem 4.1 shows that only the average reward R^ν(s,a) = Σ_b ν(b|s)R(s,a,b) is recoverable up to a constant under a rank-2|S|−1 condition, while Proposition 4.3 proves full reward identifiability (up to constants) is achievable under linearly separable rewards R(s,a,b) = R_A(s,a) + R_B(s,b) with appropriate rank conditions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a primarily theoretical paper without direct algorithmic baselines or benchmark comparisons. The paper positions its contributions relative to prior work in three ways: (1) It extends the single-agent feasible reward set framework of Metelli et al. [2021] to multi-agent settings, showing that the standard value-based gap objectives are inapplicable and introducing the Nash Imitation Gap instead. (2) It builds on the entropy-regularized identifiability results of Cao et al. [2021] and Rolland et al. [2022], demonstrating that full reward identifiability is impossible in general multi-agent games (unlike the single-agent case) but achievable under linear separability. (3) It extends the observation by Fu et al. [2021] that single NE observations are limited, formalizing this as a non-expressive feasible reward set (Proposition 3.4) and showing empirically (Fig. 2 vs. Fig. 3) that QRE observations yield significantly higher Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients between recovered and true rewards, while avoiding the emergence of spurious pure equilibria.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the single-agent feasible reward set framework of Metelli et al. (2021) and the entropy-regularized IRL identifiability results of Cao et al. (2021) and Rolland et al. (2022) to the multi-agent setting, where the key complication is equilibrium multiplicity. It builds on prior MAIRL work (Lin et al. 2014/2018, Fu et al. 2021, Goktas et al. 2024) but departs from it by showing that single Nash equilibrium observations yield a fundamentally uninformative feasible reward set, and by introducing entropy-regularized Markov games (QRE) as the solution to the equilibrium selection problem. The Nash Gap objective is adapted from multi-agent imitation learning (Tang et al. 2024, Ramponi et al. 2023), and the matrix-based NE characterization follows Lin et al. (2018).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first rigorous theoretical characterization of the feasible reward set in multi-agent IRL, establishing that the problem is fundamentally harder than its single-agent counterpart due to equilibrium multiplicity and the under-determined nature of the reward system. The result that reward identifiability is only achievable in the average-reward sense (or under linear separability) sets clear boundaries for what MAIRL can and cannot recover. Key open questions include removing the generative model assumption, designing algorithms that guarantee unique equilibrium recovery, and extending to offline or active exploration settings where multi-agent coverage requirements differ substantially from single-agent IRL.
