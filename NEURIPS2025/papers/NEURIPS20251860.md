## Problem

Language-conditioned RL agents struggle with task ambiguity: as the number of instructions grows, distinct tasks share overlapping linguistic expressions while similar tasks use divergent phrasings, causing the agent to conflate tasks and degrade in performance. The paper demonstrates empirically that vanilla offline RL (CQL) fails as instructions multiply and that simply scaling model size does not resolve this fundamental limitation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251860/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The agent receives a state observation (structured grid cells in BabyAI or first-person RGB frames in ALFRED), a natural-language instruction, and a trajectory-history embedding x_{t-1} computed by a sequence encoder over past state-action pairs to handle partial observability. It outputs a discrete categorical value distribution over M uniformly spaced atoms in [V_MIN, V_MAX], from which the Q-value is recovered as the expectation and the action is selected by argmax over that expectation.

## Method

DAIL combines two complementary mechanisms to improve task differentiability. First, a distributional language-guided policy replaces scalar Q-value estimation with a categorical value distribution (M discrete atoms), trained via KL divergence between the projected distributional Bellman update and the current estimate; this preserves distributional shape information that scalar expectations discard, enabling finer-grained task separation. Second, a trajectory-wise semantic alignment module encodes full trajectories with a sequence model and instructions with a language encoder, then maximizes their mutual information through an InfoNCE contrastive loss with positive pairs from the dataset and negative instructions sampled uniformly. A CQL regularization term handles offline distribution shift, and the total loss is L_Dist + λL_c + αL_CQL. Theoretically, the paper proves that distributional estimation requires fewer samples (n_dist) than value-based estimation (n_value) to achieve task-level semantic disambiguation with confidence 1−η, since the Wasserstein distance between return distributions is always at least as large as the absolute difference of their means.

## Results

On BabyAI SynthLoc out-of-distribution tasks, DAIL achieves 81.7% overall success rate, outperforming the best baseline CQL (72.6%) by 9.1 points, with the largest gain on the PutNext task (49.1% vs. 27.6% for CQL). On ALFRED GOTO validation, DAIL reaches 56.8% success rate and 50.3% path-length weighted success rate, surpassing the strongest baseline IQL (52.0% SR, 47.0% PLW SR). The toy Minigrid experiment further shows that vanilla CQL degrades sharply as the number of instructions grows beyond 16, while DAIL's distributional and alignment components maintain task discrimination.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DAIL outperforms all five baselines across every task category on both BabyAI and ALFRED. The most dramatic improvement is on BabyAI PutNext, where DAIL (49.1%) nearly doubles the best baseline CQL (27.6%) and GCBC (27.4%), indicating that task ambiguity is most severe for compositional instructions. On PickUp, DAIL (87.6%) leads GCBC (78.4%) by 9.2 points, while the RL baselines IQL and CQL trail further behind at 70.4% and 71.9%. On ALFRED validation, DAIL achieves 56.8% SR versus IQL's 52.0% and GRIF's 48.6%, with a 3.3-point margin over the best baseline. BC-Z consistently performs worst (57.9% overall on BabyAI, 43.0% SR on ALFRED validation), which the authors attribute to its contrastive objective being fragile under noisy or suboptimal data. The ablation confirms both the distributional policy and the semantic alignment module contribute independently, with their combination yielding the largest gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on distributional RL (Bellemare et al., 2017) and conservative offline RL (CQL, Kumar et al., 2020), combining them with contrastive trajectory-instruction alignment (InfoNCE, van den Oord et al., 2018) to address a specific gap in language-conditioned RL: task ambiguity. Prior language-conditioned RL and imitation learning methods (GCBC, BC-Z, GRIF) treat instructions as conditioning signals but do not formally characterize or bound the difficulty of distinguishing tasks as instruction count grows. DAIL departs from this by providing a sample-complexity theorem showing distributional estimation is strictly more sample-efficient than point-estimate Q-learning for task disambiguation, and by adding a trajectory-level semantic alignment objective that prior offline RL baselines lack.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The formalization of task ambiguity and the accompanying sample-complexity bound give practitioners a principled reason to prefer distributional value estimation in multi-task language-conditioned settings, beyond the empirical gains already known from distributional RL. The method's plug-in design (distributional policy + alignment loss + CQL regularization) makes it straightforward to integrate into existing language-conditioned pipelines. Key limitations remain: all experiments are in simulation (BabyAI, ALFRED), the theoretical analysis assumes offline data collected by an ε-optimal policy, and no real-robot validation is provided. Open questions include whether the sample-efficiency advantage persists in online or partially-observed settings, and how the method scales to thousands of instructions with limited per-task data.
