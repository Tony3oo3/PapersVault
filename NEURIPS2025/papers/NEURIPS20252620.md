## Problem

Chatbot Arena has become the dominant leaderboard for ranking LLMs, yet no prior work had systematically quantified how its operational policies—private variant testing with selective retraction, unequal data access, and mass model deprecation—distort Bradley-Terry-based rankings. This paper identifies and measures these systematic biases, showing that a handful of providers can inflate their scores through best-of-N selection, receive disproportionate shares of evaluation data, and that silent deprecations violate core BT model assumptions.

## Input / Output

The analysis consumes approximately 2M Chatbot Arena battles spanning 243 models from 42 providers over 16 months (Jan 2024–Apr 2025), supplemented by a three-month scrape of live arena battles to identify private variants, provider-side API logs, and leaderboard snapshots. Outputs include quantified Arena Score inflation from best-of-N selection (simulated and real-world), per-provider data access shares, fine-tuned 7B model win rates on ArenaHard under varying Arena-data proportions, and six concrete policy recommendations. A distinctive aspect is the use of the authors' own model submissions (identical and distinct Aya-Vision checkpoints) as controlled real-world probes of the Arena's scoring mechanism.

## Method

The paper combines theoretical analysis, large-scale empirical auditing, and controlled experiments. Theoretically, it proves (Theorem 1) that selecting the maximum of N i.i.d. skill estimators strictly inflates expected Arena Score relative to a single submission, violating the BT model's unbiased sampling assumption. Empirically, it scrapes live Arena battles to catalog private variants per provider (finding up to 27 for one provider), measures sampling-rate disparities (34% for top providers vs. 3.3% for smaller ones), and tracks 205 silently deprecated models. For the overfitting analysis, it fine-tunes a 7B Cohere Command base model with 0%, 30%, and 70% Arena-mix training data, then evaluates win rates on ArenaHard using GPT-4o as judge, showing a jump from 23.5% to 49.9% while MMLU scores slightly decline—evidence of distribution-specific overfitting. Simulations of BT rankings under shifting task distributions and sparse win graphs further demonstrate how deprecation fragments the comparison network and destabilizes rankings.

## Results

In a controlled fine-tuning experiment, increasing Chatbot Arena data in the training mix from 0% to 70% more than doubles win rates on ArenaHard against Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct: 23.5% (0_arena), 42.7% (30_arena), and 49.9% (70_arena), representing relative gains of 81.7% and 112.3% respectively. Data access is heavily skewed: the combined share of OpenAI, Google, Meta, and Anthropic is 62.8% of all Arena data (68× more than top academic labs), while 41 fully open-source models received only 8.9%. A real-world experiment with just two Aya-Vision-32B checkpoints produced a 37-point Arena score gap (1060 vs. 1097), and simulation shows that testing 20 private variants inflates the expected maximum Arena Score by ~50 points over a single submission.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an audit/critique paper rather than a method paper, so it does not compare a proposed method against external baselines. Instead, it contrasts internal configurations to isolate causal effects. The fine-tuning experiment compares three Arena-data proportions (0%, 30%, 70%) against the Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct baseline, showing monotonic ArenaHard gains but slight MMLU declines, indicating distribution-specific overfitting rather than general capability improvement. The private-testing analysis contrasts a single public submission against best-of-N strategies (N up to 50 in simulation, 27 observed in practice), demonstrating that selection bias inflates Arena Scores by 50–70 points. The data-access comparison contrasts proprietary providers (62.8% combined share, up to 34% daily sampling rate) against open-source models (8.9% for 41 models, 3.3% peak sampling rate), quantifying a 68× asymmetry. The deprecation analysis contrasts 47 officially deprecated models against 205 silently removed ones, with 64% of silent removals affecting open-weight/open-source models, undermining BT model transitivity assumptions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the long tradition of benchmarking critiques (Ethayarajh & Jurafsky 2020, Ruder 2021, Orr & Kang 2024) to the specific case of Chatbot Arena, the dominant live LLM evaluation platform. Theoretically, it builds on the Bradley-Terry model (Bradley & Terry 1952) and order statistics to formalize how best-of-N private submissions violate the unbiased sampling assumption, and on transitivity/connectivity properties to show how mass deprecation fragments the win graph. Empirically, it departs from prior leaderboard audits (Huang et al. 2025 on adversarial voting, Min et al. 2025 on vote rigging) by focusing on structural policy asymmetries—private testing, selective retraction, sampling rates, and data access—rather than user-level manipulation, and by combining a 16-month data audit with controlled real-world experiments (identical and distinct checkpoint submissions).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Chatbot Arena is the de facto standard for ranking LLMs, and this paper demonstrates that its scores are systematically inflated for a small set of providers through policy asymmetries that most participants are unaware of. The finding that 7.3–9% of prompts recur across months, combined with a 112% relative win-rate gain from arena-mix training data, challenges the core assumption that dynamic, user-driven benchmarks are immune to overfitting. Open questions include whether the 3-month scrape window (Jan–Mar 2025, coinciding with Llama 4 launch) generalizes to other periods, whether the single 7B-model fine-tuning experiment underestimates the overfitting potential at scale, and how adversarial voting (acknowledged but not studied) compounds these structural biases. The paper's six concrete recommendations (prohibit retraction, cap private variants, stratified deprecation, fair sampling, transparency reports, regular data releases) are actionable but depend on organizer adoption.
