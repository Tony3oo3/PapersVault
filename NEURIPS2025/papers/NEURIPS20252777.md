## Problem

Multimodal reasoning models generate extended reasoning chains that improve performance on complex tasks, yet consistently exhibit higher hallucination rates on perception-focused tasks than their non-reasoning counterparts. No prior work systematically quantifies how reasoning chain length modulates the trade-off between reasoning quality and visual grounding, nor provides a unified metric to capture this dynamic balance across varying generation lengths.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252777/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The evaluated models (Qwen2.5-VL-3B/7B backbones) take an RGB image and a text question as input and produce a reasoning chain followed by a final answer. RH-Bench comprises 1,000 samples split evenly between reasoning tasks (MathVision, MathVista, MMMU, ScienceQA) and perception tasks (MMhalu, MMVP, HallusionBench, VMCBench), each with both multiple-choice and open-ended variants. For open-ended perception questions, GPT-4o assigns a hallucination severity score from 0 to 6 (scores below 3 count as hallucinations), and the RH-AUC is computed from (reasoning_accuracy, hallucination_accuracy) pairs evaluated at multiple controlled reasoning lengths.

## Method

The paper introduces RH-AUC, a trapezoidal-rule area-under-curve metric computed over min-max-normalized (R_T, H_T) pairs at varying reasoning lengths T, where higher values indicate better reasoning-perception balance. To systematically vary reasoning length, the authors employ three control strategies—token budget forcing, test-time scaling, and a novel latent state steering method that computes per-layer direction vectors from the difference between average hidden states of long and short reasoning traces, then applies a scaling factor α ∈ [−0.15, 0.15] to steer generation length. Attention analysis across all decoder layers reveals that reasoning models allocate significantly less attention to visual tokens than non-reasoning baselines, and this deficit worsens as reasoning chains lengthen, providing a mechanistic explanation for the amplified hallucination. The authors further demonstrate that the reasoning-length–performance relationship is non-monotonic and task-specific, motivating the curve-based RH-AUC over single-point accuracy metrics.

## Results

On RH-Bench, Ocean-R1-7B (RL-only) achieves the highest RH-AUC of 0.63, followed by MM-R1-7B (0.57) and MM-Eureka-7B (0.55), while the weakest model LLM-R1-3B scores only 0.46. Perception accuracy ranges from 48.7% (LLM-R1-3B) to 63.3% (ThinkLite-VL-7B), and reasoning accuracy from 42.5% (Curr-ReFT-3B) to 54.0% (MM-Eureka-7B and MM-R1-7B). The paper demonstrates a non-monotonic relationship between reasoning length and performance: moderate reasoning depth yields optimal results, while both overly short (zerothink) and excessively long chains degrade accuracy. RL-only training consistently outperforms SFT+RL in RH-AUC (e.g., Ocean-R1-7B at 0.63 vs. R1-OneVision-7B at 0.46), and 7B models generally surpass their 3B counterparts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 9 multimodal reasoning models (all built on Qwen2.5-VL backbones) against their non-reasoning baselines across 10 benchmarks. All reasoning models show uniformly higher hallucination rates than their non-reasoning Qwen2.5-VL counterparts on perception benchmarks (MMVP, MMEval-Pro, VMCBench, Bingo, MMHAL-Bench), with the deficit consistent at both 3B and 7B scales. Among the 9 reasoning models, RL-only training (Ocean-R1, MM-R1, MM-Eureka, ThinkLite-VL) consistently achieves higher RH-AUC than SFT+RL training (R1-OneVision, OpenVLThinker, Curr-ReFT): Ocean-R1-7B (0.63) outperforms R1-OneVision-7B (0.46) by 0.17 RH-AUC despite using far less training data (83k vs. 157k). Within the 7B group, MM-R1-7B (0.57) and MM-Eureka-7B (0.55) lead on reasoning accuracy (54.0%) while maintaining strong perception scores, whereas R1-OneVision-7B, despite the largest training dataset (157k samples), achieves the lowest RH-AUC (0.46) among 7B models. The 3B models (LLM-R1, Curr-ReFT, Ocean-R1-3B) all score below 0.53 RH-AUC, confirming that model scale is a significant factor in the reasoning-hallucination balance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on hallucination in multimodal LLMs (MMVP, MMEval-Pro, HallusionBench) by introducing a length-dependent evaluation axis: rather than measuring hallucination at a single generation length, it tracks how perception accuracy evolves as reasoning chains are stretched or compressed. The length-control mechanism adapts latent space steering (Liu et al., ICLR 2025; Liu et al., 2023) from safety/behavior control to reasoning-length modulation, and incorporates test-time scaling (Muennighoff et al., 2025) as a complementary strategy. Conceptually, the paper departs from the dominant framing in multimodal reasoning literature—which treats longer chains as uniformly beneficial—by showing that the reasoning-perception trade-off is non-monotonic and task-dependent, a dimension prior benchmarks do not capture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As multimodal reasoning models (Ocean-R1, MM-Eureka, R1-OneVision, etc.) become the default for complex visual tasks, the finding that extended reasoning systematically erodes visual grounding is a practical warning for deployment: a model that excels at MathVista may simultaneously degrade on perception-critical applications. The RH-AUC metric and RH-Bench provide the first systematic tool for quantifying this trade-off, but the analysis is confined to the Qwen2.5-VL backbone, leaving open whether the attention-shift mechanism generalizes to other architectures (e.g., LLaVA, InternVL) or to video/audio modalities. A key open question is whether the reasoning-perception conflict can be resolved architecturally (e.g., through attention re-weighting during decoding) rather than merely measured.
