## Problem

Existing transferability estimation (TE) methods are primarily validated on homogeneous model collections (e.g., supervised CNNs) and rely on hand-crafted rules to simulate embedding space evolution, causing them to fail when generalizing to diverse architectures (ViTs) and pre-training strategies (contrastive, masked image modeling) that exhibit markedly different convergence behaviors. Additionally, most TE methods are designed exclusively for image classification and lack mechanisms to adapt to dense prediction tasks such as semantic segmentation.

## Input / Output

ITM consumes pre-extracted backbone embeddings (feature vectors from the penultimate layer of each pre-trained model on the downstream training set) and the corresponding class labels. During estimation, 4/5 of the training split is used to optimize the latent transferability mapping and task head, while the remaining 1/5 serves as the evaluation set for computing the final transferability score. The output is a single scalar score per model, from which a ranking is derived and compared against ground-truth fine-tuning accuracy via weighted Kendall's τ_w. The TE computation itself runs on CPU in ~8.4 seconds on average, excluding the shared ~738-second GPU feature extraction step.

## Method

ITM introduces a latent transferability variable z for each model-task pair, implicitly encoded through a learnable linear mapping f(·; W_z) that fuses z into each mini-batch of pre-trained embeddings to form a posterior condition Θ_j. A Divide-and-Conquer Variational Approximation (DVA) then partitions the full embedding space into K independent mini-batch subspaces, modeling each subspace's evolution toward pseudo-cluster centers (one-hot class vectors shifted by the initial feature statistics) via a closed-form linear recurrence E_j^(n+1) = (I − ηC)E_j^(n) + ηC·Ē_j, where C = Θ_jΘ_j^T/B. This deparametric formulation eliminates the need for per-batch iterative optimization of a mapping layer, replacing it with a fixed number of algebraic steps n (adaptively set based on the model's initial feature-to-cluster distance). The latent mapping W_z and a downstream task head h are jointly trained for 500 iterations using the task-specific objective (cross-entropy for classification), enabling ITM to generalize across architectures and extend to dense prediction tasks by swapping in a segmentation head.

## Results

ITM achieves an average weighted Kendall's τ_w of 0.61 across 10 downstream classification datasets, substantially outperforming the best prior method LogME (0.45) by a margin of 0.16. The method is stable across random seeds (0.60 ± 0.01 over 5 runs) and requires only 8.42 seconds average wall-clock time for the TE process (excluding shared feature extraction). On the semantic segmentation extension (CamVid and Cityscapes), ITM achieves τ_w of 0.61 and 0.72 respectively, while prior methods designed for classification fail to generalize.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ITM outperforms all seven baselines on every one of the 10 classification datasets, with the largest average gap over LogME (0.61 vs. 0.45 τ_w) and PED (0.61 vs. 0.21). The most pronounced individual wins are on Stanford Cars (0.61 vs. 0.36 for LogME) and DTD (0.77 vs. 0.61), where prior dynamic methods like PED and SFDA produce near-zero or negative correlations. In terms of efficiency, ITM (8.42 s) is slightly slower than LogME (1.93 s) and ETran (3.58 s) but far faster than NLeep (266.61 s) and PARC (41.27 s), while delivering 0.16–0.47 higher τ_w than those fast static methods. On the extended CNN-only benchmark (14 supervised models, Table 9), ITM still leads with 0.624 average τ_w, narrowly beating SA+LogME (0.593) and LogME (0.597), confirming that the gains are not solely due to architectural diversity. For semantic segmentation, ITM achieves τ_w of 0.61 (CamVid) and 0.72 (Cityscapes), whereas classification-oriented baselines such as PED, LogME, and SFDA cannot be directly applied, underscoring ITM's task-agnostic design.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the dynamic transferability estimation line (PED, LEAD, SA) that simulates embedding space evolution during fine-tuning, but departs fundamentally by replacing explicit hand-crafted mapping rules with an implicit latent variable z that captures model-task interaction, and by factorizing the full-space evolution into independent subspace posteriors via a divide-and-conquer variational approximation. The deparametric approximation (Eq. 5), which converts per-batch iterative optimization into a closed-form linear recurrence, is a key technical departure from prior dynamic methods that require explicit simulation of the entire embedding trajectory. The work also extends TE beyond the supervised-CNN regime that dominated prior benchmarks, incorporating ViTs trained with contrastive (DINO, MoCo) and masked image modeling (MAE, SimMIM) strategies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As the pre-trained model zoo grows in architectural and training-strategy diversity, reliable model selection without exhaustive fine-tuning becomes increasingly critical for practical deployment. ITM's demonstrated generalization across CNNs, ViTs, and multiple pre-training paradigms—along with its extension to semantic segmentation where prior TE methods fail—makes it a more broadly applicable tool. However, the authors acknowledge that ITM still relies on final output features and cannot yet handle detection, vision-language, or parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) scenarios, leaving open the question of how to incorporate intermediate representations or predict performance under LoRA/prompt-tuning-style adaptation.
