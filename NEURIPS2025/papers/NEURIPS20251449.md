## Problem

NVIDIA's Blackwell architecture natively supports 4-bit floating-point (MXFP4) matrix multiplications with near-2× throughput over FP8, yet no existing algorithm can train LLMs end-to-end in this precision without significant accuracy degradation or mixed-precision fallbacks. The paper asks whether native FP4 training can be made 'optimal' on the accuracy-versus-computation trade-off, and if so, what algorithmic design achieves it.

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard LLM pre-training inputs: text tokens from the C4 dataset fed into Llama-2-style Transformer architectures (30M–7B non-embedding parameters). The output is a fully trained LLM whose linear-layer weights, activations, and gradients are all represented and computed in MXFP4 (1 sign + 1 mantissa + 2 exponent bits per value, with a shared 8-bit E8M0 scale per 32-element group). A key structural constraint is that the forward pass applies a fixed Hadamard rotation before quantization, while the backward pass applies a randomized Hadamard rotation, making the two passes use different transforms on the same group size g=32.

## Method

Quartet combines four ingredients into a single training pipeline. (1) A scaling-law framework decomposes each quantized method's impact into a parameter-efficiency factor eff_N (forward pass) and a data-efficiency factor eff_D (backward pass), enabling principled comparison and identification of the optimal precision under a compute budget. (2) The forward pass uses QuEST: a fixed block-wise Hadamard transform followed by RMSE-optimal clipping and round-to-nearest quantization to MXFP4, minimizing forward MSE. (3) The backward pass uses round-to-nearest (RTN) quantization with a 3/4 rescaling factor and a randomized Hadamard transform, balancing quadratic error against gradient-magnitude misalignment to preserve data efficiency. (4) A two-stage CUDA kernel implementation on Blackwell fuses the Hadamard transform, quantization, scale computation, and QuEST mask generation into a single Stage-1 kernel (using CUTLASS multilevel tiling and PTX FP4 downcast instructions), followed by a Stage-2 block-scaled GEMM kernel leveraging the tcgen05.mma instructions, achieving up to 2× speedup over FP8 on the forward pass and 1.6× on training overall.

## Results

On C4 validation loss for 30M-parameter Llama models, Quartet achieves the lowest loss across all data-to-parameter ratios (25×–400×), reaching 3.29 at 100× D/N compared to 3.66 for the strongest prior method (LUQ-INT4) and 6.55–6.76 for Jetfire-FP4 and HALO-FP4. Quartet's fitted efficiency coefficients (eff_N = 0.65, eff_D = 0.95) indicate it requires roughly 15% fewer parameters and 5× less data than LUQ-INT4 to reach the same loss. On the 7B model, Quartet achieves 17.77 PPL on C4, outperforming the QuaRot PTQ baseline (18.19) while being closer to the BF16 reference (16.40). Kernel-level speedups on RTX 5090 reach up to 2× over FP8 and 4× over BF16 on the forward pass, yielding an overall training speedup of ~1.6× over FP8.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Quartet decisively outperforms all four fully-quantized 4-bit training baselines on C4 validation loss. Against LUQ (Chmiel et al.), the strongest prior method, Quartet improves relative loss by ~10% at 100× D/N (3.29 vs. 3.66), with the gap widening at higher data budgets; LUQ-FP4 is substantially worse (4.88 at 100×). Jetfire (Xi et al.) and HALO (Ashkboos et al.) both become unstable when ported to FP4, producing losses 2–3× higher than Quartet and failing to converge reliably. LSS (Xi et al.) is competitive only at 50× D/N (3.40 vs. Quartet's 3.38) but diverges to NaN at all longer training budgets. On the 7B model, Quartet (17.77 PPL) also beats the QuaRot post-training quantization baseline (18.19 PPL) by 0.42 points, while remaining closer to the BF16 reference (16.40) than QuaRot does.

## Relation to Prior Work

Quartet composes two prior techniques—QuEST's Hadamard-plus-RMSE-clipping forward quantization [37] and Tseng et al.'s RTN-with-rescaling backward quantization [47]—into a single end-to-end MXFP4 pipeline, adding a novel error-bias trade-off analysis that justifies the specific forward/backward rounding choices. It departs from prior 4-bit training work (LUQ, JetFire, HALO, LSS) by targeting the hardware-native MXFP4 format on Blackwell rather than custom or INT4 formats, and by introducing a scaling-law-based framework for comparing quantized training methods via parameter and data efficiency factors. The GPU implementation is the first to fuse Hadamard transforms, quantization, and block-scaled GEMMs for microscaling formats on Blackwell's 5th-gen Tensor Cores.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first demonstration that native FP4 training can be 'optimal' on the accuracy-vs-computation trade-off for LLMs in the 100M–7B parameter range, with a ~2× speedup over FP8 on Blackwell hardware, making it a practically relevant path toward halving training energy costs. The scaling-law comparison framework (eff_N, eff_D) offers a reusable methodology for evaluating future quantized training schemes beyond MXFP4. Key limitations include hardware specificity (Blackwell/MXFP4 only), validation primarily on C4 with models up to 7B, and the open question of whether the approach generalizes to other microscaling formats (NVFP4) or distributed multi-node training at frontier scale.
