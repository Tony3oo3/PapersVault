## Problem

Split conformal prediction provides finite-sample coverage guarantees only when calibration and test data are exchangeable, a condition routinely violated by distribution shifts in practice. Existing remedies for non-exchangeability require prior knowledge of the shift type (e.g., covariate vs. label shift) or access to labeled test data, leaving no principled, broadly applicable solution for arbitrary, unknown distribution shifts. This paper asks whether optimal transport can both quantify and mitigate the resulting coverage gap using only unlabeled test samples.

## Input / Output

The method consumes n labeled calibration samples from the source distribution P, m unlabeled test samples from the shifted distribution Q, and a pretrained model that outputs a nonconformity score (1 − predicted probability) for every candidate label. Crucially, for unlabeled test points the method exploits the full vector of scores across all classes to construct auxiliary score distributions, rather than requiring the true label. It produces (i) a set of learned weights over the calibration samples, (ii) prediction sets with improved coverage under shift, and (iii) a computable upper bound on the total coverage gap (integrated over all target coverage rates α ∈ [0,1]).

## Method

The paper defines the total coverage gap as the expected gap over all α and derives two upper bounds expressed in terms of the 1-Wasserstein distance (or equivalently a weighted CDF distance) between calibration and test score distributions. The key theoretical contribution is a label-free bound (Theorem 3.3) that replaces the unknown test score distribution with two auxiliary distributions constructed from unlabeled test data—either the min/max scores per instance or scores sampled from the model's predictive vs. a uniform label distribution—linked via stochastic dominance. In practice, the bound is minimized with respect to calibration weights using gradient descent: the weighted empirical CDF is differentiable in the weights after sorting, and a Gaussian KDE estimates the calibration score density, yielding an O((m+n) log(m+n)) per-iteration cost. Two weight parametrizations are offered—free-form per-sample weights and a learnable score-to-weight function—both optimized via softmax-normalized gradients, with the weighted-CDF variant preferred for image classification and the 1-Wasserstein variant for regression.

## Results

On ImageNet-C, uncorrected split conformal prediction drops from the 90% target coverage to ~80% at severity level 1 and ~30% at severity level 5. The proposed OT-weighted methods (both (min,max) and (f,U) variants) recover coverage substantially across all severity levels, achieving near-target coverage even under combined covariate and label shift. On iWildCam, the (f,U) variant improves coverage by almost 10% over the uncorrected baseline, while the (min,max) variant improves it by more than 7%. Both free-form and weight-function parametrizations yield very similar performance, with the weight-function approach performing slightly better on iWildCam and the free-form variant stronger on mild ImageNet-C shifts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against learned likelihood ratios (Tibshirani et al. [42]), the proposed OT methods clearly win on both datasets: LR provides only modest coverage improvements over the uncorrected baseline on both ImageNet-C and iWildCam, while the OT-weighted methods recover near-target coverage. Against ECP (Kasa et al. [21]), the comparison is dataset-dependent: ECP achieves almost perfect coverage on iWildCam even under label shift, but on ImageNet-C it only produces competitive results in the absence of label shift and degrades significantly at higher severity levels, whereas the OT methods remain robust across all shift types. Against Gibbs et al. [15] (conditional conformal prediction), the OT methods achieve comparable or better coverage on ImageNet-C with dramatically smaller prediction sets (e.g., ~3.3 vs. ~424–548 classes at severity 5), and on iWildCam the Gibbs method actually hurts coverage, likely due to class imbalance or mismatch with the assumed RBF kernel shift class. Overall, the OT methods offer the best trade-off between coverage recovery and prediction set compactness across the range of distribution shifts tested.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the non-exchangeable conformal prediction line initiated by Tibshirani et al. (likelihood-ratio reweighting for covariate shift) and Barber et al. (data-independent weights for arbitrary shifts) by replacing their need for prior knowledge of the shift type with a learned reweighting derived from optimal transport bounds. The key conceptual departure is framing the coverage gap through the 1-Wasserstein distance between score distributions and, crucially, constructing stochastic-dominance auxiliary distributions from unlabeled test scores (min/max or model-predicted vs. uniform) to obtain label-free bounds. This positions the work as a principled, shift-agnostic alternative to both the likelihood-ratio approach and the entropy-scaled heuristic of Kasa et al. (ECP), while sharing the weighted-empirical-distribution machinery of Barber et al.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a broadly applicable, label-free recipe for mitigating arbitrary distribution shifts in split conformal prediction, which is increasingly relevant as CP is deployed in safety-critical and real-world settings where exchangeability is routinely violated. A key limitation is that the unlabeled bounds depend on auxiliary distributions that may be loose under general shifts, and the authors caution that in low-shift regimes the method can underperform the uncorrected baseline. Open questions include how to construct tighter auxiliary distributions for regression (where scores are not restricted to a finite set), whether the approach extends to other CP variants (e.g., weighted or adaptive CP), and how the learned weights interact with the finite-sample coverage guarantee when exchangeability is already broken.
