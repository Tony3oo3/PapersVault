## Problem

Existing multi-objective Bayesian optimization (MOBO) methods either rely on scalarization techniques that fail to capture diverse Pareto fronts, cannot scale beyond three objectives, or incur prohibitive computational cost in batch settings. The paper addresses the gap of generating a well-distributed set of Pareto optimal solutions under a limited evaluation budget while supporting both sequential and batch optimization and scaling to an arbitrary number of objectives.

## Input / Output

The method takes a vector-valued black-box objective function f: X→Y with M objectives (M from 2 to 6 in experiments), a total evaluation budget T, a batch size b, and a number of weight vectors n_β (default 20) that controls the density of search directions. It produces a Pareto set P_s of D-dimensional input points and the corresponding Pareto front P_f in M-dimensional objective space, evaluated via the hypervolume indicator against a problem-specific reference point. All objective values are assumed non-negative (achieved by offsetting the current worst observed value per objective), and observations are assumed noiseless.

## Method

MOBO-OSD adapts the Normal Boundary Intersection (NBI) geometric principle to the MOBO setting by first approximating the convex hull of individual minima (CHIM) using M boundary points constructed from the ideal and nadir points of the observed dataset, then generating well-distributed orthogonal search directions (OSDs) via Riesz s-Energy minimization over the unit simplex. For each OSD, a constrained subproblem (Eq. 2) is solved using SLSQP with multiple starting points to find the intersection between the search line and the GP posterior mean surface, with constraints ensuring the projected mean lies within a δσ confidence band of the line. A Pareto Front Estimation step (First Order Approximation) then locally explores around each OSD solution to generate additional candidates without requiring more subproblems. Batch selection uses the Hypervolume Improvement acquisition function on the posterior mean, combined with Kriging Believer re-training and a constraint that limits selections from any single exploration space to at most one more than any other, ensuring spatial diversity across the batch.

## Results

MOBO-OSD is evaluated on 9 benchmark problems (5 synthetic: DTLZ2-M2/M3/M4, ZDT1, VLMOP2; 4 real-world: Speed Reducer, Car Side Design, Marine Design, Water Planning) with 2–6 objectives, using logarithmic hypervolume difference as the metric. Across all benchmarks and both sequential (batch size 1) and batch (batch sizes 4, 8, 10) settings, MOBO-OSD consistently achieves the highest hypervolume among all 8 baselines. The ablation study (Table 1) shows that with PFE, MOBO-OSD reaches HV of 0.4217 on DTLZ2-M2, 0.3383 on VLMOP2, and 177.48 on Car Side Design, compared to 0.4041, 0.2713, and 145.12 respectively without PFE at n_β=20.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MOBO-OSD outperforms all 8 baselines across all 9 benchmark problems in both sequential and batch settings. qEHVI is the strongest baseline, showing competitive performance in most cases but eventually converging to suboptimal solutions; it also suffers from prohibitively high computational cost (exceeding 12 hours, 64 GB memory) for M≥4 objectives with batch sizes 8–10, limiting its iterations. DGEMO is the second-strongest baseline but is outperformed by MOBO-OSD on VLMOP2 and Speed Reducer, and is architecturally limited to M≤3 objectives. qParEGO (using Tchebychev scalarization) and USeMO are consistently outperformed, confirming the advantage of the OSD subproblem formulation over scalarization-based approaches. PDBO and JES (information-theoretic) are also outperformed; JES is only compared in the sequential setting due to its prohibitive computational cost. NSGA-II, the evolutionary baseline, generally cannot compete with BO-based methods under the limited evaluation budgets used. NBI, the classical geometric method, is not sample-efficient and often depletes the entire budget before completing even the first round of subproblems.

## Relation to Prior Work

MOBO-OSD adapts the Normal Boundary Intersection (NBI) geometric framework—originally designed for large-budget multi-objective optimization—to the limited-evaluation-budget MOBO setting by approximating the convex hull of individual minima (CHIM) from observed data and formulating relaxed constrained subproblems along orthogonal search directions. It departs from the dominant scalarization-based MOBO approaches (ParEGO, USEMO, PDBO) and HV-based acquisition function methods (qEHVI, DGEMO) by operating directly on a multi-objective acquisition function (Hypervolume Improvement) over candidates generated through deterministic, data-driven search directions in the objective space rather than random or stochastic search. The Pareto Front Estimation (First Order Approximation) component from Schulz et al. and the Kriging Believer batch strategy are incorporated as auxiliary mechanisms for diversity and parallel evaluation, respectively.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MOBO-OSD addresses a key scalability gap in MOBO: unlike DGEMO (limited to M≤3) and qEHVI (prohibitively expensive for M≥4 in batch settings), it scales to an arbitrary number of objectives with manageable computational cost, making it relevant for many-objective problems in engineering and design. The noiseless observation assumption limits direct applicability to real-world noisy settings, and the reliance on GP surrogate models constrains its use with non-Gaussian or multi-fidelity objectives. Open questions include whether the OSD subproblem formulation can be extended to handle observation noise, whether the approach integrates naturally with multi-fidelity or decoupled MOO settings, and whether the geometric CHIM approximation remains effective when the true Pareto front is highly non-convex or disconnected.
