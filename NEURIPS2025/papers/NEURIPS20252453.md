## Problem

Despite the sophistication of frontier LLMs, there is no simple, scalable, black-box automated red-teaming method that uniformly applies across text, vision, and audio modalities with predictable scaling behavior. Prior jailbreak techniques are either modality-specific, require white-box access (gradients or logprobs), or lack a principled way to estimate how much additional compute an adversary would need to succeed. This paper asks whether a straightforward repeated-sampling strategy with modality-specific augmentations can reliably jailbreak all frontier models and whether its success rate follows a predictable scaling law.

## Input / Output

The method consumes 159 direct harmful requests from the HarmBench standard test set, rendered in three modalities: plain text prompts, images containing typographic text on randomized colored-block backgrounds (dimensions 240–600 px, 104 fonts, random RGB text color and position), and audio waveforms of human-vocalized requests (≈30–40 s each, 774 utterances). The target LLM produces a text response, which is classified for harmfulness by GPT-4o using the HarmBench grader prompt; a jailbreak is counted if the response contains information relevant to the harmful request, even if incomplete. All classifier-flagged outputs undergo manual human review to eliminate false positives.

## Method

BoN Jailbreaking repeatedly applies randomly sampled combinations of modality-specific augmentations to a harmful request and queries the target LLM until a harmful response is elicited or a maximum sample budget N is reached. For text, augmentations include character scrambling (probability 0.6 on middle characters of words >3 chars), random capitalization (p=0.6), and ASCII character noising (p=0.06); for images, font, scale, color, position, and background blocks are randomized; for audio, a six-dimensional Gaussian-sampled vector controls speed, pitch, volume, and background noise/music/speech SNR, applied in a fixed order. The key empirical finding is that ASR as a function of N follows a power law (−log ASR = aN^(−b)) across models and modalities, enabling accurate forecasting of attack success from 10× fewer samples (4.6% average error). The paper further shows that composing BoN with optimized prefix attacks (PrePAIR) or many-shot jailbreaking (MSJ) improves sample efficiency by up to 28× for text, 68× for vision, and 250× for audio, and that the method's effectiveness stems from exploiting input-space variance and sampling stochasticity rather than targeting specific model weaknesses.

## Results

BoN Jailbreaking achieves 78% ASR on Claude 3.5 Sonnet and 89% on GPT-4o with N=10,000 text augmentations, and 37% ASR on the more robust o1 reasoning model at N=5,000. Across vision and audio modalities, BoN reaches 56% ASR on GPT-4o (vision) and 72% on GPT-4o Realtime API (audio) at N=7,200. ASR follows power-law-like scaling in N, enabling accurate forecasting (4.6% average error) of performance at 10× more samples. Composing BoN with prefix jailbreaks (PrePAIR/MSJ) improves sample efficiency by up to 28× for text, 68× for vision, and 250× for audio.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BoN outperforms PAIR (Chao et al., 2023) by 33% ASR on average across text models and by 22% on vision models, and outperforms TAP (Mehrotra et al., 2023) by 39% ASR on average across text models. BoN beats both baselines on all tested models except o1, where the deliberative alignment provides enough robustness that the gap narrows. In the audio modality, BoN exceeds PAIR by 15% ASR. The paper also shows that removing BoN from composed attacks causes dramatic ASR drops: PrePAIR alone loses 4.2× ASR on GPT-4o (97%→23%), 41× on Gemini Pro, and MSJ alone loses 14× on Claude 3.5 Sonnet (89%→6%), confirming that the repeated-sampling component is essential to the method's effectiveness.

## Relation to Prior Work

BoN Jailbreaking sits at the intersection of several prior lines of work: it shares the repeated-sampling philosophy of decoding-variation attacks (Huang et al., 2023) and input-fuzzing approaches (Yu et al., 2024), but deliberately avoids the optimization machinery of PAIR/TAP (Chao et al., 2023; Mehrotra et al., 2023) and gradient-based or log-probability-based methods (Zou et al., 2023; Andriushchenko et al., 2024). The paper's PrePAIR component explicitly extends PAIR by restricting edits to a prefix and optimizing for universality across requests, while the MSJ composition builds on Anil et al.'s (2024) many-shot jailbreaking. The most distinct conceptual contribution is the discovery of power-law scaling in ASR as a function of sample count N, connecting jailbreak search to the broader inference-time compute scaling literature (Snell et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024a) in a way no prior jailbreak paper has done.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The power-law scaling finding is the most consequential result: it implies that BoN's effectiveness is unbounded in principle and predictably improves with compute, giving defenders a concrete (and alarming) scaling curve to plan against rather than a fixed vulnerability. The low per-jailbreak reliability (~20–30% on resampling) reframes the defense problem from 'block this specific prompt' to 'reduce the tail probability of harmful outputs across a high-dimensional input space,' which is fundamentally harder for stochastic systems. Open questions include whether guard models or cluster-based account banning can flatten the power-law slope, whether the scaling holds at N >> 10,000 without classifier false-positive interference, and whether optimized augmentations (ciphers, SVGs, rewording) can steepen the curve further. The practical accessibility—$9 for 50% ASR on GPT-4o—makes this a near-term operational concern rather than a theoretical one.
