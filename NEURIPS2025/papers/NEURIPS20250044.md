## Problem

Existing reasoning benchmarks for LLMs suffer from three critical flaws: performance saturation on oversimplified tasks (e.g., DeepSeek-R1 reaches 97.3% on MATH-500), data contamination from publicly available problems that models may have memorized during pretraining, and flawed evaluation items with ambiguous phrasing or incorrect ground-truth answers. These deficiencies make it difficult to reliably distinguish between models' genuine reasoning capabilities and their pattern-matching or memorization abilities.

## Input / Output

Each problem is a text-only physics scenario (no multimodal inputs) requiring the model to derive a single symbolic expression for a target physical quantity using only the given conditions. The model's final answer must be a readable LaTeX formula inside a \boxed{} environment; equations, floating-point approximations, or multiple expressions are rejected. Scoring produces two outputs: a binary accuracy score (100/0 via SymPy equivalence checking) and a continuous EED Score (0–100) that awards partial credit for structurally similar but incorrect expressions.

## Method

The core technical contribution is the Expression Edit Distance (EED) Score, a model-free metric that converts both the ground-truth and model-generated LaTeX expressions into SymPy canonical forms, builds regularized expression trees, and computes a relative edit distance r using an extended Zhang-Shasha algorithm. The score is mapped as: 100 for exact match (r=0), 60−100r for 0<r<0.6, and 0 for r>0.6, thereby acknowledging partial correctness for minor coefficient or structural errors. The algorithm is further extended with subtree insertion and deletion operations whose cost is discounted to 60% of the standard per-node cost for subtrees exceeding five nodes, allowing the metric to efficiently align expressions that share large physical components (e.g., a correct dipole term alongside an incorrect external-field term). This design improves sample efficiency by 204% over binary scoring, making 500 problems under EED equivalent to roughly 1500 problems under accuracy scoring. The benchmark itself comprises 500 original problems curated through a multi-stage pipeline involving 178 contributors, LLM-assisted ambiguity detection, and validation by 81 human experts (50 of whom are Chinese Physics Olympiad gold medalists), ensuring novelty, solvability, and unambiguous scoring.

## Results

On PHYBench (500 original physics problems), the best-performing model Gemini 2.5 Pro achieves only 36.9% accuracy and 49.5 EED Score, well below the human expert baseline of 61.9% accuracy and 70.4 EED Score. DeepSeek-R1 scores 25.0% accuracy (37.8 EED), while 32B distilled models (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B, QwQ-32B) drop to 0.7% and 1.6% accuracy respectively. The EED Score improves sample efficiency by 204% (σ=80%) over binary accuracy scoring, making 500 problems under EED equivalent to ~1500 problems under binary scoring.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PHYBench is substantially harder than all four comparison benchmarks: DeepSeek-R1 scores 25.0% on PHYBench versus 97.3% on MATH-500, 71.5% on GPQA, 58.7% on OlympiadBench, and 79.8% on AIME 2024. PHYBench also demands the longest reasoning chains, with an average of 10,636 output tokens—nearly 6× MATH-500 (1,857) and ~1.4× AIME 2024 (7,741). The gap between reasoning and non-reasoning models is much larger on PHYBench than on other datasets, making it more effective at distinguishing reasoning capacity. The EED Score provides a continuous 0–100 metric that yields lower statistical uncertainty than binary accuracy (e.g., CV of 3.47% vs 5.38% for Gemini 2.5 Pro), enabling statistically significant pairwise model separations that binary scoring cannot resolve at n=500.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of reasoning benchmarks (MATH-500, GPQA, OlympiadBench, AIME 2024) by addressing three identified limitations: task oversimplification, data contamination, and insufficient verification. Methodologically, the EED Score builds on the Zhang-Shasha tree edit distance algorithm, extending it with subtree insertion/deletion operations and a cluster-editing discount to handle multi-component physical expressions. The chain-of-thought poisoning protocol for probing reasoning robustness is a novel evaluation methodology not present in prior benchmark work, drawing conceptually on adversarial perturbation ideas but applied specifically to intermediate reasoning steps.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PHYBench provides a contamination-free, rigorously verified benchmark that reveals a substantial gap (36.9% vs. 61.9%) between the best LLM and human experts, suggesting current models have not yet achieved robust physical reasoning. The error analysis localizing >90% of failures to semantic reasoning (introducing incorrect physical equations) rather than symbolic manipulation reframes where future model improvements should focus. The EED Score's 204% sample efficiency gain offers a practical tool for more reliable model comparison. Open questions include whether the EED Score can be extended to evaluate intermediate reasoning steps, and whether models can develop genuine physical understanding beyond the compensatory strategies (dimensional analysis, limiting-case checks) identified in the perturbation experiments.
