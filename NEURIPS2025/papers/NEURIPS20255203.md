## Problem

Neural machine translation models suffer from a well-known miscalibration issue where hypothesis likelihood is poorly correlated with actual translation quality, causing MAP decoding (e.g., beam search) to select suboptimal or even pathological translations. Existing remedies such as Best-of-N sampling and MBR decoding address this gap at inference time but incur prohibitive latency, as demonstrated by the WMT24-winning Tower system requiring 100× sampling from a 70B model. The paper asks whether this calibration gap can be closed directly during training with minimal data, without resorting to expensive test-time search.

## Input / Output

During training, the method takes a source sentence paired with a language-pair instruction prompt and samples k hypotheses from the target LLM via nucleus sampling (k=16 off-policy using GPT-4o-mini, k=5 on-policy from the model itself). Each hypothesis is scored by both the model's log-likelihood and an external quality metric (CometKiwi-XXL by default), and the resulting (likelihood, quality) pairs drive the calibration loss. At inference, the calibrated model produces a single translated sentence via standard beam search (beam size 5), and its average log-likelihood additionally serves as a reference-free quality estimate that rivals or surpasses dedicated QE models like CometKiwi.

## Method

The core loss is the negative Pearson correlation between the log-likelihoods z_θ(y_i|x) and external quality scores q(y_i|x) of k sampled hypotheses per source sentence, computed under the nucleus-induced distribution and minimized with Adam. An SFT regularizer on the highest-scoring hypothesis is added to anchor the likelihood scale, since Pearson alone enforces correlation without constraining absolute values. Training uses LoRA (rank 8) on translation-specialized LLMs (Tower-Base, ALMA-Base) with only ~2K source sentences per direction from Flores-200. The key distinction from preference optimization methods (CPO, DPO) is that the objective optimizes correlation rather than expected reward, making it inherently shared with metric meta-evaluation and thus unifying quality optimization and estimation in a single model. The Pearson formulation reduces to a dot product of mean-centered, ℓ2-normalized vectors, making it differentiable, scale-invariant, and computationally cheap.

## Results

On WMT24 en→xx translation (9 language pairs), calibrating TowerInstruct-Mistral-7B yields an average of 73.9 CometKiwi-XL and 89.4 XCOMET, improving over the baseline by +2.8 and +2.7 points respectively. The calibrated TowerBase-13B (73.8 KIWI-XL, 89.3 XCOMET) matches or exceeds the much larger Tower-70B-v2+MBR/TRR system (which requires 100× sampling) while being ~200× faster. In human evaluation (en→zh, 200 samples), the calibrated model achieves a mean score of 5.49/6 with a 106-win vs. 32-loss record over the baseline. The calibrated model's log-likelihood also serves as a quality estimator, surpassing CometKiwi in Spearman correlation on WMT22 QE.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GPT-4o (Hendy et al., 2023), the calibrated Mistral-7B model wins by +4.1 KIWI-XL and +2.8 XCOMET on average, and the calibrated 13B model extends this gap further. Against the state-of-the-art Tower-70B-v2+MBR/TRR system (Rei et al., 2024), the calibrated TowerBase-13B achieves comparable KIWI-XL scores (e.g., 72.5 vs. 72.3 on en→de, 73.6 vs. 74.2 on en→ru) while being approximately 200× faster since it uses single-pass beam search instead of 100× sampling. Against CPO (Xu et al., 2024), calibration consistently wins by +1.0 KIWI-XL and +0.8 XCOMET on the Mistral-7B base, and by +1.9 KIWI-XL and +2.2 XCOMET on the 13B base. On the ALMA base model (Xu et al., 2023), calibration outperforms both DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) and CPO by +0.5 COMET, +1.4 KIWI-XXL, and +2.0 XCOMET on out-of-English translation. On human-annotated MAPLE data (Zhu et al., 2024), calibration beats the Plackett-Luce preference baseline by +0.8 COMET on average (83.4 vs. 82.6 for Mistral-Instruct; 87.8 vs. 86.8 for TowerMistral).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the calibration framing introduced by Ott et al. (2018), who identified the beam search curse and the weak correlation between hypothesis likelihood and translation quality. It departs from two main lines of prior work: (1) test-time quality-aware decoding methods (MBR, Best-of-N sampling) that add expensive inference-time computation, and (2) preference optimization methods (DPO, CPO) that maximize expected rewards under Bradley-Terry or Plackett-Luce frameworks. The key conceptual departure is optimizing Pearson correlation between likelihood and quality at training time rather than maximizing expected reward or performing test-time reranking, which the authors argue unifies quality optimization and quality estimation under a single differentiable objective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: a 7B calibrated model with beam search matches the 70B Tower-70B-v2+MBR/TRR system while being approximately 200× faster, using only 2K training instances per direction. The unification of quality optimization and estimation—where the model's own log-likelihood becomes a competitive QE signal surpassing CometKiwi—offers a parsimonious framework that could reduce the need for separate QE models in deployment pipelines. Open questions include whether the approach extends to exact MAP decoding (computationally intractable), substantially larger beam sizes, and language pairs beyond the base model's coverage; the authors also note fairness concerns inherited from the underlying LLMs.
