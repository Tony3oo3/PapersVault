## Problem

Existing theoretical analyses of multi-modal contrastive learning (CLIP) are limited to linear representations under specific factor-model data distributions, or to alignment-uniformity frameworks that require the true data dimension to be correctly specified in advance. This paper asks: what statistical properties do learned representations actually possess when using general (nonlinear) function classes and general data distributions, and in particular, can the infoNCE loss with temperature optimization automatically discover the intrinsic dimension of shared latent variables without any prior dimension specification?

## Input / Output

The method takes paired multi-modal observations (X ∈ R^{d₁}, Y ∈ R^{d₂}) from arbitrary distributions and produces two representation maps f: R^{d₁} → R^d and g: R^{d₂} → R^d (implemented as 5-layer ReLU networks with width 50) along with an optimized temperature τ. The similarity measure is the inner product with population-level normalization, σ(f(X), g(Y)) = ⟨f(X), g(Y)⟩ / (E‖f(X)‖ · E‖g(Y)‖). Although the user specifies an output dimension d, the learned representations are shown to concentrate on a lower-dimensional manifold of intrinsic dimension k* < d, which is estimated empirically via MLE-based intrinsic dimension estimation (skdim.id package).

## Method

The core technical contribution is a variational decomposition of the population infoNCE loss into −2·I(f(X); g(Y)) plus two KL divergence terms (Lemma 8), where the KL terms measure the gap between the true joint distribution and its kernel-smoothed versions parameterized by temperature τ. To handle the unboundedness of mutual information for continuous aligned representations, the authors introduce a discretization framework over the representation space and define near-minimizers relative to a discretized mutual information benchmark I_M*(H). The key insight is that temperature optimization (τ → 0) is what drives the KL divergence terms to zero, which simultaneously enforces similarity maximization (positive-pair similarities concentrate at a constant) and intrinsic dimension adaptation: representations with intrinsic dimension larger than k* cannot align (excess randomness prevents alignment), while those with smaller dimension cannot maximize mutual information. The main theorem (Theorem 2) proves that any minimizer of the infoNCE loss with optimized temperature satisfies ID(f) = ID(g) = k*, the intrinsic dimension of the shared latent variables, without requiring representations to be uniform over the full output space as in prior work.

## Results

On synthetic data with true intrinsic dimension k*=5 (d1=d2=20), the MLE-based estimated intrinsic dimension of learned representations saturates at approximately 5 once the output dimension d≥5, and both in-sample and out-of-sample top-1 matching accuracy plateau. On the CITE-seq bone marrow dataset (24-dim protein + 200-dim RNA), the estimated intrinsic dimension does not exceed 10 regardless of output dimension, and classification accuracy for two cell-type groups saturates when d>10. On ImageNetV2 (1024-dim ViT-L14 image embeddings + 768-dim text embeddings), the intrinsic dimension of image representations stabilizes at approximately 8 and classification accuracy over 67 coarse classes saturates near d=20. In all settings, the optimized temperature τ converges to zero and positive-pair similarities concentrate at a constant that dominates negative-pair similarities, consistent with the theoretical predictions of Theorem 2.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper that does not benchmark against competing representation-learning methods; instead, it validates its theoretical predictions (intrinsic dimension adaptation, temperature convergence, similarity concentration) against empirical observations across synthetic and real-world datasets. The key 'comparison' is between the user-specified output dimension d and the discovered intrinsic dimension: in every experiment, the effective dimensionality of learned representations is substantially lower than d (e.g., ID≈8 vs. d=20 on ImageNetV2; ID≤10 vs. d up to 29 on CITE-seq), confirming that CLIP automatically adapts to the intrinsic dimension k* without requiring prior knowledge of it. The paper also contrasts its theoretical framework with prior work: unlike Wang & Isola [79], it does not require representations to be uniform over the entire output space, and unlike mutual-information-maximization analyses [75, 76], it explains why representations settle on a specific-dimensional manifold rather than degenerating to curves or points. The empirical results on CITE-seq and ImageNetV2 demonstrate that the small number of effective shared features (≈8–10) is sufficient to capture downstream-relevant information, supporting the practical relevance of the intrinsic-dimension adaptation property.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the theoretical analysis of multi-modal contrastive learning beyond the restrictive assumptions of prior work. While Wang & Isola (2020) established the alignment-uniformity framework for single-modality contrastive learning, and subsequent multi-modal analyses (Nakada et al. 2023, Liu et al. 2024, Chen et al. 2023) were limited to factor models with shared latent variables and linear representations, this paper handles general distributions and general function classes. The key technical departure is incorporating temperature optimization into the analysis, which prior theoretical works either fixed or ignored, and using a variational decomposition of the infoNCE loss (building on van den Oord et al. 2018) to show that temperature is precisely what enables adaptation to intrinsic dimension without requiring prior knowledge of the true dimension.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result provides the first theoretical explanation for why CLIP naturally produces low-dimensional representations in practice, resolving a gap between the alignment-uniformity theory (which requires correct dimension specification) and observed behavior. The paper suggests a practical two-stage strategy: train with a large output dimension for expressiveness, then post-process to the discovered intrinsic dimension for faster inference. Open questions include characterizing the precise limit of optimized temperature in general settings, understanding finite-sample behavior, and relaxing the assumption that aligned representations with maximal mutual information exist in the function class. The theory also connects to sufficient dimension reduction, potentially unifying representation learning with classical statistical methodology.
