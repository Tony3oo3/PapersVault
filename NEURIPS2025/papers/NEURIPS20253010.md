## Problem

The standard prefix-forcing objective used in automated LLM jailbreaks (eliciting 'Sure, here is [harmful request]') suffers from two coupled failures: it is misspecified, because even when the target prefix is generated the model often self-corrects into incomplete or unfaithful responses, and it is overconstrained, because rigid handcrafted prefixes misalign with individual LLMs' natural response styles (e.g., Llama-3 prefers 'Here' over 'Sure'), stalling optimization. Additionally, existing jailbreak judges overestimate attack success rates by up to 30% on newer LLMs by miscounting these incomplete and unfaithful responses as successes.

## Input / Output

The method requires white-box access to the victim LLM's log probabilities and takes a malicious request as input. Prefix selection is a one-off offline cost (≈5 min per prefix on an A100 80G) that uses publicly available uncensored LLMs (not necessarily the uncensored version of the target) with guided decoding to generate diverse candidate prefixes, which are then evaluated and stored for reuse. The final output is an optimized adversarial prompt (suffix or full prompt) produced by plugging the selected multi-prefix objective into an existing attack such as GCG or AutoDAN.

## Method

AdvPrefix replaces the single rigid prefix with a set of model-dependent target prefixes selected by two complementary criteria: high prefilling ASR (the probability that completing the prefix yields a complete, faithful harmful response, approximated via a manual attack prompt) and low initial negative log-likelihood (ease of elicitation by the pre-optimization prompt). The resulting objective minimizes the negative log-sum-probability over all selected prefixes, computed efficiently in one forward pass via the tree attention trick. An automated pipeline generates candidates from uncensored LLMs with guided decoding, applies rule-based augmentation and filtering, scores each candidate on both criteria with a weighted sum, and selects the top-k prefixes (ensuring no prefix is a sub-prefix of another). The approach is plug-and-play: it drops into existing attacks like GCG and AutoDAN without modifying their optimization algorithms, and the released prefixes can be reused directly by other researchers.

## Results

Replacing GCG's default 'Sure, here is...' prefix with AdvPrefix's model-dependent prefixes dramatically improves nuanced attack success rates (ASR) across all four victim LLMs. On Llama-3-8B-Instruct, ASR jumps from 12.8% (original) to 54.6% (single-prefix) and 54.0% (multi-prefix); on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, it rises from 16.8% to 60.0% (multi-prefix). Under the less restrictive full-prompt optimization threat model (Table 2), GCG with AdvPrefix reaches 79.5% ASR on Llama-3 (vs. 14.1% original), and AutoDAN reaches 77.9% (vs. 5.2% original). The failure breakdown shows incomplete responses drop from ~20% to 1–2% and unfaithful responses are halved on Gemma-2, confirming the objective addresses both misspecification and overconstraint.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GCG with the original 'Sure, here is...' prefix-forcing objective (Zou et al., 2023), AdvPrefix achieves 3–5× higher nuanced ASR on newer LLMs: 12.8%→54.6% on Llama-3, 16.8%→60.0% on Llama-3.1, and 11.2%→42.0% on Gemma-2. Against AutoDAN with the original objective (Zhu et al., 2023), the improvement is even more dramatic under full-prompt optimization: 5.2%→77.9% on Llama-3 and 19.7%→36.0% on Gemma-2. On the evaluation side, the paper's refined judge outperforms all three existing judges (HarmBench, JailbreakBench, StrongReject) in human agreement rate, averaging 91.8% vs. 86.6%, 85.5%, and 89.3% respectively, with the largest gap on Llama-3 (93.0% vs. 84.5%/84.0%/90.0%). The paper also shows that existing judges overestimate ASR by up to 30% on newer LLMs (e.g., ~10% ground truth reported as ~40% on Gemma-2), while StrongReject is the most accurate of the three but still inferior to the proposed judge. Overall, AdvPrefix wins on every comparison, with the largest gains on the most recently aligned models (Llama-3, Llama-3.1, Gemma-2), suggesting current safety alignment fails to generalize to unseen prefixes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the GCG adversarial suffix optimization framework (Zou et al., 2023) and AutoDAN (Zhu et al., 2023), but shifts the focus from the optimization algorithm to the optimization *objective* itself. Rather than using a fixed, handcrafted prefix like 'Sure, here is...', it introduces an adaptive, model-dependent prefix selection process based on two criteria (high prefilling ASR and low initial NLL), leveraging the tree attention trick from Medusa (Cai et al., 2024) for efficient multi-prefix computation. The paper also departs from prior jailbreak evaluation by showing that standard judges (HarmBench, JailbreakBench, StrongReject) systematically overestimate attack success rates on newer LLMs, and proposes a refined nuanced evaluation that counts only complete and faithful responses as successful jailbreaks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that current safety alignment fails to generalize to even slightly different affirmative prefixes, with ASR jumping from ~10% to 70-80% on Llama-3 simply by changing the target prefix—poses a direct challenge to the robustness of deployed safety training. The plug-and-play design means any existing white-box jailbreak attack can benefit immediately, and the released prefixes lower the barrier for red-teaming. Key limitations include the white-box requirement, the computational cost of prefix selection (requiring a 70B judge), and the fact that the objective does not address loss landscape properties. An open question is whether safety alignment can be made to generalize across the space of possible affirmative prefixes rather than being brittle to specific token sequences.
