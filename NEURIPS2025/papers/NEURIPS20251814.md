## Problem

Existing LLM content moderators rely on full detection, judging harmfulness only after the complete output is generated, which introduces high service latency. Naive partial detection attempts to apply these full-detection-trained moderators to incomplete outputs midway through generation, but this creates a training-inference gap because the models were never trained to reason about incomplete semantics, resulting in late and unreliable stopping. The paper asks how to construct both the data (fine-grained token-level annotations) and the model (a native streaming detector) that close this gap.

## Input / Output

During inference, the SCM receives the user prompt and the LLM's autoregressive output stream token-by-token, fetching the latest token at each generation step in parallel with the monitored LLM. It produces a per-token harmfulness probability c_{t_i} at every step; when the cumulative count of tokens exceeding threshold θ reaches a configurable value k (Delay-k), an early-stopping signal terminates generation. The holistic scorer used during training is detached at inference, leaving only the feature extractor and token scorer as a lightweight plug-and-play module whose per-token latency is comparable to the generation latency of the same-scale model.

## Method

SCM is built on Qwen2.5 (0.5B–7B) with a feature extractor producing token representations h_i, a token scorer f_tok for per-token harmfulness, and a holistic scorer f_hol for response-level harmfulness. Training uses a three-component loss: binary cross-entropy on token-level labels (L_tok), binary cross-entropy on response-level labels (L_hol), and a propositional-logic consistency loss (L_logic) that penalizes the model when the holistic scorer predicts a response as harmful while all token scores remain benign (or vice versa), formalized as −log(1 − c_hol + c_hol · c_tok) with max-pooling over token scores. This hierarchical consistency-aware learning injects future response-level knowledge into every token's representation, enabling the model to anticipate potential harmfulness from incomplete prefixes. The FineHarm dataset (29K prompt-response pairs) provides token-level supervision via a POS-based heuristic that labels notional words (nouns, verbs, adjectives) within LLM-annotated harmful sentences as harmful, avoiding the need for costly human span annotation. At inference, the Delay-k mechanism lets service providers tune sensitivity: smaller k yields lower missing-alarm rate for strict scenarios (e.g., children's content), while larger k reduces false alarms for user-experience-sensitive applications.

## Results

SCM achieves 95%+ macro F1 with partial detection (early stopping) by seeing only the first 18% of response tokens on average, matching or exceeding full-detection performance at the same model scale. Specifically, SCM-0.5B reaches 95.64 macro F1 (vs. 95.74 for full Qwen2.5-0.5B), SCM-1.5B reaches 97.91 (vs. 98.19 full), and SCM-7B reaches 97.45 (vs. 92.75 full), with the 7B variant actually outperforming its full-detection counterpart. Over 80% of harmful responses are detected within the first 30% of tokens, and ~50% within the first 10%, enabling timely intervention in streaming LLM outputs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The most critical comparison is SCM (partial) vs. Qwen2.5 (partial) at the same scale, where SCM gains +6.87, +10.12, and +18.85 macro F1 at 0.5B, 1.5B, and 7B respectively, demonstrating that the hierarchical consistency-aware training strategy effectively closes the training-inference gap that plagues naively applied full-detection models. Against full-detection baselines, SCM-0.5B and SCM-1.5B achieve near-parity (within 0.3 macro F1) with their full-detection counterparts while only processing ~18% of tokens, and SCM-7B actually surpasses full Qwen2.5-7B by 4.7 macro F1. Among external full-detection services, ToxDectRoBERTa (96.85) and ModernBERT (96.44) are the strongest baselines, but both require complete outputs; Google Perspective (75.50) and OpenAI Moderation (76.51) lag significantly. On OOD benchmarks (ToxicChat, ToxiGen), SCM-7B achieves 70.08 and 70.88 macro F1 respectively, outperforming HateBERT (54.29/64.39) and ToxDectRoBERTa (62.99/60.66), confirming generalization beyond the training distribution.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant full-detection paradigm (HateBERT, LlamaGuard, OpenAI Moderation) where harmfulness is judged only after the complete LLM output is generated, and from the ad-hoc partial-detection approaches (ProtectAI, GuardrailsAI) that simply apply full-detection-trained models to truncated outputs, creating a training-inference gap. Conceptually, it repurposes the autoregressive streaming architecture (analogous in structure to speculative decoding) for a fundamentally different purpose—real-time safety monitoring rather than inference acceleration—and draws on logic-driven loss functions from prior neural-symbolic work to enforce consistency between token-level and response-level predictions. The FineHarm dataset extends the fine-grained toxic span detection literature (Mathew et al., SemEval 2021) from short human-written tweets to LLM-generated responses, using a POS-based heuristic annotation that outperforms more sophisticated likelihood-difference and LLM-deletion strategies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is substantial: LLM service providers need low-latency safety guardrails, and detecting harmful content after only 18% of tokens (vs. 100% for full detection) directly reduces user exposure and service latency. The Delay-k mechanism gives operators a tunable sensitivity knob, and the token-level DPO application demonstrates that the fine-grained signals can improve safety alignment beyond standard DPO. However, the OOD generalization is notably weak (macro F1 drops from ~97% in-distribution to ~70% on ToxicChat/ToxiGen), the heuristic POS annotation may miss pragmatic or context-dependent harmfulness, and the safety-alignment application remains preliminary—leaving open whether more principled token-level annotation and continual adaptation can close these gaps.
