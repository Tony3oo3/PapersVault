## Problem

Standard inverse reinforcement learning assumes decision-makers act optimally with full access to task-relevant information, but humans are attention-limited and their behavior is systematically shaped by learned cognitive biases. This paper formalizes the attention-aware inverse planning problem: given observed behavior, can we recover the specific attentional biases (e.g., a tendency to ignore ice patches while driving) that explain why a person's actions deviate from idealized rationality?

## Input / Output

The method consumes state-action trajectories generated by an attention-limited agent, where states are represented in an object-oriented format (maps from object-feature pairs to values, e.g., vehicle positions and speeds). A pre-trained generalist policy network (PPO in the driving experiments) is used to amortize the cost of computing optimal policies over masked/construed state spaces. The output is a small set of interpretable heuristic bias weights (λ) parameterizing a linear bias function over pre-specified construal feature functions, rather than a full reward function as in standard IRL.

## Method

The approach extends value-guided construal by adding a parameterized bias function H_λ(s,C) to the construal selection policy, which becomes a softmax over the value of representation (behavioral utility minus representational cost) plus the bias term. Inverting this generative model, attention-aware inverse planning finds λ* via maximum likelihood over observed trajectories, marginalizing over all possible construals. In tabular domains, construal policies are computed exactly via dynamic programming in JAX and λ* is found with gradient-based optimizers; in the GPUDrive driving simulator, a pre-trained PPO policy serves as the generalist π*, behavioral utilities are estimated via Monte Carlo rollouts on masked states, and λ* is recovered using Bayesian optimization. The paper demonstrates that standard IRL, even with auxiliary reward terms, cannot capture the behavior of attention-limited agents in certain scenarios, while AAIP reliably recovers the true bias weights.

## Results

In the DrivingWorld tabular domain, attention-aware inverse planning (AAIP) achieves a negative log-likelihood (NLL) of 265, substantially outperforming the most expressive IRL variant (NLL=513) that fits all five parameters (β, ε, r̃_Ice, r̃_Ice+Cone, γ). In the GPUDrive driving simulator with Waymo Open Dataset scenarios, AAIP reliably recovers true heuristic bias weights (λ_DfEH, λ_RH, λ_DfEC) across 215 simulated agents using only 80 trajectories (~12 minutes of driving data) per agent, with the algorithm converging at approximately 12 minutes of observed data. In the tabular setting, less consequential biases (e.g., bias to ignore cones) are more readily identifiable from behavior (higher R²), while more consequential biases (e.g., bias to ignore parked cars) are harder to recover because they are outweighed by value-guided construal effects.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary baseline is standard inverse reinforcement learning (IRL; Abbeel & Ng, 2004), which assumes the decision-maker has unlimited attention and optimizes an unknown reward function. On a DrivingWorld scenario generated by an attention-limited policy (λ_Ice=−10), even the most expressive IRL variant—fitting softmax inverse temperature (β), random action selection (ε), two auxiliary ice rewards (r̃_Ice, r̃_Ice+Cone), and discount rate (γ)—achieves only NLL=513, nearly double the NLL=265 achieved by AAIP. Qualitatively, the IRL-fitted policy is substantially noisier, sometimes fails to reach the goal, and visits the two ice patches between cones in unequal proportions (visiting only the first), whereas the true attention-limited policy visits both in roughly equal proportion. The paper concludes that IRL is not guaranteed to recover attention-limited decision-making: no reward function can fully capture the systematic behavioral regularities produced by attentional biases, making AAIP a strictly more appropriate model for such data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the value-guided construal framework (Ho et al., 2022, 2023), a computational cognitive science model of how resource-limited agents form simplified task representations, by incorporating parameterized attentional biases and inverting the resulting generative model to define a new inference problem. It departs from standard inverse reinforcement learning (Abbeel and Ng, 2004; Ziebart et al., 2008), which assumes decision-makers have unlimited attention and act optimally with respect to a latent reward, by explicitly modeling attentional selection as a stochastic process influenced by learned heuristics. The practical scaling leverages a pre-trained PPO generalist policy in the GPUDrive simulator to amortize the cost of computing construed policies over masked state spaces, connecting resource-rational cognitive modeling with modern deep RL infrastructure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first formal articulation of attention-aware inverse planning as a distinct problem from IRL, demonstrating that no amount of reward-function expressiveness in standard IRL can recover the behavior of attention-limited agents—a conceptual result with implications for human-robot interaction and AI safety. Key limitations include the assumption of a single fixed construal per episode, pre-specified heuristic features, a known reward model, and validation only on synthetic (not human) driving data. Open questions include whether dynamic construal switching, joint reward-and-bias inference, and learned (rather than hand-specified) heuristics can close the gap between this framework and real human behavior at scale.
