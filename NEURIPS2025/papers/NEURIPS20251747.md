## Problem

LLMs remain prone to hallucination and inconsistency, and existing alignment methods (RLHF, supervised fine-tuning) are computationally expensive, require human annotation, and lack theoretical guarantees of truthful behavior. The consensus game framework, which rewards agreement between a generator and a single discriminator, can produce collusive equilibria where agents reinforce factually incorrect outputs. This paper asks how to elicit provably truthful behavior from LLMs in a training-free, supervision-free setting with formal incentive-compatibility guarantees.

## Input / Output

The generator receives a text question X and a binary correctness label V ∈ {0,1} (conditioning it to produce either a correct or incorrect answer), and outputs a free-form text response Y. Each of n discriminator LLMs independently receives (X, Y) and outputs a binary correctness judgment. The discriminators' judgments are aggregated via majority vote to produce a consensus label, which serves as the final output and as a feedback signal for the generator. Utilities for discriminators are computed over batches of K tasks (typically K=8) using 2×2 co-report matrices, requiring at least 4 tasks per batch to form valid determinant-based scores.

## Method

PEG casts LLM alignment as a multi-agent peer elicitation game: a generator produces responses, and n independently instantiated discriminator LLMs evaluate those responses while being rewarded by their peers. The key mechanism is a determinant-based mutual information score: for each pair of discriminators, 2×2 co-report matrices are constructed over two disjoint task subsets, and the payment is the product of their determinants, which provably maximizes when agents report truthfully (strict information monotonicity). Policies are updated via Online Mirror Descent with KL divergence, operating directly in the output probability space rather than modifying model weights. The paper proves three theoretical results: (i) truthful reporting is a dominant strategy for each discriminator (incentive compatibility), (ii) each agent achieves O(√T) sublinear regret under OMD updates, and (iii) last-iterate convergence to a unique truthful Nash equilibrium with decaying learning rates. Empirically, PEG improves accuracy by over 10% on ARC-Challenge and MMLU, and enables 7B models to match or outperform 65B baselines.

## Results

PEG achieves majority-vote accuracy of 91.78% on ARC-Easy, 87.01% on ARC-Challenge, 70.73% on MMLU, and 22.54% on GPQA-Main, outperforming all baselines on every dataset. The largest gains appear on the hardest benchmarks: +10.75 points over the best baseline (Generative Ranking, 59.98%) on MMLU and +8.98 points over Mutual Information Ranking (78.03%) on ARC-Challenge. Individual discriminators also improve substantially after PEG updates; for example, deepseek-Qwen-7B jumps from 57.18% to 86.15% on ARC-Challenge, and even the weakest model (OQwen-7B) becomes the most accurate discriminator after PEG.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PEG dominates all four baselines across every dataset. Against Generative Ranking (G, Touvron et al. [56]), PEG leads by +3.59 on ARC-Easy (91.78 vs 88.19), +10.00 on ARC-Challenge (87.01 vs 77.01), +10.75 on MMLU (70.73 vs 59.98), and +4.46 on GPQA (22.54 vs 18.08). Against the Consensus Game baselines (Jacob et al. [27]), PEG outperforms Discriminative Ranking (D) by 7.60–61.58 points and Equilibrium Ranking Discriminator (ER-D) by 3.21–10.21 points, with the largest ER-D gap on MMLU (70.73 vs 59.66). Against Mutual Information Ranking (MI, Li & Jurafsky [35]), PEG wins by +3.17 on ARC-Easy, +8.98 on ARC-Challenge, +10.88 on MMLU, and +6.25 on GPQA. The paper attributes the advantage to PEG's determinant-based mutual information utility, which incentivizes truthful reporting rather than mere agreement, thereby eliciting latent model capabilities that agreement-based methods (MI, ER-D) fail to unlock.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Consensus Game framework (Jacob et al., ICLR 2023), which aligns a single generator-discriminator pair via agreement-based rewards, by replacing the single discriminator with a panel of multiple heterogeneous LLMs that evaluate each other through a peer elicitation mechanism. The scoring mechanism is adapted from multi-task peer prediction (Kong, SODA 2020; Agarwal et al., 2020), where determinant-based mutual information scores incentivize truthful reporting without ground-truth labels. The key conceptual departure is that PEG rewards discriminators for mutual agreement with peers rather than agreement with the generator, which the authors argue eliminates the collusion incentive inherent in consensus-based approaches. Policy updates use online mirror descent, connecting the framework to the no-regret learning literature in multi-agent games.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PEG offers a training-free, supervision-free path to improving LLM factual accuracy with theoretical guarantees (dominant truthfulness, sublinear regret, last-iterate convergence to a truthful Nash equilibrium), which is rare in the LLM alignment literature where most methods rely on RLHF or fine-tuning without such guarantees. A notable limitation is the conditional independence assumption among discriminators: when models are too similar (e.g., 7 discriminators), the mutual information signal degenerates and performance drops, suggesting that model diversity is critical. Open questions include whether PEG generalizes to open-ended generation (beyond multiple-choice QA), how it behaves under adversarial or strategic agents, and whether the theoretical guarantees hold when the local concavity assumption is relaxed.
