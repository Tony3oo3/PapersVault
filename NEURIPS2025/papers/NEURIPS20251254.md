## Problem

Score-based Bayesian Network Structure Learning (BNSL) via integer programming suffers from exponentially many variables and constraints. While column generation (CG) addresses the variable explosion, the pricing problem that identifies new columns is a computationally expensive mixed-integer nonlinear program (MINLP) that severely limits scalability. This paper tackles the pricing bottleneck by showing it can be reformulated as a difference-of-submodular (DS) optimization problem solvable approximately via the Difference of Convex Algorithm (DCA).

## Input / Output

The method takes an observational data matrix D ∈ ℝ^{N×n} (continuous Gaussian or discrete multinomial) and a regularization parameter Λ (which recovers BIC or AIC scores), and produces a directed acyclic graph (DAG) over n nodes that maximizes the ℓ₀-penalized likelihood score. The IP is solved with Gurobi using lazy constraints for acyclicity, and the CG loop iteratively enriches the candidate parent-set space per node before solving the restricted master IP. A 3-hour time limit is imposed per instance, and results are averaged over 10 independent data realizations per graph structure.

## Method

The key insight is that for ℓ₀-penalized likelihood scores, the pricing objective z_i(J;λ*) decomposes into a difference of two submodular set functions: for Gaussian data, g(J) = (N/2)log det(Σ̂_{J∪i,J∪i}) + Λ|J| + dual terms and f(J) = (N/2)log det(Σ̂_{J,J}); for discrete data, the decomposition involves joint entropy functions and a supermodular arity penalty. Minimizing the Lovász extension of this DS function is then solved via DCA, which iteratively linearizes the concave component f^L and solves a convex subproblem (using Kelley's cutting-plane method). Subgradients of f^L are computed efficiently via a single Cholesky decomposition of the permuted covariance matrix rather than O(n) separate log-det evaluations. The full algorithm embeds DCA pricing within a simultaneous row-and-column generation loop: CG adds columns with negative reduced cost, row generation adds violated cluster constraints via a separation IP, and the restricted master IP is solved with branch-and-cut and lazy constraints. A hybrid DCA initialization strategy—random exploration early, then warm-start from the current RMLP solution once 50 candidate parent sets exist—balances global search and local refinement.

## Results

On simulated Gaussian data (n=15–25, N=5,000–20,000, d=0.5–2.0), CG-DCA consistently achieves lower BIC score gaps than GOBNILP, reaching 0.00% gap (optimal) on sparse graphs (d=0.5) and maintaining gaps under 1% where GOBNILP reaches up to 5.15% (e.g., n=25, d=2.0: CG-DCA 0.86% vs. GOBNILP 5.15%). In terms of graph quality, CG-DCA achieves the best average recall across most Gaussian instances (e.g., 0.84 vs. 0.67 for GOBNILP at n=25, d=0.5) while remaining competitive on precision and SHD. For pricing speed, DCA solves each pricing problem in ~0.3 s/node versus ~35.1 s/node for the exact MINLP solver (n=20, d=1). On discrete benchmarks (LUCAS, INSURANCE, ALARM), GOBNILP dominates, with CG-DCA showing a 0.03% BIC gap on LUCAS but degrading to 2.85% on ALARM.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GOBNILP (the state-of-the-art score-based IP method), CG-DCA wins on BIC score gap for all Gaussian instances with d≥1.0 and matches it on d=0.5, while also achieving better recall and lower SHD on most configurations; however, GOBNILP dominates on all three discrete benchmarks (LUCAS, INSURANCE, ALARM), achieving 0.00% BIC gap where CG-DCA reaches 0.03–2.85%. Against the exact CG-MINLP pricing approach [18], CG-DCA is approximately 100× faster per pricing call (0.3 s vs. 35.1 s per node at n=20, d=1) and produces a valid DAG in 1.2 s where CG-MINLP takes 19.2 s on the 7-node Gaussian.test instance. Against Stable-PC (constraint-based), CG-DCA achieves higher recall on nearly all Gaussian instances (e.g., 0.84 vs. 0.84 at n=25, d=0.5; 0.66 vs. 0.46 at n=25, d=2.0) while PC retains an edge on precision for denser graphs. Against MMHC (hybrid), CG-DCA outperforms on all three metrics (precision, recall, SHD) for the majority of Gaussian instances, with the gap widening as density increases (e.g., SHD 23.90 vs. 34.10 at n=25, d=2.0). Against Hill Climbing (HC), CG-DCA is uniformly superior on all metrics, with HC showing the worst SHD (e.g., 77.00 vs. 23.90 at n=25, d=2.0).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Cussens' (2023) branch-price-and-cut framework for Bayesian Network Structure Learning, which introduced column generation to BNSL but relied on computationally expensive MINLP solvers for the pricing subproblem. The key departure is reformulating the pricing problem as a difference-of-submodular (DS) optimization and applying the Difference of Convex Algorithm (DCA) from El Halabi et al. (2023) as an inexact pricing method, drawing on the Lovász extension to bridge set-function submodularity with convex optimization. The approach also connects to the broader column generation literature in operations research (vehicle routing, crew scheduling) where inexact pricing is standard practice, and to the GOBNILP line of work that formulates BNSL as an IP with cluster constraints but restricts parent set size to control variable count.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By showing the BNSL pricing problem admits a DS decomposition solvable via DCA, the paper removes the main scalability bottleneck of CG-based BNSL, enabling solutions without the parent-set-size restriction that limits GOBNILP. However, the discrete-data case reveals a fundamental limitation: the supermodular penalty term (a_i−1)∏a_j in the multinomial BIC score makes DCA ineffective for larger networks, leaving a gap for discrete Bayesian networks. Open questions include developing alternative DS decompositions for discrete scores, extending to other scoring functions (e.g., BDeu), and building exact pricing algorithms that leverage submodular/supermodular optimization to provide optimality guarantees within the CG framework.
