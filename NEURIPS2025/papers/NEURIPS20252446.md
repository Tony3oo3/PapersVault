## Problem

Most LLM merging methods operate exclusively in weight space, ignoring activation-space information that encodes which parameters are most critical to the base model's capabilities. This omission makes merged models fragile to low-quality fine-tuned experts and can cause catastrophic forgetting of the base model's generalist performance, sometimes yielding merged models that underperform the unmerged base. The paper asks whether activation-space saliency can be incorporated into merging to selectively protect important weights while still integrating expert knowledge.

## Input / Output

AIM is a post-merging relaxation step that takes the output of any existing merging algorithm (the merged weight tensor Δ_merged relative to the base model) and a small task-agnostic calibration corpus (~256 sequences of ~2048 tokens from the Pile validation set). The calibration data is consumed via a single forward pass through the base model to record per-channel activation magnitudes—no gradients or training are required. The output is a modified merged weight tensor where updates to high-activation channels are attenuated by a factor controlled by a single hyperparameter ω, producing a merged model that better preserves base-model capabilities.

## Method

AIM formulates merging as a continual learning problem and applies an activation-aware relaxation to the weight update. A calibration corpus is passed through the pre-trained base model to compute the average magnitude of activations per input channel in every linear layer; these magnitudes are normalized to [0,1] to form a diagonal saliency matrix A_pre. The relaxation scheme then scales the merged weight update element-wise: θ_AIM = θ_pre + (1 − A_pre(1−ω)) · Δ_merged, so that weights in high-activation channels (where perturbations cause large output errors, as shown by the norm bound ‖diag(x)δw‖) receive proportionally smaller updates. The hyperparameter ω ∈ [0,1] controls the strength of protection (ω=0 fully reverts salient weights to base values; ω=1 applies no relaxation), and the scheme is algorithm-agnostic—it wraps around DARE, TIES, Task Arithmetic, or WIDEN without modifying their internals. A sensitivity-based variant replaces activation magnitudes with gradient-based importance scores (|∂L/∂θ|), yielding comparable performance at higher compute cost, confirming that activation magnitudes are a sufficient proxy for parameter importance.

## Results

Applying AIM to five merging methods across four expert-combination scenarios (using Llama-2-13B experts for code, math, and instruction following) yields an average 13% improvement across six benchmarks, with HV Gain increases up to 40.6% (DARE Task Arithmetic, 3-model merge: 0.16→0.23) and 41.3% (DARE TIES, 3-model merge: 0.17→0.24). The most dramatic single-benchmark gains occur when merging Instruction Tuned + Math experts, e.g., HumanEval improving from 5.49 to 12.20 (+122%) under DARE Task Arithmetic + AIM. Across all 20 merge configurations, 80% show improved HV Gain, and the top-performing model on every individual benchmark is an AIM-enhanced merge. Generalization to a different architecture (Qwen 2.5-VL-7B, multimodal) confirms consistent HV Gain improvements across all four tested merging methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AIM is not a standalone merging method but a post-hoc relaxation applied on top of existing baselines, so comparisons are framed as 'baseline + AIM vs. baseline alone.' Against DARE (Yu et al., ICML 2024), AIM produces the largest gains: DARE Task Arithmetic 3-model merge improves HV Gain from 0.16 to 0.23 (+40.6%) and DARE TIES from 0.17 to 0.24 (+41.3%), with HumanEval jumping 37–45% in both cases. Against Task Arithmetic (Ilharco et al., ICLR 2023), the 3-model merge HV Gain rises from 0.16 to 0.22 (+32.0%), though 2-model Code+Math merges show slight regressions (−2.7% HV Gain), indicating AIM helps most when the base model's generalist capabilities are at risk. Against TIES Merging (Yadav et al., NeurIPS 2023), gains are modest for the 3-model merge (+4.3% HV Gain) and some individual benchmarks (MATH, GSM8K) actually degrade, suggesting TIES' sign-conflict resolution already partially protects salient weights. Against WIDEN (Yu et al., 2024), AIM provides the smallest improvements (0.29→0.30 HV Gain for 3-model merge; some 2-model merges show −1.2% HV Gain), consistent with WIDEN's weight-disentanglement approach already being more robust to forgetting. Overall, AIM wins or ties on HV Gain in 80% of the 20 tested configurations, with the largest relative gains concentrated in scenarios where the base model's generalist performance is most threatened by aggressive specialization.

## Relation to Prior Work

AIM repurposes the activation-magnitude analysis from AWQ (Lin et al., 2024) — originally designed for LLM quantization — into the model-merging setting, where prior methods (DARE, TIES, Task Arithmetic, WIDEN) operate exclusively in weight space. Conceptually, the relaxation scheme is a lightweight analogue of Elastic Weight Consolidation (Kirkpatrick et al., 2017) from continual learning, substituting Fisher-information-based importance scores with activation magnitudes obtained from a single forward pass on a task-agnostic calibration set. The paper positions AIM as an algorithm-agnostic post-hoc correction that composes with any existing merging procedure rather than replacing it, and introduces a hypervolume-gain metric to evaluate merging from a multi-objective optimization perspective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical appeal is strong: AIM requires only one inference pass over ~256 calibration sequences (robust down to 8 blocks) and can be bolted onto any merging pipeline with a single hyperparameter (ω), making it a low-cost, high-utility addition for practitioners merging fine-tuned LLMs. However, the headline 40% HV Gain improvement is concentrated in specific two-expert merges (notably instruction-tuned + math), while the average gain across all 20 merge configurations is ~13%, and some individual benchmarks (GSM8K, IFEval) occasionally degrade. Open questions include whether incorporating expert-model activations (currently only base-model activations are used), non-linear relaxation schedules, or theoretically grounded sensitivity formulations could close the remaining gap, and whether the approach scales to larger model families or more than three experts.
