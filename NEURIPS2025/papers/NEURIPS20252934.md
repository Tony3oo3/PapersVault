## Problem

State-of-the-art membership inference attacks (MIAs) require training many reference models, making them impractical at LLM scale. Prior work has either used weaker reference-free attacks (shown to be brittle) or strong attacks on small models and datasets whose insights do not generalize to modern LLMs. This paper asks whether the poor performance of weaker attacks stems from omitting reference models or reflects a fundamental limitation of MIAs on LLMs, by running the strongest known attacks at unprecedented scale.

## Input / Output

The attack takes a target GPT-2 model (10M–1B parameters), a query text sample, and 128 IN/OUT reference models trained on complementary subsets of C4, using model loss as the observation statistic. It produces a per-sample likelihood-ratio membership score, a binary membership decision calibrated at a chosen FPR on non-members, aggregate performance metrics (ROC-AUC, TPR at fixed FPR), and a novel per-sample flip rate that quantifies how often the binary decision changes across 127 identically-configured target replicas trained with different random seeds.

## Method

The paper scales LiRA—the Likelihood Ratio Attack—to GPT-2 architectures (10M–1B parameters) pre-trained via NanoDO on C4 subsets of 50M+ samples, three orders of magnitude larger than prior MIA studies, training over 4,000 reference models in total. For each query sample, LiRA fits Gaussian densities to the IN and OUT reference loss distributions, then computes a likelihood ratio against the target's loss to score membership. The authors systematically vary model size (under Chinchilla-optimal and fixed-dataset conditions), training epochs (1–20), and dataset size (50K–10M samples) to map the upper bound of strong MIA success. A key diagnostic contribution is the per-sample flip rate: by training 127 target replicas on the identical dataset with different batch-order seeds and measuring pairwise decision disagreement at a calibrated FPR, they show that even when aggregate AUC exceeds 0.7, a large fraction of individual membership decisions are statistically indistinguishable from coin flips. They also compare LiRA against RMIA across reference counts and observation statistics, and analyze per-sample vulnerability patterns (training-step recency, sequence length) and their (lack of) correlation with extraction risk.

## Results

Scaling LiRA to GPT-2 models (10M–1B parameters) trained on C4 with 128 reference models, Chinchilla-optimal 1-epoch training yields ROC-AUC between 0.547 (489M) and 0.699 (85M), with no model exceeding AUC=0.7 under standard training conditions. Over-training amplifies vulnerability: the 140M model's AUC rises from 0.573 (1 epoch) to 0.797 (10 epochs), and the 44M model reaches AUC=0.744 after just 2 epochs on half the dataset. Per-sample analysis reveals that even at FPR=10⁻³, approximately 15.4% of true-positive decisions are statistically indistinguishable from coin flips (flip rate ≥ 0.487, α=0.05), and this rises to ~42.2% when including highly unstable decisions (flip rate ≥ 0.4). No correlation is found between MIA success and probabilistic extraction of training data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper directly compares LiRA against RMIA (Zarifzadeh et al., ICML 2024), the other leading reference-based MIA. LiRA outperforms RMIA in ROC-AUC for all reference counts above 16 IN references: at 64 IN refs, LiRA achieves AUC=0.678 versus RMIA's 0.658, and the gap widens at 128 refs where RMIA has already plateaued. In the narrow 4–16 IN reference range, RMIA is competitive (e.g., RMIA AUC=0.643 vs. LiRA AUC=0.594 at 4 IN refs), making RMIA preferable for low-compute settings. Both attacks clearly exceed the random-guessing baseline (AUC=0.5) but neither reaches AUC=0.7 under Chinchilla-optimal 1-epoch training, establishing that even the strongest known MIAs have limited practical effectiveness on pre-trained LLMs. The paper also shows that using model loss as the observation statistic (AUC=0.678) substantially outperforms using averaged logits (AUC=0.576) for LiRA, and that increasing references beyond 128 yields negligible gains (0.2% AUC improvement from 128 to 256 IN refs).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper scales the Likelihood Ratio Attack (LiRA) from Carlini et al. (2022) to GPT-2 architectures (10M–1B parameters) trained on C4 subsets three orders of magnitude larger than prior MIA studies, bridging a gap where strong reference-based attacks had only been evaluated on small models while weaker fine-tuning attacks were applied to LLMs. It also introduces a per-sample 'flip rate' diagnostic that probes decision stability across equally plausible target replicas, going beyond the aggregate ROC-AUC and TPR-at-fixed-FPR metrics standard in the MIA literature. The comparison with RMIA (Zarifzadeh et al., 2024) is the first head-to-head evaluation of these two strong attacks in the pre-trained LLM setting with large reference counts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By establishing that even the strongest known MIA achieves AUC < 0.7 under Chinchilla-optimal training, the paper provides a practical upper bound on what weaker, more feasible attacks can hope to achieve on LLMs, reframing the threat model for privacy assessments. The flip-rate diagnostic reveals that aggregate AUC can mask widespread per-sample decision instability, raising open questions about whether any MIA on LLMs produces reliable per-sample membership evidence or whether the task is fundamentally limited by training randomness. The absence of correlation between MIA success and extraction challenges the commonly assumed memorization link, and the non-monotonic size-vulnerability relationship suggests that scaling laws for MIA risk are not yet understood. Future work should investigate whether alternative scaling laws, deduplication, or architectural choices can stabilize per-sample decisions and whether the instability finding generalizes beyond GPT-2.
