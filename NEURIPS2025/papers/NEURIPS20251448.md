## Problem

Existing general-domain preference datasets for RLHF are limited in diversity (English-only), lack specialist annotation for challenging STEM, coding, and multilingual tasks, or rely on LLM annotators (e.g., GPT-4) that introduce self-enhancement bias and commercial-use restrictions. There is no openly licensed, human-annotated preference dataset that covers the full range of real-world LLM use-cases with domain-expert annotators.

## Input / Output

Each preference sample pairs a prompt (sourced from WildChat-1M or ShareGPT) with two responses generated by different models from a pool of 17 commercially-permissive LLMs, optionally preceded by multi-turn context (up to 2000 words). Annotators assign a 7-point preference label (-3 to +3) plus a 'neither valid' option, with a 10-50 word justification. Downstream, Bradley-Terry reward models consume a prompt-response pair and emit a scalar reward from a feedforward head on the end-of-response token, while Generative RMs first produce a textual critique before scoring; aligned policy models take a text instruction and generate a free-form response.

## Method

The dataset is built by engaging specialist annotator pools (requiring relevant degrees, software-engineering experience, or language fluency) to provide 3-5 independent preference judgments per sample, followed by post-processing that removes 'neither valid' samples, filters outlier annotations, and excludes samples with inter-annotator disagreement > 2. Reward models are trained from Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct using the Scaled Bradley-Terry Loss, and a separate Generative RM pipeline (following DeepSeek-GRM) is trained with reinforcement learning on Llama-3.3-Nemotron-Super-49B-v1 to produce critiques before scores. For policy alignment, the authors apply REINFORCE Leave-One-Out (RLOO) using the trained reward models, which outperforms PPO and DPO under matched conditions. The key differentiator is the combination of domain-specialist human annotation across STEM, Code, and Multilingual subsets with a permissive CC-BY-4.0 license, enabling both Bradley-Terry and Generative reward modeling without LLM-annotator bias.

## Results

The best Bradley-Terry Reward Model trained on the Multilingual subset of HelpSteer3-Preference achieves 82.4% overall accuracy on RM-Bench, while the English RM (General+STEM+Code) achieves 73.7% on JudgeBench — both representing ~10% absolute improvement over the previously best-reported reward models (70.1% on RM-Bench and 64.3% on JudgeBench). Generative Reward Models trained on all HelpSteer3-Preference subsets with Voting@32 further push performance to 85.5% on RM-Bench and 78.6% on JudgeBench. For RLHF alignment, RLOO with the English RM improves Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct from 62.4 to 87.0 on Arena Hard and from 52.5 to 60.0 on WildBench Overall.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to HelpSteer2-Preference (the direct predecessor), the Multilingual RM trained on HelpSteer3-Preference improves RM-Bench Overall from 77.3% to 82.4% (+5.1%) and the English RM improves JudgeBench from 68.6% to 73.7% (+5.1%). Against INF-ORM-Preference, gains are +6.1% on RM-Bench (76.3%→82.4%) and +6.8% on JudgeBench (66.9%→73.7%). Against Skywork-Preference, improvements are even larger: +8.8% on RM-Bench (73.6%→82.4%) and +8.8% on JudgeBench (64.9%→73.7%). Versus the strongest published external baselines (Llama-3.1-Nemotron-70B-Reward at 70.7% RM-Bench / 66.9% JudgeBench, and Skywork-Reward-Gemma-2-27B at 70.5% / 64.3%), the HelpSteer3-Preference RMs lead by 11.7–11.9% on RM-Bench and 6.8–9.4% on JudgeBench. The Generative RM with Voting@32 extends these leads to 15.0% on RM-Bench and 11.7% on JudgeBench over the best external baselines, though at substantially higher inference compute cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper is the direct successor to HelpSteer2-Preference (same NVIDIA team) and the fourth generation in the lineage of general-domain preference datasets (HH-RLHF → Open Assistant → UltraFeedback/HelpSteer/Nectar → Skywork/HelpSteer2/INF-ORM). The key conceptual departure is replacing general crowdworkers with specialist annotator pools (requiring domain degrees, work experience, or language fluency) for STEM, Code, and Multilingual subsets, and sourcing prompts from WildChat-1M alongside ShareGPT to increase task diversity. For reward model training, it follows the Scaled Bradley-Terry loss from HelpSteer2-Preference and adopts the DeepSeek-GRM reinforcement learning approach for Generative RMs, while using RLOO (over PPO/DPO) for policy alignment based on prior findings from the same group.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As a CC-BY-4.0 licensed dataset with 40K+ specialist-annotated samples spanning 14 programming languages and 13 natural languages, HelpSteer3-Preference fills a critical gap: no prior general-domain preference dataset of comparable quality was both multilingual and commercially permissive. The ~10% absolute improvement over prior SOTA RMs on both RM-Bench and JudgeBench is well-supported by ablations across subsets and model architectures. Open questions include whether the annotation-vendor difference (Scale AI vs. Translated) introduces systematic preference-strength biases, whether the Code subset's stylistic annotation guidelines (comments, coding style) will transfer to correctness-focused benchmarks, and whether Generative RMs can become practical for RLHF given their >100× inference compute cost.
