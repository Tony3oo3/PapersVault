## Problem

Serial dependence produces two opposing perceptual biases—repulsion at the sensory stage and attraction at the post-perception stage—but existing Bayesian and efficient-coding models lack a biologically grounded neural mechanism and fail to capture the temporal dynamics (e.g., bias decay with longer inter-stimulus intervals). The paper asks what specific synaptic mechanism in a hierarchical cortical circuit can simultaneously generate both effects and explain their distinct timescales.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253568/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives two sequential visual orientation stimuli (angles uniformly sampled in [−90°, 90°)) as Gaussian-shaped external inputs to the lower layer, followed by a weak, ambiguous retrieval cue signal (lower amplitude, wider spatial profile, higher noise) that triggers recall of one stimulus. The output is a decoded orientation read from the higher layer via population vector during the cue period, and the primary behavioral metric is the adjustment error (decoded minus actual angle) plotted against within-trial and between-trial stimulus differences, fitted with a derivative-of-Gaussian curve to quantify bias amplitude and peak location.

## Method

The paper proposes a two-layer continuous attractor neural network (CANN) with heterosynaptic short-term plasticity (STP) following Tsodyks–Markram dynamics, where each layer's neurons are arranged on a 1-D ring by preferred orientation and connected via translation-invariant Gaussian kernels. The lower layer (modeling V1) is STD-dominated (τd = 3 s ≫ τf = 0.3 s), so sequential stimuli deplete the available neurotransmitter resource x asymmetrically, creating a double-trough profile that weakens synaptic efficacy on the side closer to the earlier stimulus and pushes the retrieved bump away (repulsion). The higher layer (modeling PFC) is STF-dominated (τf = 5 s ≫ τd = 0.3 s), so the release probability u remains elevated around previously active orientations, creating a three-peak profile that strengthens connections toward the prior trial's cued target and pulls the retrieved bump toward it (attraction). The key mechanistic insight is that the spatial asymmetry in x or u—determined by the relative timing of stimulus presentation and the STP recovery time constants—directly shifts the CANN attractor, and the STP time constants set the temporal windows over which repulsion and attraction persist, providing a neural substrate for the Bayesian likelihood/prior modulation framework.

## Results

The two-layer CANN with heterosynaptic STP reproduces both within-trial repulsion (A_DoG = −0.91°, peaking at ΔS = 38.13°) and between-trial attraction (A_DoG = 1.18°, peaking at ΔS = 17.83°), with magnitudes comparable to psychophysical measurements (A_DoG = 1.17° [14], 1.32° [20], 1.59° [59]). In single-layer ablations, STD-dominance yields repulsion of −2.29° (peak at 31.71°) while STF-dominance yields attraction of 1.48° (peak at 24.52°). The model further predicts that the STD time constant (τ_d = 3 s) sets the repulsion window (ISI < τ_d) and the STF time constant (τ_f = 5 s) sets the attraction window (ITI < τ_f), with repulsion reversing to attraction when ISI exceeds τ_d. Under a partial no-report paradigm, between-trial attraction becomes non-significant (A_DoG = 0.14°, p = .802) while within-trial repulsion slightly increases (A_DoG = −1.29°), matching behavioral data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against competing computational models in a head-to-head manner; instead, it validates its STP-based CANN predictions against published psychophysical and neurophysiological measurements. The model's between-trial attraction amplitude (1.18°) falls within the range of experimentally reported serial dependence effects (1.17°–1.59° across [14, 20, 59]), confirming quantitative agreement. The within-trial repulsion (−0.91°) is consistent with V1-level repulsive biases documented in [15, 16, 17]. A critical control experiment—reversing the STP configuration (STF in the lower layer, STD in the higher layer)—produces exclusively attractive biases (within: 1.79°, between: 3.81°), demonstrating that the specific STD-then-STF ordering is necessary to reproduce the experimentally observed dissociation. The model also qualitatively matches the Bayesian two-process framework [13, 20] by mapping STD to likelihood modulation (repulsion) and STF to prior modulation (attraction), but goes further by capturing temporal dynamics (ISI/ITI dependence) that existing Bayesian models cannot explain. Sensitivity analyses confirm robustness: ±10% perturbations to STP parameters produce only minor amplitude changes, and the effects depend on relative rather than absolute timescales.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the continuous attractor neural network (CANN) framework (Wu et al., 2016) and the Tsodyks-Markram model of short-term synaptic plasticity (STP) to propose a unified neural mechanism for serial dependence. It departs from prior Bayesian models of serial dependence (Fischer & Whitney, 2014; Fritsche et al., 2017; Pascucci et al., 2019), which explain repulsion and attraction through likelihood/prior modulation but lack biological substrates and temporal dynamics. It also extends earlier neural models (Barbosa et al., 2020; Stein et al., 2020) that attributed attraction to STF or NMDA currents in PFC but could not account for the coexisting repulsive bias. The key departure is the heterosynaptic STP architecture—STD in the lower (sensory) layer and STF in the higher (post-perceptual) layer—providing a single mechanistic account for both effects within a hierarchical CANN.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a biologically grounded neural implementation of the Bayesian interpretation of serial dependence, mapping STD-mediated neurotransmitter depletion to likelihood modulation and STF-sustained release probability to prior modulation, thereby bridging a long-standing gap between computational and mechanistic accounts. The testable prediction that STP time constants define the temporal windows of repulsion and attraction offers concrete experimental targets for neurophysiology. Limitations include the absence of feedback projections (top-down modulation), the inability to explain long-timescale repulsive effects (>50 s), and the lack of released code, which limits immediate reproducibility. Open questions include whether the STD/STF hierarchy generalizes beyond orientation to other continuous features and how attentional control in PFC modulates the balance between the two effects.
