## Problem

Existing chart VQA benchmarks cannot evaluate whether MLLM failures on infographic charts stem from data complexity or from the additional pictorial visual elements (pictograms, icons, metaphors), because they lack paired plain-chart counterparts sharing the same underlying data. Moreover, no prior benchmark includes questions specifically targeting the visual elements themselves—such as mapping a cropped icon to its data value or interpreting a metaphorical image—leaving a critical gap in assessing the unique reasoning demands of infographic design.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254563/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each question is paired with either an infographic chart image or its plain-chart counterpart; visual-element-based questions additionally supply one or more cropped icon images alongside the full chart. Answers span five formats—numeric values (evaluated with relaxed accuracy after normalization), free-text names (ANLS > 0.8), yes/no, four-option multiple choice, and ordered rankings—across 54 fine-grained chart types. The benchmark contains 50,920 text-based questions shared between both chart variants and 7,900+ visual-element-based questions unique to infographics, totaling roughly 59K QA pairs over 5,948 chart pairs.

## Method

The benchmark is built in three stages. First, 5,948 infographic charts are collected from 11 visualization platforms and filtered through a semi-automatic pipeline (Gemini 2.0 Flash candidate selection + human verification) to ensure ≥30 charts per each of 54 fine-grained types. Second, paired plain charts are generated by extracting tabular data via an ensemble of Gemini 2.0 Flash and GPT-4o with expert correction, then rendering standard charts with Python libraries (plotly, matplotlib, seaborn). Third, questions are constructed in two families: text-based questions are semi-automatically generated from templates keyed to 11 data-fact types (value, rank, trend, outlier, etc.) and rewritten by MLLMs for linguistic diversity, while visual-element-based questions are split into basic questions (icon-to-data mapping and icon-function identification, generated with InternImage detection + templates) and metaphor-related questions (462 items hand-crafted by two visualization experts in multiple-choice format). The paired design enables controlled ablation—progressively removing visual elements, adding auxiliary lines, or perturbing label positions—to isolate the specific mechanisms (visual complexity, text-element connectivity, label-order sensitivity) driving the observed 8–19-point accuracy drop on infographics versus plain charts.

## Results

On InfoChartQA, the best proprietary model (Gemini 2.5 Pro Preview) achieves 79.23% accuracy on infographic text-based questions and 91.16% on plain charts, while the best open-source model (Qwen2.5-VL-72B) reaches 61.08% and 77.92% respectively. All models show a substantial performance drop (8–19 percentage points) when moving from plain to infographic charts. On the hardest subset—metaphor-related visual-element questions—even the top model (Gemini 2.5 Pro) scores only 60.42%, well below the human baseline of 88.69%. The human baseline overall achieves 90.79% average on visual-element-based questions, leaving a 16+ point gap to the best MLLM.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among proprietary models, Gemini 2.5 Pro Preview leads on all metrics (79.23% infographic text, 88.91% basic visual, 60.42% metaphor), outperforming OpenAI O4-mini by 3.0 points on infographic text and 5.7 points on metaphor questions. Claude 3.5 Sonnet shows the largest plain-to-infographic drop (18.57 points), indicating it is particularly sensitive to pictorial visual elements. Among open-source models, Llama-4 Scout (63.68%) and Intern-VL3-78B (63.42%) slightly edge out Qwen2.5-VL-72B (61.08%) on infographic text-based questions, but Qwen2.5-VL-72B leads on basic visual-element questions (76.71%). Chart-specific models (ChartGemma, TinyChart, ChartInstruct) perform substantially worse than general-purpose MLLMs, with TinyChart scoring only 9.03% on metaphor questions—near chance level. A strong Spearman correlation (ρ=0.895, p<0.01) between text-based and visual-element-based scores confirms that chart comprehension is a prerequisite for visual-element reasoning, and no model currently closes the ~16-point gap to human performance on visual-element tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the chart QA benchmark lineage (FigureQA → ChartQA → ChartBench → ChartX) and the infographic-specific benchmarks (InfographicVQA, ChartQAPro) by addressing two specific gaps: the absence of paired infographic/plain charts sharing identical underlying data, and the lack of questions targeting pictorial visual elements (pictograms, icons, metaphors). Rather than proposing a new model or method, it repurposes MLLMs (Gemini 2.0 Flash, GPT-4o) as tools for chart-to-table translation and semi-automated question generation, then uses the paired-chart structure to enable controlled ablation studies that prior benchmarks could not support. The visual-element-based question design, particularly the metaphor-related subset, draws on visualization design principles (data facts taxonomy from Wang et al.) but applies them to a multimodal evaluation context not previously explored.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paired-chart design provides a methodologically clean way to isolate the effect of visual elements from data complexity, yielding actionable insights for MLLM developers (e.g., proximity of icons to data values, sensitivity to label ordering). The finding that even top proprietary models drop ~20 points on metaphor questions and rely on superficial label order rather than actual data values highlights fundamental limitations in current multimodal reasoning that are unlikely to be resolved by scale alone. Open questions include whether the 462 metaphor questions are sufficient to draw robust conclusions, whether template-based question generation limits out-of-distribution generalizability, and how these findings translate into concrete training or prompting strategies for improving infographic understanding.
