## Problem

Existing voice-cloning defenses assume manually annotated transcripts and target only DNN-based TTS, leaving two critical gaps unaddressed: (1) production LLM-based TTS systems that encode audio into discrete tokens rather than continuous embeddings, and (2) the realistic end-to-end scenario where transcripts are auto-generated by an ASR system rather than provided by the user. This paper introduces a unified defense for both the LLM-based architecture and the ASR-driven E2E attack surface.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a raw audio waveform (normalized to [-1, 1]), a target ASR system (e.g., Wav2vec2, Whisper), and optionally a target speaker's audio for targeted timbre mode. It outputs a single protected waveform with perturbation bounded by an ℓ₂-norm of ε=8/255, optimized via 500 PGD iterations on a single NVIDIA 4090 GPU (~98–111 s per sample). The output is a standard playable audio file that is perceptually natural to human listeners but carries embedded adversarial structure targeting both timbre encoders and ASR recognition.

## Method

E2E-VGuard jointly optimizes three loss terms under a single PGD loop: (1) an ASR-targeted adversarial loss that forces the ASR to transcribe the protected audio as a specific target text, thereby corrupting the text–pronunciation alignment used during TTS fine-tuning; (2) a feature loss computed over an ensemble of five timbre/style encoders (VITS, GSV, WavLM, CAM++, StyleTTS2) plus an MFCC extractor, maximizing (untargeted) or minimizing (targeted) cosine similarity to disrupt speaker identity at the discrete-token level relevant to LLM-based TTS; and (3) a psychoacoustic masking loss that constrains the perturbation's power spectral density to remain below the original audio's frequency masking threshold, supplemented by an ℓ₂-norm penalty for perceptual naturalness. The weighted combination (α=500, β=5×10⁻³) is optimized with projected gradient descent, and the final waveform is clamped to [-1, 1]. The key architectural insight is that MFCC features protect articulation and prosodic patterns encoded as discrete tokens in LLM-based TTS, while the encoder ensemble ensures transferability across unseen synthesizer backbones (VAE, diffusion, flow-matching, in-context learning).

## Results

On the end-to-end fine-tuning benchmark (Table 1), E2E-VGuard (UT) achieves the lowest speaker similarity (SIM) across all six TTS models, ranging from 0.082 (StyleTTS2) to 0.155 (Llasa-1B), compared to the best baseline AntiFake (0.149–0.284). E2E-VGuard (T) achieves the highest word error rate (WER) on all models, peaking at 125.3% on VITS and 94.8% on GSV, versus SafeSpeech's best of 105.5% (VITS) and 44.8% (GSV). The paper reports an average WER increase of 19.775% (T) and SIM reduction of 0.043 (UT) over the best baselines, while maintaining the highest SNR (18.5–20.5 dB) and competitive PESQ (1.95–2.32) among strong-protection methods. On zero-shot LLM-based models (Table 2), E2E-VGuard (UT) achieves SOTA timbre protection with SIM as low as 0.008 on Step-Audio-TTS and 0.127 on FishSpeech.

## Baselines & Comparisons

E2E-VGuard dominates all four baselines on both timbre (SIM) and pronunciation (WER) metrics across the six fine-tuning models. Against AntiFake [7], the strongest prior adversarial-example method, E2E-VGuard (UT) reduces SIM by 0.026–0.201 (e.g., 0.091 vs. 0.232 on CosyVoice, 0.082 vs. 0.283 on StyleTTS2) while also achieving higher WER (e.g., 66.5% vs. 28.8% on GSV). Against SafeSpeech [10], the strongest unlearnable-example method, E2E-VGuard (T) more than doubles WER on GSV (94.8% vs. 44.8%) and CosyVoice (72.1% vs. 8.6%), and reduces SIM from 0.339 to 0.284 (GSV) and 0.459 to 0.375 (CosyVoice). POP [9] and POP+ESP [9] are substantially weaker: POP provides almost no protection (SIM ≈ 0.67–0.74, near clean), while POP+ESP is outperformed by E2E-VGuard on every model for both WER and SIM. In terms of imperceptibility, E2E-VGuard (UT) achieves the best SNR (18.5 dB) among methods with strong protection, and E2E-VGuard (T) reaches 20.5 dB SNR and 2.32 PESQ, surpassing AntiFake (12.8 dB, 1.76 PESQ) and SafeSpeech (7.6 dB, 1.41 PESQ), though POP (18.4 dB, 3.32 PESQ) retains a slight PESQ edge at the cost of negligible protection.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the adversarial-example-based voice protection line (AntiFake, AttackVC) and the unlearnable-example line (POP, SafeSpeech) by addressing two gaps: (1) LLM-based TTS models that operate on discrete audio tokens rather than continuous embeddings, and (2) the end-to-end scenario where transcripts are generated by an ASR system rather than manually annotated. It builds directly on AntiFake's encoder ensemble for timbre disruption and V-Cloak's psychoacoustic masking for imperceptibility, but introduces a novel pronunciation-protection channel by generating targeted adversarial examples against the ASR system to corrupt the text-audio alignment during fine-tuning. The combination of timbre-level (encoder ensemble + MFCC) and pronunciation-level (ASR-targeted AE) protection within a single multi-objective optimization is the key architectural departure from prior single-axis defenses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As commercial voice-cloning APIs (ByteDance, Alibaba, MiniMax) and LLM-based TTS systems become mainstream, the E2E fine-tuning scenario where adversaries only have audio (no transcripts) is the realistic threat model, and prior defenses that assume clean text are insufficient. The paper's validation across 19 open-source/commercial TTS models, 7 ASR systems, and real-world over-the-air recording demonstrates practical deployability. Key limitations include the ~100-second per-sample optimization time, dependence on a specific target ASR system (though transferability is shown), and residual protection degradation when adversaries have manually annotated text. Open questions include whether universal (ASR-agnostic) pronunciation attacks can match targeted ones, and how the defense holds against future TTS architectures that may not rely on speaker encoders at all.
