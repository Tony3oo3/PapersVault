## Problem

Existing speech tokenizers for language modeling suffer from three intertwined limitations: they rely on multi-layer residual vector quantization or high frame rates (typically >25 Hz), depend on external pre-trained SSL models for semantic distillation, and require complex two-stage training pipelines. No prior tokenizer achieves ultra-low token rates below 20 tokens/s with a single-layer codebook while maintaining reconstruction quality sufficient for downstream speech generation, leaving a significant gap between reconstruction fidelity and generation performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254932/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The tokenizer operates on mel-spectrograms (not raw waveforms) at 24 kHz, with the encoder producing a downsampled latent sequence that is quantized via Binary Spherical Quantization (L=14, yielding a single codebook of 2^14=16384 entries) at a frame rate of 6.25 Hz. The diffusion decoder reconstructs the mel-spectrogram conditioned on the quantized tokens, the corresponding text transcription, and an optional prompt prefix (up to 25% of the sequence length) that is preserved without noise. The final waveform is obtained via a standard vocoder, and the entire system achieves a bitrate of 0.0875 kbps.

## Method

TaDiCodec unifies quantization and reconstruction within a single end-to-end diffusion autoencoder, eliminating the need for adversarial training, commitment losses, or external SSL models. The encoder and decoder are fully Transformer-based (Llama-style with bidirectional attention, RoPE, and RMSNorm), and the decoder is a flow matching model trained to predict the velocity field v = x − ε along a linear interpolation path between Gaussian noise and the target mel-spectrogram. The key architectural insight is text-aware de-tokenization: the diffusion decoder is conditioned on the paired text sequence (adapted from a pretrained LLM vocabulary and concatenated along the time axis), which provides semantic guidance that enables extreme compression while preserving intelligibility. A prompt mechanism further reduces the quantizer's burden by providing a clean prefix as a global conditioning signal (e.g., speaker identity). Training uses only the diffusion loss with a Straight-Through Estimator for gradient flow through BSQ, and an optional decoder continued-training phase (freezing encoder and VQ) yields additional WER and SIM improvements.

## Results

TaDiCodec achieves an extremely low frame rate of 6.25 Hz and bitrate of 0.0875 kbps with a single-layer codebook (2^14 = 16,384 entries), while maintaining strong reconstruction quality: WER 2.73, SIM 0.69, and UTMOS 3.73 (with decoder continued-training) on SeedTTS test-en. For zero-shot TTS, TaDiCodec-AR (4B) achieves WER 2.28 (en) and 1.19 (zh), outperforming all baselines, with RTF as low as 0.13 using vLLM. The reconstruction-generation gap is notably small (−16.5% en WER, +26.5% zh WER), indicating the tokenizer is highly generation-friendly.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TaDiCodec outperforms all 18 baselines on the combined metric profile at a bitrate 3.5–17× lower. Against the closest compression-rate competitor, Ints (12.5 Hz, 0.175 kbps), TaDiCodec achieves substantially lower WER (2.73 vs. 7.14) and higher UTMOS (3.73 vs. 3.37) while matching SIM (0.69 vs. 0.67), and does so without two-stage training or semantic distillation. Among single-stage, distillation-free tokenizers, TaDiCodec beats BigCodec (WER 2.73 vs. 3.25, SIM 0.69 vs. 0.61) and TAAE (WER 2.73 vs. 11.08, SIM 0.69 vs. 0.41) at 12× and 4.6× lower bitrates respectively. The only baselines that narrowly beat TaDiCodec on individual metrics are DualCodec (WER 2.57 vs. 2.73, UTMOS 3.78 vs. 3.73) and X-codec2 (WER 2.63 vs. 2.73), both at 9–14× higher bitrates; TaDiCodec surpasses both on SIM (0.69 vs. 0.64/0.62). For zero-shot TTS, TaDiCodec-AR (4B) achieves the best WER on Regular en (2.28) and Regular zh (1.19), beating CosyVoice 2 (2.89/1.29), MaskGCT (2.40/2.28), and SparkTTS (2.50/1.78), with particularly large margins on challenging sets (e.g., Code-switching en: 9.16 vs. 28.32 for CosyVoice 2; Cross-lingual en2zh: 2.91 vs. 7.59 for CosyVoice 2). TaDiCodec-MGM (0.6B) matches or exceeds NAR baselines like MaskGCT and F5-TTS on most test sets while operating at 8× lower token rate (6.25 Hz vs. 50/93.75 Hz) and achieving RTF 0.12 vs. 0.47–0.59.

## Relation to Prior Work

TaDiCodec sits at the intersection of three prior threads: (1) flow-matching-based generative decoders (Lipman et al., Liu et al.) used in two-stage TTS pipelines like CosyVoice and FireRedTTS, (2) Binary Spherical Quantization (Zhao et al.) as a codebook-free alternative to standard VQ/RVQ, and (3) text-conditioned speech generation (Voicebox, F5-TTS). The key departure is unifying quantization and reconstruction into a single end-to-end diffusion autoencoder that conditions the decoder on both the quantized tokens and the target text, eliminating the need for pretrained SSL models for semantic distillation, adversarial training, or multi-stage pipelines that characterize prior tokenizers (Mimi, DualCodec, CosyVoice, FireRedTTS).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Achieving 6.25 Hz / 0.0875 kbps with a single codebook while matching or exceeding the WER, SIM, and UTMOS of tokenizers operating at 25–75 Hz with multi-layer RVQ and 10× higher bitrates is a substantial compression win for speech language modeling, directly reducing sequence length and compute for downstream AR or MGM models. The notably small (even negative) reconstruction-generation gap suggests the tokens transfer well to generation, a property prior tokenizers lack. Open questions include whether single-step or few-step inference can match multi-step quality (the authors note 5 steps already degrades performance), whether the text-conditioning requirement limits applicability to speech understanding or non-paired settings, and whether the scaling law hinted at by decoder-size ablations holds at larger scales.
