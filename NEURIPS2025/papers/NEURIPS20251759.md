## Problem

While finding global optima of contrastive learning objectives is known to be NP-hard, the computational complexity of finding local optima—representations that cannot be improved by gradient-based or other local search methods—remained completely open. This paper settles that question by proving that even finding a local optimum for standard contrastive objectives (maximizing satisfied triplets in Euclidean space or on trees, minimizing Triplet Loss) is PLS-hard or CLS-hard, implying no polynomial-time algorithm exists unless unlikely complexity class collapses occur.

## Input / Output

The discrete problems take a set of n items with m weighted contrastive triplets (anchor, positive, negative) and a target dimension d, producing an embedding f: V → R^d (or a binary rooted tree with n leaves) that is a local optimum under single-vertex moves. The continuous Triplet Loss problem additionally requires two fixed pivot points A, B in R^d (necessary to avoid trivial all-zeros stationary points) and a margin α > 0, and outputs a first-order stationary point of the sum of weighted hinge losses over the triplets. All embeddings map to non-overlapping points, and the tree problem restricts moves to the 2|V|−3 candidate leaf positions.

## Method

The paper constructs PLS-reductions from LOCALMAXCUT to discrete contrastive objectives using novel gadgets: heavy-weight 'boundary' triplets pin special vertices (X, Y, Z) so that data vertices are forced onto two segments (d=1) or two opposing rays (d≥2), where being on opposite sides encodes a graph cut. For the continuous Triplet Loss, a CLS-reduction from QUADRATICPROGRAM-KKT encodes each quadratic form q(x,y,z) using 12 carefully weighted contrastive triplets whose weights are solved from a linear system matching the quadratic coefficients, with dual triplets canceling unwanted cross-terms. The reductions work even for d=1 and for linear embedding models f_θ(x) = θ^⊤x, and the paper experimentally verifies exponential local search runtimes by transforming known hard LOCALMAXCUT instances from Monien and Tscheuschner (2010) through the reductions.

## Results

The paper proves that LOCALCONTRASTIVE-EUCLIDEAN (even for d=1), LOCALCONTRASTIVE-TREE, and LOCALBETWEENNESS-EUCLIDEAN are PLS-hard, and LOCALTRIPLETLOSS-EUCLIDEAN is CLS-hard, implying no polynomial-time algorithm can find a local optimum unless PLS ⊆ P or CLS ⊆ P. Experimentally, using hard instances from Monien and Tscheuschner (2010), local search requires exponentially many iterations to reach a local optimum: for instance H_8 (233 vertices), local search takes 13,271 iterations, and for H_9 (261 vertices) it exceeds 26,000, growing roughly exponentially. Crucially, the iteration counts are identical across all four problems (LOCALMAXCUT, LOCALCONTRASTIVE-EUCLIDEAN, LOCALBETWEENNESS-EUCLIDEAN, LOCALCONTRASTIVE-TREE), confirming the reductions preserve local search dynamics exactly.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical complexity paper, so there are no method-vs-method performance comparisons. Instead, the paper establishes hardness by reducing from two well-known hard problems: LOCALMAXCUT (Schäffer & Yannakakis, 1991) for the discrete PLS-hardness results, and QUADRATICPROGRAM-KKT (Fearnley et al., STOC 2024) for the continuous CLS-hardness result. The key comparison is across the four contrastive learning problems themselves: the paper shows that LOCALCONTRASTIVE-EUCLIDEAN, LOCALBETWEENNESS-EUCLIDEAN, and LOCALCONTRASTIVE-TREE all inherit the same PLS-hardness from LOCALMAXCUT, while LOCALTRIPLETLOSS-EUCLIDEAN inherits CLS-hardness from QUADRATICPROGRAM-KKT. The experimental verification confirms that the reductions preserve local search dynamics exactly — all four problems require the same number of iterations on the transformed hard instances, with iteration counts growing exponentially in instance size. The paper also notes that random initialization typically avoids the worst-case exponential behavior, leaving the average-case complexity as an open question.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of computational complexity theory and contrastive learning, applying the PLS (Polynomial Local Search) and CLS (Continuous Local Search) frameworks—originally developed for discrete and continuous local search problems respectively—to the specific setting of contrastive triplet objectives. The PLS-hardness reductions draw on the classic LOCALMAXCUT problem (Schäffer & Yannakakis, 1991), while the CLS-hardness reduction leverages the recent CLS-completeness of QUADRATIC PROGRAM-KKT (Fearnley et al., 2024) and the CLS = PPAD ∩ PLS equivalence (Fearnley et al., 2023). The contrastive objectives themselves—triplet maximization in Euclidean space and on trees, and the Triplet Loss from FaceNet (Schroff et al., 2015)—are well-studied in the contrastive learning and ordinal embedding literature, but their local-search complexity had not been formally analyzed before.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first formal complexity-theoretic analysis of local optima in contrastive learning, establishing that no polynomial-time algorithm (local search or otherwise) can find a local optimum for these objectives unless PLS ⊆ P or CLS ⊆ P. The unconditional consequence—that there exist instances where gradient-based local search requires exponential time even for d=1 line embeddings—provides a rigorous worst-case warning for practitioners relying on gradient descent for triplet loss. Key open questions include whether these worst-case dynamics persist under random initialization or smoothed analysis, and whether the quality of local solutions (not just the time to reach them) is also hard to guarantee.
