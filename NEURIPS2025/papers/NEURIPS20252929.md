## Problem

In simulation-based inference (SBI), the likelihood function is intractable because the data distribution is defined only through a simulator, yet many scientific fields prefer fast, gradient-based maximum likelihood estimation over computationally demanding Bayesian methods. Existing SBI tools are predominantly Bayesian (ABC, neural density estimators), leaving MLE practitioners without comparably mature, simulation-efficient gradient-based techniques for point estimation.

## Input / Output

The method takes N i.i.d. observed data points, access to a simulator that generates samples for any parameter setting, an initial parameter θ₀, and a scalar proposal variance σ that controls the width of the local smoothing region. At each iterate it produces a closed-form linear Fisher score model (a weight matrix W) that, when evaluated at the observations, yields an approximate gradient of the log-likelihood; the final output is a Polyak-Ruppert averaged parameter estimate approximating the MLE, with the Fisher information matrix available for uncertainty quantification. The procedure is inherently sequential and local—only simulations in a small neighborhood of the current iterate are needed at each step, rather than a global likelihood approximation.

## Method

The core contribution is a local Fisher score matching (FSM) technique that adapts classical score matching to estimate the Fisher score ∇_θ log p(x|θ) (gradient w.r.t. parameters) rather than the Stein score (gradient w.r.t. data). A local isotropic Gaussian proposal q(θ|θ_t) = N(θ_t, σ²I) is placed around the current parameter iterate, and an integration-by-parts trick eliminates the intractable likelihood from the matching objective, leaving only the tractable proposal gradient. A linear score model S_W(x) = W^T x is used, yielding a closed-form least-squares solution via normal equations—no inner neural network optimization loop is required. Under the Gaussian proposal, the Bayes-optimal FSM estimator is proven equivalent to the gradient of a Gaussian-smoothed likelihood, establishing a direct connection to zeroth-order optimization and inheriting its robustness to non-smooth likelihoods. The resulting approximate gradient drives a sequential SGD/Adam loop, with a bias-variance trade-off governed by σ: larger σ reduces estimator variance but increases smoothing bias, while smaller σ sharpens the estimate at the cost of higher variance.

## Results

In the multivariate Gaussian numerical study, FSM matches the gradient approximation accuracy of KDE-SP given sufficient simulation budget, even under unfavourable hyperparameter choices (Figure 3). In higher-dimensional parameter spaces, FSM maintains parameter estimation quality while KDE-SP degrades significantly (Figure 4). On the LSST weak-lensing cosmology benchmark, sequential gradient-based methods (FSM and KDE-SP) outperform the neural likelihood estimator (NLE) under the same simulation budget, with FSM achieving better parameter estimation accuracy and smaller variance (Figure 5). In the GAN inversion task on 16×16 MNIST, FSM recovers target observations with reconstruction error comparable to direct optimization, while KDE-SP produces visibly degraded pixel quality (Figures 6–7).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is KDE-SP (Bertl et al., 2017), which estimates a log-likelihood via kernel density estimation and applies a simultaneous perturbation gradient estimator. FSM matches or outperforms KDE-SP across all experimental settings: in the 2D Gaussian model, FSM achieves comparable gradient accuracy with a more robust hyperparameter sensitivity (Figure 3); in higher dimensions, KDE-SP's parameter estimation degrades while FSM remains stable (Figure 4); in the LSST weak-lensing task, FSM achieves better parameter estimation with smaller variance than KDE-SP (Figure 5); and in GAN inversion, KDE-SP fails to recover pixel-level detail that FSM achieves (Figures 6–7). The second baseline is a neural likelihood estimator (NLE) implemented via the SBI package, trained with the same total simulation budget. Both sequential gradient-based methods (FSM and KDE-SP) outperform NLE on the LSST benchmark, with FSM showing the best overall accuracy and lowest variance, suggesting that the amortized NLE approach is simulation-inefficient relative to the sequential FSM approach under matched budgets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts classical score matching (Hyvärinen and Dayan, 2005), which targets data-space gradients for density estimation, to the parameter-space Fisher score for likelihood maximization in simulation-based inference. The closest prior method is Bertl et al. (2017), which estimates the likelihood via kernel density estimation before applying simultaneous perturbation stochastic approximation; FSM merges density and gradient estimation into a single step. A key conceptual departure is the demonstration that local Fisher score matching under a Gaussian proposal is exactly equivalent to the Gaussian smoothing gradient estimator (Nesterov and Spokoiny, 2017; Duchi et al., 2012), providing a unifying lens between zeroth-order optimization and likelihood-free MLE. Independent concurrent work by Sui et al. (2025) proposes a similar Fisher score matching estimator but targets broader forecasting rather than SBI-specific MLE.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method offers a simulation-efficient, closed-form (linear) gradient estimator for MLE in SBI that avoids the computational overhead of KDE or neural likelihood estimation, with practical demonstrated gains in cosmological weak-lensing and GAN inversion tasks. The Gaussian smoothing equivalence inherits robustness to non-smooth likelihoods and provides a bias bound scaling with σ, making the bias-variance trade-off explicit and actionable. Open questions include principled selection of the proposal variance σ (currently grid-searched), extending beyond isotropic Gaussian proposals for anisotropic curvature, and developing a semi-amortized variant coupling a pretrained encoder with the linear FSM model. The method's sequential nature also limits its applicability to amortized inference settings where many parameter queries are needed.
