## Problem

Despite extensive empirical progress in neural network pruning, there is no unified theoretical framework explaining why different pruning methods produce sparse networks with markedly different trainability at the same sparsity level. Standard NTK theory cannot be directly applied to sparse networks because the infinite-width limit of sparse networks of varying sizes is ill-defined, leaving the relationship between connectivity patterns and training dynamics largely unexplained.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251775/cropped_page5_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes binary pruning masks (biadjacency matrices between adjacent MLP layers) at a fixed sparsity level and produces a limiting graphon — a symmetric measurable function on [0,1]² encoding the connection probability between continuous neuron positions. From this graphon, the Graphon NTK is derived as a deterministic kernel, and its spectral metrics (eigenvalue decay rate, effective rank, spectral gap, and top-5 eigenvalue energy concentration) serve as training-free indicators of sparse network trainability. The graphon estimation uses degree-based sorting (SAS method) to approximate the measure-preserving transformation needed for cut-distance convergence.

## Method

The core contribution is the Graphon Limit Hypothesis, which posits that sequences of pruning masks from increasingly wide networks converge layer-wise to deterministic graphons in cut distance, with each pruning method (Random, SNIP, GraSP, Synflow) yielding a distinct limiting graphon. The Graphon NTK is derived by treating graphon values as position-dependent weight variances, converting the discrete forward pass into integral equations over [0,1], and computing the gradient inner product in the infinite-width limit under Lindeberg-Feller conditions for the non-i.i.d. setting. This yields a kernel where graphon functions explicitly modulate signal propagation through position-dependent covariance structures, in contrast to the uniform covariance of standard NTK. For Random Pruning (constant graphon), the framework recovers the standard NTK scaled by c^L, while structured methods produce non-uniform graphons that concentrate kernel energy in dominant eigen-directions, empirically correlating with their faster early training convergence.

## Results

The paper's empirical results are primarily qualitative and spectral rather than accuracy-based. On MNIST with 4-layer MLPs (hidden size 1024), SNIP and Synflow subnetworks exhibit faster initial training loss reduction than Random Pruning across all sparsity levels (50%–95%) in the first 200 gradient steps. Spectral analysis of the Graphon NTK reveals that Random Pruning maintains a stable eigenvalue decay rate and high effective rank (consistent with uniform kernel scaling by c^L), while SNIP and Synflow increasingly concentrate kernel energy in the top-5 eigenvalues as sparsity grows, correlating with their faster early convergence. All four PaI methods (Random, SNIP, GraSP, Synflow) demonstrate monotonic convergence to distinct graphons in cut distance as network width increases from 100 to 2000.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four Pruning at Initialisation methods—Random, SNIP [43], GraSP [65], and Synflow [62]—under a unified graphon framework rather than a standard accuracy benchmark. In the graphon limit analysis (Figure 1), Random Pruning converges to a constant (Erdős-Rényi) graphon, SNIP and GraSP produce structured graphons with density gradients favouring high-centrality nodes, and Synflow yields a block-like graphon with sharp transitions that strongly prioritises high-centrality connections. In training dynamics (Figure 2), SNIP and Synflow consistently outperform Random Pruning in initial convergence speed across all sparsity levels from 50% to 95%, while GraSP is only evaluated in the graphon limit. In spectral analysis (Figure 3), Random Pruning's Graphon NTK shows uniform eigenvalue scaling (consistent with the theoretical c^L factor), whereas SNIP and Synflow exhibit increasing energy concentration in dominant eigen-directions at higher sparsity, which the authors link to their faster early training. The paper positions its framework as generalising the prior NTK analysis of randomly pruned networks [74], recovering that result as the constant-graphon special case.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate threads: graph limit theory (Lovász & Szegedy, Borgs et al.) and neural tangent kernel analysis (Jacot et al., Lee et al.), applying them to the problem of sparse network trainability. It directly generalizes the NTK analysis of randomly pruned networks by Yang & Wang (AISTATS 2023), which showed random pruning preserves the standard NTK up to a constant scaling factor, by replacing the constant graphon with arbitrary graphon limits induced by structured pruning methods (SNIP, GraSP, Synflow). The continuous network formulation with position-dependent weight variances extends the graphon neural network framework of Ruiz et al. (NeurIPS 2020) from GNNs to MLP weight connectivity, and the SAS-based graphon estimation (Chan & Airoldi, ICML 2014) is repurposed to visualize pruning mask limits.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Graphon NTK provides the first principled, training-free theoretical tool for comparing how different pruning strategies affect convergence speed, potentially guiding the design of future PaI algorithms that target specific spectral properties. However, the foundational Graphon Limit Hypothesis remains empirically motivated rather than proven, and all experiments are confined to 4-layer MLPs on MNIST, leaving open whether the framework extends to CNNs, Transformers, deeper networks, or dynamic/iterative pruning. A key open question is whether graphon-guided mask design can close the theory-practice gap for finite-width networks, and whether the spectral predictions hold beyond the narrow experimental scope tested here.
