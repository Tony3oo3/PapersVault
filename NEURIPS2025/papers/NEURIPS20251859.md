## Problem

Most species recognition models are trained under a closed-world assumption, yet in biodiversity monitoring, unknown species (undescribed, invasive, or out-of-scope) are routinely encountered. Existing open-set recognition benchmarks are derived from coarse-grained datasets like ImageNet and do not capture the extreme fine-grained similarity and long-tailed distribution of biodiversity data, particularly for poorly documented taxa like insects where over 80% of species remain undescribed.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251859/cropped_page0_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes high-resolution RGB images of moths and non-moth arthropods captured by ultraviolet camera traps and produces a species-level class label for closed-set species plus a scalar open-set detection score. The dataset is organized into three geographic regions (NE-America, W-Europe, C-America), each with closed-set, local open-set, non-local open-set, non-moth open-set, and auxiliary splits, with a small BCI split of 197 images of potentially undescribed species for realistic validation. The local open-set species are taxonomically closer (more 1-hop same-genus species) to the closed-set than non-local species, creating a graduated difficulty axis.

## Method

The core contribution is the Open-Insect benchmark, which curates ~2.9M images from GBIF and the AMI dataset into region-specific splits designed to minimize covariate shift while varying semantic difficulty via geographic proximity. The paper benchmarks 38 OSR methods across three categories (post-hoc, training-time regularization, training with auxiliary data) using a ResNet-50 backbone trained from scratch for 120 epochs, with AUROC as the primary metric. Two simple baselines for auxiliary-data training are proposed: NovelBranch (adds a C+1 dimensional head treating all auxiliary species as one novel class) and Extended (adds a C+A dimensional head treating each auxiliary species as a separate class), both sharing the feature extractor with the original C-dimensional closed-set head and trained jointly with cross-entropy. The auxiliary data is curated to be taxonomically realistic (same order, excluding genera present in open-sets) rather than generic, and the paper demonstrates that this realistic selection outperforms TinyImageNet by ~5 AUROC points.

## Results

Across 38 OSR methods on three geographic regions, MSP-based post-hoc methods (TempScale, RP_MSP) achieve the strongest local open-set AUROC: 86.8 on NE-America, 86.0 on W-Europe, and 86.8 on C-America. Training with auxiliary data (Energy) yields the best overall local AUROC of 87.4 on NE-America and 95.1 on non-local. With ImageNet-pretrained ViT-B-16 weights, the proposed NovelBranch and Extended baselines reach 92.05 and 91.19 AUROC on C-America local open-set, the highest reported. All best methods exceed 85% AUROC on local open-set, confirming strong potential for automated species discovery.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MSP and its variants (TempScale, RP_MSP) consistently outperform all other post-hoc methods on local and non-local open-sets across all three regions, with TempScale leading on NE-America (86.8 AUROC local) and W-Europe (86.0) and RP_MSP leading on C-America (86.8). Training-time regularization methods (LogitNorm, ARPL, G-ODIN) fail to surpass MSP on local open-set for the well-documented regions (NE-America, W-Europe) but show gains on C-America where the closed-set is smaller (LogitNorm: 87.6 vs. MSP 86.7). Training with auxiliary data provides the clearest improvement: Energy achieves 87.4 AUROC on NE-America local (vs. 86.8 for TempScale) and 95.1 on non-local (vs. 93.7), while on C-America the auxiliary-data methods (Energy, OE) reach 89.5 AUROC local, a 2.7 pp gain over the best post-hoc. The ablation in Table 4 confirms that realistic auxiliary data (Open-Insect C-America species) outperforms TinyImageNet by 4.82 pp on local and 5.54 pp on non-local AUROC. With ImageNet-pretrained ViT-B-16 weights, the proposed NovelBranch baseline achieves 92.05 AUROC on C-America local open-set, surpassing all other methods by over 2 pp, and BioCLIP-pretrained weights further improve to 90.22 AUROC on a subset excluding species in BioCLIP's training set.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of open-set recognition (OSR) and biodiversity monitoring, building on the extensive OSR/OOD detection literature (MSP, ODIN, Energy, LogitNorm, etc.) and large-scale species recognition datasets (iNaturalist, AMI, BIOSCAN). It departs from prior OSR benchmarks (OpenOOD, SSB, COOD, iNat21-OSR) in three key ways: (1) focusing on extremely fine-grained insect species rather than birds or ImageNet categories, (2) using geographic proximity as a structured difficulty axis (local vs. non-local open-sets) grounded in Tobler's first law of geography, and (3) providing a consistent auxiliary dataset across all methods, which prior benchmarks lacked. The proposed NovelBranch and Extended baselines repurpose the multi-head classification idea from auxiliary-data OSR methods but simplify them to two linear heads, showing that the core benefit comes from data curation rather than architectural complexity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

With ~90% of Earth's species undescribed and most existing OSR benchmarks derived from ImageNet or well-studied vertebrates, this benchmark fills a critical gap for the poorly documented, highly diverse taxa where species discovery actually matters. The finding that simple post-hoc methods (MSP, TempScale) remain competitive with complex training-time approaches on fine-grained OSR provides practical guidance for resource-constrained field deployments, while the demonstration that realistic auxiliary data (regionally relevant moth species) outperforms generic data (TinyImageNet) by ~5 pp offers an actionable curation principle. Open questions include whether these results extend to lower-resolution camera-trap imagery, other insect orders, and whether the geographic-difficulty framing generalizes beyond Lepidoptera. The BCI validation with potentially undescribed species strengthens confidence in real-world applicability but remains a small (197-image) test case.
