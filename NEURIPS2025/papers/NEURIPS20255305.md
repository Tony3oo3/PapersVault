## Problem

Frontier LLMs are increasingly accessible only through closed-source APIs, making white-box interpretability methods (hidden-state probes, activation analysis) inapplicable. This paper asks whether reliable, instance-level predictions of model behavior—correctness, adversarial manipulation, and model identity—can be made using only black-box input-output interactions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255305/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Given a text question and the LLM's greedy response, the method appends ~50 follow-up questions (e.g., 'Do you think your answer is correct?') and extracts the probability of the 'yes' token for each as a d-dimensional feature vector. On closed-ended tasks, answer probability distributions and pre/post-confidence scores are concatenated to this vector. The output is a binary label (correct/incorrect, clean/adversarial, model A/model B) produced by a linear classifier; when top-k probabilities are unavailable, features are approximated via k Bernoulli samples per question.

## Method

QueRE constructs a low-dimensional representation by querying the LLM with a fixed set of ~50 follow-up questions (a small hand-specified set plus 40 GPT-4-generated yes/no questions) and using the probability of the 'yes' token under each question as one feature dimension. A simple logistic regression (or linear probe) is then trained on these features to predict a downstream label. The key insight is that the distribution over yes/no responses to self-inquiry questions meaningfully varies with correctness, model family, and adversarial influence, making these token-level probabilities highly informative even without access to model internals. The authors prove that when true probabilities are unavailable, averaging k Bernoulli samples per question yields a consistent MLE converging at rate O(1/√n + √n/k), and empirically show that even small k (e.g., 5–10 samples) preserves performance. All follow-up queries can be issued in parallel, keeping computational overhead minimal.

## Results

On open-ended QA benchmarks (NQ, SQuAD), QueRE achieves AUROC of 0.95–0.96 on LLaMA3 models (3B/8B/70B), dramatically outperforming white-box RepE (0.65–0.71) and all black-box baselines (0.50–0.85). On reasoning tasks (GSM8K, Code Contests), QueRE reaches 0.77 and 0.79 AUROC respectively, while the next-best black-box method (Semantic Entropy) achieves only 0.75 and 0.56. For adversarial system-prompt detection, QueRE with a linear probe achieves 0.93–1.00 accuracy, outperforming all alternatives including white-box Full Logits (0.88–0.95).

## Baselines & Comparisons

QueRE most dramatically outperforms the white-box RepE baseline on open-ended QA: on NQ with LLaMA3-3B, QueRE reaches 0.96 AUROC versus RepE's 0.66, a gap of nearly 30 points. Against the other white-box method (Full Logits), QueRE wins on all open-ended QA settings (e.g., 0.96 vs. 0.59 on NQ/LLaMA3-3B) and is competitive on closed-ended tasks, though RepE and Full Logits retain an edge on DHate and CS QA for LLaMA3-70B. Among black-box baselines, QueRE consistently beats Post-conf (0.51–0.85), Self-Consistency (0.46–0.83), and Semantic Entropy (0.50–0.79) across nearly all datasets and model scales. The one notable exception is Post-conf on SQuAD/LLaMA3-3B (0.92 AUROC), where it approaches QueRE's 0.96. On reasoning benchmarks (GSM8K, Code Contests), QueRE's advantage over Semantic Entropy is most pronounced (0.77 vs. 0.75 on GSM8K/GPT-3.5; 0.79 vs. 0.56 on Code Contests/GPT-4o-mini), while all other baselines cluster near chance (0.42–0.60). For adversarial detection, QueRE achieves 0.93–1.00 accuracy, beating Full Logits (0.88–0.95) and all other methods by a wide margin.

## Relation to Prior Work

QueRE departs from the white-box interpretability line (RepE, mechanistic circuit analysis) by requiring only input-output access, and from the uncertainty quantification literature (Xiong et al. 2023, Kadavath et al. 2022) by extracting token-level 'yes' probabilities across ~50 diverse follow-up questions rather than relying on a single verbalized confidence score. Conceptually, it repurposes the LLM's own self-assessment behavior as a black-box feature extractor, drawing an analogy to weak learners in boosting or weak labelers in programmatic weak supervision, but applied to model behavior prediction rather than data labeling. The most directly related prior work is Pacchiardi et al. (2024), which asks unrelated questions to detect untruthfulness in black-box LLMs, and MacDiarmid et al. (2024), which detects sleeper agents via white-box probes; QueRE generalizes both to a broader set of prediction and detection tasks under strict black-box constraints.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is substantial: as frontier LLMs become increasingly accessible only through closed-source APIs, the ability to monitor correctness, detect adversarial manipulation (e.g., hidden system-prompt injections), and verify model identity without internal access is critical for safe deployment in autonomous and high-stakes systems. Key limitations include the latency cost of multiple follow-up queries per example, the assumption that model responses to self-inquiry questions meaningfully vary (which may fail for very low-quality or heavily sycophantic models), and the lack of interpretability in the extracted features. Open questions include how to optimally select or prune follow-up questions, whether the approach degrades for models specifically trained to resist such probing, and whether the black-box ≈ white-box equivalence holds as model architectures and training objectives evolve.
