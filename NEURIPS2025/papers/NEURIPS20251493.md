## Problem

Simpson's paradox creates a decision-making impasse where aggregated and fine-grained probabilities yield contradictory conclusions about the association between two events. Existing resolutions rely on time-ordered directed acyclic graphs (TODAGs) or exchangeability assumptions that do not hold for many real-world examples (e.g., smoking/survival, COVID-19 mortality), leaving the paradox unresolved in precisely the cases where it matters most. The paper also shows the paradox is not a data artifact: under an unbiased Dirichlet prior it occurs in ~4.29% of binary three-variable configurations.

## Input / Output

For the discrete case, the method consumes the joint probability table p(A1, A2, B) and the conditional probabilities p(A1|A2, B) — no information about the common cause C is required beyond knowing it exists and is binary. For the continuous case, it takes Gaussian covariance matrices (block-structured as Q + CSC^T) and mean vectors. The output is a deterministic resolution: the sign of the association between a1 and a2 and which decision option (aggregated vs. fine-grained) to follow. A key constraint is that the resolution is guaranteed only for minimal (binary or scalar) common causes; for tertiary or higher-dimensional C, all paradox options remain possible and detailed knowledge of C becomes necessary.

## Method

The core mechanism is to postulate a (typically unobserved) common cause or screening variable C that renders A and B conditionally independent, per Reichenbach's common cause principle. The central result (Theorem 1) proves that when A1, A2, B, and C are all binary, every admissible binary C yields the same sign of association p(a1|a2,c) − p(a1|ā2,c) as the fine-grained option, thereby resolving the paradox without needing to identify C. The proof proceeds by algebraically inverting the screening equation p(A1,A2,B) = Σc p(c)p(A1,A2|c)p(B|c) to express the unknown conditional p(a1|a2,c) in terms of the observed joint probabilities and free parameters p(B|C). For Gaussian variables, an analogous result (Theorem 2) is derived via block-matrix inversion of the covariance structure, showing that in the minimal three-scalar-plus-scalar-cause setting the conditional covariance always shares the sign of the common-cause-conditioned covariance. This approach differs from TODAG/do-calculus methods because it requires only the existence of a minimal common cause rather than a fully specified causal graph, and it provides a counter-example showing that DAG structure alone cannot determine which paradox option applies.

## Results

The paper's central quantitative finding is that Simpson's paradox occurs with frequency ≈4.29% ± 0.001% when the 8 joint probabilities of three binary variables are drawn from the non-informative Dirichlet density (α_k = 1/n), compared to ≈1.66% under the homogeneous prior (α=1) and ≈2.67% under Jeffreys' prior (α=0.5). Theorem 1 proves that for binary A₁, A₂, B, and a binary common cause C, all admissible C yield the same sign of association as the fine-grained option, resolving the paradox definitively. Theorem 2 extends this to the minimal Gaussian case (3 scalar variables + scalar cause), showing the conditional covariance sign always matches the fine-grained option. Two applied examples (smoking/survival and COVID-19 Italy vs. China) demonstrate the resolution in practice, with the common-cause approach reversing the conclusion of the TODAG-based analysis in the COVID-19 case.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against competing ML methods but instead critiques and supersedes four previously proposed resolutions of Simpson's paradox. (1) Barigelli & Scozzafava's retrodiction criterion (replacing prediction with retrodiction) is shown to be conceptually different from the original decision problem and to depend on the ratio p(a₂)/p(ā₂), making it sensitive to treatment allocation. (2) Rudas' modified criterion shares the same allocation-dependence drawback. (3) Lindley & Novick's exchangeability-based resolution and Pearl's TODAG/do-calculus approach are shown to be inapplicable to several important examples (e.g., the medical treatment and plant yield examples need not support the assumed causal arrows), and the do-calculus only resolves the paradox for one of the two TODAG structures (7) but not (8). (4) The paper provides a counter-example (tertiary C in Eq. 17) showing that TODAG structure alone cannot determine which option of the paradox applies, whereas the common-cause framework with a binary C gives a unique, data-free resolution. The common-cause approach thus wins on generality (applies without requiring a specific TODAG), on definitiveness (binary C gives a unique answer without additional data), and on scope (extends to Gaussian variables where standard DAG methods are inapplicable).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on Reichenbach's and Suppes' common cause principle to resolve Simpson's paradox, departing from the dominant TODAG/do-calculus framework of Pearl and the exchangeability approach of Lindley and Novick. The authors argue that the TODAG structures typically assumed for the paradox (e.g., sex → treatment → recovery) are not always physically justified, and that a common cause (or screening) variable C provides a more general and defensible resolution. The key conceptual departure is showing that TODAG structure alone cannot determine which option of the paradox applies (via a tertiary-C counter-example), while the mere existence of a binary C suffices to select the fine-grained option without observing C.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first resolution of Simpson's paradox for Gaussian (continuous) variables, which is more frequent in practice and relevant to big-data and machine-learning settings. The clean binary-variable theorem—that any binary common cause resolves the paradox in favor of the fine-grained option without requiring C to be observed—offers practical guidance for decision-making under uncertainty. Key limitations include the restriction to binary C for a definitive resolution (non-binary C requires detailed knowledge), the assumption of a precise (not approximate) screening relation, and the fact that all worked examples rely on inferred rather than observed common causes. Open questions include extending the resolution to approximate common causes (as in NMF or PLSI) and to higher-dimensional continuous settings.
