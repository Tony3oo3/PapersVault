## Problem

Pseudo-labeling in semi-supervised regression (SSR) is fundamentally harder than in classification because continuous outputs with heteroscedastic noise make it difficult to assess pseudo-label reliability, and naive pseudo-labeling causes error accumulation and overfitting to incorrect labels. Existing SSR methods rely primarily on consistency regularization without an effective mechanism to dynamically calibrate the influence of unreliable pseudo-labels during training.

## Input / Output

The method consumes facial images (UTKFace, IMDB-WIKI) or sentence pairs (STS-B) with a small labeled subset (5–20%) and a large unlabeled pool. Pseudo-labels are generated from weakly augmented inputs while model predictions come from strongly augmented versions of the same samples (FixMatch-style). The uncertainty-learner does not see the raw input; instead it takes the regression model's prediction and the corresponding pseudo-label as a 2-dimensional input to produce a per-sample log-variance, which is then used to weight the unsupervised loss.

## Method

The paper proposes a bi-level optimization framework with two coupled components: a regression network f_θ (ResNet-50 or BiLSTM) and a lightweight uncertainty-learner g_φ (single-hidden-layer MLP). In the inner loop, f_θ is trained on labeled data plus pseudo-labeled data where each pseudo-label is weighted by a heteroscedastic Gaussian NLL loss whose variance σ_j² is predicted by g_φ; in the outer loop, g_φ is updated by minimizing the regression loss on a separate held-out batch of labeled data, effectively using labeled-data generalization as the signal for calibrating uncertainty. This resolves a key ambiguity in naive joint training—where both 'hard but correct' and 'easy but incorrect' samples produce large squared errors—by ensuring uncertainty selectively suppresses unreliable pseudo-labels without suppressing learning on genuinely difficult samples. A theoretical result (Theorem 1) shows the bi-level objective is equivalent to maximizing gradient alignment between the inner and outer losses with respect to θ. For computational efficiency, second-order derivatives are unrolled only through the single-layer regression head rather than the full network, adding less than 9 ms and ~17 MB GPU memory per iteration over the baseline.

## Results

On IMDB-WIKI (the largest dataset, 191.5K training images), the proposed method achieves MAE of 9.177 and R² of 0.664 at 5% labeled data, outperforming the best baseline (Mean Teacher, MAE 9.492) by 3.3% in MAE. At 20% labeled data, it reaches MAE 8.166 / R² 0.712, approaching the fully-supervised upper bound (MAE 7.974 / R² 0.724). On UTKFace at 5% labels, the method achieves MAE 5.639 / R² 0.523, and on STS-B at 5% labels it achieves MSE 1.540 / R² 0.270, both the best among all compared methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all 11 baselines across all three datasets and all label ratios. Against the strongest consistency-based baselines, it beats RankUp [18] by 1.4% MAE on UTKFace (5.639 vs. 5.719 at γ=5%) and by 3.3% MAE on IMDB-WIKI (9.177 vs. 10.251 at γ=5%), and surpasses UCVME [10] by 5.8% MAE on IMDB-WIKI (9.177 vs. 9.730 at γ=5%). Among uncertainty-based methods, it outperforms SimRegMatch [21] by 7.3% MAE on IMDB-WIKI (9.177 vs. 9.908) and SSDKL [20] by 9.5% MAE (9.177 vs. 10.116) at γ=5%. The classification-derived baselines (Mean Teacher [43], Temporal Ensembling [26]) and contrastive/ranking methods (CLSS [9], TNNR [47]) are all consistently surpassed, with the largest gaps appearing at low label ratios (5–10%), confirming that the heteroscedastic uncertainty calibration is most beneficial when labeled data is scarce. In the extended comparison (Table 7), the method also beats PIM [7] (8.166 vs. 8.441 MAE at γ=20%), UPS_Reg [36] (8.166 vs. 8.415), and CADR [17] (8.166 vs. 8.536) on IMDB-WIKI.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends pseudo-labeling from semi-supervised classification to semi-supervised regression (SSR), a setting where the continuous output space and heteroscedastic noise make confidence-based filtering inapplicable. It departs from prior SSR methods that rely on consistency regularization (TNNR, UCVME, RankUp) or ad-hoc uncertainty estimation (SSDKL's Gaussian Process, SimRegMatch's MC Dropout) by introducing a bi-level optimization framework: an inner loop trains the regression model with uncertainty-weighted pseudo-labels, while an outer loop explicitly optimizes a lightweight uncertainty-learner to ensure the resulting model generalizes well on held-out labeled data. The heteroscedastic Gaussian noise model for pseudo-labels draws on Bayesian regression (Kendall & Gal), but the key novelty is the gradient-alignment theorem (Theorem 1) that shows the bi-level objective is equivalent to aligning inner and outer gradients, providing a principled mechanism to suppress incorrect pseudo-labels without suppressing hard-but-correct ones.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper fills a genuine gap in SSR by showing that pseudo-labeling, long the dominant paradigm in semi-supervised classification, can be made effective for regression when uncertainty is properly calibrated through bi-level optimization. The practical overhead is minimal (~17 MB extra GPU, <9 ms per iteration), making the approach broadly adoptable. However, the authors acknowledge that the framework does not address systematic biases in labeled/unlabeled data (e.g., demographic imbalances in age estimation) and assumes simultaneous access to both data splits, raising privacy concerns in sensitive applications. Open questions include whether the bi-level formulation extends to multi-output or non-Gaussian regression settings, and whether the gradient-alignment principle generalizes beyond the single-head unrolling approximation used for efficiency.
