## Problem

Multimodal reward models (CLIP-based scorers, open-source VLMs, and closed-source VLMs) are widely deployed to provide feedback for aligning text-to-image generation models, yet their capabilities and failure modes have never been systematically evaluated across the full range of alignment objectives. Without understanding where these judges are accurate versus biased or unreliable, practitioners risk misaligning or even making models less safe through RLAIF fine-tuning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252081/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance is a triplet (instruction, chosen image, rejected image) drawn from 22 fine-grained subcategories across six perspectives. Single-input judges (CLIP scorers, single-image VLMs) receive one image plus the prompt and produce a scalar score; the preference is determined by comparing the two scores against a configurable tie threshold. Multi-input VLMs receive both images simultaneously and are prompted with an Analyze-then-Judge CoT rubric, outputting a preference in either numerical [0,5] or [0,10] scale or a 5-point Likert scale. For the bias perspective, the judge scores each demographic variant independently, and fairness is quantified via three metrics (ACC, NDS, GES) rather than win rate. End-to-end evaluation fine-tunes SD-1.5 via DPO or DDPO on judge feedback, then human evaluators rank the resulting models on 100 test prompts per perspective.

## Method

MJ-BENCH constructs a human-verified preference dataset of 8K samples (with a 2K confidence-sampled subset) through a three-stage pipeline: VLM pre-filtering with LLaVA-NeXT-34B, human verification with difficulty ratings, and confidence-interval-based subsampling using three surrogate models. The dataset spans six perspectives—text-image alignment (object, attribute, action, spatial, count), safety (toxicity: crime/shocking/disgust; NSFW: evident/subtle/evasive), quality (faces, limbs, objects, color, lighting, texture via blur and distortion), bias (80 occupations × 5 demographic dimensions; 60 education majors × 3 dimensions), composition (physics, perspective, occlusion), and visualization (coherence, OCR, structure)—with negative images constructed via controlled perturbations (inpainting, Gaussian/motion blur, geometric editing) to isolate specific failure modes. The benchmark evaluates 20+ judges across four architectural families (scoring models, single-input VLMs, multi-input VLMs, and decomposition/probability-based judges) using both automatic preference accuracy and end-to-end human evaluation of DPO/DDPO fine-tuned SD-1.5 models, revealing that closed-source VLMs (especially GPT-4o and OpenAI o1) lead overall, while smaller CLIP-based scorers outperform open-source VLMs on alignment and quality, and VLMs dominate on safety and bias due to stronger reasoning.

## Results

On MJ-BENCH, closed-source VLMs dominate across all six perspectives. OpenAI o1 achieves the highest average accuracy, reaching 81.0% (w/o tie) on alignment, 97.8% on safety, 90.1% on quality, 74.3% on composition, and 83.6% on visualization. Gemini-2.5-Pro leads in safety (98.3% w/o tie) and alignment (83.0% w/o tie), while GPT-4o achieves a perfect 100% on safety (w/o tie) and 88.7% on quality. Among scoring models, HPS-v2.1 leads in quality (93.6% w/o tie) and PickScore-v1 in alignment (64.6% w/o tie), but both trail significantly behind top VLMs on safety and composition. For bias evaluation, Claude-3.7-Sonnet achieves the best ACC (85.8%) and OpenAI o1 the best GES (93.1%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Closed-source VLMs (GPT-4o, OpenAI o1, Gemini-2.5-Pro, Claude-3.7-Sonnet) consistently outperform all open-source VLMs and CLIP-based scoring models across nearly every perspective. OpenAI o1 leads in alignment (81.0%), quality (90.1%), and visualization (83.6%), while Gemini-2.5-Pro achieves the best alignment (83.0%) and safety (98.3%), and GPT-4o reaches a perfect 100% on safety. Among scoring models, HPS-v2.1 (93.6% quality) and PickScore-v1 (64.6% alignment) outperform open-source VLMs like Qwen-VL2.5-7B (72.0% quality, 69.7% alignment) on their respective strengths, confirming that smaller CLIP-based models retain an edge in text-image alignment and image quality due to extensive pretraining. However, open-source VLMs like LLaMA-3.2-11B-Vision (82.0% safety) and Qwen-VL2.5-7B (68.3% safety) surpass all scoring models on safety, reflecting their stronger reasoning capabilities. For bias, Claude-3.7-Sonnet achieves the highest ACC (85.8%) and OpenAI o1 the best GES (93.1%), while scoring models like PickScore-v1 (ACC 31.0%) and HPS-v2.1 (ACC 55.0%) show substantially more demographic bias in their feedback.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM-as-a-judge paradigm (MT-Bench, RewardBench) from textual evaluation to the multimodal text-to-image generation domain, where prior benchmarks (HRS-Bench, T2I-CompBench, GenEval) assess generation quality rather than the evaluative capacity of reward models themselves. It builds on the existing ecosystem of CLIP-based scoring models (CLIP, HPS, PickScore, ImageReward) and VLM-based judges (GPT-4o, Gemini, Claude) by systematically comparing them under a unified, fine-grained protocol across six perspectives. The concurrent VisionPrefer work addresses reward model evaluation for image generation but with only four coarse subsets, whereas MJ-BENCH introduces 22 fine-grained subcategories with human-verified preference pairs and novel bias metrics (ACC, NDS, GES).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As text-to-image models are increasingly aligned via RLAIF/DPO using multimodal judge feedback, understanding which judge to use for which objective is a practical bottleneck; MJ-BENCH provides the first comprehensive, validated reference for this decision. The finding that no single judge dominates all perspectives (CLIP-based models excel at alignment/quality while VLMs excel at safety/bias) suggests that hybrid or perspective-specific judge selection may be necessary, an open question the paper raises but does not fully resolve. Limitations include evaluation on SD-1.5 for end-to-end validation (a relatively old architecture), and the bias subset, while comprehensive in demographic dimensions, relies on image editing that may introduce artifacts confounding the measurement.
