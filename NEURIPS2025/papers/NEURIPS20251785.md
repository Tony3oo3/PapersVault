## Problem

Citizens' assemblies are typically selected to be 1-representative, meaning each individual feature (age, gender, etc.) is proportionally reflected on the panel. However, it is unclear whether this level of representation is sufficient to ensure the panel's decisions maximize social welfare for the population, particularly under non-utilitarian welfare functions (e.g., Nash or egalitarian welfare). This paper asks whether stronger representation—requiring proportional representation of intersections of multiple features—guarantees better welfare estimation, and whether such panels are feasible to construct in practice.

## Input / Output

The theoretical analysis takes a population characterized by feature-value distributions, a set of alternatives with non-negative feature-level benefits (utilities bounded in [0,1]), and a power-mean parameter p ≤ 1, and produces tight ε-accuracy bounds quantifying the worst-case gap between panel and population p-mean welfare. The empirical component takes a pool of volunteers with their feature values, aggregate population intersection data (from the European Social Survey or Australian Census), and a desired panel size k, and outputs the maximum feasible panel size for m-representation up to rounding, or the minimum ε for ε-approximate m-representation at a given k, solved via integer linear programming.

## Method

The key insight is that an m-representative panel shares the first m moments of the population's utility distribution for any alternative (Lemma 13), reducing the welfare-estimation problem to a truncated moment problem. The paper then leverages the classical result that extreme points of moment problems are probability measures supported on at most m+1 points, yielding explicit optimization programs for the maximum and minimum welfare achievable by any distribution with fixed moments. For m=2, this reduces to a two-variable program in (κ₁, κ₂) that can be solved numerically, giving significantly tighter ε-bounds than the m=1 case (e.g., 0.039 vs. 0.155 for Nash welfare at u_min=1/5). For empirical feasibility, the paper formulates panel selection as an ILP with binary variables per volunteer and constraints enforcing m-representative quotas, solved with Gurobi on real volunteer pools from four citizens' assemblies.

## Results

The paper's central theoretical result (Theorem 9) shows that 2-representative panels achieve significantly tighter worst-case ε-accuracy bounds than 1-representative panels for all p < 1. Concretely, for Nash welfare (p = 0) and u_min = 1/5, 2-representation guarantees ε = 0.039 accuracy versus ε = 0.155 for 1-representation; for u_min = 4/5, the relative improvement reaches 2700%. Empirically, (approximate) 2-representative panels are feasible at or near desired panel sizes for 3 of 4 real-world citizens' assembly datasets (EUR1, AUS1, AUS2), with AUS2 requiring a modest contraction from 328 to 278 members.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external algorithmic baselines; instead, its core comparison is between the status quo of 1-representation (proportionally matching each individual feature) and the proposed 2-representation (proportionally matching all pairwise feature intersections). For utilitarian welfare (p = 1), 1-representation already guarantees exact welfare estimation (Theorem 6), so no improvement is possible. For all p < 1, 2-representation strictly dominates 1-representation in worst-case accuracy: the gap is largest when utilities are high (u_min close to 1) and p is near 0, where the relative improvement reaches 2700%. The one case where 2-representation offers no gain over 1-representation is p ≤ 0 with u_min = 0, where both are limited to ε = 1 (Theorem 11 shows this holds for any m). Empirically, the paper demonstrates that the theoretical advantage of 2-representation is practically attainable: integer linear programming (solved with Gurobi) confirms feasible 2-representative panels exist for 3 of 4 real assembly datasets at or near their operational sizes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the citizens' assembly selection literature (Flanigan et al., Barrett et al.) by shifting the focus from representation as a selection constraint to representation as a welfare-estimation guarantee. It builds on the moment problem framework (Karr 1983, Stieltjes 1894) to derive explicit bounds on how well m-representative panels approximate population welfare, a connection not previously explored in the sortition literature. The paper explicitly positions itself against the representation-fairness tradeoff line of work (Barrett et al. 2023, Ebadian & Micha 2024), instead investigating representation-welfare, and introduces the m-representation generalization (requiring proportional representation of feature intersections up to order m) as a new formal concept.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first quantitative, model-based justification for why descriptive representation matters beyond the normative legitimacy debate, with direct implications for citizens' assemblies increasingly used in AI governance (Meta, DeepMind). The practical finding that 2-representation is feasible in most real-world datasets (3 of 4) gives assembly designers a concrete, actionable recommendation. Key open questions include: the tradeoff between m-representation and individual fairness (the feasible set shrinks as m grows), whether the moment-problem bounds tighten for m>2, and how to empirically validate welfare gains without access to true post-deliberation utilities.
