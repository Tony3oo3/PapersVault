## Problem

Conventional wisdom holds that speculative decoding (SD) is ineffective for Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models because verifying multiple draft tokens activates additional experts, inflating memory access and negating speedup. This paper challenges that belief by showing the moderate-batch-size regime—where all experts are already activated but arithmetic units remain underutilized—was overlooked, and that sparser MoEs actually benefit more from SD than dense models in this regime.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt, an MoE target model (e.g., Qwen2-57B-A14B-Instruct), and a smaller draft model (e.g., Qwen2-0.5B-Instruct or Eagle speculative heads), and produces lossless generated text tokens. Beyond the tokens, it outputs quantitative performance metrics: end-to-end SD speedup as a function of batch size, and the proposed target efficiency ratio T_T(B,1)/T_T(B,γ). The performance model (Algorithm 1) further decomposes forward-pass time into roofline-effect, expert-loading, and expert-load components, making the speedup transparent and explainable for any given workload configuration.

## Method

The paper derives a closed-form expression for the expected number of activated experts N(t) = E·(1−(1−ρ)^t) and shows that the average tokens per expert T̄_exp(T;ρ) = ρT/(1−(1−ρ)^T) decreases as sparsity ρ decreases, proving sparser MoEs delay the memory-bound-to-compute-bound transition and thus offer more free compute for SD verification. It introduces target efficiency as a systemic metric that decouples algorithmic acceptance-rate effects from architectural and workload bottlenecks. A quantitative performance model with 10 physically-interpretable relaxation parameters (bias, k1–k3, draft_bias, draft_k, reject_bias, reject_k, λ, s) is fitted via least-squares to a small set of GPU measurements, capturing the roofline transition, expert count, and per-expert load simultaneously. Experiments on 2×A800, 2×H800, 4×A800, and 4×L40 GPUs with Qwen2-57B-A14B and Mixtral-8x7B validate the model and demonstrate up to 2.29× speedup at moderate batch sizes, with the effective batch-size range broadening as MoE sparsity increases.

## Results

On 2×H800, speculative decoding achieves a peak speedup of 2.29× for Qwen2-57B-A14B-Instruct (γ=4, HumanEval, temperature 0.0), with σ=0.90. Across hardware platforms (2×A800, 2×H800, 4×A800, 4×L40), peak speedups range from 1.05× to 2.29×, with higher ridge-point GPUs yielding larger gains. The SD speedup for MoE follows a characteristic increase-then-decrease trend with batch size, peaking at moderate batch sizes where all experts are activated but the system remains memory-bound. Sparser MoE configurations (lower ρ) shift the peak to larger batch sizes and widen the effective batch size range.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between MoE and dense models (OPT-30B with OPT-350M draft) using the proposed target efficiency metric (T_T(B,1)/T_T(B,γ)) to isolate systemic effects from acceptance-rate variations. The MoE target efficiency first increases then decreases with batch size, while the dense model's target efficiency decreases monotonically, meaning SD for MoE exhibits stronger acceleration potential across a wider range of larger batch sizes. In end-to-end speedup (Figure 6), SD speedups for MoE become more pronounced than for dense models once batch size exceeds 16. The paper also compares different MoE sparsity levels (K=1,2,4,8,16,32) and finds that sparser MoEs delay the memory-bound-to-compute-bound transition, widening the effective batch size range for SD acceleration. Very sparse configurations (K=1,2) show continuously decreasing speedup due to Amdahl's Law (Attention dominates when FFN ratio is artificially low), but practical sparser MoEs that add more FFN parameters follow the expected increase-then-decrease pattern.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the foundational speculative decoding framework of Chen et al. (2023) and Leviathan et al. (2023), extending the analysis to Mixture-of-Experts architectures where prior work (Eagle, Saxena et al.) had concluded SD is ineffective due to increased expert activation during verification. It departs from the conventional wisdom by showing that at moderate batch sizes—where all experts are already activated in a single decode step—SD actually benefits MoE more than dense models, a regime overlooked by prior studies. The paper also positions itself relative to MagicDec, which showed SD helps large batches in long-sequence settings, by identifying a complementary moderate-batch regime specific to MoE sparsity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that SD is more effective for sparser MoE at moderate batch sizes directly addresses a practical gap in private serving and latency-critical deployments where large batches are infeasible, offering a lossless acceleration path where existing MoE optimizations (expert prefetching, caching) lose efficacy. The introduced 'target efficiency' metric provides a useful diagnostic that decouples systemic bottlenecks from algorithmic acceptance rates, enabling practitioners to identify when SD will or won't help. Limitations include the assumption that KV-cache is smaller than model parameters (the complementary regime is covered by MagicDec), the reliance on balanced expert loads, and the fact that very sparse MoE (K=1,2) with attention-dominated architectures do not exhibit the predicted speedup pattern.
