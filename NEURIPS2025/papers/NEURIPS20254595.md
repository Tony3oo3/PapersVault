## Problem

Although scaling BPE vocabulary size from 24K to 196K consistently reduces perplexity and improves downstream accuracy, the underlying mechanism remains unexplained. Prior work attributes the gain to better word-level segmentation, but it is unclear whether this mechanism persists beyond the point where frequent words are already single tokens, and how the resulting token-frequency imbalance redistributes modelling capacity between frequent and rare tokens in a conditional language model.

## Input / Output

The study takes a raw text corpus (FineWeb-Edu or OpenWebText) and a BPE vocabulary size as input, producing three families of outputs: (1) an upper bound on Kolmogorov complexity K(X^N) ≈ NH(p) and normalized compression ratio for the tokenized text, (2) a word-level loss decomposition that accumulates total loss, average per-word loss, and global cross-entropy separately for the top 2,500 frequent words and the rarest 20,000 words (summing subword losses when a word is split), and (3) downstream accuracy on ARC, HellaSwag, SciQ, PIQA, and CC-Main-2023-40. The key constraint is that data, compute, and optimization are held fixed across vocabulary sizes, isolating the tokenizer as the sole variable.

## Method

The paper performs a controlled scaling study of BPE vocabulary from 24K to 196K on 85M and 450M parameter Transformer LMs, using a computable Kolmogorov complexity upper bound (NH(p), where H(p) is unigram Shannon entropy) to show that larger vocabularies reduce tokenized-text complexity. It then disentangles two effects: segmentation efficiency (fraction of top-2,500 words encoded as a single token, which saturates above 24K) and relative token-frequency imbalance (measured by Jensen-Shannon divergence from uniform, which grows monotonically). A word-level loss decomposition reveals that the global cross-entropy reduction comes almost entirely from lowering uncertainty on the ~2,500 most frequent words (~75% of all tokens), even as rare-word loss increases. The paper confirms the causal role of frequency imbalance by showing that constraining input/output embedding norms to unit length erases the frequent-word loss advantage, and that scaling model parameters (Pythia 160M→6.9B, OLMo-2 1B→7B) with a fixed tokenizer reproduces the same frequent-word benefit, pointing to a shared optimization dynamic between vocabulary and parameter scaling.

## Results

Scaling BPE vocabulary from 24K to 196K reduces the upper bound on Kolmogorov complexity of tokenized FineWeb-Edu text from 10.74B to 10.16B bytes (NCR: 0.234 → 0.221). Word-level loss decomposition on an 85M model (40B bytes training, 5B eval) shows the average per-word loss on the top 2,500 frequent words drops by ~0.1 nats while the rarest 20,000 words' loss rises from 11.183 to 13.399 nats; nonetheless, global cross-entropy falls from 3.179 to 3.136 nats because frequent words account for ~75% of total loss. The same pattern holds at larger scale: a 450M model on 10B tokens sees CE drop from 2.989 to 2.888 nats, and Pythia 6.9B reduces frequent-word loss to 3.38 nats with CE of 2.26 nats on Paloma.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary external comparison is against SuperBPE [Liu et al., 2025], a two-stage BPE variant that abandons whitespace pre-tokenization after a threshold vocabulary size to limit rare-token introduction. At a fixed 200K vocabulary, standard BPE achieves K(X^N)=10.21B and NCR=0.222 with average downstream score 39.8, while the best SuperBPE variant (t=180K) reaches K(X^N)=10.03B, NCR=0.218, and average downstream 43.8—confirming that both vocabulary expansion and SuperBPE exploit the same mechanism (reducing tokenized-text complexity) but SuperBPE does so with a less skewed frequency distribution. Internally, the paper isolates the vocabulary effect from parameter growth via a size-matched control (Table 3): a 49K model with reduced hidden/FFN dimensions (124M params) actually performs worse than the 24K model (122M params; CE 3.563 vs 3.179, downstream 50.68% vs 54.73%), showing the gain is not merely from extra embedding parameters. Parameter scaling with a fixed tokenizer (Pythia 160M→6.9B) reproduces the frequent-word loss reduction without inflating rare-token loss, yielding a larger CE drop (4.32→2.26 nats) than vocabulary scaling alone (3.179→3.136 nats), indicating the two levers share an optimization dynamic but model scaling is strictly more effective.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on Rajaraman et al.'s observation that larger BPE vocabularies lower unigram cross-entropy and the broader 'tokenization as lossless compression' framing from Delétang et al. and Lester et al., but departs from the dominant narrative that vocabulary growth helps by approximating word-level tokens. The key conceptual departure is showing that segmentation saturates around 24K tokens, so the operative mechanism above that threshold is sharpening token-frequency imbalance rather than improving single-token coverage. The paper also connects to SuperBPE as an alternative route to the same complexity-reduction benefit, and contrasts with Zouhar et al.'s finding that frequency imbalance harms machine translation, explaining the discrepancy via cross-lingual vocabulary mismatch.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By identifying that the top ~2,500 frequent words drive ~75% of total loss and that vocabulary scaling (or equivalently, parameter scaling) primarily reduces their uncertainty, the paper reframes 'bigger vocabularies help' as a frequency-imbalance exploitation problem, offering Kolmogorov complexity of tokenized text as a principled design metric for tokenizer-model co-design. Open questions include whether deduplication interacts with frequency imbalance in predictable ways, how to reduce tokenized-text complexity without further skewing the distribution, and whether the mechanism holds in multilingual or code-heavy settings where the frequent-word overlap with benchmarks may be weaker. A limitation is that controlled experiments use 85M–450M models; while Pythia and OLMo-2 validations support the trend, the causal isolation (e.g., norm-constraining ablation) is only tested at the smaller scale.
