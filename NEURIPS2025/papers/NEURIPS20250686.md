## Problem

Diffusion-Based Purification (DBP) is widely regarded as a robust defense against adversarial examples because it projects inputs onto the natural data manifold via a diffusion model. This paper refutes that claim by proving that standard gradient-based attacks, when properly backpropagated through DBP, actually target the diffusion model's score function rather than the classifier, forcing it to generate adversarial samples. The paper further shows that prior robustness estimates were inflated by broken gradient implementations and statistically invalid single-purification evaluation protocols.

## Input / Output

The method takes standard RGB images (32×32 CIFAR-10 or 224×224 ImageNet) and produces adversarial perturbations constrained either by an ℓ∞ norm bound (8/255 for CIFAR-10, 4/255 for ImageNet) or by an LPIPS perceptual distance threshold (τ_p = 0.05) for the low-frequency attack. The adversarial image, when passed through the DBP purification pipeline (forward diffusion to time t* followed by stochastic reverse denoising) and then classified, yields a misclassification. For the majority-vote protocol, K independent stochastic purifications are generated and the final label is the mode of their individual predictions.

## Method

The paper's core theoretical contribution (Theorem 3.1) proves that an adaptive attack optimizing x through DBP implicitly shapes the score model's effective parameters {θ_x^t} at each reverse step, redirecting the purification trajectory to produce adversarial outputs rather than natural ones. To enable reliable attacks, the authors introduce DiffGrad, a checkpointing-based backpropagation module that fixes five critical issues in prior implementations: high-variance Monte Carlo gradients (resolved via parallelized EOT with up to 128 samples), time-step rounding mismatches in torchsde, failure to reproduce stochastic noise between forward and backward passes, missing second-order guidance gradients, and incorrect surrogate-method usage. For evaluation, the paper proposes a majority-vote (MV) protocol that aggregates predictions across K purified copies, replacing the statistically invalid single-purification scheme. Finally, the authors adapt UnMarker's Optimizable Filters (OFs) into a low-frequency attack that applies per-pixel learned convolutional kernels in a chain, producing systemic perturbations that resist DBP's stochastic dilution and achieve near-zero robust accuracy even under MV.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 (ε∞=8/255), the standard AutoAttack-ℓ∞ with correct DiffGrad gradients (Full-DiffGrad) reduces DiffPure's worst-case robustness to 17.19% and GDMP's to 7.03%, compared to 62.11% and 24.53% reported in prior work. Under the proposed majority-vote (MV) protocol, Full-DiffGrad achieves MV.Rob of 39.45% (DiffPure) and 16.8% (GDMP). On ImageNet (ε∞=4/255), Wor.Rob drops to 12.11–21.09% and MV.Rob peaks at 32.83%. The novel low-frequency (LF) attack further collapses Rob-Acc to 0.00–3.13% under MV across all classifiers and both defenses.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DiffAttack [20], the paper shows that with correct DiffGrad gradients, DiffAttack provides no meaningful advantage over the standard Full (AutoAttack) method: on DiffPure both achieve identical MV.Rob of 39.45%, while on GDMP DiffAttack-DiffGrad (14.45%) edges out Full-DiffGrad (16.8%) by only 2.35%—a margin the authors deem statistically insignificant given 256 test samples. Against DiffHammer [41], the outcome is worse: DiffHammer-DiffGrad actually degrades attack performance on GDMP (MV.Rob 25.39% vs. 16.80% for Full-DiffGrad), confirming the paper's theoretical claim that constraining optimization to 'feasible paths' disrupts the gradient signal. Compared to the original reported numbers from both [20] and [41], DiffGrad reduces Wor.Rob by 15–25 percentage points, exposing that prior gradient implementations (checkpointing bugs, time inconsistencies, missing guidance gradients) had inflated DBP's apparent robustness. Against MimicDiffusion [35], the paper demonstrates that its reported 92.67%/92.26% Rob-Acc collapses to Wor.Rob of 2.73%/4.3% and MV.Rob of 25.78%/27.73% once guidance-path gradients are properly computed via DiffGrad, revealing that MimicDiffusion's claimed SOTA robustness was an artifact of broken second-order differentiation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of adversarial attack research and diffusion-based purification (DBP) defenses, building directly on the DBP framework of DiffPure [30] and GDMP [40] and the prior attack lineage of DiffAttack [20], DiffHammer [41], and surrogate-based methods [24]. Its key conceptual departure is a theoretical proof (Theorem 3.1) that standard gradient-based attacks, when backpropagated correctly through DBP, implicitly target the score model rather than the classifier—reframing DBP from an immutable pre-processor into an active optimization target. Empirically, the paper departs from prior work by identifying and fixing critical gradient implementation bugs (time consistency, stochasticity reproduction, guidance gradients) that had inflated robustness estimates, and by adapting UnMarker's [21] Optimizable Filters from watermarking to craft low-frequency adversarial perturbations that resist DBP's stochastic dilution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper fundamentally challenges the field's working assumption that DBP provides meaningful robustness, showing that reported gains were largely artifacts of broken gradients and single-purification evaluation. The collapse of MimicDiffusion's 92%+ robustness to ~3% under proper evaluation, and the LF attack reducing robustness to near 0% even under majority-vote, raise serious questions about whether any purification-based defense can withstand adaptive attacks. Open questions include whether private stochastic dynamics at inference time (as the authors suggest) could break the gradient-access assumption, whether the results extend to non-vision modalities, and whether the vast LF hyperparameter space makes adversarial training a viable countermeasure. The DiffBreak toolkit also addresses a practical gap: prior DBP implementations lacked generalizability and reliable differentiation, making rigorous evaluation difficult for the broader community.
