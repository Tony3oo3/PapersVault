## Problem

Implicit Neural Representations (INRs) have shown impressive results in imaging and inverse problems, but their fundamental capacity, implicit biases, and scaling behavior remain poorly understood, limiting confidence in when to deploy them. This paper asks: under what conditions do INRs actually outperform simpler alternatives like interpolated grids, and how does representation capacity scale with model size across different signal types?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251731/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each model receives spatial coordinates (2D or 3D) and is trained to output the corresponding signal value, with model sizes swept from 10^4 to 3×10^6 parameters on signals containing ~10^6 spatial values (e.g., 1000×1000 images or 100^3 volumes). Synthetic signals include bandlimited noise, spheres, Sierpinski fractals, and a radial Star Target, each parameterized by an effective bandwidth from 0.1 to 0.9; real signals include DIV2K images, chest CT scans, and the 3D Stanford Dragon (occupancy and surface). Evaluation spans overfitting, 4× super-resolution, Gaussian denoising, and CT reconstruction, with PSNR as the primary metric.

## Method

The paper is a systematic empirical benchmark comparing eight representative signal representations—pure INRs (FFN, SIREN, WIRE), hybrid models (GA-Planes, Instant-NGP), a discrete model (Gaussian Splatting), an adaptive-bandwidth INR (BACON), and a simple interpolated grid—across synthetic and real 2D/3D signals stratified by bandwidth, model size, and task. All models are trained with Adam using MSE loss (plus TV regularization for noisy/underdetermined tasks), with hyperparameters tuned on the Star Target image and parameter counts matched via a quadratic equation for MLP-based models. The key quantitative finding is a power-law relationship between model size and effective bandwidth for bandlimited signals, and the central negative result that no INR reliably outperforms a regularized grid for dense bandlimited signals, while INRs and hybrid models excel specifically on signals with lower-dimensional structure (sharp edges, constant regions, surfaces embedded in higher dimensions).

## Results

On 2D Bandlimited signals (bandwidth 0.5), the simple interpolated Grid achieves PSNR of 50.91 dB at 10⁴ parameters, outperforming all INRs (FFN: 21.65, SIREN: 30.15, WIRE: 27.45, GA-Planes: 22.06, Instant-NGP: 20.91, BACON: 19.42) by 20–32 dB. At 10⁶ parameters, Grid reaches 140.45 dB while the best INR (FFN) reaches only 56.53 dB. For 3D Dragon occupancy overfitting, GA-Planes and WIRE outperform Grid at the smallest model sizes (10⁴ params), but Grid with TV regularization is optimal for CT reconstruction, 2D denoising, and 2D super-resolution across all model sizes. The paper identifies a power-law relationship between model size and effective bandwidth for nearly all models on bandlimited signals.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The Grid baseline decisively outperforms all seven compared methods on 2D Bandlimited signals at every model size, with margins of 20+ dB at small sizes (e.g., Grid 50.91 vs. best INR SIREN 30.15 at 10⁴ params). For 2D signals with lower-dimensional structure (Spheres, Sierpinski, Star Target), GA-Planes and Instant-NGP outperform Grid at intermediate model sizes (3×10⁴ to 10⁵), exploiting the constant-value regions and sharp edges; for example, GA-Planes reaches 51.20 dB on 2D Spheres (bandwidth 0.1, 3×10⁴) vs. Grid's 31.06 dB. On 3D Dragon occupancy and surface tasks, GA-Planes and WIRE beat Grid at the smallest model sizes, consistent with the lower-dimensional-structure hypothesis. For inverse problems on 2D natural signals (CT, denoising, 4× super-resolution on DIV2K), Grid with TV regularization is optimal across all model sizes, while WIRE produces sharper but artifact-laden outputs. SIREN is notably unstable at larger model sizes (PSNR drops from 32.89 to 16.19 between 10⁵ and 10⁶ on Bandlimited signals), and GSplat and BACON are consistently the weakest performers across nearly all tasks and model sizes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by systematically benchmarking signal representation methods under a unified protocol, rather than proposing a new architecture. It departs from the INR literature (SIREN, WIRE, FFN, BACON) and hybrid/encoding literature (Instant-NGP, GA-Planes) that has largely evaluated these methods in isolation or on specific tasks, by stratifying performance across signal bandwidth, model size, and signal type simultaneously. The grid-with-interpolation baseline draws on classical sampling theory (Nyquist-Shannon) and prior work on plenoxels and NUFFT-based gridding, repurposing a decades-old technique as a competitive reference point. The paper also incorporates discrete representations (Gaussian Splatting) and adaptive-bandwidth networks (BACON) to cover the full landscape of parameterization strategies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that a simple interpolated grid with TV regularization is Pareto-optimal in memory, speed, and quality for most dense signals—challenges the implicit assumption in the INR community that neural parameterizations are inherently superior for signal compression. This provides practitioners with clear, evidence-based guidance: use grids for dense natural signals (images, CT, bandlimited content) and reserve INRs for signals with lower-dimensional structure (object surfaces, shape masks). Open questions include why dense natural signals behave more like bandlimited noise than the structured signals where INRs excel, whether future INR architectures can close the gap on dense signals, and whether the power-law bandwidth scaling observed here generalizes to higher dimensions or more complex signal classes.
