## Problem

Prior memorization studies focus on single modalities (text in LLMs, images in diffusion models), leaving cross-modality knowledge transfer in vision-language models largely unexplored. This paper asks: when a VLM learns a fact from one modality, how effectively does that knowledge transfer to the other, and what structural asymmetries or failure modes emerge in this transfer?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254292/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives either a synthetic persona photograph (generated by Imagen 3) or a natural-language description (generated by Gemini 2.0) paired with a name-query prompt, and outputs the associated person's name. For multi-hop evaluation, the output extends to a three-digit 'favorite number' linked to the persona. Critically, training uses only one modality per experiment while testing probes both modalities with held-out variants (100 training variants vs. 1 unseen test variant per persona), so the model must generalize from seen to unseen surface forms within and across modalities.

## Method

The authors construct a synthetic persona dataset of 100 profiles, each defined by a combinatorial attribute pool (demographics, clothing, hairstyle, expression, background) yielding ~67M possible combinations, with images generated by Imagen 3 and descriptions by Gemini 2.0. They fine-tune Gemma-3 models (4B, 12B, 27B) via LoRA (rank 32) on a single modality—either images or descriptions—across up to 100 epochs, then measure recognition accuracy in both the source and target modalities to quantify the cross-modal transfer gap. Key findings include an asymmetric transfer (image→text outperforms text→image), a cross-modal multi-hop curse where single-hop associations fail to compose, and modality-dependent unlearning effects. As a mitigation, they augment training with in-distribution synthetic image-caption pairs (generated from the same persona attributes), which narrows the transfer gap, whereas out-of-distribution COCO data provides no comparable benefit.

## Results

The paper's central finding is a significant asymmetric gap between source and target modality accuracy: knowledge transfer from image to text is consistently more effective than from text to image, even as model size scales from 4B to 27B parameters. In the multi-hop setting (Table 1), a model trained on Desc→Name and Name→FN achieves 1.00 accuracy on both individual tasks but only 0.11 on the composed Desc→FN query, demonstrating a severe cross-modal multi-hop curse. In-distribution image-caption augmentation narrows the cross-modal gap, while out-of-distribution (COCO) augmentation provides no meaningful benefit. Membership inference on unintentionally memorized PII in images reaches ~70% AUC after 100 epochs, despite the PII being irrelevant to the training task.

**Table 1: Cross-Modal Multi-Hop Learning. Cross-modal models suffer from the multi-hop curse, where accurate performance on individual tasks does not translate to multi-hop reasoning.**

| Base Data | Multi-Hop Data | Desc→Name | Image→Name | Desc→FN | Image→FN | Name→FN |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Desc→Name | Desc→FN | 1.00 | 0.35 | 1.00 | 0.31 | 0.08 |
| Desc→Name | Image→FN | 1.00 | 0.36 | 0.39 | 1.00 | 0.05 |
| Desc→Name | Name→FN | 1.00 | 0.36 | 0.11 | 0.05 | 0.99 |
| Image→Name | Desc→FN | 0.68 | 1.00 | 1.00 | 0.47 | 0.07 |
| Image→Name | Image→FN | 0.62 | 1.00 | 0.50 | 1.00 | 0.07 |
| Image→Name | Name→FN | 0.64 | 1.00 | 0.27 | 0.65 | 0.99 |

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper is an empirical study rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so the 'baselines' are internal training configurations. Training on diverse input variants (100 paraphrased descriptions or images per persona) eliminates overfitting seen with single-input training (where image-trained models degrade after ~25 epochs) and pushes source-modality accuracy near 1.00, but the cross-modal transferability slope remains essentially unchanged. Scaling from Gemma-3-4B to 12B to 27B improves absolute accuracy in both modalities but leaves the transferability rate (slope of target vs. source accuracy) nearly identical. Augmenting training with in-distribution synthetic image-text pairs is the only strategy that meaningfully improves cross-modal transferability; out-of-distribution COCO data provides no comparable benefit. For unlearning, performing unlearning in the same modality as the original learning yields consistent accuracy drops across both modalities, whereas cross-modal unlearning (e.g., unlearning descriptions in an image-trained model) produces asymmetric and incomplete forgetting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the memorization literature from unimodal settings (LLM text memorization via canaries, diffusion model image replication) to the cross-modal domain, asking how factual knowledge learned in one modality transfers to another in vision-language models. It departs from prior CLIP-style VLM memorization work (Jayaraman et al., Kokhlikyan et al.) by focusing on LLaVA-style generative VLMs and by introducing a controlled synthetic persona dataset that enables clean source/target modality separation. The multi-hop curse analysis builds directly on Balesni et al.'s two-hop curse for LLMs, while the unlearning experiments extend gradient-ascent-based unlearning to a cross-modal setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that cross-modal transferability does not improve with model scale challenges the common assumption that larger VLMs will naturally close modality gaps, and the asymmetry (image→text > text→image) has direct implications for how personal-assistant VLMs should be trained and evaluated. The cross-modal unlearning gap raises practical privacy concerns: a model that 'forgets' a fact in one modality may retain it in the other. Key limitations include the restriction to text-image modalities, a single model family (Gemma-3), and entirely synthetic data, leaving open whether these effects persist with real-world data, additional modalities (audio, video), or architectural changes beyond LoRA fine-tuning.
