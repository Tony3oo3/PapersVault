## Problem

Existing influence functions (e.g., TracIn) treat a multivariate time series sample as an indivisible unit, making it impossible to quantify how individual channels contribute to model behavior. No prior work has estimated channel-wise influence for MTS, and standard influence-based methods fail on MTS anomaly detection and data pruning tasks where channel-level granularity is essential.

## Input / Output

Given a trained MTS model with parameters θ and a multivariate time series sample x ∈ R^{T×N} (T timesteps, N channels), the method computes an N×N channel-wise influence matrix M_CInf where each entry a_{i,j} = η∇_θ L(c_i'; θ)ᵀ ∇_θ L(c_j; θ) captures the influence of training channel i on test channel j. For anomaly detection, the diagonal elements (per-channel self-influence) are aggregated via max to yield a scalar anomaly score per timestep; for channel pruning, channels are ranked by self-influence and sampled at regular intervals to produce a representative subset D̂ ⊂ D.

## Method

ChInf extends TracIn's first-order gradient approximation to the channel level via Theorem 3.1, which proves that the whole-sample TracIn decomposes into a double sum over channel pairs, yielding an N×N influence matrix. The key insight is that the diagonal of this matrix (self-influence per channel) serves as a natural anomaly score: anomalous channels exhibit higher self-influence because the model reduces more loss when trained on them. For anomaly detection, the max diagonal element across channels at each timestep forms the anomaly score, replacing reconstruction/prediction error. For channel pruning, channels are sorted by self-influence and selected at equidistant intervals, exploiting the observation that similar channels cluster in influence ranking. The approach is model-agnostic (applied to GCN-LSTM, iTransformer, PatchTST, MLP-Mixer, Transformer) and requires only gradient computation on a subset of parameters, making it computationally efficient without retraining.

## Results

On the five MTS anomaly detection benchmarks, ChInf (with GCN-LSTM) achieves the best F1 on SMD (58.8), SMAP (48.0), MSL (47.1), and WADI (47.2), while ChInf (with iTransformer) achieves the best F1 on SWaT (84.0) and WADI (50.5). In the channel pruning task, ChInf-based selection matches or exceeds full-channel performance using only 20–50% of channels (e.g., 0.165 MSE on ECL at 20% vs. 0.148 full), outperforming LIME, SHAP, NFS, and continuous selection baselines. The method also generalizes across architectures (MLP, MLP-Mixer, Transformer, GCN-LSTM, iTransformer, PatchTST), consistently improving F1 by 4–18 points over reconstruction-error baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ChInf outperforms all deep anomaly detection baselines (DAGMM, OmniAnomaly, USAD, GDN, TranAD, AnomalyTransformer) by 5–25 F1 points across all five datasets, with the largest gains on SMAP (+5.1 over GDN) and WADI (+12.8 over GDN). Against the simple baselines from [34], ChInf wins on SMD (+1.6 over PCA ERROR, +3.8 over GCN-LSTM), SMAP (+8.8 over PCA ERROR, +5.4 over GCN-LSTM), and MSL (+4.5 over PCA ERROR, +0.8 over GCN-LSTM), while roughly matching PCA ERROR on SWaT (82.9 vs. 83.3) and WADI (50.5 vs. 50.1). Compared to the closest model-centric baseline iTransformer (reconstruction), ChInf improves F1 by 6.7 on SMAP, 0.6 on MSL, and 1.7 on WADI, confirming that channel-wise self-influence captures anomaly signals that reconstruction error misses. Against the prior influence-function method Timeinf [29], ChInf wins on every dataset by 4–23 F1 points (e.g., 48.0 vs. 35.1 on SMAP, 50.5 vs. 27.1 on WADI), demonstrating that channel-wise decomposition is essential for MTS. In channel pruning (Table 3), ChInf selection achieves lower MSE than LIME, SHAP, NFS, and continuous selection at every retention ratio for both iTransformer and PatchTST, and matches full-channel performance at 20–50% retention—whereas MoSo sample pruning underperforms even random pruning, highlighting the inadequacy of sample-level influence for temporally dependent data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the influence function lineage from Koh & Liang (2017) and its first-order TracIn approximation (Pruthi et al., 2020) by decomposing the whole-sample influence into a channel-wise influence matrix for multivariate time series. It departs from TimeInf (Zhang et al., 2024), which addresses temporal dependencies but explicitly ignores channel-wise structure, by arguing that the channel dimension is the critical axis of influence in MTS. The channel pruning task is positioned as a natural data-centric analogue of dataset pruning in CV/NLP, but applied along the channel axis rather than the sample axis, motivated by iTransformer's observation that partial-channel training can recover full-channel predictions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The channel-wise influence matrix provides a post-hoc interpretability tool that reveals how a specific model utilizes inter-channel relationships, with the practical consequence that the size of the representative channel subset serves as a model-quality metric (e.g., iTransformer > PatchTST). A notable open question is whether ChInf generalizes beyond anomaly detection and pruning to other MTS tasks such as forecasting or classification, which the authors acknowledge as unexplored. The finding that traditional sample-level influence pruning (MoSo) underperforms random pruning on time series highlights a gap in applying influence-based data curation to sequential data, suggesting that temporal dependencies fundamentally alter how influence should be computed.
