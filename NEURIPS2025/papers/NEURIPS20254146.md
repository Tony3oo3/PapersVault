## Problem

Existing covariate selection methods for nonparametric causal effect estimation either require learning the full global causal structure (e.g., LV-IDA, EHS), which is computationally wasteful when only a single treatment-outcome pair is of interest, or operate locally but sacrifice completeness (e.g., CEELS, LDP), potentially missing valid adjustment sets that global methods would find. No prior method achieves both locality and completeness in the presence of latent confounders.

## Input / Output

The method takes only an observational dataset with a designated treatment variable X, outcome variable Y, and a set of pretreatment covariates O—no prior knowledge of the causal graph is required. It produces a three-valued output: an estimated causal effect with a valid adjustment set (S1), a determination that no causal effect exists (S2), or an indeterminate result when the effect cannot be identified from the available independence/dependence relationships (S3). All inferences are made through conditional independence and dependence tests on the observed variables, with the pretreatment assumption that X and Y are not causal ancestors of any covariate in O.

## Method

The LSAS algorithm operates in two stages: first, it discovers the Markov blankets of X and Y using the Total Conditioning (TC) algorithm; second, it searches for valid adjustment sets restricted to MB(Y)\{X} by checking two local rules (R1 and R2) for each candidate S ∈ MB(X)\{Y} and subset Z ⊆ MB(Y)\{X}. Rule R1 identifies a valid adjustment set when S is dependent on Y given Z but independent given Z∪{X}, implying all active paths from S to Y pass through X; Rule R2 certifies the absence of a causal effect via direct independence or a latent-confounder witness. A key theoretical result (Theorem 1) proves that if any valid adjustment set exists within O, then one exists within MB(Y)\{X}, justifying the local restriction. The algorithm is proven sound and complete under the causal Markov and faithfulness assumptions, with worst-case complexity O[(|MB(X)|−1) × 2^{|MB(Y)|−1} + n], substantially lower than global methods when the Markov blanket is much smaller than the full variable set.

## Results

LSAS achieves the lowest Relative Error (RE) and fewest conditional independence tests (nTest) across all synthetic structures (specific DAGs, random Erdős-Rényi graphs, and four benchmark networks: INSURANCE, MILDEW, WIN95PTS, ANDES) at all sample sizes (1K–15K). On the real-world Cattaneo2 dataset, LSAS requires only 158 conditional independence tests (vs. 1,284 for CEELS and 266 for LDP), and its estimated causal effect of maternal smoking on birth weight falls within the 200–250 g baseline interval established by Almond et al. (2005), matching EHS while being far more efficient. LSAS also maintains superior RE accuracy even when the pretreatment assumption is violated (e.g., on MILDEW and WIN95PTS where selected variable pairs may have descendants).

Note: n = total observed variables including (X,Y); q = max(|Adj(X)\{Y|, |Adj(Y)\{X|). LSAS's complexity depends on Markov blanket sizes rather than total variable count, yielding substantial gains when n ≫ |MB(Y)|.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LV-IDA (global FCI-based), LSAS achieves comparable or lower RE while requiring dramatically fewer independence tests, since LV-IDA must learn the full PAG and enumerate all Markov-equivalent MAGs. Against EHS (global search without graph learning), LSAS matches RE accuracy on the Cattaneo2 dataset but uses far fewer tests (158 vs. EHS's excessive count that obscures other methods in Figure 6); EHS also fails to terminate within 2 hours on denser random graphs (40 nodes, degree 9). Against CEELS (local, COSO-based), LSAS achieves consistently lower RE because CEELS is not complete—it misses valid adjustment sets in structures like Figure 1(b) and shows limited improvement with increasing sample size. Against LDP (local, relaxed pretreatment assumption), LSAS achieves substantially lower RE on benchmark networks (e.g., WIN95PTS, MILDEW), though LDP is faster at large sample sizes (15K on WIN95PTS); LDP's incompleteness causes it to fail in cases where EHS succeeds (e.g., Figure 1(c)). Overall, LSAS is the only method that is simultaneously local (no global graph learning), sound, and complete, while also being the most efficient in nTest across nearly all settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the EHS framework (Entner et al., 2013), which established sound and complete local rules for covariate selection via conditional independence/dependence testing, but EHS requires an exhaustive O(t × 2^t) search over all covariates. The key departure is restricting the search to Markov blankets (MB(X) and MB(Y)) discovered via the TC algorithm, and extending the reasoning to MAGs/PAGs to handle latent confounders—something EHS and CEELS (Cheng et al., 2022) do not address. The theoretical backbone (Theorem 1: an adjustment set exists in O iff one exists in MB(Y)\{X}) is a clean structural result that justifies the local restriction without sacrificing completeness, in contrast to CEELS which was shown to miss valid adjustment sets even under the pretreatment assumption.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper closes a gap between global methods (LV-IDA, which learn full PAGs and enumerate MAGs) and prior local methods (CEELS, LDP) that either ignore latent variables or sacrifice completeness. The practical impact is significant for applied causal inference: one can estimate a single causal effect with far fewer independence tests (158 vs. thousands for EHS on Cattaneo2) while retaining the same identification guarantees. However, the pretreatment assumption remains a real limitation—no existing method can locally and completely identify descendants of the treatment in the presence of latent variables, and the paper acknowledges this as an open problem. Scenario S3 (incomplete identification) also means the method can sometimes only report 'unknown' rather than a definitive answer.
