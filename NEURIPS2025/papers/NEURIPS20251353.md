## Problem

The classical explanation of the law of supply and demand does not rule out persistent price oscillations—where surpluses and shortages alternate indefinitely—preventing prices from ever converging to the market-clearing price. This paper asks why selling prices eventually stabilize rather than oscillating, and provides a rigorous mathematical answer by framing the law as an emergent property of adaptive learning among sellers.

## Input / Output

The pricing game takes as input discrete marginal costs s_i and buyer valuations b_j over a price set [k] = {0, 1, ..., k}, with each seller and buyer trading exactly one unit. In the repeated-game setting, each seller at round t observes only the prices chosen by other sellers in prior rounds (p_{-i}^1, ..., p_{-i}^{t-1}) and selects a price from its feasible set {s_i, ..., k}. The output is the time-averaged selling price, which the paper proves converges to the highest market-clearing price p* under no-swap regret dynamics, and the joint distribution over pricing profiles that constitutes a correlated equilibrium.

## Method

The paper models price competition as a one-shot pricing game where sellers choose prices and buyers arrive sequentially to purchase from the cheapest available seller. The main technical result (Theorem 3) shows that any Correlated Equilibrium of this game places all probability mass on valid pricing profiles where every seller with s_i ≤ p* sets price p*. The proof employs a novel dual-fitting technique: a linear program is constructed whose optimal value lower-bounds the probability that a CE selects the market-clearing price, and the dual is shown to admit a feasible solution with objective value 1 via a carefully structured assignment of dual variables that incentivizes each seller to deviate exclusively toward p*. Combined with the known result that no-swap regret dynamics converge to CE, this yields convergence to the market-clearing price. The paper further shows that the weaker Coarse Correlated Equilibrium concept does not guarantee this alignment, constructing a counterexample with n=2 sellers where the CCE assigns O(1/k) probability to the market-clearing price.

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 1) establishes that any Correlated Equilibrium of the pricing game places probability 1 on the market-clearing price p*, meaning all sellers with s_i ≤ p* set price p* and all buyers with b_j ≥ p* transact at p*. The negative result (Theorem 2) shows that Coarse Correlated Equilibria can have probability at most O(1/k) of selecting the market-clearing price, ruling out convergence guarantees for general no-regret dynamics. Empirically, both the Hedge no-regret algorithm (step-size γ=0.1) and the Blum–Mansour no-swap regret algorithm converge to p* in all tested instances (n=2 with λ∈{0.2,1,3} and n=100 with four different supply/demand curve configurations).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares two online learning algorithms on identical pricing game instances: the Hedge no-regret algorithm [20] and the Blum–Mansour no-swap regret algorithm [7]. Both algorithms converge to the market-clearing price in every tested configuration (n=2 with varying λ, and n=100 with linear, quadratic, and square-root supply curves), so empirically they tie. However, the theoretical guarantees differ sharply: no-swap regret is provably guaranteed to converge to p* (via Theorem 1, which shows any CE coincides with p*), while no-regret dynamics only converge to CCE, and Theorem 2 constructs a family of instances where CCE places probability O(1/k) on the market-clearing price. Thus, no-swap regret strictly dominates no-regret in terms of theoretical assurance, even though both succeed in practice. The Hedge algorithm's empirical success suggests that specific classes of no-regret algorithms (e.g., mean-based) may also always converge, but this remains an open question.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of research on online learning dynamics in market settings (Cournot competition, Fisher markets, Bertrand duopoly) to the specific and previously unstudied case of perfectly competitive markets governed by the law of supply and demand. It builds directly on the no-swap regret framework of Blum and Mansour [7] and the known convergence-to-CE results of Foster and Vohra [17] and Hart and Mas-Colell [19], but introduces a novel dual-fitting technique from approximation algorithms [29] as the first application of this method to establish convergence properties of learning dynamics. The pricing game formulation is related to Babaioff et al. [4,5] but differs in its focus on equilibrium characterization rather than price-of-anarchy bounds.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first rigorous mathematical foundation for the law of supply and demand in terms of learning dynamics, answering a fundamental question (why prices stabilize rather than oscillate) that has remained open despite the principle's centrality in economics. The negative result showing that CCE does not guarantee market clearing is important: it delineates the boundary between no-regret and no-swap regret as sufficient conditions, and the empirical observation that Hedge (a simple no-regret algorithm) still converges suggests that specific algorithmic properties beyond the CCE guarantee may be at play. A key open question is whether formal convergence guarantees can be established for specific classes of no-regret algorithms (e.g., mean-based or Hedge-type), which would strengthen the theoretical foundation considerably.
