## Problem

Existing preference-based RL methods improve feedback efficiency through query optimization but overlook the preference buffer's restricted coverage, which fundamentally determines reward model reliability. This creates a distributional mismatch: reward models trained on static buffers accurately assess in-distribution trajectories but produce unreliable evaluations for out-of-distribution trajectories encountered during policy exploration, directly misguiding iterative policy updates. The paper identifies buffer coverage as an explicit optimization objective that prior work has systematically neglected.

## Input / Output

The method consumes trajectory segments (sequences of state-action pairs of length H) paired with binary preference labels, plus live environment states for online interaction. It produces a Gaussian behavior policy with a shifted mean for exploration, a reward model trained via Bradley-Terry cross-entropy loss, per-transition OOD scores bounded to [0,1] from the Morse network (0 = in-distribution, approaching 1 = OOD), and an expanded preference buffer. The Morse network's RBF-kernel output is specifically designed so that M_φ(s,a)=0 holds only for (s,a) pairs originating from the preference buffer, providing a calibrated uncertainty signal rather than an unbounded distance.

## Method

PPE comprises two complementary components integrated into the QPA backbone. First, a proximal-policy extension method trains a Morse neural network (RBF kernel, parameter λ) to score per-transition OOD degree, then solves a constrained optimization—maximizing expected OOD score subject to a KL divergence bound ε on the behavior policy—yielding a closed-form solution (Proposition 3.1) that shifts the policy mean along the OOD gradient direction scaled by √(2ε) while preserving the target policy's covariance. Second, a mixture distribution query method selects a fraction κ of out-of-distribution segments and (1−κ) of in-distribution segments from the candidate pool for preference labeling, using the Morse scores to define two normalized sampling distributions P^out and P^in. The components are mutually compensating: the extension method generates novel OOD transitions in the replay buffer, while the query method ensures the preference buffer receives balanced in- and out-of-distribution data, preventing the reward model from degrading in already-covered regions.

## Results

PPE achieves the best or tied-best performance on 11 out of 13 benchmark tasks across DMControl and MetaWorld. On the most challenging DMControl locomotion task (Walker-walk), PPE reaches 908.09 ± 55.30 episode return, surpassing the previous state-of-the-art QPA (796.08 ± 147.94) by 14.1% while also reducing variance by 62%. On MetaWorld manipulation tasks, PPE attains 100% success rate on Door-open, Door-unlock, and Window-open, and leads on Drawer-open (79.61% vs. 67.20% for RUNE) and Hammer (96.27% vs. 91.86% for RUNE). The only task where PPE does not lead is Sweep-into, where RUNE achieves 99.62% vs. PPE's 96.47%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PPE is compared against four PbRL baselines: PEBBLE (relabeling + unsupervised pre-training), SURF (semi-supervised reward learning with data augmentation), RUNE (ensemble-based reward uncertainty for exploration), and QPA (query-policy alignment, the previous state-of-the-art). PPE outperforms QPA on all six DMControl locomotion tasks, with the largest margin on Walker-walk (+112.01 return) and Humanoid-stand (+123.47 return), and also on five of seven MetaWorld tasks. Against RUNE, PPE wins on all DMControl tasks and on Drawer-open, Hammer, Button-press, and Window-open in MetaWorld, but RUNE narrowly edges PPE on Sweep-into (99.62% vs. 96.47%). PEBBLE and SURF are consistently the weakest baselines, with PEBBLE particularly struggling on MetaWorld manipulation (e.g., 20.00% on Drawer-open) and high-variance results on DMControl. Welch's t-tests confirm that PPE's improvements over QPA are statistically significant (p < 0.05) on all tasks except Quadruped-walk (p = 0.2977), where the gap is small (660.71 vs. 621.51). The paper also demonstrates that PPE is compatible with different backbones: when applied to PEBBLE, PPE improves Walker-walk from 453.43 to 499.73, and when applied to QPA, it improves Walker-walk from 796.08 to 908.09, confirming its plug-and-play nature.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the QPA (Query-Policy Alignment) framework as its backbone, extending it with a new exploration mechanism that addresses a gap QPA and other PbRL methods (PEBBLE, SURF, RUNE) leave unaddressed: the distributional coverage of the preference buffer. Where RUNE drives exploration via ensemble reward-model disagreement, PPE replaces this with a Morse neural network for OOD detection and a closed-form proximal-policy extension that explicitly pushes the behavior policy toward underrepresented state-action regions. The mixture distribution query method further departs from prior active-learning-style query selection by deliberately balancing in-distribution and out-of-distribution samples, a design motivated by the finding that ensemble variance alone cannot identify OOD transitions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete and previously underappreciated failure mode in PbRL: reward models trained on static preference buffers systematically mis-evaluate out-of-distribution trajectories generated by policy exploration, thereby misguiding iterative policy updates. The demonstration that ensemble-based uncertainty (the basis of RUNE) fails to flag these OOD transitions challenges a common assumption in the field. However, the work is limited to continuous-control benchmarks (DMControl, MetaWorld) with scripted preferences in the main experiments, and the query method does not yet account for information content differences between trajectory pairs. Open questions include whether the Morse-network-based OOD detection and the KL-constrained proximal extension generalize to discrete action spaces, language-conditioned tasks, or settings where the preference buffer grows very large.
