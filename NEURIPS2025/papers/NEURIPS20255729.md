## Problem

While LLMs are increasingly deployed in high-stakes settings, their ability to abstain—recognizing when a question is unanswerable, underspecified, or based on false premises—remains largely unmeasured. No prior benchmark holistically evaluates abstention across the diverse scenarios where it is warranted, and the effect of reasoning fine-tuning on this capability is unknown despite reasoning models being explicitly optimized for complex problem-solving.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255729/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each benchmark sample consists of a text question with an optional context passage and a binary 'should abstain' label; for non-abstention samples, ground-truth answers are provided. The evaluated LLM generates a free-text response (capped at 4k tokens, temperature 0.8, top-p 0.95), which is then scored by a Llama 3.1 8B Instruct judge that outputs a binary abstention/non-abstention classification and, for non-abstention responses, a correctness label. The primary reported metric is abstention recall (proportion of should-abstain samples where the model correctly refrained), supplemented by precision, F1, and response accuracy.

## Method

AbstentionBench curates 17 existing datasets through a systematic literature review (82 candidates screened) and constructs 3 new underspecified reasoning variants (GSM8K-Abstain, GPQA-Abstain, MMLU-Math-Abstain) by removing context from answerable problems, yielding 20 datasets spanning six abstention scenarios (Answer Unknown, False Premise, Stale, Subjective, Underspecified Context, Underspecified Intent). Evaluation uses a quality-verified LLM-as-Judge (Llama 3.1 8B Instruct, 82.3% agreement with human annotations) to classify whether a response constitutes an abstention, enabling consistent scoring across the heterogeneous dataset collection. The paper evaluates 20 frontier LLMs and isolates the contribution of each post-training stage (SFT, DPO, RLVR) using Tulu 3 checkpoints, revealing that RLVR degrades abstention. A key finding is that reasoning fine-tuning (DeepSeek R1 Distill, s1.1) worsens abstention by ~24% on average compared to their instruction-tuned bases, even on math and science domains where reasoning models excel, with models hallucinating missing context and providing definitive answers despite expressing uncertainty in their reasoning traces.

## Results

AbstentionBench evaluates 20 frontier LLMs across 20 datasets (35k+ unanswerable questions spanning 6 scenarios: answer unknown, false premise, stale, subjective, underspecified context, and underspecified intent). Abstention recall ranges from near-perfect on BIG-Bench Known Unknowns to near-zero on MediQ, with Qwen 2.5 32B and GPT-4o performing best on average. Critically, reasoning fine-tuning degrades abstention by ~24% on average (e.g., DeepSeek R1 Distill Llama 70B and s1.1 32B vs. their instruction-tuned bases), and increasing model scale (Llama 3.1 8B→70B→405B) yields almost no improvement in mean abstention recall. The LLM-as-judge (Llama 3.1 8B Instruct) achieves 82.3% accuracy against human annotations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a benchmark paper, AbstentionBench's primary comparison is across 20 frontier LLMs rather than a single proposed method. The headline finding is that reasoning fine-tuned models (DeepSeek R1 Distill Llama 70B, s1.1 32B, Magistral Small, QwQ-32B) consistently underperform their instruction-tuned counterparts (Llama 3.3 70B, Qwen 2.5 32B) on abstention recall across all 20 datasets, including the math/science domains (GSM8K-Abstain, GPQA-Abstain, MMLU-Math-Abstain, UMWP) where reasoning models are explicitly optimized. Among non-reasoning models, Qwen 2.5 32B and GPT-4o achieve the highest average abstention recall, while o1 outperforms GPT-4o on specific subsets (QAQA, CoCoNot/False Premise) but not overall. Scale comparisons within the Llama family (8B, 70B, 405B) and Qwen family (7B, 14B, 32B, 72B) show negligible gains, indicating that abstention is not a capability that emerges with scale. Post-training stage analysis using Tulu 3 checkpoints reveals that SFT and DPO improve abstention recall relative to the base model, but the RLVR (verifiable reward) stage reverses this gain, suggesting that optimizing for clear-cut correctness rewards biases models toward definitive answers. A simple system prompt inspired by Brahman et al. boosts abstention for both standard and reasoning models, but the authors note this is unlikely to address the fundamental inability to reason about uncertainty.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the fragmented abstention evaluation literature—where prior datasets have each targeted a single scenario (unanswerable questions, false premises, underspecification)—into a unified 20-dataset, 6-scenario benchmark. Methodologically, it builds on the LLM-as-judge paradigm from Brahman et al. (CoCoNot) and the SimCSE-based question-type reconstruction from Amayuelas et al. (KUQ), but departs from prior work by systematically isolating the effect of reasoning fine-tuning (via paired comparisons of DeepSeek R1 vs. Llama 3.3 and s1 vs. Qwen 2.5) and post-training stages (via Tulu 3 checkpoints), a dimension no prior abstention study has addressed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that reasoning fine-tuning degrades abstention by ~24%—even in math and science domains where reasoning models are explicitly optimized—challenges the assumption that chain-of-thought reasoning generalizes to uncertainty reasoning, and suggests that verifiable-reward optimization (RLVR) may be structurally misaligned with abstention. The benchmark itself (35k+ questions, 20 frontier LLMs) provides the first systematic evaluation framework for a capability critical to reliable deployment. Open questions include whether post-training data explicitly covering underspecified contexts can close the gap, whether the LLM-judge evaluation introduces systematic bias, and whether the findings generalize beyond English and beyond the 20 models tested.
