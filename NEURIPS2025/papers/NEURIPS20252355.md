## Problem

Standard conformal risk control (CRC) provides distribution-free, finite-sample guarantees only for expected loss, leaving tail risks like CVaR unaddressed—an open question posed by the original CRC paper. Additionally, because CRC is applied purely post-hoc to a pre-trained model, it can severely degrade average-case performance (e.g., inflating false positive rates) with no mechanism to feed risk constraints back into training.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained model (PraNet for colonoscopy segmentation or a neural price forecaster for battery storage), a calibration set of loss functions, a risk threshold α, and a disutility function φ (e.g., the CVaR disutility [x]₊/(1−δ)). It outputs a fine-tuned model and a risk-controlling parameter λ selected via bisection on a pseudo-calibration subset. For segmentation, λ is a pixel-level decision threshold in [0,1] applied to per-pixel tumor probabilities; for battery storage, λ is a decision-scaling factor in [0,1] that multiplies the optimal charge/discharge vector, with the upper bound B(λ)=100λ derived empirically from training data.

## Method

The key theoretical insight is that any OCE risk R[L] can be bounded by a monotone transformation t̃(λ) = t + φ(L(λ) − t), preserving the monotonicity structure that CRC's bisection search requires (Theorem 1); for CVaR specifically, the nondecreasing assumption on losses can be relaxed to mere monotonicity (Theorem 2). Conformal risk training (Algorithm 2) then solves the resulting bi-level problem end-to-end: each minibatch is split into a pseudo-calibration set D_cal (used to compute λ(θ) via CORC bisection) and a prediction set D_pred (used to compute the cost gradient). The critical technical step is computing dλ/dθ exactly—possible in closed form when losses are piecewise-constant and costs strictly decreasing in λ (e.g., FNR with indicator losses), or when both losses and costs are convex in λ (e.g., the battery storage task loss), in which case the gradient is obtained by differentiating through the KKT conditions of the inner convex program.

## Results

On the tumor image segmentation task (4 colonoscopy datasets), conformal risk training reduces false positive rate (FPR) by 23–42% compared to post-hoc CRC while maintaining the target false negative rate (FNR): at α=0.01, FPR drops from 0.841 (post-hoc CRC) to 0.465 (CRT); at α=0.1, FPR drops from 0.256 to 0.152. On the battery storage task, conformal risk training achieves 7.2%–22.6% mean improvement in task loss (i.e., higher average profit) over the post-hoc conformal CVaR control baseline across all tested CVaR quantile levels δ∈{0.9, 0.95, 0.99} and risk thresholds α∈{2, 5, 10}, while all methods satisfy the CVaR constraint. In both tasks, the proposed method selects a larger (less conservative) decision threshold λ than the baselines, indicating it learns to be less conservative while preserving the risk guarantee.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: (1) post-hoc CRC applied directly to a pre-trained model, and (2) fine-tuning with a standard task loss (cross-entropy for segmentation, decision-focused task loss for battery storage) followed by post-hoc risk control. On tumor segmentation, conformal risk training dramatically outperforms both baselines on FPR: at α=0.01 it achieves 0.465 FPR vs. 0.841 (post-hoc CRC) and 0.811 (cross-entropy + CRC), a reduction of ~45% and ~43% respectively; at α=0.1 it achieves 0.152 vs. 0.256 and 0.293. Notably, the cross-entropy fine-tuning baseline provides essentially no improvement over raw post-hoc CRC, confirming that standard training objectives do not account for the downstream risk constraint. On battery storage, conformal risk training achieves 7.2%–22.6% lower task loss (higher profit) than post-hoc conformal CVaR control, while the 'fine-tune task loss' baseline shows no meaningful improvement over post-hoc control when the CVaR constraint is active. In both tasks, all methods satisfy their respective risk constraints (FNR ≤ α or CVaR ≤ α), so the comparison is purely on the secondary performance metric (FPR or profit).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Conformal Risk Control (CRC) framework of Angelopoulos et al. [6] in two complementary directions: (1) theoretically, it generalizes CRC from controlling expected loss to the broader family of Optimized Certainty Equivalent (OCE) risks, including CVaR, by showing that any OCE risk can be bounded via a monotone transformation of the loss; and (2) algorithmically, it extends the conformal training paradigm of Stutz et al. [47] and Yeh et al. [51]—which differentiates through conformal prediction during training—to the conformal risk control setting, enabling end-to-end optimization of model parameters and the risk-controlling threshold simultaneously. The paper explicitly positions itself as answering an open question posed in the original CRC paper about whether the framework can be extended to CVaR, and as the first conformal-style approach providing a certain (not high-probability) bound on risk measures beyond expected loss for monotone losses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that post-hoc risk control can substantially degrade model performance (e.g., 80% false positive rate when controlling FNR at 1%), and this paper shows that end-to-end conformal risk training recovers 23–42% of that FPR cost while maintaining provable risk guarantees. The OCE framework provides a clean, unified theoretical lens for tail risk control that is directly applicable to high-stakes domains (medical imaging, energy trading, LLM alignment) where CVaR or other tail-sensitive risk measures are the natural objective. Key limitations include the requirement for monotone and exchangeable losses, and the gradient computation is only characterized for specific structural cases (piecewise-constant or convex losses), leaving a gap for general nonconvex settings. Future work on tightness of the conformal OCE bound and extension to distortion or coherent risk measure families would broaden applicability.
