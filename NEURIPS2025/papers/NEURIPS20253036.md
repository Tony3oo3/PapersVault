## Problem

Existing evaluations of LLM research agents either rely on subjective LLM-as-a-judge scoring of full papers (AI Scientist) or on Kaggle-style engineering tasks that rarely demand genuine methodological novelty. Neither setup can objectively measure whether an agent can propose and implement a research idea that is both novel and effective relative to established baselines and top human solutions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253036/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each task provides a standardized repository containing a baseline model, training/inference/evaluation scripts, and explicit dev/test splits, with agents restricted to modifying only the methods/ directory. Optionally, a human-curated or CoI-Agent-generated idea is supplied as conceptual guidance. The agent's output is the edited codebase, scored on three objective axes: effectiveness (the competition's native metric, normalized so baseline=0 and top human=100), efficiency (training+inference runtime), and simplicity (logical lines of code). All runs are subject to per-task GPU memory and wall-clock limits (e.g., 48 GB / 1 hour for LLM merging, 16 GB / 0.5 hour for most others).

## Method

MLRC-BENCH curates seven open research problems from recent ML conference competitions (NeurIPS, ECCV, KDD) that require algorithmic creativity rather than brute-force tuning, and wraps them in an agent-agnostic, repository-level execution environment with tamper-proof evaluation (read-only eval scripts, hidden test data, snapshot-based dev-to-test selection). The primary agent scaffold is MLAB, a ReAct-style loop that alternates reasoning and file/Python actions over local resources; two augmented variants prepend an ideation phase using either Chain-of-Ideas (o1 with Semantic Scholar retrieval) or human-extracted ideas from top-participant reports. Evaluation uses a linear normalization (Relative Improvement to Human Solution) to make scores comparable across heterogeneous tasks, and a separate LLM-judge protocol (o1 scoring validity, clarity, rigor, generalizability, innovativeness on 1–5 Likert scales) is run solely to quantify the correlation between subjective novelty judgments and objective performance. The benchmark is designed to grow dynamically as new conference competitions are added and saturated tasks are retired.

## Results

The best-performing agent, gemini-exp-1206 under the MLAB scaffolding, closes only 9.3% of the gap between the competition baseline and top human participant scores on average across 7 ML research tasks. Other tested LLMs perform worse: Llama 3.1 405B Instruct achieves 6.3%, GPT-4o 5.4%, o3-mini 4.2%, and Claude 3.5 Sonnet v2 averages -5.2% (dragged down by a catastrophic failure on machine unlearning). Providing additional ideas—whether human-curated (3.5%) or AI-generated via CoI-Agent with o1 (7.1%)—does not consistently improve over the implementation-only MLAB setting. The Spearman correlation between LLM-judged innovativeness and actual effectiveness is near zero (-0.06), indicating that subjective LLM-as-a-judge scores are unreliable proxies for real research impact.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates five frontier LLMs (gemini-exp-1206, Llama 3.1 405B, o3-mini, Claude 3.5 Sonnet v2, GPT-4o) under the MLAB ReAct-style scaffolding, with and without supplementary ideas from human experts or the CoI-Agent (o1). Gemini-exp-1206 leads overall at 9.3% average relative improvement, but no agent exceeds 50% on any single task except rainfall prediction (GPT-4o: 47.5%), where the weak U-Net-style baseline and publicly available similar solutions lower the difficulty. Claude 3.5 Sonnet v2 performs best on 4 of 7 tasks individually but collapses to -94.7% on machine unlearning due to treating forgetting and retention as separate objectives rather than jointly optimizing them. Adding human-curated ideas (3.5% avg) or AI-generated ideas via CoI-Agent (7.1% avg) does not consistently outperform the baseline MLAB-only setting (5.4% for GPT-4o), suggesting the bottleneck is implementation rather than ideation. In the inference-time scaling experiment (Pass@k), human ideas yield higher success rates than AI-generated ideas, but under a fixed compute budget, allocating resources to more ideas versus more trials per idea produces no significant difference due to diminishing returns.

## Relation to Prior Work

MLRC-BENCH carves out a specific niche between two existing evaluation paradigms for LLM research agents: end-to-end scientific discovery pipelines (AI Scientist, MLR-Copilot) that rely on subjective LLM/human judges, and Kaggle-style ML engineering benchmarks (MLAgentBench, MLE-Bench, MLGym-Bench) that skip idea generation entirely. It departs from both by isolating the method-proposal-plus-implementation stages, sourcing tasks from ML conference competitions to ensure research-grade novelty, and evaluating with objective performance metrics under explicit compute constraints. The benchmark also extends prior work on repository-level code generation (RepoMaster, OpenHands) by requiring agents to navigate and modify multi-file research codebases rather than produce single-script solutions, and it repurposes the MLAB ReAct scaffolding and Chain-of-Ideas agent as evaluation vehicles rather than as the primary contribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark provides the field with a scalable, objective, and continually-updating evaluation framework for AI research agents at a moment when subjective LLM-as-a-judge evaluations dominate. The finding that the best tested agent closes only 9.3% of the baseline-to-human gap, combined with the near-zero Spearman correlation between LLM-judged innovativeness and empirical effectiveness, raises serious questions about whether current agentic pipelines can meaningfully advance ML research or whether the field's reliance on LLM-judged novelty is masking fundamental implementation failures. Open questions include whether performance-informed tree search, multi-agent collaboration, or substantially longer inference budgets can close the gap, and whether the benchmark's 7-task scope adequately represents the breadth of ML research challenges.
