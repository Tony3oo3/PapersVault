## Problem

Intracortical brain-machine interface decoders suffer from significant signal non-stationarity over time, requiring frequent recalibration, yet no standardized dataset spanning year-long timescales exists to evaluate robustness and domain adaptation methods. Existing benchmarks like FALCON and NLB cover only months or single sessions, making it impossible to study how neural population dynamics and decoder performance evolve over the full lifespan of an implanted array.

## Input / Output

Each of the 312 sessions provides 96-channel neural data preprocessed into spiking-band power (300–1000 Hz bandpass, 20 ms bins) and threshold crossing rate (−4.5×RMS crossings, 20 ms bins), alongside 2-DOF finger position and velocity at the same temporal resolution. Sessions are stored as NWB-compliant HDF5 files on the DANDI archive, with 375 trials per session (capped to limit day-to-day imbalance) and metadata including target positions, trial timestamps, and per-channel impedance. For decoding experiments, z-scored SBP serves as the model input and predicted finger-group position and velocity as the output, with each session split into 300 training and 75 holdout trials.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is the LINK dataset itself—312 sessions over 1,242 days from a single NHP performing a 2-DOF dexterous finger task—paired with baseline analyses that establish reference performance for future methods. Decoding is performed with ridge regression and LSTM networks trained on z-scored SBP, evaluated via R² on same-day holdouts and on future sessions up to 100–200 days out to quantify performance decay. Two recalibration strategies are tested: multi-session pretraining (1–5 consecutive sessions) and daily fine-tuning with as little as 30 s of labeled data, the latter showing that even minimal continual adaptation can reverse long-term accuracy decay. Population-level analyses use full-dataset PCA, per-day participation ratios, and per-channel preferred tuning vectors to characterize how neural activity and its behavioral relationship evolve across the ~3.5-year recording span.

## Results

Across the 1,242-day dataset, average spiking-band power (SBP) decreased by ~0.033 μV per month, and the participation ratio (PR) of active channels declined by ~0.07 PR per month, with ~10 dimensions sufficient to capture 80% of population variance. For same-day decoding (300-trial train / 75-trial holdout), LSTM consistently outperformed ridge regression (RR) across all days and both degrees of freedom, with position predictions outperforming velocity predictions and MRS finger group predictions outperforming IDX. When evaluated on non-training days within 100 days, both decoders showed sharp short-term performance decay that stabilized on longer timescales. Daily fine-tuning of the LSTM with as little as 30 s of labeled data per session reduced both short- and long-term decay, and fine-tuning with 120 s or 300 s allowed later decoders to surpass the original day-0 baseline performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against external methods but provides internal comparisons between two decoder architectures (ridge regression and LSTM) and three training paradigms (single-session, multi-session up to 5 sessions, and daily fine-tuning). LSTM outperforms RR on every single day for both position and velocity prediction across both finger groups, confirming the benefit of capturing temporal dynamics. Multi-session pre-training (up to 5 consecutive sessions) reduces the initial performance drop within ~5 days of training but does not significantly alter the long-term decay curve, and slightly decreases day-0 performance. In contrast, daily fine-tuning with small amounts of labeled data (30 s to 5 min per session) addresses both the short-term and long-term decay, with 120 s or 300 s of fine-tuning data enabling later decoders to exceed the original day-0 R². These results suggest that continual adaptation with minimal labeled data is more effective than one-time multi-session calibration for maintaining long-term iBMI performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

The LINK dataset carves new ground by providing the first open-access, preprocessed intracortical neural and kinematic dataset spanning multi-year timescales (~3.5 years, 312 sessions) from a single non-human primate. It explicitly addresses gaps left by prior benchmarks: FALCON covers broad task variety but only months of data per task, NLB offers task diversity but mostly single sessions, and other open datasets (e.g., Gallego-Carracedo et al.) span long periods but with very few recording sessions. The analyses applied to characterize the dataset (PCA, preferred tuning, ridge regression, LSTM decoding) are standard methods from the iBMI and neural population dynamics literature, repurposed here to validate and describe the new resource rather than to introduce new decoding algorithms.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LINK fills a critical gap by enabling systematic comparison of domain adaptation and decoder recalibration methods over year-long timescales, a regime previously inaccessible to the community. It also supports neuroscience questions about the stability of low-dimensional neural manifolds and somatotopic tuning over extended periods, and provides a testbed for continuous domain adaptation in machine learning. Key limitations include the single-subject design, extended time gaps, absence of raw voltage signals for most sessions, and the constrained 2-DOF task, which limits generalizability to more naturalistic or complex movements. Open questions include whether the observed stability of tuning directions and separable neural trajectories persists for more complex tasks or with further array degradation.
