## Problem

In Skill Incremental Learning (SIL), as the skill library evolves through continual updates, previously trained downstream policies lose compatibility with the updated skills, limiting reusability and generalization. Existing SIL approaches assume synchronous updates between skills and policies, so adding or modifying skills invalidates existing hierarchical policies without re-training. The paper addresses this by formalizing two compatibility requirements—Forward Skill Compatibility (new skills aid future policy training) and Backward Skill Compatibility (existing policies remain functional and can benefit from improved skills)—and designing a framework that satisfies both without policy re-training or structural adaptation.

## Input / Output

At inference, the system receives a robot state vector (60-dim in Franka Kitchen, 140-dim in Meta-World) and a task identifier, and outputs a 9-dim (Kitchen) or 4-dim (Meta-World) robot action. Internally, the high-level policy proposes a discrete subtask index from a shared latent space Z_p that grows append-only across SIL phases; the interface then maps this subtask to a skill index via trajectory distribution similarity over (state, subgoal) pairs, where the subgoal is the state m=20 steps ahead. The skill decoder is a goal-conditioned diffusion model that conditions on the hashed skill embedding and current state to denoise the action.

## Method

SIL-C introduces a bilateral lazy learning interface that decouples skill evolution from policy execution through a two-stage instance-based classifier operating on multimodal Gaussian prototypes (with diagonal covariance) stored in append-only memories. Stage 1 (skill validation) predicts a subgoal from the current state using task-side prototypes, then checks via Mahalanobis distance whether the proposed subtask's skill prototype is within a 99% chi-square threshold of that subgoal; if it passes, the subtask is accepted unchanged. Stage 2 (skill hooking) activates on validation failure, restricting the candidate skill set to those whose subgoal prototypes are in-distribution and selecting the best match by state-space prototype distance. The skill space is updated each SIL phase by unsupervised clustering of streamed demonstrations followed by K-means sub-clustering in state and subgoal spaces, while the subtask space is built per-task from expert demonstrations using the same procedure. This design defers all alignment decisions to inference time, preserving existing training pipelines (diffusion-based skill decoder with LoRA adapter append, energy-based prior for high-level policy labels) while enabling skills and policies to evolve independently.

## Results

On the Franka Kitchen Emergent SIL scenario, SIL-C (w/ PTGM, AA) achieves an Overall AUC of 71.8% and a Final FWT of 87.2%, matching the joint-training upper bound of 86.9% while improving BwSC AUC from 46.9% (PTGM+AA) to 66.8%. In the Explicit SIL scenario, SIL-C achieves a BwSC BWT of +42.5pp, nearly tripling the best Type I baseline (PTGM+AA at +0.0pp) and substantially outperforming Type II methods (Skill-prototypes at +0.0pp). In the few-shot 1-shot/20% transition setting, SIL-C achieves a BwSC AUC of 46.4% versus 23.5% for the PTGM+AA baseline, nearly doubling backward compatibility performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SIL-C outperforms all Type I baselines (BUDS and PTGM with FT/ER/AA) across every metric in both Emergent and Explicit scenarios. Against the strongest Type I baseline (PTGM+AA), SIL-C improves BwSC AUC by 19.9pp (66.8% vs. 46.9%) and Overall AUC by 13.6pp (71.8% vs. 58.2%) in Emergent SIL, and by 31.9pp and 17.4pp respectively in Explicit SIL. Against Type II methods that require predefined semantic skill labels, SIL-C matches or exceeds their BwSC performance (66.8% vs. 67.8% for Instructions+ER in Emergent; 46.5% vs. 14.6% for Skill-prototypes in Explicit) while achieving a higher Final FWT (87.2% vs. 77.4% and 75.0%), and does so without any semantic supervision. In the few-shot 1-shot/20% setting, SIL-C nearly doubles the BwSC AUC of PTGM+AA (46.4% vs. 23.5%) and maintains positive BWT (+12.0pp) where the baseline degrades (−2.9pp). SIL-C's Final FWT (87.2%) matches the joint-training upper bound (86.9%), confirming that the lazy learning interface enables full utilization of accumulated skills without retraining.

## Relation to Prior Work

SIL-C builds directly on PTGM's goal-conditioned diffusion skill decoder and TAIL's adapter-append strategy for the low-level skill library, and on Implicit Behavioral Cloning's energy-based prior for high-level policy supervision. Its key departure is the insertion of a bilateral lazy learning interface between the policy and decoder layers, which reframes subtask-to-skill alignment as an instance-based classification problem over append-only Gaussian mixture prototypes rather than a parametric mapping. This contrasts with prior SIL work (BUDS/LOTUS, IMANIP, SPECI) that assumes synchronous updates between the skill library and downstream policies, and with continual pre-training approaches that modify the backbone to preserve forward compatibility. The paper thus carves out a specific sub-problem—maintaining bidirectional skill-policy compatibility without retraining—that prior hierarchical SIL methods leave unaddressed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a practical, modular solution to a real deployment bottleneck: as a robot's skill library grows, previously learned task policies silently degrade or break, forcing costly retraining. By deferring alignment to inference time via prototype matching, SIL-C decouples skill evolution from policy maintenance, enabling append-only skill libraries that are attractive for long-lived embodied agents. However, all experiments are confined to two simulation benchmarks (Franka Kitchen, Meta-World) with well-defined low-dimensional state spaces and only four SIL phases; the reliance on unsupervised clustering quality for prototype construction, the absence of real-robot validation, and the limited exploration of noisy or high-dimensional skill distributions leave open whether the approach generalizes to the messier skill distributions encountered in practice.
