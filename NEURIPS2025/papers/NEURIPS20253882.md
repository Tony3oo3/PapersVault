## Problem

Existing defenses against poisoning attacks in federated learning focus on protecting the training phase via robust aggregation or client detection, but they fail when client data is highly non-IID or the fraction of malicious clients is large, leaving a poisoned global model deployed. No prior method can trace back which specific clients performed the poisoning attack after the fact, creating a critical accountability gap in post-deployment forensics.

## Input / Output

FLForensics takes a misclassified target input x with its target label y, a synthesized non-target input x' (random pixels for images, random tweet of matching length for text), and stored check points consisting of intermediate global models and per-client model updates at k selected training rounds. Each client's update is ℓ₂-normalized before score computation to offset scale differences. The output is a set of predicted malicious client identifiers, derived from per-client two-dimensional influence scores (s_i, s_i') computed via first-order Taylor expansion of cross-entropy loss change across the check points.

## Method

FLForensics operates in two steps. First, it computes each client's influence score on the misclassified target input by summing the inner product of the loss gradient with the client's update across check-point rounds (Equation 4), and a second score on a non-target input (Equation 5), yielding a 2-D score (s_i, s_i'). The key insight is that non-IID data creates Category I benign clients with many target-label examples whose s_i resembles that of malicious clients; the non-target score s_i' separates them because malicious clients have large s_i but small s_i', while Category I benign clients have both large. Second, clients are clustered using HDBSCAN with scaled Euclidean distance (each dimension normalized by its range); clusters with positive mean s_i are flagged as potentially malicious, and within each such cluster the ratio s_i'/s_i is compared against a global threshold to distinguish truly malicious clients (small ratio) from Category I benign clients (large ratio). Theoretical guarantees under local linearity and behavioral-difference assumptions prove that malicious clients always have smaller influence-score gaps s_i' − s_i than benign clients, justifying the ratio-based separation.

## Results

FLForensics achieves near-perfect detection across all five datasets and three poisoning attacks: DACC is 1.000 or ≥0.95 in every setting, with FPR and FNR at 0 or ≤3% in only a handful of cases (e.g., Edge attack on CIFAR-10: DACC=0.980, FNR=0.100). Under the ALIE attack, FLForensics attains perfect 1.000/0.000/0.000 (DACC/FPR/FNR) on all five datasets. The variant FLForensics-A, which uses only the target input, degrades substantially (e.g., DACC=0.900, FPR=0.125 on CIFAR-10 for Scaling), confirming the necessity of the non-target input for separating malicious from Category I benign clients.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FLForensics consistently outperforms Poison Forensics (PF) [36], a centralized-learning method that requires direct access to clients' local data. On CIFAR-10 under the Scaling attack, FLForensics achieves DACC=1.000 with FPR=0.000, while PF reaches only DACC=0.900 with FPR=0.125; the gap widens on ImageNet-Fruits where PF drops to DACC=0.600 and FNR=0.500. Against FLDetector [43], a training-phase detection method, FLForensics is categorically superior: FLDetector reports FNR=1.000 (missing every malicious client) under the ALIE attack on all five datasets, and DACC as low as 0.020 on Sentiment140 under Scaling, whereas FLForensics maintains DACC≥0.95 in the same settings. The internal variant FLForensics-A (target input only) confirms the importance of the non-target input: it mislabels 12.5% of benign clients on CIFAR-10 (Scaling) and 10% on Sentiment140 (ALIE), while full FLForensics keeps FPR at 0 or ≤2.5%. FLForensics-G, which extends the GAS influence estimator to FL, performs worst among all methods on several settings (e.g., DACC=0.480, FNR=0.950 on MNIST for Scaling), underscoring that the two-dimensional scoring and HDBSCAN-based detection are critical to FLForensics's effectiveness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing poison forensics to federated learning, a setting where prior work has focused almost exclusively on training-phase defenses (robust aggregation like Trim/Median/FLTrust/FLAME, or client-detection methods like FLDetector). Conceptually, it repurposes influence-function ideas (first-order Taylor expansion of cross-entropy loss) from centralized learning and adapts them to operate on federated model updates rather than raw training data, combining them with HDBSCAN clustering and a two-dimensional scoring scheme to handle non-IID client data. The closest prior work—centralized poison forensics (PF, GAS, Beagle)—assumes the server has access to all training examples, an assumption that does not hold in FL; the authors show these methods degrade when naively ported to the federated setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FLForensics fills a critical operational gap: when training-phase defenses fail (which the paper demonstrates is common under non-IID data or high fractions of malicious clients), there was previously no mechanism to identify which clients poisoned the deployed model. The near-perfect detection accuracy (DACC ≈ 1.0, FPR/FNR ≈ 0) across five datasets and three attack families, backed by formal guarantees, makes this a practically deployable post-incident tool. Key limitations include restriction to targeted attacks (untargeted poisoning remains open), storage overhead for checkpoints (up to ~22 GB for ImageNet-Fruits), and the assumption that a misclassified target input has already been identified. Future work on untargeted attacks and adversarially crafted misclassified inputs would broaden applicability.
