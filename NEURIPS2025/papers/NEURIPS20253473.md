## Problem

While memorization in language models is well-documented as a privacy risk and capability, the mechanisms by which it emerges during training remain largely unexplored. Prior work characterizes memorization in fully-trained models but does not explain when, what, or why specific sequences get memorized, leaving open whether memorization is a simple accumulation, a recency effect, or something more structured.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253473/cropped_page4_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The study trains small decoder-only GPT-2 models (0.5M–12M non-embedding parameters, with E/L ratio 100–130 following Llama 3) on text sequences of 100–300 tokens drawn from WikiText-103 or synthetic datasets with controlled pattern structure. Memorization is measured by generating 30-token completions from 50-token prompts of each training sequence and tracking exact match per sequence every 100 training steps over 50k total steps, alongside validation-set generalization metrics. The per-sequence binary memorized/forgotten tracking over many epochs is the key measurement that reveals non-monotonic dynamics invisible to aggregate loss curves.

## Method

The paper is an empirical study that tracks memorization dynamics by training multiple model sizes on both natural (WikiText-103) and synthetic datasets with systematically varied pattern difficulty (monotonic increasing sequences, in-context mappings, random digits, shuffled text, etc.). The central finding is that memorization is pattern-driven rather than monotonic or recency-based: models memorize sub-datasets in distinct bursts ordered by pattern simplicity, individual sequences are repeatedly forgotten and re-memorized, and the model generalizes static lookup patterns to unseen data while failing on dynamic lookups. A second key finding, termed 'overthinking,' shows that larger models delay memorization on low-pattern data relative to smaller models, suggesting an inductive bias toward pattern-seeking that backfires when patterns are absent. The experimental design uses equal-proportion data mixtures to observe phase transitions in memorization order, and ablations over shuffle degree, jump probability, and attention head count confirm the robustness of the pattern-dependence finding.

## Results

The paper's central quantitative finding is that shuffling WikiText word order reduces memorization (exact match) by ~30%, from 0.96 (baseline) to 0.63–0.72 across model sizes (0.5M–8M), demonstrating that linguistic patterns accelerate memorization. Individual training sequences are forgotten on average 19–39 times during training (Table 1), and newly memorized sequences overlap with the most recent batch by only 1.5–14.7% (Table 2), ruling out recency as a driver. On generalization, models achieve EM=1 on an unseen ICM validation set (static lookup) and 97.3–98.1% monotonically-increasing bigram adherence on MIJ validation (vs. 10% random chance), but EM=0 on Random Seed Lookup validation (dynamic lookup), showing that only simple static patterns are acquired.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical study without external method baselines; comparisons are internal, across model sizes, dataset types, and data perturbations. On pattern-rich data (WikiText, MI, ICM), larger models (8M, 12M) match or outperform smaller ones (4M, 2M), but on low-pattern data (Random Digits, Random Seed Lookup, Repeat 5 Tokens Ago), larger models memorize significantly slower — the 'overthinking' effect. Shuffling ablations (Table 5) show a graded effect: preserving top-50% 3-grams retains EM≈0.78, while full word-level shuffling drops to 0.63–0.68, confirming that finer-grained n-gram structure drives memorization speed. In mixture experiments, WikiText is almost fully memorized before Random Digits memorization begins, and the phase boundary shifts later as the random-data fraction decreases, indicating a curriculum-like ordering by pattern strength.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the memorization-in-LLMs literature (Carlini et al., Nasr et al.) by shifting the lens from post-hoc analysis of fully-trained models to tracking memorization dynamics during training. It connects the recitation/reconstruction/recollection taxonomy of Prashanth et al. to a pattern-driven mechanism, drawing on the curriculum-learning and simplicity-bias literature (Arpit et al., Bengio et al.) to explain why models memorize in ordered bursts. The 'overthinking' finding is positioned alongside grokking (Power et al.) and phase-transition analyses (Chen et al.), but applies specifically to memorization rather than generalization, carving out a new sub-phenomenon within the pattern-seeking framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By showing that memorization is pattern-driven rather than arbitrary, the paper provides a mechanistic account that could inform privacy-preserving training strategies (e.g., targeting pattern detection to reduce verbatim recall of sensitive data). The overthinking phenomenon—where larger models delay memorization on low-pattern data—challenges the assumption that scale uniformly accelerates learning and may have practical implications for model sizing decisions. Key limitations include the use of small models (≤12M in main experiments), multi-epoch training (unlike single-epoch foundation model training), and 30-token completion evaluation. Open questions include whether a hierarchy of patterns can be formalized, the mechanistic basis of overthinking, and whether these dynamics persist at foundation-model scale with single-epoch training.
