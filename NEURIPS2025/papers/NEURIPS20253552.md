## Problem

Existing AIGI detectors overfit to spurious correlations (image format, size, semantic content) in biased training datasets, causing severe performance degradation on unbiased or in-the-wild test sets. Prior data-alignment methods (VAE or diffusion reconstruction) align real and synthetic images at the pixel level but introduce a subtle frequency-level misalignment: reconstructed images restore high-frequency details that JPEG compression removes from real images, creating a new bias that detectors exploit as a shortcut for classification.

## Input / Output

The detector accepts RGB images resized to 336×336 (random crop during training, center crop during validation, with padding for insufficient dimensions) and outputs a binary real/synthetic prediction. Training uses only MSCOCO validation images and their DDA-aligned synthetic counterparts; no dataset-specific fine-tuning or threshold adjustment is applied at evaluation time. The backbone is DINOv2 ViT-L/14 fine-tuned with LoRA (rank 8), optimized with a base batch size of 16 and gradient accumulation over 4 iterations to reach an effective batch size of 64.

## Method

DDA is a three-step data alignment pipeline that generates synthetic training images closely matching real ones in both pixel and frequency domains. Step 1 performs VAE reconstruction (using Stable Diffusion 2.1's VAE) to produce pixel-wise similar images containing VAE-specific generative artifacts. Step 2 applies frequency-level alignment by estimating each real image's JPEG quality factor and applying the same compression to its VAE-reconstructed counterpart (with 50% probability during training), eliminating the high-frequency energy discrepancy. Step 3 applies pixel mixup (x_mix = r_pixel · x_real + (1−r_pixel) · x_syn, with r_pixel sampled from U(0, R_pixel)) to further tighten pixel-domain alignment. The key insight is that VAE artifacts generalize across generators because the VAE decoder is the final shared stage in diffusion-based pipelines, so a detector trained on these closely aligned pairs learns a tighter, more transferable decision boundary that does not rely on format, size, or frequency-band biases.

## Results

DDA achieves an average balanced accuracy of 90.7% across 11 benchmarks, a 15.7% improvement over the second-best method (AlignedForensics at 75.0%). DDA also attains the highest minimum accuracy of 81.4% (vs. 53.9% for AlignedForensics), with the smallest cross-benchmark deviation (±5.3 vs. ±11.1 for the runner-up). On the newly proposed EvalGEN benchmark featuring the latest auto-regressive and diffusion generators, DDA reaches 97.2% balanced accuracy, outperforming DRCT (77.8%) and AlignedForensics (68.0%). On the in-the-wild Chameleon benchmark, DDA scores 82.4%, the only method above 70%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DDA outperforms all eight baselines on the average across 11 benchmarks, with the largest gaps over AlignedForensics (+15.7%) and DRCT (+20.6%). Against AlignedForensics (the closest competitor, also using VAE reconstruction), DDA wins on 10 of 11 datasets, with the most dramatic gains on BFree-Online (+26.6%), Chameleon (+11.4%), and EvalGEN (+29.2%), while AlignedForensics retains a narrow edge only on DDA-COCO (86.5 vs. 92.2 is actually DDA winning; AlignedForensics leads on no dataset in the main table). Against DRCT (diffusion-reconstruction alignment), DDA leads on every benchmark, most notably on EvalGEN (97.2 vs. 77.8) and Chameleon (82.4 vs. 56.6). Frequency-based detectors (NPR, SAFE) and CLIP-based detectors (UnivFD, C2P-CLIP, FatFormer) all fall below 65% average, with NPR and SAFE collapsing to near-chance on EvalGEN (2.9% and 1.1% respectively), confirming that methods without data alignment fail catastrophically on unseen generators. The only exception where DDA does not rank first is ForenSynths (81.4%), where FatFormer (90.0%) and C2P-CLIP (92.0%) benefit from having been trained on ProGAN, a generator also present in that benchmark.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the data-alignment line of work initiated by AlignedForensics [33], which showed that VAE reconstruction can align synthetic images with real ones at the pixel level. The key departure is the identification of a residual frequency-domain misalignment: JPEG compression strips high-frequency details from real images, while VAE reconstructions preserve them, creating a spurious cue that detectors exploit. DDA addresses this by adding frequency-level alignment (matching JPEG quality factors) and pixel mixup on top of the VAE reconstruction step, positioning itself as a strict superset of prior reconstruction-based alignment methods (DRCT, B-Free, SemGIR) that only address pixel or semantic alignment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that pixel-level alignment is insufficient because of a hidden frequency-domain gap challenges a working assumption in the AIGI detection community and provides a concrete, actionable fix (JPEG matching + mixup) that is cheap to implement. The introduction of EvalGEN—the first benchmark to include auto-regressive generators (FLUX, GoT, Infinity, NOVA, OmniGen)—and DDA-COCO, a controlled test for bias exploitation, will likely become standard evaluation tools as the field moves beyond diffusion-only testing. A key open question is whether DDA's alignment strategy remains effective under heavy real-world post-processing (smartphone AI enhancements, social-media compression), which the authors acknowledge as a limitation.
