## Problem

Classical fMRI studies of visual selectivity depend on experimenter-curated categories (faces, places, words), leaving unknown what additional visual concepts have dedicated cortical regions. Data-driven alternatives are constrained by the high cost of fMRI data collection and by simple, typically linear encoding models that cannot generalize beyond the limited stimuli actually shown to participants in the scanner.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255490/cropped_page1_idx5.webp)

## Input / Output

The brain encoder takes a natural scene image, processes it through a frozen DINOv2 ViT-B backbone to produce patch embeddings, and outputs vertex-wise fMRI beta predictions across 327,684 cortical vertices organized into 1,000 Schaefer parcels. Downstream, the pipeline consumes large image datasets (ImageNet, BrainDIVE-generated images) to rank stimuli by predicted parcel activation, producing parcel-level selectivity labels as CLIP-space concept vectors (averaged embeddings of top-32 maximally-activating images) and curated superstimulus collages for targeted fMRI experimentation.

## Method

The core architecture is a single-layer transformer decoder with parcel-specific learnable queries that attend to frozen DINOv2 patch embeddings via cross-attention, followed by a linear projection to vertex-level fMRI predictions; only the queries, decoder layer, and linear mappings are trained (MSE loss on NSD 7T fMRI data), while the backbone remains frozen. An ensemble of 16 models (4 DINOv2 layers × 2 hemispheres × 2 seeds) is combined with softmax-weighted averaging based on per-vertex validation accuracy. The key differentiator is the cross-attention mechanism, which enables nonlinear routing of visual information to parcels and significantly outperforms a parameter-matched linear CLS-token baseline. Because the encoder is fully differentiable, it supports gradient-based superstimulus generation via BrainDIVE (a diffusion model guided by encoder gradients) and large-scale in silico ranking of millions of ImageNet images to discover selectivity hypotheses for concepts never presented in the scanner.

## Results

The DINOv2 (ViT-B) + Transformer brain encoder achieves noise-ceiling-corrected Pearson correlations of 0.43–0.48 across four subjects, substantially outperforming the linear-mapping baseline (0.33–0.39) and the RADIOv2.5-H backbone (0.26–0.35). For within-subject selectivity mapping, the encoder-selected ImageNet/BrainDIVE stimuli label a comparable or greater fraction of parcels significantly above chance (e.g., 170/192 for S2 with BrainDIVE vs. 163/192 for the NSD-train baseline) and achieve mean Spearman's ρ of 0.13–0.19, matching or exceeding the NSD-train baseline (0.15–0.16). Cross-subject validation on 49 shared parcels yields similar patterns, with the encoder outperforming the baseline in 22–36 of 49 parcels on a head-to-head basis.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the linear-mapping baseline, the DINOv2 (ViT-B) + Transformer encoder improves encoding accuracy by approximately 0.10–0.12 across all subjects (e.g., 0.45 vs. 0.33 for S1), confirming that the cross-attention mechanism flexibly routes information better than a simple linear projection. Compared to the CLIP vision backbone, DINOv2 performs substantially better in the visual cortex but similarly in many extra-visual regions; compared to RADIOv2.5-H, DINOv2 is consistently superior (0.43–0.48 vs. 0.26–0.35). For the selectivity-mapping task, the encoder-selected stimuli (from ImageNet or BrainDIVE) generally outperform the NSD-train baseline, which relies solely on stimuli shown in the scanner: in head-to-head comparisons (Table 8), the encoder beats the baseline in 62–123 of ~180–196 parcels per subject, with BrainDIVE-selected stimuli showing the largest advantage (e.g., 123/192 for S2). The mean Spearman's ρ for the encoder (0.13–0.19) is comparable to or slightly above the NSD-train baseline (0.15–0.16), and the authors note these values fall in the range of well-established regions like PPA and RSC, indicating meaningful selectivity detection.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the transformer-based brain encoder architecture of Adeli et al. [2] from visual-cortex prediction to whole-brain vertex-wise fMRI prediction, and repurposes it as a 'digital twin' for in silico hypothesis generation. It integrates BrainDIVE's diffusion-based superstimulus generation [46] and large-scale image datasets (ImageNet, BrainDIVE) to go beyond the limited stimulus sets available in fMRI experiments, building on the broader semantic-mapping literature (Huth et al., Kay et al.) and prior superstimulus work validated in non-human primates and mice. The key departure from prior work is the pipeline-level contribution: using a differentiable encoder to rank millions of images for parcel selectivity, then verifying those hypotheses in silico against held-out fMRI data, rather than relying on experimenter-curated category hypotheses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This framework offers a scalable, data-driven path toward whole-brain semantic atlases that go beyond the handful of well-known category-selective regions, potentially revealing human-specific selectivity in areas previously uncharacterized. The in silico verification (showing encoder-derived labels predict held-out activation better than training-set baselines) provides a principled way to generate fMRI hypotheses without expensive scanning, which could accelerate the field's iteration cycle. Key open questions include whether the discovered superstimuli elicit even stronger responses in new fMRI experiments (not yet tested), how dataset biases (e.g., over-representation of certain ImageNet categories) affect discovered selectivity, and whether the category-selectivity framing captures the full computational structure of these regions.
