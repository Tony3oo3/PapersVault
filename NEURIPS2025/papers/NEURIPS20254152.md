## Problem

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models achieve task specialization by partitioning work across multiple expert networks, but the resulting computational overhead in parameters, memory, and latency makes them impractical for resource-constrained edge devices with strict real-time constraints, such as cochlear implants requiring causal processing under 10 ms. The paper asks whether the divide-and-conquer specialization of a full MoE ensemble can be replicated within a single expert model at a fraction of the cost.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254152/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes 65-dimensional log-compressed time-frequency features extracted from 8 ms frames (2 ms overlap) of reverberant speech using the Nucleus CI ACE strategy, conditioned on a 40-class one-hot phoneme label produced by a separate causal phoneme classifier. It outputs a 65-dimensional ideal ratio mask (values in [0,1]) that is element-wise multiplied with the magnitude spectrum of the reverberant signal to yield the enhanced T-F representation, which is then resynthesized into CI electrodograms via a sine-wave vocoder for intelligibility evaluation.

## Method

The Omni-Expert replaces N separate expert networks with a single lightweight RNN (LSTM or GRU+multi-head attention) whose input features are modulated by a phoneme-specific affine transformation z_n = a_n ⊙ x + b_n, where the scale vector a_n and shift vector b_n are predicted by two small MLPs from the one-hot phoneme encoding during training and stored in a lookup table at inference. A phoneme classifier serves as the gating network, weighting the single expert's output across all N subtask-transformed inputs. The key insight is that learned per-subtask scale-and-shift transformations partition the feature space into homogeneous, subtask-specific regions, making a single expert effectively specialize without the parameter and compute cost of N experts. The single expert is initialized from a pretrained phoneme-independent model and fine-tuned on the full dataset with a T-F signal loss, while the transformation MLPs are trained jointly; at inference only the precomputed 40×65 scale and shift vectors are needed, reducing the model to roughly the size of one expert plus the phoneme classifier.

## Results

Using the GRU+A architecture, the Omni-Expert with predicted phonemes (OE^p) achieves a SRMR-CI of 2.014 ± 0.011 and STOI of 0.829 ± 0.001, outperforming the phoneme-independent baseline (PI: 1.873 / 0.812) and the counterpart MoE with predicted phonemes (MoE^p: 1.948 / 0.829). With ideal phoneme knowledge, OE^k reaches SRMR-CI of 2.113 ± 0.010 and STOI of 0.850 ± 0.001, surpassing MoE^k (1.945 / 0.833) and approaching the ideal ratio mask (IRM: 2.187 / 0.972). The OE model uses only ~133K parameters (vs. ~5.1M for the 40-expert MoE) and trains in 1 hr 21 min (vs. 10 hr 47 min) on an NVIDIA Titan V GPU.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the phoneme-based dense Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) model from Chu et al. [33], which uses 40 separate expert networks (one per phoneme) gated by a phoneme classifier. The OE consistently outperforms the MoE on SRMR-CI across both architectures: with predicted phonemes, OE^p achieves 2.014 vs. MoE^p's 1.948 (GRU+A) and 1.794 vs. 1.744 (LSTM); with known phonemes, OE^k reaches 2.113 vs. MoE^k's 1.945 (GRU+A) and 1.938 vs. 1.841 (LSTM). On STOI, the two methods tie with predicted phonemes (0.829 for GRU+A, 0.807 for LSTM) but OE^k clearly wins with known phonemes (0.850 vs. 0.833 for GRU+A; 0.836 vs. 0.822 for LSTM). The advantage is most pronounced under the GRU+A architecture, where the MoE's known-phoneme condition (1.945) actually underperforms its predicted-phoneme condition (1.948), while the OE shows a clear monotonic improvement from predicted to known phonemes. In terms of efficiency, the OE uses roughly 1/38th the parameters of the MoE (133K vs. ~5.1M for GRU+A) and trains ~8× faster, making it far more suitable for resource-constrained CI deployment.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends the phoneme-based dense MoE architecture for CI dereverberation from Chu et al. [33], replacing 40 separate expert networks with a single expert conditioned on learned per-phoneme affine (scale + shift) feature transformations. The transformation mechanism is conceptually borrowed from conditional batch normalization / FiLM-style conditioning [42], but applied to raw input features rather than internal normalization parameters, and the transformation parameters are precomputed into a lookup table at inference. The approach also contrasts with sparse MoE and expert-merging strategies [34–40], which still require training and storing multiple experts, by eliminating multi-expert inference entirely.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

For resource-constrained edge devices like cochlear implants with strict latency budgets (<10 ms), the OE's ~40× parameter reduction and faster training over a 40-expert MoE while matching or exceeding intelligibility scores (SRMR-CI, STOI) makes phoneme-conditional dereverberation practically deployable. The finding that feature-space partitioning via affine transforms outperforms parameter-space partitioning (OE^k > MoE^k) suggests a broader principle: subtask specialization may be more effectively encoded in the input representation than in separate network weights. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to tasks with ill-defined or continuous subtask labels, whether performance holds under combined noise + reverberation (Appendix A.8 shows degradation), and whether objective metric gains translate to perceptual benefits in clinical CI users.
