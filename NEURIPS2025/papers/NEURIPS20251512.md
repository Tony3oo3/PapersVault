## Problem

Existing spatial transcriptomics research has focused on the forward problem of predicting gene expression from histology images, while the inverse problem—generating realistic histopathology images from transcriptomic profiles—remains largely unexplored. This gap limits the ability to visualize how specific gene expression patterns manifest as cellular morphology, hindering hypothesis generation and minimally invasive disease diagnosis from molecular data alone.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251512/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a gene expression matrix of shape C×G (C cells, G genes) from single-cell or multi-cell spatial transcriptomics patches as input, where C varies per patch and is handled via masking. It produces a 256×256 pixel multi-channel histopathological image (H&E staining plus optional DAPI for nuclear visualization). For cross-dataset generalization, the model operates on 126 shared genes across heterogeneous Xenium panels, and the multi-cell variant aggregates transcriptomic profiles from all cells fully enclosed within a 256×256 sliding window with 100-pixel overlap.

## Method

GeneFlow combines a transformer-based RNA encoder with a conditional UNet guided by rectified flow dynamics. The encoder uses three mechanisms: (1) a Gene Relation Network that learns cell-specific low-rank factorizations (rank K ≪ G) to model gene-gene interactions, (2) global gene attention to weight biologically relevant genes, and (3) multi-head cell attention to aggregate variable numbers of cell embeddings into a single patch-level conditioning vector. The UNet predicts a time-dependent velocity field v_θ(x(t), t, X) that deterministically transports Gaussian noise to the image distribution via a sinusoidal interpolation path with small stochastic perturbation (σ=0.05), solved at inference with a fifth-order Runge-Kutta ODE integrator. Training uses velocity-matching loss plus L1 sparsity regularization on the encoder's first layer, and a spatial graph loss (segmentation-based or gradient-based) is gradually introduced to enforce biologically plausible cell arrangements. This rectified flow formulation creates a continuous bijective mapping between expression and image manifolds, addressing the many-to-one relationship inherent in gene-to-morphology translation.

## Results

On the three Xenium melanoma datasets, GeneFlow (rectified flow) achieves FID scores of 20.73–47.95 for single-cell and multi-cell models, substantially outperforming the diffusion baseline (FID 119.22–213.60) by 3–6×. The best single-cell result is Xenium_C1 with FID 20.73±8.45 and SSIM 0.24±0.04. Domain-specific metrics (Table 5) confirm the advantage: UNI2-h FID drops from 405.98 (diffusion) to 39.27 (rectified flow) for the C1 single-cell model, while nuclear eccentricity similarity rises from 0.880 to 0.964. Cross-dataset generalization (Table 4) shows the model trained on Xenium_C1 tested on Xenium_P1 achieves FID 67.00 (single-cell) despite only 126 shared genes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is a diffusion model sharing the same RNA encoder and UNet architecture but using stochastic denoising dynamics instead of rectified flow. GeneFlow (rectified flow) wins decisively on FID across all six dataset/model configurations, with FID scores 3–6× lower (e.g., 20.73 vs. 171.06 on Xenium_C1 single-cell). On SSIM, the two methods are broadly comparable, with diffusion occasionally edging ahead (e.g., 0.36 vs. 0.35 on Xenium_C2 single-cell), while Feature Distance favors rectified flow in most settings. A simpler conditional UNet trained with MSE and perceptual losses (Table 6) is far worse, achieving FID 525.51 versus 39.27 for rectified flow on the C1 single-cell model, and substantially lower nuclear morphology similarity (circularity 0.659 vs. 0.874, solidity 0.479 vs. 0.867). In a human evaluation by three ACVP-certified pathologists, rectified flow images were preferred over diffusion images in 86% of cases for cell and tissue classification clarity, with median similarity ratings above 6/10.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper addresses the inverse problem of spatial transcriptomics—generating histopathology images from gene expression—whereas the well-studied forward problem (predicting gene expression from histology) has been tackled by methods like HisToGene, THIToGene, BLEEP, and STEM. The generative backbone builds on rectified flow (Liu et al., 2022), adapting its straight-line ODE transport with sinusoidal interpolation paths, stochastic perturbation, and fifth-order Runge-Kutta integration for the gene-expression-to-image setting. The RNA encoder introduces a low-rank factorization for gene-gene relationships and multi-head cell attention for variable-length cell aggregation, departing from prior partial solutions like RNA-GAN (bulk RNA-seq, no spatial structure) and HistoXGAN (requires pre-extracted histological features rather than raw expression).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GeneFlow is the first framework to synthesize realistic single- and multi-cell H&E/DAPI images directly from spatial transcriptomic gene expression, opening a path toward minimally invasive, transcriptome-guided pathology where molecular profiles substitute for tissue biopsy. The gene importance analysis independently recovering EMT and KRAS signaling pathways—without any explicit biological priors—suggests the model captures genuine gene-morphology couplings relevant to melanoma progression. Key limitations include operation on 256×256 tiles (not whole-slide images), dependence on Xenium's true single-cell resolution, and evaluation restricted to melanoma; scaling to broader tissue types, larger gene panels, and hierarchical whole-slide generation remain open challenges.
