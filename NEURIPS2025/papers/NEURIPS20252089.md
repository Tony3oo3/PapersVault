## Problem

Existing RL-based humanoid whole-body control methods can only track smooth, low-speed human motions because (1) human motions extracted from videos often violate the physical constraints of humanoid robots (joint limits, dynamics, kinematics), and (2) there is no tolerance mechanism for difficult-to-track motions during training. This paper tackles both gaps to enable tracking of highly-dynamic behaviors such as kungfu and dancing.

## Input / Output

The pipeline takes monocular videos as raw input, processes them through GVHMR into SMPL parameters (β∈R¹⁰, θ∈R²⁴ˣ³, ψ∈R³), then retargets to the Unitree G1's 23-DoF joint space via differentiable IK. At deployment, the actor consumes a 380-dim observation (5-step history of joint positions, velocities, root angular velocity, projected gravity, and last action, plus a time-phase variable), while the critic receives a 630-dim augmented observation including reference body positions and domain-randomized physical parameters. The output is a 23-dim vector of target joint positions fed to a PD controller that computes motor torques.

## Method

PBHC is a two-stage framework. Stage 1 (motion processing) extracts SMPL motions from video via GVHMR, filters them using a physics-based CoM–CoP stability criterion (projected distance threshold plus maximum instability gap), estimates foot contact masks from ankle displacement, corrects floating artifacts via vertical offset with EMA smoothing, and retargets to G1 through differentiable IK. Stage 2 (adaptive motion tracking) formulates a bi-level optimization where the lower level is standard PPO training and the upper level selects the exponential tracking factor σ; the derived optimum σ* equals the mean optimal tracking error, realized in practice by a closed-loop update σ ← min(σ, x̂) where x̂ is an EMA of instantaneous tracking error, progressively tightening tolerance as the policy improves. The policy is trained with an asymmetric actor-critic (reward vectorization with multi-head critic, privileged information), Reference State Initialization for parallel phase learning, and domain randomization enabling zero-shot sim-to-real transfer to the Unitree G1.

## Results

On the highly-dynamic motion dataset (13 motions across easy/medium/hard difficulty), PBHC achieves a global mean per-body position error (E_g-mpbpe) of 53.25 mm (easy), 126.48 mm (medium), and 290.36 mm (hard), substantially outperforming OmniH2O (233.54/433.64/446.17 mm) and ExBody2 (588.22/619.84/689.68 mm) across all difficulty levels. The oracle variant of PBHC (which ignores partial observability and action smoothness constraints) reaches 45.02/66.85/79.25 mm, approaching MaskedMimic's oracle performance (41.79/150.92/47.74 mm). Real-world deployment on the Unitree G1 robot shows tracking errors closely aligned with simulation (e.g., E_mpbpe: 36.64 mm real vs. 33.18 mm MuJoCo for Tai Chi), confirming zero-shot sim-to-real transfer.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PBHC consistently outperforms both deployable baselines (OmniH2O and ExBody2) across all six tracking metrics and all three difficulty levels, with statistically significant improvements (p<0.05). On the hardest motions, PBHC reduces E_g-mpbpe by ~35% over OmniH2O (290.36 vs. 446.17 mm) and ~58% over ExBody2 (290.36 vs. 689.68 mm), while also achieving the lowest joint position error (1326.60 vs. 1939.50 and 4037.40 ×10⁻³ rad). Against the non-deployable MaskedMimic oracle, PBHC's oracle variant is competitive on easy motions (45.02 vs. 41.79 mm E_g-mpbpe) and clearly superior on medium (66.85 vs. 150.92 mm) and hard (79.25 vs. 47.74 mm) settings, though MaskedMimic retains an edge on the hardest motions. The ablation study (Fig. 7, Table 12) further confirms that the adaptive tracking mechanism outperforms all four fixed-σ configurations (Coarse, Medium, UpperBound, LowerBound) across diverse motions, with the largest gains on complex motions like Charleston Dance where LowerBound degrades to 358.82 mm E_g-mpbpe versus 94.81 mm for the adaptive method.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the humanoid motion imitation line of work (DeepMimic, H2O/OmniH2O, ExBody/ExBody2) by addressing two gaps that prior methods leave open: (1) physically infeasible reference motions that cause RL training to fail, and (2) the lack of a tolerance mechanism for difficult-to-track motions. It builds on DeepMimic's Reference State Initialization and time-phase conditioning, adopts the asymmetric actor-critic architecture from ASAP, and uses reward vectorization from Xie et al. The key departure is formulating the tracking factor selection as a bi-level optimization problem and solving it online via a simple EMA-based feedback loop, rather than relying on fixed, hand-tuned reward tolerances as in OmniH2O or ExBody2.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ability to track highly-dynamic motions (kungfu kicks, 360° spins, dance) with a single RL policy per motion, deployed zero-shot on a real Unitree G1, pushes humanoid control beyond the smooth, low-speed regime that prior methods were limited to. The adaptive tracking factor is a general principle—dynamically tightening reward tolerance as the policy improves—that could transfer to other tracking or imitation tasks. However, the current design trains one policy per motion (13 motions total), raising the open question of how to maintain this level of dynamic performance while enabling a single policy to generalize across a diverse skill repertoire. The lack of environment awareness (terrain, obstacles) also limits deployment to structured settings.
