## Problem

EEG-based ML models are typically trained on short segments extracted from longer recordings, making overall sample size much larger than participant count, yet the hierarchical data structure has been largely ignored in scaling studies. No prior work had systematically disentangled the effects of participant diversity from data volume per participant on cross-participant generalisation, leaving unclear whether collecting more data from fewer subjects can substitute for recruiting more participants.

## Input / Output

Models consume fixed-length multi-channel EEG epochs (2 s for TUAB/CAUEEG, 30 s for PhysioNet, 15 s for LaBraM on PhysioNet) band-pass filtered to 1–50 Hz at 100 Hz (200 Hz for LaBraM), with 19 standard 10-20 channels (TUAB, CAUEEG) or 6 EEG channels (PhysioNet), re-referenced to average reference. Outputs are discrete class labels: normal vs. abnormal (TUAB), normal/MCI/dementia (CAUEEG), or AASM sleep stages (PhysioNet). A key design constraint is that train, validation, and test splits have zero participant overlap, and the experimental grid independently controls both the number of participants (n) and segments per participant (m) to isolate their respective contributions.

## Method

The paper formalises EEG data as a two-level generative process (participants drawn from a population distribution Q, each generating m segments from a participant-specific distribution P_i) and uses statistical learning theory to show that under distribution shift, the generalisation gap scales as Õ(1/√n + 1/√(mn)), making participant count the dominant factor. Empirically, a grid design subsamples training data across combinations of n (25–2000) and m (5–320) on three datasets with >1,000 participants each, training three architecturally distinct models (TCN, mAtt, LaBraM) with early stopping on a fixed validation set. The same grid is repeated with three data augmentations (AmplitudeScaling, FrequencyShift, PhaseRandomisation) and with LaBraM self-supervised pre-training (fine-tuned from weights pretrained on ~500 participants across 16 datasets) to test whether these strategies can compensate for limited participant diversity. Each (n, m) cell is repeated over 25 random subsampling seeds to estimate uncertainty via standard error of the mean.

## Results

Across all three datasets (TUAB, CAUEEG, PhysioNet) and all three model architectures (TCN, mAtt, LaBraM), accuracy improves substantially as the participant count increases from 25 to several hundred, after which gains diminish. On TUAB and CAUEEG, model performance is dominated by participant count rather than data per participant: increasing segments per participant at a fixed participant count yields minimal improvement. On PhysioNet sleep staging, the pattern differs—25 participants with the maximum number of segments per participant matches the performance of 400 participants with only 5 segments. Self-supervised pre-training of LaBraM consistently improves performance across all data regimes (except the most severely limited fine-tuning setting), enabling the larger LaBraM model to match or outperform the smaller TCN and mAtt architectures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three EEG model architectures—TCN [30], mAtt [32], and LaBraM [15]—across three tasks and datasets. TCN and mAtt achieve slightly higher accuracy than LaBraM on TUAB (normality prediction) and CAUEEG (dementia diagnosis), and markedly higher accuracy on PhysioNet (sleep staging), where LaBraM's lower performance is partly attributed to preprocessing differences (15s vs 30s epochs) and its larger parameter count. When LaBraM is pre-trained on ~500 participants from 16 datasets and then fine-tuned, it closes or reverses the gap, matching or exceeding TCN and mAtt performance across all three tasks. Data augmentations (AmplitudeScaling, FrequencyShift, PhaseRandomisation) fail to provide consistent improvements over unaugmented training for any model; PhaseRandomisation is actively detrimental for TCN and mAtt on PhysioNet. The key takeaway is that participant diversity, not model architecture choice or data augmentation, is the primary driver of performance in cross-participant generalisation settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior EEG scaling studies (Banville et al., Kiessner et al.) by formalizing the multi-level data generation process—participants as the unit of distributional diversity, segments as the unit of sample size—and systematically disentangling these two axes. It repurposes the statistical learning theory framework of Gouk et al. on domain generalization to predict that participant count, not total sample size, should govern overfitting under distribution shift, then validates this empirically across three architectures (TCN, mAtt, LaBraM) and three clinical tasks. The augmentation and pre-training evaluations build on Rommel et al., Mohsenvand et al., and the LaBraM foundation model, but reframe them through the participant-diversity lens rather than the more common total-sample-size lens.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that collecting more data per participant cannot compensate for low participant diversity in most EEG tasks provides concrete, actionable guidance for data collection budgets and study design in clinical EEG ML. The consistent failure of standard augmentations to help—despite being widely adopted—challenges a default assumption in the field and motivates the development of augmentations that explicitly simulate inter-participant variation. Open questions include the nature of the distribution shifts (anatomical vs. phenotypic), whether larger pre-training corpora or architecturally stronger invariances can further close the gap, and whether the conclusions extend to participant-specific settings like BCI where the generalization requirement differs.
