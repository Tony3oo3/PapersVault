## Problem

Supervised fine-tuning for tool use struggles to generalize to unfamiliar or complex tool scenarios, and while RL has shown promise for reasoning tasks, reward design for tool use is uniquely challenging: multiple tools with diverse parameters are invoked per step, and coarse-grained signals like answer matching fail to provide the fine-grained feedback needed. This paper presents the first systematic study of reward design for general-purpose tool selection and application under RL.

## Results

On the BFCL V3 benchmark, the proposed GRPO Cold Start approach achieves 46.20% overall accuracy on Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct (vs. 19.41% raw), 52.98% on Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct (vs. 33.04% raw), and 58.38% on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (vs. 41.97% raw), representing roughly 17% absolute gains over base models. On API-Bank, the method reaches 63.15% (1.5B), 67.00% (3B), and 64.66% (7B) overall accuracy. On the Bamboogle QA benchmark, the model achieves 44.0% (1.5B), 60.0% (3B), and 72.0% (7B) accuracy while using a moderate average of 1.19–1.63 tool calls per question, outperforming SFT baselines by approximately 15%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is against internal baselines: raw instruct models, SFT (400 and 4K data), SFT+PPO, SFT+GRPO, and PPO Cold Start. GRPO Cold Start (Ours) consistently achieves the highest BFCL overall accuracy across all model sizes, beating the strongest internal baseline (PPO Cold Start) by 7.88 points on Qwen2.5-3B (52.98% vs. 51.15%) and 11.70 points on Qwen2.5-7B (58.38% vs. 46.68%). On API-Bank, GRPO Cold Start leads all baselines, e.g., 67.00% vs. 65.16% (SFT400+PPO) on Qwen2.5-3B. The only external method compared is Search-R1 [17] on the Bamboogle QA benchmark: the proposed GRPO approach achieves 60.00% (Qwen2.5-3B) and 72.00% (Qwen2.5-7B), substantially outperforming Search-R1's 23.20% and 40.00% respectively under the same GRPO training protocol. Notably, SFT initialization (even with 4K data) underperforms GRPO Cold Start, suggesting that RL from scratch generalizes better than memorizing SFT trajectories.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the RL-for-LLM line of work (GRPO, PPO) from narrow tool domains—search-only QA (Search-R1) and code-only math (TORL)—to general-purpose tool selection and application across diverse tool sets. It departs from the dominant SFT-based paradigm for tool learning (ToolACE, Hammer, AgentTuning) by showing that RL with a carefully designed fine-grained reward (decomposing correctness into tool-name, parameter-name, and parameter-value matching) outperforms SFT by ~15%. The reward formulation builds on the structural+correctness decomposition used in Logic-RL and Search-R1, but introduces a principled multi-component matching scheme and a systematic ablation across reward type, scale, granularity, and temporal dynamics that prior tool-use RL work had not addressed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic reward-design framework for tool-use RL, yielding actionable design principles (fine-grained > coarse rewards, dynamic > static scaling, no length reward, correctness-weighted over format) that practitioners can directly apply when training LLM agents. Key open questions remain: whether the findings scale beyond 7B models, whether the reward framework transfers to preference-based methods (DPO, SimPO) or embodied/multi-modal tool use, and whether the data-efficiency result (4K samples suffice) holds for more diverse or open-ended tool ecosystems. The observation that SFT initialization can hurt GRPO generalization also raises broader questions about the interaction between supervised pre-training and RL fine-tuning for agentic capabilities.
