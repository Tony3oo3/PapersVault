## Problem

Traditional federated learning assumes all clients are fully cooperative and share a single global objective, but many real-world applications (Cournot competition, smart grids, multi-robot control) involve rational players with individual, potentially competing objectives. No existing FL framework can model or solve such non-cooperative multiplayer settings where each client optimizes its own utility while strategically interacting with others, leaving a gap between game-theoretic equilibrium search and communication-efficient distributed learning.

## Input / Output

Each player i receives local data samples ξ^i ~ D_i and the frozen strategies x^{-i} of all other players from the previous synchronization round; the algorithm is configured with step-sizes γ_k and a synchronization interval τ. The output is the joint strategy vector x_{τR} ∈ R^D (the concatenation of all n players' final local models), which should approximate the Nash equilibrium. A notable structural difference from classical FL is that the synchronization vector has dimension D = d_1 + ... + d_n, scaling with the number of players, since each player contributes distinct coordinates to the equilibrium.

## Method

PEARL-SGD (Per-Player Local SGD) is a local-update algorithm where each player independently runs τ stochastic gradient descent steps on its own objective f_i(·; x^{-i}) while holding all other players' strategies fixed, then a central server collects and redistributes the concatenated updated strategies. The approach is inspired by stochastic gradient descent-ascent for minimax games but generalized to n-player games, with convergence analyzed under quasi-strong monotonicity (QSM) and star-cocoercivity (SCO) of the joint gradient operator. In the deterministic setting, PEARL-SGD converges linearly to the exact equilibrium for any τ > 1 with step-size γ = O(1/τ); in the stochastic setting, constant step-sizes yield linear convergence to a neighborhood whose size shrinks as O(1/τ + L_max/ℓ), while decreasing step-sizes give exact sublinear O(1/T) convergence. The key communication-efficiency result shows that in the stochastic regime, choosing τ = O(√(μT/L_max)) reduces the number of communication rounds from T to Θ(√T) without degrading the convergence rate, a benefit absent in the deterministic case due to the player-drift phenomenon where local minimizers depend on other players' strategies.

## Results

PEARL-SGD achieves linear convergence to an equilibrium in the deterministic setting (Theorem 3.3) and linear convergence to a neighborhood of equilibrium in the stochastic setting (Theorem 3.4). In the stochastic regime, with the largest admissible step-size, the convergence neighborhood scales as O(1/τ + L_max/ℓ)·σ²/(ℓμ), meaning that with τ = Ω(√κ) the algorithm reaches approximately √κ times smaller neighborhood within the same number of communication rounds compared to τ=1. Corollary 3.5 shows Õ(1/T) convergence with communication complexity reduced by a factor of τ, yielding Θ(√T) total communications at the optimal τ = O(√(μT/L_max)). Theorem 3.6 establishes exact (non-neighborhood) sublinear convergence with decreasing step-sizes without requiring T to be fixed in advance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark PEARL-SGD against named competing algorithms; instead, the primary comparison is against its own non-local counterpart (τ=1), which corresponds to the standard distributed stochastic gradient descent-ascent (SGDA) method. In the stochastic setting, numerical experiments (Figure 2b) show that PEARL-SGD with larger synchronization intervals τ ∈ {2, 4, 5, 8, 20} achieves strictly smaller relative error ‖x_k − x_⋆‖²/‖x_0 − x_⋆‖² within the same number of communication rounds compared to τ=1, confirming the theoretical communication gain. When τ=1, the step-size constraint and convergence rate of Theorem 3.3 reduce exactly to the GDA analysis under QSM and SCO from Loizou et al. [89], confirming tightness and consistency with existing literature. The concurrent work by Zindari et al. [148] (Decoupled SGD) proposes the identical update rule but requires an additional weakly-coupled game assumption (small κ_c = L_c/μ̄) to show communication acceleration even in the deterministic regime, whereas PEARL-SGD's communication gain is established in the stochastic setting under the standard QSM+SCO assumptions. In the deterministic setting, all values of τ produce indistinguishable performance (Figure 2a) because the step-size must scale as O(1/τ), so the communication benefit is specific to the stochastic regime.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper introduces Multiplayer Federated Learning (MpFL), which repurposes the local-update-then-synchronize paradigm of FedAvg/Local SGD (McMahan et al., 2017) for a fundamentally different objective: finding a Nash equilibrium among rational players rather than minimizing a shared loss. The convergence analysis directly builds on the quasi-strong monotonicity (QSM) and star-cocoercivity (SCO) framework from Loizou et al. (2021) for GDA/SGDA, extending it to the local-update setting. The paper explicitly positions itself as distinct from classical FL (cooperative clients), federated minimax optimization (two-player, each client adjusts both variables), and personalized FL (a special case of MpFL), arguing that none of these prior frameworks can model non-cooperative strategic behavior among clients.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MpFL opens a new research direction at the intersection of game theory and federated learning, with immediate applications to distributed control (robot swarms), electricity markets, and Cournot competition that were previously unaddressed by FL. The key practical insight is that in the stochastic regime, local updates reduce the convergence neighborhood by a factor of O(1/τ + L_max/ℓ), enabling communication savings of Θ(√T) — a result that contrasts with classical FL where local updates typically degrade convergence under heterogeneity. Limitations include the requirement of QSM and SCO (which may not hold for all games), the fact that synchronization vectors scale with total dimension D = Σd_i (making it more suitable for cross-silo than cross-device settings), and the identification of 'player drift' as a distinct and potentially more severe phenomenon than classical client drift. Open questions include whether extragradient or optimistic methods can further improve rates, how to handle asynchronous updates, and whether the framework extends to non-convex or non-smooth games.
