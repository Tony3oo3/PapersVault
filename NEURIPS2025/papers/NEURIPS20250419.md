## Problem

Deep Vision Transformers execute every layer uniformly during fine-tuning, incurring substantial computational overhead, yet existing efficiency methods (token sparsification, early exit, learned gates) either still run all retained layers or introduce auxiliary parameters without a theoretically grounded criterion for which entire layers have converged. This paper asks whether the differential metastability across ViT layers—observable through self-attention's Hopfield energy landscape—can be exploited to freeze or skip layers that no longer need adaptation, without adding any new modules.

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard 224×224 RGB images and outputs class labels, operating within a supervised fine-tuning pipeline on pretrained ViT-S/16, ViT-B/16, ViT-L/16, and DeiT-B backbones. Unusually, during a short warm-up phase the forward pass additionally extracts per-layer attention score distributions to compute the operational mode k̄ (the median minimum number of tokens accumulating 90% of attention mass), which drives the freeze decision. No architectural modification is made to the input pipeline or output head; the only change is which layers receive gradient updates after the one-shot freeze decision.

## Method

ELA-ViT reformulates each self-attention head as a modern Hopfield energy minimisation step and introduces the Layer Instability Index (LII), defined as the Median Absolute Deviation of the operational mode k̄ over a sliding window of mini-batches, to quantify how much a layer's attention basin width varies across inputs. A three-stage training procedure—warm-up (accumulate LII estimates over ~60 steps), one-shot freeze decision (mark layers with LII below a relative threshold as requires_grad=False), and consolidation (fine-tune only the remaining adaptive layers)—requires no additional parameters, gates, or loss terms. Theoretically, the paper proves (Lemma 1) that LII upper-bounds the expected Hopfield energy gap between the metastable attention state and the global minimum, and that low-LII layers are Fisher-flat in the Riemannian metric, justifying that freezing them preserves generalisation. Empirically, the method locks 40–70% of ViT parameters after the first epoch, reducing fine-tuning time by up to 12.2% while maintaining or improving accuracy across CUB-200-2011, CIFAR-10/100, Food-101, Stanford Dogs, NABirds, Beans, and ImageNet-1k.

## Results

On CUB-200-2011, ELA-ViT improves top-1 accuracy by up to +6.9 pp over full fine-tuning (ViT-L: 85.8% vs. 78.9%), with consistent gains of +2.1 to +4.3 pp across ViT-S and ViT-B. On ImageNet-1k (ViT-B/16), the balanced ELA-ViT-50% configuration reduces wall-clock training time by 10.7% and inference latency by 16.7% while maintaining top-1 accuracy within 0.61 pp of the fully fine-tuned baseline (89.10% vs. 89.71%). Across Food-101, CIFAR-100, and Stanford Dogs, ELA-ViT (50% frozen) achieves 90.0%, 84.3%, and 85.9% top-1 accuracy respectively, matching or exceeding full fine-tuning while cutting training time by 9.7–14.2%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary external baseline is ALaST [7], a learned layer-budget method that trains an auxiliary gating network to decide which layers to update. On all three shared datasets (Food-101, CIFAR-100, Stanford Dogs), ELA-ViT decisively outperforms ALaST at 75% freezing: ELA-ViT achieves 90.0% vs. 77.0% on Food-101 (+13.0 pp), 84.3% vs. 76.8% on CIFAR-100 (+7.5 pp), and 85.9% vs. 75.7% on Stanford Dogs (+10.2 pp). ALaST's aggressive budget learning incurs a catastrophic accuracy cost of 6.8–11.6 pp below full fine-tuning, whereas ELA-ViT matches or exceeds the full fine-tuning baseline on every dataset. In terms of speed, ALaST achieves larger time savings (19.5–39.2%) but only after the gating network stabilises in later epochs, while ELA-ViT's one-shot LII-based freezing (≈1% overhead) delivers 9.7–14.2% time reduction from epoch two onward with no auxiliary parameters. The paper also compares against full fine-tuning as an internal baseline, showing that ELA-ViT's parameter-free layer freezing acts as a regulariser that improves generalisation on fine-grained datasets (CUB, Stanford Dogs) while causing minor 1–2 pp drops on near-ceiling small datasets (Beans, CIFAR-10 with ViT-S).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper operationalises the Hopfield-network interpretation of self-attention (Ramsauer et al.) into a practical fine-tuning criterion, bridging a gap between theoretical energy-landscape analyses (Geshkovski et al., Bruno et al.) and applied ViT efficiency. It departs from token-level sparsification (DynamicViT, EViT) by acting on the layer axis, and from learned-gate PEFT methods (ALaST, SimFreeze) by introducing a parameter-free metric (LII) computed directly from existing attention scores. The closest prior work—SmartFRZ and Unified ViT Compression—also observes layer-wise attention stability, but ELA-ViT adds a formal energy-gap bound and a Fisher-information connection that those methods lack.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The parameter-free, one-shot freezing criterion is attractive for practitioners who want efficiency gains without auxiliary modules or co-optimised gates, and the finding that freezing stable layers can *improve* fine-grained accuracy (up to +6.9 pp on CUB with ViT-L) is practically useful. However, the theoretical guarantees rest on a strong exponential-tail assumption on attention scores, and the practical speed-up (10–12%) is modest compared to the 39% time reduction ALaST achieves (at a large accuracy cost). Open questions include whether LII generalises to LLMs and multimodal transformers, whether the one-shot freeze decision is sufficient for long training runs, and whether lighter-weight instability proxies can avoid the multi-step attention tracking overhead.
