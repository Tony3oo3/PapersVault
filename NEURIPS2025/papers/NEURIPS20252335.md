## Problem

Current protein-ligand binding affinity prediction models are trained and benchmarked on oversimplified datasets like DAVIS that either exclude modified kinase proteins or treat them as equivalent to wild-type, creating a significant gap between reported performance and real-world biological scenarios where substitutions, insertions, deletions, and phosphorylation events substantially alter binding. This paper identifies that models overfit to wild-type proteins and fail to generalize to unseen modifications, a critical limitation for precision medicine applications.

## Input / Output

Docking-free models consume protein amino acid sequences (kinase domain only) and ligand SMILES strings, while docking-based models (FDA, Boltz-2) additionally require 3D protein structures (predicted via AlphaFold3 for modified proteins) and 3D ligand poses (generated via DiffDock). The output is a continuous pKd value, though approximately 70% of ground-truth entries are capped at pKd=5 (Kd > 10 μM), making modification-induced affinity changes partially unobservable. The curated DAVIS-complete dataset adds 4,032 modified protein-ligand pairs (56 modified sequences × 72 ligands) to the original 31,824 entries, with train/test splits enforcing constraints such as Tanimoto similarity ≤ 0.5 (Morgan fingerprints) for ligand novelty and ≤ 50% sequence identity for protein novelty.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is the curation of DAVIS-complete and the design of three benchmark settings rather than a new predictive model. DAVIS-complete is built by mapping Entrez Gene Symbols to UniProt IDs, manually curating 56 modified amino acid sequences for 11 kinases (substitutions, indels, phosphorylation, and combinations), and refining domain annotations for multi-domain kinases (JAK, TYK2, RSK family). Three benchmarks are defined: (1) Augmented Dataset Prediction mixes wild-type and modified pairs under seven train-test splits of increasing strictness; (2) Wild-Type to Modification Generalization trains exclusively on wild-type pairs and evaluates zero-shot on modified variants across global, same-ligand/different-modification, and same-modification/different-ligand settings, with informative baselines (WT ground truth and WT model prediction); (3) Few-Shot Modification Generalization fine-tunes on 80% of modified pairs and tests on the remaining 20%. Seven models are evaluated: five docking-free (DeepDTA, AttentionDTA, GraphDTA, DGraphDTA, MGraphDTA) and two docking-based (FDA, Boltz-2), revealing that docking-based models generalize better zero-shot while docking-free models overfit to wild-type but improve notably after fine-tuning on limited modified examples.

## Results

On the Augmented Dataset Prediction benchmark (Table 2), Boltz-2 achieves the best overall performance across most splits, reaching MSE 0.47 / Rp 0.61 (ligand-name) and MSE 0.62 / Rp 0.58 (ligand-structure & protein-seqid, the strictest both-new setting) on the complete test set. Its advantage is most pronounced on the modification subset under the strictest split, where it achieves MSE 0.95 / Rp 0.61 versus DeepDTA's MSE 1.67 / Rp −0.02. In the Wild-Type to Modification Generalization benchmark, docking-free models (e.g., DeepDTA, MGraphDTA) report Rp ≈ 0.80 on WT-uncapped & modification-uncapped pairs, but this is largely an artifact of overfitting to wild-type predictions (Rp(y, ŷ_WT) ≈ 0.79); when the WT value is capped, Boltz-2 outperforms all others (Rp 0.20, C-index 0.58). In the Few-Shot benchmark, docking-free models improve after fine-tuning (e.g., AttentionDTA MSE drops from 0.62 to 0.42 in same-modification, different-ligands), while FDA and Boltz-2 stagnate or deteriorate.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Boltz-2 dominates the zero-shot Augmented Dataset Prediction benchmark, outperforming FDA by ~0.11 MSE and ~0.13 Rp on average, and surpassing all five docking-free models by a wide margin on the strictest splits (e.g., Rp 0.58 vs. 0.12 for DeepDTA on ligand-structure & protein-seqid). FDA is the second-best docking-based method but still trails Boltz-2 consistently; both docking-based models outperform all docking-free baselines in new-ligand and both-new settings. Among docking-free models, DeepDTA and MGraphDTA are the strongest, with MGraphDTA achieving the best Rp (0.34) under ligand-name and DeepDTA the best MSE (0.29) in the protein-modification split where all variants of the same kinase are treated as distinct proteins. In the Wild-Type to Modification Generalization benchmark, the apparent strong Rp (~0.80) of DeepDTA, AttentionDTA, DGraphDTA, and MGraphDTA on WT-uncapped pairs is revealed as WT overfitting (their predictions are nearly identical to their own WT predictions), whereas Boltz-2 (Rp 0.75, lower WT correlation) and FDA (Rp 0.62) capture genuine modification effects. In the Few-Shot benchmark, AttentionDTA achieves the best post-fine-tuning performance in same-modification, different-ligands (MSE 0.42, Rp 0.80, C-index 0.82), while FDA and Boltz-2 paradoxically degrade after fine-tuning, suggesting their pre-trained representations are not easily adapted with limited modified examples.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the standard DAVIS benchmark (Davis et al., 2011) used by docking-free models (DeepDTA, GraphDTA, MGraphDTA, etc.) by explicitly incorporating 4,032 modified kinase-ligand pairs that prior studies either excluded or treated as equivalent to wild-type. It also benchmarks two recent docking-based pipelines—FDA (Wu et al., 2025) and Boltz-2 (Passaro et al., 2025)—that were not evaluated on modification-aware settings. Conceptually, the paper departs from the 'one-size-fits-all' treatment of protein variants by introducing three biologically motivated evaluation protocols (zero-shot WT-to-modification generalization, few-shot adaptation, and augmented mixed prediction) that go beyond the standard new-drug/new-protein splits used in prior affinity prediction literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that docking-free models so severely overfit to wild-type proteins that a trivial baseline (copying the WT ground-truth affinity) matches or exceeds their performance on modification-specific tasks is a sobering result for the field, suggesting that current sequence-based architectures may not be capturing modification-induced structural effects at all. The observation that AlphaFold3 predicts the same phosphorylated conformation for both phosphorylated and non-phosphorylated ABL1 further underscores that upstream structure prediction remains a bottleneck. Key limitations include the kinase-only scope, the 70% capping at 10 μM that obscures true affinity changes, and potential data leakage for docking-based models, all of which constrain generalizability and call for larger, more diverse modification-aware datasets.
