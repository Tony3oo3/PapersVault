## Problem

Neural activity in the visual cortex varies not only across stimuli but also across repeated presentations of the same stimulus due to unobserved internal brain states. No existing dynamic encoding model simultaneously captures this stimulus-independent variability through an explicit latent state and models the full neuronal response distribution, while operating on video-based stimuli. Prior latent-state models either ignore sensory input, assume linear-Gaussian relationships, or are limited to static images with quadratic scaling in neuron count.

## Input / Output

The model takes naturalistic grayscale video clips (64×36×80 frames at 30 Hz during training, full-length during evaluation) and, in the conditioned mode, a subset of deconvolved calcium responses from ~40,000 excitatory neurons in mouse V1 layers 2–5 (recorded at 8 Hz, upsampled to 30 Hz). It outputs, for every neuron and time point, the full parameters of a Zero-Inflated Gamma distribution (nonzero probability q, scale θ, and per-neuron shape κ), from which the predicted response is the distribution mean. In the non-conditioned mode the latent is marginalized out via Monte-Carlo sampling from a Gaussian prior, yielding a video-only predictive distribution; in the conditioned mode the latent is inferred from the held-out neuron subset via an approximate posterior.

## Method

The architecture extends a factorized 3D-CNN core with a Gaussian readout to predict ZIG distribution parameters (q, θ) rather than point estimates, and adds a variational latent variable z ∈ R^{k×T} (k ≪ N) with an isotropic Gaussian prior. A GRU-based encoder takes a dropout-masked subset of neuronal responses, reduces them via a shared linear layer, and produces the approximate posterior q(z|y) = N(μ(y), σ²I); the latent is then combined with video features through per-neuron affine projections (w_i^(q)·z_t, w_i^(θ)·z_t) that modulate the ZIG parameters. Training maximizes the ZIG log-likelihood via the ELBO with 150 Monte-Carlo samples for the likelihood term and an analytic KL divergence, initialized from a pretrained video-only ZIG model. The ZIG likelihood is chosen over Poisson because calcium traces exhibit a point mass at zero plus positively skewed activity, and the per-neuron shape parameter κ is fixed by moment matching to avoid identifiability issues. The model is evaluated in two regimes—non-conditioned (latents sampled from the prior) and conditioned (latents inferred from a neuron subset)—and the learned latents are validated against behavioral variables and cortical coordinates that were never provided during training.

## Results

On the SENSORIUM dataset (mouse V1, ~40,000 neurons, naturalistic video), the proposed Latent ZIG model achieves a log-likelihood of −0.30 ± 0.05 bits per neuron and time, substantially outperforming the video-only ZIG baseline (−0.98 ± 0.04) while maintaining comparable Pearson correlation (0.182 ± 0.004 vs. 0.183 ± 0.003). The Poisson baseline achieves the highest correlation (0.195 ± 0.003) but cannot report a meaningful log-likelihood for continuous calcium data. When conditioned on half the neuron population, the latent model boosts correlation to 0.27, and on out-of-distribution stimuli (pink noise, gratings) the conditioned model reaches 0.17 average correlation versus 0.08 for the Poisson baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the SENSORIUM Poisson baseline [43], the Latent ZIG model trades a small correlation drop (0.182 vs. 0.195) for a large log-likelihood gain (−0.30 vs. undefined for Poisson), reflecting the trade-off between modeling full response distributions and conditional means inherent to non-exponential-family losses. Against the video-only ZIG baseline (same architecture, no latent), the latent model improves log-likelihood by ~0.68 bits per neuron and time while keeping correlation essentially identical, confirming that the latent state captures shared variability not explained by the video alone. Against CEBRA [36] on behavioral correlation (canonical correlation analysis), the latent model achieves 0.60 for pupil dilation (vs. CEBRA's 0.66) but substantially outperforms CEBRA on treadmill speed (0.42 vs. 0.27), yielding overall comparable performance despite CEBRA being explicitly trained with behavioral supervision while the latent model sees no behavioral data during training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the deterministic core-readout framework (factorized 3D CNN core from Vystrčilova et al. [46] with a Gaussian readout from Lurz et al. [29]) into a probabilistic latent-variable model by adding a GRU-based encoder that infers a stimulus-independent latent state from a subset of neuronal responses. It draws on Bashiri et al. [5] for the concept of a stimulus-conditioned latent state and Zhu et al. [57] for the Zero-Inflated Gamma likelihood, but departs from both by operating on dynamic video stimuli and predicting per-neuron marginal distributions rather than a full covariance matrix. The key positioning is that prior video encoding models (Sinz et al., Wang et al.) are deterministic point estimators, while prior latent-state models (GPFA, LFADS, CEBRA) ignore sensory input; this work is the first to unify dynamic video encoding with a stimulus-independent latent state in a single probabilistic framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that unsupervised latent factors learned purely from population responses exhibit topographic organization on the cortical surface and correlate strongly with behavior (treadmill speed, pupil dilation) — despite no behavioral or positional data during training — provides a principled way to bridge sensory encoding and internal brain state without expensive behavioral annotations. The ability to extrapolate latent-to-neuron mappings to unseen neurons using only cortical coordinates suggests a path toward reducing the number of simultaneously recorded neurons needed for accurate prediction. Open questions include whether the topographic structure reflects true cortical organization or residual retinotopy, whether the approach scales to state-of-the-art transformer cores, and whether non-Gaussian or non-isotropic latent priors would capture richer brain-state dynamics.
