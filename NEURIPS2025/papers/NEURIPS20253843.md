## Problem

When ML models guide resource allocation, affected agents may strategically alter their features, but prior work cannot distinguish genuine adaptation (real behavioral change) from misreporting (falsely reported values). Existing strategic classification methods either assume known cost functions, focus on model robustness rather than quantifying misreporting, or conflate the two phenomena. This paper tackles the fundamental challenge of identifying and quantifying the misreporting rate without access to true feature values.

## Input / Output

The method requires two tabular datasets: a manipulated dataset D containing reported binary features X, a downstream outcome Y, observed confounders C, and agent identifiers A, plus an unmanipulated dataset D* containing true features X*, the same outcome Y, and confounders C (e.g., pre-deployment data or Traditional Medicare claims where no misreporting incentive exists). The output is a per-agent misreporting rate P_a(X*=0|X=1) for each binary feature, along with intermediate CATE estimates. The unmanipulated dataset need not share agent identities with the manipulated one, and the method assumes X=1 is the direction of higher payout so agents only misreport X*=0 to X=1.

## Method

The core insight is an asymmetry: genuine adaptation causally changes downstream variables Y, while misreporting only changes the reported feature X without affecting Y. The paper defines three estimands—TAFR (true causal effect of X* on Y for the reported group), NAFR (nominal causal effect of X on Y for the reported group), and TAFM (true causal effect of X* on Y for the misreported group)—and proves the misreporting rate equals (TAFR − NAFR)/TAFM (Lemma 1). Since X* is unobserved in D, they replace TAFR and TAFM with counterparts estimated from D* under an invariance assumption on conditional causal effects (Assumption 4), yielding the identifiable estimator (τ′_a − τ_a)/δ′_a (Theorem 1). In practice, CATEs are estimated via S-learners with XGBoost on both datasets, and the final MR is computed as a ratio of averaged CATE predictions. The paper further characterizes the asymptotic variance via the delta method (Theorem 2), showing that selecting the causal descendant with the largest δ′_a minimizes estimator variance.

## Results

On the real Medicare dataset, CMRE estimates misreporting rates (MR) of 0.015 (95% CI: −0.138, 0.091) and 0.019 (−0.103, 0.120) for two nonpayment HCCs (HCC50, HCC117), both statistically indistinguishable from zero as expected. For payment HCCs, CMRE yields significantly positive MR estimates: 0.130 (0.037, 0.238) for HCC8 (Metastatic Cancer), 0.217 (0.109, 0.285) for HCC21 (Protein-Calorie Malnutrition), and 0.241 (0.066, 0.439) for HCC103 (Hemiplegia). On the semi-synthetic loan fraud dataset, CMRE produces unbiased MR estimates across all levels of genuine adaptation (β_A from 0 to 0.3), causal effect of X* on Y (β_X* from 0.1 to 0.5), and true MR (0 to 0.2), while all baselines exhibit systematic bias.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CMRE is the only method that passes the critical sanity check on Medicare data: it yields MR estimates statistically indistinguishable from zero for nonpayment HCCs (where no misreporting incentive exists) while producing significantly positive estimates for payment HCCs. NDEE (Natural Direct Effect Estimator) systematically underestimates MR, producing negative estimates for all HCCs in the Medicare setting and biased estimates whenever genuine adaptation is non-zero in the semi-synthetic experiments, because it cannot separate the direct causal effect of the agent on X (misreporting) from the effect mediated through X* (genuine adaptation). NMRE (Naive Misreporting Estimator) overestimates MR for all HCCs, including nonpayment ones, because it uses a difference-in-means estimator that fails to control for confounding between X* and Y. OC-SVM (One-Class SVM, [Manevitz & Yousef, JMLR 2001]) produces nearly identical MR estimates across all HCC codes and agents, indicating it is completely unable to distinguish misreported data points from legitimate ones, consistent with the fact that misreported values remain plausible under the unmanipulated distribution.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of strategic classification and causal inference. Prior work on strategic classification (Hardt et al., Dong et al.) focuses on building models robust to gaming, while causal strategic classification (Miller et al., Shavit et al., Horowitz & Rosenfeld) handles only genuine adaptation without addressing misreporting. The closest prior work, Chang et al. [6], can only partially identify misreporting propensity and does not distinguish it from genuine adaptation. This paper departs from all of these by recasting misreporting estimation as a causal identifiability problem, exploiting the key asymmetry that misreported features do not causally affect their descendants, and leveraging an unmanipulated reference dataset to recover the true causal effects needed for full identification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical stakes are substantial: Medicare upcoding has led to an estimated $50 billion in overpayments, and insurers routinely claim diagnosis-rate differences reflect improved care (genuine adaptation) rather than misreporting. This paper provides the first framework that can fully identify the misreporting rate while disentangling it from genuine adaptation, offering a principled tool for oversight bodies to prioritize auditing. Key limitations include the assumption of no unobserved confounding, restriction to binary features, and the need for access to an unmanipulated dataset. Open questions include extending to continuous/categorical features, handling delayed or unobserved downstream outcomes via surrogate indices, and incorporating sensitivity analysis for untestable causal assumptions.
