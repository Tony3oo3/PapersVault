## Problem

The brain dynamically organizes heterogeneous regions into task-specific processing pathways, but the mechanisms driving this organization remain unclear. In machine learning, Mixture-of-Experts architectures are theorized to form specialized pathways based on input complexity, yet in practice experts do not reliably specialize into distinct, task-driven pathways. This paper asks whether heterogeneous experts automatically form such pathways or whether specific inductive biases are required, and whether the resulting pathways can replicate known brain dynamics.

## Input / Output

At each 100 ms timestep the model receives a 115-dimensional vector comprising a 1-D fixation signal, two 16-D stimulus channels, and an 82-D one-hot task identity passed through a learned 16-D embedding. Tasks last 0.8–3 s (~20 timesteps on average); during the fixation phase the model must output zero, and during the response phase it must produce 16-D response logits matching the task-specific target. Training batches are 128×350×115 tensors containing ~27 trials of each of the 82 Mod-Cog tasks per step.

## Method

The architecture extends Heterogeneous Mixture-of-Experts by replacing feed-forward experts with recurrent GRUs (16-neuron, 32-neuron, and a skip connection per layer) routed by 64-neuron GRU routers across three layers. Three inductive biases are introduced to drive pathway formation: (i) a Learned Pathway Complexity (LPC) routing loss that penalizes activation of larger experts proportional to their squared hidden dimension, (ii) normalization of this penalty by the task-specific response loss so the cost is relaxed when performance is poor, and (iii) stochastic expert dropout that deactivates low-weighted experts during training to enforce self-sufficiency. Trained with Schedule-Free AdamW on 82 Mod-Cog tasks, the resulting Mixture-of-Pathways model produces consistent, task-difficulty-matched pathways that mirror cortical–subcortical learning dynamics observed in the brain, including the transient recruitment of complex pathways for difficult tasks followed by transfer to simpler ones.

## Results

The baseline HMoE model shows no consistent pathway formation across training runs (mean pairwise LPC correlation of 0.0324), while the proposed Mixture-of-Pathways model achieves a correlation of 0.71 with routing cost and task-performance scaling alone, and 0.51 with all three inductive biases including expert dropout. In terms of self-sufficiency, removing low-weighted experts drops baseline accuracy from 98.2% to 16.4%, whereas the MoP model (β=0.8) only drops from 86.5% to 77.7%. The MoP model also exhibits brain-like difficulty scaling: a significant positive correlation between task difficulty and pathway complexity (r=0.54, p<0.001) and a positive correlation between task difficulty and initial pathway complexity increase during learning (r=0.31, p=0.004), both of which are absent or reversed in the baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between the proposed Mixture-of-Pathways (MoP) model and a baseline Heterogeneous Mixture-of-Experts (HMoE) model with identical architecture but no inductive biases. The baseline achieves higher raw task accuracy (91.1% vs. 83.0%) but fails all three pathway criteria: it shows no consistent routing across runs (correlation 0.0324 vs. 0.51), is not self-sufficient (accuracy collapses from 98.2% to 16.4% when low-weighted experts are removed, vs. 86.5%→77.7% for MoP), and exhibits no brain-like difficulty scaling (Fig. 5 correlation of −0.01 vs. 0.54 for MoP). Critically, the baseline shows the *opposite* of the expected learning dynamic: difficult tasks receive *less* pathway complexity at the start of training (r=−0.49) rather than more (r=0.31 for MoP), because the baseline prematurely routes unsolved tasks to simpler experts to minimize routing loss. Ablations confirm that all three inductive biases are necessary: removing dropout eliminates the brain-like learning dynamic (Fig. 6b correlation drops to 0.03), while removing task-performance scaling reduces pathway consistency from 0.71 to 0.15.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Heterogeneous Mixture-of-Experts (HMoE) architecture [22, 23] by replacing feed-forward MLP experts with recurrent GRU networks and adding a skip-connection expert inspired by Mixture-of-Depths [17], creating a model where each expert is a standalone recurrent unit analogous to a brain region. It departs from prior multi-region brain models [35–40] that predefine fixed circuit topologies by allowing pathways to emerge dynamically through learned routing, and from brain-inspired modularity work [13, 14, 26–30] that typically starts from fully-connected networks. The key conceptual departure is the demonstration that heterogeneity alone is insufficient for pathway formation, and that three specific inductive biases (routing-cost regularization, task-performance scaling, and expert dropout) are jointly necessary to produce stable, self-sufficient, and task-differentiated pathways.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a mechanistic account of how heterogeneous brain regions might organize into task-specific processing pathways, linking the Mixture-of-Pathways architecture to established neuroscience phenomena including the multiple-demand system's role in difficult tasks and the cortical-to-subcortical transfer of learned skills. The ablation showing that the brain-like learning dynamics (complex tasks initially routed to complex pathways, then transferred to simpler ones) require the interaction of all three biases—particularly dropout combined with LPC scaling—highlights that no single inductive bias suffices. Key limitations include the small model scale (3 layers × 3 experts), the absence of recurrent connectivity loops present in the primate brain, the lack of an explicit mapping from the router to a specific brain structure, and the use of relatively simple cognitive tasks, leaving open whether these findings generalize to larger, more complex architectures and tasks.
