## Problem

Existing einsum implementations commit to a static tensor format (dense multidimensional arrays or sparse coordinate lists) before evaluation begins, yet sparsity in intermediate tensors evolves dynamically during contraction. The paper shows that coordinate lists can be exponentially more efficient than arrays for certain expressions, but can also be significantly slower for dense ones, and it is generally intractable to predict the optimal format from the input tensors alone.

## Input / Output

The method takes an einsum expression specifying index strings for input tensors and an output tensor, along with the input tensors themselves (which may be dense or sparse). During evaluation, the system tracks the average density of all not-yet-contracted tensors and uses this running statistic to decide when to switch representation. The output is the fully contracted output tensor; the key operational detail is that the tensor format of intermediates changes mid-evaluation, with the switch triggered when the average density of remaining tensors drops below a configurable threshold (5% in experiments).

## Method

The paper introduces a hybrid einsum evaluation algorithm that begins in dense (multidimensional array) format and switches to sparse (coordinate list) format on the fly once the average density of the remaining uncontracted tensors falls below a threshold. The density is computed incrementally—only the newly produced intermediate tensor's density is calculated and folded into running totals after each contraction—and the check is skipped for cheap contractions or once density exceeds 95% to minimize overhead. The paper also provides a theoretical foundation by proving an exponential flop separation between the two formats on a family of grid model-counting formulas (GRID_n), where dense evaluation requires at least 2^n flops while coordinate lists need only O(n^2). Empirically, on a 158-instance benchmark, the hybrid approach matches or beats the better of the two static backends with a median overhead of only ~2% relative to the virtual best, while achieving up to 1000× speedup on hyper-sparse instances.

## Results

Theoretically, contracting the GRID_n tensor hypernetwork requires at least 2^n flops with multidimensional arrays but only 4n²−4n−1 flops with coordinate lists, confirming an exponential separation (Proposition 1). Empirically on the 158-instance Einsum Benchmark, the hybrid algorithm achieves a median slowdown of only ~1.02× relative to the virtual best (min of dense and sparse), with >2× slowdown on just 8 of 158 instances. On the 28 sparse instances, the hybrid reaches a median speedup of ~6.2× over the dense (PyTorch) baseline and up to ~1000× on the most hyper-sparse case. On dense instances, the hybrid incurs negligible overhead (median ~1.01× vs. dense) while being ~22× faster than the sparse baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the hybrid algorithm against three references: the dense PyTorch baseline, their own sparse (coordinate-list) implementation, and the virtual best (the faster of the two static choices). Against PyTorch (dense), the hybrid is essentially matched on dense instances (median ~1.01× overhead) but achieves a median 6.2× speedup on sparse instances, reaching up to ~1000× on the most hyper-sparse case. Against the sparse baseline, the hybrid is a median ~22× faster on dense instances (where sparse is ~17× slower than dense overall) while incurring only a ~1.08× median slowdown on sparse instances. Against the virtual best, the hybrid is a median ~1.02× slower overall, with more than 2× slowdown on only 8 of 158 instances, and it actually beats both static choices on 3 of the 28 sparse instances. The key takeaway is that the hybrid consistently matches or exceeds the better static choice at negligible cost, whereas either static choice alone can be up to 30× suboptimal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends static sparse einsum implementations (PyData's `sparse` package and SQL-based einsum) by introducing on-the-fly format switching between dense multidimensional arrays and coordinate lists during tensor contraction. The theoretical exponential separation result builds on tensor network contraction complexity analyses from quantum computing (Markov & Shi) and model counting via tensor hypernetworks, while the practical motivation aligns with the broader ML community's shift toward dynamic, input-dependent sparsity. The key conceptual departure is treating tensor format as a runtime decision rather than a static one, motivated by the observation that sparsity in intermediate tensors is unpredictable from input tensors alone.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Einsum is a core primitive in TensorFlow, PyTorch, and cuQuantum, yet all currently support only dense formats; this work provides both a theoretical justification (exponential separation) and a practical hybrid algorithm for exploiting sparsity that arises dynamically during evaluation. The finding that 104 of 158 real-world benchmark instances exhibit significant density evolution suggests dynamic sparsity is common, not pathological, in expressions with many input tensors relevant to probabilistic circuits, quantum ML, and neuro-symbolic models. Key open questions include bidirectional format switching (sparse→dense), sparsity-aware contraction order optimization, and GPU/accelerator support, all of which the authors identify as necessary for full practical adoption.
