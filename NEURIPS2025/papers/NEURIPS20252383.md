## Problem

Existing concept erasure methods for text-to-image diffusion models either degrade overall image quality, rely on brittle adversarial losses vulnerable to reward hacking, or require hours of compute per concept. The paper traces these failures to a myopic treatment of denoising trajectories that ignores the evolving conditional marginal distributions across timesteps, and to the dependence on hand-crafted reward models that are themselves adversarially exploitable.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252383/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

EraseFlow takes a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model (SD v1.4 or Flux), a target prompt to erase (e.g., 'nudity'), and a semantically safe anchor prompt (e.g., 'fully dressed person'). It produces a LoRA-fine-tuned diffusion model whose generation under the target prompt is distributionally aligned with the anchor prompt's output, effectively transplanting the safe distribution onto the unsafe prompt. Training requires only 20 iterations on a single A100 GPU (~3 minutes), sampling anchor trajectories from the safe prompt and applying the loss across a subset of timesteps (10 from the first 40 steps plus 10 from the last 10).

## Method

EraseFlow casts concept erasure as a GFlowNet trajectory-balance (TB) problem over the full denoising path, where the forward policy is the diffusion reverse step and the backward policy is the noising kernel. The key innovation is a reward-free alignment strategy: a constant reward β is assigned to all anchor-prompt trajectories and zero otherwise, eliminating any external classifier or reward model. The resulting TB loss (Eq. 8) forces the log-probability of the target-prompt denoising trajectory to match that of the anchor trajectory, redistributing probability mass from target regions to anchor regions. Proposition 4.1 proves that zero constant-reward TB loss guarantees exact per-timestep and marginal distributional equality between the erased model under the target prompt and the original model under the anchor prompt. Empirically, TB outperforms the detailed-balance (DB) objective for erasure because it propagates credit to early denoising states more effectively, avoiding the noisy intermediate reward estimates that destabilize DB-based training.

## Results

On the UDAtk nudity benchmark (lower ASR is better), EraseFlow achieves 33.89% attack success rate, outperforming all non-adversarial baselines (ESD 78.81%, DUO 64.40%, MACE 72.81%) and even the adversarial method R.A.C.E (50.84%), while requiring only 2.8 minutes of training versus 225 min for R.A.C.E and 1440 min for AdvUnlearn. Combined with AdvUnlearn, EraseFlow reduces the UDAtk ASR to 1.42% (vs. 16.94% for AdvUnlearn alone). On image quality, EraseFlow achieves a FID of 17.93 on MSCOCO, competitive with the best baselines (MACE 17.11, UCE 18.42) while maintaining a CLIP score of 25.67. On fine-grained erasure, EraseFlow attains the best Total Score (76.01) among all methods, balancing concept removal with preservation of unrelated content.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against non-adversarial fine-tuning baselines, EraseFlow dominates on robustness: it reduces UDAtk nudity ASR from 78.81% (ESD), 87.28% (UCE), 72.81% (MACE), and 64.40% (DUO) to 33.89%, and achieves near-zero ASR on Ring-a-Bell (0.00%) and MMA-Diffusion (0.60%) where ESD scores 13.92% and 11.00% respectively. On fine-grained erasure, ESD achieves a higher Concept Score (93.97 vs. 83.24) but at the cost of excessive erasure, reflected in its much lower Total Score (59.40 vs. EraseFlow's 76.01), indicating EraseFlow better preserves unrelated content. Against adversarial baselines, EraseFlow outperforms R.A.C.E on UDAtk (33.89% vs. 50.84%) and MMA-Diffusion (0.60% vs. 2.80%) with 80× less training time, while AdvUnlearn achieves lower standalone ASR (16.94%) but requires 514× more compute; combining EraseFlow with AdvUnlearn yields the best overall result (1.42% UDAtk ASR). Against the inference-time method SAFREE, EraseFlow substantially outperforms on all robustness metrics (e.g., 33.89% vs. 85.59% UDAtk) while also improving FID (17.93 vs. 20.62). On the Flux architecture (Table 7), EraseFlow improves I2P by 31.3% over EraseAnything (16.90 vs. 24.60) and reduces Ring-a-Bell by 16.0% compared to UCE (53.16 vs. 63.29).

## Relation to Prior Work

EraseFlow builds on the GFlowNet framework (Bengio et al., 2023) and its trajectory-balance objective (Malkin et al., 2023), adapting both to the diffusion denoising process following the GFlowNet–diffusion connection established by Zhang et al. (2024, DAG-KL). It departs from prior concept-erasure methods (ESD, UCE, MACE, DUO) that fine-tune denoising predictions at individual timesteps with uniform weighting, and from RL-based alignment approaches (Diffusion-DPO, RAFT) that require learned reward models. The key conceptual departure is showing that a constant reward combined with the TB objective is provably sufficient for exact distributional erasure, eliminating the need for any external classifier or adversarial signal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By removing the dependency on hand-crafted reward models, EraseFlow addresses a practical bottleneck in concept erasure: designing reliable, non-hackable rewards for arbitrary visual concepts (e.g., a specific brand logo) is difficult and often infeasible. The 3-minute training cost versus hours for adversarial baselines makes iterative or multi-concept erasure practically viable, and the plug-and-play composition with SAFREE or AdvUnlearn offers a path to near-perfect erasure (1.42% ASR) without full adversarial retraining. Open questions include whether the constant-reward guarantee holds under finite training (the proof assumes zero TB loss), how the method scales to video diffusion or multi-concept simultaneous erasure, and whether the SD v1.4 results transfer robustly to larger architectures beyond the preliminary Flux experiments.
