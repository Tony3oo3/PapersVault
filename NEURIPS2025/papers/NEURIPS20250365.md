## Problem

Large Reasoning Models produce excessively verbose chain-of-thought traces, incurring high inference costs, yet it remains unclear how far current fine-tuning methods are from the optimal length-accuracy trade-off. Existing efficiency-focused methods are evaluated under inconsistent setups, making it impossible to determine whether they approach or fall short of the best achievable reasoning efficiency at each token budget.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250365/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a mathematical reasoning question and generates an autoregressive chain-of-thought trace followed by a final answer. Evaluation is conducted under a fixed token budget L: if the trace exceeds L tokens, a fallback prompt instructs the model to produce a final answer directly from the truncated prefix y_{:L}. Accuracy is reported as the average over 32 sampled responses (temperature 0.6, top_p 0.95) on AMC23, AIME24, and AIME25, with REG computed over budgets up to 16K tokens to emphasize the low-budget regime.

## Method

REO-RL approximates the full efficiency objective (sum of length-constrained rewards over all token budgets) via the trapezoidal rule applied to only N=5 strategically selected token budgets, achieving <1% approximation error. At each selected budget L_i, the model is forced to generate a final answer from the truncated trace, yielding a dense reward signal; the policy gradient (REINFORCE) assigns each token segment between consecutive budgets a return equal to the weighted sum of rewards from that budget onward, with weights c_i = (L_{i+1} − L_{i−1})/2. Token budgets are selected either by an oracle greedy procedure that minimizes trapezoidal approximation error against the estimated efficiency frontier, or by a simpler exponentially spaced schedule L_i = L_min·(L_max/L_min)^{(i−1)/N}. This avoids the infeasible cost of evaluating rewards at every budget while preserving the optimization direction of the full efficiency objective.

## Results

On the 1.5B model, REO-RL(Exp)-Coef=1 achieves 54.3% average accuracy with 9,043 tokens length and a REG of 320.8, reducing the efficiency gap by 74.5% compared to Vanilla RL (REG 1,257.0). On the 7B model, the same variant achieves 67.6% accuracy, 6,160 tokens, and REG 605.9 (↓64.2% vs. Vanilla RL's 1,691.6). In a controlled comparison on 71 questions where all models exceed 50% accuracy, the 7B REO-RL model produces correct responses averaging 4,524 tokens—shorter than DeepSeek R1 (5,156), Qwen3-4B (8,632), Qwen3-8B (8,899), Qwen3-32B (7,756), and Claude Sonnet 3.7 Thinking (17,479)—while matching Vanilla RL-7B at 93.5% accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

REO-RL(Exp)-Coef=1 dominates all baselines on REG: it reduces the gap by 74.5% (1.5B) and 64.2% (7B) over Vanilla RL, and by 48.5% and 37.5% over the strongest RL baselines (Len-Harmonizing and Len Group Norm. Rew., respectively). Hard token-budget RL (1K/2K/4K) drastically cuts length but collapses accuracy (e.g., 32.2% vs. 51.6% on 1.5B at 1K budget) and actually *increases* REG by 124.6% and 114.4% on 1.5B and 7B, confirming that aggressive shortening is counterproductive. Among SFT and preference-learning baselines, SFT Shortest is the closest competitor (REG 770.2 / 976.8) but still trails REO-RL(Exp)-Coef=1 by 58.5% and 38.1% on REG. SimPO variants are inconsistent: SimPO Shortest *worsens* REG on 1.5B (2,318.3 vs. 1,257.0 for Vanilla RL) due to large accuracy drops, while SimPO TOPS and SimPO DAST remain well above REO-RL. In the frontier LRM comparison (Table 2), REO-RL(Exp)-7B matches Vanilla RL-7B at 93.5% accuracy but uses 41.5% fewer tokens (4,524 vs. 7,732), and is more concise than DeepSeek R1, all Qwen3 variants, and Claude Sonnet 3.7 Thinking at comparable or higher accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes two active research threads—efficient reasoning (reducing overthinking in LRMs via length rewards, SFT on short traces, preference learning) and RL-based LRM training—into a unified framework. Rather than proposing yet another length-penalizing reward, it introduces reasoning efficiency frontiers (empirical upper bounds from 180+ fine-tuned models) and the Reasoning Efficiency Gap (REG) metric to quantify how far any method is from the optimal length-accuracy trade-off. The REO-RL algorithm then departs from prior single-budget or single-reward approaches by using the trapezoidal rule from numerical integration to approximate the full efficiency objective with only 5 token budgets, a conceptual repurposing of a classical numerical method for RL reward design.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The REG metric addresses a real gap in the field: prior efficient-reasoning methods were evaluated under inconsistent protocols, making cross-method comparison nearly impossible. The finding that base LRMs waste 3.4× (1.5B) and 1.3× (7B) tokens relative to the empirical frontier quantifies the overthinking problem in a way that motivates further work. However, the study is limited to 1.5B/7B models and mathematical reasoning, and the frontier is only a lower bound (not the true optimum), so the open question of whether any method can fully match the frontier remains. The framework is broadly applicable and could serve as a standard evaluation tool for future efficient-reasoning research.
