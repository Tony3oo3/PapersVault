## Problem

Widely used post-hoc XAI methods (LIME, KernelSHAP, gradients) assign non-zero importance to suppressor variables—features that influence model predictions without any direct statistical dependence on the target—producing false-positive attributions that undermine explanation reliability. While the Pattern method corrects this for linear models by converting backward weights to forward (generative) patterns, no principled extension existed for non-linear models or instance-level explanations, leaving a gap in explainability for the deep networks that dominate practice.

## Input / Output

PatternLocal consumes a trained classifier f, the specific instance x⋆ to explain, a pool of representative training samples, and a user-chosen input-simplification map h_{x⋆} (identity, superpixels, or low-rank projection) that reduces dimensionality from D to D′≪D. It outputs a single feature-importance vector a in the simplified space, which is then mapped back to the original pixel or feature grid as a normalized attribution map in [−1, 1]. Unlike pure gradient or Shapley-based methods, PatternLocal requires access to the training distribution in the local neighborhood of x⋆ and a meaningful spatial or feature alignment shared across samples, making it most natural for structured domains like medical imaging or EEG where channels and spatial locations are fixed.

## Method

PatternLocal operates in two stages. First, it fits a standard local linear surrogate (LIME, KernelSHAP, or gradient) around x⋆ to obtain discriminative weights w, exactly as in Eq. (5). Second, it converts these weights into a generative pattern by solving a kernel-weighted regression: it regresses the simplified input h_{x⋆}(x) onto the surrogate response ỹ = wᵀh_{x⋆}(x) over the local neighborhood, weighted by the same proximity kernel Π used in the surrogate fit. For ℓ₂ regularization this yields the closed-form a = Cov_Π[h(x), ỹ] / (Var_Π[ỹ] + λ), i.e., the kernel-weighted cross-covariance between features and the surrogate output scaled by its regularized variance. The key insight is that this forward-model regression naturally cancels suppressor contributions: in the XOR toy example, the covariance of the suppressor x₃ with ỹ vanishes identically because the weight identity w₁ − w₂ + w₃ = 0 makes the correlated-noise terms sum to zero, driving a₃ to exactly zero while preserving the true signal components. The method is model-agnostic (works with any classifier), requires no retraining, and adds only a single weighted regression per explanation on top of the surrogate-fitting cost.

## Results

On the XAI-TRIS benchmark (64×64 images, MLP model, identity mapping, ℓ₂ regularization), PatternLocal substantially outperforms all other XAI methods in the XOR and RIGID scenarios across both EMD and IME metrics, with the advantage growing as correlated noise (suppressor variables) increases. On the Artificial Lesion MRI dataset (128×128, CNN, superpixels), restricting to high-confidence superpixels (importance > 0.9), PatternLocal achieves the lowest EMD (0.102 ± 0.065) and IME (0.827 ± 0.057), compared to LIME (EMD 0.196, IME 0.976) and gradient-based methods (EMD 0.158–0.171, IME 0.538–0.804 with high variance). On the EEG motor imagery dataset, PatternLocal yields the highest physiological plausibility via dipole fit (0.756 ± 0.090) versus LIME (0.604 ± 0.013) and raw EEG (0.738 ± 0.013).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PatternLocal consistently outperforms LIME across all three datasets: on XAI-TRIS it reduces false-positive attributions to suppressor variables (driving the XOR suppressor attribution to near zero vs. substantial non-zero values from LIME), on the MRI dataset it achieves roughly half the EMD of LIME when restricted to high-confidence superpixels (0.102 vs. 0.196), and on EEG it produces dipole fits 0.15 higher than LIME. Against gradient-based methods (Integrated Gradients, Saliency, DeepLift, GradientShap, GuidedBackProp), PatternLocal achieves the lowest EMD on the MRI top-features evaluation, while gradient methods show extreme IME variance (±0.42) indicating their high-magnitude attributions are correctly positioned only about half the time. The edge-detection filters (Sobel, Laplace) are competitive on XAI-TRIS RIGID CORR due to sharp tetromino boundaries but fail completely on the MRI dataset (IME ≈ 1.0) where lesions are diffuse and lack strong edges. In the XAI-TRIS XOR and RIGID scenarios, PatternLocal's advantage over all baselines is most pronounced under correlated noise (CORR), where suppressor variables are present, confirming the method's core design goal. PatternLocal is slightly slower than LIME (12.9 s vs. 7.4 s per explanation on average) but the overhead is modest and justified by the consistent quality gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

PatternLocal extends the Pattern method (Haufe et al., 2014), which showed that forward-model parameters of linear classifiers reveal statistically relevant features while backward-model weights are contaminated by suppressor variables, to the non-linear and instance-level setting. It does so by first obtaining a local linear surrogate (LIME, KernelSHAP, or gradients, following the unified formulation of Tan et al., 2023) and then regressing the simplified input onto the surrogate's response under a kernel-weighted objective, effectively converting discriminative weights into a generative attribution. The theoretical motivation draws on the suppressor-variable analysis of Wilming et al. (2022, 2023) and the XAI-TRIS benchmark of Clark et al. (2024), which demonstrated that standard XAI methods systematically assign false-positive importance to correlated noise features.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical, model-agnostic remedy for a well-documented failure mode of XAI: false-positive attributions to suppressor variables that undermine the scientific and clinical utility of explanations. The EEG motor-imagery validation, where PatternLocal explanations yield physiologically plausible source localizations (dipole fit 0.756 vs. 0.604 for LIME) and support meaningful time-frequency decomposition, suggests the method could improve trust in neuroimaging-based AI. Key limitations include the requirement for representative training data near each explained instance and an assumption of spatial alignment across samples, which may not hold for natural images or user-generated content. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to multi-class or regression settings, how it interacts with self-explainable architectures, and whether the local generative assumption degrades in highly heterogeneous data regimes.
