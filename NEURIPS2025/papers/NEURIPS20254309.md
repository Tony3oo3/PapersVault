## Problem

Artificial palpation for medical examination (e.g., breast cancer detection) currently relies on producing force maps from sensor arrays, which are difficult to interpret and do not capture the full mechanical structure of soft tissue. No prior work has learned a general, self-supervised representation from sequences of tactile measurements on soft bodies that could support downstream tasks like tactile imaging and change detection. This paper asks whether an encoder-decoder trained to predict tactile forces can learn a representation rich enough to reconstruct internal object structure.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sequence of 90-dimensional force readings (30 triaxial sensors from a Xela uSkin at 85 Hz) paired with 6D end-effector poses, collected over 110 pokes on a fixed matrix per phantom trial. The encoder compresses this sequence into a single 1024-dimensional latent vector z_T. The downstream imaging head produces a 128×128 image with three discrete classes per pixel (background, body, lump), corresponding to a horizontal MRI slice of the phantom insert. Change detection is performed by comparing predicted lump sizes across two palpation trials of the same or different phantoms.

## Method

The core architecture is a self-supervised encoder-decoder: a Force-Location Encoder (FLE) maps each (force, pose) pair via a two-layer MLP on forces plus sinusoidal positional encoding on poses, and a single-layer GRU (1024 hidden) encodes the resulting sequence into latent representations z_t. The decoder predicts the force at an arbitrary target pose x_{t'} from any representation z_t using MLP(MLP(z_t) + PE(x_{t'})), trained with MSE loss over uniformly subsampled (K=64) source-target index pairs to avoid O(T²) cost. The key insight is that a representation sufficient for predicting both past and future forces must encode the object's mechanical structure. For tactile imaging, the frozen z_T is fed into a conditional flow-matching generator that produces the 3-class MRI slice, trained separately with per-pixel cross-entropy. This two-stage design—large-scale self-supervised pretraining on tactile-only data followed by small-scale supervised imaging—allows the system to benefit from abundant unlabeled palpation data while requiring only a limited number of MRI-annotated phantoms.

## Results

On the real-world breast phantom dataset, the proposed self-supervised representation combined with a flow-matching image predictor achieves 23.0% lump size error, 2.4 mm center-of-mass (CoM) error, and 74.4% F1 score for tactile imaging. For change detection (detecting whether a lump grew between two palpation sessions), the system achieves a recall of 0.82 and false-alarm rate of 0.19, outperforming human participants (recall 0.62, FAR 0.32) despite using a sensor with roughly two orders of magnitude lower spatial density than a human finger. In simulation, self-supervised pretraining consistently improves image prediction F1 across all data scales, with the largest gains when labeled data is scarce.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its learned-representation approach against three baselines. Against the non-learning Average Pred. baseline (which simply outputs the mean lump size and location), the proposed method reduces size error from 51.9% to 23.0% and CoM error from 12.1 mm to 2.4 mm, demonstrating that the representation captures object-specific structure beyond a global average. Against the Force Map + Image Pred. baseline (which feeds a non-learnable force map into the same flow-matching predictor), the learned representation nearly halves the size error (23.0% vs. 46.6%) and more than doubles the F1 score (74.4% vs. 47.3%), confirming that self-supervised sequence modeling extracts information not present in a static force snapshot. A direct MLP predictor on the frozen representation achieves a lower size error (10.5% vs. 23.0%), indicating the representation encodes even more lump information than the image pipeline extracts, likely due to limited MRI training data. For change detection, the system (recall 0.82, FAR 0.19) outperforms ~15 human participants (recall 0.62, FAR 0.32) on the same test set. Finally, a data-scaling experiment shows that doubling unsupervised palpation data yields an 11% F1 gain, while doubling supervised (MRI-labeled) data on top adds only 2%, suggesting the approach scales favorably with cheap tactile data rather than expensive imaging labels.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the self-supervised encoder-decoder pretraining paradigm from vision (MAE) and language (BERT) to the domain of soft-body tactile sequences, where prior tactile representation learning work (Guzey et al. 2023, Higuera et al. 2024, Feng et al. 2025) has focused almost exclusively on rigid bodies and instantaneous or short-horizon measurements. It departs from conventional tactile imaging (force maps à la Sarvazyan et al. 2012) by framing palpation as a sequence-level inference problem over mechanical structure, and from prior robotic palpation studies (Scimeca et al. 2022, Sanni et al. 2022) by using a learned GRU representation rather than hand-crafted features or simple classifiers. The use of conditional flow matching to generate MRI-like images from a frozen tactile representation is a novel architectural choice not seen in prior tactile-to-imaging work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first proof-of-concept demonstrating that a self-supervised tactile representation on soft bodies can support both tactile imaging (predicting MRI-like slices) and change detection at a level comparable to or exceeding human performance, opening a path toward data-driven artificial palpation. The data-scaling result—that additional unsupervised tactile data contributes more to imaging accuracy than additional MRI-labeled data—has practical implications for scaling to human studies where MRI acquisition is costly. Key open questions include whether the approach generalizes to anatomically realistic tissue with complex microstructure (ducts, vessels), whether more complex palpation motions (circular, adaptive) improve the representation, and whether multi-sensor or vision-based tactile inputs can be integrated. The current prototype is limited to simple linear pokes, a single low-density sensor, and non-anatomical phantoms, so clinical translation remains distant.
