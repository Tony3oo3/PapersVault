## Problem

The debate on democratizing AI is mired in two unresolved problems: persistent conceptual disagreement about what 'democratizing AI' concretely entails, and persistent normative disagreement about whether it is ethically and politically desirable. The authors argue that much of this impasse stems from eight common misconceptions—termed 'traps'—that distort both the conceptual and normative landscape, leading critics to dismiss democratization for the wrong reasons and proponents to fail to articulate its strongest version.

## Input / Output

As a conceptual and normative paper, the analysis takes as input the existing interdisciplinary discourse on AI democratization spanning philosophy, computer science, and science and technology studies, drawing on concrete policy cases (e.g., facial recognition bans, citizen assemblies, open-source AI initiatives). The output is a structured taxonomy of eight traps with individual rebuttals, followed by four actionable recommendations for citizens, researchers, and policy experts regarding levers of democratic control over AI design and deployment.

## Method

The paper identifies and systematically rebuts eight 'traps' organized into two domains. Five conceptual traps (Democratic Minimalism, Access, Preference Satisfaction, AI Exceptionalism, Egalitarian Illusion) challenge reductive or misleading conceptions of what democratizing AI means, arguing instead for a multi-faceted, ongoing process involving deliberation, citizen assemblies, third-party audits, and resource reallocation rather than one-off interventions or mere tool access. Three normative traps (Stupid Democracy, Unjust Democracy, Slow Democracy) address common skeptical arguments, showing each fails to decisively undermine democratization's normative desirability by appealing to the epistemic value of non-technical citizen expertise, the intrinsic value of autonomy, and the availability of transnational cooperation. The authors ground their preferred substantive view in the distinctive value of local, experiential knowledge held by 'regular' citizens for enhancing collective AI policy decisions, while acknowledging democratization's limits and treating it as a weighty but defeasible normative goal rather than an absolute override on all other values.

## Results

This is a conceptual and normative position paper with no quantitative experiments or empirical benchmarks. The paper's main analytical contribution is the identification of eight 'AI democratization traps'—five conceptual misconceptions (Democratic Minimalism, Access, Preference Satisfaction, AI Exceptionalism, Egalitarian Illusion) and three normative objections (Stupid Democracy, Unjust Democracy, Slow Democracy)—each of which the authors argue is a misconception that should be resisted. The authors propose a substantive account of AI democratization centered on harnessing non-technical citizen expertise for collective AI policy decisions, adopting a realistic view of democracy's limits, and rejecting the assumption that democratic processes are objectionably slow.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare methods against empirical baselines, as it is a normative-theory and conceptual-analysis contribution rather than an empirical study. Instead, it critically evaluates eight specific argumentative positions (the 'traps') that appear in the AI democratization debate, arguing that each is vulnerable to plausible objections and fails to decisively undermine the normative desirability of AI democratization. The authors position their view against (i) reductive access-based accounts (e.g., Zuckerberg's open-source framing), (ii) preference-satisfaction approaches (e.g., Anthropic's Collective Constitutional AI), (iii) AI exceptionalism arguments, and (iv) speed-first technocratic governance (e.g., Thiel's 'education of a libertarian' framing), while remaining compatible with civic-tech approaches and acknowledging that other, unaddressed normative objections may exist.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes and reorganizes arguments from the participatory AI literature (Birhane et al., Sloane et al., Feffer et al.), the AI fairness and accountability literature (Raji et al., Selbst et al., Madaio et al.), and political philosophy (Christiano, Cohen & Sabel, Dworkin) into a unified 'traps' taxonomy. It extends the 'ethics-washing' critique (Bietti, Wagner) to a parallel 'democracy-washing' concept and engages the emerging 'AI as normal technology' debunking school (Narayanan & Kapoor) to challenge AI exceptionalism. Rather than proposing a new empirical method, the paper carves new conceptual ground by identifying eight specific misconceptions that have derailed the democratization debate and offering a constructive normative position that treats citizens as 'representative experts' with domain-specific, non-technical knowledge.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a structured diagnostic framework for a debate that the authors argue has been mired in conceptual confusion, giving researchers and policymakers a shared vocabulary for identifying weak arguments on both sides. Its explicit limitations—no empirical feasibility study, no comprehensive policy blueprint, no comparative ranking of normative values—leave substantial open questions about whether the proposed multi-mechanism participatory design is practically implementable at scale. The 'democracy-washing' concept and the coarse-grained acceptability threshold (no single actor can unilaterally decide when and how to consult others) offer testable criteria that could ground future empirical work on whether AI governance processes genuinely redistribute decision power.
