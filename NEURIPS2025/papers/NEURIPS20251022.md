## Problem

Despite widespread empirical evidence that gradient-based training on imbalanced data amplifies majority bias, the mathematical mechanisms behind this phenomenon—particularly in nonlinear, nonconvex deep learning regimes—remained poorly understood. The paper asks: why does standard training first produce stereotypical predictors aligned only with the majority group, and what is the minimum additional training time required to escape this bias and capture minority-specific features?

## Input / Output

The method takes standard supervised learning data (RGB images at 32×32 for CIFAR-10/EuroSAT, or tabular census features for Adult) partitioned into a majority group A=1 and a minority group A=0, with the total loss decomposed as L = L₁ + L₀ where L₀ ≪ L₁. Rather than producing only a class label, the theoretical framework outputs geometric quantities in parameter space: a stereotype gap bound (Hausdorff distance between representative and stereotypical critical points), a lower bound on debiasing duration, and a catch-up overcost ratio quantifying the relative extra training time needed for minority accuracy to reach a target threshold κ. Empirically, the experiments report per-subgroup accuracy curves and catch-up overcost percentages across multiple architectures and imbalance levels.

## Method

The core contribution is a Kantorovich-type perturbation framework showing that under strong imbalance, every critical point of the full loss L is paired with a nearby critical point of the majority loss L₁ (the stereotypical predictor), with distance bounded by 4τ/δ where τ bounds the minority gradient/Hessian and δ bounds the majority Hessian's smallest singular value. The authors partition parameter space into a majority-training zone (where ∇L and ∇L₁ align) and a majority-adverse zone (where minority features are learned), proving the adverse zone has volume O(4^{-d}) and thus occupies negligible space. They then derive logarithmic lower bounds on both the time to reach a stereotypical predictor and the additional debiasing time to reach a representative predictor, showing these scale as (1/M)·log(1/ε) and grow with imbalance severity and small step sizes. The theory is validated through deep learning experiments (ResNet, VGG, MobileNet, TabNet) on imbalanced CIFAR-10, EuroSAT, and Adult datasets, confirming catch-up overcosts of 25%–450% and demonstrating that the phenomenon persists under AdamW and XGBoost.

## Results

The paper's central quantitative finding is the catch-up overcost ratio, which measures the extra training time needed for minority accuracy to reach a threshold κ after global accuracy has already reached it. On imbalanced CIFAR-10, this overcost ranges from 0% (at 80% balance) to 450% (MobileNetV2 at 1% balance, κ=90%), with ResNet-18 showing 292% at 1% and 164% at 10%. On the Adult dataset (3% minority), the overcost reaches 416% at κ=90%. The theoretical stereotype gap bound (4τ/δ) is validated on imbalanced CIFAR-2 with ResNet-18, where the measured gap ‖θ̂−θ̂₁‖ = 0.6723 (relative gap 0.006) and debiasing time is 469 epochs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper that does not propose a new method to compare against competing baselines; instead, it validates its theoretical predictions (stereotype gap bounds, debiasing time lower bounds) across multiple model architectures and imbalance levels. The key empirical comparison is across model sizes: larger models (ResNet-101, 42M params) consistently reduce the catch-up overcost relative to smaller ones (MobileNetV2, 543K) at the same imbalance level—for example, at 1% imbalance and κ=90%, the overcost drops from 450% (MobileNetV2) to 145% (ResNet-101)—but never eliminates it. The paper also confirms the phenomenon persists under alternative optimizers (AdamW) and algorithms (XGBoost), showing the bias amplification is not an artifact of vanilla gradient descent. Across all settings, the majority-adverse zone (where minority features are actually learned) occupies a vanishingly small fraction of parameter space (volume < O(4^{-d})), confirming that standard training overwhelmingly learns majority traits first.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Newton-Kantorovich theorem (Ciarlet & Mardare, 2012) to develop a model-agnostic geometric framework for bias amplification in majority-minority learning, departing from prior theoretical work that relies on analytic solutions in Gaussian or ridge-regression settings (Subramonian et al. 2024; Sarao Mannelli et al. 2025) or focuses on loss trajectories and per-class gradients (Francazi et al. 2023). While the empirical phenomenon of bias amplification has been documented since the 1990s (Anand et al. 1993) and in the deep learning era (Johnson & Khoshgoftaar 2019; Bell & Sagun 2023), the mathematical mechanisms—especially in nonlinear nonconvex regimes—remained poorly understood. The paper's key conceptual departure is framing the problem through the geometry of critical points in parameter space, introducing the 'stereotype gap' and 'catch-up overcost' as formal, model-agnostic quantities rather than analyzing specific loss landscapes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first general geometric theory explaining why standard gradient-based training produces stereotypical predictors in imbalanced settings, with practical implications: the catch-up overcost of 25%–450% means practitioners must train substantially longer to achieve minority-aware predictions, a finding with direct relevance under regulatory frameworks like the EU AI Act. The geometric result that the majority-adverse zone has volume O(4^{-d}) in high dimensions explains why random initialization almost always begins in the majority-training regime. Limitations include the Morse-function assumption, the absence of worst-case biasing complexity bounds, and the focus on training-time dynamics rather than generalization; open questions include whether more refined training protocols (curriculum learning, group-aware scheduling) can reduce the overcost without data-level interventions.
