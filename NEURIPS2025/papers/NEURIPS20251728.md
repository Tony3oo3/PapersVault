## Problem

Connectome proofreading—the manual correction of segmentation errors in neural imaging data—remains a severe bottleneck, requiring an estimated 33 human-years for a single Drosophila brain. While multimodal LLMs have shown rapid gains in visual reasoning, no systematic benchmark exists to evaluate whether they can perform the specific perceptual and judgment tasks (segment classification, split/merge error detection) that human proofreaders perform daily.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251728/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance presents the LLM with three orthographic views (top, side, front) of a 3D segmentation mesh rendered at 1024×1024 pixels, cropped to a task-specific bounding box (e.g., 4096 nm³ for split errors, 8192 nm³ for merge errors), accompanied by a structured text prompt. Outputs are task-specific: a 5-way segment type label (single neuron, merged neurons, processes without soma, nucleus, non-neuronal), a binary 1/−1 decision on whether a proposed merge or split is correct, or a multiple-choice selection identifying the correct merge partner among two candidates. Proprietary models are queried 5–10 times per instance with majority voting; open-source models use greedy decoding for deterministic outputs.

## Method

ConnectomeBench constructs ground-truth evaluation sets by mining the full edit history of human proofreaders through the CAVEClient API on two large open-source connectomes (MICrONS mouse visual cortex and FlyWire Drosophila brain), extracting verified split corrections, merge corrections, and segment type annotations. The benchmark evaluates proprietary multimodal LLMs (Claude 3.7/4 Sonnet, o4-mini, GPT-4.1, GPT-4o) and open-weight models (InternVL-3 8B/78B, NVLM) in a zero-shot setting, comparing 'Null' prompts against 'Description' prompts that provide category definitions. A key methodological contribution is the iterative heuristic-refinement loop: the authors analyze o4-mini's natural-language reasoning traces to identify systematic visual biases (e.g., assuming correct merges must be small/thin, or that large gaps invalidate a merge), then encode seven corrective heuristics into the prompt, yielding measurable accuracy gains across models. Fine-tuned ResNet-50 classifiers serve as a compact-model baseline, and a NEURD auto-proofreading pipeline is reproduced as an external method baseline.

## Results

On segment type identification, proprietary LLMs achieve 52–82% balanced accuracy (vs. 20–25% chance), with Claude 3.7 Sonnet reaching 82.2% on MICrONS and GPT-4.1 reaching 52.9% on FlyWire. For split error correction, o4-mini with heuristics achieves 78.6% binary accuracy on MICrONS and 85.0% multiple-choice accuracy, compared to 50% and 33% chance respectively, though human annotators reach 90.2% and 92.0%. On merge error identification, performance is weakest: o4-mini+Description reaches only 62.8% binary accuracy on FlyWire, and o4-mini+Null reaches 74.0% multiple-choice accuracy, both well below human performance (74–84%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary external baseline is NEURD (Celii et al., 2025), a heuristic graph-based merge error correction system. NEURD was run on 14 nucleus-backed merge error examples from MICrONS and achieved a detection rate of 0% (zero suggestions within 3000 nm of any ground-truth split location), whereas o4-mini+Description achieved 61.5% binary accuracy on the full 100-example merge error subset. Against the fine-tuned ResNet-50 baseline, LLMs generally outperform on segment identification (e.g., Claude 3.7 Sonnet: 82.2% vs. 58.7% on MICrONS) and split error correction (o4-mini+Heuristics: 78.6% vs. 66.7% binary on MICrONS), but underperform on merge error identification (o4-mini+Description: 61.5% vs. 79.8% on MICrONS). Human annotators remain the upper bound across all tasks, with 84–92% accuracy on split error tasks and 74–84% on merge error tasks, indicating a 10–25 point gap between the best LLM and expert performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by repurposing the multimodal LLM evaluation paradigm (previously applied to general visual reasoning benchmarks like CharXiv) to the specialized domain of connectome proofreading. Prior automated proofreading work has relied on heuristics over graph representations (Joyce et al. 2023, NEURD/Celii et al. 2025), CNN-based subcompartment classification (Li et al. 2020, RoboEM/Schmidt et al. 2024), point affinity transformers (Troidl et al. 2025), and reinforcement learning agents (RLCorrector/Nguyen et al. 2021), but none have evaluated whether general-purpose multimodal LLMs can perform these tasks zero-shot. The paper thus sits at the intersection of two threads: the connectomics proofreading literature and the growing LLM benchmarking literature, introducing the first systematic evaluation of frontier LLMs on connectome-specific visual reasoning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Connectome proofreading is a massive human bottleneck (33 person-years for a single Drosophila brain), and this benchmark provides the first quantitative evidence that current LLMs can meaningfully assist with key sub-tasks, particularly split error correction (75–85% with heuristics vs. 90% human). The finding that merge error identification remains a clear weakness (best ~63% binary) and that the tasks evaluated do not cover the full proofreading workflow (synapse identification, multi-step editing) leaves open whether LLM-based agents can realistically replace human proofreaders. The methodological insight that analyzing LLM reasoning failures and injecting corrective heuristics into prompts yields consistent gains across models is a transferable technique for improving LLM performance on specialized scientific tasks.
