## Problem

Existing feature selection methods optimize for a single data distribution, while distributionally robust optimization (DRO) methods train a single model to perform well across all populations. Neither addresses the practical problem of selecting a small, fixed set of features that enables independently trained, high-performing models for each subpopulation simultaneously under a collection budget. This paper tackles that unexplored intersection: model-agnostic, distributionally robust feature selection.

## Input / Output

The method takes historical tabular data {(X_j, Y_j)} drawn from P known populations (with group labels), a feature budget k < m, and a regularization parameter λ. It outputs a global subset of k feature indices shared across all populations, determined by selecting the k features with the smallest optimized noise parameters α* ∈ R^m_{≥0}. Notably, the method is model-agnostic: it requires only a one-time fit of E[Y|X] per population and never differentiates through downstream model training, so the selected features can be used with any predictor (random forests, MLPs, etc.).

## Method

The core mechanism replaces the discrete binary feature mask with a continuous Gaussian noise injection: each covariate X_i is observed as S_i = X_i + √α_i · ε_i, where α_i controls the information loss. The key theoretical insight (Theorem 1) shows that minimizing the Bayes-optimal MSE under this noise model reduces to minimizing the conditional variance of μ_i(X) = E[Y|X] given S(α), which after dropping α-constant terms becomes maximizing E_S[E[μ_i(X)|S(α)]²]. Theorem 2 then provides a closed-form Gaussian kernel estimator for this conditional expectation, where the kernel bandwidth is governed by α. The resulting objective is optimized end-to-end via gradient descent using the reparameterization trick (S = X + √α ⊙ ε), with the DRO max-over-populations (or softmax) taken over per-population losses. In practice, the inner kernel sum is restricted to k-nearest neighbors for efficiency, and the final feature set is the k indices with the smallest α* values.

## Results

On the ACS household income regression task (budget 7 of 18 features), the proposed method achieves MSE of 0.016 (CA), 0.017 (FL), and 0.040 (NY) with R² scores of 0.985, 0.983, and 0.967 respectively—an order-of-magnitude reduction in MSE over the best baseline (DRO Lasso: MSE 0.226–0.318). On the UCI Adult Income classification task (budget 5 of 44 features), the method achieves 91.8% accuracy on the Female population and 78.6% on the Male population, outperforming all baselines on accuracy in both groups. The method also exhibits consistently low run-to-run variance (±0.006–0.052) compared to baselines with substantially higher standard deviations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method decisively outperforms all four baselines on the ACS regression task, achieving an order-of-magnitude lower MSE (0.016–0.040 vs. 0.226–1.098) and R² above 0.96 in every state, while the best baseline (DRO Lasso) reaches only R² ≈ 0.69–0.77. On the UCI classification task, the method wins on accuracy in both populations (91.8% Female, 78.6% Male) versus the next-best XGBoost (90.4% Female, 69.9% Male) and DRO XGBoost (89.7% Female, 71.1% Male), though XGBoost achieves a slightly lower log loss on the Female group (0.279 vs. 0.300). Vanilla Lasso and XGBoost perform poorly on ACS (R² near zero or negative), confirming that standard feature importance or coefficient-magnitude criteria fail under population heterogeneity. The DRO variants (iterative population reweighting à la Sagawa et al.) improve over their vanilla counterparts but remain far behind the proposed method, suggesting that reweighting a single model's training is insufficient when the goal is selecting a fixed feature subset that supports per-population model training. The method also shows markedly lower variance across random seeds than all baselines, indicating more stable feature selection.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two well-studied but previously disjoint threads: noise-based feature selection (Grandvalet 2000; Bayesian relevance estimation via Neal 1996, Tipping 2001) and group distributionally robust optimization (Sagawa et al. 2019; Hashimoto et al. 2018). The key conceptual departure is from MAML-style differentiable optimization through model training: instead of backpropagating through an inner risk-minimization loop, the authors target the Bayes-optimal predictor's conditional variance, which—via the law of total variance and a Gaussian noise model—collapses to a closed-form kernel-weighted objective over pre-computed conditional-mean estimates. This makes the method model-agnostic and avoids the computational and stability costs of differentiating through arbitrary model fitting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical motivation (selecting a fixed, small set of survey questions or sensors that support high-quality models across multiple subpopulations) is concrete and under-addressed; the model-agnostic property is a genuine advantage over embedded or DRO-model baselines that lock in a specific architecture. However, the empirical validation is limited to two real datasets (both heavily subsampled, with at most 3 populations), and the theoretical derivation is specific to MSE loss, leaving open whether the bias-variance decomposition trick extends cleanly to cross-entropy or other proper scoring rules. The plug-in estimator for μ_i(X) introduces estimation bias that could be problematic for small or imbalanced populations, a limitation the authors acknowledge but do not fully resolve.
