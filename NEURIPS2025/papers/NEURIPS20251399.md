## Problem

Classical neural network training maintains fixed class proportions within batches, mirroring the dataset's original distribution, which fails to account for varying difficulty across classes. Existing imbalance-handling methods (oversampling, class-balanced losses, importance sampling) adjust sample weights or resample datasets but do not dynamically alter batch class composition during training using real-time performance metrics. The paper asks whether adaptively adjusting class proportions within batches, guided by instantaneous class-wise error rates, can accelerate model convergence.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a labeled training dataset (which may be manually imbalanced), a fixed neural network architecture, and three hyperparameters: learning rate η, mixing rate γ ∈ (0,1), and batch size M. The dataset is pre-split into k class-specific subsets J_i, and each batch is constructed by sampling α_i·M examples from each J_i using a cyclic selection mechanism that wraps around when a class's samples are exhausted. The output is the trained parameter vector θ, with the mixing parameters α converging to a stable distribution proportional to the optimal class-wise error rates.

## Method

Learn2mix is a bilevel optimization procedure: the inner loop updates network parameters θ via gradient descent on the mixed loss L(θ, α^t) = Σ α_i^t · L_i(θ^t), while the outer loop updates the mixing parameters via α^t = α^{t-1} + γ(L(θ^{t-1})/Σ_j L_j(θ^{t-1}) − α^{t-1}), nudging class proportions toward the normalized class-wise loss distribution so that harder classes receive a larger share of samples. The key distinction from prior work is that batch composition changes every epoch based on real-time per-class error rates rather than a fixed resampling or weighting scheme. Theoretically, under strong convexity and Lipschitz continuity of per-class losses, the paper proves that θ converges to θ* and α converges to α* = L(θ*)/1_k^T L(θ*), and that learn2mix updates bring parameters closer to θ* than classical updates under a stated condition on the mixing rate. A cyclic index mechanism (τ_i^p) ensures that the required α_i·M samples per class per batch are always available even as α adapts, by wrapping around each J_i.

## Results

On the six classification benchmarks, learn2mix achieves the highest test accuracy at full training time (t=E) across all datasets: 98.46% on MNIST, 85.85% on Fashion-MNIST, 60.49% on CIFAR-10, 42.95% on Imagenette, 54.50% on CIFAR-100, and 82.38% on IMDB. The convergence advantage is most pronounced on imbalanced datasets, where learn2mix reaches 40% accuracy on Imagenette in 185 s (vs. 230 s for the next-best method) and 70% on IMDB in 35 s (vs. 60 s for others). On regression tasks, learn2mix reduces MSE substantially, e.g., 1.07 vs. 1.17 on Mean Estimation and 0.77 vs. 0.99 on California Housing at full training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Learn2mix outperforms all five baselines (classical training, focal loss, SMOTE, importance sampling, and curriculum learning) on every one of the six classification datasets. On balanced datasets (MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, CIFAR-10), the margins are modest—0.3–3.1% over classical training—reflecting the smaller benefit of adaptive mixing when class difficulties are similar. On imbalanced datasets the advantage is far larger: on Imagenette, learn2mix achieves 42.95% vs. 34.53% (classical), 40.60% (FCL), 39.59% (SMOTE), 35.33% (IS), and 35.26% (CURR); on CIFAR-100 it reaches 54.50% vs. 52.30% (classical) and 49.33% (FCL); and on IMDB it attains 82.38% vs. 80.84% (classical) and 74.64% (SMOTE). The convergence-speed advantage is equally consistent: learn2mix reaches target accuracies 10–40% faster in wall-clock time than all baselines, with the largest speedups on imbalanced data. No baseline matches or exceeds learn2mix on any dataset under the reported settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

Learn2mix synthesizes ideas from curriculum learning (adaptive difficulty scheduling), boosting (prioritizing misclassified examples), and class-imbalance literature (oversampling, focal loss, importance sampling) into a single mechanism: dynamically reweighting batch class proportions via real-time class-wise error rates. The paper explicitly positions itself against methods that adjust sample weights, resample datasets, or reorder examples, arguing that none of these dynamically modify the class-wise composition of batches during training. Conceptually, it is closest to a bilevel optimization view of SGD where the outer loop adjusts data distribution and the inner loop updates parameters, but the specific formulation—nudging mixing parameters toward normalized class-wise losses each epoch—is new.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a simple, drop-in training modification (one extra hyperparameter γ) that consistently accelerates convergence across classification, regression, and reconstruction tasks, which is practically appealing in resource-constrained settings. However, the theoretical guarantees (Propositions 2.3 and 2.5) rely on strong convexity and Lipschitz continuity of each class-wise loss, assumptions that are unlikely to hold for deep neural networks, limiting the formal applicability of the convergence proofs. Open questions include how the method behaves with very large models or multi-task objectives, whether the cyclic sampling mechanism introduces bias at scale, and whether the gains persist when combined with modern optimizers like LAMB or with distributed training.
