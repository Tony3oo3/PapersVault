## Problem

While deep neural networks dominate representation learning, extracting principled latent embeddings from tree-based ensembles like random forests remains largely heuristic, and the inverse problem of decoding latent vectors back to the input space is unresolved. This is particularly important because RFs often outperform DNNs on tabular data with mixed continuous and categorical features, yet no method provides a theoretically grounded autoencoding pipeline for them.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255487/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method accepts any pre-trained random forest (supervised or unsupervised) fitted on mixed-type tabular data, images, or gene expression profiles, and produces a user-specified d_Z-dimensional spectral embedding (d_Z < n) via diffusion maps. Decoding reconstructs data points by sampling from the intersection of assigned leaf hyperrectangles, with the reconstruction quality controlled by the number of nearest neighbors k and the latent dimensionality. The pipeline is modular: encoding and decoding are independent, so any valid encoder can be paired with any of the three decoders.

## Method

The encoding stage extracts the normalized RF similarity kernel k_n^RF (the average of per-tree colocation indicators weighted by inverse leaf size), which the paper proves is positive semidefinite, doubly stochastic, asymptotically universal, and asymptotically characteristic. Spectral decomposition of this kernel matrix yields diffusion map eigenvectors that form the embedding Z, with test points projected via the Nyström formula. For decoding, three strategies are proposed: (1) an integer linear program that recovers exact leaf assignments by matching estimated kernel entries to the forest's leaf structure, (2) split relabeling that rewrites each tree split as an axis-aligned cut in embedding space so the forest can be traversed directly on latent vectors, and (3) k-nearest-neighbors regression in latent space using synthetic samples drawn uniformly from each training point's leaf intersection. All three decoders are shown to be universally consistent under standard RF regularity conditions, and the k-NN variant is the fastest and most accurate in practice.

## Results

On 20 benchmark tabular datasets, RFAE achieves the best average rank of 1.80 (out of 5 methods) in mean distortion, a combined metric of 1−R² for continuous features and classification error for categorical features. RFAE attains the lowest distortion in 12 of 20 datasets, with particularly strong margins on abalone (0.167 vs. next-best 0.211), banknote (0.100 vs. 0.276), and churn (0.352 vs. 0.422). On MNIST, RFAE produces recognizable digit reconstructions even at d_Z=2 latent dimensions, with quality improving substantially at d_Z=32. In a scRNA-seq denoising experiment, RFAE harmonizes two mouse brain transcriptome studies (Zeisel et al. and Tasic et al.), visibly reducing batch effects in PCA and t-SNE projections.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RFAE is compared against four baselines: TVAE and TTVAE (state-of-the-art neural autoencoders designed for tabular data), and standard MLP-based AE and VAE (not optimized for mixed-type data). RFAE achieves the best mean distortion in 12 of 20 datasets and the best overall average rank (1.80 vs. 2.45 for TTVAE, 3.27 for AE, 3.38 for TVAE, and 4.10 for VAE). RFAE dominates on datasets with moderate categorical content (e.g., churn: 0.352 vs. 0.422 for TTVAE; hd: 0.432 vs. 0.582 for TVAE; wq: 0.240 vs. 0.691 for TVAE), while TTVAE wins on highly categorical or low-dimensional datasets (car: 0.107 vs. 0.320; telco: 0.091 vs. 0.177; mushroom: 0.011 vs. 0.083). The standard AE and VAE baselines perform poorly on mixed-type data, with VAE consistently the worst performer (average rank 4.10), confirming that generic neural autoencoders struggle with tabular data containing categorical features. In runtime, RFAE is faster than both TVAE and TTVAE (e.g., 1058s vs. 3611s and 5149s on the student dataset) but slower than the simpler AE and VAE, which nonetheless produce substantially worse reconstructions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the well-established connection between random forests and kernel methods (Scornet 2016, Breiman 2000) and the spectral embedding framework of diffusion maps (Coifman & Lafon 2006), but departs from prior RF embedding work (Shi & Horvath 2006, Pliakos & Vens 2018) by providing the first principled, theoretically grounded autoencoding pipeline for forests. Unlike eForest (Feng & Zhou 2018), which maps points to hyperrectangles without dimensionality reduction, or RF-informed neural autoencoders (Aumon et al. 2025), which require an auxiliary network, this work learns embeddings directly from the RF kernel and provides multiple decoding strategies with universal consistency guarantees. The key theoretical departure is proving asymptotic universality and characteristic properties of the true adaptive RF kernel, rather than the simpler Scornet or non-adaptive variants studied previously.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical, post-processing tool for extracting interpretable low-dimensional representations from pre-trained RFs without end-to-end retraining, which is valuable given that RFs remain dominant on mixed tabular data where DNNs underperform. The decoupled encode/decode architecture means the split-relabeling and k-NN decoders can pair with any valid encoding scheme, broadening applicability. Key limitations include nontrivial computational cost for decoding (especially the ILP and LASSO variants) and sensitivity of the k-NN decoder to the choice of k. Open directions include extending to generative modeling, gradient boosting, and model distillation, where the current method still requires the original forest to compute adjacencies and leaf bounds.
