## Problem

Despite the rapid proliferation of graph unlearning (GU) methods, the field lacks a unified benchmark enabling fair, apples-to-apples comparison. Existing evaluations are fragmented across heterogeneous datasets, incompatible GNN backbones, and rigid single task-request pairings, preventing consistent conclusions about which methods generalize. The non-Euclidean structure of graphs and the recursive message-passing mechanism of GNNs make GU uniquely challenging relative to unlearning in other modalities, yet no prior work has systematically isolated these difficulties.

## Input / Output

The benchmark accepts a trained GNN on a graph G=(V,E,X) together with an unlearning request ΔG (specifying nodes, edges, or features to remove) and a downstream task label, then produces the unlearned model's predictive metrics (F1, AUC-ROC, Accuracy) on retained data alongside privacy-verification metrics (MIA AUC targeting 0.5, PA AUC improvement) and efficiency metrics (wall-clock time, peak memory). The critical design choice is enabling all 3×3 combinations of unlearning request type and downstream task, rather than the conventional one-to-one pairing (e.g., node unlearning only with node classification), which is what prior work has restricted itself to.

## Method

OpenGU integrates 16 SOTA GU algorithms across five categories—partition-based (GraphEraser, GUIDE, GraphRevoker), influence-function-based (GIF, CGU, CEU, GST, IDEA, ScaleGUN), learning-based (GNNDelete, MEGU, SGU, D2DGN, GUKD), projection-based (Projector), and structure-based (UtU)—reproduced under a unified API with 13 GNN backbones spanning traditional, sampling, and decoupled paradigms. The cross-over design decouples each method's task-specific components to enable flexible recombination across the full 3×3 task-request matrix on 37 multi-domain datasets. Evaluation proceeds along three axes: effectiveness (reasoning accuracy on retained data plus forgetting verification via membership inference and poisoning attacks), efficiency (theoretical and empirical time/space complexity and scalability to large graphs), and robustness (performance under varying deletion intensities, label/feature noise, and sparsity). Through this standardized framework, the authors derive 10 conclusions identifying systematic strengths and weaknesses of each GU family, such as learning-based methods excelling at node-node but failing at edge-edge, and IF-based methods offering broad task coverage but sometimes failing to fully eliminate privacy leaks.

## Results

OpenGU benchmarks 16 GU algorithms across 37 datasets with 13 GNN backbones. In the primary node-node experiment (Table 2), Learning-based methods SGU (89.26% F1 on Cora) and D2DGN (88.41%) lead, followed by CEU (87.12%) and IDEA (87.71%), while Partition-based methods (GraphEraser 81.14%, GUIDE 73.89%) and several IF-based methods (CGU 86.37% but OOM/OOT on large datasets) trail. On the large-scale ogbn-arxiv dataset, only 6 of 16 methods complete execution without OOM or OOT, with SGU achieving the best F1 of 67.20%. For forgetting verification via MIA, most methods cluster near the ideal AUC of 0.5, but CGU deviates significantly (AUC > 0.6), indicating incomplete unlearning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a benchmark, OpenGU does not propose a new method but systematically compares 16 existing GU algorithms. In node-node experiments, Learning-based methods SGU and D2DGN consistently outperform all others (e.g., SGU 89.26% vs. GraphEraser 81.14% on Cora), while IF-based methods GIF and IDEA dominate edge-edge link prediction, and Partition-based methods (GraphEraser, GUIDE, GraphRevoker) struggle with link prediction due to edge sparsity. For forgetting verification, MIA shows most methods achieve AUC near 0.5 (ideal), but CGU fails with AUC > 0.6, and only GNNDelete among Learning-based methods successfully handles edge-level poisoning attacks. Scalability is a critical differentiator: on ogbn-arxiv, GUIDE, GNNDelete, GUKD, and Projector hit OOM, while CGU and Projector hit OOT, leaving only 6 methods viable. The paper concludes that no single method family dominates across all task-request combinations, with Learning-based methods excelling in targeted node tasks, IF-based methods offering broader flexibility, and Partition-based methods remaining competitive but less scalable.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper does not propose a new graph unlearning algorithm but instead unifies the fragmented GU literature—spanning partition-based (GraphEraser, GUIDE, GraphRevoker), influence-function-based (GIF, CGU, CEU, IDEA, ScaleGUN), learning-based (GNNDelete, MEGU, SGU, D2DGN, GUKD), projection-based (Projector), and structure-based (UtU) methods—into a single reproducible benchmark. It departs from prior GU studies, which typically evaluate one or two methods on a single task-request pair with ad-hoc datasets and backbones, by standardizing 37 datasets, 13 GNN backbones, and enabling systematic 3×3 cross-experiments over unlearning requests and downstream tasks. Conceptually, it extends the broader machine unlearning paradigm (Cao & Yang 2015; Bourtoule et al. 2021) to the graph domain, where non-Euclidean structure and recursive message passing introduce unique challenges absent in tabular or vision unlearning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

OpenGU addresses a critical gap: without a unified benchmark, GU methods have been evaluated under inconsistent protocols, making cross-method comparisons unreliable and hindering progress. The 10 distilled conclusions—particularly that IF-based methods with theoretical guarantees can still leak information under MIA, that learning-based methods are largely inapplicable to edge-level unlearning, and that most methods fail at scale (OOM/OOT on ogbn-arxiv/products)—provide a clear roadmap for future research. Key open questions include designing generalized frameworks for mixed unlearning requests and dynamic/heterophilic graphs, developing unified metrics that jointly capture forgetting and reasoning, and improving robustness to realistic noise and sparsity. As the first comprehensive GU benchmark, it is likely to become the standard evaluation reference for the field, much as OGB did for general graph ML.
