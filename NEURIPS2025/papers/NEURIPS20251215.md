## Problem

As data discovery systems and LLMs automate hypothesis generation, a new bottleneck emerges: assessment. Experts are flooded with thousands of machine-suggested correlations and causal links but lack an automated signal for which are novel, non-trivial, or worth deeper investigation. This paper asks whether the vast knowledge encoded in LLM weights can be leveraged to automatically assess which observed correlations are surprising relative to prior expectations, thereby surfacing hypothesis-worthy relationships without labor-intensive human triage.

## Input / Output

The method takes a variable pair (names and descriptions), a source table name, and a dataset description as structured prompt context for GPT-4o. It produces a calibrated continuous probability density over Pearson correlation values in [-1,1], constructed by first extracting a discrete distribution from the model's raw token logits at each decoding position (top-k enumeration of valid numeric token sequences) and then smoothing with Gaussian kernels. The resulting density enables computing surprise (negative log-likelihood) at any observed correlation value, which serves as the ranking signal for hypothesis assessment.

## Method

The Logit-based Calibrated Prior (LCP) avoids parametric assumptions by directly prompting the LLM to predict a correlation coefficient and extracting the full belief distribution from raw output logits rather than asking the model to parameterize a fixed form (e.g., Gaussian mean and std). Algorithm 1 enumerates all valid top-k token sequences at each decoding step for the numeric value field, computes joint log-probabilities, aggregates sequences mapping to the same float value, and normalizes via softmax to yield a discrete distribution. A single-path decoding approximation (conditioning on the most probable sign token) is used for efficiency, justified by the model's high sign accuracy (78.8%) and near-deterministic sign confidence (median 99.8% probability gap). The discrete distribution is then smoothed into a continuous density via a weighted sum of Gaussian kernels truncated to [-1,1], with the kernel bandwidth σ=0.4 globally tuned on a 300-sample held-out set by minimizing negative log-likelihood at observed correlations. This non-parametric approach is motivated by a chi-square goodness-of-fit test showing the LLM's output distributions reject normality in 2,095 of 2,096 cases, and the tuned σ corrects for the model's systematic overconfidence (the Gaussian prior baseline defaults to σ=0.1 in 74% of cases due to an implicit fixed sample-size assumption).

## Results

On a benchmark of 2,096 real-world variable pairs, LCP achieves 78.8% sign accuracy, a mean absolute error of 0.26, and 95% credible interval coverage of 89.2% in predicting Pearson correlation coefficients. It reduces the average information content from 0.69 (uniform prior) to 0.27, indicating substantially higher likelihood assigned to observed correlations. In the hypothesis-retrieval task on noisy Chicago Open Data (Nexus), LCP achieves 0.80 Precision@10 and an average rank of 21.5 for expert-flagged correlations, outperforming all baselines. Under a contextual-contradiction probe, LCP maintains 95.2% sign accuracy and 92.9% coverage, confirming context-sensitive reasoning rather than memorization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Gaussian prior from Capstick et al. [1], LCP dramatically outperforms on calibration (89.2% vs. 49.1% 95% coverage) and information content (0.27 vs. 4.10), because the LLM defaults to σ=0.1 in 74% of cases, producing overconfident, sharply peaked densities. Against the fine-tuned RoBERTa classifier from Trummer [30], LCP wins on binary correlation classification (up to 0.84 accuracy, 0.79 F1, 0.53 MCC vs. lower values for RoBERTa across all thresholds) and on the retrieval task (0.80 Precision@10 vs. 0.60 for RoBERTa; average rank 21.5 vs. 30.9). LCP also matches the KDE prior's sign accuracy (78.8%) while achieving substantially better calibration (89.2% vs. 59.9% coverage) and lower information content (0.27 vs. 1.73). The uniform prior, while offering the highest coverage (92.3%), is far less informative (0.69 information content) and has much worse accuracy (~50% sign accuracy, 0.51 MAE). Overall, LCP provides the best accuracy–calibration trade-off among all compared priors and outperforms the classification-based RoBERTa approach on both binary prediction and ranking tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the LLM-elicited prior paradigm from Capstick et al. (Gaussian parameterization) and Requeima et al. (LLM processes) by replacing the parametric assumption with a non-parametric, logit-based extraction of the full output distribution, justified by a chi-square test rejecting Gaussian shape in 2095/2096 cases. It extends Trummer's RoBERTa-based binary correlation prediction to a calibrated continuous distribution and reframes the objective from classification to surprise-based assessment. The contextual contradiction evaluation, inspired by counterfactual reasoning in NLP, is introduced here as a novel probe to disentangle memorization from context-sensitive reasoning in LLM statistical predictions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical bottleneck—triaging thousands of machine-surfaced correlations for expert attention—and provides a scalable, zero-shot method that outperforms both magnitude-based ranking and fine-tuned classifiers. The contextual contradiction experiment offers a reusable evaluation methodology for testing whether LLM statistical predictions reflect genuine reasoning or memorized associations. Key limitations include dependence on a single expensive LLM (GPT-4o), restriction to Pearson correlation, and per-pair API cost that limits scalability; open questions include generalization to other relationship types (causality, mutual information), smaller/cheaper models, and multi-modal variable contexts.
