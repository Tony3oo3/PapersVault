## Problem

Perturbation response prediction models are evaluated across inconsistent datasets, metrics, and protocols, making direct comparison between published architectures difficult. Traditional fit-based metrics (RMSE, cosine similarity) fail to detect mode collapse, where a model generates the same prediction regardless of the target perturbation yet still achieves acceptable aggregate scores. This paper addresses both gaps by introducing a standardized benchmarking suite with diverse datasets, biologically relevant tasks, and new rank-based metrics that expose degenerate model behavior.

## Input / Output

Models consume control cell single-cell RNA-seq expression vectors, one-hot or embedded perturbation labels (gene or chemical), and covariate identifiers (cell line, cytokine treatment, donor), and produce counterfactual predicted gene expression profiles for the perturbed state. Because RNA-seq destroys the cell, predictions are inherently counterfactual: the model must infer what a control cell's transcriptome would look like under a given perturbation. Evaluation compares predicted population aggregates (mean expression, log fold-change) and full distributions (MMD in PCA space, DEG recall) against observed perturbed cell populations, with rank metrics measuring whether the model correctly orders perturbations by their distinct effects.

## Method

PerturBench is a modular PyTorch Lightning + Hydra framework that unifies six published datasets (Srivatsan20, Frangieh21, Jiang24, McFalineFigueroa23, Norman19, OP3) under standardized covariate-transfer and combinatorial-prediction splits, with two training strategies: control matching (pairing a perturbed cell with a same-covariate control) and disentanglement (autoencoder architectures separating basal state from perturbation effect). The model zoo includes reimplementations of CPA, SAMS-VAE, BioLord, and GEARS alongside strong baselines (Linear, Latent Additive, Decoder-Only), with ablation variants that remove adversarial classifiers or sparsity masks to isolate each architectural assumption. A key methodological contribution is the rank metric, which for each observed perturbation measures how many other predicted perturbations are closer to it than the correct prediction, yielding a score between 0 (perfect) and 0.5 (random) that directly detects mode collapse invisible to RMSE or cosine similarity. Hyperparameter optimization via Optuna (≥60 trials per model/dataset/task) and multi-seed evaluation (5 runs) ensure fair comparisons, while data-scaling and imbalance experiments probe how architectures behave as training data grows or becomes unevenly distributed across covariates.

## Results

Across six datasets and two task types (covariate transfer and combinatorial prediction), no single model architecture consistently dominates. On the Srivatsan20 covariate transfer task, CPA*(noAdv) achieves the best Cosine LogFC rank (0.09) and MMD PCA (0.49), while SAMS-VAE*(S) achieves the best Cosine LogFC (0.53). On the larger Jiang24 dataset (1.6M cells, 30 biological states), the simple Decoder-Only baseline achieves the best Cosine LogFC (0.64) and the Latent Additive baseline achieves the best rank (0.38), outperforming all published models. Ablating the adversarial classifier from CPA and the sparsity constraint from SAMS-VAE consistently improves performance, and the rank metrics reveal mode collapse in models (e.g., Decoder(Cov) with rank ≈ 0.50, equivalent to random) that traditional fit metrics (RMSE, cosine similarity) fail to detect.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CPA (Lotfollahi et al., 2023): The full CPA* model is competitive on rank metrics in Srivatsan20 (0.15) but is outperformed by its own ablated version CPA*(noAdv) (0.09 rank, 0.49 MMD PCA), indicating the adversarial classifier is counterproductive. On Norman19, CPA* achieves 0.76 Cosine LogFC vs. the Latent Additive baseline's 0.79. SAMS-VAE (Bereket & Karaletsos, 2024): SAMS-VAE* achieves the best Cosine LogFC in Srivatsan20 (0.44) but suffers from mode collapse in Frangieh21 (rank ≈ 0.48, near-random), and its ablated version SAMS-VAE*(S) outperforms it on all metrics (0.53 Cosine LogFC, 0.12 rank in Srivatsan20). BioLord (Piran et al., 2024): BioLord* underperforms all other models on every metric across all datasets, with particularly poor MMD PCA (4.3 in Srivatsan20 vs. 0.49 for CPA*(noAdv)) and near-random rank scores. GEARS (Roohani et al., 2023): Evaluated only on Norman19 combo prediction, GEARS achieves 0.44 Cosine LogFC and 0.051 rank, substantially below the Latent Additive baseline (0.79 Cosine LogFC, 0.005 rank) and CPA*(noAdv) (0.77, 0.0057). Overall, the paper's key finding is that simpler baselines (Latent Additive, Decoder-Only) match or exceed all four published models, especially on larger datasets and rank metrics, while the published models' core architectural assumptions (adversarial disentanglement, sparse additive mechanisms) do not provide the expected benefit.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a unifying benchmark for the perturbation response prediction field, which has been fragmented across inconsistent evaluation protocols. It builds on the NeurIPS 2023 perturbation challenge (Szalata et al.) and prior systematic comparisons (Kernfeld et al., Li et al.) by extending the scope to multiple datasets, tasks (covariate transfer and combinatorial prediction), and a modular codebase. Conceptually, it repurposes the disentanglement and control-matching paradigms from CPA, SAMS-VAE, and BioLord as components to be ablated and compared under a common protocol, rather than treating them as monolithic architectures. The introduction of rank-based metrics departs from the field's reliance on global fit metrics (RMSE, cosine similarity) by targeting the specific failure mode of mode collapse that those metrics cannot detect.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The PerturBench codebase and rank metrics provide the field with much-needed infrastructure for fair model comparison, addressing a gap that has hampered progress since the NeurIPS 2023 challenge. The finding that ablating the adversarial classifier from CPA and the sparsity constraint from SAMS-VAE consistently improves performance challenges the core design assumptions of two widely-cited architectures, suggesting that the community may have been over-engineering models with components that actively hurt generalization. Open questions remain: no single architecture dominates across all datasets and metrics, distributional prediction (MMD) still favors autoencoders while average-effect prediction favors simpler baselines, and it is unclear whether the scaling advantage of simple models will persist as datasets grow to giga-scale (e.g., Tahoe-100m). The practical impact is significant for in-silico screening applications where ranking perturbations by effect size is the end goal.
