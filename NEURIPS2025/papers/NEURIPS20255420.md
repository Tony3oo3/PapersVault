## Problem

Improving language model generation quality requires specifying concrete behavioral attributes (principles) that guide refinement, but curating such principles across domains is labor-intensive and existing constitutional approaches rely on static, human-authored rule sets that are brittle and slow to update. This paper asks whether a language model can autonomously discover the latent principles that drive its own self-correction, eliminating the need for human annotation of behavioral criteria.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255420/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a mixed-domain corpus of 100k instruction-response pairs (from HH-RLHF, UltraFeedback, TL;DR, and HotpotQA) where the gold response serves as a training-time hint for principle elicitation but is never seen at inference. The model produces a two-turn trajectory: an initial response, an invoked natural-language principle, and a refined response. The discovered principles are further compressed via agglomerative clustering into a small set of medoid representatives forming a human-readable constitution. At inference, the fine-tuned model can intrinsically invoke a principle and self-correct without any external feedback or gold reference.

## Method

STaPLe formulates principle discovery as a Monte Carlo EM procedure over a latent variable z (the principle) that bridges an initial response y¹ and a refined response y². The E-step uses rejection sampling with a cycle-consistency score: the model is prompted with the gold response to propose N candidate principles, generates principle-conditioned refinements, and retains only the Best-of-N refinement that maximizes Rouge-L F1 similarity to the gold. The M-step performs supervised fine-tuning on the collected (prompt, y¹, z, y²) trajectories. A key design choice is factoring the proposal as p(y²|x,y¹,z)·p(z|x,y¹,y^G) so the gold is only visible to principle generation, preventing collapse to the trivial solution y²=y^G. Posterior regularization via agglomerative clustering over sentence embeddings (all-MiniLM-L6-v2) projects the thousands of discovered principles onto a small set of medoid representatives, yielding an interpretable constitution while preserving downstream performance. Iterating the EM loop over fresh on-policy data enables progressive self-improvement across multiple rounds.

## Results

STaPLe achieves +8–10% AlpacaEval win-rate improvement, +0.3 average MT-Bench score gain, and +19–23% IFEval principle-following win-rate over the initial policy across three 7–8B models (Llama-3.1-8B, Granite-3.1-8B, Qwen2.5-7B) after four iterations. The constrained (clustering) variant matches or slightly exceeds the unconstrained version on AlpacaEval and IFEval for Llama and Granite (e.g., 34.9 vs. 33.4 AlpacaEval for Llama), while Qwen shows a small trade-off (39.9 vs. 40.2). Gains are consistent across MT-Bench Turn 1 and Turn 2, with Turn 2 improving by an average of +0.22 over the best baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

STaPLe outperforms the STaR baseline (which trains only on the best refined response without an explicit principle) on all three benchmarks across all models: +0.08 to +0.15 on MT-Bench average, +1.6 to +2.8 on AlpacaEval, and +5.0 to +6.6 percentage points on IFEval principle-following win-rate. Against the Self-Refine prompted baseline (no training, zero-shot self-critique), STaPLe gains +0.18 to +0.33 on MT-Bench, +6.7 to +9.5 on AlpacaEval, and +10.5 to +17.7 percentage points on IFEval; notably, Self-Refine actually degrades Llama-3.1-8B's MT-Bench score (7.40 vs. 7.46 initial), while STaPLe improves it. The constrained (clustering) variant of STaPLe largely matches the unconstrained version, in fact outperforming it on AlpacaEval and IFEval for Llama and Granite (e.g., 34.9 vs. 33.4 AlpacaEval for Llama), suggesting that posterior regularization via agglomerative clustering does not sacrifice performance while yielding a more interpretable constitution.

## Relation to Prior Work

STaPLe sits at the intersection of three research threads: Constitutional AI (which uses human-curated principle sets to steer model behavior), self-correction methods like STaR and SCoRe (which train models to refine their own outputs but do not explicitly model *what* to improve), and latent-variable EM frameworks (Monte Carlo EM, posterior regularization). The key departure is treating principles as *latent variables* discovered by the model itself via a Monte Carlo EM procedure, rather than being hand-written (Constitutional AI) or proposed by a stronger frontier model (LEAP, SAMI, IterAlign). The posterior-regularization-based clustering step, borrowed from structured prediction, is a novel application for compressing thousands of discovered principles into a small, interpretable constitution without sacrificing downstream performance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that 7–8B models can iteratively self-improve on alignment benchmarks without human-curated principles or strong-model supervision, which reduces the annotation bottleneck that currently limits Constitutional AI-style alignment. The finding that agglomerative clustering of principles (constrained STaPLe) matches or slightly outperforms the unconstrained version is practically important: it means the discovered constitutions are both interpretable and effective, enabling human oversight as a post-processing step. Open questions include whether the approach extends to multi-turn refinement, task-aware principle curricula, and whether the reliance on gold references (or preference pairs) can be relaxed for truly open-ended settings where no reference exists.
