## Problem

Text-to-image diffusion models for image editing treat all users identically, optimizing for an average aesthetic rather than adapting to individual preferences. Existing personalization methods for image generation ignore collaborative signals from like-minded users and none address the more complex task of personalized image editing, which must preserve spatial consistency while capturing nuanced per-user editing styles.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255592/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system takes a base image (described by a caption), a user preference profile encoded as liked/disliked attributes in a bipartite user-attribute graph, and a high-level text editing instruction. It first produces a personalized natural-language editing prompt (e.g., 'change the sofa's color to hot pink') via a LoRA-tuned Qwen2.5 LLM conditioned on GNN-derived soft prompt tokens, then feeds that prompt to FLUX.1-DEV with ControlNet (Canny conditioning, scale 0.4, 60 steps, guidance 35) to produce the final edited image. The framework is editing-backend agnostic and can be paired with faster samplers like TurboEdit to reduce inference from ~1 minute to ~1 second.

## Method

The core contribution is Collaborative Direct Preference Optimization (C-DPO), which extends the standard DPO loss with a graph-regularized collaborative term: for each user, the loss combines their individual DPO objective with a weighted average of DPO objectives computed over their K nearest neighbors in a heterogeneous bipartite user-attribute graph, where edge weights reflect shared preference attributes. User embeddings are produced by a 2-layer GraphSAGE GNN pretrained on edge classification, then converted to soft prompt tokens via a 2-layer MLP and prepended to the editing instruction, enabling user conditioning without architectural changes. Training is two-stage: first, supervised fine-tuning of a LoRA adapter on InstructPix2Pix pairs to learn instruction generation (serving as the frozen reference policy), then C-DPO fine-tuning of a separate LoRA copy at a low learning rate (2e-7) with the GNN at an even lower rate (2e-8) to preserve pretrained graph structure. The collaborative term is scaled by λ=0.15 to balance individual alignment against neighborhood influence, and the inductive GraphSAGE design allows new users to be embedded at inference without retraining.

## Results

On prompt-level alignment (CLIP similarity to user's liked instructions), C-DPO achieves 0.354 under the Like+Dislike condition, outperforming the strongest baseline DPO-User (0.307) by 4.7 points. Under the more challenging Dislikes-only condition, C-DPO scores 0.294 versus 0.269 for DPO-User. On image-level alignment, C-DPO attains the best DINO-Ref (0.782), CLIP-Ref (0.652), HPS (0.249), and CLIP-T (0.358) scores. In the user study, C-DPO achieves a 61.6% win rate on general preference (Q1) and 46.5% on visual appeal (Q2), substantially exceeding DPO-Vanilla (21.0%/21.0%) and DPO-User (17.4%/32.5%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

C-DPO consistently outperforms all six baselines across every prompting condition and evaluation metric. Against the two generic VLMs (Qwen-VL and LLaVA), C-DPO gains 4.7–11.0 CLIP points, confirming that task-agnostic models cannot capture user-specific editing preferences. The SFT variants (SFT-Vanilla, SFT-Pix2Pix) show that editing-aware fine-tuning alone is insufficient; C-DPO surpasses them by 7.8–12.7 points, demonstrating that preference optimization is critical. DPO-Vanilla (no user conditioning) improves slightly over SFT but still trails C-DPO by 8.2 points under Like+Dislike, showing that global preference signals without personalization are inadequate. DPO-User (per-user embeddings without collaboration) is the closest competitor at 0.307, but C-DPO's graph-based collaborative term adds a further 4.7-point gain, validating the benefit of sharing preference signals among like-minded users. In the user study, C-DPO's 61.6% win rate on Q1 is nearly 3× that of DPO-Vanilla and 3.5× that of DPO-User, providing strong perceptual evidence for the collaborative personalization approach.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) from Rafailov et al. by adding a graph-regularized collaborative term that shares preference signals across stylistically similar users, and by conditioning the policy on user-specific embeddings derived from a GraphSAGE encoder over a bipartite user-attribute graph. For the editing backend, it repurposes FLUX with ControlNet (Canny conditioning) and a LoRA-tuned Qwen2.5 LLM for personalized prompt generation, building on InstructPix2Pix-style instruction editing. Conceptually, it departs from prior personalized generation work (ViPER, PPD, Pigeon) by targeting the harder editing task—where spatial consistency must be preserved—rather than free-form generation, and by introducing cross-user collaboration as a first-class training signal rather than relying solely on an individual's own history.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes personalized image editing as a distinct problem and provides the first benchmark dataset (144K synthetic preference pairs across 3,000 users) for evaluating it, which is a practical contribution for the community. The collaborative-filtering angle—inferring preferences a user has never explicitly expressed from neighbors' histories—is a natural fit for data-sparse personalization and could transfer to other preference-aligned generation tasks. Key limitations include the entirely synthetic dataset (no real user interaction data), dependence on off-the-shelf FLUX/ControlNet backbones that may propagate their own biases, and the acknowledged risk of aesthetic filter bubbles. Open questions include whether the collaborative signal generalizes to real, noisy, and temporally evolving user preferences, and whether the framework extends to video or multi-object editing where temporal and spatial consistency add further constraints.
