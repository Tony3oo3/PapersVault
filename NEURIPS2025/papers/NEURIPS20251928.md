## Problem

While it is well established that attentional cues can prioritize WM resource, whether humans can allocate WM resource based on learned stimulus-reward associations alone to maximize utility remains unresolved, with prior studies reporting mixed or null effects. Additionally, existing efficient coding frameworks minimize estimation loss but do not account for reward-driven utility maximization or the temporal dynamics of WM maintenance, leaving a gap in normative accounts of how resource should be allocated over time under reward constraints.

## Input / Output

The behavioral paradigm presents a single orientation grating (0–180°, 0.5 s) followed by a 1 s or 5 s delay, after which participants report the orientation; reward is either uniform (baseline) or increases linearly with diagonality (reward context), and the stimulus prior is either uniform or the natural environmental distribution (cardinals more prevalent). The RNN receives the same orientation encoded by 32 circular-normal tuning units, maintains it through recurrent dynamics over a 5 s delay with per-step Gaussian noise (σ=0.5), and decodes the estimate via population vector averaging from a linear readout. Outputs include the trial-by-trial memory report, orientation-binned variability and bias profiles, and the estimated Fisher-information resource function J(θ) fitted via an extended Variable Precision model.

## Method

The paper extends power-law efficient coding from minimizing expected estimation loss to maximizing expected utility, yielding the optimal resource allocation J_opt ∝ [p(θ)v(θ)]^q, where v(θ) is the learned value function; incorporating the time domain shows this optimal strategy is stationary over the delay, so resource differences between frequent and infrequent (or high- and low-reward) stimuli accumulate, producing a growing oblique effect. To test this normatively, the authors train 32-unit RNNs with tanh recurrent dynamics under a 2×2 design (uniform vs. natural prior × baseline vs. reward context), using a reward-weighted squared-error loss plus L2 regularization on both recurrent activities and weights to mimic biological resource constraints. Transfer-learning experiments—freezing either the recurrent or readout layer while retraining the other in a new condition—reveal that recurrent dynamics, not the readout, are the critical substrate for adapting to different priors and reward policies. A variant with non-homogeneous input noise (stronger for diagonal orientations) reproduces the repulsive bias observed in humans, linking encoding-stage noise to the bias-variability dissociation.

## Results

In the behavioral experiment (n=14), WM variability showed a significant 3-way interaction of context × delay × orientation (p < .05): in the baseline context, the oblique effect (lower variability at cardinals) strengthened over the 5s delay, while in the reward context it was attenuated. The oblique amplitude parameter confirmed a significant context × delay interaction (p < .05) with no main effect of context (p = .19) or delay (p = .20). WM bias was largely unaffected by either factor (context × delay interaction p = .78). RNNs trained under a 2×2 design (uniform vs. natural prior × baseline vs. reward context) reproduced these patterns: memory variability was lower for high-probability or high-reward orientations, and the effects grew over the 5s delay. Transfer learning showed that freezing the recurrent weights (and retraining only the readout) substantially weakened prior/reward effects, whereas freezing the readout and retraining the recurrent layer preserved them, indicating recurrent dynamics are critical for adaptive resource allocation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against external methods; instead, it makes internal comparisons across experimental conditions. Behaviorally, the reward context (where reward opposes the natural prior) attenuated the oblique effect at the 5s delay relative to the baseline context, while the baseline context showed an amplified oblique effect over time—confirming that both prior and reward jointly shape WM resource allocation. The RNN results closely mirrored the human data: natural-prior + baseline RNNs showed a growing oblique effect, natural-prior + reward RNNs showed an attenuated oblique effect, and uniform-prior + reward RNNs isolated the reward-driven stabilization of diagonal orientations. In transfer learning, freezing the recurrent layer and retraining only the readout produced flat variability profiles (weaker prior/reward effects), while freezing the readout and retraining the recurrent layer recovered patterns close to fully trained networks, establishing that recurrent dynamics—not the linear readout—carry the adaptive allocation. Robustness checks (128-unit networks, varied noise levels, shifted tuning centers, stronger L2 regularization) confirmed that the prior/reward effects on variability were stable across architectures and hyperparameters.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the power-law efficient coding framework (Wei & Stocker, 2015; Morais & Pillow, 2018) in two key ways: reformulating the objective from minimizing estimation loss to maximizing expected utility (J_opt ∝ [p(θ)v(θ)]^q), and incorporating the temporal dimension to show that resource allocation differences accumulate over delay. It also extends the Variable Precision model (Van den Berg et al., 2012) by allowing Fisher information J to vary as a function of orientation, enabling estimation of resource allocation profiles from behavioral data. The RNN component builds on attractor-dynamics models of WM (Wimmer et al., 2014; Compte et al., 2000) but departs from prior reward-WM studies by establishing stable learned stimulus-reward associations rather than using random per-trial reward cues, which is the critical design choice that resolves previously null or mixed findings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This paper resolves an active debate in the WM literature by demonstrating that reward does modulate WM resource allocation when it is a stable learned environmental feature, providing a normative account (utility-maximizing efficient coding) that unifies the effects of stimulus priors and reward on memory precision. The converging evidence from behavioral experiments, theoretical derivation, and RNN simulations strengthens the claim, and the transfer-learning analysis revealing that recurrent dynamics (not readout) drive adaptation to new priors/reward policies offers a mechanistic insight relevant to both neuroscience and AI. Open questions include how repulsive bias emerges through learning in hierarchical architectures, whether biologically plausible local learning rules reproduce the same utility-optimal allocation, and whether the framework generalizes beyond orientation to other continuous WM domains.
