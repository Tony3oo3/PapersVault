## Problem

Popular saliency maps for vision-language encoders like CLIP capture only first-order token attributions, overlooking the cross-modal interactions between image and text tokens that are essential for faithful explanation of similarity predictions. Additionally, removal-based explainability methods suffer from an out-of-distribution problem: masking many tokens produces unrecognizable images and ambiguous captions, degrading explanation quality. The paper addresses both gaps by introducing a game-theoretic framework with controllable mask weighting.

## Input / Output

Given a pre-trained vision-language encoder (e.g., CLIP ViT-B/32 with 49 image patches + 8 text tokens, or ViT-B/16 with 196 + 30 tokens) and an image-text pair, FIXLIP produces a 2-additive explanation: a constant, individual attribution for each token, and a pairwise interaction score for every token pair (growing quadratically, ~25k interactions for ViT-B/16). The explanation is structured as a symmetric weighted graph over all tokens, where edges encode cross-modal and intra-modal interactions, and can be reduced to a first-order saliency map via a closed-form conversion (Theorem 1). For large models, a two-step filtering heuristic restricts the interaction basis to a top-k clique or cross-modal pairs only.

## Method

FIXLIP frames explanation as a cooperative game where each token is a player and the game value ν(M) is the model's similarity score under mask M. The explanation is the weighted Banzhaf interaction index with a tunable parameter p ∈ (0,1) that controls the probability of each token being active, allowing the user to prioritize in-distribution (p > 0.5) or sparse (p < 0.5) masks. A key efficiency contribution is the cross-modal sampling strategy: instead of sampling m joint masks, it samples m_I image masks and m_T text masks independently, then evaluates all m_I × m_T combinations by encoding each modality once and computing similarities in a vectorized manner, yielding a 5–20× speedup over the model-agnostic estimator with provably bounded variance (Theorem 2). The explanation is obtained by solving a weighted least squares regression that fits a 2-additive game to the sampled game values. For models with hundreds of tokens, a two-step filtering approach first computes cheap first-order attributions to select a relevant token subset, then approximates interactions only within that subset.

## Results

On the Pointing Game Recognition (PGR) metric for CLIP ViT-B/32 on ImageNet-1k, FIxLIP with Shapley interactions achieves the best 1-object score (0.83) and competitive multi-object scores (0.82–0.86), substantially outperforming all first-order baselines (GAME: 0.28–0.61, Grad-ECLIP: 0.28–0.68, Shapley values: 0.37–0.70). On the Area between Insertion/Deletion Curves (AID) metric for CLIP ViT-B/32 on MS COCO, FIxLIP faithfully recovers nonlinear token-subset rankings, achieving the highest AID score while first-order methods fail to identify the correct deletion order. The cross-modal estimator achieves a 5–20× computational speedup over the model-agnostic estimator, enabling explanations with over 10⁶ model inferences.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FIxLIP decisively outperforms both first-order baselines—GAME (attention-based) and Grad-ECLIP (gradient-based)—across all three evaluation metrics (PGR, AID, and p-faithfulness correlation). On PGR, first-order methods collapse from ~0.6–0.7 (1 object) to ~0.28 (4 objects), confirming they cannot disentangle multiple objects, while FIxLIP maintains 0.82–0.86. Against the second-order baseline exCLIP (which approximates only cross-modal interactions via Integrated Hessians), FIxLIP wins on 1-object PGR (0.83 vs. 0.73) and on AID and p-faithfulness, but exCLIP scores slightly higher on multi-object PGR (0.88–0.92 vs. 0.82–0.86) because it specializes exclusively in cross-modal interactions. The paper attributes exCLIP's AID failure to its omission of first-order effects and intra-modal interactions. FIxLIP with weighted Banzhaf interactions (p=0.5 or 0.7) is within 1–3% of the Shapley-interaction variant on PGR while offering the practical advantage of controllable in-distribution weighting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the game-theoretic interaction index framework (Faith-Shap, KernelSHAP-IQ) to vision-language encoders by adopting weighted Banzhaf interactions rather than Shapley interactions, motivated by the need for a tunable sparsity parameter p that prioritizes in-distribution masks. It departs from concurrent work (exCLIP) that approximates only cross-modal interactions via Integrated Hessians, by including intra-modal and first-order effects in a unified 2-additive game. The cross-modal sampling strategy is a novel computational contribution that exploits the separable architecture of VLEs (independent image and text encoders) to obtain m_I·m_T game values from only m_I + m_T model inferences.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As VLEs are deployed in high-stakes domains like medical imaging, the inability of first-order saliency maps to capture cross-modal token interactions represents a genuine interpretability gap. The p-parameter offers practical control over the in-distribution/out-of-distribution trade-off in masking, addressing a known failure mode of removal-based explainability. Key limitations include restriction to second-order interactions (higher-order and tri-modal settings remain open), faithfulness degradation at larger model scales (ViT-L/16), and the need for further human-computer interaction studies on the usability of interaction graph visualizations.
