## Problem

Standard algorithmic fairness research evaluates whether individual classifiers satisfy criteria like Equal Opportunity, but real-world lending, hiring, and insurance markets involve multiple competing firms whose joint decisions determine borrower outcomes. This paper asks whether a collection of individually fair classifiers necessarily produces a fair ecosystem, and shows that it does not—identifying two distinct forces (correlation differences and data overlap differences) that cause ecosystem-level unfairness even when every classifier is individually fair.

## Input / Output

The theoretical model takes a set of competing classifiers, each mapping borrower features (x) and protected attribute (a) to a binary loan-offer decision, along with the Pearson correlation structure between classifiers on each protected group and the overlap fractions of their served borrower populations. The output is a scalar EOC level (the absolute difference in the fraction of deserving borrowers receiving at least one offer across groups) or a welfare-based v-EOC level under a specified utility function. Empirically, the experiments consume Lending Club loan records (~890k loans, 2007–2015) and Adult Census Income records (~40k individuals), using logistic regression, decision tree, and random forest classifiers with the Fairlearn package for post-processing fairness adjustment.

## Method

The paper defines Equal Opportunity under Competition (EOC) as the difference in the probability that a deserving borrower receives at least one offer across protected groups, and a welfare-based variant v-EOC. The central theoretical result (Proposition 1) shows that for two EO classifiers with false-negative rates β₁, β₂, the EOC level equals σ₁·σ₂·|ρ⁰ − ρ¹|, where ρ⁰ and ρ¹ are the Pearson correlations between the classifiers' true-positive outputs on each group—so any correlation imbalance across groups produces ecosystem unfairness. A second force (Proposition 4) arises when classifiers serve partially overlapping borrower pools, with the EOC level driven by the difference in overlap fractions γ⁰ − γ¹. The paper further proves (Section 4) that post-processing fairness adjustment to enforce EO can increase the EOC level, because the adjustment alters the correlation structure or the effective served population in ways that widen the cross-group gap. Empirically, across nine experiment configurations on two datasets, fairness adjustment worsened EOC in 15–80% of runs depending on training set size and classifier type, with mean effect sizes up to 19× for small training sets.

## Results

Theoretically, the paper shows that two individually EO classifiers can have an EOC level of up to min{β₁,β₂} − max{0,β₁+β₂−1} (Proposition 1), driven by differences in Pearson correlation between classifiers across groups, or up to max{β₁,β₂} − β₁β₂ (Proposition 4), driven by differences in borrower-set overlap. Empirically, on Lending Club data with 100k training samples, fairness adjustment worsened the EOC level in 30.1%, 15.8%, and 17.4% of runs for Experiments 1, 2, and 3 respectively (95% CIs: [26.2, 34.0], [12.6, 19.0], [14.2, 20.6]). When the EOC did worsen, the mean increase factor was as high as 19× (Exp. 1, 100k samples), indicating that individual fairness post-processing can substantially degrade ecosystem-level fairness.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing fairness methods; instead, its central empirical comparison is between the EOC level before and after applying standard fairness post-processing (via Fairlearn) to each individual classifier. The key finding is that this adjustment, which reduces each classifier's individual EO level, frequently increases the ecosystem-level EOC. Across all three main experiments on Lending Club data, the EOC worsened after adjustment in a majority of runs for small training sets (300–3k samples: 50–83% of runs) and remained significantly above 50% even at 10k samples. The effect is most pronounced when classifiers share training data (Exp. 1) or use different model families (Exp. 3), consistent with the theoretical prediction that correlation differences between classifiers drive ecosystem unfairness. The effect diminishes with larger training sets (approaching 0% at 100k for Exp. 2), aligning with the theoretical result that EOC magnitude scales with false-negative rates, which shrink as classifiers become more accurate.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the standard single-classifier fairness literature (EO, DP, ED) to a multi-firm competitive setting, introducing Equal Opportunity under Competition (EOC) as a new ecosystem-level fairness metric. It is conceptually adjacent to Liu et al. (2018) on delayed impacts of fair ML and to Bower et al. (2017) and Dwork and Ilvento (2019) on fairness under composition, but departs from all three: the failure mechanism here is parallel competition rather than temporal dynamics or sequential composition, and the paper identifies and quantifies the specific forces (correlation asymmetry and borrower-pool overlap) that drive ecosystem unfairness. The welfare-based v-EO notion is adapted from Ben-Porat et al. (2021), and the derived-classifier framework from Hardt et al. (2016) underpins the fairness-adjustment analysis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges the widely held assumption that mandating individual fairness guarantees ecosystem fairness, showing both theoretically and empirically that post-processing for EO can increase the EOC level. This has direct regulatory implications: current fairness mandates applied per-firm may be counterproductive at the ecosystem level. Key limitations include the focus on binary groups and simple utility functions, the use of mortgage status as a proxy for protected attributes in experiments, and the observation that the effect diminishes as classifiers become more accurate. Open questions include what regulatory mechanisms could achieve ecosystem fairness while preserving competition, and how results extend to richer utility structures and more than two protected groups.
