## Problem

Existing RAG benchmarks are siloed by task type (predominantly factoid queries), rely on heterogeneous knowledge bases that introduce domain bias and memorization artifacts, and lack systematic coverage of diverse information-seeking behaviors. This makes it impossible to fairly assess whether RAG methods possess the resilience needed for general-purpose information seeking across varying query complexities and domains.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252087/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each test sample pairs a stratified query (one of four levels defined by explicit/implicit referencing × factoid/rationale reasoning) with a domain-specific knowledge base drawn from 229 long texts spanning 8 domains (technology, novel, art, humanities, paper, science, finance, legal). The RAG system retrieves relevant passages and generates a free-form answer; factoid levels are scored with Rouge-L and lexical F1, while rationale levels use a novel S-F1 metric that tokenizes both predicted and ground-truth answers into sentences and uses an LLM judge to assess sentence-level semantic support in both directions (precision and recall).

## Method

HawkBench stratifies information-seeking tasks along two orthogonal dimensions—referencing (whether the query explicitly or implicitly states its intent) and reasoning (whether the answer is a direct fact or requires synthesis)—yielding four levels of increasing difficulty. The corpus comprises 229 domain-specific texts (textbooks, academic papers, financial reports, legal contracts, novels) selected to avoid overlap with common LLM pretraining sources. Annotation follows a three-step hybrid pipeline: (1) configuration of target level and domain, (2) LLM-assisted QA pair generation (GPT-4o or DeepSeek-V3) with level-specific context sampling (≈1K tokens for L1, top-10 retrieved passages for L2, up to 120K tokens for L3/L4), and (3) human quality control by three PhD annotators who discard invalid pairs and manually edit answers for clarity and accuracy. The resulting 1,600 samples are evenly distributed (400 per level, 200 per domain). The empirical evaluation compares eight representative methods—vanilla RAG, HyDE, RQRAG, MemoRAG, GraphRAG, and three long-context LLM variants (Qwen2.5-7B, Lingua-2, MInference)—using BGE-M3 as retriever and Qwen2.5-7B-instruct as generator, analyzing performance across levels, domains, top-k settings, retrieval strategies, and latency.

## Results

On HawkBench (1,600 samples across 8 domains and 4 task levels), no single RAG method achieves consistent top performance across all levels. Enhanced RAG methods (HyDE, RQRAG) lead on factoid queries, with RQRAG scoring 73.6 F1 on Level-1 and 46.8 F1 on Level-2, while Global RAG methods (MemoRAG, GraphRAG) dominate rationale queries, with MemoRAG reaching 34.1 S-F1 on Level-3 and 35.0 S-F1 on Level-4. Long-context LLMs (Qwen2.5-7B) are competitive on Level-4 (33.2 S-F1) but score below 13 Rouge-L on Level-1, confirming that retrieval is essential for precise factoid extraction. Standard RAG (BGE-M3 + Qwen2.5-7B) achieves 57.5 F1 on Level-1 but drops to 18.3 S-F1 on Level-4, illustrating the resilience gap across task types.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 8 baselines spanning three categories: long-context LLMs, enhanced RAG, and global RAG. On Level-1 and Level-2 factoid queries, RQRAG (73.6 F1 / 46.8 F1) and HyDE (73.5 F1 / 44.5 F1) substantially outperform standard RAG (57.5 F1 / 38.6 F1) by 15–8 points, while Global RAG methods (MemoRAG, GraphRAG) underperform standard RAG, confirming that global awareness is counterproductive for precise factoid retrieval. On Level-3 and Level-4 rationale queries, the ranking inverts: MemoRAG (34.1 / 35.0 S-F1) and GraphRAG (32.5 / 28.7 S-F1) surpass both enhanced RAG (28.6 / 17.4 S-F1) and standard RAG (27.3 / 18.3 S-F1) by 5–17 points, demonstrating that structured global representations are critical for information aggregation. Long-context LLMs (Qwen2.5-7B) are competitive with MemoRAG on Level-4 (33.2 vs. 35.0 S-F1) but collapse on Level-1 (12.9 F1 vs. 73.6 for RQRAG), and efficiency methods (Lingua-2, MInference) yield only marginal gains over the vanilla LLM. The central finding is that no method is resilient across all four levels: the best Level-1 method (RQRAG) scores near the bottom on Level-4, and the best Level-4 method (MemoRAG) is weakest on Level-1, highlighting the absence of a unified RAG strategy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground in RAG evaluation by introducing a unified, stratified benchmark that departs from the siloed design of prior RAG benchmarks (LegalBench-RAG, MultiHop-RAG, CRAG, KILT), each of which targets a single task type or domain. The four-level query stratification (explicit/implicit × factoid/rationale) is inspired by the taxonomy in Zhao et al. [2024a] but refined into a concrete evaluation protocol with a shared knowledge base across all levels, enabling direct cross-task comparison. On the method side, the paper does not propose a new RAG architecture but systematically evaluates existing paradigms—vanilla RAG, query-enhancement methods (HyDE, RQRAG), global-aware methods (MemoRAG, GraphRAG), and long-context LLMs (Lingua-2, MInference)—under a common protocol, and introduces the S-F1 metric for sentence-level semantic evaluation of rationale answers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that no single RAG method is resilient across all four task levels, with standard RAG excelling at factoid queries while global RAG methods dominate rationale queries—provides concrete evidence that the field needs adaptive, task-aware RAG pipelines rather than one-size-fits-all architectures. The practical observation that GraphRAG's graph construction takes up to 30 minutes while only improving higher-level tasks raises important efficiency questions for real-time deployment. Open questions include how to build dynamic decision-making modules that route queries to appropriate retrieval strategies, how to accelerate global intermediate construction (graphs, memory stores), and whether agentic information-seeking systems can subsume the need for explicit task stratification. Limitations include the relatively small corpus (229 texts, 8 domains), exclusion of commercial RAG systems, and reliance on a single 7B generator model.
