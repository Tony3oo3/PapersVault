## Problem

GNNs process graphs through bottom-up message-passing, fundamentally misaligned with how humans perceive global graph structures through visual Gestalt principles. Existing benchmarks conflate domain-specific node features with topological understanding, making it impossible to isolate whether models genuinely comprehend graph structure or merely exploit feature shortcuts. This paper asks whether vision models, which naturally capture holistic patterns, can be leveraged for graph structural understanding and how they compare to GNNs on tasks requiring global topological awareness and scale-invariant reasoning.

## Input / Output

For vision models, graphs are rendered as 224×224 RGB images using one of three layout algorithms (Kamada-Kawai, Spectral, ForceAtlas2) with nodes drawn as skyblue circles and edges as white lines; no node features, adjacency matrix, or explicit connectivity information is provided to the model. For GNNs, input consists of the graph adjacency structure with one-hot degree encoding (max degree 100) augmented by 16-dimensional positional encoding vectors (LapPE, SignNet, or SPE). Outputs span four task types: 6-way topology classification, 2-way symmetry classification (based on whether |Aut(G)| > 1), spectral gap regression (predicting λ₂ of the normalized Laplacian), and bridge count regression, with evaluation at three scale shifts (ID: 20–50 nodes, Near-OOD: 40–100, Far-OOD: 60–150).

## Method

The core approach renders graph topology into the visual domain via standard layout algorithms and applies off-the-shelf vision encoders (ResNet-50, ViT-B/16, Swin-Tiny, ConvNeXtV2-Tiny) to graph-level tasks without any graph-specific architectural modifications, mirroring how humans perceive network structures visually rather than through explicit message-passing. To rigorously evaluate topological understanding independent of domain features, the authors introduce GraphAbstract, a benchmark with four tasks—high-level topology classification (six archetypes: cyclic, random geometric, hierarchical, community, bottleneck, multicore-periphery), symmetry classification (using Cayley graphs, bipartite double covers, Cartesian products, and cyclic covers for symmetric graphs; double-edge-swap perturbations and real-data Cartesian products for asymmetric graphs), spectral gap regression (SBM, geometric, and configuration model graphs), and bridge counting—each with systematically varied graph scales between training and testing. GNN baselines (GCN, GIN, GAT, GPS) are evaluated with both degree encoding and three positional encoding schemes to isolate the contribution of global structural priors. The key empirical finding is that vision models maintain near-constant accuracy across scale shifts (5–6% drop from ID to Far-OOD) while GNNs degrade by over 45%, and that positional encodings substantially outperform architectural innovations in message passing, pointing to a unified 'global-first' principle for graph understanding.

## Results

On the GraphAbstract benchmark, vision models demonstrate superior scale-invariant structural understanding: on topology classification, Swin drops only 5.7% accuracy from ID (94.80%) to Far-OOD (89.13%), while basic GCN+Degree suffers a catastrophic 47% drop (80.67% → 33.67%). On symmetry classification, vision models with spectral layouts achieve ~92-94% ID accuracy versus ~70-73% for the best GNN variants, a gap of over 20%. On the standard TUDataset benchmarks (Table 1), vision models are competitive with GNNs (e.g., ConvNeXtV2: 70.2% NCI1, 53.5% IMDB-MULTI, 41.7% ENZYMES) despite having no graph-specific inductive biases. For regression tasks, vision models achieve lower MAE on spectral gap (0.0312 for Swin vs. 0.0203 for GCN+SignNet on ID) and bridge counting (0.6526 for Swin vs. 0.5673 for GSN+Spe on ID), though GNNs with strong PEs remain competitive in-distribution.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On the standard TUDataset benchmarks (Table 1), vision models (ResNet, ViT, Swin, ConvNeXtV2) achieve performance comparable to GNNs (GCN, GIN, GAT, GPS) without any graph-specific architecture, e.g., ConvNeXtV2 reaches 41.7% on ENZYMES vs. GPS's 34.3%, and ViT achieves 83.1% on PROTEINS vs. GIN's 75.0%. On the GraphAbstract benchmark, the key finding is that vision models significantly outperform all GNN variants on out-of-distribution generalization: on topology Far-OOD, the best GNN (GAT+Net, 85.27%) is outperformed by Swin (89.13%) and ConvNeXtV2 (90.33%), while basic GNNs like GCN+Degree collapse to 33.67%. Positional encodings (LapPE, SignNet, SPE) substantially improve GNN performance over degree-only baselines (e.g., GCN+SignNet: 94.17% ID vs. GCN+Degree: 80.67% on topology), but even the best PE-enhanced GNNs (GAT+Net, GAT+Spe) still trail vision models on Far-OOD topology (85.27%/85.33% vs. 89.13%/90.33%) and symmetry (67.27%/67.47% vs. 84.20-85.16%). I²-GNN [32], an advanced structure-aware GNN, achieves competitive in-distribution performance but shows significantly worse OOD generalization compared to vision models. On regression tasks, GNNs with strong PEs (GAT+Net: 0.0030 MAE on spectral Near-OOD; GSN+Spe: 0.5673 on bridge ID) remain competitive or slightly better than vision models in-distribution, but vision models maintain more consistent performance across OOD shifts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant GNN message-passing paradigm and the extensive line of work attempting to fix its limitations (graph transformers, positional encodings like LapPE/SignNet/SPE, graph rewiring). It also distinguishes itself from recent efforts that incorporate visual layouts into existing architectures (GITA, DEL, GraphTMI), which treat layouts and vision models as auxiliary black boxes, by instead making the vision model the primary processing mechanism with no graph-specific architectural modifications. Conceptually, the paper repurposes the well-developed computer vision ecosystem (ResNet, ViT, Swin, ConvNeXtV2) for graph understanding, framing graph topology perception as a visual pattern recognition problem aligned with human Gestalt cognition rather than iterative local aggregation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that off-the-shelf vision models achieve competitive performance with superior scale-invariant generalization (5-6% vs. 45%+ accuracy drop from ID to Far-OOD) challenges the field's working assumption that graph-specific inductive biases are necessary for structural understanding, opening a path toward vision-centric graph foundation models. The GraphAbstract benchmark addresses a genuine gap by isolating topological comprehension from domain-specific features, enabling cleaner evaluation of structural reasoning. Key open questions include the 10× computational overhead, the substantial memorization-generalization gap in vision models, the lack of theoretical characterization of why certain layout algorithms benefit specific tasks, and whether these advantages persist on more complex, feature-rich real-world graphs.
