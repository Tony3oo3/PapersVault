## Problem

No prior work provided a sample complexity characterization for learning Nash equilibria from expert data in Markov Games. Tang et al. (2024) offered only an error propagation analysis without specifying how many expert samples suffice, and their algorithms (MALICE, BLADES) incur exponential computational cost in the number of states. This paper asks: what is the fundamental sample complexity of multi-agent imitation learning, and can it be achieved with polynomial-time algorithms?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253267/cropped_page5_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

In the non-interactive setting, the method consumes a dataset of N trajectories generated by the expert Nash equilibrium policy pair (μ^E, ν^E) in a two-player zero-sum Markov Game, optionally with known transition dynamics. In the interactive setting, the learner additionally queries the expert at states it visits during rollouts; MAIL-BRO further requires a best response oracle that returns a best-response policy to the current estimate, while MURMAIL replaces this with an inner UCBVI loop. The output in all cases is a policy pair (μ̂, ν̂) whose Nash-Gap is at most ε in expectation.

## Method

The paper first proves that behavioral cloning in Markov Games requires a novel all-policy deviation concentrability coefficient C_max, and constructs a hardness example showing that even with fully known transitions, no non-interactive algorithm can recover an equilibrium when this coefficient is unbounded. To bypass this limitation, MAIL-BRO uses a best response oracle to sample from the relevant best-response occupancy distributions, then applies exponential weights updates with stochastic unbiased gradient estimates derived from the performance difference lemma, achieving an ε-NE with Õ(ε^{-4}) expert queries. MURMAIL eliminates the oracle by introducing a maximum uncertainty response principle: at each outer iteration, each agent solves a single-agent MDP (via UCBVI) whose reward is the squared TV distance between the expert and current policy, directing exploration toward states of highest uncertainty. This yields an ε-NE with Õ(ε^{-8}) expert queries and no concentrability assumption, while remaining fully decentralized and polynomially computable.

## Results

The paper establishes the first expert sample complexity bounds for learning Nash equilibria in two-player zero-sum Markov Games. For non-interactive BC, the Nash-Gap is bounded by C_max · 8/(1−γ)² · √(|S||A_max| log²(2|S|/δ)/N), where C_max is the all-policy deviation concentrability coefficient. In the interactive setting, MAIL-BRO achieves an ε-NE with Õ(|S||A_max|²/((1−γ)¹²ε⁴)) expert and oracle queries, while MURMAIL (without a best-response oracle) requires Õ(|S|⁴|A_max|⁵/((1−γ)¹²ε⁸)) expert queries. A lower bound (Theorem 3.2) proves that when C(μ^E,ν^E)=∞, no non-interactive algorithm can achieve a Nash-Gap below (1−γ)⁻¹ even with infinite data and known transitions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is Tang et al. (2024), which introduced BC, MALICE, and BLADES for multi-agent imitation learning. Compared to Tang et al.'s BC, this paper's BC analysis removes the restrictive β > 0 assumption (requiring only C_max < ∞, which is strictly weaker) and provides an explicit sample complexity bound in N, whereas Tang et al. only gave an error propagation analysis without specifying the number of expert samples needed. Compared to MALICE and BLADES, both of which incur exponential computational cost exp(|S|) due to loops over all possible deviations, MAIL-BRO and MURMAIL achieve polynomial computational cost in all problem parameters. In the numerical validation (Fig. 2), BC completely fails (Nash-Gap remains constant) when C(μ^E,ν^E)=∞, while MURMAIL successfully drives the Nash-Gap to zero; however, in environments where C(μ^E,ν^E) is bounded, BC can be more sample-efficient (ε⁻² vs. ε⁻⁸ scaling). The key trade-off is that MAIL-BRO and MURMAIL eliminate dependence on the concentrability coefficient entirely at the cost of higher polynomial dependence on ε and the need for interactive expert queries.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the multi-agent imitation learning (MAIL) framework introduced by Tang et al. (NeurIPS 2024), who first studied Nash Gap minimization from expert data in Markov Games but provided only an error propagation analysis without sample complexity guarantees. The concentrability coefficient concept is borrowed from offline zero-sum Markov game learning (Cui and Du, 2022; Zhong et al., 2022), but the paper shows it plays a fundamentally different and more restrictive role in the imitation learning setting. The proposed algorithms (MAIL-BRO, MURMAIL) draw on no-regret online learning (exponential weights) and model-based RL (UCBVI), combining them in a novel 'maximum uncertainty response' principle that replaces the best response oracle with a decentralized single-agent exploration loop.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes the first sample complexity characterization for MAIL and proves a fundamental separation from single-agent imitation learning: even with full knowledge of transition dynamics, no non-interactive algorithm can recover an equilibrium when the concentrability coefficient is unbounded. This has practical implications for multi-agent systems (autonomous driving, traffic control) where expert data may not cover strategically critical state regions. Key open questions include whether the all-policy deviation concentrability coefficient C_max is truly the fundamental quantity (vs. the smaller expert-policy coefficient), whether BC is rate-optimal in ε, and how to extend the analysis beyond tabular games to deep function approximation settings.
