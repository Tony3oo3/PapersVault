## Problem

Most current parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods for MLLMs (e.g., LoRA and its variants) are directly borrowed from LLMs and use shared low-rank parameters across all modalities. The paper empirically shows that this shared design causes optimization to be dominated by text tokens, leaving non-text modalities underutilized. The authors argue that both unimodal adaptation (independent compression per modality) and cross-modal adaptation (explicit interaction between modalities) are essential but have been largely neglected in prior work.

## Input / Output

Non-text inputs (audio clips, video frames, images, or speech) are first encoded by modality-specific encoders (BEATs, CLIP-ViT/L-14, or Whisper) and projected into the text embedding space via a Q-Former (32 query tokens) followed by a two-layer MLP. The resulting multimodal token sequence is concatenated with text tokens and fed into a frozen LLM backbone (LLaMA2/3, Qwen2, Qwen2.5-VL). Outputs are task-dependent: free-form text answers for QA, event localization labels for AVE, and hallucination judgments for POPE, all generated autoregressively by the LLM.

## Method

MokA redefines the roles of LoRA's A and B matrices to accommodate multimodal structure. Matrix A is split into modality-specific sub-matrices (A^a, A^v, A^t), each independently compressing its modality's tokens into a shared low-rank space to prevent cross-modal interference. A task-centric cross-attention module is then applied in this low-rank space, where non-text tokens serve as queries and text tokens serve as keys and values, explicitly injecting task-relevant textual information into non-text representations while leaving text tokens unchanged. Finally, a single shared matrix B projects all enhanced unimodal representations back into the full-dimensional space for cross-modal alignment. This three-part design (unimodal A → cross-attention → shared B) ensures both unimodal and cross-modal adaptation with only a modest increase in trainable parameters (~1.33% vs. 1.27% for LoRA) and a small inference latency overhead (~7%).

## Results

On the audio-visual-text scenario (LLaMA2 backbone), MokA achieves 75.71% on MUSIC-AVQA and 74.68% on AVE, outperforming standard LoRA (73.41/69.84) by +2.30 and +4.84 points respectively. On visual-text benchmarks, MokA reaches 1025.86 on MME_percep, 52.74 on MMBench, 74.23 on POPE, and 40.45 on SEED-Bench (LLaMA2), surpassing LoRA by 117.34, 2.10, 3.95, and 0.74 points. On speech-text, MokA scores 38.44 on MMAU_mini-speech and 39.64 on AIR-Bench_speech-en, improving over LoRA by +8.11 and +7.89 points. The method consistently outperforms all baselines across LLaMA2, LLaMA3, Qwen2, and Qwen2.5-VL backbones.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MokA outperforms all five compared baselines across every scenario and LLM backbone tested. Against standard LoRA, MokA gains +2.30 on MUSIC-AVQA, +4.84 on AVE, +3.95 on POPE, and +7.89 on AIR-Bench (all LLaMA2). LoRAMoE (73.57/72.81) and DoRA (73.97/72.18) both underperform MokA on audio-visual-text, with DoRA showing the largest gap on AVE (−2.50). HydraLoRA (74.12/72.27) is the closest LoRA variant but still trails MokA by 1.59 and 2.41 points. Uni-modal LoRA from Phi-4-mini (74.37/71.44) improves over LoRA on MUSIC-AVQA but degrades on AVE, while MokA improves on both. Notably, the two custom baselines (Uni LoRA + MM LoRA + Gate) that also follow the multimodal-aware design achieve 74.94/73.56, confirming the importance of the design principle, yet MokA still leads by 0.77 and 1.12 points with fewer parameters (N A-matrices vs. N+1, and 1 B-matrix vs. 2).

## Relation to Prior Work

MokA extends the LoRA framework (Hu et al., ICLR 2022) by redefining the roles of the low-rank matrices A and B to accommodate multimodal structure: A becomes modality-specific to preserve unimodal information independently, while a cross-attention module explicitly enhances text–non-text interaction before a shared B projects into a unified space. This departs from prior LoRA variants for MLLMs (LoRAMoE, DoRA, HydraLoRA, Uni-modal LoRA), which the authors argue are 'directly borrowed from LLMs' and treat all modalities uniformly through shared parameters. The paper also introduces two additional baselines (Uni LoRA + MM LoRA, with and without gating) that share MokA's multimodal-aware philosophy but rely on more parameters and implicit rather than explicit cross-modal interaction, positioning MokA as a more parameter-efficient instantiation of the same principle.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies and empirically demonstrates a concrete failure mode of standard LoRA in multimodal settings—text tokens dominate the shared low-rank update, leaving non-text modalities underutilized—which provides a useful diagnostic for practitioners fine-tuning MLLMs. The consistent 1–4% gains across three modality combinations and four LLM backbones, with only a modest increase in trainable parameters (~1.33% vs. 1.27%) and inference latency (~7% overhead), make MokA a practical drop-in replacement. Open questions include whether the text-as-task-description assumption generalizes to settings where no single modality plays the 'query' role, whether the cross-attention design can be replaced by cheaper alternatives without performance loss, and how the method behaves when the visual connector (as in Qwen2.5-VL) is frozen rather than jointly trained.
