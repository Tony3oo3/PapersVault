## Problem

Spiking Neural Networks rely on rate coding where high-precision inference requires many time-steps, causing significant latency and energy cost on neuromorphic hardware. Existing post-training methods for reducing SNN latency (burst spikes, local firing-step increases) yield only modest gains, and most neuromorphic chips lack support for multi-bit neurons or dynamic time-steps, leaving a critical gap for hardware-friendly, training-free latency reduction.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pretrained SNN (converted from an ANN via calibration or directly trained) along with a small calibration set (2,000 samples for CIFAR-100, 20,000 for ImageNet) and produces a modified SNN where selected neurons are replaced by groups of binary IF neurons with optimized thresholds. For classification, RGB images (32×32 CIFAR-100, 224×224 ImageNet) map to class labels; for generation, noise vectors or noisy images map to 32×32 RGB images. The output remains strictly binary spikes ({0,1}), preserving compatibility with existing neuromorphic hardware without requiring multi-bit or multi-threshold neuron support.

## Method

Adaptive Fission is a two-stage post-training pipeline that selectively splits high-sensitivity neurons into groups with varying scales and weights, enabling neuron-specific precision allocation while maintaining binary spike compatibility. Stage 1 estimates per-neuron sensitivity via a first-order Taylor expansion combining residual membrane potentials and ANN-like gradient backpropagation, then ranks neurons and selects a top fraction (fission rate) for splitting. Stage 2 derives optimal thresholds for each fissioned group by solving a CDF-based optimality condition (Theorem 5.1) using Monte Carlo sampling and Newton's method, guaranteeing at least halving of quantization error per additional neuron. The resulting descending-threshold neuron groups are precompiled into parallel comparison logic, replacing sequential cascade dynamics with hardware-friendly binary indicator functions. This generalized population coding approach achieves exponential precision scaling with only moderate spatial overhead (~20% additional neurons for comparable capacity), and is compatible with both ANN-to-SNN conversions and directly trained SNNs without any weight updates or architectural modifications.

## Results

On CIFAR-100 with ResNet20, Adaptive Fission reduces inference from 32 to 4 time-steps (8×) while retaining 73.11% top-1 accuracy (vs. 76.32% at T=32), cutting on-chip energy from 0.64 Wh to 0.18 Wh (72% reduction) and latency from 56.8 s to 12.0 s per epoch (79% reduction). On ImageNet with VGG16, QCFS+Fission at T=4 achieves 72.15% accuracy with 0.18 Wh energy versus 0.70 Wh at T=32 (74% savings). For the Spike-driven Transformer on ImageNet, Fission maintains 85.3% accuracy at T=2 compared to 85.25% at T=4, demonstrating near-lossless compression even at extreme latency budgets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Calib conversion baseline [28], Fission recovers most of the accuracy lost by reducing time-steps: on CIFAR-100/ResNet20, Calib drops from 76.32% (T=32) to 20.43% (T=4), while Calib+Fission retains 73.11% at T=4. Similarly, QCFS [4] degrades from 76.78% to 55.25% at T=4, whereas QCFS+Fission achieves 72.15% at the same budget. For the SRP quantization method [17], Fission lifts T=4 accuracy from 59.34% to 65.39% on CIFAR-100. On the directly-trained TEBN model [10], Fission improves T=2 accuracy from 56.04% to 73.46% and enables T=1 inference at 69.20%. For the Spike-driven Transformer [49] on ImageNet, Fission maintains 85.3% at T=2 versus 85.25% at T=4, showing near-zero accuracy loss. In direct comparison with the Group Neuron population-coding method [30], Fission achieves 73.28% accuracy with only 78% additional neurons, surpassing Group Neuron's 72.85% at 400% overhead. Against Spatio-temporal Approximation [24], Fission reaches 68.22% at 100% neuron overhead versus 52.51% for Spatial alone, and 73.28% at 78% versus 66.43% at 600% for Spatial.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the population coding paradigm in SNNs from fixed-scale, uniform neuron groups [30, 45, 37] to an adaptive, neuron-specific splitting mechanism inspired by post-training quantization in ANNs (HAWQ [9], dynamic quantization [51]). Unlike prior post-training SNN methods that modify spike bits [42, 41] or locally increase firing steps [27], Adaptive Fission redistributes precision spatially by fissioning high-sensitivity neurons into variable-size groups with optimized thresholds, while remaining compatible with binary-only neuromorphic hardware. The theoretical analysis generalizes the uniform-distribution results of [30] to arbitrary residual potential distributions, providing a principled optimality condition (Theorem 5.1) that prior heuristic groupings lacked.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 80% reduction in latency and energy on a commercial neuromorphic chip (Lynxi HP201) without accuracy loss is practically significant for deploying SNNs on resource-constrained hardware, particularly for tasks like image generation where conventional SNNs require 64+ time-steps. The method's compatibility with both ANN-to-SNN conversion and directly trained SNNs, combined with its training-free nature, makes it broadly applicable to proprietary models where retraining is infeasible. Open questions include scalability to very large models (LLMs, where attention precision demands and error accumulation are flagged), applicability to event-driven datasets, and whether the spatial overhead becomes prohibitive at scale. The spatial-temporal trade-off—trading neurons for time-steps—remains a fundamental constraint that limits how far latency can be pushed.
