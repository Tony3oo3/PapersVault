## Problem

Time series domain adaptation is fundamentally constrained by the sensor-limited single view: discrete observations at a fixed sampling rate and record duration only partially reflect the underlying continuous-time dynamics, hindering the modeling of intricate temporal patterns and domain-invariant representation learning. Existing methods either extract temporal features from raw data alone or use fixed temporal-frequency integration paradigms, failing to exploit the richer semantic information available across multiple explicit domains derived from the same underlying process.

## Input / Output

The method consumes discrete multivariate time series (C variates × T time steps) from a labeled source domain and an unlabeled target domain. Each raw sample is expanded into multiple explicit domains: K segments of varying length (multi-segment), each further split into fine-scale (original) and coarse-scale (downsampled by factor M) versions (multi-scale), and transformed into the frequency subspace via FFT (multi-subspace). The output is a single class label per raw sample, obtained by ensembling predictions over the two test-time segments.

## Method

EDEN integrates three types of expanded explicit domains through coordinated modules. Multi-Scale Curriculum Adaptation progressively shifts the feature mixing weight λ from coarse-scale to fine-scale over training iterations, stabilizing global domain alignment before refining local discriminative details. Quality-Aware Feature Fusion introduces per-subspace scorers that measure transferability (A-distance proxy via a domain classifier), discriminability (entropy and confidence of a 1-layer MLP), and diversity (coefficient-of-variation perturbation) to adaptively weight temporal and frequency features per instance. Temporal Coherence Learning enforces consistency between nearby segments at both the logit level (KL divergence) and the score level (absolute difference of QFusion scores). The overall training objective combines supervised cross-entropy, adversarial domain alignment via a GRL-equipped discriminator, scorer auxiliary losses, and the coherence regularizer, with a 1D-CNN for temporal features and a complex-valued linear layer for frequency features.

## Results

EDEN achieves an average accuracy of 87.72% across six time series benchmarks (UCIHAR, HHAR-P, WISDM, HHAR-D, FD, EMG), each evaluated over 10 source-target domain pairs. The method outperforms the strongest baseline ACON by 1.08% on UCIHAR, 5.62% on HHAR-P, 2.78% on WISDM, 6.31% on FD, 5.60% on HHAR-D, and 1.70% on EMG, yielding an average improvement of 4.80% over state-of-the-art methods. EDEN also reduces variance (error bars) across repeated runs, indicating more reliable cross-domain generalization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

EDEN outperforms all 10 baselines on all 6 datasets. Among general UDA methods, CDAN (74.99% avg) and DIRT-T (73.56%) are the strongest, yet EDEN surpasses them by over 12 percentage points on average. Among time-series-specific UDA methods, ACON (83.88% avg, same authors, NeurIPS 2024) is the closest competitor, with EDEN gaining 3.84 points on average and up to 6.31 points on FD. RAINCOAT (76.83%), which introduces frequency features via Sinkhorn divergence, is outperformed by EDEN by 10.89 points on average, with particularly large gaps on UCIHAR (+3.67) and HHAR-D (+21.57). CLUDA (72.73%), AdvSKM (71.94%), and CoDATS (73.17%) represent earlier temporal-only or contrastive approaches that EDEN surpasses by 14–16 points. The consistent margin over ACON across all datasets (ranging from +1.08 to +6.31) suggests that the multi-scale curriculum, quality-aware fusion, and temporal coherence modules collectively address limitations of fixed temporal-frequency integration.

## Relation to Prior Work

EDEN extends the adversarial domain adaptation framework (DANN-style gradient reversal) into the time series setting, building directly on the authors' prior work ACON (NeurIPS 2024) which introduced temporal-frequency subspace alignment for TSDA. The key conceptual departure is reframing the raw time series as a 'sensor-limited single view' of an underlying continuous-time process, then expanding it into multiple explicit domains (multi-scale via downsampling, multi-subspace via FFT, multi-segment via cropping) to better approximate implicit temporal dynamics. This departs from prior TSDA methods (RAINCOAT, ACON, CLUDA) that operate on a fixed single representation, and from general UDA methods (CDAN, DIRT-T, AdaMatch) that ignore time-series-specific structure. The domain adaptation theory of Ben-David et al. (A-distance, HΔH-bound) underpins both the curriculum scheduling rationale and the transferability criterion in QFusion.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The multi-explicit-domain framing offers a principled lens for understanding why single-view time series representations struggle with domain shift: sensor constraints (sampling rate, record duration) create an information bottleneck that entangles domain-specific artifacts with class-relevant temporal patterns. The practical impact is a consistent 4.8% average accuracy gain across six sensor-based benchmarks, with particularly large improvements on harder domains (HHAR-D: +8.61%, FD: +6.88%). Limitations include evaluation restricted to sensor-based time series (human activity, EMG, machine fault), reliance on 1D-CNN backbones, and the assumption that nearby segments share class semantics (which may not hold for non-stationary or multi-activity sequences). Open questions include whether the multi-domain expansion generalizes to non-sensor time series (financial, clinical) and whether the curriculum schedule can be made adaptive per-domain rather than fixed by training progress.
