## Problem

As protein and genome language models scale to billions of parameters, their sequence likelihoods increasingly capture phylogenetic structure and sampling biases alongside true fitness constraints, causing variant effect prediction performance to plateau or regress rather than improve. This widening gap between model likelihood and biological fitness limits the utility of the largest sequence models for zero-shot variant impact estimation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254359/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Given a pre-trained sequence language model (ESM-2, ProGen2, or Evo 2), a reference sequence with one or more point mutations, and a set of homologous sequences obtained via MMseqs/UniRef50 (proteins, filtered at 30% minimum identity) or Zoonomia 447-way mammalian alignment (genomes), the method produces a single scalar fitness score per variant. For masked models the score is computed via unmasked-marginal log-likelihood differences in a single forward pass per homolog; for autoregressive models it uses exact sequence log-likelihoods ensembled over forward and reverse (or reverse-complement) directions. The final output is the mean log-likelihood difference across all homologs, yielding a lower-variance Δf estimate.

## Method

Likelihood-Fitness Bridging (LFB) is a post-hoc inference strategy that injects the same substitution into multiple homologous sequences and averages the resulting log-likelihood differences, reducing noise from phylogenetic drift without any model retraining. The theoretical justification formalizes evolution under an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, showing that a sufficiently expressive model's likelihood around sequence x_i is centered at μ+ε_i (where ε_i is the drift offset) rather than the true fitness optimum μ, making the single-sequence score a noisy fitness estimate. Averaging across n homologs reduces variance by a factor of (1/n + (n-1)/n·ρ), where ρ is the average pairwise correlation among homologs, so phylogenetically dispersed sequences yield greater gains. In practice, LFB requires only ~10× the runtime of a single forward pass (as few as 10 homologs retain most of the benefit) and reverses the scaling plateau: the largest ESM-2 (15B) and ProGen2 (6.4B) models become the best-performing when combined with LFB, while smaller models also gain substantially.

## Results

On the ProteinGym DMS benchmark (mean Spearman correlation), LFB improves the best ESM-2 model from 0.417 (650M baseline) to 0.436 (15B + LFB), and the best ProGen2 model from 0.389 (6.4B baseline) to 0.433 (6.4B + LFB). On clinical variant classification (mean AUC across 305 genes), ESM-2 15B + LFB reaches 0.938 AUC versus 0.895 baseline, and ProGen2 6.4B + LFB reaches 0.930 versus 0.869 baseline. Critically, LFB reverses the performance plateau: the largest models in both ESM-2 and ProGen2 families become the best-performing when combined with LFB, whereas without LFB the largest models underperform smaller ones.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the ESM-2 family, LFB enables the 15B model to achieve 0.436 mean Spearman on DMS, surpassing the 650M baseline (0.417) by 5.3% and the 3B baseline (0.404) by 8.0%; on clinical AUC, ESM-2 15B + LFB (0.938) matches the 3B + LFB (0.938) and exceeds the 650M baseline (0.895) by 4.3 points. Against the ProGen2 family, LFB lifts the 6.4B model to 0.433 DMS Spearman, outperforming the 2.7B baseline (0.380) by 14.0% and the 764M baseline (0.379) by 14.3%, making it the best model in the family; on clinical AUC, ProGen2 6.4B + LFB (0.930) exceeds the 764M baseline (0.894) by 3.6 points. Against the Evo 2 genomic language model family, LFB provides more modest gains (e.g., Evo 2 base 1B: 0.868 → 0.878 AUC) and does not reverse the scaling trend, with the smaller 1B model remaining the strongest even after LFB. Overall, LFB wins on every model size tested in both pLM families, with the largest relative gains observed for the largest models, confirming that the method specifically addresses the phylogenetic-structure confound that limits large-model fitness prediction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck tree (OUT) evolutionary model from Weinstein et al. (NeurIPS 2022), which formalized the non-identifiability between fitness and phylogenetic structure in sequence distributions. It positions LFB as a third strategy—modifying inference—distinct from the two previously explored approaches of modifying training data (Ding & Steinhardt 2024) or modifying the model architecture (e.g., joint fitness-phylogeny modelling). Conceptually, LFB draws an analogy to test-time augmentation in computer vision but departs from it by explicitly targeting fitness estimation rather than likelihood estimation, and it leverages the OUT model to show that averaging over homologous sequences reduces variance in the fitness estimate by marginalizing drift-induced noise.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The reversal of the performance plateau in large protein language models (ESM-2 15B, ProGen2 6.4B) is practically significant: it implies that further scaling of sequence models will yield better variant effect predictors when paired with LFB, unblocking a previously stalled scaling trend. The method requires no retraining and only ~10× forward-pass overhead, making it immediately deployable on existing models. Key open questions include extension to non-coding genomic regions, handling of indels, explicit incorporation of phylogenetic tree structure into the averaging, and whether the gLM scaling behavior (where LFB helps but does not reverse the plateau) reflects a fundamentally different source of the likelihood-fitness gap.
