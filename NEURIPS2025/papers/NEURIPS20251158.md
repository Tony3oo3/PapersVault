## Problem

Existing embodied AI simulators and benchmarks are designed for household or general-purpose environments and cannot model the chemical dynamics, diverse instrument configurations, and long-horizon experimental workflows that define laboratory settings. This gap has hindered the development of scientific embodied agents that must perceive physical-chemical transformations, plan multi-step protocols, and operate heterogeneous lab equipment. LabUtopia addresses this by providing a unified simulation, scene-generation, and hierarchical benchmarking suite purpose-built for laboratory robotics.

## Input / Output

Manipulation policies consume 256×256 RGB frames from two or three cameras (wrist, front-facing, overhead) concatenated with absolute robot joint positions, and output predicted joint positions for the next timestep (action chunks of length 60 for ACT/DP, 8 for π₀). The simulation engine additionally accepts chemical substance specifications (reactant names, solvent, temperature, concentration) and produces updated physical states including color changes, product formation, and fluid dynamics. Evaluation is reported as binary task success rates, where success requires the goal state to persist for 2 seconds after the final action.

## Method

LabUtopia integrates three components: (1) LabSim, a high-fidelity simulator on Isaac Sim that extends rigid/deformable/fluid physics (GPU-accelerated PBD) with a chemical engine coupling a 200-compound PubChem-derived knowledge base to GPT-4o-based reaction inference, enabling visually grounded simulation of color changes and product generation; (2) LabScene, a procedural generation pipeline that places ~100 expert-verified lab scenes and 100+ instrument assets using a hybrid strategy of grid stochastic sampling with constraint-aware depth-first search fallback; (3) LabBench, a five-level hierarchical benchmark spanning atomic manipulation, short-horizon sequences, generalization under distributional shift, long-horizon multi-step protocols, and mobile manipulation with navigation. Demonstration trajectories are auto-collected via RMPflow-based atomic action controllers organized by finite-state-machine task-level planners (manipulation) and A*-on-occupancy-grid path planning (navigation), enabling scalable imitation learning data for ACT, Diffusion Policy, and π₀ baselines.

## Results

On Level-1 atomic manipulation tasks, both ACT and Diffusion Policy (DP) achieve high success rates (73–100%), with DP leading on Close Drawer (100%) and Press (96.7%). Performance drops notably at Level-2, where DP struggles severely (e.g., Operate Drawer: 10.0% vs. ACT's 73.3%; Stir w/ GlassRod: 10.0% vs. 55.0%). At Level-3 generalization, ACT maintains strong ID/OOD stability (e.g., Heater Beaker: 86.7/80.0%) while DP collapses on OOD (21.6/8.3%), and the VLA model π₀ shows minimal OOD degradation but does not consistently surpass from-scratch models. On Level-4 long-horizon tasks, both models suffer sharp cumulative-error declines, with ACT achieving 14.0% single-stage success on Clean Beaker versus DP's 2.5%, and both dropping below 2% by the final sub-step (A7).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks three existing imitation-learning policies—ACT, Diffusion Policy (DP), and the VLA model π₀—on the new LabBench suite. ACT consistently outperforms DP on Level-2 composed tasks (e.g., Operate Drawer 73.3% vs. 10.0%; Heater Beaker 86.7% vs. 25.0%) and Level-3 generalization tasks (e.g., Heater Beaker OOD 80.0% vs. 8.3%; Transport Beaker OOD 73.3% vs. 15.0%), while DP edges out ACT on a few Level-1 primitives (Close Drawer 100% vs. 96.7%). The VLA model π₀, after fine-tuning, exhibits the smallest ID-to-OOD drop (e.g., Pick 83.3→85.8; Press 92.5→89.1) yet still trails ACT on several tasks (Open Door 51.6 vs. 73.3 ID; Pour Liquid 40.0 vs. 75.0 ID). On Level-4 long-horizon tasks, ACT dominates DP across all sub-steps (Clean Beaker SP: 14.0% vs. 2.5%; Liquid Fusion SP: 8.3% vs. 6.7%), though both collapse below 2% by the seventh sub-step due to cumulative error. Overall, no single method generalizes well across all five levels, highlighting the benchmark's difficulty and the gap toward general-purpose scientific embodied agents.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Isaac Sim robotics simulation platform into a laboratory-specific environment by adding a chemical process engine (combining a PubChem-derived substance database with GPT-4o-based reaction reasoning) and a procedural scene generator for lab layouts, neither of which exists in prior embodied AI simulators. It departs from the household-centric focus of benchmarks like ALFRED, Behavior, ManiSkill3, and ClevrSkills by introducing domain-specific assets (100+ lab scenes, 100+ instruments), chemically meaningful interactions (color changes, product formation), and a five-level hierarchical task taxonomy spanning atomic manipulation to mobile manipulation. For trajectory collection, it repurposes established techniques—RMPflow for manipulation, A* with occupancy grids for navigation, and Slerp for articulated objects—into an automated demonstration pipeline tailored to lab protocols.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LabUtopia fills a clear gap: no prior simulator or benchmark supports chemical reaction modeling alongside physical dynamics in a laboratory context, making it the first comprehensive testbed for scientific embodied agents. The key open question is sim-to-real transfer—the entire benchmark operates in simulation, and the authors acknowledge that bridging to physical labs is future work. The finding that even the VLA model π₀ does not consistently outperform simpler from-scratch policies (ACT, DP) on these lab tasks, and that shape generalization collapses to near-zero success, suggests that current imitation learning approaches are far from ready for the heterogeneous, long-horizon demands of real laboratory automation. Limitations include support for only two robot embodiments and a relatively small set of chemical reactions, constraining the breadth of scenarios that can be explored.
