## Problem

Machine unlearning has been widely invoked as a general-purpose solution for diverse policy goals—privacy (GDPR right to be forgotten), copyright compliance, and safety—but the technical literature conflates two fundamentally different operations: targeted removal of training data from model parameters and targeted suppression of content in model outputs. This paper identifies that these confluations produce fundamental conceptual mismatches between what unlearning methods can technically achieve and what specific legal and policy regimes actually require, making unlearning far less capable of delivering full compliance than commonly assumed.

## Input / Output

The paper's analytical subject spans two categories of unlearning methods—removal methods (retraining from scratch, structural/exact unlearning, approximate/inexact unlearning) and suppression methods (model editing, output filters, system-level guardrails)—applied across policy domains including GDPR Article 17, U.S. copyright law, and AI safety. The output is a framework of five formalized mismatches between unlearning motivations and methods, instantiated concretely in U.S. copyright (substantial similarity, fair use, causation) with analogous analyses for privacy and safety in appendices, plus actionable recommendations for ML researchers and policymakers.

## Method

The paper constructs a conceptual taxonomy distinguishing removal (acting on training data before or during training to eliminate the influence of specific examples on model parameters) from suppression (acting on a trained model or its surrounding system to reduce the likelihood of undesirable outputs). It then formalizes five mismatches: (1) suppression does not replace removal when legislation requires explicit data deletion; (2) removal does not guarantee output suppression because models generalize from latent information in remaining data; (3) models are not equivalent to their outputs, as in-context information can reintroduce 'unlearned' knowledge; (4) models are not equivalent to downstream use of outputs; and (5) unlearning produces unintended side effects on unrelated capabilities. The framework is then applied to U.S. copyright, showing that no unlearning method can distinguish transformative fair uses from infringing copies, that the 'gold standard' of retraining is infeasible at scale and still cannot prevent substantially similar outputs (illustrated with CommonCanvas generating Mickey Mouse from licensed personal photographs), and that counterfactual causation arguments based on retrained models are undermined by training non-determinism.

## Results

This is a position paper that does not report quantitative experimental results or benchmark comparisons. Its central contribution is a conceptual framework identifying five fundamental mismatches between machine-unlearning motivations and feasible technical implementations: (1) output suppression ≠ removal of training data, (2) removal of training data does not guarantee output suppression, (3) models are not equivalent to their outputs, (4) models are not equivalent to how outputs are used downstream, and (5) unlearning can have unintended consequences on unrelated content. The closest empirical illustration is the CommonCanvas example (Figure 1), showing that a text-to-image model trained exclusively on Creative-Commons-licensed images (with no in-copyright Mickey Mouse images) can still generate outputs substantially similar to 'Mickey Mouse' from licensed personal photographs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare a proposed method against baselines; it is a position/analysis paper rather than a method paper. Instead of benchmarking, the authors contrast two broad categories of unlearning techniques—removal (retraining from scratch, structural removal, approximate removal) and suppression (model modification, system-level output filters)—and argue that conflating them leads to incorrect policy expectations. The CommonCanvas model [60] is used as a concrete illustration (not a baseline comparison) to demonstrate that even a 'gold-standard' retraining without in-copyright training data does not prevent generation of substantially similar outputs. The WMDP benchmark [91] is referenced as a common evaluation tool for safety-related unlearning, but the paper does not report new WMDP scores. The authors' key finding is qualitative: no existing unlearning method can simultaneously satisfy the legal requirements of targeted data removal and guaranteed output suppression, and policymakers should set expectations around 'reasonable best efforts' rather than perfect compliance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes the machine unlearning literature (from Cao & Yang's 2015 'forgetting' work through recent generative-AI unlearning methods like TOFU, ROME, and WMDP-based evaluations) with U.S. copyright law, GDPR analysis, and the broader 'generative technology' literature (Felten's 'Fallacy of the Almost-General-Purpose Computer,' Zittrain's work on the PC and Internet). Rather than proposing a new unlearning method, it reframes the field by arguing that the community has conflated two fundamentally different goals—removal of training data from model parameters and suppression of content in model outputs—and that this conflation produces unrealistic policy expectations. The paper departs from the technical ML literature's tendency to treat unlearning as a single unified problem, instead insisting that removal and suppression require different methods, different evaluations, and different legal treatments.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's five 'mismatches' (removal ≠ suppression, models ≠ outputs, outputs ≠ downstream use, unintended consequences) provide a conceptual framework that directly challenges the widespread assumption in both research and policy that unlearning can serve as a general-purpose compliance tool for generative AI. For policymakers and courts, it argues that 'reasonable best efforts' standards must replace expectations of perfect compliance, and that domain-specific evaluation (e.g., U.S. copyright's 'substantial similarity' test) is essential rather than generalizable. Open questions include: what concrete thresholds should constitute 'reasonable best efforts' in different legal regimes, how should open-weight models (which cannot implement system-level guardrails) be treated differently, and whether the field should pivot research emphasis from parameter-level removal toward output-level suppression, which is what stakeholders actually care about.
