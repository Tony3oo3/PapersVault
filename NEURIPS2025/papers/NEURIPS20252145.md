## Problem

Prior work established that the implicit bias of gradient flow over overparameterized SSMs leads to generalization when data is generated by a low-dimensional teacher, but this result relied on an unrealistic assumption of infinitely many training sequences. This paper revisits the setting with finite training data and formally proves a phenomenon undetected by all prior work: certain cleanly-labeled training examples can completely distort the implicit bias, causing generalization to fail entirely. This constitutes the first formal proof of SSM susceptibility to clean-label poisoning.

## Input / Output

The SSM consumes a scalar sequence of length κ and produces a single scalar output equal to the last element of the internal state trajectory. In the theoretical teacher-student setting, the teacher SSM (dimension d* = 2) assigns ground-truth labels to input sequences, and the student SSM (dimension d > κ) is trained to match these labels. In real-world experiments, CIFAR-10 images are flattened into sequential inputs for SSM-based networks (S4, Mamba-2, LRU), which output 10-class predictions. The critical input detail is that 'poisonous' sequences are those whose last elements are relatively large, and they carry clean labels from the teacher.

## Method

The paper derives a dynamical characterization (Proposition 1) showing that during gradient flow, each diagonal entry of the state transition matrix A evolves according to a degree-(κ−2) polynomial whose constant coefficient depends on the last elements of training sequences. When these last elements are small, the dynamics exhibit greedy low-rank learning—a sufficient condition for generalization with a low-dimensional teacher. The key poisoning result (Theorem 1) then shows that adding a single cleanly-labeled sequence with large last elements (e.g., e_{κ−1}) to the training set introduces a non-negligible constant term that disrupts greedy learning. The proof employs a non-resonance linearization theorem from dynamical systems theory to establish that gradient flow trajectories near saddle points escape sufficiently fast, ensuring convergence to non-generalizing solutions. Empirically, the authors validate both the dynamical characterization and the poisoning phenomenon across synthetic teacher-student settings and real-world SSM-based networks on CIFAR-10, where poisonous examples are generated via an adapted Gradient Matching method that amplifies the tail of input sequences while preserving labels.

## Results

In the synthetic teacher-student settings (Table 1), adding a single cleanly labeled poisonous sequence increases generalization error by 1–2 orders of magnitude: from 1.27×10⁻³ to 5.01×10⁻² (Theorem 1 setting), from 0.194 to 16.62 (standalone SSM beyond Theorem 1), and from 1.64×10⁻³ to 5.45×10⁻² (SSM in a non-linear network). In real-world CIFAR-10 experiments (Table 2), adding cleanly labeled poisonous examples degrades test accuracy by 1.9–4.1 percentage points across S4 (78.8%→76.9%), Mamba-2 (77.8%→75.1%), and LRU (72.4%→68.3%), while increasing cross-entropy loss by 0.10–0.22.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external methods or baselines; instead, it demonstrates a vulnerability by comparing the same SSM architecture trained with and without cleanly labeled poisonous examples. Across all three synthetic settings in Table 1, the addition of a single special sequence (with a clean label from the teacher) causes generalization error to increase by a factor of 40× to 85×, confirming the theoretical prediction of Theorem 1. In the real-world CIFAR-10 experiments (Table 2), the same poisoning strategy—adding human-imperceptible noise with large tail elements to training inputs while preserving labels—degrades accuracy by 1.9 pp (S4), 2.7 pp (Mamba-2), and 4.1 pp (LRU), with LRU being most susceptible. The paper positions its contribution as the first formal proof of SSM susceptibility to clean-label poisoning, extending the infinite-training-data assumption of Cohen-Karlik et al. [12] to the realistic finite-data regime where poisoning becomes possible.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper revisits the teacher-student implicit bias framework for SSMs established by Cohen-Karlik et al. (ICLR 2023), which proved generalization under an idealized assumption of infinitely many training sequences. By replacing that assumption with a realistic finite training set, the authors derive a dynamical characterization (Proposition 1) showing that greedy low-rank learning—a sufficient condition for generalization—is induced only under certain training sequence configurations. The clean-label poisoning result (Theorem 1) connects the SSM implicit bias literature to the adversarial ML poisoning literature (Shafahi et al., Geiping et al.), but is the first formal proof that SSMs, with their non-convex optimization landscape, are susceptible to this attack. The proof technique draws on a non-resonance linearization theorem from dynamical systems theory (Sell, 1985) to handle trajectory tracking near saddle points.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Given the rapid adoption of SSMs (Mamba, S4, LRU) in large language models and their reliance on fine-tuning over public data, identifying a vulnerability where a single cleanly-labeled example can flip generalization from success to failure is practically significant. The result also challenges two dominant theoretical narratives for SSM generalization—implicit complexity minimization and typicality—since both would predict that adding clean examples can only help, not hurt. Key limitations include the highly specific theoretical setting (fixed B and C, near-zero initialization, restricted sequence lengths) and the moderate empirical effect sizes (2–4% accuracy drops on CIFAR-10). Open questions include whether the phenomenon persists under modern SSM initializations designed to avoid vanishing gradients, and whether monitoring the dynamical factor γ⁽⁰⁾(t) can serve as a practical defense.
