## Problem

Existing graph classification backdoor attacks introduce two detectable anomalies: structural deviation from rare subgraph triggers and semantic deviation from label flipping, making poisoned graphs easily distinguishable by anomaly detection models. The paper asks whether a graph-level backdoor can simultaneously preserve the clean data distribution, avoid label manipulation, and maintain high attack success rate.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252196/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a clean graph classification dataset (e.g., TUDataset benchmarks with 600–4337 graphs), a target class label, and a 5% poisoning budget under a clean-label constraint (no label modification). It produces a poisoned training set where trigger subgraphs of fixed size 4 are injected into selected target-class graphs, along with learned topology and feature generator parameters. At inference, the attacker crafts adaptive triggers per test graph to activate the backdoor, and the backdoored GNN (GCN, GIN, or SAGPool) outputs the attacker-specified target class for any triggered graph while maintaining normal accuracy on clean inputs.

## Method

DPSBA operates in two stages. First, it constructs poisoned samples by selecting low-confidence target-class graphs (hard samples), identifying injection sites via degree-centrality pre-filtering followed by surrogate-based node ablation scoring, and generating triggers with a topology generator (MLP on the local adjacency matrix with straight-through binarization) and a feature generator (MLP on local node features). Second, it jointly optimizes both generators through staged adversarial training: a surrogate classifier provides the attack gradient (cross-entropy toward the target class), while a GCN-based topology discriminator and an MLP-based feature discriminator penalize structural and attribute-level anomalies via minimax objectives. The key distinction from prior work is that triggers are learned in-distribution from target-class data rather than hand-designed or sampled from rare motifs, and the adversarial anomaly loss directly minimizes the total variation distance between clean and poisoned distributions, for which the paper derives a formal lower bound linking TV distance to the optimal anomaly detection AUC.

## Results

DPSBA achieves the highest or near-highest Attack Success Rate (ASR) across all four datasets and three surrogate models while simultaneously attaining the lowest anomaly detection AUC. For example, on AIDS/GCN, DPSBA reaches 94.76% ASR with only 72.65% AUC, compared to Motif's 92.69% ASR at 99.71% AUC and GTA's 93.21% ASR at 99.34% AUC. On ENZYMES/GIN, DPSBA achieves 99.33% ASR with 41.20% AUC (below random), while all baselines score 62–76% AUC. Clean Accuracy Drop (CAD) remains below 5% in nearly all settings, confirming minimal utility degradation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DPSBA dominates ER-B and LIA on both ASR and AUC across all four datasets; for instance, on ENZYMES/GCN, DPSBA reaches 96.67% ASR versus ER-B's 26.09% and LIA's 30.43%, while also achieving the lowest AUC (66.11%). Against GTA, DPSBA matches or slightly exceeds ASR (e.g., 94.76% vs. 93.21% on AIDS/GCN) but reduces AUC by 26–31 percentage points (72.65% vs. 99.34%), demonstrating that GTA's high attack success comes at the cost of near-perfect detectability. Compared to Motif, DPSBA achieves comparable or higher ASR while cutting AUC by roughly 20–33 points (e.g., 96.67% ASR at 66.11% AUC vs. Motif's 21.74% ASR at 71.35% AUC on ENZYMES/GCN). Versus the stealthier Motif-S variant, DPSBA recovers the lost attack effectiveness (e.g., 94.76% vs. 56.08% ASR on AIDS/GCN) while maintaining a lower or comparable AUC (72.65% vs. 89.43%). The only case where a baseline slightly edges DPSBA on ASR is GTA on FRANKENSTEIN/GCN (99.35% vs. 98.37%), but GTA's AUC is 100% there, making it trivially detectable.

## Relation to Prior Work

DPSBA builds directly on GTA's (Xi et al., USENIX Security 2021) topology and feature generator architecture for subgraph trigger construction, but departs from it by replacing GTA's bilevel optimization with a staged adversarial training loop that jointly optimizes attack effectiveness against anomaly-aware discriminators. It extends the distribution-preserving perspective introduced by Zhang et al. (KDD 2024) from node-level backdoor attacks to the more challenging graph-level setting, where global message passing amplifies distributional shift. The clean-label hard-sample selection strategy is adapted from Gao et al. (2023), and the overall framework positions itself against prior graph classification backdoor methods (ER-B, LIA, Motif) that rely on structurally rare or manually designed triggers, which the paper shows are inherently detectable via anomaly scoring.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper formalizes why graph-level backdoor triggers are inherently more detectable than node-level ones (via TV distance lower bounds) and demonstrates that adversarial training against anomaly detectors can close much of the detectability gap while preserving high attack success rates. However, the clean-label constraint limits applicability to imbalanced or data-scarce settings, and the partial-data-access assumption excludes fully black-box or test-time attack scenarios. Open questions include whether distribution-preserving triggers can be generated without any training data access, how the approach generalizes to heterophilic or dynamic graphs, and whether theoretically grounded defenses can detect triggers that are statistically indistinguishable from clean data.
