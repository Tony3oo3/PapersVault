## Problem

Despite widespread adoption of multi-agent LLM systems (MAS), their performance gains over single-agent baselines remain minimal, with failure rates of 41–87% across seven state-of-the-art frameworks. No principled, empirically grounded taxonomy or large-scale annotated dataset existed to systematically characterize why MAS fail, making it impossible to diagnose root causes or guide targeted improvements.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254082/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The annotation pipeline consumes full multi-agent execution traces (averaging 15,000+ lines of inter-agent conversation text) along with the 14 MAST failure-mode definitions and few-shot human-annotated examples, producing a structured multi-label output: which of the 14 failure modes are present, their category assignment (system design, inter-agent misalignment, or task verification), a binary task-completion flag, and a free-text justification per detected failure. The dataset spans 1,642 traces across 7 MAS frameworks and 4 model families, with a smaller 21-trace human-annotated subset (MAST-Data-human) providing ground-truth labels for validation.

## Method

The paper builds MAST, a 14-mode failure taxonomy in 3 categories, through a two-stage empirical process. First, six experts apply Grounded Theory (open coding, constant comparative analysis, memoing, theorizing) to 150+ traces from five MAS frameworks until theoretical saturation, then iteratively refine definitions through three rounds of inter-annotator agreement studies (3 annotators × 5 traces per round) reaching κ=0.88. Second, an LLM-as-a-Judge pipeline using OpenAI's o1 model with few-shot examples from the human-annotated subset is calibrated to achieve κ=0.77 agreement with human experts, enabling scalable annotation of the full 1,642-trace MAST-Data. The key design insight is that MAST focuses on failure patterns addressable through system architecture, agent coordination, and verification improvements—rather than attributing all failures to base-model limitations—and the taxonomy is validated for generalizability on two unseen MAS (OpenManus, Magentic-One) and benchmarks (MMLU, GAIA) with κ=0.79.

## Results

Across 7 state-of-the-art open-source MAS frameworks, the paper reports failure rates ranging from 41% to 86.7% on diverse benchmarks. The MAST taxonomy achieves strong inter-annotator agreement (Cohen's κ = 0.88) across three expert annotators over three IAA rounds, and generalizes to unseen systems with κ = 0.79. The LLM-as-a-Judge annotator (o1 with few-shot) reaches 94% accuracy and κ = 0.77 against human experts. Targeted MAST-guided interventions yield a +9.4% success rate improvement (role specification fix in ChatDev) and +15.6% (adding high-level task verification), demonstrating that system design changes—not just model upgrades—can meaningfully reduce failures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a dataset and taxonomy paper rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so no external baselines are benchmarked. Internally, the paper compares failure profiles across LLMs and MAS architectures: within MetaGPT on ProgramDev-v2, GPT-4o exhibits 39% fewer FC1 (System Design Issues) failures than Claude-3.7-Sonnet, while both models show high FC3 (Task Verification) failure counts. Comparing architectures on the same benchmark, MetaGPT achieves 60–68% fewer FC1 and FC2 failures than ChatDev, but suffers 1.56× more FC3 failures, revealing a trade-off between coordination robustness and verification quality. The LLM annotator with few-shot prompting substantially outperforms zero-shot o1 (κ rising from 0.58 to 0.77), validating the few-shot calibration approach for scalable annotation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm (Zheng et al., 2023) for scalable annotation, but repurposes it for a novel application: systematic failure-mode classification in multi-agent system execution traces. It departs from prior agentic-systems work (Agent Workflow Memory, DSPy, StateFlow) that addresses individual challenges in isolation, and from surveys that offer high-level overviews, by providing the first empirically grounded, fine-grained taxonomy (MAST) derived via Grounded Theory from 150+ traces across five frameworks. It also distinguishes itself from failure-attribution tools like AGDebugger and the Who&When dataset, which localize blame to specific agents and steps in individual tasks, by offering a generalizable, cross-system classification of *why* MAS fail at the architectural and coordination level.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 41–86.7% failure rates across seven SOTA MAS frameworks, combined with the finding that system-design and inter-agent-misalignment failures (not raw model capability) dominate, reframes the MAS reliability problem as an organizational-design challenge rather than a model-scaling one. The MAST taxonomy and MAST-Data (1,642 annotated traces) provide the first shared vocabulary and empirical substrate for diagnosing MAS failures, and the agentdash library makes this immediately actionable for practitioners. Key open questions remain: whether MAST is exhaustive, whether the modest intervention gains (+9.4%, +15.6%) can compound into robust reliability, and whether the 'theory-of-mind collapse' identified in FC2 can be addressed through targeted model training or requires fundamentally new communication architectures.
