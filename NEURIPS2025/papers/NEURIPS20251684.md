## Problem

Standard Gaussian Thompson Sampling achieves near-optimal expected regret but can exhibit poor tail behavior for cumulative regret and fragile performance under model mis-specification (e.g., heavy-tailed or mis-specified noise). This paper asks how to modify TS to simultaneously guarantee efficiency (optimal expected regret), safety (fast-decaying regret and estimation-error tails), and robustness (stability under hyperparameter and environment mis-specification), both during and after an experiment.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251684/cropped_page7_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The policy takes the arm set [K], a prior variance σ₀², and sequentially observed rewards r_t = μ_{a_t} + ε_{t,a_t} where noise is (σ,ν)-sub-exponential. At each step it outputs an arm action a_t; post-experiment, it outputs a best-arm selection (the arm pulled most often in the second half of the horizon) and per-arm empirical mean estimates. The analysis is worst-case over all mean vectors with |Δ_k| ≤ 1, and the key design parameter is the inflation factor t/(K·n_{t,k}²) applied to the sampling variance, which is the only structural change from standard TS.

## Method

TS-VI modifies Gaussian Thompson Sampling with a single change: the posterior sampling variance for arm k at time t is inflated from σ₀²/n_{t,k} to (t/K)/(n_{t,k}²)·σ₀², introducing a time- and arm-dependent factor t/(K·n_{t,k}) that grows as an arm is under-explored relative to the horizon. The Bayesian posterior update (improper flat prior, Gaussian likelihood) remains unchanged; only the distribution from which the sample X_{t,k} is drawn is widened. This adaptive inflation provably yields worst-case optimal regret tail decay exp(−x/(C·√(KT))) and expected regret Õ(√(KT·ln K·ln T)) under sub-exponential noise, without requiring knowledge of the true noise parameters. For post-experiment decisions, the policy guarantees simple regret and per-arm estimation error tails of the form exp(−y·√(T/K)) and exp(−√(y·√(T/K)·T/K)) respectively. The design is extended to unknown arm-specific variances via Gamma-Normal conjugate updates, preserving the same qualitative guarantees.

## Results

Theoretically, TS-VI achieves a worst-case optimal regret tail bound of exp(−x/(C·M·√(KT))) and expected regret Õ(√(KT ln K ln T)), with worst-case optimal simple regret Õ(√(K/T)) and estimation error Õ(√(K/T)) for post-experiment decisions. Empirically, on a 2-armed bandit (δ=0.3, 10⁴ trajectories), TS-VI incurs a moderate increase in expected regret of 20–100 (or 20–40 for σ₀=0.3σ) at T=10,000 relative to standard TS and UCB, while its tail probability of negative cumulative reward and large estimation error decays to near zero by T≈2,000–5,000, compared to much slower decay for baselines. TS-VI consistently achieves lower mean absolute per-arm estimation error across all tested environments (Gaussian well-specified, Laplace mis-specified with known and unknown variances).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares TS-VI against two baselines: standard Gaussian Thompson Sampling (TS) and KL-UCB, each tested under well-specified (σ₀=σ), under-specified (σ₀=0.9σ), and over-specified (σ₀=1.1σ) variance settings. On expected within-experiment regret, both TS and UCB consistently outperform TS-VI, as the variance inflation encourages additional exploration of sub-optimal arms; however, the gap is moderate (20–100 regret units at T=10,000) and narrows for smaller horizons (T≤1,000), where TS-VI can even match or beat the baselines. On tail safety (probability of negative cumulative reward or large estimation error), TS-VI substantially outperforms both TS and UCB, with tail probabilities approaching zero by T≈2,000–5,000 while baselines exhibit much slower decay. On mean absolute per-arm estimation error, TS-VI consistently achieves lower error than both TS and UCB across all three environments. Under environment mis-specification (Laplace noise treated as Gaussian), the safety performance of TS and UCB deteriorates significantly, whereas TS-VI maintains stable tail behavior, confirming its robustness advantage. Over-specified baselines (σ₀=1.1σ) show some improvement in tail behavior but still decay slowly as T grows, indicating that explicit variance inflation is necessary for intrinsic safety gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the regret tail analysis framework of Simchi-Levi, Zheng, and Zhu (NeurIPS 2022, 2023), which established that deterministic UCB-like policies can achieve the optimal exp(-x/√T) regret tail decay rate, by showing that the randomized Thompson Sampling policy can be amended to achieve the same guarantee through a single variance inflation factor. The method is rooted in the classical Gaussian TS framework (Thompson 1933; Agrawal & Goyal 2012, 2017) and the Bayesian posterior-sampling literature, but departs from prior TS analyses that focused on instance-dependent expected regret by targeting worst-case tail behavior under sub-exponential noise. The post-experiment guarantees connect to the best-arm identification and mean estimation literature (Audibert & Bubeck 2010; Simchi-Levi & Wang 2022), where standard bandit policies are known to under-explore sub-optimal arms.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The one-line variance inflation modification offers a practically appealing, nearly parameter-free way to simultaneously improve safety (regret tail decay), robustness (to noise mis-specification and hyperparameter errors), and post-experiment decision quality in Thompson Sampling, at the cost of a modest increase in expected regret. This is particularly relevant for deployment settings where tail risk and model misspecification are concerns (clinical trials, financial decisions). Key open questions include whether the worst-case guarantees extend to instance-dependent settings, what the asymptotic arm-pulling proportions are, and how the approach generalizes to linear bandits, MDPs, or heavier-than-sub-exponential noise. The empirical validation is limited to 2-armed (and 6-armed in supplementary) settings, leaving open how the method scales in high-dimensional or contextual bandit problems.
