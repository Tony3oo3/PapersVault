## Problem

Despite documented GenAI failures in reasoning, spatial planning, and hallucination rates of 28–79%, defense companies and policy advocates are actively pushing GenAI into weapon control and targeting systems. The paper addresses the gap between these optimistic deployment claims and the demonstrated technical limitations, arguing that no existing framework adequately explains why GenAI specifically—unlike traditional symbolic or rule-based AI—is fundamentally unsuitable for safety-critical weapon governance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255682/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

As a position paper, the work does not process data or produce model outputs. It synthesizes evidence from published GenAI reasoning benchmarks, federal self-driving car accident databases, hallucination-rate studies across domains, and Technology Readiness Level (TRL) development timelines to produce a policy recommendation: a moratorium on GenAI in any form of weapon control until hallucinations can be reliably predicted and controlled real-world test facilities are established.

## Method

The core analytical framework is the SRKE (Skill-, Rule-, Knowledge-, Expertise-based) reasoning taxonomy, which maps Agentic AI capabilities onto four hierarchical reasoning levels and demonstrates that GenAI cannot achieve the knowledge- or expert-based reasoning required under high uncertainty such as fog-of-war targeting decisions. The author supports this with empirical evidence from self-driving car failures (missed articulated-bus detection, phantom braking, inability to handle reversing trucks), quantified hallucination rates (28.6% for GPT-4 in scientific writing, 58% in legal analysis, 33–79% per OpenAI's own system card), and a TRL comparison showing GenAI bypassed the 30-year military maturation process that drones underwent. A risk framework plots Agentic AI systems by safety-criticality and non-determinism, placing GenAI-in-weapons at the highest-risk intersection. The paper concludes by identifying two prerequisites before any deployment: a well-funded national program to model and predict hallucinations in connectionist AI, and a new science of testing and certification with physical test ranges for controlled real-world exposure.

## Results

The paper cites hallucination rates of 28.6% for GPT-4 in scientific writing, 58% for ChatGPT-4 in legal analyses, and 33–79% for OpenAI's latest models (o3/o4-mini). GPT-4 achieves only ~12% success on planning tasks, 10.3–14.7% on spatial route planning, and 43.1–67.6% on complex ophthalmology diagnoses. Even with retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), hallucination rates remain at 27.6% (news summarization) and 68.6% (data-to-text). Table 1 shows GenAI reached commercial market in just 6 years with zero military R&D, versus 30 years for drones and 10 years for self-driving cars.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper with no direct method-vs-baseline comparison; the 'compares_to' set is empty. Instead, the paper uses self-driving vehicles as the closest real-world proxy for evaluating Agentic AI reliability in safety-critical settings, noting that even the most advanced public-facing Agentic AI (self-driving cars) still requires remote human supervision and suffers from missed detections (~10% of crashes) and false positives (twice the rate of missed detections). The paper contrasts symbolic AI (deterministic, used in mature drone autopilots) with connectionist/GenAI (non-deterministic, high hallucination rates) within the SRKE reasoning framework, arguing that only skill- and rule-based reasoning are currently reliable enough for autonomous operation, while knowledge- and expert-based reasoning remain beyond all current AI systems. The R&D timeline comparison (Table 1) highlights that GenAI bypassed the 30-year military maturation process that drones underwent, meaning its failure modes are far less characterized.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes evidence from GenAI reasoning benchmarks, LLM hallucination studies, and NHTSA self-driving car accident data to argue against GenAI in weapon control, building on the author's own SRKE (Skill-, Rule-, Knowledge-, Expertise-based) reasoning framework from 2014. It departs from the prevailing pro-AI policy rhetoric (e.g., Elbaum and Panter's call for reduced human intervention) and from industry claims (Anduril–OpenAI partnership) by grounding its argument in documented failure rates rather than capability demonstrations. The paper also repurposes the Technology Readiness Level framework to highlight that GenAI received zero years of military R&D compared to 30 years for drones, framing the rapid commercial-to-weapon pipeline as a safety gap rather than an innovation win.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper makes a concrete policy proposal—prohibit GenAI in any weapon control role until hallucinations can be reliably predicted—and identifies two prerequisite research programs: a national effort to model and predict hallucination conditions, and a new science of testing and certification for connectionist AI with physical test ranges. Open questions include whether hallucination prediction is even achievable for transformer architectures (some authors argue it is mathematically inevitable), how to certify non-deterministic systems under the same standards as deterministic ones, and whether the self-driving car analogy adequately captures the compressed timelines and fog-of-war conditions of weapons engagement. The paper's limitation is that it is a position piece without new empirical data; its force rests on the curation and synthesis of existing evidence.
