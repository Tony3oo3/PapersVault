## Problem

Existing feed-forward 3D Gaussian Splatting networks fail to produce geometrically accurate 2D Gaussian surfels from sparse views: predicted surfels orient parallel to the image plane rather than aligning with true surface geometry. The paper identifies the root cause as a violation of the Nyquist sampling theorem—the spatial frequency of pixel-aligned surfels exceeds the sampling rate, so the network receives insufficient supervisory signal to recover correct covariance and orientation. Optimization-based methods (2DGS, Gaussian Surfels) achieve accurate surfaces but require dense views and per-scene training lasting minutes to hours.

## Input / Output

The method consumes exactly 2 sparse-view RGB images (downsampled to 256×320) along with their camera intrinsics and extrinsics, and produces a pixel-aligned set of 2D Gaussian surfels in a single feed-forward pass (~1 second on an RTX A6000). Each pixel in each input view yields one surfel parameterized by 3D center, rotation (two tangent vectors), anisotropic scale, opacity, and spherical-harmonic color coefficients. Because the representation is pixel-aligned, the number of surfels scales linearly with input resolution, which the authors note becomes a bottleneck at 1024×1024 (over 1 million surfels per view).

## Method

SurfelSplat builds on the pixelSplat architecture (epipolar transformer + DINO backbone + cost-volume depth head) but adds two Nyquist-guided modules. First, a surfel adaptation module computes each primitive's spatial sampling rate ν̂ = f_x·f_y/d² across all views, then convolves the 2D Gaussian with an adaptive low-pass filter whose bandwidth is set by ν̂, analytically guaranteeing the post-adaptation frequency stays below ν̂/2 (the Nyquist threshold). Second, a cross-view feature aggregation module projects the enlarged (low-pass-filtered) surfels back into every input view to identify the pixel regions that actually carry geometric information for each surfel, then uses a cross-attention transformer (query = original per-pixel feature, key/value = pooled features from those identified regions) to refine the Gaussian attribute prediction. Training follows a two-stage curriculum: 300k iterations on RealEstate10K followed by 2k fine-tuning iterations on DTU, with a combined rendering loss (MSE + LPIPS) and geometric alignment loss (depth/normal MSE + normal continuity).

## Results

On the DTU sparse-view surface reconstruction benchmark (2 input views, 256×320 resolution), SurfelSplat achieves a mean Chamfer Distance (CD) of 1.12, the best among all compared methods, narrowly outperforming FatesGS (1.18) and significantly surpassing NeuSurf (1.44), VolRecon (1.67), and UFORecon (1.91). In terms of efficiency, SurfelSplat completes inference in approximately 1 second per scene, representing a ~100× speedup over the next-fastest feed-forward method (VolRecon at 60 s) and orders of magnitude faster than per-scene optimization methods (NeuS: 10 h, 2DGS: 10 min, FatesGS: 14 min).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SurfelSplat achieves the best mean Chamfer Distance (1.12) on DTU, beating the closest competitor FatesGS (1.18) by 0.06 and the next-best implicit method NeuSurf (1.44) by 0.32. Against optimization-based 3DGS methods, the advantage is substantial: 2DGS (3.02) and GausSurf (4.36) are outperformed by 1.90 and 3.24 CD respectively, reflecting their tendency to overfit or produce incomplete surfaces with only 2 views. Among feed-forward methods, SurfelSplat also surpasses VolRecon (1.67) and UFORecon (1.91) by 0.55 and 0.79 CD. In efficiency, SurfelSplat's 1-second inference is roughly 60× faster than VolRecon (60 s), 100× faster than UFORecon (100 s), and 840× faster than FatesGS (14 min), while matching or exceeding their reconstruction quality. The only method with comparable accuracy (FatesGS) requires per-scene optimization and 14 minutes of inference, making SurfelSplat the clear winner on the accuracy–efficiency trade-off.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two active research threads: (1) 2D Gaussian surfel representations for surface reconstruction (2DGS, Gaussian Surfels, SuGaR), which require per-scene optimization, and (2) feed-forward Gaussian prediction networks (pixelSplat, MVSplat), which achieve fast generalizable reconstruction but target novel view synthesis rather than precise surface geometry. The key conceptual departure is the application of the Nyquist sampling theorem to diagnose why pixel-aligned feed-forward networks fail to recover accurate surfel orientations: the spatial frequency of single-pixel-aligned primitives exceeds the sampling rate, violating a fundamental signal-processing constraint. This motivates the frequency-domain surfel adaptation (low-pass filtering) and cross-view feature aggregation modules that are absent from prior feed-forward Gaussian methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 1-second inference time represents a ~100× speedup over per-scene optimization methods (hours for NeuS/NeuSurf, minutes for 2DGS/FatesGS), making high-quality surface reconstruction accessible in real-time or interactive settings. The Nyquist-based theoretical framing provides a principled, reusable diagnostic for why certain geometric attributes are unrecoverable from pixel-aligned representations, which could inform future feed-forward 3D reconstruction designs beyond surfels. However, the method is currently limited to 2 input views, is sensitive to input resolution (over 1M Gaussians at 1024×1024), and relies on relatively limited training data (~10K scenes), leaving open questions about scalability to more views, higher resolutions, and integration with generative priors for unseen regions.
