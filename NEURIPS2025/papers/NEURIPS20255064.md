## Problem

Curriculum learning for language models has historically yielded mixed results because existing approaches rely on ranking examples by difficulty within a fixed dataset, which is both hard to estimate accurately and insufficient to isolate the atomic skills needed for complex problem-solving. This paper asks whether a teacher model's step-by-step reasoning can be leveraged to *generate* a curriculum from scratch by recursively decomposing complex problems into progressively simpler sub-problems, rather than merely reordering a static dataset.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255064/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a dataset of problems paired with multi-step chain-of-thought solutions (e.g., MATH, AIME, CodeForces-CoTs) and a capable teacher LM (GPT-4o for math, o4-mini for code). It produces, for each original problem, a recursive tree of verified sub-problems—each annotated with an atomic concept tag, a depth index, and a composite difficulty score—plus a dataset-wide Concept Dependency Graph (a DAG over clustered concept tags). The final training artifact is a curriculum-ordered sequence of (question, solution) pairs partitioned into K quantile bins by difficulty, used for supervised fine-tuning of small student models (Qwen2.5-1.5B, Qwen3-4B-Base).

## Method

DECOMPX operates in two phases. First, a recursive decomposition loop: the teacher model segments a solution into at most k conceptual steps, assigns each step an atomic concept tag, generates a grounded sub-problem from that step, solves it step-by-step, and verifies correctness by independently re-solving without the original context and comparing answers via a symbolic verifier; the process recurses on each sub-solution up to a maximum depth D. Second, a dataset-level Concept Dependency Graph is built by connecting parent tags to child tags across all decomposition trees, with near-duplicate tags merged via embedding-similarity clustering. Difficulty of each sub-problem is a weighted sum of structural complexity (number of direct children in the decomposition tree) and conceptual depth (node depth in the DAG). During training, the decomposed dataset is partitioned into K quantile bins and the student model is fine-tuned sequentially from the easiest bin to the hardest, with early stopping at each stage.

## Results

On MATH-500, DECOMPX+Curriculum achieves 51.6% accuracy with Qwen2.5-1.5B using only 4,500 decomposed examples, outperforming standard SFT on the full 7,500-example dataset (47.6%) and MuggleMath with 147,787 examples (50.4%). On AIME 2025, DECOMPX+Curriculum reaches 16.7% with Qwen3-4B-Base trained on just 385 decomposed examples, surpassing Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct (15.0%) and GPT-4o (7.6%). On HumanEval, DECOMPX improves pass@1 from 36.0% to 57.9% for DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B and from 35.4% to 42.7% for Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DECOMPX+Curriculum (51.6%) outperforms standard SFT on the full 7,500-example MATH dataset (47.6%) by 4.0 points while using fewer training examples, and beats direct distillation from GPT-4o (49.6%) by 2.0 points. The method substantially outperforms MetaMath augmentation (37.2%) by 14.4 points and edges out MuggleMath (50.4%) by 1.2 points despite using ~33× fewer training examples (4,500 vs. 147,787). On AIME 2025, DECOMPX+Curriculum (16.7%) surpasses the much larger Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct (15.0%), GPT-4o (7.6%), and the sample-efficient S1-32B model (13.3%), while standard SFT on AIME 2024 actually degrades below the base model (3.3% vs. 10.0%), highlighting overfitting on the tiny 30-problem dataset. On HumanEval, DECOMPX yields a 21.9-point gain over the base model for DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B (57.9% vs. 36.0%) and a 7.3-point gain over SFT for Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct (42.7% vs. 35.4%). The curriculum ordering provides an additional 0.8-point gain on MATH and 3.4-point gain on AIME over randomly ordered training on the same decomposed data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the curriculum learning paradigm (Bengio et al., 2009; Elman, 1993) by addressing a key weakness identified in prior work: that ranking examples by difficulty within a monolithic dataset is insufficient when the dataset lacks granularity to isolate atomic skills. Building on Lee et al. (2024), who showed that decomposed datasets isolating fundamental skills improve curriculum effectiveness, DECOMPX operationalizes this by recursively decomposing multi-step solutions into a hierarchy of verified sub-problems, each tagged with an atomic concept. Unlike data augmentation methods (MetaMath, MuggleMath) that increase volume without structural organization, or direct distillation that transfers knowledge flatly, DECOMPX constructs a concept dependency graph encoding prerequisite relationships, enabling principled difficulty scoring and staged training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that structured recursive decomposition with only 385 samples can outperform a 72B model and data-heavy augmentation baselines on AIME2025, suggesting that how data is organized for learning matters as much as how much data is available. The concept dependency graph offers a reusable 'cartography' of mathematical skills that could inform dataset design and coverage analysis beyond training. Key limitations include reliance on strong teacher models (GPT-4o, o4-mini), vulnerability to teacher hallucination, and validation primarily on math and code domains where reasoning decomposes naturally into discrete steps. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to domains with less obviously decomposable reasoning paths, and whether the method supports weak-to-strong generalization where teacher and student have similar capacity.
