## Problem

Existing graph pooling layers predominantly adopt a node-centric view—either dropping nodes or clustering them—which discards fundamental edge-level structural information and leads to counter-intuitive pooling decisions that fail to preserve key geometric structures. While edge-based pooling (e.g., EdgePool) exists, it relies on learned edge scores that are computationally expensive and do not explicitly account for a graph's topology, making it less flexible and interpretable. The paper asks whether geometric invariants of metric spaces can guide edge contraction to preserve structural diversity while reducing graph size.

## Input / Output

The method takes an undirected graph with node features, its adjacency matrix, and a user-specified pooling ratio r ∈ (0,1], and produces a sparsely-connected pooled graph with ⌊r·|X|⌋ nodes and mean-aggregated node features. Unlike trainable pooling layers, no additional parameters are learned; the only tunable hyperparameter is the pooling ratio. The method is non-trainable and can be pre-computed, decoupling pooling cost from GNN training iterations.

## Method

The core mechanism is iterative edge contraction guided by the magnitude (or spread) of the graph's diffusion metric space. Diffusion distances are computed via the eigendecomposition of the normalised graph Laplacian, embedding nodes in Euclidean space. For each candidate edge e, a selection score s(e) = |Mag(G) − Mag(G/e)| measures how much contracting that edge would alter the graph's structural diversity; the edge with the lowest score (most redundant) is contracted first, with a constraint preventing contraction of edges adjacent to already-contracted edges. This yields MagEdgePool (using magnitude, O(|X|³) per score) and SpreadEdgePool (using spread, O(|X|²) per score), the latter being a faster and more stable alternative that avoids matrix inversion. Theoretical results establish isometry invariance, monotonicity under 1-Lipschitz contractions, and a bound linking magnitude change to spread change, while expressivity is preserved under the Select-Reduce-Connect framework.

## Results

On graph classification across 8 TUDataset benchmarks, MagEdgePool achieves the best mean rank (2.4) and SpreadEdgePool the second-best (3.0), consistently ranking among the top methods on every dataset. The most striking gain is on DHFR, where SpreadEdgePool reaches 90.7% accuracy versus 71.4% for the no-pooling baseline (≈17 pp improvement), demonstrating that structure-preserving pooling can regularise and boost performance on small, geometrically rich graphs. On ENZYMES, SpreadEdgePool attains 92.8% and MagEdgePool 91.5%, both exceeding the no-pooling baseline (87.3%). For graph regression on OGB (MolEsol, MolFreeSolv, MolLipo), both methods achieve RMSEs comparable to or better than no pooling (e.g., MolLipo: 0.91 vs. 0.98).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the non-trainable baselines, MagEdgePool and SpreadEdgePool clearly outperform NDP (mean rank 3.6) on DHFR (88.0/90.7% vs. 79.6%), Mutagenicity (77.5/76.0% vs. 73.4%), and NCI1/NCI109, while NDP edges ahead on ENZYMES (92.2% vs. 91.5/92.8%) and IMDB-B (73.3% vs. 72.4/71.8%). Graclus (rank 5.6) and NMF (rank 6.0) are consistently outperformed, with the gap most pronounced on DHFR (64.4% and 66.5% respectively vs. 88.0/90.7%). Among trainable methods, TopK (rank 4.9) and SAGPool (rank 4.7) are competitive on NCI1/NCI109 (75.3/74.3% and 74.3/74.0%) but fall well behind on DHFR (68.9/69.9% vs. 88.0/90.7%) and ENZYMES (82.2/82.4% vs. 91.5/92.8%). DiffPool (rank 7.6) and MinCut (rank 7.4) are the weakest baselines overall, with DiffPool particularly poor on ENZYMES (74.0%) and NCI109 (68.3%). The authors note that on ENZYMES, DHFR, IMDB-B, and IMDB-M, non-trainable methods (including their own) generally outperform all trainable pooling layers, suggesting that the additional trainable parameters in DiffPool/MinCut/TopK/SAGPool do not translate into consistent gains on these benchmarks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant node-centric pooling paradigm (clustering via Graclus/NMF/DiffPool, dropping via TopK/SAGPool, or spectral decimation via NDP) by returning to edge-contraction pooling, as first explored by EdgePool (Diehl 2019), but replacing learned edge scores with geometry-guided scoring based on magnitude and spread of metric spaces. The key conceptual novelty is importing magnitude—a diversity invariant from theoretical ecology and metric geometry (Leinster 2013)—into graph learning for the first time, and proposing spread (Willerton 2015) as a computationally cheaper proxy. Diffusion distances (Coifman & Lafon 2006) provide the metric structure that makes these invariants well-defined and comparable across graphs, while the theoretical analysis draws on positive-definite metric space theory to establish invariance, monotonicity, and expressivity guarantees.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, interpretable, and non-trainable pooling operator that preserves spectral and geometric structure, addressing a long-standing concern that existing pooling methods discard topology in favor of task optimization. The practical finding that spread (O(n²)) approximates magnitude (O(n³)) with near-perfect correlation on real datasets opens a path to scaling geometry-guided pooling to larger graphs. Limitations include the implicit assumption that graph structure preservation is beneficial (which may not hold for all tasks), the requirement of redundancy/homophily in the input, and current scalability to only a few hundred nodes. Open questions include automatic pooling-ratio selection, alternative distance metrics, and whether the geometric objective can be combined with task-specific learnable components without losing interpretability.
