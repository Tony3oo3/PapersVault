## Problem

Misinformation detectors trained on static benchmarks tend to exploit superficial cues (sentiment, style, topic, perplexity) that correlate with labels in training data but are not causally linked to authenticity, leading to brittle generalization. While prior work has examined isolated biases (entity, source, domain), no systematic framework exists to measure the full spectrum of shortcut behaviors under both naturally occurring and adversarially induced conditions, especially as LLMs can now generate convincing misinformation that evades existing detectors.

## Input / Output

The evaluation framework takes textual content (news articles, tweets, claims) with binary real/fake labels and produces detector accuracy under controlled shortcut conditions where the joint distribution of a shortcut factor and the authenticity label is deliberately shifted between train and test splits. For extrinsic injection, LLMs (Meta-Llama-3-8B-Instruct at temperature 0) rewrite both real and fake instances to embed predefined factors (sentiment, tone, word choice, age, gender) while preserving factual authenticity, verified via ROUGE-L, BERTScore, and human evaluation. SMF additionally outputs augmented texts (paraphrased, summarized, or sentiment-normalized) that are fed to detectors at both training and inference time.

## Method

TRUTHOVERTRICKS is a unified evaluation paradigm with two complementary axes: (1) intrinsic shortcut induction, which partitions existing benchmarks so that the joint distribution P(shortcut_factor, label) differs between train-validation and test sets, testing whether four indicators (sentiment, style, topic, perplexity) naturally induce spurious reliance; and (2) extrinsic shortcut injection, which uses zero-shot LLM prompts to adversarially rewrite texts with explicit factors (positive/negative sentiment, formal/informal tone, simple/complex word choice) or implicit author attributes (age, gender), creating six attack variants. To mitigate shortcut reliance, the Shortcut Mitigation Framework (SMF) applies three LLM-based augmentation strategies—paraphrase generation, factual summarization (retaining only authenticity-relevant content), and sentiment normalization (overwriting a specific shortcut with a neutral alternative)—before both training and inference, forcing models to rely on deeper semantic signals. The paper also introduces NQ-Misinfo and Streaming-Misinfo, two factual misinformation benchmarks constructed by distorting answers from Natural Questions and StreamingQA into declarative sentences, requiring detectors to use factual knowledge rather than stylistic cues.

## Results

Under the standard setting, LM-based detectors (BERT: 78.1%, DeBERTa: 80.2%) and debiasing detectors (CMTR: 76.9%, DISC: 78.4%, CATCH: 78.2%) achieve comparable accuracy across 16 misinformation benchmarks, while LLM-based detectors (MistralV3: 53.9%, Llama3: 58.8%) lag behind. Under explicit shortcut injection (Sentiment, Tone, Word Choice), LM-based detectors suffer catastrophic degradation to 6.4–19.2% accuracy (80–92% relative drop), and debiasing detectors drop to 6.8–17.0%. In contrast, LLM-based detectors remain robust with only 0.7–10.6% relative changes. The proposed SMF framework recovers performance substantially, with the Summary variant achieving up to 838.4% relative improvement (e.g., BERT on Word Choice: 7.0% → 45.2%) and the Paraphrase variant excelling against Word Choice shortcuts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates seven detectors across three categories. LM-based detectors (BERT, DeBERTa) achieve the highest standard accuracy (78–80%) but are the most vulnerable to explicit shortcut injection, dropping to 6–19% (80–92% relative loss). Debiasing detectors (CMTR, DISC, CATCH) offer marginal improvements over LM-based baselines under standard settings (76.9–78.4% vs. 78.1–80.2%) but suffer similarly severe degradation under explicit attacks (6.8–17.0%), confirming that model-side debiasing alone is insufficient. LLM-based detectors (MistralV3, Llama3) have the lowest absolute accuracy (53.9–58.8%) but are remarkably robust, with only 0.7–10.6% relative changes under all shortcut types, suggesting they capture deeper semantic signals rather than superficial correlations. Under implicit injection (Age, Gender), all trainable detectors degrade only 4–9%, consistent with the finding that LLMs struggle to reliably simulate author attributes. The SMF framework, when applied to LM-based and debiasing detectors, consistently recovers 50–840% of lost accuracy, with the Summary variant performing best overall and Paraphrase excelling specifically against Word Choice shortcuts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the shortcut learning literature (Geirhos et al., 2020) into misinformation detection, where prior work has examined individual biases (entity, source, domain) in isolation but lacks a unified measurement framework. The LLM-based injection attacks build on Chen & Shu (2024), who first explored LLM-generated misinformation, but here LLMs are repurposed as adversarial shortcut injectors rather than misinformation generators. The SMF mitigation framework inverts the injection logic—using the same LLMs that create shortcuts to strip them away—while the debiasing baselines (CMTR, DISC, CATCH) are borrowed from graph learning and hate speech detection, highlighting the cross-domain applicability of causal disentanglement ideas.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that detectors collapse to near-random accuracy (as low as 0%) under explicit shortcut injection reveals that current misinformation detection systems are far more fragile than their benchmark scores suggest, with practical implications for content moderation pipelines that rely on these detectors. The observation that shortcut-trained detectors effectively become sentiment classifiers (confirmed via SST-2 transfer) provides a diagnostic tool for identifying what models actually learn. Key limitations include the restriction to textual content-based detection (excluding social context, multimodal signals), the use of a single 8B LLM for all injections, and the fact that SMF's large relative gains are measured from very low post-attack baselines. Open questions include whether the framework generalizes to multimodal misinformation, whether more diverse LLMs as injectors would produce qualitatively different shortcut patterns, and whether SMF can be integrated into online/continual learning settings.
