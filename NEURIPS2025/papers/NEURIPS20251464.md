## Problem

Gradient-based data attribution methods (e.g., influence functions) require computing and storing per-sample gradients of dimension p for n training samples, incurring O(np) memory and compute that becomes prohibitive for billion-parameter models. Existing compression approaches—dense random projection (O(pk)), FJLT (O((p+k)log p)), and LOGRA's factorized Gaussian projection (O(√(pk)) per layer)—do not exploit the inherent sparsity of per-sample gradients induced by activation functions like ReLU, leaving a gap between theoretical compression cost and practical throughput.

## Input / Output

GRASS takes a full per-sample gradient vector g ∈ R^p and produces a compressed vector ĝ ∈ R^k (k ≪ p) via a two-stage pipeline. FACTGRASS, the linear-layer variant, never materializes the full gradient: it consumes only the layer input z_{i,l}^{in} ∈ R^{d_l^{in}×T} and the pre-activation gradient Dz_{i,l}^{out} ∈ R^{d_l^{out}×T} obtained from a forward/backward pass, and outputs the compressed gradient ĝ_{i,l} ∈ R^{k_l} per layer. The compressed vectors are then used to build a projected FIM and compute influence scores ⟨g_test, F̂^{-1} ĝ_i⟩ for attribution.

## Method

GRASS is a two-stage compression: first, a sparsification step (Random Mask or Selective Mask) selects k′ coordinates from the p-dimensional gradient, reducing it to a sub-vector g′ ∈ R^{k′}; second, a Sparse Johnson-Lindenstrauss Transform (SJLT) with s=1 non-zeros per column projects g′ down to the target dimension k, yielding overall O(k′) time and space complexity—sub-linear in p. The key insight is that SJLT's cost scales with input sparsity (nnz(g)) and is independent of the target dimension k, making it ideal when the input has already been sparsified. FACTGRASS adapts this to linear layers by exploiting the Kronecker-product structure of weight gradients: it sparsifies the layer input and pre-activation gradient separately (to dimensions k_l^{in′} and k_l^{out′}), reconstructs the sparsified gradient via their Kronecker product (dimension k_l′ = k_l^{in′}·k_l^{out′}), and then applies SJLT to reach k_l. This avoids both materializing the full O(p_l) gradient and the small-problem-size regime where SJLT's CUDA kernel overhead dominates, achieving O(k_l′) per layer and up to 165% faster throughput than LOGRA on Llama-3.1-8B.

## Results

On Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct with 1B tokens of OpenWebText, FACTGRASS achieves 72,218 tokens/sec compression throughput versus 27,292 for LOGRA (a 160% speedup), and improves overall caching throughput from 7,307 to 8,584 tokens/sec (~17% gain). On GPT2-small (WikiText), FACTGRASS (SJLT∘RM) reaches an LDS of 0.2360 at k_l=4096 in 6.3 s, matching the LDS of standalone SJLT (0.2389) while being 21× faster (136.5 s) and only 1.1× slower than standalone Random Mask (5.6 s, LDS 0.2391). The paper reports up to 250% speedup over LOGRA on GPT2-small while preserving competitive attribution quality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against FJLT (Ailon & Chazelle, 2009), GRASS/SJLT is dramatically faster: on Music Transformer + MAESTRO, FJLT requires 100–270 s for compression while GRASS (SJLT∘RM) completes in ~0.75 s, a >100× speedup, while also achieving comparable or better LDS (0.3755 vs 0.4011 at k=8192). Against dense Gaussian projection (Wojnowicz et al., 2016), SJLT is 20–70× faster on small models (e.g., 0.15 s vs 10.8 s on MLP/MNIST at k=8192), and Gaussian projection becomes infeasible on larger models due to GPU memory constraints. Against LOGRA (Choe et al., 2024), the primary SOTA baseline for linear-layer gradient compression, FACTGRASS wins decisively on efficiency: 160% faster compression throughput and ~17% faster end-to-end caching on the 8B-parameter Llama model, and up to 250% faster on GPT2-small, while maintaining LDS within 0.003 of LOGRA's quality. FACTGRASS does not sacrifice attribution fidelity: its LDS scores are statistically indistinguishable from both standalone SJLT and standalone Random Mask across all tested models.

## Relation to Prior Work

GRASS builds on the sparse Johnson-Lindenstrauss transform (SJLT) as its projection backbone and on LOGRA's Kronecker-product factorization of linear-layer gradients, but departs from both by explicitly exploiting the inherent sparsity of per-sample gradients (induced by ReLU and similar activations) that neither method leverages. Where FJLT and LOGRA treat the input as a dense vector and achieve O((p+k)log p) or O(√(pk)) per projection respectively, GRASS introduces a two-stage pipeline—sparsification (Random Mask or Selective Mask) followed by SJLT—that reduces complexity to O(k′), sub-linear in the parameter count p. FACTGRASS further adapts this pipeline to the factorized gradient structure so that full layer gradients are never materialized, going beyond LOGRA's approach of projecting two smaller dense factors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Gradient-based data attribution is essential for data curation, fact tracing, and copyright attribution in LLMs, but has been bottlenecked by O(np) memory and O(nkp) or O(n√(pk)) compute for gradient compression; GRASS/FACTGRASS removes this bottleneck with a tunable sub-linear complexity that scales gracefully with model size. The 165% throughput improvement on Llama-3.1-8B makes billion-scale attribution practically feasible on a single GPU, and the Selective Mask's data-driven mask selection offers a principled alternative to purely random coordinate selection. Open questions include whether the per-sample gradient sparsity assumption degrades for architectures without ReLU-like activations, whether the block-diagonal FIM approximation remains adequate as models grow, and whether the one-time Selective Mask optimization cost is amortizable across multiple attribution queries.
