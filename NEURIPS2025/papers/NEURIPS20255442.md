## Problem

Existing detectors for diffusion-model-generated faces yield only a binary real/fake decision, lacking the ability to localize partially synthesized regions or attribute forgeries to specific generators. While MLLMs offer a path toward explainable forensics, they suffer from hallucination when describing subtle artifacts in high-fidelity DM-based images, and no prior work unifies detection, localization, and attribution into a single framework.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255442/cropped_page6_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a single facial RGB image paired with a randomly formatted natural-language question (e.g., 'Is this image real or fake? Can you provide the reasoning behind your judgment?'). It produces a structured chain-of-thought text response that sequentially reports: a detection verdict with supporting reasoning, a forgery type label (partial synthesis, identity insertion, or non-existent face), the specific falsified facial region(s) (nose, brow, hair, ears, or combinations), and the attributed generator model. Localization is expressed as natural-language region names rather than pixel-level masks, reducing sensitivity to background interference.

## Method

VLForgery builds on LLaVA-1.5-7B and introduces three key components. First, a low-level vision comparison pipeline uses Llama-3.2-11B-Vision to generate differential descriptions across 10 visual aspects (edges, noise, lighting, etc.) between real and fake image pairs, then extracts top-10 compound noun phrases with the largest proportion difference as 'judgment biases.' Second, an Extrinsic knowledge-guided Chain-of-thought (EkCot) method integrates these judgment biases, the original generation prompts, and ground-truth labels into structured forgery analysis descriptions that guide the MLLM's reasoning. Third, the model is fine-tuned in two steps—projector alignment between frozen CLIP visual features and text, followed by LoRA (rank=128) on the language model—using triplet data of (image, diverse question, structured answer) drawn from the newly constructed VLF dataset spanning 9 diffusion generators and both partial and full synthesis types.

## Results

On the VLF dataset, VLForgery achieves an average detection accuracy of 93.82% across all nine generator subsets (both Partial and Full Synthesis), outperforming the strongest baseline F3Net (89.01%) by 4.81 points. The advantage is most pronounced on Partial Synthesis detection, where VLForgery reaches 78.62% on SDXL-trained PS samples versus 74.09% for F3Net and 69.92% for LLaVA-1.5-7B. On Full Synthesis, VLForgery achieves near-perfect accuracy (99.97–99.98%) across all generators, matching or exceeding all baselines. For localization, VLForgery outperforms the IFDL method (PSCC-Net) by 23–48 points on individual facial regions (e.g., 92.44% vs. 60.71% on hair for Kandinsky2.2).

## Baselines & Comparisons

VLForgery outperforms all 16 baselines on average detection accuracy (93.82%), with the largest margins over spatial detectors (DRCT: 81.83%, +12.0 pts; SAFE: 61.66%, +32.2 pts) and the VLLM-based Qwen2.5-VL-7B (14.02%, +79.8 pts). Among frequency-domain detectors, F3Net is the closest competitor at 89.01%, but VLForgery still leads by 4.81 points, driven primarily by superior Partial Synthesis detection (78.62% vs. 74.09% on SDXL-PS). Among VLLM-based methods, CLIPping Linear Probing (88.55%) and LLaVA-1.5-7B (87.07%) are the strongest, yet both trail VLForgery by 5.3 and 6.8 points respectively; Llama-3.2-11B-Vision (59.66%) and Qwen2.5-VL-7B (14.02%) fail to generalize without task-specific fine-tuning. On Full Synthesis, most strong baselines (Xception, F3Net, CLIPping LP, LLaVA) achieve >99% accuracy, making the gap negligible; VLForgery's decisive advantage lies in Partial Synthesis and cross-generator generalization. For localization, VLForgery surpasses the IFDL method PSCC-Net by 23–48 points per region (e.g., 92.44% vs. 60.71% on hair, 91.34% vs. 44.44% on nose for Kandinsky2.2). For attribution, VLForgery achieves 82.03% average, slightly exceeding the best CNN baselines (Xception: 80.30%, Guarnera et al.: 80.22%), though it lags on Full Synthesis subsets where CNNs reach >99%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the LLaVA-1.5-7B architecture and its two-step training paradigm (projector alignment followed by LoRA fine-tuning), repurposing it for forensic analysis of diffusion-model-generated faces. It departs from prior MLLM-based forensic work (Zhang et al., Huang et al., Jia et al.) that focused exclusively on traditional face-swapping deepfakes and single-task detection, by extending the setting to high-fidelity DM-generated images and unifying detection, localization, and attribution into a single 'triad' framework. The key methodological departure is the EkCot description generation pipeline, which injects extrinsic knowledge from the generation pipeline and low-level vision comparison findings into the chain-of-thought, addressing the hallucination problem that plagues prior VLM-based forgery description approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The VLF dataset fills a notable gap by providing large-scale partially synthesized face data with ground-truth masks, prompts, and generator labels, enabling fine-grained forensic evaluation that was previously infeasible. The finding that natural-language region descriptions outperform pixel-level mask localization (comparing against PSCC-Net/IFDL) suggests a potentially simpler and more robust paradigm for forgery localization. However, cross-generator generalization for partial synthesis remains weak (as low as 34% accuracy), and attribution for partial synthesis falls below 70%, indicating that the framework has not yet solved the hardest aspects of the triad. Open questions include whether the EkCot approach generalizes to unseen generators and whether the low-level vision bias extraction can be made fully automated without manual filtering.
