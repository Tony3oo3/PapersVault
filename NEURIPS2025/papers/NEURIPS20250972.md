## Problem

Token embeddings are a critical component of attention-based language models, yet their theoretical understanding remains limited: existing analyses of transformer architectures either assume orthogonal embedding structures or omit embeddings entirely by treating inputs as unprocessed. This paper asks what structure gradient descent actually learns in the embedding matrix and how that structure relates to the empirical statistics (frequencies) of tokens in the training data.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a text sequence of T tokens drawn from a vocabulary S, embeds each token into a d-dimensional vector, appends a <cls> token embedding p, and produces a scalar binary classification score via one-layer softmax attention with a fixed output vector v. The key learned outputs are the per-token embedding vectors E_s, whose alignment with v encodes token importance (proportional to average signed frequency α_s) and whose alignment with p encodes the attention selection weight. Experiments use IMDB and Yelp sentiment reviews (binary labels) with sequences of ~239 words on average, tokenized via BERT.

## Method

The paper analyzes a one-layer softmax attention model f(X; p, E) = Softmax(p^T E_X^T) E_X v trained with logistic loss, decomposing the dynamics into two stages. First, a single gradient step on all embeddings (with Gaussian initialization) provably aligns each token embedding E_s with the output vector v in proportion to its average signed frequency α_s (Lemma 4.1), capturing first-order data statistics. Second, fixing the context embeddings at their post-first-step values and training only the <cls> embedding p via gradient flow, the paper proves that p diverges in norm and its directional limit is the max-margin solution that selects all completely positive/negative tokens (Theorem 4.3). Additional lemmas characterize when irrelevant tokens are excluded from the selection (Lemmas 4.4–4.5), and the analysis extends to multiclass classification and two-layer architectures with layer norm and skip connections, where experiments on IMDB and Yelp confirm the predicted phenomenology.

## Results

The paper's main theoretical results establish that (i) after a single gradient step, each token embedding E_s aligns with the output vector v proportionally to its average signed frequency α_s (Lemma 4.1), and (ii) the <cls> embedding p trained via gradient flow converges in direction to the max-margin separator that selects all completely positive/negative tokens (Theorem 4.3). Experimentally, training all embeddings to convergence on IMDB and Yelp yields ~95% test accuracy (test error ~0.1), whereas fixing context embeddings at random initialization and training only the <cls> token and classifier drops accuracy to ~72% (test error ~0.6), confirming the critical role of embedding training. The two-layer model (with skip connection and layer norm) achieves a final loss of order 10⁻⁵ versus 10⁻¹ for the one-layer model, yet exhibits qualitatively identical embedding structure.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is primarily a theoretical paper without a standard method-vs-method comparison table; the 'compares_to' set is empty. The key empirical contrast is between fully trained embeddings and randomly initialized (frozen) context embeddings: the trained model reaches ~95% accuracy on IMDB/Yelp while the frozen-embedding baseline achieves only ~72%, a gap of roughly 23 percentage points that directly validates the theoretical claim that gradient training of embeddings is essential for token selection. The paper also compares the one-layer attention model (Eq. 1) against a two-layer model with skip connections and layer norm (Eq. 18), showing that the two-layer model achieves substantially lower loss (10⁻⁵ vs. 10⁻¹) but produces the same qualitative embedding structure (dot products with v and p correlated with token importance). In Appendix F.1, a single gradient step on embeddings reduces the standard deviation of the ⟨E_s, v⟩ correlation by ~8× and ⟨E_s, p⟩ by ~6× compared to full training, supporting the theory that first-order statistics are captured immediately while higher-order structure requires additional training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the implicit bias literature (Soudry et al., 2018) and the max-margin token selection framework for attention (Tarzanagh et al., NeurIPS 2023) by explicitly studying the training dynamics of token embeddings, which prior analyses either ignore via orthogonal parameterizations or omit entirely. While Tarzanagh et al. characterize the <cls> token's convergence to locally optimal max-margin solutions under specific initialization conditions, this paper characterizes all possible limiting directions and connects them to data statistics (signed token frequencies). The work also departs from the broader transformer theory line (Makkuva et al., Li et al.) by focusing on the embedding layer rather than attention weight matrices, revealing that a single gradient step already encodes first-order token importance into the embedding geometry.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical characterization of how gradient descent shapes token embeddings in attention models, showing that the learned structure is remarkably simple: alignment with the output vector proportional to empirical signed frequency. This explains a practical observation (embeddings capture token importance) with a clean mechanistic account. The main limitation is that the analysis covers only first-order statistics and a one-layer architecture; the authors conjecture that in deeper models, earlier layers learn causal structure (induction heads) while later layers exploit empirical statistics, but this remains unproven. Open questions include extending the max-margin characterization to multi-head, multi-layer settings and understanding how higher-order token interactions emerge with additional training.
