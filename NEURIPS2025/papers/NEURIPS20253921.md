## Problem

The ML4CO community lacks a unified taxonomy, reproducible implementations, and standardized evaluation protocols for neural combinatorial optimization solvers. Existing works use heterogeneous datasets, inconsistent decoding heuristics, uncontrolled post-processing (e.g., MCTS with varying time limits), and ad-hoc reporting that obscures the raw contribution of learning components. This paper establishes a modular benchmark with a tri-leveled paradigm-model-learning taxonomy and 65 standardized datasets to enable fair, transparent comparison across methods.

## Input / Output

Input is a graph G=(V,E) with problem-specific attributes: a cost/distance matrix C∈R^{N×N} for TSP/ATSP, plus a demand vector d and vehicle capacity Q for CVRP; node-oriented problems (MIS, MCl, MVC, MCut) use only the adjacency structure. Solutions are binary decision vectors x∈{0,1}^M where M=N for node problems and M=N² for edge problems. The benchmark evaluates across three scales (SMALL 200–300, LARGE 800–1200, GIANT 2000–3000 nodes) on RB, ER, BA, and uniform graphs, reporting objective value, relative drop vs. a learning-free baseline solver, and per-instance solving time under single-thread, batch-size-1 conditions.

## Method

The paper introduces a tri-leveled taxonomy: (1) Paradigm classifies solvers by the number of determination iterations k—Global Prediction (k=1, predict all variables at once), Local Construction (k=N, predict one variable per step), and Adaptive Expansion (k∈(1,N), predict a variable subset per step); (2) Model distinguishes One-Shot architectures (GCN, GAT) from Generative models (Diffusion, Consistency) that iteratively denoise; (3) Learning covers SL, UL, RL, and MAML. Building on this taxonomy, the authors integrate shared architectural components into three unified solvers (GP4CO, LC4CO, AE4CO) that can be instantiated with any model-learning combination. Evaluation uses greedy decoding as the default to isolate learning contributions, with beam search (k=16) and parallel mechanisms (×4) as optional enhancements, and standardized post-processing (Two-Opt/MCTS for edge problems, RLSA for node problems) applied under controlled time budgets.

## Results

On the summarized benchmark (Table 8), the best ML4CO configurations achieve near-optimal solutions with performance drops of 0.001%–1.45% on TSP (vs. Concorde/LKH), 0.17%–1.57% on ATSP (vs. LKH), 1.18%–5.47% on CVRP (vs. HGS), and 0.00%–2.74% on node-oriented problems (vs. Gurobi/KaMIS), while being 10–100× faster. For example, AE-Gen-SL on TSP-1K reaches 0.662% drop in 2.6s versus Concorde's 84.4s, and GP-OS-SL on MCl-RB-SMALL matches Gurobi exactly (0.000% drop) in 0.041s vs. 0.900s. On MCut-BA-GIANT, GP-Gen-SL even surpasses Gurobi (−2.383% drop) in 2.2s vs. 3601s. Cross-distribution generalization (Table 9) confirms strong transfer on Cluster/Gaussian TSP and HK/WS node problems, with the notable exception of SAT-reduced ATSP instances.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Concorde on TSP, the best ML4CO configuration (AE-Gen-SL with 4×Greedy+Two-Opt) achieves 0.257% drop at TSP-500 (16.588 vs. 16.546) in 0.695s vs. 18.672s, and 0.662% drop at TSP-1K in 2.6s vs. 84.4s — a 30× speedup with negligible quality loss. Against LKH on ATSP, AE-Gen-SL stays within 0.17%–1.57% drop across scales 50–500, with the gap widening slightly at larger instances. Against HGS on CVRP, LC-OS-RL achieves 1.18% drop at CVRP-50 in 0.154s vs. 1.005s (6.5× faster), while LC-OS-SL with Beam-16 reaches 2.03% at CVRP-500 but requires 64.6s (still 5.6× faster than HGS's 360s). Against Gurobi on node-oriented problems, ML4CO methods match or exceed the solver: MCl-RB-SMALL is exact (0.000% drop, 22× faster), MVC-RB-LARGE is within 0.018% (182× faster), and MCut-BA-GIANT actually surpasses Gurobi by 2.38% in 2.2s vs. 3601s. Against KaMIS on MIS, AE-Gen-SL achieves 0.093% drop at RB-SMALL (1.14× faster) and 1.366% at RB-LARGE (31× faster), confirming that the speed advantage grows with instance size while quality remains competitive.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on COExpander [37] for its AE paradigm, training protocols, and dataset generation, while extending the scope from a single method to a unified benchmark spanning three solving paradigms (GP, LC, AE) and seven CO problems. It generalizes earlier partial benchmarks—RL4CO [41] (RL-only, no taxonomy), ML4TSPBench [17] (TSP-only), and PredictiveCO [54] (predictive-only)—by introducing a tri-leveled 'paradigm-model-learning' taxonomy and consolidating 20+ prior methods into three reproducible unified solvers (GP4CO, LC4CO, AE4CO). The key conceptual departure from prior work is the insistence on separating raw neural model capability from post-inference heuristic tricks, which the authors argue has obscured true methodological contributions in the literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first comprehensive, open-source benchmark (65 datasets, >100GB) covering both edge-oriented (TSP, ATSP, CVRP) and node-oriented (MIS, MCl, MVC, MCut) CO problems under a single evaluation protocol, explicitly addressing the field's reproducibility crisis from heterogeneous datasets, inconsistent post-processing, and uncontrolled randomness. The finding that UL is competitive with or superior to SL on node-oriented problems (especially MCut) while SL dominates on edge problems provides actionable guidance for method selection. Open questions include whether the benchmark extends to time-window variants (TSPTW, CVRPTW), scheduling (JSSP), and whether the paradigm-model-learning taxonomy holds for divide-and-conquer or learning-to-search approaches that are currently excluded.
