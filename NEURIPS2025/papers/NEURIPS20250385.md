## Problem

Existing MLLM benchmarks evaluate spatial and temporal reasoning under offline settings with fixed-length pre-recorded inputs, failing to capture the online nature of embodied perception where an agent must incrementally process observations and maintain a growing memory of the scene. This paper asks whether current MLLMs can perform dynamic spatio-temporal reasoning—integrating current visual input with historical memory to track their own state and spatial relationships with objects—as they actively explore an environment.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250385/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a multi-round dialogue where each round appends 4–5 new egocentric RGB frames to the accumulated history, simulating a streaming video input that grows over time; a single text question is posed at the end of each round, grounded in the agent's current situation. Outputs span four formats: binary/multiple-choice judgments (e.g., left vs. right), exact counts, round indices for temporal localization, and floating-point estimates for distances or angles. All spatial references are relative (no absolute coordinate system), and questions are designed so that the answer cannot be derived from the current round's frames alone, forcing reliance on prior observations.

## Method

OST-Bench is constructed via a rule-based generation pipeline over 1.4k scenes from ScanNet, Matterport3D, and ARKitScenes, leveraging 9-DoF bounding box annotations from EmbodiedScan and semantic annotations from MMScan. For Matterport3D (which lacks continuous trajectories), synthetic exploration paths are generated by building a minimum spanning tree over camera viewpoints and performing a constrained random walk that enforces both path continuity and inter-frame visual overlap. Questions are organized into three categories (Agent State, Agent Visible Info, Agent-Object Spatial Relationship) with 15 fine-grained subtypes, and a visibility annotation system (attribute visibility via projected point-cloud area thresholds; spatial visibility via ≥5 visible bounding-box vertices) determines which objects can be referenced. Evaluation is conducted zero-shot in multi-turn dialogue across 13 proprietary and open-source MLLMs, supplemented by a cross-view analysis isolating single- vs. multi-step spatial reasoning and keyframe- vs. sequence-based memory retrieval, and a fine-tuning study on 50k in-domain samples to probe whether data scaling alone can close the performance gap.

## Results

On OST-Bench, the best-performing model (Gemini-2.0-Flash with Thinking mode) achieves 54.2% overall average accuracy, lagging behind the human baseline (83.5%) by nearly 30 percentage points. Performance is highly uneven across task categories: models score ~72% on Agent Visible Info (vs. 93.4% human) but drop to ~43% on Agent-Object Spatial Relationship (vs. 81.0% human), which is close to the chance level of 35.7%. Estimation questions are the hardest format for all models (24–29% vs. 56.9% human). Accuracy further declines as the exploration horizon extends, with spatial-reasoning tasks dropping to near chance within 2–4 turns.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among proprietary models, Gemini-2.0-Flash (Thinking) leads with 54.2% overall, outperforming GPT-4.1 (53.4%) by 0.8 points and Claude-3.5-Sonnet (47.8%) by 6.4 points; the Thinking mode specifically boosts Temporal Localization from 45.8% to 61.1% and Agent-Object Spatial from 33.8% to 42.8% over the base Gemini-2.0-Flash. All proprietary models substantially outperform open-source counterparts: the best open-source model (InternVL-2.5-78B, 51.1%) trails the best proprietary model by 3.1 points, while smaller open-source models (LLaVA-Video-7B at 39.3%, QwenVL-2.5-7B at 41.2%) fall below 45%. Across all 14 evaluated models, Agent-Object Spatial Relationship scores remain within 7 points of the chance baseline (35.7%), indicating that no current MLLM can reliably perform complex 3D spatial reasoning in an online setting. Fine-tuning on 50k in-domain samples yields >10% absolute gains (e.g., QwenVL-2.5-7B: 41.2→54.0; InternVL-2.5-38B: 50.8→60.2) but still leaves a 23+ point gap to human performance, and the gains on spatial-reasoning categories remain below 50%, suggesting that data scaling alone is insufficient to close the spatio-temporal reasoning gap.

## Relation to Prior Work

OST-Bench carves new ground at the intersection of two previously separate benchmark lineages: offline spatial reasoning benchmarks (ScanQA, SQA3D, VSI, CV-Bench) that evaluate 3D spatial understanding over fixed-length inputs, and online video understanding benchmarks (OVO-Bench, VideoLLM Online, HourVideo) that test streaming perception but largely limit spatial tasks to 2D relationships or short-term motion cues. Rather than building on a specific prior method, the paper repurposes existing scene datasets (ScanNet, Matterport3D, ARKitScenes) and their high-quality annotations (EmbodiedScan, MMScan) to construct an agent-centric, multi-round dialogue evaluation that simultaneously demands online incremental processing and complex 3D spatio-temporal reasoning — a combination absent from prior work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a fundamental capability gap: even the strongest MLLMs (GPT-4.1, Gemini-2.0-Flash Thinking) lag humans by ~30% overall, and spatial-reasoning subtasks collapse to near-chance within just 2–4 exploration turns, suggesting an architectural limitation in long-term memory retrieval and multi-step spatial chaining rather than a mere data shortage. The identification of 'Spatio-temporal Reasoning Shortcut' as a systematic failure mode provides a concrete diagnostic for future model development. Key open questions include whether improved memory architectures (e.g., explicit spatial memory modules) or training objectives can close the gap, and whether the static-scene assumption limits generalizability to dynamic real-world environments where object states change.
