## Problem

Existing graph OOD methods attempt to directly identify invariant subgraph edges, which is error-prone when spurious edges exhibit strong correlations with target labels, causing some invariant edges to be misclassified as unimportant. The paper argues that pruning the majority of uninformative spurious edges is a more reliable strategy than directly identifying the invariant subgraph, since most spurious edges have weak label correlations and are easier to eliminate.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251208/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an undirected graph represented by its adjacency matrix A ∈ {0,1}^{n×n} and node feature matrix X ∈ R^{n×D} as input. It produces a graph-level class label via a GNN encoder and classifier, along with a pruned subgraph where each edge is assigned a differentiable probability score via a Bernoulli-parameterized subgraph selector. The sampled adjacency matrix Ẑ is binary but differentiable through the Gumbel-Softmax reparameterization trick, enabling end-to-end gradient-based optimization of the pruning process.

## Method

PrunE regularizes a Gumbel-Softmax-based subgraph selector with two terms that act directly on edge probabilities without any additional OOD objective: (1) a graph size constraint L_e that penalizes deviation of the total sampled edge weight from a budget η|G| (η < 1), forcing the selector to prune uninformative edges while ERM's inductive bias naturally retains the strongly label-correlated invariant edges G_c; and (2) an ε-probability alignment L_s that pushes the predicted probabilities of the bottom K% edges toward a small value ε = 1/|E|, further suppressing residual spurious edges. The total loss is L_GT + λ₁L_e + λ₂L_s, where L_GT is standard cross-entropy. Theoretically, the paper proves that L_e provably shrinks |G_s| and tightens an OOD generalization bound, and that the optimal selector t*(G) in expectation retains only G_c.

## Results

On the synthetic GOODMotif-base dataset, PrunE achieves 91.48% accuracy, surpassing the best baseline AIA (72.91%) by 24.19 percentage points. On GOODMotif-size, PrunE reaches 66.53% versus AIA's 55.85% (a 19.13-point gap). Across 7 real-world datasets (GOODHIV, EC50, OGBG-Molbbbp), PrunE outperforms AIA by an average of 2.38% ROC-AUC while maintaining notably lower variance (e.g., ±0.40 on Motif-base vs. ±5.62 for AIA). PrunE also outperforms ERM by 10.86% under concept shift on Motif-base (90.28% vs. 81.44%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PrunE decisively outperforms all 15 baselines on 8 of 9 evaluation settings, with the largest margins on synthetic datasets: 91.48% vs. AIA's 72.91% on Motif-base (+18.57 pp) and 66.53% vs. 55.85% on Motif-size (+10.68 pp). Among graph-specific OOD methods that use subgraph selectors (GSAT, CIGA, AIA, DIR, GREA), PrunE consistently leads, with AIA being the closest competitor on real-world data (e.g., 67.56 vs. 64.71 on EC50-scaffold, 78.01 vs. 76.01 on EC50-assay). On EC50-size, AIA narrowly edges out PrunE (70.79 vs. 70.32), the only setting where PrunE does not achieve the top score. General-purpose OOD methods (IRM, VREx, GroupDRO) and data augmentation baselines (DropEdge, G-Mixup, FLAG, LiSA) are substantially outperformed, with many underperforming even plain ERM on real-world datasets. Under concept shift (Table 3), PrunE achieves 90.28% on Motif-base vs. 82.51% for AIA and 81.44% for ERM, confirming robustness beyond covariate shift. The paper also shows that using GIN instead of GCN as the encoder further improves PrunE's OOD performance across all four tested datasets, highlighting the benefit of more expressive architectures for the subgraph selector.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the subgraph selector architecture from DIR, GSAT, and PGExplainer, which model independent Bernoulli edge probabilities with Gumbel-Softmax sampling. However, it departs fundamentally from the dominant graph OOD paradigm that directly identifies invariant subgraphs (as in CIGA, AIA, GSAT) by instead proposing to prune spurious edges, leveraging ERM's inductive bias to naturally preserve informative edges. The conceptual motivation draws on the spurious-feature learning literature (Kirichenko et al., Chen et al.) showing that ERM captures all useful features, making pruning uninformative edges easier and more robust than explicitly identifying the invariant set.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a core assumption in graph OOD research—that directly identifying invariant subgraphs is the right objective—and provides both theoretical guarantees and empirical evidence that a simpler pruning-based approach preserves invariant structure more effectively. The 24% gap on synthetic benchmarks is striking, though real-world gains (~2.4% average) are more modest. Key limitations include reliance on ERM's inductive bias (precluding self-supervised settings), the assumption of a stable invariant subgraph, and inapplicability to node-level tasks. Open questions include extending the pruning paradigm to self-supervised learning, dynamic graphs, and scenarios where the invariant subgraph assumption is violated.
