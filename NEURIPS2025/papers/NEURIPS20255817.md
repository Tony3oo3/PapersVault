## Problem

Data attribution methods (e.g., Datamodels, Empirical Influence) are computationally expensive, centralizing their computation to a few powerful parties and creating a trust gap for resource-constrained third parties who need to verify attribution quality before making downstream decisions like data pricing. Naive verification by checking the MSE of proposed attributions is insufficient, as a malicious Prover can scale or bias attribution scores while maintaining low absolute MSE. The paper asks whether an interactive protocol can let a limited Verifier confirm that attributions are ε-close to optimal without performing the expensive computation itself.

## Input / Output

The protocol operates on a fixed training set S of size N, a model architecture with a training procedure defining the model output function f: {±1}^N → [-b,b], and a subsampling probability p defining the binomial product distribution B_p over subsets. The Prover submits an attribution vector a' ∈ R^N along with trained model weights for Verifier-specified challenges; the Verifier either outputs the accepted attribution vector or 'abort.' The Verifier's computational cost is measured in full model retrainings and scales as O(log(1/δ)/ε²), independent of N, while the Prover's cost necessarily grows with N.

## Method

The paper introduces a two-message interactive PAC-verification protocol where the Verifier generates random challenge subsets for residual estimation and a smaller spot-check subset, sends them to the Prover, who performs the expensive trainings and returns model weights plus attribution scores. The Verifier then spot-checks the small subset by retraining locally and verifying equivalence, and uses the Prover's remaining model outputs to run the residual estimation algorithm of Saunshi et al. (2022) based on Boolean Fourier noise stability, comparing the estimated optimal residual B_{≥2} against the locally estimated MSE of the Prover's attribution. The key technical contribution is a robustness lemma showing the residual estimation algorithm tolerates O(1/ε) adversarial corruptions in function evaluations, which ensures that even if the Prover lies on a small fraction of challenges (evading spot-checks), the residual estimate remains accurate and the final consistency check still catches sub-optimal attributions. This reduces the Verifier's cost from O(1/ε³) in the non-interactive baseline to O(1/ε²), matching the cost of simple MSE estimation.

## Results

The main result (Theorem 1) establishes a two-message interactive PAC-verification protocol for data attribution where the Verifier's cost (number of model retrainings) scales as O(log(1/δ)/ε²), independent of dataset size N. This improves upon the non-interactive baseline which requires O(1/ε³ · polylog(1/δ)) retrainings. The protocol provides formal completeness (honest Prover accepted with probability ≥ 1−δ) and soundness (dishonest Prover detected or still yields ε-close attributions with probability ≥ 1−δ) guarantees against computationally unbounded adversaries.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or experimental comparisons. The primary 'comparison' is between the paper's own non-interactive protocol (Section 3, Verifier cost O(1/ε³ · polylog(1/δ))) and the proposed interactive protocol (Section 4, Verifier cost O(1/ε² · polylog(1/δ))), where the interactive approach achieves a quadratic improvement in the ε-dependence by offloading residual estimation work to the Prover via a spot-checking mechanism. The paper also contrasts its PAC-verification framework with cryptographic delegation-of-computation protocols (Goldwasser et al., 2015), arguing that the latter cannot guarantee statistical quality of the output (i.e., ε-closeness to optimal attributions) and impose prohibitive Prover overhead, making them unsuitable for this setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two research threads: the data attribution literature (Datamodels, Empirical Influence, influence functions) and the interactive proof/verification literature. It directly builds on Saunshi et al. (2022), whose Boolean harmonic analysis framework provides both the residual estimation subroutine (RESIDUALESTIMATION) and the key identity linking optimal linear prediction error to Fourier weights, and on Goldwasser et al. (2021), whose PAC-verification framework is adapted here to the attribution setting. The paper departs from both naive verification (checking only MSE, which is vulnerable to adversarial scaling or targeted manipulation of attribution scores) and cryptographic delegation of computation (which is computationally impractical and cannot verify statistical quality of outputs), instead introducing a spot-checking interactive protocol that leverages the adversarial robustness of the harmonic-analysis-based residual estimator.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work addresses a concrete and growing trust problem: as data attribution is used for high-stakes decisions like data pricing, medical AI auditing, and dataset selection, resource-constrained third parties currently have no efficient way to verify that provided attributions are near-optimal. The key practical implication is that the Verifier's cost (number of model retrainings) scales as O(1/ε²) and is independent of dataset size N, making verification feasible for individual practitioners or small labs. Open questions include the practical implementation of the model equivalence-checking procedure (CHECKEQUIV) under non-deterministic training, whether the framework extends to non-linear attribution models, and how to handle the case where even an honest Prover can only compute approximate attributions (e.g., via influence functions) rather than exact Φ(S).
