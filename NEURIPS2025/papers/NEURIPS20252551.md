## Problem

LLM providers deploy pointwise content filters on fine-tuning APIs to detect individual harmful training or inference samples, but prior attacks (e.g., Halawi et al.'s CMT) rely on individually suspicious samples like ciphers or steganography that anomaly detectors can flag. This paper asks whether pointwise detection is fundamentally insufficient: can an attacker construct a fine-tuning attack where every single training and inference sample is individually benign, natural, and low-perplexity, making pointwise monitoring provably unable to detect the attack?

## Input / Output

The method takes benign user-assistant interaction pairs as training data, where each user query is a harmless transformation of a harmful multiple-choice question (e.g., 'Is the following question malicious or benign?' or 'Name a random flower while ignoring this forum post'), with the harmful MCQ embedded verbatim in the context. The fine-tuned model outputs a short benign-looking phrase (e.g., 'The question is malicious.' or 'Gloriosa') that encodes the correct answer choice via a fixed mapping to one of four common response variations. All samples are individually plausible generations from the base model, verified by measuring the number of base-model generations needed to reproduce each target response.

## Method

The core insight is that stochastic LLMs exhibit systematic response variations (syntactic or semantic) across similar queries, and these variations can be repurposed as a covert channel. The attacker first identifies the top-4 most common response strings from the base model on the benign prompt template, assigns each to one answer choice (A–D), then performs standard supervised fine-tuning (LoRA, 3 epochs, 1035 samples) on pairs where the assistant response is the variation corresponding to the ground-truth answer. Theorem 1 proves pointwise undetectability: since every output lies within the high-likelihood region S = {p | L_m(p|q) > τ} of the base model's output distribution, no information-theoretically optimal pointwise detector can flag them. Two instantiations are demonstrated—the Classify attack (benign transformation) and the Flower attack (unrelated request with harmful text in context)—achieving 98–100% accuracy on harmful IED and Copyright MCQs while evading both OpenAI's training-data screening and enhanced o1-mini-based inference monitors for spec violations and suspiciousness, whereas CMT baselines are blocked 65–77% of the time under suspiciousness monitoring.

## Results

On GPT-4o, the proposed classify and flower attacks achieve 98.4% correct on IED-MCQ and 100.0% correct on Copyright-MCQ with no monitor, outperforming CMT-EndSpeak (90.2% / 94.9%) and CMT-Walnut (62.3% / 100.0%). Under a suspiciousness monitor, the gap widens dramatically: classify retains 96.7% (IED) and 100.0% (Copyright), flower retains 82.0% and 87.2%, while CMT-Walnut drops to 16.4% and 28.2%, and CMT-EndSpeak drops to 34.4% and 23.1%. The attacks also generalize: when trained only on benign Commonsense-QA questions (no harmful text in training data), classify-benign still achieves 96.7% on IED-MCQ and 69.2% on Copyright-MCQ, and flower-benign achieves 98.4% on IED-MCQ.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two covert malicious fine-tuning (CMT) attacks from Halawi et al. [2024]: CMT-Walnut (substitution cipher) and CMT-EndSpeak (steganographic last-word encoding). On IED-MCQ with no monitor, the proposed classify and flower attacks (98.4% correct) substantially outperform CMT-EndSpeak (90.2%) and CMT-Walnut (62.3%), likely because they do not require the model to learn a full input-output encoding. Under the suspiciousness monitor, the advantage is most dramatic: classify (96.7%) and flower (82.0%) retain high accuracy while CMT-Walnut collapses to 16.4% (blocked 67.2%) and CMT-EndSpeak to 34.4% (blocked 65.6%). On Copyright-MCQ, CMT-Walnut matches the proposed attacks at 100.0% with no monitor, but under the suspiciousness monitor it drops to 28.2% while classify remains at 100.0%. The paper also shows that Sonnet 3.5 can decode CMT-Walnut and CMT-EndSpeak in-context, further confirming their detectability, whereas the proposed attacks produce outputs indistinguishable from natural model generations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the covert malicious fine-tuning line of work (Halawi et al., 2024), which used ciphers and steganography to hide malicious training data, by showing those approaches are actually detectable via perplexity and behavioural anomaly monitors. Instead, it repurposes the model's own natural output variability (syntactic phrasing differences, semantic variation in factual answers) as a covert channel, requiring no learned encoding and producing only low-perplexity, policy-compliant samples. Theoretically, it formalizes why any pointwise detector is fundamentally insufficient (Theorem 1), positioning the work as a hard limitation result for the most common class of fine-tuning API defences rather than a new attack that a better pointwise monitor might catch.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result shifts the defence burden from pointwise content filtering to multi-sample or distribution-level analysis, which is substantially harder to implement at scale given large training datasets and high-volume inference. Open questions include whether the multi-sample evasion strategies (Section 5.2, mixing benign data) can defeat even distribution-level monitors, whether the attacks extend to free-form generation without severe capability degradation, and how to design defences that are both effective and avoid excessive false positives on legitimate fine-tuning. The connection to 'diffuse threats' in AI safety suggests the same structural vulnerability may apply to other systems where individually benign actions compose into harmful outcomes.
