## Problem

Despite DPO's widespread adoption for LLM alignment, it remains unclear which properties of preference data actually drive performance: do chosen and rejected responses contribute symmetrically, how does contrastiveness between pairs affect learning, and under what conditions does on-policy data help? This paper provides a systematic theoretical and empirical answer to these questions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251488/cropped_page5_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis operates on preference triplets (x, y_w, y_l) where the chosen and rejected responses are drawn from marginal distributions π_w(·|x) and π_l(·|x) respectively, alongside a fixed reference model π_ref and an SFT-initialized policy π_θ. The theoretical output is a closed-form characterization of the optimal DPO policy as π_DPO(y|x) ∝ (π_w(y|x)/π_l(y|x))^(1/β) · π_ref(y|x), while the empirical output is a set of DPO-trained Llama-3.1-Tulu-3-8B checkpoints evaluated on five benchmarks (AlpacaEval-2, MMLU, IFEval, TruthfulQA, GSM8K) under controlled data-quality configurations.

## Method

The paper derives four key theoretical results: (1) a coverage theorem showing DPO cannot increase the likelihood of any response absent from the data support; (2) a characterization of the optimal DPO policy as a reweighting of the reference model by the chosen-to-rejected distribution ratio, revealing that chosen quality fundamentally bounds performance while rejected quality has limited impact; (3) an equivalence showing DPO implicitly learns a reward function r̃(y|x) = log(π_w/π_l), explaining why DPO may diverge from RLHF when the Bradley-Terry assumption fails; and (4) a proof that online DPO with fixed high-quality chosen samples and on-policy rejected samples reduces to SFT on the chosen responses plus a KL regularization term. Empirically, the authors construct controlled DPO datasets from Open Assistant 2 and UltraFeedback with systematically varied chosen/rejected quality tiers (scored by Skywork-Reward-Gemma-2-27B), demonstrating that improving chosen quality yields +7 to +9 point gains on LC-AE2 while varying rejected quality at fixed chosen quality produces negligible changes, and that widening the preference gap contributes only +0.8 to +1.4 points when chosen quality is held constant.

## Results

On UltraFeedback, DPO with Best/Worst pairs achieves 36.5 LC-AE2 (vs. 12.7 SFT baseline), 64.8 MMLU, 77.4 IFEval, 62.2 TruthfulQA, and 80.4 GSM8K. The key finding is asymmetric: fixing the chosen response at Best and varying rejected quality (Worst→High) yields only a 2.9-point LC-AE2 range (36.5→33.6), while fixing rejected at Worst and varying chosen quality (Low→Best) produces a monotonic 10.7-point LC-AE2 gain (25.8→36.5). In the controlled gap experiment (Table 3), improving chosen quality at constant gap yields +7.1 to +8.7 LC-AE2, versus only +3.0 to +4.6 from widening the gap at constant quality. Online-DPO with fixed chosen samples nearly matches Continual SFT (37.6 vs. 35.8 LC-AE2 on UltraFeedback), empirically confirming Theorem 4.5.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new DPO variant to benchmark against competing methods; instead, it compares different data configurations against each other and against the SFT baseline to isolate what drives DPO performance. Against the SFT baseline (12.7 LC-AE2 on UltraFeedback), all DPO configurations improve substantially, with Best/Worst reaching 36.5. The critical comparison is within-DPO: fixing chosen at Best and degrading rejected from Worst to High changes LC-AE2 by only 2.9 points (36.5→33.6), whereas fixing rejected at Worst and upgrading chosen from Low to Best yields a 10.7-point monotonic gain (25.8→36.5). In the controlled gap experiment, chosen quality improvement (+7.1 to +8.7 LC-AE2) outperforms gap widening (+3.0 to +4.6) by roughly 2×, and counterfactuals with identical chosen responses show gap changes contribute only +0.8 to +1.4 points. Online-DPO (37.6 LC-AE2) nearly matches Continual SFT (35.8) on UltraFeedback, confirming the theoretical reduction to SFT on chosen samples. On-policy data mixing (10–20%) boosts LC-AE2 by ~5 points only when base chosen quality is high (−0.93 avg score), but adds merely ~2 points when quality is low (−4.18), indicating exposure bias mitigation amplifies rather than compensates for quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the DPO framework (Rafailov et al., 2023) and its derivation from RLHF (Ouyang et al., 2022), but shifts focus from the optimization algorithm to the data distribution that drives it. While prior empirical work (Khaki et al., 2024; Gou and Nguyen, 2024) observed that larger preference gaps or best-vs-worst pairings help DPO, and on-policy DPO literature (Tajwar et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2024) noted distributional benefits, no prior work provided a unified theoretical characterization of *which* aspects of the preference data distribution actually determine the optimal policy. The paper departs from the standard BT-assumption analysis by deriving the DPO minimizer (Theorem 4.3) without requiring BT validity, and by showing that the widely adopted contrastiveness heuristic is effective primarily through its effect on chosen-sample quality rather than through margin maximization per se.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is significant: practitioners constructing DPO datasets should invest computational and annotation budget in improving chosen-response quality rather than in making rejected responses as bad as possible, and the benefit of rejection sampling comes from selecting better positives, not from the specific negative. The reduction of online DPO to SFT on chosen samples (Theorem 4.5) provides a clean theoretical justification for why iterative on-policy DPO works and when it might not (if chosen samples are not high quality). Limitations include reliance on a single 8B base model, a simplified online DPO setting where chosen samples are fixed, and the use of a reward model as a quality proxy rather than human judgment. Open questions include whether these findings hold for multi-turn preferences, for DPO variants like IPO or KTO, and for settings where the chosen and rejected distributions are not independently sampled.
