## Problem

Computing persistent homology on large-scale point clouds (millions of points) is infeasible with standard simplicial complexes: Vietoris-Rips complexes grow exponentially, and even the more scalable Alpha complex requires prohibitive memory and cubic-time PH computation. Existing workarounds—subsampling, sparse Rips approximations, or witness complexes—either irreversibly lose small topological features or become impractical at scale, leaving a gap between the topological information available in large point clouds and what can actually be computed.

## Input / Output

The method takes a Euclidean point cloud X (up to 16M points in 3D) and a small landmark subset L (typically 2k points selected via farthest point sampling). It produces persistence diagrams in dimensions H0, H1, and H2, which are vectorized into 192-dimensional feature vectors (64 per dimension via ATOL-style structure elements with 64 k-means++ centers) and fed to a LightGBM or logistic regression classifier. The key unusual aspect is that filtration values for each simplex in Del(L) are determined by the directed Hausdorff distance to the full point cloud X, not just to L, making the complex 'aware' of all points while only computing on the small landmark triangulation.

## Method

The Flood complex is a filtered simplicial complex on the Delaunay triangulation of a small landmark set L, where a simplex σ appears at radius r if its convex hull is fully covered by the union of balls of radius r centered on the full point cloud X (the 'flooding' process). This design decouples the combinatorial structure (small, from Del(L)) from the filtration values (informed by all of X), yielding a complex orders of magnitude smaller than Alpha(X) while preserving topological fidelity. Computationally, the directed Hausdorff distance for each simplex is approximated via a barycentric-coordinate grid (20–30 points per edge) and computed on GPU using a custom Triton kernel with a masking step that restricts distance evaluations to points within √2·r of each simplex's enclosing ball (via Ritter's heuristic). The paper proves bottleneck stability with respect to the point cloud, homotopy equivalence to the union of balls when L=X (in 2D), and a 2·d_H(X,L) approximation bound to Alpha PH, with the discretization error bounded by 1/m·√(Σ‖v_i−v_j‖²) for grid resolution m.

## Results

On the topologically complex synthetic swisscheese dataset, Flood PH achieves 98% balanced accuracy versus 85% for Alpha† PH and only 49–51% for all three neural network baselines (PointNet++, PointMLP, PVT). On the porous-materials rocks dataset, Flood PH reaches 88% classification accuracy and (0.5±0.2)×10⁻³ MSE for regression, while neural networks achieve only 51–54% accuracy and MSEs 17–63× larger. On real-world data, Flood PH outperforms Alpha† PH by 8–19 percentage points on corals (77% vs 58%) and mcb-c (74% vs 66%), but neural networks dominate on the geometrically simple modelnet10 (93–94% vs 72%). Runtime per point cloud is ~1–2 s for Flood PH versus 107–153 s for Alpha PH (full), a 50–150× speedup.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Alpha complex (Edelsbrunner 1993), Flood PH achieves substantially higher accuracy at comparable or lower runtime: on corals, Flood PH (77%) beats Alpha† PH (58%) by 19 points and Alpha PH (full) (44%) by 33 points, while running 85× faster than the full version. The averaged subsampling approach of Chazal et al. (2015), represented as Alpha† PH (avg.), is consistently outperformed by Flood PH across all five datasets (e.g., 77% vs 52% on corals, 74% vs 69% on mcb-c), confirming that the data-aware filtration of the Flood complex preserves topological information lost by subsampling. Against the three neural network baselines, the picture is dataset-dependent: on topologically complex data (swisscheese, rocks, corals), Flood PH dramatically outperforms PointNet++, PointMLP, and PVT (e.g., 98% vs 49–51% on swisscheese; 77% vs 53–59% on corals), while on geometrically simple modelnet10 the neural networks lead by ~20 points (93–94% vs 72%). On mcb-c, Flood PH (74%) is competitive with PointMLP (74%) and slightly below PVT (77%) and PointNet++ (76%), demonstrating that topological features remain informative even when objects have moderate geometric complexity.

## Relation to Prior Work

The Flood complex synthesizes ideas from two distinct lineages: the Delaunay-based Alpha complex (which provides a homotopy-equivalent combinatorial representation of the union of balls) and the Witness complex (which builds a smaller complex on a landmark subset). The key departure from both is that filtration values are computed against the full point cloud X rather than the landmark set L, making the complex 'aware' of the entire dataset while retaining the small size of a landmark-based construction. This contrasts with subsampling approaches (Chazal et al., 2015) where the complex is agnostic of the original data, and with sparse Rips or collapsing strategies that lose approximation guarantees at aggressive sparsification levels. The GPU-accelerated implementation via a custom Triton kernel with a principled masking procedure (Lemma 7) is a novel computational contribution not present in prior PH tooling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Flood complex removes a long-standing practical barrier: PH computation on point clouds with millions of points, which was previously infeasible due to the cubic complexity of matrix reduction on large filtered complexes. The homotopy equivalence proof (Theorem 3) is currently restricted to R², and the approximation guarantee (Corollary 4) involves a factor of 2 in the Hausdorff distance, leaving room for tighter bounds and higher-dimensional extensions. The empirical finding that PH-based methods outperform strong neural network baselines on topologically complex data (swisscheese, rocks, corals) suggests that scale-aware topological features capture information that learned representations miss, but this remains to be validated on larger and more diverse benchmarks. Open questions include whether the theoretical guarantees extend to arbitrary dimensions, whether tighter landmark-stability bounds exist, and whether the approach generalizes to non-Euclidean or dynamic point clouds.
