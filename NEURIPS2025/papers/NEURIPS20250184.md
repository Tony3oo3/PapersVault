## Problem

Preference-based reinforcement learning (PbRL) struggles in multi-stage tasks because comparing segments from mismatched stages (e.g., navigation vs. grasping) yields uninformative human feedback, a phenomenon the paper calls stage misalignment. The paper provides theoretical analysis showing that conventional cross-stage query selection requires quadratically more feedback than stage-aligned selection, especially under stage reward bias where humans inherently prefer certain stages over others.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250184/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes fixed-length state-action segments (length H) sampled from the current policy's trajectories, along with binary human preference labels indicating which of two segments is more task-relevant. The temporal distance model is trained on trajectory pairs via contrastive learning, while the reward model is trained on labeled segment pairs using the Bradley-Terry model. Outputs include the learned policy π(a|s), the reward model r̂_ψ, and a selection score I(σ₀,σ₁) that combines stage alignment with ensemble reward uncertainty to prioritize which segment pairs to query next.

## Method

STAIR addresses stage misalignment through two coupled components. First, it learns a temporal distance (successor distance) via contrastive learning with symmetrized infoNCE loss, grouping temporally close states into coherent stages without predefined task knowledge; this distance is updated on-policy at a configurable frequency K_SD to track policy evolution. Second, it defines a quadrilateral distance d_stage(σ₀,σ₁) that averages four temporal distances between the start and end states of two segments, capturing both endpoint alignment and temporal span. The final query selection score I(σ₀,σ₁) = (c_stage − d_stage)(c_state + d_state) multiplicatively combines stage alignment (smaller d_stage preferred) with reward model ensemble uncertainty (larger d_state preferred), ensuring that only informative, same-stage comparisons are sent to human labelers.

## Results

On MetaWorld multi-stage tasks (door-open, window-open, sweep-into, etc.), STAIR achieves success rates close to 100% with faster convergence than all baselines. On the door-open task with N_total=5000 feedback queries, STAIR reaches 100.00% success rate versus 85.57% for PEBBLE, and with only 500 queries, STAIR achieves 52.01% versus 20.00% for PEBBLE. On single-stage DMControl locomotion tasks, STAIR outperforms PEBBLE and remains competitive with other baselines. On RoboDesk multi-stage manipulation, STAIR similarly outperforms all baselines in success rate.

## Baselines & Comparisons

STAIR outperforms all five PbRL baselines (PEBBLE, RUNE, MRN, RIME, QPA) across all evaluated multi-stage tasks in MetaWorld and RoboDesk, achieving higher success rates and faster convergence. The most pronounced advantage is over PEBBLE: on door-open with 5000 queries, STAIR reaches 100% success while PEBBLE plateaus at 85.57%, and with only 500 queries the gap widens to 52% vs. 20%. On single-stage DMControl locomotion tasks, STAIR still outperforms PEBBLE and is competitive with RUNE, MRN, RIME, and QPA, suggesting the stage-aligned mechanism does not hurt performance when stages are not explicitly defined. STAIR approaches but does not exceed the SAC upper bound (ground-truth reward), which is expected since PbRL methods rely on learned reward estimates. The ablation in Table 1 confirms STAIR's robustness to hyperparameter choices (c_stage, c_state), with door-open success rates ranging only from 98.67% to 100.00% across configurations.

## Relation to Prior Work

STAIR repurposes the successor distance from Myers et al. (ICML 2024)—originally developed for goal-conditioned RL and exploration—as a stage difference approximator in preference-based RL, a setting where temporal distance had not previously been applied. The overall PbRL pipeline (Bradley-Terry reward model, experience relabeling, ensemble disagreement for query selection) follows PEBBLE (Lee et al., ICML 2021), but the key departure is the stage-aligned query selection criterion that filters out cross-stage comparisons. The contrastive learning objective (symmetrized infoNCE) is borrowed directly from van den Oord et al. (2018), and the theoretical framing draws on event segmentation theory from cognitive science to motivate why cross-stage comparisons are cognitively costly for human labelers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies and formally quantifies a practical bottleneck in PbRL for multi-stage tasks (common in robotics) that prior methods silently ignored, providing both theoretical bounds (quadratic vs. linear query complexity under stage reward bias) and empirical validation. The method's task-agnostic stage approximation via on-policy temporal distance avoids the need for expert-defined stage boundaries, making it broadly applicable. Key limitations include the quadrilateral distance being restricted to pairwise segment comparisons (incompatible with other preference formats like rankings or ratings), and the reliance on an oracle script teacher in experiments that may not reflect noisy real-world human feedback. Open questions include whether the implicit curriculum effect observed in single-stage tasks can be deliberately exploited, and whether the approach extends to non-pairwise or multi-objective preference settings.
