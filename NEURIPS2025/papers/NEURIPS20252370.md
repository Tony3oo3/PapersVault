## Problem

Existing multimodal reasoning models for chart understanding rely predominantly on text-based chain-of-thought, which cannot correct errors originating from flawed visual perception. When a model misreads a data point or misidentifies a trend line, purely textual reasoning has no mechanism to re-examine the visual evidence, leading to compounding errors on high-density charts with overlapping elements and dense numerical information.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252370/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a chart image and a natural-language question as input and produces a final text answer, but the reasoning process is structured as a multi-turn dialogue in which the model interleaves natural-language reasoning with drawing commands (pseudocode between BEGIN/END markers) that are rendered onto the chart image. Each rendered sketch is re-encoded as visual tokens and fed back to the model as a new user turn, forming a closed action-perception-reflection loop that terminates when the model stops emitting drawing code.

## Method

ChartSketcher introduces Sketch-CoT, a mechanism where an MLLM (initialized from Qwen2VL) annotates intermediate reasoning steps directly onto the chart via a lightweight programmatic sketching library supporting points, lines, circles, arrows, rectangles, and their transformations in normalized coordinates. A visual feedback pipeline parses the model's drawing commands, renders them, and re-encodes the annotated image as new visual tokens for the next reasoning step, enabling the model to visually verify and correct its own work. Training follows a two-stage strategy: a cold-start phase that distills sketch-based reasoning and reflective self-correction patterns from a 32B LLM into the MLLM using 300K synthetic samples (with 1:1 mixing of reflective and non-reflective data trained via RPO), followed by an off-policy RL phase using Sketch-MCTS—a modified Monte Carlo Tree Search that collects step-level positive/negative contrasts from unlabeled data—and KTO optimization on 50K sampled trajectories.

## Results

ChartSketcher-72B achieves 85.20 on ChartQA-H, 92.64 on ChartQA-A, 63.28 on EvoChart-QA, 68.33 on ChartBench, and 76.72 on PlotQA, outperforming all proprietary and open-source baselines on chart-specific benchmarks. On the general VisualCoT composite score, it reaches 76.59, slightly below GPT-4o (78.45) and InternVL2.5-78B (78.93) but above its Qwen2VL-72B base (72.14). The 2B variant (ChartSketcher-2B) improves over Qwen2VL-2B by +5.12 on ChartQA-H, +5.04 on ChartQA-A, and +8.53 on VisualCoT, while also surpassing the chart-expert model ChartGemma-2B on ChartBench (30.10 vs. 23.87) and PlotQA (41.12 vs. 25.76).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ChartSketcher-72B outperforms GPT-4o on all five chart-specific benchmarks (e.g., +10.48 on EvoChart-QA, +33.76 on PlotQA) but trails it on the general VisualCoT composite (76.59 vs. 78.45). Against QvQ-Preview-72B, ChartSketcher wins decisively on ChartBench (68.33 vs. 42.40) and EvoChart-QA (63.28 vs. 54.32) while essentially tying on VisualCoT (76.59 vs. 76.52). Compared to its base Qwen2VL-72B, ChartSketcher gains +2.72 on ChartQA-H, +4.08 on ChartQA-A, and +13.56 on ChartBench. On the ChartX benchmark, ChartSketcher surpasses ChartVLM by +12.47 (strict SE) and +13.10 (QA). In the direct ReFocus comparison on its own test set, ChartSketcher-phi3.5-4.2B edges out ReFocus-phi3.5-4.2B on both chartqa-vbar (74.35 vs. 72.20) and chartqa-hbar (72.30 vs. 71.00). Against chart-expert models, ChartSketcher-72B achieves 85.20 on ChartQA-H, far exceeding OneChart (49.10) and ChartAssistant (65.90), though it slightly trails ChartAssistant on ChartQA-A (92.64 vs. 93.90).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior multimodal CoT approaches (VisualCoT, Refocus, SketchPad) that rely on image cropping to focus on key regions, arguing that cropping inherently limits simultaneous analysis of multiple visual elements. Instead, ChartSketcher introduces a programmatic sketching library that lets the MLLM draw annotations directly onto the chart and receive the rendered result as visual feedback, creating a closed action-perception-reflection loop. The training pipeline synthesizes ideas from cross-modal distillation (transferring LLM reasoning patterns to MLLMs via EvoChart-Corpus annotations) and off-policy RL (adapting MCTS into Sketch-MCTS for step-level contrastive sampling), drawing on the RL-for-reasoning paradigm established by DeepSeek-R1 and QvQ but applying it to a visual-feedback setting rather than purely textual CoT.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ChartSketcher offers a genuinely interpretable and interactive alternative to black-box chart QA: users can inspect each sketch step and see where the model's visual grounding succeeds or fails, which is valuable for trust-critical data-analysis applications. The finding that the 2B variant achieves substantial gains over its baseline (e.g., ChartQA-H 55.60 vs 50.48) while retaining general vision capabilities suggests the sketch-feedback mechanism is not merely a scaling artifact. Key open questions include the significant inference-speed penalty (output throughput drops from 685 to 329 tokens/s at 72B scale), the lack of formal convergence guarantees for the sketch-feedback loop, and whether the approach extends to 3D or dynamic visualizations. The paper also acknowledges persistent issues with ineffective self-reflection and overthinking that cap practical reliability.
