## Problem

Virtual furniture synthesis for home design suffers from two critical gaps: the absence of a publicly available, ready-to-use benchmark with realistic reference-background pairs, and the inability of existing image composition methods (encoder-based and dual U-Net) to meet the stringent geometric and visual fidelity requirements of practical furniture placement. Conventional dual-branch architectures process reference and background images independently, leading to inconsistent intermediate features that degrade synthesis quality with copy-paste artifacts and misalignments.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251099/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a masked background image and a masked reference image, both at 512×512 resolution, and produces a single composited image where the reference furniture is seamlessly integrated into the masked region of the background. Masks can range from precise pixel-level annotations (used in training) to coarse bounding boxes (used in testing to simulate imperfect user-provided segmentation), and the model is robust to both. The inpainting U-Net receives a 9-channel input (noisy image, masked background, mask) while the shared reference branch processes a 4-channel input, and the final output is a full-resolution RGB image.

## Method

RoomEditor replaces the conventional dual U-Net with a single parameter-sharing U-Net that simultaneously extracts reference features and performs background inpainting, eliminating the need for auxiliary image encoders (CLIP, DINO). Reference features are computed via self-attention at mid- and upper-attention layers, then injected into background features through a mixture attention mechanism that concatenates background and reference features for the key and value projections while using background features alone for the query. The key insight is that sharing weights between the two branches enforces a unified feature space, so the reference features naturally align with the background feature representations at every layer, reducing the ℓ₂ gap to ground-truth features compared to frozen or independently trained reference U-Nets. Training follows standard diffusion denoising (minimizing noise prediction error) on the RoomBench dataset of 7,298 furniture-background pairs, initialized from Stable Diffusion 1.5 inpainting weights, with mask and image augmentations to improve robustness.

## Results

On RoomBench, RoomEditor achieves FID 18.42, SSIM 0.793, PSNR 21.15, LPIPS 0.094, CLIP 90.51, and DINO 85.47, outperforming the re-trained MimicBrush† (FID 21.12, LPIPS 0.106) and all other baselines on reconstruction and perceptual metrics. In the user study, RoomEditor is ranked best in 56.6% of fidelity trials, 43.9% of harmony trials, and 53.6% of quality trials, substantially ahead of MimicBrush* (29.4%, 30.5%, 38.3%). On the unseen DreamBooth dataset, RoomEditor achieves FID 68.78 versus 77.16 for MimicBrush and 108.57 for PBE, demonstrating strong zero-shot generalization without fine-tuning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RoomEditor outperforms PBE [53] and AnyDoor [6] across all six metrics in every evaluation setting, with the largest gaps on FID (18.42 vs. 26.85/26.62 for fine-tuned versions) and LPIPS (0.094 vs. 0.136/0.135), indicating substantially better perceptual realism. Against the closest competitor MimicBrush [7], RoomEditor wins on FID (18.42 vs. 19.04 fine-tuned, 21.12 re-trained), SSIM (0.793 vs. 0.791/0.785), PSNR (21.15 vs. 20.44/20.39), and LPIPS (0.094 vs. 0.098/0.106), but trails the fine-tuned MimicBrush★ by a narrow margin on CLIP (90.51 vs. 90.87) and DINO (85.47 vs. 85.65). In the user study, RoomEditor dominates all three perceptual criteria, while MimicBrush★—despite its slight edge on CLIP/DINO—ranks second in every category. Against GPT-4o [34] on a 200-sample subset, RoomEditor achieves FID 44.78 vs. 72.12 and LPIPS 0.094 vs. 0.290, showing GPT-4o's weakness in background preservation and fine-grained object fidelity, though GPT-4o remains competitive on DINO (83.30 vs. 85.12). On the unseen 3D-FUTURE dataset, RoomEditor (FID 13.91) marginally beats MimicBrush (14.20) without any fine-tuning, confirming the generalization advantage of the parameter-sharing design.

## Relation to Prior Work

RoomEditor directly extends the dual U-Net + mixture attention architecture of MimicBrush (Chen et al., NeurIPS 2025) by replacing the separate frozen reference U-Net with a single parameter-sharing U-Net, so that reference feature extraction and background inpainting operate in a unified feature space. It also departs from encoder-based composition methods (PBE, AnyDoor) that rely on external CLIP or DINO encoders to inject reference features, instead leveraging the shared U-Net's own attention layers to achieve the same function without additional modules. On the data side, the paper positions RoomBench as the first ready-to-use open benchmark for furniture synthesis, filling a gap left by proprietary datasets (Amazon's Diffuse-to-Choose) and 3D model collections (InteriorNet, 3D-FUTURE, 3D-FRONT) that require non-trivial scene setup to produce usable reference-background pairs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

RoomBench provides the community with a reproducible, annotated benchmark (7,298 training pairs, 895 test samples, 27 categories) that was previously unavailable, enabling fair comparison and iterative research in furniture synthesis. The parameter-sharing insight, while architecturally simple, offers a principled explanation for why dual-branch methods suffer from feature misalignment and provides a drop-in improvement for any dual U-Net composition pipeline. Key open questions include whether the approach extends to DiT-based diffusion backbones, how to handle long-tail furniture categories with high intra-class geometric variation, and how to model shadows and illumination effects outside the masked region—limitations the authors acknowledge but leave for future work.
