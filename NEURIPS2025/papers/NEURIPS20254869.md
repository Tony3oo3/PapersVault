## Problem

Existing inference scaling methods for LLMs rely on static decomposition strategies—fixed token-level, sentence-level, or single-step partitions—where step sizes are predetermined or designed via domain-specific heuristics. This rigidity wastes compute on steps that are easy for the model while undersampling genuinely challenging steps, and identifying which steps are critical is non-trivial even for humans. The paper asks whether decomposition granularity can be adapted automatically during inference based on the model's own feedback, without hand-crafted rules or process reward models.

## Input / Output

DISC takes a text problem prompt, an autoregressive LLM policy π, and a scalar outcome reward model R (ground-truth tests, numerical verification, or self-generated unit tests) as inputs. It produces a complete solution (code or math answer) structured as a sequence of committed prefix steps, each annotated with a z-score reflecting its importance. The algorithm is parameterized by an initial partition fraction α₀ (default 0.15), a sampling threshold σ (default 1.0), and a total sample budget N, and it can be paired with greedy, beam, or MCTS search strategies as a plug-and-play expansion operator.

## Method

DISC iteratively refines a base prefix by proposing candidate extensions of length α·|best_suffix|, sampling completions from each, and comparing their reward z-scores (max − mean)/std. A candidate is accepted if its z-score is lower than the base's—under a location-scale distribution assumption, a lower z-score implies a higher tail probability and thus greater potential for improvement. If rejected, the partition fraction contracts (α ← α₀·α), forcing finer-grained proposals around the same region. This accept-or-contract loop continues until the sample budget is exhausted or a correct solution is found, yielding a solution composed of a small number of high-impact committed steps. The decomposition operator is decoupled from the search strategy, making it directly composable with greedy search, beam search, or MCTS, where each node stores its own base prefix and the EXPANDNODE function performs the same z-score-based acceptance logic locally.

## Results

On APPS (competition level), MATH500, and LiveCodeBench, DISC reduces the pass@10 error proportion from 0.50→0.475, 0.15→0.14, and 0.57→0.51 respectively, corresponding to 5.0%, 6.7%, and 10.5% relative error reductions over the best static baseline. With DeepSeek-R1 on APPS, DISC improves accuracy by over 85% relative to the base model using only 10 samples, and still achieves 33% relative improvement under a token budget matched to a single base-model sample. DISC also yields up to 4× accuracy gains over base models (e.g., LLaMA pass@10: 0.01→0.04) and requires only 1–5 committed partition steps versus significantly more for token- or line-split methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DISC consistently outperforms all three static decomposition baselines—BoN (single-step, Snell et al.), TokenSplit (token-level, Feng et al.), and LineSplit (newline/sentence-level, Hao et al.; Yao et al.)—across all three benchmarks under both fixed token and fixed sample budgets, with the largest gains (10.5%) on the hardest benchmark (LiveCodeBench). Among search strategies paired with DISC, MCTS (Light et al., Scattered Forest) outperforms beam search (Xie et al.) by allocating its budget more strategically to promising candidates, while greedy search explores deeper but irrevocably commits to prefixes. DISC also substantially improves weaker open-source models (LLaMA, Mistral, Qwen) by 79–300% relative, and achieves a 54% relative improvement over the base model even under noisy self-generated validation tests. The z-score-based acceptance criterion (DISC-Z) is critical: the inverted variant (DISC-negZ) performs worse than random selection, confirming that prioritizing prefixes with higher tail-probability of improvement is essential.

## Relation to Prior Work

DISC departs from the static decomposition strategies that dominate inference scaling—token-level, sentence-level, and single-step (best-of-n) generation—by making step boundaries adaptive and data-driven. It repurposes the z-score statistic from sampled reward distributions as a criterion for where to subdivide, replacing domain-specific heuristics (Tree of Thoughts, PARSEL, least-to-most prompting) and fixed delimiters with a recursive, budget-aware refinement loop. The method is explicitly positioned as a plug-and-play decomposition operator that composes with existing search frameworks (greedy, beam, MCTS) rather than proposing a new search algorithm, and it draws on the observation from prior work that transition tokens ('which', 'therefore') serve as critical autoregressive decision points.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DISC offers a general, supervision-free decomposition mechanism that can be dropped into any inference-scaling pipeline, reducing the need for hand-crafted step boundaries or process reward models. Its ability to identify high-impact reasoning pivots automatically has downstream implications for curriculum learning, fine-tuning data selection, and dataset augmentation. Key limitations include dependence on a reasonably accurate scalar reward model, restriction to single-turn generation, and diminished gains on trivial or non-compositional tasks. Open directions include extending to multi-turn/interactive settings, integrating learned verifiers to reduce reward-model dependence, and exploring joint reasoning-evaluation co-evolution.
