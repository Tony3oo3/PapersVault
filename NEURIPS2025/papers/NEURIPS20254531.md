## Problem

Existing diffusion-based recommenders rely on continuous Gaussian or Bernoulli noise priors that are intrinsically mismatched with the discrete, sparse nature of user-item interaction data. Item-level methods overlook negative signals, while preference-score-level methods face optimization difficulties from probability simplex constraints. This paper asks whether a fully discrete diffusion process that directly models preference ratios—without any prior noise assumption—can better capture user preferences for ranking.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254531/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a user's interaction sequence (encoded by a SASRec backbone into a user embedding) and a discrete item corpus of size N. Rather than producing a continuous score vector over items, it estimates log-ratio preference scores s_Θ(x_t, t, u)_y for every item y ∈ X at each diffusion timestep, then iteratively grows preferences backward to yield a final ranking from which top-K items are selected. The personalization strength w is a free inference-time parameter that interpolates between user-specific and non-preference-user signals without retraining.

## Method

PreferGrow replaces the standard continuous-noise forward process with a discrete 'preference fading' step: at each timestep t, the preferred item x_0 is retained with probability α_t or replaced according to an idempotent fading matrix E (E²=E), physically analogous to negative sampling. The key theoretical result is that idempotence of E guarantees both the Chapman-Kolmogorov (Markov) property and invertibility of the transition matrices, enabling a well-defined reverse 'preference growing' process. Training uses the score entropy loss to match a neural network's estimated preference ratios against analytically computed reference ratios derived from the fading process; the paper proves this loss shares the same gradient direction and optima as a soft-label binary cross-entropy loss, linking it to the Bradley-Terry preference model. By parameterizing the rank-1 fading matrix, PreferGrow flexibly supports point-wise, pair-wise, and hybrid preference-ratio variants corresponding to distinct negative-sampling strategies, and introduces a non-preference user embedding for personalized contrast during inference.

## Results

PreferGrow (Hybrid variant) achieves the best performance across all five benchmark datasets. On MoviesLens, it reaches HR@10 of 0.2229 and NDCG@10 of 0.1174, outperforming the strongest classical baseline SASRec (0.1709 / 0.0760) by ~30% and ~54% respectively. On Steam, PreferGrow attains HR@10 of 0.0935 and NDCG@10 of 0.0508, roughly doubling the performance of SASRec (0.0572 / 0.0272). The Adaptive variant yields marginally higher scores on most datasets (e.g., MoviesLens HR@20: 0.3362 vs. 0.3355 for Hybrid), confirming the robustness of the framework.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PreferGrow (Hybrid) consistently outperforms all seven baselines across the five datasets. Against classical recommenders, the gains are substantial: on MoviesLens, PreferGrow achieves HR@10 of 0.2229 versus SASRec's 0.1709 (+30%) and Caser's 0.1605 (+39%). Among diffusion-based recommenders, PreferGrow dominates item-level methods (DreamRec, PreferDiff) by wide margins—e.g., on Steam, HR@10 of 0.0935 vs. DreamRec's 0.0157 and PreferDiff's 0.0297. It also surpasses preference score-level diffusion methods (DiffRec, DDSR) on nearly all metrics; on Beauty, PreferGrow reaches NDCG@10 of 0.0358 versus DDSR's 0.0262 and DiffRec's 0.0094. The closest competitor is DDSR on Toys (NDCG@10: 0.0332 vs. 0.0356) and Sports (HR@10: 0.0213 vs. 0.0267), where PreferGrow still leads but by a narrower margin. The Adaptive variant trades a small amount of performance on Steam and Sports for slightly better results on MoviesLens, confirming that the Hybrid setting is the most robust default.

## Relation to Prior Work

PreferGrow extends the discrete diffusion modeling framework (SEDD, CSM, D3PM, LDR) to sequential recommendation by replacing the standard absorbing/uniform transition matrices with an idempotent 'fading matrix' that physically corresponds to negative sampling. It departs from existing diffusion-based recommenders (DreamRec, PreferDiff, DiffRec, DDSR) in two key ways: it operates directly on the discrete item set rather than continuous embeddings or one-hot preference vectors, and it models log-ratio preference scores (Bradley-Terry style) rather than simplex-constrained probabilities. The score entropy loss from SEDD is adopted as the training objective, and the paper proves a gradient-equivalence between this loss and soft-label binary cross-entropy, bridging the discrete diffusion literature with standard ranking losses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a theoretically clean unification of negative sampling and discrete diffusion for recommendation, with provable Markov and reversibility properties that prior diffusion-based recommenders lack. However, the O(N) per-step complexity (where N is corpus size) limits practical deployment to very large catalogs, and the authors themselves identify rank-r fading matrices and Semantic ID quantization as necessary extensions. The single-seed evaluation (no error bars) and the observation that the final 50% of training yields only ~5% NDCG gain suggest the empirical gains, while consistent, may be somewhat overstated. Open questions include whether the preference-ratio formulation's higher expressiveness justifies its increased training cost, and whether curriculum scheduling over timesteps can close the efficiency gap.
