## Problem

Standard PAC learning methods like Empirical Risk Minimization provably fail when only positive examples are observed (one-sided feedback), as there is no way to estimate precision loss without knowing which labels are incorrect. This paper introduces a PAC framework for set-valued predictions under partial information—where each training example reveals only a single label sampled uniformly from its ground-truth set—and establishes sharp statistical and algorithmic separations from full-information settings, including impossibility of additive regret in the agnostic case.

## Input / Output

The algorithm receives m IID pairs (x_i, v_i) where x_i is drawn from an unknown distribution D over input space X, and v_i is a single label sampled uniformly at random from the ground-truth set g_target(x_i). The output is a set-valued hypothesis g: X → 2^Y that maps each input to a set of labels, evaluated by expected precision loss (fraction of output items not in the ground-truth set) and recall loss (fraction of ground-truth items missing from the output). The critical constraint is that the learner never observes the full ground-truth set or any negative labels, making direct estimation of precision loss impossible.

## Method

In the realizable setting, the paper proposes two algorithms with O(log(|H|/δ)/ε) sample complexity: (1) a maximum likelihood method that, among all hypotheses consistent with the training data, returns the one minimizing the sum of log output sizes, thereby penalizing over-prediction and bounding precision loss; and (2) a surrogate loss approach that defines a metric d_H between hypotheses via counterfactual label-distribution vectors, which upper- and lower-bounds the scalar (average precision-recall) loss within constant multiplicative factors. In the agnostic setting, the maximum likelihood method is modified by first filtering to hypotheses with recall loss at most r+2ε, then minimizing a truncated log output size objective to limit the influence of inputs with very large ground-truth sets. The paper also proves that additive error is impossible (requiring multiplicative factors ≥1.05 for scalar loss) and characterizes the Pareto-loss frontier, showing (p,r) ⇒ (5(p+r), r) is achievable while (p+0.01, r+0.01) is not.

## Results

In the realizable setting, the paper achieves both precision and recall loss ≤ ε with O(log(|H|/δ)/ε) samples (Theorem 1), matching the optimal PAC sample complexity. In the agnostic setting, a 5-approximate scalar loss is achievable (Theorem 4), while a 1.05-approximation is provably impossible (Theorem 2), leaving a gap between 1.05 and 5. For the Pareto-loss objective, the paper shows (p,r) ⇒ (5(p+r), r) is achievable but (p,r) ≠ (p+0.01, r+0.01) (Theorem 3). In the semi-realizable setting, zero precision loss is achievable with O(log(|H|/δ)/Δ²_D) samples when the target's output size is bounded (Theorem 5), but is impossible without this bound (Theorem 6).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or experimental comparisons. The key 'comparison' is against the standard PAC learning paradigm: the paper proves that Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM) provably fails even for simple hypothesis classes, since the complete set function (outputting all labels) is always consistent with positive-only training data yet can incur high precision loss. The paper further establishes that additive regret (the standard PAC guarantee) is impossible in the agnostic setting, requiring multiplicative approximation factors instead. The two proposed algorithms—maximum likelihood estimation and surrogate loss minimization—are the first to achieve optimal sample complexity in the realizable case and constant-factor guarantees in the agnostic case for this one-sided feedback model. The open gap between the upper bound (α=5) and lower bound (α=1.05) for the optimal multiplicative factor remains unresolved.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of PAC learning, multi-label learning, and positive-example learning by formalizing a fundamentally different feedback model: the learner observes only a single uniformly-sampled positive label per input (rather than the full label set as in multi-label learning, or the complete ground-truth set as in standard PAC learning). It departs from the multi-label learning literature (Gao & Zhou 2011, Menon et al. 2019, Mao et al. 2024) which assumes access to all correct labels, and from positive-example learning (Denis 1998, Bekker & Davis 2020) which is restricted to binary classification. The ERM failure result connects to a broader line of work showing ERM's limitations in multi-class classification (Daniely et al. 2015), density estimation (Devroye & Lugosi 2001), and partial concept learning (Alon et al. 2022), while the surrogate loss approach draws on distribution learning via total variation distance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that standard tools (ERM, additive-regret guarantees) fundamentally break down under one-sided set-valued feedback, providing sharp impossibility results (additive regret is impossible; the optimal multiplicative factor lies between 1.05 and 5) and matching upper bounds via maximum-likelihood and surrogate-loss algorithms. The semi-realizable setting result—that achieving zero precision loss requires sample complexity depending on the target's output size—highlights a structural limitation with direct implications for applications like photo tagging and recommendation where output sets can be large. Major open questions remain: closing the 5-vs-1.05 gap, identifying a combinatorial dimension (analogous to VC dimension) that characterizes learnability, and extending beyond the counting measure to richer precision-recall notions.
