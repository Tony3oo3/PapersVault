## Problem

Reasoning chains distilled from large reasoning models (LRMs) like DeepSeek-R1 and QwQ contain verbose functional elements—verification, multi-method validation, and error correction—that mirror human problem-solving but inflate inference cost without proportionally improving accuracy. Existing pruning methods either treat all reasoning steps uniformly or rely on prompting strategies, risking removal of critical progressive reasoning that forms the essential solution backbone. The paper asks: can we quantitatively identify which functional steps are truly low-value and selectively remove them while preserving the core deductive path?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255246/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes question-reasoning-answer triplets (q, r, a) where r is a sequence of intermediate reasoning steps and a is the final answer. It outputs a refined reasoning chain r' that is strictly shorter than r while preserving answer correctness. The segmentation produces multi-sentence logical units (not single sentences), and the pruning operates at the step level with a configurable ratio threshold (0.2–0.8) controlling how aggressively low-PIR functional steps are removed. The refined chains are then used as SFT training data for downstream models.

## Method

PIR (Perplexity-based Importance Refinement) operates in three phases: (1) hierarchical decomposition of reasoning chains into coherent logical steps via Claude 3.7 Sonnet, followed by hybrid classification into four cognitive patterns (progressive reasoning, verification, multi-method validation, error correction) using rule-based phrase matching plus LLM contextual analysis; (2) computation of a PIR score for each functional step, defined as the log-ratio of answer-token perplexity with versus without that step, calculated using Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct; (3) pattern-specific selective pruning that removes only the lowest-PIR functional steps according to a ratio threshold while preserving all progressive reasoning steps intact. The key distinction from prior work (e.g., SPIRIT) is this discriminative treatment: progressive reasoning is never pruned, and among functional steps, importance is quantified by actual impact on answer confidence rather than uniform perplexity thresholds. Models fine-tuned on the resulting PIR-optimized datasets (LIMO-P, LIMO-V2-P, S1K-P) achieve +0.9% to +6.6% accuracy with −3% to −41% token reduction across AIME, AMC, and GPQA Diamond.

## Results

PIR-optimized models consistently improve accuracy while reducing token usage across all three benchmarks. On AIME, LIMO-P achieves 63.3% accuracy (+6.6 over LIMO) with 15% fewer tokens, and S1-32B-P reaches 42.1% (+4.2) with 29% fewer tokens. On GPQA Diamond, LIMO-V2-P attains 74.2% accuracy (+3.0) with 13% token reduction, while S1-32B-P achieves the largest efficiency gain of +71% (2.49E-04 vs. 1.46E-04) with 41% fewer tokens. Across all dataset families, efficiency (ACC/TOK) improves by 7%–71% without sacrificing accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the unrefined baselines (S1-32B, LIMO, LIMO-V2), PIR-optimized variants win on every benchmark across all three dataset families, gaining +0.9 to +6.6 percentage points in accuracy while reducing tokens by 3%–41%. Compared to SPIRIT (S1-SPIRIT), which applies uniform perplexity-based pruning to all steps, PIR (S1-32B-P) achieves a 5.0-point accuracy advantage on AIME (42.1 vs. 37.1) and a 1.5-point gain on GPQA (61.6 vs. 60.1), confirming that preserving progressive reasoning steps is critical. Against Prompt Reduction (S1-PROMPT), PIR dominates on accuracy across all benchmarks (42.1 vs. 36.7 on AIME, 83.1 vs. 72.5 on AMC, 61.6 vs. 58.0 on GPQA) while also using fewer tokens on AIME and GPQA. The rule-based ablation (S1-RULE), which removes functional steps randomly without PIR scoring, underperforms PIR on AIME (36.7 vs. 42.1) and GPQA (58.1 vs. 61.6), validating that perplexity-guided selective pruning outperforms indiscriminate category-based removal. LIMO-V2-P reaches 74.2% on GPQA Diamond, surpassing the QwQ reference (70.2%) while using 7% fewer tokens, and 71.2% on AIME, approaching R1-Distill-Qwen-32B (69.2%) with 12% shorter responses.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LIMO/S1 data-distillation paradigm for test-time scaling by introducing a data-refinement layer that operates on the reasoning chains before fine-tuning. Its closest conceptual predecessor is SPIRIT, which also uses perplexity to prune reasoning steps, but PIR departs fundamentally by first classifying steps into progressive reasoning versus functional patterns (verification, multi-method validation, error correction) and then applying perplexity-based importance scoring only to functional steps, preserving the deductive backbone intact. The cognitive pattern taxonomy draws on recent work characterizing LLM reasoning behaviors, and the selective-pruning philosophy contrasts with indiscriminate compression methods like C3ot or TokenSkip that treat all tokens/steps uniformly.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for deploying reasoning LLMs in latency-sensitive settings: PIR-optimized models achieve 3–41% token reduction while simultaneously improving accuracy by 0.9–6.6% across AIME, AMC, and GPQA Diamond, validated across three distillation sources and model sizes from 3B to 32B. The counterintuitive finding that removing verification and error-correction steps from training data *improves* downstream accuracy challenges the assumption that human-like metacognitive patterns in LRM outputs are beneficial for fine-tuning. Open questions include generalization beyond math/science to logical and commonsense reasoning, whether perplexity alone captures semantic step importance, and whether adaptive (rather than fixed-ratio) pruning thresholds can be learned per problem difficulty.
