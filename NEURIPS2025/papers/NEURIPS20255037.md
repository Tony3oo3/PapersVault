## Problem

Responsible AI metrics are frequently mathematically incompatible—impossibility theorems show no nontrivial predictor can satisfy multiple fairness definitions simultaneously, and structural trade-offs exist between accuracy, privacy, and interpretability—yet conventional practice treats these contradictions as bugs to be patched by selecting a single 'right' metric or deriving consistency constraints. No existing framework explains how to practically navigate and document these inconsistencies while preserving the diverse stakeholder values they encode. The paper argues that enforcing theoretical consistency risks narrowing value diversity, losing conceptual depth, and degrading model generalization.

## Input / Output

The proposed protocol takes an ML model evaluated against a suite of potentially conflicting RAI metrics (e.g., demographic parity, equalized odds, ε-differential privacy) along with stakeholder value specifications and an evaluation dataset, and produces a structured documentation package rather than a single scalar compliance score. The output is a per-metric Metric Provenance Sheet (stating what aspect of the concept it measures, what it omits, and which stakeholder values it reflects), an inconsistency profile quantified along four axes—magnitude (gap/ratio), directionality (cross-regime pairwise correlation), locality (slice-level contrast relative to the full domain), and sensitivity (variation under alternative labeling or features)—and a SPHERE-organized evaluation card with pre-registered tolerance bands, change logs, and versioned drift monitoring.

## Method

The paper formalizes two types of metric inconsistency—intra-concept (variants of the same ideal collide, e.g., three fairness definitions) and inter-concept (distinct ideals compete, e.g., accuracy vs. privacy)—and argues each serves three purposes: encoding pluralistic human values, preserving the full information content of fuzzy concepts via Wittgenstein's family-resemblance logic, and acting as implicit regularization through gradient conflict that narrows the train-validation gap. The practical mechanism leverages the Rashomon set: by relaxing perfect satisfaction to approximate satisfaction within a near-optimal region, jointly inconsistent metrics become simultaneously achievable, and the functional diversity within this region enables model swapping for downstream constraints without retraining. The proposed protocol replaces fixed tolerances with transparent documentation: pre-registering the inconsistency functional, publishing Metric Provenance Sheets adapted from Model Cards and Data Statements, and reporting empirical inconsistency along four axes with uncertainty, stakeholder review, and drift monitoring via versioned SPHERE cards. Conflict-aware optimizers (PCGrad, CAGrad) and post-processing threshold adjustment are identified as the primary computational tools for achieving approximate joint satisfaction at bounded cost, with the key insight that complementary metrics block shortcut features and counteract Goodhart's Law.

## Results

This is a position paper without a primary experimental results table; it synthesizes quantitative evidence from cited works to support its argument. Key cited numbers include: Bell et al. [19] found across 18 real-world datasets that loosening fairness constraints from zero to minimum disparity yields many approximately fair models, showing impossibility theorems overstate practical trade-offs; Fisher et al. [12] evaluated 9 LLMs on 7,314 political queries, demonstrating measurable trade-offs (e.g., refusal achieves 100% safety but poor utility); PCGrad [57] improves mean IoU by 2.3 pp and reduces depth RMSE by 0.04 m in NYUv2 co-training; and Ziller et al. [30] show a DP loss improves external-hospital AUC by 5% despite a 2% internal-test decline. Dai et al. [71] derive that the Rashomon Set size grows exponentially with √ε, implying larger error tolerances make fairer models more discoverable.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not propose a new method to benchmark against baselines; it argues for a paradigm shift in how RAI metrics are treated. The empirical evidence it marshals contrasts two stances: (1) the 'consistency-first' approach (e.g., Hardt et al. [26] showing post-processing can satisfy equalized odds with negligible accuracy loss, Rodolfa et al. [27] observing virtually no precision reduction after recall-disparity mitigation) versus (2) the 'embrace inconsistency' approach the authors advocate, supported by Bell et al. [19] demonstrating approximate joint satisfaction of theoretically incompatible fairness metrics, and Wick et al. [20] showing engineered representations can mitigate fairness trade-offs. The paper positions its protocol (Metric Provenance Sheets, SPHERE cards, four-axis inconsistency reporting) as complementary to rather than competing with existing tools like Model Cards [72] and Data Statements [73], and frames multi-objective optimization methods (PCGrad [57], CAGrad [58], NSGA-II [66]) as practical enablers rather than baselines to be outperformed.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper departs from the dominant RAI paradigm of resolving metric contradictions by selecting a single 'correct' fairness definition or deriving consistency constraints (e.g., Dwork et al.'s fairness-through-awareness, Agarwal et al.'s reductions approach). Instead, it synthesizes moral philosophy (Berlin's value pluralism, Rawls/Raz on neutrality), multi-objective optimization theory (PCGrad, CAGrad, Pareto-front geometry), and Rashomon set analysis to argue that theoretical inconsistency is a structural feature of pluralistic alignment. Its practical protocol builds directly on Model Cards and Data Statements for documentation, and on SPHERE for structured evaluation reporting, while drawing on Bell et al.'s empirical demonstration that approximate joint satisfaction of 'impossible' fairness metrics is routinely achievable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a deeply embedded assumption in RAI practice—that metric inconsistency is a defect to be engineered away—and proposes a concrete documentation protocol (Metric Provenance Sheets, four-axis inconsistency reporting, SPHERE cards) that could become a standard for transparent, pluralistic evaluation. However, as a position paper it offers no new empirical validation in RAI-specific settings; the regularization and Pareto-front arguments are borrowed from multi-task learning and decision-tree literature without dedicated experiments on fairness or privacy tasks. Open questions include how regulators will operationalize 'profile-based compliance' versus single-number thresholds, whether the computational overhead of multi-objective training is acceptable in high-stakes deployments, and whether the proposed documentation protocol scales beyond small teams.
