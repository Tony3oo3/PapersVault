## Problem

Score-based black-box variational inference requires a variational family that is both expressive enough to capture skew, heavy tails, and multimodality, and tractable enough to sample from and optimize. Products of experts offer high expressivity but are generally intractable to sample from or normalize, and existing score-based VI methods (Gaussian families, normalizing flows) either lack the needed flexibility or lack convergence guarantees. This paper identifies a specific PoE family for which both sampling and optimization become tractable while retaining the expressivity of t-distributed experts.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input a black-box target score function ∇log p(z) (evaluated at sampled points), a pre-placed pool of K t-distributed experts with locations μ_k and inverse scale matrices Λ_k (obtained via hill-climbing and Hessian-based heuristics), and initial weights α^(0). It produces optimized expert weights α that are typically sparse (many entries driven to zero by the nonnegativity constraint), samples from the fitted PoE via importance-weighted draws over the simplex, and a normalizing constant estimated by Monte Carlo integration over Δ^{K-1} rather than ℝ^D. The constraint set enforces α_k ≥ 0 and Σα_k ≥ D/2 + ε for integrability.

## Method

The key technical insight is applying the Feynman parameterization (originally for QFT loop integrals) to show that a product of t-distributed experts equals a continuous mixture of t-distributions indexed by a Dirichlet variable w on the simplex Δ^{K-1}, with location μ(w), inverse scale Ω(w), and degrees of freedom ν = 2Σα_k − D. This representation yields a latent variable model (w ~ weighted Dirichlet, z|w ~ t) that enables sampling via importance sampling with a Dirichlet proposal, and converts the normalizing constant into an integral over the simplex. For optimization, the score of the PoE is linear in the expert weights α, so minimizing a regularized empirical Fisher divergence at each iteration reduces to a convex quadratic program (nonnegative least squares with a linear sum constraint), solvable by standard QP solvers. The paper proves that with constant learning rate, the iterates converge exponentially fast to a neighborhood of the optimal weights, with the error floor governed by the misspecification of the variational family.

## Results

On four synthetic 2D targets (Gaussian mixture, product of t-distributions, diamond, funnel), the PoE family achieves substantially lower KL and Fisher divergences than Gaussian BBVI (ADVI, BaM), with the largest gains on heavy-tailed targets where it also outperforms normalizing flows. On a 50-dimensional sinh-arcsinh target with skew (ε=0.3) and heavy tails (τ=0.7), the PoE attains the lowest KL and Fisher divergences among all methods, with the normalizing flow converging more slowly over 10⁷ gradient evaluations. On three posteriordb posteriors (garch11, 8schools-noncentered, gp-regr), the PoE achieves the best negative expected log-likelihood and Fisher divergence in all three cases, including the light-tailed gp-regr where Gaussian BBVI is competitive.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the PoE score-matching method against three baselines: ADVI (Gaussian BBVI via automatic differentiation), BaM (Gaussian BBVI via batch-and-match score matching), and a RealNVP normalizing flow with a Gaussian base distribution. The PoE consistently outperforms both Gaussian baselines (ADVI and BaM) across all targets, with the advantage most pronounced on skewed and heavy-tailed posteriors (garch11, 8schools-noncentered, diamond, product-of-experts) where the Gaussian family is structurally unable to match the target shape. Against the normalizing flow, the PoE wins on heavy-tailed targets (product of experts, diamond, 8schools-noncentered) and the 50D sinh-arcsinh, while the flow is competitive on lighter-tailed targets. Notably, even on the light-tailed gp-regr posterior where Gaussian BBVI performs well, the PoE still achieves lower divergence than both the flow and Gaussian methods, demonstrating that the t-expert degrees of freedom (ν = 2Σαₖ − D) can be tuned to approximate near-Gaussian shapes while retaining flexibility.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of score-based black-box variational inference (BaM, EigenVI, Gaussian score matching) and products of experts (Hinton 1999; Welling et al. 2002 for t-distributed PoE in generative modeling). The key departure is the repurposing of the Feynman parameterization—a tool from quantum field theory loop integrals—to represent a product of t-experts as a continuous Dirichlet-indexed mixture of t-distributions, thereby unlocking tractable sampling and normalizing-constant estimation that were previously intractable for PoE. On the optimization side, the paper extends the score-matching framework of Cai et al. (2024, BaM) by exploiting the linearity of the PoE score in expert weights, reducing each VI iteration to a convex quadratic program, and provides a convergence guarantee that is absent from prior score-based BBVI work. The variational family also connects to implicit VI (Titsias & Ruiz 2019) and boosting VI (Guo et al. 2016), though the PoE construction is distinct in its use of geometric (multiplicative) rather than additive (mixture) combination of experts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Feynman-parameterization trick is a genuinely novel cross-disciplinary insight that makes an otherwise intractable variational family (PoE with t-experts) usable for BBVI, and the resulting convergence guarantee (exponential rate to a misspecification-dependent neighborhood) is rare in the score-based VI literature. The practical limitation is the upfront, heuristic expert selection: the method overspecifies experts and prunes via sparsity, but a more adaptive or boosting-style expert placement could substantially improve both accuracy and efficiency. Open directions include using the Feynman identity for generative modeling with PoE, employing the PoE as a proposal in MCMC, and extending the convergence analysis to time-varying or adaptive step-size schedules.
