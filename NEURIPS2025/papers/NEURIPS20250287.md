## Problem

Multi-agent reinforcement learning faces an exponential curse of dimensionality in the joint state-action space, and existing mean-field MARL methods, while reducing this to polynomial time in n, remain computationally prohibitive for large agent populations. This paper asks whether one can achieve a polylogarithmic runtime in n for cooperative MARL while retaining a provably decaying optimality gap as the number of sampled agents grows.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250287/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The algorithm takes a cooperative system with one global agent and n homogeneous local agents (each with finite state and action spaces), a sampling parameter k ≤ n, a sample budget m for the empirical Bellman operator, and access to transition kernels P_g and P_l. It produces a decentralized stochastic policy π_{k,m}^{est} in which, at execution time, the global agent only observes its own state plus k sampled local-agent states, and each local agent observes its own state plus k−1 sampled peer states—never the full n-agent state. The intermediate learned artifact is a Q-function Q̂_{k,m}^{est} whose size scales polynomially in k (or exponentially in k for large state spaces) rather than in n.

## Method

SUBSAMPLE-MFQ operates in two phases. In offline learning (Algorithm 1), the global agent draws a fixed subset Δ of k local agents and runs value iteration (or mean-field value iteration, selected by a regime threshold on |Z_l|^k vs. |Z_l|·k^{|Z_l|}) on the k-agent surrogate subsystem using m Monte Carlo samples per Bellman update to obtain a deterministic policy π̂_{k,m}^{est}. In online execution (Algorithm 2), the global agent resamples a fresh k-subset uniformly at every time step and applies π̂_{k,m}^{est} to produce its action, while each local agent independently samples k−1 peers and applies the same policy to produce its own action, yielding the stochastic policy π_{k,m}^{est}. The key theoretical result is that the optimality gap V^{π*} − V^{π_{k}^{est}} is bounded by Õ(1/√k) with high probability, independent of n; setting k = O(log n) yields the first centralized MARL algorithm with polylogarithmic runtime in n. The proof hinges on a Lipschitz continuity bound between Q̂_k* and Q* in total-variation distance (rather than KL divergence), a DKW-type concentration inequality for sampling without replacement, and an adaptation of the Kakade–Langford performance-difference lemma to the multi-agent setting.

## Results

The main theoretical guarantee (Theorem 4.4) shows that the optimality gap V^{π*}(s₀) − V^{π_k^{est}}(s₀) is bounded by Õ(1/√k) with high probability, independent of the total number of agents n. When k = O(log n), SUBSAMPLE-MFQ achieves a polylogarithmic sample complexity in n, representing an exponential speedup over mean-field MARL (poly(n)) and traditional Q-learning (exp(n)). In the Gaussian squeeze experiment with n=50 agents, the discounted cumulative reward improves monotonically as k increases toward n, and both reward and computation time outperform the mean-field value iteration benchmark. The constrained exploration experiment (n=8, m=20 samples) confirms that the reward at k=n recovers the value-iteration baseline, while k<n yields only marginally lower reward.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's sole explicit baseline is mean-field value iteration (Yang et al., ICML 2018), which achieves an approximation error of O(1/√n) but requires sample complexity polynomial in n (specifically O(n^{|S||A|}·|S||A|)). SUBSAMPLE-MFQ outperforms this baseline on both metrics in the Gaussian squeeze experiments: it achieves higher discounted cumulative rewards and substantially lower computation time (exponential improvement in Figure 2b for k ≤ n=8). The key advantage is that SUBSAMPLE-MFQ with k=O(log n) reduces the sample complexity from poly(n) to poly(log n) while maintaining a decaying optimality gap of O(1/√(log n)), whereas mean-field MARL's error only vanishes as n→∞. When k=n, SUBSAMPLE-MFQ exactly recovers the mean-field Q-learning updates (by Lemma C.19), confirming it is a strict generalization. The paper does not compare against traditional tabular Q-learning or sparse-network MARL methods (e.g., Qu et al. 2020) in experiments, though it notes those methods become intractable on the dense star-graph topology used here.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends mean-field MARL (Yang et al., 2018; Gu et al., 2021), which achieves polynomial-in-n sample complexity by approximating agent interactions through an empirical distribution, by introducing a subsampling step that reduces the effective problem size from n to k agents. The algorithmic core builds on classical Q-learning's Bellman update structure, while the theoretical analysis relies on two key tools: a recent extension of the DKW concentration inequality to sampling without replacement (Anand and Qu, 2024) and an adaptation of the Kakade-Langford performance-difference lemma to the multi-agent setting. The paper positions itself as the first centralized MARL algorithm achieving polylogarithmic runtime in n (when k = O(log n)), representing an exponential speedup over prior poly(n) mean-field methods, and as a reduction of the joint *state* space complexity (not just the action space, as in V-learning approaches).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result establishes a clean fundamental trade-off: the Q-table size scales polynomially in k while the optimality gap decays as Õ(1/√k) independent of n, giving practitioners a principled knob to balance computational cost against solution quality. However, the experiments are small-scale (n ≤ 50), the setting assumes a specific global-plus-local-agent structure with homogeneous local agents and a structured reward, and the cooperative assumption limits applicability to competitive or heterogeneous settings. Open questions include extending to online no-regret learning, weaker network assumptions beyond the star-graph topology, and whether the 1/√k rate is tight or can be improved with variance-reduction techniques.
