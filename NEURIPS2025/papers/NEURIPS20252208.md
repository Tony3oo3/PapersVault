## Problem

While LLMs can answer multi-hop compositional queries without verbalizing intermediate steps, the internal mechanisms enabling this implicit reasoning remain poorly understood. Prior analyses either rely on pretrained models with opaque training data or symbolic datasets lacking fine-grained query-level control, making it difficult to distinguish genuine step-by-step reasoning from memorization or shortcut solutions. This paper asks how implicit multi-hop reasoning emerges during training and what internal representational structures support it.

## Input / Output

The model receives compositional queries of the form (e1, r1, r2) and must predict the target entity e3 by implicitly traversing an intermediate entity e2, all within a symbolic vocabulary of 2000 entities and 200 relations with no inherent semantics. For diagnostic analysis, hidden states are extracted at the r1 token position in layer 5 of an 8-layer GPT-2 (768-dim, 12 heads) and subjected to cross-query patching and cosine-similarity clustering across queries sharing the same bridge entity. Outputs include per-query-type accuracy (Test-II, Test-OI, Test-IO, Test-OO), patching success rates across layers and positions, and two clustering metrics: ID Cohesion (within-ID cosine similarity) and OOD Alignment (OOD-to-ID-centroid cosine similarity).

## Method

The paper trains GPT-2 from scratch on a controlled symbolic environment with targeted omissions and query-level ablations, revealing a three-stage developmental trajectory: memorization, in-distribution generalization, and cross-distribution generalization. Two diagnostic tools are introduced: (1) cross-query semantic patching, which tests whether a hidden state at a given position encodes a transferable intermediate entity by inserting it into a structurally similar target query and checking if the prediction shifts to the source's reasoning path; and (2) a cosine-based representational lens, which measures whether hidden states for the same bridge entity form tight clusters in cosine space across diverse queries. The key mechanistic finding is that successful implicit reasoning correlates with cosine-space clustering of intermediate representations rather than explicit decodability, and that first-hop OOD generalization is an artifact of representational alignment induced by ID triple supervision rather than true compositional transfer.

## Results

The paper identifies a three-stage developmental trajectory for implicit multi-hop reasoning: memorization, in-distribution (ID) generalization, and cross-distribution generalization. A key quantitative finding is that explicit decodability of intermediate entities does not predict reasoning emergence: ID-derived representations achieve 92.1% immediate-probing accuracy in Phase I (memorization) yet the model only begins generalizing to unseen ID-ID compositions in Phase II. Cross-query semantic patching localizes intermediate entity representations at the r₁ token position in middle layers (layer 5 of 8), with effective patching success concentrated there. Cosine-space clustering metrics (ID Cohesion and OOD Alignment) robustly track behavioral generalization, and only an 80/20 ID/OOD atomic-triple ratio enables Phase III cross-distribution generalization, while 50/50 and 30/70 ratios fail.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a mechanistic interpretability study rather than a method-comparison paper, so it does not benchmark against external baselines. Instead, it uses controlled training-configuration ablations as its primary comparative evidence. Training with only Train-II 2-hop queries (no atomic triples) still achieves ID generalization but significantly later than when ID atomic triples are included, showing that atomic facts accelerate but are not necessary for in-distribution reasoning. Removing a subset of atomic triples from second-hop positions in training causes consistent failure on corresponding Test-II queries, even when the same triples appear in first-hop or atomic roles, establishing a strict query-level matching requirement for second-hop generalization. Varying the ID/OOD ratio (0.8/0.2 vs. 0.5/0.5 vs. 0.3/0.7) shows that only the 80/20 split supports Phase III cross-distribution generalization, with both Test-OI accuracy and OOD Alignment rising jointly only in that setting. Finally, removing all ID triples while retaining OOD triples and Train-II queries causes Test-OI accuracy to remain near chance, confirming that ID-based representational anchoring is essential for first-hop OOD generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the symbolic multi-hop reasoning framework of Wang et al. (NeurIPS 2024), which established the grokking phenomenon for implicit reasoning in transformers, by introducing fine-grained query-level control configurations and targeted omissions that prior symbolic datasets lacked. It departs from the dominant decoding-based probing paradigm (logit lens, linear probing) used in prior implicit-reasoning analyses by introducing two new diagnostic tools: cross-query semantic patching for causal localization of intermediate entities, and a cosine-based representational lens that examines geometric clustering rather than token-level decodability. The work also connects to the 'two-hop curse' literature (Balesni et al., Xu et al.) by providing a mechanistic explanation for why single-hop knowledge fails to transfer to multi-hop reasoning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a coherent mechanistic account of how implicit multi-hop reasoning emerges, linking representational clustering in cosine space to behavioral generalization across three developmental stages. Its most impactful finding—that apparent OOD generalization at the first hop is an artifact of representational alignment induced by ID supervision rather than genuine compositional transfer—challenges optimistic interpretations of LLM generalization and has implications for how we evaluate and trust model reasoning. Key limitations include the restricted symbolic setting (2000 entities, 200 relations), primary reliance on an 8-layer GPT-2, and the acknowledgment that real LLMs operate with far richer knowledge bases. Open questions include whether the clustering mechanism and the decodability-reasoning dissociation persist in larger, more complex models trained on natural language, and whether the representational anchoring effect can be deliberately leveraged to improve multi-hop generalization.
