## Problem

Prevailing pretraining data selection methods evaluate each data point's utility individually, implicitly assuming group utility equals the sum of individual contributions. However, theoretical analyses and empirical evidence show that group-level influence is shaped by complex interactions among data points, and individual selection diverges from true group selection after only ~100 data points. Direct group-level optimization is computationally infeasible due to the exponential search space over all possible subsets, leaving a gap between the demonstrated potential of group selection and its practical tractability.

## Input / Output

The method consumes an intermediate language model checkpoint (e.g., DCLM 400M at step 12,288), a large pretraining corpus (DCLM), and a size-128 reference set sampled from FLAN for influence measurement. Data points are encoded as 768-dimensional BGE embeddings (with 2048-token sequences split into four 512-token chunks and averaged). The output is a selected subset of size n (50% of the pool by default) for the next pretraining stage, produced by iteratively selecting the data point with the lowest predicted group-level influence within each of d=10,000 influence-aware clusters. The process is repeated across S=2 pretraining stages in a model-aware loop.

## Method

Group-MATES parameterizes costly group-level selection with a relational data influence model that predicts each candidate's oracle influence given the previously selected set, formulated as a relationship-weighted individual influence: [α − α/(β·(t−1)) · Σ R_{x_i,x_t}] · (w_o · h_{x_t}), where R is cosine similarity between BGE embeddings. The model is trained by sampling training trajectories under a random rollout policy and a bootstrapping policy that targets the tails of the influence distribution, collecting oracle influences via single-step local probing at each step, and minimizing MSE. For efficient inference, the dataset is partitioned into d clusters using k-means with relationship weights as the similarity metric (influence-aware clustering), and selection proceeds independently within each cluster, reducing complexity from O(N·n) to O(N·n/d) while preserving strong intra-cluster relational structure. The entire pipeline is integrated into pretraining in a model-aware manner: after each stage, oracle influences are collected, the relational model is retrained, and data is selected for the next stage.

## Results

On the DCLM benchmark, Group-MATES achieves Core scores of 0.23362 (400M-4x), 0.30747 (1B-1x), and 0.36846 (3B-1x), yielding 3.5%–9.4% relative gains over random selection across 22 downstream tasks. In the 400M-4x setting, Group-MATES reduces the number of pretraining tokens needed to reach a target Core score by 1.8× compared to random selection, and achieves 31.5% net H100-hour efficiency gain (vs. 16.4% for MATES). The method consistently outperforms all individual data selection baselines (MATES, Quad, Edu Classifier, WebOrganizer) on the Core metric across all three scales.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Group-MATES outperforms random selection by 9.4% (400M-4x), 4.4% (1B-1x), and 3.5% (3B-1x) relative Core score gains. Against the primary individual-selection baseline MATES, Group-MATES achieves 5.0% (400M-4x), 1.5% (1B-1x), and 2.0% (3B-1x) relative improvements, effectively doubling MATES's own gain over random in the 400M-4x and 3B-1x settings. Group-MATES also surpasses Quad (0.23362 vs. 0.22358 in 400M-4x; 0.30747 vs. 0.29340 in 1B-1x) and the LLM-based Edu Classifier (0.23362 vs. 0.21821 in 400M-4x; 0.30747 vs. 0.29257 in 1B-1x), as well as WebOrganizer (0.30747 vs. 0.29488 in 1B-1x). Notably, in the 3B-1x setting where MATES shows diminishing returns over random (only 1.5% gain), Group-MATES maintains a consistent 3.5% advantage, highlighting better scalability of group-level selection. On the speed-quality frontier, Group-MATES requires 1.8× fewer tokens (400M-4x) and 1.4× fewer tokens (1B-1x) to reach a given Core score, and delivers 31.5% and 20.5% net H100-hour efficiency gains respectively—nearly double MATES's 16.4% and 10.1%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends MATES [50], which parameterizes individual data influence with a learned model, by introducing a relational data influence model that weights individual influences by pairwise relationship scores (cosine similarity of BGE embeddings) to approximate group-level effects. The theoretical motivation draws on second-order group influence functions [4, 34] and trajectory-specific influence functions [37], which show that group influence contains interaction terms invisible to individual-level approximations. For efficient inference, the paper introduces influence-aware clustering (using the relationship weight itself as the clustering metric) to reduce the O(N·n) selection cost to O(N·n/d), a practical departure from the brute-force sequential selection used in prior group-influence work [38, 5].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The near-doubling of pretraining gains over individual selection (3.5%–9.4% relative over random vs. ~2% for MATES) and the 1.8× token reduction establish that the individual-level paradigm, which dominates current practice, leaves substantial efficiency on the table. The method is practical—only ~12% additional FLOPs at 400M scale, decreasing to ~3% at 3B—and the open-sourced pipeline makes it immediately adoptable. Key limitations include testing only up to 2.8B parameters, the cluster-based inference ignoring inter-cluster relationships (validated but not eliminated), and the reliance on a single BGE backbone for embeddings. Open questions include whether the relationship-weight formulation generalizes to production-scale (100B+) pretraining and whether richer relational models (beyond pairwise cosine similarity) can further close the gap with true greedy group selection.
