## Problem

LLMs are increasingly deployed as agents navigating environments saturated with motivated communication (ads, reviews, partisan content), yet no systematic evaluation existed for whether they can exercise 'motivational vigilance'—the capacity to discount information based on a source's intentions and incentives. Prior work identified related failures (jailbreaking, sycophancy, susceptibility to pop-ups) but lacked a normative benchmark grounded in cognitive science to rigorously measure this specific social-cognitive capacity.

## Input / Output

Across three experiments, inputs range from noisy RGB images of colored circles (Experiment 1) to structured text vignettes specifying character relationships and dollar-denominated incentives (Experiment 2) to 300 real YouTube sponsorship transcripts with brand names censored via GPT-4o (Experiment 3). Outputs are 0–100 integer ratings for product quality, speaker reward benefit, and source trustworthiness, plus proportion-shift values in Experiment 1. The primary evaluation metric is the Pearson correlation between each LLM's elicited influence scores and the posterior predictions of a Bayesian rational model fitted to that same LLM's elicited trust and incentive priors.

## Method

The paper uses the Oktar et al. rational model of vigilance in motivated communication as a normative benchmark, which formalizes the interaction as recursive social inference: a speaker's utterance probability depends on a joint reward R_Joint = λR_L + (1−λ)R_S weighted by benevolence λ, and a vigilant listener inverts this to compute P(R_L | u) by marginalizing over priors on speaker incentives, listener outcomes, and benevolence. Experiment 1 adapts the Watson–Morgan two-player circle-counting task (with image noise replacing time pressure) to test whether LLMs shift their estimates differently for deliberately communicated advice versus incidentally spied answers. Experiment 2 uses a 4×4 factorial of character trustworthiness (romantic partner → stranger) and incentive magnitude across credit-card, medicine, and real-estate vignettes, eliciting scores in independent context windows under direct/CoT and first-person/user-assistant conditions. Experiment 3 scales to 300 SponsorBlock-sourced YouTube transcripts, revealing that vigilance collapses (r < 0.2) in naturalistic settings but is partially recovered by appending a steering phrase emphasizing the speaker's 'intentions and incentives'—outperforming alternative Gricean or bias-oriented steers.

## Results

In controlled vignette-based settings (Experiment 2), frontier non-reasoning LLMs (GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Gemini 2.0 Flash, Llama 3.3-70B) achieve Pearson correlations of 0.788–0.911 with the Oktar Bayesian rational model and 0.889–0.929 with human influence scores, indicating near-rational and highly human-like motivational vigilance. Reasoning models (o1, o3-mini, DeepSeek-R1) show substantially lower Bayesian-model correlations (0.326–0.716), with DeepSeek-R1 dropping to −0.141 in the user-assistant role. In naturalistic YouTube sponsorship settings (Experiment 3), all models' correlations with the Bayesian model collapse to below 0.2, though a simple prompt steer emphasizing speaker intentions and incentives recovers some rationality (e.g., GPT-4o Direct User improves from −0.006 to 0.312).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Oktar et al. [64, 65] Bayesian rational model (the normative benchmark), frontier non-reasoning LLMs achieve strong internal consistency (r ≈ 0.8–0.9), while reasoning models are notably less aligned (o1/o3-mini at r ≈ 0.7, DeepSeek-R1 at r ≈ 0.33), and small models fall further (r ≈ 0.29–0.61). Against the Watson & Morgan [92] human participant data, frontier LLMs actually correlate *better* with human influence scores (r ≈ 0.93–0.94) than with the rational model fitted to their own elicited priors (r ≈ 0.79–0.91), suggesting they capture residual human heuristics beyond pure rational inference. In Experiment 1, LLMs replicate the human pattern of shifting less for deliberate advice than for spied information, but CoT prompting makes them significantly more susceptible than humans (shifts >60% vs. human 0.34–0.37). In the naturalistic YouTube setting, no model matches the Bayesian model well (all r < 0.2), and the steering prompt only partially closes the gap, indicating that vigilance does not yet generalize to noisy, context-rich real-world inputs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts two established cognitive science paradigms for LLM evaluation: the Watson-Morgan two-player judgment task [92] for testing discrimination between deliberate and incidental social information, and the Oktar et al. rational model of vigilance [64, 65] as a normative Bayesian benchmark for how much one should trust a motivated source. It positions itself within the growing line of work using controlled psychological experiments to probe LLM social cognition (e.g., theory of mind, sycophancy, conformity), but carves out a specific gap: no prior work has formally measured 'motivational vigilance'—the capacity to calibrate belief updates based on a speaker's intentions and incentives—against a rational model. The paper also extends the cognitive-science-evaluates-LLMs methodology to a naturalistic domain (YouTube sponsorships), going beyond the vignette-based settings typical of prior work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that LLMs' vigilance collapses from r > 0.78 in controlled settings to r < 0.2 in naturalistic sponsored-content settings has direct implications for deploying LLMs as agents in environments saturated with motivated communication (ads, reviews, financial advice). The counterintuitive result that reasoning models (o1, o3-mini, DeepSeek-R1) are *less* vigilant than non-reasoning frontier models challenges the assumption that extended deliberation improves social reasoning and raises concerns for agentic deployments. Open questions include whether integrating competence-based vigilance models, handling non-verbal cues, and addressing diverse motivation types (relational, affiliative, presentational) will close the gap between controlled and naturalistic performance, and whether the prompt-steering intervention can be made more robust at scale.
