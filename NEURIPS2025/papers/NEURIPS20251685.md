## Problem

Standard counterfactual explanations treat each individual in isolation, recommending minimal-cost feature changes to flip a classifier's decision. When many individuals follow similar recommendations, this individual-centric approach creates competition, overcrowding, and externalities (a tragedy-of-the-commons effect), while also potentially directing people toward low-density, resource-poor regions that are impractical. The paper asks how to generate recourse recommendations that balance individual modification costs with their collective impact on the population.

## Input / Output

The method consumes discrete sample sets (1000 instances per class) from the feature space, a binary classifier (black-box compatible), and an l2 Euclidean cost function, without requiring explicit density estimation of the underlying distribution. Rather than producing a single target point per individual, the output is a joint optimal transport plan over the product space X- x X+, from which individual recommendations are sampled via a multinomial draw. An optional temporal extension produces a sequence of intermediate states (path-guided CE) via dynamic OT, and an ordered-classifier extension outputs recourse maps over a joint feature-and-amount space.

## Method

The core insight is to model population redistribution using a mean-field game PDE in which individuals are attracted toward resource-rich regions and repelled by local competition; at equilibrium, the population density is proportional to the resource distribution. CCE is then formulated as minimizing the L^q modification cost of a transport map T from X- to X+ plus a chi-squared-divergence penalty on the push-forward distribution T#P- relative to P+, which penalizes deviations from equilibrium and thus externalities. To guarantee solution existence, the map-based problem is relaxed to an unbalanced optimal transport (UOT) problem over couplings with divergence penalties on both marginals, yielding a convex program solvable by projected-gradient or Sinkhorn-style algorithms in O(Tmn) time. Extensions leverage dynamic OT (back-and-forth method) for temporal path-guided recourse and linear-constrained OT for ordered classifier families (e.g., loan amounts).

## Results

Across 100 experiments on Adult, COMPAS, HELOC, and Moons datasets, CCE achieves the lowest competition cost (measured as λ₂·D_χ²(T_#P_- ‖ P_+)) among all seven methods, while its modification cost is slightly higher than the best individual-cost baselines since it routes individuals toward higher-density regions. On the combined metric (modification cost + competition cost), CCE outperforms all six baselines, offering the best trade-off. Varying λ₂ from 0.01 to 0.3 reveals a clear trade-off: lower λ₂ reduces modification cost at the expense of higher competition cost, and vice versa.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CCE is compared against six individual-centric CE baselines: Wachter et al. [2017], Growing Spheres (Laugel et al. [2017]), CLUE (Antorán et al. [2020]), FOCUS (Lucic et al. [2022]), C-CHVAE (Pawelczyk et al. [2020]), and ROAR (Upadhyay et al. [2021]). On the competition cost metric, CCE consistently achieves the lowest value across all four datasets, as it explicitly penalizes deviations from the equilibrium distribution via the χ²-divergence term. On pure modification cost, CCE does not achieve the minimum — methods like Wachter and Growing Spheres, which place all recommendations on the decision boundary, yield lower individual costs but concentrate individuals in low-resource regions. On the combined metric (modification cost + λ₂·competition cost), CCE outperforms all six baselines, confirming that the collective optimization yields a more balanced and socially efficient outcome. The advantage is most pronounced on the Moons dataset with SVM classifiers, where standard methods pile all recommendations onto the margin, creating severe overcrowding that CCE avoids by distributing individuals across higher-density regions of X⁺.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the standard counterfactual explanation (CE) framework by importing population dynamics from mean-field game theory (Lasry & Lions, Carmona et al.) to model competition among individuals following recommendations. It departs from prior distribution-aware CE methods (Poyiadzi et al. 2020, Kanamori et al. 2020) that encode density into the cost function on an individual basis, and from group CE approaches (Warren et al. 2023, Carrizosa et al. 2024) that optimize over multiple instances without modeling externalities. The key technical move is reformulating collective CE as an unbalanced optimal transport problem with a χ²-divergence penalty on the target marginal, drawing on the Kantorovich relaxation and Sinkhorn-style solvers from the OT literature (Peyré & Cuturi, Séjourné et al.), and extending to dynamic OT for path-guided recourse (Benamou & Brenier, Jacobs & Léger).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine gap in the CE literature: when many individuals act on the same recommendation, the resulting congestion can undermine the value of the advice (a 'tragedy of the commons' effect). The OT reformulation is practically useful because it yields scalable algorithms with amortized inference and naturally satisfies multiple desiderata (data manifold closeness, robustness, actionability via linear constraints). However, the competition model is simplified (fixed resources, equilibrium assumption, single γ compliance rate), the choice of the regularization parameter λ₂ is acknowledged as non-trivial and application-specific, and the framework does not model strategic deviations from recommendations. Open questions include whether the equilibrium-based penalty generalizes to non-stationary or adversarial settings, and how to calibrate the competition cost in high-dimensional real-world deployments.
