## Problem

Prior memorization evaluations in LLMs implicitly assumed uniform behavior across the context window by probing prefixes only from the beginning of training sequences, overlooking that the absolute position of a prefix within the window—the offset—dramatically reshapes memorization behavior. This paper identifies the offset effect: verbatim recall drops sharply as prefixes shift even modestly away from the initial tokens, revealing a previously unexplored axis for assessing memorization risk.

## Input / Output

The method probes memorization by feeding prefixes of 50–5000 tokens extracted at controlled offsets (0–2048 tokens from the BOD token) out of 8192-token training sequences, then generating suffixes of 50–8000 tokens for comparison against ground truth. Training corpora are constructed with precisely controlled per-sequence exposure frequencies (1–128 repetitions) via the FM-Probe bucketing scheme, where each bucket holds 500 distinct Gutenberg sequences at a fixed repetition count within a mixed 83B-token corpus (2% Gutenberg, 98% FineWeb-Edu), enabling concurrent evaluation across frequency levels in a single training pass.

## Method

The authors pretrain LLaMA-style decoder-only transformers (1B, 3B, 8B) from scratch on 83B tokens under two complementary configurations: a Dense Gutenberg setup (10K sequences, 80 epochs) for extreme memorization and a Sparse Gutenberg setup with FM-Probe buckets for realistic frequency-controlled analysis. The core experimental protocol independently varies prefix length and offset, revealing that short prefixes at offset 0 trigger the strongest verbatim recall while even modest positional shifts cause a sharp decline, an effect the authors attribute to positional fragility rooted in the attention-sink mechanism where early tokens serve as disproportionate retrieval anchors. A BOD-masking ablation confirms that positional fragility persists without the special boundary token, and a Swapped Gutenberg proof-of-concept—replacing the first 4000 tokens of each sequence with random Gutenberg content—displaces the memorization target far from the retrieval anchor, suppressing extractable memorization to baseline levels without degrading generation quality or downstream performance.

## Results

At offset 0 with a 50-token prefix, the 8B model achieves perfect verbatim reproduction of up to 8,000-token suffixes (248 exact matches at frequency 128), while ROUGE-L drops from 0.965 (offset 0) to 0.192 (offset 2048) under greedy decoding. The Swapped Gutenberg mitigation (shifting sensitive content 4,000 tokens deep) suppresses ROUGE-L to ~0.18 across all frequencies (1–128) for both 1B and 8B models, compared to 0.744 (1B) and 0.965 (8B) under standard Sparse Gutenberg at frequency 128. Smaller batch sizes (60 vs. 240 global batch size) reduce ROUGE-L by up to 0.15 at high repetition frequencies while improving downstream accuracy (e.g., ARC-c: 33.36% vs. 29.52%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the standard Sparse Gutenberg setup (no positional mitigation), where the 8B model reaches ROUGE-L of 0.965 and LCCS of 0.951 at frequency 128, indicating near-perfect verbatim extraction. The paper's Swapped Gutenberg mitigation reduces these to 0.181 and 0.011 respectively — essentially at the unrelated-text baseline (~0.18 ROUGE-L) — across all frequencies and both model scales, while maintaining or improving MAUVE (0.958 vs. 1.000) and TTR. Against Goldfish Loss (Hans et al., 2025), the paper's positional displacement is more effective at high frequencies: Goldfish Loss keeps ROUGE-L below 0.4 even at 128 exposures, but the 8B Goldfish model still shows memorization trends that grow with scale, whereas Swapped Gutenberg holds ROUGE-L flat at ~0.18 regardless of frequency. On downstream tasks (Table 6), Goldfish Loss models match or slightly exceed standard models (e.g., 8B Goldfish: MMLU 26.98% vs. 24.53% standard; HellaSwag 50.29% vs. 49.74%), confirming that pretraining-time memorization mitigation need not sacrifice capability. The paper also shows that positional fragility persists under beam search (ROUGE-L 0.999 at offset 0, dropping to 0.186 at offset 2048) and nucleus sampling, and survives continued pretraining from Llama 3.2 1B, ruling out decoding or training-paradigm artifacts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the memorization analysis framework established by Carlini et al. and Nasr et al. by introducing the prefix offset within the context window as a critical evaluation axis, a dimension that prior work implicitly assumed to be uniform by probing only from document beginnings. It builds directly on Hans et al.'s Goldfish Loss and pretraining-time memorization scenarios, scaling them to 4× longer sequences and 10× longer suffixes, while also connecting memorization failure to text degeneration—a mechanism beyond the 'repetition in, repetition out' hypothesis of Li et al. The paper positions itself as a legally motivated study, using Project Gutenberg books to simulate copyright risk at controlled frequencies, and complements rather than replaces existing mitigation strategies like deduplication and Goldfish Loss.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The offset effect reveals that standard memorization risk assessments are systematically distorted because they probe only from position zero, potentially overestimating extractability at the beginning while underestimating it at other positions—or vice versa. The proposed mitigation (shifting sensitive content deeper into the context window) is remarkably simple and effective across model scales, offering a practical pretraining-time strategy that complements existing approaches. However, the study is limited to 83B tokens (orders of magnitude below production scale), assumes prior knowledge of which segments are sensitive, and is restricted to left-to-right autoregressive LLaMA-style models, leaving open whether positional fragility persists in FIM-trained or retrieval-augmented architectures and at trillion-token scale.
