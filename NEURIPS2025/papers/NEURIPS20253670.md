## Problem

Vision-language models have shown strong perceptual capabilities but lack rigorous evaluation of their physical understanding and multi-step planning in structured 3D environments. Existing benchmarks either emphasize single-step perception without long-horizon planning or rely on idealized physical assumptions that ignore real-world interactions like gravity and structural dependencies. PhyBlock addresses this gap by providing the first benchmark that couples high-level language reasoning with dynamic physical constraints through interactive 3D block assembly.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253670/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For the assembly planning branch, the model receives an RGB-D scan of the initial scene, a goal image showing the target structure, and a text instruction, then outputs a complete ordered sequence of block placements (each specifying block type, color, position, and orientation) or generates the next action incrementally in step-by-step mode. For the VQA branch, the model receives a rendered block scene image plus a natural-language question and selects from multiple-choice options. Evaluation uses AOV-graph-based precision/recall/F1 for planning (treating correct steps as TP, incorrect as FP, missing as FN) and simple accuracy for VQA, with a random baseline at ~8.8% F1 and a human expert upper bound at ~94.7%.

## Method

PhyBlock is a two-branch benchmark built on the Genesis physics simulator with 8 parametric LEGO-like block geometries in 5 colors. The planning branch contains 400 scenes across four ascending difficulty tiers (up to 22 blocks, 8 layers), where each scene's assembly dependencies are encoded as an Activity-on-Vertex (AOV) directed graph—blocks as vertices, prerequisite relations as edges—enabling fine-grained partial-completion scoring and failure-mode diagnosis beyond final-state-only evaluation. The VQA branch comprises 2,200 questions across 16 subcategories in four groups (Object Property, Object Relationship, Scene Understanding, Dynamic Reasoning), generated via two paradigms: LLM-based static question construction and simulation-driven dynamic perturbation (e.g., removing a support block and rendering the resulting collapse), all verified by three annotators over 500+ hours. The benchmark evaluates 23 VLMs under both one-time full planning and interactive step-by-step planning, revealing a steep performance cliff from Level-1 to Level-4, a perception-reasoning gap where models excel at color/shape but fail on counterfactual and affordance queries, and two universal error modes (orientation mis-estimation and ignored support dependencies) that persist even with thinking-mode prompting.

## Results

On the PhyBlock 3D Block Assembly Planning task (one-time full planning), the best-performing model Claude-3.7 Sonnet achieves an overall F1 of 47.4%, but performance drops sharply from Level-1 (F1 = 76.8%) to Level-4 (F1 = 41.8%), a decline of over 45%. On the Physical Understanding VQA branch, GPT-o3 attains the highest overall accuracy of 70.0%, with strong performance on Object Property (82.0%) and Dynamic Reasoning (64.3%), while the random baseline sits at 24.1%. A human expert upper bound of 94.7% accuracy highlights the substantial gap between current VLMs and human-level physical reasoning. No model maintains high recall once tasks demand long-horizon sequencing or hidden-support reasoning, and enabling thinking mode (e.g., Claude-3.7 Sonnet-Thinking) yields virtually no improvement (47.1% vs. 47.4% F1).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 23 VLMs (13 proprietary, 8 open-source) on PhyBlock. Among proprietary models, Claude-3.7 Sonnet leads on assembly planning (47.4% overall F1), narrowly ahead of GPT-o1 (43.4%) and GPT-4o (38.8%), while GPT-o3 dominates the VQA branch (70.0% accuracy vs. GPT-4.1's 62.8% and Gemini-2.0-flash's 62.1%). All proprietary models exhibit a steep performance cliff: F1 drops by 30–45 percentage points from Level-1 to Level-4, with no model exceeding 42% F1 on the hardest tier. Open-source models trail significantly—InternVL2.5-78B (23.6% F1) and Qwen2.5-VL-7B (20.4% F1) are roughly half the performance of the best proprietary models, while LLaVA-OneVision-7B (14.7% F1) approaches the random baseline (8.8%). Notably, enabling thinking mode in Claude-3.7 Sonnet provides no meaningful gain (47.1% vs. 47.4% F1), and the step-by-step interactive setting (Table 3) reveals that even Qwen2.5-VL-72B achieves only 52.1% F1 with 94.9% precision but just 35.9% recall, indicating models can identify correct blocks but fail to sequence them properly.

## Relation to Prior Work

PhyBlock carves new ground at the intersection of physical understanding benchmarks and embodied planning evaluation. Prior manipulation benchmarks (RLBench, LIBERO) assess low-level object interaction without testing structural reasoning, while physical-reasoning datasets (CLEVRER, PhysBench) focus on passive prediction in toy-like scenes rather than goal-conditioned, multi-step construction. The paper repurposes the cognitive-development literature on structured block play as a principled difficulty hierarchy and introduces an Activity-on-Vertex graph formalism for fine-grained assembly evaluation—neither of which has a direct predecessor in the VLM benchmarking literature. It does not build on a specific prior method but synthesizes physics simulation (Genesis), graph-based dependency modeling, and progressive task design into a unified testbed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PhyBlock provides the first systematic diagnostic of where VLMs fail in physically grounded, multi-step planning, revealing a sharp perception-reasoning gap (high accuracy on color/shape vs. collapse on counterfactual and dependency queries) and two universal error modes (orientation mis-estimation and ignored support dependencies) that persist across all 23 evaluated architectures. The finding that enabling thinking mode yields virtually no improvement challenges the assumption that longer reasoning chains compensate for missing physical priors, pointing toward the need for architectures that explicitly fuse visual embeddings with physics-aware representations. Limitations include the narrow scope (eight simple shapes, five colors, no real-world textures or motor control), the modest dataset size (400 assembly scenes), and the absence of actual robot deployment, leaving open whether the identified gaps persist in more realistic, continuous-action settings.
