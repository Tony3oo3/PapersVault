## Problem

While chain-of-thought reasoning and test-time compute scaling have become central to LLM performance, it remains unclear whether the globally optimal algorithmic strategy is the best one to teach a next-token predictor. This paper asks whether the inductive bias of autoregressive transformers favors reasoning traces that are globally efficient (e.g., dynamic programming) or those that are longer but locally systematic and incremental, and whether trace structure matters beyond mere length.

## Input / Output

The model receives a tokenized layered DAG encoded in a custom task-specific vocabulary (unique tokens for each layer label, node label, integer cost, and syntax delimiters), where each layer's edge list is enclosed in brackets. It must produce a reasoning trace between BoT/EoT tokens—each step being a partial path with cumulative cost and a separator—followed by the optimal source-to-destination path. All traces satisfy strict optimality criteria: correct path costs, incremental single-node extension, no reuse of sub-optimal partial paths, and deterministic recoverability of the optimal answer.

## Method

The paper introduces a controlled shortest-path benchmark on layered DAGs with a tunable trace generator whose efficiency parameter η biases the exploration queue toward layer-by-layer (positive η, DP-like) or depth-first (negative η, DFS-like) ordering, with optional deterministic or randomized step repetition to inject redundancy. A 28.5M-parameter Phi-3 transformer (3 layers, 12 heads) is trained from scratch via supervised next-token prediction on question-trace-answer triples, with the question masked from the loss. The key analytical contribution is measuring next-token confidence (average sampled token probability) along generated traces, which the authors show correlates with generalization accuracy: DFS-like traces yield higher confidence than DP-like traces because their locally incremental, auto-correlated step sequences are easier for the autoregressive objective to optimize, even though they are globally less efficient. Injecting arbitrary redundancy (repeating steps) without changing the exploration structure fails to replicate this benefit, confirming that trace structure—not length—drives the effect.

## Results

On a 28.5M-parameter Phi-3 model trained on layered-DAG shortest-path instances (L=7, K=6, C=5, p_e=0.6), the model trained on inefficient depth-first traces (η=−5, avg. 58±54 CoT steps) achieves the highest generalization accuracy on unseen graphs, outperforming the globally optimal DP traces (η=+5, avg. 33±20 steps) and the random-exploration traces (η=0, avg. 43±33 steps) at both 32M and 128M token budgets. The DFS model sees roughly one-third fewer unique graph examples under the same token budget yet absorbs information more effectively. Next-token surprisal confirms the inductive bias: S(η=−5)=0.4821, S(η=+5)=1.3262, S(η=0)=1.905, showing that systematic backtracking traces yield the most predictable (and thus most learnable) next-token signal.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between training conditions rather than external baselines. The inefficient DFS trace (η=−5) consistently outperforms the globally optimal DP trace (η=+5) in answer accuracy on unseen graphs at both 32M and 128M token budgets, despite the DFS model seeing ~1/3 fewer unique graph instances. The random-exploration trace (η=0) performs worst of the three, even though its traces are longer than DP's, because the non-systematic exploration order inflates next-token surprisal (1.905 vs. 0.4821 for DFS). Injecting deterministic redundancy (DR: repeating each step twice) into DP traces does not recover the DFS advantage and slightly degrades performance, ruling out length alone as the explanatory factor. Randomized redundancy (RR) further degrades accuracy and induces repetition loops at zero temperature, confirming that structural coherence—not mere verbosity—is what next-token predictors exploit.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of chain-of-thought reasoning research (Wei et al., Wang et al.), test-time compute scaling (Snell et al., Muennighoff et al.), and transformer-based algorithmic reasoning on graph tasks (Veličković et al., Sanford et al., Bachmann & Nagarajan). It most directly extends the line of work on learning search dynamics with transformers (Lehnert et al. 2024) by introducing a tunable-efficiency trace generator and a custom token language, enabling systematic isolation of trace structure from trace length. The key departure is from the implicit assumption in prior CoT work that the globally optimal algorithm (here, bottom-up DP) is the best trace to teach; the paper instead shows that the inductive bias of next-token predictors favors longer, locally incremental but globally suboptimal traces, a finding that resonates with recent observations that CoT structure matters more than content (Li et al. 2025).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that systematically inefficient (DFS-like) traces outperform globally optimal (DP-like) traces for next-token predictors challenges a natural design principle for CoT training data and suggests that the 'right' reasoning trace to teach is not the one a human would consider optimal. The next-token confidence metric provides a practical, interpretable diagnostic for predicting which trace structures will be easier for a model to learn. However, the results are confined to a 28.5M-parameter Phi-3 model on a synthetic task with a custom tokenizer, leaving open whether the bias persists in larger, natural-language LLMs or across different architectures. Future work on curriculum-based trace shortening, RL-based verbosity penalties, and mechanistic interpretability of the 'eureka' optimization transitions are natural next steps.
