## Problem

Prior test-time scaling laws (Snell et al., Wu et al., Brown et al.) optimize resource allocation based solely on FLOPs, ignoring memory access costs that dominate wall-clock latency in long-generation regimes (Best-of-N, long CoTs). This oversight systematically overestimates the effectiveness of small models and leads to suboptimal deployment decisions, as the quadratic growth of attention KV-cache access with generation length is never accounted for in the cost model.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a reasoning problem (text prompt of length L_in) and a total inference budget expressed in eFLOPs (a unified cost unit combining FLOPs and memory accesses weighted by hardware arithmetic intensity I=562.5 for B200), and produces an optimal allocation across model size P, number of reasoning trials N, maximum generation length n, and KV budget B. The output is a Pareto frontier mapping cost to problem-solving accuracy (Pass@1 for Long-CoT, Pass@K coverage for Best-of-N), with the key decision variable being whether to invest in larger models or more/longer generations.

## Method

The paper introduces an eFLOPs cost model (Eq. 4) that adds memory-access cost (KV cache loading) to the standard FLOPs computation, weighted by hardware arithmetic intensity, revealing that attention-related cost exceeds parameter-related cost by 100–1000× in long-CoT regimes. From this, the Kinetics Scaling Law shows that resources should first be allocated to model size (up to ~14B) before investing in TTS strategies, because KV cache grows sublinearly with parameters (1.18× per 2× parameter doubling) while attention cost grows quadratically with generation length. The paper then proposes the Kinetics Sparse Scaling Law: by applying block top-k sparse attention (which converts the quadratic L²D term to linear LBD), the bottleneck shifts back to parameter computation, re-enabling smaller models on the Pareto frontier and allowing 60-point accuracy gains in low-cost regimes. A greedy discretized search over (N, n, B) per task identifies the optimal resource allocation, and a practical block top-k implementation on FlashInfer with paged attention achieves up to 25× wall-clock speedup on H200 GPUs.

## Results

On AIME24 and LiveCodeBench, sparse attention (block top-k) achieves over 60-point accuracy gains in the low-cost regime and over 5-point gains in the high-cost regime compared to dense attention, while dense models require over 10× more eFLOPs to match the same solving rate. The Kinetics Scaling Law reveals that the 14B Qwen3 model dominates the Pareto frontier across both Best-of-N and Long-CoT strategies, with smaller models (0.6B–4B) being significantly overestimated under prior FLOPs-only cost models. Block top-k attention delivers up to 25× wall-clock speedup on H200 GPUs, with the Qwen3-0.6B model showing 12.6×–25× throughput improvement as generation length extends from 16K to 32K tokens. In the Best-of-N setting, each doubling of compute budget increases optimal generated tokens by 1.74× but KV budget by only 1.18×, confirming that sparsity should increase as more test-time compute is invested.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its Kinetics Scaling Law (eFLOPs-based) against three prior FLOPs-only scaling laws: Brown et al. [4] (repeated sampling), Snell et al. [74] (compute-optimal TTS), and Wu et al. [87] (inference scaling laws). Under all three baselines, smaller models (0.6B–4B) occupy a meaningful portion of the Pareto frontier, suggesting that extending CoTs or increasing Best-of-N trials on small models is cost-effective. The Kinetics Scaling Law overturns this: the 14B model outperforms both 4B and 8B even below 40% accuracy, and the 0.6B model only appears on the frontier where accuracy is negligible. For Best-of-N, the baselines recommend repeated sampling with smaller models, while the Kinetics law shows that deploying a 14B model with fewer trials is strictly more efficient. The critical finding is that attention cost (quadratic in generation length) is 100–1000× larger than parameter computation in long-CoT regimes, a factor entirely absent from the FLOPs-based baselines. Sparse attention further differentiates: it restores smaller models (0.6B, 1.7B, 4B) to the Pareto frontier by converting the quadratic KV cost to linear, a benefit not captured by any of the three baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the test-time scaling law lineage from Brown et al. (Large Language Monkeys), Snell et al., and Wu et al., which all optimize inference compute under a FLOPs-only cost model. The key departure is introducing memory access costs (via an eFLOPs metric grounded in roofline analysis) into the cost function, revealing that attention-related KV cache access—not parameter computation—dominates in long-CoT and Best-of-N regimes. The paper also repurposes sparse attention, previously studied as a training or inference efficiency technique (H2O, Quest, MagicPIG), as a fundamental scaling enabler that reshapes the TTS Pareto frontier rather than merely reducing constant-factor overhead.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that prior FLOPs-based scaling laws overestimate small-model effectiveness by up to 3× in resource requirements has immediate practical impact on deployment decisions for reasoning LLMs. The sparse-attention-as-scaling-paradigm framing opens a new co-design axis (architecture × inference strategy × hardware) that could sustain TTS gains even as pretraining scaling plateaus. Open questions include whether the 14B threshold generalizes across architectures, whether KV memory efficiency correlates with reasoning capability, and how the conclusions shift as the compute-to-bandwidth gap (arithmetic intensity I) continues to widen on future hardware.
