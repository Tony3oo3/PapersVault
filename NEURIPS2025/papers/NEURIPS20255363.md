## Problem

Existing single-cell dynamics models either ignore mass imbalance caused by cell proliferation and death, or handle it through simulation-based training that scales poorly to high dimensions, or via Wasserstein-Fisher-Rao formulations that impose biologically unjustified constraints (e.g., v=∇g). The paper addresses the gap of jointly learning state transition and mass growth from unpaired, unbalanced snapshot data in a simulation-free, scalable framework grounded in optimal transport theory.

## Input / Output

The method takes unpaired single-cell snapshot populations at discrete time points (scRNA-seq data with no tracked individual cell fates and differing total cell counts across time due to proliferation/death) and produces two neural-network-parameterized fields: a velocity field v_θ(x,t) governing state transition and a growth function g_ω(x,t) governing mass variation. The growth function outputs a scalar per cell that, when integrated over time, yields time-dependent weights used to compute predicted total mass and weighted distributions at future time points, enabling both trajectory generation and mass-matching evaluation via relative mass error.

## Method

VGFM derives its training targets from a two-period dynamic reformulation of semi-relaxed optimal transport (SROT): mass growth is performed in period [0,λ] and state transition in period (λ,1], with Proposition 1 establishing equivalence to the static SROT objective. Theorem 1 then shows that a reparameterized joint dynamics—where velocity and growth act simultaneously over [0,1] via time-rescaling and flow-map conjugation—reproduces the same endpoint distribution, making the formulation biologically realistic. The flow matching loss (Eq. 12) trains MLPs to approximate the ideal velocity and growth using SROT couplings solved via entropy-regularized Sinkhorn, with the growth target set to a time-independent Malthusian form justified by L² energy minimization. A Wasserstein-based distribution fitting loss (Eq. 13) is added after a warm-up phase to correct accumulated ODE integration errors, and the combined loss is optimized with a two-stage schedule (matching-only warm-up, then joint training).

## Results

On synthetic datasets, VGFM achieves the lowest W₁ and RME across all settings: W₁=0.046 / RME=0.006 on Simulation Gene (2D), W₁=0.420 / RME=0.053 on Dyngen (5D), and W₁=3.010 / RME=0.037 on Gaussian (1000D). Notably, VGFM is the only method that converges on the 1000-dimensional Gaussian mixture, while all simulation-based baselines (UDSB, TIGON, DeepRUOT) fail to converge. On real-world hold-one-out evaluation, VGFM achieves the best W₁ on EB (5D: 0.676), CITE (5D: 0.745), and CITE (50D: 37.386), outperforming all baselines including the strongest unbalancedness-aware method DeepRUOT.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VGFM outperforms all seven baselines on every dataset where a fair comparison is possible. Against the strongest unbalancedness-aware baseline DeepRUOT [26], VGFM reduces W₁ by 32% (0.046 vs. 0.068) and RME by 63% (0.006 vs. 0.016) on Simulation Gene, and by 11% W₁ (0.420 vs. 0.474) and 73% RME (0.053 vs. 0.199) on Dyngen. The critical scalability advantage is that VGFM is the only method converging on the 1000D Gaussian mixture (W₁=3.010), while UDSB [57], TIGON [23], and DeepRUOT [26] all fail to converge. Among mass-conservation methods, OT-CFM [22] and OT-MFM [38] achieve W₁ of 0.302 and 0.311 on Simulation Gene respectively—nearly 7× worse than VGFM—and cannot report RME since they do not model growth. On real-world CITE (50D), VGFM achieves W₁=37.386 versus 38.681 for DeepRUOT and 36.394 for OT-MFM, confirming consistent gains in higher dimensions. On the mouse hematopoiesis dataset (Table A-9), VGFM achieves W₁=0.115/0.094 and RME=0.043/0.019, outperforming DeepRUOT (W₁=0.145/0.132, RME=0.140/0.202) by a wide margin on both metrics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the flow matching paradigm (Lipman et al., Liu et al.) for simulation-free velocity field learning and extends it to jointly model a growth function, motivated by the unbalanced mass in single-cell snapshot data. The theoretical core—a two-period dynamic interpretation of static semi-relaxed optimal transport (Proposition 1) and its reparameterization into a joint velocity-growth dynamics (Theorem 1)—provides a principled bridge between the Brenier-Benamou formulation and unbalanced transport, departing from WFR-based methods [44,45] that enforce v=∇g and from simulation-based unbalanced methods (TIGON, DeepRUOT) that require expensive ODE solving during training. The distribution fitting loss via Wasserstein distance complements the matching objective, a design choice shared with recent simulation-based approaches but here applied after a flow-matching warm-up for stability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

VGFM addresses a practical bottleneck in single-cell dynamics modeling: existing unbalanced methods (TIGON, DeepRUOT, UDSB) fail to converge or scale beyond ~50 dimensions, while balanced flow-matching methods ignore mass changes entirely. The demonstrated scalability to 1000D and 2000D gene spaces, combined with the theoretical grounding, makes this a practically useful tool for high-dimensional scRNA-seq analysis. Key limitations acknowledged by the authors include the residual dependence on simulation for the distribution fitting loss (making it not fully simulation-free) and the risk that the learned growth function conflates biological proliferation with sampling artifacts. Open questions include integrating biological priors into the growth function and developing fully simulation-free alternatives that match the current performance.
