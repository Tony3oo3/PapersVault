## Problem

Existing reduced-order models for complex system prediction compress spatially-correlated observations into latent vectors, losing the inherent spatial structure of spatiotemporal dynamics. This prevents predictors from effectively modeling spatial interactions, and the misalignment between encoder-decoder and predictor progressively worsens during long-term prediction as new spatiotemporal patterns emerge. The paper asks whether a reduced-order model can both preserve crucial spatial structure and continuously adapt to newly emerging dynamics without retraining.

## Input / Output

The method takes a lookback trajectory of 2D spatial field frames (h×w grid, typically 128×128) as input and autoregressively predicts one future step at a time, outputting a full-resolution h×w field. A key design choice is that the model operates on a sparse probe topology comprising only ~1% of grid points (e.g., ~150 probes for a 128×128 grid), with probe values filled into their codebook-defined governing regions to guide a diffusion decoder that reconstructs the full spatial field. The probe topology itself is dynamically updated during prediction via re-encoding, so the effective input structure changes over the prediction horizon.

## Method

SparseDiff combines three components: (1) a VQ-VAE-style codebook-based sparse encoder that discretizes spatiotemporal patterns into codewords, then at inference identifies k hit codewords, assigns each a governing region of grid points, selects one probe per region, and constructs a probe graph with region-aware edge weights quantifying spatial association strength; (2) a graph neural ODE predictor (GRAND-style) on the probe topology that uses edge-weight-aware multi-head attention to model spatiotemporal dynamics among probes; and (3) a pretrained unconditional UNet diffusion decoder that reconstructs the full spatial field from probe values filled into their governing regions via Bayes-rule-guided denoising. The key innovation is test-time adaptation: during long-term prediction, the model monitors a latent consistency score (mean cosine similarity between probe encodings and their assigned codewords), and when it drops below a threshold, it decodes the current probe states back to full space and re-encodes to update the probe topology, adapting to emerging spatiotemporal patterns without any weight updates.

## Results

SparseDiff achieves the best RMSE and SSIM across all five evaluation systems (Lambda-Omega, Navier-Stokes, Swift-Hohenberg, Cylinder Flow, and SEVIR real-world data), with an average prediction error reduction of 49.99% over the strongest baseline. On Lambda-Omega, SparseDiff reaches an RMSE of 2.912×10⁻² and SSIM of 9.601×10⁻¹, outperforming the next-best G-LED (6.506×10⁻² / 8.992×10⁻¹) by 55% in RMSE. On Cylinder Flow, the RMSE improvement over G-LED is 14.8% (8.544 vs. 10.021 ×10⁻²), and on the real-world SEVIR dataset, SparseDiff reduces RMSE from 10.304 to 7.957 ×10⁻² while improving SSIM from 6.768 to 7.781 ×10⁻¹. All predictions are made using only ~1% of the full spatial grid points as probes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SparseDiff outperforms all four main baselines (ConvLSTM, FNO, UNet, G-LED) on every metric across all five systems, with no losses or ties. Against the strongest baseline G-LED, SparseDiff reduces RMSE by 55.3% on Lambda-Omega (2.912 vs. 6.506 ×10⁻²), 9.8% on Navier-Stokes (11.130 vs. 12.334 ×10⁻²), 7.1% on Swift-Hohenberg (7.628 vs. 8.214 ×10⁻²), 14.8% on Cylinder Flow (8.544 vs. 10.021 ×10⁻²), and 22.8% on SEVIR (7.957 vs. 10.304 ×10⁻²). ConvLSTM and UNet are consistently the weakest baselines, with ConvLSTM showing particularly poor SSIM on Navier-Stokes (4.696 vs. SparseDiff's 8.492 ×10⁻¹). FNO is competitive on Swift-Hohenberg SSIM (9.614 vs. 9.675) but degrades severely on Cylinder Flow (RMSE 19.854 with ±25.248 std). In the graph construction comparison (Table 2), SparseDiff's region-aware edge weighting (RMSE 2.873) also beats the learnable GKN (4.210) and heuristic kNN/spectral alternatives (3.287/3.019) on Lambda-Omega.

## Relation to Prior Work

SparseDiff synthesizes three established threads: VQ-VAE-style codebook discretization [25] for compressing spatiotemporal patterns into a finite set of codewords, the GRAND graph neural diffusion ODE [26] for modeling dynamics on a graph, and Bayes-rule-guided diffusion posterior sampling [24] for reconstructing full-resolution fields from sparse observations. Its key departure from prior reduced-order models (autoencoder-based [6, 11, 10] and coarse-grid [7, 33]) is that the latent representation is not a fixed vector but an explicit spatial probe topology whose structure is re-derived at test time via re-encoding, rather than being static after training. Compared to diffusion-based predictors like G-LED [31] and Dyffusion [45], which operate in a fixed latent or pixel space, SparseDiff's probe graph preserves spatial adjacency and interaction strength, and its adaptive re-encoding mechanism has no direct analogue in prior work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in achieving ~50% average error reduction over strong baselines while using only ~1% of grid points as probes, which could make long-horizon PDE prediction feasible on resource-constrained hardware or for high-resolution climate data. The test-time adaptation without parameter updates is a lightweight mechanism that could be transplanted to other reduced-order settings. However, the method's reliance on heuristic edge-weight computation, average aggregation for probe initialization (which may discard high-frequency detail), and a UNet-based decoder that requires regular grids limits its generality; the authors acknowledge that end-to-end graph structure learning and irregular-domain support remain open. Whether the codebook-based probe selection generalizes beyond the tested 2D PDE systems to truly 3D or high-dimensional state spaces is an important open question.
