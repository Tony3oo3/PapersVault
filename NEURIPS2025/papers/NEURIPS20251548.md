## Problem

Multi-LLM judge ensembles typically rely on static aggregation (e.g., majority voting), which can yield incorrect consensus even when individual agents are correct, particularly in complex or ambiguous evaluation tasks. While iterative multi-agent debate can correct such errors, existing approaches lack both a formal theoretical guarantee that debate improves over static voting and an efficient mechanism to determine when the debate has converged, leading to either premature stopping or unnecessary computation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251548/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes an evaluation task prompt (e.g., pairwise response comparison, fact-checking, or multiple-choice selection) along with the candidate responses to judge; for visual tasks, an image is additionally provided. An ensemble of 7 LLM agents (e.g., Gemini-2.0-Flash, Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen-2.5-7B, Gemma-3-4B) each produce a judgment label per round, and the system outputs a single consensus judgment. The adaptive stopping mechanism monitors the distribution of correct-agent counts across rounds and halts the debate once the Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic between consecutive rounds falls below 0.05 for two consecutive rounds, typically within 4–8 of the 10-round maximum.

## Method

The paper formalizes a multi-agent debate process in which n LLMs iteratively observe each other's responses and update their beliefs over a latent concept space Θ via Bayesian inference, proving under mild assumptions (true-concept predictiveness, task-concept alignment, positive priors, conditional independence) that the presence of even one strongly consistent response amplifies ensemble correctness and that the final debated outcome strictly outperforms initial majority vote. For computational efficiency, the paper introduces an adaptive stability detection mechanism that models the per-round count of correct judgments as a time-varying two-component Beta-Binomial mixture, estimates its parameters via EM with L-BFGS-B optimization, and tracks distributional stability using the KS statistic between consecutive rounds' fitted CDFs. The debate terminates when the KS statistic remains below a threshold (ε=0.05) for two consecutive rounds, reducing average rounds from 10 to 4–8 while preserving accuracy within ~0.5% of the full 10-round result.

## Results

Across six benchmarks (BIG-Bench, JudgeBench, LLMBar, TruthfulQA, MLLM-Judge, JudgeAnything) and four models, the Debate method (10-round max, ensemble of 7) generally outperforms both Single sampling and SoM (Majority Vote), with the largest gains on complex tasks: Gemini-2.0-Flash improves from 77.75% (SoM) to 81.83% on LLMBar (+4.08 pp) and from 72.01% to 74.30% on TruthfulQA (+2.29 pp). On simpler, high-consensus tasks like BIG-Bench, SoM can match or exceed Debate (e.g., Llama-3.1-8B: SoM 81.80% vs. Debate 74.00%). The adaptive stopping mechanism (KS threshold ε=0.05) typically halts debate in 4–8 rounds versus the 10-round cap, with accuracy differences of ≤0.67 pp compared to running all 10 rounds.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is SoM (Simple Majority Vote), the standard ensemble aggregation method. Debate wins on complex, high-variance tasks—most notably LLMBar (Gemini: 81.83% vs. 77.75%, +4.08 pp; Qwen: 69.81% vs. 66.22%, +3.59 pp) and TruthfulQA (Gemini: 74.30% vs. 72.01%, +2.29 pp; Llama: 55.34% vs. 53.94%, +1.40 pp)—but loses on simpler tasks where initial consensus is already high, such as BIG-Bench (Llama: 74.00% vs. 81.80%, −7.80 pp; Qwen: 72.20% vs. 76.60%, −4.40 pp). The second baseline is the MAD adversarial debate framework (Liang et al., 2024), which structures debates with opposing debaters and a judge; MAD fails to exceed the single-model baseline on most tasks and consistently underperforms both SoM and the proposed collaborative Debate method, suggesting that balanced adversarial exposure to correct and incorrect arguments is counterproductive for judgment tasks. Overall, the paper's method provides targeted gains where collaborative belief refinement corrects correlated biases, while SoM remains competitive on straightforward tasks with low initial disagreement.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the multi-agent debate paradigm introduced by Du et al. (2024) by grounding it in a formal Bayesian framework over latent concepts (following Xie et al., 2022, and Estornell & Liu, 2024), and by proving that iterative debate strictly improves correctness over static majority voting under mild assumptions. The adaptive stopping mechanism repurposes the Beta-Binomial mixture model for judge correctness dynamics from Qu et al. (2025), coupling it with a Kolmogorov-Smirnov stability test to terminate debates early. Unlike prior debate frameworks that rely on fixed round counts or heuristic consensus checks, this work provides both a theoretical guarantee (Theorem 4.2) and a statistically principled stopping criterion, positioning itself at the intersection of ensemble theory, Bayesian inference, and LLM evaluation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theoretical guarantee that debate outperforms majority voting is valuable for justifying the computational overhead of iterative refinement, and the adaptive stopping mechanism offers a practical path to reducing that overhead (converging in 4–8 rounds vs. a fixed 10). However, the empirical gains are modest and inconsistent—on several model-dataset combinations (e.g., Qwen-2.5-7B on BIG-Bench, Llama-3.1-8B on BIG-Bench), debate actually underperforms majority voting, suggesting the theoretical assumptions (particularly conditional independence and true-concept predictiveness) may not hold in practice. Open questions include whether the framework scales to open-ended generation tasks with unbounded answer spaces, whether the Beta-Binomial mixture is the right generative model for more heterogeneous judge populations, and whether the strong Assumption 4.5 (at least one correct seed response) can be relaxed or verified a priori.
