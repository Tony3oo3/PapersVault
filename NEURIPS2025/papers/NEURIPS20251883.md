## Problem

Autoformalization of mathematical theorem statements into formal languages like Lean 4 is bottlenecked by the scarcity of large-scale, high-quality parallel corpora mapping natural language to formal language. Existing approaches either extract formal statements from Mathlib (constraining dataset size) or scrape natural language from the web (requiring extensive preprocessing to filter formalizable content). ATLAS addresses this by inverting the data pipeline: it starts from a concept repository mined directly from Mathlib and synthesizes both sides of the parallel pair.

## Input / Output

The model takes a natural language theorem statement (e.g., 'For any commutative ring R and any two n×n matrices A and B over R, det(AB) = det(A)det(B)') and produces a syntactically valid Lean 4 theorem statement with the standard `import Mathlib` header. During training, NL statements are generated by sampling two concepts from a 350-concept repository organized into 13 domains and 55 topics. Validation of outputs involves a four-step pipeline: Lean compilation for syntactic validity, back-translation via InternLM2-Math-Plus-7B, and an NLI check using Qwen2.5 to confirm semantic equivalence with the original NL statement.

## Method

ATLAS is a three-component data generation framework executed over 10 rounds of expert iteration. **Data Lifting** extracts 350 mathematical concepts from Mathlib to form a concept repository, guaranteeing every synthesized statement has a valid formalization path. **Data Synthesis** uses a teacher-student distillation loop: the teacher (DeepSeek-V2.5) generates NL statements from sampled concept pairs and revises FL statements that fail Lean compilation, while the student (Llama3.1-8B-Instruct) translates NL to FL; the Lean compiler enforces syntactic validity and the teacher performs semantic alignment scoring. **Data Augmentation** expands the synthetic data via two Lean-specific strategies: (1) executing proofs with DeepSeek-ProverV1.5 and harvesting intermediate proof states from the Infoview, and (2) applying the `contrapose!` tactic to generate contrapositive equivalents, with Levenshtein-distance filtering to maximize diversity. The student model is fine-tuned with LoRA on the accumulated 117k parallel statements after each iteration, and the student's contribution to synthetic data grows from 42.68% to 82.30% across rounds.

## Results

The ATLAS Translator (Llama3.1-8B-Instruct fine-tuned with LoRA on 117k parallel statements) achieves pass@1 scores of 39.46% on ProofNet, 23.16% on PutnamBench, and 22.75% on MathQual, establishing a new state of the art across all three benchmarks. On the pass@8 and pass@32 metrics, it reaches 67.28%/78.71% (ProofNet), 55.51%/72.93% (PutnamBench), and 45.85%/58.23% (MathQual). All improvements over the strongest baselines (Herald Translator and Kimina-Autoformalizer) are statistically significant (p < 0.05, two-sided t-test). Additionally, full-parameter fine-tuning of DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-7B-Base on the ATLAS dataset pushes ProofNet pass@1 to 54.99% and PutnamBench pass@1 to 42.49%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the teacher model DeepSeek-V3, ATLAS Translator nearly doubles ProofNet pass@1 (39.46% vs. 18.82%) and more than triples MathQual pass@1 (22.75% vs. 4.90%), confirming that the iterative distillation framework substantially surpasses the teacher. Compared to the previous SOTA Herald Translator (trained on 1,160k data points), ATLAS Translator (trained on only 117k) wins on every reported metric, with the largest gaps on MathQual pass@1 (+11.83 pp) and ProofNet pass@1 (+8.03 pp). Against the newer Kimina-Autoformalizer (which involves Lean 4 experts during training), ATLAS Translator leads on all three MathQual metrics (22.75% vs. 19.01% pass@1; 45.85% vs. 38.97% pass@8; 58.23% vs. 50.71% pass@32). All improvements over both Herald and Kimina are statistically significant at p < 0.05. The paper further shows that full-parameter fine-tuning of DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-7B-Base on the same ATLAS dataset yields the strongest overall results (54.99% ProofNet pass@1, 42.49% PutnamBench pass@1), suggesting that a stronger base model within the iterative loop would yield further gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

ATLAS departs from two dominant paradigms for constructing autoformalization training data: (1) extracting FL statements from Mathlib and generating NL counterparts (MMA, Herald), which is bottlenecked by Mathlib's finite size, and (2) scraping NL statements from the web and formalizing them (Lean Workbook, DeepSeek-Prover), which requires extensive preprocessing. Instead, ATLAS inverts the pipeline by first lifting mathematical concepts from Mathlib into a structured repository, then synthesizing NL statements from those concepts via a teacher LLM, and using a student model with Lean compiler feedback for iterative refinement. The expert-iteration loop follows Polu & Sutskever's curriculum learning framework, while the data augmentation via proof steps and contraposition is novel, exploiting Lean 4's Infoview to extract intermediate proof states as new training examples.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 117k ATLAS dataset and the MathQual benchmark (465 graduate-level problems) address a critical data bottleneck in autoformalization, and the framework's design—starting from Mathlib concepts—guarantees a valid formalization path for every generated statement, eliminating the preprocessing overhead of web-scraping approaches. A key open question is the ~40% false-proposition rate in the synthetic data, which the authors argue is acceptable for translation fidelity but poses risks for downstream theorem proving. Future directions include curriculum learning over a concept graph, extending from individual statements to theory-level autoformalization, and integrating dependency-aware retrieval into the data generation process.
