## Problem

Public pedestrian datasets used to train AI systems frequently contain identifiable individuals without consent or adequate anonymization, and existing protection methods are either insufficient (face-only blurring vulnerable to reversal, GAN-based generation with temporal inconsistency and copyright risks) or significantly degrade data utility for downstream tasks. The paper argues that the AI community has not paid sufficient attention to this gap and calls for a coordinated push toward utility-preserving, full-body, and gait-level anonymization that keeps pace with advancing identification capabilities.

## Input / Output

The paper surveys anonymization methods operating on RGB images and video clips (the dominant case), and extends the discussion to thermal images and event camera data as emerging modalities that pose similar privacy risks. Outputs are anonymized images or videos intended to remain useful for downstream tasks such as pedestrian detection, tracking, trajectory prediction, and crowd counting. A key constraint highlighted is that anonymized video must preserve temporal consistency across frames and maintain task-relevant attributes (age, gender, body shape) while concealing identity, a trade-off that most existing methods fail to balance.

## Method

As a position paper, the core contribution is a structured critique and roadmap rather than a new algorithm. The authors survey representative anonymization techniques (Gaussian blurring, StyleGAN-based face swapping, GAN/diffusion-based full-body synthesis such as DeepPrivacy2 and SG-GAN, and face-swap pipelines like 3PFS), catalog their limitations in a comparative table, and identify six open challenges: real-time performance, scaling to crowded scenes, temporal consistency, copyright/ethical risks of generative AI, preserving individual demographic differences, and resistance to reconstruction attacks. The central technical argument is a progression from face anonymization to full-body anonymization to gait anonymization, coupled with the requirement that methods provide formal privacy guarantees (e.g., differential privacy) and standardized benchmarks for the privacy–utility trade-off.

## Results

This is a position paper and does not report quantitative experimental results or a main results table. The only quantitative data point referenced is from the authors' prior work (Zhao et al. [2024]), where 99.5% of faces remain detectable after anonymization, used to argue that utility-preserving anonymization is feasible. The paper's contribution is a qualitative critique of existing anonymization methods (Table 1 surveys 12 representative approaches) and a call to action for the AI community.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not propose a new method or conduct experimental comparisons against baselines. Instead, it qualitatively surveys and critiques 12 representative anonymization approaches (Table 1), identifying shared limitations: most methods target only faces rather than full bodies, fail to maintain temporal consistency in video, lack real-time performance, and offer no formal privacy guarantees. The authors argue that face anonymization alone is insufficient because gait and body shape can still reveal identity, and that generative-AI-based methods (e.g., DeepPrivacy, DeepPrivacy2, SG-GAN) introduce copyright and ethical risks. They position their prior 3PFS method (Zhao et al. [2024]) as evidence that utility-preserving face anonymization is achievable, but note it requires several seconds per image and does not extend to full-body or gait anonymization. The paper calls for future work on full-body and gait anonymization, temporal consistency, real-time deployment, and formal theoretical guarantees such as differential privacy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes and critiques the existing landscape of pedestrian anonymization methods, spanning face-swapping approaches (DeepPrivacy, LDFA, 3PFS), full-body generative methods (DeepPrivacy2, SG-GAN), and traditional techniques (blurring, masking). It departs from prior work primarily in scope: whereas most prior methods and benchmarks focus narrowly on face anonymization, this paper argues the task must extend to full-body and gait anonymization, citing pedestrian re-identification and gait recognition literature to show that non-facial cues remain identifying. It also integrates perspectives from privacy law (GDPR), federated learning, and generative-AI ethics that are rarely discussed together in computer-vision anonymization papers, positioning pedestrian privacy as a cross-disciplinary challenge rather than a purely technical one.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper highlights a critical gap: no standardized benchmark or evaluation protocol exists for full-body or gait anonymization, making cross-study comparison impossible and leaving the privacy-utility trade-off poorly quantified. It also raises the underexplored issue that current regulations (e.g., GDPR) were designed around RGB imagery and offer no clear guidance for emerging modalities such as thermal and event-camera data, which can still reveal identity through body-heat patterns or motion signatures. Open questions include how to achieve real-time, temporally consistent, utility-preserving anonymization for crowded scenes, how to provide formal theoretical guarantees (e.g., differential privacy) for generative anonymization, and how to prevent demographic bias in anonymization models. The paper's broader call for interdisciplinary collaboration between AI researchers, legal experts, and policymakers is timely as multimodal sensing becomes ubiquitous in autonomous vehicles and robotics.
