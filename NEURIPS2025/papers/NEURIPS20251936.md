## Problem

SeeA* introduces sampling-based exploration into A* search but relies on uniform sampling that ignores heuristic knowledge associated with open nodes, and the theoretical advantage of cluster sampling over uniform sampling remained unexplained. Despite inherent biases, heuristic estimations still encode valuable distributional information that can be leveraged to calibrate the sampling process and improve decision-making quality.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251936/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At each search iteration, KeeA* takes the current OPEN set of unexpanded nodes (represented as 2048-dim Morgan fingerprint vectors for retrosynthesis or AIG graph encodings for logic synthesis), a neural heuristic function f, a fixed number of clusters K, and a candidate set size N^c. It dynamically constructs a candidate set D by first scouting clusters and then performing path-aware intra-cluster sampling, from which the node with the highest f-value is selected for expansion. The final output is a complete synthesis route (sequence of chemical reactions) or an optimized AIG with minimized area-delay product.

## Method

KeeA* builds on SeeA*'s sampling framework with two key innovations. First, cluster scouting scores each cluster by the ratio of mean to standard deviation of f-values among scouted nodes, then allocates the sampling budget across clusters via a softmax over these scores (controlled by hyperparameter α), thereby increasing the expected mean of sampled candidates. Second, path-aware sampling biases intra-cluster selection toward shallower nodes using a depth-based softmax (controlled by β), increasing sampling variance and mitigating concentration in deep branches. The theoretical foundation establishes that cluster sampling yields a higher expected extreme value of true reward f* than uniform sampling (via the Gumbel extreme value theorem and a variance decomposition), and that larger f-value disparities between nodes monotonically increase the probability that the higher-f node also has a greater true reward (Theorem 5.1). Nodes are assigned to K clusters online using nearest-centroid assignment with incremental center updates, and the algorithm terminates when the goal node is selected or the OPEN set is exhausted.

## Results

On retrosynthetic planning across seven benchmarks (USPTO, logP, logS, Toxicity LD50, Ames, BBBP, ClinTox), KeeA* achieves a mean success rate of 63.76% and mean solution length of 14.14, outperforming SeeA*(Cluster) (63.56%, 14.31) and all other baselines. On logic synthesis (12 MCNC circuits), KeeA* achieves a 25.2% mean ADP reduction versus 23.5% for SeeA*(Cluster) and 20.9% for ABC-RL. A McNemar test confirms the success-rate improvement over SeeA* is statistically significant (χ²=3.615, p=0.0286).

## Baselines & Comparisons

KeeA* outperforms all eight baselines on mean success rate across the seven retrosynthetic planning datasets. The closest competitor, SeeA*(Cluster) [Zhao et al., NeurIPS 2024], achieves 63.56% versus KeeA*'s 63.76%, with the gap confirmed statistically significant via McNemar test (p=0.0286). Among classical search methods, LevinTS [Orseau et al., NeurIPS 2018] is the strongest at 61.01%, followed by MCTS [Coulom, 2006] at 59.20% and A* [Hart et al., 1968] at 58.73%. The retrosynthesis-specific baselines Retro* [Chen et al., ICML 2020] and Retro*+ [Kim et al., ICML 2021] trail at 54.66% and 59.93% respectively, while PHS [Kishimoto et al., NeurIPS 2019] is weakest at 56.16%. On logic synthesis, KeeA* (25.2% ADP reduction) surpasses DRiLLS [Hosny et al., ASP-DAC 2020] (14.8%), ABC-RL (20.9%), and SeeA*(Cluster) (23.5%), winning on 11 of 12 MCNC circuits. KeeA* also produces shorter synthesis routes (mean length 14.14 vs. 14.31 for SeeA*) with comparable runtime (~37.4s vs. ~38.0s per molecule).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends SeeA* (Zhao et al., NeurIPS 2024), which introduced selective sampling into A* search to escape local optima caused by biased heuristics. While SeeA* demonstrated that uniform and cluster sampling improve over greedy A* expansion, it treated all open nodes symmetrically within clusters and provided no theoretical justification for why cluster sampling outperforms uniform sampling. KeeA* departs from this by (1) formally proving via the Gumbel extreme value theorem that cluster sampling yields a higher expected maximum of true path reward due to greater sampling variance, and (2) introducing cluster scouting and path-aware sampling to exploit f-value statistics and node depth for non-uniform inter- and intra-cluster allocation, respectively. The broader lineage traces back to Hart et al.'s A* and the MCTS/PUCT exploration literature, but the specific contribution is calibrating the sampling distribution using epistemic knowledge from the heuristic itself.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theoretical formalization that both the mean and variance of sampled candidates govern the expected extreme value (via Gumbel) provides a principled lens for designing any sampling-based search, not just A* variants. However, the empirical gains over SeeA* are modest (63.76% vs. 63.56% retrosynthesis success rate; 25.2% vs. 23.5% ADP reduction), and the optimal sampling distribution shown in Appendix C depends on unknown true-reward statistics, leaving a gap between theory and practice. Open questions include whether the Gaussian noise assumption in Theorem 5.1 holds for real neural heuristics, whether the method transfers to MCTS or other tree-search frameworks, and how to estimate the cluster-level statistics needed for the optimal allocation without access to ground-truth rewards.
