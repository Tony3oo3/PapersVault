## Problem

Decoder-only Transformers are designed for sequential text, yet LLMs can identify graph substructures from textual descriptions. Prior mechanistic analyses focused on simple linear paths (shortest path, reachability), leaving unexplained how Transformers detect complex nonlinear substructures such as cycles, trees, and multi-node motifs. This paper asks: what internal mechanism enables a sequence-based Transformer to progressively identify arbitrary substructures from a textual graph representation?

## Input / Output

The input is a concatenated token sequence: a textual graph representation (adjacency list or edge list, with nodes and edges as individual tokens separated by ':' and ',' or '|' delimiters) followed by a question prompt specifying the target substructure (either a terminology label like 'triangle' or a topology description like 'A:BC,B:C'). Graphs are directed with 4–16 nodes and 3–120 edges; target substructures span 3–5 nodes. The output is a comma-separated sequence of node-ID groups, each group listing the vertices of one identified substructure instance. Under the Tins extension, the output additionally includes intermediate decomposing substructures before a special <ANS> token precedes the final answer.

## Method

The paper introduces Induced Substructure Filtration (ISF), formalizing substructure extraction as a k-node m-filtration: a nested sequence of induced subgraphs V₁′⊆…⊆Vₘ′=V′ where each step corresponds to one Transformer layer aggregating node information. Theoretically, they prove a log-precision Transformer with m+2 layers, constant heads, and O(n^k) hidden dimension can compute the subgraph isomorphism indicator tensor T(G,G′) progressively (Theorem 3.3) and extract the unique matching k-tuple under a single-instance assumption (Theorem 3.5). Empirically, GPT-2-style Transformers (384 hidden dims, 2–5 layers) are trained from scratch on 100K–400K synthetic directed graphs, and t-SNE visualizations of final-token embeddings across layers confirm the progressive clustering predicted by ISF. The same ISF dynamics are validated in fine-tuned LLaMA 3.1-8B-Instruct via increasing ARI/NMI scores with depth. Building on ISF, the Thinking-in-Substructure (Tins) method decomposes complex targets into smaller components, reducing required hidden dimension from O(n^k) to O(n^q) (q<k), and the framework extends to attributed graphs (e.g., molecular functional-group detection) by appending node features to the adjacency-list tokens.

## Results

On the substructure extraction task, a 3-layer GPT-2-style Transformer trained on 100K graphs achieves 96.6% accuracy on triangle detection, while a 5-layer model with 400K training reaches 97.1% on triangles and 96.8% on squares. The minimum number of layers required to exceed 85% accuracy scales with substructure size: 3-node patterns need 3 layers, 4-node patterns need 4 layers, and 5-node patterns need 5 layers. The Thinking-in-Substructure (Tins) decomposition method improves 3-layer diagonal extraction from 19.98% to 66.06% (+46%) and 4-layer performance from 63.14% to 86.48% (+23%) with only 100K training samples. On molecular functional group detection, the Transformer achieves 92.07% accuracy on Hydroxyl (C-O(H)) and 91.59% on Carboxyl (COO(H)) groups.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external baseline methods; its contribution is interpretive and theoretical rather than a new architecture. The key internal comparisons are: (1) layer depth vs. substructure complexity — 3-node substructures (triangles) reach >96% with 3 layers, while 5-node substructures (pentagons, houses) require 5 layers to exceed 85%, confirming the ISF filtration depth hypothesis; (2) AL vs. EL graph representations — both achieve comparable accuracy in theory, but EL requires more layers empirically due to longer token sequences (3×|V|²−3|V| vs. 2×|V|²−|V| tokens for a complete graph); (3) direct extraction vs. Thinking-in-Substructure (Tins) — Tins decomposes complex patterns into simpler components, yielding a 46% absolute improvement on 3-layer diagonal extraction (19.98% → 66.06%) and ~23% on 4-layer (63.14% → 86.48%) with only 100K training samples; (4) terminology-based vs. topology-based question prompts — terminology prompts (e.g., 'Triangle') consistently outperform topology prompts (e.g., 'A:BC,B:C'), with the gap widening for more complex structures like diagonals (90.82% vs. 23.32% in Group 2).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the graph pattern comprehension benchmark of Dai et al. (ICLR 2025) [7] by shifting focus from *what* LLMs can extract to *how* decoder-only Transformers internally perform substructure extraction. The theoretical machinery builds directly on Feng et al. (NeurIPS 2023) [11], whose lemmas for MLP expressiveness and attention COPY/MEAN operations underpin all filtration-based proofs. Conceptually, the work departs from prior path-finding analyses (SLN [5], ALPINE [24], Sanford et al. [17]) that were limited to linear shortest-path structures, generalizing the interpretive lens to arbitrary substructures (cycles, trees, motifs) via the novel Induced Substructure Filtration (ISF) framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ISF provides a unified, layer-wise account of how Transformers progressively assemble substructure representations, bridging the gap between sequence processing and graph structure understanding. The Thinking-in-Substructure (Tins) decomposition offers a practical recipe for reducing extraction complexity from O(n^k) to O(n^q), with empirical gains of up to ~46% on limited-data settings, and the extension to attributed molecular graphs suggests a path toward graph foundation models. Open questions include whether ISF scales to larger graphs (beyond 16 nodes), how it interacts with positional encoding choices, and whether the filtration depth–node count correlation holds for undirected or weighted graphs.
