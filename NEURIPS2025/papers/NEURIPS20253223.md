## Problem

Coreset selection methods reduce training data size while preserving average accuracy, but their impact on spurious correlations and group robustness has been entirely unexplored. Real-world datasets contain biases that cause models to rely on spurious features, and it is unclear whether subsampling perpetuates, amplifies, or mitigates these biases. This paper conducts the first systematic study of how different scoring methods, selection policies, and coreset sizes interact with dataset bias to affect downstream worst-group accuracy.

## Input / Output

The analysis takes a training dataset with class labels (but no spurious-feature annotations) across ten visual and text classification benchmarks, assigns sample-level scores using one of five characterization methods (EL2N, Uncertainty, Forgetting, SelfSup, SupProto), and applies one of five selection policies (Difficult, Easy, Median, Stratified, Random) at varying coreset sizes from 2% to 100%. The outputs are the selected coreset, its quantified bias level (defined as the maximum P(a|y)/P(a) over all class-spurious-feature pairs), and downstream classifier performance measured by both average accuracy and worst-group accuracy. Class balancing is enforced during selection to disentangle class imbalance from spurious-correlation effects.

## Method

The paper formalizes coreset selection into two stages—sample characterization (scoring) and sample selection (policy)—and systematically crosses five scoring methods from two families (learning-based: EL2N, Uncertainty, Forgetting; embedding-based: SelfSup, SupProto) with five selection policies across ten spurious-correlation datasets and a broad range of coreset sizes. Bias is quantified via a dependency measure B(y,a)=P(a|y)/P(a), and samples are categorized as bias-aligning (B>1) or bias-conflicting (B<1). The key empirical findings are: (1) embedding-based scores carry near-random signal for identifying bias-conflicting samples on real-world datasets, posing lower risk of bias exacerbation than learning-based scores; (2) lower coreset bias does not reliably translate to higher worst-group accuracy, especially at small coreset sizes where Difficult-selected coresets suffer catastrophic robustness drops; and (3) group-balancing alone is insufficient—sample difficulty within each group matters, with the most difficult samples per group yielding worse robustness than random or easy selections. The paper concludes with a practical algorithm (Algorithm 1) that selects the smallest coreset size at which Difficult-policy average accuracy matches Random-policy accuracy, falling back to Stratified or Median policies in the small-data regime.

## Results

At 40% coreset selection rate, Difficult selection with EL2N scores achieves the best worst-group accuracy on 5 of 10 datasets (e.g., 87.2% on cMNIST vs. 62.0% random, 74.6% on Waterbirds vs. 58.2% random, 78.5% on Metashift vs. 73.8% random). However, no single policy dominates across all datasets: SelfSup Stratified achieves the best worst-group accuracy on Civilcomments (78.4%) and is competitive on MultiNLI (60.8%). For very small coresets (≤10%), Difficult EL2N selection suffers catastrophic drops in worst-group accuracy (e.g., 31.9% on Waterbirds at 5% vs. 44.7% random), while Easy selection masks poor robustness with high average accuracy (94.8% avg but only 24.8% worst-group on Waterbirds at 5%). Embedding-based scores (SelfSup, SupProto) show near-random Average Precision (≈5–6% AP) for detecting bias-conflicting samples on real-world datasets, versus 23–36% AP for learning-based scores (EL2N, Uncertainty), indicating lower risk of inadvertently exacerbating bias.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper systematically compares five scoring methods (EL2N, Uncertainty, Forgetting, SelfSup, SupProto) across five selection policies (Difficult, Easy, Median, Stratified, Random) on 10 spurious-correlation benchmarks. EL2N with Difficult selection (Paul et al., 2021) achieves the best worst-group accuracy on 5/10 datasets at 40% selection rate, outperforming Random by up to 25.2 points (cMNIST: 87.2 vs. 62.0) and the oracle R-Gbal baseline on cMNIST (87.2 vs. 84.4). SelfSup with Stratified selection (Sorscher et al., 2022; Zheng et al., 2023) wins on Civilcomments (78.4% vs. 77.4% Random) and is competitive on MultiNLI (60.8% vs. 65.6% EL2N Stratified). Uncertainty (Coleman et al., 2020) and Forgetting (Toneva et al., 2019) scores show strong bias-conflicting detection signal (23–36% AP) but are not used in the main accuracy tables. SupProto (Xia et al., 2022) and SelfSup embeddings achieve near-random AP (≈5–6%) for bias-conflicting detection on real-world datasets, indicating they pose lower risk of inadvertently amplifying bias. Critically, the paper shows that lower coreset bias does not reliably translate to higher worst-group accuracy: at 5% selection rate, Difficult EL2N coresets have the lowest bias but only 31.9% worst-group accuracy on Waterbirds (vs. 44.7% Random), while Easy EL2N coresets have the highest bias yet 24.8% worst-group accuracy with 94.8% average accuracy—masking their poor robustness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two well-developed research threads—coreset/data selection (EL2N, SelfSup, SupProto, Forgetting, Uncertainty scores with various selection policies) and spurious correlation mitigation (group-balanced training, distributionally robust optimization)—but is the first to systematically study their interaction. It departs from prior coreset selection work, which evaluates on CIFAR/ImageNet using average accuracy, by introducing worst-group accuracy and bias-level metrics as primary evaluation criteria. It also extends the limited findings of Pote et al. [55] (who showed EL2N pruning could reduce bias on one dataset) and concurrent work by Mulchandani and Kim [56] (pruning for spurious correlations with weaker signals) into a comprehensive multi-dataset, multi-method analysis. The paper carves new ground by revealing that the 'difficulty' signal used in data selection is entangled with bias alignment, a connection not previously formalized.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that reducing bias in a coreset does not reliably improve downstream group robustness, particularly at small coreset sizes—challenges a core assumption in the group-robustness literature that group-balanced training data automatically yields robust models. This has direct practical implications for data-efficient learning on real-world datasets, where practitioners must now consider whether their selection method inadvertently amplifies or masks bias. Open questions include: what constitutes 'sufficiently large' coreset size (which varies empirically across datasets), whether the findings generalize to unknown/unlabeled spurious features, and whether the proposed heuristic (Algorithm 1) scales to the very high pruning rates common in practice. The paper's scope is limited to 10 datasets with known spurious features and a finite set of scoring methods, leaving the full landscape of real-world bias patterns unexplored.
