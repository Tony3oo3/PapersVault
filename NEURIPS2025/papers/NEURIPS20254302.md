## Problem

Most brain-to-speech interface research targets speech production via articulatory motor regions, rendering it inapplicable to patients with speech motor impairments such as aphasia or locked-in syndrome. Auditory speech perception—the neural encoding of heard speech—remains largely unexplored in neural decoding, and no publicly available intracranial EEG dataset existed for this task. This paper addresses both gaps by releasing the first sEEG dataset for auditory reconstruction (NeuroListen) and proposing a dedicated decoding architecture.

## Input / Output

The model consumes bipolar-referenced sEEG signals from 8–13 electrode shafts (118–186 contacts total), split into two parallel frequency bands: high-gamma activity (70–150 Hz analytic amplitude) and low-frequency signal (<100 Hz), each downsampled to 200 Hz and normalized per 2-second window. The output is an 80-bin mel-spectrogram (64 ms window, 20 ms hop) that is vocoded into a speech waveform via HiFi-GAN. The dual-band design means the network processes two independent hypergraph pipelines before fusing their representations, rather than operating on a single broadband signal.

## Method

HyperSpeech models sEEG dynamics with dynamic spatio-temporal hypergraph neural networks that capture high-order (multi-way) dependencies, in contrast to pairwise graph edges. At each time step, a spatial hypergraph is constructed by connecting each brain-region node to its k nearest neighbors, forming hyperedges that encode coordinated multi-regional activity; a temporal hypergraph then links each time frame to its k most similar neighbors to capture non-local temporal structure. The two frequency bands (HGA and LFS) are processed through parallel spatio-temporal hypergraph convolutions, fused via a fully connected layer, and refined by a Bi-LSTM for fine-grained temporal modeling before projecting to mel-spectrogram features. A HiFi-GAN vocoder (trained with multi-scale and multi-period discriminators) converts the predicted mel-spectrogram into the final waveform. Ablations confirm that both the spatial and temporal hypergraph modules, as well as the dual-band fusion, contribute meaningfully to decoding accuracy over GNN and single-band baselines.

## Results

On the NeuroListen dataset (5 subjects, 5-fold cross-validation), HyperSpeech achieves average PCC of 0.9488, MCD of 1.993, RMSE of 0.2522, and STOI of 0.8667, outperforming all three baselines on every objective metric. In subjective evaluation by 30 raters, HyperSpeech scores 3.92 SMOS and 4.55 CMOS (on a 1–5 scale), again leading all competitors. The largest per-subject gains appear on Subject 4 (PCC 0.9781, STOI 0.8955) and Subject 5 (PCC 0.9645, STOI 0.8936), where the hypergraph model's advantage over baselines is most pronounced.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HyperSpeech outperforms all three baselines on every metric for every subject, with no losses or ties. Against CNN-LSTM (the strongest baseline on PCC at 0.9393), HyperSpeech gains +0.0095 PCC, −0.079 MCD, −0.0153 RMSE, +0.0165 STOI, +0.16 SMOS, and +0.22 CMOS on average. Against Braintalker, the margins are larger: +0.0163 PCC, −0.066 MCD, −0.0294 RMSE, +0.0131 STOI, +0.33 SMOS, and +0.30 CMOS. Against FastSpeech, HyperSpeech leads by +0.0192 PCC, −0.092 MCD, −0.0275 RMSE, +0.0146 STOI, +0.36 SMOS, and +0.12 CMOS. The most consistent and largest advantage is in subjective similarity (SMOS), where HyperSpeech exceeds the best baseline by 0.16–0.36 points, suggesting the hypergraph model produces speech that listeners perceive as more closely matching the original. Notably, on Subject 3 (where baselines are already strong, with CNN-LSTM reaching PCC 0.9421 and STOI 0.8758), HyperSpeech still edges ahead on PCC (0.9523) and SMOS (3.90), though it trails CNN-LSTM slightly on STOI (0.8550 vs 0.8758) and MCD (1.716 vs 1.688) for that single subject.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of two underexplored directions: (1) shifting neural speech decoding from production (articulatory motor cortex, ECoG-based work by Metzger, Moses, Chang) to auditory perception, and (2) moving from fMRI-based auditory reconstruction (Park, LeBel, Liu) to the intracranial sEEG modality with millisecond temporal resolution. Methodologically, it repurposes dynamic spatio-temporal hypergraph neural networks—previously applied in action recognition and time-series analysis—into the neural speech decoding pipeline, combining them with standard CNN-LSTM and HiFi-GAN components. The empty 'builds_on' field reflects that no prior work has attempted sEEG-based auditory speech reconstruction, making this a genuinely new modality-task pairing rather than an incremental extension of an existing method.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The release of NeuroListen as the first public sEEG dataset for auditory speech reconstruction is the paper's most broadly impactful contribution, providing a foundation for the entire subfield of perception-based neural decoding that has been dominated by production-focused work. Clinically, this opens a path to assistive communication for locked-in syndrome and aphasia patients who cannot produce speech, since the method decodes perceived rather than produced language. Key open questions include cross-subject generalization (limited by variable electrode placement across the 5 participants), scalability to larger cohorts, and whether the hypergraph architecture's advantage over standard GNNs persists at scale. The paper itself acknowledges these limitations and points toward transferable decoding frameworks and multilingual generalization as future directions.
