## Problem

Conventional LLM evaluation metrics such as perplexity and n-gram statistics emphasize local prediction accuracy but fail to capture long-range structural complexity, leaving puzzling behaviors like repetition, incoherence, and hallucination unexplained even when perplexity is low. There is no single metric that bridges microscopic (token-level) and macroscopic (long-range, structural) perspectives of model behavior in a unified, interpretable framework.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a text sequence and an autoregressive LLM, extracting the full next-token log-probability vector (dimension ≈ vocabulary size, ~10⁵) at each position. For long sequences exceeding the model's context window, a moving-window approach with a fixed context length (e.g., 512 tokens) is used; for shorter sequences, the full context is provided. The output is a scalar correlation dimension (the power-law exponent of the correlation integral) along with the full S(ε) curve, computed via Euclidean distances between log-probability vectors in FP32 regardless of model precision.

## Method

The paper applies the Grassberger-Procaccia correlation dimension to sequences of next-token log-probability vectors, defining recurrences as pairs of vectors within a Euclidean distance threshold ε and estimating the dimension as the slope of log S(ε) versus log ε. A key empirical finding is that single-step (k=1) log-probability vectors suffice without time-delayed embeddings, as they inherently encode long-range structural information. For efficient computation, the authors fuse pairwise distance calculation and threshold counting into a single CUDA kernel (inspired by FlashAttention), achieving zero additional memory overhead and ~2× speedup, and apply a deterministic modulo-based linear projection to reduce vocabulary dimension by ~10× with negligible accuracy loss. The method is robust to 4-bit quantization (mean absolute change < 0.14) and reveals three distinct pretraining phases, distinguishes hallucination from memorization on knowledge-intensive texts, and detects all three forms of degeneration (repetition, incoherence, blandness) where perplexity gives inconsistent signals.

## Results

On the SEP dataset, correlation dimension stabilizes around 6.5 across well-trained LLMs (Qwen2.5-32B: 6.31, Qwen2.5-7B: 6.27, Falcon3-10B: 6.56), confirming convergence as perplexity decreases. For degeneration detection (Table 4), normal texts yield CorrDim 5.04, while repetitive (3.80), incoherent (3.96), and bland (4.51) texts all show significantly lower dimensions (Wilcoxon p < 0.01), whereas perplexity gives inconsistent directional signals across degeneration types. In the stress-test against HelloEval (Table 5), CorrDim achieves Spearman's ρ = 0.952 with HelloEval scores across 8 models, ranging from 3.54 (Qwen2-7B-Instruct) to 8.89 (Yi1.5-34B). The metric remains stable under 4-bit quantization (GPTQ/AWQ) with mean absolute change of only 0.14 across 60 SEP articles.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Rep-N (Holtzman et al., ICLR 2020), correlation dimension demonstrates a key advantage: it captures semantic repetition rather than surface-level word overlap. On Japanese texts, converting kanji to kana (reducing vocabulary ~10×) changes CorrDim by only 5.7% (6.44 → 6.57) but shifts Rep-N by 29.8% (0.60 → 0.78), showing CorrDim is invariant to morphological variation. Against HelloEval (Que et al., 2024), correlation dimension achieves a near-perfect rank correlation (Spearman's ρ = 0.952) without requiring complex evaluation tasks or reference texts, serving as a lightweight proxy for long-text generation quality. Compared to perplexity, CorrDim provides consistent directional signals across all three degeneration types (repetition, incoherence, blandness), while perplexity decreases for repetition (1.25 vs. 10.79) but increases for incoherence (13.24 vs. 10.79), making it an unreliable intrinsic degeneration indicator.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes Grassberger–Procaccia's correlation dimension from chaotic dynamical systems to the domain of autoregressive language model evaluation, operating on next-token log-probability vectors rather than static word embeddings or perplexity sequences. It extends the line of work on statistical self-similarity in language (Zipf's law, long-range correlations, Doxas et al.'s semantic-space fractals) by making the measure model-dependent and context-sensitive, and departs from Alabdulmohsin et al. [2], who measured fractal exponents on cumulative log-perplexity, by instead analyzing the intrinsic recurrence structure of the model's predictive state. The authors' own prior work [13] on semantic self-similarity across languages provides the conceptual groundwork, but this paper introduces the specific computational recipe (Euclidean distance on log-probability vectors) and a suite of practical applications—training diagnostics, hallucination detection, and degeneration quantification—that were absent from prior fractal-language studies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The three-stage evolution of correlation dimension during pretraining (rapid drop, rise, gradual decline) is invisible in perplexity and offers a new diagnostic signal for training stability, particularly the late-stage dimension spike in small models that correlates with in-context learning failure. The hallucination case study—where a 10B model outperforms a 32B model on knowledge recall, with correlation dimension cleanly separating the two regimes—suggests a lightweight, inference-time proxy for model confidence on knowledge-intensive content. Open questions include formal statistical properties of the finite-sample estimator, whether the ~6.5 convergence value is truly universal across domains, and how the metric behaves under conditional generation or multi-modal settings. The method's robustness to 4-bit quantization and its O(N²) cost (mitigated by the FlashAttention-inspired fused kernel) make it practically deployable, but broader validation across more architectures and tasks would strengthen the claims.
