## Problem

Meritocratic selection systems are formally neutral yet produce persistent group disparities in representation and outcomes. Prior explanations attribute these gaps to structural barriers or statistical discrimination through group-dependent beliefs, but it remains unclear how differences in perceived post-selection value alone—under perfect institutional information and symmetric evaluation—can rationally generate systematic effort and outcome disparities. This paper is the first to characterize equilibrium behavior in large-agent contests where groups have asymmetric valuation distributions.

## Input / Output

The model takes a group proportion α, selection fraction c, a base valuation density p₁, a bias parameter ρ (defining p₂ as the ρ-scaled version of p₁), an ability distribution p_a, and a strictly increasing merit function m. It outputs a scalar Nash equilibrium threshold t that fully determines the threshold policy, along with closed-form expressions for representation ratio, social welfare ratio, and average revenue. The intervention design module additionally takes a fairness target τ (e.g., the 80% rule) and cost functions, producing an optimal pair (Δ_ρ, Δ_c) that minimizes total cost subject to the fairness constraint.

## Method

The paper formulates a two-group all-pay auction where each agent's payoff equals selection probability times group-specific valuation minus effort cost, with selection based on a strictly increasing merit function applied to score (effort + ability). The key result (Theorem 3.1) shows that in the large-n limit, the unique NE is a threshold policy: agents exert effort max{t−a, 0} if their combined type v+a exceeds threshold t, and zero otherwise, where t solves a single equation involving the mixture CDF (1−α)F₁ + αF₂. The proof introduces a proxy sequence with threshold shifts Δ_n = O(√(log n/n)) to establish convergence and approximate NE guarantees, bypassing the absence of explicit finite-n equilibrium forms. For uniform densities, closed-form expressions for t, r_R, r_S, and RV reveal a sharp threshold at ρ = 1 − c/(1−α) below which the disadvantaged group exerts no effort at all. A cost-sensitive optimization (Problem 6) then trades off increasing selectivity c (revenue loss) against reducing bias ρ (resource cost) under fairness constraints, solved in closed form for linear cost functions.

## Results

The paper's central quantitative finding is a sharp threshold at ρ_c = 1 − c/(1−α): below this value, the representation ratio r_R and social welfare ratio r_S are exactly zero (no disadvantaged-group agents participate), while above it they increase monotonically to 1 at ρ=1. Calibrating on JEE Advanced 2024 data (180,200 candidates, 48,248 qualified), the model recovers ρ ≈ 0.882 from the observed representation ratio r_obs ≈ 0.671, with c ≈ 0.268 and α ≈ 0.228. The cost-sensitive intervention analysis reveals a crossover at target threshold τ = 0.92: for τ ≤ 0.92, increasing selectivity (raising c) is more cost-effective, while for τ > 0.92, reducing valuation bias (raising ρ) is preferable. The finite-n Nash equilibrium converges to the large-n threshold policy at rate O(√(log n/n)).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper without head-to-head empirical baselines; its contribution is a new equilibrium characterization rather than a method that outperforms prior approaches on a benchmark. The paper positions itself against three prior frameworks: (1) undifferentiated all-pay auctions [76, 52, 82], where all agents share the same valuation distribution — the two-group model here generalizes this by introducing asymmetric ρ-biased valuations, which the authors note makes the Nash equilibrium significantly harder to compute (Section A.1 shows the winning-probability expressions become asymmetric double sums); (2) strategic selection models [29, 27] that introduce bias through effort costs or head-starts while keeping valuations symmetric — the present model instead places asymmetry in valuations, a structurally different source of disparity; and (3) statistical discrimination models [3, 21, 23] that generate gaps through imperfect group-level beliefs — here the institution has perfect information and symmetric evaluation, so disparities arise purely from rational agents responding to different perceived post-selection values. The JEE Advanced 2024 calibration serves as a validation: the model's predicted representation ratio (0.671) matches the observed data exactly by construction, and the recovered ρ ≈ 0.882 is consistent with the known gender pay gap literature. No competing model is run on the same data for direct comparison.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper integrates the ρ-biased valuation model from algorithmic fairness (Kleinberg & Raghavan, ITCS 2018) into a game-theoretic contest framework, extending all-pay auction and Tullock contest models that previously assumed symmetric valuations or introduced bias only through effort costs and headstarts (Seel & Wasser 2014; Emelianov et al. EC 2022). The key departure is treating group-level valuation asymmetry as the sole source of strategic disparity, with the institution having perfect information and symmetric evaluation rules. The authors explicitly position this as the first study of Nash equilibrium in large-agent contests with asymmetric valuation distributions across groups, bridging contest theory and fairness-in-selection literature that had previously been analyzed in isolation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a formal, quantitative mechanism explaining how perceived-value disparities (from wage gaps, biased LLM recommendations, or structural inequality) rationally propagate into effort and representation gaps even under formally meritocratic selection — a result that bridges rational-choice and structural explanations of inequality. The sharp threshold at ρ = 1 − c/(1−α), below which the disadvantaged group is entirely excluded from competition, highlights how small perceptual biases can cause complete dropout in selective environments. Limitations include the static (single-round) setting, two-group restriction, full rationality assumption, and single-dimensional merit; open questions include embedding the model in dynamic feedback loops where perceptions evolve across repeated selection cycles, extending to multi-group and multi-dimensional settings, and incorporating bounded rationality or noisy information. The cost-sensitive optimization framework and JEE Advanced calibration offer a template for data-grounded institutional design, though operationalizing the model in practice requires richer empirical validation.
