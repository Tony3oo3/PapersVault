## Problem

Deploying RL policies trained in simulators to the real world risks violating safety constraints due to the sim-to-real gap. Robust safe RL methods offer provable guarantees but are difficult to scale, while domain randomization is practical yet provides no safety guarantees and empirically violates constraints upon deployment. The paper seeks a method that is both provably safe under sim-to-real transfer and seamlessly compatible with standard training pipelines.

## Input / Output

The policy receives standard robot state vectors (joint positions, velocities, etc.) or, in the vision-based variant, sequences of 3 stacked 64×64 grayscale images, and outputs continuous control actions. The key unusual aspect is that during training, the cost signal is augmented with a penalty computed from an ensemble of domain-randomized dynamics: for each state-action pair, n parallel next-state rollouts are executed and their component-wise empirical variance (L1 norm) is added to the cost, making the training objective pessimistic without requiring any real-world data.

## Method

SPiDR (Sim-to-real via Pessimistic Domain Randomization) modifies standard domain randomization by penalizing the cost function with an uncertainty estimate derived from ensemble disagreement. Specifically, for each sampled dynamics ξ_i used to roll out the policy, an additional ensemble of n dynamics {ξ_ij} is sampled, and the L1 norm of the component-wise empirical variance of their next-state predictions serves as a proxy for the worst-case L1-Wasserstein model discrepancy. This penalty is added to the cost to form a pessimistic CMDP, which is then solved with any standard CMDP solver (SAC+CRPO, PPO+primal-dual, etc.). The core theoretical result (Theorem 4.2) shows that any solution to this penalized CMDP provably satisfies the safety constraint in the real environment, because the penalty dominates the true sim-to-real gap. The only modification relative to standard domain randomization is the ensemble rollout and variance computation (Algorithm 1, Lines 8–10), making the method drop-in compatible with existing pipelines.

## Results

On two real-world robotic platforms, SPiDR achieves zero-shot constraint satisfaction while domain randomization dramatically violates safety constraints. On the Unitree Go1 quadruped, SPiDR completes all 10 trials without falling, whereas RAMU (with default hyperparameters) falls in 12±4.8% of trials. Across six sim-to-sim tasks spanning RWRL, Safety Gym, and RaceCar benchmarks, SPiDR is the only method that satisfies the safety constraint on all tasks, while both Nominal and Domain Randomization fail on every task. On the RaceCar task specifically, SPiDR also outperforms domain randomization on the objective (reward), as its more conservative policy avoids overshooting the target. For ensemble size n≥8, the safety constraint is maintained across all environments with only marginal runtime overhead relative to standard domain randomization (training SAC for 5M steps in under an hour on a single RTX 4090).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Domain Randomization, SPiDR wins decisively on safety: on both the remote-controlled race car and Unitree Go1, domain randomization satisfies the constraint in simulation but dramatically violates it in the real world, while SPiDR maintains constraint satisfaction in both settings. This pattern holds across all six sim-to-sim benchmark tasks, where domain randomization fails to meet the constraint on every task. On the RaceCar task, SPiDR additionally outperforms domain randomization on the objective, because DR's aggressive policy overshoots the target while SPiDR's pessimistic penalty yields a more controlled trajectory. Against RAMU (Queeney & Benosman, NeurIPS 2024), SPiDR achieves comparable performance on the RWRL locomotion environments but significantly outperforms it on the more challenging RaceCar task with realistic model discrepancies. On the real Unitree Go1, SPiDR completes all trials without falling while RAMU falls in 12±4.8% of trials. SPiDR also demonstrates greater versatility, working with both SAC+CRPO and PPO+primal-dual solvers, whereas RAMU is tied to TD-based methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

SPiDR builds directly on domain randomization (Tobin et al., 2017) as its training backbone, modifying only the trajectory collection procedure to add a pessimistic penalty based on ensemble variance. It departs from the robust optimization line of work (Zhang et al., 2024; Kitamura et al., 2024) that requires solving minimax formulations and significant pipeline changes, and from scalable robust methods like RAMU (Queeney & Benosman, 2024) that lack formal safety guarantees. The key conceptual departure is framing the sim-to-real safety problem as a penalized CMDP where the penalty is estimated from simulated data alone via ensemble disagreement, yielding a modular algorithm compatible with any existing CMDP solver (SAC+CRPO, PPO+primal-dual, etc.) without requiring access to real-world data or intermediate high-fidelity simulators.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Safe zero-shot sim-to-real transfer is a critical bottleneck for deploying RL in high-stakes domains (robotics, healthcare, autonomous driving), and this paper provides a theoretically grounded yet practically simple solution that integrates into existing training pipelines with minimal overhead. The real-world validation on two distinct robotic platforms (Unitree Go1 quadruped and a remote-controlled race car) strengthens confidence that the guarantees translate beyond simulation. Limitations include the zero-shot restriction (no online adaptation), the requirement of bounded model mismatch, and potential over-conservatism in regions that are actually safe but uncertain in simulation. An important open direction the authors identify is combining simulation-trained policies with safe online exploration for settings where the sim-to-real gap exceeds what domain randomization can capture.
