## Problem

While correlated equilibria are computationally tractable in standard games, it remained open whether efficient algorithms exist for computing *any* correlated equilibrium in generalized games (games with coupled constraints between players). This paper resolves the question in the negative, proving that computing an approximate Φ-equilibrium in generalized games is PPAD-complete, even for two-player games or for games with a constant number of actions and constraints per player.

## Input / Output

The problem takes as input an n-player game with utility functions, per-player cost functions defining coupled constraints, a deviation polytope Φ (given as linear inequalities over right stochastic matrices), an approximation parameter ε', and a slackness parameter ν. The output is a correlated strategy z (represented as a mixture of product distributions to ensure polynomial-size representation) that is ν-safe for all players and satisfies incentive compatibility up to ε' against all safe deviations. The hardness result holds for CCE deviations with ε' = O(ε/n) and ν = O(ε/(nk·deg(G))), and under the Exponential Time Hypothesis for PPAD, quasi-polynomial time is required even for constant approximation and constant number of players.

## Method

The PPAD-hardness proof reduces from POLYMATRIX by creating left and right copies of each player, forming two opposing teams in a zero-sum polymatrix game. Coupled cost constraints on left-team players force their marginals to match those of the corresponding right-team players, which eliminates the correlation-dependent terms in the incentive compatibility constraints and reduces the problem to computing Nash equilibria of the original polymatrix instance. For PPAD-membership, the paper reduces CONSTRAINED-Φ-EQUILIBRIUM to the QUASIVI problem by restricting to product distributions and showing that CCE deviations are the most expressive class when applied to product distributions, then constructing a Lipschitz operator F (stacked utility gradients) and a Lipschitz correspondence Q encoding both simplex and cost constraints.

## Results

The main result (Theorem 3.1) establishes PPAD-hardness of computing an approximate constrained Φ-equilibrium in generalized games via a polynomial-time reduction from POLYMATRIX, with approximation ε' = O(ε/n) and slackness ν = O(ε/(nk·deg(G))). The hardness holds even for two-player games and for games with a constant number of actions and constraints per player. Proposition 4.2 proves PPAD-membership by reducing to QUASIVI, establishing PPAD-completeness. Under the Exponential Time Hypothesis for PPAD, the paper shows that computing a constant-approximation constrained CE requires quasi-polynomial time k^{log^{1-o(1)}(k)}, matching the quasi-polynomial upper bound of Bernasconi et al. [2023].

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a pure theory paper with no empirical baselines or comparison tables. The key theoretical comparison is with Bernasconi et al. [2023], who proved NP-hardness for computing a social welfare maximizing correlated equilibrium in generalized games and provided a quasi-polynomial time algorithm k^{log(k)} for constant-approximation CCE with a constant number of players. The present paper resolves the open question of computing *any* equilibrium by showing PPAD-hardness (a stronger intractability result than NP-hardness for search problems), and shows that the quasi-polynomial upper bound of Bernasconi et al. is tight under ETH for PPAD. The reduction from POLYMATRIX (Rubinstein 2015) is the hardness source, and the PPAD-membership relies on the QUASIVI framework of Bernasconi et al. [2024]. The paper also contrasts with the positive convergence guarantees for no-regret algorithms in unconstrained settings (Hart and Mas-Colell 2000), showing that no general-purpose no-regret algorithm can converge to constrained correlated equilibria.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper resolves the open complexity question left by Bernasconi et al. [2023], who proved NP-hardness of computing a social welfare maximizing correlated equilibrium in generalized games but left the complexity of computing any equilibrium unresolved. The hardness proof reduces from POLYMATRIX (Rubinstein, 2015), leveraging the PPAD-completeness of Nash equilibrium computation in polymatrix games, while the PPAD-membership proof reduces to the QUASIVI problem shown to be in PPAD by Bernasconi et al. [2024]. The key technical insight is that coupled constraints can be used to force marginals of correlated players to coincide, effectively simulating the independence structure of Nash equilibria within a correlated framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result establishes that correlation is not a sufficient relaxation to overcome the computational intractability introduced by coupled constraints, resolving a fundamental tension in the field. Under the Exponential Time Hypothesis for PPAD, the quasi-polynomial time algorithm of Bernasconi et al. [2023] is essentially tight, closing the gap between upper and lower bounds. A significant practical implication is that no general-purpose no-regret algorithm can converge to constrained correlated equilibria, with direct consequences for autobidding systems in online advertising and other constrained learning environments. An open question remains whether hardness holds for constant approximations with a constant number of players, actions, and constraints.
