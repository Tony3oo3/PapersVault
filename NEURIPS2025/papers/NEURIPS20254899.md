## Problem

Existing evaluation approaches for Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) suffer from three key limitations: reliance on public datasets risks data leakage since training sets are no longer unified, open-ended evaluation is difficult to quantify objectively, and single-aspect benchmarks (e.g., hallucination or robustness) cannot capture the full capability spectrum. The paper addresses this gap by introducing MME, the first comprehensive benchmark that simultaneously covers both perception and cognition abilities across 14 subtasks with manually constructed annotations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254899/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each test image is paired with two manually designed yes/no questions (one whose ground truth is 'yes' and one whose ground truth is 'no'), followed by the fixed suffix 'Please answer yes or no.' The MLLM produces a free-text response that is scored by checking whether 'yes' or 'no' appears at the beginning of the output. Scoring uses two metrics: ACC (per-question accuracy, random baseline 50%) and ACC+ (per-image accuracy requiring both questions correct, random baseline 25%), with subtask scores being the sum of both. The full perception score is 2000 (10 subtasks × 200) and the full cognition score is 800 (4 subtasks × 200).

## Method

MME is structured around 14 subtasks divided into perception (existence, count, position, color, OCR, poster, celebrity, scene, landmark, artwork) and cognition (commonsense reasoning, numerical calculation, text translation, code reasoning). All instruction-answer pairs are manually constructed rather than sourced from existing dataset annotations, even when images are sampled from public datasets like COCO, to eliminate data leakage risk. The instruction design is deliberately minimal—a single concise question plus 'Please answer yes or no'—to ensure fair comparison across models without prompt engineering confounds and to enable objective, automated scoring. The dual-question-per-image design (one positive, one negative) reduces the chance that a model merely guesses, since ACC+ requires both answers to be correct. The benchmark evaluates 30 state-of-the-art MLLMs in a zero-shot setting, revealing four recurring failure modes: inability to follow basic instructions, lack of basic perception, broken reasoning chains, and instruction-following hallucination where models affirm objects not present in the image.

## Results

On the MME benchmark (full scores: Perception 2000, Cognition 800), WeMM achieves the highest overall perception score of 1621.66, while Lion and SPHINX tie for the top cognition score at 153.33. GPT-4V ranks 10th in perception (1409.43) and 7th in cognition (95.00), but dominates OCR (185/200) and code reasoning (170/200), far ahead of all other models. No model exceeds 150 on any individual cognition subtask, and object position is consistently the weakest coarse-grained perception task across all 30 evaluated MLLMs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The benchmark evaluates 30 MLLMs, revealing clear specialization patterns. WeMM leads overall perception (1621.66) by excelling in fine-grained recognition (celebrity: 179.12, scene: 176.25), while GPT-4V trails at 1409.43 partly due to a zero score on celebrity recognition (it refuses to identify individuals) and lower scores on position and count. GPT-4V, however, dominates OCR (185 vs. Skywork-MM's 162.5) and code reasoning (170 vs. WeMM's 117.5), showing a 22+ point lead over the second-best model in both. On cognition, Lion and SPHINX (153.33) outperform GPT-4V (95.00), with GPT-4V's weakness in text translation (75.00) and numerical calculation (130.00) offsetting its code reasoning strength. Coarse-grained perception shows model-specific strengths: Muffin tops count (163.33), Lion tops position (153.33), and InfMLLM tops color (185.00), while all models struggle most with position (best ACC+ only 70%). Older models like MiniGPT-4 (perception 830, cognition 63.86) and Multimodal-GPT (perception ~1050) lag significantly behind instruction-tuned models, confirming that instruction tuning is critical for MLLM performance on this benchmark.

## Relation to Prior Work

MME carves new ground in MLLM evaluation by departing from three existing approaches: (1) evaluating on traditional multimodal datasets like COCO captions or VQA, which risks data leakage and fails to probe emergent abilities; (2) open-ended conversational evaluation (e.g., ChatBridge), which is either unavailable or too small-scale; and (3) single-aspect evaluations targeting only hallucination or adversarial robustness. Rather than building on a specific prior method, MME synthesizes the field's evaluation needs into a unified framework with manually designed yes/no instruction-answer pairs, a concise instruction format to eliminate prompt-engineering confounds, and a dual ACC/ACC+ metric that jointly measures per-question and per-image correctness. The benchmark's design philosophy—fair comparison under identical minimal instructions and objective binary scoring—repurposes the VQA paradigm in a controlled, leakage-free setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MME provides the first standardized, comprehensive yardstick for MLLMs, and its results reveal that even the strongest models (GPT-4V, WeMM, Lion) leave substantial room for improvement, particularly in cognition subtasks where no model exceeds 150/200. The identification of four recurring failure modes—instruction non-compliance, perception fragility, broken reasoning chains, and instruction-induced hallucination—gives the community concrete targets for model optimization. Key limitations include the relatively small per-subtask sample sizes (20–200 images), the restriction to yes/no answers which may underrepresent open-ended reasoning, and the absence of video or multi-turn evaluation. As MLLMs evolve rapidly, the benchmark will need iterative updates to maintain discriminative power, and extending it to more complex reasoning scenarios remains an open direction.
