## Problem

Video generation models encode strong visual and motion priors from internet-scale pretraining but lack precise physical control signals beyond text and camera parameters. While trajectory-based motion control has been explored, physically meaningful interactions—applying forces like pokes or wind to objects—remain largely unaddressed. The central challenge is obtaining high-quality paired force-video training data, which is difficult to capture in the real world (force signals are hard to measure) and limited in synthetic settings (physics simulators produce constrained visual quality and domain diversity).

## Input / Output

The model takes a text prompt, an initial RGB frame (480×720), and a continuous physics control signal, producing a 49-frame video at 8 fps. The control signal is encoded differently per model: the global wind model maps a 2D force vector (magnitude ∈ [0,1], angle ∈ [0,360°)) to a constant 3-channel tensor across all 49 frames, while the local point force model maps the same vector plus pixel coordinates (x,y) to a temporal sequence of frames showing a Gaussian blob translating from the application point in the force direction, with total displacement affinely proportional to force magnitude (w/8 pixels at F=0 to w/2 at F=1).

## Method

Force Prompting attaches a ControlNet branch to CogVideoX-5B-I2V that processes the physics control tensor through downscaling, encoding, and temporal compression before injecting it into the base model's hidden states via a zero convolution; the ControlNet clones the first six transformer layers and is fine-tuned while the base model stays frozen. Training uses synthetic paired data generated in Blender (15k flag-waving videos for global wind; 23k ball-rolling and plant-poking videos for local force), with randomized object counts, colors, positions, camera angles, and force parameters to ensure visual diversity. The key insight is that the pretrained video model already encodes strong priors about visual dynamics, and the paired force-video data serves to elicit rather than teach its understanding of physics-based control, enabling remarkable sim-to-real generalization to diverse objects, materials, and geometries unseen during training. Ablations identify two factors critical to this generalization: visual diversity in the training data relative to the control signal, and the inclusion of force-related text keywords (e.g., 'wind,' 'breeze') during training.

## Results

In 2AFC human studies (N=10), Force Prompting achieves 86–97% win rates on Force Adherence and 75–93% on Realistic Physics against Motion Prompting across all six motion categories (linear, oscillatory, complex, tethered, aerodynamic, fluid dynamics). Against text-only zero-shot baselines, Force Adherence ranges from 67–97%, while against text-only fine-tuned baselines it ranges from 57–79%. Visual Quality is competitive, with Force Prompting preferred 95–100% of the time over Motion Prompting but roughly at parity (47–58%) with text-only fine-tuned baselines. The model was trained on only ~15k synthetic flag videos (global) and ~23k ball/plant videos (local) in one day on four A100 GPUs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against **Motion Prompting** (Geng et al., 2024), Force Prompting wins decisively on Force Adherence (86–97%) and Realistic Physics (75–93%) across all six motion categories, while also winning on Visual Quality (95–100%), likely because Motion Prompting is built on the lower-fidelity Lumiere backbone. Against **text-only zero-shot** (CogVideoX with force described in a text suffix), Force Prompting wins on Force Adherence in 5 of 6 categories (67–97%) but is at or below parity on Realistic Physics (48–53%) and Visual Quality (47–54%), indicating that text descriptions alone cannot reliably convey precise force magnitude and direction. Against **text-only fine-tuned** (same ControlNet architecture with zero control signal plus force text suffixes), Force Prompting wins on Force Adherence in 5 of 6 categories (57–79%) but shows mixed results on Realistic Physics (48–70%) and Visual Quality (47–58%), confirming that the structured vector control signal is essential for precise force adherence. In the separate comparison against **PhysDreamer** (Table 2), Force Prompting achieves 58.33% mean win rate on Force Adherence and 48.33% on Motion Realism, while PhysDreamer retains an advantage on Visual Quality (36.67% for Force Prompting), reflecting the physics simulator's rendering fidelity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on CogVideoX-5B-I2V as a video generation backbone and ControlNet as a mechanism for injecting additional conditioning signals, extending the controllable video generation literature (which has focused on camera control, trajectory-based motion, and optical flow) with a fundamentally different control paradigm: physical force vectors (magnitude and direction) rather than pixel trajectories or camera parameters. It explicitly departs from trajectory-based methods like Motion Prompting by arguing that forces are causally distinct from specifying locations over time, and from physics-simulator-in-the-loop approaches (PhysDreamer, PhysGen, PhysMotion) by eliminating the need for 3D geometry or physics solvers at inference. The training data pipeline repurposes Blender and PhysDreamer as data generators rather than inference-time components, positioning synthetic simulation as a teacher signal for eliciting physics priors already latent in the pretrained video model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical recipe—15k synthetic videos, one day on four A100s, a ControlNet adapter—provides a cheap and general template for adding new physical control signals to video generation models, potentially applicable beyond the two force types explored here. The key open question is whether this approach scales to richer interaction spaces (multi-body contact, fluid-structure interaction, 3D force fields) or remains limited to the 'intuitive physics' regime where the base model's priors already encode plausible dynamics. The paper is explicit that this is not a substitute for precise simulation, and the generalization, while impressive, is bounded by the base model's physical understanding; extending to out-of-prior physics (e.g., non-spherical rolling, complex multi-body collisions) remains unresolved.
