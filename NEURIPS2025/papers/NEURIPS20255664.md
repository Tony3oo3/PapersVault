## Problem

When LLM backbones are upgraded (e.g., Llama-2→Llama-3, Qwen-1.5→Qwen-2.5), previously trained LoRA adapters become incompatible due to changes in vocabulary size, hidden dimension, intermediate size, layer depth, attention head count, and attention type. The standard remedy—retraining LoRA from scratch on the new model—is expensive, slow, and environmentally costly, yet no systematic method existed to transfer existing LoRA weights across such architectural changes.

## Input / Output

The method requires access to the full parameter sets of both the old and new LLM versions (embedding matrices, up/down projection weights, and attention Q/K/V/O matrices) in addition to the old LoRA weights (A_o, B_o). A small-scale fine-tuning set of only ~100 samples (versus 10k for full retraining) is used in the final stabilization step. The output is a new pair of low-rank matrices (A_n, B_n) obtained via a single SVD decomposition of the transformed weight update at each layer, ready to be plugged into the upgraded model's LoRA slots.

## Method

LoRASuite is a modular pipeline that sequentially resolves six architectural mismatches. For dimension changes (vocabulary, hidden, intermediate size), it computes linear transfer matrices from known parameters of both models (e.g., W_h = E_o^{-1}E_n). For layer-depth differences, it builds a CKA similarity matrix between all layer pairs and solves an ordered alignment problem via dynamic programming with a maximum-offset constraint. For attention-head count differences, it characterizes each head by input-independent interaction matrices (W_QK^i, W_VO^i), computes cosine similarities across old and new heads, and finds the optimal one-to-one assignment via the Hungarian algorithm. Head-level weight updates are then transformed through a sandwich product involving the hidden-size transfer matrix and the original/new projection weights (Eq. 3), and a final SVD recovers the low-rank A_n, B_n. A brief fine-tuning step (~100 samples, no warmup, elevated learning rate) stabilizes the numerically transformed parameters, reducing memory by 5.5 GB and compute time by 78% relative to full LoRA retraining.

## Results

On the MiniCPM-S-1B → MiniCPM-2B upgrade (covering all six architectural changes), LoRASuite with lightweight fine-tuning on only 100 samples achieves 43.80 average accuracy on math tasks, surpassing full-scale LoRA retraining on 10k samples (42.39) by +1.4 points and same-scale LoRA (22.82) by +21.0 points. On commonsense tasks, LoRASuite reaches 42.34 vs. 40.85 for full-scale LoRA and 38.60 for same-scale LoRA. Across the Qwen-1.5-1.8B → Qwen-2.5-3B upgrade, LoRASuite achieves 56.88 on math tasks vs. 50.32 for full-scale LoRA (+6.6 points). LoRASuite also reduces memory consumption by 5.5 GB and computational time by 78.23% compared to retraining.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against vanilla LoRA, LoRASuite with only 100 fine-tuning samples consistently and substantially outperforms same-scale LoRA (100) across all model upgrades and task types (e.g., 43.80 vs. 22.82 on MiniCPM math; 56.88 vs. 19.33 on Qwen math). More notably, LoRASuite also exceeds full-scale LoRA retraining (10k samples) on MiniCPM (+1.4 avg on math) and Qwen (+6.6 avg on math), while on Llama-2-7B→Llama-3-8B it reaches 40.18 vs. 49.52 for full-scale LoRA, showing the gap narrows but does not close for all backbones. Against AdaLoRA, LoRASuite w LFT(1k) achieves 51.03 avg on MiniCPM math, surpassing AdaLoRA(1k) (45.80) and AdaLoRA(10k) (50.46). Against DoRA, the improvement is most dramatic: LoRASuite w LFT(100) reaches 42.88 vs. DoRA(100) at 23.54 (1.8× improvement), nearly matching DoRA(10k) at 43.19. The paper also shows that without the lightweight fine-tuning step (LoRASuite w/o LFT), performance drops to near-vanilla-model levels, confirming that the small-scale fine-tuning is critical for numerical stability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on LoRA as the base PEFT method and repurposes tools from two distinct literatures: centered kernel alignment (CKA) from representation similarity analysis for layer mapping, and the Hungarian algorithm from combinatorial optimization for attention-head assignment. It departs from prior knowledge-transfer-between-LLMs work (Trans-LoRA, Trans-PEFT, MUSCLE), which typically requires modifying the fine-tuning procedure or introducing distillation steps, by instead computing a closed-form transfer matrix from known parameters of both model versions and applying it via matrix multiplication. The approach is explicitly orthogonal to LoRA variants (AdaLoRA, DoRA, PiSSA, LoRA+), and the authors demonstrate compatibility with at least two of them (AdaLoRA, DoRA) in their experiments.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As LLM backbones are updated on multi-month cycles, the inability to reuse previously trained LoRA adapters forces costly retraining at scale—a problem that will intensify as on-device and multi-adapter deployments grow. LoRASuite's modular design (transfer matrix → CKA layer mapping → Hungarian head mapping → SVD → tiny fine-tuning) offers a practical recipe that cuts training time by ~78% and memory by 5.5 GB, and in some backbones (MiniCPM, Qwen) even surpasses full-scale retraining using 100× less data. Key open questions include: whether the mandatory small-scale fine-tuning step can be eliminated entirely, whether the method extends to cross-architecture upgrades (e.g., different activation functions or attention mechanisms), and how implicit upgrades (changed pre-training corpora, RLHF) interact with the explicit dimensional transformations the method handles.
