## Problem

Domain generalization, class-incremental learning, and domain-incremental learning are treated as separate paradigms in deep learning, despite the brain handling analogous task-switching and knowledge-retention demands conjointly via shared circuitry modulated by neuromodulatory signals. Prior work has not explored associative memories as integral components of the forward pass for jointly addressing these settings, and existing PEFT methods lack explicit memory-based retrieval mechanisms for per-sample adapter composition.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255034/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes 224×224 RGB image crops (resized from 256×256) and outputs class labels via a frozen ViT-B/16 backbone initialized with CLIP weights. At inference, no task or domain identifier is provided; instead, each input image generates per-layer queries through lightweight query modules that drive the Hopfield memory retrieval, producing a sample-specific affine combination of stored LoRA adapters that modulates the backbone layer-by-layer.

## Method

MIRA attaches a Universal Hopfield Network memory unit to every ViT layer, storing rank-4 LoRA adapters as values and retrieving them via learned keys. Training proceeds in two stages: (1) Adaptation trains independent LoRA adapters per task and writes them into the AM with randomly initialized keys; (2) Consolidation backpropagates through the full forward pass to learn the retrieval keys and per-layer query modules so that the AM's inner-product-based retrieval (with an affine separation function) yields the optimal per-sample adapter ensemble. The key insight is that the Hopfield retrieval dynamics naturally implement the non-convex optimization over adapter combination weights (Eq. 3) without per-sample gradient computation at test time, and the post-hoc key learning allows the model to discover appropriate indexing rules rather than relying on fixed or random keys. This single architecture handles DG, CIL, and DIL by varying only the task-specific objective and data scheduling, with standard forgetting-mitigation techniques like DualGPM incorporated during the Consolidation stage.

## Results

On the CIL setting, MIRA achieves 83.00% Avg. Accuracy on iDigits (vs. 71.53% for ICON), 83.39% on CORe50 (vs. 80.85%), and 67.29% on DomainNet (vs. 65.43%), while also reducing forgetting to 10.62%, 7.99%, and 7.60% respectively. On DIL, MIRA reaches 93.89% on CORe50 with 0.00% forgetting and 69.18% on DomainNet with 4.07% forgetting, substantially outperforming ICON (89.01% and 54.44%). For Domain Generalization, MIRA attains 97.01% on PACS, 87.36% on OfficeHome, and 61.19% on DomainNet, surpassing the prior SoTA PEGO (96.50%, 84.20%, 57.30%) on three of four benchmarks. On ImageNet-R CIL (5-task), MIRA reaches 78.06% vs. 75.85% for C-LoRA, and on CDDB-hard DIL it achieves 77.37% vs. 74.51% for S-iPrompts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MIRA outperforms all 25 baselines on CIL across iDigits, CORe50, and DomainNet, with the largest margin over ICON (the strongest unified CIL+DIL method): +11.47% on iDigits CIL (83.00 vs. 71.53) and +14.74% on DomainNet DIL (69.18 vs. 54.44). On DIL, MIRA wins on CORe50 (93.89 vs. ICON's 89.01) and DomainNet (69.18 vs. 54.44), but ICON retains a narrow edge on iDigits DIL (84.83 vs. 82.46). For Domain Generalization, MIRA surpasses PEGO (the prior SoTA) on PACS (+0.51%), OfficeHome (+3.16%), and DomainNet (+3.89%), but trails PEGO on VLCS (82.10 vs. 83.20) and GESTUR (82.80). On the harder ImageNet-R CIL benchmark, MIRA leads C-LoRA by 2.21% (5-task) and 1.19% (10-task), and on CDDB-hard DIL it exceeds S-iPrompts by 2.86% while remaining 8.13% below the joint-training upper bound. Classical regularization baselines (EWC, LwF) and prompt-based methods (L2P, DualPrompt, CODA-Prompt, S-Prompts) are consistently outperformed by 10–30+ points across all settings, confirming that the associative-memory retrieval mechanism provides a substantial advantage over both rehearsal-free prompting and explicit regularization approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

MIRA sits at the intersection of three research threads: parameter-efficient fine-tuning (LoRA-style adapters as the stored 'values'), Universal Hopfield Networks (the retrieval mechanism with learned keys and separation functions), and the continual learning / domain generalization literature (the evaluation settings). It departs from prior PEFT work by treating adapters not as fixed per-task modules selected by a task ID, but as entries in an associative memory whose retrieval keys are learned post-hoc via backpropagation. It also departs from prior associative-memory-in-CL work (e.g., SHARC, which stores data representations) by storing weight parameters themselves and embedding the Hopfield read operation directly into the forward pass of every ViT layer. The closest unified competitor, ICON, handles CIL and DIL but lacks DG capability; MIRA subsumes all three settings by varying only the task-specific objective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that a single frozen ViT backbone with per-layer Hopfield memories and a two-stage (adapt-then-consolidate) training loop can match or beat task-specialized architectures across DG, CIL, and DIL simultaneously, with negligible inference overhead (~0.4%). This suggests that the siloing of DG and CL methods in the literature may be partly an architectural artifact rather than a fundamental requirement. Open questions include whether the affine separation function and post-hoc key learning generalize to non-ViT backbones (ResNets, diffusion models), whether non-linear retrieval (mixture-of-experts-style gating) can push DG further, and whether the theoretical guarantees (Lemma 1, Theorem 1) hold under the weaker assumptions needed for real-world deployment. The biological framing (neuromodulatory task-switching, TAM hippocampal model) is suggestive but remains unvalidated by neuroscience experiments.
