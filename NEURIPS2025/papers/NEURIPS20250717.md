## Problem

Conventional learning-based filters are tailored to specific dynamical systems and require full retraining when the system changes or transitions to a new environment, limiting their practical utility. While large control models have begun leveraging LLM pre-training knowledge for generalization, no filtering method had yet integrated LLMs to exploit their reasoning capabilities for state estimation, leaving a gap in generalist filtering.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250717/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sliding window of T=40 noisy observations (Y_t ∈ R^{T×N}), segmented into N_s chunks of length L=20 that preserve multi-dimensional structure (e.g., position-velocity coupling), plus a System-as-Prompt (SaP) text containing task instructions and a few input-output examples. The observation chunks are normalized and embedded into the LLM's D-dimensional latent space via a trainable MLP, while the SaP is processed by the standard LLM tokenizer; the output is a full T-step estimated state sequence X̂_t ∈ R^{T×M} projected from the LLM's output token embeddings.

## Method

LLM-Filter reprograms a frozen LLaMA-7B for state estimation by establishing modality alignment between continuous observations and discrete token space: observation segments are embedded via a trainable MLP (replacing the language embedding layer), concatenated with tokenized SaP context, and fed into the frozen LLM core layers, whose output embeddings are then projected back to state space via a second MLP. Only the ObsEmbedding and StateProjection modules (4.22M parameters) are trained with a standard L2 loss, preserving the LLM's pre-trained knowledge. The key insight is that the LLM's next-token prediction mechanism naturally mirrors the recursive prediction-update structure of Bayesian filtering, and the SaP prompt provides in-context task specification that enables cross-system generalization without retraining. The paper demonstrates scaling-law behavior (accuracy improves with larger LLM backbones from GPT-2 124M to LLaMA-13B) and shows that replacing the LLM with an MLP, RNN, or Transformer of similar size degrades performance by ~30%, confirming the LLM's essential role.

## Results

On six classical dynamical systems (Selkov, Oscillator, Hopf, Double Pendulum, Lorenz96, VL20), LLM-Filter achieves an average RMSE of 0.6712, outperforming the best learning-based baseline (RStateNet, 0.8558) by 17.93% and the best online Bayes filter (EnKF, 2.6097) by 21.65%. The maximum per-system RMSE reduction reaches 32.00% (on the Double Pendulum system, where LLM-Filter scores 0.8348 vs. EnKF's 1.4117). On high-dimensional chaotic systems (Lorenz96, VL20 with 72 state dimensions), LLM-Filter achieves RMSE of 0.9149 and 0.7717 respectively, while KalmanNet diverges entirely (NaN). Inference runtime remains stable at ~1.0 ms/step regardless of system dimensionality, compared to EnKF which scales from 2.2 to 6.7 ms/step.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LLM-Filter achieves the lowest RMSE on all six systems against every learning-based baseline: it beats RStateNet (best learning-based competitor) by 17.93% on average, MEstimator by 26.1%, ProTran by 26.3%, and KalmanNet (which diverges on Lorenz96 and VL20) by a wide margin. Against online Bayes filters, LLM-Filter outperforms EnKF by 21.65% on average and PF by 33.3%, with the gap widening dramatically on high-dimensional systems (e.g., 0.9149 vs. 6.6024 on Lorenz96). In the model-mismatch robustness evaluation (Figure 5), LLM-Filter maintains near-constant RMSE as the observation covariance expansion ratio (OCER) increases from 1× to 100×, while EnKFI, EnKFS, and HubEnKF all degrade sharply (e.g., on Selkov, EnKFI goes from 0.6703 to 4.8417). In cross-system generalization (Table 9), LLM-Filter with SaP achieves the lowest RMSE in all four cross-system scenarios (e.g., 0.6613 for Oscillator→Hopf vs. 3.8645 for RStateNet), and its performance gap versus same-system training is minimal, whereas all other learning-based baselines degrade severely or diverge. The ablation LLM-Filter-O (without SaP) is 10.18% worse on average, confirming the prompt's contribution to generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes frozen LLMs (specifically LLaMA-7B) for state estimation by adapting the embedding/projection strategy from AutoTimes for time-series-in-LLM tasks, but applies it to the fundamentally different problem of estimating hidden states from noisy observations rather than forecasting future values. It draws conceptual motivation from the duality between optimal control and filtering, positioning itself as the filtering counterpart to large control models like RT and OpenVLA that leverage LLM/VLM pretraining for cross-embodiment generalization. The key departure from prior learning-based filters (KalmanNet, MEstimator, RStateNet, ProTran) is the introduction of System-as-Prompt (SaP) in-context guidance, which enables cross-system generalization without retraining—something no prior filtering method has achieved. Unlike LLM-based time series forecasters (TimeLLM, AutoTimes), LLM-Filter estimates unobserved states from noisy measurements in dynamical systems, a task with a distinct information-theoretic structure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first demonstration that pre-trained LLM knowledge can be transferred to the filtering domain, opening a path toward generalist state estimation models that eliminate the retraining bottleneck of current learning-based filters. The observed scaling-law behavior (accuracy improves with model size and training duration) suggests a viable route to increasingly capable foundation filters. However, the generalization is currently restricted to same-dimensionality systems, and the practical deployment is constrained by the 7B parameter backbone (~27 GiB peak memory, ~1 ms/step). Open questions include whether cross-dimensionality generalization is achievable, whether the in-context mechanism can handle truly novel system dynamics (not just parameter changes), and whether the frozen-LLM approach will continue to outperform fine-tuned alternatives as system complexity grows.
