## Problem

Standard Transformers rely on dense softmax attention that indiscriminately mixes all token information, lacking the structural inductive bias needed for in-context compositional learning. This makes it difficult for Transformers to infer compositional rules from context examples and transfer them to a target task, particularly when the target involves novel combinations of primitives unseen during training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251484/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives L−1 context panels (8×8 binary images composed of basic shapes in the synthetic task, 40×40 pixel images in RAVEN, or integer tuples in S-RAVEN) with the target panel masked to zero, and produces the predicted target panel as output. In the RAVEN setting, the model generates the answer directly rather than selecting from eight predefined candidates, making the task more demanding. The synthetic task uses 16 basic elements arranged in 4-position panels, yielding roughly 4×10⁹ distinct configurations.

## Method

The paper reformulates multi-head attention as a sparse coding problem over two input-dependent learned dictionaries: an encoding dictionary φ(X) = W_qk^(h)X that decomposes the input into coefficients representing compositional structure, and a decoding dictionary ψ(X) = W_vo^(h)X that reconstructs the output from those coefficients. Instead of softmax, a soft-thresholding (prox) operator is applied to the coefficients, producing sparse representations that explicitly encode which dictionary atoms are active and suppress redundant interactions. To transfer compositional rules from context to target, the target coefficients α_L (which are zero when the target is masked) are estimated as a linear combination of the context coefficients with learnable weights λ_i, inspired by the lifting scheme. This adds only L−1 parameters per layer, making the approach parameter-efficient while enabling structured rule transfer that standard dense attention cannot achieve.

## Results

On S-RAVEN, the proposed method achieves 82.7% accuracy with 8 layers and 40M training tasks, outperforming the standard Transformer (65.1%) and HYLA (79.3%). With only 4 layers and 10M tasks, it already reaches 63.1% versus 51.6% for the Transformer baseline. On RAVEN, the method retains over 30% of test samples with PSNR above 40, while the standard Transformer achieves nearly 0%. On commonsense reasoning benchmarks (BoolQ, PIQA, HellaSwag, WinoGrande, ARC-c, OBQA), integrating the sparse-coding attention into Llama-7B improves average accuracy from 70.5% to 71.9% using only 128 additional parameters.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the standard Transformer [26], the proposed method wins decisively on both S-RAVEN (82.7% vs. 65.1% at 8L/40M) and RAVEN (PSNR>40 coverage of ~30% vs. ~0%). Against HYLA [20], the method also wins on S-RAVEN across all configurations, with the largest gap at 4L/10M (63.1% vs. 55.0%). On language modeling, the method with only 128 trainable parameters improves Llama-7B [25] from 70.5% to 71.9% average accuracy, but falls short of LoRA [10] (75.6%) and DoRA [13] (77.8%), which use 55–57M parameters; however, combining the method with LoRA or DoRA yields further gains (76.3% and 78.1% respectively). On the Im-promptu benchmark [5], the method reduces MSE over the Object-Centric Learner baseline across all three datasets (e.g., CLEVr Objects: 36.23 vs. 37.54), confirming its benefit for in-context composition from image prompts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reinterprets the Transformer attention mechanism through the lens of sparse coding, replacing the softmax attention map with soft-thresholded coefficients over learned encoding and decoding dictionaries. It builds directly on the standard multi-head attention formulation (Vaswani et al.) and the HYLA architecture for S-RAVEN, but departs from prior sparse-attention work (Longformer, BigBird, Reformer) by using sparsity not for computational efficiency but to enforce structured, compositional representations. The coefficient transfer step draws on the lifting scheme from wavelet theory, providing a principled mechanism for propagating compositional rules from context to target tasks that has no direct analogue in prior attention-based approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a recognized weakness of Transformers—lack of structural inductive bias for compositional generalization—by embedding sparse coding principles directly into the attention computation. The practical impact is demonstrated on S-RAVEN and RAVEN, where the method outperforms standard Transformers and HYLA, and on Llama-7B commonsense reasoning with only 128 additional parameters. However, the evaluation is limited to small-scale models and restricted dataset variants (2×2 RAVEN grid, single-layer synthetic tasks), and the authors explicitly note that integration into large pre-trained models remains unexplored. Open questions include whether the sparse-coding reformulation scales to deeper architectures, more complex compositional hierarchies, and whether the lifting-scheme coefficient transfer generalizes beyond the linear-combination assumption.
