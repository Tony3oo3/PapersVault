## Problem

Enforcing complex, input-dependent convex constraints in GNN outputs remains challenging: existing methods either provide only approximate feasibility, require expensive per-instance convex solves for reference points (making large batches impractical), or rely on interior-point solvers whose matrix factorizations do not scale to the sparse, heterogeneous batches typical of graph-structured data. No prior approach simultaneously guarantees strict feasibility, handles input-dependent constraints, and exploits the sparsity of graph problems for GPU-accelerated training.

## Input / Output

The method takes a constrained input graph G_C, a heterogeneous graph combining variable nodes (k-dimensional features from X), constraint nodes (scalar features b_i), and two edge types (E for variable-variable relations, A for variable-constraint connections). The output is a strictly feasible point y ∈ C = {x : Ax ≤ b}, where the constraint set C varies per input instance. Batched graph inputs are handled natively by interpreting multiple graphs as one large disconnected graph, and the architecture is fully GPU-accelerated via scattering operations that exploit the sparsity of A.

## Method

ProjNet is a three-stage GNN architecture: (1) a Graph Attention Network with dynamic attention computes an unconstrained output w over the constrained graph; (2) the Component-Averaged Dykstra (CAD) algorithm projects w onto C, for which the paper proves a convergence result (Theorem 1) showing CAD converges to a sparsity-weighted projection, with a simple input/output rescaling by 1/√l_j to recover the orthogonal projection; (3) sparse vector clipping layers exploit the connected-component partition of the bipartite constraint graph to compute local scaling factors per independent constraint group, allowing more expressive outputs than global clipping. For end-to-end training, a surrogate gradient (projection onto the hyperplane H_x only, rather than the full tangent-cone intersection) is used for the CAD step, which is provably rank-n-1, aligns with the true descent direction, and is computationally cheaper than the exact Jacobian.

## Results

On linear programming, ProjNet (c_h=0) achieves 99.73% of the optimal objective value (0.9973 ± 0.0001), nearly matching the exact Gurobi solver (1.0000). On non-convex quadratic programs, ProjNet is competitive with trust-constr on Erdős-Rényi graphs (6.83 vs. 6.86) and slightly below on Barabási-Albert graphs (7.73 vs. 7.98), while clearly outperforming the DCP baseline (6.38 and 7.60 respectively). On transmit power optimization, ProjNet achieves the best objective value (3.01 ± 0.23), substantially outperforming trust-constr (2.15) and fractional programming (2.79). The GPU-accelerated CAD projection provides up to two orders of magnitude speedup over Gurobi for large-scale projection subproblems when the initial point is near the feasible set.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Gurobi (exact LP solver), ProjNet achieves 99.73% of the optimal LP objective, a negligible gap that comes with a significant runtime advantage on large-scale instances due to GPU acceleration. On non-convex quadratic programs, trust-constr [Byrd et al.] edges out ProjNet slightly on Barabási-Albert graphs (7.98 vs. 7.73) and is essentially tied on Erdős-Rényi graphs (6.86 vs. 6.83), while the DCP method [Lipp & Boyd] is consistently worse (6.38 and 7.60). On transmit power optimization, ProjNet clearly wins, achieving 3.01 versus 2.15 for trust-constr and 2.79 for fractional programming [Shen & Yu], a 13–40% improvement in objective value. The random hit-and-run baseline [Lovász & Vempala] is dramatically inferior across all four problem classes, confirming that the learned GNN output is far more informative than uniform sampling. The PDLP first-order solver [Applegate et al.] is compared primarily on runtime (Figure 3), where ProjNet with c_h=1 shows competitive or superior scaling for large n. Ablations confirm that removing CAD causes the largest performance drop (e.g., LP ratio drops from 0.9973 to 0.2954), while removing SVC has a smaller but consistent effect.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of Dykstra-style projection algorithms (Han 1988; Censor 2006; Censor et al. 2001) and GNN-based optimization (Gasse et al. 2019; Chen et al. 2023), combining the Component-Averaged Dykstra (CAD) algorithm with a refined 'sparse vector clipping' layer that extends the standard vector clipping method (Tordesillas et al. 2023; Konstantinov & Utkin 2023). It departs from differentiable optimization layers (Agrawal et al. 2019; Amos & Kolter 2017) by avoiding interior-point solvers in favor of GPU-friendly iterative projections, and from Sinkhorn-style unrolling by proposing an analytic surrogate gradient. The key conceptual departure is proving that CAD converges to a *non-orthogonal* (sparsity-weighted) projection rather than the standard orthogonal projection, a result that was previously absent from the literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ProjNet provides strict feasibility guarantees for input-dependent convex constraints in a fully GPU-accelerated, end-to-end differentiable GNN framework, addressing a gap where prior methods either sacrifice exact feasibility (barrier/duality approaches) or scale poorly (interior-point solvers on sparse, heterogeneous graph batches). The surrogate gradient being both cheaper and more effective than the exact gradient is a practically useful finding for training models that embed iterative projection steps. Open questions include extension beyond linear constraints to general convex sets, handling of infeasible or poorly-scaled instances, and whether the convergence and surrogate gradient results generalize to non-polyhedral constraint sets. The lack of released code and the restriction to randomly generated (well-scaled, feasible) test instances limit immediate practical adoption.
