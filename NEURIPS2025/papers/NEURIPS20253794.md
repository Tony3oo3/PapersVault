## Problem

Only 9% of FDA-registered AI-based healthcare tools include a post-deployment surveillance plan, and existing monitoring practices are manual, sporadic, and reactive—relying on coarse average metrics that can mask subgroup-specific degradation. The paper argues that post-deployment monitoring lacks a principled statistical foundation and should be grounded in label-efficient, statistically valid testing frameworks that provide explicit error-rate guarantees aligned with regulatory expectations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253794/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework operates on paired i.i.d. samples collected at pre-deployment (t₀) and post-deployment (t₁) time points, where each sample consists of input features S, clinical/demographic variables C, and (when available) ground-truth labels Y. The model f maps S to predictions Ŷ, and a user-specified evaluation function g (e.g., accuracy, AUC) produces performance scores M. The output is a sequential cascade of binary hypothesis-test decisions: first a data-shift flag (covariate, label, or concept drift), then a performance-degradation flag relative to a user-defined threshold τ, and finally an impacted-subgroup specification for targeted relabeling and recalibration.

## Method

The core contribution is reframing every post-deployment monitoring task as a two-sample hypothesis test comparing pre- and post-deployment distributions, organized into two stages. Stage I (Data Shift Detection) tests H₀: p_{t₀}(s,c) = p_{t₁}(s,c) for covariate shift and H₀: p_{t₀}(s,c,y) = p_{t₁}(s,c,y) for concept drift, using parametric tests (z-test, Welch's t-test, F-test) when normality holds or non-parametric tests (KS, MMD, Friedman-Rafsky, Anderson-Darling) otherwise. Stage II (Model Performance Monitoring) includes a one-sided performance-deviation test H₀: M_{t₀} − M_{t₁} ≤ τ_deg and a distributional-shift test on the joint (S,C,Z) where Z is a binary correctness indicator, enabling detection of local subgroup degradation even when overall accuracy is stable. The framework is modality-agnostic, requires only that data be representable as feature vectors and clinical variables, and addresses label scarcity through active sequential two-sample testing and surrogate-label estimation via the PAST algorithm.

## Results

This is a position paper that proposes a conceptual framework rather than reporting experimental results. The key motivating statistic is that only 9% of FDA-registered AI-based healthcare tools include a post-deployment surveillance plan [1]. The paper's main contribution is a formal decomposition of post-deployment monitoring into two stages—data shift detection (covariate shift, label shift, concept drift) and model performance monitoring (performance score deviation, prediction correctness distribution shifts)—each cast as a two-sample hypothesis testing problem with explicit Type I/II error control. No quantitative benchmarks or ablation studies are presented.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not run experiments or compare against quantitative baselines. However, Section 6 provides a qualitative comparison of three alternative monitoring paradigms: (1) Continual learning, which offers low-latency adaptation but requires frequent ground-truth labels, complicates regulatory version control, and risks silent accuracy drops; (2) Bayesian Online Change-Point Detection (BOCPD), which provides probabilistic uncertainty quantification but relies on calibrated priors rarely available in clinical settings and cannot localize where a change occurred; and (3) Conformal prediction / exchangeability martingales, which are distribution-free but assume exchangeability (violated by temporal and site-level correlations in healthcare) and are reactive rather than proactive. The authors argue that two-sample hypothesis testing dominates on all three axes—supervision cost, statistical guarantees, and auditability—making it the most regulator-ready foundation, though they acknowledge each alternative addresses a complementary aspect of the monitoring problem.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper builds on the dataset shift taxonomy of Moreno-Torres et al. [26] to structure post-deployment monitoring into three distinct shift types (covariate shift, label shift, concept drift), and draws on active sequential two-sample testing [23], Black Box Shift Estimation [43], and surrogate training [54] as label-efficient tools within its framework. It departs from the MLOps literature [41, 42], which focuses on system infrastructure and deployment pipelines, by arguing that the statistical foundation of monitoring—explicit error-rate guarantees, formal hypothesis tests, and reproducibility—has been neglected. The paper also positions itself against continual learning, Bayesian change-point detection, and conformal prediction as alternative monitoring paradigms, arguing that two-sample hypothesis testing uniquely combines label efficiency, interpretability, and regulatory alignment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper highlights a critical gap: only 9% of FDA-registered AI healthcare tools include a post-deployment surveillance plan, yet regulatory guidance (FDA SaMD, NIST AI RMF) explicitly calls for statistically valid ongoing monitoring. Several open problems remain unresolved—label scarcity in post-deployment settings, identifying impacted subgroups under distributional shift, attributing drift mechanisms when multiple shift types co-occur, and detecting gradual degradation with limited data. The framework is modality-agnostic but assumes local i.i.d. sampling within pre/post-deployment windows, and does not yet address generative models, temporal dependence, or causal attribution of failures, all of which are natural extensions the authors flag for future work.
