## Problem

Neural combinatorial optimization (NCO) models achieve state-of-the-art performance on routing problems but remain black boxes: the nature of the knowledge they encode and how they use it for decision-making is unexplored. This paper is the first to systematically apply probing to NCO models, addressing two fundamental questions: what decision-related knowledge do their representations capture, and how do they learn and organize that knowledge across embedding dimensions?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254389/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes frozen node embeddings extracted at specific layers (initial, encoder, decoder) from trained NCO models (AM, POMO, LEHD), forming paired feature vectors [h_i, h_j] for a current node and a candidate node, optionally augmented with a Hadamard interaction term [h_i ⊙ h_j] to emulate attention computation. Probing outputs include regression R² (for distance prediction) and classification AUC (for myopia avoidance and same-route detection), plus CS-Probing's coefficient magnitude and Benjamini-Hochberg-corrected significance levels per embedding dimension, yielding interpretable 2D visualizations and ablation-ready dimension indices.

## Method

The paper designs four probing tasks spanning low-level features (Euclidean distance, additive demand) and high-level decision knowledge (myopia avoidance, same-route membership) for TSP and CVRP, then trains linear probes on frozen NCO embeddings to test linear decodability. The key technical contribution is CS-Probing (Coefficient Significance Probing), which extends standard linear probing by jointly analyzing the absolute magnitude and statistical significance of each probe coefficient across all 256 embedding dimensions (128 per node), identifying which specific dimensions encode task-relevant knowledge. CS-Probing reveals that different NCO architectures impose distinct inductive biases (e.g., LEHD concentrates activation in <20 fixed dimensions with large magnitudes, while AM/POMO show dispersed, low-magnitude patterns) and that superior generalization correlates with stable reuse of the same key dimensions across problem scales. The practical validation shows that retaining only two CS-Probing-identified dimensions (31 and 97) in LEHD's 128-dim output preserves near-full performance, and that zeroing those dimensions collapses the optimality gap from 0.57% to 60.43%.

## Results

On TSP-20, LEHD's decoder embeddings achieve an R² of 0.9418 for Euclidean distance regression (Probing Task 1) and an AUC of 0.86 for myopia-avoidance classification (Probing Task 2), substantially outperforming AM (R²=0.2529, AUC=0.76) and POMO (R²=0.1981, AUC=0.76) without interaction terms. CS-Probing reveals that LEHD consistently reuses embedding dimensions 31 and 97 across problem scales (TSP-20→TSP-100), while AM and POMO shift their key dimensions. Remarkably, retaining only these two dimensions from LEHD's 128-dim output yields a 0.65% optimality gap on TSP-100 (vs. 0.57% with all dimensions), and adding L1 regularization (λ=1e-4) improves generalization to TSP-200 from 0.86% to 0.73% gap.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three transformer-based NCO models—AM (Kool et al., 2018), POMO (Kwon et al., 2020), and LEHD (Luo et al., 2023)—using identical probing tasks and metrics. LEHD dominates on both probing tasks: its decoder embeddings achieve R²=0.9418 for distance perception (vs. 0.2529 for AM and 0.1981 for POMO) and AUC=0.86 for myopia avoidance (vs. 0.76 for both AM and POMO), without requiring interaction terms. CS-Probing further differentiates the models: LEHD concentrates statistically significant coefficients in a small set of fixed dimensions (31 and 97) that remain stable across TSP-20→TSP-100 generalization, whereas AM and POMO shift their top dimensions entirely (e.g., AM's top dim changes from 54 to 86; POMO's from 89 to 89 but with different significance). This dimensional stability directly correlates with LEHD's superior generalization (0.86% gap on TSP-200 vs. AM/POMO's degradation). The practical implication is validated: zeroing LEHD's key dimensions 31 and 97 collapses performance to 60.43% gap, while zeroing non-key dimensions (98, 126) causes negligible change (0.59–0.62%), confirming these dimensions are causally responsible for decision quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper transplants the linear probing methodology well-established in NLP and computer vision (Alain & Bengio, Liu et al.) into the neural combinatorial optimization domain, where no prior systematic interpretability framework existed. It extends probing by designing domain-specific tasks (Euclidean distance perception, myopia avoidance, constraint awareness) that exploit CO structure, and introduces CS-Probing—a coefficient-significance analysis layer on top of standard linear probes—to identify which individual embedding dimensions carry task-relevant knowledge. The NCO models analyzed (AM, POMO, LEHD) represent the dominant transformer-based architecture family, and the paper positions itself as the first study to directly investigate their learned embeddings rather than treating them as black boxes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first mechanistic explanation for why certain NCO architectures (LEHD) generalize better than others: they concentrate decision-relevant knowledge into a small, stable set of embedding dimensions that are reused across problem scales, while less generalizable models scatter this knowledge across shifting dimensions. The finding that LEHD's 128-dimensional output can be reduced to just 2 dimensions with negligible performance loss (and that zeroing those 2 dimensions collapses performance by >60%) opens concrete avenues for model compression and distillation. Limitations include the study's focus on only three transformer-based models and two primary problem types (TSP, CVRP), and the probing tasks, while well-designed, capture only a subset of the knowledge encoded in these models. Open questions include extending CS-Probing to feed-forward layers, exploring what additional knowledge LEHD leverages beyond the identified dimensions, and whether the sparsity-regularization insight generalizes to other architectures.
