## Problem

While transformer hallucinations are well-documented as a behavioral phenomenon, the internal mechanisms by which they arise remain poorly understood. Specifically, it was unclear whether transformer models impose semantic structure on inputs independently of the input's actual content, how internal representations shift as input uncertainty increases, and whether patterns in intermediate activations could predict hallucinated outputs. This paper addresses these gaps by probing transformer internals under experimentally controlled input uncertainty.

## Input / Output

The method consumes residual stream activations (768-dim for ViT-B/32 and Pythia-160m) from individual transformer layers, fed with either pure Gaussian noise images (3×224×224, clipped to ±3σ), natural images, text articles, or experimentally perturbed variants (28×28/56×56/112×112 patch-shuffled images; 1/2/6/10/30-word n-gram-shuffled text). The SAE projects these into a sparse overcomplete concept space (49,152-dim for ViT, 24,576-dim for Pythia, 16,384-dim for Gemma 2B), producing per-concept activation vectors. Downstream, maximum concept activations across input tokens feed a PLS regression that outputs a continuous hallucination score (0.0–1.0) and identifies VIP-ranked concepts for targeted suppression.

## Method

The paper trains per-layer sparse autoencoders under three regimes: (1) noise-trained SAEs on 1.3M Gaussian noise samples to expose input-insensitive inductive biases, (2) conventionally-trained SAEs on natural ImageNet/FineWeb-Edu data to quantify concept expansion (L0) under controlled perturbation, and (3) pre-trained Gemma Scope SAEs for hallucination prediction. Concept interpretability is validated via semantic purity (cosine similarity of CLIP text embeddings for top-16 maximally activating images, threshold 0.75) and steerability (32/32 neutral images must flip to the concept's label after clamping the SAE decoder direction). The key finding is a three-phase layer-wise pattern: early layers share concepts across SAE initializations (35% Jaccard overlap), middle layers diverge (14%) while activating the most concepts, and later layers reconverge (25%). For hallucination prediction, a 4-component PLS regression on layer-11 maximum concept activations achieves R²=0.271 on continuous HHEM-2.1 scores and 73% binary accuracy, and suppressing the top-10 VIP concepts in layer 11 reduces mean hallucination scores by 0.19 in the most hallucinated quartile.

## Results

On the Vectara hallucination leaderboard (1,006 source articles), PLS regression on Gemma 2B-IT layer-11 SAE concept activations predicts continuous hallucination scores with R² = 0.271 ± 0.010 (10-fold CV) and achieves 73.0% ± 5.3% binary accuracy for above/below-median hallucination classification. Suppressing just the top 10 VIP-identified concepts in layer 11 reduces the mean hallucination score from 0.91 to 0.72 (Δ = 0.19) for the top-quartile most hallucinated examples. On the TriviaQA generation benchmark, the PLS-Concepts method (Layer 11) reaches 70.8 ± 2.6 AUC for hallucination detection, outperforming all five baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The PLS-Concepts method outperforms all five baselines on three of four benchmarks. On TriviaQA (generation), Layer 11 PLS-Concepts achieves 70.8 AUC, beating the strongest baseline (Lexical Similarity, 68.9) by ~2 points. On TruthfulQA (generation), Layer 11 PLS-Concepts reaches 60.0 AUC (BLEU), surpassing EigenScore (53.9) and all other baselines which cluster near chance (~50). On TruthfulQA (multiple choice), PLS-Concepts (Layer 11) scores 61.1–63.2 AUC versus 49.1–50.0 for the best baselines, a clear ~12-point margin. The sole exception is CoQA (generation), where Lexical Similarity (66.4 AUC) substantially outperforms PLS-Concepts (53.9 AUC), likely because CoQA's exact-match evaluation rewards surface-level lexical overlap that the concept-based approach does not capture. Notably, the PLS-Concepts method only inspects input-prompt activations, whereas all baselines require at least partial output generation, making it a strictly cheaper detection signal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the sparse autoencoder (SAE) interpretability framework established by Cunningham et al. and Bricken et al. by training SAEs on pure Gaussian noise activations rather than natural inputs, revealing the transformer's input-insensitive inductive biases. It departs from prior hallucination research (Yu et al., Jiang et al., Kadavath et al.) that operates at the level of token probabilities and attention heads, instead linking hallucination to high-level SAE-identified semantic concepts in the residual stream. The use of pre-trained Gemma 2B SAEs (Lieberum et al.) enables the hallucination prediction experiments, while the controlled perturbation protocol (patch/n-gram shuffling) repurposes adversarial-robustness methodology as a diagnostic tool for probing internal concept dynamics across both vision and language transformers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first mechanistic, concept-level account of how hallucinations arise in transformers, showing that input-side concept patterns predict output hallucination and that targeted suppression of a handful of concepts reduces hallucination scores. This opens a path toward real-time, layer-level hallucination monitoring and intervention without requiring the generated output. Key limitations include testing only on models up to 2B parameters, leaving open whether the three-phase concept dynamics and the noise-to-concept mapping persist in frontier-scale models; the authors also note that sequence length and sampling temperature effects remain unexplored. The framework's modality-agnostic design (validated on both ViT and LLM) suggests broad generalizability, but the moderate R² (0.271) for hallucination prediction indicates substantial unexplained variance.
