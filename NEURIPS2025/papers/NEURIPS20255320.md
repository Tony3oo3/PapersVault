## Problem

The origin of neural scaling laws—why loss decreases as a power law with model size—remains unclear. Prior mechanistic explanations (manifold fitting, skill learning) implicitly assume a weak superposition regime where only the most frequent features are represented, yet LLMs must encode far more tokens and concepts than their hidden dimension allows, placing them in a strong superposition regime that has not been systematically studied for its scaling implications.

## Input / Output

The toy model takes n-dimensional sparse input vectors (n = 1000 or 10240) where each coordinate is activated with a rank-dependent probability p_i and scaled by a uniform value, and recovers them through an m-dimensional bottleneck (m ≪ n) with a ReLU autoencoder. For LLM validation, the method extracts the language model head weight matrix W from pre-trained models (OPT, GPT-2, Qwen, Pythia) and computes row-norm distributions and pairwise cosine overlaps. Outputs include the fitted loss scaling exponent α_m, mean squared overlap statistics, and the fraction of represented features φ_{1/2}, which together characterize whether and how loss follows a power law with model dimension.

## Method

The paper adapts Anthropic's superposition autoencoder toy model and introduces a decoupled weight decay (or growth) term that robustly tunes the degree of superposition: positive γ drives the system toward weak superposition (only m/n features represented), while negative γ enforces unit-norm rows, yielding strong superposition where nearly all n features are represented with overlap. In the weak regime, loss is analytically shown to equal the sum of frequencies of unrepresented features, producing a power law in m only when feature frequencies themselves are power-law (exponent α_m = α − 1). In the strong regime, loss arises from squared overlaps between representation vectors; for even frequencies the vectors are isotropic and overlaps scale as 1/m, giving a robust α_m = 1 independent of the underlying frequency distribution, while for skewed frequencies an ETF-like structure for the most important features yields α_m ≈ 2(α − 1). The authors validate these predictions on four open-source LLM families, showing that language model head rows exhibit 1/m-scaled squared overlaps and that the model-size-dependent loss component has α_m = 0.91 ± 0.04, consistent with the strong superposition prediction.

## Results

In the strong superposition regime, the toy model exhibits robust 1/m loss scaling (α_m ≈ 1.01 ± 0.05 for exponential, 1.0 ± 0.1 for power-law, 0.89 ± 0.05 for linear feature frequencies), independent of the underlying data distribution. In the weak superposition regime, the model exponent is α_m ≈ α − 1, matching the data exponent minus one. For actual LLMs (OPT, GPT-2, Qwen, Pythia; ~100M to 70B parameters), the fitted model-size loss exponent is α_m = 0.91 ± 0.04, and the Chinchilla-based inference gives α_m = 0.88 ± 0.06, both consistent with the predicted 1/m scaling from strong superposition geometry.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against competing methods in the traditional sense; instead, it validates its theoretical predictions against both prior analytical frameworks and actual LLM behavior. In the weak superposition regime, the predicted α_m = α − 1 matches the resolution-limited scaling derived in prior kernel-method and feature-learning analyses [15–20], confirming those results as a special case. In the strong superposition regime, the paper's geometric argument (squared overlaps scaling as 1/m via ETF-like structure) yields α_m ≈ 1, which is quantitatively confirmed by measuring mean squared overlaps of the language model head across OPT, GPT-2, Qwen, and Pythia models (Fig. 6a) and by fitting their evaluation losses on Wikitext-103, Pile-10k, C4, and BookCorpus (Fig. 6b). The Chinchilla scaling laws [3] are shown to be consistent with the 1/m prediction: using N ∝ m^{2.52±0.03} and the known α_N = 0.35±0.02, one recovers α_m = 0.88±0.06, within error bars of the directly fitted 0.91±0.04. The paper thus positions superposition as the mechanistic explanation unifying these previously separate observations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Anthropic's toy model of superposition [27] by introducing decoupled weight decay as a knob to systematically control the degree of superposition, enabling a direct study of how superposition affects loss scaling with model width. Prior theoretical explanations of neural scaling laws (Bahri et al. [15], Bordelon et al. [16,17], Michaud et al. [20], Hutter [19]) implicitly assumed weak or no superposition, where power-law loss scaling requires power-law feature frequencies. This paper departs from that line by showing that in the strong superposition regime—where actual LLMs operate—the scaling is instead governed by the geometry of representation vectors (squared overlaps scaling as 1/m), making it robust across diverse feature frequency distributions. The analysis also draws on equal angle tight frame (ETF) theory from signal processing and quantum measurements to characterize the structure of strongly represented features.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By identifying representation superposition as a central mechanistic driver of neural scaling laws, this work reframes a long-standing question: the ~1/width scaling exponent is not an accident of data structure but a geometric consequence of packing more features than dimensions. This has practical implications—encouraging superposition (e.g., via nGPT-style unit-sphere constraints or weight-decay-free optimizers) could make smaller models more efficient. Key open questions remain: why LLMs settle into the strong superposition regime, how data-size and training-step scaling behave under strong superposition, how parsing loss in transformer layers interacts with representation loss, and whether the naive token-as-feature mapping to LLMs holds up under more rigorous analysis. The toy model's lack of transformer layers and the suggestive (rather than definitive) LLM connection are acknowledged limitations.
