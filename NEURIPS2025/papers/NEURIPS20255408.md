## Problem

Simulating high-order nonlinear RC interconnect networks in VLSI backend design relies on Newton-Raphson iterative solvers with O(n³) complexity, creating a severe computational bottleneck for signal-integrity and timing signoff. While deep learning has been applied to general nonlinear system identification, no existing method explicitly leverages the modal (pole-residue) structure of S-domain transfer functions to perform scalable, physics-aware time-domain waveform prediction for high-order nonlinear driver-load systems.

## Input / Output

The method consumes per-mode features extracted from partial-fraction decomposition of the circuit's transfer function: normalized pole-residue pairs (p_i, r_i) sorted by descending |r_i|, discrete time points t_k (1000 samples over 10 ns at 10 ps resolution, log-normalized), and a device-type label. The base predictor processes each (p_i, r_i, t_k) triplet independently, while the compensation module additionally receives the adjacent-order pair (p_{i-1}, r_{i-1}) to capture inter-modal coupling. The output is the full time-domain voltage waveform V_out(t_k) at all T time steps, validated against HSPICE golden waveforms.

## Method

S-Crescendo decomposes the n-th order transfer function via partial-fraction expansion into first-order modal terms, then predicts each modal response independently with a lightweight 3-layer Transformer (multi-head self-attention, GELU, layer norm) before summing them into a baseline waveform—reducing complexity from O(n³) to O(n). A cascade of residual-correction Transformers is then trained recursively: each module e_{φ_j} takes the current and previous pole-residue pairs plus time, and learns the discrepancy between the accumulated prediction and the HSPICE ground truth, progressively refining higher-order accuracy. A log-centered time warping preprocessing step (τ = sgn(t−t_c)[ln(|t−t_c|/T₀+ε)−ln ε]) equalizes the dynamic range of local slopes, boosting R² from ~0.97 to ~0.9998 by densifying samples near the steep rising edge. The recursive training scheme ensures each residual module depends only on two adjacent pole-residue pairs, granting structural agnosticism and enabling extrapolation to orders beyond the training range (validated up to 200 poles with R² = 0.9838).

## Results

S-Crescendo achieves R² up to 0.99 against HSPICE golden waveforms on order-1 to order-10 RC networks, with a single-pole fit of R² = 0.999 and a 3-pole error-corrected fit of R² = 0.9975. Trained exclusively on orders ≤ 3, the model generalizes to orders 4–9 with R² ranging from 0.997 (order 5) down to 0.964 (order 9), and extrapolates to a 200-pole network with R² = 0.9838. Inference is 5.9×–18.6× faster than HSPICE across orders 1–10, with the largest speedup (18.6×) at order 1 and a conservative worst-case of 6.2× at order 10.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks S-Crescendo against three baselines: HSPICE (commercial SPICE simulator), DCM (Dynamic Circuit Macromodeling), and NGSPICE (open-source SPICE). On the 10-order RC test case, S-Crescendo completes inference in 0.042 s versus 0.26 s for HSPICE (6.2× faster), 0.6 s for DCM (14.3× faster), and 1.08 s for NGSPICE (25.7× faster). In terms of accuracy, DCM achieves R² = 0.9983 on the same 10-order file, while S-Crescendo reports R² up to 0.99 across orders 1–10 and 0.9838 on the 200-pole extrapolation test, indicating comparable fidelity at a fraction of the runtime. HSPICE serves as the ground-truth reference rather than a competing method, so accuracy comparisons are framed as S-Crescendo's R² against HSPICE waveforms rather than a head-to-head accuracy contest. Overall, S-Crescendo wins decisively on speed across all three baselines while maintaining accuracy within a narrow margin of the physics-based solvers.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes classical S-domain partial-fraction decomposition (a staple of control theory and model-order-reduction methods like PRIMA) as the structural backbone for a Transformer-based neural operator, a combination not previously explored for circuit simulation. It departs from the three established EDA paradigms for RC response modeling—Current Source Models, Voltage Response Models, and direct waveform fitting—by operating in the modal (pole-residue) domain rather than the time or frequency domain, and from general neural system-identification work (DeepONet, FNO, PINNs) by explicitly encoding the linear modal structure as input features rather than learning it implicitly. The residual-correction cascade design draws conceptually on iterative refinement in numerical methods but is implemented as a sequence of lightweight Transformer modules trained recursively.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a practical path to accelerating high-order RC transient simulation in VLSI signoff flows, with the O(n³)→O(n) complexity reduction and ~10× speedup over HSPICE being directly relevant to industry workloads. However, several open questions remain: accuracy degrades with order (R² drops from 0.997 at order 5 to 0.964 at order 9), inference latency scales linearly with pole count (unlike HSPICE's near-constant runtime via internal MOR), repeated poles are unhandled, and the 200-order extrapolation result rests on a single case study without error bars. The log-centered warping preprocessing (boosting R² from ~0.97 to ~0.9998) is a useful and generalizable technique for any step-response regression task, potentially applicable beyond circuit simulation.
