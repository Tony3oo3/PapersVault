## Problem

Existing video generation models produce photo-realistic videos but lack physical plausibility and explicit control over dynamic physical behaviors, since they are trained purely on 2D video data. Classical physics simulators (MPM, rigid-body solvers) can model diverse materials but suffer from high computational cost, hyperparameter sensitivity, numerical instabilities, and poor generality across material types, making them impractical as a direct control signal for video generation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255598/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image of a scene containing objects, along with user-specified physics parameters (Young's modulus E, Poisson's ratio ν), an external force vector (direction and magnitude), a drag point, a material type token (elastic, sand, plasticine, rigid), and a floor height. The image is lifted to 3D via SAM segmentation, SV3D novel-view synthesis, and LGM Gaussian reconstruction, yielding 2048 surface points. The output is a sequence of 24-timestep 3D point trajectories, which are projected into 2D pixel-space tracking videos and fed as conditioning to the DaS video diffusion model to synthesize the final image-to-video result.

## Method

PhysCtrl's core is a conditional diffusion transformer that learns the distribution of physical dynamics as 3D point trajectories, trained on 550K synthetic animations from MPM and rigid-body simulators over ObjaverseXL objects. The architecture uses a novel spatiotemporal attention block in each layer: spatial attention first aggregates influences from neighboring points within a frame (mimicking particle interactions in continuum mechanics), then temporal attention propagates each point's state forward across timesteps, reducing the combinatorial cost of naive 4D attention while reflecting the P2G→G2P structure of physics simulation. Physical conditioning (force, drag point, E, ν, floor height, material token) is injected via adaptive layer norm on separate condition tokens concatenated with point positions. A physics-based loss derived from MPM's deformation gradient update equation (Eq. 8–9) regularizes training to enforce that predicted point velocities are consistent with the ground-truth deformation gradient, alongside standard diffusion MSE, velocity, and floor-penetration losses. At inference, the generated 3D trajectories are projected to 2D and used as a tracking-video condition for DaS, decoupling physics-grounded motion planning from visual synthesis.

## Results

On the image-to-video generation task (evaluated via GPT-4o 5-point Likert scores), PhysCtrl achieves the best scores across all three criteria: Semantic Adherence (SA) 4.5, Physical Commonsense (PC) 4.5, and Video Quality (VQ) 4.3, outperforming the strongest baseline Wan2.1 (3.8/3.7/3.6). On the trajectory generation task, PhysCtrl achieves 77.59% vIoU, 0.0028 Chamfer Distance, and 0.0015 L2 correlation error, substantially surpassing MDM (53.78% vIoU) and Motion2VecSets (24.92% vIoU). In the user study, PhysCtrl is preferred for physics plausibility 81.0% of the time and for video quality 66.0% of the time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PhysCtrl outperforms all four video generation baselines on every metric. Against the strongest text-prompted baseline Wan2.1 [82], PhysCtrl gains +0.7 on SA, +0.8 on PC, and +0.7 on VQ, primarily because Wan2.1 lacks explicit physics conditioning and cannot precisely follow force direction. CogVideoX [94] (3.2/3.2/3.1) and DragAnything [89] (2.9/2.8/2.8) trail further, with DragAnything's purely 2D trajectory control unable to distinguish camera motion from object motion. ObjCtrl-2.5D [87] performs worst (1.5/1.3/1.4) due to its coarse single-point trajectory conditioning. On trajectory generation, PhysCtrl (77.59% vIoU) substantially beats MDM [79] (53.78%) and Motion2VecSets [6] (24.92%), with the gap attributed to the spatiotemporal attention block capturing per-point spatial correlations that MDM's single-latent projection and M2V's frame-by-frame encoding miss. For physics parameter estimation, PhysCtrl achieves MAE 0.506 on log₁₀(E) in ~2 minutes, compared to differentiable MPM [32] which requires 20–60 minutes for MAE 0.439–0.394, demonstrating a 10–30× speedup at a small accuracy cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

PhysCtrl sits at the intersection of learned physics dynamics and controllable video generation. It builds on MPM's deformation gradient formulation to derive a physics-informed training loss, borrows the velocity regularization from MDM, adopts CogVideoX's adaptive layer norm for conditioning injection, and uses DaS as the downstream trajectory-conditioned video model. The key departure from prior physics-grounded video work (PhysGaussian, PhysGen, PhysMotion) is replacing explicit simulation with a diffusion model that learns the distribution of dynamics across materials, and the departure from prior generative dynamics work (Motion2VecSets, MDM) is the introduction of a spatiotemporal attention block that explicitly models per-point spatial interactions before temporal propagation, along with a point-cloud representation that generalizes across elastic, sand, plasticine, and rigid materials in a single model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first framework to inject a learned physics prior into a video generative model, enabling users to control video dynamics via explicit physical parameters (Young's modulus, Poisson's ratio) and external forces rather than relying on text prompts or hand-tuned simulators. The 550K-object synthetic dataset and the demonstration that a learned model can match a traditional simulator for video generation (Table 4) while being orders of magnitude faster for inverse problems (0.1s vs 3min for differentiable MPM) make this practically relevant. Open questions include extending to multi-object interactions, additional materials (fluids), complex boundary conditions, and resolving conflicts between the learned physics trajectory and the video model's learned priors, which remain a source of failure cases.
