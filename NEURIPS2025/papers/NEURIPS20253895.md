## Problem

Using a single reward model (RM) to align LLMs is suboptimal because RMs generalize poorly across heterogeneous tasks, leaderboard rankings are unreliable, and over-optimization on one RM causes reward hacking. The standard remedy—ensembling multiple RMs—requires simultaneously loading and scoring with all RMs, incurring high computational cost and introducing conflicting preference signals that degrade training data quality. The paper addresses the gap of efficiently and adaptively selecting the most suitable RM per training instance without manual tuning or oracle knowledge.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pool of K pretrained reward models (e.g., four 7B RMs from RewardBench), a policy LLM (Llama-3-8B), and a set of training prompts. At each training batch, the bandit consumes the mean last-token sentence embedding of the batch (computed by the current policy model) as context and outputs a single selected RM. The selected RM scores n=30 sampled responses per prompt, producing preference pairs that drive DPO/NLL fine-tuning. In the best-of-n inference variant (used for long-context tasks), the learned bandit parameters are reused at test time to select which RM reranks generated responses, outputting the highest-scoring response.

## Method

LASER casts per-batch RM selection as a contextual multi-armed bandit problem solved with LinUCB, where each arm is a reward model and the context is the mean sentence embedding of the current training batch. At each step, the bandit selects one RM via an upper-confidence-bound rule balancing exploitation of known-good RMs against exploration of under-tried ones; the selected RM scores the LLM's sampled responses to form preference pairs, and the LLM is fine-tuned with DPO (plus NLL on preferred responses for reasoning tasks). The MAB reward is the negative training loss after the batch update, which is used to update the LinUCB parameters (covariance matrix A_k and bias vector b_k) for the selected arm. This entire select-score-train-update cycle repeats over M iterations, with the bandit progressively learning which RMs produce the most informative preference signals for different input contexts. A key design choice is selecting only one RM per batch rather than ensembling all K simultaneously, which avoids conflicting reward signals and reduces per-step compute to a single RM forward pass.

## Results

On reasoning benchmarks (StrategyQA, GSM8K, MMLU) with Llama-3-8B, LASER achieves 76.32% average accuracy, outperforming the RM Score Ensemble by 2.67% and the RM Agreement Ensemble by 0.91%. On WildChat instruction-following, LASER attains a 72.69% length-controlled AlpacaEval win rate over the RM Score Ensemble baseline. On LongBench long-context tasks, LASER improves by ~3 F1 points over the RM Score Ensemble on single-document QA (37.47 vs. 34.51) and few-shot learning (73.31 vs. 70.34). LASER also achieves a 2× training speedup over the RM Score Ensemble and 3× over sequential RM selection.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the RM Score Ensemble (Coste et al., 2023), which averages scores across all RMs simultaneously, LASER wins decisively: +2.67% average accuracy on reasoning (76.32 vs. 73.65), a 72.69% AlpacaEval win rate on WildChat, and ~3 F1 points on LongBench single-doc QA and few-shot learning. Against the RM Agreement Ensemble (Wang et al., 2024e), which selects pairs with highest cross-RM ranking agreement, LASER still leads by 0.91% average accuracy on reasoning (76.32 vs. 75.41) and by 1.68 F1 on LongBench single-doc QA (37.47 vs. 35.79) and 1.16 F1 on few-shot learning (73.31 vs. 72.15). The paper attributes these gains to LASER's ability to avoid conflicting signals from multiple RMs by selecting only one RM per batch, whereas ensemble methods aggregate potentially contradictory preferences. LASER also achieves these gains with 2× less wall-clock training time than the RM Score Ensemble, since it loads and scores with only one RM per step rather than all four simultaneously.

## Relation to Prior Work

LASER sits at the intersection of iterative preference optimization (building on DPO from Rafailov et al. and the self-rewarding/iterative training pipelines of Yuan et al. and Pang et al.) and multi-armed bandit algorithms (specifically LinUCB from Li et al.). It departs from the dominant multi-RM paradigm of score ensembling (Coste et al., Wang et al.) by selecting a single RM per training batch rather than combining all RMs simultaneously, and from prior MAB applications in NLP (which used bandits for sample selection or test-time model routing) by applying them to the novel sub-problem of adaptive reward model selection during iterative LLM training. The paper positions itself as the first to cast RM selection as a contextual bandit problem in the LLM alignment setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical finding that adaptive single-RM selection outperforms multi-RM ensembles while being 2× faster challenges the common assumption that aggregating more reward signals is always beneficial, and provides a concrete, low-overhead mechanism (LinUCB with ~1.6M parameters) that practitioners can plug into existing iterative training pipelines. Key limitations include reliance on a high-quality RM pool, no explicit handling of safety/fairness trade-offs in RM selection, and evaluation primarily on 7B-scale RMs with 8B policy models. Open questions include whether RL-based selection policies could further improve adaptivity, how the approach scales to hundreds of RMs or non-stationary reward landscapes, and whether the bandit's learned policy transfers across domains without retraining.
