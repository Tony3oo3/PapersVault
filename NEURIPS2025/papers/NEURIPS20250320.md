## Problem

Existing LLM-based agents can automate narrow tasks like scheduling or information retrieval but cannot conduct the full scientific research lifecycle—from literature analysis and hypothesis generation through algorithm implementation to publication-quality reporting. No standardized benchmark exists to systematically evaluate autonomous research capabilities across diverse scientific domains, making progress in this frontier difficult to measure.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250320/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system requires only 10–15 reference papers as minimal user input; in Level-1 tasks it additionally receives an explicit research instruction extracted from a target paper, while Level-2 tasks omit this instruction to test open-ended ideation. The output is a self-contained code project (no imports from reference repositories) plus a publication-ready LaTeX manuscript covering background, methodology, experiments, and results. Evaluation uses Scientist-Bench, a 22-paper benchmark spanning diffusion models, vector quantization, GNNs, and recommendation systems, with pairwise LLM-as-judge comparison against the original human-authored papers.

## Method

AI-Researcher employs a three-stage multi-agent pipeline. Stage 1 (Literature Review & Idea Generation): a Knowledge Acquisition Agent selects high-quality code repositories and supplementary papers, while a Resource Analyst decomposes research concepts into atomic components with explicit bidirectional mappings between mathematical formulations (extracted via RAG over LaTeX sources) and code implementations, reducing hallucination risk; an Idea Generator then uses a divergent-convergent process to produce and rank novel research directions. Stage 2 (Implementation & Validation): a Code Agent builds the project iteratively under the guidance of an Advisor Agent (comprising Judge, Code Review, and Experiment Analysis sub-agents) in a mentor-student feedback loop, progressing from minimal prototype runs (1–2 epochs) to full-scale experiments with structured refinement cycles. Stage 3 (Documentation): a Documentation Agent produces the manuscript through a three-phase hierarchical process—structural outlining, template-guided section elaboration, and checklist-based verification—to overcome LLM coherence limitations over long-form academic text.

## Results

AI-Researcher achieves a 93.8% implementation completeness rate with Claude-series models and an average correctness score of 2.65/5. In pairwise comparisons against human-authored top-tier papers, AI-generated research achieves comparable quality rates of 13.64%–81.82% depending on the LLM evaluator (GPT-4o: 81.82%, o1-mini: 54.55%, o3-mini: 13.64%, Claude-3.5: 13.64%, Claude-3.7: 22.73%). In open-ended exploration (Level-2), comparable rates improve to 50%–100%, with GPT-4o as evaluator rating all AI-generated papers as comparable to human work. Claude-series models substantially outperform 4o-series on implementation (87.5% vs 50% completeness; 2.75 vs 1.0 correctness).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against other autonomous research systems; instead, it benchmarks AI-Researcher's output against human-authored top-tier papers as the ground-truth baseline. Across five LLM evaluators, AI-generated papers consistently receive slightly lower ratings than human papers (overall average ratings range from −0.53 to −1.70), but a substantial fraction (13.64%–81.82%) are rated as comparable in quality. GPT-4o as evaluator is the most lenient (81.82% comparable), while o3-mini and Claude-3.5 are the strictest (13.64% comparable), highlighting significant evaluator bias. In open-ended Level-2 tasks, performance improves markedly: GPT-4o rates 100% of AI papers as comparable, and even the strictest evaluator (o3-mini) rates 66.67% as comparable. For implementation quality, Claude-series backbones dominate 4o-series (87.5% vs 50% completeness; 2.75 vs 1.0 correctness), and Claude-3.5 as the research agent backbone outperforms GPT-4o under most evaluators (e.g., 85.71% vs 71.43% comparable under GPT-4o evaluation; 14.29% vs 0% under o3-mini).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the autonomous scientific discovery line initiated by The AI Scientist (Lu et al., 2024) and AI Scientist-v2 (Yamada et al., 2025), which demonstrated that LLM agents could generate and validate small-scale research contributions. Unlike those systems and concurrent efforts like AgentRxiv and AI Co-Scientist that focus on isolated stages (idea generation, experiment design, or literature analysis), AI-Researcher orchestrates the full pipeline from literature review through implementation to publication-ready manuscript using a multi-agent architecture with explicit bidirectional math-to-code mapping and iterative mentor-student refinement. The introduction of Scientist-Bench as a formalized two-level benchmark (guided vs. open-ended) departs from prior work that lacked standardized evaluation protocols for autonomous research, while the multi-agent design draws on broader agentic frameworks (MetaGPT, AutoGen, CAMEL) but applies them specifically to the scientific research workflow.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Scientist-Bench provides the first standardized benchmark for evaluating autonomous research systems across diverse AI domains, filling a gap the authors identify as making progress in this area difficult to measure. The finding that open-ended exploration outperforms guided implementation challenges the assumption that explicit instructions should yield better results, suggesting LLM agents may be better at synthesizing their own research directions than following prescriptive ones—though this is based on only 5 papers and LLM-based evaluation. Key limitations include modest correctness scores (2.25–2.65/5), significant divergence among LLM evaluators (comparable rates ranging from 13.64% to 81.82%), and computational resource constraints that suppress performance in domains like diffusion models. Open questions include whether the system produces genuinely novel contributions versus sophisticated recombination, how it scales to non-AI scientific domains, and whether the multi-agent overhead is justified relative to simpler single-agent approaches.
