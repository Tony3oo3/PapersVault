## Problem

Most functional anomalies in articulated objects (e.g., a drawer that won't fully close, a door with a stuck hinge) are invisible from passive visual observation and only manifest during physical interaction. No prior framework simultaneously learns *how* to interact with an object to reveal such hidden anomalies and *what* normal motion to expect, making it impossible to flag deviations from expected behavior.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253920/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a single RGBD image (converted to an N×6 point cloud of RGB + surface normals) and a sequence of atomic actions, each parameterized by a 3D end-effector position and a 3D movement direction. It produces, per articulated part, a binary anomaly label (via Hausdorff distance between anticipated and observed trajectories), a predicted action-motion sequence over up to 15 timesteps, a per-point segmentation mask identifying the moving part, and a scalar motion confidence score that modulates both training loss and inference-time action filtering.

## Method

A Point Transformer v3 backbone encodes the point cloud into per-point features, feeding two decoders: a motion head that regresses an SE(3) rigid transformation in a local frame centered at the action position (using a 6D rotation representation and Gram-Schmidt decomposition), and a segmentation head that predicts which points move coherently with the interaction point. Training is fully supervised on normal objects only, with a confidence-weighted loss that down-weights unreliable motion predictions. At inference, a long-term anticipation loop maintains part memory masks, applies a two-stage filter (confidence threshold + DBSCAN clustering of predicted rotations) to select informative actions via a movability score, and iteratively simulates the object state without physical contact. Anomaly detection then compares the simulated 'normal' trajectory against the trajectory observed by executing the same actions on the real object, using the Hausdorff distance between the two sequences as the decision criterion.

## Results

On the PartNet-IAD benchmark, the proposed method achieves 83.9% average AUROC for functional anomaly detection, substantially outperforming the strongest baseline (Where2Act + MotionPrior at 77.3%) by 6.6 points. On unseen objects within training categories, the method reaches 86.1% average AUROC, and on entirely unseen testing categories it achieves 84.2%, demonstrating strong cross-category generalization. The method also reports 98.35% action success rate and 94.12% part success rate on normal objects, with motion estimation MSE of 0.05 and segmentation mIoU of 0.72.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against five baselines of increasing sophistication. The passive no-interaction classifier (A, 55.5%) and random-interaction classifier (B, 56.1%) both fail to reveal hidden functional anomalies, confirming that interaction is necessary. Where2Act + Classifier (C, 62.0%) improves by using learned action prediction but still relies on frame-wise classification without motion anticipation. Where2Act + Heuristics (D, 70.1%) adds joint-type inference and axis comparison, closing the gap to 13.8 points. The closest baseline, Where2Act + MotionPrior (E, 77.3%), applies the paper's motion anticipation module to Where2Act's predicted actions but lacks the joint action-motion representation and long-horizon temporal consistency, resulting in a 6.6-point gap. The proposed method wins on all comparisons, with the largest gains over passive and random baselines (>27 points) and a consistent 6–14 point advantage over Where2Act-based approaches across both evaluation settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the passive-observation paradigm of standard anomaly detection (MVTec AD, MVTec 3D-AD) by introducing active physical interaction as a necessary component for detecting functional anomalies in articulated objects. It builds on the interactive perception line (Where2Act, UMPnet, AtP) that learns to predict actions on articulated parts, but extends these by jointly predicting the anticipated rigid motion (SE(3) transformation) conditioned on each action, following the local-coordinate-frame formulation from PointFlowNet. The key conceptual departure is that prior interactive methods only predict *where* and *how* to act, whereas this work additionally predicts *what should happen* as a result, enabling anomaly detection via motion discrepancy rather than frame-level classification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that passive visual observation is fundamentally insufficient for detecting functional anomalies (e.g., a drawer that won't fully close), and that learned interaction with motion anticipation is both necessary and effective, with strong generalization to unseen object categories (~77% AUROC on testing categories). The PartNet-IAD benchmark provides the first standardized evaluation for this new task. However, significant open questions remain: all experiments are simulation-only with no real-robot validation, the framework is limited to 1-DoF prismatic/revolute joints and rigid parts, and it cannot handle composite actions (e.g., locking a door) or multi-link/soft-body systems. Extending to real-world deployment with diverse joint types and contact-rich interactions is the most pressing next step.
