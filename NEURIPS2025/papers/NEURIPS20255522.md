## Problem

Biological neural networks exhibit diverse and sometimes opposing plasticity rules (Hebbian/anti-Hebbian, excitatory/inhibitory), yet it remains unclear whether such sign diversity precludes gradient-based optimization or whether networks can still effectively minimize a loss. This paper investigates the previously overlooked possibility that learning dynamics may contain fundamentally non-gradient 'curl' components that nonetheless support effective optimization, challenging the assumption that biological learning must approximate gradient descent.

## Input / Output

The framework operates on a two-layer linear (or tanh-activated) feedforward network with whitened input vectors x ∈ R^M and a scalar teacher target y. The key control parameter is a pair of diagonal sign-flip matrices D₁ ∈ {±1}^{M×M} and D₂ ∈ {±1}^{N×N}, where each entry determines whether the corresponding presynaptic neuron follows standard or rule-flipped plasticity. The output is the trained weight pair (W₁, W₂) and the student's scalar prediction ŷ = W₂W₁x, with performance measured by quadratic loss ⟨e²⟩ over the input distribution.

## Method

The paper introduces 'curl descent,' a learning rule that flips the sign of the gradient update for a randomly selected fraction of synapses via diagonal matrices D₁ and D₂, provably producing non-gradient dynamics (no scalar potential exists when both signs are present). Using a student-teacher framework with whitened inputs, the authors derive the Jacobian of the continuous-time weight dynamics at the origin and on the solution manifold W₂W₁ = s, then apply random matrix theory in the infinitely wide limit to compute the spectral support and identify a dynamical phase transition where the solution manifold loses stability. The critical threshold depends on the compression ratio c = M/N and the fraction of flipped neurons α_h or α_r: expansive networks (c < 1) are more robust to hidden-layer curl, while at most half the readout layer can be flipped before destabilization. Simulations reveal that hidden-layer curl beyond the boundary induces chaotic weight dynamics analogous to the chaos transition in disordered networks, whereas readout-layer curl allows weights to escape the unstable manifold and find alternative low-error minima. In nonlinear tanh networks, even a single flipped readout weight can accelerate convergence by helping the dynamics escape saddle points through temporary loss ascent.

## Results

In linear networks (M+N=220), the solution manifold loses stability when the compression ratio exceeds c≈0.3 for hidden-layer curl terms, or when more than 50% of readout neurons are rule-flipped. In nonlinear tanh networks (M=100, N=10), a single rule-flipped readout weight (1 out of 1010 parameters) produces significantly faster convergence than gradient descent (p<.01, paired t-test), with the advantage growing in the high-data regime (N_train≥500) and persisting up to 50% rule-flipped readout neurons. The speed advantage is robust across a broad range of student weight initialization scales and teacher complexity settings, though both methods degrade sharply above a critical teacher initialization scale.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is standard gradient descent (backpropagation) on the same student-teacher architecture. In linear networks, curl descent with hidden-layer sign flips transitions to chaotic weight dynamics beyond the stability boundary (c≈0.3), destroying performance, whereas readout-layer sign flips still reach low test error even when the solution manifold is formally unstable, thanks to the low-dimensional scalar output. In nonlinear networks, curl descent consistently outperforms gradient descent in convergence speed: with a single flipped readout weight, the advantage is statistically significant (p<.01) for N_train≥500 and persists across initialization scales. The speed advantage diminishes as the fraction of rule-flipped readout neurons increases but remains significant up to 50% flipped weights (p<.01). Above a critical teacher weight initialization scale (indicating high task complexity), both curl descent and gradient descent perform similarly poorly, eliminating the advantage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the exact learning dynamics framework of Saxe et al. (2014) for linear networks by introducing sign-flipped plasticity rules that provably break the gradient structure, producing non-gradient 'curl' terms analogous to the rotational component in a Helmholtz decomposition. It departs from the broader biologically-plausible learning literature (feedback alignment, natural gradients, Hebbian/anti-Hebbian normative derivations) by showing that sign diversity in plasticity—motivated by Dale's law and mixed Hebbian/anti-Hebbian rules—generates dynamics that cannot be written as gradient descent on any objective, rather than merely approximating it. The random-matrix-theory analysis of the Jacobian spectrum draws on tools from the chaos-in-random-networks literature (Kadmon & Sompolinsky, 2015) to identify a dynamical phase transition in the stability of the solution manifold.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges the dominant assumption that effective neural learning must follow (or approximate) a gradient, showing that biologically ubiquitous sign diversity in plasticity rules naturally produces non-gradient dynamics that can still optimize—and in some cases accelerate—learning. The identification of architectural conditions (compression ratio, layer of sign-flips) that determine whether curl terms lead to chaos or benign transients provides concrete constraints for understanding which biological circuits can support gradient-like learning. Key limitations include restriction to two-layer feedforward networks with scalar outputs, i.i.d. inputs, and local stability analysis; open questions include whether these findings extend to deeper, recurrent, or structured-input architectures, and whether the saddle-escape mechanism generalizes to high-dimensional non-convex landscapes.
