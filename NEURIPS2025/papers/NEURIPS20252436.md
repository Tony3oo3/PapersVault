## Problem

Accurate Q-value estimation remains a core bottleneck in model-free RL, and existing distributional RL methods (DSAC, DSACT) constrain value distributions to unimodal Gaussians, introducing bias when true return distributions are multimodal under non-stationary policies. The paper asks whether a universal, multimodal value distribution representation can be built for continuous control while retaining a tractable optimization objective.

## Input / Output

The method consumes state vectors of varying dimensionality (R^6 to R^111) from MuJoCo continuous-control environments and produces two outputs: a continuous action sampled from a Gaussian policy (dimension R^1 to R^17 depending on the task) and a full probability distribution over the scalar cumulative return Z(s,a), parameterized by a conditional diffusion model conditioned on (s,a). The value distribution is not a single scalar Q-value but a generative model that can sample multiple return values, enabling Monte Carlo estimation of the expected return for policy updates.

## Method

VDRL replaces the parametric (Gaussian) value distribution in distributional RL with a DDPM-style conditional diffusion model that denoises the target return T^πZ(s,a) = R(s,a) + γZ(s',a'). The key theoretical result (Theorem 1) proves that the standard diffusion variational lower bound is a tight lower bound for the KL-divergence objective of value distribution learning, yielding a simple noise-prediction loss (Eq. 10) with no additional constraints. To stabilize training against the high variance of iterative diffusion sampling, the method employs double value diffusion learning with sample selection: two independent diffusion-based value networks are maintained, and the target return is computed using the minimum of the two sampled next-state returns (analogous to TD3's twin-critic min). The policy is a standard Gaussian actor updated by maximizing the minimum expected return across the two value distributions, with target networks updated via exponential moving average.

## Results

On the eight MuJoCo continuous control tasks, VDRL achieves the best mean return on five of eight tasks: Ant-v3 (7236.67), HalfCheetah-v3 (11815.20), Hopper-v3 (3679.41), Humanoid-v3 (5497.15), and Walker2d-v3 (5223.77). On the remaining tasks, VDRL is competitive: Swimmer-v3 (144.40 vs. DACER's 151.55), Pusher-v2 (−26.92 vs. SAC's −20.28), and Inverted2Pendulum-v2 (9359.89, essentially tied with all converged baselines). In terms of value estimation bias (Table 2), VDRL consistently achieves the lowest |bias| across all seven reported tasks, e.g., −7.36 on Ant-v3 vs. +37.94 for DSAC and −373.20 for TD3.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VDRL outperforms all 11 baselines on the four most complex locomotion tasks: Ant-v3 (7236.67 vs. next-best DIPO 6224.93, +16.3%), HalfCheetah-v3 (11815.20 vs. DSACT 11670.82, +1.2%), Hopper-v3 (3679.41 vs. DSACT 3654.39, +0.7%), and Humanoid-v3 (5497.15 vs. SAC 5314.84, +3.4%). On Walker2d-v3, VDRL leads by a clear margin (5223.77 vs. DSACT 5134.79, +1.7%). On the simpler tasks, VDRL is slightly behind on Swimmer-v3 (144.40 vs. DACER 151.55) and Pusher-v2 (−26.92 vs. SAC −20.28), while essentially tying all converged methods on Inverted2Pendulum-v2 (~9359.8). Among diffusion-based policy RL baselines, VDRL consistently surpasses DIPO, QSM, QVPO, and DACER on the locomotion tasks, with the largest gap on Ant-v3 where QSM collapses (839.53) and QVPO shows high variance (±439.56 on Hopper). In value estimation bias (Table 2), VDRL achieves the smallest absolute bias on 6 of 7 reported tasks, e.g., −7.36 on Ant-v3 versus +37.94 (DSAC) and −373.20 (TD3), confirming that the diffusion-based multimodal value representation reduces both over- and under-estimation.

## Relation to Prior Work

VDRL sits at the intersection of distributional RL and diffusion generative modeling, but departs from both in a distinctive way. While prior diffusion-based RL methods (DIPO, QSM, QVPO, DACER) use diffusion models to represent the *policy* (action distribution), VDRL repurposes the diffusion model to represent the *value distribution* (return distribution), which is a fundamentally different role. The method extends DSAC/DSACT by replacing their unimodal Gaussian value distribution with a diffusion-based multimodal one, and adapts TD3's double-Q min-operation to the distributional setting as 'double value diffusion learning with sample selection.' The theoretical core—proving the diffusion VLB is a tight lower bound for the KL-divergence value distribution objective—draws directly on the VAE analogy from DDPM but applies it to a new optimization target.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to integrate diffusion models into distributional RL for value estimation, offering a principled way to capture multimodal return distributions that unimodal Gaussian critics (DSAC, DSACT) cannot represent. The tight variational bound result provides a clean theoretical justification for the training objective, which is more satisfying than ad-hoc loss designs. However, the iterative diffusion sampling introduces meaningful computational overhead (though lower than diffusion-policy RL methods), and evaluation is limited to MuJoCo benchmarks without real-world robotics or partial observability. Open questions include whether lightweight/distilled diffusion models can close the efficiency gap, and whether the approach scales to high-dimensional or partially observable settings.
