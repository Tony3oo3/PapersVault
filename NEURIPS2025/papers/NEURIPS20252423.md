## Problem

Minimizing the number of iterations to reach a target loss (stopping time) is the practical goal in deploying optimization algorithms, but it is traditionally considered non-differentiable with respect to algorithm hyperparameters because stopping time is an integer-valued, non-smooth function. Existing differentiable formulations instead minimize loss at a fixed time budget, which does not directly optimize convergence speed toward a desired accuracy threshold.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252423/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a forward Euler discretization of a continuous ODE (x_{k+1} = x_k − h·A(θ, x_k, t_k)) with learnable hyperparameters θ, an initial point x₀, and a stopping criterion J(x) = ε. It produces the sensitivity ∂N/∂θ of the discrete stopping time N, computed via a backward adjoint pass over the stored trajectory {x_k}_{k=0}^N. This gradient is then used to update θ in L2O training (combined with a weighted loss sum) or to adapt learning rates online in a single-step truncated fashion, requiring only O(Nd + d) memory rather than the full unrolled computation graph.

## Method

The paper establishes differentiability of the continuous stopping time T_J via the implicit function theorem (Theorem 1), then defines a discrete stopping time sensitivity (Definition 3) that approximates ∇_θ T_J with O(h) error (Theorem 2), where h is the Euler step size. The key computational contribution is a discrete adjoint algorithm (Algorithm 1) that propagates a co-state vector λ backward through the trajectory to compute the vector-Jacobian products ∇J(x_N)ᵀ(∂x_N/∂θ) and ∇J(x_N)ᵀ(∂x_N/∂x₀) without forming full Jacobians, reducing memory from O(N × graph) to O(Nd + d). In L2O, the stopping time N_J is added as a penalty term to the standard weighted loss sum, and an identity (Eq. 12) shows that uniform-weight loss minimization is equivalent to minimizing a sum of greedy stopping times. For online adaptation, a single-step truncated sensitivity S_k = ∇f(x_{k+1})ᵀd_k / (f(x_{k+1}) − f(x_k)) drives learning rate updates in Adam-OLA, providing a principled signal for when to increase or decrease the step size.

## Results

On the quadratic validation problem (d up to 10⁴, condition number 100), the discrete stopping time gradient achieves O(h) relative error versus the continuous ODE-based ground truth, confirming Theorem 2, while requiring substantially fewer function evaluations (NFE ratio < 1) than the adaptive ODE solver. In the L2O logistic regression experiments (d=512, n=256), adding the stopping time penalty (λ=1, criterion f(x_{k-1})−f(x_k)≤10⁻⁵) to L2O-RNNprop reduces the number of iterations to meet the target by hundreds of steps compared to the standard L2O-RNNprop, and the learned optimizer generalizes to 4× larger instances (d=2048, n=1024). On smooth SVM problems across four LIBSVM datasets, Adam-OLA consistently outperforms Adam-HD, Adagrad, Heavy-Ball, and NAG-SC, with the largest gains appearing in the later stages of convergence.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against L2O-DM [28], the comparison is limited: L2O-DM does not reach the stopping criterion within the maximum horizon of 100 steps, so it cannot be evaluated under the stopping-time penalty and serves only as a reference curve in Figure 3. Against L2O-RNNprop [29], the proposed L2O-RNNprop-Time (with λ=1 stopping time penalty) clearly outperforms the standard L2O-RNNprop (λ=0) on both same-size (d=512) and 4× larger (d=2048) logistic regression test problems, reducing iterations to target by hundreds of steps while also outperforming hand-designed baselines (GD, NAG, Adam with fixed 1/L step size). Against Adam-HD [32] on smooth SVM (LIBSVM a1a, a2a, a3a, w3a), Adam-OLA achieves lower function values at convergence, with the advantage growing in later iterations; the paper notes that Adam-OLA uses a single-step truncated sensitivity (Eq. 14) rather than full multi-step backpropagation, making it computationally cheaper per adaptation step. Overall, the proposed method wins on all three application axes (gradient accuracy, L2O convergence speed, and online LR adaptation), though the authors acknowledge that without extensive hyperparameter tuning for the stopping-time variants, SOTA performance is not always reached.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the ODE-based perspective on optimization algorithms (Su et al. 2016; Shi et al. 2019, 2022) by making the *stopping time*—the number of iterations to reach a target loss—differentiable with respect to algorithm hyperparameters, a quantity previously considered non-differentiable and addressed only via zeroth-order methods. It builds directly on Xie, Yin, and Wen (2024) [25], which established path differentiability for ODE-based L2O and proved global error convergence for certain discretizations, and on the discrete adjoint method from optimal control for efficient sensitivity computation. The work also connects to the implicit differentiation literature (Blondel et al. 2022; Bolte et al. 2021) by providing a memory-efficient alternative to unrolling computation graphs through long iterative procedures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By enabling direct gradient-based optimization of convergence speed rather than loss-at-fixed-time, this framework opens a new axis for L2O training and online hyperparameter adaptation that was previously inaccessible to first-order methods. The O(h) error bound (Theorem 2) provides theoretical grounding, and the discrete adjoint algorithm achieves substantial NFE savings over ODE-solver-based approaches, making it practical for high-dimensional problems. Key limitations include reliance on forward Euler discretization (acknowledged as potentially too coarse) and the need for the non-vanishing gradient condition ∇J(x(T))ᵀẋ(T)≠0; future work on symplectic integrators and higher-order schemes could tighten the error bounds and broaden applicability.
