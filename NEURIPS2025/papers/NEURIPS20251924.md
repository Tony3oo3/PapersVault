## Problem

Estimating both the first and second moments of a data stream under differential privacy in a continual release setting is challenging because naive approaches must split the privacy budget between the two estimates, inflating noise for both. This paper identifies a privacy regime in which the second-moment estimate can be obtained at no additional privacy cost, resolving the fundamental tension between estimating multiple quantities from the same private data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251924/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sequential stream of d-dimensional vectors with bounded ℓ₂ norm (‖x_t‖₂ ≤ ζ), two lower-triangular workload matrices specifying the weighting scheme (e.g., prefix sum, exponential decay, sliding window), and (ε, δ)-DP parameters. At each release step t it outputs a private weighted first-moment vector in ℝ^d and a private weighted second-moment matrix in ℝ^{d×d} (or just its diagonal for Adam), both unbiased with symmetric noise distributions. Optionally, invertible noise-shaping matrices with decreasing column norms can be supplied to exploit structure in the workload.

## Method

JME applies the matrix factorization mechanism to jointly privatize both x_t and x_t ⊗ x_t by adding correlated Gaussian noise shaped by invertible matrices C₁ and C₂, with the second-moment noise scaled by a parameter λ. The key theoretical contribution is a joint sensitivity analysis: by defining the combined sensitivity sens_λ(C₁, C₂) and characterizing it via a dimension-dependent function r_d(ν), the paper shows that choosing λ so that r_d(ν) = 4 makes the total sensitivity equal to that of the first moment alone, yielding 'privacy for free' for the second moment. This Pareto-dominates both independent moment estimation (IME, which splits the budget via composition) and concatenate-and-split (CS, which inflates sensitivity by the higher-dimensional norm). The approach generalizes to arbitrary workload matrices and supports problem-specific adaptations, such as estimating only the diagonal of the second moment in DP-Adam without changing the sensitivity.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with DP-Adam, JME achieves 30.38% top-1 accuracy at epoch 10 under high privacy (ε≈0.16, batch size 1), outperforming debiased DP-Adam (29.14%) and vanilla DP-Adam (10.48%). Under medium privacy (ε≈1.7, batch size 256), JME reaches 47.94%, slightly below debiased DP-Adam (48.53%) but well above vanilla DP-Adam (43.07%). For private Gaussian density estimation, JME achieves lower KL divergence than post-processing in the high-privacy regime (σ=2, σ=4) after approximately 10 samples, with the gap widening as privacy tightens.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against vanilla DP-Adam (post-processing, Li et al. [36]), JME achieves a substantial advantage in the high-privacy regime: 30.38% vs. 10.48% on CIFAR-10 (ε≈0.16), a gap of nearly 20 percentage points, because vanilla DP-Adam cannot handle the large noise variance. In the medium-privacy regime (ε≈1.7), JME still leads by ~5 points (47.94% vs. 43.07%). Against debiased DP-Adam (DP-AdamBC, Tang et al. [42]), JME slightly wins in high privacy (30.38% vs. 29.14%) but is marginally behind in medium privacy (47.94% vs. 48.53%), consistent with the theoretical prediction that post-processing becomes competitive when noise is small. Against the concatenate-and-split (CS) heuristic (Sheffet [40]-style joint clipping), JME outperforms in both privacy regimes (47.94% vs. 44.34% at ε≈1.7; 30.38% vs. 28.14% at ε≈0.16). Theoretically, JME Pareto-dominates both IME and CS in the privacy-accuracy trade-off (Theorems 3.6–3.7), and for the second moment specifically, JME's error scales as O(σ²) while PP's scales as O(σ⁴) in high dimensions, giving JME a clear advantage as privacy tightens.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the matrix mechanism (Li et al., VLDB 2015) and its recent refinements for continual-release DP to the joint estimation of first and second moments. Prior work on private covariance estimation (Sheffet 2019; Amin et al. 2019) and DP-Adam (Li et al. 2022; Tang et al. 2024) either estimates moments separately via composition or derives the second moment by post-processing the privatized first moment. The key departure here is a joint sensitivity analysis (Lemma 3.5) that reveals a privacy regime where the second-moment estimate incurs no additional noise cost over the first-moment estimate alone, a property the authors term 'privacy for free.'

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 'privacy for free' result provides a clean theoretical justification for why joint moment estimation can outperform naive composition, and the Pareto-dominance theorems (vs. IME and CS) give practitioners a principled reason to prefer JME in high-privacy settings. However, the practical gains are regime-dependent: in low-privacy or large-batch settings, post-processing remains competitive or superior, and the CIFAR-10 improvements are modest (a few accuracy points in the high-privacy, small-batch regime). Open questions include whether post-processing is truly optimal in the low-privacy regime and whether problem-specific noise-shaping factorizations can further tighten the bounds.
