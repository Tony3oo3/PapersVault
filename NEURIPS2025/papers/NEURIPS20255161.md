## Problem

Existing loss-range adaptive algorithms for prediction with expert advice include terms like the maximum loss or maximum regret in their regret bounds, which are typically treated as lower-order. This paper shows that under heavy-tailed losses with only bounded second moments, these terms can scale as √(KT) even when the second moment θ is O(1), making them the dominant factor and rendering the regret bound exponentially worse in K. The paper further argues this is not an artifact of loose analysis but is inherent to the structure of prior algorithms.

## Input / Output

In the Hedge setting, the algorithm receives a loss vector ℓ_t ∈ ℝ^K sequentially over T rounds with no prior knowledge of the loss range or the second-moment bound θ; in the quadratic loss setting it receives expert predictions z_{t,i} (bounded by Y) and an outcome y_t (with E[y_t²] ≤ σ). Each round the algorithm outputs a probability distribution p_t over K experts, and the guarantee is a regret bound of O(√(θT log K)) in the worst case and O(θ log(KT)/Δ_min) in self-bounded (e.g., i.i.d.) environments, where Δ_min is the gap between the best and second-best expert's expected loss.

## Method

The paper introduces two algorithms—LoOT-Free OMD and LoOT-Free FTRL—that both employ the multi-scale entropic regularizer of Bubeck et al. [2019] but, critically, clip the instantaneous regrets r_t(i) = ⟨p_t, ℓ_t⟩ − ℓ_t(i) rather than the raw losses. The clipping threshold 1/η_{t,i} is set adaptively from the cumulative variance, so that the cost of clipping is bounded by η_{t,i}·v_t(i) and sums to O(√(V_T)) rather than O(max |ℓ_t(i)|). For the FTRL variant, an additional rescaling factor b_{t-1}(i)/b_t(i) compensates for the one-step lag in the learning rate, and the Bregman divergence of the regularizer (rather than the regularizer itself) is used to ensure monotonicity required by the FTRL analysis. For the squared loss, a surrogate linear loss ℓ_t(i) = z_{t,i}(z̄_t − y_t) + ½(z_{t,i} − y_t)² is constructed via convexity inequalities, reducing the problem to the linear case and yielding an O((Y²+σ) log(KT)) regret that avoids the O(T) dependence on max|y_t|.

## Results

The paper provides theoretical regret bounds rather than empirical metrics. Algorithm 2 (LoOT-Free FTRL) achieves O(√(θT log(K))) worst-case regret under Assumption 2.1 (bounded second moments), completely eliminating the M_T lower-order term present in all prior loss-range adaptive algorithms. Algorithm 1 (LoOT-Free OMD) achieves O(√(θT log(KT))) worst-case regret and O(θ log(KT)/Δ_min) in self-bounded (stochastic) environments. For the squared loss setting, Algorithm 3 guarantees O((Y²+σ) log(KT)) regret, avoiding an O(T) dependence that arises for heavy-tailed outcomes. The paper further proves (Propositions 4.2–4.3) that the M_T term in prior bounds can scale as Ω(√(KT)), making it the dominant factor and rendering those guarantees exponentially worse in K.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All three baselines—Cesa-Bianchi et al. [2007] / De Rooij et al. [2014] and Mhammedi et al. [2019]—carry an additive M_T (or L_T) term in their regret guarantees. The paper's central contribution is proving that this term, previously dismissed as 'lower-order,' can be as large as Ω(√(KT)) under heavy-tailed losses with bounded second moment θ = O(1) (Propositions 4.2 and 4.3), making the effective regret O(√(KT)) rather than the desired O(√(θT log K)). Algorithm 2 matches the ideal O(√(θT log K)) worst-case bound of the baselines but without the M_T term, strictly dominating them. Algorithm 1 improves further in self-bounded environments, achieving O(θ log(KT)/Δ_min) versus the baselines' O(θ log K/Δ_min + M_T), which the paper shows can degrade to O(√(KT))—an exponential gap in K. The paper also argues (Appendix A.4) that the M_T dependence is not merely an artifact of loose analysis but is inherent to the exponential-weights-based structure of all three baselines, making a tighter analysis of those algorithms unlikely to close the gap.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on three key ingredients from the online learning literature: the coordinate-wise clipping technique of Cutkosky (2019), the null-update mechanism of Orseau and Hutter (2024), and the multi-scale entropic regularizer of Bubeck et al. (2019). It departs from prior loss-range adaptive algorithms (Cesa-Bianchi et al. 2007, de Rooij et al. 2014, Mhammedi et al. 2019) by demonstrating that their 'lower-order' terms (M_T or L_T) are not merely loose analysis artifacts but are fundamentally unavoidable in those algorithmic frameworks, and by combining the three techniques above to eliminate such terms entirely. The paper positions itself as the first to address heavy-tailed losses in the full-information expert setting, contrasting with the multi-armed bandit literature where adaptivity to an unknown second moment is provably impossible without additional assumptions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete failure mode of all existing loss-range adaptive algorithms: under heavy-tailed losses with bounded second moments, the 'lower-order' maximum-loss term can scale as √(KT), rendering the regret bound exponentially worse in K than the ideal √(θT log K). This has practical implications for applications involving noisy data, outliers, or local differential privacy mechanisms. Open questions include whether the log(T) factor in the best-of-both-worlds guarantee is necessary, whether the squared-loss analysis extends to general strongly convex or exp-concave losses, and whether the approach can adapt to arbitrary moments of the loss distribution. The results are purely theoretical with no empirical validation beyond a brief appendix comparison.
