## Problem

Prior work studied the trade-off between backdoor-based watermarks and adversarial defenses primarily through empirical observations, without a formal framework that captures the full spectrum of attacker and defender strategies under differing computational resources. This paper identifies that the binary watermark-vs-defense trade-off is incomplete: a third, counterintuitive option—transferable attacks that are indistinguishable from the data distribution and fool all efficient defenders—must also be accounted for. The central question becomes a trichotomy: for any learning task, which of these three schemes is guaranteed to exist?

## Input / Output

The framework takes a learning task L (a distribution over pairs of data distributions D_n and ground truth functions h_n on {0,1}^n), an error parameter ε, and circuit size bounds S_A(n) and S_B(n) specifying the computational resources of Alice (attacker/verifier) and Bob (defender/prover). The output is an existence guarantee that at least one of three schemes—Watermark, Adversarial Defense, or Transferable Attack—holds for the given task and resource allocation. Models are transmitted white-box via a fixed function class encoding, and the interaction is structured as an interactive protocol with q queries, where the specific circuit size separation (e.g., S(n) vs. √S(n)/log S(n)) determines which scheme is guaranteed.

## Method

The core technique formalizes the watermark-defense interaction as a two-player zero-sum game between circuit families of bounded size, where Alice's pure strategies are candidate watermarking circuits and Bob's are candidate watermark-removal circuits, with payoffs determined by error and rejection probabilities. By applying the Lipton–Young theorem on simple strategies for large zero-sum games, the Nash equilibrium value partitions the parameter space: a high equilibrium value yields an adversarial defense, while a low value yields either a watermark or a transferable attack. For the transferable attack construction, the paper uses Fully Homomorphic Encryption on a 'Lines on Circle' learning task, where the attacker encrypts a point near the decision boundary so that the query is computationally indistinguishable from the data distribution yet fools all classifiers learnable within the defender's circuit budget. The paper further proves a converse: any task admitting a transferable attack implies the existence of EFID pairs (a cryptographic primitive equivalent to pseudorandom generators), establishing that such attacks require computational complexity. Finally, bounded VC-dimension tasks are shown to admit adversarial defenses against all (even unbounded) attackers, while a subclass admits watermarks secure against fast adversaries, demonstrating that watermarks and defenses can coexist.

## Results

The main theorem (Theorem 1) establishes that for every learnable task with circuit complexity S(n), at least one of three schemes exists: a Watermark (attacker size S(n), defender size o(√S(n)/log S(n))), an Adversarial Defense (attacker size o(√S(n)/log S(n)), defender size O(S(n))), or a Transferable Attack (both sizes S(n)). The FHE-based construction (Theorem 2) demonstrates a concrete task where the attacker requires only ~1/ε circuit size while the defender needs Ω(1/ε²), a quadratic separation. Theorem 3 shows that any task admitting a Transferable Attack necessarily implies an EFID pair (equivalent to pseudorandom generators), establishing that such attacks can only exist for computationally complex tasks. Bounded VC-dimension tasks (Lemma 1) admit Adversarial Defenses against even unbounded attackers, while a specific bounded VC-dimension task (Lemma 2) admits a Watermark secure against adversaries with circuit size d/100.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or dataset comparisons. Its contributions are framed relative to prior theoretical work: it extends Christiano et al. [2024] by fixing the data distribution (rather than letting the attacker choose it) and by introducing Transferable Attacks as a third option, yielding an incomparable but complementary defendability framework. It generalizes Pal and Vidal [2020], who showed a Nash equilibrium between Fast Gradient Methods and Randomized Smoothing under a restricted additive-noise model, to a fully model-agnostic circuit-complexity setting. It subsumes the detection-based defenses of Goldwasser et al. [2020] (bounded VC-dimension, rejection-based) and the undetectable backdoors of Goldwasser et al. [2022] (cryptographic, computationally intractable detection) as special cases within a unified interactive protocol. The paper also clarifies the Tramèr et al. [2017] transferability conjecture by showing that whether adversarial examples transfer depends crucially on the relative computational resources of attacker and defender, rather than on model class alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the empirical and theoretical study of the watermark-versus-defense trade-off (Weng et al. 2020, Pal & Vidal 2020, Christiano et al. 2024) by formalizing it as an interactive protocol between two computationally bounded players and introducing transferable attacks as a necessary third option. It draws on Goldwasser et al. (2020, 2022) for detection-based defenses and undetectable backdoors, on Gentry's FHE (2009) to construct the transferable attack, and on Lipton & Young (1994) for approximating Nash equilibria in large zero-sum games. Unlike Christiano et al. (2024), who let the attacker choose the distribution, this paper fixes the distribution and instead lets the attacker choose specific queries, yielding a complementary but incomparable formulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The trichotomy theorem provides a clean theoretical lens for understanding when watermarks, defenses, or transferable attacks are possible, and the T² resource-allocation rule-of-thumb offers a (theoretical) guideline for defenders. However, the results are entirely theoretical: the FHE-based construction is not practically deployable, the circuit-complexity model is abstract, and no empirical validation is provided. Open questions include whether the trichotomy holds under more realistic (e.g., polynomial-time) resource bounds, whether the EFID-pair implication of transferable attacks can be tightened to yield PRGs, and whether the bounded VC-dimension separation extends to richer function classes relevant to modern deep learning.
