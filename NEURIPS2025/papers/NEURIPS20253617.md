## Problem

Existing differentiable QP methods (OptNet, QPLayer, SCQPTH) are tightly coupled to specific solvers, forcing users to sacrifice solver quality for differentiability. No prior framework enables plug-and-play differentiation of arbitrary black-box QP solvers, limiting applicability in neural network architectures and bi-level optimization where solver choice is critical for large-scale or structured problems.

## Input / Output

The method accepts QP data matrices (P, q, A, b, C, d) parameterized by a vector θ, along with an active-set tolerance ε_J, and supports both dense and sparse formats end-to-end. It outputs the primal solution z*, dual variables λ* and μ* (recovered via the reduced KKT system if the solver returns only primal), and their derivatives with respect to θ. In practice, as a PyTorch module, the backward pass computes Jacobian-vector products rather than full Jacobians, and gradients are projected onto the symmetric subspace for P and onto non-zero entries for sparse A and C.

## Method

The key insight is that once the active inequality set J is identified (by thresholding the primal residual Cz*−d ≥ −ε_J), the QP is locally equivalent to a simpler equality-constrained QP whose KKT matrix K_J is symmetric and reduced in dimension. Both the primal-dual solution and its derivative are expressed as K_J^{-1} applied to known right-hand sides, so a single factorization of K_J suffices for recovering duals and computing all gradients. The algorithm is: (1) solve the QP with any solver via qpsolvers, (2) identify the active set, (3) factorize the reduced KKT matrix, (4) optionally recover duals, (5) compute derivatives via explicit differentiation of the closed-form solution. This fully decouples the forward solve from the backward differentiation, enabling use of state-of-the-art solvers (Gurobi, MOSEK, PIQP, etc.) that would otherwise be inaccessible to differentiable optimization frameworks.

## Results

On the 129-problem Maros-Meszaros sparse QP benchmark, dQP solves all 129 problems with an average total (forward+backward) time of 1467 ms and mean duality gap of 7.39×10⁻⁶, while OptNet, QPLayer, and SCQPTH solve only 38, 77, and 55 problems respectively. On the 24-problem subset solvable by all methods, dQP achieves 93 ms average total time and 1.73×10⁻⁷ duality gap, outperforming QPLayer (3261 ms, 1.77×10⁻⁴), OptNet (9758 ms, 1.71×10⁻⁴), and SCQPTH (17067 ms, 8.75×10⁻³). For large-scale instances (dimension > 1000), dQP is fastest in 97% and most accurate in 93% of cases. On projection-to-simplex problems, dQP with Gurobi scales to 100,000 dimensions (477 ms) while competing methods fail beyond 10,000 dimensions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

dQP dominates all four QP-specific baselines on the Maros-Meszaros benchmark in both coverage and quality: it solves all 129 problems versus 38 (OptNet), 55 (SCQPTH), and 77 (QPLayer), and achieves a mean duality gap roughly 300× smaller than QPLayer (7.39×10⁻⁶ vs 2.21×10⁻²) and ~300× smaller than SCQPTH. On the 24-problem common subset, dQP is 35× faster than QPLayer (93 ms vs 3261 ms total), 105× faster than OptNet (93 ms vs 9758 ms), and 184× faster than SCQPTH (93 ms vs 17067 ms), while also achieving the best accuracy. CVXPYLayers (SCS) failed on all but 17 small-scale problems and is excluded from aggregate statistics. On the projection-to-simplex scalability test (Table 2), dQP with Gurobi handles 100,000-dimensional problems in 477 ms with 6.67×10⁻¹⁰ duality gap, whereas OptNet and QPLayer fail entirely beyond 10,000 dimensions and BPQP degrades to 4.02×10⁻² accuracy at 10,000 dimensions. The only scenario where dQP does not clearly win is small dense QPs (n ≤ 1000), where OptNet and QPLayer with their native solvers (qpth, ProxQP) are competitive in both speed and accuracy, as expected since those solvers are well-optimized for that regime.

## Relation to Prior Work

dQP synthesizes observations scattered across prior differentiable QP work—particularly the role of the active constraint set noted in [8, 17, 86, 83]—into a unified framework that no single prior method achieved. While OptNet, QPLayer, SCQPTH, and Alt-Diff each differentiate QPs through their respective solver's internal machinery (interior-point factorization, ADMM updates, or custom ProxQP internals), dQP's key departure is showing that once the active set is identified from the primal solution, both the primal-dual point and its derivative reduce to the same symmetric KKT matrix, fully decoupling the forward solve from the backward pass. This repurposes classical sensitivity analysis (Fiacco's Basic Sensitivity Theorem) and multi-parametric programming results in a way that makes the differentiation step a simple linear solve independent of the solver's algorithm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: practitioners can now embed any state-of-the-art QP solver (Gurobi, MOSEK, PIQP, etc.) as a differentiable layer, unlocking 2–3 order-of-magnitude speedups on large-scale sparse problems where specialized solvers dominate. Key limitations remain—no GPU acceleration, no infeasibility handling (unlike QPLayer), and reliance on strict convexity and feasibility—which restricts applicability in some ML pipelines. Open questions include extending the active-set decoupling to other problem classes (SDPs, mixed-integer programs) and developing GPU-capable sparse QP solvers that would make the framework competitive in the batched-small-problem regime where OptNet currently excels.
