## Problem

Existing flow-based image editing methods struggle to maintain structural consistency in non-targeted regions between original and edited images, because small inaccuracies at each editing step accumulate and propagate into significant deviations. Prior controller-based approaches (e.g., LQR) either over-constrain the trajectory (failing to apply the edit) or under-constrain it (producing structural inconsistencies), without a principled mechanism to selectively recover fine details from the original image.

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image along with a source text prompt (describing the original content) and a target text prompt (describing the desired modification), and produces a single edited RGB image. It operates entirely in the latent space of a pretrained rectified flow model (FLUX.1 dev) using 28 Euler-sampling steps, requiring no additional training. The key operational detail is that the inversion trajectory of the original image (a sequence of intermediate latents) is retained and reused as a measurement sequence to guide the forward generation, rather than being discarded after inversion.

## Method

Kalman-Edit reformulates flow-based image editing as a Linear Quadratic Gaussian (LQG) control problem, where each velocity step incorporates a noise term that is estimated and corrected via a Kalman filter. The algorithm is two-stage: Stage 1 applies an LQR controller during the early (semantic-formation) steps to produce an intermediate latent that blends source and target semantics; Stage 2 inverts this intermediate latent and applies the Kalman filter during the later (refinement) steps, using a measurement sequence constructed from inversion latents of both the original image and the intermediate image to recover structural details. The Kalman gain modulates how much historical information is injected at each step, with a small filter strength (0.1–0.2) and a long measurement window (14 steps) yielding the best consistency. An accelerated variant, Kalman-Edit*, replaces the second inversion with a parallelogram-law shortcut (x_orig2 = x_orig1 + x_mid − x_s), halving the inversion cost at a modest quality trade-off.

## Results

On the ZONE+DIV2K benchmark (Table 3), Kalman-Edit achieves the best CLIP-I (0.9492), LPIPS (0.1407), DINO (0.9141), and DreamSim (0.0793) among all methods, while Kalman-Edit* attains the highest CLIP-T (0.3220). On SFHQ (face editing), Kalman-Edit leads in CLIP-I (0.9167), LPIPS (0.1332), and DreamSim (0.1408). On the HQ instruction-editing benchmark, Kalman-Edit achieves the best CLIP-T (0.1943), LPIPS (0.2345), and DreamSim (0.1353), confirming consistent gains in structural fidelity across diverse editing tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Kalman-Edit dominates on structural-consistency metrics: it outperforms the strongest baseline SDEdit by +0.023 on CLIP-I (0.9492 vs 0.9264), +0.059 on DINO (0.9141 vs 0.8547), and by a large margin on DreamSim (0.0793 vs 0.1148) on ZONE+DIV2K. Among flow-based baselines, Kalman-Edit improves LPIPS over RF-Edit (0.1407 vs 0.2039) and FlowChef (0.1407 vs 0.2563), indicating substantially better pixel-level preservation. The main trade-off is prompt adherence: Kalman-Edit scores 0.2957 on CLIP-T, trailing MasaCtrl (0.3103) and FlowEdit (0.3096), while the accelerated Kalman-Edit* recovers the best CLIP-T (0.3220) at the cost of weaker consistency (LPIPS 0.2488, DreamSim 0.1454). On SFHQ, Kalman-Edit achieves the lowest Face Rec. score (0.3958) among flow-based methods, slightly below FlowChef (0.4013), but leads all methods in CLIP-I and LPIPS. On HQ, Kalman-Edit wins CLIP-T (0.1943) and DreamSim (0.1353) but trails RF-Edit on DINO (0.7929 vs 0.8197), suggesting the shortcut variant's approximation is less effective for high-level semantic preservation on complex instruction edits.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the controller-based flow editing framework of Rout et al. (RF-Inversion, ICLR 2025), which formulated rectified flow editing as a Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR) problem, by upgrading it to a Linear Quadratic Gaussian (LQG) formulation with a Kalman filter that integrates historical inversion trajectories as measurements. It also builds on the broader rectified flow editing line (RF-Edit, FlowEdit, FlowChef) and the optimal-control-for-diffusion line (Koo et al., Tzen & Raginsky), but departs from prior work by identifying error accumulation across individual editing steps as the root cause of structural inconsistency and by introducing a two-stage design (LQR for semantic blending, then Kalman filtering for detail recovery) rather than applying a single uniform controller. The shortcut estimation via the parallelogram law is a practical departure that eliminates the need for a second full inversion pass.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, training-free mechanism for preserving structural consistency in flow-based image editing, which is a persistent weakness of current rectified flow editing methods. The Kalman filter formulation offers a natural and extensible framework for incorporating arbitrary historical information into the generation trajectory, potentially applicable to video editing or multi-step editing pipelines. However, the method still requires per-task hyperparameter tuning (notably for object removal), and the accelerated variant (Kalman-Edit*) shows degraded low-level consistency, suggesting the shortcut approximation has meaningful limits. Open questions include whether the two-stage design is strictly necessary or whether a single-stage Kalman formulation with better initialization could match its performance, and whether the approach transfers to video or 3D generation settings.
