## Problem

Token premiums—differences in the number of tokens needed to encode parallel text across languages—had only been documented in multilingual tokenizers, where unequal data proportions per language could confound the effect. This paper asks whether identically trained monolingual tokenizers still exhibit wide crosslingual compression disparities, and if so, which factors (data similarity, vocabulary size, pre-tokenization) drive them and how they can be mitigated.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252126/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes 300MB monolingual text corpora per language (sourced from the Goldfish dataset) and produces subword tokenizers (BPE, Unigram, and SuperBPE) at vocabulary sizes from 8192 to 262144, all divisible by 128 for hardware throughput. Compression is measured by computing Corpus Token Count (CTC) over FLORES-200 parallel text, holding information content constant so that lower CTC indicates better compression. The released MonTok dataset includes all ~7,000 trained tokenizers and their corresponding CTC measurements across 97 languages.

## Method

The paper trains approximately 7,000 comparable monolingual tokenizers across 97 languages, systematically manipulating tokenizer type (BPE vs. Unigram), vocabulary size, and byte-premium scaling of training data. It identifies three factors correlated with token premiums—train-eval data similarity, mean token length, and proportion of whitespaces—then tests three interventions: (1) training on parallel NLLB data (minimal effect, ~1% CTC reduction), (2) fitting power-law curves per language to determine an 'optimal' vocabulary size that equalizes CTC across languages (significant variance reduction via F-test), and (3) training SuperBPE tokenizers that allow merges across whitespace boundaries, which both reduce token premium variance and improve overall compression. The key finding is that no single vocabulary size equalizes compression across languages, but per-language optimal vocabulary sizes combined with whitespace-free pre-tokenization substantially narrow the crosslingual gap.

## Results

Across ~7,000 monolingual BPE tokenizers for 97 languages, data similarity explains the most variance in FLORES CTC (R²=0.239), followed by mean token length (R²=0.168) and proportion of whitespaces (R²=0.157); combined they explain R²=0.297. Training on parallel data yields only a ~1% CTC reduction (t(152)=−2.356, p=0.0197), and simply increasing vocabulary size does not reduce crosslingual variance (F(96,96)=1.125, p=0.565). However, assigning each language an 'optimal' vocabulary size significantly reduces CTC variance (F(80,387)=0.150, p<0.001), and SuperBPE tokenizers achieve both lower average CTCs and less variance at every vocabulary size tested (all F-test p<0.001 for sizes 65536–114688).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's BPE tokenizers trained on 300MB of data achieve lower FLORES CTCs (better compression) than the OLMo, Pythia, and SmolLM tokenizers at most vocabulary sizes, despite those models being trained on substantially more data (Appendix D, Figure 5). Against the Goldfish SentencePiece Unigram tokenizers (trained on 5MB BP-scaled data, vocab 50000), the paper's BPE tokenizers show the most compact CTC distribution and lowest overall CTCs, while the Goldfish SP-Unigram tokenizers exhibit the worst compression rates of all tokenizer types tested (Figure 3, left). The paper's HF-Unigram tokenizers fall between BPE and SP-Unigram in compression quality. These comparisons confirm that the observed crosslingual token premium disparities are not artifacts of training data scale or a particular tokenizer implementation, but are inherent to the interaction between tokenization algorithm design and language-specific properties.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the token premium literature (Petrov et al., 2023; Ahia et al., 2023), which had only documented compression disparities in multilingual tokenizers, to the monolingual setting by training ~7,000 comparably-configured tokenizers across 97 languages. It builds directly on SuperBPE (Liu et al., 2025) for the whitespace pre-tokenization intervention, providing the first crosslingual application of superword tokenization. The work also connects to the byte premium measurement framework (Arnett et al., 2024) and the Goldfish monolingual model suite (Chang et al., 2024), which supplies the training data. Conceptually, the paper departs from the common assumption that train-eval data similarity drives compression differences, instead identifying pre-tokenization design and vocabulary size allocation as the primary levers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Token premiums directly translate into higher inference costs and lower training throughput for non-English languages, making equitable tokenizer design a practical concern for any multilingual deployment. The actionable finding that language-specific optimal vocabulary sizes and SuperBPE-style pre-tokenization can substantially reduce inequities provides concrete design guidance for multilingual tokenizer developers. Open questions remain: significant residual variance is explained by length ratio and UTF-8 byte encoding, the relationship between compression and downstream model performance for non-English languages is untested, and the small 300MB training corpus may understate effects for SuperBPE. The released MonTok dataset (~7,000 tokenizers) is a valuable resource for future tokenizer research.
