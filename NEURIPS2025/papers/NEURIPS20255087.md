## Problem

RNNs represent latent processes as trajectories through continuous state spaces, while HMMs rely on discrete latent states with stochastic transitions, making the two frameworks appear fundamentally incompatible. Prior RNN work has focused on input-driven, largely deterministic tasks, leaving open whether and how RNNs can generate the spontaneous, stochastic, discrete-state dynamics characteristic of natural behaviors. This paper asks whether vanilla RNNs can replicate HMM emission statistics and, if so, what continuous dynamical mechanisms underlie the apparent discrete transitions.

## Input / Output

At each timestep the RNN receives an i.i.d. Gaussian noise vector x_t ~ N(0, I_d) with d ∈ {1, 10, 100, 200}; there is no task-specific or structured input signal. The hidden state (size |h| ∈ {50, 150, 200}) is projected linearly to three logits, which are converted to categorical samples via Gumbel-Softmax (temperature τ=1) to mimic discrete HMM emissions over the alphabet {1, 2, 3}. Training targets are sequences generated from linear-chain (M=2–5 states), fully-connected (3 states), or cyclic (4 states) HMMs, and the loss compares full output distributions rather than pointwise predictions.

## Method

The core contribution is a training-and-reverse-engineering pipeline: vanilla ReLU RNNs are trained to match HMM emission statistics using Sinkhorn divergence (an entropy-regularized optimal transport distance) over batches of sequences, with Gumbel-Softmax providing a differentiable relaxation of the discrete sampling step. Rather than discovering one fixed point per HMM state, trained networks converge to a single fixed point in the absence of input and, under stochastic drive, exhibit noise-sustained orbital dynamics with slow 'cluster' regions (encoding distinct emission probabilities) separated by fast deterministic transitions initiated by small groups of 'kick-neurons.' These kick-neurons sit near the ReLU threshold in 'kick-zones' and are driven by structured excitatory/inhibitory connections from larger 'noise-integrating populations,' implementing a self-induced stochastic resonance mechanism. The same dynamical motif is reused compositionally across HMM architectures, with multiple instances of the kick-circuit combining to govern transitions between specific state pairs in fully-connected and cyclic models.

## Results

Trained vanilla RNNs (|h|=150, d=100) replicate the emission statistics of target HMMs across all four evaluation metrics—Sinkhorn-aligned Euclidean distance, transition-matrix squared differences, observation frequencies, and observation volatility—approaching the HMM self-comparison baseline. Increasing input noise dimensionality (d) from 1 to 200 systematically improves alignment, while increasing hidden size beyond 150 yields only marginal gains. Training converges within ~500 epochs, exhibiting a characteristic double-descent loss profile where the second drop coincides with the onset of noise-sustained orbital dynamics. The orbit radius in latent space scales linearly with input variance (σ²), confirmed by both empirical contour plots and a second-order perturbation analysis.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing RNN architectures or alternative training methods; instead, the HMM target itself serves as the reference baseline. The key comparison is whether the RNN's output distribution matches the HMM's emission statistics, quantified via Sinkhorn-aligned Euclidean distance (global sequence similarity) and element-wise transition-matrix squared differences (temporal structure). Across all six HMM architectures (linear-chain M=2–5, fully-connected, cyclic), the trained RNNs at |h|=150, d=100 achieve distances statistically indistinguishable from the HMM self-comparison lower bound, indicating faithful replication. A systematic sweep over hidden size (50, 150, 200) and input dimensionality (1, 10, 100, 200) reveals that input dimensionality is the dominant factor: d=1 yields the largest deviations, while d≥100 achieves near-perfect alignment regardless of hidden size. The paper also contrasts the emergent RNN dynamics (single fixed point + noise-sustained orbits) against the naive expectation of an n-well landscape with one attractor per HMM state, showing the RNN discovers a fundamentally different but functionally equivalent solution.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the mechanistic interpretability program for RNNs (Sussillo & Barak 2013; Mante et al. 2013; Sussillo et al. 2015; Zemlianova et al. 2024), which has so far focused on deterministic, input-driven tasks like motion discrimination and reach planning, to the fundamentally different regime of internally-driven, stochastic discrete state transitions. It draws on HMM-based behavioral segmentation (Weinreb et al. 2024) as the target structure, and on optimal transport (Sinkhorn divergence) and Gumbel-Softmax as training tools, but the core contribution is a new dynamical solution: rather than the expected multi-attractor landscape, trained RNNs settle into a single fixed point that noise destabilizes into orbital dynamics. The self-induced stochastic resonance framing connects to a physics literature (Muratov et al. 2005; Zhu & Nakao 2021) that had not previously been applied to trained neural networks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first mechanistic account of how continuous recurrent dynamics can implement discrete stochastic state transitions, identifying a reusable 'dynamical primitive' (slow noise-integrating populations + fast kick-neurons) that composes across HMM architectures. This offers testable hypotheses for how biological circuits might implement behavioral state switching, and positions RNNs as a flexible alternative to HMMs for inferring latent structure in naturalistic behavior. Key limitations include the restriction to small networks (≤200 units), symmetric transition graphs, and only 2–3 output classes; whether the motif generalizes to asymmetric, high-dimensional HMMs or to real neural data remains open. The single-fixed-point solution also raises the question of how robust the orbital regime is to architectural changes (e.g., GRU, LSTM) and to the specific noise statistics assumed here.
