## Problem

While word2vec is known to implicitly factorize the PMI matrix, it remains unknown which specific low-rank approximation is learned and how the training dynamics unfold. This gap prevents a mechanistic understanding of how semantic structure emerges during self-supervised contrastive training, and existing alternatives (e.g., SVD of PMI or PPMI) perform significantly worse on downstream tasks than the embeddings actually produced by gradient descent.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250140/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a text corpus from which unigram and skip-gram distributions are computed, along with reweighting hyperparameters that encode word2vec's engineering tricks (subsampling, dynamic windows, negative sampling rates). It produces a V×d word embedding matrix in the underparameterized regime (d ≪ V), where the learned feature subspaces are given by the top-d eigenvectors of the target matrix M*. Unusually, both the final embeddings and the full training trajectory can be computed in closed form from corpus statistics alone, without running any optimization.

## Method

The paper defines Quadratic Word Embedding Models (QWEMs) by taking the quartic Taylor approximation of word2vec's contrastive loss around the origin and restricting to symmetric reweighting with constant aggregate weight (Setting 3.1). Under this setting, the loss reduces exactly to unweighted matrix factorization ‖WWᵀ − M*‖²_F, where M* encodes relative deviations of co-occurrence statistics from independence (Theorem 1). The global minimizer is then given by the Eckart-Young-Mirsky truncated eigendecomposition of M*. For training dynamics, the paper solves gradient flow from vanishing random initialization in closed form (Result 3), revealing that the model acts as a greedy spectral method: it learns orthogonal linear subspaces one at a time, each incrementing the effective rank, with sigmoidal singular value growth on characteristic timescales τ_k = λ_k⁻¹ ln(λ_k/σ²). Empirically, QWEMs trained on 2 billion Wikipedia tokens closely match word2vec in training dynamics, learned features, and benchmark performance (65.1% vs 68.0% on Google Analogies), validating the approximation.

## Results

On the Google Analogies benchmark, QWEM achieves 65.1% top-1 accuracy, closely matching word2vec (68.0%) and the explicit SVD factorization of M* (66.3%), while substantially outperforming svd(PPMI) (50.6%) and svd(PMI) (8.4%). On semantic similarity, QWEM scores 0.755 Spearman correlation on MEN and 0.682 on WordSim353, matching or slightly exceeding word2vec (0.744 and 0.698 respectively). The learned principal components of QWEM embeddings align closely with word2vec's, representing interpretable topic-level concepts (biography, government, geography) in the top components.

## Baselines & Comparisons

QWEM closely matches word2vec across all three benchmarks: within 3 points on Google Analogies (65.1% vs 68.0%), slightly ahead on MEN (0.755 vs 0.744), and within 0.016 on WordSim353 (0.682 vs 0.698), confirming QWEM as a faithful analytical proxy. Against svd(PPMI) (the positive PMI factorization from Levy et al., 2015), QWEM wins decisively on Google Analogies (65.1% vs 50.6%) and is competitive on the similarity benchmarks, demonstrating that the implicit bias of gradient descent matters beyond what a simple rank-truncated PMI approximation captures. Against svd(PMI) (the least-squares rank-d approximation of the full PMI matrix), QWEM wins by a wide margin on every benchmark (e.g., 65.1% vs 8.4% on analogies), confirming that the divergence of PMI at rare co-occurrences makes least-squares factorization a poor predictor of what word2vec actually learns. The explicit factorization svd(M*)—obtained by directly diagonalizing the QWEM target matrix—nearly matches word2vec's performance, validating the theoretical prediction that QWEMs learn the top d eigenvectors of M*.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the implicit matrix factorization interpretation of word embeddings (Levy & Goldberg, 2014) by identifying *which* rank-d approximation is learned, not just that a factorization occurs. It builds on the exact gradient-flow solutions for deep linear networks (Saxe et al., 2014) and the broader literature on rank-incremental dynamics in matrix factorization (Li et al., 2018; Arora et al., 2019; Gissin et al., 2019), but departs from those works by requiring no assumptions on the data distribution and no special initialization structure. The closest prior work on contrastive learning theory (HaoChen et al., 2021; Tian et al., 2021; Simon et al., 2023b) studies vision tasks with linearized architectures; this paper instead studies a natural language task with a different loss, obtains closed-form dynamics without linearizing the model, and connects the learned geometry to spiked random matrix models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first closed-form theory of training dynamics for a practical self-supervised contrastive NLP task, resolving the open question of which low-rank approximation of the co-occurrence structure word2vec actually learns (answer: top eigenvectors of M*, not PMI or PPMI). The spiked random matrix model description of task vectors provides a quantitative SNR framework for understanding when linear semantic representations are robust enough to support analogy completion, with implications for the broader linear representation hypothesis in LLMs. Key limitations include the restriction to tied weights (W=W'), the quartic loss approximation, and Setting 3.1; the authors note these are supported empirically but leave their relaxation as future work. Extending the framework to asymmetric factorizations, finite-step-size dynamics, and other contrastive objectives (e.g., SimCLR) are natural next steps.
