## Problem

Prior work established that LLMs can improve with external feedback, but the upper bounds of such improvement remained unexplored. This paper asks whether models can fully incorporate near-perfect, ground-truth-informed feedback to reach their theoretical maximum accuracy, and finds they consistently cannot—a limitation the authors term FEEDBACK FRICTION.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251831/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The solver model receives a problem prompt concatenated with the full history of prior (answer, feedback) pairs, up to 10 iterations. The feedback generator receives the problem, the solver's current answer, the ground-truth answer (and solution steps when available), but must mask the final answer using a '[masked]' token to prevent direct revelation. Outputs include per-iteration accuracy curves, a target accuracy ceiling (computed by excluding feedback-quality errors), and per-question error category labels (feedback resistance, feedback quality, other) assigned by an automated annotator validated against human labels.

## Method

The paper constructs a controlled iterative self-improvement loop where a solver model attempts a problem, a separate feedback model (GPT-4.1 mini) generates targeted guidance informed by ground truth, and the solver retries with the accumulated history—repeating up to 10 times. Three feedback mechanisms of increasing specificity are compared: binary correctness, self-generated reflective feedback, and strong-model reflective feedback. To isolate feedback incorporation from feedback quality, the authors compute a target accuracy ceiling by excluding questions where the feedback itself was flawed. They then analyze persistent failures via semantic entropy (measuring model confidence through 50 sampled outputs clustered by final answer), finding that high-confidence predictions are most resistant to correction. Mitigation strategies—progressive temperature increases and rejection sampling that explicitly forbids repeating prior incorrect answers—yield modest gains but do not close the gap to target accuracy. Finally, the authors rule out data familiarity, reasoning complexity, and cross-model question overlap as explanations, and document a striking disconnect where models claim >95% understanding and willingness to update yet fail to change their answers.

## Results

Across all nine tasks and five solver models, every model plateaus below its theoretical target accuracy (the accuracy achievable if all valid feedback were fully incorporated), with gaps of 15–25% on AIME and 3–8% on GPQA even after 10 feedback iterations. On the counterfactual Hexadecimal 5-Digit Multiplication task, no model exceeds 20% accuracy after 10 iterations. Error categorization of persistent failures shows feedback resistance (models ignoring correct feedback) accounts for 62.8–100% of unsolved problems, far exceeding feedback quality issues (0–30.8%). Semantic entropy analysis reveals that high-confidence predictions (low entropy) show near-zero improvement from feedback, while low-confidence predictions improve by 0.4–0.8 in absolute rate.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new method to compare against external baselines; instead, it benchmarks solver models against their own theoretical target accuracy under three feedback mechanisms (Binary F1, Self-Generated F2, Strong-Model F3). Higher-quality feedback consistently improves performance: Llama-4-Maverick gains +26.7% on AIME and +10.6% on GPQA moving from F1 to F3, while Llama-4-Scout gains +33.3% and +13.1% respectively. However, no feedback level enables any model to reach its target ceiling. Among mitigation strategies, combining progressive temperature increases with rejection sampling (forcing novel answers) outperforms temperature alone across both multiple-choice and open-ended tasks, yet still falls short of target accuracy. Across solver models, Claude 3.7 Thinking achieves the highest initial accuracy on AIME, TriviaQA, GPQA, and MATH-500, but exhibits the same plateauing behavior as Llama models, indicating FEEDBACK FRICTION is not model-family-specific.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the self-improvement-with-feedback literature (Reflexion, Self-Refine, and related work) by decoupling two previously conflated factors: feedback quality and the model's ability to incorporate that feedback. Prior studies established that LLMs can improve with external feedback at test time, but left the upper bound of such improvement unexplored. By constructing a controlled environment where the feedback generator has access to ground-truth answers, the paper isolates the incorporation step and reveals a persistent ceiling it terms FEEDBACK FRICTION. The analysis also connects to the broader elasticity-plasticity tension in LLMs (catastrophic forgetting, model editing, sycophancy) by showing that models resist updating beliefs even when explicitly told their prior answer is wrong.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that even near-perfect feedback cannot push models to their theoretical accuracy ceiling has direct implications for applications relying on iterative refinement—scientific discovery, complex planning, and agentic systems—where repeated correction cycles are assumed to converge. The disconnect between models' stated understanding (>95% 'yes, I will update') and actual behavior raises concerns for AI reliability and alignment. The semantic entropy diagnostic (high confidence predicts resistance) offers a practical signal for when to trust vs. override model outputs. Open questions include the mechanistic cause of feedback resistance, whether fine-tuning or RL can genuinely increase receptiveness, and whether the phenomenon is architecture-specific or a fundamental property of autoregressive sequence generation.
