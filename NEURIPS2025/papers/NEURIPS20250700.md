## Problem

Existing single-positive multi-label learning (SPMLL) methods assign pseudo-labels to unannotated labels under the assumption that all classes share identical prior probabilities. In real-world datasets, class priors differ significantly, and this unrealistic assumption introduces severe bias into pseudo-labels, degrading the learned multi-label classifier. The paper asks whether explicitly estimating and incorporating class-priors can resolve this gap.

## Input / Output

For MLIC datasets (VOC, COCO, NUS-WIDE, CUB-200), the input is an RGB image with exactly one observed positive label per training example; for MLL datasets (Image, Scene, Yeast, Corel5k, Mirflickr, Delicious), the input is a fixed-dimension feature vector with the same single-positive annotation constraint. The model outputs a probability score per class, from which a set of relevant labels is predicted. Validation and test sets retain full multi-label annotations, while training uses only the single observed positive label per instance.

## Method

CRISP alternates between class-prior estimation and model training. The class-priors estimator, inspired by the top-bin concept from PU learning, determines an optimal threshold z* by minimizing an upper confidence bound on the ratio of total samples to positive-labeled samples scoring above z, yielding a theoretically guaranteed estimate that converges to the ground-truth prior. Using these estimated priors, an unbiased risk estimator is derived that decomposes into (i) a positive-labeled term penalizing 1 − f^j(x) over observed positives, and (ii) a class-prior alignment term |E[f^j(x)] − π̂_j| that pulls the model's expected output toward the estimated prior. A sigmoid-based modification with a prior-shifted logit (g^j(x) + λπ^j) is introduced to counteract decision-boundary bias toward rare classes. Theoretical analysis via Rademacher complexity proves that the empirical risk minimizer converges to the optimal fully-supervised risk minimizer as n → ∞.

## Results

On the four MLIC datasets, CRISP achieves the best mAP on VOC (89.82), COCO (74.64), and NUS (49.996), and remains competitive on CUB (21.65, vs. PLC's 21.84). On the six MLL datasets, CRISP achieves the lowest Ranking loss across all datasets (e.g., 0.164 on Image vs. SMILE's 0.260, 0.112 on Scene vs. SMILE's 0.161). The Wilcoxon signed-ranks test confirms statistical significance, with CRISP winning 49 out of 55 test cases at the 0.05 level.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CRISP outperforms all five SPMLL baselines from Cole et al. (AN, AN-LS, WAN, EPR, ROLE) by substantial margins, e.g., +4.3 mAP over AN on VOC and +10.3 mAP over ROLE on CUB. Against SMILE (Xu et al., NeurIPS 2022), CRISP gains +2.5 mAP on VOC and +4.2 mAP on COCO, while on MLL datasets it reduces Ranking loss from 0.260 to 0.164 on Image. Compared to PLC (Xie et al., NeurIPS 2022), CRISP wins on VOC (+1.8), COCO (+4.2), and NUS (+3.8) but trails by 0.19 mAP on CUB. Against the MLML baselines LL-R/LL-CP/LL-CT (Kim et al., CVPR 2022), CRISP leads by 2.0–2.5 mAP on VOC and COCO. The strongest competitor is BOOSTLU+LL-R (Kim et al., CVPR 2023), which CRISP surpasses by +0.6 mAP on VOC, +1.4 on COCO, and +0.4 on NUS, though BOOSTLU+LL-R is not applicable to MLL tabular datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

CRISP builds directly on the PU learning framework of Garg et al. (2021), repurposing the 'top bin' mixture proportion estimation concept to estimate per-class priors in the SPMLL setting. It departs from all prior SPMLL methods (Cole et al. 2021, Xu et al. 2022, Xie et al. 2022, Zhou et al.) that share the critical assumption of identical class-priors across all labels, replacing this with a data-driven estimator that has a convergence guarantee. The unbiased risk estimator derived from the estimated priors connects to the broader PU learning and semi-supervised learning literature on risk-consistent estimators, while the overall framework sits within the SPMLL sub-problem of multi-label learning with missing labels.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that the uniform class-prior assumption — a shared, unexamined premise across all prior SPMLL work — is the primary source of bias provides a clear diagnostic for why existing methods underperform on imbalanced real-world datasets. The theoretical guarantees (convergence of prior estimates and learning consistency via Rademacher complexity bounds) give practitioners confidence that the approach generalizes, not just on the ten benchmark datasets tested. Open questions include how the exhaustive threshold search scales to extreme multi-label settings (thousands of classes), whether the class-prior estimation can be made differentiable for end-to-end training, and whether the absolute loss in the risk estimator is optimal compared to other convex alternatives.
