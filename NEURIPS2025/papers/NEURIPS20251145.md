## Problem

Prior work on transformers as implicit algorithm learners has been confined to first-order methods (e.g., gradient descent) in single-task, single-regime settings, leaving open whether a transformer trained purely on input-output pairs can discover a unified, second-order numerical algorithm that automatically adapts to diverse computational constraints—communication bandwidth, memory, and per-layer compute—while spanning related tasks such as prediction, estimation, and Nyström extrapolation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251145/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The transformer receives a block-partitioned low-rank matrix X = [A C; B D] with the lower-right block D zeroed out, and must reconstruct D from the visible blocks A, B, C under a mean-squared error objective; the same prompt template generalizes to scalar regression (d′=n′=1), Nyström kernel extrapolation, and parameter estimation. In the distributed regime the matrix is partitioned across M machines with per-machine local blocks A^μ, B^μ and shared C, D, while in the computation-limited regime attention is bottlenecked to rank r ≪ n, cutting per-layer cost from Θ(nd²) to Θ(nrd). Training uses n=d=18, n′=d′=2, rank s=10, anisotropy α=0.7, with optional Gaussian noise (σ²=0.01); evaluation scales to n=d=240 and real SuiteSparse matrices up to 10 million coefficients.

## Method

The paper trains a 4-layer linear-attention transformer under three architectural visibility constraints—unconstrained (full attention), computation-limited (rank-r query/key bottleneck), and distributed (one head per machine with local self-attention)—using only masked-block input-output pairs and MSE loss, with no explicit algorithmic guidance. After training, progressive weight quantization, sparsification, and matrix property tests reveal that all three regimes collapse to the same parameter-free two-line update rule, EAGLE, which applies a Newton-Schulz-style conditioning step to A and B while performing a coupled gradient-like update on C and D. The key structural finding is that learned weight products W_QK and W_VP become near-block-diagonal with scalars tuned to the batch-maximum spectral norm of A, making each attention layer equivalent to a continuous matrix-conditioning loop rather than a parameter-space gradient step. Theoretically, EAGLE achieves quadratic convergence with iteration complexity O(log κ) in the centralized setting, O(α⁻¹) in the distributed setting (where α is a data-diversity index), and O(n/r) slowdown under sketching, outperforming Conjugate Gradient and Gradient Descent by orders of magnitude in both iteration count and communication cost.

## Results

On seven real-world SuiteSparse matrices (ranging from 1007×1007 to 9728×10178, condition numbers up to 1.38×10⁷), EAGLE achieves an average wall-clock time of 1.14 s, outperforming SVD (2.29 s), Conjugate Gradient (2.86 s), and LSMR (3.71 s). In synthetic unconstrained experiments (n=d=240, κ up to 10⁶), EAGLE exhibits second-order (quadratic) convergence with iteration counts scaling as log κ, requiring roughly 100× fewer iterations than CG at κ=10⁴. In the distributed setting, EAGLE's iteration complexity grows linearly with the data-diversity index α⁻¹ and is independent of the number of workers M, while GD requires 10–100× more communication rounds.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Conjugate Gradient (Hestenes & Stiefel, 1952), EAGLE wins on 6 of 7 SuiteSparse matrices in wall-clock time (e.g., 0.847 s vs. 16.7 s on Priebel/162bit, a 20× speedup), losing only on the well-conditioned, sparse Meszaros/pf2177 matrix (3.43 s vs. 1.75 s). In synthetic experiments, EAGLE requires ~100× fewer iterations than CG at κ=10⁴ due to its log κ vs. κ iteration scaling. Against LSMR (Fong & Saunders, 2011), EAGLE wins on all 7 matrices, with the largest gap on Priebel/162bit (0.847 s vs. 57.1 s, a 67× speedup). EAGLE also consistently outperforms the SVD-based solver (torch.linalg.lstsq) on every matrix despite its nominal log κ overhead, attributed to its heavy use of massively parallel matrix–matrix multiplication. In the distributed regime, EAGLE transmits only O((d+d′)n′) floats per round (identical to GD) but converges in O(log(κ_max + α⁻¹ log(1/ε))) iterations versus GD's O(κ(E₀) log(1/ε)), yielding 10–100× fewer communication rounds under typical ML-scale conditioning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work showing that transformers implicitly learn numerical algorithms (Von Oswald et al., 2023; Ahn et al., 2023; Akyürek et al., 2023), which had recovered only first-order gradient-descent-like updates in a single centralized regime. The key departure is twofold: the emergent algorithm (EAGLE) is a second-order Newton-Schulz-type conditioning loop rather than gradient descent, and it emerges identically across three architecturally distinct resource regimes (centralized, distributed, compute-limited) from the same base architecture. Structurally, the update is shown to be exactly the Newton-Schulz iteration for the matrix sign function (Higham, 2008, §5.3), connecting transformer in-context computation to classical iterative matrix inversion in a way prior work had not established.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a single parameter-free update rule unifies prediction, estimation, and Nyström extrapolation across centralized, distributed, and sketched settings offers a concrete mechanistic account of how in-context learning can produce resource-adaptive solvers without explicit algorithmic supervision. Practically, the extracted EAGLE algorithm achieves quadratic convergence with only log κ iteration complexity, outperforming Conjugate Gradient by ~100× at κ=10⁴ and cutting distributed communication to O(d) floats per round. Open questions include numeric stability beyond κ > 10⁸, whether non-linear attention variants yield analogous unification, and how stronger topological or bandwidth constraints reshape the learned algorithm. The scope is currently limited to linear-attention transformers on Gaussian-distributed low-rank data, so generalization to broader architectures and data regimes remains to be established.
