## Problem

Prior work on test-time scaling for reasoning models (e.g., Muennighoff et al., 2025) reported that extending thinking traces via prompts like "Wait" or "Think more" improves accuracy, fostering the belief that more thinking is always better. This paper reveals that this picture is incomplete: performance exhibits a consistent non-monotonic pattern across models and benchmarks, with initial gains followed by degradation beyond a critical thinking-token threshold—a phenomenon the authors term 'overthinking.' The paper further asks whether the early gains reflect genuine reasoning improvement or are merely an artifact of increased output variance.

## Input / Output

The method takes a mathematical reasoning prompt x and produces a final answer y, with an intermediate thinking trace z generated autoregressively by the model. For sequential test-time scaling, the thinking trace is extended iteratively (x → z₁ → z₂ → … → z_k → y) by suppressing the end-of-thinking delimiter and appending "Wait" tokens, with the compute budget measured in total thinking tokens (ranging from 256 to 16384). For the proposed parallel thinking, N independent thinking traces are generated within the same token budget B, and the final answer is selected via majority vote over the N candidate responses.

## Method

The paper contributes a variance-based explanation and a practical alternative to sequential overthinking. A simple probabilistic model (Gaussian policy π(y|x) and Gaussian reward r(x,y)) shows that increasing the policy variance σ²_π produces a non-monotonic expected reward: a coverage effect initially improves overlap with the reward peak, but a dilution effect eventually dominates as the prefactor 1/√(2π(σ²_r+σ²_π)) shrinks faster than the exponential term gains. Empirically, the authors confirm that extended thinking increases the entropy of the model's response distribution (e.g., 12× entropy increase on GSM-8K), validating the variance mechanism and showing that early accuracy gains are a mirage driven by randomness rather than policy refinement. The proposed alternative, parallel thinking, allocates a fixed token budget B across N independent reasoning paths z⁽ⁱ⁾ ~ π(·|x) and selects the final answer via a self-reward R_self(x,y) = Σᵢ I[y⁽ⁱ⁾=y] (majority vote), circumventing entropy overgrowth. Under a 16K token budget, parallel thinking achieves up to 22% higher accuracy than sequential "Wait & Think More" scaling across GSM-8K, MATH-500, and AIME 2024.

## Results

Across GSM-8K, MATH-500, and AIME 2024 with three DeepSeek-R1 distilled models (1.5B, 7B, 8B), sequential test-time scaling via 'Wait' prompts exhibits a consistent non-monotonic pattern: accuracy initially rises (e.g., GSM-8K 1.5B: 82.2% → 87.3% as thinking tokens grow from 385 to 1,100) but then degrades sharply (to 70.3% at ~16K tokens). The proposed parallel thinking method, which splits the same token budget across N independent reasoning paths and selects via majority vote, achieves up to 22% higher accuracy than sequential scaling under a 16K token budget, and up to 47% higher than the Exact Thinking TTBC. Entropy analysis confirms that extended thinking inflates output variance (e.g., 12× entropy increase on GSM-8K from 385 to 6,136 tokens), explaining the overthinking degradation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the 'Wait & Think More' sequential scaling strategy from Muennighoff et al. (2025, s1), which suppresses the end-of-thinking token and appends 'Wait' to extend reasoning. The paper demonstrates that this approach, previously reported as monotonically improving, actually peaks and then degrades across all three models and all three benchmarks. Under a fixed 16K thinking-token budget, parallel thinking outperforms Wait & Think More by approximately 22% in accuracy (e.g., on GSM-8K with the 1.5B model, sequential scaling at 16K tokens drops accuracy by 11.8% below standard thinking, while parallel thinking at the same budget improves accuracy by 10.1% above standard thinking). The Exact Thinking TTBC (fixed token count) shows a similar degradation trend, with parallel thinking achieving ~47% higher accuracy at 16K tokens. The paper also contrasts with the Minimum Thinking TTBC (Appendix E.1), confirming the same non-monotonic pattern, and positions parallel thinking as a strictly superior allocation of the inference budget compared to any sequential extension strategy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends and challenges the test-time scaling narrative established by Muennighoff et al. (2025, 's1') and Aggarwal & Welleck (2025, 'L1'), which demonstrated that prompting models with 'Wait' or 'Think more' monotonically improves reasoning accuracy. The authors depart from this by revealing a non-monotonic 'overthinking' regime and providing a variance-based probabilistic explanation for why extended thinking initially helps but ultimately degrades performance. The proposed parallel thinking solution repurposes the well-studied Best-of-N / self-consistency paradigm (Beirami et al., 2024; Nakano et al., 2021) as a principled alternative to sequential budget extension, reframing it specifically as a test-time compute allocation strategy for reasoning models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work corrects a widely-held assumption in the reasoning-models community that more thinking tokens always yield better answers, providing both empirical evidence (consistent across three models and three math benchmarks under three budget-control protocols) and a mechanistic explanation rooted in output-distribution entropy. The practical implication is significant: under a fixed inference budget, distributing tokens across parallel reasoning paths with majority voting outperforms sequential extension by up to 22%, offering a simple drop-in improvement. Key limitations include evaluation only on mid-sized distilled models (1.5B–8B) and exclusively mathematical benchmarks, leaving open whether the overthinking threshold and the parallel-thinking advantage persist at larger scale or on open-ended reasoning tasks. A formal theoretical treatment linking entropy growth to reward degradation in autoregressive models remains an important open direction.
