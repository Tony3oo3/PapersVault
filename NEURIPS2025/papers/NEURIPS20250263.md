## Problem

Whether structured internal representations of a partner's task abilities can emerge spontaneously in simple model-free recurrent agents trained only with shared task reward, without any dedicated architectural components or auxiliary objectives for agent modelling. A secondary question is whether such emergence depends on specific environmental pressures—particularly the ego agent's ability to influence partner behaviour through task allocation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250263/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The ego agent receives a local, self-centred grid observation (processed by a CNN into a 128-dim embedding) or, in the blind variant, only egocentric signals (own position, orientation, and held object) with no visual access to the partner. The partner is characterised by a 2D latent vector of task cooldown intervals (or skill probabilities in CoinGame) that is never directly observable. The agent outputs a discrete action (navigate, execute a subtask, or assign the partner's current subtask) and maintains a 128-dim GRU hidden state that, after training, linearly encodes the partner's relative task speeds.

## Method

A GRU recurrent policy (128-dim hidden state, ReLU activations) is trained with PPO in JAX across 256 parallel Overcooked-AI instances for 15M timesteps against a distribution of partners whose per-subtask cooldown intervals are sampled from {1,2,3,4,7,9}. The critical design choice is granting the ego agent an additional action that dictates which subtask the partner performs at each timestep, creating direct pressure to represent partner abilities in order to allocate tasks optimally. Three experiments probe different dimensions: (1) whether task-allocation pressure drives structured partner representations (compared against MLP, single-partner, and non-influential RNN baselines), (2) whether the agent can adapt online to a mid-episode partner switch, and (3) whether blind agents with only egocentric input can still develop partner models. Analysis combines UMAP projections of hidden states, linear probes trained to decode partner task speeds from accumulated hidden states, and throughput/adaptation curves across five kitchen layouts and unseen partner configurations.

## Results

In the primary Overcooked-AI experiment, the multi-partner RNN ego agent outperforms both the feedforward MLP and the single-partner RNN across five layouts, with the highest correlation between partner speed and task allocation (Figure 2C). In the blind-agent experiment (Experiment 3), the RNN trained with diverse partners achieves 9.43 soups/episode, compared to 5.8 for the single-partner RNN and 1.08 for the MLP. Linear probe analysis confirms that hidden states from the multi-partner RNN are significantly more decodable for partner task speeds than those from the non-influential RNN, which performs even worse than the single-partner variant. In the CoinGame replication, the same ordering holds: multi-partner RNN > single-partner RNN > no-influence RNN > MLP.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the multi-partner RNN against three baselines designed to isolate different factors: (1) a feedforward MLP (no memory), (2) a single-partner RNN (no training diversity), and (3) a non-influential RNN (diverse partners but no control over partner task allocation). The multi-partner RNN wins on all three comparisons in both Overcooked-AI and CoinGame. The single-partner RNN outperforms the MLP, confirming that memory is necessary for partner adaptation. Critically, the non-influential RNN (which sees diverse partners but cannot direct their behaviour) shows significantly degraded partner encoding in its hidden states—worse than even the single-partner RNN—demonstrating that the pressure to influence partner task allocation is the key driver of emergent partner modelling. The one exception is the fivebyfive_v1 layout, where the MLP achieves the highest reward, likely because that simpler layout requires less spatial coordination.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in agent modelling (Rabinowitz et al.'s Machine ToM, encoder-decoder architectures, auxiliary reconstruction losses) by showing that structured partner representations can emerge in a plain GRU trained with PPO and task reward alone—no dedicated modelling module, no auxiliary objective, no architectural prior. It connects to the emergent-representations literature in model-free RL (Wijmans et al.'s blind navigation maps, Guez et al.'s planning in RNNs) by extending the 'what does a model-free agent implicitly encode?' question from environment structure to social structure. The ad hoc teamwork (AHT) framing positions the work within the established problem of generalising to unseen teammates, but the paper's contribution is diagnostic: it isolates the environmental condition (ability to influence partner task allocation) under which partner modelling actually emerges.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical implication is that building collaborative AI agents may not require bespoke Theory-of-Mind modules; instead, the right environmental pressures (diverse partners + ability to allocate tasks) can drive partner-aware representations to emerge in standard recurrent architectures. However, the scope is notably limited: two-agent settings, 2D skill profiles, simple grid-world environments, and a very strong form of 'influence' (direct task assignment) that bears little resemblance to how humans actually coordinate. Open questions include whether findings scale to more agents or richer latent trait spaces, whether more nuanced influence mechanisms (communication, persuasion) yield similar or different representations, and whether the results generalise beyond Overcooked-style cooperative games to open-ended or partially conflicting settings.
