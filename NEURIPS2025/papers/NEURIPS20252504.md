## Problem

Most MLLMs adopt a static inference paradigm that encodes the entire image into a fixed set of visual tokens in a single pass, leaving no mechanism to iteratively refine visual understanding, recover from ambiguity, or adapt attention during reasoning. This contrasts with human perception, which is dynamic, selective, and feedback-driven. The paper addresses the absence of a unified framework that gives the model agency to choose what to observe next, how to focus visual attention, and when to stop reasoning.

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image and a text question as input and produces a structured multi-step reasoning trajectory of the form (s₁, t₁, a₁, o₁, …, t_H, a_H, o_H, t_{H+1}), where each step comprises a planning text, a tool action drawn from a 10-tool action space (grounding, depth, zoom-in, OCR, crop, image segmentation, captioning, similarity computing, visual search, overlay), and a resulting visual observation (e.g., a depth map or cropped region). The final planning token t_{H+1} serves as the answer. The number of steps H is not fixed a priori but is determined dynamically by a verifier's termination criterion.

## Method

The paper formulates visual reasoning as a Markov Decision Process with two cooperating components: a reasoner (a VLM augmented with plug-and-play visual tools) that proposes planning text and selects actions, and a verifier trained via multi-step DPO that scores each reasoning step and terminates the loop when the reward difference between consecutive states drops below a threshold ε. The reasoner is fine-tuned with SFT on 315K verified tool-grounded trajectories (VTS-SFT), while the verifier is trained on 301K preference pairs (VTS-DPO) constructed by pairing correct trajectories with deliberately corrupted ones. A key theoretical contribution is proving that the total number of reasoning steps is finite almost surely (Theorem 3.2), via showing the verifier reward forms a submartingale under mild KL-divergence conditions, thereby guaranteeing the algorithm terminates without looping. The framework is plug-and-play: it works with closed-source models like GPT-4o without fine-tuning, and with open-source models (Qwen2-VL, Qwen2.5-VL, LLaMA3.2-Vision) after SFT on the VTS-SFT dataset.

## Results

On the BLINK benchmark (13 subtasks), GPT-4o + VTS-V achieves 69.15% average accuracy, outperforming GPT-4o + MMFactory (68.90%), GPT-4o + CoT (64.96%), GPT-4o + Sketchpad (66.78%), and GPT-4o + SoM (60.13%). The largest gains appear on fine-grained tasks: Counting (+18.33 over base GPT-4o), Functionally-Correlated (+12.91), and Visually-Correlated (+7.56). For open-source models fine-tuned on VTS-SFT, VTS-V improves Qwen2.5-VL-7B from 49.07% to 56.65% (+7.58) and Qwen2-VL-7B from 48.01% to 52.19% (+4.18) on BLINK. On generalization benchmarks, Qwen2.5-VL-7B reaches 75.13 on V*Bench, 57.93 on MMStar, and 66.50 on MathVista after full training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VTS-V outperforms all four baselines on the BLINK average: it beats GPT-4o + MMFactory by +0.25 (69.15 vs. 68.90), GPT-4o + CoT by +4.19 (69.15 vs. 64.96), GPT-4o + Sketchpad by +2.37 (69.15 vs. 66.78), and GPT-4o + SoM by +9.02 (69.15 vs. 60.13). The margin over MMFactory is narrow, suggesting that structured tool-use search engines are competitive, but VTS-V's verifier-guided iterative refinement provides a consistent edge. The largest absolute gains over the base GPT-4o model appear on Counting (+18.33) and Functionally-Correlated (+12.91), tasks requiring precise spatial and structural reasoning where static token encoding is most limiting. Among open-source models, VTS-V without fine-tuning actually degrades performance (e.g., Qwen2-VL-7B drops from 48.01% to 38.54%), but after SFT on VTS-SFT the same models surpass their baselines, confirming that the framework requires task-specific alignment for smaller models. On V*Bench, the fine-tuned Qwen2.5-VL-7B + VTS-V reaches 75.13, exceeding the base model's 73.30, while on MathVista the improvement is more modest (66.50 vs. 67.50), suggesting the method is most effective for perceptual rather than purely mathematical reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the multi-step DPO framework of Xiong et al. (2024) and the foundational DPO formulation of Rafailov et al. (2024) from text-only reasoning to the visual domain, introducing a verifier that operates over multi-step visual reasoning trajectories involving tool invocations. It departs from prior visual tool-use approaches (VisProg, ViperGPT, Visual Sketchpad, MMFactory) by replacing fixed 1–2-step pipelines with an open-ended MDP where the model dynamically selects from a 10-tool action space at each step, guided by a DPO-trained verifier that determines both action quality and termination. The 'visual token scaling' framing positions iterative visual perception as analogous to test-time compute scaling in LLMs, a conceptual bridge not explicitly drawn in prior visual reasoning work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a practical, theoretically grounded recipe for making MLLMs perform iterative, grounded visual reasoning, and the released 315K SFT + 301K DPO dataset is a valuable resource for the community. A practically important finding is that open-source VLMs (Qwen2-VL, LLaMA3.2-Vision) actively degrade in performance when VTS-V is applied without prior SFT on tool-use trajectories, highlighting that tool-interaction behavior is not emergent in current open models. Open questions include whether the verifier generalizes across tasks without retraining, whether the 10-tool action space is sufficient for truly open-ended visual exploration, and whether the MDP formulation offers advantages over simpler iterative prompting strategies at scale.
