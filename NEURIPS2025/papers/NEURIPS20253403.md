## Problem

Reasoning VLMs trained with SFT or RL are highly sensitive to errors in multi-step reasoning: once a mistake occurs, it propagates through subsequent steps and the model cannot recover. The paper shows empirically that existing reasoning VLMs fail at both step-wise and response-wise self-correction (fewer than 10% of error cases trigger any reflection, and external critiques or correction prompts do not improve accuracy), while simultaneously requiring large volumes of annotated data or accurate verifiers to train.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253403/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes an RGB image paired with a text question and produces a structured four-stage reasoning trajectory (SUMMARY, CAPTION, REASONING, CONCLUSION) ending in a final answer. In self-correction mode, the model additionally receives its own prior response and a correction prompt, then generates a revised trajectory. A key design choice is trajectory-level correction: the model is conditioned on the correct prefix of the original response and only revises the erroneous suffix, rather than regenerating the entire answer from scratch.

## Method

Sherlock is a three-stage training framework built on Llama3.2-Vision-11B-Instruct that requires only 20k annotated examples. Stage 1 (SFT cold-start) uses a pairwise objective that jointly trains direct generation and response-wise self-correction. Stage 2 (offline preference training) introduces the core novelty: a trajectory-level self-correction loss (Eq. 6) that extends DPO/SRPO from the full-response level to the suffix level, explicitly encouraging the model to revise only the erroneous portion while preserving correct prefixes. Negative examples are constructed by randomly truncating the reasoning trajectory and injecting visual noise into the image to create controlled quality gaps, and a sample-specific dynamic β (Eq. 8) scales the learning signal inversely with the quality gap to stabilize training. Stage 3 (online self-improvement) iteratively generates preference pairs from the model's own outputs using self-consistency filtering (retaining only responses where three consecutive corrections agree on the final answer), enabling continued improvement without any external supervision.

## Results

Sherlock Iter2, trained on only 20k randomly sampled annotated examples from LLaVA-CoT, achieves an average accuracy of 64.1 across eight multimodal benchmarks (MMBench-V1.1, MMVet, MME, MMStar, MathVista, AI2D, MMMU, HallusionBench) with direct generation, and 65.4 after three rounds of self-correction. This outperforms LLaVA-CoT (100k data, 63.2), Mulberry (260k, 63.9), and LlamaV-o1 (175k, 63.4) while using less than 20% of the annotated data. When combined with MMVerify as a stopping criterion on MathVista, Sherlock achieves 55.9 accuracy with 40% less GPU usage compared to parallel voting (55.1 at 40.2 GPU hours vs. 8.7 GPU hours).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Sherlock Iter2 (20k annotated data) outperforms all three main reasoning baselines on average accuracy: LLaVA-CoT (100k, 63.2), Mulberry (260k, 63.9), and LlamaV-o1 (175k, 63.4), achieving 64.1 direct and 65.4 after self-correction. A critical finding is that existing reasoning models fail to benefit from self-correction: LlamaV-o1 degrades sharply by 15.2 points (from 63.4 to 48.2) because its multi-turn prompting format breaks when a correction prompt is appended, while Mulberry and LLaVA-CoT show negligible or negative gains. In contrast, Sherlock's self-correction consistently improves performance by 1.2–1.3 points across iterations. For inference-time scaling (Table 3), Sherlock's learned self-correction (65.4 avg) outperforms external critique methods using LLaVA-Critic (62.9) and Critic-V (63.2), and matches or exceeds Majority Vote @8 (65.1) while requiring only half the inference time. The RL-based VL-Rethinker, evaluated in the step-wise self-correction analysis (Table 1), exhibits 'aha moments' in fewer than 10% of cases and achieves only 52.2% accuracy on MMStar under the Modify One Step setting, confirming that even RL-trained models lack reliable self-correction.

## Relation to Prior Work

Sherlock extends the preference-optimization lineage of SRPO and ARIES from response-level to a finer-grained trajectory-level objective, where only the erroneous suffix of a reasoning chain is corrected rather than the full response. It builds on LLaVA-CoT's four-stage CoT template and dataset as its training substrate, and incorporates DPO as an auxiliary loss alongside the novel self-correction objective. The key conceptual departure from prior self-correction work (which targets LLMs or uses external critics) is the use of visual perturbation to synthesize preference pairs with controllable quality gaps, combined with a dynamic β that scales learning signals to the magnitude of each gap. The online self-improvement stage draws on iterative self-refinement ideas from ARIES but removes the need for ground-truth labels entirely.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's most impactful contribution is the empirical demonstration that both SFT- and RL-trained reasoning VLMs fundamentally lack self-correction ability, a gap that Sherlock addresses with a principled trajectory-level training objective. The data-efficiency result (20k annotated samples vs. 100k–260k for baselines) and the ability to self-improve without external supervision make the framework attractive for settings where high-quality multimodal annotations are scarce. However, the self-correction gains remain modest (1.2–1.3 points average), the framework is tested only on Llama3.2-Vision-11B, and the three-stage pipeline adds training complexity. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to larger models or different base architectures, whether more online iterations yield diminishing returns, and whether step-wise (intra-trajectory) correction can be integrated for greater efficiency.
