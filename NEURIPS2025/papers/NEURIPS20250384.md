## Problem

Standard clinical ML evaluation metrics—accuracy and AUC-ROC—each capture only a subset of the three priorities essential for deployment: calibration, robustness to label shift, and sensitivity to asymmetric error costs. Reporting them separately (e.g., AUC-ROC alongside ECE) yields incommensurable scores with no principled way to combine them into a single decision-relevant quantity, leaving practitioners without a unified criterion for model selection under real-world prevalence uncertainty and cost asymmetry.

## Input / Output

The method consumes real-valued calibrated model scores s(x) ∈ [0,1], binary labels, a fixed cost parameter c ∈ (0,1) encoding the FP/FN trade-off, and lower/upper prevalence bounds (a, b) specified in log-odds space (i.e., σ⁻¹(π) ~ Uniform(σ⁻¹(a), σ⁻¹(b))). It outputs a single scalar Bounded DCA Log Score in units of true positives, which decomposes additively into a calibration-loss component and a sharpness component by recalibrating the model on the test set. Because the loss is computed pointwise, confidence intervals are obtained by trivial bootstrap resampling of per-example losses.

## Method

Building on the Schervish representation—which shows that any proper scoring rule is an integral over cost-weighted binary losses at varying thresholds—the paper derives a closed-form clipped cross-entropy that averages cost-weighted net benefit over a bounded, user-specified range of class prevalences rather than the full [0,1] interval. The key mechanism is to fix the cost ratio c, apply prior adjustment via odds multiplication (⊗) to correct for label shift, clip the adjusted score to the decision-relevant band, and integrate under a logit-uniform prior on prevalence, yielding the Bounded DCA Log Score. This avoids the double integration over both cost and prevalence proposed by Hand and Anagnostopoulos, and the resulting pointwise loss is computationally simple and semantically interpretable. The score is further decomposed into miscalibration loss (difference between original and recalibrated model) and sharpness (performance of the recalibrated model), resolving the long-standing difficulty of weighing calibration failures against discrimination failures on a common scale.

## Results

On the eICU APACHE IV mortality dataset, the Bounded DCA Log Score resolves ambiguities that arise when combining AUC-ROC and ECE. For the race subgroup comparison, AUC-ROC favors African-American patients (0.907 vs. 0.868) while ECE favors Caucasian patients (0.829 vs. 0.889), leaving the combined judgment undefined; the Bounded DCA Log Score yields a single interpretable ranking favoring Caucasian patients (0.956 vs. 0.900). For the sex comparison, Cross-Entropy/NLL slightly favors males (0.214 vs. 0.218) due to disproportionate weighting of clinically unrealistic high prevalences, whereas the Bounded DCA Log Score corrects this by restricting to plausible prevalence ranges and favors females (0.926 vs. 0.917).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the proposed Bounded DCA Log Score against AUC-ROC [Bradley 1997], ECE [Pakdaman Naeini 2015], Brier score [Brier 1950], and Cross-Entropy/NLL [Gneiting & Raftery 2007] on the eICU APACHE IV dataset. The central finding is not that the Bounded DCA Log Score 'wins' on a single number, but that it resolves a genuine ambiguity: AUC-ROC and ECE point in opposite directions for the race subgroups (AUC-ROC favors African-American, ECE favors Caucasian), making their combination ill-defined, while the Bounded DCA Log Score provides a single unified score. For the sex comparison, Cross-Entropy/NLL (0.214 vs. 0.218) and Brier score (0.062 vs. 0.062) either slightly favor males or tie, because they integrate over the full [0,1] prevalence range and overweight clinically unrealistic high-prevalence regions; the Bounded DCA Log Score (0.926 vs. 0.917) reverses this to favor females by restricting integration to a clinically plausible prevalence band. The Brier score is essentially uninformative here (identical values for both sexes), illustrating its insensitivity to the prevalence range that matters clinically. Overall, the paper demonstrates that no single conventional metric captures all three clinical priorities (calibration, label-shift robustness, cost asymmetry) simultaneously, and the Bounded DCA Log Score is the first to do so in a unified, decomposable form.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes three established threads—Schervish's integral representation of proper scoring rules as mixtures of cost-weighted accuracy, Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) from clinical decision theory, and label-shift correction via Bayes' rule—into a single bounded, cost-sensitive evaluation metric. It departs from AUC-ROC by restricting the implicit mixture over class balances to a clinically specified interval and by incorporating calibration (which AUC-ROC ignores), and from standard cross-entropy by narrowing the integration range from [0,1] to a bounded logit-uniform prior. The Gneiting–Raftery decomposition of log score into calibration and sharpness components is extended to the bounded, cost-weighted setting, enabling a unified metric where AUC-ROC and ECE previously could not be meaningfully combined.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a concrete and persistent gap in clinical ML: no existing metric simultaneously captures calibration, robustness to uncertain label shift, and asymmetric error costs in a single interpretable quantity. The Bounded DCA Log Score is computationally simple (pointwise loss, trivially bootstrappable) and directly actionable for model selection under deployment uncertainty. Key open questions remain: integrating over cost-ratio uncertainty introduces analytically opaque dilogarithmic terms, sampling variability under asymmetric costs with population shift is poorly characterized, and the framework assumes deployment prevalence is known at use time. The empirical validation on eICU is illustrative rather than conclusive, with small subgroups and a single model (APACHE IV), so broader adoption will require validation across more clinical settings.
