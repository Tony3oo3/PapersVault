## Problem

Causal language models and masked diffusion models generate tokens in fixed (left-to-right) or random orders, causing them to fail on adaptive reasoning and planning tasks where the tractable generation order is data-dependent (e.g., Sudoku cells that must be filled in a specific sequence). Existing remedies either require ground-truth order annotations or apply adaptive strategies only at inference, creating a distribution shift between training and inference that degrades performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253558/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt of fixed length N (e.g., a flattened 81-cell Sudoku grid or an arithmetic expression) and produces a text response of fixed length M (the full solution). Response tokens are generated in a learned, data-dependent order rather than left-to-right: at each step the policy selects which unfilled position to fill next, and the token predictor fills it. The prompt is always encoded left-to-right, while response tokens retain their original positional embeddings regardless of generation order, enabling KV-cached autoregressive inference with O(M²) complexity.

## Method

ReCOR frames token-order recovery as a Markov decision process where the state is the prompt plus partially generated response, the action is which position to fill next, and the reward is the log-probability assigned by a jointly trained token predictor. The order policy is trained with discrete soft Q-learning (entropy-regularized RL), while the token predictor is trained online with a permuted language-modeling loss on orders sampled from the current policy, ensuring both components stay on-policy. A key design choice is using the same adaptive order during both training and inference, avoiding the train-test distribution mismatch of inference-only adaptive methods. Architecturally, a multi-stream transformer (inspired by XLNet) separates a main stream for self-attention over the sequence from a token query stream that cross-attends to predict individual tokens at queried positions, with an optional order query stream for more expressive Q-value computation. The entire system is self-supervised: no order annotations are needed, as rewards are generated internally by the token predictor's own confidence estimates.

## Results

ReCOR achieves full-response accuracy of 0.987 on Autoregression (ARG), 0.964 on Multiplication (MUL), 0.9017 on Sudoku, and 0.9905 on Zebra, outperforming all baselines on every task. Notably, ReCOR surpasses the oracle AR-GT (supervised with ground-truth generation orders) on both puzzle datasets: 0.9017 vs. 0.8718 on Sudoku and 0.9905 vs. 0.9117 on Zebra. On ARG, ReCOR improves over the strongest baseline AdaMDM by a factor of ~5.7× (0.987 vs. 0.174), while CLM and vanilla MDM remain near chance level (0.017 and 0.035 respectively).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CLM (Radford et al., 2019), ReCOR achieves dramatic gains on all four tasks, most strikingly on ARG (0.987 vs. 0.017) and Sudoku (0.9017 vs. 0.0973), confirming that fixed left-to-right generation is fundamentally inadequate for adaptive reasoning. Against MDM (Austin et al., 2021), ReCOR wins on every task; the gap is largest on ARG (0.987 vs. 0.035) where random masking during training prevents MDM from ever seeing the required long complete contexts. Against AdaMDM (Kim et al., 2025), the most competitive baseline, ReCOR still leads on all four tasks, with the margin being largest on ARG (0.987 vs. 0.174) and Sudoku (0.9017 vs. 0.8949); the paper attributes AdaMDM's weakness to a train-inference distribution shift caused by random masking at training time. Against the oracle AR-GT (Shah et al., 2024), which is trained and evaluated with ground-truth generation orders, ReCOR is competitive on arithmetic (0.987 vs. 0.994 on ARG, 0.964 vs. 0.999 on MUL) and actually surpasses it on both puzzle datasets (0.9017 vs. 0.8718 on Sudoku, 0.9905 vs. 0.9117 on Zebra), which the authors attribute to ReCOR's denser per-token reward signal at each step.

## Relation to Prior Work

ReCOR sits at the intersection of three research threads: the V-information framework for characterizing computational hardness under model capacity constraints [30], soft Q-learning for discrete-action RL [33], and the multi-stream cross-attention architecture of XLNet [21]. It departs from the dominant paradigm of fixed (CLM) or random (AO-ARM, MDM) generation orders by learning a data-dependent order via RL, and from recent adaptive-inference methods like AdaMDM [12] by applying the learned order during both training and inference rather than only at test time. The key conceptual departure is treating order recovery as a self-supervised decision-making problem where the token predictor's own prediction confidence serves as the reward signal, eliminating the need for ground-truth order annotations that prior oracle baselines require.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a self-supervised method without ground-truth orders can outperform an oracle supervised with them (Sudoku: 0.902 vs 0.872; Zebra: 0.991 vs 0.912) challenges the assumption that explicit order supervision is necessary for adaptive reasoning. The ablation showing that training-time adaptivity is critical (AdaMDM at 0.174 vs ReCOR at 0.987 on ARG) provides a clear diagnosis of why inference-only adaptive decoding fails: random masking during training starves the model of the tractable sub-problems it needs. However, all experiments use 6M–11M parameter GPT-2 variants on synthetic and puzzle datasets; whether the approach scales to billion-parameter LLMs and open-ended reasoning tasks remains the central open question, as does integration with modern architectures beyond the GPT-2 backbone.
