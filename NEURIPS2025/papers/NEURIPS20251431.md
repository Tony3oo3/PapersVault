## Problem

Existing multilingual mathematical reasoning benchmarks (MGSM, XSVAMP) are too simple to discriminate among advanced reasoning LLMs, while challenging datasets remain almost exclusively English. The few multilingual efforts that do exist (e.g., MT-AIME) contain very few samples per language and rely entirely on LLM-generated translations without human validation, raising data-quality concerns. PolyMath addresses this gap by providing a high-quality, difficulty-stratified multilingual benchmark that can meaningfully separate reasoning capabilities across 18 languages.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251431/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each input is a mathematical problem rendered in one of 18 languages (covering ~75% of the world's native speakers), with a language-specific instruction prompt appended (e.g., 'Please put the final answer in $\boxed{}$'). Problems span four difficulty levels (Low, Medium, High, Top) with 125 problems per level per language, totaling 9,000 samples. Answers take diverse forms—numeric, expression, equation, interval, set, or tuple—and are verified by a rule-based matching script achieving >98% precision. The benchmark produces a Difficulty-Weighted Accuracy (DW-ACC) score that exponentially weights levels (w = 1, 2, 4, 8), along with per-language accuracy, input-output language consistency (LC), and thinking-length statistics.

## Method

The benchmark is constructed around two macro difficulty dimensions—Thought Depth and Knowledge Breadth—rather than conventional learning-stage or problem-source labels, yielding four levels from K-12 word problems through IMO/Putnam Olympiad problems to frontier mathematics (HLE). Data collection sources range from MGSM (low) through Gaokao/AMC/CNMO (medium), AIME/CNMO (high), to IMO Shortlist/Putnam/HLE (top), with all difficulty labels assigned by doctoral students with competition experience and reviewed by two additional experts. Translations follow a three-stage pipeline: GPT-4o pre-translation, expert extraction of ~30 key mathematical terms per problem, and calibration by language experts (linguistics graduate students or native speakers) who verify term accuracy and formula migration; this process revealed 4–20% content disagreement rates in GPT-4o's raw translations, justifying human calibration. Evaluation uses 16 sampling runs (T=0.6, p=0.95, k=20) for reasoning models and greedy decoding for non-reasoning models, with a 65,536-token output limit. Beyond standard accuracy, the paper introduces the DW-ACC metric, analyzes input-output language consistency via langdetect, tests three language-control prompt strategies (query-language, English, preferred-language), and measures thinking-length variation across languages.

## Results

On PolyMath, the best-performing reasoning LLM, Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking, achieves a Difficulty-Weighted Accuracy (DW-ACC) of 54.6, while Gemini-2.5-pro follows at 52.2. At the highest (Top) difficulty level, even these top models reach only ~40% average accuracy across 18 languages, whereas non-reasoning models drop to 5–15%. The benchmark reveals significant cross-lingual gaps: at higher levels, the range between best and worst languages reaches 10–20 percentage points for several reasoning models, and input-output language consistency in reasoning LLMs is often below 50% in the thinking phase.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PolyMath is a benchmark rather than a method, so the comparisons are among 16 evaluated LLMs (8 non-reasoning, 8 reasoning). Reasoning LLMs substantially outperform non-reasoning LLMs at higher difficulty: at the Top level, Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking (40.3%) and Gemini-2.5-pro (38.0%) achieve roughly 3× the accuracy of the best non-reasoning model (GPT-4.5-preview, 14.7%). Among reasoning models, Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking leads at every level, with its advantage widening at higher difficulty (e.g., 63.3% vs. 53.9% for Qwen-QwQ-32B at High level). Non-reasoning models exhibit sharp geometric drops with difficulty (e.g., ChatGPT-4o-latest: 91.6→40.8→20.4→13.7 from Low to Top), while reasoning models degrade more gradually (Gemini-2.5-pro: 72.0→62.2 from Medium to High). The paper also shows that forcing reasoning LLMs to output in English improves DW-ACC (e.g., Qwen-QwQ-32B: 45.9→47.9) and reduces cross-lingual variance, whereas forcing the query language worsens performance for low-resource languages (Swahili: 40.0→28.0 for Qwen-QwQ-32B).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the multilingual mathematical reasoning benchmark line (MGSM, XSVAMP, MT-AIME) by introducing a four-level difficulty hierarchy spanning K-12 to frontier mathematics, replacing the single-difficulty design of prior work. It departs from MT-AIME's fully LLM-generated translations by employing a three-stage human-calibrated annotation pipeline, and from English-only challenging benchmarks (OlympiadBench, FrontierMath, HLE) by providing 18 parallel language versions. The Difficulty-Weighted Accuracy (DW-ACC) metric is a novel scoring mechanism that accounts for the non-uniform effort across difficulty levels, and the language-control experiments (forcing English vs. query-language output) represent a new empirical axis not explored in prior multilingual math evaluations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PolyMath fills a critical gap: existing multilingual math benchmarks are too simple to differentiate advanced reasoning LLMs, while the hardest benchmarks are English-only. The finding that even Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking and Gemini-2.5-pro achieve only ~54% overall and ~40% at the top level, combined with up to 20-point cross-language gaps, establishes that multilingual reasoning remains a major unsolved challenge. The practical insight that forcing English output improves low-resource language performance (e.g., Arabic +8.4, Japanese +8.2 for Qwen-QwQ-32B) offers an immediate mitigation strategy. Open questions include whether the 500-problem scale is sufficient for stable rankings, whether the DW-ACC weighting (powers of 2) is optimal, and whether the language-gap findings generalize beyond mathematical reasoning to other domains.
