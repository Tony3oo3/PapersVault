## Problem

Energy has become a critical bottleneck for generative AI services, yet it remains poorly measured and understood in the ML systems community. Existing approaches either rely on TDP-based estimation (overestimating by up to 4.1×), require physical hardware access (MLPerf Power), or fix batch size to 1 (Hugging Face AI Energy Score), none of which reflect how generative AI services are actually deployed. There is no open, extensible benchmark that measures inference energy under realistic serving conditions and translates measurements into actionable optimization recommendations.

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes a generative AI model (LLM, VLM, or diffusion model), a task-specific request dataset (e.g., ShareGPT for chat, PartiPrompts for text-to-image), and a configuration sweep space (GPU model, max batch size, tensor/pipeline parallelism, denoising steps). It produces per-request energy in Joules computed via service-aware accounting—simple batch division for diffusion models, but steady-state per-token energy multiplied by per-request output token counts for LLMs with iteration-level batching. Given a user-specified latency constraint (e.g., 100 ms TPOT for chat), it outputs the energy-optimal configuration from the time-energy Pareto frontier along with the expected energy savings percentage.

## Method

The ML.ENERGY Benchmark is built on four design principles: software-based GPU energy measurement via the Zeus library for portability across systems, production-grade runtimes (vLLM, Diffusers) on real hardware (A100/H100) with realistic workloads, per-request energy granularity that captures model-specific verbosity and task-specific output length distributions, and actionable optimization output. The key technical mechanism is service-aware energy accounting: for LLMs with iteration-level batching, the benchmark identifies the steady state (when batch size is saturated at the server's maximum), computes average energy per token during that window, and multiplies by each request's output token count to derive per-request energy. For diffusion models, energy is simply divided by batch size. The optimization component sweeps the configuration space, constructs a time-energy Pareto frontier across all measured operating points, and selects the minimum-energy configuration that satisfies the user's latency constraint, yielding savings of up to 44% for LLMs and 21% for diffusion models without changing model outputs.

## Results

The benchmark measures per-request energy consumption across 40 generative AI model architectures on 6 tasks (LLM chat, code, visual chat, text-to-image, text-to-video, image-to-video) using NVIDIA A100 and H100 GPUs. TDP-based energy estimation (a common practice) overestimates actual consumption by up to 4.1× (CodeGemma 2B on H100). Automated optimization recommendations yield up to 44% energy savings for LLM chat (Llama 3.1 8B, targeting 100 ms TPOT) and 21% for diffusion image generation (SD 2.1, targeting 5 s latency) without changing model outputs. Reasoning models (e.g., Qwen 3 235B-A22B thinking) consume 33,096–122,523 J per generation on GPQA, one to two orders of magnitude more than standard chat models due to vastly longer outputs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a benchmark paper, ML.ENERGY does not propose a new method to compare against competing algorithms; instead, it benchmarks existing models and configurations against each other. The key comparison is measured GPU energy versus TDP-based estimation: real measurements are consistently lower, with worst-case overestimation of 4.1×, demonstrating that the common TDP shortcut is unreliable for energy planning. Architecturally, Phi-3 Small (7B, GQA) can consume less energy than Phi-3 Mini (3.8B, MHA) at larger batch sizes because Mini's 3× larger KV cache prevents scaling to higher batch sizes. Hardware-wise, Llama 3.1 8B's Pareto frontier mixes A100 and H100 configurations (memory-bound decoding makes them comparable), while Stable Diffusion 2.1's frontier is dominated by H100 (compute-bound, so H100's higher TDP is offset by proportionally greater speedup). The paper also positions itself against prior benchmarks (LLM-Perf, MLPerf Power, AI Energy Score, Google's internal disclosure), noting that it is the first to combine per-request granularity, realistic batching, software-based portability, and automated optimization in a single open-source suite.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Zeus for software-based GPU energy measurement and extends the ML energy measurement line (MLPerf Power, Hugging Face LLM-Perf, AI Energy Score) to the specific domain of modern generative AI inference under realistic serving conditions. It departs from prior work in three key ways: (1) using software-based measurement for portability rather than requiring physical power analyzers, (2) reporting energy at per-request granularity with steady-state accounting that captures iteration-level batching, and (3) coupling measurement with automated Pareto-frontier-based optimization recommendations. The paper positions its contribution as complementary to existing energy optimization systems (μ-Serve, DynamoLLM, EnvPipe) by providing the measurement foundation and cross-layer configuration recommendations they operate on.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As energy becomes the dominant bottleneck for scaling generative AI services, this benchmark provides the first open-source, portable, and extensible tool for measuring and optimizing inference energy across 40 models and 6 tasks, with the practical finding that automated configuration optimization can yield 40%+ energy savings without altering model computation. The demonstration that TDP-based estimation overestimates energy by up to 4.1× and that architectural choices (MHA vs. GQA) can invert the expected parameter-count-to-energy relationship challenges common heuristics in the field. Key limitations include coverage restricted primarily to NVIDIA GPUs, a single benchmark run without error bars due to cost, and the inherent challenge of keeping pace with rapid model releases. Open questions include extending to non-NVIDIA hardware, incorporating dynamic workload patterns, and integrating with carbon-aware scheduling.
