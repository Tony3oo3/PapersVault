## Problem

Machine learning for oligonucleotide therapeutics (ASOs, siRNAs, shRNAs) lags far behind small molecule and protein drug discovery due to the absence of standardized, ML-ready datasets and evaluation benchmarks. Existing oligonucleotide databases are limited to single modalities or endpoints, use inconsistent molecular representations, and lack rigorous benchmarking protocols, making it difficult to develop or compare predictive models.

## Input / Output

Input consists of oligonucleotide sequences with chemical modification patterns (sugar, phosphate, nucleobase modifications) represented in HELM notation, with SMILES and natural base sequences also provided. The HELM string is parsed into an intermediate XNA format, then featurized either as k-mer frequency vectors (optionally augmented with modified monomer counts) or as one-hot encoded matrices of shape L×(4+S+P) capturing base, sugar, and phosphate identities at each position. For double-stranded siRNAs, both guide and passenger strands are encoded separately and concatenated. Output is a continuous regression value representing a therapeutic endpoint: percentage target knockdown (efficacy), caspase/calcium oscillation scores (cytotoxicity/neurotoxicity), or NF-κB luciferase reporter levels (immunomodulation).

## Method

OligoGym curates 12 regression datasets spanning ASO, siRNA, and shRNA modalities and endpoints (efficacy, cytotoxicity, neurotoxicity, immunomodulation), all unified under HELM notation with comprehensive metadata. Two modular featurizers are developed: a k-mer frequency encoder and a one-hot encoder that extends standard nucleobase encoding to include sugar and phosphate monomer identities, enabling explicit representation of chemical modifications. Eight model architectures (Linear, KNN, Random Forest, XGBoost, MLP, CNN, GRU, Transformer) plus a Graph Convolutional Network are benchmarked across a grid of hyperparameters under two splitting strategies: standard 5-fold random cross-validation and a nucleobase-based k-means clustering split that simulates distributional shift. Key findings include Random Forest as the top performer on 8/12 datasets under random splits, the critical importance of encoding chemical modifications for modification-sensitive endpoints, and the emergence of deep learning models as top performers under the more challenging nucleobase split, suggesting greater generalization capacity across sequence distribution shifts.

## Results

Across 12 oligonucleotide datasets, Random Forest emerges as the top-performing model for 8 of 12 datasets under random 5-fold cross-validation, with PCC ranging from 0.35 (OpenASO) to 0.92 (Sherwood). CNNs achieve the best PCC on 3 datasets (Neurotox MOE: 0.75, Ichihara: 0.56, Huesken: 0.65), while XGBoost leads on Sherwood (0.96). Under the more challenging nucleobase-based splitting strategy, deep learning models (CNN, MLP, Transformer) become top performers on 6 of 12 datasets, suggesting greater generalization capacity across distributional shifts. Overall PCC values are modest, with the hardest datasets (Shmushkovich, OpenASO, Ichihara) yielding best-model PCCs below 0.55.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Random Forest (Breiman 2001) is the strongest classical model, achieving the best or tied-best PCC on 8 of 12 datasets under random CV, with particularly strong results on Cytotox LNA (0.91) and Sherwood (0.92). XGBoost (Chen & Guestrin 2016) matches RF on ASOptimizer and Cytotox LNA and uniquely leads on Sherwood (0.96 vs. RF's 0.92), but underperforms RF on most other datasets. CNN (LeCun 1998) wins on 3 datasets (Neurotox MOE 0.75, Ichihara 0.56, Huesken 0.65) but collapses on TLR8 (0.19) and Shmushkovich (0.27), showing high dataset sensitivity. GRU (Cho 2014) is consistently the weakest deep learning model, with PCC below 0.5 on 9 of 12 datasets and the lowest overall score (0.05 on Shmushkovich). Transformer (Vaswani 2017) underperforms both CNN and classical ML across the board, likely due to the short sequence lengths (<30 nt) and small sample sizes. Under nucleobase splits, the ranking shifts: CNN, MLP, and Transformer collectively become top performers on 6 of 12 datasets, indicating that sequence-based deep models generalize better across sequence-pattern distribution shifts, while RF retains the lead on 5 datasets and XGB on Sherwood (0.90).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by creating the first standardized, ML-ready benchmark collection for oligonucleotide therapeutics, drawing conceptual inspiration from MoleculeNet, TDC, ProteinGym, and RNAGym/BEACON, none of which cover ONTs. It repurposes existing scattered databases (siRNAmod, AOBase, various patent-derived datasets) and unifies them under HELM notation with consistent metadata, addressing the fragmentation that has hindered ML adoption in this modality. The featurization (k-mers, one-hot encoding) and model architectures (RF, XGBoost, CNN, GRU, Transformer, GNN) are all well-established techniques from small-molecule and protein ML, applied here to a domain where no prior systematic benchmarking existed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

OligoGym fills a critical infrastructure gap: without standardized datasets and evaluation protocols, the ONT field has lagged behind small molecules and proteins in ML-driven discovery. The finding that classical tabular models (especially Random Forest) match or exceed sequence-based deep learning architectures on most datasets, and that positional context appears less important than compositional features, provides useful guidance for practitioners working in this low-data regime. Key open questions include whether larger datasets with technical replicates would shift the balance toward deep learning, whether thermodynamic/structural features or domain-specific inductive biases could close the remaining performance gaps, and how to leverage foundation models given the synthetic and scarce nature of ONT sequences.
