## Problem

Current LLMs are predominantly trained and evaluated on single-turn problem-solving, leaving their collaborative reasoning abilities—effective disagreement, persuasion, and assertiveness in multi-turn conversations—largely unmeasured and unimproved. The paper identifies that frontier models exhibit pathological social behaviors in collaborative settings, including >90% agreement rates regardless of correctness and assertiveness rates as low as 0.2–5.5%, causing them to collapse even on problems they can solve independently. No existing framework systematically evaluates or trains these multi-turn collaborative skills.

## Input / Output

Each collaborative session takes a reasoning problem (from MATH, MMLU-Pro, GPQA, ExploreToM, Hi-ToM, or MBPP-CR) as the conversation starter, interleaved with a system prompt per agent, and proceeds for up to 20 turns (10 rounds) until mutual agreement is reached. The primary evaluation metric is agreement correctness—whether both agents converge on the gold answer—supplemented by turn-level social metrics (persuasiveness and assertiveness) computed via LLM-as-judge belief extraction after each turn. The training pipeline outputs a DPO-finetuned model that, at inference time, produces the next conversational turn given the conversation prefix and its system prompt.

## Method

The self-improvement pipeline has three stages: (1) tree sampling, where at each conversational turn the model generates d=5 candidate responses and 5 independent conversation trees are sampled per problem to diversify collaborative behaviors; (2) belief filtering, where an LLM-as-judge extractor determines each candidate turn's current belief about the final answer, and turns are labeled positive or negative based on whether the belief matches the gold answer, with per-problem and per-turn caps to balance difficulty; (3) DPO preference finetuning on the resulting correct-vs-incorrect turn pairs, which directly optimizes the model to produce assertive, persuasive responses without a separate reward model. The authors also build Matrix, a Slurm+Ray-based serving framework supporting gRPC, auto-scaling, and multi-model deployment, to generate thousands of parallel conversations across hundreds of GPU instances. Experiments on Llama-3.1, Qwen-2.5, and Ministral show consistent gains of up to 16.7% absolute over CoT+DPO baselines, with trained 70B models rivaling O1 and Gemini-Pro on MMLU-Pro and ExploreToM.

## Results

On the collaborative reasoning (Coral) evaluation, current frontier LLMs are inconsistent: Llama-3.1-8B drops 18.4% on ExploreToM and 4.2% on MATH relative to its CoT score, while O1 drops 4.9% on MATH. After self-improvement via synthetic conversation DPO, Llama-3.1-8B gains 11.2%–29.5% absolute and Llama-3.1-70B gains 11.0%–19.0% absolute on MBPP-CR, MMLU-Pro, and ExploreToM. Coral + DPO outperforms the single-agent CoT + DPO baseline by up to 14.7% (8B) and 16.7% (70B) absolute, and the trained 70B model rivals O1 and Gemini-Pro on MMLU-Pro and ExploreToM.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GPT-4o, the untrained Llama-3.1-70B Coral score (65.8 on MMLU-Pro) lags behind GPT-4o's Coral score (69.5), but after Coral DPO training the 70B model reaches 75.2, surpassing GPT-4o by 5.7 points. Compared to O1, the trained Llama-3.1-70B narrows the gap substantially on MMLU-Pro (75.2 vs. O1's 82.8) and ExploreToM (95.0 vs. 86.8, where it actually exceeds O1). Gemini-1.5-Pro consistently underperforms its own CoT in the Coral setting (e.g., 48.2 vs. 54.2 on GPQA), while Gemini-2.5-Flash shows the opposite pattern (69.4 vs. 46.4 on GPQA), highlighting that collaboration gains are model-dependent. Claude-3.7-Sonnet benefits from collaboration on MATH (79.1 vs. 74.4 CoT) and GPQA (65.6 vs. 59.8) but slightly regresses on ExploreToM (84.3 vs. 86.3). The key finding is that Coral + DPO consistently outperforms CoT + DPO by 1.0%–16.8% across all tested base models (Llama-3.1, Qwen-2.5, Ministral) and all six datasets, demonstrating that multi-turn collaborative training yields gains that single-agent preference tuning cannot match.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on DPO (Rafailov et al., 2024) for preference optimization but repurposes it for a fundamentally different setting: multi-turn collaborative conversations rather than single-turn response ranking. It departs from structured multi-agent frameworks (AutoGen, Agentverse, MALT) that assign fixed roles and use curated prompts, instead evaluating generalist agents in free-form dialogue where social skills like assertiveness and persuasiveness are necessary. The self-play data generation pipeline with tree sampling and belief-based filtering extends the self-refinement literature (Self-Refine, Reflexion, STaR) from single-agent iterative improvement to two-agent interactive collaboration, while the social metrics (persuasiveness, assertiveness) draw on social science but are novel in their LLM evaluation application.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals a fundamental and somewhat uncomfortable limitation of current frontier LLMs: RLHF-induced agreeableness makes them poor collaborators, with >90% agreement rates that mask incorrect answers, causing them to underperform their own single-agent CoT baselines. The self-improvement method is practical and scalable (up to 16.7% absolute gains), and the Matrix infrastructure addresses a real bottleneck for multi-agent research. Open questions include whether the verbosity trade-off can be resolved, whether out-of-domain generalization holds beyond in-domain transfer, and how these findings extend to human-AI collaboration settings where the social dynamics are more complex.
