## Problem

Given that we know the effect of interventions on various variables in a system, how do we attribute the total causal power to the different variables, distinguishing between unique, redundant, and synergistic contributions? While Partial Information Decomposition (PID) decomposes observational mutual information into these three components, and recent work decomposed an observational measure labeled 'causal,' the author argues that a proper causal decomposition must be interventional in nature, not derivable from the joint distribution alone. The paper fills this gap by providing a principled, nonnegative decomposition of interventional causal effects over the full redundancy lattice of antichains.

## Input / Output

The method requires a set of variables X_S, an outcome variable Y, a causal DAG (for identifiability of interventional probabilities via do-calculus), and the interventional probabilities P(Y|do(X_S=x)) for all configurations. It produces a partial causal effect C(α;Y) for every antichain α in the redundancy lattice, which can be aggregated into total synergistic, redundant, and unique causal power. Because the decomposition is over antichains rather than individual variables, the number of terms grows as the Dedekind number (superexponentially), limiting practical application to roughly five variables, though the fast Möbius transform can handle larger systems computationally.

## Method

The core contribution defines the Maximal Average Causal Effect (MACE) as the interventional quantity to decompose, extends it to antichains via MACE_∩ (the minimum MACE over antichain elements, capturing shared causal power), and applies Möbius inversion on the antichain poset ordered by redundancy (α ≤ β iff every element of β contains some element of α). The Möbius inversion theorem then recovers the partial causal effect of each antichain from the redundant MACE values, with a closed-form expression for the Möbius function of the redundancy lattice enabling efficient computation. Monotonicity of MACE_∩ on the antichain poset guarantees nonnegativity of all partial causal effects, ensuring that partial sums provide valid lower bounds on aggregated synergy. The framework is demonstrated on logic gates, cellular automata, chemical reaction networks, and a transformer language model, revealing how the distribution of causal power is context- and parameter-dependent.

## Results

The paper validates the interventional causal decomposition across four domains. In logic gates, the OR gate's causal power shifts from fully redundant (at low input probability p) to fully synergistic (at high p), while XOR at p=1/2 requires purely synergistic control. In cellular automata, the decomposition is strongly context-dependent: Rule 90 (B_{t+1} = A_t XOR C_t) exhibits purely synergistic causal power under random initialisation but mixed synergy and redundancy under a middle-1 initialisation. In the chemical network, varying the combinatorial rate k_3 shifts causal power from unique to X_1 (at low k_3, where k_1 > k_2 dominates) to synergistic control of both X_1 and X_2 (at high k_3). In the transformer sentiment model, the decomposition correctly identifies 'horribly bad' as redundant, 'not bad' as synergistic (semantic negation), and 'really bad' as unique to the word 'bad'.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not perform standard quantitative baseline comparisons, as it introduces a new decomposition framework rather than a predictive model. The closest comparison is with Martínez-Sánchez et al. [19], who decompose an observational (mutual-information-based) measure they call 'causal.' The author argues that Martínez-Sánchez et al.'s approach cannot yield true causal insight because it is derived purely from the joint distribution, and that it conflates multiple sources of redundancy by only considering redundancies among individual variables (e.g., it omits antichains like {{X_1,X_2},{X_3}}). The paper also contrasts its method with Shapley values [24,22], noting that Shapley values are closely related but fail to disentangle unique, redundant, and synergistic contributions. The PID framework [25] serves as the structural template but operates on observational information rather than interventional quantities. No head-to-head numerical benchmark against these prior methods is provided; the validation is instead through qualitative correctness of the decompositions across diverse systems.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper transplants the antichain decomposition and Möbius inversion machinery of Partial Information Decomposition (Williams & Beer, 2010) from observational mutual information to an interventional causal quantity (the Maximal Average Causal Effect), using the fast Möbius transform (Jansma et al., 2024) as its computational backbone. It explicitly departs from Martínez-Sánchez et al. (2024), who decomposed a mutual-information-based measure they labeled 'causal,' arguing that a genuine causal decomposition must be defined via the do-operator and cannot be recovered from the joint distribution alone. The framework also subsumes Shapley-value-based attribution, which the paper notes is closely related but fails to separate unique, redundant, and synergistic contributions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The decomposition offers a principled, nonnegative attribution of causal power that is directly actionable for intervention design in complex systems (gene regulation, climate feedback, AI interpretability), and the demonstrated context- and parameter-dependence shows that causal structure is not static but shifts with the operating regime. Key open questions include: how to define redundancy measures beyond the simple minimum that capture richer interaction structure, how to scale past the ~5-variable Dedekind limit in practice, and how to handle settings where causal effects are not identifiable without strong structural assumptions. The language-model application is a proof-of-concept that the formalism can capture semantic phenomena (negation as synergy), but the computational cost of estimating MACEs in high-dimensional settings remains a significant barrier to broader adoption.
