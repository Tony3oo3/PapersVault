## Problem

There is an unresolved debate over whether explicit group-equivariance in neural networks provides tangible benefits or over-constrains models, with conflicting empirical evidence across domains. No prior work has conducted a controlled, unified comparison of different equivariance levels (SE(3), T(3), none), symmetry-breaking mechanisms, and equivariance-breaking strategies within a single architecture across multiple geometric tasks, leaving unclear under what conditions each design choice is most effective.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254050/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The architecture consumes 3D point clouds with task-specific features (atom types, surface normals, velocity vectors, spatial coordinates) and optionally a global pose frame, operating on the position-orientation space R³×S² where each point carries a scalar signal over a discretized spherical fiber. A key design detail is that geometric inputs can be specified in multiple ways that differentially affect equivariance: coordinates as fixed scalars break SE(3), coordinates as transforming vectors preserve SE(3) but break T(3), and normals/velocities can be treated as scalars or vectors. Outputs range from scalar molecular properties and per-point segmentation labels to generated 3D molecules, generated point clouds, and velocity vectors for motion prediction.

## Method

Rapidash is a unified regular group convolutional architecture that processes scalar feature fields over the position-orientation space R³×S² (equivalent to SE(3)/SO(2)), using separable kernels factorized into spatial, orientation, and channel components inspired by ConvNeXt. Unlike steerable tensor field networks, the regular group convolution formulation allows standard pointwise nonlinearities (GELU, ReLU) without breaking equivariance, while a Fourier-domain equivalence to steerable networks guarantees universal approximation. The architecture supports fine-grained control over equivariance constraints (SE(3), T(3), or none), base space (R³ vs. R³×S²), input representation (scalars vs. vectors for coordinates, normals, velocities), and symmetry breaking via an explicit global pose frame. Theoretically, the paper proves that pose-conditioned models gain expressivity over standard invariant models (Corollary 3.1) by reducing effective pose entropy from maximum to zero, enabling disambiguation of symmetric inputs. Empirically, Rapidash with equivariance-breaking and symmetry-breaking achieves state-of-the-art results on QM9 molecule generation and CMU motion prediction, while systematic ablations across 19+ model variants reveal that well-aligned inductive bias matters more than raw capacity for closing generalization gaps.

## Results

On QM9 property prediction, the best SE(3)-equivariant Rapidash (R³×S²) achieves MAE μ = 10.39, substantially outperforming EGNN (29.0), DimeNet++ (29.7), and SE(3)-Transformer (53.0). For QM9 molecule generation, Rapidash reaches 93.12% molecule stability and 91.54% discovery, surpassing EGNN-EDM (90.7% / 89.5%) and MuDiff (89.9%). On CMU motion prediction, the best Rapidash variant achieves MSE = 4.69×10⁻², beating CSMPN (7.55), CGENN (9.41), EGNN (31.70), and TFN (66.90). On ShapeNet part segmentation under random rotation (out-of-distribution), Rapidash maintains 85.74% mIoU while non-equivariant baselines drop to 32–62%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On QM9 property prediction, Rapidash (MAE μ = 10.39) decisively outperforms EGNN (29.0), DimeNet++ (29.7), and SE(3)-Transformer (53.0) by margins of 18.6, 19.3, and 42.6 respectively. For QM9 generation, Rapidash (93.12% mol. stability, 91.54% discovery) surpasses EGNN-EDM (90.7% / 89.5%), MuDiff (89.9% stability), and PΘNITA (87.8% stability), establishing new SOTA. On CMU motion prediction, Rapidash (MSE 4.69) beats CSMPN (7.55), CGENN (9.41), EGNN (31.70), and TFN (66.90) by wide margins. On ShapeNet generation, Rapidash (1-NNA 62.01) outperforms LION (51.85) and PVD (58.65) but is marginally below DPM (62.30). On ShapeNet aligned segmentation, Rapidash (85.76) trails GeomGCNN (89.10), PointNeXt (87.00), and DeltaConv (86.90), though these baselines do not report rotated-test results where Rapidash (85.74) dominates all generation baselines (51.85–62.30).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the PONITA regular group convolution framework (Bekkers et al., 2024) operating on position-orientation space R³×S², adapting ConvNext-style separable blocks as its core layer. It departs from prior equivariant network papers by introducing Rapidash as a unified architecture with fine-grained, switchable controls over equivariance constraints (SE(3) vs. T(3)), input representations (scalars vs. vectors), and symmetry-breaking mechanisms, enabling controlled ablations that prior work comparing disparate architectures could not. The theoretical analysis draws on the SymPE probabilistic symmetry-breaking framework (Lawrence et al., 2025b) and formalizes the expressivity gap between maximum-entropy and zero-entropy pose conditioning, while the Fourier-domain equivalence between regular and steerable convolutions connects to the broader tensor field network literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the most systematic empirical evidence to date on when equivariance actually helps versus when it is over-constraining, offering practitioners task-specific guidance (e.g., SE(3)×S² models for molecular and motion tasks, T(3) or broken-equivariance variants for aligned segmentation). The finding that capacity scaling does not close the performance gap between constrained and unconstrained models challenges the 'just scale up' narrative and suggests architectural inductive bias remains a first-order design choice. Limitations include a modest computational budget (9-hour runs, up to 1000 epochs) that constrains exhaustive scaling-law exploration, and the scope is limited to convolutional architectures on point clouds, leaving open whether conclusions transfer to transformers or other modalities.
