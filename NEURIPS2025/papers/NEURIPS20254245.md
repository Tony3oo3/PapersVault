## Problem

Molecular discovery inherently requires predicting properties for molecules whose values extrapolate beyond the training distribution, yet no systematic benchmark exists to evaluate out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization for molecular property prediction models. Without standardized OOD splits and metrics, current models are optimized for in-distribution performance on insufficiently challenging datasets, leaving their real-world extrapolation capability unmeasured.

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes molecules in four representation formats—SMILES strings, molecular graphs (atom/bond features), 3D atomic coordinates, and RDKit molecular descriptors—and predicts one of 10 quantum chemical properties (e.g., HOMO/LUMO energy, heat of formation, polarizability, dipole moment). The key design choice is the OOD split: a kernel density estimator fitted over the property distribution identifies the lowest-probability tail samples as the OOD test set, ensuring the test molecules have property values the model has never seen in training. Performance is reported as RMSE plus a novel binned R² metric that averages R² over the lower and upper tails of the OOD prediction range, avoiding the inflated total sum of squares that makes standard R² misleading for OOD samples.

## Method

BOOM defines OOD as a complement distribution over the property (y) space using KDE, holding out the lowest-density property values as the test set across 10 datasets derived from QM9 and a 10K Cambridge Structural Database subset. The benchmark evaluates 15 model architectures spanning traditional ML (Random Forest, MLP), SMILES-based transformers (ChemBERTa, MoLFormer, Regression Transformer, ModernBERT), 2D/3D GNNs (Chemprop, EGNN, IGNN, MACE, GotenNet), and hybrid GNN-Transformer models (Graphormer-3D, GeoFormer, ET), all trained and evaluated under identical splits. Extensive ablations isolate the impact of pretraining strategy (MLM/PLM vs. supervised property pretraining), hyperparameter optimization, data augmentation with OOD molecules, and molecular representation choice. The central finding is that no model achieves strong OOD generalization across all tasks—the best model still shows 3× higher OOD error than ID—and that standard language-modeling pretraining actually degrades OOD performance, while high-inductive-bias 3D equivariant models and correlated supervised pretraining offer the most promising paths forward.

## Results

Across 15 models and 10 OOD property prediction tasks, no model achieves strong OOD generalization: even the top-performing model (GotenNet) exhibits an average OOD RMSE approximately 3× higher than its in-distribution RMSE. GotenNet achieves the best OOD performance on 7 out of 10 tasks, while MACE leads on 2 tasks and GeoFormer achieves the best ID performance on 3 tasks. 3D equivariant/invariant models (GotenNet, MACE, EGNN) significantly outperform SMILES-based transformers on OOD splits (Kruskal-Wallis p=1.85e-9 for 3D vs. transformers), and ModernBERT reduces OOD HoF and C_v RMSE by >58% and >78% respectively over other transformer baselines. Pretraining via masked or permutation language modeling improves ID RMSE by 12–35% but yields no significant OOD improvement, with binned OOD R² actually decreasing by 39–53% for ChemBERTa and MoLFormer.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GotenNet (3D equivariant GNN, 6M params) dominates OOD performance, achieving top OOD RMSE on 7/10 tasks and substantially outperforming all SMILES-based transformers; its OOD RMSE is roughly 2× lower than the best graph-based model on most properties. MACE (3.9M params) achieves top OOD on 2/10 tasks, while GeoFormer (47.1M params, hybrid) leads ID performance on 3/10 tasks but does not consistently transfer to OOD. Among SMILES-based transformers, ModernBERT (111M params) is the clear winner, reducing OOD HoF and C_v RMSE by >58% and >78% over ChemBERTa, MoLFormer, and Regression Transformer, bridging much of the gap to GNN-based models. Regression Transformer suffers from autoregressive numerical tokenization errors (e.g., predicting '00913' for '0.913'), producing the worst OOD RMSE among all models. ChemBERTa and MoLFormer show that MLM pretraining improves ID RMSE by 31% and 35% respectively but degrades OOD binned R² by 39% and 53%, indicating that current pretraining objectives do not confer OOD extrapolation ability. The Llama 3 LLM (Appendix E) performs poorly on OOD tasks, confirming that general-purpose LLMs lack the inductive bias needed for molecular property extrapolation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the molecular property prediction benchmarking tradition established by MoleculeNet (Wu et al., 2018) by introducing a property-space OOD evaluation protocol, departing from prior OOD work in materials (MatBench, Omee et al. 2024) that defines OOD in the input/structure space. It also builds on the chemical foundation model line (ChemBERTa, MoLFormer, Regression Transformer) by being the first to systematically ablate their pretraining strategies for OOD generalization rather than in-distribution accuracy. Unlike Mole-OOD (Yang et al., 2022b) which focuses on graph structural invariance, or DrugOOD (Ji et al., 2023) which uses structure-based OOD definitions for drug-like molecules, BOOM defines OOD via kernel density estimation over the property distribution and is representation-agnostic, covering SMILES, graph, and 3D inputs across 15 model architectures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that no model achieves strong OOD generalization across all tasks, and that standard pretraining (MLM/PLM) actually degrades OOD performance while improving ID performance, challenges the core assumption that large-scale chemical pretraining yields better generalization. This positions OOD generalization as a concrete frontier challenge for chemical foundation models, with the open-source BOOM benchmark providing a standardized evaluation protocol. Key open questions include: designing pretraining objectives that convey property-relevant chemical information for extrapolation, incorporating explicit electronic structure into molecular representations (since HOMO/LUMO/Gap/μ are universally poor), and reconciling the strong OOD performance of small equivariant GNNs with the scalability advantages of large transformer models.
