## Problem

Scattered, individual-level tree mortality has substantial impacts on carbon budgets and wildfire risk, yet it remains largely unmonitored because moderate-resolution satellite imagery (e.g., 30 m Landsat) cannot resolve single dead trees, and very-high-resolution drone imagery is too costly for wall-to-wall national coverage. No existing dataset combines high spatial resolution, broad geographic coverage, and pixel-level manual labels for individual tree death detection across diverse climates and forest types.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255526/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each sample is a 224×224 pixel patch from 0.6 m NAIP aerial imagery with four spectral bands (R, G, B, NIR), paired with a binary pixel-level mask where 1 denotes a dead tree crown. A third binary mask layer flags null pixels arising from ortho-rectification so they can be excluded during evaluation. The full dataset comprises 15,489 patches drawn from 1,000 sites across 48 CONUS states (over 23,000 hectares), each annotated with geographic coordinates, Köppen-Geiger climate zone, and primary tree type metadata.

## Method

The primary contribution is the TreeFinder dataset itself: dead-tree polygons are manually delineated in Google Earth Engine using multi-spectral visual cues (true-color, NIR-R-G false-color, and NDVI composites) and validated against multi-temporal NAIP imagery, achieving >95% cross-annotator agreement on a 20% audit subset. For benchmarking, six segmentation architectures spanning CNNs (U-Net, DeepLabV3+) and transformer-based models (ViT, SegFormer, Mask2Former, DOFA) are trained or fine-tuned with AdamW (lr 1e-4, weight decay 0.01), a combined BCE + Dice loss, batch size 32, and early stopping at 100 epochs. Generalizability is stress-tested across four scenarios—random split with incremental training sizes, cross-region (east/west and single-state), cross-climate-zone, and cross-forest-type—each with both a balanced (~50/50) and a harder single-source training split, revealing that F1 scores drop by 50–70% relative to the in-distribution baseline under domain shift.

## Results

Under the standard 80-20 random split on TreeFinder (15,489 patches, 224×224, 4-band NAIP imagery), Mask2Former achieves the highest dead-tree F1 of 51.9%, precision of 55.5%, and IoU of 35.1%, while U-Net attains the highest recall of 65.6% but lower precision (35.7%). All models report near-perfect overall accuracy (99.4–99.6%) due to the extreme class imbalance (dead trees are a small fraction of pixels). Cross-region generalization degrades performance dramatically: F1 drops by up to 50% in the West→East scenario and 70% in the single-state (Colorado→all others) scenario, with SegFormer and Mask2Former retaining the top F1 and IoU positions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Mask2Former leads on F1 (51.9%), precision (55.5%), and IoU (35.1%) in the random-split setting, outperforming DeepLabV3+ (F1 49.7%) and SegFormer (F1 47.8%) by roughly 2–4 points. U-Net trades precision for recall, achieving 65.6% recall versus Mask2Former's 49.4%, making it the most sensitive detector but with a higher false-positive rate. In cross-region scenarios (e.g., West→East, Colorado→all), SegFormer and Mask2Former consistently occupy the top F1 and IoU positions, while U-Net retains the best recall; DOFA and ViT fall well behind (DOFA F1 ≈ 4–5% in cross-region vs. ~22% for SegFormer/Mask2Former). DOFA, a remote-sensing foundation model pretrained on multi-sensor data including NAIP, underperforms all other models across every scenario, suggesting its broad-spectrum pretraining does not transfer well to the narrow 4-band, high-resolution NAIP setting. Overall, no model generalizes well beyond its training domain: the single-state (Colorado) scenario reduces F1 by ~70% relative to the random split, highlighting a significant gap for current segmentation architectures on ecologically heterogeneous, low-contrast targets like individual dead trees.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of remote sensing semantic segmentation and ecological monitoring by introducing the first CONUS-scale, high-resolution (0.6m NAIP) dataset for individual-level tree mortality detection. It departs from two existing lines of work: moderate-resolution satellite-based mortality mapping (Landsat 30m, AVHRR 1km) that can only detect large contiguous patches, and very-high-resolution drone-based datasets (deadtrees.earth, FORinstance) that are limited to local study areas due to cost. Rather than proposing a new architecture, the paper repurposes a suite of established segmentation models (U-Net, DeepLabV3+, ViT, SegFormer, Mask2Former, DOFA) as benchmarks, positioning its contribution squarely in the dataset-and-evaluation-framework space with structured generalization scenarios across geography, climate, and forest type.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TreeFinder addresses a critical gap in ecological monitoring: scattered individual tree deaths, which recent work (Cheng et al., Nature Communications 2024) shows substantially impact carbon budgets and wildfire risk, have been largely unmonitored at national scale. The structured generalization scenarios (cross-region, cross-climate, cross-forest-type) reveal that even the best models suffer 50–70% F1 drops when evaluated outside their training domain, underscoring that domain generalization remains the central unsolved challenge for operational forest monitoring. Key limitations include CONUS-only coverage, single 0.6m resolution, no multi-source data fusion, and no evaluation of active learning or meta-learning strategies — all flagged as important future directions. The dataset is likely to become a standard reference for ecological remote sensing ML given its scale, public availability, and practical metadata enrichment.
