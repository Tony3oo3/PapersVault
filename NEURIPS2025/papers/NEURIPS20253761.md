## Problem

Competing hypotheses in cognitive neuroscience—Hebbian integration, pattern separation, and the Non-Monotonic Plasticity Hypothesis (NMPH)—make different predictions about how associative learning reshapes internal representations, but testing them in biological systems is constrained by the difficulty of precisely controlling stimulus similarity, densely sampling the similarity spectrum, and isolating global competitive effects like vocabulary interference. This paper proposes using pretrained LLMs as scalable model organisms to test these hypotheses under controlled in-context learning conditions.

## Input / Output

The method takes a sequence of r-1 repeated token pairs (x, y) followed by a final x-only cue, presented in-context to a frozen pretrained LLM (no weight updates). Token pairs are systematically sampled into 17 groups spanning cosine similarity [0.1, 0.95) in 0.05-wide bins, with 12 pairs per group per model, and an extended set uniformly sampled over the joint distribution of pair similarity and vocabulary interference. Outputs include per-repetition prediction accuracy, representational change scores (ΔS, the difference in last-layer cosine similarity between the pair at repetition r versus the first occurrence), vocabulary interference scores (median cosine similarity of the target token's representation to 1,000 sampled alternative tokens conditioned on x), and phase-aligned learning dynamics across Encoding, Consolidation, and Forgetting stages.

## Method

The paper adapts a hippocampal associative learning fMRI paradigm (Wammes et al., 2022) to the in-context learning setting by repeatedly presenting token pairs in-context and measuring how last-layer hidden representations evolve. To construct token pairs with precise pre-learning similarity, the authors repurpose the Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG) adversarial optimization algorithm: starting from a duplicate pair (x, x), they iteratively replace the second token using gradient signals (without updating model weights) to steer the pair's cosine similarity into a target interval. Vocabulary interference is estimated by computing the cosine similarity between the target token y's representation (conditioned on x) and 1,000 randomly sampled alternative tokens from the model's vocabulary, yielding a median interference score per pair. Across six open-source LLMs (Llama2-7b, Llama3.1-8b, Llama3.2-1b/3b, Gemma2-9b, Mistral-7b), the authors identify three learning phases—Encoding, Consolidation, and Forgetting—by tracking accuracy trajectories, and find that the NMPH-predicted U-shaped differentiation pattern (moderately similar pairs differentiating while low- and high-similarity pairs integrate or remain stable) emerges specifically during the Consolidation phase. They further show that vocabulary interference modulates this effect: higher interference consistently amplifies differentiation across all similarity levels, revealing that representational change is governed by both local pair similarity and global competitive pressure from the model's prior knowledge.

## Results

All six LLMs (Llama2-7b, Llama3.1-8b, Llama3.2-1b, Llama3.2-3b, Gemma2-9b, Mistral-7b) achieve 90–100% prediction accuracy on the in-context associative learning task, with three distinct learning phases (Encoding, Consolidation, Forgetting) identified. During the Consolidation phase, moderately similar token pairs (cosine similarity 0.55–0.75 before learning) show significant differentiation (reduced pairwise representational similarity), producing a U-shaped pattern consistent with the Non-Monotonic Plasticity Hypothesis (NMPH), while low-similarity pairs integrate and high-similarity pairs remain stable. Vocabulary interference (measured as median cosine similarity between the target token and 1,000 sampled alternative tokens) modulates this effect: higher interference consistently amplifies differentiation across all similarity levels. Forgetting was observed only in Llama2-7b (onset at r=40) and Mistral-7b (onset at r=3,000), with the non-monotonic pattern disappearing during this phase.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing ML methods or baselines in the traditional sense; it is an empirical study using LLMs as model organisms to test cognitive neuroscience hypotheses. The primary 'comparison' is across the six LLMs themselves, all of which exhibit the NMPH-consistent U-shaped differentiation pattern during Consolidation, confirming the effect is not model-specific. A second comparison is against the three competing hypotheses from the neuroscience literature: Hebbian integration (predicts monotonic integration), pattern separation (predicts monotonic differentiation), and NMPH (predicts U-shaped change). The observed data support NMPH, as differentiation is significant only for mid-similarity pairs (0.55–0.75) and absent for both low- and high-similarity extremes during Consolidation. The paper also contrasts its findings with prior computational models (Ritvo et al. 2024, [33]) that were explicitly designed to produce U-shaped dynamics, noting that the LLMs here are general-purpose pretrained models with no architectural constraints or task-specific fine-tuning to elicit this behavior. Finally, the vocabulary interference analysis (Figure 3b) extends the comparison by showing that the apparent stability of high-similarity pairs in the NMPH curve is partly an artifact of averaging over heterogeneous behaviors: under high interference, high-similarity pairs do differentiate, while under low interference they remain stable.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the cognitive neuroscience paradigm of Wammes et al. [46]—which parametrically manipulated visual stimulus similarity to test the Non-Monotonic Plasticity Hypothesis in the human hippocampus—to the in-context learning setting of LLMs, replacing visual stimuli with token pairs. It repurposes the GCG token-optimization algorithm from Zou et al. [55] (originally for adversarial attacks) to systematically construct token pairs at controlled representational similarity levels. Unlike prior computational models of NMPH (e.g., Ritvo et al. [33]), which were hand-crafted recurrent networks designed to produce U-shaped dynamics, this work tests whether the pattern emerges naturally in general-purpose pretrained transformers, and introduces vocabulary interference—a global competition factor difficult to isolate in biological systems—as a second analytical dimension.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that NMPH-consistent differentiation emerges spontaneously in six general-purpose LLMs during the Consolidation phase, without task-specific architectural constraints, strengthens the case for LLMs as scalable model organisms for cognitive neuroscience. The vocabulary interference framework opens a new axis of analysis that is intractable in human studies (where the full distribution of competing representations cannot be accessed), potentially helping reconcile diverging integration-vs-differentiation findings in the hippocampal literature. Key limitations include reliance on the final hidden layer only, geometric rather than semantic token pairs, and sampled vocabulary subsets that may underrepresent true competitive structure; open questions include how these dynamics evolve across layers, during fine-tuning, and in developmental/curriculum-learning settings.
