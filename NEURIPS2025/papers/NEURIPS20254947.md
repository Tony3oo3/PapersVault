## Problem

Existing generalization bounds for neural networks either rely on products of spectral/Frobenius norms (yielding exponential depth dependence) or count all parameters (ignoring the low-rank structure that gradient-based training naturally induces in weight matrices). The few prior bounds that do exploit low-rank structure, such as [64], carry intractable exponential constants in depth. This paper asks whether one can derive generalization bounds that smoothly interpolate between norm-based and parameter-counting regimes while capturing the approximate low-rank structure of each layer's weights.

## Input / Output

The method consumes the trained network's weight matrices A_ell (along with their spectral norms and Schatten p quasi-norms), the training sample size N, loss function constants (Lipschitz constant L_1 and uniform bound B), and user-chosen reference matrices M_ell (typically zero or the initialization). It outputs a high-probability upper bound on the generalization gap that holds simultaneously over all per-layer Schatten indices p_ell ∈ [0,2], allowing post-hoc tuning to balance norm-based and rank-based contributions. For CNNs, the input additionally includes per-layer channel counts U_ell, patch dimensions d_ell, and spatial dimensions w_ell, with the bound accounting for weight sharing so each filter's weights are counted only once.

## Method

The core technique is a parametric interpolation adapted from matrix completion [28]: for each layer, the weight matrix is decomposed via a tunable singular-value threshold τ into a low-rank component (bounded by a parameter-counting argument) and a small-Frobenius-norm residual (bounded by a norm-based covering argument), with τ optimized to balance the two terms. This yields a per-layer L^∞ covering number that scales as [m+d]·(M/ε)^(2p/(p+2)), interpolating between rank-based complexity at p→0 and norm-based complexity at p=2. The per-layer covers are then chained across layers using standard chaining techniques (with an L^2-to-L^∞ conversion at intermediate layers incurring a √w factor), and Loss Function Augmentation [5,6] replaces the worst-case product of spectral norms with empirical maximum activation norms at each layer. The resulting bound for a fixed-width network with rank-r weight matrices achieves sample complexity Õ(W·L²·r) rather than Õ(W²·L²), and the per-layer indices p_ell can be tuned independently after training to exploit approximate (not just exact) low-rank structure.

## Results

The paper's main contribution is theoretical: for fully-connected networks with low-rank weight matrices, the generalization bound achieves a sample complexity of $\tilde{O}(\overline{W} L^2 \underline{r})$ (where $\overline{W}$ is width, $L$ is depth, and $\underline{r}$ is the minimum rank across layers), improving upon the $\tilde{O}(\overline{W}^2 L^2)$ of pure parameter-counting bounds. For linear networks, the bound reduces to $\tilde{O}([\mathcal{C}+d]\,\text{rank}(A))$ as $p \to 0$, removing a factor of $L^3$ present in the prior bound of Neyshabur et al. [3]. Experimental validation in Appendix D on MNIST and CIFAR-10 (both DNNs and CNNs) confirms that the proposed bounds track test performance across varying widths, with the Loss Function Augmentation variant (Theorem 3.3) yielding substantially tighter numerical values than the non-augmented version (Theorem 3.2).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not include a direct empirical baseline comparison table; its comparisons are theoretical. Against Neyshabur et al. [3] (covering-number bounds), the linear network result removes a factor of $L^3$ in sample complexity, enabling the $L \to \infty$ limit where the bound converges to a rank-based parameter count. Against Golowich et al. [13] (Schatten-norm peeling bounds), this paper achieves the standard $O(1/\sqrt{N})$ decay rate rather than a worse rate, and uses a tunable exponent $2p_\ell/(p_\ell+2)$ on the product of spectral norms (which can be made arbitrarily small as $p_\ell \to 0$) versus a fixed exponent of 1. Against Pinto et al. [64] (low-rank layer bounds), this paper avoids the intractable exponential factor $C_1^L$ and provides a continuous interpolation between norm-based and parameter-counting regimes via the Schatten index $p_\ell$, rather than a discrete rank assumption. For multi-class linear classification, the result provides a new characterization incorporating low-rank structure over classes, complementing the state-of-the-art bound of Lei et al. [65] which does not exploit rank structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) norm-based generalization bounds that express capacity through weight matrix norms rather than parameter counts (Bartlett et al. 2017, Neyshabur et al. 2018), (2) the neural rank collapse phenomenon showing that gradient-based training at large depth induces low-rank representations (Dai et al. 2021, Jacot 2023), and (3) the parametric interpolation technique from matrix completion (Ledent & Alves, ICML 2024) that estimates covering numbers under Schatten p quasi-norm constraints. The key methodological departure is adapting this interpolation—originally designed for discrete entry-wise observations in matrix completion—to the fundamentally different setting of neural network weight spaces where observations are network outputs. The paper also extends results via Loss Function Augmentation (Wei & Ma 2019, Nagarajan & Kolter 2019) and builds on the CNN norm-based framework of Ledent et al. (AAAI 2021).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first generalization bounds that smoothly interpolate between parameter-counting and norm-based regimes via a tunable Schatten index p_ell per layer, yielding a sample complexity of Õ(W·r·L²) for fixed-width networks with rank-r weight matrices—improving over the Õ(W²·L²) of pure parameter counting. However, the bounds remain vacuous for large-scale networks without aggressive optimization, and the low-rank assumption on weight matrices (as opposed to the more commonly observed low-rank activations) limits direct applicability. Open questions include extending the analysis to low-rank activations, proving fast rates in N, and adapting the framework to ResNets and transformers. The parametric interpolation technique itself may find broader use in other structured function class analyses.
