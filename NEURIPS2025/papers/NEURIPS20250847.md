## Problem

Most autonomous scientific discovery (ASD) systems operate in a goal-driven setting where a human specifies the research question, leaving the open-ended setting—where the system must decide which hypotheses to pursue on its own—largely unaddressed. The few existing open-ended approaches rely on diversity heuristics that cannot meaningfully navigate the vast hypothesis space, or on subjective LLM-as-judge proxies for interestingness that suffer from imprecise definitions and high inter-annotator disagreement. The paper asks: what principled, automatic metric can guide open-ended hypothesis search at scale?

## Input / Output

The system takes a dataset with its metadata (e.g., column descriptions, domain context) and a Python execution environment as input, with no research question or goal specified; a fixed exploration budget (500 hypothesis verifications) constrains the search. Output is a tree-structured collection of verified hypotheses, each annotated with prior and posterior Beta belief distributions and a surprisal score, from which the system reports the set of unique surprising discoveries after LLM-based hierarchical agglomerative clustering deduplication. The discovery agent internally cycles through hypothesis generation, experiment planning, code writing, execution, analysis, and review, with up to 6 code-attempt retries and 1 experiment revision per node.

## Method

AUTO DISCOVERY formalizes Bayesian surprise as the KL divergence between an LLM's prior and posterior Beta distributions over a hypothesis's truth value, where beliefs are elicited by sampling n boolean responses from the LLM before and after revealing experimental results (using Beta-Bernoulli conjugacy for updates). A surprisal is registered only when the posterior mean crosses a decision threshold (δ=0.5) relative to the prior mean, capturing directionally significant belief shifts. This surprisal serves as the reward function in a Monte Carlo tree search with progressive widening: UCT balances exploitation of high-surprisal regions against exploration of under-visited nodes, while progressive widening dynamically caps the number of children per node based on its visitation count to prevent unbalanced expansion. A multi-agent LLM pipeline (hypothesis generator, experiment programmer, code executor, analyst, reviewer) handles the inner-loop verification of each sampled hypothesis, and a post-hoc LLM-based HAC procedure merges semantically duplicate hypotheses using text-embedding linkage plus GPT-4o equivalence judgments.

## Results

Under a fixed budget of 500 evaluations across 21 real-world datasets, AUTODISCOVERY (MCTS with Bayesian surprise) discovers 5–29% more LLM-surprising hypotheses than the strongest baselines (greedy tree search and beam search), and bests all methods in 17 of 21 datasets. In a human study with 1,620 hypotheses annotated by STEM MS/PhD annotators, 67% of AUTODISCOVERY's surprisals are also rated surprising by domain experts (95% CI [0.63, 0.71]), compared to only 8–24% for alternative automatic rewards (LLM surprisal, interestingness, utility). The discovery agent achieves >98% validity for both experiment design and code implementation (IAA > 0.95), and the LLM-based deduplication pipeline groups duplicates with 91% validity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AUTODISCOVERY is compared against four search baselines under identical budget (500) and reward (Bayesian surprisal): repeated independent sampling, last-K linear sampling, greedy tree search (MCTS with C=0), and beam search (width 8, branching factor 8). All tree-based methods outperform the non-tree baselines, but AUTODISCOVERY's MCTS with progressive widening achieves the highest cumulative surprisal count and the most stable search-efficiency gradient over time, whereas greedy and beam search show measurable efficiency decay. AUTODISCOVERY wins on 17 of 21 individual datasets. In a separate reward-function ablation (Table 2), Bayesian surprise dramatically outperforms direct LLM surprisal (0.67 vs. 0.11), LLM interestingness (0.15), and LLM utility (0.21) in correlating with human surprisal, with a gap of 0.46 points over the next-best reward. The paper also notes that when switching from GPT-4o to the reasoning model o4-mini, MCTS and greedy tree search become statistically indistinguishable under the 500-hypothesis budget, suggesting reasoning models reduce the need for explicit exploration.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the data-driven discovery (DDD) framework of Majumder et al. from a goal-driven setting (where a human specifies a research question) to an open-ended setting where the system autonomously decides what to investigate. For search, it adapts MCTS with progressive widening and UCT—techniques well-established in game playing and program synthesis—to the hypothesis space, using a novel Bayesian surprise reward computed via Beta-Bernoulli belief elicitation from LLM sampling. It departs from prior open-ended discovery work (e.g., AI Scientist, OMNI) that relies on diversity heuristics or subjective LLM-as-judge proxies (interestingness, utility) by proposing a principled, information-theoretic criterion grounded in the KL divergence between prior and posterior beliefs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 67% correlation between Bayesian surprise and human surprisal (versus 8–24% for alternative metrics) provides the first evidence that a non-subjective, computable quantity can effectively guide open-ended scientific exploration, addressing a key gap in autonomous discovery. The finding that interestingness and utility metrics lack discriminative semantics (all four rewards correlate similarly with human ratings of those qualities) is a useful negative result that cautions against using subjective proxies. Open questions include whether LLM surprisal can be grounded in the broader literature to close the 33% gap with human surprise, how the approach scales beyond a 500-hypothesis budget, and what guardrails are needed to prevent ungrounded or spurious discoveries in fully autonomous operation.
