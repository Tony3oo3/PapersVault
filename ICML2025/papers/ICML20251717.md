## Problem

Existing empirical privacy auditing methods either require multiple expensive training runs or, in the single-run setting of Steinke et al. (2023), rely on (ε,δ) parameters that produce suboptimal privacy estimates with a gap that worsens as the number of canaries grows (due to an O(m·δ) term). This paper addresses the gap by showing that auditing the full f-DP trade-off curve, rather than a single (ε,δ) point, yields simultaneously tight bounds across all probability levels, enabling accurate empirical privacy estimation in a single training run.

## Input / Output

The auditor receives oracle access to a black-box mechanism M, a fixed training set T, an indexed canary set C of size m (each canary having k options, with k=2 for membership inference or k>2 for reconstruction), a hypothesized f-DP trade-off function f, and an attack algorithm A. The canaries are injected via Poisson sampling (Bernoulli(0.5) per canary) or uniform sampling over [k]^m, the mechanism is run once on T∪S, and the attack produces a vector of guesses with abstentions (⊥). The output is a binary accept/reject decision for the privacy hypothesis and, by scanning an ordered family of f-DP curves (e.g., Gaussian mechanisms at varying noise levels), the strongest curve that passes the test, reported as an empirical ε at a chosen δ.

## Method

The core technical contribution is a recursive invariant (Theorem 3.1) that bounds the probability p_i of exactly i correct guesses in terms of p_1,…,p_{i-1} using the f-DP curve, derived by combining a per-canary conditioning bound (Lemma A.1, generalizing Hayes et al. 2023) with a permutation-invariance (shuffling) argument and Jensen's inequality on the convex trade-off function. Algorithm 3 then numerically computes an upper bound on the tail probability Pr[c ≥ c_target] by recursively applying this relation from i=c down to i=0, using oracle access to the inverse of the f-DP curve, and rejecting the privacy hypothesis if the implied lower bound on total probability exceeds 1. The key advantage over (ε,δ)-based auditing is that the f-DP curve provides a tight bound at every probability level simultaneously, eliminating the O(m·δ) degradation that causes Steinke et al.'s empirical ε to worsen with more canaries. The framework generalizes to reconstruction games with k>2 options per canary, increasing per-canary entropy and enabling tighter audits with fewer canaries.

## Results

On the idealized Gaussian mechanism setting (Table 1), the proposed f-DP auditing procedure achieves empirical ε values of 8.16, 3.61, 1.59, and 0.82 at δ=10⁻⁵ for noise levels σ=0.5, 1.0, 2.0, and 4.0 respectively, compared to 4.99, 2.61, 1.33, and 0.61 for Steinke et al. (2023), while the theoretical ε values are 9.99, 4.37, 1.99, and 0.92. On CIFAR-10 with DP-SGD (WRN16-4, ε=8.0, δ=10⁻⁵, 5,000 canaries, 2,500 steps), the model achieves 77% accuracy with injected canaries versus 80% without. The proposed method's empirical ε bounds continue to improve as the number of canaries increases, whereas Steinke et al.'s bounds degrade due to an O(m·δ) penalty term.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is Steinke et al. (2023/2024b), which audits (ε,δ)-DP using a binomial tail bound on membership inference success. The proposed f-DP auditing method consistently outperforms Steinke et al. across all experimental settings: on the idealized Gaussian mechanism (Table 1), it recovers 81.7% of the theoretical ε at σ=0.5 (8.16 vs 9.99) versus only 49.9% for Steinke et al. (4.99 vs 9.99); on CIFAR-10 white-box (Figure 2) and black-box (Figure 3) settings, it achieves tighter empirical ε lower bounds for all tested theoretical ε values; and on the Purchase tabular dataset (Figure 12) against the SoTA RMIA attack, it outperforms Steinke et al. for all bounded-guesses configurations. A key structural advantage is that Steinke et al.'s bound degrades with increasing canary count m due to an O(m·δ) error term, while the proposed method's bound improves with more canaries because it uses the exact f-DP curve rather than a linear (ε,δ) approximation that is tight at only a single point. The paper also shows that a naive multi-hypothesis approach (auditing (ε_δ, δ) at 60 different δ values with shared randomness) still yields a large gap compared to direct f-DP auditing, confirming that observing the entire privacy curve is essential.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the single-run privacy auditing framework of Steinke et al. (2023/2024), which used (ε,δ) point estimates to bound adversary success in canary-based membership inference games, by replacing the point-estimate analysis with the full f-DP trade-off curve from Dong et al. (2019). The technical core builds on and generalizes the reconstruction bounds of Hayes et al. (2023) (Lemma A.1), but introduces a novel recursive relation (Theorem 3.1) that handles the dependency structure across multiple canaries via a shuffling argument combined with convexity of trade-off functions. The key departure from prior work is that auditing the entire f-DP curve simultaneously, rather than a single (ε,δ) pair, avoids the fundamental limitation that a linear privacy approximation is tight at only one probability value.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides a practically important improvement for auditing DP-SGD in real training pipelines: tighter empirical privacy estimates from a single run, with bounds that improve (rather than degrade) as the number of canaries increases, directly addressing the m·δ degradation that plagued prior single-run methods. The combination of trade-off functions with shuffling is identified as a new technique with potential broader applications beyond privacy auditing. However, a non-trivial gap between empirical and theoretical privacy remains (e.g., 8.16 vs. 9.99 for σ=0.5), attributed to relaxations in the numerical tail-bounding algorithm, which is an open problem for future work. The approach also does not provide a strict upper bound on privacy, only a lower bound on what can be certified.
