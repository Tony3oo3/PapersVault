## Problem

Simulating diffusion bridges (diffusion processes conditioned to hit a target at a future time) is nontrivial because transition densities are generally intractable. Existing guided-proposal methods require expensive MCMC or SMC updates to correct for approximation error, while score-matching methods rely on unconditional samples and fail for rare events or hypo-elliptic diffusions where σσᵀ is singular. This paper seeks a method that produces independent bridge samples at forward-process cost without MCMC, score modeling, or ellipticity assumptions.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input the SDE coefficients (drift b, diffusion σ), an initial state x₀, a terminal observation v (possibly partial via a linear map L), a time horizon T, and a user-specified linear auxiliary process (β, B, σ̃). It outputs independent sample paths from the conditioned bridge in C([0,T], R^d), generated by a single Euler-Maruyama pass through the learned SDE at a cost comparable to simulating the unconditioned forward process. The framework handles both fully and partially observed conditioning and supports hypo-elliptic diffusions, with the observation noise ε kept strictly positive for numerical stability.

## Method

The neural guided diffusion bridge augments the classical guided proposal (Schauer et al., 2017) with an additional drift correction σ(s,x)ϑ_θ(s,x) parametrized by a neural network, defining a new measure via Girsanov's theorem. Training minimizes the KL divergence D_KL(P_θ^•‖P^★) between the neural bridge law and the true bridge law, which reduces to a tractable loss L(θ) = E^•∫{½‖ϑ_θ‖² − G(s,X_s)}ds where G encodes the mismatch between the true and auxiliary generators. Gradients are computed via the reparameterization trick: the SDE is solved with Euler-Maruyama on a fixed Wiener realization, and backpropagation through the computational graph yields ∇_θ L. Crucially, the network learns from conditional (guided-proposal) samples rather than unconditional forward samples, making it effective for rare events where the unconditioned process rarely visits the target region. The auxiliary linear process is typically chosen via a first-order Taylor expansion of the drift at the conditioning point, and the network uses LipSwish or tanh activations with gradient clipping to satisfy the boundedness and Lipschitz conditions required for the Girsanov change of measure to be well-defined.

## Results

The neural guided bridge uses dramatically fewer network parameters than both competing deep-learning methods across all four test cases: 921 vs. 21,969 (adjoint forward) and 26,353 (score matching) for the 1D OU process, and 15,188 vs. 114,744 and 26,766 for the 100-dimensional stochastic landmark matching problem. Training time is also consistently lower or competitive: 44.12s vs. 162.68s and 65.55s (OU), and 122.48s vs. 543.80s and 353.05s (landmark). For the partially observed FitzHugh-Nagumo model, neither adjoint forward nor score matching is applicable, while the neural guided bridge trains in 113.77s with only 3,362 parameters. Qualitatively, the method matches the guided proposal's trajectory quality in rare-event and multi-modal settings where score matching and adjoint forward fail to capture the correct dynamics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the guided proposal (Schauer et al., 2017), the neural guided bridge achieves comparable trajectory quality in all tested scenarios (normal, rare, and multi-modal events) but generates independent samples at a cost similar to unconditioned forward simulation, whereas the guided proposal requires long MCMC chains (e.g., 50,000 pCN iterations for the multi-modal cell model with 26.81% acceptance). Against score matching (Heng et al., 2022), the neural guided bridge clearly outperforms in rare-event and multi-modal settings: score matching exhibits higher variance in early-time marginals for rare events and completely fails to capture multi-modal structure, because it learns from unconditioned samples that rarely visit the conditioning region. Against the adjoint forward method (Baker et al., 2024a), the neural guided bridge again wins in rare-event and multi-modal cases where the adjoint approach produces overly dispersed or incorrect trajectories, and it is the only method applicable to the partially observed FHN model. In the normal-event cell diffusion case, all four methods perform comparably, confirming that the neural guided bridge does not sacrifice quality in easier settings. Overall, the neural guided bridge is the only method that combines competitive trajectory quality with independent sampling, small model size, and applicability to both fully and partially observed conditioning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper combines the guided proposal framework of Schauer et al. (2017) with variational inference and a neural network to learn a correction drift term, departing from both MCMC-based corrections (pCN) and score-matching approaches that learn bridge dynamics from unconditional or adjoint samples. The auxiliary linear process construction follows the Taylor-expansion strategy of Mider et al. (2021) and the hypo-elliptic extension of Bierkens et al. (2020). The key conceptual departure is formulating the bridge approximation as a KL-minimization over a variational family parameterized by a neural network, trained via the reparameterization trick on conditional samples rather than unconditional ones.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method eliminates the need for MCMC or SMC to correct guided proposals, enabling independent sample generation at a cost comparable to forward simulation, and handles rare-event conditioning and hypo-elliptic diffusions where score-matching methods fail. A notable limitation is the mode-seeking behavior inherent to KL minimization, which may under-explore multimodal bridge distributions compared to multi-chain MCMC. Open directions include jointly learning the auxiliary process parameters alongside the neural correction, extending to partial observations at multiple future times, and improving gradient computation via adjoint sensitivity methods for higher-dimensional problems.
