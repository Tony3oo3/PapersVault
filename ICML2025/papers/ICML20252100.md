## Problem

LLM judges are expensive to evaluate (a single Alpaca-Eval run costs ~$24 per model, multiplied by the number of models needed for correlation), and prior work has changed the judge model, prompt, score format, and instruction set simultaneously, making it impossible to isolate which design choices actually matter. No prior work has systematically tuned the full space of judge hyperparameters—model, prompt strategy, temperature, output format, and parsing mechanism—while jointly optimizing for both accuracy and cost.

## Input / Output

At inference time, the judge receives an instruction prompt and two model completions (Assistant A and B) and outputs a preference in one of five formats: a letter, a Likert scale (A>>B through B>>A), a 0–1 preference score, a pair of 0–10 scores, or a multi-criteria average. At tuning time, the method consumes a search space of 4,480 configurations (7 open-weight models × 4 temperatures × 2 order-averaging options × 80 prompt templates) and a set of 6,548 human-annotated battles from LMSys, and produces a Pareto front of judge configurations ranked by non-dominated sort over human-agreement accuracy and per-annotation cost. The 80 prompt templates are generated by combining 5 output formats with 4 binary prompt features (provide example, provide answer, provide explanation, use JSON).

## Method

The paper introduces a three-stage multi-fidelity, multi-objective search over 4,480 zero-shot judge configurations. In the first fidelity, all configurations are evaluated on 400 instructions; the top 1,200 by non-dominated sort (balancing human agreement and cost) advance to 1,200 instructions; the top 400 advance to the full 3,548 validation instructions, reducing total annotation cost from N×P to N×P/3. The key metric choice is human agreement rather than Spearman correlation, which the paper shows has a much lower coefficient of variation (1.27 vs. 8.43 for 72B models) and can statistically distinguish 32B from 72B judges with the same number of instructions. The search space spans 7 open-weight models (Llama3.1 8B/70B, Qwen2.5 7B/27B/70B, Gemma2 9B/27B), 4 temperatures, order-averaging, and 80 prompt templates. Survival analysis over the top 100 configurations reveals that output format (pair scoring) and order-averaging are nearly as important as model choice, while providing an explanation or the judge's own answer hurts performance, and examples help only for large models.

## Results

On the LMSys test set (3K instructions), the tuned judges achieve human agreement of 0.45 (small, <10B), 0.47 (medium, <32B), and 0.49 (large, <72B), outperforming PandaLM-7B (0.38) and JudgeLM-7B (0.42) while matching or slightly underperforming Arena-Hard (0.50). Critically, the cost per 1K annotations drops from $1.2 (Arena-Hard) to $0.21–$0.48 for the proposed judges, a 2.5–6× reduction. On the PandaLM test set, the medium judge reaches 0.78 human agreement, surpassing PandaLM-70B (0.67) by 11 points despite being zero-shot rather than fine-tuned. On Arena-Hard Spearman correlation, the medium judge achieves 0.93, exceeding Arena-Hard+GPT-4 (0.90) at 1/100th the cost.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against PandaLM (Wang et al., 2024), the proposed zero-shot judges clearly win: on the PandaLM test set, Ours-medium (0.78) and Ours-large (0.76) both exceed PandaLM-70B (0.67) by 9–11 points, and even Ours-small (<10B) matches PandaLM-70B at 0.67. On LMSys, PandaLM-7B underperforms the simple length baseline (0.38 vs. 0.42), confirming distribution-shift fragility of fine-tuned judges. Against JudgeLM (Zhu et al., 2023), the proposed judges win on LMSys (0.45–0.49 vs. 0.42) at 18–41× lower cost ($0.21–$0.48 vs. $8.6 per 1K annotations). Against Arena-Hard (Li et al., 2024), the proposed judges slightly underperform on LMSys human agreement (0.49 vs. 0.50) but achieve 2.5–6× lower cost; on Arena-Hard Spearman correlation, Ours-medium (0.93) actually exceeds Arena-Hard+GPT-4 (0.90) while costing $0.48 vs. $50.0 per 1K annotations. All proposed judges use open-weight models (Llama3.1, Qwen2.5, Gemma2), unlike Arena-Hard which relies on closed GPT-4o mini.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions LLM judge design as a hyperparameter optimization problem, departing from prior work (Alpaca-Eval, Arena-Hard, PandaLM, JudgeLM) where each benchmark changed the judge model, prompt, scoring format, and instruction set simultaneously, making isolated contributions hard to attribute. Methodologically, it repurposes successful-halving (Karnin et al., 2013) for multi-fidelity evaluation and non-dominated sort (Emmerich & Deutz, 2018) for multi-objective selection, applying these established HPO tools to the novel domain of judge configuration search. The key conceptual departure is using human agreement on pairwise battles as a cheaper, lower-variance proxy metric in place of Spearman correlation over a full model grid, which the paper shows enables statistically distinguishing model sizes that Spearman correlation cannot.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 1/1000 cost reduction makes systematic judge tuning feasible for the community, and the finding that open-weight models (Llama3.1, Qwen2.5, Gemma2) can match or exceed GPT-4-based judges on both accuracy and cost has direct implications for building reproducible, community-owned leaderboards. However, the paper only optimizes accuracy and cost, leaving position bias, verbosity bias, and self-preference unaddressed as optimization objectives, and the authors acknowledge conclusions may shift with new model capabilities (e.g., reasoning). Open questions include whether the identified hyperparameter rankings (e.g., pair format > Likert, lower temperature better) transfer to future model generations and whether multi-objective tuning can incorporate bias metrics without degrading accuracy.
