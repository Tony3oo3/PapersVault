## Problem

Standard transformer models struggle to generalize to longer sequences or harder problem instances than those seen during training, and prior solutions rely on task-specific architectural modifications (positional encodings, looping, attention changes) that are incompatible with large language models. This paper asks whether a standard transformer, without any architectural changes, can systematically extend its capabilities to arbitrarily harder problems through iterative self-training on its own predictions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251292/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Inputs are character-level tokenized prompts: arithmetic expressions (e.g., '31558+91786=' for reverse addition, '34895*148=' for CoT multiplication), raw strings for copy/reverse, or adjacency-list maze descriptions with specified start and end nodes (numbers 0–99 tokenized individually). Outputs are the corresponding answer string, the copied/reversed sequence, or the shortest path as a '>'-separated node sequence. The training loss is computed only on the completion tokens (the prompt is masked), and evaluation uses greedy decoding with exact-match accuracy over the full output sequence.

## Method

The core procedure is an iterative self-improvement loop over a standard 14M-parameter LLaMA decoder (NoPE, 6 layers) that exploits the model's 'transcendence'—its ability to occasionally solve slightly harder instances than its training distribution. Starting from a labeled dataset D₀ up to difficulty d₀, each round r generates N_r new examples at difficulty d_r using the previous model M_{r-1}, applies unsupervised filtering (relative length filtering to discard truncated outputs, and majority voting across k independently seeded models to enforce consensus), then fine-tunes on the cumulative dataset D₀∪…∪D_r with 50% upsampling of the newest round. A controlled weak-to-strong curriculum gradually increases difficulty (one digit per round for addition, one hop per round for mazes, or a fine-grained operand schedule for multiplication), and an accelerated variant samples all difficulty levels already achieving >99% accuracy to yield exponential extrapolation. Pretrained Llama-1B/3B models fine-tuned with LoRA further accelerate the process, generalizing to longer sequences in fewer rounds than from-scratch training.

## Results

Starting from a 14M-parameter model trained on 1–16 digit reverse addition, self-improvement achieves near-perfect (>99%) accuracy up to 100+ digits without any architectural changes. For forward addition, length filtering enables generalization from 10 to 75 digits over 60+ rounds, while without filtering the model collapses after a few rounds. On multiplication, the combined majority-voting + length-filtering strategy reaches near-perfect 6×6 accuracy by round 7 and 10×10 in just 19 accelerated rounds (vs. 41 standard rounds). Pretrained Llama-3B models generalize further and faster than the 14M from-scratch model, and majority voting alone boosts 5×6 multiplication data quality from 31% average accuracy to 93.3%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external baseline methods; instead, it ablates the self-improvement framework itself. Without any data filtering, self-improvement collapses on forward addition, multiplication, and maze tasks due to error avalanche, while it succeeds unfiltered on reverse addition and string copy. Among filtering strategies, majority voting outperforms length filtering alone on multiplication (Figure 8), and the combined approach achieves the best results. For maze solving, majority voting matches or exceeds an oracle verifier that checks move validity and endpoint correctness, suggesting self-consistency is a viable alternative to external verification. On model scale, Llama-3B > Llama-1B > 14M from-scratch in both the number of OOD digits achieved per round and the total rounds needed, confirming that pretraining accelerates self-improvement. The accelerated schedule (sampling all difficulty levels >99% accurate each round) halves the number of rounds needed for 100-digit reverse addition and reduces multiplication 10×10 from 41 to 19 rounds.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper applies the iterative self-training framework of STaR (Zelikman et al., 2022) and ReST (Gulcehre et al., 2023) to algorithmic tasks (arithmetic, string manipulation, mazes), departing from prior length-generalization work that relies on architectural modifications (positional encodings, looping, attention biases) or input-format changes. The key conceptual shift is that instead of modifying the model to extrapolate, the model's training distribution is progressively expanded through self-generated data with unsupervised quality control (length filtering, majority voting). The paper also connects to the easy-to-hard generalization literature (Schwarzschild et al., 2021; Burns et al., 2023) and explicitly addresses model collapse concerns by retaining the original labeled dataset throughout training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The demonstration that a 14M-parameter standard transformer can generalize from 16-digit to 100-digit addition purely through self-improvement—without any architectural or positional-encoding changes—provides a practical, scalable alternative to the many task-specific modifications proposed for length generalization. The error avalanche analysis and the finding that majority voting matches oracle verification offer actionable guidance for practitioners deploying self-improvement pipelines. Open questions remain: whether the approach transfers to complex reasoning tasks where difficulty is harder to define, whether the assumption of initial OOD 'transcendence' always holds, and how the framework scales when the gap between training and target difficulty is large rather than incremental.
