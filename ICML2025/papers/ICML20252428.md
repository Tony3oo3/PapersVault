## Problem

Increasing decoding temperature is the standard approach to boost diversity in autoregressive language models, yet it often fails to improve Recall (coverage of the reference distribution) and can even degrade it. The paper identifies the root cause: for temperature to be an effective Precision-Recall tuning knob, the model must be trained toward coverage, but all existing modified training losses (Trunc, GOLD, TaiLr) implicitly optimize Precision surrogates rather than Recall.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252428/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt or context as input and produces autoregressively generated text sequences, code, or math solutions. During training, the proposed losses consume the model's own detached per-token probability estimates to compute reweighting factors, with TruncR and λ-PR additionally requiring dynamic quantile estimation of sequence-level likelihood to set a threshold δ. At inference, a scalar temperature t is applied to the softmax logits to form the tempered distribution Q^t, which is then sampled token-by-token; the key finding is that this temperature becomes a more effective diversity control only when the underlying model was trained with a Recall-oriented loss.

## Method

The paper first provides a theoretical bound (Theorem 4.2) showing that for sparse target distributions—typical in natural language where the conditional support is far smaller than the vocabulary—increasing temperature eventually degrades both Precision and Recall, and characterizes the exact PR-Curve behavior under temperature scaling in a controlled artificial setting (Proposition 4.3). To make temperature scaling effective, they propose three Recall-oriented reweighted-NLL losses: TruncR trains exclusively on low-probability samples to optimize Recall at a fixed Precision level; c-Div generalizes GOLD to conditional Tsallis α-divergence with α>1, yielding mass-covering (Recall-favoring) behavior; and λ-PR generalizes TaiLr to target any point (α_λ, β_λ) on the PR-Curve for λ≤1 by combining a likelihood threshold with a per-position geometric weighting. The unifying mechanism is that, unlike the baselines which upweight high-probability tokens, these losses upweight low-probability tokens, shifting the model's learned distribution toward covering more of the reference support. Empirically across WritingPrompts, MathQA-Python, and integer multiplication tasks, models trained with these losses achieve substantially higher Recall at comparable Precision, and temperature tuning on them yields a strictly better P&R trade-off than temperature tuning on NLL-trained models.

## Results

On WritingPrompts (Olmo-1B), the proposed λ-PR loss (λ=0.1) achieves the best Recall of 12.6 while maintaining Precision of 81.3, nearly matching NLL's Precision (81.4) but substantially outperforming its Recall (8.9). The c-Div loss (α=1.4) pushes Recall to 11.9 at the cost of lower Precision (54.2), while TruncR (Δ=0.25) achieves P=85.5, R=9.3. On MathQA-Python, c-Div-trained models achieve higher Recall than NLL at the same Precision level (e.g., 0.50 vs 0.47 Recall at ~0.20 Precision for Olmo-1B), and higher Recall at the same Precision (0.14 vs 0.10 at P=0.10 for Llama-Alpaca).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Trunc (Kang & Hashimoto, 2020), the proposed TruncR inverts the trade-off: Trunc improves Precision from 81.4 to 85.5 but leaves Recall unchanged at 8.9, while TruncR achieves the same Precision (85.5) with a Recall gain to 9.3. Against GOLD (Pang & He, 2021), the contrast is stark: GOLD pushes Precision to 99.3 but collapses Recall to 0.2, whereas c-Div (the generalized GOLD with α=1.4) achieves Recall of 11.9 at Precision 54.2, and λ-PR achieves Recall 12.6 at Precision 81.3. Against TaiLr (Ji et al., 2023), the proposed λ-PR (which generalizes TaiLr to arbitrary λ on the PR-Curve) achieves substantially higher Recall (12.6 vs 9.3) at comparable Precision (81.3 vs 83.9). Across all three baselines, the paper's core finding is that existing losses systematically favor Precision over Recall, while the proposed Recall-oriented losses (TruncR, c-Div, λ-PR) consistently improve Recall by 0.4–3.7 points over NLL while maintaining or only modestly sacrificing Precision.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies three previously independent training losses—Trunc (Kang & Hashimoto, 2020), GOLD (Pang & He, 2021), and TaiLr (Ji et al., 2023)—within the Precision-Recall framework of Sajjadi et al. (2018), showing that all three implicitly optimize Precision rather than Recall. It then departs from this lineage by proposing Recall-oriented counterparts (TruncR, c-Div via Tsallis α-divergence, and λ-PR) and, crucially, by providing the first theoretical analysis (Theorem 4.2, Proposition 4.3) explaining *why* temperature scaling fails to improve Recall: the sparsity of the true conditional distribution means higher temperature disproportionately inflates probability on unacceptable tokens. The work also connects to the f-divergence literature on generative models (Verine et al., 2023) by showing that the c-Div loss is a conditional Tsallis divergence minimization, bridging the image-generation and language-modeling communities.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that increasing decoding temperature, the most widely used 'diversity knob,' often *reduces* Recall rather than improving it—challenges a default assumption in LLM deployment and suggests that diversity must be addressed at training time, not merely at inference. The practical implication is clear: practitioners seeking diverse outputs should consider Recall-oriented training objectives rather than relying on temperature alone. Limitations include the acknowledged instability of the proposed losses relative to NLL, the reliance on simplified theoretical settings (two-position artificial distributions) whose tightness is only partially validated, and modest absolute Recall gains (e.g., 8.9→12.6 on WritingPrompts). Open questions include whether these losses compose well with RLHF or DPO pipelines, how they behave at much larger scales (70B+), and whether the sparsity-based bounds can be made tighter for practical model selection.
