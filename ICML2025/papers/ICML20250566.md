## Problem

LLMs have been observed to process non-human-readable text sequences (e.g., jailbreak prompts, filler tokens) in ways that appear to be alignment bugs, yet no systematic study has investigated whether these strings are truly devoid of meaning or contain latent features usable by models. This paper asks whether such 'unnatural languages' possess generalizable semantic content that can be transferred across models and tasks, and whether they can serve as a valid training signal for instruction tuning.

## Input / Output

The searching method takes a natural string S and produces a fixed-length token sequence S′ whose semantic equivalence is defined by the probability that multiple LLMs can translate S′ back to S under reconstruction prompts. For downstream evaluation, the input is an unnatural context (or instruction) paired with a natural-language question (or answer), and the output is either a question answer or a fine-tuned model. The unnatural strings are constrained to a predefined token count n per model, and the search space is the discrete vocabulary space of each participating model.

## Method

The core contribution is a multi-model, multi-task discrete optimization procedure that searches for a semantically equivalent but syntactically unnatural string by maximizing the joint log-probability of reconstructing the original natural string across a set of models and reconstruction tasks. At each iteration, the algorithm identifies top-k gradient-influential token positions, generates B single-token-replacement candidates per model, and selects the candidate with the highest cross-model reconstruction score, repeating until convergence. The paper then demonstrates that the resulting unnatural strings transfer across architectures (open-source and commercial LLMs), that fine-tuning on unnatural LIMA instructions yields on-par instruction-following performance (≈49.7% average win rate on LC AlpacaEval 2.0), and that mechanistic analysis (token-importance ablation, embedding inverse-similarity tracking, and structural probing for dependency parsing) reveals LLMs filter noise tokens and infer contextual keyword organization from the remaining signal.

## Results

On SynContextQA, transferred models achieve 80.4% average accuracy on unnatural contexts versus 98% on natural (82.0% recovery), while on SimGSM8K they reach 54% versus 88% natural (61.6% recovery). For instruction tuning, models fine-tuned on unnatural LIMA achieve a 49.71% average win rate on LC AlpacaEval 2.0 (49.78% Llama-3-8B, 47.13% Gemma-2-9B, 52.22% Llama-3-70B), indicating on-par performance with natural-language-tuned counterparts. On MixEval, unnatural-LIMA models match or exceed natural-LIMA models and outperform random/empty-instruction baselines by over 11% on Llama-3-70B.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares unnatural-language inputs against four baselines: natural language, shuffled-with-injected-tokens (Shuf-InJ, the GCG initialization), random tokens, and empty instructions (Hewitt et al., 2024). On SynContextQA, unnatural contexts achieve 0.80 average transfer accuracy versus 0.54 for Shuf-InJ and 0.98 for natural, recovering 82% of natural performance while Shuf-InJ recovers only 55%. On SimGSM8K, the gap is larger: unnatural reaches 0.54 versus 0.27 for Shuf-InJ and 0.88 for natural. For instruction tuning on MixEval, the unnatural-LIMA model outperforms the empty-instruction baseline (Hewitt et al., 2024) by 11.7% overall on Llama-3-70B (0.748 vs 0.631) and by 9.2% on Llama-3-8B (0.547 vs 0.516), while also exceeding the random-token baseline by 4.8% and 13.1% respectively. The unnatural-LIMA model matches or slightly trails the natural-LIMA model on most MixEval subsets, confirming that the latent features in unnatural strings are sufficient for instruction-following.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the gradient-guided discrete token-replacement paradigm from GCG (Zou et al., 2023) and AutoPrompt (Shin et al., 2020)—previously used for adversarial jailbreaking and prompt optimization—to search for semantically equivalent but syntactically unnatural strings, then systematically studies whether these strings carry transferable latent features. It connects to a scattered line of observations: Ilyas et al. (2019) on adversarial features in vision, Pfau et al. (2024) on filler tokens enabling hidden computation, Sinha et al. (2020) on NLI with permuted inputs, and Kervadec et al. (2023) on LLMs interpreting machine-generated prompts. The key departure is framing these isolated phenomena as a unified property—'unnatural languages'—and providing the first systematic cross-model, cross-task evaluation plus a mechanistic account (noise filtering + contextual keyword reorganization) via structural probing (Hewitt & Manning, 2019).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that instruction-tuning on syntactically garbage instructions yields on-par chat performance (~49–52% win rate on LC AlpacaEval 2.0) challenges the assumption that human-readable instruction text is necessary for alignment, with implications for data augmentation, robustness auditing, and understanding what transformers actually learn from instruction data. However, practical adoption is limited by GCG's high computational cost and the fact that searched strings still retain original tokens, reducing true 'unnaturalness.' Open questions include whether more efficient discrete optimizers can produce fully token-disjoint unnatural strings, whether the phenomenon persists at larger scales or with different architectures, and what security implications follow from the cross-model transferability (including to GPT-4o).
