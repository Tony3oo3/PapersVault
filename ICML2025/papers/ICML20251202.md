## Problem

In multi-stakeholder RL, the choice of social welfare function—parameterized by p in generalized p-means—dramatically affects the optimal policy, yet decision-makers rarely know the appropriate p in advance. Existing MORL methods assume a fixed p and compute a single optimal policy, offering no guidance when the fairness criterion is uncertain. This paper asks whether a small, finite set of policies can be computed that is approximately optimal across the entire family of p-means for all p ≤ 1, with formal guarantees on portfolio size and computational cost.

## Input / Output

The method takes a multi-objective MDP with N reward functions (one per stakeholder), a feasible policy set Π (either all policies or a finite set of pre-trained policies), an aggregation rule ℓ ∈ {1,2} (expected scalarized returns or scalarized expected returns), and a target approximation factor α ∈ (0,1). It outputs a portfolio Π′ ⊆ Π of size O(ln κ / ln(1/α))—where κ is the reward condition number—along with the specific p values at which each policy was optimized. Each oracle call solves the welfare-maximizing policy problem for a single p, and the total number of such calls is bounded by Õ((ln κ)² ln ln N / ln(1/α)).

## Method

The main algorithm, p-MEANPORTFOLIO, sequentially selects p values from −∞ to 1 using a LINESEARCH subroutine that binary-searches for the largest interval [p, b*] over which the current optimal policy π_p remains an α-approximation; a key trick is checking a √α threshold (rather than α) at the midpoint to accelerate convergence while preserving correctness. A complementary heuristic, BUDGET-CONSTRAINEDPORTFOLIO, operates under a fixed budget K of oracle calls by adaptively selecting the midpoint of the interval with the worst estimated approximation factor, rather than sweeping p monotonically. The paper also proves a warm-start guarantee showing that the optimal policy for p is within (q−p)UHκ ln κ of optimal for any nearby q > p, justifying the use of previously computed policies to accelerate successive oracle calls. Theoretical analysis establishes that the portfolio size is O(ln κ / ln(1/α)) and the oracle complexity is Õ((ln κ)² ln ln N / ln(1/α)), with the initial p₀ = −ln N / ln(1/α) ensuring coverage of the p → −∞ (Egalitarian) regime.

## Results

Across all three environments (Taxi, Natural Disaster, Healthcare Intervention), p-MEANPORTFOLIO achieves approximation factors of 0.99–1.000 with portfolio sizes of at most 10 policies, while random policy sampling plateaus at 0.60–0.67 and random p-sampling at 0.65–0.96. The heuristic BUDGETCONSTRAINEDPORTFOLIO matches or nearly matches p-MEANPORTFOLIO's approximation quality (e.g., 0.993 vs 1.000 on Healthcare at size 7) while using only K oracle calls instead of up to 144. The theoretical portfolio size bound of O(ln κ / ln(1/α)) is confirmed empirically, with actual portfolios remaining small (≤10) even for tight approximation factors.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Generalized Policy Improvement (GPI) algorithm of Alegre et al. (AAMAS 2023), p-MEANPORTFOLIO achieves substantially higher approximation ratios: on the Natural Disaster environment, p-MEANPORTFOLIO reaches 1.000 at portfolio size 4 while GPI plateaus at 0.520; on Healthcare Intervention, p-MEANPORTFOLIO reaches 1.000 at size 7 versus GPI's 0.641 ceiling. Against random p-sampling, p-MEANPORTFOLIO wins decisively on the Taxi environment (0.99 vs 0.65 at size 10) and Natural Disaster (1.000 vs 0.888 at size 4), though random p-sampling occasionally matches or slightly exceeds p-MEANPORTFOLIO at very small portfolio sizes (e.g., 0.832 vs 0.706 at size 1 on Natural Disaster). Against random policy sampling, p-MEANPORTFOLIO dominates across all environments and sizes, with gaps of 0.3–0.4 in approximation ratio. The BUDGETCONSTRAINEDPORTFOLIO heuristic achieves comparable quality to p-MEANPORTFOLIO (within 0.01–0.08) while reducing oracle calls by 5–15× (e.g., 7 vs 61 calls on Healthcare at size 7).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of multi-objective RL, social choice theory, and approximation algorithms. Prior MORL work (Pareto front coverage, convex coverage sets) characterizes trade-offs without committing to a specific welfare function, while social-welfare RL work (Fan et al., 2022; Zhong et al., 2024; Chakraborty et al., 2024) assumes a fixed p-mean and computes a single optimal policy. The portfolio-with-guarantees concept from combinatorial optimization (Gupta et al., 2023; Goel & Meyerson, 2006) operates in structured settings like facility location, not the vast policy space of RL. This paper is the first to formalize and algorithmically solve the problem of constructing a small policy set that is α-approximate across the entire family of p-means (p ≤ 1) in an MDP, bridging these three threads.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical motivation is strong: decision-makers in healthcare, cloud computing, and LLM alignment must choose a fairness criterion (i.e., a p value) without knowing in advance which is appropriate, and small changes in p can yield dramatically different policies. The paper's guarantee that a portfolio of ≤10 policies suffices to cover all p ≤ 1 with near-optimal approximation is practically empowering. Limitations include restriction to p ≤ 1 (excluding more utilitarian regimes), restriction to p-means (not Gini, proportional fairness, or other welfare families), and reliance on oracle access to solve the p-mean MDP exactly. Open questions include extending to p > 1, other welfare function families, and whether the portfolio concept generalizes to non-stationary or partially observable settings.
