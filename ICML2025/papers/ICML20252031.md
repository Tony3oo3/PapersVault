## Problem

Current approaches to training LLMs to use test-time compute—fine-tuning on search traces or running RL with sparse 0/1 outcome rewards—do not incentivize the model to make steady progress across its reasoning trace, leading to unnecessarily long outputs on easy problems and an inability to discover solutions on harder, out-of-distribution problems when given larger token budgets. This paper asks whether these methods efficiently allocate test-time compute and whether they would continue to scale as the budget grows, formalizing the question through the lens of meta-reinforcement learning.

## Input / Output

The method takes a math problem as a text prompt and produces a reasoning trace segmented into contiguous 'episodes' (logical blocks of thought, e.g., separated by phrases like 'Wait' or 'Alternatively' in the open-ended setting, or alternating generation/backtracking steps in the structured setting) followed by a final numeric or symbolic answer. A key design choice is that the model is trained to be budget-agnostic: it should make meaningful progress per episode regardless of the total token budget at deployment, rather than being locked into a specific output length. The meta-prover μ, used to compute progress rewards, is instantiated by forcing the model to terminate its thinking block and produce a best-guess answer conditioned on the episodes generated so far.

## Method

MRT (Meta Reinforcement Fine-Tuning) reframes test-time compute optimization as a meta-RL problem where the LLM's output stream is segmented into episodes and the goal is to minimize cumulative regret—the gap between the model's success probability and an oracle comparator—as a function of token budget. The core mechanism is a dense 'progress' reward (Definition 4.1) that measures the change in the meta-prover's likelihood of producing a correct answer before and after each episode, capturing the information gained by that episode. This progress bonus is added to the standard 0/1 outcome reward in two instantiations: an STaR variant that filters self-generated rollouts for both correctness and maximum cumulative progress before SFT, and an RL variant (built on GRPO) that computes prefix rewards via the meta-prover and optimizes the combined reward. The method uses off-policy contexts from a previous checkpoint (π_old) for stability, analogous to trust-region methods, and operates at the episode level rather than the individual token or step level, distinguishing it from prior process-reward approaches.

## Results

On math reasoning benchmarks (AIME 2024/2025, AMC 2023, MinervaMATH, MATH500), MRT achieves a 2–3× relative improvement in pass@1 accuracy over outcome-reward RL (GRPO) when fine-tuning 1.5B base models (e.g., 55.9 vs. 55.0 avg. on DeepScaleR-1.5B; 47.1 vs. 46.0 on R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B). In terms of token efficiency, MRT (RL) requires ~5× fewer tokens on AIME 2024 and ~4× fewer on MATH500 to match the base model's performance, and improves over GRPO by 1.2–1.6× in token efficiency. In the backtracking search setting (Llama-3.1 3B/8B), MRT (STaR) achieves 30% token efficiency gains over STaR and MRT (RL) achieves 38% over GRPO on AIME 1989–2023.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MRT consistently outperforms outcome-reward RL (GRPO) across all benchmarks: on DeepScaleR-1.5B, MRT gains +1.4 avg. vs. GRPO's +0.5 (a ~2.8× relative improvement), and on R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B, MRT gains +2.2 vs. GRPO's +1.1 (exactly 2×). The explicit length-penalty baseline (following Arora & Zanette, 2025) actually degrades performance (−3.7 avg. on DeepScaleR), confirming that naive length constraints sacrifice accuracy. In the backtracking search setting, MRT (STaR) beats RISE (Qu et al., 2024) on both peak accuracy and token efficiency on AIME 1989–2023, while MRT (RL) reduces tokens by 1.6× compared to GRPO. MRT also demonstrates better out-of-distribution robustness: when fine-tuned on only 919 AIME problems, MRT preserves AMC 2023 performance (83.1) while GRPO drops it (81.5). In token-efficiency plots (Figure 7), MRT requires 5× fewer tokens on AIME 2024 and ~4× fewer on MATH500 to match the base model, and improves over GRPO by 1.2–1.6× in tokens-per-accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the STaR self-training pipeline and GRPO reinforcement learning by adding a dense progress reward computed at the episode level, drawing the progress formalism from Setlur et al. (2024b) but shifting it from step-level to episode-level credit assignment. Conceptually, it departs from the dominant outcome-reward RL paradigm (DeepSeek-R1, Kimi) by reframing test-time compute optimization as a meta-RL problem where the LLM must learn an explore-exploit policy over episodes, with cumulative regret as the guiding objective. It positions itself between SCoRe/RISE (too exploitative, constraining each episode by its own outcome) and pure outcome-reward RL (too permissive, reinforcing all intermediate tokens equally), and contrasts with length-penalty approaches that sacrifice accuracy for brevity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled diagnostic (cumulative regret) that reveals SOTA reasoning models fail to make steady progress with additional episodes, and that naive early-termination-plus-majority-voting often outperforms continued reasoning — a finding that challenges the assumption that longer RL-trained traces are monotonically beneficial. The MRT method is practical (plug-and-play with existing GRPO/STaR infrastructure, only 1.01–1.08× FLOPs overhead) and yields 2–3× relative accuracy gains and 1.5–5× token efficiency over outcome-reward RL at the 1.5B–8B scale. Open questions include the optimal meta-prover design, whether models with greater strategy diversity amplify the benefits, and a formal FLOPs-matched comparison at larger scales, since all experiments use relatively small models already trained with RL or distillation.
