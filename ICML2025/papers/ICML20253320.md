## Problem

Human baselines in foundation model evaluations are widely used to support 'super-human' performance claims, yet they are neither sufficiently rigorous nor sufficiently transparent to enable reliable comparisons of human vs. AI performance. Existing baselines commonly suffer from small or biased samples, inconsistent test sets between human and AI evaluation, uncontrolled method effects, and omitted study details, making it difficult to assess whether reported performance differences are meaningful.

## Input / Output

The method consumes two input streams: a purposively sampled set of 29 articles from measurement theory and social sciences (psychology, economics, political science, education) plus AI evaluation literature, and 115 published human baselines extracted from foundation model evaluation papers via keyword-based search and manual screening. The output is a five-stage recommendations framework (design, recruitment, execution, analysis, documentation), a detailed reporting checklist, and per-criterion summary statistics (e.g., percentage of baselines meeting each item) from the systematic review.

## Method

The paper employs a two-stage methodology adapted from prior work: first, a meta-review of measurement theory literature is synthesized into a reporting checklist through internal discussion and expert validation; second, that checklist is applied as a coding instrument in a systematic review of 115 human baselines, with the codebook iteratively refined during coding. The framework is organized around the full baselining lifecycle—design, recruitment, execution, analysis, and documentation—and draws on measurement theory concepts of validity and reliability to operationalize best practices. Key empirical findings include that 41% of baselines used different test sets for humans vs. AI, only 2% conducted power analyses (median sample size was 8), only 14% reported ethics compliance, 88% displayed UI differences between human and AI evaluation, and only 8% performed any statistical test. The authors also address four alternative views (cost, obsolescence, sufficiency of existing baselines, and one-size-fits-all applicability) to scope where maximal rigor is expected versus where transparency and narrowed interpretation suffice.

## Results

The authors systematically reviewed 115 published human baselines in foundation model evaluations using a reporting checklist derived from measurement theory. Key findings: only 2% of baselines reported conducting power analyses (median sample size was just 8, mean 90); only 14% reported ethics review compliance; 41% used different test sets for AI vs. human evaluation; 88% displayed UI differences and 76% had different instructions/prompts between human and AI conditions; and only 8% performed any statistical test. All 115 baselines failed to report at least some items on the checklist, and 78% did not publicly release human baseline responses.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper with a systematic review rather than a method comparison, so there are no traditional baseline-vs-method results. The review reveals that existing human baselines fall short across the entire lifecycle: at the design stage, 41% used mismatched test sets and only 35% iteratively developed instruments; at recruitment, 31% used convenience samples, 32% used crowdsourcing platforms, none used random sampling, and 37% did not report their sampling strategy; at execution, only 23% employed quality controls and 88% had UI discrepancies with the AI evaluation; at analysis, only 37% reported uncertainty estimates and 8% ran statistical tests; and at documentation, only 23% provided detailed baseliner demographics and 21% included paradata such as response times. The most striking gaps are the near-absence of power analyses (2%), ethics reporting (14%), and statistical testing (8%), which the authors argue undermines the reliability of 'super-human' performance claims.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper imports measurement theory from the social sciences (psychology, survey methodology, education) into the AI evaluation context, building on prior calls to apply social science methods to ML benchmarks (Chang et al., 2024b; Wallach et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2025). It extends the partial frameworks of Cowley et al. (2022), who adapted psychology best practices to computer vision baselines, and Tedeschi et al. (2023), who critiqued LLM baseline practices, by covering the full baselining lifecycle (design, recruitment, execution, analysis, documentation) and grounding recommendations in a meta-review of 29 measurement theory articles. The five-stage structure adapts Reuel et al. (2024) and Paskov et al. (2025), but the paper's distinctive contribution is the systematic review of 115 published human baselines using a derived reporting checklist, which no prior work had attempted at this scale.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The systematic review reveals that methodological gaps in human baselines are pervasive and severe: only 2% conducted power analyses, only 14% reported ethics compliance, 88% had UI differences between human and AI evaluation, and the median baseliner sample size was 8. These findings undermine the reliability of widely-cited 'super-human' performance claims and have direct implications for AI governance, risk assessment, and policy decisions that depend on human-AI performance comparisons. Open questions include how to handle inevitable method effects arising from fundamental differences in human vs. AI cognition, whether regularly updated 'living' baselines are feasible, and how to validly simulate or augment human baselines as AI adoption changes human behavior. The paper's scope is limited to foundation model evaluations and does not address construct validity or human-AI interaction studies in depth.
