## Problem

Natural energy gradient descent (ENGD) achieves state-of-the-art accuracy for PINNs, often outperforming ADAM or L-BFGS by orders of magnitude, but requires explicitly computing and storing the full |θ|² change-of-coordinate Gram matrix, making it infeasible for networks beyond a few thousand parameters. This paper asks whether the structural properties of this Gram matrix can be exploited to approximate the natural gradient direction at a fraction of the computational cost while maintaining or improving accuracy.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250147/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a PDE specified by its differential operator, boundary conditions, and source terms, along with a neural network architecture and a set of collocation/quadrature points. Rather than explicitly forming the |θ|² Gram matrix, the algorithm computes the product GM (where M is a random Gaussian sketch matrix of size |θ|×l) directly via quadrature, requiring only O(|θ|(r+p)) memory instead of O(|θ|²). The per-iteration output is a sketched natural gradient direction computed from a truncated randomized eigenvalue decomposition, which drives a line-search parameter update toward the PDE solution.

## Method

SNGD replaces the full Gram matrix pseudoinverse in ENGD with a sketched approximation obtained via randomized singular value decomposition. The key structural insight is that the Gram matrix G is numerically low-rank with exponentially decaying eigenvalues (proven for 1D one-layer networks with analytic activations), which guarantees that single-pass or two-pass RSVD yields a near-optimal low-rank approximation with spectral error bounded by C·λ_{r+1} ≈ 10^{-16}. At each iteration, the algorithm samples a fresh Gaussian sketch matrix, computes GM implicitly through quadrature (avoiding explicit formation of G), performs a truncated eigenvalue decomposition with a tolerance cutoff, and uses the resulting low-rank factorization to compute the sketched natural gradient direction. This implicit computation reduces memory from O(|θ|²) to O(|θ|(r+p)) and per-iteration cost from O(|θ|³) to O(|θ|(r+p)²), enabling training of networks with over a million parameters in minutes while consistently achieving errors an order of magnitude smaller than full ENGD.

## Results

On the heat equation with a 5,301-parameter network, two-pass SNGD achieves a median relative L² error of 1.24×10⁻⁸, outperforming ENGD (5.66×10⁻⁷) by nearly two orders of magnitude and ADAM (6.13×10⁻⁵) by three orders of magnitude. For a 1,085,761-parameter network, SNGD reaches 1.49×10⁻⁷ in ~11 minutes, while ENGD and BFGS are infeasible due to memory constraints. On the transport equation (c=30), a notoriously difficult PINN benchmark, two-pass SNGD converges in all 5 random initializations with median error ~3.78×10⁻⁴, whereas ENGD fails to converge in 3 of 5 runs. Training time for the 5,301-parameter network drops from ~222 minutes (ENGD) to under 2 minutes (SNGD).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against ENGD (Müller & Zeinhofer, 2023), SNGD consistently achieves 1–2 orders of magnitude lower relative L² error across all four test problems (heat, Poisson, non-linear BVP, transport) while reducing training time by a factor of ~100× for the 5,301-parameter network (1.86 min vs. 221.89 min). On the transport equation, ENGD fails to converge in 3 of 5 random initializations (errors >1.0), while SNGD converges in all 5 runs with errors 1–2 orders of magnitude smaller than the successful ENGD runs. Against ADAM (Kingma & Ba, 2015), SNGD improves accuracy by approximately three orders of magnitude (e.g., 1.24×10⁻⁸ vs. 6.13×10⁻⁵ on the 5,301-parameter heat equation) while training in a fraction of the time (1.86 min vs. 44.15 min). Against BFGS (Zhu et al., 1997), SNGD achieves 1–2 orders of magnitude lower error on the heat and Poisson problems with comparable or shorter runtimes, and BFGS is infeasible for the million-parameter network where SNGD completes in ~11 minutes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends Müller & Zeinhofer's (2023) Energy Natural Gradient Descent (ENGD) for PINNs by replacing the full Gram matrix pseudoinverse with a randomized sketch-based approximation. The sketching machinery draws on Halko et al.'s (2011) randomized matrix decomposition framework and Saibaba et al.'s (2016) single-pass/two-pass RSVD, but applies them to a new object: the change-of-coordinate Gram matrix specific to PINN loss geometry. The key departure from prior sketching-for-optimization work (Yang et al. 2022 for empirical Fisher, Gower et al. 2019 for Hessian) is the exploitation of the Gram matrix's exponentially decaying eigenvalue structure, which the paper proves for 1D one-layer networks and verifies empirically across four PDE problems.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SNGD makes natural gradient methods for PINNs practically scalable, reducing training of a ~5,000-parameter network from ~4 hours to under 2 minutes and enabling 1M+ parameter networks that are infeasible for ENGD due to memory. The counterintuitive finding that the sketched approximation consistently outperforms the full natural gradient in accuracy (often by an order of magnitude) suggests that the truncation of near-zero eigenvalues and per-iteration randomness act as beneficial regularization against spectral bias. Open questions include whether the eigenvalue decay structure and accuracy advantage persist in higher-dimensional PDEs, the sensitivity to the truncation tolerance (acknowledged as a practical concern), and whether the mechanism behind the accuracy improvement over ENGD can be more rigorously characterized.
