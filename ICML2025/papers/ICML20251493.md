## Problem

Recovering the reward functions that drive agents' behavior in competitive (zero-sum) games is inherently ill-posed: multiple reward matrices can produce the same equilibrium, and offline data may not cover the full state-action space. While entropy-regularized IRL has been shown to restore identifiability in single-agent settings, the competitive multi-agent case introduces strategic coupling between players that makes identifiability substantially more subtle and had not been formally characterized.

## Input / Output

The method consumes i.i.d. observed action pairs (a^k, b^k) sampled from the true QRE (or full trajectories (s_h, a_h, b_h) in the Markov game setting), along with a user-specified feature kernel φ: A×B → R^d and entropy parameter η. Rather than returning a single point estimate, the output is a confidence set Θ̂_N of all reward parameters θ consistent with the observed QRE within a tolerance κ_N = O(1/N) and a norm bound ‖θ‖ ≤ M, from which feasible payoff matrices Q̂(a,b) = φ(a,b)ᵀθ̂ are recovered for every θ̂ in the set. In the Markov game extension, the algorithm additionally outputs estimated transition kernels via ridge regression.

## Method

Under a linear parametric assumption Q(a,b) = ⟨φ(a,b), θ⟩, the QRE fixed-point equations are algebraically reduced to a linear system [A(ν*); B(μ*)]θ = [c(μ*); d(ν*)], where A and B are constructed from feature differences weighted by the opponent's equilibrium policy. A necessary-and-sufficient rank condition on this (m+n−2)×d coefficient matrix determines strong identifiability; when the rank is deficient, the feasible set is a polytope intersected with a ball, and the algorithm constructs a confidence set via a least-squares residual threshold κ_N = O(1/N). For Markov games, the same per-state linear system is applied at each time step h, and the Bellman equation r_h = Q_h − γE[V_{h+1}] is used to back out reward functions from the feasible Q-function set, with transition kernels estimated by ridge regression on observed next-states. The key insight is that entropy regularization linearizes the otherwise nonlinear QRE constraints, converting the ill-posed inverse problem into a tractable linear feasibility problem with explicit Hausdorff-distance convergence rates of O(N^{-1/2}).

## Results

In the synthetic Markov game experiment (S=4 states, m=n=5 actions, H=6 steps, η=0.5), the reward estimation error (Frobenius norm) decreases from 2.4611 ± 0.1596 at N=10,000 to 1.4398 ± 0.0499 at N=100,000 samples. More importantly, the QRE discrepancy (total variation distance between estimated and true equilibrium strategies) is already small at N=10,000 (7.08×10⁻³) and further decreases to 2.41×10⁻³ at N=100,000, confirming that the recovered reward functions faithfully reproduce the observed equilibrium behavior. The convergence rate is consistent with the theoretical O(N^{-1/2}) bound established in Theorem 3.9.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing inverse game theory or IRL baselines; it is primarily a theoretical contribution establishing identifiability conditions and sample complexity guarantees. The numerical experiments serve as validation of the theoretical predictions rather than a benchmarking exercise. The key empirical finding is that while the absolute reward function error remains non-trivial (≈1.44 at N=100,000), the induced QRE error is an order of magnitude smaller (≈2.4×10⁻³), demonstrating that the confidence set approach captures the behaviorally relevant reward structure even when the pointwise reward values are not tightly pinned down. The paper notes that the framework is adaptable to incorporate MLE as an alternative to frequency estimators, but does not provide a head-to-head comparison between these two estimation strategies in the experiments.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the single-agent identifiability results of Cao et al. (2021) and Rolland et al. (2022)—which showed that entropy-regularized policies transform ill-posed IRL into identifiable problems—to competitive multi-agent settings where strategic interactions make identifiability more subtle. It builds on the QRE framework of McKelvey & Palfrey (1995) and Mertikopoulos & Sandholm (2016) for the entropy-regularized game formulation, and on the linear MDP assumptions of Jin et al. (2020) for the Markov game extension. The key departure from prior inverse game theory work (Lin et al. 2014, Yu et al. 2019) is the unified treatment of both static matrix games and dynamic Markov games under a single confidence-set construction, rather than treating them as separate problems.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first unified theoretical framework that handles the fundamental ill-posedness of inverse games by constructing confidence sets of feasible rewards rather than forcing a single point estimate, with explicit sample complexity guarantees in both static and dynamic settings. Key limitations include the reliance on linear payoff parameterization, the restriction to zero-sum two-player games, and the offline setting requiring sufficient state coverage (Assumption 3.8). Open questions include extending to general-sum games, non-linear payoff functions, partially observable settings, and online learning, all of which the authors identify as future directions. The empirical validation is limited to a single small-scale Markov game (S=4, m=n=5, H=6), leaving open whether the theoretical rates hold in more complex or high-dimensional environments.
