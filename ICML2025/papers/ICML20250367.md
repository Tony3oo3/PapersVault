## Problem

Multi-objective optimization algorithms (e.g., weighted sum, approximation heuristics, local search) contain tunable parameters whose selection is critical to performance, yet parameter tuning in practice is purely empirical with no theoretical guarantees. This paper establishes the first theoretical framework for data-driven multi-objective optimization, providing sample complexity bounds that quantify how many problem instances are needed to learn a good parameter configuration with high probability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250367/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes m i.i.d. problem instances sampled from an unknown domain-specific distribution, a parameterized algorithm A(ρ, I) that maps a parameter ρ and instance I to a d-dimensional objective vector, and a performance metric u(P, I) (e.g., Pareto volume of k algorithm outputs). It produces k parameter values P = (ρ₁, …, ρₖ) maximizing expected performance, accompanied by a formal generalization bound of the form Õ(k log t / ε²) samples, where t is the number of pieces in the algorithm's piecewise-constant structure. In the smoothed setting, t is replaced by its expectation, yielding Õ(k/ε²) bounds independent of problem size.

## Method

The core insight is that parameterized multi-objective optimization algorithms exhibit a piecewise-constant structure: for each instance, the parameter space can be partitioned into finitely many intervals where the algorithm output is constant. The paper bounds sample complexity via pseudo-dimension (worst case) or a smoothed Rademacher complexity with Massart's lemma (smoothed case), where the key quantity is the number of pieces t. For Pareto volume as the metric, the submodularity of Vol enables a (1−1/e)-approximate PAC-learning algorithm via greedy submodular maximization, with a matching hardness result under P≠NP. To handle algorithms where t is exponentially large (local search, LPs, MDPs), the paper applies smoothed analysis: under κ-bounded noise on instance costs or LP coefficients, the expected number of pieces drops to polynomial, reducing sample complexity from Õ(kT/ε²) or Õ(kn/ε²) to Õ(k/ε²). The framework is instantiated for SALT (shallow-light Steiner trees), Ravi-Goemans (bi-criterion MST), vanilla local search, simulated annealing, general LPs via shadow paths, and multi-objective MDPs via the dual LP's shadow path.

## Results

The paper establishes sample complexity bounds for data-driven multi-objective optimization, showing that for algorithms with piecewise-constant structure (e.g., shallow-light Steiner trees, bi-criterion MSTs), the worst-case sample complexity is Õ(k/ε²), while for local search, simulated annealing, general LPs, and MDPs, smoothed analysis reduces the bound from Õ(kT/ε²) or Õ(kn/ε²) to Õ(k/ε²). Empirically, on shallow-light Steiner tree benchmarks from VLSI designs, the ERM-based parameter selection achieves Pareto volumes of 0.1157–0.1639 (k=1 to 12), consistently outperforming the vanilla geometric-series SALT baseline (0.0159–0.1626). The (1−1/e) approximation ratio for Pareto volume maximization is proven tight under P≠NP.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's sole empirical baseline is Vanilla SALT (Chen & Young, 2020), which selects parameter values ρ from a fixed geometric series rather than learning them from data. The ERM-based method (this work) achieves strictly larger Pareto volume at every value of k from 1 to 12. The advantage is most pronounced at small k: at k=1, ERM achieves 0.1157 Pareto volume versus 0.0159 for Vanilla SALT (a ~7.3× improvement), and at k=3 the gap is still 4× (0.1487 vs. 0.0374). As k grows, the gap narrows because the geometric series baseline can approximate the optimal parameter set more closely with more parameters; by k=12 the difference is only 0.0013 (0.1639 vs. 0.1626). This confirms that data-driven parameter selection is most valuable when the number of configurations is limited, which is the practical regime where users cannot afford to enumerate many parameters.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the data-driven algorithm design framework (Gupta & Roughgarden, 2017; Balcan et al., 2018a, 2021a; Sakaue & Oki, 2022) from single-objective to multi-objective optimization, where prior work had no theoretical treatment. The core technical tool—smoothed analysis to reduce exponential piecewise-structure complexity to polynomial—borrows from Spielman & Teng's (2004) analysis of the Simplex method, but is repurposed here to bound the number of parameter intervals where algorithm behavior changes. The paper also draws on the submodularity of Pareto volume to obtain a (1−1/e)-approximate PAC-learning guarantee, and on shadow path results for smoothed LPs to handle linear programming and MDP instances. Unlike Röglin & Teng (2009), which studied smoothed Pareto sets of the problem itself, this work focuses on the behavior of heuristic algorithms that may not produce optimal solutions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first theoretical foundation for data-driven multi-objective algorithm configuration, providing practitioners with sample complexity guarantees that were previously absent (the field relied on empirical tuning). The smoothed analysis results are practically significant: they show that the exponential worst-case piece complexity of local search and LPs collapses to polynomial under mild noise assumptions, which the experiments confirm on VLSI and MDP benchmarks. Open questions include extending smoothed bounds to more than two objectives, handling continuous-state MDPs, and analyzing branch-and-bound solvers for integer programs. The tight (1−1/e) approximation bound for Pareto volume maximization sets a clear ceiling for what ERM can achieve in polynomial time.
