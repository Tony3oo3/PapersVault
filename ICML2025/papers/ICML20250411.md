## Problem

Scaling laws are widely used to predict LLM performance from smaller models, yet there has been little systematic study of how to best estimate them: which models to train, how many, how large, and whether intermediate training checkpoints are useful. Most prior scaling law work fixes a single model family and exhaustively trains models, leaving practitioners without guidance on cost-effective estimation strategies or on how much accuracy to expect.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250411/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes per-checkpoint training losses (negative log-likelihood on held-out data) from multiple models within a scaled family, along with each model's parameter count and token count. It produces five fitted scaling-law parameters (E, A, α, B, β) and a predicted loss for a target model, evaluated via mean absolute relative error (ARE) against the 30%-maximal token family. A key input detail is that intermediate checkpoints (not just final losses) are included, and the very early phase of training (first ~10B tokens) is optionally excluded to avoid noisy, uninformative data points.

## Method

The paper performs a large-scale meta-analysis by collecting 485 pretrained models across 40+ scaled families (1.9M training steps) and fitting over 1,000 scaling laws using the Hoffmann et al. (2022) functional form L̂(f) = e^E + e^A/#params^α + e^B/#toks^β, with parameters estimated via scikit-learn's curve_fit under square loss. The core experimental design systematically varies three axes—number of models, size of the largest model (scale-up factor), and fraction of training tokens used—to isolate their effects on prediction accuracy. Key findings include: (1) fitting on intermediate checkpoints substantially improves ARE over using only final losses, (2) dropping the first ~10B tokens of training removes a noisy, uninformative phase, (3) ~5 models of varying sizes is a robust minimum, and (4) cross-family prediction is feasible by fixing #params scaling parameters from one family and estimating only the intercept and #toks parameters from a single model in the target family. The authors also show via PCA that the five scaling-law parameters have only ~3 effective degrees of freedom, with A–α and B–β pairs exhibiting near-linear coupling across families.

## Results

The paper estimates over 1,000 scaling laws across 485 pretrained models and finds that 4% absolute relative error (ARE) is the best typically achievable, while ARE up to 20% can still distinguish meaningful modeling choices (most published A/B tests report 4–50% changes). Fitting scaling laws to intermediate training checkpoints (rather than only final losses) substantially improves accuracy, with final loss predictable from roughly 1/3 of the way through training. Using a single model from a new family combined with scaling parameters borrowed from another family (e.g., Muennighoff et al.'s values) can yield less than 1% error in some cases (e.g., OLMO 7B predicted from 1B checkpoints), though errors grow for larger extrapolations (OPT: 37% error extrapolating 8.7B→175B).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares scaling law predictions against two simple baselines: (1) the best-performing model in the training set, and (2) the most-compute-consuming model. Both baselines suffer 18% ARE on average across all scaled families, with errors mostly above 10% and almost never below 5%. In contrast, properly fitted scaling laws achieve 4–10% ARE in most settings, representing a 2–4× improvement in prediction accuracy. The paper further shows that including intermediate checkpoints (dropping only the first ~10B tokens) outperforms using only final losses, and that training 5+ smaller models is generally more reliable than training fewer larger ones, though a single badly-behaved model can inflate error by 15%+ even in otherwise well-behaved families.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Hoffmann et al. (2022) Chinchilla scaling law functional form and the Muennighoff et al. (2024) data-constrained scaling analysis, but shifts the question from 'what is the scaling law?' to 'how should we best estimate one?' Prior work typically fixes a single model family and exhaustively trains models to derive a new law; this paper instead performs a large-scale meta-analysis across 40+ scaled families (485 models, 1.9M training steps) to study estimation quality, data selection, and cross-family transfer. It departs from the standard assumption that only final training losses are informative by showing intermediate checkpoints substantially improve fit, and from the assumption that each new architecture requires a full family of models by demonstrating that a single model plus borrowed scaling parameters can sometimes suffice.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The released 485-model dataset and the derived best practices (use intermediate checkpoints, drop the first ~10% of training, train 5+ models of varying sizes, target ~4% ARE as a practical floor) give practitioners a concrete, evidence-based protocol for scaling law estimation under compute constraints. Open questions remain around optimal model-size spacing, accounting for learning rate schedule effects in the functional form, and aggregating information across incomparable model families. The finding that scaling laws may have fewer effective degrees of freedom (3 PCA components capture 99.5% of parameter variance) suggests the standard 5-parameter form may be over-parameterized, pointing toward simpler or differently structured alternatives.
