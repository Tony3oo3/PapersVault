## Problem

Assistance games, which explicitly model the assistant-user interaction as a two-player game with a hidden shared goal, are a principled alternative to RLHF that removes incentives for deception and encourages clarifying behavior. However, they have only been solved in toy settings with fewer than 20 possible goals, because scaling requires both solving intractable sequential decision-making under uncertainty and accurately modeling human behavior. This paper introduces the first scalable approach to solving assistance games in a complex environment with over 10^400 possible goals.

## Input / Output

The assistant observes a 3D block grid (11×10×10, 10 block types per cell), both players' positions and inventories, and the full state-action history, but critically cannot observe the goal structure. It outputs a distribution over 20,000+ possible actions (no-op, 6-directional movement, place/break block at a location with a block type). The network additionally produces a per-cell probability distribution over goal block types and a distribution over the human's next action, both of which are consumed internally by MCTS rather than exposed as final outputs. The reward signal is the change in edit distance between the current grid and the hidden goal, giving +1 for correct and −1 for incorrect place/break actions.

## Method

AssistanceZero extends AlphaZero by adding two extra heads to the recurrent CNN (ResNet backbone with 3D 5×5×5 convolutions plus LSTM layers): a reward-parameter head that predicts the hidden goal structure and a human-action head that predicts the human's next move. These predictions allow MCTS to estimate both the reward and the next state during search, which would otherwise be impossible since the assistant cannot observe θ or the human's action. The key design insight is separating goal prediction from action selection: PPO fails because it must learn both simultaneously from a high-variance shared reward signal, whereas AssistanceZero first learns a goal predictor and then uses it for planning. Training alternates between collecting MCTS-guided rollouts in parallel environments and running SGD on a five-term loss (policy KL toward MCTS, value MSE, reward-parameter NLL, a KL regularizer preventing overfitting to recent goals, and human-action NLL). For the human model, the paper finds that piKL—MCTS with a behavior-cloning prior—best matches real human performance, outperforming both pure reward-based (PPO/AlphaZero) and pure imitation (BC) models.

## Results

In the Minecraft Building Assistance Game (MBAG) with a fixed piKL human model, AssistanceZero achieves 79.8% goal completion while reducing human actions by 42% (158 vs. 203) and building 27% of the goal structure itself, compared to PPO's 71.6% goal, 203 human actions, and 0% assistant contribution. In a human study with 16 participants, the AssistanceZero assistant is rated 3.1±0.4/5 in helpfulness (vs. 1.7±0.3 for SFT and 4.0±0.5 for an expert human) and significantly reduces the number of actions participants need to complete building tasks (one-sided t-test p<0.05). Against pretraining+SFT pipelines, the assistance-game-trained assistant achieves 92.6% goal completion with 179 human actions versus 90.4% and 241 for SFT.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AssistanceZero decisively outperforms PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) as an assistant policy: it increases goal completion from 71.6% to 79.8% and reduces human actions from 203 to 158, while building 27% of the goal structure itself versus PPO's 0%. Even the best PPO variant (with reward engineering and auxiliary losses) only reaches 74.1% goal and 7.2% assistant contribution, a substantial gap. As a human model, AlphaZero (Silver et al., 2017) achieves 6.85 cross entropy with real human actions and builds houses much faster than humans (100% at 10 min vs. 80%), making it a poor predictor of human behavior. In contrast, piKL (Jacob et al., 2022) achieves 1.98 cross entropy and closely matches human building speed (75% at 10 min vs. 80%), making it the best human model for training effective assistants. The paper also shows that assistance-game training outperforms pretraining+SFT pipelines (analogous to Copilot/RLHF-SFT) by 2.2% goal completion and 62 fewer human actions, with the assistance-game assistant building 26% of the goal versus 2.9% for SFT.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends AlphaZero (Silver et al., 2017) by adding reward-parameter and human-action prediction heads to its MCTS + neural network framework, enabling planning under the partial observability inherent in assistance games. It builds on the assistance game formalism of Hadfield-Menell et al. (2016) and Shah et al. (2020), which had previously been limited to 2D gridworlds with fewer than 20 reward parameters, and on piKL (Jacob et al., 2022) for human modeling. The key conceptual departure is from RLHF as the dominant assistant-training paradigm, arguing that explicitly modeling the human as a co-agent in a shared environment removes incentives for deception and encourages uncertainty-aware behavior.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first demonstration that assistance games can be solved at scale (10^400 goals, 3D environment), opening the door to using them as a practical alternative to RLHF for training AI assistants. The human study validates the approach with real users, though a significant gap remains between the AI assistant and an expert human, suggesting the benchmark is still challenging. The paper's vision of applying assistance games to LLM post-training—where the assistant would be incentivized to ask clarifying questions and optimize over hidden test cases—raises important open questions about whether this framework can address RLHF's known failure modes (deception, lack of uncertainty) at the scale of modern language models.
