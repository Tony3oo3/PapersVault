## Problem

Prior generative social choice frameworks assumed perfect query answers and uniform statement costs, making their proportionality guarantees inapplicable when LLMs introduce errors or when statements have varying lengths. This paper addresses both gaps by introducing cost/budget constraints (mirroring participatory budgeting) and establishing proportionality guarantees that gracefully degrade under approximate queries with bounded errors.

## Input / Output

PROSE takes unstructured textual user data (e.g., drug reviews, Polis deliberation comments) and a single target slate length B (in words) as input, requiring no structured surveys or pre-rated statements. It outputs a slate of text statements whose total word count does not exceed B, where each statement's cost is its length in words. The candidate universe is effectively infinite (all well-formed statements), accessed only through LLM-mediated discriminative and generative queries rather than a fixed candidate set.

## Method

The core algorithm (DemocraticProcess) iteratively builds a slate by sweeping over utility levels and cost values: at each (ℓ, x) pair it calls a generative query to find the statement approved by the most agents at level ℓ within cost x, then adds it to the slate if enough agents 'afford' it (|S| ≥ ⌈c(α)·n/B⌉). Two query types are defined with explicit error parameters: discriminative queries predict utilities up to additive error β, while generative queries allow multiplicative error γ in supporter count, additive error δ in utility, and multiplicative error μ in cost. The main theoretical result shows the Complex variant (iterating over all costs and a range of utility levels) achieves (2β+δ, 1/(γμ))-cBJR, with matching lower bounds. In practice, PROSE implements both queries via GPT-4o, using multiple generation strategies (embedding-based nearest-neighbor grouping, weighted sampling, and LLM consensus writing) to produce diverse candidate statements per query call, and a two-score discriminative query (agreement minus specificity penalty) to calibrate utility predictions across statements of varying length.

## Results

PROSE consistently outperforms all four baselines across all four datasets (Birth Control Uniform, Birth Control Imbalanced, Obesity, Bowling Green). On mean CoT utility (scale 1–6), PROSE achieves 3.39–3.87, compared to 2.54–3.28 for the best baseline, representing 10–40% relative improvements that are statistically significant (p < 0.05 in most cases). On the 25th-percentile utility, PROSE reaches 3.00–3.12 versus 2.00–2.80 for baselines (~40% improvement), indicating strong representation of minority opinions. On proportionality, PROSE exhibits 7–49 percentage points fewer cBJR violations than any baseline (e.g., 0.05 on Bowling Green vs. 0.37 for Clustering).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PROSE outperforms all four baselines on every metric across all four datasets. Against PROSE-UnitCost (which follows Fish et al.'s original unit-cost assumption and produces slates 2–3× longer than the budget), PROSE achieves higher mean utility (e.g., 3.87 vs. 3.11 on Birth Control Imbalanced) and substantially fewer cBJR violations (0.14 vs. 0.34), showing that the cost-aware budget allocation is critical. Against the Clustering baseline (affinity propagation on text-embedding-3-large vectors followed by per-cluster LLM generation), PROSE wins on mean utility by 0.11–0.75 points and on cBJR violations by 0.04–0.32 percentage points, with the largest gap on Bowling Green (0.05 vs. 0.37). The two zero-shot baselines (with and without agent descriptions) perform worst overall: Contextless Zero-Shot achieves the lowest mean utility on three of four datasets (2.54–3.15) and the highest cBJR violation rates (0.19–0.68), while Zero-Shot is slightly better but still trails PROSE by 0.33–0.78 in mean utility. Notably, PROSE's advantage is most pronounced at the 25th percentile, where baselines frequently assign agents to statements they barely approve (Q1 = 2.00), whereas PROSE maintains Q1 ≥ 3.00, confirming its ability to represent minority viewpoints proportionally.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the generative social choice framework of Fish et al. (2024), which first proposed using LLMs to implement discriminative and generative queries for producing representative slates of statements. The extension is twofold: it introduces statement costs and a global budget constraint (mirroring the shift from committee elections to participatory budgeting in social choice), and it replaces the assumption of perfect query answers with approximate queries carrying explicit error parameters (β, γ, δ, μ). The theoretical analysis draws on the proportionality axiom hierarchy (BJR, JR, EJR) from computational social choice, while the implementation (PROSE) builds on prior work using GPT-4o to model human preferences and on embedding-based clustering for identifying cohesive agent groups.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By providing proportionality guarantees that gracefully degrade as a function of LLM query error—and proving near-optimality via matching lower bounds—this work moves generative social choice from a proof-of-concept toward a deployable framework with formal fairness guarantees under realistic (imperfect) LLM behavior. Key open questions include whether the approach extends to ordinal preferences, how to improve the generative query for highly diverse agent populations, and whether the framework can be applied to real participatory budgeting elections where 'statements' become projects with nontrivial costs. The paper also flags important deployment risks: LLM opacity, potential bias in utility prediction, and vulnerability to prompt injection attacks.
