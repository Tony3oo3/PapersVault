## Problem

Karger's and Karger-Stein algorithms are the de facto practical approaches for global minimum cut, yet each instance is solved from scratch, discarding information from similar prior instances. This paper asks how machine-learned predictions about which edges belong to the minimum cut can be used to warm-start these randomized edge-contraction algorithms, and how robust the resulting speedups are to prediction errors.

## Input / Output

The algorithm takes a weighted undirected graph G=(V,E,w) and per-edge real-valued predictions p_e ∈ [0,1] (or equivalently, a binary predicted cut set) as input, and outputs the global minimum cut as an edge set. Prediction quality is measured asymmetrically: η captures the fraction of true cut edges missed (false negatives) and ρ captures the fraction of non-cut edges wrongly included (false positives), both normalized by the true cut weight. Two algorithm parameters—a scaling factor B and a vertex threshold t—control the degree of boosting and the point at which the algorithm reverts to the unboosted baseline.

## Method

The core mechanism reweights each edge e to w_B(e) = (1+(B-1)(1-p_e))w(e), amplifying the contraction probability of edges unlikely to be in the minimum cut so that the true cut survives longer. For Boosted Karger's, this reweighting is applied while more than t vertices remain, after which standard Karger's algorithm takes over; the success probability improves from Ω(1/n²) to Ω(1/(n^{2η} ρ^{2(1-η)})), eliminating an entire factor of n when predictions are good. For the Karger-Stein analogue, the paper adapts the FPZ branching-contraction framework, modifying both the edge-contraction distribution and the recursive branching probability q_n to exploit the improved survival rate, yielding a runtime that interpolates between Karger-Stein's O(n² log²n) and near-linear O(m) depending on η. A learning algorithm uses online gradient descent on a convex relaxation of the runtime upper bound (replacing the non-convex ρ term with a free variable b optimized via grid search) to compute near-optimal predictions from past instances in polynomial time and sample complexity.

## Results

The Boosted Karger's algorithm achieves a minimum cut recovery probability of Ω(1/(n^{2η}·ρ^{2(1-η)})), compared to Ω(1/n²) for standard Karger's, eliminating an entire factor of n from the runtime when predictions are good. The Boosted FPZ algorithm runs in O(m^{1-η}·n^{2η}·log n) time when ρ=O(√m), interpolating between Karger-Stein's O(n²log²n) and near-linear O(m) time. In experiments on synthetic graphs (n=600), Boosted Karger's outperforms standard Karger's by two orders of magnitude (≈100×) when η≤0.5 and ρ∈{0,10}, and still by one order of magnitude (≈10×) even with ρ=100 and η∈[0,0.6]. On TSP-derived instances and three real Network Repository datasets, the boosted variant consistently requires fewer repetitions to recover the minimum cut.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is standard Karger's algorithm (Karger, 1993), compared across all three experimental settings. On synthetic bipartite graphs (n=600, k=100, ℓ=10), Boosted Karger's requires roughly 100× fewer repetitions than Karger's when η≤0.5 and ρ∈{0,10}, and still ~10× fewer when ρ=100 and η∈[0,0.6]. On TSP subtour-elimination instances (n=500, k=50, ε=0.5), the boosted variant achieves an order-of-magnitude improvement specifically on the harder instances where Karger's needs many repetitions, while matching or slightly exceeding Karger's on easier instances. On three real Network Repository datasets, Boosted Karger's consistently requires fewer trials across all 100 repeated runs, with the gap most pronounced on denser graphs. The paper does not compare against Karger-Stein or the near-linear deterministic algorithms (Henzinger et al., 2024) empirically, as those are noted to be unimplemented in practice; the comparison is purely against the practical Karger's baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the learning-augmented algorithms framework—previously applied mainly to online settings for solution-quality improvements (Lykouris & Vassilvitskii, 2021) and more recently to runtime improvements for matching, shortest paths, and max-flow (Dinitz et al., 2021; Davies et al., 2023)—to the offline, batch setting of global minimum cut. Methodologically, it builds directly on Karger's randomized edge-contraction algorithm (1993) and the FPZ branching-contraction variant (Fox et al., 2019; Karger & Williamson, 2021), modifying the edge-contraction distribution via prediction-based reweighting. A key conceptual departure from prior prediction-augmented work is the introduction of an asymmetric error measure (separating false negatives η from false positives ρ), in contrast to the symmetric error metrics standard in the literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that predictions can eliminate an entire factor of n from Karger's runtime (rather than merely providing a linear speedup) is a strong theoretical signal that learning-augmented approaches can yield superlinear gains in randomized combinatorial algorithms. The robustness guarantee—that the algorithm degrades gracefully to standard Karger's performance even under very poor predictions—makes the approach practically deployable without precise error calibration. Key open questions include designing algorithms that are oblivious to the error parameters η and ρ (currently required for parameter setting), exploring alternative prediction models (e.g., vertex partitions rather than edge subsets), and extending the framework to other randomized combinatorial optimization problems.
