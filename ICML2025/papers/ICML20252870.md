## Problem

Classical preference learning requires sample size n to scale linearly with feature dimension d (minimax rate Θ(d/n)), which is prohibitive when feature spaces have thousands to millions of dimensions and human-annotated preference data is expensive. Although human preferences are often driven by only a few critical factors, the theoretical impact of sparsity on preference learning estimation rates had not been studied. This paper is the first to establish minimax bounds and analyze sparsity-aware estimators in the preference learning setting.

## Input / Output

The method consumes n fixed-design pairwise comparison samples (x_{0,i}, x_{1,i}, y_i), where x vectors are d-dimensional feature vectors from a bounded feature space and y_i ∈ {0,1} is the binary preference label generated by a random utility model (e.g., BTL or TM). The output is an estimated reward parameter θ̂ that is k-sparse in d dimensions, from which reward scores r(x) = ⟨θ̂, x⟩ are computed for any feature vector. Estimation error is measured in the data-induced semi-norm ‖·‖_Σ, which depends on the Gram matrix of the observed feature differences and yields distribution-free error rates.

## Method

The paper formulates a sparse random utility model where the ground-truth reward parameter θ* satisfies ‖θ*‖₀ ≤ k and ‖θ*‖₂ ≤ B, then establishes an information-theoretic minimax lower bound of Ω((k/n)log(d/k)) via a Fano-style packing argument over sparse binary vectors. An ℓ₀-constrained maximum likelihood estimator is shown to achieve the matching upper bound Θ((k/n)log(d/k)) under strong log-concavity of the link function F (satisfied by BTL and TM models), serving as a theoretical benchmark. For computational tractability, an ℓ₁-regularized MLE (LASSO-style) with penalty β‖θ‖₁ is analyzed: it achieves O(√((k/n)log d)) unconditionally and the nearly minimax-optimal O((k/n)log d) under a restricted eigenvalue condition on the Gram matrix, with β scaling as n^{-0.5}. Empirically, ℓ₁ regularization applied to the final layer of pretrained LLMs (Pythia-70M, Llama-3.2-1B) for reward modeling on rm-static and SHP datasets yields 0.9–3% accuracy improvements over unregularized baselines, with learned sparsity ratios around 4–7%.

## Results

Theoretically, the paper establishes that the minimax optimal estimation error rate for sparse preference learning is Θ(k/n·log(d/k)), improving upon the non-sparse Θ(d/n) rate. The ℓ₁-regularized estimator achieves a nearly minimax-optimal rate of O((k/n)log d) under a restricted eigenvalue condition on the Gram matrix. Empirically, on the rm-static dataset with full fine-tuning, ℓ₁ regularization improves test accuracy by ~0.9–1.0% across Pythia-70M (60.5→61.8%), Llama-3.2-1B (66.8→67.7%), and Llama-3.2-3B (68.4→69.4%). In frozen backbone training (d=2048), the ℓ₁-regularized method improves accuracy by at least 3% over the unregularized baseline on both rm-static and SHP datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the unregularized maximum likelihood estimator (β=0), which is the standard approach in preference learning and reward modeling. The ℓ₁-regularized estimator consistently outperforms this baseline across all model sizes and both datasets (rm-static and SHP) in both full fine-tuning and frozen backbone settings. In full fine-tuning, the improvement is modest (~0.9–1.3%), while in frozen backbone training the gain is substantially larger (≥3%), suggesting sparsity is most beneficial when the model capacity is constrained. The paper does not compare against other sparse estimation methods (e.g., group lasso, elastic net) or alternative preference learning formulations (e.g., DPO), so the empirical evidence is limited to a single ablation-style comparison. Theoretically, the ℓ₀-constrained estimator is shown to be minimax optimal but computationally intractable, while the ℓ₁-regularized estimator is the practical alternative that achieves a near-optimal rate.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper transplants the sparse estimation framework from compressed sensing and high-dimensional statistics (LASSO, restricted eigenvalue conditions) into the preference learning setting. It extends the non-sparse minimax analysis of Shah et al. (2016) and Zhu et al. (2023), which established Θ(d/n) rates for maximum likelihood estimation under the random utility model, by introducing a k-sparsity assumption on the reward parameter and showing the rate improves to Θ(k log(d/k)/n). The ℓ₁-regularized estimator is a direct adaptation of LASSO (Tibshirani, 1996) to the pairwise-comparison likelihood, and the proof techniques (Fano's inequality, Bernstein concentration for sub-Gaussian quadratic forms) are standard tools from the sparse linear regression literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first theoretical treatment of sparsity in preference learning, providing a principled justification for why ℓ₁ regularization should help in reward modeling when the true reward depends on few features. The practical relevance to RLHF is clear: human preference data is expensive, and if the reward is sparse, sample complexity drops from linear in d to linear in k log(d/k). Open questions include whether the sparse reward model induces optimal RLHF policies, whether the framework extends to DPO (which bypasses explicit reward modeling), and whether the restricted eigenvalue condition needed for the fast rate holds for real LLM feature representations. The empirical gains are modest (0.9–3% accuracy), suggesting the theoretical promise may not yet be fully realized at scale.
