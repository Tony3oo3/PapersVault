## Problem

Existing CIR methods rely on contrastive learning that treats all non-target images in a batch as negatives, inadvertently penalizing false negatives (relevant images that are not the annotated target). This causes models to retrieve irrelevant images alongside the target, reducing user satisfaction even when the target is found. Additionally, standard Recall@k evaluation only verifies target retrieval and ignores the relevance of other images in the result set.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251260/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a reference image (resized to 224×224 with 1.25 padding ratio) and a relative text query describing desired modifications, processed jointly through BLIP-2's ViT-L image encoder and Q-Former cross-attention to produce a bi-modal query embedding. The output is a ranked list of images from a corpus, scored by the inner product of the query embedding and each image embedding divided by a learned temperature. During training, only a single hard negative is sampled per query per epoch from a dynamically defined hard negative set, rather than using all in-batch negatives.

## Method

QURE replaces the standard contrastive loss with a Bradley-Terry reward model objective that optimizes the probability of ranking the target image above a single sampled hard negative, minimizing the NLL (equivalently, KL divergence to a target distribution). The key innovation is a query-specific hard negative sampling strategy: the corpus is periodically sorted by relevance scores from the current model, and hard negatives are defined as images falling between the two largest drops in relevance scores occurring after the target image, ensuring they are less relevant than the target (C1) yet still semantically close (C2). The hard negative set is redefined every ⌊n_epoch/n_def⌋ epochs (n_def=6), with a warm-up phase where all non-target images initially serve as negatives. This is built on BLIP-2, fine-tuning only the Q-Former module, and the approach is validated on FashionIQ, CIRR, CIRCO (zero-shot), and a newly introduced HP-FashionIQ dataset with human preference annotations.

## Results

On FashionIQ, QURE achieves the best average Recall@10 of 52.60 and Recall@50 of 73.48, outperforming the previous best (SPRC) by 1.74 and 0.07 points respectively. On CIRR, QURE attains 52.22 Recall@1, 82.53 Recall@5, and 90.31 Recall@10, surpassing SPRC by 1.47 and 1.95 points on R@1 and R@5. On the newly introduced HP-FashionIQ dataset, QURE achieves the highest human-preference alignment at 74.55% preference rate. In zero-shot evaluation on CIRCO, QURE reaches 23.22 mAP@5, outperforming the second-best CoVR-BLIP by 4.87 mAP.

## Baselines & Comparisons

QURE outperforms all six main baselines on both FashionIQ and CIRR. Against the strongest competitor SPRC, QURE gains +1.74 on FashionIQ avg R@10 (52.60 vs 50.86) and +1.47/+1.95 on CIRR R@1/R@5 (52.22/82.53 vs 50.75/80.58), though it slightly trails SPRC on CIRR Recall_S@1 (78.51 vs 79.57), which the authors attribute to false negatives being allowed to score above the target by design. Against CoVR-BLIP, QURE improves CIRR R@1 by 12.46 points (52.22 vs 39.76) and FashionIQ avg R@10 by 4.07 points. On the HP-FashionIQ human-preference benchmark, QURE achieves the best preference rate (74.55%), narrowly edging out CLIP4CIR (74.45%) and MGUR (73.99%), while substantially outperforming Bi-BLIP4CIR (67.33%). In zero-shot CIRCO evaluation, QURE leads by 4.87 mAP@5 over CoVR-BLIP (23.22 vs 18.35) and 5.65 over SPRC (23.22 vs 17.57). With a matched BLIP-2 backbone, QURE also surpasses CoVR-2 on CIRR R@1 by 9.42 points (52.22 vs 42.80), confirming gains are not due to backbone differences.

## Relation to Prior Work

QURE builds on the BLIP-2 vision-language architecture (Q-Former + ViT-L image encoder) but departs fundamentally from the contrastive learning paradigm that dominates CIR (CLIP4CIR, Bi-BLIP4CIR, SPRC, CoVR-BLIP, MGUR), replacing batch-level InfoNCE with a Bradley-Terry reward model objective that pairs each positive with a single sampled hard negative. Its hard negative sampling strategy redefines the classical HCL/UnReMix conditions (different class + close embedding) for the label-free CIR setting by exploiting the geometry of sorted relevance scores—specifically, the transition zone between two sharp drops after the target. The HP-FashionIQ dataset extends the CIR evaluation landscape by introducing human preference annotations over retrieved sets, a dimension absent from all prior CIR benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper exposes a meaningful gap between target-retrieval accuracy (Recall@k) and user satisfaction, showing that models ranking higher on Recall@k (e.g., SPRC) can align worse with human preferences than lower-accuracy models (e.g., CLIP4CIR). This reframes what 'good' CIR means and motivates preference-aware training objectives beyond contrastive loss. Limitations include reliance on periodic full-corpus re-ranking for hard negative selection (costly at scale), evaluation restricted to fashion and general-domain datasets, and the modest preference-rate margin (74.55% vs. 74.45%) that leaves open whether the reward model objective generalizes to multi-negative or open-vocabulary settings.
