## Problem

AI systems make decisions with systemic societal impacts, yet there is no shared language or structured framework to define what 'democratic AI' means in practice, measure how much decision-making power has been transferred to democratic processes, or evaluate whether a given democratic system is adequate for the level of authority it holds. Early experiments (e.g., Anthropic's Collective Constitutional AI, Meta's Community Forums) lack a common vocabulary for describing and comparing their democratic credentials.

## Input / Output

The framework takes as input a decision context (scope of authority, affected populations, the unilateral authority's organizational and legal profile) and a description of the democratic process or system in place. It produces two structured outputs: a Democracy Level classification (L0–L5) indicating which of five roles—informing, specifying, making binding decisions, automatic triggering, and metagovernance—are performed by democratic systems versus the unilateral authority, and a Democratic System Card that scores the system across three dimension categories (process quality, delegation, trust) with specific sub-dimensions such as representation, robustness, legibility, and buy-in. The Levels Decision Tool additionally outputs a recommended target level based on context-dependent questions about legitimacy needs, collective intelligence benefits, viability, and speed constraints.

## Method

The Democracy Levels Framework decomposes democratic AI into two orthogonal axes: Levels (L0–L5), which capture the degree of decision-making power transferred from a unilateral authority to democratic systems by specifying which of five escalating roles (informing decisions, specifying decisions, making binding decisions, automatically triggering binding processes, and metagovernance) are delegated, and Dimensions, which capture the quality of the democratic system across process quality (representation, informedness, deliberation, substantiveness, robustness, legibility), delegation (integration, ability to bind, commitment), and trust (awareness, participation, accountability, buy-in). Two operational tools ground the framework: the Levels Decision Tool, a set of targeted context-dependent questions that guide unilateral authorities and advocates in selecting an appropriate target level, and the Democratic System Card, a structured assessment template analogous to model cards that documents and evaluates a democratic system's fitness for a given level. The design draws explicitly on SAE International's autonomy levels for self-driving cars (shifting power from human driver to autonomous system) and existing democratic evaluation frameworks (Arnstein's ladder, V-Dem, IAP2 spectrum), while remaining agnostic to specific democratic theories.

## Results

This is a position paper proposing a conceptual framework rather than reporting quantitative experimental results. The core contribution is the Democracy Levels Framework, which defines six discrete levels (L0–L5) capturing the degree of decision-making power transferred from a unilateral authority to a democratic system, along with three dimension categories (Process Quality, Delegation, Trust) comprising 13 specific evaluation dimensions. The paper also introduces two practical tools: the Levels Decision Tool (a set of context-dependent questions for choosing an appropriate target level) and the Democratic System Card (a structured assessment template analogous to model cards). No datasets are evaluated and no quantitative benchmarks are reported.

**Figure 2: Overview of the Democracy Levels (L0–L5), assessing how much decision-making power has been transferred from a unilateral authority to a democratic process. Example domain: rules governing AI persuasion.**

| Level | Roles Performed by Democratic System(s) | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| L0 | — | Unilateral decision-making; all formal authority lies with the unilateral authority. | Rules on AI persuasion are simply created by the unilateral authority. |
| L1 | Informing decisions | Outputs inform the unilateral authority; processes initiated ad-hoc with remit chosen by the authority. | Process outputs recommendations, interpreted by the authority for implementation. |
| L2 | Specifying decisions | Outputs a fully specified decision implemented by default unless amended/vetoed via predetermined criteria. | Rules implemented as-is, unless amended or vetoed. |
| L3 | Making binding decisions | Outputs are binding and cannot be vetoed (within remit and feasibility). | Rules implemented as-is (unless pre-established process finds infeasibility). |
| L4 | Initiating processes | Authority pre-commits to automatic initiation of binding processes when conditions are met. | Processes run yearly or whenever a new pretrained model is deployed. |
| L5 | Metagovernance | Authority shifts power to an adaptive constitutional order with checks and balances over when/how processes are used. | Decisions on when to trigger processes are themselves under democratic control. |

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not empirically compare against baselines or report head-to-head performance metrics. Instead, it positions the Democracy Levels Framework relative to several existing conceptual frameworks: Arnstein's Ladder of Citizen Participation (1969), the IAP2 Public Participation Spectrum, V-Dem and Global State of Democracy indices for measuring democracy, and SAE International's autonomy levels for self-driving cars (which directly inspired the L0–L5 structure). The authors also relate their work to the broader literatures on democratic AI (CIP, 2024), participatory AI (Delgado et al., 2023), pluralistic alignment (Sorensen et al., 2024), and public AI (Vincent et al., 2023), arguing that their framework provides a more granular, actionable, and evaluation-oriented structure than these prior efforts. The paper applies the framework illustratively to real-world cases (Anthropic's Collective Constitutional AI at ~L1, Meta's Oversight Board at L4 for individual decisions but L1 for policy opinions, Meta's Community Forums at L1) to demonstrate its descriptive utility, but does not benchmark against alternative evaluation methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes deliberative democracy theory (Fishkin, Landemore, Dryzek), existing democratic evaluation frameworks (Arnstein's ladder, V-Dem, IAP2 spectrum), and the SAE autonomy-levels taxonomy for self-driving cars into a unified governance framework for AI. It positions itself relative to early democratic AI experiments (Anthropic's Collective Constitutional AI, Meta's Community Forums, OpenAI's democratic grant inputs, Google DeepMind's STELA) by providing the missing evaluative vocabulary to classify and compare them. The framework also connects to the broader participatory AI, pluralistic alignment, and human-centered AI literatures, arguing that a structured levels-and-dimensions approach can clarify how these overlapping efforts relate to one another.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a concrete, actionable map for organizations navigating the transition from unilateral to democratically-informed AI decision-making, with practical tools (Levels Decision Tool, Democratic System Card) that can be used for both internal planning and external accountability. Key open questions include whether the levels are granular enough for rapidly evolving AI capabilities, how to handle transnational scope of authority, and whether the framework can be empirically validated as democratic processes mature. The paper acknowledges that the framework is intentionally agnostic on which decisions *should* be made democratically, leaving that normative question to stakeholders.
