## Problem

Empirical studies show that Q-learning agents in pricing games learn to sustain collusive prices, but prior theoretical analyses were restricted to memoryless, averaged, time-continuous formulations that cannot capture retaliatory strategies. This paper provides the first proof that standard stochastic self-play ε-greedy Q-learning with one-step memory provably converges from an always-defect policy to the cooperative Pavlov (win-stay, lose-shift) policy in the iterated prisoner's dilemma.

## Input / Output

The agent observes a state consisting of the pair of actions played in the previous time step (CC, DD, CD, DC) and selects an action (cooperate or defect) via an ε-greedy policy over a Q-table of 8 entries. The critical input constraint is an optimistic initialization of Q-values (Assumption 3.1), requiring certain entries to be set above their optimal values to trigger the defection-to-cooperation transition. The output is the learned Q-table and resulting policy, which provably converges to the Pavlov strategy: cooperate after synchronized states (CC, DD) and defect after desynchronized states (CD, DC).

## Method

The proof decomposes the learning trajectory into three phases—always defect → lose-shift → Pavlov—with linear convergence at rate O(1/α) in each phase. For the fully greedy case (ε=0), convergence follows directly from tracking which Q-table entries are updated in each phase. The main technical contribution is extending this to ε>0 via a high-probability argument: the authors bound the number of non-greedy actions (at most k in T steps) and show that with probability 1−δ, the Q-values still track the deterministic trajectory closely enough to reach the cooperative fixed point, requiring α ≤ C·log(1/ε)/log(1/δ). Empirical validation extends the results to deep Q-networks, confirming the same three-phase transition occurs with neural network parameterization.

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 3.3) proves that for α ≤ C·log(1/ε)/log(1/δ) with C independent of γ, α, rewards, and initialization, self-play ε-greedy Q-learning achieves the cooperative Pavlov policy with probability 1−δ in O(1/α) iterations. Empirically (Figure 4, g=1.8, γ=0.6), cooperation probability approaches 100% for small α and ε (e.g., α=0.01, ε=0.01), while no cooperation is achieved when ε=0.2 (Pavlov is no longer a stable fixed point). The convergence is linear in each of the three phases (always defect → lose-shift → Pavlov), with phase transition times scaling as O(1/α). Deep Q-learning (Figure 5) reproduces the same qualitative behavior, reaching the Pavlov policy around iteration 6000 after initialization against a random agent.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing learning algorithms in a standard benchmark sense; its contribution is a theoretical guarantee absent from prior work. The key contrast is with memoryless Q-learning (Banchio & Mantegazza, 2022), which proved that no cooperative fixed point exists when agents have no access to previous states — the present paper shows that adding one-step memory fundamentally changes the landscape, making the Pavlov (win-stay, lose-shift) policy both a fixed point of the self-play Bellman equation and provably reachable from always-defect initialization. The empirical validation extends the guarantee to deep Q-networks (two linear layers with ReLU), confirming that the collusion phenomenon is not an artifact of tabular Q-learning. The paper also notes that the self-play assumption is currently essential for the proof; relaxing it (as in Barfuss & Meylahn, 2023) still yields cooperation 'most of the time' but requires analyzing stationary distributions, which is left as future work.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the theoretical analysis of Q-learning dynamics in the iterated prisoner's dilemma from the memoryless, averaged, continuous-time setting studied by Banchio & Mantegazza (2022) and Banchio & Skrzypacz (2022) to the standard stochastic, discrete-time algorithm with one-step memory. The key departure is that memory enables game-theoretic collusion (subgame-perfect equilibria like Pavlov), whereas the memoryless case admits no cooperative fixed point. The convergence proof leverages the optimistic initialization framework of Even-Dar & Mansour (2001) and the standard Q-learning update of Watkins & Dayan (1992), but applies them to a self-play multi-agent Bellman equation with multiple fixed points, requiring a three-phase trajectory analysis (always defect → lose-shift → Pavlov).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This provides the first rigorous theoretical explanation for the empirically-observed algorithmic collusion phenomenon documented in economics (Calvano et al., 2019; Assad et al., 2024), showing that standard Q-learning provably converges to a cooperative subgame-perfect equilibrium under explicit conditions. The major limitation is the self-play assumption, which is crucial for the proof; relaxing it to heterogeneous agents would require analyzing stationary distributions and is left as future work. Open questions include whether the result extends to policy gradient methods, deeper architectures beyond the small DQN tested, and richer environments like Bertrand oligopoly games. The practical implication is that antitrust regulators should consider the learning dynamics of deployed pricing algorithms, not just their equilibrium outcomes.
