## Problem

Self-supervised learning methods rely on cosine similarity, which appears to make embedding norms irrelevant since only directions on the hypersphere enter the loss. However, prior empirical observations suggested norms affect convergence speed and encode model confidence, without any theoretical explanation. This paper resolves the apparent contradiction by showing that norms play a dual role: they inversely scale gradients (causing quadratic convergence slowdown) and naturally encode network certainty through differential gradient accumulation.

## Input / Output

The SSL models consume augmented image pairs (two views of the same image) for contrastive methods like SimCLR, or a single image with a momentum/stop-gradient branch for non-contrastive methods like SimSiam. The network produces an unnormalized embedding vector in R^d (256-dim for SimCLR, 2048-dim for SimSiam), from which the paper extracts both the normalized direction (for the loss and kNN evaluation) and the raw norm magnitude (as a confidence/novelty signal). Evaluation uses kNN classification accuracy on normalized embeddings, with the norm itself serving as an auxiliary output for OOD detection and uncertainty estimation.

## Method

The paper proves that cosine-similarity-based gradients are inversely scaled by the embedding norm and lie in the tangent space, meaning every gradient step must increase the norm (Corollary 3.3) and convergence slows quadratically with norm magnitude (Theorem 3.4). This creates an 'embedding-norm effect' catch-22: small norms are needed for fast training, but optimizing the SSL loss grows them. To mitigate this, the paper proposes three interventions: (1) appropriate weight decay to counteract norm growth, (2) cut-initialization (dividing all network weights by a constant c at init to start with small norms), and (3) a GradScale layer that multiplies each sample's gradient by ||z_i||^p in the backward pass to cancel the norm-dependent scaling. Empirically, these interventions yield 1-10% kNN accuracy improvements, with the largest gains on class-imbalanced datasets where norm variance destabilizes training. The paper further demonstrates that the norm naturally encodes confidence: embeddings for frequent/prototypical samples grow larger, while OOD and ambiguous samples retain smaller norms, a property that holds across SimCLR, SimSiam, BYOL, and MoCo.

## Results

On standard (balanced) datasets, cut-initialization improves SimCLR kNN accuracy by 1.5–3.5% (e.g., 56.6→60.1 on Cifar-100, 59.4→60.9 on ImageNet-100) and SimSiam by 0.3–5.2% (e.g., 62.0→67.2 on ImageNet-100). The gains are substantially larger under class imbalance: SimSiam on Cifar-100 Unbalanced jumps from 21.6% to 31.5% (+9.9%), and SimCLR on Flowers102 Unbalanced improves from 42.6% to 61.6% (+19.0%). GradScale (p=1) provides comparable or slightly better gains on balanced data (e.g., 61.0% on ImageNet-100 for SimCLR) but fails to converge on non-contrastive models and ImageNet-100. MoCo V3 benefits from cut-initialization (54.8→58.8 Top-1 on ImageNet-100), while Dino, which does not use cosine similarity, shows no benefit (43.7→29.0).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper applies its interventions (cut-initialization and GradScale) on top of five existing SSL methods. Against default SimCLR [Chen et al., 2020], cut-initialization (c=3) yields +3.5% on Cifar-100 and +1.5% on ImageNet-100, while GradScale achieves +1.6% and +1.6% respectively. Against default SimSiam [Chen & He, 2021], cut-initialization (c=9) provides the largest single-dataset gain of +5.2% on ImageNet-100 (62.0→67.2) and +0.8% on Cifar-100. Against MoCo V2 [Chen et al., 2020b] and MoCo V3 [Fan et al., 2021], cut-initialization (c=3) improves Top-1 accuracy by +5.1% (38.7→43.8) and +4.0% (54.8→58.8) on ImageNet-100. In contrast, Dino [Caron et al., 2021] does not use cosine similarity in its objective and therefore does not benefit: cut-initialization actually degrades its Top-1 accuracy from 43.7% to 29.0%, confirming the effect is specific to cos.sim.-based losses. Under class-imbalanced settings (Table 5), the advantages over default baselines are amplified to double-digit gains, particularly for SimSiam on Cifar-100 Unbalanced (+9.9%) and Flowers102 Unbalanced (+17.4%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the gradient analysis of cosine similarity from Wang et al. (2017) and Zhang et al. (2020) to the full InfoNCE loss used in contrastive SSL, and provides the first systematic treatment of how embedding norms interact with SSL training dynamics. Prior work noted the norm-gradient inverse relationship but largely dismissed it as a minor implementation detail; here the authors show it is a first-order effect that quadratically slows convergence and creates a fundamental catch-22 (norms must be small for fast training, yet the loss itself grows them). The confidence interpretation builds on empirical observations by Kirchhof et al. (2022) and Scott et al. (2021) but supplies the missing mechanistic explanation via Corollary 3.3 (gradient updates are tangent to the embedding, so they must increase its norm).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper resolves a long-standing apparent contradiction in SSL theory (cosine similarity should make norms irrelevant, yet they clearly matter) and provides simple, practical interventions—cut-initialization and GradScale—that yield meaningful accuracy gains, especially for non-contrastive methods (SimSiam) and class-imbalanced datasets (double-digit improvements). The dual role of norms as both a gradient scalar and a confidence signal opens new directions for reliability estimation during inference and for understanding the modality gap in multimodal models. Key open questions remain: why non-contrastive architectures are disproportionately sensitive to the norm effect, whether the effect can be deliberately leveraged (e.g., for prototypical anchoring in transfer learning), and what formal bounds connect norm magnitude to classification accuracy or OOD distance.
