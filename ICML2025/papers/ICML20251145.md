## Problem

Unconfoundedness (no unmeasured confounding) is a fundamental but untestable assumption in observational causal inference, and existing falsification strategies either rely on transportability conditions that can be violated by unmeasured effect modifiers, or depend on conditional independence testing that is notoriously difficult and loses power as adjustment sets grow. This paper asks how to efficiently test causal mechanism independencies to falsify unconfoundedness in multi-environment settings without requiring transportable treatment effects or access to randomized data.

## Input / Output

The method consumes multi-environment observational data consisting of tuples (X, A, Y, S) across K≥2 environments, along with pre-specified feature representations ψ(X) and φ(X,A) that must be known up to permutation and element-wise scaling, with dimensionality strictly below the smallest per-environment sample size. It produces a binary falsification decision (reject or accept H₀: P(ω,γ)=P(ω)P(γ)) at a user-specified significance level α, along with the Frobenius-norm test statistic value. The procedure is a joint test: rejection falsifies both unconfoundedness and the independent causal mechanisms assumption simultaneously, so a positive result does not pinpoint which assumption failed.

## Method

The MINT algorithm is a two-stage procedure grounded in the principle of independent causal mechanisms. In stage 1, it estimates treatment-assignment parameters ω_s and outcome-mechanism parameters γ_s per environment via least-squares regression on the feature representations. In stage 2, it tests independence of (ω_s, γ_s) across environments using the Frobenius norm of their cross-covariance matrix as a test statistic, calibrated via a bootstrap-permutation hybrid that accounts for estimation uncertainty from stage 1. The key theoretical insight (Theorem 4.5) is that an unmeasured confounder U creates shared parameter dependencies between ω_s and γ_s through the treatment mechanism and the conditional expectation E[U|X,A,S], making H₀ false whenever at least one of the confounding-related parameters (α_s^(0), α_s^(X), α_s^(U), μ_s^(U)) varies non-degenerately across environments. Unlike transportability-based falsification, MINT explicitly allows outcome mechanisms to vary across environments, making it robust to violations of transportability; unlike HGIC, it tests independence at the level of learned mechanism parameters rather than performing conditional independence tests on raw variables, avoiding the power degradation associated with growing adjustment sets.

## Results

On the Twins semi-synthetic dataset (5 confounders, quadratic DGP), MINT achieves the highest falsification power among all methods, reaching near-perfect detection (≈1.00) when unmeasured confounders are present, while maintaining Type I error below the nominal α=0.05 when all confounders are observed. In the well-specified linear setting (Table 2), MINT(Cubic) achieves falsification rates of 0.00–0.06 under the null (no confounding) and 1.00 under the alternative (confounding present), across both transportability-holding and transportability-violated scenarios. The transportability test, by contrast, shows elevated false positive rates of 0.68–0.81 when transportability is violated, even in the absence of unmeasured confounding.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MINT outperforms both baselines on the Twins semi-synthetic dataset in terms of statistical power, detecting unmeasured confounding more reliably as the number of unobserved confounders increases. Against the transportability test (Dahabreh et al., 2020b), MINT has a decisive advantage when transportability is violated: the transportability test's false positive rate jumps to 0.68–0.81 (well above α=0.05) in the absence of confounding, while MINT(Cubic) stays at 0.04–0.06. Against HGIC (Karlsson & Krijthe, 2023), MINT consistently achieves higher falsification rates as the number of environments K increases, and is more robust to increasing covariate dimensionality d (HGIC with KCIT loses power fastest). In the well-specified linear setting, MINT(Cubic) achieves perfect power (1.00) across all confounding scenarios, whereas the transportability test only reaches 0.65–0.75 under transportability-holding conditions. Both baselines also suffer elevated Type I errors under model misspecification, but MINT's parametric encoding of both treatment and outcome models makes it more aligned with standard causal inference practice.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the principle of independent causal mechanisms (ICM) from causal discovery (Janzing et al., 2012; Peters et al., 2017) for a new application: falsifying unconfoundedness in treatment effect estimation. It departs from the closest prior work, HGIC (Karlsson & Krijthe, 2023), by replacing the notoriously difficult conditional independence test with a direct test of independence between learned mechanism parameters (ω, γ) via a Frobenius-norm statistic. Unlike transportability-based falsification (Dahabreh et al., 2020b; Hussain et al., 2023), the approach does not require invariant outcome mechanisms across environments, making it robust to transportability violations caused by unmeasured effect modifiers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical, two-stage algorithm (MINT) that can empirically test whether unconfoundedness is violated in multi-environment observational data without requiring randomized data or transportability assumptions. Key limitations include the need for known (or well-specified) feature representations, parametric working models, and at least two environments with non-degenerate parameter variation. Open questions highlighted by the authors include learning feature representations from data, extending to kernelized/implicit representations, and mitigating the power loss from model misspecification. For practitioners in meta-analysis or multi-site observational studies, this offers a concrete diagnostic tool beyond sensitivity analysis.
