## Problem

Existing GNN-based Graph Foundation Models rely on fixed, hand-designed GNN architectures, yet the optimal architecture varies across domains and tasks (architecture inconsistency). This paper is the first to formalize graph neural architecture search (GNAS) for GFMs and provides a theoretical proof that mainstream differentiable GNAS methods suffer optimization conflicts when datasets require different optimal operations, making them unable to find a single architecture that performs well across all domains.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250352/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes text-attributed graphs (TAGs) as input, where node and edge text sentences are encoded via a Sentence Transformer into 768-dimensional features, and tasks are unified as NOI-graph subgraph classification following the OFA/GFT formulation. The output is a per-dataset customized GNN architecture (a probability distribution over operations like GCN, GAT, GIN, GraphSAGE, GraphConv at each of 2 super-network layers), which is then plugged into the GFT base model to produce node, edge, or graph-level classification predictions. The architecture mapping function π: G → A is learned jointly across all datasets within a shared weight-sharing super-network.

## Method

AutoGFM trains a mapping function π: G → A that customizes architectures per dataset while sharing weights across domains. It first uses a disentangled contrastive graph encoder with two-channel GNNs and NOI-graph-level contrastive learning to extract invariant patterns Z_I (reliably predicting architecture) and variant patterns Z_V (spurious, dataset-specific). An invariant-guided architecture customization module then employs a weight-sharing super-network with learnable prototypes, where an invariant predictor ψ_I maps Z_I to operation probabilities and an auxiliary predictor ψ_E maps fused Z_I+Z_V to a second prediction; minimizing their difference enforces the conditional independence P(A|Z_I,Z_V)=P(A|Z_I), shielding architecture selection from variant patterns. A curriculum architecture customization mechanism adds a coefficient-of-variation penalty on average operation probabilities that decays over training steps, preventing early-dominant operations from crowding out others and ensuring diverse architecture exploration across datasets.

## Results

AutoGFM achieves an average accuracy of 80.86% across eight diverse datasets spanning node classification (Cora, PubMed, WikiCS, Arxiv), link prediction (WN18RR, FB15K237), and graph classification (HIV, PCBA), outperforming the best single-GNN baseline (GraphSAGE at 79.84%) and all GNAS baselines (DARTS 75.51%, GraphNAS 74.75%, GASSO 75.82%, GRACES 76.29%). The method wins on every individual dataset, with particularly large gains on WN18RR (93.17% vs. 92.20% for GCN) and FB15K237 (90.27% vs. 89.92% for GraphSAGE). In few-shot settings (Table 2), AutoGFM also leads across all N-way K-shot configurations, e.g., 46.29% on Cora-7way-1shot vs. 45.43% for GRACES.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AutoGFM outperforms all 14 baselines on every dataset. Against manually designed GNNs plugged into GFT, the best single architecture (GraphSAGE, 79.84% avg) still trails AutoGFM by ~1 point, and no fixed GNN dominates all domains—e.g., GCN excels on WN18RR (92.20%) but collapses on FB15K237 (77.35%), while GraphSAGE shows the reverse pattern. Among GNAS baselines, GRACES (76.29%) and GASSO (75.82%) are the strongest but remain 4.5–5 points below AutoGFM on average, confirming that existing differentiable GNAS methods suffer from the architecture inconsistency problem identified in the paper. Self-supervised GNNs (BGRL 72.67%, GraphMAE 73.07%, GIANT 74.11%) and vanilla GNNs (GCNv 73.76%, GATv 75.44%) are substantially weaker, underscoring the benefit of the GFM framework combined with architecture customization. In few-shot settings, AutoGFM leads by 0.86–1.5 points over the best GNAS baseline (GRACES) on Cora-7way and by ~0.5 points on WN18RR-10way, demonstrating faster adaptability of the customized architectures under label scarcity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the GNAS literature (DARTS, GraphNAS, GASSO, GRACES) to the setting of GNN-based graph foundation models, building directly on GFT as the base GFM and OFA's NOI-graph formulation for task unification. It departs from prior GNAS by identifying and formally analyzing 'architecture inconsistency'—the fact that optimal GNN operations differ across domains and tasks—and showing via Proposition 3.2 that existing differentiable GNAS methods face irreconcilable optimization conflicts under this condition. The method repurposes invariant representation learning (from DIR, DIDA) by shifting the invariant target from label prediction to architecture prediction, and borrows the weight-sharing super-network mechanism from DARTS while replacing learnable architecture parameters with a data-conditioned predictor guided by disentangled contrastive representations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work addresses a fundamental limitation of current GNN-based GFMs—their reliance on a single fixed GNN architecture—and provides the first theoretical justification for why this is problematic, which should inform future GFM design. The per-dataset architecture customization within a shared super-network is a practical recipe that could be adopted by other GFM frameworks. However, the search space is narrow (2 layers, 4 operations), the approach is limited to GNN-based GFMs (not LLM-based), and the empirical gains, while consistent, are moderate (roughly 1–3 points over the best fixed-architecture baseline). Open questions include whether the invariant/variant decomposition generalizes to larger search spaces, deeper architectures, or non-GNN foundation models, and whether the curriculum mechanism remains necessary at scale.
