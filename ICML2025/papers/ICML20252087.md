## Problem

Frontier LLMs possess detailed factual knowledge about decision-making tasks (e.g., chess rules, game strategies) yet fail to translate this into effective action selection, a 'knowing-doing gap' that prior benchmarks like BALROG have only examined in the zero-shot regime. Whether conditioning on large numbers of multimodal expert demonstrations (state-action trajectories) in very long contexts (up to 1M tokens) can unlock in-context imitation learning capabilities remains unexplored, as no unified benchmark covers the full zero-shot through many-shot spectrum for interactive, long-horizon tasks.

## Input / Output

The model receives a generic decision-making preamble, followed by 0 to 512 frozen expert demonstration episodes (each up to 100 state-action steps), a separator, and the growing evaluation trajectory. Observations are encoded as text (ASCII grids, FEN/PGN strings, coordinate tuples, position/velocity vectors) or RGB images, with the maximum number of demonstrations constrained by each model's context window and the observation format (e.g., 16 RGB episodes vs. 256 ASCII episodes for the same model). The output is a single action string extracted after the 'Action:' keyword; chain-of-thought reasoning traces are discarded, and semantically correct but misformatted actions are rejected.

## Method

LMAct is a unified benchmark of six interactive decision-making tasks (Atari Phoenix, chess vs. Stockfish level 0, 7×7 crosswords, DM Control Cheetah Run, 12×12 grid world navigation, and tic-tac-toe vs. random) that evaluates eight frontier LLMs by varying the number of in-context expert demonstration episodes from 0 to 512. For each model-task pair, the authors ablate chain-of-thought prompting and whether to display legal actions, selecting the best configuration per combination. Evaluation uses 100 episodes with unseen initial states, deterministic sampling (temperature 0), and a replay control experiment where the model must copy actions from a single demonstration episode. The key empirical finding is that performance is largely independent of the number of demonstrations in most settings, with only a few model-task combinations (e.g., o1 on tic-tac-toe ASCII, Gemini on grid world coordinates) showing steady in-context learning, while others exhibit 'in-context interference' where more demonstrations degrade performance.

## Results

Across six interactive decision-making tasks (Atari Phoenix, chess, crosswords, DM Control Cheetah Run, grid world, tic-tac-toe), frontier LMs rarely reach expert performance even with up to 512 demonstration episodes in context (up to 1M tokens). The o1 models achieve near-optimal scores on tic-tac-toe (ASCII) and crosswords, while Gemini 1.5 Pro and Gemini 2.0 Flash Experimental reach roughly half the expert score on Cheetah Run. All models outperform the random-action baseline except on Atari Phoenix, where their tendency to repeat actions (holding the fire button) results in fewer successful shots than random play. Performance is largely independent of the number of demonstrations in most settings, with notable exceptions: o1-mini and o1-preview improve steadily on tic-tac-toe (ASCII) to reach expert performance at 256 episodes, and Gemini models show steady improvement on grid world with coordinate/RGB observations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares eight frontier LMs (Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Gemini 1.5 Flash/Pro, Gemini 2.0 Flash Experimental, GPT-4o, o1-mini, o1-preview, o1) against two baselines: an expert policy (upper bound) and a random-action policy (lower bound). No model reaches expert performance on Atari Phoenix, chess, or Cheetah Run; the best Cheetah Run score (Gemini 1.5 Pro, ~38) is roughly half the expert (~75). On chess against the weakest Stockfish (level 0, ~1320 Elo, single node), all models lose the majority of games (score ≈ -1), with PGN observations yielding the best results for GPT-4o. On tic-tac-toe (ASCII), o1-mini and o1-preview reach expert performance at 256 demonstrations, while other models struggle near the random baseline. On crosswords, o1-preview and o1 nearly solve all puzzles (score ≈ 0.95), while other models score 0.3–0.6. The key finding is that increasing demonstrations from 0 to 512 has little effect in most settings, with the notable exceptions of o1 models on tic-tac-toe and Gemini models on grid world (coordinates/RGB), indicating that in-context imitation learning is not yet reliably unlocked by simply adding more expert trajectories.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of in-context imitation learning and long-context multimodal evaluation. While prior work has demonstrated in-context imitation in robotics (Palo & Johns, 2024; Fu et al., 2024) and many-shot learning on non-interactive tasks (Agarwal et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2024), no prior benchmark systematically evaluates frontier LMs on interactive decision-making with contexts up to one million tokens and up to 512 full demonstration episodes. The concurrent BALROG benchmark (Paglieri et al., 2025) addresses zero-shot evaluation on similar tasks, and Atari-GPT (Waytowich et al., 2024) evaluates zero-shot Atari play, but LMAct uniquely unifies the zero-, few-, and many-shot regimes in a single multimodal evaluation framework, using expert demonstrations sourced from Gato (Reed et al., 2022) and Stockfish.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a persistent 'knowing-doing gap': frontier LMs possess detailed factual knowledge about chess, Atari, and control tasks yet cannot translate it into effective decision-making even with hundreds of expert demonstrations in context. The discovery of 'in-context interference'—where performance degrades with more demonstrations on certain tasks—raises fundamental open questions about whether models fail to comprehend the task from demonstrations or struggle to effectively attend over dense long contexts. The open-source benchmark provides a controlled testbed for diagnosing these limitations and measuring progress toward general agentic capabilities, though its reliance on closed-source APIs and simple (fully observable) environments limits direct transfer to real-world robotics or partially observable settings.
