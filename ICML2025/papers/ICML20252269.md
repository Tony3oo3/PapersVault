## Problem

Deterministic data-driven weather forecasting models trained with mean squared error (MSE) loss universally produce overly smooth forecasts due to a 'double penalty' effect: MSE encourages the model to reduce the amplitude of unpredictable fine scales rather than learn to predict them. This smoothing degrades predictions of localized extremes (tropical cyclone intensity, surface wind extremes) and limits the model's effective resolution far below its native grid spacing. The paper asks whether this smoothing can be corrected in a purely deterministic setting without resorting to ensemble or generative methods.

## Input / Output

The model consumes two time levels of atmospheric state on a 1/4° latitude/longitude grid (13 vertical levels plus surface, ~170 million input degrees of freedom) and produces a single 6-hour deterministic forecast of 6 variables at 13 levels plus 5 surface variables (~86 million output degrees of freedom). Training uses the HRES-fc0 dataset (ECMWF IFS initial conditions, 2016–2021) supplemented with ERA5 accumulated precipitation, and evaluation is performed on the equivalent 2022 data. The fine-tuned model is autoregressively rolled out up to 12 steps (72 h) for multi-step forecasts.

## Method

The core contribution is the Adjusted Mean Squared Error (AMSE) loss, a parameter-free modification that decomposes MSE via spherical harmonic transform into a spectral-amplitude term (PSD mismatch) and a decorrelation term (1 − coherence). The key change replaces the geometric mean of the forecast and analysis PSDs in the coherence term with their maximum, decoupling the penalty for amplitude error from the penalty for phase mismatch so that the model is no longer incentivized to smooth fine scales it cannot yet predict. AMSE is zero iff forecast equals analysis, retains MSE units, and requires no cutoff scales or weighting parameters. The method is applied as a fine-tuning pass on the publicly available GraphCast operational checkpoint using a staged curriculum (1-step → 12-step autoregressive training with decreasing learning rates), requiring only ~26 GPU-days on A100 hardware.

## Results

Fine-tuning GraphCast with the AMSE loss function (AMSE AR12) increases the model's effective resolution from ~1,250 km (control) to ~160 km, as measured by the wavenumber at which the amplitude ratio drops below √0.75. In lagged ensemble verification over 2022, AMSE AR12 achieves lower CRPS than the control for most variables at 5-day lead time (e.g., z500hPa: 80.47 vs. 84.32; t850hPa: 0.653 vs. 0.691; q700hPa: 0.510 vs. 0.523), while eRMSE sees little change, indicating better-calibrated ensemble spread without degrading point-forecast skill. For tropical cyclones (20 Jun–19 Sep 2022), the control model exhibits a growing weak-intensity bias with lead time, whereas AMSE AR12 retains the quality of the HRES analysis, and storm position errors are equivalent between the two models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the unmodified GraphCast operational checkpoint (Control), which is also fine-tuned with standard MSE (MSE AR12) or MAE (MAE AR12) under the identical curriculum for ablation. AMSE AR12 outperforms the Control on CRPS for z500hPa (80.47 vs. 84.32 at 5d), t850hPa (0.653 vs. 0.691), and q700hPa (0.510 vs. 0.523) at 5-day lead time, with the largest relative gains (~5–6%) at 7.5-day lead. For u850hPa, AMSE AR12 is marginally worse at 2.5d (0.832 vs. 0.823) but recovers by 7.5d (1.882 vs. 1.904). The single-step AMSE AR1 model, while sharper, degrades CRPS substantially (e.g., z500 5d: 94.70 vs. 84.32), confirming that multi-step autoregressive fine-tuning is necessary. In tropical cyclone verification, AMSE AR12 eliminates the control's weak-intensity bias (mean max surface wind speed and min central pressure track HRES quality) while maintaining equivalent position error, and quantile-quantile plots show AMSE AR12 produces more realistic 10 m wind speed extremes than the control, particularly in summer.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on GraphCast (Lam et al., 2023), fine-tuning its operational checkpoint with a modified loss function. It departs from prior approaches to fixing forecast smoothing—ensemble/diffusion methods (GenCast, AIFS-CRPS, SEEDS), hybrid physics-ML architectures (NeuralGCM), and multi-term spectral losses—by proposing a purely deterministic, parameter-free modification to MSE that separates spectral amplitude error from coherence (decorrelation) error via a spherical harmonic decomposition. The key conceptual departure is replacing the geometric mean of power spectral densities in the cross-term with their maximum, which breaks the coupling that causes MSE to penalize amplitude reduction as a hedge against imperfect phase prediction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The smoothing problem is universal across deterministic data-driven weather models and limits their utility for extreme-event prediction, data assimilation, and spectral nudging. A parameter-free, drop-in loss function that fixes this without requiring ensemble infrastructure or architectural changes is practically significant for the field. Open questions include whether AMSE should replace MSE throughout full training (not just fine-tuning), whether it generalizes to local-area models without regular global grids, and whether the small residual mesoscale smoothing under autoregressive training can be eliminated. The paper also notes it has not demonstrated improved out-of-distribution behavior, which is critical for climate applications.
