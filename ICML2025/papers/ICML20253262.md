## Problem

Graph learning benchmarks are misaligned with transformative real-world applications, often relying on narrow 2D molecular graphs, arbitrarily constructed graph structures, and inconsistent evaluation protocols that inflate apparent progress. The paper argues these systemic issues have prevented the emergence of graph foundation models and threaten the field's long-term relevance, providing empirical evidence that much reported performance gains are artifacts of poor benchmarking rather than genuine architectural advances.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20253262/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Experiments span four graph modalities: small 2D molecular graphs (PCQM4Mv2 with ~14 nodes, OGB molecular sets), medium super-pixel graphs (COCO-SP with ~477 nodes), large function-call graphs (MALNETTINY with ~1410 nodes, entirely unattributed), and social network graphs (STARGAZERS with ~114 nodes). A key unusual design choice is that the same node/edge features are fed through three structural variants—the original graph, a fixed Cayley expander graph, and an empty graph (DeepSets baseline)—to isolate whether the relational structure itself contributes predictive signal. Outputs range from graph-level regression (HOMO-LUMO gap MAE) and node-level classification (F1, accuracy) to multi-task representations learned jointly across all domains.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a suite of controlled empirical experiments exposing benchmarking failures: (1) replicating Cayley graph propagation on OGB benchmarks to show that a non-informative fixed expander or no graph structure at all matches or beats the original graph in 5 of 9 settings; (2) re-tuning a 20-layer GINE (~20M params) on PCQM4Mv2 with modern practices (SiLU, LayerNorm, cosine schedule), closing the gap to graph transformers from 25%+ to under 2% MAE difference; (3) re-evaluating heterophilic baselines without the two-layer MLP modifications from Platonov et al., revealing 14–44% inflated gains; and (4) training an encoder-processor-decoder framework (GINE or Graphormer processor with domain-specific encoders and task-specific decoders) on a cross-domain pre-training mix, then fine-tuning frozen processors on new tasks to measure transfer. The paper pairs these findings with concrete remedies: shifting benchmarks toward combinatorial optimization, requiring justification for graph construction choices, mandating unstructured-set baselines, and building large-scale diverse pre-training corpora with decontamination.

## Results

On PCQM4Mv2, a re-tuned 20-layer GINE (22.7M params) achieves 0.0913 ± 0.0002 validation MAE, closing the gap to graph transformers (0.0832–0.0910 MAE) from the previously reported ~25% error reduction to roughly 5–10%. Adding RWSE structural features further improves GINE to 0.0898 ± 0.0001 MAE, matching or exceeding several transformer baselines. On OGB graph-level benchmarks, a non-informative Cayley expander graph outperforms or matches the original molecular graph in 5 out of 9 model-dataset combinations, and on MOLBBBP the best AUC-ROC (64.90) is achieved by a DeepSets baseline that discards graph structure entirely. On heterophilous datasets, removing the architectural modifications (two-layer MLP, linear encoder/decoder, GeLU) used in Platonov et al. (2023) reduces reported baseline gains by +43.56% (ROMAN-EMPIRE), +14.63% (AMAZON-RATINGS), and +19.49% (MINESWEEPER).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central empirical finding is that the widely cited ~25% MAE gap between graph transformers and MPNNs on PCQM4Mv2 largely vanishes under fair tuning: the re-tuned GINE (0.0913) matches TokenGT (0.0910) and approaches GRPE (0.0890), while GINE+RWSE (0.0898) is competitive with Graphormer (0.0864) and GPS (0.0858), though ET (0.0832) retains a modest edge. On OGB molecular benchmarks (Table 1), the non-informative Cayley graph matches or beats the original graph for GIN on MOLBACE (56.94 vs. 53.44) and GAT on MOLBACE (62.75 vs. 53.21), while DeepSets (no graph) achieves the best MOLBBBP score (64.90), suggesting the 2D molecular graph structure adds little predictive signal. On heterophilous datasets (Table 3), the paper shows that Platonov et al.'s reported GCN/SAGE/GAT numbers are inflated by unacknowledged architectural changes: re-evaluating without those modifications drops GCN on ROMAN-EMPIRE from 73.69% to 44.41% and GAT from 80.87% to 51.05%, meaning the apparent superiority of heterophily-specific GNNs is substantially overstated. In the multi-task pre-training setting (Table 4), the pre-trained MPNN and GT outperform their randomly initialized counterparts on all three tasks and beat the empty-graph baseline, with the MPNN achieving 83.20% accuracy on MALNETTINY (vs. 81.80% single-task) and the GT reaching 88.90% (vs. 81.90% single-task), demonstrating positive cross-domain transfer. Overall, the paper does not propose a new architecture but rather re-evaluates existing baselines (GCN, GINE, GAT, SAGE, Graphormer, GPS, ET, TokenGT, GRPE, DeepSets, GraphConv) under corrected protocols, consistently showing that prior performance gaps are artifacts of uneven tuning and evaluation rather than genuine architectural superiority.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper critically re-examines the benchmarking infrastructure of graph learning, building on and departing from the major benchmarking efforts of Dwivedi et al. (2020), OGB (Hu et al., 2020a), and the Long Range Graph Benchmark (Dwivedi et al., 2022b), which it argues focus on narrow or poorly-justified graph constructions. Empirically, it extends the Cayley graph propagation findings of Wilson et al. (2024) and Bechler-Speicher et al. (2024) to OGB molecular benchmarks, re-evaluates GINE baselines on PCQM4Mv2 using modern training practices, and re-assesses the heterophilic graph baselines of Platonov et al. (2023) without their architectural modifications. For the multi-task pre-training experiments, it adapts the encoder-processor-decoder framework of Battaglia et al. (2018) and Ibarz et al. (2022) to a cross-domain graph setting, using GINE (Xu et al., 2018) and Graphormer (Ying et al., 2021) as processors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central claim—that graph learning risks losing relevance if benchmarks are not fundamentally revised—carries significant implications for how the community allocates research effort. The empirical findings that non-informative Cayley graphs match or beat original graph structure, that properly tuned simple MPNNs close most of the gap with graph transformers, and that evaluation-protocol choices can inflate reported gains by 14–44% collectively challenge the field's progress narrative and suggest that much reported improvement may be methodological artifact rather than genuine capability gain. Key open questions include: what constitutes a 'meaningful' graph construction for a given task, how to build large-scale diverse pre-training corpora for graph foundation models, and whether the field should pivot toward combinatorial optimization and other graph-native problems. The paper's limitations include a relatively small number of datasets per experiment and single-seed results in the multi-task pre-training section, leaving some claims suggestive rather than conclusive.
