## Problem

Gradient boosting trees (GBTs) excel at structured and categorical data in supervised learning but have been absent from the RL toolbox. Traditional GBT libraries are designed for static datasets with fixed labels, making them incompatible with RL's dynamic nature where state distributions and reward signals evolve during training. This paper asks whether GBTs can serve as effective function approximators in RL and whether their inductive biases (piecewise-constant partitions, single-feature splits) confer robustness advantages over neural networks.

## Input / Output

The method takes a state observation s that may include heterogeneous numerical and categorical features (e.g., a 51-dimensional categorical encoding of a 7×7 MiniGrid tile map plus mission strings, or a 115-dimensional vectorized Football observation) and produces both an action distribution (discrete or continuous Gaussian) and a value estimate from a single shared tree ensemble. Each leaf of the ensemble outputs multiple targets simultaneously—e.g., F_k(s) = [μ(s), σ(s), V(s)] for a Gaussian policy—enabling joint actor-critic prediction without separate networks.

## Method

GBRL reinterprets GBT's functional gradient descent for RL by computing the policy gradient in function space rather than parameter space, then projecting that gradient onto a new decision tree fitted to minimize the sampled loss. At each training iteration, a rollout is collected, the policy gradient ∇_{F_k} J(π_{F_k}) is computed, and a new tree h_k is constructed to approximate this gradient, updating the ensemble as F_K(s) = F_0 + Σ ε h_k(s). A shared actor-critic architecture uses a single tree ensemble for both policy and value, with cosine-similarity-based split scoring to handle multi-objective gradient imbalance. The framework is CUDA-accelerated for incremental tree construction (5.47s per 1000 iterations vs. 36–67s for CatBoost/XGBoost) and integrates directly into Stable-Baselines3, supporting PPO, A2C, and AWR with GBT backends.

## Results

On MiniGrid (structured/categorical environments), GBRL-PPO achieves 0.96 on Fetch-5x5-N2 and GoToDoor-5x5, and 0.95 on KeyCorridorS3R1, substantially outperforming NN-PPO (0.89, 0.60, 0.76 respectively). On Google Research Football, GBRL-PPO leads in most academy scenarios (e.g., 1.87 on Pass&Shoot keeper vs. 1.31 for NN-PPO; 1.95 on Run to Score w/ keeper vs. 1.13). On unstructured Atari RAM, GBRL underperforms NNs (e.g., 1163.86 vs. 1555.32 on Alien with PPO), while on Classic Control the two are comparable (both reach 500 on CartPole). GBRL is also 6.6× faster than CatBoost and 12.3× faster than XGBoost for incremental learning (5.47s vs. 36.17s and 67.11s per 1000 boosting iterations).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against NN-PPO, NN-A2C, and NN-AWR (the three standard RL baselines), GBRL wins decisively on structured/categorical environments: on MiniGrid, GBRL-PPO outperforms NN-PPO on 7 of 9 tasks (e.g., PutNear 0.44 vs. 0.001, GoToDoor 0.96 vs. 0.60), and on Football it leads in most academy scenarios (e.g., Pass&Shoot keeper 1.87 vs. 1.31). On unstructured Atari RAM, GBRL consistently loses to NNs across all three algorithms (e.g., PPO: 1163.86 vs. 1555.32 on Alien; 19.96 vs. 32.88 on Breakout), confirming that tree-based splits struggle with implicit feature interactions in raw memory states. On Classic Control, the two are roughly comparable (both solve CartPole at 500; MountainCar: −110.55 vs. −115.53). Against traditional GBT libraries, GBRL is dramatically faster for the incremental learning regime required by RL: 5.47 s per 1000 boosting iterations versus 36.17 s for CatBoost and 67.11 s for XGBoost, and it avoids the throughput collapse that CatBoost and XGBoost exhibit as ensemble size grows on CartPole.

## Relation to Prior Work

GBRL extends the functional gradient ascent framework of Non-Parametric Policy Gradients (Kersting & Driessens, 2008) from discrete-action Gibbs policies to general Actor-Critic algorithms (PPO, A2C, AWR) with arbitrary policy representations. It repurposes the core GBT machinery (Friedman, 2001) by reinterpreting the boosting iteration as a policy/value gradient step in function space, and addresses the key engineering gap that prior GBT-in-RL work (Abel et al., 2016; London et al., 2023; Brukhim et al., 2022) left open: scalability to large, high-dimensional environments through purpose-built incremental tree construction and a shared actor-critic architecture. The paper explicitly contrasts its approach with off-the-shelf GBT libraries (CatBoost, XGBoost), showing their supervised-learning-oriented design makes them intractable for RL's iterative, multi-objective, online nature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GBRL establishes gradient boosting as a practical, GPU-accelerated function approximator for RL, filling a gap in the RL toolbox for structured and categorical observations where NNs underperform. The robustness findings—superior OOD behavior, resistance to spurious correlations, and immunity to signal dilution—translate GBT's known supervised-learning advantages into concrete RL benefits, with SHAP-based interpretability as a bonus. Key open questions include managing unbounded ensemble growth (memory/compute) and extending to off-policy continuous control (DDPG, SAC), where GBT's non-differentiability blocks the standard Q-differentiation pathway. The clear failure on unstructured data (Atari RAM) delineates the method's boundary, reinforcing that the ideal function approximator depends on observation structure.
