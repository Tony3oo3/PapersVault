## Problem

General-purpose AI systems are deployed to hundreds of millions of users yet lack the evaluation and flaw-reporting infrastructure that mature fields like software security have built over decades. Third-party evaluators face legal uncertainty, restrictive terms of service, and no standardized reporting channels, while transferable flaws (e.g., jailbreaks) can affect multiple providers simultaneously with no coordination mechanism to alert all affected stakeholders.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20253293/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The proposed system consumes structured AI Flaw Reports containing a unique report ID, system version(s), session ID for traceability, a detailed flaw description grounded in specific policy violations (ToS, AUP, model card), and a set of triage tags (developer, system, severity, prevalence, impacts, risk source, bounty eligibility). Reports are routed via a tag-subscription mechanism: stakeholders subscribe to relevant tags (e.g., 'Meta', 'Risk Source: Pre-Training Data', 'Impacts: Cybersecurity') and receive notifications with a pre-agreed disclosure window before public release. For output-generating flaws, reports must include statistical validity metrics describing the frequency of undesirable outputs across relevant prompts.

## Method

The paper proposes a three-part policy framework adapted from coordinated vulnerability disclosure and bug bounty practices in software security. First, it defines a standardized AI Flaw Report schema (derived from the AI Incident Database, MITRE STIX, CISA VEX, and OASIS CSAF) paired with codified good-faith rules of engagement for evaluators (evaluate only in-scope systems, protect privacy, do not generate illegal content, responsibly disclose). Second, it prescribes that GPAI providers adopt legal safe harbors (waiving ToS restrictions and anti-hacking/anti-circumvention claims for rule-abiding research), moderation-exempt research access plans, and broadly-scoped flaw disclosure programs with dedicated reporting interfaces. Third, it advocates for a lightweight AI Disclosure Coordination Center that receives flaw reports and routes them via tag-based subscriptions to all affected supply-chain stakeholders—data providers, model developers, hosting services, app developers, distribution platforms, and government agencies—before public release, addressing the transferability of flaws across systems that siloed per-provider disclosure cannot handle.

## Results

This is a position paper with no empirical experiments or quantitative results tables. The key empirical evidence cited includes: 495 hackers participated in the DEFCON 2024 Generative Red Teaming event testing OLMo; GPAI systems now serve over 300 million weekly users; and software security's coordinated disclosure ecosystem has amassed millions of volunteer researchers and thousands of organizations with bug bounty programs. The paper's contribution is normative—proposing a standardized AI Flaw Report schema, good-faith rules of engagement, legal safe harbor language, and a Disclosure Coordination Center—rather than presenting measured performance metrics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not compare methods against baselines or present experimental results. It is a policy and framework proposal that draws analogies from the established software security ecosystem (coordinated vulnerability disclosure, bug bounties, safe harbors) to argue for analogous infrastructure in GPAI. The paper addresses two alternative views: (1) that first- and second-party evaluation plus commercial API access is sufficient, which the authors rebut by citing the chilling effect of legal uncertainty on third-party researchers and the prevalence of flaws reported only on social media; and (2) that resource constraints make safe harbors and coordination centers impractical, which the authors counter by noting that leading GPAI developers have billions in resources and that the return on investment for shared disclosure infrastructure will be substantial. No quantitative head-to-head comparisons are made.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper repurposes the mature infrastructure of software security's coordinated vulnerability disclosure (CERT, CISA, HackerOne safe harbors, bug bounty programs) and extends it to the broader, less-defined domain of GPAI 'flaws' that encompass safety, security, and sociotechnical risks beyond traditional vulnerabilities. It builds on prior AI incident reporting efforts (AI Incident Database, AVID, MITRE/OECD incident forms) and Cattell et al.'s early proposal for AI-specific coordinated disclosure, but departs from them by introducing a standardized AI Flaw Report schema, codified good-faith rules of engagement, and a proposed Disclosure Coordination Center to handle transferable flaws across the complex GPAI supply chain. The paper also draws on legal scholarship around safe harbors (DOJ CFAA guidance, DMCA §1201 exemptions) to argue for extending protections from security research to the wider category of AI safety and trustworthiness research.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a critical infrastructure gap: despite GPAI systems serving hundreds of millions of users with documented harms (electoral misinformation, medical record corruption, non-consensual imagery), there is no standardized, legally protected pathway for third-party researchers to report and coordinate disclosure of flaws across the supply chain. Key open questions remain around governance of the proposed Disclosure Coordination Center, resolving disagreements between reporters and providers on what constitutes a flaw, and developing tooling for triage and remediation. The concrete deliverables (flaw report template, safe harbor language, rules of engagement) provide actionable starting points, but the paper's impact depends on adoption by major providers and policymakers, which remains uncertain given the competitive and resource-constrained environment the authors acknowledge.
