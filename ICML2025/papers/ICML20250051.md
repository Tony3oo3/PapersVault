## Problem

Prior work on training instability (Frankle et al., Fort et al.) measured sensitivity to training noise such as batch order and data augmentation, but could not disentangle whether instability arises from noise, the network's current state, or the training procedure itself. This paper asks how sensitive deterministic training trajectories are to controlled perturbations of initial weights, and precisely when and at what scale such perturbations cause otherwise identical trajectories to diverge into distinct loss basins.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250051/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a network at a specific training state θ_t (randomly initialized or from a pre-trained checkpoint at a chosen fraction of pre-training), applies a single perturbation of controlled magnitude σ (either a batch gradient step or Gaussian noise, both normalized so that σ=1 equals the initialization scale), and trains both the original and perturbed copies with identical deterministic noise to a common time T. It produces four divergence metrics between the two final networks: L2 parameter distance, training cross-entropy loss barriers, barriers after permutation alignment via weight matching, and Angular CKA representational similarity of penultimate-layer activations.

## Method

The core contribution is the spawn-and-perturb experiment, which adapts the parent-child spawning framework of Frankle et al. by replacing independent training noise after time t with a single controlled perturbation, then training both copies under identical deterministic conditions to isolate the perturbation's effect from stochastic training effects. This design allows quantifying instability across a continuous range of perturbation magnitudes—from a single weight (σ~10⁻⁶) to 10% of initialization—far beyond the scale of typical training noise. Two perturbation types probe different aspects: batch perturbations (one simulated SGD step) test stability along training-relevant gradient directions, while Gaussian perturbations matching the Kaiming initialization distribution test stability in all directions. Divergence is captured by four complementary metrics—L2 distance, loss barriers, barriers modulo permutation (using the weight-matching algorithm of Ainsworth et al.), and Angular CKA—jointly measuring weight-space separation, loss-landscape basin membership, and functional representational difference. The method is applied across architectures (ResNet-20/50, ViT, Multi-BERT, OLMo-1B) and training regimes (from-scratch on CIFAR-10/100, fine-tuning from multiple pre-training durations on GLUE and GSM8K) to map where in the loss landscape trajectories are chaotic versus stable.

## Results

The paper demonstrates that a single perturbed weight (fraction 10⁻⁶, σ=10⁻⁴) at initialization is sufficient to produce non-zero training loss barriers in ResNet-20 on CIFAR-10, while batch perturbations of just 0.01% of the network's weights cause large barriers at t=0.5% of training. Stability increases rapidly: after 50% of training, only perturbations of 10% of initialization scale produce non-zero barriers. For fine-tuning ResNet-50 from the 191ep319st checkpoint (CIFAR-100→CIFAR-10), barriers are 0.00±0.00 for all σ≤0.01, confirming near-perfect stability. In contrast, BERT fine-tuning on QNLI shows the 2000k pre-training checkpoint has the *largest* barriers, indicating that more pre-training can reduce fine-tuning stability for language models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary baseline is the parent-child spawning experiment of Frankle et al. (2020a), which induces instability via independent training noise (batch order, data augmentation) after a spawn point. The authors verify in Figure 10 that their single-perturbation (butterfly) approach yields a strict lower bound on Frankle et al.'s spawning barriers, confirming that instability to a single controlled perturbation implies instability to ongoing training noise. The key distinction is that the butterfly method isolates instability to specific training times and perturbation scales, whereas the spawning baseline conflates all stochastic effects. The paper also contrasts its findings with prior observations that training becomes 'stable' after an early chaotic phase (Fort et al., 2020; Frankle et al., 2020a), showing that while stability to training noise increases, the perturbation scale needed to induce barriers continues to grow throughout training rather than reaching a hard threshold. For fine-tuning, the paper extends beyond Neyshabur et al. (2020) (who showed pre-trained models are stable to noise) by quantifying the exact perturbation magnitudes that break stability, revealing that language models (BERT, OLMo) can become *less* stable with more pre-training—a finding not captured by noise-only baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the parent-child spawning experiments of Frankle et al. (2020a) and Fort et al. (2020) by eliminating all training noise and instead applying a single controlled perturbation at a chosen training time, thereby isolating the deterministic dynamical-systems instability of the training map from stochastic effects. It further generalizes the analysis from randomly initialized networks to pre-trained checkpoints undergoing fine-tuning, and introduces multiple functional-similarity metrics (barriers modulo permutation, Angular CKA) beyond the single barrier measure used in prior spawning studies. The dynamical-systems framing connects to Wu et al. (2018) and Jastrzebski et al. (2020), but departs by measuring divergence relative to a moving trajectory rather than a fixed minimum, and by asking whether networks diverge in function, not just in weight space.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The findings have direct practical implications for model merging (identifying when networks remain in the same linearly connected basin), ensembling (quantifying when intentional perturbation increases functional diversity), and fine-tuning (revealing that more pre-training can paradoxically reduce stability for language models). Key open questions include why L2 divergence and barriers do not grow exponentially as linearized dynamical-systems theory predicts, whether any hyperparameter combination can fully eliminate initialization instability, and what causes the non-monotonic relationship between pre-training duration and fine-tuning stability in large language models. The spawn-and-perturb methodology provides a reusable framework for characterizing stability across architectures, tasks, and training regimes that prior noise-based methods could not access.
