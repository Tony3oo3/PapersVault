## Problem

Pre-trained vision language models perform poorly on visual cognition tasks such as intuitive physics and causal reasoning, and do not align well with human behavioral data. While task-specific fine-tuning is increasingly used to improve VLM performance, it remains unclear whether such fine-tuning produces robust, human-like generalization to new visual characteristics, different object configurations, or entirely different cognitive domains. This paper systematically probes these limits by testing fine-tuned models across four generalization conditions.

## Input / Output

Models receive an RGB image of a block tower or pyramid (rendered in ThreeDWorld or photographed in the real world) paired with a fixed text prompt asking a binary question (e.g., 'Will the tower fall?'). The output is computed by sampling the log probabilities of the 'Yes' and 'No' tokens conditional on the input, exponentiating, and applying softmax to yield a relative probability score; correctness is determined by which token receives the higher probability. Human alignment is additionally quantified via bootstrapped Cohen's κ between model and human binary judgments on identical stimuli.

## Method

The authors construct the Cubeworld dataset in ThreeDWorld, containing block towers for intuitive physics (2–7 blocks, random size/color/offset) and block pyramids with a designated red block for causal reasoning (3–15 blocks, counterfactual 'would other blocks fall?' question). They fine-tune Qwen2-VL-7B and Llama 3.2-11B/90B using QLoRA (r=16, α=16) applied to the ViT vision encoder and attention/MLP layers of the language decoder, training for 10 epochs on 10,000 text-image pairs with three random seeds. Evaluation spans four conditions: in-distribution test, out-of-distribution tower/pyramid sizes, naturalistic images from Lerer et al. (2016), and cross-domain transfer (physics↔causal reasoning), with a paired-image ablation that removes only the red block to minimize task differences. A separate fine-tuning condition uses individual human judgments (100 raters per image) instead of ground truth to test whether human-aligned training improves behavioral alignment, measured via Cohen's κ.

## Results

Fine-tuning Qwen2-VL-7B, Llama 3.2-11B, and Llama 3.2-90B via QLoRA substantially improves accuracy over zero-shot (chance-level) baselines, with 11B models fine-tuned on 2–4 block intuitive physics towers reaching 0.78–0.83 accuracy on their fine-tuning distribution (human mean: 0.63). For causal reasoning, models improve across all pyramid sizes regardless of fine-tuning split (human mean: 0.72). However, single-domain fine-tuning fails to generalize: models fine-tuned on intuitive physics show near-chance performance on causal reasoning pyramids (κ = −0.08 to 0.09), and models fine-tuned on Cubeworld perform below chance on some Lerer et al. (2016) realistic tower sizes. Fine-tuning on human judgments (rather than ground truth) raises Cohen's κ with human raters from 0.23 to 0.37 on the same task and to 0.37 on the Lerer dataset, but still yields κ = −0.02 on the causal reasoning domain.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against competing fine-tuning methods; instead, it compares fine-tuning strategies against zero-shot base models and against each other. Zero-shot base models perform at chance level across all tower and pyramid sizes, while QLoRA fine-tuning lifts accuracy to 0.60–0.92 depending on model size and task. Single-domain fine-tuning (intuitive physics or causal reasoning) fails to transfer to the other cognitive domain, with cross-domain accuracy remaining at or below chance, even when the visual stimuli are nearly identical (paired pyramid images with/without the red block). Joint fine-tuning on both domains recovers good performance in both, confirming the models have sufficient capacity but that task-specific fine-tuning does not induce transferable intuitive theories. Fine-tuning on human judgments (correlation with ground truth: 0.27; label overlap: 63.5%) yields higher human alignment (κ = 0.37) than ground-truth fine-tuning (κ = 0.23) on the same task and on the Lerer realistic dataset, but still fails to transfer to causal reasoning (κ = −0.02). Generalization to the Lerer et al. (2016) realistic images is poor for single-domain models (below chance for 2- and 3-block towers), though joint-domain models show somewhat better performance, suggesting data diversity partially mitigates the visual-domain gap.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work by Balazadeh et al. (2024), who fine-tuned PaliGemma-3B on intuitive physics tasks and showed smaller fine-tuned models could outperform larger base models, but did not investigate generalization. It also builds on Schulze Buschoff et al. (2025), which established that VLMs lack human-like intuitive physics and causal reasoning, and on the broader PEFT/QLoRA literature as the dominant fine-tuning paradigm. The key departure is a systematic, counterbalanced evaluation of four distinct generalization axes (same-distribution, different tower sizes, different visual characteristics, different cognitive domain) using a newly introduced dataset (Cubeworld) built in ThreeDWorld, combined with human-judgment alignment analysis via Cohen's kappa—none of which prior fine-tuning studies on visual cognition had addressed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central negative finding—that task-specific fine-tuning on one cognitive domain (e.g., causal reasoning) does not transfer to a conceptually prerequisite domain (intuitive physics), even when the visual stimuli are nearly identical—challenges the optimistic narrative that fine-tuning builds robust, human-like intuitive theories. This has direct practical implications: practitioners seeking multi-domain visual reasoning must fine-tune on all target domains rather than expecting cross-domain transfer. Open questions include whether larger models (beyond 90B), RL-based post-training, or symbolic physical priors could overcome these limits, and whether the observed brittleness reflects the binding problem or data memorization. The Cubeworld dataset and four-axis evaluation protocol provide a reusable benchmark for future work on fine-tuning generalization in visual cognition.
