## Problem

Charikar et al. (2024) proved that in weak-to-strong generalization for regression, the strong model's performance gain over its weak supervisor is lower-bounded by the squared-loss misfit between them. However, this characterization was limited to the squared loss and a convex strong model class, leaving open whether a similar misfit-gain relationship holds for classification (cross-entropy loss) or for the non-convex model classes used in practice.

## Input / Output

The method consumes weak labels produced by a frozen weak model (e.g., GPT-2 or AlexNet) on a held-out dataset, together with a frozen strong model backbone (e.g., GPT-2-XL, ResNet-50, or ViT-B/8 with DINO representations). It outputs a trained strong model structured as a convex combination of k logistic regression heads (k=100 in experiments) and a quantitative bound: the cross-entropy gain of the strong model over the weak model is at least the reverse KL divergence (strong‖weak) minus an O(√(c/k)) error term. The unusual aspect is that the strong model's output appears in the first argument of the KL divergence, reversing the standard cross-entropy training direction.

## Method

The key insight is that the Pythagorean inequality underlying Charikar et al.'s squared-loss result is a special case of a generalized Pythagorean inequality that holds for all Bregman divergences. Since cross-entropy equals KL divergence plus a task-dependent entropy constant, the misfit-gain inequality extends directly to classification with KL divergence as the misfit measure. To handle the non-convexity of practical strong model classes (e.g., logistic regression heads with sigmoid), the paper trains a convex combination of k heads from the class, showing via a probabilistic method (inspired by Zeevi & Meir, 1997) that the approximation error in the misfit-gain bound scales as O(√(c/k)), where c is the number of classes. The training objective minimizes the expected reverse KL divergence D_KL(strong‖weak) over the k-head ensemble, which the authors argue is mode-seeking and thus better suited for weak-to-strong training than the standard forward cross-entropy, since the strong student should be free to disagree with an imperfect weak teacher.

## Results

The paper establishes a misfit-gain inequality for Bregman divergences (Theorem 4.1) and its cross-entropy corollary (Corollary 4.2), showing that the performance gain of a strong model over its weak supervisor is at least the KL misfit between them. For non-convex model classes, Theorem 4.3 extends this to convex combinations of k logistic heads with an O(√(c/k)) error term. Empirically, on six datasets (BoolQ, SciQ, CosmosQA, Amazon Polarity, CIFAR-10, ImageNet), the reverse KL recipe with k=100 heads yields test accuracy improvements over the weak model on all datasets, with the misfit faithfully tracking the gain. On ImageNet with ViT-B/8, the reverse KL approach achieves 69.86% weak-to-strong accuracy, surpassing the 64.2% reported by Burns et al. (2024) for the same setup.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary empirical comparison is against the standard forward cross-entropy weak-to-strong training recipe used in Burns et al. (2024). The proposed reverse KL objective with k=100 convex-combined logistic heads achieves higher test accuracy than forward XE on 5 out of 6 datasets (CosmosQA: 64.07% vs 63.78%; Amazon Polarity: 89.84% vs 89.63%; SciQ: 62.54% vs 61.71%; CIFAR-10: 90.05% vs 89.81%; BoolQ: 61.41% vs 61.40%), with the sole exception being ImageNet (69.86% vs 71.33%). Critically, the reverse KL misfit quantitatively aligns with the gain (e.g., CIFAR-10: misfit 0.3185 vs gain 0.2447), whereas the forward XE misfit is wildly disconnected from the gain (e.g., CIFAR-10: misfit 1.1024 vs gain 0.1772), confirming the theoretical prediction that the Pythagorean inequality holds only in the reverse direction. Against Burns et al. (2024) specifically, the paper reports that its ViT-B/8 weak-to-strong accuracy on ImageNet (69.86%) exceeds the 64.2% reported in their Table 3, attributed to the reverse KL training of the linear probe rather than standard cross-entropy on a single head.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the geometric misfit-gain characterization of weak-to-strong generalization from Charikar et al. (2024), which was limited to squared loss in regression, to arbitrary Bregman divergences, thereby covering classification via KL divergence. The key conceptual move is recognizing that the Pythagorean inequality underlying Charikar et al.'s result holds for all Bregman divergences, not just the squared loss. To handle the practically important case where the strong model class is non-convex (e.g., logistic regression heads with sigmoid), the paper draws on the probabilistic method of Zeevi & Meir (1997) to show that convex combinations of k models approximate the full convex hull with an O(√(c/k)) error. The experimental setup and model architectures follow Burns et al. (2024), but the training objective is modified to use reverse KL divergence on a k-head ensemble rather than standard forward cross-entropy on a single head.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first theoretical justification for why weak-to-strong generalization should hold in classification tasks, not just regression, and identifies a concrete, theoretically grounded training recipe (reverse KL on a convex combination of k heads) that practitioners can adopt. The finding that reverse KL outperforms or matches standard forward cross-entropy across six diverse datasets is practically significant, as it suggests the standard weak-supervision objective may be suboptimal. Key limitations include the realizability assumption (the target must be exactly representable by the strong model), which the authors acknowledge does not always hold in practice, and the O(√(c/k)) error term that grows with the number of classes. Open questions include whether the error bound can be tightened, whether the framework extends to non-linear probes or full finetuning, and how the theory applies to the broader superalignment setting where the strong model's representational capacity far exceeds the weak model's.
