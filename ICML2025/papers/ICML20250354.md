## Problem

Existing Pareto Front Learning (PFL) methods using hypernetworks face two persistent limitations: (a) high-dimensional ray sampling (J≥3) suffers from exponential computational complexity in uniform partitioning schemes, and (b) the resulting Pareto front fails to cover convex-shaped regions, with solutions clustering in the middle due to HV gradient weight decay. These gaps prevent PFL from producing complete, well-distributed Pareto fronts needed for downstream tasks like federated learning benefit graph construction.

## Input / Output

The method takes a J-dimensional preference vector r (constrained to the simplex ∑r_j=1, r_j≥0) as input to a multi-sample hypernetwork, which generates N target network parameter sets θ_i = h(r^i, φ). The output is a set of N Pareto-optimal solutions spanning the full front, and in the federated learning application, an n×n directed weighted benefit graph G_b where edge weights r_j^{i*} quantify data complementarity between FL participants. The number of rays N is freely specifiable regardless of the number of objectives J, unlike combinatorial partitioning methods.

## Method

PHN-HVVS introduces two key mechanisms. First, a GA-based Voronoi Sampling procedure (Algorithm 1) optimizes N Voronoi site positions on the preference simplex using Monte Carlo simulation points and KD-tree nearest-neighbor queries, maximizing a uniformity objective that equalizes point counts per grid—this replaces intractable combinatorial partitioning in high dimensions. Second, a novel composite loss (Eq. 13) combines negative hypervolume (Q = −HV) with a perpendicular-distance penalty D(r^i, ℓ^i) that measures the Euclidean distance from each loss point to its corresponding preference ray along direction (1,…,1). The HV term drives global convergence toward the true front, while the distance penalty explicitly prevents solutions from collapsing into the central region of convex fronts by penalizing deviation from the ray geometry. The hypernetwork is trained via gradient descent on the combined objective, with HV gradients computed through a multi-sweep algorithm (GRADMULTISWEEP) that projects solutions into (J−1)-dimensional subspaces for efficient partial derivative calculation.

## Results

On the six multi-task learning benchmarks (Table 1), PHN-HVVS achieves the highest Hypervolume (HV) across all datasets, e.g., 3.008 on MultiMNIST, 2.947 on MultiFashionMNIST, 0.935 on Jura (4-task), and 0.939 on SARCOS (7-task), consistently outperforming the strongest baseline PHN-HVI by 0.005–0.011 HV. On the eight toy MOO problems (Table 8), PHN-HVVS attains the best HV in 7 out of 8 cases, with the largest margin on ZDT1 (3.735 vs. 3.654 for PHN-LS) and ZDT2 (3.323 vs. 3.321 for PHN-HVI). In federated learning, integrating PHN-HVVS into CE on CIFAR-10 under pathological distribution raises mean test accuracy from 74.38% to 86.88% (+12.5 pp), and into FedEgoists on eICU improves average AUC from 74.79 to 79.80 (+5.0 pp).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PHN-HVVS outperforms all five PFL baselines (PHN-EPO, PHN-LS, PHN-TCHE, COSMOS, PHN-HVI) on every multi-task learning dataset in Table 1, with the largest gaps on SARCOS (7-task) where PHN-TCHE collapses to 0.726 HV versus 0.939 for PHN-HVVS, and on Drug Review where COSMOS fails to converge entirely. Among the PFL baselines, PHN-HVI is the closest competitor, winning no dataset but trailing PHN-HVVS by only 0.005–0.011 HV; PHN-LS and PHN-EPO are consistently 0.02–0.08 HV behind. In the federated learning application, PHN-HVVS improves CE on CIFAR-10 by +12.5 pp (pathological) and +11.2 pp (Dirichlet β=0.5), FedCompetitors by +0.25 pp and +3.76 pp respectively, and FedEgoists on eICU by +5.0 pp average AUC, though individual FL-PTs v0, v3, and v5 show small AUC drops (−0.24, −9.01, −1.61 pp) indicating the gains are not uniform across all participants. The paper also ablates the Voronoi sampling against five alternative strategies (random, Latin hypercube, polar, Dirichlet, K-means) on Jura and SARCOS, where Voronoi sampling yields the best HV (0.935 and 0.949 vs. 0.877–0.928 for the others), confirming the sampling design as a key contributor.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Pareto HyperNetwork (PHN) framework introduced by Navon et al. (2020) and the multi-sample hypernetwork with HV optimization from Hoang et al. (2023), replacing their reliance on the Das & Dennis uniform partitioning (which scales exponentially with dimensionality) with a GA-based Voronoi grid decomposition for high-dimensional preference-space sampling. The key methodological departure is a new composite loss that pairs HV maximization (via HIGA-MO-style gradients from Wang et al., 2017) with a Euclidean distance-based penalty term, replacing the cosine-similarity penalty in PHN-HVI to specifically target boundary solutions on convex Pareto fronts. The Voronoi partitioning itself draws on classical computational geometry (Aurenhammer & Klein, 2000) and Monte Carlo methods, repurposed here for the first time in the PFL context.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a concrete and well-recognized gap in PFL: existing gradient-based methods systematically under-cover convex Pareto front boundaries due to HV gradient 'weight decay' favoring intermediate solutions. The Voronoi sampling technique provides a practical, dimension-agnostic way to set the number of preference rays, which is useful for FL settings where the number of participants (and thus objectives) varies. However, the FL improvements are mixed—substantial for CE under pathological distribution (74→87 MTA) but modest for FedCompetitors (82.19→82.44)—and the GA-based Voronoi partitioning adds a non-trivial computational pre-step. Open questions include whether the distance penalty generalizes to non-convex, disconnected, or degenerate fronts, and whether the approach scales beyond ~10 objectives without the Monte Carlo HV approximation introducing significant noise.
