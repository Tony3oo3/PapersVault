## Problem

Current AI alignment research predominantly focuses on algorithmic-centric strategies (RLHF, DPO, reward model design) that implicitly assume the training data perfectly reflects true human preferences. This paper argues that the data side of alignment is critically underexplored: human feedback suffers from systematic unreliability, and AI-generated feedback inherits biases from its underlying models, yet neither issue has been rigorously characterized or addressed.

## Input / Output

The qualitative study consumes 160 prompt-response pairs (80 from the harmless split, 80 from the helpful split) of the Anthropic-HH dataset, where each pair contains a multi-turn conversation and two candidate responses originally labeled as chosen/rejected. Three annotators re-label each sample into one of four categories (chosen better, rejected better, both good, both bad) and record their reasoning, with majority voting resolving disagreements. The output is a structured taxonomy of six sources of unreliability (categorized as human-related or data-related) with quantitative proportions from low-IAA and 'both are bad' subsets, plus seven concrete future research directions organized under data collection, data cleaning, and feedback verification.

## Method

The paper combines a qualitative empirical study with a comprehensive literature review to characterize data-centric alignment challenges. The empirical component involves three experienced annotators re-labeling 160 Anthropic-HH samples using an expanded four-way protocol (adding 'both are good' and 'both are bad' beyond the original binary choice), followed by thematic analysis of disagreement cases to extract six recurring sources of unreliability: mis-labeling, high subjectivity/lack of context, different preference criteria, different thresholds, harmful suggestions in both responses, and misinformation/irrelevance in both responses. The study further decomposes low inter-annotator agreement (Fleiss's κ = 0.46) and 'both are bad' samples to show that disagreement is driven primarily by subjectivity and threshold differences, while poor response quality (harmful or irrelevant content in both options) forces arbitrary binary choices. The paper then contrasts these data-side challenges with the algorithmic-centric alignment literature (RLHF, DPO, reward modeling) and proposes seven future directions spanning holistic and longitudinal data collection, human-AI collaborative data cleaning, and standardized feedback verification.

## Results

Through qualitative re-annotation of 160 samples from Anthropic-HH (80 from each of the harmless and helpful splits) by three experienced annotators, the paper finds that 25% of samples are labeled 'rejected is better' (contradicting the original dataset label) and another 25% are labeled 'both are bad,' indicating substantial unreliability in the original annotations. Inter-annotator agreement is moderate (Fleiss's κ = 0.46). Among low-agreement samples, the dominant sources of unreliability are different thresholds of criteria (37%), different preference criteria (29%), and high subjectivity (28%); among 'both are bad' samples, harmful suggestions in both responses (39%) and misinformation/irrelevance (36%) dominate.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper and does not propose a new alignment method to benchmark against baselines; it instead identifies data-quality challenges that undermine existing algorithmic-centric approaches (RLHF, DPO, and variants). The qualitative findings directly challenge the implicit assumption in RLHF and DPO pipelines that human preference labels are reliable: 25% of Anthropic-HH labels appear to be mislabeled, and 25% of response pairs are both inadequate, meaning that even well-designed reward models and optimization algorithms are trained on fundamentally flawed supervision signals. The paper contrasts data-centric alignment with algorithmic-centric alignment conceptually (Table 1), arguing that the latter's core bottleneck is its reliance on unverified data quality. No head-to-head quantitative comparison between data-centric and algorithmic-centric methods is provided, as the contribution is diagnostic rather than a new training procedure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper carves out 'data-centric alignment' as a distinct research axis complementary to the dominant algorithmic-centric approaches (RLHF, DPO, and their variants) that have shaped the alignment literature since Christiano et al. (2017). It builds on the PRISM dataset (Kirk et al., 2024) for diversity in human feedback, LLM-as-a-judge work (Zheng et al., 2023) for AI-based feedback, and recent data-cleaning efforts (Wang et al., 2024a; Yeh et al., 2024a), but departs from them by systematically identifying six concrete sources of unreliability in human preference labels through qualitative analysis of Anthropic-HH. Rather than proposing a new algorithm, the paper reframes the alignment problem around data quality and representativeness, arguing that even well-designed reward models and optimization objectives fail when trained on flawed preference data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's qualitative finding that 25% of Anthropic-HH samples are mislabeled and another 25% contain 'both are bad' responses provides concrete evidence that the data underpinning much of the RLHF/DPO literature is substantially noisier than commonly assumed. The six-source taxonomy of unreliability (mis-labeling, subjectivity, differing criteria, differing thresholds, harmful content in both responses, misinformation in both) gives practitioners a structured vocabulary for diagnosing and mitigating data problems. Key open questions remain: whether these unreliability sources generalize beyond Anthropic-HH, how to automatically detect and categorize them at scale, and how to handle temporal drift in human preferences without prohibitive re-annotation costs. The paper's seven future directions—spanning holistic collection, longitudinal tracking, human-AI collaborative cleaning, and standardized verification—outline a research agenda but leave most questions empirically unresolved.
