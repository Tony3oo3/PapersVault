## Problem

The conditions under which learned models can successfully transfer to new tasks remain poorly characterized, and existing inductive transfer approaches (meta-learning, transformers) fail at extrapolation beyond the convex hull of source tasks. This paper introduces a geometric characterization of inductive transfer in Hilbert spaces—defining three types from interpolation within the convex hull to extrapolation outside the linear span—and asks whether a single principled method can achieve all three.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251042/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference, the method receives a small few-shot dataset of (x, y) pairs for the target task and produces a full function approximation f_T(x) = Σ c_j g_j(x) by solving a k×k linear system for the coefficients c. The inner product defining the Hilbert space is task-dependent: L² for regression, a logit-space inner product for discrete probability distributions (classification), or a Bochner-Lebesgue integral for general output spaces. The basis functions g_j are fixed after offline training, so inference requires only a sample-mean inner product computation and a small matrix inverse, making it computationally cheap even for large datasets.

## Method

The function encoder learns k neural network basis functions offline by minimizing the mean approximation error over source tasks, with a novel least-squares coefficient computation (solving the normal equations via the Gram matrix) replacing the original inner-product method. This least-squares formulation is the key insight: it yields the optimal projection of the target onto the learned basis without requiring orthonormality, which naturally enables extrapolation to the linear span (type 2) and beyond (type 3) by optimally utilizing unconstrained dimensions. A regularization term (‖g_i‖² − 1)² prevents basis function magnitude growth caused by the ridge-regularized Gram matrix inverse. The paper also proves a universal approximation theorem showing that any separable Hilbert space can be arbitrarily well approximated by sufficiently many neural network basis functions, and generalizes the framework to probability-distribution output spaces via Aitchison geometry.

## Results

The paper evaluates L² error across four datasets (Polynomial, CIFAR-100, 7-Scenes, MuJoCo Ant) and three geometrically-defined transfer types (convex hull interpolation, linear span extrapolation, and full Hilbert space extrapolation). On the Polynomial dataset, FE (LS) achieves orders of magnitude lower L² error than all baselines on Type 2 and Type 3 transfer, while all methods achieve only moderate Type 1 performance. On the three real-world datasets, FE (LS) consistently achieves the best or near-best transfer performance, with the largest margins on Type 2 (linear span) transfer where most baselines fail entirely. The method also matches ad-hoc classification-specific baselines (Siamese, Prototypical networks) on CIFAR-100 despite being designed for a more general setting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FE (LS) outperforms the original function encoder FE (IP) [Ingebrand et al., 2024b] across all four datasets, with the largest gains on Type 2 and Type 3 transfer, because least-squares coefficient computation provides the optimal projection onto the learned basis without requiring orthonormality. The encoder-decoder Transformer [Vaswani et al., 2017] performs poorly on transfer: on the Polynomial dataset it fails to extrapolate beyond Type 1, and on MuJoCo it is comparable to FE (LS) on Type 3 but clearly worse on Type 2. MAML [Finn et al., 2017] achieves reasonable Type 1 transfer on MuJoCo but fails at Type 2 and Type 3, consistent with its reliance on task-specific fine-tuning that breaks down when the target task is outside the training span. On CIFAR-100, FE (LS) matches or slightly outperforms the ad-hoc Siamese network [Bromley et al., 1993] and Prototypical network [Snell et al., 2017] baselines on both Type 1 and Type 3 transfer, while these ad-hoc methods show a significant performance drop from training to transfer settings. Overall, FE (LS) is the only method that reliably achieves all three types of transfer across all four benchmarks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the function encoder framework from Ingebrand et al. (2024b), which originally used an inner-product method to compute basis coefficients, by replacing it with a least-squares projection that does not require orthonormality and enables principled extrapolation. It also draws on the Hilbert space structure for probability distributions from Egozcue et al. (2003) to generalize the method to classification tasks. Conceptually, the paper departs from meta-learning (MAML) and kernel methods by computing a task representation from data at inference time without any retraining or fine-tuning, and it introduces a three-type geometric taxonomy of transfer (convex hull, linear span, full Hilbert space) that has no direct analogue in prior transfer learning literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The three-type geometric characterization provides practitioners with a principled way to reason about when transfer will succeed or fail, which prior work has lacked. The universal approximation theorem gives theoretical grounding that the method can represent any separable Hilbert space, while the least-squares mechanism explains *why* the method extrapolates: it computes the optimal projection onto the learned basis. Key open questions include designing inner products for sequences and graphs, and efficiently representing low-dimensional manifolds embedded in infinite-dimensional spaces, which the current basis-function approach handles poorly. The method's applicability is also constrained by the need for a well-defined inner product over the output space.
