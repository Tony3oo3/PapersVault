## Problem

Current AI existential risk discourse focuses predominantly on abrupt scenarios—deliberate misuse or misaligned autonomous systems seizing control—leaving unexplored the possibility that incremental, capability-neutral AI adoption could progressively erode human influence over the economic, cultural, and political systems society depends on. This paper identifies and formalizes this 'gradual disempowerment' pathway, arguing it constitutes an existential risk that is structurally distinct from, and substantially underappreciated relative to, the abrupt scenarios that dominate the field.

## Input / Output

The paper takes as analytical input the structure of three interdependent societal systems (economy, culture, nation-states) and their current mechanisms for maintaining alignment with human interests, then produces a structured risk taxonomy distinguishing relative from absolute disempowerment within each domain, a mutual-reinforcement model across domains, and four categories of mitigation strategies (measurement, influence limitation, human influence strengthening, and system-wide alignment). The output is a position argument rather than an empirical result, with no datasets, models, or quantitative benchmarks.

## Method

The argument is organized around six core claims that build from the observation that societal systems maintain alignment with human interests through both explicit mechanisms (voting, consumer choice) and implicit ones (reliance on human labor and cognition for functioning). Each of the three domains—economy, culture, and states—is analyzed through a consistent four-part structure: AI as a unique disruptor, current human alignment mechanisms, transition dynamics driven by competitive and efficiency incentives, and the resulting relative versus absolute disempowerment. A key insight is that the implicit alignment channel is particularly vulnerable because it has never been observed in its absence, making its erosion difficult to recognize in real time. The paper then argues that misalignment in one domain propagates to others through interdependencies (e.g., economic power shaping policy, cultural shifts altering economic behavior), creating mutually reinforcing feedback loops that accelerate disempowerment. Finally, the paper distinguishes its scenario from abrupt takeover risks and proposes that mitigation requires not just aligning individual AI systems but maintaining human relevance across complex, interconnected societal systems.

## Results

This is a position paper presenting a theoretical argument rather than an empirical study. It contains no quantitative results, experiments, or performance metrics. The paper's contribution is a structured argument (six core claims) that incremental AI adoption could lead to irreversible human disempowerment across economic, cultural, and political systems, constituting an existential catastrophe.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not compare methods against baselines or evaluate on datasets. It positions its 'gradual disempowerment' scenario as an alternative to two dominant AI risk framings: (1) deliberate misuse (cyberattacks, bioweapons) and (2) abrupt misaligned AI takeover. The authors argue their scenario is distinct because it requires no acute capability jump or apparent misalignment, and they note it has been 'mostly unconsidered' because it has been 'eclipsed' by those two camps. They also address two alternative views: that innate human demand will always preserve human economic relevance, and that a sufficiently aligned AGI could forge cooperative equilibria — countering both by arguing purchasing power will diminish and that even superhuman AGIs may be unable to overcome global competitive dynamics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes and extends several scattered threads in the AI risk literature—Kasirzadeh's accumulative x-risk hypothesis, Critch's 'extinction by industrial dehumanization,' Hanson's cultural drift, and Korinek & Stiglitz's Malthusian AI dynamics—into a unified multi-system framework of 'gradual disempowerment' spanning economy, culture, and states. It explicitly departs from the two dominant AI risk framings (deliberate misuse and abrupt misaligned takeover) by arguing that no acute capability jump or alignment failure is necessary; instead, competitive pressures and the loss of implicit human-participation-based alignment suffice. The mutual-reinforcement analysis (Section 5) is the paper's most original contribution, arguing that the checks-and-balances between societal systems become vectors for misalignment propagation rather than correction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reframes the AI safety problem from 'align a single powerful agent' to 'maintain human relevance across complex, interdependent societal systems that drift even when each local AI is well-specified,' which has significant implications for both technical research (measuring disempowerment, AI delegates, civilization-scale simulation) and governance (regulatory stopgaps, progressive taxation of AI revenue). Key open questions include whether technical alignment of individual AIs suffices to prevent multi-system drift, how coordination dynamics change when many principals possess superhuman bargaining capabilities, and how to quantify the degree of disempowerment in the absence of external reference points. As a position paper with no empirical grounding, its claims remain speculative, but it identifies a research agenda that is largely absent from current AI safety work focused on single-agent alignment.
