## Problem

Reinforcement learning requires millions of environment interactions, but in many real-world systems (molecular docking, epidemic simulation, urban mobility simulation) reward evaluation takes 1–15 seconds per sample versus millisecond-scale decision-making, creating a severe fast-decision/slow-evaluation bottleneck. Existing mitigations—proxy rewards, reduced-scale evaluations, and fixed world models—either sacrifice reward accuracy or fail to track the agent's evolving exploration, leading to growing prediction errors and suboptimal policies.

## Input / Output

The agent consumes graph-structured states (atom-bond graphs for molecules, contact networks for epidemics, spatial adjacency graphs for city layouts) and outputs sequential node/edge selection actions via a GNN encoder shared with the reward model. The reward model produces a scalar reward estimate in ~0.01–0.02 s, while the offline system computes precise rewards (8.6–15.2 s) using task-specific simulators (AutoDock Vina, SIR model, SAND). A fixed-size FIFO memory pool stores recent state-action pairs for offline evaluation, and a fine-tune pool accumulates (state, action, RM estimate, true reward) tuples for periodic RM updates.

## Method

AdaReMo decouples RL into an online decision system (agent + fast RM) and an offline evaluation system (direct reward calculators), bridged by an MLP-based reward model that shares the agent's GNN encoder to minimize parameters. The key mechanism is adaptive reward modeling: the full state space is implicitly decomposed into localized subspaces corresponding to the agent's current exploration, and the RM is periodically fine-tuned (every N policy iterations, for M epochs) on the latest direct-evaluation samples to stay aligned with the evolving policy. Three acceleration components complement this core loop: synchronous correction blends RM estimates with cheap simplified evaluations to handle out-of-subspace outliers, model warm-up delays policy optimization until the RM has undergone several fine-tuning cycles, and parallel computation runs K reward calculators concurrently to enlarge the fine-tuning dataset. The agent is trained with PPO using the RM's fast reward signals, achieving >1000× speedup over direct-evaluation RL while improving final performance by 14.6% over state-of-the-art baselines.

## Results

On molecular generation across three protein targets (FA7, PARP1, 5HT1B), AdaReMo achieves the best Top-5% Score (10.4, 13.1, 12.7) and Hit Ratio (0.29, 0.42, 0.48), improving over the best baseline by 1.6–3.2% in T5 and 17.1–20.0% in HR. On epidemic control, it reaches 39.8% Healthy and 10.4% Contained on CA-GrQc (+7.4%, +7.2% over best baseline) and 28.2% Healthy on SNAP (+10.2%). In urban spatial planning, it reduces average travel distance by 9.2% and increases green space exposure by 9.9% over the best baseline. Overall, AdaReMo delivers a >1,000× speedup in reward evaluation while improving final performance by 14.6% on average over state-of-the-art approaches.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AdaReMo outperforms all seven baselines across all three tasks. In molecular generation, it beats the strongest MFRL baseline FREED (which uses precise docking evaluation) by 1.6–3.2% in T5 and 17.1–20.0% in HR, and surpasses the best MBRL method RLOO by similar margins. MolDQN, which relies on simplified evaluation, suffers a catastrophic 93.5% drop in Hit Ratio, confirming that reward precision is critical. In epidemic control, AdaReMo improves over the best MFRL baseline RLGN by 7.4% in Healthy and 7.2% in Contained on CA-GrQc, and by 10.2% and 11.5% on SNAP; it also outperforms MBPO by 45% in Contained, highlighting MBRL's inability to track the evolving reward landscape. In urban spatial planning, MBPO performs worst among all baselines (18.8% worse in Distance, 14.2% worse in Greenness), while AdaReMo achieves the best layout on all three communities. Classic heuristics (HSB, KED, GBP) and non-RL methods (HierVAE, LIMO) are consistently outperformed, with HSB showing at least 11.7% higher infection risk in epidemic control.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes ideas from model-based RL (world models à la MBPO), RLHF-style reward models, and asynchronous training to address the fast-decision/slow-evaluation bottleneck in RL. It departs from fixed world models (which struggle with complex, locally-varying reward functions) and from static proxy rewards (which oversimplify evaluation) by introducing a reward model that is periodically fine-tuned to the agent's current exploration subspace, effectively decomposing the full state space into tractable local approximations. The GNN-based agent and PPO optimizer are standard; the core contribution is the adaptive, asynchronous reward modeling framework rather than any single architectural component.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 1,000× speedup makes RL training feasible for domains where reward evaluation takes seconds per sample (molecular docking, SIR simulation, urban mobility simulation), a bottleneck that previously rendered standard RL impractical. The approach is validated across three structurally different tasks, strengthening generalizability claims. Limitations include the current restriction to GNN-based agents (though the authors note model-agnosticism), sensitivity of training efficiency to fine-tuning hyperparameters (though final performance is robust), and the reliance on a simplified evaluation for synchronous correction. Open questions include whether the adaptive RM framework transfers to non-graph architectures (CNNs, transformers) and to tasks with non-stationary or multi-objective reward functions.
