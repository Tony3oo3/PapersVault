## Problem

Identifying multiple change points in a piecewise constant function under bandit feedback with a fixed confidence guarantee has not been studied theoretically. Prior work addressed either single change points, fixed-budget settings, or clustering without the piecewise constant structure, leaving a gap for adaptive, confidence-driven identification of N change points where the true number may be unknown.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251283/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The algorithm receives a confidence level δ ∈ (0,1) and a target count N, then sequentially selects arms from a discrete action space [K] with known-variance Gaussian rewards whose means are piecewise constant. It adaptively stops and returns a set of N estimated change point locations (indices i where μ_i ≠ μ_{i+1}), without requiring the true total number of changes m ≥ N as input. The stopping time is data-dependent, and the algorithm guarantees P(estimate ⊆ true change points) > 1−δ.

## Method

MCPI (Multiple Change Point Identification) is a computationally efficient variant of Track-and-Stop that sequentially identifies one change point at a time, analogous to binary segmentation. The key insight is that the optimal sampling proportions have a closed-form solution: samples should be allocated equally to the two arms adjacent to each change point, with the fraction around each change inversely proportional to the squared change magnitude. This eliminates the need for numerical optimization in both the sampling rule (D-Tracking with √t forced exploration) and the stopping rule (an explicit Chernoff-type threshold check). The algorithm is simultaneously Exact-(N,δ) and Any-(N,δ), and is proven asymptotically optimal with matching constants for the Any-(N,δ) lower bound, while matching the Exact-(N,δ) bound up to a factor of 2.

## Results

The main quantitative contribution is the asymptotic optimality of MCPI: for any environment with m ≥ N change points, limsup_{δ→0} E[τ_δ]/log(1/δ) ≤ 8σ²(Σ_{i=1}^N 1/Δ_{(i)}²), which matches the lower bound in Theorem 5.5 with the correct constant. In the single change point case (N=1), the bound reduces to 8σ²/Δ², matching Corollary 4.3. In synthetic experiments across four environments (1 to 5 change points, K=9 to 19 arms), MCPI's average stopping time over 100–1000 runs tracks the theoretical lower bound closely across a range of δ values, confirming asymptotic optimality empirically.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares MCPI against the Bandit Online Clustering (BOC) algorithm (Yang et al., 2022) in four synthetic environments (Figures 2–5). In every environment, MCPI's average stopping time increases approximately parallel to the theoretical lower bound from Theorem 5.5, while BOC's stopping time grows at a substantially faster rate. Notably, BOC was given additional information that MCPI does not require: the true number of change points and the oracle optimal sampling proportions from the clustering literature. Despite this advantage, BOC fails to exploit the piecewise constant structure of the mean rewards across the action space, resulting in significantly higher sample complexity. The gap between MCPI and BOC widens as δ decreases (i.e., as higher confidence is demanded), emphasizing that BOC is asymptotically suboptimal in the fixed-confidence piecewise constant bandit setting. MCPI also demonstrates robustness to unknown additional change points: in environment v4 with 5 true change points but N=1 requested, MCPI's stopping time still tracks the N=1 lower bound, confirming its Any-(N,δ) guarantee.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Track-and-Stop framework (Garivier & Kaufmann, 2016) and Chernoff stopping times (Kaufmann & Koolen, 2021) to the novel setting of fixed-confidence multiple change point identification under bandit feedback. It departs from prior fixed-budget change point work (Lan et al., 2009; Hall & Molchanov, 2003; Lazzaro & Pike-Burke, 2025) by replacing the total-sample constraint with a confidence-level constraint, and from clustering bandits (Yang et al., 2022) by exploiting the piecewise-constant structure across the action space. A key methodological departure is that the optimal sampling proportions admit a closed-form solution (equal allocation to the two arms adjacent to each change point, weighted inversely by change magnitude squared), eliminating the need for per-round numerical optimization that plagues general Track-and-Stop variants.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper fills a clear gap in the change point identification literature by providing the first comprehensive fixed-confidence treatment of multiple change points under bandit feedback, with both lower bounds and a matching algorithm (MCPI) that is simultaneously optimal for known and unknown numbers of changes. The practical relevance is substantial in domains where data is expensive and safety-critical (material phase transitions, seafloor mapping, system overload detection). Open questions include extending to piecewise-smooth continuous action spaces, identifying all changes above a threshold ε without specifying N, and adapting to alternative objectives like simple regret or cumulative reward. A limitation is the discrete action space assumption, though the authors note this corresponds to a fine discretization of the continuous case.
