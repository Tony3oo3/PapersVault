## Problem

Testing all 2^p combinatorial treatment combinations is infeasible for large p, and fractional factorial designs require pre-selecting a subset of combinations, which is difficult without prior knowledge and laborious to implement (e.g., synthesizing unique guide sequences per combination). The paper asks: can we design a scalable, unbiased factorial experiment that avoids pre-specifying which combinations to test while still efficiently learning treatment interactions?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252204/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes p treatments, a budget of n units per round, a bounded interaction order k, and (in the active setting) previously collected design matrices from prior rounds. It produces a dosage vector d ∈ [0,1]^p that parameterizes a product Bernoulli distribution over treatment combinations, and ultimately estimates the Fourier coefficients β_S for all subsets S ⊆ [p] with |S| ≤ k. The dosage vector is the actionable output: each treatment i is independently applied to each unit with probability d_i, so the experimenter only needs to set p continuous parameters rather than enumerate 2^p combinations.

## Method

The paper models outcomes via real-valued Boolean functions decomposed in the Fourier basis, where bounded-order interactions correspond to vanishing coefficients for |S| > k. Each unit independently receives a random treatment combination drawn from a product Bernoulli distribution controlled by the dosage vector, generalizing both full and fractional factorial designs as special cases. In the passive setting, the key insight is that the expected Gram matrix Σ(d) has constant trace K for all dosages, and by Cauchy-Schwarz the sum of inverse eigenvalues is minimized when Σ(d) = I, which holds iff d = (1/2,...,1/2); concentration inequalities on the random design matrix then yield near-optimality to a factor of 1 + O(ln(n)/n). In the active setting, the acquisition function minimizes the sum of inverse eigenvalues of Σ(d) plus the accumulated empirical Gram matrix from prior rounds, solvable via SLSQP numerical optimization. A truncated OLS estimator handles ill-conditioned design matrices by falling back to a zero estimate when eigenvalues are too small, enabling clean theoretical bounds.

## Results

In the passive setting, the paper proves that a uniform dosage of 1/2 per treatment is near-optimal (within a factor of 1 + O(ln(n)/n)) for estimating any k-way interaction model, with a sample complexity of O(kp^{3k} ln(p)) observations. The estimation error decays at rate O(Kσ²/n) where K = O(p^k) is the number of interaction terms. In simulations (p=8, degree-1, 64 samples), the half dosage achieves MSE 0.16 ± 0.078 versus 0.14 ± 0.062 for a Resolution V fractional design in the passive setting, but the optimal active acquisition strategy outperforms both the half-dosage and partial-design baselines when n is small relative to K.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the probabilistic factorial design against fractional factorial designs (Resolution V 2^{8−2} and 2^{5−1}) and against random and fixed half-dosage baselines in the active setting. In the passive setting with 64 samples, the carefully selected fractional design slightly outperforms the half dosage (MSE 0.14 vs. 0.16), as expected when sample budgets are tight and aliasing can be controlled. However, in the active (multi-round) setting, the proposed optimal acquisition function clearly outperforms both the fixed half-dosage and the partial fractional design, particularly when n is small relative to the number of interaction terms K (e.g., p=5, k=1, n=16, σ=5). When n is large relative to K (p=15, k=2, n=75), the optimal and half-dosage strategies perform nearly identically, both substantially outperforming random dosages. The key practical advantage of the probabilistic design is scalability: it requires specifying only p dosages (linear) rather than assembling 2^p specific combinations (exponential), making it feasible in settings like high-MOI perturbation experiments where synthesizing unique guide sequences for each combination is intractable.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Boolean function / Fourier coefficient framework for modeling combinatorial interventions introduced by Agarwal et al. (2023), and situates itself within the classical factorial design literature (Fisher, Box & Hunter, Gunst & Mason). The key conceptual departure is the introduction of a *probabilistic* factorial design where each unit independently receives treatments drawn from a product Bernoulli distribution parameterized by continuous dosages, rather than being assigned to a pre-specified discrete combination. This framework is shown to strictly generalize both full factorial designs (dosages at 0 or 1) and fractional factorial designs (specific dosage patterns), while remaining unbiased and scalable. The optimal design analysis then leverages Fourier analysis on the Boolean cube and concentration inequalities for the design matrix to characterize near-optimal dosage choices.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The main practical takeaway is that a uniform half-dosage is near-optimal for estimating any bounded-order interaction model regardless of the interaction degree k, which means experimenters do not need to know the interaction order in advance to design their experiment. The active-setting acquisition function provides a principled, numerically optimizable strategy for multi-round experiments that outperforms fixed half-dosage when per-round sample sizes are small. Key limitations include the assumption of a product (independent) infection mechanism, the absence of unit-specific covariates, and the bounded-order interaction assumption; extending to spatial interference, personalized treatment effects, and unbounded interactions are natural open questions. The sample complexity bound O(kp^{3k} ln(p)) also highlights the combinatorial cost of higher-order interactions, motivating sparsity-aware extensions.
