## Problem

LLMs and existing fine-tuning algorithms treat uncertainty passively and lack mechanisms for strategically selecting questions to reduce uncertainty about new, unseen latent entities (e.g., a student's skill profile or a patient's health status). Directly modeling a probability distribution over such complex latent entities in natural language is intractable, so the paper asks: can we instead quantify and reduce uncertainty through a predictive view—treating uncertainty about the latent entity as uncertainty about its unobserved future responses?

## Input / Output

At each adaptive round, the method takes a pool of N natural-language candidate questions and the running interaction history (prior question-answer pairs with the current entity) as input, and outputs the single next question to ask along with calibrated probability estimates over held-out target answers. The model operates autoregressively over concatenated Q&A text sequences, with a gradient mask applied so that only answer tokens contribute to the training loss. Evaluation is performed over 10,000 trials per dataset, measuring accuracy, perplexity, ECE, and Brier score on K=5 held-out target questions after sequentially selecting from N=20 candidates.

## Method

The core contribution is a meta-learned autoregressive language model (Llama-3.1-8B + LoRA) trained on diverse historical Q&A trajectories from many latent entities, where the training objective is the joint log-likelihood of each answer conditioned on all prior observations. This predictive model replaces the intractable task of specifying a distribution over the latent entity: epistemic uncertainty is simply the conditional entropy of unobserved future answers given current data, and aleatoric uncertainty is the irreducible noise that remains even with infinite data. For question selection, the model computes Expected Information Gain (EIG) by autoregressively simulating candidate future answers from its own predictive distribution, then either greedily picks the question maximizing one-step EIG or uses a Monte Carlo Tree Search policy that simulates multi-step question-answer sequences to capture long-horizon information gains. The meta-learning step is essential—ablations show that planning-based selection on a base LLM or in-context-tuned model performs poorly or shows no gain, while the meta-trained model's calibrated predictive distribution makes EIG-guided selection effective.

## Results

Across all three datasets (Twenty Questions, OpinionQA, EEDI), the adaptive elicitation framework consistently outperforms both the Base LLM (random selection) and In-Context Tuning (embedding-similarity selection) baselines on accuracy and perplexity for predicting held-out target questions. At the standard setting (20 candidate questions, 5 targets, 4 questions selected), the method achieves 0.6784 accuracy on EEDI (vs. 0.6005 Base, 0.6278 ICT) and 0.5987 on OpinionQA (vs. 0.4089 Base, 0.5023 ICT). The advantage grows with more selected questions, and the method is especially effective on rare/hard questions, where the relative gain over random selection exceeds 10× on EEDI and Twenty Questions and 5× on OpinionQA.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: (1) Base LLM — Llama-3.1-8B with no fine-tuning and random question selection, and (2) In-Context Tuning (ICT) — the same model meta-trained via in-context learning (Chen et al., 2022) with questions selected by embedding similarity to target questions. The proposed method wins decisively on all three datasets. On EEDI, the method achieves 0.6784 accuracy vs. 0.6278 for ICT and 0.6005 for Base LLM (a ~7.8% absolute gain over ICT). On OpinionQA, the gap is even larger: 0.5987 vs. 0.5023 (ICT) and 0.4089 (Base), a ~9.6% absolute gain over ICT. The training ablation (Figure 5) further shows that planning (EIG-based selection) actually *hurts* the Base LLM (reducing accuracy ~15% on hard questions) and leaves ICT largely unchanged, while the meta-trained model benefits substantially from planning — confirming that the meta-learning procedure is essential for enabling strategic information gathering. MCTS planning further improves over greedy EIG in all settings, with the largest gains on rare/hard questions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the predictive view of uncertainty from Ye et al. (2024) and Fong et al. (2023)—where latent variables are treated as unobserved data and uncertainty is quantified through prediction of future observations—into the natural language setting using LLMs. It departs from traditional Computerized Adaptive Testing (IRT, DINA, knowledge tracing) and Bayesian experimental design, which require explicit parametric models of the latent entity, by instead meta-learning an autoregressive language model over historical Q&A trajectories and using its predictive perplexity as an uncertainty signal. The adaptive question selection mechanism draws on Expected Information Gain (Chaloner & Verdinelli, 1995) and MCTS, but the key departure is that the 'environment model' for simulating future responses is a meta-learned LLM rather than a hand-specified probabilistic model, enabling operation in the high-dimensional space of natural language.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a practical path to adaptive information gathering in natural language without requiring domain-specific parametric models of the latent entity, and it scales with LLM capability. The finding that adaptive strategies provide >10× greater gains for rare/atypical latent traits (e.g., a student struggling with a concept most find easy) has direct implications for personalized education and diagnostics. Key limitations include the need for substantial historical Q&A data for meta-training, the submodularity assumption required for theoretical guarantees, and the relatively small experimental scale (20 candidate questions, 8B model). Open questions include whether the approach extends to truly open-ended question generation (beyond selecting from a fixed pool), computational cost at longer horizons, and robustness when the meta-training distribution shifts significantly from the test distribution.
