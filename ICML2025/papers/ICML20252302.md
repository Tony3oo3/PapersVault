## Problem

Existing LLM safety benchmarks evaluate refusal behavior on isolated queries, ignoring the context in which a query occurs. This leads to over-refusal of benign queries under safe contexts and fails to capture the substantial influence context has on whether a response is appropriate. The paper asks whether LLMs can make context-aware safety judgments that align with human assessments.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252302/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance pairs a potentially harmful user query (drawn from SORRY-Bench's 450 instructions across 45 categories) with a formally structured context description using Contextual Integrity parameters (sender, recipient, transmission principle). The LLM acts as a judge rather than a responder, producing either a binary safe/unsafe label with explanation, a calibrated 1–10 safety score, or a normalized token probability over 'safe' vs. 'unsafe' tokens. Contexts are assumed verified and reliable, and the LLM is explicitly instructed not to respond to the query but to judge its safety given the context.

## Method

CASE-Bench formalizes context using Nissenbaum's Contextual Integrity theory, decomposing it into sender (nature of interaction, platform type), recipient (type, background), and transmission principle (sender purpose, confidentiality, source accountability) parameters. For each of 450 queries, two distinct contexts (one safe, one unsafe) are generated via GPT-4o and then manually revised by two researchers plus a domain expert, yielding 900 query-context pairs. Human ground truth is collected from 2,000+ MTurk annotators (21 per task, determined via G*Power analysis with f=0.4, α=0.05, power=0.8), producing 47,000+ annotations. LLMs are evaluated as judges using three prompting strategies—binary classification, direct 1–10 scoring (averaged over 3 stochastic samples), and normalized token probabilities for open-source models—with agreement measured via accuracy, per-class recall, Pearson correlation, and binary cross-entropy against human ratings.

## Results

On CASE-Bench, Claude-3.5-sonnet achieves the highest accuracy (90.1%) and Pearson Correlation Coefficient (76.27) using the direct score method, with a well-balanced recall of 90.7% (safe) and 89.6% (unsafe). GPT-4o exhibits severe over-refusal, with safe-context recall dropping to 54.6% (binary) and 58.1% (score), while maintaining high unsafe recall (94.8%). DeepSeek-R1, despite not being safety-optimized, achieves 88.6% accuracy and 73.34 PCC, closely matching Claude-3.5-sonnet and significantly outperforming GPT-4o. Statistical analysis confirms context has a substantial and significant influence on human safety judgments (p < 0.0001 via z-test across all four context conditions).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Claude-3.5-sonnet outperforms all other models on accuracy (90.1% vs. 88.6% for DeepSeek-R1 and 78.6% for GPT-4o) and PCC (76.27 vs. 73.34 for DeepSeek-R1), while also achieving the best safe-context recall (90.7%), indicating superior context-aware safety judgment. GPT-4o and GPT-4o-mini suffer from pronounced over-refusal: GPT-4o's safe recall is only 54.6–58.1%, roughly 30–36 points below Claude-3.5-sonnet, despite comparable unsafe recall (~94.8%). DeepSeek-R1, a reasoning-focused model not specifically safety-tuned, nearly matches Claude-3.5-sonnet (88.6% vs. 90.1% accuracy), suggesting strong reasoning capabilities transfer to safety judgment. Among open-source 70B models, Llama-3-70B-Instruct (87.2% with token probabilities) and Qwen2-72B (86.4% binary) perform competitively on accuracy but lag on PCC (70.91 and 69.64 respectively) and show imbalanced safe/unsafe recall. Smaller 7B/8B models (Llama-3-8B, Mistral-7B, Zephyr-7B) degrade substantially, with Zephyr-7B achieving only 56.0% binary accuracy and 2.8% safe recall, confirming that context-aware safety judgment requires substantial model capacity. Combining all models via averaging yields the lowest BCE (0.6814) but does not surpass the best single model on accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM safety evaluation task by incorporating formalized context into safety judgments, departing from prior benchmarks (SORRY-Bench, XSTest, HarmBench, SimpleSafetyTests) that assess safety as a property of isolated queries. It repurposes Nissenbaum's Contextual Integrity theory—originally developed for privacy norms in information flows—as a structured framework for describing the sender, recipient, and transmission principles that govern whether a chatbot response is appropriate. The query set is drawn directly from SORRY-Bench, while the context generation and annotation methodology (power analysis, between-subjects design, 2,000+ annotators) represents a methodological departure from prior safety benchmarks that typically rely on small annotator pools without statistical justification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CASE-Bench provides the first rigorous, statistically powered evidence that context substantially shifts human safety judgments (p < 0.0001), validating a concern previously acknowledged anecdotally but never quantified at scale. The finding that dedicated safety classifiers (Llama-Guard-3-8B) perform far worse than general-purpose LLMs on context-aware judgment, and that safety-optimized GPT-4o exhibits pronounced over-refusal in safe contexts, highlights a practical gap in current deployment pipelines. Open questions include how to verify context reliability in production systems, whether the CI framework generalizes beyond the 45 SORRY-Bench categories, and whether smaller safety classifiers can be fine-tuned on context-dependent samples to close the performance gap.
