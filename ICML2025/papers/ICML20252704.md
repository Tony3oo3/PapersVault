## Problem

Sampling from high-dimensional, multi-modal distributions remains a fundamental challenge: MCMC methods require exponentially many steps for mixing even with just two modes, and recent Normalizing Flow-based annealing methods (CRAFT, LFIS, PGPS) depend heavily on score estimation, Monte Carlo kernel assistance, or Langevin corrections, making them unstable and computationally expensive. This paper addresses the gap of achieving efficient, stable sampling in high-dimensional multi-modal settings with minimal reliance on auxiliary MCMC or score-matching machinery.

## Input / Output

The method requires only the unnormalized target density q̃(x) (no normalizing constant Z is needed), a base distribution π₀(x) (typically N(0, I_d)), and a user-chosen annealing schedule {β_k}. Training is block-wise—only one velocity-field neural network is trained at a time, reducing memory to a single 32-32 hidden-layer network (2.1 MB total for 20 blocks in the 50D experiment). After a one-time offline training phase, sampling is extremely fast: 10,000 samples are generated in ~2.1 seconds in 50D. The Importance Flow extension additionally produces a low-variance importance sampling estimator via a learned density-ratio network.

## Method

Annealing Flow (AF) is a Continuous Normalizing Flow that learns a piecewise velocity field v_k(x,t) to transport mass from an easy base density π₀ through a sequence of annealed intermediate densities f_k(x) ∝ π₀(x)^(1−β_k) q̃(x)^(β_k) to the target q(x). Each block is trained by minimizing a dynamic Optimal Transport objective: a KL divergence term (expressed via the log-density and the divergence of v_k) plus a dynamic W₂ regularization that discretizes the kinetic energy as a sum of stepwise squared displacements. A key theoretical result (Theorem 3.3) shows that in the infinitesimal limit, the optimal velocity field equals the score difference s_k − s_{k−1} between consecutive annealing densities—a property unique to this dynamic OT formulation and absent in score-matching-based alternatives. The divergence ∇·v_k is computed via the Hutchinson trace estimator, and trajectories are integrated with Runge-Kutta, avoiding the need for explicit score estimation or MC kernel tuning that plagues competing NF methods.

## Results

On the most challenging benchmark—a 50-dimensional Exponentially Weighted Gaussian with 1,024 widely separated modes—AF explores all 1,024 modes, while the next-best methods (SVGD: 916.4, MIED: 890.6, PGPS: 813.6) miss a substantial fraction, and PT explores fewer than 10 modes. On Bayesian logistic regression across 8 LIBSVM datasets, AF achieves competitive accuracy (e.g., 86.9% on Splice, 92.0% on Ijcnn1) with the best log-posterior on several datasets. On 10-mode GMMs arranged on a circle of radius 12, AF achieves the lowest Mode-Weight MSE among all methods with only 12 time steps, matching or exceeding baselines that require 128–256 steps.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AF decisively outperforms all seven baselines on the high-dimensional multi-modal ExpGauss benchmark: in 50D with 1,024 modes, AF recovers all modes while PT explores fewer than 10, AI-Sampler only 125.6, LFIS 298.2, and CRAFT 473.2. Among NF baselines, PGPS (813.6) and SVGD (916.4) come closest but still miss 100+ modes, whereas AF achieves perfect coverage with only 20 time steps versus 128–256 for CRAFT/LFIS/PGPS. On Bayesian logistic regression, AF is competitive but not uniformly dominant: CRAFT and PGPS slightly edge AF on Cancer (98.9% vs 97.9%) and Heart (88.4% vs 88.5%), while AF clearly wins on Splice (86.9% vs 81.1% CRAFT, 82.8% PGPS) and Ijcnn1 (92.0% vs 88.8% CRAFT). The paper notes that CRAFT heavily depends on MC kernel choice, LFIS requires accurate score estimation with many more steps, and PGPS degrades significantly without Langevin corrections—limitations AF avoids through its dynamic OT objective with minimal MC assistance.

## Relation to Prior Work

Annealing Flow (AF) sits at the intersection of three established threads: the Benamou-Brenier dynamic optimal transport formulation (used here as the per-segment training objective), the classical annealing schedule for constructing intermediate densities (Neal, 2001), and the JKO/Wasserstein gradient flow framework (Jordan et al., 1998), whose equivalence to AF's objective is proven in Appendix B when all β_k = 1. The key departure from recent NF-based samplers (CRAFT, LFIS, PGPS) is the replacement of score-matching or MCMC-correction losses with a dynamic OT objective augmented by a stepwise W₂ regularization, which the authors show (Theorem 3.3) yields an optimal infinitesimal velocity equal to the score difference between consecutive annealing densities—a property absent in prior annealing-like NFs. Block-wise training of separate CNF segments, combined with the Hutchinson trace estimator for divergence computation, makes the approach computationally tractable without requiring a single monolithic network.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

AF addresses a persistent gap: high-dimensional, multi-modal sampling where MCMC suffers exponential mixing times and existing NF methods require 10–20× more time steps or heavy MCMC/score-matching assistance. The 50D ExpGauss experiment (1024/1024 modes vs. <10 for PT, ~473 for CRAFT) and the complete failure of all baselines on the truncated normal distribution highlight a practical regime where AF is currently the only method that succeeds. Limitations include the offline training cost, the somewhat manual choice of annealing schedule and α values, and evaluation confined to synthetic distributions and small-scale Bayesian problems (≤61 dimensions). Open questions include whether the approach scales to thousands of dimensions, whether the annealing schedule can be learned automatically, and whether the preliminary Importance Flow extension can be made distribution-free as the authors sketch in Appendix D.4.
