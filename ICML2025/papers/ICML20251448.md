## Problem

Pre-trained Vision-Language Models (VLMs) like CLIP produce unreliable zero-shot class labels for weakly supervised learning because the highest-confidence prediction is often wrong, and existing weakly supervised methods cannot effectively leverage VLM knowledge to generate accurate labels. This paper introduces True-False Labels (TFLs) as a novel labeling setting where an annotator (human or VLM) only judges whether an instance belongs to a randomly sampled class, achieving >99.5% annotation accuracy with VLMs and ~70× faster human labeling.

## Input / Output

During training, each RGB image (resized to 224×224) is paired with a TFL: a randomly and uniformly sampled class label ȳ from the K-class candidate set plus a binary flag s∈{0,1} indicating whether the instance belongs to ȳ. At test time, only the image is provided and the model outputs a single class label from the K-class set. The key distinction from standard supervision is that the vast majority of training samples carry s=0 (False), meaning the sampled label is *not* the true class, so the model must learn from negative evidence combined with VLM-estimated conditional probabilities.

## Method

The method (TMP) has two core components. First, a risk-consistent estimator decomposes the classification risk into a standard cross-entropy term for s=1 (True) instances and a weighted loss for s=0 (False) instances, where the weights are conditional probabilities p(y=i|ȳ=j,s=0,x) estimated as a linear blend (hyperparameter λ) of a learned linear classifier and CLIP's zero-shot softmax. Second, a Multi-modal Prompt Retrieving (MPR) module uses a small 4-block CNN (150K params) fed with an all-ones matrix to produce a prompt embedding, which is then used via cosine similarity to retrieve top-K text embeddings (K_T=15) from CLIP's text encoder and top-K image embeddings (K_I=5) from CLIP's image encoder; these retrieved embeddings are concatenated with the image features before the linear classifier. The CLIP ViT-L/14 backbone remains frozen throughout training, and only the CNN prompt network and linear head are optimized with cross-entropy loss.

## Results

On five image classification benchmarks, TMP (human) achieves an average accuracy of 82.07%, outperforming all weakly supervised baselines by over 29% on average and surpassing Zero-shot CLIP (79.80%) by approximately 2.3%. On the fine-grained Stanford Cars dataset, TMP (human) reaches 72.60%, exceeding the best weakly supervised baseline (CLL with WL at 9.64%) by over 63%. The VLMs-generated TFLs (TMP VLMs, 81.71% average) perform within 0.3% of human-generated TFLs, confirming that CLIP can produce near-perfect TFL annotations with >99.5% accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TMP decisively outperforms all four weakly supervised baselines across every dataset. Against ORCA (Cao et al., 2022), TMP (human) leads by 62.6% on average (82.07% vs. 19.48%), with the gap exceeding 65% on Stanford Cars. NACH (Guo et al., 2022) trails by 57.2% on average, and PaPi (Xia et al., 2023) by 35.1%, both struggling severely on fine-grained datasets (Stanford Cars: 5.57% and 6.00% respectively vs. TMP's 72.60%). CLL with WL (Gao & Zhang, 2021) is the strongest weakly supervised baseline at 52.60% average but still lags TMP by 29.5%, particularly on CIFAR-100 (63.25% vs. 78.72%) and Stanford Cars (9.64% vs. 72.60%). Compared to CLIP-based methods (Radford et al., 2021), TMP surpasses Zero-shot CLIP by 2.3% on average and dramatically outperforms CLIP LPT (28.05%) by over 54%, while remaining within 8% of the fully supervised CLIP LPS upper bound (90.11%) despite using only weakly supervised TFL annotations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing True-False Labels (TFLs) as a novel weakly supervised supervision setting, positioned between complementary-label learning (the 'False' case) and standard positive labels (the 'True' case), but distinct from both. The risk-consistent estimator follows the theoretical tradition of unbiased estimators in CLL (Ishida et al.) and PLL (Feng et al.), while the Multi-modal Prompt Retrieving (MPR) component departs from prior prompt learning work (CoOp, CoCoOp, VPT, MaPLe) by using a convolutional network to retrieve prompt embeddings rather than directly optimizing text or image prompts. The paper repurposes CLIP's zero-shot capability not as a label generator but as a binary verifier, which is a conceptual shift from prior VLM-based pseudo-labeling approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The TFL setting offers a practically significant annotation paradigm: VLMs can verify binary membership with >99.5% accuracy (far exceeding their ~80% zero-shot classification accuracy), and human annotators gain a ~70× speedup by eliminating the need to browse candidate label sets. The near-equivalence between VLM-generated and human-generated TFLs (≤0.3% gap) suggests this could substantially reduce annotation costs in practice. Open questions include whether the approach extends beyond classification (e.g., detection, segmentation), whether the convolutional prompt network generalizes to non-CLIP VLMs, and the authors' noted limitation that test-time prompt retrieval remains unexplored.
