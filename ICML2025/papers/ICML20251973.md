## Problem

Popular approximate inference algorithms for Bayesian Neural Networks (VI, Laplace, SWAG, SGLD) are widely assumed to respect the theoretical properties of Bayesian belief updating, yet no systematic empirical study has tested whether they actually do. This paper addresses the gap by investigating whether BNNs preserve key Bayesian invariants—functional posterior variability, sequential coherence of belief updates, and predictive self-consistency—beyond static prediction settings where they are typically evaluated.

## Input / Output

The diagnostics take as input approximate BNN posteriors (mean-field VI, diagonal Laplace, SWAG, HMC/NUTS, SGLD) trained on 1-D synthetic regression data, 32×32 CIFAR-10/100 images (ResNet-20-FRN), and IMDB text (CNN-LSTM), using the exact hyperparameters from Izmailov et al. (2021b). Rather than producing a new model, the outputs are behavioral measurements: functional posterior samples in predictive space, BALD acquisition scores, sequential-inference accuracy/log-likelihood/ECE comparisons between iterated and single-step posteriors, and martingale posterior BMA metrics with bootstrap confidence intervals that quantify the degree of Bayesian incoherence.

## Method

The paper introduces three complementary diagnostic tests for Bayesian coherence. First, it examines functional-space posterior variability by drawing predictive function samples from each approximate posterior and assessing whether they capture qualitatively distinct models, particularly in unobserved regions. Second, it tests sequential coherence (Proposition 5.1) by training an intermediate posterior on a data split, using it as the prior for a second split, and comparing the iterated posterior's BMA against a single-step posterior trained on all data simultaneously. Third, and most novelly, it adapts the martingale posterior framework of Fong et al. (2023) to BNNs via a single-step predictive resampling procedure: synthetic labels are drawn from the posterior predictive on an unlabeled split, the BNN is retrained on the combined labeled + synthetic data, and the empirical average over many such retrained posteriors is compared to the original posterior's BMA. Under exact Bayesian inference, Proposition 6.1 guarantees this martingale posterior recovers the initial beliefs; the observed deviations (improved accuracy, altered calibration) falsify Bayesian coherence for both VI and SGLD.

## Results

The paper finds that all tested BNN algorithms (VI, Laplace, SWAG, SGLD, HMC) fail to respect key Bayesian properties. In sequential inference on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and IMDB, iterated MFVI significantly underperforms single-step MFVI across accuracy, log-likelihood, and ECE, with the performance gap exceeding that of a non-Bayesian sequential SGD baseline. In the martingale posterior coherence test on CIFAR-10, both VI and SGLD violate predictive coherence: the empirical martingale posterior deviates significantly from the initial posterior (beyond bootstrap confidence intervals), yet paradoxically achieves *better* BMA accuracy and lower ECE, indicating the initial BMA is suboptimal. On synthetic regression, Laplace and SWAG posteriors collapse to MAP-like behavior with negligible functional variability, while even HMC fails to capture qualitatively distinct function behaviors in extrapolation regions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates five approximate inference algorithms against theoretical Bayesian properties rather than proposing a new method. HMC/NUTS (Neal 2011) is the closest to true Bayesian behavior, retaining more functional variability and sensible BALD scores, but still fails to capture non-monotonic behavior in extrapolation regions and produces worse fits than VI in sequential settings when used as a final-step sampler. Mean-field VI (Graves 2011) shows moderate functional variability but suffers significant catastrophic forgetting in sequential inference on CIFAR-10/100 and IMDB, where iterated VI underperforms single-step VI by a margin comparable to a non-Bayesian sequential SGD baseline. Diagonal Laplace (Daxberger 2021) and SWAG (Maddox 2019) exhibit the most severe failures: their function-space posteriors collapse to near-deterministic MAP-like behavior with negligible uncertainty in extrapolation regions, and they expect near-zero information gain from new observations far from training data. SGLD (Welling 2011) is tested only in the martingale coherence experiment, where it also violates predictive coherence, though the performance improvement from the martingale ensemble is less pronounced than for VI due to SGLD's already underconfident calibration.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of BNN evaluation work by Izmailov et al. (2021b) and Sommer et al. (2024), which focused on static predictive performance and inference convergence, by systematically testing whether approximate BNN posteriors respect the *behavioral* properties of Bayesian inference: sequential coherence (Proposition 5.1), predictive coherence via the martingale posterior framework of Fong et al. (2023), and functional-space variability. It builds on prior critiques of BNN fidelity (Foong et al. 2020, Coker et al. 2022, Sharma et al. 2023) and the cold posterior effect literature (Wenzel et al. 2020, Noci et al. 2021), but reframes the question from 'how well does the BMA predict?' to 'does the posterior actually update like a Bayesian model should?' The adaptation of the martingale posterior diagnostic to BNNs is the key methodological contribution, repurposing a theoretical framework from Fong et al. (2023) as an empirical coherence test.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The findings directly challenge the justification for using BNNs in active learning (BALD, EPIG), continual learning, and transfer learning, where sequential Bayesian updating is a core assumption. The result that iterated VI performs no better than a naive 'sequential SGD' baseline (initializing from the first split's MAP) is a strong practical warning. The paper leaves open whether these pathologies are fundamental to approximate inference or fixable with better samplers, and whether entirely non-Bayesian methods (deep ensembles, epistemic neural networks) might provide more coherent sequential behavior. The martingale posterior ensemble as a practical remedy—improving accuracy without new data—suggests a promising but computationally expensive direction for future work.
