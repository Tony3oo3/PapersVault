## Problem

Prediction Powered Inference (PPI++) is designed to combine a large pool of pseudolabels with a small pool of gold labels to produce unbiased, low-variance mean estimates, but prior work assumes 50+ labelled examples. This paper shows that in the few-label regime (n < 50), PPI++ can perform worse than classical sample-mean estimation, a critical gap given that many practical evaluation settings (e.g., qualitative LLM assessments, rare-event rate estimation) have only a handful of human annotations.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a small labelled set D_n of n (X, h(X)) pairs, a large pseudolabelled set D_N of N (X, f(X)) pairs with N >> n, and a binary predictive model f. It outputs a single point estimate of E_P[h(X)] (a rate or mean), with the key constraint that n is very small (as low as 5–20 in experiments) while N is fixed at 1000. The estimate remains unbiased for any choice of the regression parameters, but variance reduction depends on how well the post-hoc regression fits f to h on the small labelled pool.

## Method

The paper reinterprets the PPI tuning parameter λ as the univariate OLS regression coefficient from regressing h(X) on f(X), revealing that PPI++'s λ selection inherits OLS's high variance when n is small. Two regression-inspired modifications are proposed: Ridge-PPI adds an L2 penalty (α) to the denominator of the λ estimator, shrinking it toward zero and reducing estimation variance at the cost of negligible bias; Sigmoid-PPI replaces the linear transform λf(X) with a two-parameter sigmoid g(f(X)) = 1/(1+exp(−αf(X)+β)) that better matches the binary nature of h. A theoretical analysis treating λ as a stochastic quantity shows that the variance of the λ estimator itself is a previously overlooked error source, and that small Var[f(X)] amplifies this effect — explaining empirically when PPI++ fails and why ridge regularization helps. Both methods select their regularization parameters via cross-validation on the labelled set.

## Results

On the LLM Refusal Dataset, Ridge-PPI and Sigmoid-PPI reduce the mean absolute error (MAE) of classical estimation by over 25% for small labelled batch sizes (n ≤ 20), while also reducing the standard deviation of estimates. On research datasets (Plankton, AlphaFold, Forest, Ballots, Census_Healthcare), both methods match or exceed the performance of classical estimation and PPI++ across all settings, with the greatest relative improvements occurring around n=20. In favorable distributions (high Var[f(X)]), PPI++ cuts classical error by ~30%; in unfavorable distributions (low Var[f(X)]), PPI++ accrues 20% more error than classical, while Ridge-PPI and Sigmoid-PPI still achieve 10% improvement over classical. A strong anticorrelation (Pearson r = −0.69) is found between the PPI++/Ridge-PPI MSE ratio and Var[f(X)], confirming that low predictor variance is the key failure mode of PPI++.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four estimation methods: Classical (sample mean), PPI++ (OLS-based λ), Ridge-PPI (ridge-regularized λ), and Sigmoid-PPI (sigmoidal post-hoc regression). Against Classical estimation, both Ridge-PPI and Sigmoid-PPI consistently achieve lower normalized MAE for small n (≤ 20) across all six datasets, with the largest gains (>25% MAE reduction) on the LLM Refusal Dataset. Against PPI++, Ridge-PPI and Sigmoid-PPI outperform for all small values of n on the LLM Refusal Dataset and on research datasets where PPI++ struggles (Plankton, AlphaFold, Forest). Critically, in distributions where PPI++ performs worse than classical (e.g., certain LLM subsets with low Var[f(X)]), Ridge-PPI and Sigmoid-PPI still beat classical by ~10%, demonstrating robustness where PPI++ fails. As n grows beyond ~20, Ridge-PPI converges to PPI++ performance, while Sigmoid-PPI's advantage decays unless a scaling factor (1+n/N)⁻¹ is applied. The paper does not report a single unified numerical table; results are presented as normalized MAE curves across varying n in Figures 1–3 and 5–6.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Prediction Powered Inference (PPI) framework of Angelopoulos et al. (2023a) and its efficient variant PPI++ (Angelopoulos et al., 2023b) by re-interpreting the tuning parameter λ as an ordinary least squares regression coefficient, connecting PPI to the broader control-variate and variance-reduction literature in statistics. Building on Boyeau et al. (2024) for auto-evaluation applications, the paper departs from prior PPI work by focusing on the few-label regime (n < 50) where previous analyses assumed 200+ labels, and by treating λ as a stochastic quantity rather than a constant, revealing that Var[f(X)] plays a previously unconsidered role in PPI's efficacy. The proposed Ridge-PPI and Sigmoid-PPI estimators draw directly on standard regression techniques (L2 regularization, non-linear function classes) to address the high-variance problem that OLS-based λ estimation exhibits with small samples.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that PPI++ can perform worse than classical inference in the few-label regime is practically important for LLM evaluation and scientific research, where hand-labelled examples are often scarce and expensive. The regression-based reinterpretation provides a principled framework for understanding when and why PPI succeeds or fails, and the proposed methods offer concrete, easy-to-implement improvements. Open questions include whether Sigmoid-PPI introduces asymptotic bias (the paper notes its proof of unbiasedness does not directly extend), how these methods behave when labelled and unlabelled data come from different distributions, and whether subgroup-level performance disparities raise fairness concerns. The scope remains limited to binary mean estimation with univariate regression, leaving multi-dimensional and non-binary settings unaddressed.
