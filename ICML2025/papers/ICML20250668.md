## Problem

Classical learning-theoretic guarantees ensure that a reward model with low training error approximates the true reward well on the training distribution, but in RL the subsequent policy optimization step induces a distributional shift that can cause the policy to exploit reward model errors in low-coverage regions. This paper formalizes the resulting 'error-regret mismatch'—where a reward model has low expected error yet its optimal policy has large true regret—and proves that for any fixed error bound ε, realistic data distributions exist that permit this mismatch, even under KL-regularized optimization as used in RLHF.

## Input / Output

The analysis takes an MDP ⟨S, A, τ, μ₀, R, γ⟩, a data distribution D over S×A, an error threshold ε, a regret threshold L, and optionally a regularization function ω with weight λ. The key output is a matrix M (depending on the MDP, reward, and set of high-regret policies) such that D is safe if and only if M·D > ε·range(R)·1, giving a complete polyhedral characterization of the safe distribution simplex. Additionally, the paper provides explicit sufficient conditions for safety (Theorem 3.2, involving min D(s,a)) and for unsafety (Theorems 3.3–3.4, 4.2, 6.1, involving D(supp D^π) being small).

## Method

The core technique pairs Berge's maximum theorem (yielding positive results: as ε→0, worst-case regret→0 for any positive data distribution) with explicit adversarial reward model constructions (yielding negative results: for any fixed ε>0, one can build a reward model that is ε-close to R under D but whose optimal policy has regret ≥ L by inflating rewards on the low-coverage support of a bad policy). The unifying contribution (Theorem 3.5) shows the safe set is exactly a polytope defined by linear constraints M·D > ε·range(R)·1, where rows of M are derived from the occupancy measures of all deterministic policies achieving regret ≥ L. The paper then extends the framework to regularized optimization (showing KL-penalty does not eliminate mismatch), generalizes the error metric from per-transition MAE to trajectory returns and preference-distribution KL divergence (Theorems 5.1–5.3), and specializes to the RLHF contextual bandit setting where it proves a stronger negative result (Theorem 6.1) showing that any reference policy assigning sufficiently low probability to bad actions yields an unsafe data distribution.

## Results

The paper establishes that while a sufficiently low reward model error ε guarantees low worst-case regret (Theorem 3.1), the required ε scales proportionally to min_{(s,a)} D(s,a) (Theorem 3.2), making it impractically small for realistic MDPs. For any fixed ε > 0, there exist data distributions that are unsafe, i.e., allow error-regret mismatch (Theorem 3.3), and for MDPs with ≥ 1/ε independent bad policies with disjoint support, *all* data distributions are unsafe (Theorem 3.4). Theorem 3.5 provides a complete characterization: safe distributions are exactly those satisfying a system of linear inequalities M·D > ε·range(R)·1. Regularization (including KL-regularization as in RLHF) does not resolve the issue (Theorems 4.1–4.2, 6.1).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or comparison tables. The closest prior work is Song et al. (2024), which analyzes RLHF with mean-squared error and shows Regret ∈ O(Cov·√ε) under a full-coverage assumption. The present paper extends this in three key ways: (1) it covers general MDPs and contextual bandits rather than only RLHF, (2) it handles both regularized and unregularized policy optimization, and (3) it provides necessary-and-sufficient conditions (Theorem 3.5) for safety rather than only sufficient conditions. The paper also shows that the positive guarantee of Song et al. becomes vacuous when coverage is insufficient, formalizing the negative results that prior sample-complexity work (Foster et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2022b; Nika et al., 2024) only addressed in terms of sample size rather than reward model error.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the offline RL and reward learning literature (Foster et al., Wang et al., Uehara & Sun, Nika et al.) by shifting the analysis from sample complexity to the direct relationship between reward model error and policy regret under worst-case assumptions. The closest prior work is Song et al. (2024), which recovers the paper's safe-distribution definition for the special case of RLHF with mean-squared error; this paper generalizes to arbitrary MDPs and contextual bandits, both regularized and unregularized optimization, and provides necessary and sufficient conditions (Theorem 3.5) rather than one-directional bounds. Conceptually, the paper formalizes a reward-hacking/Goodhart's-law concern that has been discussed qualitatively in the AI safety literature (Krakovna, Skalse et al., Zhuang & Hadfield-Menell) into a precise geometric characterization of safe versus unsafe data distributions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central result—that for any fixed reward model error ε > 0, there exist realistic data distributions (and in large MDPs, ALL distributions) that permit high-regret policies despite low training error—challenges the foundational assumption in RLHF that 'train the reward model well enough and the policy will be safe.' This has direct implications for the safety of frontier LLM alignment, where the action space is enormous and coverage is inherently sparse. Key open questions include whether implicit reward models (DPO and variants) are more robust, whether on-policy reward learning or ensemble methods can close the gap, and how to develop evaluation metrics for reward models that go beyond in-distribution training error. The paper is purely theoretical and provides no algorithmic fix, so translating these worst-case guarantees into practical mitigations remains an important next step.
