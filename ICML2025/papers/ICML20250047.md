## Problem

Bias mitigation methods for machine learning frequently fail to outperform the ERM baseline in new settings, yet the literature has focused almost exclusively on designing new mitigation algorithms while largely ignoring the crucial step of defining which subgroups to apply them to. This paper asks: can the choice of subgroup definition explain why these methods often fail, and does observing a performance disparity across a set of subgroups actually justify using those subgroups for mitigation?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250047/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The experiments use four semi-synthetic or real datasets (MNIST with added background/foreground color attributes, CheXpert chest X-rays with pacemaker and sex annotations, CelebA face images with perceived gender and smiling attributes, and Civilcomments text with gender and religion mention attributes), each constructed to satisfy a specific spurious-correlation distribution where attribute A is spuriously correlated with label Y in training but not in testing, with the correlation unevenly distributed across attribute S. The method takes these inputs plus one of 15 (reweighting) or 12 (model-based) subgroup definitions and produces binary classification predictions, evaluated by AUC on the unbiased test set, worst-group accuracy, and accuracy gap across S subgroups. A key design choice is that reweighting methods (gDRO, resampling) can use subgroups over A×Y, while model-based methods (DomainInd, CFair) use subgroups over A alone, since the latter require each subgroup to contain both positive and negative classes.

## Method

The paper constructs a controlled semi-synthetic bias setting inspired by a real chest X-ray example (Olesen et al., 2024) where a spurious correlation between attribute A and label Y is present in 95% of one S-subgroup and 80% of the other, then systematically evaluates four state-of-the-art bias mitigation methods (gDRO, resampling, DomainInd, CFair) across 15 and 12 different subgroup definitions respectively, including coarse, fine-grained, intersectional, SC/no-SC, and noisy subgroups. The core theoretical contribution is showing that mitigation success is predicted by the minimum KL divergence between the subgroup-weighted training distribution P_train^w and the unbiased test distribution P_unbiased: subgroupings that allow reweighting to recover the unbiased distribution (e.g., (A,Y) achieving KL≈0.113 vs. S achieving KL≈0.527) yield the best generalisation, while those that cannot (S, SY, Random) degrade performance below ERM. A counter-intuitive finding is that the best fairness outcomes with respect to S subgroups are achieved by using (A,Y) subgroups for mitigation rather than S subgroups, because the (A,Y) spurious correlation is the actual cause of the S-disparity and can only be unlearned by grouping around it. The paper further validates that increasing subgroup granularity has negligible effect, methods are robust to up to ~10% annotation noise, and the KL-divergence–performance correlation (r=0.73–0.97) holds across all four datasets and both reweighting methods.

## Results

The ERM baseline drops 0.10–0.25 AUC on the unbiased test set across all four datasets (e.g., 0.943→0.698 on MNIST, 0.898→0.659 on CheXpert). Using (A,Y) subgroups with gDRO or resampling improves worst-group accuracy by up to 0.21 and reduces the S-group accuracy gap by up to 10× relative to ERM, while S-based subgroups consistently harm performance, decreasing AUC by up to 0.08. The minimum achievable KL divergence between the reweighted training distribution and the unbiased test distribution correlates strongly with generalisation performance (Pearson r = 0.73–0.97 across datasets), providing a quantitative explanation for why subgroups capturing the spurious-correlation mechanism succeed while those that do not fail.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new method but instead evaluates five existing mitigation methods—gDRO, resampling, DomainInd, CFair, and JTT—across 15+ subgroup definitions on four datasets (MNIST, CheXpert, CelebA, Civilcomments). The central finding is that subgroup definition dominates method choice: (A,Y) subgroups consistently outperform S subgroups across all five methods and all four datasets, despite S showing the largest observed disparity in ERM performance. gDRO and resampling with (A,Y) subgroups achieve the strongest gains (up to +0.07 AUC over ERM on CheXpert), while DomainInd with A subgroups improves CheXpert AUC by 0.07. JTT without subgroup labels for model selection fails to beat ERM on three of four datasets, confirming that even label-free methods are sensitive to subgroup choice during validation. S-based subgroups are detrimental in 10 of 16 reweighting experiments and up to 0.14 AUC worse for CFair on MNIST, demonstrating that mitigating the most visible disparity can paradoxically worsen both generalisation and fairness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by treating subgroup definition as a first-class optimization variable in bias mitigation, rather than a fixed input. Prior work (Zhou et al. 2021, Awasthi et al. 2020, Stromberg et al. 2024) explored isolated flaws in subgroup annotations (noise, imperfect groups) for a single mitigation method, while this paper systematically varies subgroup granularity, intersectionality, and noise across four state-of-the-art methods (gDRO, resampling, DomainInd, CFair) and four datasets. The theoretical analysis builds on the distributional divergence bounds from Ben-David et al. (2010) and the causal bias framework of Jones et al. (2025), but applies them specifically to the subgroup-weighted training distribution as a diagnostic for mitigation success.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that mitigation on the subgroups where disparity is observed can be worse than no mitigation at all is a practical warning for practitioners who routinely apply group-based strategies to underperforming groups. The KL divergence framework offers a concrete, computable criterion for evaluating whether a proposed subgrouping can recover the unbiased test distribution, potentially guiding subgroup selection in practice. However, the analysis is restricted to spurious-correlation bias in semi-synthetic settings, and in real deployments the cause of underperformance is often unknown, limiting direct applicability. Open questions include extending the framework to other bias types (under-representation, label shift), developing automated subgroup discovery that optimizes for distributional recovery, and validating findings in fully real-world medical and social settings.
