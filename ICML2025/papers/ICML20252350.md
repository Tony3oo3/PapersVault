## Problem

In collaborative/federated learning, participants contribute heterogeneous amounts of data quality and quantity, yet existing model-reward mechanisms (CGSV, IAFL) either lack convergence guarantees, require periodic full-model broadcasts that enable free-riding, or fail to ensure that low-contribution participants actually receive lower-quality models. The paper asks how to distribute differentiated final models whose performance is provably commensurate with each participant's contribution while maintaining standard optimization guarantees.

## Input / Output

The method takes each participant's local dataset (partitioned in homogeneous, Dirichlet-heterogeneous, quantity-skewed, or label-skewed fashion), a contribution score per participant (from any assessment method such as CGSV, ShapFed, or standalone accuracy), and a width range [p_min, p_max] (default 0.25 to 1.0). It produces a single global slimmable model with nested subnetworks at multiple discrete widths, from which each participant is assigned a subnetwork whose width is proportional to their contribution. The output is thus a set of differentiated models of varying capacity rather than a single shared model, and the allocation is computed via simulated annealing over the discrete width set.

## Method

Aequa repurposes slimmable neural networks as a fairness mechanism: a single global model embeds nested subnetworks at multiple widths (e.g., 0.25×, 0.5×, 1.0×), and participants are assigned subnetworks whose width scales with their contribution. During federated training, each client samples a width uniformly from [p_min, p_max] at every local iteration and updates both the full-width and sampled-width parameters (a variant of the sandwich rule), ensuring all subnetworks are trained. Post-training, a simulated annealing allocation algorithm assigns widths by minimizing a cost function that simultaneously maximizes average collaboration gain, minimizes utility variance, and enforces individual rationality (no client receives a model worse than their standalone performance). The approach extends to training-time rewards by sending each client only their allocated subnetwork, dynamically updating contributions via a momentum rule, and mapping normalized contributions to widths. Theoretical analysis proves that L-smoothness and convexity are preserved under width reduction, yielding standard Local SGD convergence bounds, and that the optimal allocation achieves perfect Pearson correlation between assigned accuracy and contribution under continuous model performance.

## Results

Aequa achieves the highest mean accuracy in 17 of 24 dataset-partition settings, matching or exceeding FedAvg across all splits (e.g., 90.84% on CIFAR-10 homogeneous vs. 89.99% for FedAvg; 67.83% on CIFAR-100 homogeneous vs. 65.92%). On the incentivization metric (Pearson correlation between final model accuracy and standalone contribution), Aequa ranks first in all 24/24 settings with correlations of 0.81–1.00, far exceeding baselines. On the large-scale FEMNIST benchmark (3,597 clients), Aequa improves accuracy by +1.6 pp over FedAvg-FT (73.10% vs. 71.48%) while achieving a Pearson correlation of 0.9888 vs. 0.2904, with only a ~31% increase in per-round wall-clock time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against FedAvg (with fine-tuning), Aequa matches or exceeds accuracy in 17/24 settings while FedAvg leads in 7/24 (typically by <1 pp); however, FedAvg-FT fails to align incentives, yielding Pearson correlations near zero or negative (e.g., −0.76 on SVHN quantity skew) versus Aequa's 0.94–1.00. Against CGSV, Aequa dominates on both metrics: CGSV's accuracy drops dramatically under heterogeneity (e.g., 61.29% on CIFAR-10 homogeneous, 48.77% under Dirichlet α=0.1) and its correlation is often negative or near zero, while Aequa maintains 90%+ accuracy and 0.96+ correlation. Against IAFL, Aequa wins on Pearson correlation in all 24/24 settings (e.g., 0.99 vs. −0.01 on CIFAR-10 homogeneous; 1.00 vs. 0.99 on SVHN quantity skew) and on accuracy in most settings, though IAFL is competitive under quantity skew (e.g., 98.40 vs. 98.62 on MNIST). On the large-scale FEMNIST experiment, Aequa outperforms FedAvg-FT by +1.6 pp accuracy and achieves a 3.4× higher Pearson correlation (0.9888 vs. 0.2904) with a tighter collaboration gain spread (2.57 vs. 5.07).

## Relation to Prior Work

Aequa repurposes slimmable networks (Yu et al., 2019; Yu & Huang, 2019b) — originally designed for efficient inference with graceful performance degradation — as a fairness mechanism in federated learning, a use the authors claim is novel. It builds on the FedAvg optimization loop (McMahan et al., 2017) and masked averaging (Mohtashami et al., 2022) for handling heterogeneous subnetwork widths, while departing from prior fair-FL methods (CGSV, IAFL) that differentiate model quality by controlling which gradients or updates a client receives. Instead of information-level differentiation, Aequa achieves quality differentiation architecturally: each client is assigned a subnetwork of a specific width, making the reward structurally inseparable from the model itself.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean, theoretically grounded mechanism for fair model rewards that avoids the performance-fairness trade-off plaguing prior approaches (CGSV, IAFL), matching or exceeding FedAvg accuracy while achieving near-perfect contribution-reward correlation. The TEE assumption for preventing free-riding is a practical limitation, though the authors note the framework can operate without TEEs by incorporating contribution assessment. Open questions include whether the ~30% per-round computational overhead remains acceptable at larger scale, how the approach behaves under adversarial contribution reporting, and whether the simulated-annealing allocation step can be replaced by a closed-form solution for real-time deployment.
