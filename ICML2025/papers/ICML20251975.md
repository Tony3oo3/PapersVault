## Problem

In hierarchical classification, decoding strategies (converting posterior probabilities to predictions) typically rely on generic heuristics like argmax or probability thresholding that are not aligned with the specific evaluation metric being optimized. This paper asks: given a posterior probability distribution over leaf nodes and a target hierarchical metric, what is the Bayes-optimal prediction, and can it be computed efficiently?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251975/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a posterior probability distribution over leaf nodes (from any trained model, assumed fixed) and a hierarchical tree structure, and produces the optimal prediction under a specified metric. The prediction space ranges from a single leaf node, to a single internal node, to a set of mutually exclusive nodes, depending on the candidate set. The approach is purely post-hoc: no retraining is required, and the decoding rule is optimized independently of how the probability estimates were produced.

## Method

The paper derives optimal decision rules by minimizing expected misclassification cost under a given hierarchical metric, organized by candidate set complexity. For the node candidate set, they introduce a 'hierarchically reasonable' condition and derive probability-based pruning thresholds (Lemma 4.3) that eliminate suboptimal nodes, yielding an O(d_max · |L| + |N|) algorithm versus O(|N| · |L|) brute force. For the most general case of predicting a set of nodes, they focus on hF_β scores and decompose the optimization into an outer maximization over the augmented set cardinality and an inner top-k node selection, achieving O(d_max² · |N|) complexity. The key insight is that hierarchical structure enables polynomial-time search by exploiting the relationship between a node's probability and its parent's, reducing the search space from exponential to near-linear in tree depth.

## Results

Across six hierarchical metrics (Tree Distance Loss, Wu-Palmer, Zhao Similarity, and hF_β for β∈{0.5,1,2}) evaluated on TieredImageNet-H and iNat19 with 15+ models, the proposed optimal decoding strategies achieve consistent relative gains of 1–5% over the best heuristic, reaching up to 10% for the Mistake Severity metric. For example, on hF_1 with ResNet-18 (pretrained, TieredImageNet-H), optimal decoding scores 93.16% versus 91.94% for the Darts Algorithm and 90.67% for argmax. The performance gap widens substantially under underdetermined conditions: when input images are progressively blurred, the relative degradation of heuristics compared to optimal decoding grows monotonically with blur level. Computationally, the proposed algorithms are ~60× faster than brute-force for node-candidate decoding and solve the subset-of-nodes problem (hF_β) in O(d_max²·|N|) time, where brute-force is intractable.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The optimal decoding strategy outperforms all five heuristic families across nearly all model–dataset–metric combinations. The Darts Algorithm (Deng et al., 2012) is the strongest single heuristic, often within 0.1–1.5% of optimal, but is consistently beaten (e.g., 91.94 vs. 93.16 on hF₁/ResNet-18). Valmadre's Majority Rule and Expected Information are competitive with Darts on precision-weighted metrics (β=0.5) but fall further behind on recall-weighted ones (β=2). Leaf-node heuristics—argmax, Top-Down (Wang et al., 2017), Karthik et al.'s LCA-optimal rule, and Jain et al.'s HiE-self—are more recall-oriented and thus relatively closer to optimal under β=2, yet still trail by 1–3%. Notably, on the strongest pretrained models (EfficientNet-V2-S, ConvNeXt-Tiny) where the posterior is highly peaked, the gap between optimal and the best heuristic shrinks to <0.2%, confirming that the advantage of optimal decoding is most pronounced in underdetermined (high-entropy) prediction regimes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the classical cost-sensitive decision-theory framework (Duda & Hart, 1973; Elkan, 2001) to hierarchical classification, where prior work has addressed only specific metrics in isolation: Ramaswamy et al. (2015) and Cao et al. (2024) for tree distance losses, Bi & Kwok (2012) for HMC-loss, and Karthik et al. (2021) for leaf-only decoding. The key departure is a unified treatment: a general algorithm for any 'hierarchically reasonable' metric (Theorem 4.4) and a dedicated polynomial-time algorithm for the hF_β family (Theorem 4.7), the latter borrowing the outer/inner maximization decomposition from Waegeman et al. (2014) on multi-label F-scores. The paper explicitly positions itself as bridging the gap between the decoding step and the evaluation metric, which prior hierarchical classification work has largely treated as independent choices.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway—that practitioners should match their decoding rule to their target hierarchical metric rather than defaulting to argmax or majority voting—is actionable and broadly applicable to any hierarchical classifier. The blurring experiment provides a clean empirical demonstration that the benefit of optimal decoding grows with task underdetermination, which is relevant for domains like medicine or fine-grained recognition where posterior uncertainty is high. However, the typical relative gains are modest (1–5%), the evaluation is limited to image datasets, and the paper does not address the arguably harder problem of calibrating the posterior distribution itself. Open questions include extending to non-tree hierarchies (DAGs), integrating cost-sensitive training, and developing post-hoc recalibration with guarantees.
