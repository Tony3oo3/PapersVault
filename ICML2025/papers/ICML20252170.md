## Problem

CLIP-based virtual screening methods like DrugCLIP construct negative pairs during training too simplistically (molecules binding to other pockets), creating a domain gap with real screening where non-binding molecules share similar physicochemical properties with actives. This makes the model struggle to distinguish hard negatives at inference. The paper addresses this by introducing test-time adaptation (TTA) to drug virtual screening for the first time, allowing the model to dynamically adjust to each unseen test instance without labeled target-domain data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252170/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a protein pocket and a set of candidate molecules, each represented at the atomic level with atom types and 3D coordinates, processed by a Uni-Mol-style transformer encoder. During inference, the model first performs a single adaptation step using self-supervised auxiliary tasks on the test instance to update only the encoder's layer normalization parameters and auxiliary branch heads, then computes cosine similarity scores between the adapted pocket and molecule features. The output is a ranked list of molecules by their binding probability to the target pocket, with the original encoder weights reloaded between test instances to prevent accumulation of offsets.

## Method

Drug-TTA builds on DrugCLIP's contrastive learning primary branch and adds an auxiliary branch with five self-supervised tasks (three fine-grained: masked atom type prediction, corrupted coordinate reconstruction, distance prediction; two coarse-grained: KL divergence minimization, SimCLR contrastive learning) that generate seven losses. A multi-scale Auxiliary Loss Balance Module (ALBM) concatenates features from the encoder's top, middle, and last layers, passes them through task-specific MLPs, and applies softmax to produce instance-adaptive weights for the seven auxiliary losses at test time. The key training mechanism is a meta-auxiliary learning loop: the encoder's layer norm parameters are first updated via the auxiliary loss, then the updated encoder is used to compute the primary contrastive loss, which updates all primary branch parameters—simulating the inference-time adaptation during training. At test time, this same two-step process (auxiliary update → primary scoring) is applied per instance, with only the layer norm and auxiliary head parameters modified, preventing the model from overfitting to auxiliary tasks at the expense of the primary screening objective.

## Results

Drug-TTA achieves state-of-the-art performance across all five virtual screening benchmarks under a zero-shot setting, with an average AUROC improvement of 9.86% over DrugCLIP. On DUD-E, it reaches 93.16% AUROC and 82.82% BEDROC (vs. 80.93% and 50.52% for DrugCLIP), and on the more challenging LIT-PCBA benchmark it attains 71.24% AUROC and 45.08% BEDROC (vs. 57.17% and 6.23%). The EF@0.5% metric shows particularly large gains, e.g., 57.50 on DUD-E (+19.43) and 74.39 on LIT-PCBA (+65.83), indicating substantially better early-ranking of active molecules.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Drug-TTA outperforms all 13 baselines on the majority of metrics across the five benchmarks. On DUD-E, it surpasses the strongest docking method Glide-SP (76.70% AUROC) by 16.46 points and the best prior learning-based method DrugCLIP (80.93%) by 12.23 points in AUROC, with an even larger 32.30-point gap in BEDROC. On LIT-PCBA, Drug-TTA (71.24% AUROC) beats all baselines except SPRINT (73.4% AUROC), but dominates SPRINT on BEDROC (45.08 vs. 12.3) and EF@0.5% (74.39 vs. 15.90), indicating far superior early enrichment. On AD, DEKOIS 2.0, and CASF-2016, Drug-TTA consistently improves over DrugCLIP by 5.6–11.4 points in AUROC and 17.8–34.7 points in BEDROC. Traditional docking methods (Glide-SP, Vina, Surflex) and supervised affinity predictors (NN-score, RFscore, Pafnucy, OnionNet, Planet, Gnina, DeepDTA, BigBind) are all substantially outperformed, confirming that the feature-alignment + TTA paradigm is markedly more effective for virtual screening than both physics-based docking and supervised affinity prediction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends DrugCLIP's CLIP-style contrastive learning paradigm for virtual screening by introducing test-time adaptation (TTA) as a mechanism to close the domain gap between training (where in-batch negatives are trivially separable) and inference (where decoys share physicochemical properties with actives). The encoder architecture follows Uni-Mol, and the meta-auxiliary learning framework is adapted from Point-TTA for point cloud registration. The key conceptual departure is from DrugCLIP's fixed training-time negative construction: rather than engineering harder negatives during training (which the authors argue is infeasible for zero-shot screening), Drug-TTA dynamically adjusts encoder parameters per test instance using five self-supervised auxiliary tasks balanced by a multi-scale feature-based ALBM module.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The work demonstrates that the training-inference domain gap in CLIP-style virtual screening is a first-order problem, not a minor artifact, and that per-instance adaptation at test time can substantially mitigate it without requiring labeled target-domain data. The ablation showing that naive TTA (without meta-learning) actually degrades performance below the DrugCLIP baseline is a useful cautionary result for practitioners attempting to port TTA to other molecular tasks. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes beyond the Uni-Mol encoder, whether the 2.1-day inference cost for 100M molecules is acceptable in iterative screening workflows, and whether similar TTA strategies could address the negative-pair problem in other structure-based prediction tasks (docking, affinity prediction).
