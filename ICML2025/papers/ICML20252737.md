## Problem

Recent proposals for faster equivariant tensor products (GTP, MTP) claim O(L³) runtimes versus O(L⁵) for the Clebsch-Gordan tensor product, but these operations are not mathematically equivalent and the expressivity lost in exchange for speed has not been systematically characterized. The paper asks: what do we actually give up when replacing the true tensor product with these faster alternatives, and do the theoretical speedups translate to practical gains?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252737/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The tensor product operations consume pairs of SO(3) irreducible representations (irreps) up to degree L and produce irreps up to degree 2L, with the constraint that all operations must be E(3)-equivariant. In the molecular force field experiments, the full network takes 3D atomic positions and types and predicts per-atom forces and total energies on 3BPA and revised MD17; in the 3D Tetris experiment, it takes randomly oriented 3D point clouds (cube centers as nodes) and outputs one of 8 class labels, where two pieces are chiral mirror images. The key structural constraint is that equivariant linear layers can only mix irreps of the same type, so the TPO is the sole mechanism for creating new irrep types from existing ones.

## Method

The paper formalizes the distinction between true tensor products and 'tensor product operations' (TPOs), defining expressivity as the dimension of constructible equivariant bilinearities and interactability via selection rules that specify which irrep triples can interact. It introduces a simpler GTP implementation (S2Grid) that replaces the original 2D Fourier basis with a direct spherical grid, achieving the same O(L³) asymptotics but 30% faster in practice, and identifies that S2FFT algorithms can further reduce the cost to O(L² log² L). The analysis shows that when normalized for expressivity, most TPOs share the same asymptotic cost as sparse CGTP, and that GTP's parity selection rule (ℓ₁+ℓ₂+ℓ₃ must be even) excludes all antisymmetric interactions, making it provably unable to distinguish chiral 3D structures. First systematic microbenchmarks on RTX A5500, A100, and CPU reveal that asymptotic complexity, FLOPs, GPU utilization, and wall-clock time are all distinct metrics that can disagree substantially, with CGTP having the fewest FLOPs but the slowest wall-clock time due to poor GPU utilization.

## Results

The paper's central finding is that asymptotic speedups in tensor product operations come at the cost of expressivity: CGTP (Sparse) achieves O(L⁵) runtime with O(L³) expressivity, while GTP and MTP achieve O(L³) runtime but only O(L) expressivity. The proposed GTP (Grid) implementation is ~30% faster in practice than the original GTP (Fourier) while maintaining identical O(L³) asymptotics. On the 3D Tetris chiral classification task, CGTP networks reach 100% accuracy at all L values (1–4), while GTP networks fail to distinguish mirror-image pieces at any L, confirming the theoretical loss of antisymmetric interactions. Microbenchmarks on RTX A5500 reveal that asymptotic complexity does not predict wall-clock time: CGTP (Sparse) has the fewest FLOPs but the slowest wall-clock time due to poor GPU utilization, while GTP achieves lower wall-clock time despite higher FLOPs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to the original GTP (Fourier) from Luo et al. (2024), the paper's GTP (Grid) implementation achieves identical O(L³) asymptotic complexity but is ~30% faster in practice on both microbenchmarks and MACE interatomic potential training (3BPA and revised MD17 datasets). The paper also identifies that GTP (S2FFT) using fast spherical harmonic transforms (Healy et al., 2003) provides the only true asymptotic speedup over sparse CGTP when normalized for expressivity, achieving O(L² log²L) vs O(L⁵). Compared to MTP (Sparse) from Unke & Maennel (2024), both GTP and MTP share O(L³) runtime and O(L) expressivity, but MTP preserves antisymmetric interactions (non-commutative matrix multiplication) while GTP does not (symmetric pointwise product on the sphere). In microbenchmarks on RTX A5500, GTP (Grid) achieves the lowest wall-clock time among all TPOs due to high GPU utilization, while CGTP (Sparse) has the lowest FLOPs but worst wall-clock time due to poor GPU utilization. After normalizing wall-clock time by expressivity, CGTP (Sparse) becomes the fastest, revealing that the apparent speedups of GTP and MTP come from removing degrees of freedom rather than genuine algorithmic improvement.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the recent wave of O(L³) equivariant tensor product operations—specifically the Gaunt Tensor Product (Luo et al., 2024) and the Matrix Tensor Product from the e3x framework (Unke & Maennel, 2024)—which were proposed as speedups over the O(L⁵) Clebsch-Gordan Tensor Product. It departs from this framing by arguing these operations are not tensor products in the mathematical sense but rather restricted bilinearities (TPOs) that sacrifice expressivity for speed, and introduces formal measures of expressivity and interactability to quantify exactly what is lost. The spherical grid implementation of GTP simplifies the original 2D Fourier approach, and the microbenchmarking effort extends prior hardware-agnostic FLOP analyses (Brehmer et al., 2024) with GPU utilization and wall-clock measurements on multiple hardware platforms.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first systematic framework for understanding the expressivity cost of faster equivariant tensor products, which is critical for practitioners choosing between CGTP, GTP, and MTP in equivariant architectures like MACE or NequIP. The chirality classification experiment offers a clean, reproducible demonstration that GTP fundamentally cannot represent antisymmetric interactions, though the paper acknowledges the impact of this limitation on real-world datasets remains unexplored. Open questions include whether the expressivity gap can be compensated by other network components, whether S2FFT-based GTP can close the asymptotic gap without further expressivity loss, and how these tradeoffs manifest at scale in production equivariant models.
