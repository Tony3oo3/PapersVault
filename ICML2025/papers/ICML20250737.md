## Problem

Existing continual multimodal instruction tuning (CMIT) methods rely on fixed architectures, which cannot adapt to the fact that different tasks require varying layer-wise parameter updates (task architecture conflict) and uneven modality reliance (modality imbalance). This paper is the first to study continual learning for MLLMs from an architectural perspective, evolving the model's structure under controlled parameter budgets rather than merely adjusting weights within a static topology.

## Input / Output

The method consumes multimodal instruction sequences (an RGB image plus a text prompt) and produces task-specific outputs: single-word/phrase answers for VQA, one-sentence captions for image captioning, and bounding-box coordinates for visual grounding. Before full training on a new task, only a 1% random subset is used to compute zero-cost gradient-norm proxies that drive both layer-wise expert placement and inter-modal budget allocation, so the full dataset is never needed for architectural decisions. At inference, a lightweight 2-layer MLP autoencoder per task routes each input to the top-2 most relevant LoRA experts, falling back to the frozen pretrained backbone if reconstruction error exceeds a task-specific threshold.

## Method

D-MoLE dynamically evolves the MLLM architecture by allocating LoRA experts only to the most task-critical transformer layers, determined by gradient-norm zero-cost proxies computed on a 1% data subset. A gradient-based inter-modal curriculum then splits the total parameter budget between the LLM and vision encoder proportionally to each module's task-specific difficulty score, ensuring the more sensitive modality receives more new experts. For expert routing, task-specific 2-layer MLP autoencoders are trained on pooled image+text embeddings; during training the prior task with the lowest reconstruction error is co-activated for knowledge transfer, and during evaluation the top-2 tasks by reconstruction loss are selected while unknown inputs fall back to the pretrained backbone. Only newly allocated LoRA experts are trained per task while all prior experts and pretrained weights remain frozen, keeping the total trainable parameter count constant across the continual sequence.

## Results

On the 9-task CMIT benchmark, D-MoLE achieves an average score of 73.87 (AVG), 82.18 (Last), and -1.49 (BWT), outperforming the best baseline O-LoRA (58.79 / 62.04 / -21.31) by 15.08%, 20.14%, and 19.82% respectively. The BWT metric is particularly striking: D-MoLE retains nearly all previously learned knowledge (only -1.49% average degradation), while all baselines suffer 20–36% forgetting. On general MLLM benchmarks (MME, MMMU, POPE), D-MoLE also outperforms Seq-FT and O-LoRA, with MME-Sum of 1754.6 vs. 1646 (O-LoRA) and 1513 (Seq-FT), though it remains below the zero-shot pretrained model (1876.8).

## Baselines & Comparisons

D-MoLE outperforms all six baselines on every metric (AVG, Last, BWT) across all nine tasks. Against the strongest baseline O-LoRA (orthogonal LoRA constraints), D-MoLE gains +15.08 points on AVG (73.87 vs. 58.79), +20.14 on Last (82.18 vs. 62.04), and dramatically reduces forgetting from -21.31 to -1.49 on BWT. Among MoLE-based methods, MoLA (which allocates more experts to higher layers) is the second-best at 56.36 AVG, still 17.5 points behind D-MoLE; Dense MoLE and Sparse MoLE trail further at 57.11 and 54.60 respectively. Traditional regularization baselines (LwF-LoRA at 54.00, EWC-LoRA at 58.57) struggle most, with EWC-LoRA showing the best BWT among non-MoLE methods (-24.55) but still far from D-MoLE's near-zero forgetting. On general MLLM benchmarks (Table 3), D-MoLE also leads among continual-learning methods (MME-Sum 1754.6 vs. O-LoRA 1646, Seq-FT 1513), though all methods remain below the zero-shot pretrained model (1876.8). D-MoLE is additionally the fastest method at 12.40 hours total training time, slightly ahead of Joint-learning (12.83h) and Seq-FT (13.15h), while MoLA and Sparse MoLE require over 23 hours.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Mixture of LoRA Experts (MoLE) framework by making expert allocation dynamic and task-dependent rather than fixed. Prior CMIT methods (O-LoRA, Dense/Sparse MoLE, MoLA) all operate on a static architecture where LoRA modules are placed uniformly or by a fixed heuristic across all layers; D-MoLE instead uses zero-cost gradient-norm proxies to evolve the architecture per task, allocating LoRA experts only to the most sensitive layers. The autoencoder-based router for task identification and expert selection departs from the learned gating in MoLE by using reconstruction error as a lightweight, training-free routing signal, and the gradient-based inter-modal curriculum introduces a new mechanism for balancing LLM and vision encoder updates that has no direct precedent in prior CMIT work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The near-zero backward transfer (BWT of −1.49 vs. −21.31 for the best baseline) suggests that architectural adaptation—deciding *where* to place new parameters—may be more effective than regularization-based anti-forgetting for MLLMs, a finding that could redirect the field's focus. However, the evaluation is limited to a single 2B-parameter model (InternVL2-2B) and nine tasks, leaving open whether the approach scales to larger MLLMs, longer task sequences, or more blurred task boundaries. The autoencoder router also introduces ~10% inference overhead, and the method requires a predefined parameter budget per task, which may not always be available in practice. Future work should explore whether the zero-cost proxy signals remain reliable as the number of accumulated experts grows and whether the approach transfers to video or audio modalities.
