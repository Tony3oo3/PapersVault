## Problem

Existing data-free knowledge distillation (DFKD) methods assume pre-trained teachers are trustworthy, yet when the teacher is a non-transferable learning (NTL) model, the adversarial exploration process is systematically misled: the synthetic distribution shifts from the ID domain toward the OOD domain, causing the student to inherit misleading OOD knowledge while suffering degraded ID knowledge transfer. This paper identifies this phenomenon as the OOD trap effect and provides the first systematic investigation of DFKD against NTL teachers.

## Input / Output

The method operates on a frozen white-box NTL teacher, a generator G that synthesizes 64×64 RGB images from random noise, and a student network (VGG-11 or ResNet-18). The key unusual input processing step is that ATEsc applies untargeted PGD (with a configurable perturbation bound ε) to every synthetic sample in each batch to probe the teacher's adversarial robustness on that sample, using the binary outcome (prediction flipped or not) as the grouping criterion. The output is a trained student model that retains ID-domain classification accuracy while suppressing OOD-domain predictions (or backdoor attack success rate in the backdoor setting).

## Method

ATEsc exploits the fact that NTL teachers are adversarially fragile on ID samples (whose decision boundaries lie close to the data) but robust on OOD samples (which are pushed far from ID in both feature and output space). After each generator update, ATEsc runs untargeted PGD on every synthetic sample: samples whose teacher prediction flips are assigned to a fragile (ID-like) group G_f, and those whose prediction persists are assigned to a robust (OOD-like) group G_r. The student is then trained with a two-part loss: a calibrated knowledge distillation term that minimizes KL divergence between student and teacher only on G_f, and a forgetting term that maximizes KL divergence on G_r (capped at 1 for stability), so the student actively unlearns the teacher's OOD behavior. The full loss is L_ATEsc = L_ckd − min(1, λ·L_forget), and the entire module is plug-and-play, inserted between the generator and distillation stages of any existing DFKD baseline (DFQ, CMI, NAYER).

## Results

On the NTL-based backdoor task (Table 3), ATEsc dramatically reduces attack success rate (ASR) from ~100% (teacher) to 2.2–65.5% depending on trigger type, while all DFKD baselines (DFQ, CMI, NAYER) and the +CKD variant maintain ASR near 100%. On close-set NTL (Table 1), ATEsc improves OOD accuracy (OAcc) substantially, e.g., from 10.4% (NTL teacher) to 51.6% for CIFAR10→STL10 with ResNet-34→ResNet-18 under DFQ, while preserving competitive ID accuracy (IAcc). On open-set NTL (Table 2), ATEsc reduces OOD-class label accuracy (OLAcc) from 100% to 0–2.5%, effectively blocking misleading OOD knowledge transfer.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DFQ, CMI, and NAYER, ATEsc achieves the most dramatic gains on the backdoor ASR metric: for BadNet(grid), CMI+ATEsc reduces ASR from 100% to 2.2%, and DFQ+ATEsc from 100% to 11.4%, while the +CKD-only variant (without the forgetting term) still leaves ASR at 96.5–100%. On IAcc, the +CKD component alone already recovers most ID accuracy (e.g., CMI+CKD reaches 76.3% vs. CMI's 10.0% on BadNet sq), and ATEsc maintains competitive IAcc (68.5%) at the cost of a few points. For the Blended trigger, NAYER+ATEsc achieves the lowest ASR (10.2%) among all methods, while CMI+ATEsc achieves 19.0%. The paper also compares against ABD (Hong et al., 2023), a prior backdoor-defense method for DFKD, and reports in Appendix E.5 that ABD struggles to mitigate backdoor transfer when the teacher is NTL-based, whereas ATEsc is specifically designed for this setting. Overall, ATEsc consistently wins on the security-critical ASR/OLAcc metric across all three DFKD baselines and all four trigger types, at a modest IAcc cost of 5–15 points relative to +CKD.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two established lines of work: data-free knowledge distillation (DFKD) via adversarial exploration (ZSKT, DFQ, CMI, NAYER) and non-transferable learning (NTL) for model IP protection. It extends the recent work by Hong et al. (2023) on DFKD with untrusted (backdoored) teachers to the broader and more structured setting of NTL teachers, where the teacher is deliberately trained to be non-transferable to an OOD domain. The proposed ATEsc method repurposes the PGD adversarial attack not as a training objective but as a diagnostic tool to partition synthetic samples by their adversarial robustness, drawing on the margin-based vulnerability theory from adversarial robustness literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of the OOD trap effect reveals a previously unrecognized failure mode in DFKD that has dual implications: it provides a defense mechanism against data-free model extraction (benign) but also enables backdoor transfer from NTL teachers to students (malign), raising security concerns for NTL-based IP protection. The paper's own impact statement acknowledges that ATEsc could undermine NTL's intended transferability restrictions, creating an open tension between knowledge transfer utility and model protection. Limitations include the requirement for white-box teacher access, the computational overhead of per-sample PGD attacks, and the fact that ATEsc does not fully close the gap to teacher-level ID accuracy, particularly in open-set and backdoor settings.
