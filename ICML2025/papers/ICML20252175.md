## Problem

Accurate and verifiable measurement of atomic abundances is essential for climate-related applications such as soil carbon sequestration verification and emissions monitoring, but existing NMR-based techniques require expensive laboratory-grade hardware (2–45 T magnets) and destructive sample preparation, precluding in-situ deployment. Prior model-based approaches like GRAPE require a complete physical Hamiltonian of the sample and become computationally intractable for complex spin systems, and no prior work has applied modern reinforcement learning to NMR pulse sequencing for abundance measurement.

## Input / Output

The agent observes a low-dimensional state vector comprising the transverse magnetization components (Mx, My), the currently applied B1 pulse, the constant B0 background field, instrument parameters (runtime T and a stochastically evolving temperature τ that modulates Johnson-Nyquist noise), and the running maximum observed magnetization. The action is a continuous B1 value in [−0.2, 0.2] Tesla (for the chirp/spoil agents) or a binary toggle decision (for the third agent), with the toggle action gated to be available only every 200 timesteps. The ultimate output is a time-series of B1 pulses whose peak transverse magnetization is proportional to the target isotope's abundance, enabling concentration estimation without a full spectral fit.

## Method

The paper trains three inter-operating PPO policies on a custom vectorized NMR simulator built on the macroscopic Bloch equations with Johnson-Nyquist instrument noise: (1) a chirping policy that maximizes Mx²+My² to drive all spins coherently into the transverse plane, (2) a spoiling policy that actively disperses spins to rapidly suppress residual transverse magnetization (achieving up to 28% lower average magnetization versus passive relaxation), and (3) a toggle policy that decides when to hand control between the first two, with a reward that credits magnetization gains weighted by spoiling time and penalizes long chirp-spoil cycles. The best-performing chirping model is initialized via behavior cloning on the standard 1D NMR π/2 pulse sequence before PPO fine-tuning, converging within 300K timesteps and achieving a steeper, more monotone magnetization-vs-concentration relationship at ~16% of the acquisition time of the baseline. Crucially, the agent only observes experimentally accessible quantities (not individual spin states or the Hamiltonian), so the approach does not require a pre-determined physical model of the sample and can in principle be trained directly on real hardware.

## Results

The learned chirping policy achieves maximum magnetization in approximately 16% of the time required by the standard 1D NMR pulse sequence (Single π/2, 8000 scans, 2 s per scan), while producing a steeper and more monotone relationship between caffeine concentration and peak magnetization. The accelerated spoiling policy reduces average transverse magnetization by up to 28% compared to passive relaxation (no pulse) within a fraction of the T₂ timescale. The toggle model, which alternates between chirping and spoiling, outperforms the chirp-only model by enabling repeated partial resets that sustain higher cumulative magnetization across multiple scan cycles.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the standard 1D NMR pulse sequence (Single π/2, 8000 scans, 2 s acquisition per scan) as described in Nishimura (1996). The learned chirping policy wins decisively on measurement speed, reaching peak magnetization in roughly 16% of the 1D NMR acquisition time, and produces a more linear, monotone concentration–magnetization relationship (Figure 1). For the spoiling stage, the learned policy outperforms the standard no-pulse relaxation by up to 28% in average transverse magnetization reduction (Figure 2), operating within a fraction of T₂ rather than multiple T₂ periods. In the toggle experiment (Figure 4), the full three-agent system (chirp + spoil + toggle) outperforms both the chirp-only model and a modified chirp with artificial magnetization resets, confirming that the spoiling agent's active spin dispersal—not merely a bookkeeping reset—is what enables sustained high magnetization across repeated scans. The behavior-cloning-initialized chirping model (pre-trained on the 1D NMR pulse) was the strongest of the three chirping variants, suggesting the standard pulse is a useful prior but not a local optimum.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the GRAPE (gradient ascent pulse engineering) lineage of NMR pulse optimization, which requires a complete Hamiltonian model and optimizes toward a general target state, by instead training RL policies on directly observable magnetization signals without needing an accurate physical model of the sample. It also distinguishes itself from prior RL-for-NMR work (Sridharan et al., 2022; Korenika et al., 2024), which applied RL to downstream classification or structure prediction without tuning the underlying pulse sequence. The multi-agent architecture (chirp, spoil, toggle) is a novel decomposition not present in prior NMR control literature, drawing conceptually from the spoiling literature (Jehenson & Bloch, 1991) and magnetic resonance fingerprinting (Ma et al., 2013) but recombining them under a PPO training framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

If validated on real hardware, this approach could enable low-cost, portable, in-situ atomic abundance measurement with direct applications to carbon crediting verification, soil health monitoring, and food science—domains where current NMR requires expensive laboratory infrastructure. However, the work is entirely simulation-based on a single compound (caffeine in water), assumes the test sample shares the same relative nuclear distribution as training data, and does not address inter-nuclear coupling effects that would arise in real samples. The clearest open question is whether the learned policies transfer to real low-field NMR hardware and to out-of-distribution analytes, which the authors identify as the most critical next step.
