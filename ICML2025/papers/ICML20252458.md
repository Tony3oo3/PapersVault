## Problem

For large-scale industrial decision-making (e.g., power grid control with 60K+ discrete actions), RL suffers from sample inefficiency and suboptimal convergence, while LLMs lack the real-time long-sequence decision-making capability required for fine-grained control. Existing LLM-RL integration methods either require continuous LLM interaction during inference (violating sub-1000ms real-time constraints) or enforce static policy-level alignment via KL divergence, which leads to overconservative decisions unsuitable for long-horizon industrial control.

## Input / Output

The RL agent consumes a 6-feature numerical state vector (active/reactive power, voltage, line capacity ratio, topology, line status) embedded to 128 dimensions with a 6-step history window, and outputs a discrete topology action (bus assignment or line switch) from a space exceeding 60K–70K actions. The LLM component receives the same state-action information converted into structured natural-language descriptions (including overloaded line details, substation topology, and bad-action examples) and produces either a refined topology action or discrete reward adjustments from the set {−2K, −K, +K, +2K}. The LLM buffer is capped at 256 entries, and the LLM response budget is limited to 512–1024 tokens per query.

## Method

ACE separates LLM reasoning (offline 'think twice') from RL execution (online 'act once') into distinct phases. During training, the LLM serves a dual role: as a Policy Actor, it identifies suboptimal transitions (reward below threshold) from the SAC replay buffer, reasons about better alternatives via multi-round prompting with bad-case examples, and validates refined actions through Grid2Op simulation; as a Value Critic, it performs trajectory-level counterfactual reasoning to reassign credit at key decision points, extending beyond TD(λ)'s exponential decay with non-parametric causal attribution. A mixed experience buffer combines RL and LLM-refined transitions using reward-based importance weighting (Eq. 9), which simultaneously drives weighted SAC policy updates and LoRA fine-tuning of the LLM, enabling co-evolution. At deployment, only the trained RL policy runs, preserving real-time inference latency comparable to pure RL baselines.

## Results

Across three L2RPN power grid competitions with action spaces exceeding 60K discrete actions, ACE (Qwen2-7B+SFT) achieves the highest episode rewards: 69.8 on WCCI 2020, 145.3 on NeurIPS 2020, and 75.9 on WCCI 2022, outperforming the strongest RL baseline by 22.2%, 13.1%, and 145% respectively. ACE also achieves the highest survival rates (92.9%, 84.8%, 54.3%) while requiring only 40K–50K RL samples plus ~300–700 LLM refinements, compared to 100K–200K samples for baselines. Test-time latency remains competitive with pure RL (38.7–3627s depending on environment), whereas pure LLM approaches take 1,480–22,436s.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ACE outperforms all four baseline categories across all three competitions. Against Expert-guided RL (the WCCI 2020 winning solution by Yoon et al.), ACE (Qwen2-7B+SFT) improves episode rewards by 22.2% (69.8 vs 57.1) on WCCI 2020, 13.1% (145.3 vs 128.5) on NeurIPS 2020, and a dramatic 145% (75.9 vs 31.0) on the larger WCCI 2022 IEEE-118 grid, while also boosting survival rates by 14.6, 11.9, and 20.6 percentage points respectively. Against LLM4Teach (Zhou et al.), which uses KL-divergence policy regularization, ACE achieves higher rewards on all three benchmarks (69.8 vs 64.4, 145.3 vs 131.5, 75.9 vs 23.6) with substantially fewer samples (40K+287 vs 100K), demonstrating that offline trajectory refinement is more effective than static policy alignment for long-horizon industrial control. Pure LLM baselines (Qwen2-7B and GPT-4) are far outperformed in both reward (e.g., 69.8 vs 29.5 for GPT-4 on WCCI 2020) and test-time latency (54.8s vs 3415.3s), confirming that direct LLM action generation is impractical for real-time sequential decision-making in large-scale power grids.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the LLM-guided RL line of work (LLM4Teach, ELLM, LINVIT, RL-GPT) but departs from the dominant paradigm of policy-level alignment (KL-divergence regularization) or continuous LLM interaction during training. Instead, ACE restricts LLMs to an offline 'think twice' phase where they perform targeted trajectory refinement in a dual role—both as a Policy Actor correcting suboptimal actions and as a Value Critic performing trajectory-level credit assignment—while the RL agent handles all online 'act once' execution. The co-evolution loop, where RL-generated experiences are used to fine-tune the LLM via LoRA and LLM-refined trajectories are injected back into RL's replay buffer with reward-based prioritization, extends the experience replay and prioritized sampling literature with a cross-agent data generation mechanism not present in prior LLM-RL integration work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ACE addresses a practical gap: LLMs have strong single-step reasoning but fail at the long-horizon, sub-1000ms sequential control required by industrial systems, while RL suffers from sample inefficiency in 60K+ action spaces. The 'think twice, act once' separation is a clean architectural principle that could generalize to other real-time control domains (robotics, traffic, process manufacturing). However, the evaluation is confined to three power-grid benchmarks from a single platform (Grid2Op), the LLM buffer is capped at 256 samples, and the GPT-4 variant (without SFT) underperforms the fine-tuned Qwen2 variant, raising open questions about whether the co-evolution loop scales to larger models or more diverse industrial tasks. The modest absolute survival rates (54% on WCCI 2022) also suggest the approach has not yet solved the core robustness challenge in large-scale grid operation.
