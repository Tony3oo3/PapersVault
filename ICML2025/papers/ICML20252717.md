## Problem

Existing functional bivariate causal discovery methods are restricted to additive noise models (ANMs) or location-scale noise models (LSNMs), which can be misspecified in many practical settings, and likelihood-based approaches require specifying the noise distribution—a component unrelated to causality that can lead to incorrect inference. No functional causal discovery framework existed that extended beyond these model classes while remaining agnostic to the noise distribution.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252717/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes bivariate observational data (X, Y pairs) and requires that the joint distribution has full support with differentiable log-densities, a constraint that excludes discrete or mixed-type variables. It produces a causal direction decision (X→Y or Y→X) by comparing goodness-of-fit losses in both directions, along with the estimated causal velocity function and its associated GoF score. The two-step pipeline first estimates joint and marginal score functions nonparametrically (via Stein or KDE estimators), then fits velocity models in each direction.

## Method

The paper parametrizes bivariate bijective SCMs as dynamical systems where the cause variable X plays the role of time, defining a causal velocity v(y,x) = d/dx′ f_{x′}(f_x^{-1}(y))|_{x′=x} that generates all counterfactual curves via an initial value problem. The key identity (Theorem 4.1) links this velocity to observable score functions through the continuity equation: s_x(x,y) = −∂_y v(y,x) − v(y,x)·s_y(x,y) + s_x(x), yielding a simulation-free, likelihood-free objective that directly regresses the velocity against the score without evaluating the mechanism or specifying noise distributions. The estimation procedure minimizes the squared residual of this identity empirically, with the velocity parametrized by basis functions (linear/quadratic) or neural networks, and the ∂_y v term obtained via automatic differentiation. Causal direction is determined by comparing the empirical GoF losses in both X→Y and Y→X directions, selecting the smaller value. The paper establishes statistical consistency (Theorem 5.1) showing the empirical loss converges at a rate controlled by the score estimator convergence rate, and derives a general non-identifiability PDE (Proposition 5.2) that specializes to known ANM and LSNM results.

## Results

On the novel Velocity benchmark (where existing functional methods are misspecified), the best velocity models achieve 91–97% accuracy (B-LIN 91%, B-QUAD 97%, V-NN 96%), dramatically outperforming all baselines which range from 33% (RECI) to 66% (IGCI). On the Sigmoid benchmark, B-LIN reaches 87% accuracy, surpassing LOCI(HSIC) at 70% and CDS at 12%. On standard benchmarks (Table 3), B-QUAD with KDE achieves 94% on SIM-G (the best overall result), while LOCI(HSIC) remains competitive on SIM (79%) and SIM-C (83%). The method's performance is highly sensitive to score estimation quality: with ground-truth scores, causal direction is identified with certainty even at n=100 samples.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against five baselines: LOCI (HSIC and Lik variants), CDS, IGCI, RECI, and CGCI. On the two novel synthetic benchmarks (Velocity and Sigmoid) designed to be misspecified for existing methods, the velocity models decisively win: B-QUAD achieves 97% on Velocity vs. the best baseline IGCI at 66%, and B-LIN reaches 87% on Sigmoid vs. LOCI(HSIC) at 70%. On the ANM benchmark where LOCI is well-specified, LOCI(HSIC) (97%) and CDS (92%) match or slightly exceed V-ANM (92%), confirming that the velocity parametrization does not sacrifice performance on its home model class. On the LSNM benchmark, LOCI(HSIC) (83%) narrowly edges out V-LSNM (82%), while both substantially outperform CDS (59%), IGCI (37%), and RECI (38%). On standard benchmarks (Table 3), the velocity models are competitive but do not uniformly dominate: LOCI(HSIC) leads on SIM (79%) and SIM-C (83%), while B-QUAD with KDE achieves the best overall result on SIM-G (94% vs. LOCI's 81%), and RECI leads on Tübingen (72%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper specializes the cocycle-based causal framework of Dance & Bloem-Reddy (2024) to the bivariate real-valued case, where the cause variable plays the role of time in a dynamical system, yielding a concrete ODE/velocity parametrization of SCMs. It draws on the nonparametric score estimation theory of Zhou et al. (2020) and the Stein gradient estimator of Li & Turner (2018) to build a simulation-free, likelihood-free learning objective. Conceptually, it unifies three previously separate threads—functional causal discovery (ANM/LSNM via RESIT), score-based causal discovery (Rolland et al. 2022, Montagna et al. 2023), and flow matching / continuous normalizing flows (Lipman et al. 2023)—under the continuity equation, showing that the causal velocity is directly recoverable from observable score functions without specifying the noise distribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The velocity parametrization extends functional causal discovery beyond the ANM and LSNM classes that dominate the literature, offering a principled way to specify more flexible mechanisms (e.g., tail-insensitive velocities) while retaining identifiability. However, the method's practical utility is tightly coupled to score estimation quality: the O(n³) Stein estimator limits scalability, and the authors demonstrate that poor score estimates can flip the causal direction, a vulnerability that remains unresolved. Open questions include a general identifiability characterization for the velocity model class (the LSNM case already yields a complex PDE), extension to multivariate settings, and whether faster score estimators (e.g., neural score networks) can close the gap between theoretical guarantees and finite-sample performance.
