## Problem

Prior work established that Transformer models fix their top-1 prediction at an early layer (the 'saturation event'), but left open what computation the remaining layers perform. This paper asks whether similar saturation events exist for lower-ranked tokens and, if so, whether they occur in a structured order. The answer reveals a sequential task-transition mechanism intrinsic to the Transformer architecture, present even in untrained models.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251515/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis takes text sequences (MMLU questions, CNN/DM passages), RGB images (CIFAR-10), or audio clips (LibriSpeech) as input to pretrained or randomly initialized Transformers. At each layer, hidden states are projected onto the vocabulary or class space via the unembedding matrix (logit lens), yielding a per-layer ranking over all tokens; from these rankings, the paper extracts the saturation layer index for each of the top-5 tokens. The probing classifier consumes raw hidden-layer embeddings and outputs a discrete task index (1–5), while the early-exit strategy outputs a per-token exit layer decision based on when the classifier first predicts task 2.

## Method

The paper extends the top-1 saturation definition to arbitrary k-th ranking tokens and uses the logit lens to track per-layer token rankings across all layers of decoder-only, encoder-only, and encoder-decoder Transformers. It demonstrates that saturation layers are monotonically ordered (1st token saturates before 2nd, etc.) using a stricter Kendall's τ that penalizes ties, validated across text, vision, and speech modalities and even in randomly initialized Llama3-8B. To explain the mechanism, the authors propose a discrete task-transition model where each task i corresponds to determining the i-th token, with the transition encoded in hidden-layer embeddings; they validate this by training a logistic regression probe that predicts the current task index from layer activations with high accuracy (e.g., 88.1% for Llama3-8B vs. 33.3% chance). A causal intervention—injecting activations from one sample's saturation layer into another sample's subsequent layer—forces the model to switch to the next task at the injected layer, producing a step-function change in saturation timing. Finally, the task-transition classifier is repurposed as a token-level early-exit criterion (exit when task 2 is first predicted), outperforming softmax-response and hidden-state-saturation baselines in the accuracy–speedup trade-off.

## Results

The paper demonstrates that top-k tokens in Transformers reach saturation in order of their ranking across text, vision, and speech models, with Kendall's τ (strict) values of 0.149–0.312 (all p < 0.001) confirming the ordering. A logistic regression classifier predicts the current task index from hidden-layer embeddings with 88.1% accuracy on Llama3-8B (vs. 33.3% chance), 63.8% on ViT-L/16, and 52.7% on Whisper-large. For the practical early-exit application, the proposed token-level strategy achieves 43.3% next-word prediction accuracy with a 1.11× speedup ratio on Llama3-8B, outperforming both softmax-response (40.6%, 1.07×) and hidden-state saturation (24.3%, 1.001×) baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its token-level early-exit strategy against two confidence measures from Schuster et al. (2022, 'Confident adaptive language modeling'): softmax response (difference between top-2 logit values) and hidden-state saturation (cosine similarity between consecutive layer embeddings). The proposed method, which uses a logistic regression classifier to detect the task-1-to-task-2 transition, achieves 43.3% next-word prediction accuracy with a 1.11× speedup, beating softmax response (40.6%, 1.07×) by 2.7 points in accuracy and 0.04× in speedup, and substantially outperforming hidden-state saturation (24.3%, 1.001×) on both metrics. The speedup advantage over softmax response is statistically significant (p < 0.001, independent samples t-test). Notably, the proposed method requires no training beyond a simple logistic regression on a small dataset, whereas the baselines require tuning confidence thresholds.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the top-1 saturation event framework of Geva et al. (2022) to arbitrary top-k tokens, using the logit lens (Nostalgebraist, 2020) as its core analytical tool to project intermediate hidden states onto the vocabulary space. It departs from the iterative inference hypothesis (Simoulin & Crabbe, 2021), which treats each layer as a gradual refinement step, by proposing instead a discrete task-transition mechanism where each layer range corresponds to determining a specific ranked token. The causal intervention methodology draws on Stolfo et al. (2023), and the early-exit comparison builds on Schuster et al. (2022) confidence-based adaptive decoding.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that ordered saturation emerges even in randomly initialized Transformers suggests a fundamental architectural bias in the Transformer stack, with implications for how we understand depth, layer specialization, and the computational role of individual layers. The task-transition framing challenges the prevailing view of fixed layer roles (early = syntactic, late = semantic) and opens questions about which specific architectural components (attention vs. FFN, residual connections) drive the phenomenon. Practical applications in early-exit inference and saturation-aware decoding are demonstrated but remain preliminary; the precise mechanism by which tokens are 'locked in' after saturation, and whether the effect extends to non-Transformer architectures like RNNs, remain open.
