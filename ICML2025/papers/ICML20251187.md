## Problem

Prior theoretical analyses of LoRA training dynamics either rely on linearization arguments (e.g., NTK regime) or consider highly simplified setups such as rank-1 or two-layer networks, leaving the loss landscape of practical LoRA fine-tuning largely uncharacterized. This paper asks: in the generic regime where linearization does not hold, can LoRA training converge to spurious local minima, and if so, what do they look like?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251187/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis takes a pretrained model (RoBERTa-base for SST-2 sentiment analysis, ViT for CIFAR-100 image classification), a downstream task dataset, and a LoRA rank r that must satisfy r ≥ r* (the rank of the full fine-tuning global minimizer). The primary output is a theoretical guarantee characterizing every second-order stationary point of the LoRA loss, validated empirically by measuring the rank and Frobenius norm of converged solutions. The restricted strong convexity and smoothness constants α and β are estimated via Monte-Carlo sampling within a rank and distance bound, and the theory requires α > 0 and β < ∞, which empirically holds for small LoRA ranks (r ≤ 32) but breaks at r = 64.

## Method

The paper analyzes the LoRA loss landscape under restricted strong convexity (RSC) and restricted smoothness (RSM) conditions, avoiding all linearization. It defines two regimes: a special regime (2α > β) where every SOSP is a global minimum, and a generic regime (2α ≤ β) where spurious local minima may exist but are provably high-rank (exactly rank r with σ_r(X) ≥ (2α/β)σ_{r*}(X)) and large-magnitude, far from the global minimizer. The proof leverages the Burer-Monteiro factorization structure, subgradient properties of the nuclear norm (equivalent to weight decay in LoRA), and the Eckart-Young-Mirsky theorem to bound the distance between any SOSP and the global minimizer. The paper further argues that zero-initialization (B=0) and weight decay induce implicit biases toward the low-rank, small-magnitude region where global minima reside, explaining why practical LoRA training typically succeeds. Experiments confirm that non-zero initialization can trap training in spurious local minima that 'fail loudly' with poor generalization.

## Results

The paper's main contribution is theoretical: in the generic regime (2α ≤ β), every second-order stationary point of LoRA training is either a global minimizer with low rank and small magnitude, or a spurious local minimum with full rank r and large magnitude (σ_r(X) > (2α/β)σ_{r*}(X)). Experimentally, Table 1 validates the low-rank assumption: on SST-2, the global minimizer rank drops from 749 (λ=0) to 1 (λ=0.1), and on CIFAR-100 from 752 to 1. Table 2 confirms restricted strong convexity (α > 0) and smoothness (β < ∞) hold for LoRA ranks 8–32 (e.g., α=0.0061, β=0.0492 at rank 8), but α becomes negative at rank 64, indicating the theory's boundary. Figure 2 demonstrates the 'failing loudly' phenomenon: with large random initialization, LoRA converges to a spurious minimum with high rank, large norm, and poor test accuracy, while zero-initialization reliably finds the global minimum.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical analysis paper and does not benchmark LoRA against other PEFT methods (e.g., LoRA+, rsLoRA, PiSSA, MiLoRA) or full fine-tuning in a head-to-head comparison. Instead, its 'comparison' is against prior theoretical results: Theorem 1 subsumes the NTK-regime analysis of Jang et al. (2024) as a special case (the special regime 2α > β), extending the guarantee to the generic regime where linearization arguments fail. The paper also builds on the Burer-Monteiro low-rank optimization framework (Ge et al. 2017, Ha et al. 2020, Park et al. 2017) but introduces novel techniques for the generic regime that were not available in the matrix sensing literature. The experimental section validates assumptions rather than comparing methods, showing that the restricted convexity/smoothness conditions hold for practical LoRA ranks (8–32) and that the low-rank global minimizer assumption is empirically supported by nuclear-norm-regularized full fine-tuning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Burer-Monteiro low-rank factorization analysis (Ge et al. 2017, Park et al. 2017, Ha et al. 2020) to the LoRA fine-tuning setting, replacing the full strong convexity and smoothness assumptions of matrix sensing with weaker restricted versions that are empirically verifiable in fine-tuning. It departs from prior LoRA-specific theory (Malladi et al. 2023, Jang et al. 2024, Dayi & Chen 2024) by removing all linearization arguments and layer/rank restrictions, operating in a 'generic regime' where the NTK approximation does not hold. The implicit bias argument draws on Wang & Jacot (2024) and Galanti et al. (2024) to explain why zero-initialization and weight decay steer training toward the low-rank global minimum rather than spurious solutions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 'fails loudly' characterization—spurious local minima, if reached, are qualitatively distinguishable by high rank and large magnitude—gives practitioners a concrete diagnostic for monitoring LoRA training, and the finding that smaller LoRA rank yields more favorable RSC/RSM constants suggests a previously underappreciated optimization benefit of low-rank parameterization beyond memory savings. Key limitations include the reliance on restricted convexity/smoothness assumptions that are hard to verify at scale, and experiments limited to two small-scale tasks (RoBERTa-base on SST-2, ViT on CIFAR-100). Open questions include a rigorous (rather than heuristic) analysis of the weight-decay implicit bias in non-linear networks, and whether the landscape-quality-vs-rank tradeoff persists in larger, deeper architectures.
