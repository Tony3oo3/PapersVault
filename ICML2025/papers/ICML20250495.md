## Problem

Supervised fine-tuning of LLMs is computationally expensive because cost scales linearly with the number of training examples, and existing data-selection methods (coverage-based coresets, quality filtering, LLM-as-judge prompting) do not leverage the statistical structure of the SFT objective. The paper asks: given a fixed budget of n training sentences, which subset maximizes the information gain of the fine-tuning process as measured by the Fisher information matrix of the SFT log-likelihood?

## Input / Output

The method takes a dataset of N prompt-response sentence pairs and the d-dimensional pre-logit layer embeddings x_{i,j} for every token position j in every sentence i (obtained by a forward pass through the LLM). It outputs a subset S of n sentences (n < N) to use for fine-tuning. The key structural constraint is that the full Fisher information matrix is dL×dL (where L is vocabulary size, ~10^5), which is intractable; the method reduces this to a d×d design matrix (d ~ 10^3) that is tractable to optimize.

## Method

FisherSFT exploits the fact that the SFT objective decomposes into a product of multinomial logistic regression models (one per token position), so its Hessian has a Kronecker-product structure. Lemma 3.1 derives a lower bound on log-det of the full dL×dL Hessian in terms of log-det of a d×d matrix (the sum of outer products of token embeddings), reducing the problem to a tractable optimal design. Because log-det is monotone submodular, the paper applies greedy selection (Nemhauser et al., 1978): at each step, pick the sentence whose token embeddings maximize the marginal log-det gain of the design matrix. A fast implementation (Algorithm 2) exploits submodularity to cache information gains and only recompute them when they could exceed the current maximum, yielding a ~4× speedup. The paper proves an O(√(dLκ/n)) prediction error bound under a diversity assumption on the dataset.

## Results

On the Sherlock Holmes corpus, FisherSFT achieves win rates of 0.64–0.93 against Uniform, 0.65–0.88 against DensitySampling, and 0.58–0.89 against AskLLM across 100–5000 selected sentences, with all but one comparison exceeding 0.6. On the Shakespeare corpus, win rates range from 0.52 to 0.84 depending on the baseline and budget. On synthetic token prediction, FisherSFT is roughly 2× more sample-efficient than the best baseline (achieving the best baseline's maximum prediction error at n=1000 vs. n=2000), and on word2vec embeddings it reaches the best baseline's mean error at n=1250 vs. n=2000.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FisherSFT outperforms all three baselines on both the Shakespeare and Sherlock Holmes corpora in LLM-as-a-judge evaluations. Against DensitySampling (Sachdeva et al., 2024), FisherSFT achieves win rates of 0.54–0.84 on Shakespeare and 0.65–0.88 on Sherlock Holmes, with the largest margins at small (n=100–200) and large (n=5000) budgets. Against AskLLM (Sachdeva et al., 2024), FisherSFT wins with rates of 0.52–0.74 on Shakespeare and 0.58–0.89 on Sherlock Holmes, though the advantage is narrower at intermediate budgets (n=500–2000). Against ClusteredSampling (Axiotis et al., 2024), FisherSFT achieves the lowest maximum and mean prediction error on the synthetic token task, reaching the best baseline's error at roughly half the sample size. The Uniform random baseline is consistently the weakest, with FisherSFT winning by the largest margins (up to 0.93 on Sherlock Holmes at n=5000).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper connects the SFT objective to a product of multinomial logistic regression models and applies classical optimal design theory (Pukelsheim, 2006; Stufken & Yang, 2012) to select informative training sentences. It departs from prior data-selection approaches for LLMs—coverage-based coresets, quality/perplexity filtering, and LLM-as-a-selector methods like ASK-LLM—by using the Fisher information matrix of the SFT loss as the selection criterion. The key technical departure is a linearization at the last logit layer that reduces the intractable dL×dL Fisher matrix to a d×d design matrix, enabling greedy submodular optimization over sentences rather than individual tokens.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FisherSFT provides a principled, analyzable alternative to heuristic data-selection methods for SFT, with a clean O(dL/√n) prediction-error guarantee under a diversity assumption. The practical impact is significant for compute-constrained fine-tuning: selecting fewer but more informative sentences directly reduces training cost. However, experiments are limited to GPT-2 on two small literary corpora with LLM-as-a-judge evaluation rather than human assessment, and the diversity assumption (Assumption 4.2) may not hold for highly skewed real-world data. Open questions include whether the token-level information gain criterion scales to trillion-parameter models, whether the inherent bias toward longer sentences is problematic, and whether the approximation quality degrades when the pre-logit embeddings are themselves being updated during fine-tuning.
