## Problem

Maximum Entropy RL methods like SAC are widely used for continuous control but consistently underperform non-MaxEnt methods like PPO in performance-critical tasks requiring precise, low-entropy policies (e.g., quadrotor trajectory tracking, high-speed vehicle control). The paper identifies and formalizes the mechanism behind this failure: entropy maximization in the soft-Q value landscape can mislead the MaxEnt-optimal policy at convergence toward suboptimal, high-entropy actions, rather than merely causing exploration difficulties during training.

## Input / Output

The theoretical construction (Entropy Bifurcation Extension) takes an arbitrary MDP with continuous action space and a target state, and produces a modified MDP with additional bifurcating intermediate states whose parameters (action interval sizes, terminal rewards) can be tuned to make any arbitrary policy distribution the MaxEnt-optimal one at the target state. The practical SAC-AdaEnt variant takes standard state observations from continuous control environments and produces control actions, but internally maintains two parallel Q-networks (soft and plain) and computes a cosine-similarity score between their sampled value vectors at each state to decide which Q-landscape to use for policy updates.

## Method

The paper introduces the Entropy Bifurcation Extension, a construction that inserts new intermediate states between a target state and its successors, each with a bifurcating action space split into a 'recover' interval (transitioning back to the original successor) and a 'trap' interval (transitioning to a new terminal state). Two lemmas—Backward Compatibility (any target value and policy at the target state can be matched by choosing appropriate soft-Q values) and Forward Compatibility (any target value at a bifurcating state can be achieved by tuning interval sizes and terminal rewards)—guarantee that the MaxEnt-optimal policy at the target state can be arbitrarily shaped without altering the optimal policy elsewhere. Empirically, the paper demonstrates the misleading effect by comparing soft-Q and plain-Q landscapes in Vehicle, Quadrotor, and Opencat environments, showing that entropy inflates values of mediocre next-states and shifts the policy away from the narrow feasible action region. To validate the theory, SAC-AdaEnt adaptively switches from soft-Q to plain-Q targets when their landscapes diverge beyond a similarity threshold, recovering performance in environments where standard SAC fails while preserving SAC's advantages in exploration-friendly environments like Hopper and Obstacle2D.

## Results

Across six continuous control environments, SAC (MaxEnt) underperforms PPO on complex dynamics tasks (Vehicle, Quadrotor, Opencat) where precise low-entropy policies are required, while significantly outperforming PPO on standard benchmarks (Acrobot, Obstacle2D, Hopper). In the Vehicle environment, SAC achieves -2003.85±867.82 reward versus PPO's successful convergence; in Quadrotor, SAC scores -475.29±244.96. The proposed SAC-AdaEnt variant improves SAC's performance in failing environments (e.g., Vehicle: -1250.45 vs. -2003.85; Quadrotor: -247.58 vs. -475.29) while preserving SAC's strengths on Hopper (3285.17 vs. 3484.46), Obstacle2D (501.50 vs. 501.98), and Acrobot (-36.31 vs. -45.25).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SAC (the primary MaxEnt method) loses to PPO on all three complex control environments (Vehicle, Quadrotor, Opencat) where the task requires precise, low-entropy actions at critical states, while SAC significantly outperforms PPO on Acrobot, Obstacle2D, and Hopper where entropy-driven exploration helps avoid local optima. DDPG, another non-MaxEnt baseline, also outperforms SAC on the complex control tasks but is less competitive on the standard benchmarks. The paper's key contribution is showing that this SAC-vs-PPO gap is not a hyperparameter or sample-efficiency issue but a fundamental consequence of the soft-Q value landscape: entropy inflates Q-values for high-entropy (but suboptimal) next states, biasing the MaxEnt-optimal policy away from the narrow feasible action regions. SAC-AdaEnt, which adaptively switches to plain Q-values when soft-Q and plain-Q landscapes diverge, recovers much of the lost performance (e.g., Vehicle reward improves from -2003.85 to -1250.45) while maintaining SAC's advantages on Hopper and Obstacle2D. Soft Q-Learning (SQL) also suffers from the same entropy-misleading effect, confirming the issue is inherent to the MaxEnt framework rather than specific to SAC's implementation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the MaxEnt RL framework (Ziebart et al., 2008; Haarnoja et al., 2018a) and its well-known benefits for exploration and robustness, but departs from the prevailing narrative by showing these same properties can mislead the converged policy in performance-critical control. It draws a conceptual parallel to the robustness-optimality tradeoff literature in supervised learning (Su et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2019; Tsipras et al., 2018), but applies it to the RL setting at convergence rather than during training. The Entropy Bifurcation Extension is a novel theoretical construction that generalizes the toy example into a general tool for characterizing when MaxEnt-optimal policies diverge from true optimal policies, and the empirical analysis connects to the practical robotics control literature where PPO has consistently outperformed SAC (Kaufmann et al., 2023; Miki et al., 2022).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete theoretical and empirical explanation for a long-observed but poorly understood practical failure mode: why SAC systematically underperforms PPO on realistic control tasks with precise low-entropy requirements. The Entropy Bifurcation Extension theorem (showing MaxEnt can be made to match any arbitrary policy, including the worst one, while preserving the true optimum) is a strong negative result that reframes entropy regularization as a double-edged sword. Key limitations include the non-scalability of SAC-AdaEnt (requiring global Q-landscape comparison) and the fact that the theoretical construction, while general, does not directly prescribe how to detect bifurcation states in high-dimensional environments. Open questions include whether state-dependent entropy scheduling can be made practical at scale, and whether the adversarial implications noted in the conclusion (entropy as an attack surface) can be formalized further.
