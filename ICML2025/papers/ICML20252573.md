## Problem

In off-policy hierarchical RL, the low-level policy changes during training, invalidating past subgoal experiences and making it difficult for the high-level policy to generate subgoals compatible with current low-level skills. Existing hindsight relabeling approaches (HIRO, HAC) do not efficiently adapt the high-level policy to these non-stationary shifts, and constraining the subgoal space (HRAC, HIGL) fails to scale to complex environments. The paper addresses this by explicitly modeling a state-conditioned subgoal distribution with uncertainty quantification, so the high-level policy can generate diverse yet reachable subgoals that track the evolving low-level policy.

## Input / Output

The high-level policy consumes the current environment state (a state vector in most tasks, or a 5×5×3 top-down image appended to zeroed coordinates for the Ant FourRooms Image variant) and outputs a subgoal in a task-specific goal space: 2D (x,y) for maze tasks, 3D (x,y,z) for Reacher, or 6D including object position for Pusher. Subgoals are generated every k=10 low-level steps via a 5-step conditional diffusion process, and between high-level decisions a goal transition function updates the subgoal relative to the current state. The low-level policy then maps (state, subgoal) pairs to continuous actions in the environment.

## Method

HIDI formulates the high-level policy as a conditional diffusion model (noise-prediction parameterization, 5 denoising steps, 3-layer MLP with 256 hidden units) trained on hindsight-relabeled subgoals to directly model the state-conditioned subgoal distribution, avoiding the instability of temporal-difference learning. A sparse Gaussian Process prior with an RBF kernel and 16 learned inducing points regularizes the diffusion objective via a negative log-likelihood term, providing explicit uncertainty estimates that focus learning on feasible, experience-supported regions of the state space. At decision time, a hybrid selection strategy uses the GP predictive mean with probability ε=0.1 and samples from the diffusion model with probability 0.9, balancing structural reliability with generative diversity. The full training objective combines the diffusion loss, GP regularization (weighted by ψ=10⁻³), and a TD3-based RL objective (weighted by η=5), with gradients backpropagated through the entire diffusion chain. The paper provides theoretical guarantees including a single-step regret bound for the subgoal selection strategy and a policy improvement proposition showing the GP-mean component does not degrade performance.

## Results

HIDI achieves the highest success rate across nearly all 12 sparse- and dense-reward MuJoCo continuous control tasks after 5M training steps. Headline sparse-reward results include 0.95 on Reacher, 0.78 on Pusher, 0.87 on Ant Maze (W-shape), 0.84 on Stochastic Ant Fall, and 0.64 on Stochastic Ant FourRooms (Image), consistently outperforming all five baselines. The largest margins appear on Pusher (0.78 vs. 0.20 for SAGA) and Stochastic Ant FourRooms (Image) (0.64 vs. 0.32 for SAGA, 0.00 for HRAC and HIRO). In dense-reward settings, HIDI also leads or ties on all four evaluated tasks (e.g., 0.92 on Stochastic Ant Maze U vs. 0.88 for HLPS).

## Baselines & Comparisons

HIDI outperforms all five baselines on 11 out of 12 tasks (tied with HLPS and HRAC on dense Point Maze at 1.00). The most dramatic wins are against SAGA on Pusher (0.78 vs. 0.20, a 3.9× gap) and Stochastic Ant FourRooms (Image) (0.64 vs. 0.32), and against HRAC/HIRO on Stochastic Ant FourRooms (Image) where both baselines fail completely (0.00). Against the closest competitor HLPS, HIDI leads by 0.06–0.11 on most sparse tasks (e.g., 0.95 vs. 0.54 on Reacher, 0.84 vs. 0.78 on Stochastic Ant Fall) and by 0.04–0.05 on dense tasks. HIGL and HRAC are consistently weaker, with HIGL struggling most on Pusher (0.31) and Ant Maze U (0.52 sparse), while HRAC's adjacency constraint causes it to fail on Stochastic Ant FourRooms (Image) and underperform on Ant Maze W (0.51 sparse). HIRO, the original hindsight-relabeling baseline, shows the largest gap between generated and reached subgoals (Δ = 10.90 vs. HIDI's 0.82 in Table 1), confirming that its subgoals are often unachievable by the low-level policy.

## Relation to Prior Work

HIDI extends the off-policy HRL framework established by HIRO (Nachum et al., 2018) by replacing the high-level policy's temporal-difference learning with a conditional diffusion model that directly models the state-conditioned subgoal distribution, drawing on the DDPM parameterization (Ho et al., 2020). It departs from prior subgoal-generation approaches—SAGA's adversarial GAN, HIGL's landmark constraints, and HRAC's adjacency restrictions—by explicitly constructing a multimodal subgoal distribution rather than constraining or adversarially shaping the action space. The sparse Gaussian Process prior (Snelson & Ghahramani, 2005) is introduced as a novel regularizer that provides principled uncertainty quantification for subgoal generation, a capability absent from both diffusion-based and GAN-based HRL methods. The overall architecture follows HRAC's two-layer TD3 structure, making the contribution primarily in the high-level policy parameterization and its training objective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a persistent practical problem in off-policy HRL—non-stationarity of the high-level policy due to a changing low-level policy—by providing a generative, distribution-matching alternative to temporal-difference learning that is less susceptible to the deadly triad. The GP prior's contribution (~15% performance gain in ablations) is notable because it demonstrates that explicit uncertainty quantification meaningfully improves subgoal generation beyond what the diffusion model alone achieves, suggesting that principled uncertainty estimates are underexploited in HRL. Limitations include evaluation restricted to MuJoCo continuous-control benchmarks, reliance on a small number of inducing points (16) that may not scale to high-dimensional state spaces, and theoretical guarantees that depend on a near-optimal diffusion policy assumption. Open questions include whether the GP regularizer generalizes to discrete or very high-dimensional subgoal spaces, and whether the diffusion+GP combination can be extended to multi-level hierarchies or model-based settings.
