## Problem

Offline RL with expressive generative policies (flow or diffusion) is difficult because steering an iterative generative model to maximize a learned value function requires backpropagation through time, which is unstable and computationally costly. This paper asks how to leverage the expressivity of flow-matching policies to capture complex, multimodal behavioral action distributions while avoiding the instability of recursive backpropagation during value maximization.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251923/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes state vectors or 64×64×3 RGB pixel observations (with 3-frame stacking and random-shift augmentation for pixel tasks) and produces continuous action vectors in R^d. A key design choice is that at deployment, only the one-step policy is used—no iterative ODE solving is required at test time, making inference comparable in speed to Gaussian policy baselines. During training, the BC flow policy requires 10 Euler steps to generate target actions for the distillation loss.

## Method

FQL introduces 'one-step guidance': instead of steering the iterative flow policy to maximize Q values (which requires unstable backpropagation through time), it trains the flow policy solely with a behavioral-cloning flow-matching loss, and separately trains a one-step policy μ_ω(s,z) that maps noise z directly to an action. The one-step policy is optimized with a reparameterized Q loss (maximizing Q_φ(s, a^π) through a^π = μ_ω(s,z)) plus a distillation loss that pulls its outputs toward the BC flow policy's ODE outputs. This distillation loss serves as a squared 2-Wasserstein behavioral regularizer, providing metric-aware constraints on the action space. The entire method fits within a standard behavior-regularized actor-critic framework with a single major hyperparameter α, and the deployed policy is the one-step model, eliminating iterative generation at inference time.

## Results

FQL achieves the best or near-best performance on the majority of 73 state- and pixel-based offline RL tasks across OGBench and D4RL. On the hardest D4RL antmaze-large-play task, FQL reaches 84% success rate, outperforming ReBRAC (78%) and IDQL (79%). On manipulation tasks with highly multimodal action distributions, FQL shows the largest margins: 96% on cube-single (vs. 91% ReBRAC), 29% on cube-double (vs. 15% FBRAC), and 60% on antsoccer (vs. 33% IFQL). For offline-to-online fine-tuning, FQL reaches 92% on cube-double-play and 100% on scene-play, surpassing both offline RL baselines and methods designed for online fine-tuning (Cal-QL, RLPD).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FQL consistently outperforms its closest diffusion baseline, Consistency-AC (CAC), across nearly all OGBench tasks (e.g., 60 vs. 2 on antsoccer, 56 vs. 40 on scene, 30 vs. 19 on puzzle-3x3), and also beats the closest Gaussian baseline ReBRAC on most manipulation tasks (96 vs. 91 on cube-single, 29 vs. 12 on cube-double, 65 vs. 60 on visual manipulation). Against rejection-sampling-based IDQL, FQL wins on most tasks (84 vs. 79 on D4RL antmaze, 60 vs. 12 on antsoccer, 96 vs. 95 on cube-single), though IDQL edges out FQL on puzzle-4x4 (29 vs. 17). Among flow-based baselines sharing the same architecture, FQL's one-step distillation extraction significantly outperforms FBRAC (backprop-through-time, e.g., 60 vs. 16 on antsoccer, 29 vs. 15 on cube-double) and FAWAC (weighted BC, e.g., 60 vs. 12 on antsoccer, 56 vs. 30 on scene). For offline-to-online fine-tuning, FQL surpasses Cal-QL and RLPD on most tasks (e.g., 92 vs. 0 on cube-double-play, 100 vs. 100 on scene-play, 99 vs. 100 on antmaze-umaze), though RLPD matches or slightly exceeds FQL on puzzle-4x4-play (100 vs. 38) where exploration is critical. ReBRAC retains an advantage on D4RL adroit (59 vs. 52) and antmaze-large (81 vs. 79), indicating FQL's expressivity benefit is most pronounced on tasks with complex, multimodal action distributions.

## Relation to Prior Work

FQL builds on the behavior-regularized actor-critic framework (ReBRAC) and flow matching as a generative modeling technique, but departs fundamentally from prior diffusion/flow policy RL methods (Diffusion-QL, Consistency-AC, SRPO) that steer the iterative generative model via backpropagation through time. The key conceptual departure is 'one-step guidance': rather than maximizing values through the recursive ODE solver, FQL trains the flow policy purely with behavioral cloning and trains a separate one-step policy that maximizes Q-values while distilling from the BC flow, thereby eliminating BPTT entirely. The distillation loss is further shown to be an upper bound on the squared 2-Wasserstein distance between policies, providing a metric-aware behavioral regularizer in contrast to the metric-agnostic KL or χ² regularizers used by TD3+BC, AWAC, and CQL.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FQL offers a practically important simplification: a single algorithm box that replaces complex BPTT-based policy extraction with a few lines of code on top of standard actor-critic, yielding a one-step policy at inference time with only one major hyperparameter (α). The consistent outperformance over both Gaussian (ReBRAC) and diffusion (CAC) baselines across 73 diverse tasks, plus direct fine-tunability for offline-to-online RL, makes it a strong default choice for practitioners working with complex multimodal action distributions. Open questions include whether one-step guidance transfers to diffusion policies (via SDE-to-ODE conversion), whether more advanced distillation (e.g., shortcut models) can further reduce training cost, and whether the lack of built-in exploration limits online fine-tuning on tasks requiring exploration (e.g., puzzle-4x4). Real-world robotic validation remains untested.
