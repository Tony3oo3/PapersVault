## Problem

The prevailing lazy-versus-rich dichotomy in feature learning research is too coarse to capture the diverse subtypes of feature learning arising from different architectures, training procedures, initializations, and data properties. Moreover, neuroscience applications require representation-based analysis rather than weight-change or NTK-based measures, since synaptic weights cannot be directly measured in biological circuits. The paper asks: what geometric changes in task-relevant neural manifolds characterize different degrees and types of feature learning beyond this binary classification?

## Input / Output

The method takes last-layer (or hidden-state) activations from a trained network, grouped by class label into P point clouds in R^N, and models each class as the convex hull of its activation vectors. It produces a scalar manifold capacity value quantifying the degree of feature learning, plus five effective geometric measures (dimension, radius, center alignment, axis alignment, center-axis alignment) that serve as mechanistic descriptors of how manifolds untangle. Capacity is computed via quadratic programming over sampled random projections and dichotomy vectors (Algorithm 2), avoiding the expensive binary-search simulation of the original definition.

## Method

The core framework treats feature learning as the untangling of task-relevant manifolds, quantified by manifold capacity — the packability of convex hulls of class representations in the neural activation space. Using the GLUE (Geometry Linked to Untangling Efficiency) theory, capacity is computed analytically via a mean-field formula involving projections onto convex cones, solvable by quadratic programs, with an error of O(1/N) relative to the simulated definition. Effective geometric measures are derived from the distribution of anchor points (support vectors under random projections) and connected to capacity through the approximation α_M ≈ (1+R_M^{-2})/D_M, where dimension captures within-manifold variability and radius captures the noise-to-signal ratio. Theoretically, the paper extends Ba et al. (2022) from regression to classification, proving that after one gradient step in a 2-layer network, capacity monotonically tracks the learning rate (ground-truth richness) and is monotonically linked to prediction accuracy. Empirically, the framework reveals distinct learning stages (clustering → structuring → separating → stabilizing) and strategies (radius compression vs. dimension compression) across 2-layer networks on synthetic data, VGG-11/ResNet-18 on CIFAR-10/100/10C, and RNNs trained on neuroscience tasks such as perceptual decision making.

## Results

The paper's primary quantitative contribution is Theorem 3.1, which proves that in a 2-layer network with proportional asymptotics, manifold capacity is a strictly increasing function of the learning rate η (the ground-truth richness parameter) and is monotonically linked to prediction accuracy. Empirically, in 2-layer networks on Gaussian clouds (Fig. 4a), capacity better discriminates between different scale parameters (richness levels) than accuracy, weight changes, NTK-label alignment, or representation-label alignment. In the initialization-wealthiness experiment (Fig. 4b), capacity correctly orders networks by their initial task-relevant feature content, whereas representation-label alignment produces the wrong ordering. For OOD generalization (Fig. 7), CIFAR-100 linear-probe accuracy peaks at moderate richness (η̄ ≈ 0.1) but drops drastically in the ultra-rich regime (η̄ ≈ 1.0), a failure explained by expansion of manifold radius and increased center-axis alignment.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against NTK-label alignment (Jacot et al., 2018) and representation-label alignment (Kornblith et al., 2019), manifold capacity is the only measure that simultaneously detects feature changes, quantifies the *amount* of task-relevant features, and delineates subtypes of feature learning (Table 1). In the Gaussian-cloud experiment (Fig. 4a), capacity separates different richness levels more cleanly than either CKA-based alignment; in the initialization experiment (Fig. 4b), representation-label alignment misorders the wealthiness of initializations while capacity gets the correct ordering. Against Liu et al. (ICLR 2024), who used weight-change magnitude to characterize effective laziness in RNNs, this paper agrees qualitatively (small initial weight rank → richer training) but adds two new findings: (i) all RNNs converge to the same final capacity, so it is the *initial* capacity difference (not the total change) that distinguishes learning regimes, and (ii) despite identical final capacity, RNNs with different initial ranks exhibit distinct manifold geometries (larger radius / smaller dimension for poorer-richer learning vs. the reverse), revealing structural inductive biases invisible to weight-change metrics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the lazy-versus-rich training framework of Chizat et al. (2019) by replacing binary regime classification with a continuous, representation-based quantification via manifold capacity (Chung et al., 2018; Chou et al., 2025). It builds directly on the high-dimensional asymptotic analysis of Ba et al. (2022) for one-step gradient updates, extending their regression results to a classification setting with a monotone capacity-accuracy connection. The effective geometric measures (dimension, radius, alignment) are analytically derived from the GLUE capacity formula, positioning this work at the intersection of statistical physics of neural networks and representational geometry in neuroscience.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework is particularly valuable for neuroscience, where synaptic weight changes cannot be directly measured, offering an activity-based alternative to NTK or weight-change metrics. The finding that ultra-rich feature learning degrades OOD generalization (CIFAR-100 linear probe) while improving in-distribution performance challenges the assumption that more feature learning is uniformly beneficial. Open questions include extending the analysis to transformers and other architectures, leveraging geometric insights to design better OOD-robust training, and inferring plasticity mechanisms from observed learning dynamics in biological neural data.
