## Problem

Spectral graph convolution relies on two key design choices—spectral basis selection and kernel parameterization—that have been developed in isolation. Existing graph wavelet methods either fix wavelet shapes (lacking adaptability to data) or learn coefficients without enforcing wavelet admissibility criteria, while Fourier-based approaches offer only a single fixed resolution. The paper addresses the lack of a unified framework that simultaneously provides multi-resolution, adaptive spectral bases and a sufficiently expressive kernel to capture both short- and long-range information.

## Input / Output

The method consumes an undirected graph's symmetric normalized Laplacian (requiring full eigendecomposition, or top-K randomized SVD for large graphs like ogbn-arxiv) and a node feature matrix X ∈ R^{N×d₀}. It produces node embeddings H ∈ R^{N×d} at each layer, which are then read out via task-specific heads for node classification, link prediction, graph classification, or graph regression. The spectral transform F stacks J wavelet transforms at different learned scales plus one scaling function transform, yielding an intermediate representation in R^{N(J+1)×d} before the matrix-valued kernel is applied.

## Method

WaveGC decomposes Chebyshev polynomials via the transform T_k(y) → ½(−T_k(y−1)+1), separating even-indexed terms (which satisfy g(0)=0 and thus the wavelet admissibility criterion) as the unit wavelet basis g(Λ) and odd-indexed terms (which satisfy h(0)=1) as the scaling function h(Λ), with learnable coefficients derived from eigenvalue embeddings via a Transformer-based encoding module. Learnable scales s_j are applied to g(s_jΛ) to create multi-resolution wavelets, and an L₂ normalization enforces tight frames so the inverse transform reduces to a transpose. The convolution kernel is a matrix-valued MLP (d×d parameters) shared across all spectral modes, replacing the conventional diagonal kernel, which the paper shows empirically outperforms vector-valued alternatives. Theoretically, the paper proves via maximal mixing bounds that as scale s→0 the wavelet requires more layers to mix distant nodes (short-range behavior), while as s→∞ it achieves the same mixing with fewer layers (long-range behavior), establishing that receptive field control is governed exclusively by the scale parameter rather than the Chebyshev order K.

## Results

WaveGC achieves state-of-the-art results on all 10 benchmark datasets. On short-range node classification, it reaches 95.89% on CiteSeer, 95.37% on Photo, 92.26% on Computer, 69.14% on CoraFull, and 73.01% on ogbn-arxiv. On long-range tasks, the most dramatic gain is on PascalVOC-SP where WaveGC scores 41.63 F1, a 15.7% relative improvement over the best baseline DEFT (35.98). It also leads on COCO-SP (35.96 F1 vs. 32.97), Peptides-func (69.73 AP vs. 68.75), and Peptides-struct (24.83 MAE vs. 25.06).

## Baselines & Comparisons

WaveGC outperforms all 14 baselines across all 10 datasets. On long-range tasks, the closest competitor is DEFT (learnable spectral wavelets via MLP-learned Chebyshev coefficients), which WaveGC surpasses by 5.65 F1 on VOC (41.63 vs. 35.98), 2.99 F1 on COCO (35.96 vs. 30.14), 2.78 AP on Pf (69.73 vs. 66.95), and 0.23 MAE on Ps (24.83 vs. 25.06). Among classical GNNs, GCN and GatedGCN are the strongest short-range baselines but lag significantly on long-range tasks (e.g., GCN scores only 12.68 F1 on VOC vs. WaveGC's 41.63). Among spectral wavelet methods, SGWT and UFGConvS/R use fixed wavelet shapes (cubic spline, exponential) and cannot adapt to the data, while GWNN uses a single exponential wavelet that omits multi-resolution information. The ablation in Table 4 confirms that the matrix-valued kernel (vs. vector-valued) contributes +2.30 accuracy on Computer and −0.47 MAE on Ps, and Table 5 shows weight sharing across spectral modes avoids overfitting (92.26 vs. 90.51 on Computer with 167k vs. 535k parameters).

## Relation to Prior Work

WaveGC sits at the intersection of three established lines: the spectral graph wavelet transform (SGWT) framework of Hammond et al. (2011), which defines wavelet admissibility and multi-scale bases on graphs; ChebNet's (Defferrard et al., 2016) Chebyshev polynomial approximation of spectral filters; and the tensor-valued kernel parameterization introduced by Fourier Neural Operators (Li et al., 2021). The key departure is a Chebyshev order decomposition—separating odd and even polynomial terms to form scaling functions and wavelets respectively—which the authors show strictly satisfies admissibility criteria, unlike prior learnable wavelet approaches (e.g., DEFT) that relax this constraint. The paper also completes the theoretical picture by analyzing receptive fields for both s→0 (short-range, covered by Hammond et al.) and s→∞ (long-range, previously unaddressed).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The consistent gains across both short-range (up to ~1.5% on Computer) and long-range (up to 15.7% on VOC) benchmarks demonstrate that multi-resolution wavelet bases with matrix-valued kernels are a practical recipe for handling heterogeneous spatial scales in a single architecture. However, the O(N³) eigendecomposition bottleneck limits applicability to medium-scale graphs, and the polynomial approximation that removes EVD (mentioned in Appendix D) is not fully validated. Open questions include whether the tight-frame normalization is always beneficial, how the method behaves on heterophilic graphs, and whether the eigenvalue-encoding module (borrowed from SpecFormer) is truly necessary or a proxy for simpler spectral conditioning.
