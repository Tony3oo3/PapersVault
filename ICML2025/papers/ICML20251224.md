## Problem

Value iteration (VI) and policy iteration (PI) for MDPs exhibit a fundamental trade-off: VI has O(n²m) per-iteration cost but only linear convergence at rate γ, while PI achieves faster (locally quadratic) convergence at the cost of an O(n³) matrix inversion per iteration. The paper asks whether one can retain PI's accelerated convergence while keeping VI's per-iteration complexity, by exploiting the dominant eigenstructure of the transition matrix rather than inverting it fully.

## Input / Output

For the planning problem (R1-VI), the algorithm takes the full transition kernel P, stage cost c, and discount factor γ, and iteratively produces the optimal value function v* ∈ R^n. For the learning problem (R1-QL), it takes a generative model providing next-state samples s⁺ ~ P(·|s,a) for each state-action pair, along with c and γ, and produces the optimal Q-function q* ∈ R^{nm}. In both cases the per-iteration complexity matches the standard first-order algorithm (O(n²m) for R1-VI, O(nm²) for R1-QL), and the only additional state carried across iterations is a probability vector d_k (or its sample-based estimate d̂_k) of dimension n (or nm).

## Method

The key mechanism replaces the full PI gain matrix (I − γP_k)^(−1) with its rank-one surrogate (I − γ·1·d_k^T)^(−1), where d_k is the stationary distribution of the greedy-policy transition matrix P_k. By the Woodbury formula this inverse reduces to I + (γ/(1−γ))·1·d_k^T, yielding the closed-form update v_{k+1} = T(v_k) + (γ/(1−γ))⟨d_k, T(v_k) − v_k⟩·1, which costs O(n) beyond the standard Bellman backup. The vector d_k is approximated by a single power-method iteration (f = P_k^T d_{k−1}; d_k = f/‖f‖₁), which is effective because the greedy policy—and hence P_k—typically remains unchanged across consecutive iterations, so the algorithm implicitly accumulates multiple power-method steps. For the learning problem, the stationary distribution is estimated from samples via a stochastic approximation of the state-action transition matrix, and the same rank-one correction is applied to the synchronous Q-learning update with Robbins-Monro step-sizes. The paper proves convergence at the same linear rate γ as VI (Theorem 3.3) and almost-sure convergence at the same rate as QL (Theorem 4.2), while empirically demonstrating substantially faster convergence than VI, Nesterov-VI, Anderson-VI, Speedy QL, and Zap QL, especially at high discount factors.

## Results

R1-VI achieves convergence comparable to Policy Iteration (PI) while maintaining the same O(n²m) per-iteration complexity as standard Value Iteration (VI), significantly outperforming VI, Nesterov-VI, and Anderson-VI—particularly at high discount factors (γ = 0.99, 0.999). R1-QL achieves comparable or lower Bellman and value errors than QL, Speedy QL, and Zap QL after 5000 synchronous iterations on both Garnet (n=200, m=5) and Graph MDPs, while maintaining the same O(nm²) per-iteration time and memory complexity as standard QL. Notably, R1-QL maintains consistent error levels across all tested discount factors (γ ∈ {0.9, 0.95, 0.99, 0.999}), a characteristic typically associated with PI-type algorithms, whereas standard QL degrades substantially at higher γ.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against standard VI, R1-VI consistently achieves lower Bellman and value errors at every iteration on both Garnet and Graph MDPs, with the gap widening as γ increases toward 1. Against PI, R1-VI matches convergence speed in the value space while reducing per-iteration cost from O(n²m + n³) to O(n²m) by replacing the full matrix inversion with a rank-one Woodbury update. Against Nesterov-VI and Anderson-VI, R1-VI outperforms both, with Anderson-VI exhibiting non-monotonic error trajectories and the highest variance across 25 Garnet realizations. In the learning setting, R1-QL outperforms standard QL and Speedy QL at γ ≥ 0.99, where the linearly decaying step-size causes QL to stagnate. Against Zap QL, R1-QL achieves lower error variance and avoids the O((nm)³) cost of full-rank matrix inversion, though both benefit from the PI-type correction at high γ. On the reducible Gridworld (Terminal variant with zero-reward absorbing state), R1-VI slightly underperforms accelerated VI baselines because the stationary distribution concentrates on the absorbing state, causing the rank-one correction term to vanish.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of the optimization-analogy line of work (VI as gradient descent, PI as Newton) and the low-rank matrix approximation literature. It departs from Zap Q-Learning, which also approximates the PI gain (I−γP̄_k)⁻¹ but requires full-rank matrix inversion, by instead using a rank-one approximation P̃_k = 1d_kᵀ derived from the stationary distribution via the power method. This yields a PI-type update at VI-level O(n²m) per-iteration cost, bridging the computational gap that has motivated accelerated VI methods (Nesterov, Anderson) and operator-splitting approaches. The Woodbury formula provides the algebraic mechanism that makes the rank-one inversion tractable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key open question is the gap between theory and practice: the convergence guarantee only matches the linear rate γ of standard VI/QL, yet empirically R1-VI/R1-QL approach PI-like convergence, particularly at high discount factors. This suggests the proof technique is loose and that exploiting the specific structure of d_k as a stationary distribution approximation could yield tighter bounds. Limitations are significant: the method is restricted to tabular MDPs with synchronous updates, does not extend to function approximation or continuous state-action spaces, and degrades in reducible MDPs where the stationary distribution concentrates on a single state. Extending to asynchronous settings, average-cost MDPs, and function approximation are natural but non-trivial next steps.
