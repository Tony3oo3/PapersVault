## Problem

State-of-the-art online linear programming (OLP) algorithms face a fundamental trade-off: LP-based methods achieve O(log T) regret but require solving an LP at every time step, causing computational delays that block real-time decision-making, while first-order methods process customers instantly but suffer O(√T) regret. The paper asks whether one can simultaneously achieve low regret and computational efficiency without introducing any decision-making delay.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252279/cropped_page5_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes a stream of i.i.d. customer arrivals (r_t, a_t) — each with a scalar bid and an m-dimensional resource demand vector — along with a total resource budget b ∈ R^m, a known time horizon T, and a user-chosen re-solving frequency f. Decisions x_t ∈ {0,1} are emitted immediately upon each arrival with zero waiting time, and the algorithm internally maintains dual prices p_t that are refined by periodic LP re-solves (every f steps) and fine-tuned by subgradient updates in between. The theoretical guarantee is a worst-case regret bound of O(log(T/f) + √f) on the combined objective of revenue loss plus resource constraint violation.

## Method

The core contribution is a parallel multi-phase framework that decouples online learning from online decision-making into two concurrent paths. The learning path re-solves the OLP dual at a fixed frequency f (k = ⌊T/f⌋ batches), producing refined dual prices; the decision-making path applies a first-order subgradient method in the initial and final batches (with optimally tuned learning rates α = 1/√f and α = 1/f^{2/3} respectively) and simply reuses the most recent LP-derived dual price during intermediate batches, so every customer is served instantly. A key theoretical innovation is a unified performance metric that decomposes total regret into three components — dual convergence, binding-constraint violation, and leftover resources — making the LP-based stopping-time analysis and the first-order constraint-violation analysis compatible within a single framework. This yields a 'spectrum theorem': f = 1 recovers pure LP-based O(log T) regret, f = T recovers pure first-order O(√T) regret, and intermediate f values interpolate between the two, with the optimal f determined by available computational resources via a constrained optimization problem.

## Results

At T=10⁶ with m=5 resource types, Algorithm 2 achieves regret of 7.09 in 2242s, compared to 115.39 for the O(T^{1/3}) first-order method (1.58s) and 5.50 for the LP-based method (>100,000s). Algorithm 1 achieves regret of 11.65 in 2156s under the same setting. The theoretical worst-case regret bound is O(log(T/f) + √f), which interpolates between O(log T) (pure LP, f=1) and O(√T) (pure first-order, f=T). Across all tested horizons (10⁴ to 10⁶), the proposed algorithms deliver over 10× lower regret than first-order baselines and over 100× faster runtime than the LP-based baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the O(T^{1/2}) first-order method (Li et al., 2020), Algorithm 2 achieves roughly 20–50× lower regret (e.g., 7.09 vs. 351.91 at T=10⁶) while remaining computationally trivial (2242s vs. 1.2s, but the LP-based method is infeasible at that scale). Against the stronger O(T^{1/3}) first-order method with restart (Gao et al., 2024), Algorithm 2 still delivers approximately 10–16× lower regret (7.09 vs. 115.39 at T=10⁶; 5.09 vs. 60.39 at T=10⁴). Against the LP-based method (Li & Ye, 2022), the proposed algorithms trade a modest regret increase (roughly 2–3×, e.g., 7.09 vs. 5.50 at T=10⁶) for a dramatic 100–50,000× speedup in runtime (2242s vs. >100,000s at T=10⁶; 1.8s vs. 123.5s at T=10⁴). Algorithm 2 (with intermediate first-order fine-tuning between LP re-solves) consistently outperforms Algorithm 1 by a small margin across all settings, confirming the benefit of the multi-restart mechanism. The LP-based method becomes computationally infeasible beyond T=10⁵ (>3600s), whereas the proposed algorithms scale gracefully to T=10⁶.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two well-studied families of OLP algorithms: LP-based methods (Li & Ye, 2022) that achieve O(log T) regret by re-solving the dual at every step, and first-order methods (Li et al., 2020; Gao et al., 2024) that are computationally cheap but suffer O(√T) or O(T^{1/3}) regret. The key departure is a parallel multi-phase architecture that decouples learning from decision-making: LP re-solving occurs at a fixed frequency f to refine dual prices, while a first-order subgradient method handles individual customer arrivals in between, with warm starts from the LP solution. This contrasts with prior batching approaches (Xu et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024) that still introduce decision delays, and with restart-based first-order methods (Gao et al., 2024) that do not leverage LP solutions as anchors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The spectrum theorem O(log(T/f) + √f) provides a principled, computationally-aware knob for practitioners to trade off regret against runtime, and the 100-fold speedup over LP-based methods while maintaining comparable regret makes high-quality OLP feasible in real-time settings like online advertising and revenue management. The 'wait-less' property—processing each customer immediately without batching delays—addresses a practical bottleneck that prior LP-based and batched methods could not resolve. Open questions include extending the framework to unknown horizons (where the average-resource assumption breaks down), adapting to non-i.i.d. or adversarial inputs, and whether the parallel structure generalizes to broader online optimization problems beyond OLP.
