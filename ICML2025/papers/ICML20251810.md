## Problem

Existing methods for evaluating LLM chain-of-thought reasoning either judge only the final answer or verify each step against the full preceding context, which includes irrelevant distractors that degrade LLM performance. Moreover, prior error taxonomies treat all post-error steps as equally wrong, failing to distinguish locally correct steps that merely inherit upstream mistakes (accumulation errors) from steps with inherent mathematical or logical flaws.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251810/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a math word problem and a linear reasoning chain (LRC) of t natural-language steps as input, and produces a Premise-Augmented Reasoning Chain (PARC) — a directed acyclic graph where nodes are steps and edges encode which prior steps serve as premises for each step — along with a per-step error label drawn from {Correct, Mathematical Error, Logical Inconsistency, Accumulation Error}. The PARC is constructed in a zero-shot setting using off-the-shelf LLMs, and the resulting graph has an average depth of ~6 steps and branching factor of ~1.4 across the PERL dataset of 607 annotated chains.

## Method

The framework operates in two phases. First, premises for each step s_k are identified via either an Aggregative approach (one LLM call with the full context up to s_k) or a Dyadic approach (pairwise LLM calls checking whether each prior step s_i is a valid premise for s_k); the Aggregative approach achieves >90% recall with larger models. Second, error identification is performed step-by-step under premises only: mathematical errors are checked in isolation, logical inconsistencies are evaluated against the step's premise set P_k (excluding all other context), and accumulation errors are detected via a depth-first search over the PARC dependency graph, flagging any locally correct step whose premises include an incorrect step. This premise-restricted verification removes distractor context and improves error-identification accuracy by 6–16% absolute over full-context baselines, with the largest gains on accumulation errors.

## Results

For premise identification, larger open-source LLMs achieve >90% recall (Llama 3.1 70b: 97.55% on GSM8K; Qwen 2.5 32b: 95.35%; GPT-4o: 94.42%), confirming that LLMs can reliably convert linear reasoning chains into PARCs. For error identification, restricting verification context to only the identified premises yields 6–16% absolute accuracy gains over full-context verification (e.g., Llama 3.1 70b on GSM8K: 71.37% → 81.92%; Qwen 2.5 72b: 63.11% → 80.56%). On ProcessBench, PARC-based verification improves F1 by up to +30.7 points (Qwen 2.5 32B on MATH: 35.9 → 66.5) and +12.5 points (Llama 3.1 70B on GSM8K: 66.1 → 78.6) over the standard critic baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is full-context verification, where the LLM sees the entire reasoning chain when classifying each step's error type. PARC (Model Premises) consistently outperforms this baseline across all models and datasets, with the largest gains on smaller models (Qwen 2.5 7b: +14.79 avg on GSM8K) and moderate but consistent gains on larger models (Llama 3.1 70b: +10.55 on GSM8K; GPT-4o: +11.30 on GSM8K). On ProcessBench, the paper compares against the standard critic model from Zheng et al. (2024), which identifies the first erroneous step from the full solution. PARC-based verification wins on 4 out of 5 models on both GSM8K and MATH splits, with F1 improvements of +12.3 to +12.5 on GSM8K and +7.1 to +30.7 on MATH. The one exception is Qwen 2.5 7B on GSM8K (−0.2 F1) and Qwen 2.5 72B on GSM8K (−1.5 F1), where the gains are marginal. Notably, the improvement is most pronounced on the MATH split (competition-level problems), suggesting PARC is particularly beneficial for longer, more complex reasoning chains where distractor context is more harmful.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the step-level reasoning verification line initiated by ROSCOE (Golovneva et al., 2023) and ProcessBench (Zheng et al., 2024) by reframing the verification problem from a linear sequence to a directed acyclic graph. Rather than evaluating each step against the full preceding context (as in prior reference-free methods like ReCEval and Socreval), the authors introduce Premise-Augmented Reasoning Chains (PARC) that explicitly link each step to its minimal set of prerequisite steps, then verify each step in isolation under only those premises. The error taxonomy is also extended beyond ROSCOE's categories by introducing 'accumulation errors'—steps that are locally correct but inherit upstream mistakes—addressing a gap the authors note in prior work that simply discards all steps after the first error.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The consistent 6–16% absolute improvement in error identification from restricting verification context to premises alone offers a practical, training-free technique for improving LLM self-verification in mathematical reasoning. The PERL dataset (607 annotated chains with premise and error labels) fills a gap in the literature for premise-level annotations, and the finding that synthetic negatives are systematically easier than genuine errors cautions against over-relying on perturbation-based evaluation. Open questions include whether the premise-graph approach generalizes beyond arithmetic/algebra to geometry or multi-domain reasoning, how it scales to much longer chains where premise identification recall may degrade, and whether the graph structure can be exploited for more principled (e.g., formal) verification rather than LLM prompting.
