## Problem

Learned Bloom Filters (LBFs) augment canonical Bloom filters with a single learned oracle as a pre-filter, but the oracle's accuracy exhibits diminishing returns with increasing model size, making it a bottleneck under tight space budgets. The paper asks how to combine multiple smaller oracles into an ensemble learned Bloom filter (ELBF) that minimizes overall false positive rate under a fixed memory budget, and how to optimally select which oracles to use and how to allocate backup filter sizes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251501/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pool of pre-trained oracles (random forest classifiers on tabular features, each characterized by its size in bits, false positive rate, and false negative rate) and a total space budget T in bits, and produces a binary membership answer for queried items. At design time, it outputs the selected oracle subset and the bit allocation for each backup Bloom filter. A degenerate 'dumb oracle' (zero size, FPR→0, FNR=1) is always included in the pool to allow the optimizer to skip oracles that would degrade performance.

## Method

For mutually independent oracles (ELBF), each oracle is paired with its own backup BF, and the overall FPR is the product of per-branch FPRs. The joint optimization over oracle selection and backup sizes is solved via Lagrangian relaxation, yielding a closed-form optimal backup size parameterized by an auxiliary variable c; the outer oracle-selection problem is then shown to be structurally identical to the Knapsack problem and solved with a scaling-based dynamic programming algorithm achieving (ε,δ)-optimality. For correlated oracles (ELBF++), multiple oracles may share a common backup filter, and a random forest model is trained to estimate the correlation factor Γ that couples branch FPRs; the inner optimization uses a secant-method fixed-point iteration, while the outer partition selection is handled by a greedy merging algorithm that iteratively creates, augments, and merges branches until no further FPR improvement is possible.

## Results

On the EMBER virus signature scanning task (800K files, 238 features), ELBF achieves the lowest FPR across all memory budgets (1000Kb–3000Kb) compared to canonical BF, LBF, Ada-BF, PLBF, and Fast PLBF++. The FPR advantage is most pronounced at larger memory budgets, where the canonical BF's exponential FPR decay causes it to catch up to single-oracle LBF variants, while ELBF maintains its lead by orchestrating multiple small oracles. In the ablation (Table 2), Algorithm 1 achieves FPR of 0.1053 at 1000Kb and 0.0173 at 3000Kb, outperforming the best simpler heuristic (HP) by ~15% and ~3% respectively. ELBF also incurs lower construction time overhead than Ada-BF, PLBF, and Fast PLBF++ due to its Knapsack-based search.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the canonical Bloom filter (Bloom, 1970), ELBF achieves substantially lower FPR under stringent memory budgets, but the gap narrows as memory increases because the BF's FPR decays exponentially with allocated bits; ELBF retains its advantage at all budgets thanks to the ensemble of oracles and the inclusion of a 'dumb oracle' that prevents degradation. Against LBF (Kraska et al., 2018), ELBF consistently outperforms across all memory budgets, confirming that multiple small oracles beat a single large oracle. Against Ada-BF (Dai & Shrivastava, 2020), PLBF (Vaidya et al., 2021), and Fast PLBF++ (Sato & Matsui, 2023), ELBF achieves lower FPR at every memory budget tested and also has lower construction time overhead, since these methods require multi-dimensional parameter searches while ELBF uses a Knapsack-based formulation. In the correlated-oracle setting, ELBF++ achieves the lowest FPR with less time overhead than Ada-BF and PLBF, though it is slightly slower than ELBF due to its greedy heuristic. The performance gap between ELBF and single-oracle baselines widens with larger memory budgets, as more space enables better orchestration of diverse oracles.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Learned Bloom Filter (LBF) line of work initiated by Kraska et al. (2018) and Mitzenmacher (2018), which augment canonical Bloom filters with a single learned oracle as a pre-filter. Subsequent variants (Ada-BF, PLBF, Fast PLBF++) refined how a single oracle's score distribution is exploited via partitioning or adaptive hash counts, but all retain the single-oracle architecture. The key departure here is borrowing the ensemble learning paradigm to replace one large oracle with multiple smaller ones, and formulating the resulting oracle-selection and backup-filter-sizing problem as a Knapsack optimization with a proven (ε,δ)-approximation guarantee. For correlated oracles, the ELBF++ variant introduces shared backup filters and a greedy merging heuristic, departing from the independence assumption that underpins all prior LBF analyses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical relevance is clear for memory-constrained membership-testing systems (virus scanning, URL filtering, UUID lookup), where even modest FPR reductions translate to fewer false alarms. The Knapsack-based formulation with tunable (ε,δ) optimality provides a principled, reproducible design procedure rather than ad-hoc tuning. However, the correlated-oracle case (ELBF++) relies on a random-forest surrogate for the correlation coefficient Γ and a greedy heuristic with no optimality guarantee, leaving open how well it scales to large oracle pools or highly structured correlations. An additional open question is the sensitivity to the assumption that oracle FPR/FNR are known exactly a priori, which may not hold under distribution shift in deployment.
