## Problem

Existing automated preference generation methods (e.g., D-REX, LERP) produce preference data by injecting noise into a single fixed behavior-cloning policy, resulting in poor coverage of the state-action space. This limited coverage causes learned reward models to generalize poorly and fail to derive policies that surpass suboptimal demonstrations. The paper asks: how can one automatically generate accurate preferences with broad coverage without human annotation?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20253068/cropped_page3_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a small set of suboptimal demonstration trajectories (1 for MuJoCo tasks, 10 for DMControl tasks, each at 50–80% of optimal performance) plus environment access for online rollouts during AIL training. It produces a preference dataset of trajectory-segment pairs (1,000 for MuJoCo, 5,000 for DMControl) and a neural reward model r_φ. Unlike full-trajectory preference methods, APEC uses randomly cropped segments of variable length for reward training, and the reward model is subsequently used to train RL agents (SAC or DrQ-v2) that must achieve better-than-demo returns.

## Method

APEC exploits the key observation that in adversarial imitation learning (specifically DAC), policy value increases monotonically with training iterations, formalized by Proposition 3.1 which proves the value gap bound diminishes as O(1/√k) in the iteration index k. The method collects policy checkpoints throughout the entire AIL training process, then constructs preference pairs by sampling two policies whose iteration indices differ by at least a threshold k, ensuring a provable value ordering. To handle trajectory-level stochasticity and occasional AIL training spikes, a Wasserstein distance criterion against the suboptimal demonstrations is imposed as a second filter, guaranteeing that the preferred trajectory is genuinely closer to the demonstration distribution. Finally, reward learning uses the Bradley-Terry model on randomly cropped trajectory segments (following T-REX), and the resulting reward model is evaluated by training RL agents that must surpass demonstration performance.

## Results

APEC achieves an average reward correlation of 0.72 on MuJoCo tasks (vs. 0.47 for AIRL, 0.23 for SSRR, and 0.05 for both D-REX and LERP) and 0.79 on DMControl tasks (vs. 0.62 for LERP, 0.59 for D-REX). On return correlation, APEC reaches 0.99 average on MuJoCo and 0.96 on DMControl, substantially outperforming all baselines. In policy learning evaluation, APEC is the only method that achieves better-than-demonstration performance on 7 out of 8 tasks, with preference labeling accuracy of 90% (MuJoCo) and 86% (DMControl) on unseen trajectory pairs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

APEC decisively outperforms all four baselines on reward correlation across nearly all tasks. On MuJoCo, APEC (0.72 avg) surpasses AIRL (0.47), SSRR (0.23), D-REX (0.05), and LERP (0.05) by large margins, with the gap most dramatic on Ant (0.94 vs. 0.61 for AIRL) and HalfCheetah (0.91 vs. 0.48 for AIRL). On DMControl, APEC (0.79 avg) beats LERP (0.62) and D-REX (0.59), while SSRR and AIRL produce negative or near-zero correlations. In policy learning, APEC is the only method achieving better-than-demo performance on 7/8 tasks, whereas all baselines fail to match demonstration performance under the paper's stricter setup (fewer demos, higher-quality demo range). The paper attributes baseline failures primarily to reward hacking, which APEC avoids due to its broader preference data coverage from diverse AIL policies.

## Relation to Prior Work

APEC sits at the intersection of adversarial imitation learning (AIL) and preference-based reward learning. It builds directly on the automated preference generation line (D-REX, LERP, SSRR), which injects noise into a single behavior-cloning policy or reward space, but departs fundamentally by exploiting the natural policy progression across the entire AIL training process. The theoretical grounding (Proposition 3.1) extends standard online-learning regret analyses to the AIL setting, providing a value-gap bound that decreases with iteration index, thereby justifying why policies from different training stages form valid preference pairs. The coverage analysis from Zhu et al. (2023) and Nika et al. (2024) motivates why the broader policy diversity in APEC yields better reward generalization than the narrow noise-injection distributions of prior methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical bottleneck in reward learning: existing automated preference generators produce data with insufficient coverage, leading to reward models that fail to support effective downstream policy learning. APEC's consistent better-than-demo performance across 7/8 tasks (while baselines suffer from reward hacking) demonstrates that coverage is not merely a theoretical nicety but a practical necessity. Key limitations remain: the method still requires online environment interaction for preference generation, and the authors acknowledge that reward hacking can persist in more complex tasks. Open directions include extending APEC to fully offline settings and integrating human prior knowledge to further constrain the learned reward.
