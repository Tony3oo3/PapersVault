## Problem

Estimating the low-dimensional, nonlinear, stochastic dynamics underlying neural population activity remains a major challenge in systems neuroscience. Existing inference methods impose restrictive structural assumptions (linearity, autonomy, switching linearity, or high dimensionality) that limit their ability to capture complex dynamical regimes, and most do not explicitly disentangle task-relevant from task-irrelevant components, hindering interpretability. Additionally, prominent deep learning-based methods can produce latent dynamics that are inconsistent across different training/test splits of the same dataset.

## Input / Output

FINDR takes binned population spike trains (10 ms bins) from N simultaneously recorded neurons, aligned to stimulus onset, along with a 2-dimensional external input vector encoding left/right auditory click times per bin. It produces an L-dimensional latent trajectory z_t (L ≪ N), a learned flow field μ(z,u) visualizable as a velocity vector field, per-neuron firing rates λ_t, and a separate task-irrelevant time-varying bias d_t per neuron. The task-irrelevant component is modeled as a sum of raised-cosine basis functions fitted independently per neuron for across-trial and within-trial fluctuations, while the task-relevant component is the low-dimensional latent state.

## Method

FINDR is a sequential variational autoencoder that models latent dynamics as a stochastic differential equation discretized via Euler–Maruyama, with the drift function parameterized by a gated MLP: μ(z,u) = σ(G(z,u)) ⊙ [−z + F(z,u)], where F and G are small MLPs with SiLU activations. The decoder is a linear-plus-bias map with softplus (λ_t = softplus(Cz_t + d_t)), and the variational posterior q is itself a gated neural SDE conditioned on a bidirectional GRU context e_t that summarizes the full trial. Training minimizes a β-VAE objective (β=2) balancing Poisson reconstruction of spike counts against a KL divergence that enforces the inferred trajectories to be generated by the learned flow field, using backpropagation through time with SGDR warm restarts. A key design choice is the explicit separation of task-irrelevant dynamics (d_t) via per-neuron raised-cosine basis function fits before the variational training, which encourages z_t to capture only task-relevant structure. Post-training, an SVD of the loading matrix C makes it semi-orthogonal, ensuring that Euclidean distances in latent space correspond to distances in the inverse-softplus rate space, enabling geometrically meaningful flow-field visualization.

## Results

On synthetic data with a known 2D disk attractor, FINDR's test log-likelihood saturates at L=2, and two principal components explain >99% of variance in inferred latent trajectories across models assuming L=2–6. On the rat dmFC/mPFC decision-making dataset (67 neurons, 448 trials), FINDR achieves consistently higher 5-fold cross-validated co-smoothing than all baselines for L<3, and higher median evidence-sign conditioned PSTH R² across all latent dimensions. For dynamical consistency across 5 training/test folds, FINDR achieves ⟨|r|⟩ = 0.99 (topological) and 0.94 (dynamical), revealing two stable slow points associated with left/right choices, whereas autoLFADS achieves only 0.53 and 0.22 respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FINDR outperforms SLDS and rSLDS on both co-smoothing (for L<3) and evidence-sign conditioned PSTH R² across all latent dimensions, indicating better reconstruction of held-out neuron responses with fewer latent dimensions. Against autoLFADS, FINDR achieves higher PSTH R² at low L (L=2–6), though autoLFADS with L=20 factor dimensions matches FINDR L=2 in reconstruction quality; however, autoLFADS fails to produce geometrically consistent dynamics across folds (⟨|r|⟩ = 0.53 topological, 0.22 dynamical) and does not form the expected two-cluster attractor structure when run forward for 5 s. GPFA is outperformed by FINDR on both metrics across all L. CEBRA achieves topological consistency (⟨|r|⟩ = 0.99) but does not learn an explicit dynamical model, making fixed-point and flow-field analysis inapplicable, and its latent trajectories depend on evidence strength in a way that obscures the underlying dynamics.

## Relation to Prior Work

FINDR sits at the intersection of sequential variational autoencoders for neural data (Chung et al. 2015; Krishnan et al. 2017) and the gated neural ODE/SDE architecture introduced by Kim et al. (2023), which provides a more expressive and trainable dynamics model than the linear or switching-linear assumptions in GPFA, SLDS, rSLDS, and LFADS. The key conceptual departure is the explicit separation of task-relevant latent dynamics (z_t) from task-irrelevant time-varying biases (d_t) via raised-cosine basis functions, inspired by β-VAE's disentanglement principle, which prior dynamics-inference methods do not perform. The variational inference framework with a bidirectional GRU encoder and a gated MLP prior/posterior pair follows the structured inference network paradigm, but the gated MLP dynamics model is what enables FINDR to capture nonlinear, input-dependent attractor geometries that linear or piecewise-linear models cannot represent.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FINDR provides a practical tool for visualizing and testing attractor-based theories of neural computation by producing interpretable, low-dimensional flow fields with identifiable slow points, bridging the gap between neuronal-level spike data and algorithmic-level computational models. The finding that FINDR achieves geometric (not merely topological) consistency across training splits—while autoLFADS does not—raises important questions about whether existing deep learning-based dynamics models are learning stable dynamical structure or dataset-specific artifacts. Limitations include validation on a single real dataset (rat dmFC/mPFC decision-making), no theoretical guarantee of consistency, and dependence on large neuron/trial counts; future work on uncertainty quantification, online learning, and application to motor cortex or visual cortex would strengthen the generalizability claims.
