## Problem

While performative risk minimization (PRM) preserves model accuracy under distribution shifts caused by performativity, its broader impact on predictions and the underlying data distribution remains unexplored. This paper initiates that analysis by asking: does PRM introduce systematic bias in predictions and non-zero shifts in the data distribution compared to standard risk minimization, and under what conditions do these effects amplify?

## Input / Output

The model operates on binary random variables z ∈ {-1/2, 1/2} with mean p_t, governed by a linear performative response p_{t+1} = αθ_t + (1-|α|)s_{t+1} where α controls feedback strength/direction, λ controls distributional friction, and π is the equilibrium mean. The method produces a scalar prediction θ_t ∈ [-1/2, 1/2] along with two impact measures: bias (expected deviation of prediction from the true test-time mean) and mean shift (deviation of the distribution mean from its non-performative counterpart). The binary variable is shifted by 1/2 for algebraic convenience, and the analysis covers both slow deployment (test on D_{t+1}) and rapid deployment (test on D_t) regimes.

## Method

The paper formulates a sequential performative mean estimation problem with MSE loss and derives closed-form PRM solutions via KKT conditions for infinite-horizon problems, yielding eigenvalue-based recurrence relations for the optimal prediction path. The key insight is that the MSE decomposes into a standard error term plus an aleatoric uncertainty term (1/4 - (p_test)²), so PRM incentivizes the model provider to trade prediction accuracy for reduced distributional uncertainty, producing systematically biased predictions and non-zero mean shifts. The analysis covers one-period and infinite-horizon settings under both slow and rapid deployment, comparing PRM to a naive path that ignores performativity. For partial information, the paper provides performative-aware statistical estimators with finite-sample loss bounds and validates results via episodic RL simulations using likelihood-based parameter learning.

## Results

The paper derives explicit closed-form expressions for the PRM solution, bias, and mean shift in both one-period and infinite-horizon settings. In the one-period slow deployment case with positive feedback (α > 0), the PRM bias is α(1−|α|)s₁/(1−2α) and the mean shift is (α−|α|+α|α|)s₁/(1−2α), both increasing in |α|. In the infinite-horizon slow case, the long-term bias θ*∞ − p*∞ = α(1−|α|−β)π/(1−2α−β+αβ−γβ(1−α−β)) is non-vanishing, confirming that PRM perpetually trades MSE for reduced aleatoric uncertainty. RL simulations (Fig. 4) validate that learned policies converge to the theoretically predicted equilibrium values after a short exploration phase.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between the PRM path and a naive path (standard ERM that ignores performativity, θ_t^n = p_{t−1}^test). In the one-period equilibrium case with positive feedback (α > 0), the naive path achieves zero bias and zero shift, while PRM introduces non-zero bias and shift; however, PRM's loss is lower by α²/(1−2α)·s₁². In the negative feedback case (α < 0), the roles reverse: PRM has strictly smaller bias and shift than the naive path (the ratio |bias*|/|bias^n| < 1), while the naive path's loss penalty grows to 9α²/(1−2α)·s₁². In the infinite-horizon setting, the naive path's long-term bias always converges to zero regardless of α, whereas PRM retains a non-vanishing bias; for α < 0, the long-term shift ratio (p*∞−π)/(p^n∞−π) < 1, confirming PRM is less disruptive to the distribution. Overall, PRM can have amplified side effects (larger bias, larger mean shift toward extremes) compared to standard RM in positive-feedback or high-performativity rapid-deployment scenarios.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the performative prediction framework of Perdomo et al. (2020) and the stateful/sequential extensions of Brown et al. (2022) and Ray et al. (2022a) by shifting the analytical focus from finding performatively optimal models to studying the downstream impact of PRM on the data distribution and predictions. While prior work in performative prediction has focused on loss minimization, stability, and (in Jin et al., 2024) fairness/polarization, this paper is the first to formalize and analyze impact metrics—bias and mean shift—under PRM. The authors isolate performativity effects from exploration by first solving the full-information case with explicit closed-form solutions, then extending to partial information via statistical estimators and RL simulations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that PRM can produce *more* biased predictions and *larger* distributional shifts than naive non-performative prediction (in positive feedback settings) challenges the implicit assumption that modeling performativity is always preferable. This opens important questions about whether performative accuracy should be the sole objective or whether impact-aware regularization is needed. However, the analysis is restricted to binary variables with linear response, limiting direct applicability to richer real-world settings; the authors note the linear model is a first-order approximation and the error-uncertainty decomposition generalizes, but full generality remains open.
