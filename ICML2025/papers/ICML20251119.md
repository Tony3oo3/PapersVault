## Problem

Existing privacy research on MLLMs has focused on sensitive data that is inherently relevant to training objectives, where parameter updates naturally encourage information retention. It remains unclear whether MLLMs can inadvertently memorize private content that is entirely irrelevant to the downstream task. This paper investigates whether randomly generated task-irrelevant watermarks embedded in VQA fine-tuning images can be spuriously encoded by MLLMs through mini-batch training dynamics.

## Input / Output

The method takes standard VQA image-text pairs and embeds synthetic task-irrelevant watermarks (GPT-4-generated username and user_id pairs) into the image region at a configurable probability r. During probing, only the username is visible in the image while the corresponding user_id is deliberately withheld, enabling a two-tiered evaluation of direct recall versus multi-hop association. Probing classifiers operate on final-token hidden states extracted from every transformer layer, producing per-layer binary accuracy scores for distinguishing seen (U1) from unseen (U2) watermarks, alongside gradient cosine similarity scores comparing privacy-embedded versus original training batches.

## Method

The paper formalizes how mini-batch training induces spurious correlations: even when task-irrelevant content u is statistically independent of the target y, the sample covariance Cov(y,u) within a small batch B can exhibit significant non-zero entries with non-negligible probability, proven via Chebyshev's inequality bounding the variance of the sample covariance. To detect this memorization, the authors propose a layer-wise probing framework where a linear classifier is trained on each layer's final-token representation to distinguish watermarks from the fine-tuning set U1 from those in the held-out set U2. Gradient cosine similarity analysis between privacy-embedded and original training batches confirms that MLLMs expend non-trivial parameter updates encoding the irrelevant content, with effects comparable to standard image transformations. Key findings show that memorization emerges from lower layers and strengthens with embedding rate, yet direct prompting and standard MIA methods (LOSS, Zlib Entropy, Min-k% Prob) fail to extract the memorized content, indicating the information is encoded in non-trivial internal representations rather than surface-level outputs.

## Results

Embedding task-irrelevant private content into VQA fine-tuning images has negligible impact on downstream performance (e.g., LLaVA-1.5 on COCO ScienceQA drops only 1.1 points from 70.0 to 68.9 at r=0.5). However, gradient cosine similarity between original and privacy-embedded batches drops substantially (e.g., 98.3→92.9 for LLaVA-1.5 on COCO; 100.0→97.0 for Qwen-VL), approaching the effect of image-level transformations (85.3 for LLaVA) and far exceeding random noise. Layer-wise probing reveals that fine-tuned MLLMs achieve >90% accuracy distinguishing seen from unseen watermarks in middle-to-upper layers, even for multi-hop user_id inference where direct prompting yields 0% accuracy. MIA (LOSS, Zlib Entropy, Min-k% Prob) fails to detect the memorization, with AUC ROC changing by less than 1 point (e.g., 50.7→49.9 for LOSS).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new method to compare against competing approaches; instead, it benchmarks the effect of task-irrelevant privacy embedding against three controlled baselines: (1) Origin vs. Origin (random noise floor), (2) Origin vs. ImageTransf. (image-level augmentation), and (3) Origin vs. TextTransf. (GPT-4 rephrasing). The w/Privacy condition consistently produces gradient shifts comparable to image transformations (e.g., 92.9 vs. 85.3 on COCO for LLaVA) but far smaller than text transformations (5.3), indicating MLLMs encode visual watermarks with moderate strength. For MIA, all three attack methods (LOSS, Zlib Entropy, Min-k% Prob) show negligible AUC ROC changes before vs. after fine-tuning (≤1 point), confirming that standard membership inference cannot detect this type of weak, task-irrelevant memorization. Batch-size ablation shows that reducing batch size from 8 to 1 lowers gradient similarity by 5–10 points (e.g., TextVQA: 84.6→78.4 for Qwen-VL), confirming that spurious correlations are amplified in smaller mini-batches.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant line of MLLM privacy research (Carlini et al. 2021; Pinto et al. 2024; Ko et al. 2023; Li et al. 2024), which has exclusively examined task-relevant private content whose retention is naturally encouraged by training objectives. By introducing the notion of task-irrelevant private content—randomly generated watermarks with zero global correlation to VQA outputs—the authors carve a new problem space grounded in the well-known phenomenon of spurious correlations in mini-batch SGD. Methodologically, the probing framework repurposes linear-probe interpretability tools (Hewitt & Liang 2019; Ju et al. 2024) in a privacy-detection context, while the gradient-similarity analysis draws on standard training-dynamics diagnostics. The theoretical appendix formalizes the spurious-correlation mechanism via sample covariance concentration, providing a mathematical justification that prior empirical privacy studies lacked.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals a previously unrecognized privacy dimension: MLLMs encode task-irrelevant data through batch-level spurious correlations, with larger models (13B) and smaller batch sizes amplifying the effect. A key open question is extractability—standard MIAs (LOSS, Zlib Entropy, Min-k% Prob) fail to recover the memorized content (AUC ≈ 50%), yet layer-wise probing achieves >90% accuracy, suggesting the information is present but not trivially accessible. This gap between internal representation and output behavior has direct implications for MLLM-based autonomous agents that process diverse user data. Future work must develop stronger extraction methods, quantify safe batch-size thresholds, and investigate whether the encoded associations can be triggered under adversarial prompting or compositional queries.
