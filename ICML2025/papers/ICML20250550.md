## Problem

Q-learning suffers from overestimation bias in the max-operator, and existing mitigations (Double Q-learning, ensembles, clipping) apply a fixed correction globally without accounting for the fact that the need for bias reduction varies across state-action pairs. The paper identifies that distributional RL naturally provides the agent with per-state-action sample variance estimates that correlate with the magnitude of overestimation, but no prior method exploited this information to locally switch between overestimating and underestimating update rules.

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard state observations (stacked RGB frames for Atari, low-dimensional state vectors for MuJoCo and tabular environments) and outputs full return distributions—either categorical (51 fixed atoms with learned probability weights) or quantile (m learned atom locations with uniform weights)—from which Q-values and greedy actions are derived. Unusually, it maintains two independently initialized online networks (A and B) plus their delayed target counterparts, and at each update computes the relative sample variance S²_rel(s,a) = S²_{s,a}/S²_s across all actions in state s to determine the interpolation weight β ∈ [0,1].

## Method

ADDQ locally interpolates between Q-learning and Double Q-learning by multiplying the DQL bias term with an adaptive weight β that depends on the relative sample variance of the estimated return distributions at the next state. The key theoretical insight (Propositions 2.1–2.2) is that for Gaussian rewards the overestimation bias scales as σ√(log k)/√N while the sample variance of the distributional estimate follows a (σ²/(N−1))χ²_{N−1} distribution, giving the agent an unbiased, directly computable proxy for the uncertainty driving overestimation. Concretely, β is set to 0.75, 0.5, or 0.25 depending on whether S²_rel(s,a) falls below 0.75, in [0.75, 1.25], or above 1.25, so that actions with disproportionately high variance receive stronger DQL correction. The convergence proof (Theorem 3.1) extends the SARSA-trick decomposition of Singh et al. (2000) to the distributional categorical setting, showing that any symmetric β-sequence with β^A − β^B → 0 yields almost-sure convergence to Q*. The method integrates into existing C51 and QRDQN implementations with only a few lines of code modifying the target distribution.

## Results

On 10 Atari environments (categorical C51 and quantile QRDQN parametrizations, 10 seeds, 20M steps), ADDQ is more stable than both distributional DQN and double DQN (it never fails completely) and achieves higher scores across RLiable probability-of-improvement and human-normalized-score metrics. On the GridWorld environment, ADDQ substantially reduces estimation bias compared to QL and DQL, particularly in high-stochasticity states (1, 4, 6, 7). On 5 MuJoCo environments (quantile SAC, 10 seeds, 10M steps), ADDQ improves over single-critic QRSAC and double QRSAC but does not match the performance of the clipped double critic (QB-SAC/TD3), which the authors note is expected given the severity of overestimation in continuous control.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against C51 (Bellemare et al., 2017) and QRDQN (Dabney et al., 2018), ADDQ consistently achieves higher RLiable probability of improvement and human-normalized scores across all 10 Atari environments, with the advantage most visible in environments where overestimation is most harmful. Against Double C51/Double QRDQN (van Hasselt et al., 2015), ADDQ is more stable (never fails completely) and achieves higher scores, confirming that the locally adaptive weighting outperforms the fixed double-estimator update. Against Clipped C51/QRDQN (Fujimoto et al., 2018, TD3 clipping), ADDQ is competitive on Atari but clearly inferior on MuJoCo, where the clipping trick provides stronger global overestimation control. In the GridWorld comparison (Appendix C.3), ADDQ reduces estimation bias more strongly than Maxmin (Lan et al., 2020) with 2–8 ensembles, EBQL (Peer et al., 2021) with 3–15 ensembles, and REDQ (Chen et al., 2021) with 3–5 ensembles, while using only two Q-estimators. Against QB-SAC (Kuznetsov et al., 2020) on MuJoCo, ADDQ does not close the gap, and the authors explicitly frame the MuJoCo results as a completeness check rather than a competitive claim.

## Relation to Prior Work

ADDQ sits at the intersection of two well-developed lines of work: overestimation reduction in Q-learning (Double Q-learning, clipped Q-learning, ensemble methods like Maxmin and REDQ) and distributional reinforcement learning (C51, QRDQN). The key conceptual departure is using the return distribution's sample variance—information naturally available in DRL but previously exploited only for exploration—as a local signal to adaptively interpolate between QL and DQL updates, rather than applying a fixed or globally uniform correction. The convergence proof extends the SARSA-trick bias decomposition of Singh et al. (2000) and the categorical DRL analysis of Rowland et al. (2018) to accommodate state-dependent mixing weights, while the theoretical lower bound on overestimation builds on Sudakov-Fernique comparison inequalities for Gaussian maxima.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical appeal is that ADDQ requires only a few lines of code added to existing DRL implementations and worked without hyperparameter tuning across tabular, Atari, and MuJoCo settings, making it an accessible drop-in improvement over standard DQL. However, the method does not surpass clipping-based approaches (TD3/QB-SAC) in continuous control, and the authors explicitly note that integrating the local variance signal into more sophisticated algorithms like REDQ or TQC remains open. The theoretical contribution—quantifying overestimation as Θ(σ√(k log k)/√N) and showing the sample variance follows a chi-squared distribution independent of the max operation—provides a clean probabilistic justification that could inform future adaptive bias-correction methods beyond the specific QL/DQL combination studied here.
