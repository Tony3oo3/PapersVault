## Problem

Despite compositionality being considered fundamental to intelligence and central to out-of-distribution generalization in AI, no satisfying formal, quantitative definition exists. The standard colloquial definition ('the meaning of a complex expression is determined by its structure and the meanings of its constituents') is binary, presupposes externally-defined symbolic expressions, places no restriction on the mapping function, and cannot account for the degree of compositionality observed in real systems like human language.

## Input / Output

The method takes a representation matrix Z ∈ R^{N×D} (e.g., layer activations from a DNN or brain region) and, for the language-system variant C^L(Z), additionally requires externally-defined sentence sequences W^L. The output is a scalar compositionality score: C(Z) = K(Z)/K(Z|W) for intrinsic compositionality, or C^L(Z) = K(Z)/K(Z|W^L) when the symbolic mapping is fixed. Crucially, the constituent parts W and semantics f are not free parameters but are determined by the joint minimization that finds the shortest compressing program, making them intrinsic to the representation itself.

## Method

The paper defines representational compositionality as a compression ratio grounded in Kolmogorov complexity: C(Z) = K(Z) / [K(f) + K(Z|W,f)], where the numerator captures the expressivity of the representation and the denominator captures how simply it can be reconstructed from discrete constituent parts via a semantics function f. The total complexity K(Z) decomposes as K(p_w) + K(W|p_w) + K(f) + K(Z|W,f), where p_w is a language over discrete token sequences, W are the sentences describing each representation row, and f maps sentences to sufficient statistics of a Normal distribution over R^D. For the language-system variant C^L(Z), W^L is externally given and only f^L is optimized. In practice, K(Z|W^L) is estimated via prequential coding (incremental encoding with a DNN predictor), while C(Z) could be estimated by training a discrete auto-encoder with a learned autoregressive prior in latent space, though the authors leave full implementation of C(Z) to future work.

## Results

On synthetic lookup-table representations, C(Z) matches intuitions across all four varied properties (sentence length, vocabulary size, dimensionality, disentanglement), while topological similarity violates intuitions in three of four cases (e.g., topological similarity *increases* with representation dimensionality and *decreases* with sentence length). On emergent multi-agent languages, C^L(Z) = 1 (the theoretical minimum) for agents trained without iterated learning, confirming entirely arbitrary sentence-to-object mappings, while iterated learning yields measurably higher scores. On natural languages (COCO captions in English, French, Spanish, German, Japanese), C^L(Z) shows all five languages have roughly equal relative compositionality (German normalized to 1, Japanese slightly higher), supporting the equal-compositionality hypothesis in linguistics, whereas topological similarity gives near-zero values for most languages with Japanese as a strong negative outlier (−0.2).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline comparison is against topological similarity (Pearson correlation between pairwise distance matrices in sentence-space and representation-space), the heuristic metric most commonly used in the compositionality literature. On synthetic lookup tables, C(Z) and topological similarity agree on the disentanglement trend but diverge on sentence length (C(Z) increases, topological similarity decreases), vocabulary size (C(Z) shows a peak, topological similarity is monotonically decreasing and maximal at vocabulary size 1), and representation dimensionality (C(Z) decreases, topological similarity increases). On synthetic context-free grammars, C(Z) cleanly decreases with grammar width and depth, while topological similarity is noisy and only loosely correlated with intuition. On emergent languages, both metrics agree that iterated learning produces more compositional systems, but C^L(Z) additionally provides an absolute, interpretable scale (C^L(Z)=1 means fully arbitrary mapping) that topological similarity cannot offer. On natural languages, C^L(Z) supports the equal-compositionality hypothesis, while topological similarity produces counter-intuitive near-zero values for most languages and a strong negative outlier for Japanese.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on Kolmogorov complexity (Li et al., 2008) as its theoretical foundation and prequential coding (Blier & Ollivier, 2018) as a practical estimation tool, but departs sharply from prior formalizations of compositionality. Reconstruction-based approaches (Andreas, 2019; Trager et al., 2023; Lewis et al., 2022) measure compositionality via error from externally-defined parts, while topological similarity serves as a heuristic proxy; this paper instead defines compositionality as a ratio of Kolmogorov complexity terms where the constituent parts are discovered intrinsically through optimal compression of the representation. It also extends Kirby's iterated learning framework from cognitive science (Kirby, 1999; Kirby et al., 2004, 2008) by formalizing the competing pressures for expressivity and compression that drive compositional language evolution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first rigorous, quantitative, and general definition of representational compositionality grounded in algorithmic information theory, unifying disparate intuitions from cognitive science (Language of Thought, systematicity, structure-preserving maps) and AI (modularity, compositional generalization) under a single formal framework. However, the core definition C(Z) remains intractable and the authors only demonstrate the simpler C^L(Z) variant (with externally fixed sentences), leaving the full estimation pipeline for C(Z) as an open problem sensitive to modeling choices. Key open questions include whether the definition generalizes to continuous parts, how it empirically relates to compositional generalization performance, and whether it can guide the design of models with principled inductive biases for compositionality.
