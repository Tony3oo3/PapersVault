## Problem

The standard interpolation paradigm assumes overparametrized networks converge to global minima of the training loss, yet empirical evidence from in-context learning and diffusion models shows that beyond a certain data size, trained models instead converge to spurious local minima that generalize better. This paper provides the first theoretical characterization of this 'optimization threshold' in a concrete setting, explaining why and when overparametrized networks fail to interpolate and how this failure yields a beneficial simplicity bias.

## Input / Output

The method takes n i.i.d. samples (x_k, y_k) ∈ R^{d+1} from a linear model y_k = x_k^⊤β* + η_k, a two-layer ReLU network of width m with small initialization scale λ, and trains via gradient flow (or SGD in experiments). The output is the converged regression function h_θ(x), which for n ≳ d³ log d takes the form of a difference of two ReLU neurons whose directions match the OLS estimators on the positive and negative activation subsets of the data. The key regime constraint is that n must exceed the optimization threshold (scaling as d³ log d) while m can be arbitrarily large, and the initialization must be sufficiently small (λ ≤ Θ(1/d)).

## Method

The analysis proceeds in two main parts. First, the paper proves uniform concentration bounds (Theorem 3.1) showing that the empirical gradient information D_n(w) converges to its expectation D(w) at rate √(d log n / n) across all directions, and that the extremal vectors driving the early alignment phase concentrate toward a small set of directions determined by the true loss (Proposition 3.1). Second, under a linear data model with symmetric inputs, the paper tracks the full training dynamics in four phases: (i) early alignment where all neurons align toward the two extremal directions ±Σβ*, (ii) slow growth, (iii) fast growth where positive and negative neurons decouple and grow toward the OLS estimators on their respective data subsets S_+ and S_−, and (iv) final convergence to a spurious local minimum. The key insight is that the early alignment quantizes the network into a small effective width, preventing the omnidirectionality needed for interpolation, so the network converges to the OLS estimator (excess risk O(d/n)) rather than fitting the noise.

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 4.1) shows that for n ≳ d³ log d training samples, an overparametrized two-layer ReLU network (any width m) trained with gradient flow and small initialization converges to the OLS estimator rather than interpolating the training data, regardless of network width. Empirically, with d=5 and m=10,000, the optimization threshold is located around n* ≈ 3,000: below this, the network interpolates and test loss is well above the noise level σ²=0.09 (tempered overfitting); above it, test loss approaches σ². At n=5,000, 91% of neurons have cosine similarity > 0.9 with the OLS estimator, versus only 31% at n=500. For d=10, the threshold shifts to n ≈ 10,000 (interpolation stops) and n ≈ 80,000 (OLS-like behavior), roughly consistent with the predicted d³ scaling.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing methods in the traditional sense; instead, it characterizes when and why the network's learned solution diverges from the OLS estimator (the natural statistical baseline). Below the optimization threshold (n < 3,000 for d=5), the network interpolates and its test loss is substantially worse than OLS, confirming the tempered overfitting regime. Above the threshold, the network's test loss matches OLS within a small margin (train loss ≈ 0.082 vs. OLS train loss slightly lower), with the small gap attributed to a few unaligned neurons. The behavior is robust to activation choice (GeLU shows similar or even earlier threshold) and optimizer (Adam vs. SGD yields comparable results). For a more complex 5-ReLU teacher, the same qualitative transition occurs but requires more samples (n* ≈ 17,000 for full OLS-like behavior vs. 3,000 for the linear teacher), suggesting the threshold scales with teacher complexity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends Boursier and Flammarion (2024), who showed that early alignment in two-layer ReLU networks can prevent interpolation despite overparametrization, by providing a full characterization of the training dynamics beyond the initial alignment phase and proving a concrete optimization threshold. It also builds on the gradient flow analysis framework of Boursier et al. (2022) for shallow ReLU networks. Conceptually, it departs from the standard simplicity bias narrative (Arpit et al., 2017; Kalimeris et al., 2019) where networks learn simple features first and then interpolate noise; here, the interpolating phase never begins once n exceeds the threshold, so the network converges to a spurious local minimum that approximates OLS rather than the global minimum. It provides a complementary theoretical explanation to Qiao et al. (2024), who attributed non-interpolation to instability of global minima under large stepsizes, by showing the mechanism is the loss of omnidirectionality due to early alignment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical grounding for an empirically observed phenomenon in diffusion models (Yoon et al., 2023; Kadkhodaie et al., 2024) and in-context learning (Raventós et al., 2024) where trained models transition from interpolation to generalization as training data grows, suggesting that 'failure' to reach the global minimum can be beneficial for test performance. Key limitations include the very specific setting (linear teacher, symmetric data, two-layer ReLU, restrictive initialization), the large d³ log d threshold, and the fact that the mechanism (early alignment) has not been verified as the actual cause in complex architectures. Open questions include whether the threshold scaling changes for deeper networks or attention-based architectures, whether the result extends to non-linear teachers, and how the effective width of the network is maintained in the underparametrized regime (n ≲ d).
