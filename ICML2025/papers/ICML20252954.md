## Problem

Offline multi-objective RL methods learn preference-conditioned policies from pre-collected datasets, but real-world datasets are often conservatively or narrowly distributed, leaving out-of-distribution (OOD) preference regions uncovered. Existing offline MORL algorithms (e.g., MORvS, MODT) exhibit poor generalization to these OOD preferences, producing policies that are misaligned with the requested preference and leaving significant gaps in the approximate Pareto front. The paper tackles both the expressiveness gap (modeling diverse preferences with a single general policy) and the generalization gap (extending to unseen preference regions).

## Input / Output

At deployment, MODULI receives the current state s_t and a target preference vector ω (2-dim or 3-dim, depending on the environment) and produces an action a_t to execute. Internally, it generates a state trajectory of horizon H (32 for Hopper/Walker2d, 4 for other MuJoCo tasks) conditioned on ω and a normalized return-to-go vector, then applies a 3-layer MLP inverse dynamics model to extract the action from consecutive generated states. The initial state of the generated trajectory is fixed to the observed s_t to ensure consistency, and the planning horizon is environment-specific to capture behavioral preferences.

## Method

MODULI reframes offline MORL as conditional generative planning: a DiT1d-based diffusion model (11M params, 4 blocks, 128-dim embeddings) is trained via score matching to denoise state trajectories conditioned on preference ω and normalized RTG g, using classifier-free guidance (w=1.5) with a 0.1 masking probability for the unconditional branch. Two multi-objective return normalization methods—Preference Predicted Normalization (training an MLP to predict per-preference maximum RTG) and Neighborhood Preference Normalization (min-max scaling within an ε-neighborhood of similar preferences)—replace naive global normalization to ensure the guidance target 1^n corresponds to genuinely high-quality trajectories under each preference. The key novelty for OOD generalization is a sliding guidance mechanism: a plug-and-play slider adapter (same DiT architecture) is trained to capture the unit-preference-shift direction in noise-prediction space, and during inference the denoising process gradually interpolates the preference from the nearest in-distribution ω_ID to the target OOD ω by adding Δω·ε_θ* at each DDIM step (10 steps, temperature 0.5), actively shifting the trajectory distribution rather than merely memorizing the dataset.

## Results

On the D4MORL Complete-Expert benchmark, MODULI achieves the best Hypervolume (HV) across all 6 environments (e.g., 6.39×10⁶ on Ant, 2.09×10⁷ on Hopper, 5.20×10⁶ on Walker2d) and the lowest Sparsity (SP) in 5 out of 6 environments (e.g., 0.07×10⁴ on HalfCheetah, 0.09×10⁵ on Hopper, 0.11×10⁴ on Walker2d), matching or exceeding the behavioral policy. On the Shattered and Narrow OOD-preference datasets, MODULI achieves substantially lower Return Deviation (RD) than all baselines—for example, 0.22×10³ vs. 1.10×10³ (MORvS) and 1.42×10³ (MODT) on Hopper-Expert-Shattered—demonstrating superior generalization to unseen preferences. The sliding guidance ablation confirms that the slider adapter further improves HV and RD on Narrow datasets without degrading SP.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MODULI outperforms all four baselines on HV in 5 out of 6 Expert environments, with the largest margins over BC(P) (e.g., 6.39 vs. 4.88 on Ant, 2.09 vs. 1.23 on Hopper) and CQL(P) (e.g., 5.20 vs. 3.21 on Walker2d, 3.57 vs. 0.78 on Hopper-3obj). On SP, MODULI achieves dramatically denser Pareto front approximations—often 10–100× lower than baselines (e.g., 0.09×10⁵ vs. 16.30×10⁵ for MODT on Hopper; 0.11×10⁴ vs. 75.60×10⁴ for BC on Walker2d). MORvS(P) is the strongest baseline, achieving competitive HV on Ant (6.36) and HalfCheetah (5.78), but MODULI still matches or exceeds it while producing far denser solutions. On the OOD generalization task (Shattered/Narrow datasets), MODULI's advantage is most pronounced on the RD metric: on Hopper-Expert-Shattered, MODULI achieves RD of 0.22×10³ versus 1.10×10³ (MORvS) and 1.42×10³ (MODT), indicating it produces solutions much closer to the predicted optimal returns in unseen preference regions where baselines show significant gaps in the Pareto front.

## Relation to Prior Work

MODULI extends the diffusion-based planning paradigm (Diffuser, Decision Diffuser) to the offline multi-objective RL setting, where prior work (MORvS, MODT, MOCQL) relied on sequence modeling or Q-learning architectures with limited expressiveness for preference-conditioned policies. The sliding guidance mechanism draws on concept sliders (Gandikota et al., 2024) and compositional energy-based generation (Du et al., 2020), repurposing the idea of learning a latent direction of attribute change as a lightweight adapter that shifts the diffusion model's target distribution from in-distribution to out-of-distribution preferences. The preference-return normalization methods (Neighborhood Preference Normalization, Preference Predicted Normalization) address a gap in directly extending single-objective RTG normalization to multi-objective settings, where conflicting objectives make a uniform max-return target unachievable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically important gap: real-world offline datasets rarely cover the full preference space, and existing offline MORL methods fail to generalize to missing preference regions. The sliding guidance adapter is a lightweight, plug-and-play mechanism that could potentially transfer to other conditional diffusion generation tasks requiring OOD extrapolation. However, the evaluation is confined to low-dimensional MuJoCo state spaces with continuous actions, and the authors acknowledge that extending to image-based multi-objective tasks, nonlinear preference spaces, and integration with evolutionary RL remain open. The finding that a simple learning-free neighborhood normalization outperforms a neural-network-based predictor on low-quality datasets is a useful practical insight for practitioners working with imperfect offline data.
