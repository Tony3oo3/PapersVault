## Problem

Hardt et al. (2023) established that collectives can influence learning platforms through coordinated data modification, but their strategies depend on population-level parameters unknown to the collective and are not always practically implementable. Collectives need a priori guarantees of success before altering their data, since modification carries risk. This paper introduces a statistical inference framework that lets collectives compute success lower bounds and optimal strategies using only their own pooled data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250732/cropped_page6_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The collective consumes only its own pooled dataset D^(n) (not the full population), a transformation g defining the signal set X̃, a target label y*, the platform size N, test set size N_test, a confidence level δ, and a classifier suboptimality parameter ε (total variation distance). It outputs a fully computable lower bound on success Ŝ(n) expressed as an empirical probability over the signal set of per-feature conditions being satisfied, plus a concrete data modification strategy h. For signal unplanting and erasing, the collective additionally outputs per-feature optimal label estimates derived from a designated sub-collective of size n_e < n.

## Method

The framework applies Hoeffding's concentration inequality with union bounds over the signal set to convert population-level classifier conditions into finite-sample, data-computable inequalities, yielding lower bounds on collective success for three objectives: signal planting (flood with (g(x), y*) pairs), signal unplanting (estimate the best non-y* label per feature using a sub-collective, then flood with those labels), and signal erasing (retain feature x but relabel to the most probable label for g(x)). The key structural insight is that the bound decomposes into per-feature 'resistance' terms—each feature in the signal set has a threshold collective size at which it is 'cracked'—producing staircase-shaped success curves rather than the smooth sigmoids of prior data-poisoning bounds. The ε-suboptimality parameter (TV distance between the empirical distribution and the classifier's effective distribution) controls how much the platform can exploit feature interactions beyond simple frequency counts. In the infinite-data limit (n, N, N_test → ∞ with n/N → α), the bounds reduce to and strictly improve upon those of Hardt et al. (2023), and the staircase structure disappears, recovering smooth curves.

## Results

On a synthetic vehicle-evaluation dataset (N=1,000,000 training, N_test=100,000, δ=0.05, ε=0), the theoretical lower bound for signal planting (Theorem 3.3) indicates approximately 10% of total agents are needed for significant influence, while the true test-time success shows just under 5% is sufficient (except for y*=Excellent, which is already the dominant label). For signal unplanting, the adaptive strategy with n_e=2,000 yields a lower bound suggesting ~10% collective size, whereas actual success is achieved at only ~3%. In the infinite data regime, the paper's signal planting bounds are strictly tighter than those of Hardt et al. (2023), and uniquely capture the staircase-shaped dependence on collective size for discrete signal sets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is Hardt et al. (2023), which provides population-level (infinite-data) lower bounds on collective success for signal planting and erasing. In the infinite data regime (n, N, N_test → ∞ with n/N → α), the paper's bounds reduce to and strictly dominate those of Hardt et al. (2023): specifically, the paper's bound (27) is a per-feature condition that is implied by but sharper than the aggregate bound in Hardt et al. (2023), as shown in Figure 4 and Appendix F. Additionally, the paper's finite-sample framework reveals a staircase structure in the success curves for discrete signal sets—a phenomenon absent from the smooth sigmoid bounds of Hardt et al. (2023). The paper also introduces signal unplanting as a new objective not covered by Hardt et al. (2023), for which it provides both a naive strategy (reducing to signal planting with a different target label) and an adaptive strategy that empirically outperforms all naive label-planting alternatives on the synthetic dataset.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the algorithmic collective action framework of Hardt et al. (2023) from the infinite-data (population-level) regime to a finite-sample setting where the collective must rely on statistical inference over its own pooled data. While Hardt et al. derive lower bounds on collective success that depend on unknown distributional parameters, this work makes those bounds fully computable by the collective using Hoeffding's concentration inequalities, and introduces a third objective—signal unplanting—beyond the original signal planting and erasing. The paper also shows that its bounds are strictly tighter than Hardt et al.'s even in the infinite-data limit, and reveals a staircase structure in the success curves for discrete signal sets that the prior work does not capture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework gives collectives a practical, a priori tool to assess their influence before committing to data modification, which is critical when members face real-world risks. The finding that absolute collective size (not just relative size n/N) determines the quality of statistical estimates implies that larger platforms are paradoxically more vulnerable to coordinated action—a non-obvious implication with governance relevance. Key limitations include the assumption of a finite feature space, i.i.d. population (collectives are likely heterogeneous), and the use of Hoeffding's inequality, which is known to be loose; extending to regression, infinite spaces, and heterogeneous populations are identified as important open directions.
