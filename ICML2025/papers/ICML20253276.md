## Problem

Despite AI for code becoming a notable success within generative AI (e.g., SWE-bench scores rising from 2% to 73%), the field lacks a unified framework for understanding what tasks remain, what fundamental bottlenecks persist, and what research directions are needed to reach high levels of software engineering automation. The paper argues against the industry view that simply scaling models and data is sufficient, identifying that today's models still struggle with larger scopes, higher logical complexity, and effective human collaboration.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20253276/cropped_page27_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The paper takes as its analytical input the full landscape of AI for SWE tasks—spanning code generation, refactoring, migration, testing, program analysis, documentation, scaffolding, and formal verification—and organizes them along three orthogonal axes: scope (function-level, self-contained unit, project-level), logical complexity (low, medium, high), and level of human intervention (low, medium, high autonomy). Its output is a structured taxonomy, a four-part challenge analysis (data, scale, interaction, measurement), and a three-part research roadmap (data collection, training, inference-time approaches) with specific open problems and community calls to action.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is an analytical framework rather than a technical system. It first defines a three-dimensional task taxonomy (scope × logical complexity × human intervention) to systematically categorize SWE tasks beyond the dominant code-generation focus. It then identifies four cross-cutting challenges: (1) data gaps in low-resource languages, OOD domains, and version-sensitive codebases; (2) scale limitations in long-context reasoning, RAG retrieval for semantically similar but syntactically different code, and long-horizon planning; (3) interaction failures including vague specifications, unaddressed trade-offs, and lack of controllability; and (4) measurement problems including benchmark contamination, construct validity gaps, and task diversity isolation. Finally, it proposes concrete research directions across data curation (program-augmented datasets, verifiable synthetic data, fine-grained developmental process data), training (RL environments from real repositories, test-time training to custom codebases, learning to quantify uncertainty), and inference-time approaches (semantic-aware embeddings, on-the-fly codebase navigation, neurosymbolic integration with PL tools, and scaffolding human supervision of AI-generated code).

## Results

This is a position paper with no experimental results or main results table. The authors cite a few illustrative numbers to motivate their challenges: SWE-Bench scores have risen from an initial 2% to 73%, and Qwen-2.5 achieves 83% accuracy on HumanEval in Python but only 27% in the low-resource language D. These figures are used to argue that progress, while significant, is uneven across languages, scopes, and task types, and that fundamental gaps remain in data, scale, interaction, and measurement.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new method and therefore does not compare against baselines. It is a position/survey paper that categorizes tasks, identifies cross-cutting challenges, and outlines future research directions. The authors do contrast their view with an alternative industry perspective (Section 5) that argues scaling up models and data alone is sufficient to achieve AI software engineers; the authors counter that this view is too optimistic and that fundamentally new forms of data, training environments, and inference-time approaches are needed. No quantitative head-to-head comparisons are presented.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper builds on a broad set of prior surveys of LLMs for software engineering (Fan et al. 2023, Hou et al. 2024, Jin et al. 2024, Roychoudhury et al. 2025a) but carves new ground by introducing a structured three-axis taxonomy (scope, logical complexity, level of human intervention) that unifies tasks from code generation through formal verification under a single framework. It departs from prior surveys that predominantly center on code generation by explicitly elevating refactoring, migration, program analysis, scaffolding, and meta-code to first-class tasks. The paper also positions itself against the industry narrative that scaling models and data alone will suffice, arguing instead that fundamentally new data pipelines, RL environments, and inference-time techniques are required.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's call for large-scale open-source community efforts (unified RL gym environments, fine-grained developmental-process datasets, diverse SWE task benchmarks) addresses a real coordination gap in the field where progress is fragmented across proprietary systems. Its identification of four cross-cutting challenges—data (OOD domains, versioning), scale (long contexts, RAG limitations), interaction (vague specs, trade-offs, controllability), and measurement (contamination, construct validity)—provides a concrete research agenda that goes beyond the code-generation-centric framing of most prior work. Key open questions include whether test-time training and neurosymbolic integration can close the gap on high-logical-complexity tasks, and how to build benchmarks with sufficient construct validity to reflect real-world SWE quality beyond pass/fail test correctness.
