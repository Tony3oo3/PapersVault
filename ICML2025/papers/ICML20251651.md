## Problem

While Mixture of Experts (MoE) architectures are widely recognized as compute-efficient, their behavior under memory constraints remains poorly understood, and they are conventionally assumed to be memory-inefficient. This paper asks whether MoE can actually outperform dense models when total parameter count (and thus GPU memory) is the binding constraint, and provides a principled framework for selecting the optimal number of experts under joint compute and memory budgets.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251651/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The scaling law takes a compute budget (FLOPs), a memory budget (total parameters, optionally including KV cache size for a given context length), and a number of experts E as inputs, and produces a closed-form prediction of final training loss along with the optimal active parameter count N_act and training token count D. The number of experts is transformed via a monotonic mapping Ê (Eq. 4) that accounts for overhead at small E and diminishing returns at large E. The framework is instantiated across four constraint scenarios: pure compute optimality, model-memory optimality, total-memory optimality (including KV cache), and joint training-plus-inference budget.

## Method

The paper derives a joint scaling law L(N_act, D, Ê) = aÊ^δ N_act^(α+γ ln Ê) + bÊ^ω D^(β+ζ ln Ê) + c that unifies dense (E=1) and MoE models by introducing power-law prefactors in Ê and log-interaction terms between Ê and the exponents on N_act and D. The functional form is motivated by three empirical observations: scaling in E is a power law, the MoE benefit diminishes with model size at fixed data, and grows with data size at fixed model. Coefficients are fitted via LBFGS (Huber loss) on 270 Switch-Transformer models up to 5B parameters trained on FineWeb-Edu, achieving RMSE_v = 0.0039 on held-out runs. A companion learning-rate scaling law LR(N_act\e, E) = exp(8.39 − 0.81 ln N_act\e − 0.25 ln E) is derived to ensure fair cross-E comparison. The key finding is that a total-parameter-matched MoE model, despite having fewer active parameters, can be trained on proportionally more tokens under the same FLOPs budget, and the data-term gain can outweigh the model-term loss, making MoE memory-efficient.

## Results

The joint scaling law (Eq. 6) achieves RMSE_v = 0.0039 on a held-out set of 30 runs and RMSE_t = 0.0062 on the full training set of 270 models (up to 5B parameters), with a maximum absolute extrapolation error of 0.018. The key empirical finding is that a 1.1B total-parameter MoE model (E=2 or E=4) achieves lower final loss than a compute-matched dense model of the same total size, even when the dense model is overtrained past its compute-optimal token count. The E=2 and E=4 MoE models use 36% and 61% fewer FLOPs per token at inference than their dense counterpart, respectively. The paper's rule of thumb: an MoE with E≤8 experts trained on E-times more tokens than a compute-optimal dense model outperforms it while maintaining the same total parameter count.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between MoE and dense (E=1) models under matched total-parameter and compute budgets. At 1.1B total parameters, the E=2 and E=4 MoE models achieve lower final training loss than the dense model trained with the same FLOPs, even when the dense model is overtrained beyond its compute-optimal point (Figure 1b). In terms of the scaling law fit quality, the joint formula (RMSE_v = 0.0039) performs comparably to fitting separate Chinchilla laws per expert count (RMSE_v^sep = 0.0041), confirming that the single joint formula captures the E-dependence without overfitting. For downstream tasks (HellaSwag, Winogrande, SciQ, LAMBADA, OpenBookQA), dense models show a slight edge on some tasks at matched pretraining perplexity, while MoE models perform similarly or slightly better on others (e.g., OpenBookQA), indicating the pretraining loss advantage does not uniformly translate to all downstream benchmarks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate lines of scaling-law research: the Chinchilla joint scaling law for dense Transformers (Hoffmann et al., 2022), which relates loss to active parameters and dataset size, and the MoE scaling law of Clark et al. (2022), which relates loss to active parameters and number of experts on a fixed dataset. By introducing interaction terms between E and both N_act and D, the authors derive a single joint formula (Eq. 6) that treats all three quantities as free variables, going beyond prior work that held one of them fixed. The analysis is further extended to memory-constrained optimality (model memory, KV cache, joint training+inference budgets), a dimension largely absent from earlier scaling-law papers that focused exclusively on compute budgets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical upshot is a concrete rule of thumb and lookup table (Table 2) for selecting the optimal number of experts under a given memory budget, which directly informs deployment decisions on hardware like a single H100 or an 8×H100 node. Key limitations include the restriction to standard (non-fine-grained) Switch MoE with ≤32 experts, the use of FLOPs rather than wall-clock time, and the fact that the memory-efficiency claim is empirically validated only at the 1.1B-parameter scale. Open questions include whether the conclusions strengthen with fine-grained or dropless MoE variants, and whether the scaling-law predictions hold at the 100B+ parameter regime where most production MoE models operate.
