## Problem

Existing text medical benchmarks (MedQA, MMLU Medical) no longer challenge state-of-the-art models—o1-preview achieves 96–99% accuracy—and lack coverage of specialized diagnostic scenarios across 17+ medical specialties. Meanwhile, traditional multimodal medical benchmarks (VQA-Rad, Path-VQA, Slake) rely on automatically generated simple QA pairs from image captions, diverging sharply from real-world clinical tasks that require integrating diverse images with rich patient records and examination results to perform expert-level diagnostic reasoning. No prior benchmark systematically combines specialty board-level exam questions with authentic multimodal clinical information.

## Input / Output

Each question is a multiple-choice item with a clinical vignette (patient history, vital signs, lab/exam results) and 10 options (Text subset) or 5 options (MM subset), optionally accompanied by 1–4 images spanning radiology, pathology, medical optical imaging, photos, vital-signs diagrams, documents, charts, and tables. The 4,460 questions (2,455 Text, 2,005 MM with 2,839 images) are annotated with task category (Diagnosis, Treatment Planning, Basic Medicine), body system (11 systems), and a Reasoning vs. Understanding label. The expected output is a single letter selection among the options, evaluated via zero-shot chain-of-thought prompting with greedy decoding.

## Method

MedXpertQA is constructed through a multi-stage pipeline: (1) collecting 37,543 questions from USMLE, COMLEX, 17 American specialty board exams, ACR/EDir radiology exams, and NEJM Image Challenges; (2) filtering via 8 AI expert models (14 sampling votes per question) and an adaptive Brier-score threshold calibrated to human expert difficulty ratings; (3) removing near-duplicates using MedCPT-Query-Encoder embeddings with IQR-based outlier detection; (4) augmenting questions via LLM rephrasing and expanding options with GPT-4o and Claude-3.5-Sonnet to mitigate data leakage; and (5) multi-round review by licensed physicians. A GPT-4o-based annotation step, validated at ~4.3% error rate on a 10% human-audited sample, partitions questions into Reasoning and Understanding subsets to enable targeted evaluation of o1-like inference-time-scaled models. The benchmark is evaluated with 18 proprietary and open-source LLMs/LMMs under zero-shot CoT, revealing that even the strongest models (o1, DeepSeek-R1) score only ~45–49% overall, well below the ~44% pre-licensed human baseline on Reasoning items.

## Results

On MedXpertQA (4,460 questions across 17 specialties), the best model o1 achieves only 49.89% average accuracy, barely exceeding the Expert (Pre-Licensed) human baseline of 43.92%. Among vanilla LMMs, GPT-4o leads at 35.96% average, while the best LLM DeepSeek-R1 reaches 37.76% on MedXpertQA Text. In stark contrast, o1 scores 96.00% on MedQA-USMLE and 95.30% on MMLU (Medical), highlighting MedXpertQA's substantially greater difficulty. Inference-time scaled models (o1, DeepSeek-R1, o3-mini) narrow the Reasoning-vs-Understanding gap compared to vanilla models, with o1 scoring 46.24% on Reasoning vs. 39.66% on Understanding for Text.

## Baselines & Comparisons

o1 (49.89% overall) is the top performer, exceeding the Expert (Pre-Licensed) human baseline (43.92%) by ~6 points, but remains far below the 96% it achieves on MedQA-USMLE. Among vanilla LMMs, GPT-4o (35.96%) outperforms Claude-3.5-Sonnet (26.65%), Gemini-1.5-Pro (26.16%), and Gemini-2.0-Flash (28.04%) by 7–10 points; the open-source Qwen2.5-VL-72B (23.35%) beats GPT-4o-mini (22.43%) but trails GPT-4o by ~13 points. On the LLM side (Table 5), DeepSeek-R1 (37.76% on Text) leads all LLMs, outperforming o3-mini (37.30%), LLaMA-3.3-70B (24.49%), and Qwen2.5-72B (18.90%) by 3–19 points. Inference-time scaling consistently benefits the Reasoning subset: DeepSeek-R1 gains +0.53 points on Reasoning over Understanding, while its backbone DeepSeek-V3 shows a smaller gap (+1.05), and QwQ-32B-Preview (+2.91) improves over Qwen2.5-32B (−4.32, i.e., worse on Reasoning). Notably, all 18 evaluated models score below 50% on MedXpertQA, confirming the benchmark's expert-level difficulty relative to existing medical benchmarks where top models exceed 90%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground in medical benchmarking by departing from two established lines: text medical QA benchmarks (MedQA, MedMCQA, MMLU Medical) that rely on general licensing exams and lack specialty coverage, and multimodal medical benchmarks (VQA-Rad, Path-VQA, Slake, OmniMedVQA) that auto-generate simple QA pairs from image captions. MedXpertQA repurposes the exam-question paradigm from MMMU (H&M) but extends it to 17 specialty board exams and real clinical scenarios with rich patient records, while introducing a multi-stage filtering pipeline (adaptive Brier score, AI expert voting, semantic similarity) and data synthesis for leakage prevention that prior medical benchmarks lacked. The reasoning/understanding subset draws on the o1 evaluation paradigm but is the first to apply it specifically to medical reasoning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

With o1 already achieving 96% on MedQA and 99% on MMLU Medical Genetics, the field urgently needs benchmarks that remain challenging for frontier models; MedXpertQA fills this gap by capping even o1 at ~50% and GPT-4o at ~36%, well below the pre-licensed human baseline of ~44%. The reasoning subset provides a targeted tool for evaluating o1-like models in medicine, a domain where prior benchmarks (MedQA) primarily test knowledge recall rather than multi-step clinical reasoning. Key open questions include whether the 10-option format for Text and 5-option format for MM introduce confounds in cross-subset comparison, whether the data synthesis fully eliminates memorization risk for future model generations, and whether benchmark performance translates to clinical readiness as the authors themselves caution.
