## Problem

Existing hallucination detection methods (confidence-based, consistency-based) become ineffective on long detailed captions because MLLMs increasingly ground their generation in prior text rather than the input image as sequence length grows, rendering hallucinations after ~192 tokens undetectable. Additionally, conventional caption evaluation metrics (BLEU, CIDEr, CLIPScore, CLAIR, ALOHa) fail to reliably distinguish factual from hallucinated content in hyper-detailed captions, and VQA-centric benchmarks do not correlate with a model's actual detailed captioning ability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251323/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

CapMAS takes an RGB image and a free-form detailed caption (of arbitrary length, generated by any MLLM) as input and produces a corrected caption with hallucinated content removed or revised. The evaluation framework separately scores factuality (proportion of atomic propositions verified as true against both image and reference caption) and coverage (accuracy of an LLM answering 19,899 multiple-choice visual questions using only the caption, without image access). The method is entirely training-free and plug-and-play, requiring only a pretrained LLM (e.g., LLaMA-3-8B) and MLLM (e.g., LLaVA-NeXT-7B) at inference time.

## Method

CapMAS is a three-stage multiagent pipeline: (i) a decomposer LLM breaks the caption into atomic propositions (minimal true/false claims); (ii) an MLLM fact-checker converts each proposition into a True/False question, computes a hallucination score H(u) = -log(min(p(T|x,Q(u)) - p(F|x,Q(u)), ε)) from token probabilities, and classifies propositions into True/False sets via a threshold π; (iii) a corrector LLM revises the caption to retain only factual content. The key insight, empirically validated, is that isolating each proposition from its surrounding context and querying the MLLM independently (the 'Isolation' method) dramatically outperforms in-context detection (AUROC 81.4 vs. 57.5–73.5), because it prevents the model from being anchored by its own prior text. For evaluation, the paper introduces a GPT-4o-based factuality metric that decomposes captions into atomic propositions and checks each against both the image and a reference caption, plus a coverage benchmark of 19,899 human-refined multiple-choice questions constructed via GPT-4o generation, Sentence-BERT deduplication, and human annotation.

## Results

On the detailed image captioning task (LLaVA-v1.5-7B backbone), CapMAS achieves a Factuality score of 63.4 and CLAIR of 66.3, substantially outperforming all competing hallucination-mitigation methods. Notably, VCD *reduces* factuality below the unmodified baseline (44.6 vs. 52.8), while SPARC achieves the highest Coverage (44.9) but lower Factuality (50.2). When applied to GPT-4V captions, CapMAS (using LLaVA-NeXT-7B as fact-checker) lifts Factuality from 77.1 to 83.3 with only a modest Coverage drop (53.5 → 50.8). The proposed factuality metric achieves a Spearman's ρ of 70.2 with human judgments, outperforming FaithScore (62.5) and FactScore (67.9).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CapMAS decisively outperforms all decoding-based methods on Factuality: VCD (44.6) and OPERA (53.0) both fail to improve upon the unmodified baseline (52.8), with VCD actually degrading factuality by 8.2 points. Among corrector-based methods, CapMAS (63.4) surpasses Volcano (53.7) by 9.7 points and LURE (51.9) by 11.5 points on Factuality, though Volcano retains a Coverage advantage (37.7 vs. 33.1). The training-based method LRV is the weakest overall (Avg. 35.5), with Factuality collapsing to 29.1. SPARC achieves the best Coverage (44.9) but trails CapMAS by 13.2 points on Factuality (50.2 vs. 63.4) and 1.6 on CLAIR. On the hallucination detection sub-task (Table 1), the Isolation strategy used by CapMAS achieves 81.4 AUROC, outperforming Confidence (57.5), Consistency (73.5), and Object Detector (61.5). For evaluation metrics, the proposed GPT-4o-based factuality score correctly ranks Clean > Object > Attribution > Relation captions, whereas CLIP-based metrics miss Relation hallucinations entirely and ALOHa also fails on Relation, while CLAIR detects all three types but conflates factuality with other quality dimensions.

## Relation to Prior Work

CapMAS synthesizes ideas from atomic proposition decomposition (FActScore, FaithScore) and multi-agent LLM collaboration into a training-free, plug-and-play pipeline for correcting detailed image captions, departing from corrector-based methods (LURE, Volcano) that require supervised fine-tuning of a dedicated corrector model. It also extends the hallucination detection literature by empirically demonstrating that confidence- and consistency-based methods (VCD, OPERA, SPARC) become unreliable as caption length grows, motivating the decomposition-then-verify architecture. On the evaluation side, the paper moves beyond reference-matching metrics (BLEU, CIDEr) and single-aspect LLM metrics (CLAIR, ALOHa) by introducing a dual factuality-coverage framework validated against human judgments.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that widely adopted hallucination-mitigation methods can actually *reduce* factuality on hyper-detailed captions is a significant negative result that should reshape how the community validates such techniques. The observation that VQA benchmark rankings do not predict detailed captioning ability (e.g., Idefics2-8B and MiniCPM-V-2.6) challenges the current VQA-centric evaluation paradigm and calls for broader assessment protocols. Open questions include whether the factuality-coverage trade-off controlled by π can be tightened, whether the multi-agent overhead is justified in latency-sensitive production settings, and whether the GPT-4o-dependent evaluation metric introduces its own systematic biases at scale.
