## Problem

Hypernetwork-based model editing methods (e.g., MEND, MALMEN) are trained on knowledge-to-update pairs collected from a fixed LLM, making them incompatible with the dynamically changing parameters encountered during lifelong editing. As a result, they degrade catastrophically after roughly 100 sequential edits, whereas locate-then-edit methods like MEMIT and AlphaEdit can sustain up to 5,000 edits. The paper asks how to keep a hypernetwork effective across thousands of sequential edits where the target LLM's parameters are continuously shifting.

## Input / Output

At inference time, RLEdit takes a single knowledge pair (x, y), performs one step of parameter-frozen fine-tuning on the current LLM to collect low-rank decomposed gradients (δ_{l+1} × u_l^T), and feeds them through a 4-layer MLP hypernetwork to produce pseudo-activation and pseudo-increment vectors whose outer product yields the parameter update Δ applied to specific MLP layers (e.g., gate[11–15] and up[18–24] for Llama-3-8B on ZsRE). The output is the updated LLM parameter tensor W_t = W_{t−1} + Δ, with each edit taking approximately 0.17 s. During training, the hypernetwork consumes an entire sequence of n knowledge samples and their corresponding gradient trajectories, optimizing its parameters against the cumulative trajectory return rather than per-sample losses.

## Method

RLEdit formulates hypernetwork training in lifelong editing as a Markov Decision Process: the hypernetwork is the agent, the LLM is the environment, the parameter update Δ is the action, the current LLM state plus new knowledge is the state, and the negative editing loss is the reward. The reward function has three components—a basic term (target-knowledge cross-entropy plus KL-divergence locality penalty), a memory backtracking term that re-evaluates the previous k edits with exponential decay to prevent catastrophic forgetting, and an L2 regularization term that constrains update magnitude. Crucially, RLEdit uses offline policy optimization: instead of updating the hypernetwork at every time step, it traverses the full knowledge sequence, accumulates the discounted total return J, and performs a single gradient step on the hypernetwork parameters, which accelerates convergence by ~100× and lets the hypernetwork learn sequential dependencies across the entire trajectory. This RL-based training enables the hypernetwork to adapt its output to the current LLM parameter state, sustaining effective editing beyond 20,000 samples where prior hypernetwork methods fail.

## Results

On the FEVER dataset (400×20 configuration), RLEdit achieves 95.34% Efficacy, 93.58% Generalization, and 70.36% Specificity on LLaMA-3-8B, with comparable scores on Gemma-2-9B (95.44/92.83/68.76) and Mistral-7B (88.99/88.25/73.64). RLEdit requires only 0.22s per edit (including hypernetwork training), compared to 6.0–8.3s for locate-then-edit methods, representing a ~2% time cost relative to most baselines. The method scales to 20,000+ sequential edits while maintaining stable performance, whereas most baselines degrade catastrophically beyond 5,000 edits. On GLUE general capability tests, LLMs edited with RLEdit for 3,000 samples retain performance comparable to the pre-trained model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RLEdit outperforms the strongest locate-then-edit baseline, AlphaEdit, on Efficacy (95.34 vs. 94.22) and Specificity (70.36 vs. 25.57) on LLaMA-3-8B/FEVER, while AlphaEdit edges ahead on Generalization (94.14 vs. 93.58); however, RLEdit is ~28× faster (0.22s vs. 6.23s per edit). Against hypernetwork-based baselines, RLEdit dominates decisively: MEND and MALMEN collapse to near-zero Efficacy and Generalization on LLaMA-3-8B (0.00–0.01%), and even their retrained variants (MEND*, MALMEN*) remain far below RLEdit. DAFNet, the strongest sequential-editing hypernetwork method, achieves only 31.27% Efficacy and 28.82% Generalization on LLaMA-3-8B/FEVER versus RLEdit's 95.34% and 93.58%. RECT and PRUNE, designed for sequential editing via regularization and singular-value constraints, score 60.95% and 56.64% Efficacy respectively but fail on Specificity (1.75% and 0.85%), while RLEdit maintains 70.36%. Across all three LLMs and three datasets, RLEdit reports average improvements of 66% in Efficacy, 65% in Generalization, and 40% in Specificity over baselines, while requiring only ~2% of the computation time.

## Relation to Prior Work

RLEdit builds directly on the hypernetwork-based editing paradigm introduced by MEND (Mitchell et al., 2022a) and extended to mass editing by MALMEN (Tan et al., 2024), adopting their low-rank gradient decomposition and hypernetwork architecture. However, it departs fundamentally from the supervised K→Δ training paradigm: prior hypernetwork methods require all training pairs to come from a fixed LLM state, making them incompatible with the evolving parameters during sequential editing. RLEdit reframes hypernetwork training as an offline RL problem (MDP with the hypernetwork as agent and the LLM as environment), enabling the hypernetwork to adapt to dynamically changing LLM states across thousands of edits. This positions RLEdit as a conceptual bridge between meta-learning-based editing and sequential decision-making, distinct from locate-then-edit methods (ROME, MEMIT, AlphaEdit) that handle sequential editing through per-edit matrix operations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

RLEdit addresses a critical bottleneck in hypernetwork-based editing: existing methods collapse after ~100 edits while RLEdit scales beyond 20,000, a 200× improvement that opens the door to truly lifelong knowledge maintenance in deployed LLMs. The RL framework's plug-and-play nature (demonstrated by 22–52% improvements when integrated into MEND and MALMEN) suggests it can upgrade a broad class of existing hypernetwork methods. Key limitations include evaluation restricted to factual knowledge on 8B-scale models, no exploration of multi-hop or relational knowledge editing, and acknowledged safety concerns around parameter modification. Open questions include whether the RL formulation extends to locate-then-edit or memory-based editing paradigms, and how to handle knowledge conflicts at scale.
