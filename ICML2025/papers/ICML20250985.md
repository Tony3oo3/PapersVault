## Problem

Learning Bayesian Nash Equilibrium (BNE) in continuous auction games faces two coupled challenges: (1) the non-smooth utility function makes gradient computation difficult, and existing smoothing-based estimators (Evolution Strategies, REINFORCE, Smooth Market) introduce model bias that distorts the learned strategies; (2) the standard utility-gradient objective suffers from a local convergence rate that degrades quadratically with the number of bidders in symmetric auctions, making large-scale equilibrium learning impractical.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input an auction mechanism specification (first-price or second-price), a joint value prior distribution over bidders (e.g., uniform, asymmetric weak/strong priors), and the number of bidders n. Each bidder's bidding strategy is parameterized by a shared 3-layer MLP that maps a private value v_i ∈ R_+ to a bid b_i ∈ R_+. The output is the learned BNE bidding strategy β*_i(·), evaluated by L2 distance against known analytic BNE solutions sampled from the prior. The method also supports risk-averse (Von Neumann-Morgenstern) utility functions and reserve-price extensions.

## Method

The paper introduces two key technical components. First, an analytic gradient solution (MC on distribution) that computes ∇_{θ_i} ū_i directly via the pdf and cdf of the market-price distribution (estimated by Monte Carlo sampling and KDE), avoiding the utility-modifying bias of Gaussian-smoothing or softmax-approximation methods. Second, a new Best Response Distance (BR Distance) objective L_BR = Σ_i ½ E_{v_i}‖β_{θ_i}(v_i) − br_i(v_i, β_{θ_{-i}})‖², which measures the strategy-to-best-response gap and provably upper-bounds the ε-BNE approximation factor. To avoid the expensive arg-max in br_i, the method uses a local Taylor expansion of the ex-interim utility: a second-order Newton-style approximation when the utility is locally concave, or a first-order gradient step when locally convex. Under a symmetric uniform first-price auction with linear bidding, the resulting Approximate BR Gradient achieves a local convergence rate of O(log(1/d)) independent of n, in contrast to the O(n² log(1/d)) rate of the utility gradient, and each iteration runs in ~0.002 s versus ~0.09 s for Smooth Market at n=10.

## Results

In symmetric FP auctions with n=10 bidders, Approx BR achieves an L2 distance of 9.65e-3 to the BNE after 2,000 steps, compared to 5.01e-2 for SM and 5.44e-2 for Utility Grad — roughly a 5x improvement. The gap widens with bidder count: at n=5, Approx BR reaches 5.20e-3 vs. 1.57e-2 (SM) and 1.51e-2 (Utility Grad). In SP auctions with n=10, Approx BR achieves 3.73e-3 vs. 3.51e-2 (SM) and 3.17e-2 (Utility Grad). Additionally, Approx BR runs at 0.002s/iteration versus 0.091s/iteration for SM at n=10, a ~45x speedup per step.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is SM (Smooth Market, Kohring et al. 2023), the state-of-the-art method for learning BNE via softmax-approximated gradients. Approx BR decisively outperforms SM in all settings with n≥5: in the 10-bidder FP auction, Approx BR achieves L2=9.65e-3 at 2,000 steps versus SM's 5.01e-2, a 5.2x reduction in error. The advantage grows with bidder count — at n=2 the methods are comparable (L2≈3e-3), but by n=10 the gap becomes an order of magnitude. Approx BR also runs ~45x faster per iteration (0.002s vs. 0.091s) because it uses analytic gradient computation via MC-on-distribution rather than SM's backpropagation through sampled utilities. The paper additionally compares against its own Utility Grad variant (same analytic gradient but optimizing the utility objective instead of BR Distance), which confirms that the convergence improvement comes from the BR Distance objective rather than the gradient estimation alone: Utility Grad still suffers the quadratic-in-n slowdown (L2=5.44e-2 at n=10 FP) while Approx BR does not.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the gradient-based learning framework for BNE in continuous auction games established by Bichler et al. (2021) and Kohring et al. (2023, Smooth Market). It departs from prior work in two key ways: (1) it replaces utility-modifying gradient estimators (Gaussian smoothing in ES/REINFORCE, softmax in SM) with an analytic gradient solution that preserves the original utility function, and (2) it introduces a new Best Response Distance objective that replaces the standard utility maximization, fundamentally changing the local convergence landscape. The approximate best response via Taylor expansion draws on Newton's method but repurposes it for local strategy correction rather than global optimization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of a Θ(n²) Hessian condition number for the utility objective—and the resulting quadratic convergence rate degradation—provides a clean theoretical explanation for why existing methods struggle in large-scale auctions, and the BR Distance objective offers a principled fix with an n-independent convergence rate. However, the theoretical guarantees are confined to the symmetric uniform first-price auction with linear strategies, leaving open whether the O(log(1/d)) rate extends to general priors, non-linear strategies, or other auction mechanisms. The practical requirement of a differentiable pdf estimate (e.g., KDE) for the second-order gradient also introduces implementation complexity that may limit adoption in highly non-standard auction settings.
