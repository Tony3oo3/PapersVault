## Problem

Despite widespread empirical use of edge-embedding GNN architectures, there is no theoretical characterization of when and why maintaining edge embeddings provides representational advantages over the standard node-embedding paradigm. The paper asks whether the benefits of edge-based architectures are inherent to the paradigm itself or merely artifacts of other design choices, and under what constraints (memory, symmetry) these benefits are provable.

## Input / Output

The theoretical framework takes a graph G with inputs distributed on edges (potential functions from a finite class Φ) and produces node-level outputs (MAP assignments or marginals), with the key parameters being memory B bits per processor and T rounds of message passing. The empirical experiments take standard node features on graphs (molecular graphs for ZINC/Peptides, image graphs for MNIST/CIFAR-10, or synthetic star/tree topologies) and produce classification or regression labels, with edge embeddings initialized as concatenations of endpoint node features to equalize input information.

## Method

The paper introduces formal node and edge message-passing protocols with explicit memory constraints, then proves a depth separation: on a graph of √n paths plus a hub vertex, MAP estimation requires Ω(√n) rounds for any node protocol with O(1) memory but only O(1) rounds for an edge protocol, because the hub node is an information bottleneck for node message passing while edges adjacent to the hub communicate directly in the line graph. The lower bound (Lemma 2) is an information-theoretic argument inspired by graph pebbling time-space tradeoffs, and a separate construction (Theorem 4) shows the separation persists even under symmetry constraints. Empirically, the architectural change is minimal: replacing node-neighborhood aggregation with edge-neighborhood aggregation (message passing over the line graph) in a standard GCN, which consistently matches or improves performance on five benchmarks and shows large gains on synthetic star-graph and Ising-model tasks with hub nodes.

## Results

On five standard graph benchmarks, the Edge-GCN architecture (message passing over the line graph) matches or improves upon the standard node-based GCN in 4 out of 5 settings. Headline improvements include ZINC MAE dropping from 0.3430 to 0.3297, CIFAR-10 accuracy rising from 55.71% to 57.44%, and Peptides-Func AP increasing from 0.6816 to 0.6867. The only regression is on MNIST, where Edge-GCN scores 94.37% vs. 95.29% for GCN. On the synthetic star-graph stress test (Table 2), the gap is far more dramatic: with 64 leaves and a 5-layer planted model, Edge-GCN achieves RMSE 0.004 versus 0.379 for the best node-based model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the standard GCN (Kipf & Welling, 2017), with all other architectural choices (depth, embedding dimensionality, training hyperparameters) held identical. Edge-GCN wins on 4 of 5 benchmarks: it reduces ZINC MAE by ~4% (0.3297 vs. 0.3430), improves CIFAR-10 accuracy by 1.73 points (57.44% vs. 55.71%), lifts Peptides-Func AP by 0.51 points (0.6867 vs. 0.6816), and lowers Peptides-Struct MAE from 0.2453 to 0.2437. It loses narrowly on MNIST (94.37% vs. 95.29% accuracy). The authors note that standard benchmarks may be too easy to reveal the full representational advantage; on the synthetic star-graph task designed to stress-test hub-node bottlenecks, Edge-GCN dominates by an order of magnitude (e.g., RMSE 0.004 vs. 0.379 at 64 leaves, depth 5), consistent with the theoretical depth separation in Theorem 4.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the local-computation abstraction for GNNs introduced by Loukas (2020a,b) by adding memory constraints and, crucially, by introducing a parallel edge-embedding message-passing model. It explicitly departs from the dominant symmetry/invariance lens (Xu et al., 2019; Maron et al., 2019) that has governed GNN expressivity theory, arguing that the Weisfeiler-Lehman perspective is insufficient to capture representational differences between architectures when memory is bounded. The information-theoretic lower-bound technique (Lemma 2) draws on graph pebbling and time-space tradeoff methods from theoretical computer science (Grigor'ev, 1976; Abrahamson, 1991), and provides a formalization of the oversquashing phenomenon previously studied empirically (Alon & Yahav, 2021; Black et al., 2023).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes the first clean theoretical depth separation between edge- and node-embedding GNNs, showing that the symmetry lens alone cannot adjudicate architectural design choices—a message that should reshape how the community thinks about GNN expressivity. However, the practical impact is tempered: standard benchmark improvements are modest (Table 1), the dramatic separations only manifest on hub-heavy topologies, and the computational overhead of edge embeddings on dense graphs remains unresolved. Open questions include whether more efficient edge-based variants can preserve the representational gains, and whether the gap widens on realistic graphs with skewed degree distributions.
