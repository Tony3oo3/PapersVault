## Problem

While undirected graph rewiring has been extensively studied (over-smoothing, over-squashing, under-reaching), feedforward computational graphs—directed acyclic graphs where information flows only from earlier to later nodes—lack principled evaluation criteria or design guidelines. Most practical architectures default to fully connected or locally connected causal masks, and prior work has identified limitations of high-in-degree feedforward graphs without offering concrete alternative structures or metrics to guide their construction.

## Input / Output

The framework takes an ordered set of n nodes (e.g., tokens in a sequence) and an indegree budget per node, and produces a feedforward adjacency matrix where edges are constrained to go only forward (a ≤ b), all self-edges are present, and a unique sink exists at position n−1. The two evaluation metrics—averaged mixing time (lower is better) and normalised minimax fidelity (higher is better)—are computed directly from the adjacency matrix via random-walk and diffusion analyses. In practice, the resulting adjacency matrix is used as a causal attention mask in GAT-style or Transformer models, replacing the standard fully connected or sliding-window mask.

## Method

The paper introduces two complementary metrics: (1) averaged mixing time, which tracks how quickly a random walk from any node reaches the sink (proven O(n) for line graphs, O(log n) for fully connected graphs), and (2) minimax fidelity, which measures the worst-case sharpness of individual node information at the sink after optimal-layer diffusion (normalised so fully connected = 1). Guided by these metrics, the authors use FunSearch (LLM-driven program search) to discover the FS graph generator: nodes are partitioned into ⌈log n⌉ blocks, successive blocks are connected via bipartite expander graphs (concatenated random perfect matchings), and intra-cluster edges are filled recursively with smaller FS graph copies to ensure self-similarity. This yields O(polylog n) mixing time (proven in Theorem 6.1) while retaining higher fidelity than the fully connected graph, with the expander indegree (e.g., 4 log n) serving as a tunable fidelity knob. Empirically, GATs trained with FS graph masks outperform all other sparse graphs on maximum retrieval, second-maximum retrieval, and parity tasks, and fine-tuning Gemma 2B with FS masks matches fully connected perplexity on Wikipedia.

## Results

The paper evaluates GAT-style models on three algorithmic tasks (maximum retrieval, second maximum retrieval, and parity) with sequence lengths from 16 to 1,024, comparing seven feedforward graph generators. The FS (FunSearch) graph achieves the best out-of-distribution generalisation across all three tasks, effectively matching the fully connected graph on the Parity task while using substantially fewer edges. On the Maximum retrieval task, FS graph performance is comparable to Poisson(0.8), but on Second Maximum and Parity tasks there is a significant performance gap between the FS graph and all other sparse graphs. The paper also shows that an in-degree of 4 log n for the expander edges yields a more graceful scaling profile than log n, and that removing self-edges causes significant performance regressions, especially on Parity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the FS graph against six baselines: fully connected, line graph, Poisson(0.2), Poisson(0.8), Erdős-Rényi (constant and √n in-degree), and oriented expander graphs. On the Parity task, the FS graph effectively matches the fully connected graph in accuracy while having a substantially lower edge count, and significantly outperforms all sparse baselines. On the Second Maximum retrieval task, the FS graph shows a clear performance gap over every other sparse graph, with Poisson(0.2) and line graphs performing worst. On the Maximum retrieval task, the FS graph performs similarly to Poisson(0.8), with both outperforming line graphs and Erdős-Rényi variants. The line graph, while having the highest normalised minimax fidelity (growing as √(n/π)), suffers from O(n) mixing time and underperforms on all three tasks at larger sequence lengths. Erdős-Rényi and oriented expander graphs perform negatively on both mixing time and fidelity metrics compared to the fully connected graph, and are also prone to introducing additional sink nodes that break theoretical assumptions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the well-studied undirected graph rewiring literature (oversmoothing, oversquashing, under-reaching) to the feedforward/directed-acyclic setting, where prior work has been sparse. It draws on expander graph constructions (Lubotzky, 1994; Sarnak, 1990) for the bipartite edges within its FS graph generator, and leverages FunSearch (Romera-Paredes et al., 2023) as an evolutionary program-search tool to discover the graph topology. The two proposed metrics—averaged mixing time and minimax fidelity—are positioned as feedforward analogues of commute time and Dirichlet energy, respectively, but are tailored to the non-strongly-connected, lower-triangular structure of causal masks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first principled framework for evaluating feedforward computational graphs, providing practitioners with two complementary, theoretically grounded metrics and a concrete sparse graph generator (FS) that achieves O(polylog n) mixing time while outperforming fully connected graphs on fidelity. Open questions include extending the analysis to dynamically changing graphs across layers, establishing a more formal connection between the two metrics, and validating at larger scales and with more diverse architectures beyond GATs. The finding that Erdős-Rényi and oriented expander graphs underperform fully connected graphs on both metrics challenges the intuition that sparser topologies are automatically better in the directed setting.
