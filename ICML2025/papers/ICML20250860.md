## Problem

The possible rates of minimax mistakes in list transductive online learning—where a learner predicts a list of L labels per instance over a pre-specified sequence—were entirely open, particularly for unbounded label spaces. This paper resolves an open question from Hanneke et al. (2024b) on generalizing multiclass transductive online learning to the list setting, and simultaneously answers an open question from Moran et al. (2023) on extending agnostic list online learning to unbounded label spaces.

## Input / Output

The learner receives a pre-specified sequence of T instances from an arbitrary instance space X before the game begins, along with a concept class C and a fixed list size L. At each round, the learner must output a set of exactly L labels (from a possibly uncountable label space Y) for the next instance, after which the adversary reveals the true label. Performance is measured by the minimax number of mistakes (realizable setting, deterministic learners) or minimax expected regret against the best concept in C (agnostic setting, randomized learners), where a mistake occurs when the true label is absent from the predicted list.

## Method

The paper introduces two new combinatorial complexity parameters—the Level-constrained (L+1)-Littlestone dimension D(Q) and the Level-constrained (L+1)-Branching dimension B(Q)—that fully characterize a trichotomy: minimax mistakes are Θ(1) when B(Q) < ∞, Θ(log T) when D(Q) < ∞ but B(Q) = ∞, and Θ(T) when D(Q) = ∞. The log T upper bound is established via a shattering-counting technique (generalizing Hanneke et al. 2024b) that bounds the number of shattered sub-sequences of the instance sequence, avoiding the Halving+SSP approach which fails for unbounded label spaces. For the agnostic setting, the paper combines a conservative version of the shattering algorithm with dynamic experts (from Hanneke et al. 2023a) and the exponential weights algorithm to achieve a Õ(√T) regret bound, thereby handling infinite label spaces. The algorithms (LTSOA and List Shattering Algorithm) select L labels by sorting the label space in decreasing order of a sequence-dependent complexity measure and taking the top L.

## Results

In the realizable setting, the paper establishes a trichotomy: the minimax number of mistakes M*(Q,T) is Θ(1) when the Level-constrained (L+1)-Branching dimension B(Q) is finite, Θ(log T) when the Level-constrained (L+1)-Littlestone dimension D(Q) is finite but B(Q) is infinite, and Θ(T) when D(Q) is infinite. In the agnostic setting, the paper proves that learnability (sub-linear regret) holds if and only if D(Q) < ∞, with an explicit upper bound of Õ(√T) on the minimax expected regret. The concrete upper bound in the log T case is O(Ld·log(eT/d)) where d = D(Q), and the agnostic regret bound is O(√(TLD(Q)·log(eT/D(Q))·log(eT/(LD(Q)·log(eT/D(Q)))))).

**Theorem 1.1: Trichotomy of minimax mistake rates in the realizable setting.**

| Condition on Q | Minimax Mistakes M*(Q, T) | Characterizing Dimension |
|---|---|---|
| B(Q) < ∞ | Θ(1) | Level-constrained (L+1)-Branching dim. finite |
| D(Q) < ∞ and B(Q) = ∞ | Θ(log T) | Level-constrained (L+1)-Littlestone dim. finite, Branching dim. infinite |
| D(Q) = ∞ | Θ(T) | Level-constrained (L+1)-Littlestone dim. infinite |

**Theorem 1.2: Agnostic learnability characterization.**

| Condition on Q | Agnostic Learnable? | Regret Bound |
|---|---|---|
| D(Q) < ∞ | Yes | Õ(√T) |
| D(Q) = ∞ | No | Ω(T/(L+1)) |

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines; the 'comparisons' are between different learning frameworks and combinatorial dimensions. The paper resolves an open question from Hanneke et al. (2024b) on multiclass transductive online learning by extending their trichotomy to the list setting, showing that list size L > 1 can strictly reduce the mistake rate (Proposition C.3: a class with D(Q) = ∞ for list size L becomes learnable with B(Q') = 0 for list size L+1). It also answers an open question from Moran et al. (2023) by extending their agnostic list online learning result to unbounded label spaces via the dynamic expert technique. Proposition C.4 shows that finite L-DS dimension (the PAC list learnability criterion of Charikar & Pabbaraju, 2023) does not imply list transductive online learnability, establishing a strict separation. Proposition C.5 shows that a class can have infinite (L+1)-Littlestone dimension (not list online learnable per Moran et al., 2023) yet be list transductive online learnable with at most 2 mistakes, highlighting the power of the transductive (pre-specified sequence) assumption.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the multiclass transductive online learning framework of Hanneke et al. (2024b) from list size 1 to arbitrary list size L, and simultaneously generalizes the list online learning framework of Moran et al. (2023) by adding the transductive assumption (instances known in advance). The proof techniques draw on Littlestone's Standard Optimal Algorithm (1988) for the constant-mistake bound, the shattering-based technique of Hanneke et al. (2024b) for the log T bound, and the dynamic expert method of Hanneke et al. (2023a) to handle unbounded label spaces in the agnostic setting. Two new combinatorial dimensions — the Level-constrained (L+1)-Littlestone dimension and the Level-constrained (L+1)-Branching dimension — are introduced to capture the specific structure of the list transductive setting, which cannot be characterized by existing dimensions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The trichotomy (Θ(1), Θ(log T), Θ(T)) provides a clean, complete characterization of realizable learnability, resolving an open question from Hanneke et al. (2024b), while the agnostic characterization resolves a separate open question from Moran et al. (2023) about extending to unbounded label spaces. The separation results (Propositions C.3–C.5) reveal that list transductive online learning is genuinely distinct from both list online learning and list PAC learning, showing that the transductive assumption can render previously unlearnable classes learnable with only a few mistakes. A key open question is eliminating the persistent factor of list size L from the upper bounds, which has appeared in all prior list learning work and may be a fundamental barrier.
