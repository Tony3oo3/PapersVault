## Problem

It has been an open question whether process supervision (step-by-step rewards) is fundamentally more statistically efficient than outcome supervision (trajectory-level rewards) in reinforcement learning, with conventional wisdom attributing a trajectory-level coverage gap to the latter. This perceived gap has motivated substantial investment in collecting fine-grained process labels. The paper resolves the debate by proving that, under standard state-action coverage assumptions, the two paradigms are statistically equivalent up to polynomial factors in horizon.

## Input / Output

The method consumes offline trajectory data containing only cumulative rewards (or Bradley-Terry preference pairs) collected by a reference policy, along with a reward model class, and produces per-step reward estimates that can be directly plugged into any existing process-supervised offline RL algorithm. The critical constraint is bounded state-action concentrability between the reference and target policies, which can be exponentially smaller than the trajectory-level concentrability previously required. In the rollout-based variant, the input is an MDP with known transitions plus environment access for final-outcome rollouts, and the output is a policy whose suboptimality is bounded by the advantage estimation error times a concentrability factor.

## Method

The core technical contribution is the Change of Trajectory Measure Lemma, which shows that small second moment of trajectory-level reward sums under a reference policy implies small second moment under any other policy at the cost of only state-action concentrability (not trajectory concentrability), up to H^3 factors. The proof exploits the Markov property to show that trajectory-level variance control forces each state to have stable prefix and suffix partial sums (a 'locking in' property), and that states violating this property have low probability under any policy bounded by the state-action concentrability ratio. The main transformation (Theorem 1) splits outcome-supervised data, fits a reward model via least squares on the first half, imputes per-step rewards on the second half, and runs any offline RL algorithm, yielding an H^{3/2}·sqrt(C_sa·log(|R|/δ)/|D|) error bound. For preference-based RL, the same change-of-measure technique improves DPO's sample complexity from trajectory-level to state-action-level concentrability. Additionally, the paper proves that the advantage function of any policy serves as an optimal process reward model (recovering the same optimal policy as the original MDP), while a lower bound shows Q-function-based reward models can provably produce suboptimal policies.

## Results

The main theorem (Theorem 1) shows that learning a reward model from outcome-supervised data via least squares yields |J_r̂(π) − J(π)| ≲ H^{3/2} · √(C_sa(π, π_off) · log(|R|/δ) / |D_O|), matching the sample complexity of process supervision up to polynomial factors in horizon H. For DPO (Theorem 5), the suboptimality bound scales with state-action concentrability C_sa(π, π_ref) rather than trajectory concentrability, a potentially exponential improvement over prior analyses. In the online rollout setting (Theorem 6), using the advantage function as a process reward model guarantees max_π J(π) − J(π̂) ≤ 2H√(C_sa(ν)) · ε_stat + ε_alg, while Theorem 7 constructs a hard MDP where the Q-function-based reward model provably yields a suboptimal policy with gap ≥ 1/3.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or experimental comparisons. The central comparison is between outcome supervision and process supervision in terms of statistical complexity: the paper proves they require the same coverage condition (state-action concentrability C_sa) and the same sample complexity up to polynomial factors in H, challenging the conventional wisdom that outcome supervision is fundamentally harder due to the trajectory-level coverage problem. For DPO specifically, prior analyses (Xie et al., 2024; Zhan et al., 2023) relied on trajectory concentrability C_traj, which can be exponentially larger than C_sa; this paper's Change of Trajectory Measure Lemma (Lemma 3) reduces the requirement to C_sa. In the advantage-vs-Q-function comparison (Section 4), the advantage function of any policy is proven to be a valid process reward model (Theorem 6), while the Q-function is shown to be theoretically unsound (Theorem 7), providing a clear theoretical justification for the empirical preference for advantage-based methods (e.g., Setlur et al., 2024) over Q-function-based ones (e.g., Wang et al., 2024).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of offline reinforcement learning theory (concentrability coefficients, pessimism-based algorithms) and the LLM-specific debate over process versus outcome supervision (Lightman et al., 2023; Uesato et al., 2022). Its key technical tool—the Change of Trajectory Measure Lemma—is positioned as the first result establishing trajectory-level change of measure via step-level distribution shift, a gap that has been a known limitation in off-policy evaluation literature (Liu et al., 2018; Xie et al., 2019). The DPO analysis extends and improves upon Xie et al. (2024) by replacing trajectory concentrability with state-action concentrability, while the advantage-function-as-reward result provides the first theoretical justification for the empirical approach of Setlur et al. (2024) and simultaneously shows why the Q-function variant (Wang et al., 2024) is theoretically unsound.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that outcome supervision is no more statistically difficult than process supervision under standard coverage assumptions—challenges the field's heavy investment in expensive step-by-step annotation and suggests the observed performance gap is algorithmic rather than fundamental. However, the guarantees carry polynomial factors in horizon (H^{3/2} or H^3) that remain unresolved, and the results are restricted to offline settings with all-policy coverage in the main theorems. Open questions include whether these polynomial gaps can be closed, whether analogous transformations exist for online algorithms, and whether the theoretical equivalence translates to practical algorithmic improvements once the right pessimism or regularization is applied.
