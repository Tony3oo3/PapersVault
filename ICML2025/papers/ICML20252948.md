## Problem

Existing graph foundation models using the 'pretrain-then-prompt' paradigm lack theoretical foundations to explain why prompt-based transfer works and how much knowledge can actually be transferred from source to target domains. Additionally, many methods over-rely on text-attributed graphs, making them impractical for the many real-world graph datasets that lack explicit textual annotations, and no generalization error bounds exist to guide or constrain the prompting optimization in few-shot settings.

## Input / Output

During pre-training, BRIDGE consumes a collection of source graphs from multiple domains, each with heterogeneous node feature dimensions and adjacency matrices, which are first unified to a common dimensionality. For downstream adaptation, it takes a single target graph (or ego-graphs constructed around target nodes for graph classification) with only m labeled samples per class (m ≪ n), and produces class labels via similarity matching against learned class prototype embeddings. The method is text-free by design, operating purely on structural and feature signals, though it can optionally incorporate LLM-generated textual descriptions as auxiliary features.

## Method

BRIDGE introduces three coupled components: (1) domain-invariant aligners that unify multi-domain features both dimensionally (SVD + MLP) and semantically (learnable 0-1 masks under an Independent Causal Mechanism assumption), trained via a link-prediction contrastive objective augmented with a min-max alignment risk regularization that penalizes variance across domain-intervened embeddings; (2) a lightweight Mixture-of-Experts network during down-prompting where pre-trained source-domain semantic aligners serve as frozen experts, and a learnable routing network selects and assembles them to adapt target features in a self-supervised manner (minimizing routing entropy); and (3) a spectral regularizer derived from a Wasserstein-distance-based generalization error upper bound under Lipschitz continuity, which constrains the graph prompt fine-tuning by penalizing signal deviations across consecutive spectral bands, thereby bounding the maximum transferable knowledge and improving few-shot convergence.

## Results

Under the challenging one-shot setting, BRIDGE outperforms all 15 baselines on both node and graph classification, with average improvements of 3.41% and 3.25% respectively across six target datasets spanning cross-dataset and cross-domain scenarios. On the most difficult cross-domain target (Reddit), BRIDGE achieves 68.55% accuracy for node classification and 75.76% for graph classification, surpassing the strongest baseline ProNoG (64.87% and 73.09%) by 3.68 and 2.67 percentage points. The advantage grows with more shots: in five-shot settings, BRIDGE reaches 80.12% (node) and 84.42% (graph) on Reddit, further widening the gap over baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BRIDGE wins against all 15 baselines on every target dataset in both one-shot node and graph classification. The closest competitors are ProNoG (KDD 2025), MDGPT (arXiv 2024), and GCOPE (KDD 2024): on the cross-domain Reddit target, BRIDGE leads ProNoG by 3.68% (node) and 2.67% (graph), MDGPT by 5.44% (node) and 2.62% (graph), and GCOPE by 4.34% (node) and 5.57% (graph). The two most closely related multi-domain pre-training baselines, GCOPE and MDGPT, underperform notably — GCOPE suffers from implicit and inadequate domain feature alignment, while MDGPT collapses due to its shallow SVD-based alignment lacking knowledge-transfer risk regularization. Among prompt-based methods, GraphPrompt and GraphPrompt+ are competitive in cross-dataset settings but fall 4–10 points behind BRIDGE on cross-domain targets. Vanilla GNNs (GCN, GAT) and self-supervised pre-training methods (GCC, DGI, GraphCL, DSSL, GraphACL) trail by 10–20+ points, confirming that the multi-domain alignment and spectral regularization components are essential for effective few-shot transfer.

## Relation to Prior Work

BRIDGE sits at the intersection of multi-domain graph pre-training (GCOPE, MDGPT), graph prompt learning (GraphPrompt, GPPT, ProNoG), and theoretical domain adaptation (Wasserstein-based generalization bounds from Redko et al. 2017, spectral regularization from You et al. 2023). It departs from text-dependent GFMs (UniGraph, GraphGPT) by operating purely on structural and feature signals, and from prior prompt-learning work by introducing a MoE-based selective knowledge assembly mechanism and, critically, the first derivable generalization error upper bound for graph prompt fine-tuning. The universal task template (link prediction as a proxy for both node and graph classification) follows Liu et al. 2023, but the alignment risk regularization and spectral regularizer are novel contributions that connect the theoretical bound to an optimizable training objective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a fundamental gap in graph foundation models: the lack of theoretical understanding of how much knowledge can actually transfer across domains. By deriving an optimizable upper bound grounded in Lipschitz continuity and graph spectral theory, it provides both a diagnostic tool (quantifying transfer limits) and a practical regularizer (constraining fine-tuning). However, the empirical validation is limited to six small-to-medium datasets across three domains, and the O(N³) eigenvalue decomposition required by the spectral regularizer raises scalability concerns for large graphs. Open questions include whether the Lipschitz assumptions hold for deeper or heterogeneous GNNs, whether the bound is tight in practice, and how the framework extends to dynamic, temporal, or truly large-scale multi-domain settings.
