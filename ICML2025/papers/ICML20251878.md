## Problem

Prior work on fine-tuning effects in LLMs has focused on aggregate benchmark evaluations or mechanistic analyses at the dataset level, leaving no quantitative method to measure how much fine-tuning contributes to an individual model response. This gap prevents understanding at the per-prompt level how safety-relevant fine-tuning is attenuated by adversarial inputs such as jailbreaks, and whether such attenuation is a causal mechanism behind attack success.

## Input / Output

The method requires simultaneous access to both the pre-trained and fine-tuned versions of a transformer LLM, plus a text prompt. It performs a forward pass through the fine-tuned model while computing the corresponding pre-trained layer outputs at each intermediate hidden state, accumulating the last-token PTC and FTC vectors across all L layers. The final output is a single TuCo scalar in [0,1] (the ratio of accumulated FTC L1-norm to total PTC+FTC L1-norm on the last token), with the complementary PreCo = 1 − TuCo. Computation costs approximately two forward passes and is designed for research-time analysis rather than deployment.

## Method

The core contribution is an exact, assumption-free decomposition of any fine-tuned transformer into a Pre-Training Component (PTC, the output of the corresponding pre-trained layer) and a Fine-Tuning Component (FTC, the difference between fine-tuned and pre-trained layer outputs) at every layer, leveraging the residual-stream architecture. A discrete Grönwall-type bound then shows that the relative magnitude of accumulated FTC to PTC across layers controls the maximum distance between the final hidden states of the two models, providing theoretical grounding for the metric. TuCo is defined as the ratio of the L1-norm of the accumulated last-token FTC to the sum of accumulated last-token PTC and FTC norms over all layers, capturing the entire forward pass rather than just the final output. The paper further introduces FTC α-Scaling (multiplying FTC by a scalar α at each layer) as a mechanism to empirically demonstrate that modulating the fine-tuning component directly controls model behaviors and benchmark performance, and uses TuCo to show that three jailbreak families (GCG, Conjugate Prompting, Many-Shot Jailbreaking) all reduce TuCo, with successful attacks producing consistently lower values than unsuccessful ones.

## Results

TuCo achieves an AUC of 0.87 for predicting jailbreak success on Llama 2 13B (Table 2), and AUCs above 0.80 for all but two models when discriminating chat-like (HH-RLHF) from web-text (OpenWebText) prompts. Scaling the fine-tuning component (FTC) by α ∈ [0.75, 1.25] yields up to 5% MMLU accuracy improvements and a 24% increase in alignment with Christian beliefs on Llama 2 13B when α is increased by 25%. All three jailbreak attacks (GCG, Conjugate Prompting, Many-Shot Jailbreaking) significantly reduce TuCo, with TuCo decreasing monotonically as many-shot attack intensity increases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark TuCo against competing fine-tuning-contribution metrics, as no prior method exists for this purpose. Instead, it contrasts TuCo with a simpler alternative, OutputCo, which compares only the final hidden states of the pre-trained and fine-tuned models. TuCo captures internal representation changes across all layers that OutputCo misses entirely: in a constructed example (Appendix B.1), fine-tuning significantly alters intermediate hidden states while leaving the final output unchanged, yielding OutputCo = 0 but TuCo = 0.25. Empirically, on prompts with many refusal examples followed by a harmless question, TuCo increases (indicating activated fine-tuning circuits) while OutputCo decreases, confirming they measure fundamentally different phenomena. The paper also positions TuCo against prior dataset-level analyses (Wei et al. 2024; Kotha et al.; Jain et al. 2024) by providing per-prompt, inference-time quantification rather than aggregate benchmark statistics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the transformer circuits framework (Elhage et al., 2021; Olsson et al., 2022) and the residual-stream decomposition idea into a model-agnostic, exact PTC/FTC split that requires no knowledge of specific circuits, going beyond the phenomenological circuit assumption in Section 4.2. It connects to the 'competing pre-training vs. fine-tuning objectives' hypothesis of Wei et al. (2024) and Kotha et al., but provides the first direct, per-prompt, inference-time measurement of that competition via a Grönwall-type bound. Unlike probing methods (Zou et al., 2023a; Li et al., 2023) that detect specific concepts, or influence functions (Koh & Liang, 2017) that attribute outputs to individual training examples, TuCo measures the aggregate fine-tuning contribution without requiring any auxiliary training data or gradient access.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TuCo offers a practical, gradient-free diagnostic that model developers and safety researchers can use to identify prompts where fine-tuning is being attenuated, potentially informing more robust fine-tuning strategies or real-time safety monitoring. A key limitation is the requirement for simultaneous access to both pre-trained and fine-tuned checkpoints, which restricts applicability to open-source or in-house models. Open questions include whether TuCo can be integrated into deployment-time defenses, whether it generalizes to non-residual architectures, and whether the observed TuCo reduction under jailbreaks reflects a single mechanism or multiple distinct pathways that happen to share this signature.
