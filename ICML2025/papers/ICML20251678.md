## Problem

In traditional Mixture-of-Experts models, the router's decision-making is separated from the experts' execution: the router cannot directly assess which expert is best suited for a given token, making its selection an unsupervised prediction that often leads to suboptimal expert assignment and inefficient learning. This paper addresses this gap by eliminating the router entirely and letting experts autonomously determine whether they should process an input.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251678/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a hidden state vector x ∈ R^{d_model} at each MoE layer and produces a layer output h ∈ R^{d_model} that feeds into the next transformer layer, ultimately yielding a next-token probability distribution. Unusually, every expert in a layer first computes a low-dimensional projection (xW_down ∈ R^{d_low}) for the token before any selection occurs; only the top-K experts by L2 activation norm then proceed with the full forward pass, while the remaining experts abort. The low-rank factorization (d_low < d_model < d_wide) keeps the pre-computation overhead manageable, with d_low typically set to roughly one-third of d_model.

## Method

AoE removes the router and replaces it with a self-evaluation mechanism: each expert's gate weight W_g is factorized into W_down ∈ R^{d_model×d_low} and W_up ∈ R^{d_low×d_wide}, so the expert computes E_i(x) = (SiLU(xW_down^i W_up^i) ⊙ (xW_p^i)) W_o^i. All experts jointly compute the low-dimensional cache C = x·Ŵ_down via a single matrix multiplication, then are ranked by the L2 norm of their cached activations; only the top-K experts use the cache to complete the forward pass. The load-balancing auxiliary loss is adapted to operate on activation norms rather than router logits. This self-evaluating-then-partner-comparing design ensures that the entity deciding whether to process a token is the same entity that processes it, resolving the decision-execution separation inherent in router-based MoE.

## Results

On 4B-parameter MoE language models (1.18B active parameters), AoE achieves an average accuracy of 49.80 across eight downstream tasks, outperforming the traditional MoE baseline (48.06) by 1.74 points. At the smaller 732M scale (247M active), the best AoE configuration (d_low=256, with L_aux) reaches 44.46 average accuracy versus 43.71 for the best traditional MoE variant. AoE also achieves up to 97% of the training throughput of traditional MoE (47.98 vs 51.42 tokens/s/GPU at d_low=256), with modest additional memory overhead (57.32 GB vs 50.61 GB).

## Baselines & Comparisons

AoE consistently outperforms traditional MoE (with router) across all evaluated scales and tasks. At 4B parameters, AoE wins on every individual task, with the largest gains on SST-2 (+4.93) and MNLI (+3.20), and the smallest on PIQA (+0.21). At 732M parameters, the best AoE configuration (d_low=256 + L_aux, Config ⑩) achieves 44.46 average accuracy, surpassing the best traditional MoE variant (large router + L_aux, Config ④, 43.71) and even the plain MoE + L_aux baseline (Config ②, 43.68). AoE also outperforms traditional MoE under alternative routing strategies: with Top-P token-choice, AoE scores 44.29 vs 43.87, and with Expert-Choice routing, AoE scores 44.17 vs 43.89. In terms of efficiency, AoE achieves 97% of traditional MoE throughput at d_low=256, with the gap widening to 89.6% at d_low=512; the paper notes that in distributed deployments, AoE's more balanced expert load can further reduce idle time by up to 64% per layer.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the foundational sparsely-gated MoE paradigm (Shazeer et al., 2017) by eliminating the router entirely and replacing it with expert self-selection based on internal activation norms. It draws on the key-value memory interpretation of FFN layers (Geva et al., 2021) to motivate the insight that activation scale reflects an expert's capability to process a given token. The load-balancing auxiliary loss from Switch Transformers (Fedus et al., 2022) is retained with minor modifications, and the low-rank factorization of the gate projection borrows from the observation that LLM weights are inherently low-rank (Li et al., 2018; Hu et al., 2022). Unlike prior router-free approaches that assign tokens to fixed experts via hashing or domain labels, AoE lets experts dynamically and autonomously decide per-token participation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

AoE offers a conceptually cleaner MoE architecture by removing the router, which the authors argue is a source of suboptimal expert selection and inefficient training. The practical gains are modest but consistent across scales (700M to 4B parameters) and tasks, and the 97% throughput retention makes it deployable. Key open questions include how AoE's efficiency scales with increasing expert counts and sparsity (the authors note diminishing returns), whether the activation-norm signal remains informative at much larger scales, and whether the low-rank factorization overhead becomes prohibitive in very wide models. The toy experiment in the appendix provides intuitive evidence for expert specialization but remains a simplified setting.
