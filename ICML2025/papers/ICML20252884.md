## Problem

Existing Influence Function (IF) methods produce unreliable influence estimates on deep neural networks, especially with noisy training data. The paper identifies that this failure stems not from parameter change estimation errors (the focus of prior work) but from loss change estimation, specifically the sharpness of the validation risk landscape. The authors establish a theoretical upper bound on influence estimation error in terms of validation set risk and its sharpness, showing that flat validation minima are necessary for accurate influence estimation.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained model, a training set, and a validation set, and first tunes the model on the validation set using a sharpness-aware optimizer (e.g., SAM with γ=0.05–0.1) to obtain flat validation minima θ̃ before computing influence. For each training sample z_tr, it outputs a single positive-valued influence score I(z_tr, S_val) = g̃_z_tr^T H̃_val^{-1} g̃_z_tr, where the inverse Hessian is approximated by a diagonal Fisher Information matrix computed on the validation set. Unlike standard IF, the scores are exclusively positive, and discrimination between useful and harmful samples relies on the magnitude distribution rather than sign.

## Method

The proposed FVM (Flat Validation Minima) influence function modifies both components of the standard two-step IF approximation. For parameter change, it replaces the training-set Hessian H_tr with the validation-set Hessian H̃_val evaluated at the flat validation minima θ̃, yielding Δθ ≈ −ε H̃_val^{-1} g̃_z_tr. For loss change, it replaces the first-order term g_val^T Δθ with a second-order term ½ Δθ^T ∇²ℓ(z_val, θ̃) Δθ, motivated by the observation that gradients at converged models are approximately zero-mean Gaussian, causing first-order estimates to vanish and the separation parameter μ in the error bound to collapse to zero. After summing over the validation set and noting that the first-order term vanishes at the minima, the final influence simplifies to I(z_tr, S_val) = g̃_z_tr^T H̃_val^{-1} g̃_z_tr. The flat minima are obtained by solving max_{‖Δ‖≤γ} R̂_val(θ+Δ) via SAM, ASAM, F-SAM, or LPF, and the inverse Hessian is efficiently approximated using a diagonal preconditioner from the empirical Fisher Information on the validation set.

## Results

On the mislabeled sample detection task (the primary evaluation), FVM achieves 96.46% ROC AUC and 94.97% AP on CIFAR-10N (Worst setting) and 90.80% ROC AUC and 85.41% AP on CIFAR-100N (Noisy setting), outperforming all baselines. On the relabeling task, FVM reaches 86.09% top-1 accuracy on CIFAR-10N (Worst) and 70.61% on CIFAR-100N, compared to 80.35% (GEX) and 22.41% (GEX on CIFAR-100N) for the strongest baselines. In text and image generation tasks, VM and FVM consistently achieve ROC AUC above 92%, substantially exceeding TracIn (60–78%) and DataInf (57–99%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FVM outperforms all five baselines across nearly all settings. Against LiSSA (Koh & Liang, 2017), FVM gains approximately 30 percentage points in ROC AUC on CIFAR-10N Worst (96.46 vs. 65.75) and ~33 points on CIFAR-100N Noisy (90.80 vs. 57.48). TracIn (Pruthi et al., 2020) is similarly outperformed by ~31 points on CIFAR-10N Worst. DataInf (Kwon et al., 2024) shows the largest gap, with FVM achieving 96.46% vs. 55.49% ROC AUC on CIFAR-10N Worst. The strongest baseline is GEX (Kim et al., 2023), which achieves 93.28% ROC AUC on CIFAR-10N Worst; FVM still leads by 3.18 points, though GEX requires 32 tuning iterations versus 1 for FVM. EK-FAC (Grosse et al., 2023), compared in Table 10, is outperformed by FVM by 24+ points in ROC AUC on CIFAR-10N Worst (96.46 vs. 72.18). The only exception where FVM does not lead is the AP metric on CIFAR-100N Noisy, where GEX achieves 89.05% vs. FVM's 85.41%, though this advantage disappears when GEX's tuning budget is matched to FVM's.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Influence Function framework of Koh & Liang (2017) by shifting the focus from parameter change estimation (the target of prior improvements like LiSSA, TracIn, DataInf, and GEX) to loss change estimation, arguing that validation risk sharpness is the true bottleneck for reliable influence estimation. It draws a novel theoretical connection between IF estimation error and the sharpness of the validation loss landscape, then leverages Sharpness-Aware Minimization (SAM) to obtain flat validation minima where a second-order loss change approximation becomes effective. The method also departs from the standard IF by replacing the training-set Hessian with a validation-set Hessian in the parameter change term, aligning the perturbation model with the flat-minima optimization objective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The reframing of the IF bottleneck from parameter change to loss change (validated by the ablation showing the second-order loss term drives most of the performance gain) offers a new diagnostic lens for why influence functions fail on noisy data, potentially redirecting future work away from ever-more-accurate Hessian inverses. Practical limitations include the extra validation-set tuning step (adding computational cost), the fact that the proposed IF only produces positive scores (losing the traditional positive/negative influence distinction), and the reliance on a diagonal Hessian approximation whose superiority over LiSSA/DataInf may not hold universally. Open questions include how the approach scales to very large foundation models where validation-set tuning becomes expensive, and whether the flat-minima requirement can be relaxed or achieved more cheaply.
