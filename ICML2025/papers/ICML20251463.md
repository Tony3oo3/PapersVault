## Problem

Pathology image segmentation is hindered by the long-tail distribution of tissue and cell types in gigapixel whole-slide images, leading to labor-intensive annotation and poor tail-class identification. Standard diffusion models rely on Gaussian noise, whose smooth gradient profile fails to sufficiently amplify distributional disparities between distinct pathological components in the latent space, limiting their utility for segmentation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251463/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes RGB pathology image patches (from datasets ranging 151–1764 images with 3–22 tissue or 3–19 cell categories) and produces pixel-wise semantic segmentation masks. Internally, the diffusion process generates a sequence of T grayscale feature maps (one per denoising step), so each pixel is represented by a latent vector of length T; the final segmentation network (ConvNeXT) consumes this T-channel feature sequence to output the mask. For cell segmentation, metrics are computed only on foreground cells to avoid background dominance in whole-slide images.

## Method

L-Diffusion replaces the standard Gaussian noise schedule with N independent Laplace distributions (one per component), exploiting the fact that the Laplace gradient ∇p_L(x) = −sign(x)/b · p_L(x) produces steeper, more abrupt changes near zero than the Gaussian's linear-in-x gradient, thereby amplifying inter-component distributional separation in the latent space. The architecture comprises a VAE encoder, a Laplace Scheduler, a U-Net noise-prediction module, and a DAE decoder; after T denoising steps, each reconstructed feature map is converted to grayscale, yielding per-pixel latent vectors v ∈ R^(1×T). A contrastive learning loss (InfoNCE-style with cosine similarity and temperature τ ∈ [0.05, 0.1]) is applied to these pixel latent vectors to further sharpen inter-component separation and intra-component cohesion. A ConvNeXT segmentation head then maps the T-channel enhanced feature sequence to the final mask, trained with DICE loss. The two-stage training (diffusion + contrastive, then segmentation) reduces annotation dependency and specifically improves tail-class accuracy, achieving up to 26.74% MPA improvement over prior methods.

## Results

On tissue segmentation, L-Diffusion achieves DICE scores of 82.38% (CRCD), 92.11% (PUMA), and 89.24% (BCSS), surpassing the best prior method by up to 7.16% DICE, 26.74% MPA, and 16.52% mIoU. On cell segmentation, it reaches 96.11% DICE on CRCD, 88.57% on PUMA, and 94.78% on PanNuke, with improvements of up to 20.09% DICE and 14.42% mIoU over the strongest baseline. On the Massachusetts-Building remote sensing dataset, L-Diffusion attains 80.30% IoU, 88.98% F1, and 96.52% Precision, outperforming all compared methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

L-Diffusion outperforms all 15 baselines across every metric on all five datasets. In tissue segmentation, the closest competitors are SAMPath (77.54% DICE on CRCD, 84.95% on PUMA) and UN-SAM (80.89% DICE on BCSS), yet L-Diffusion exceeds them by 4.84–7.16% DICE and up to 26.74% MPA (on PUMA, where SAMPath scores only 57.79% vs. L-Diffusion's 88.03%). In cell segmentation, SAMPath leads prior work on CRCD (79.02% DICE) and BONUS on PUMA (78.44% DICE), but L-Diffusion achieves 96.11% and 88.57% respectively, yielding gains of 17.09% and 10.13% DICE. On PanNuke, DenseASPP is the strongest prior method (81.00% DICE), still trailing L-Diffusion's 94.78% by 13.78%. For remote sensing, UANet is the best baseline (76.41% IoU, 87.94% Precision), while L-Diffusion reaches 80.30% IoU and 96.52% Precision. Notably, the largest relative gains appear on MPA and mIoU metrics, suggesting L-Diffusion's Laplace-based distributional separation is particularly effective for tail-class and minority-component identification.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the DDPM framework (Ho et al., 2020) by replacing the standard Gaussian noise schedule with multiple Laplace distributions, one per tissue/cell component, and by repurposing the sequence of intermediate reconstructed feature maps (rather than just the final denoised output) as the input to a downstream segmentation network. It departs from prior diffusion-based segmentation methods (SegDiff, SegRefiner, MedSegDiff) that use diffusion purely as a generative or refinement mechanism, instead treating the diffusion trajectory itself as a source of discriminative pixel-level representations. The contrastive learning objective applied to pixel latent vectors across diffusion steps is a novel combination, drawing on the broader contrastive learning literature but applied in a way not previously seen in diffusion-based segmentation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in reducing annotation burden for gigapixel pathology images: the paper shows that 30% of annotated samples yield performance close to full annotation, which is meaningful given the extreme cost of expert pathology labeling. The long-tail distribution challenge (rare tissue/cell types being poorly segmented) is directly addressed by the Laplace distribution's sharper gradients, which amplify distributional separation for minority components. Open questions include whether the approach truly generalizes beyond the four pathology datasets tested, whether the contrastive learning component can be made fully unsupervised (as the authors acknowledge it currently requires some annotations), and whether the substantial computational overhead of the multi-stage pipeline (VAE + diffusion + contrastive learning + ConvNeXT) is justified relative to simpler architectures in clinical deployment.
