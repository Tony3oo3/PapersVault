## Problem

Model alignment via SFT and DPO depends on high-quality synthetic data, but relying on a single strong proprietary model (e.g., GPT-4) introduces bias, reproducibility concerns, and cost. Naive strategies for combining multiple open-source models—such as concatenating all their outputs or randomly sampling one—fail to outperform a single capable model. The paper asks whether the structured Mixture-of-Agents (MoA) architecture can be repurposed to generate alignment data and serve as a reward model, leveraging collective intelligence without any proprietary model.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252583/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For the SFT stage, the method takes instruction prompts (from Ultrafeedback ~61k and a 5k subset of Ultrachat-200k) and produces one synthetic response per instruction via the final MoA layer. For the DPO stage, it takes 5 candidate responses sampled from the SFT model at temperature 0.8 per instruction (6k instructions from Ultrafeedback) and produces a single chosen/rejected pair by ranking all 10 pairwise comparisons through the MoA reward model. The final output is an aligned 8B/9B LLM (LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct or Gemma-2-9B-it) that can be deployed as a standalone model at ~5% of MoA's inference cost.

## Method

MoAA is a two-stage alignment recipe built on a two-layer proposer-aggregator MoA architecture. In Stage 1 (MoAA-SFT), four open-source proposers (WizardLM-8x22B, Qwen2-72B-Instruct, Gemma-2-27B-it, LLaMA-3.1-70B-Instruct) generate reference responses, which a single aggregator (Qwen1.5-110B-Chat) synthesizes into one high-quality response per instruction; the target model is then fine-tuned on this synthetic data. In Stage 2 (MoAA-DPO), a different MoA configuration (Gemma-2-27B-it, LLaMA-3.1-70B-Instruct, Qwen2-72B-Instruct as proposers; Qwen2-72B-Instruct as aggregator) acts as a generative reward model, performing pairwise comparisons with position-bias mitigation (dual evaluation with swapped order) and a criteria-filtering step that dynamically selects the three most relevant evaluation dimensions per query. The key architectural insight is that MoA's iterative aggregation—where each layer's models see and refine the previous layer's outputs—produces substantially higher-quality data than naive ensembles (Combined 5, Random 5, Best-of-5 via ArmoRM) or even GPT-4o, and the strongest model in the MoA can be fine-tuned on MoA-generated data to surpass its own initial performance, enabling a self-improvement loop without external supervision.

## Results

MoAA-DPO improves LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct from 22.33 to 57.23 on AlpacaEval 2 (LC) and from 19.5 to 48.3 on Arena-Hard, while Gemma-2-9B-it improves from 47.43 to 63.75 on AlpacaEval 2 and from 42.0 to 55.6 on Arena-Hard. The SFT stage alone roughly doubles LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct's win rates (22.33→43.77 on AlpacaEval 2, 19.5→40.8 on Arena-Hard). Notably, Gemma-2-9B-it-MoAA-DPO (63.75) surpasses the MoA-Data-Generator reference (62.50) on AlpacaEval 2, and the MoA reward model achieves 85.6 average on RewardBench, outperforming the best individual open-source model (84.0) and GPT-4o (84.6).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MoAA-DPO substantially outperforms the base models: LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct gains +34.9 on AlpacaEval 2 and +28.8 on Arena-Hard, while Gemma-2-9B-it gains +16.3 and +13.6 respectively. Against GPT-4o-05-13 as a data generator, MoAA-SFT achieves higher AlpacaEval 2 (43.77 vs. ~35) and Arena-Hard (40.8 vs. ~38) scores while costing 17.3% less ($365.95 vs. $429.45). As a reward model, MoA (85.6 avg on RewardBench) beats ArmoRM-Llama3-8B-v0.1 (90.4 avg) on Safety (+0.1) and Reasoning (−9.6) but loses overall; however, the DPO model tuned with MoA preference data outperforms ArmoRM-tuned on Arena-Hard (48.3 vs. 42.3) and MT-Bench (8.58 vs. 8.56), with only a 0.56-point deficit on AlpacaEval 2. MoAA-DPO also surpasses MagPie-Pro-DPO (50.10 on AlpacaEval, 25.7 on Arena-Hard) and Meta-RewardingLM Iter4 (39.44, 29.1) by wide margins. On the self-improvement front, Gemma-2-9B-it fine-tuned on a small-scale MoA (itself as aggregator) outperforms its own original scores (54.19 vs. 48.54 on AlpacaEval, 44 vs. 40.6 on Arena-Hard), demonstrating that the method can push a model beyond its own initial capability without external stronger supervision.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Mixture-of-Agents (MoA) proposer-aggregator architecture—previously designed for inference-time response generation—into a training-time alignment pipeline. It builds directly on MoA (Wang et al., 2024c) for the layered collaboration structure, on DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) for the Stage 2 optimization objective, and on ArmoRM's criteria filtering (Wang et al., 2024a) adapted here as a prompt-based rather than trained filtering step. The key departure from prior alignment work is replacing a single strong proprietary model (e.g., GPT-4) or human-annotated data with a collective of open-source LLMs, and replacing a separately trained reward model with MoA itself as a generative judge.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The self-improvement pipeline—where the strongest model in the MoA ensemble (Gemma-2-9B-it) surpasses its own baseline after fine-tuning on MoA-generated data—suggests a path for open-source LLMs to push their own frontier without dependence on stronger external supervision. The cost analysis (17.3% savings vs. GPT-4o while outperforming it) and inference efficiency (90.6% of MoA quality at 5.4% of inference cost) make this practically attractive for resource-constrained settings. Open questions include whether the architecture search (currently manual/empirical) can be automated at scale, how the approach generalizes to domain-specific tasks (math, code) where the current instruction sets are chat-focused, and whether the self-improvement loop can be iterated without degradation.
