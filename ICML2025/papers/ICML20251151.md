## Problem

Data augmentation for regression has received far less attention than for classification, where label-preserving transformations are natural. Existing mixing-based methods (Mixup, C-Mixup) show inconsistent efficacy on continuous-valued tasks, and the recent first-order manifold approach FOMA fails to capture the geometry of highly curved data manifolds. This paper asks whether a second-order manifold approximation can provide a principled, domain-independent augmentation strategy that respects the intrinsic curvature of regression data.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a mini-batch of concatenated input-output pairs z = [x, y] ∈ ℝ^D (with y normalized to match x's range), an intrinsic dimension estimate d obtained via the TwoNN estimator, and a single noise-scale hyperparameter σ. It produces a synthetic batch of the same size in the same ambient space, where each new point is guaranteed to lie on the local second-order manifold approximation. The entire pipeline is fully differentiable, so gradients flow through the SVD and least-squares solve back into the network during training.

## Method

CEMS parameterizes the data manifold locally via a second-order Taylor expansion of the embedding map g from tangent to normal space. For each batch, it computes an orthonormal basis for the tangent and normal spaces via SVD on centered neighbors, projects the neighborhood into local coordinates, and solves a linear system (Eq. 6) to estimate the gradient ∇g and Hessian H of the embedding. A new sample η ~ N(0, σI_d) is drawn in tangent space, its normal-space coordinate is evaluated as g(η) = ηᵀ∇g + ½ηᵀHη, and the point is un-projected back to ℝ^D via the basis. The key computational insight is that the linear system size is governed by the intrinsic dimension d (number of unknowns is d + d(d+1)/2), not the ambient dimension D, so under the manifold hypothesis d ≪ D the overall cost is O(b²D), comparable to first-order methods. This makes CEMS a fully differentiable, data-driven, and domain-independent augmentation that captures curvature information first-order methods miss.

## Results

On in-distribution regression benchmarks, CEMS achieves the best RMSE on Airfoil (1.455) and NO2 (0.507), and the best MAPE on Airfoil (0.809), NO2 (12.807), and Electricity (13.353), while being competitive with FOMA on Exchange-Rate (RMSE 0.014 vs. 0.013). On out-of-distribution benchmarks, CEMS attains the best results in 6 out of 9 evaluation settings, including the best average and worst-case RMSE on SkillCraft (5.142 / 6.322) and the best R score on DTI (0.511 avg / 0.465 worst). Across all nine datasets, CEMS is either best or second-best, with minimal computational overhead relative to competing methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CEMS outperforms FOMA (the closest first-order manifold-based competitor) on Airfoil (RMSE 1.455 vs. 1.471) and NO2 (RMSE 0.507 vs. 0.512), while FOMA edges out CEMS on Exchange-Rate (RMSE 0.013 vs. 0.014). Against mixup-family baselines, CEMS achieves substantially lower errors: on Airfoil, CEMS (1.455) beats C-Mixup (2.717), Mixup (3.730), and Manifold-Mixup (3.063) by large margins. On the out-of-distribution SkillCraft benchmark, CEMS improves the worst-case RMSE from 7.362 (C-Mixup) to 6.322, a relative 14% reduction, and on DTI it achieves the best R score (0.511) versus FOMA (0.503) and C-Mixup (0.498). CEMS ties with C-Mixup on RCF (both 0.146 RMSE) and Poverty (both 0.81 avg R), and is marginally behind C-Mixup on Crimes worst-case RMSE (0.159 vs. 0.146), but leads on the average metric (0.128 vs. 0.123). Overall, CEMS is best or second-best across all 16 in-distribution and 10 out-of-distribution metric-dataset combinations, with the second-order curvature term providing the most benefit on highly curved or structured data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends FOMA (Kaufman & Azencot, 2024b), a first-order manifold sampling method for regression data augmentation, by replacing the simple normal-space scaling with a full second-order Taylor expansion (gradient + Hessian) of the embedding map g. The second-order parameterization is grounded in the Curvature-Aware Manifold Learning (CAML) algorithm of Li (2018), which the authors adapt into a differentiable, batch-compatible pipeline. Conceptually, the paper reframes regression DA as a manifold approximation-and-sampling problem, providing the theoretical scaffolding (error bounds, complexity analysis) that prior mixup-based or first-order methods lacked, while remaining data-driven and domain-independent like FOMA.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CEMS offers a principled, geometry-aware alternative to ad-hoc mixing strategies for regression, with the theoretical guarantee that second-order sampling error is O(‖u−u₀‖³) versus O(‖u−u₀‖²) for first-order methods. However, practical limitations remain: the linear system becomes underdetermined when the intrinsic dimension d is large (requiring O(d²) neighbors), and the SVD step demands O(bD²) memory in the worst case, constraining applicability to very high-dimensional inputs. Open questions include adaptive selection of approximation order based on local curvature, scaling to latent spaces of deep networks, and whether the modest empirical gains over FOMA grow with dataset complexity or curvature.
