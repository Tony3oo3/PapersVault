## Problem

Extending Double Machine Learning (DML) to adjust for confounding from non-tabular data (images, text) requires pre-trained neural network representations, but naively plugging these into DML is theoretically unjustified: representations are only identifiable up to invertible linear transformations (ILTs), which destroy the sparsity and additivity assumptions that standard convergence-rate results rely on. The paper formalizes when pre-trained representations are valid for adjustment and identifies what structural conditions actually enable fast nuisance estimation in this setting.

## Input / Output

The method consumes raw non-tabular data (chest X-ray images at 224×224, or movie review text truncated to 128 tokens), a binary treatment indicator, and a continuous outcome. Pre-trained models (Densenet-121 from TorchXRayVision for images, BERT-base for text) extract fixed last-layer representations of 1024 and 768 dimensions respectively, which serve as the covariates for all downstream nuisance estimation. The output is a DML ATE point estimate with asymptotically valid 95% confidence intervals, produced via cross-fitted orthogonalized scores that combine estimated outcome regression and propensity score functions.

## Method

The paper establishes that smoothness and low intrinsic dimensionality of the data manifold are invariant under ILTs, while sparsity and additivity are not, ruling out standard high-dimensional assumptions for latent features. It then proposes Assumption 5.2, which decomposes the target function as a composition of a smooth embedding map ψ from a low-dimensional manifold M into R^p followed by a Hierarchical Composition Model (HCM) of level k, and proves this assumption is itself ILT-invariant. Theorem 5.5 shows that feed-forward ReLU networks can approximate such functions at rate n^{-s*/(2s*+p*)} (up to log factors), where (s*, p*) is the worst-case smoothness-dimension pair in the constraint set, by exploiting the manifold structure to reduce the effective dimension from the ambient d to d_M. Theorem 5.7 then combines these rates with the DML cross-fitting framework to prove asymptotic normality of the ATE estimator under the condition that the product of the minimum smoothness-to-dimension ratios for the outcome and propensity score functions exceeds 1/4.

## Results

Across all experiments (true ATE = 2), DML with appropriately chosen nuisance estimators achieves unbiased ATE estimates with well-calibrated 95% confidence intervals, while the Naive estimator and all ILT-non-invariant methods (Lasso, Random Forest) exhibit substantial negative bias. In the Label Confounding setting, DML with linear nuisance estimators is the only method yielding valid inference; in the Complex Confounding setting, DML with neural network nuisance estimators (100 layers × 50 neurons, ReLU) is the sole estimator with both low bias and proper coverage. The intrinsic dimension of pre-trained X-ray representations is estimated at approximately 12 (vs. ambient dimension 1024), and DML with pre-trained Densenet-121 features produces unbiased estimates where DML with CNNs trained from scratch on 500 images fails.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares six ATE estimation approaches: Naive (unadjusted), Oracle (adjusts for true label), S-Learner, and DML with three nuisance estimator types (Linear, Lasso, Random Forest) plus a neural network variant. In the Label Confounding experiments, DML with unpenalized linear nuisance estimators is the only method producing unbiased estimates with correct 95% CI coverage, while DML with Lasso or Random Forest fails due to their reliance on sparsity/additivity assumptions that are broken by the non-identifiability of representations under invertible linear transformations. In the Complex Confounding experiments, DML with neural network nuisance estimators (100×50 ReLU network) achieves both low bias and valid inference, whereas the S-Learner (NN) still produces overly narrow confidence intervals and DML (RF) remains biased. In the pre-training comparison, DML using pre-trained Densenet-121 representations yields unbiased ATE estimates with well-calibrated CIs, while DML using CNNs trained from scratch on only 500 X-ray images shows strong negative bias, demonstrating the critical benefit of transfer learning in low-sample regimes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Double Machine Learning (DML) framework from tabular features to non-tabular data by formalizing when pre-trained neural network representations can validly serve as confounding adjustments. It builds directly on Christgau & Hansen (2024) for sufficiency conditions on representations and on Kohler & Langer (2021) for HCM-based neural network convergence rates, but departs from both by showing that standard high-dimensional assumptions (sparsity, additivity) are fundamentally incompatible with the non-identifiability of representations under invertible linear transformations. The key conceptual departure is proposing a new assumption (Assumption 5.2) that combines low intrinsic manifold dimensionality with hierarchical composition structure, yielding convergence rates that are invariant to the ILT equivalence class of representations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical justification for using pre-trained representations in DML-based causal inference, identifying precisely why common nuisance estimators (lasso, random forests) fail in this setting and why neural networks succeed. This has direct practical implications for medical and social science applications where confounding information resides in images or text. Key limitations include the restriction to a single non-tabular confounding source, binary treatment, and ATE (rather than ATT or CATE), and the experiments rely on simulated confounding rather than real causal structures. Open questions include extending the framework to multiple modalities, where the interplay between representations from different networks introduces additional structural assumptions.
