## Problem

Existing cross-problem neural VRP solvers (MTPOMO, MVMoE, RouteFinder) are constraint-unaware: they do not explicitly represent which of the five VRP constraints (capacity, open route, backhaul, duration limit, time window) are active, limiting their ability to distinguish between problem variants that share identical input structures. Additionally, their transformer encoders maintain full global connectivity throughout node encoding, diluting representations with irrelevant nodes and failing to focus on the key node pairs most likely to be adjacent in the solution.

## Input / Output

The model takes a fully-connected graph with 2D node coordinates, customer demands (normalized to [0,1]), a 5-dimensional multi-hot constraint vector indicating which of the five constraints are active, and per-node time windows and service times when applicable. During autoregressive decoding, the context embedding at each step also incorporates remaining linehaul/backhaul capacity, current time, remaining route length, and an open-route indicator. The output is a sequence of vehicle sub-routes (each starting and ending at the depot unless the open-route constraint is active), with the objective being total tour length.

## Method

CaDA introduces two key components into a transformer encoder-decoder framework. First, a constraint prompt: the 5-dimensional multi-hot constraint vector is mapped through a two-layer MLP to a d_h-dimensional embedding that is concatenated to node embeddings in the global branch, giving the encoder explicit awareness of which constraints are active. Second, a dual-attention mechanism with two parallel branches: a global branch using standard MHA with Softmax (capturing graph-wide information) and a sparse branch using Top-k sparse attention that zeros out all attention scores below the k-th highest per row (focusing on the most promising adjacent node pairs). Both branches use SwiGLU feed-forward layers and RMSNorm, and fuse information at each layer via learned linear projections. Training uses REINFORCE with a POMO-style shared baseline and mixed-batch training across all 16 VRP variants simultaneously, with the decoder computing action probabilities via MHA between a context embedding and feasible node embeddings, clipped by a tanh function.

## Results

On 16 VRP variants with 50 nodes, CaDA achieves an average gap of 1.714% to the best traditional solver (HGS-PyVRP), outperforming the second-best neural solver (RF-TE) by 0.26% in average gap. At 100 nodes, CaDA achieves a 1.578% average gap on CVRP (best among neural solvers) and surpasses the second-best method by 0.32% in average gap across all 16 variants. CaDA ranks first among all neural solvers on all 16 VRP50 variants and 13 out of 16 VRP100 variants, while solving instances in ~2s (n=50) and ~8s (n=100) versus 10.4m and 20.8m for traditional solvers.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CaDA outperforms all three cross-problem neural baselines (MTPOMO, MVMoE, and all RouteFinder variants RF-POMO/RF-MoE/RF-TE) on every one of the 16 VRP variants at n=50, and on 13 out of 16 at n=100. The margin over the strongest neural baseline (RF-TE) is 0.26% average gap at n=50 and 0.32% at n=100, with the largest individual gains on complex multi-constraint variants (e.g., VRPBLTW: 1.117% vs. 1.517% gap at n=50; VRPTW: 1.536% vs. 1.933% at n=50). Against the traditional HGS-PyVRP solver, CaDA remains 1.2–5% worse in objective value but is roughly 300–1500× faster (2s vs. 10.4m at n=50; 8s vs. 20.8m at n=100). On the CVRPLib real-world benchmark (99 instances, n=16–200), CaDA with ×32 prompt augmentation achieves a 2.00–2.02% gap, outperforming RF-TE (2.58–2.86%) and MVMoE (2.83–3.94%). For zero-shot generalization to unseen constraints (Multi-Depot, Mixed Backhaul), CaDA achieves the lowest average gap (39.34% MD, 8.46% MB) among all compared methods, and with ×32 augmentation further reduces MD gap to 28.86%.

## Relation to Prior Work

CaDA extends the encoder-decoder attention model framework for VRPs (Kool et al., 2019) and the POMO training recipe (Kwon et al., 2020) into the cross-problem setting established by MTPOMO, MVMoE, and RouteFinder. It departs from these prior cross-problem solvers in two specific ways: introducing a lightweight constraint prompt (a 5-dimensional multi-hot vector passed through an MLP) to make the encoder aware of which constraints are active, and replacing the single global-attention encoder with a dual-attention mechanism that pairs standard softmax attention with a Top-k sparse branch. The modern transformer components (SwiGLU, RMSNorm, pre-norm) are inherited directly from RouteFinder, and the mixed-batch multi-task training protocol follows the same setup.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The constraint prompt is a remarkably simple mechanism that demonstrably shifts attention patterns in a constraint-appropriate way (e.g., reducing depot attention in OVRP), and the Top-k sparse branch outperforms more principled alternatives like sparsemax and 1.5-entmax, suggesting that hard thresholding is well-suited to the discrete adjacency structure of routing solutions. However, the practical gains are modest (0.26–0.32% average gap over the second-best neural solver), and zero-shot generalization to genuinely unseen constraints (multi-depot, mixed backhaul) remains poor at ~39% gap, indicating that the constraint prompt captures only surface-level constraint identity rather than deeper structural understanding. Open questions include whether the dual-attention design scales to larger instance sizes or more diverse constraint families, and whether the sparse branch's fixed Top-k can be made adaptive per problem variant.
