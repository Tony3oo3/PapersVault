## Problem

Two families of theories describe feature learning in neural networks—kernel rescaling (predicting a scalar change to the NNGP kernel) and kernel adaptation (predicting directional, structural changes)—but their relationship and respective domains of validity have remained unresolved. This paper asks how these qualitatively different descriptions can both be valid and provides a unified framework that recovers each as a limit of a single theory.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252085/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The theory takes a single hidden-layer network with training pairs (x_α, y_α), a test input x*, and scaling parameters (width N, input dimension D, scaling exponent γ ∈ [1,2], regularization κ₀) as input. It produces closed-form expressions for the mean network output, output covariance, training and test discrepancies, and a directional feature learning measure Φ(φ̂) that quantifies amplification along specific feature directions. Unusually, the same framework yields different levels of approximation (tree-level vs. one-loop) depending on the scaling regime, and the output kernel is not merely a scalar rescaling but a matrix-valued adaptation that encodes directional information from the training data.

## Method

The paper recasts the Bayesian posterior of network outputs into a minimization problem over an 'order parameter' (the mean conjugate field, proportional to output discrepancies) via the Legendre transform of the cumulant-generating function, yielding an effective action Γ. In mean-field scaling (γ=2), a saddle-point (tree-level) approximation of Γ suffices because output fluctuations vanish as 1/N; in standard scaling (γ=1), a one-loop correction involving the Hessian of the action captures non-negligible fluctuations. The key insight is that the dimensionality of the order parameter determines the resulting theory: a scalar order parameter (‖w‖²) yields kernel rescaling, while the full P-dimensional discrepancy vector yields directional kernel adaptation. For linear networks in the proportional limit, the adaptive kernel reduces to a rescaled NNGP for mean outputs (via the matrix-determinant lemma), reconciling the two views; for non-linear networks, the adaptive approach predicts directional effects in mean outputs and changes to sample complexity class that rescaling theories cannot capture.

## Results

The paper's primary contribution is theoretical: it derives a multi-scale adaptive framework that unifies kernel rescaling and adaptive feature learning theories across all scaling regimes (γ ∈ [1,2]). For linear networks, the adaptive theory's mean output predictions reduce exactly to a rescaled NNGP kernel (Eq. 23), explaining why rescaling theories appear successful. However, the adaptive theory additionally captures directional feature learning in the output covariance (Fig. 4) and, for non-linear networks, predicts a change in sample complexity class (e.g., learning a cubic target component at P ~ O(D) rather than P ~ O(D³), Fig. 5). Empirically, the tree-level and one-loop approximations accurately match training and test discrepancies on Ising tasks and MNIST across the full scaling spectrum, while NNGP predictions deviate significantly in the mean-field regime.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the NNGP baseline, the multi-scale adaptive theory clearly outperforms in predicting network outputs: on the Ising task in mean-field scaling (Fig. 2), NNGP fails to match empirical training and test discrepancies, while the tree-level adaptive prediction aligns closely with measurements. Against the kernel rescaling theory of Li & Sompolinsky (2021), the paper shows that for linear networks the mean output predictions are equivalent in the proportional limit (the one-loop solution agrees with the rescaling prediction, Fig. 3), but the adaptive theory goes further by capturing directional feature learning in the output covariance (Fig. 4), where the rescaling theory predicts isotropic behavior independent of direction. For non-linear networks (erf activation, Fig. 5), the adaptive theory (via VGA approximation) correctly predicts that the network learns the cubic component of the target at P ~ O(D), while both NNGP and rescaling predict the non-linearity would not be learned without P ~ O(D³) samples. Thus the adaptive theory wins on directional covariance structure and non-linear sample complexity, ties on linear mean outputs, and both outperform NNGP across all tested regimes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate theoretical families for describing feature learning in neural networks: kernel rescaling theories (Li & Sompolinsky, 2021; Pacelli et al., 2023) and kernel adaptation theories (Seroussi et al., 2023; Fischer et al., 2024b; Rubin et al., 2024). Using statistical field theory methods (Legendre transforms, saddle-point and loop expansions from Helias & Dahmen, 2020), it shows both approaches derive from the same joint posterior distribution of network outputs, differing only in the dimensionality of the chosen order parameter (scalar Q vs. high-dimensional mean conjugate field). The framework generalizes prior adaptive approaches, which were restricted to specific scaling regimes, by systematically including fluctuation corrections that bridge mean-field (γ=2) and standard (γ=1) scaling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper resolves a fundamental open question in NN theory—how two qualitatively different descriptions of feature learning (isotropic rescaling vs. directional adaptation) can both be valid—by showing rescaling is a special-case approximation of the adaptive theory for linear network means. This clarifies the boundary conditions under which each theory applies and reveals that directional FL manifests in output covariances (linear networks) and in sample complexity class changes (non-linear networks), phenomena invisible to rescaling. Limitations include restriction to single hidden-layer networks and primarily linear activations for the main results; extending to deeper architectures (CNNs, transformers, residual networks) and incorporating input-data fluctuations are identified as important open directions.
