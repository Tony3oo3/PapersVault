## Problem

While learning-based spoofing attacks on Red-Green LLM watermarks have been shown to successfully forge watermarked text, no prior work has proposed post-hoc methods to distinguish spoofed from genuinely watermarked text. This paper identifies that all current learning-based spoofers leave observable artifacts in their output—specifically, a dependence between token color and context frequency that is absent in genuine watermarked text—and constructs statistical tests to exploit this gap.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text ω of length T flagged as watermarked by the detector D_ξ, the private key ξ, and (for the Reprompting method) access to the watermarked model M to generate a reference continuation from a 25-token prefix. Multiple texts from the same source may be concatenated to increase T. The output is a p-value from a two-sided Z-test and a binary rejection decision at a chosen significance level α. The (h+1)-gram score variant requires a reference corpus (e.g., C4) to approximate n-gram frequencies, while the unigram score variant only needs token frequencies from human text.

## Method

The key insight is that a spoofer can only reliably emit a green token when the surrounding context appears in its training data D, creating a measurable dependence between the color sequence X and context-frequency scores Y that is absent in genuine watermarked text. The test statistic S is the Spearman rank correlation (with Fisher arctanh transformation) between the observed color sequence and a score sequence, with repeated h-grams removed to enforce independence. Two instantiations are proposed: the Standard method (using unigram frequency scores, valid when cross-independence between X and Y holds, e.g., for h=3 SumHash) and the Reprompting method (using (h+1)-gram frequency scores, where the model M is prompted with a 25-token prefix to generate a reference sequence ω′, and the Z-statistic is computed as the difference S(ω_{>c}) − S(ω′) normalized by combined variance). Under the null hypothesis, both statistics follow N(0,1), enabling rigorous Type 1 error control, while under the alternative (spoofed text) the correlation is systematically shifted, yielding arbitrarily high power as T grows.

## Results

At T=3000 tokens, the proposed statistical tests achieve >90% True Positive Rate (TPR) at a 1% False Positive Rate (FPR) for both Stealing and Distillation spoofing methods across all context sizes (h=1,2,3). For example, Stealing with h=1 reaches 100% TPR at 1% FPR, while Distillation with h=2 reaches 100% TPR at 1% FPR. The Reprompting method consistently outperforms the Standard method (e.g., Stealing h=3 at T=500: 47% vs. 27% TPR at 1% FPR), though the Standard method avoids the computational cost of reprompting the model. Type 1 error is properly controlled, with experimental FPR closely matching the nominal α across all settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper is the first to propose a post-hoc detection method for watermark spoofing, so there are no prior detection baselines to compare against. The key comparison is between the two proposed test instantiations: the Reprompting method (with (h+1)-gram score) and the Standard method (with unigram score). The Reprompting method achieves substantially higher power across all settings (e.g., Stealing h=3 at T=500: 47% vs. 27% TPR at 1% FPR; at T=1000: 85% vs. 55%), at the cost of requiring an additional model generation call. The Standard method is only applicable when cross-independence between the color sequence and scores holds (verified for h=3 with SumHash), making it a lighter-weight but less powerful option. Both methods successfully detect both Stealing and Distillation spoofing, confirming that the detected artifacts are a fundamental limitation of learning-based spoofing rather than specific to one attack. The test generalizes across watermark variants (SumHash, SelfHash), watermarked models (LLaMA2-7B, Mistral-7B, LLaMA3-8B), spoofer models (Mistral-7B, Pythia-1.4B, Gemma-2B, Qwen2.5-7B), and prompt datasets (C4, Dolly), with consistent performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the KGW Red-Green watermarking framework (Kirchenbauer et al., 2023) and the spoofing attacks demonstrated by Jovanović et al. (2024, Stealing), Gu et al. (2024, Distillation), and Zhang et al. (2024, integer programming). While prior work focused on demonstrating that these attacks succeed in fooling the watermark detector, this paper is the first to address the inverse problem: post-hoc statistical detection of whether a flagged text was genuinely watermarked or spoofed. The method repurposes classical hypothesis-testing tools (Spearman correlation, Fisher transformation, Z-tests) in a novel application to watermark forensics, with the key conceptual insight that all learning-based spoofers share a common vulnerability arising from finite training data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Since watermarks are actively deployed in consumer LLMs (e.g., SynthID-Text) and embraced by regulators, the ability to detect spoofing provides a practical mitigation that preserves watermark credibility without redesigning the watermarking scheme itself. The finding that all current learning-based spoofers—despite fundamentally different mechanisms—leave the same detectable artifact suggests spoofing is less effective than previously assumed. Key limitations include the absence of a theoretical power guarantee, the requirement for sufficiently long text (or concatenation of texts), and the assumption that the private key has not been leaked. An open question is whether future adaptive spoofing methods that dynamically learn vocabulary splits could avoid leaving these artifacts, which the authors identify as a natural next attack direction.
