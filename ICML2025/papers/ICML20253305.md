## Problem

User studies are essential for evaluating whether XAI methods satisfy societal desiderata (trust, fairness, downstream task performance), yet the current state of XAI user studies is methodologically poor—most lack reproducibility, baselines, placebo controls, and real-world fidelity. This deficiency, combined with an overreliance on functional evaluation, means the field has little reliable understanding of when, why, and for whom XAI methods actually work in practice.

## Input / Output

The analysis takes 607 XAI papers featuring user studies as input, collected via three complementary search strategies: a direct Scopus keyword search (221 papers), an indirect snowballing search from 18 review/survey papers (358 additional papers), and a targeted search of four top ML venues (AAAI, ICLR, ICML, NeurIPS, 2020–2024; 47 papers, 28 new). The output is a multi-dimensional characterization of user study quality across three paper groups (pre-2020, 2021+, top-4 venues), reporting proportions for reproducibility, participant type, evaluation type, fidelity level, and methodological features, with 95% posterior confidence intervals from Binomial-Beta Bayesian models.

## Method

The paper combines a systematic empirical analysis with a position argument. For the empirical component, the authors manually coded 607 papers on participant count and type, Nauta et al. evaluation categories, fidelity level (high/medium/low/absent context), objective vs. subjective evaluation, and methodological quality indicators (reproducibility, pre-registration, pre-testing, validated questionnaires, baselines, placebo controls, limitations discussion, attention checks). A random subsample of 116 papers (30 per non-top-4 group, all 56 top-4 papers) received deeper coding, with proportions estimated via a Binomial-Beta Bayesian model (Beta(1/2, 1/2) prior). The position argument draws on a literature review of XAI-specific challenges—stakeholder diversity, personalization, the placebo effect, crowdsourcing data quality, and the unvalidated link between functional metrics and real-world performance—to argue that the field must raise methodological standards for user studies, create dedicated venues for user-centric ML research, and reduce overreliance on functional evaluation as a proxy for societal desiderata.

## Results

The analysis of 607 XAI papers with user studies reveals that most studies have fewer than 50 participants, with over two-thirds using crowdsourcing platforms (primarily Amazon MTurk) or student samples. A striking finding is that very few user studies from top-4 ML venues (NeurIPS, ICLR, ICML, AAAI) contain enough information to be considered reproducible, in stark contrast to 50–90% reproducibility estimated for the broader paper set. Preregistration and pretesting are rare across all groups, validated questionnaires appear in less than half of studies, placebo-controlled designs are nearly absent, and at least a minimal discussion of limitations is present in only about half of studies. Most studies evaluate user understanding on toy applications or without an application context, with very few high-fidelity studies reflecting real-world deployment.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not compare a proposed method against baselines; instead, it contrasts the quality and characteristics of user studies across three publication groups: papers up to 2020, papers from 2021 onward, and papers from top-4 ML venues (2020+). The top-4 group stands out negatively: its user studies are far less reproducible, lean more heavily toward crowdsourcing or unstated participant types, include fewer domain experts, and are more frequently conducted without an application context. The 2021+ group shows modest improvement over the pre-2020 group in reproducibility, limitation discussion, pre-testing, and attention checks, but the gap remains large. The paper also contrasts functional evaluation (no human subjects) with user-study-based evaluation, arguing that functional metrics lack validation against downstream task performance and cannot compare XAI methods with different output types (e.g., feature importance vs. counterfactuals vs. heatmaps), making user studies indispensable despite their methodological challenges.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes and extends the evaluation taxonomy of Doshi-Velez & Kim (2017; 2018) and the comprehensive survey by Nauta et al. (2023) by shifting focus from *what* evaluation methods exist to *how well* user studies are actually conducted. It adopts the societal-desiderata model of Speith & Langer (2023) as its theoretical backbone and aligns with the broader call for more empirical rigor in ML articulated by Herrmann et al. (2024). The paper's distinctive contribution is a large-scale empirical audit of 607 XAI papers with user studies, introducing a fidelity categorization (high/medium/low/none) and quantifying methodological gaps (reproducibility, baselines, placebo controls) that prior surveys identified qualitatively but did not measure at scale.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

With over 14,000 XAI publications and rapid legislative oversight of AI systems, the paper's central claim—that our understanding of XAI's practical value is undermined by poorly designed user studies—carries significant implications for how the field allocates research effort. Key open questions include: how to validate functional evaluation metrics against real-world downstream performance, how to develop practical placebo-controlled study designs for diverse explanation types, and whether crowdsourced or student-based findings generalize to domain experts. The paper's limitation as an exploratory, single-rater analysis with potential collection biases means its quantitative estimates should be treated as directional rather than definitive, and a dedicated venue for user-centric ML research remains unrealized.
