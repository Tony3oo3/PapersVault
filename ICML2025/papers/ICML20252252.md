## Problem

The standard assumption in LLM development is that extending pre-training monotonically improves downstream performance after fine-tuning. This paper challenges that assumption by showing that beyond a certain token budget, additional pre-training actually degrades post-trained model performance—a phenomenon the authors term catastrophic overtraining. The key open question is what mechanism drives this degradation and how the interplay between pre-training duration, modification magnitude, and task alignment determines when it occurs.

## Input / Output

The empirical analysis takes intermediate pre-trained checkpoints (OLMo-1B, OLMo-2-7B, LLM360-Amber-7B, and small 15M–90M models trained on C4) at varying token budgets and applies either isotropic Gaussian perturbations (scaled by a factor γ relative to the initialization covariance) or structured fine-tuning on downstream datasets (GSM8k, Starcoder-Python, SocialIQA, MR, RTE, TREC, Anthropic-HH, TULU, LLaVA). Outputs are C4 perplexity (measuring preservation of pre-trained knowledge), in-domain perplexity on the fine-tuning task, and standard benchmark scores (ARC, PIQA, HellaSwag, AlpacaEval, Winogrande). The theoretical analysis takes a two-layer linear network trained via gradient flow on a full-rank linear regression task and produces a closed-form characterization of the inflection point as a function of the perturbation scale γ, the singular value spectrum, and the regularization parameter λ.

## Method

The paper identifies progressive sensitivity as the underlying mechanism: for a fixed magnitude of parameter modification, the degree of forgetting increases monotonically with pre-training tokens, so that beyond an inflection point the degradation from modification overtakes the improvement from additional pre-training. Empirically, this is demonstrated first with Gaussian perturbations (showing a U-shaped perplexity curve) and then with fine-tuning at fixed and tuned learning rates, revealing a trade-off where smaller learning rates delay the inflection point but sacrifice in-domain performance. Theoretically, in a two-layer linear network with incremental feature learning (Saxe et al. 2018), the authors prove that when the pre-training and fine-tuning tasks are (α,r)-misaligned (i.e., the fine-tuning spectrum exceeds the pre-training spectrum beyond rank r), progressive sensitivity is inevitable and catastrophic overtraining occurs without regularization. They further show that adding an L2 regularization term to the fine-tuning loss can delay the inflection point, but at the cost of increasing the unregularized fine-tuning loss, formalizing a fundamental trade-off between adaptability and preservation.

## Results

The headline finding is that OLMo-1B pre-trained on 3T tokens performs 3% worse on AlpacaEval and 2% worse on ARC-Easy/Challenge compared to its 2.3T-token counterpart after instruction tuning on Anthropic-HH, effectively dropping to the level of a model pre-trained on only 1.5T tokens (50% fewer tokens). In the controlled 30M-parameter experiments, progressive sensitivity is confirmed: for a fixed Gaussian perturbation magnitude or fixed fine-tuning learning rate, the C4 perplexity degradation increases monotonically with pre-training tokens, producing a U-shaped curve in post-modification perplexity. For multimodal fine-tuning (LLaVA), overtraining causes active degradation on PIQA and larger forgetting gaps on ARC, HellaSwag, and Winogrande. Theoretically, the inflection point for catastrophic overtraining is characterized as the smallest n where 2dγ² > σₙ^pre in the Gaussian setting, and is shown to be inevitable without regularization when pre-training and fine-tuning tasks are (α,r)-misaligned with α > α₀.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing methods in the traditional sense; instead, it compares models at different pre-training token budgets (intermediate checkpoints) to demonstrate that more pre-training can hurt post-training. The key comparison is between OLMo-1B checkpoints at 1.5T, 2.3T, and 3T tokens: while base-model performance improves monotonically with tokens, post-trained performance peaks around 2.3T and degrades at 3T, with the 3T model underperforming even the 1.5T model after fine-tuning. The paper further validates this across OLMo-2-7B and LLM360-Amber-7B (Appendix F), confirming the phenomenon is not model-specific. In the controlled 30M experiments, the comparison across learning rates (4e-6 to η_max) shows that larger learning rates accelerate the inflection point, and tuning the learning rate to optimize ID performance can delay but not always prevent OOD degradation. The theoretical analysis (Theorem 4.7) establishes that without regularization, catastrophic overtraining is inevitable for sufficiently misaligned tasks, and regularization can only delay the inflection point at the cost of downstream fine-tuning performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges the loss-of-plasticity literature (primarily studied in small-model continual learning and RL, e.g., Kumar et al. 2020, Lyle et al. 2023) with the LLM pre-training/post-training paradigm, showing that the same mechanism—increased sensitivity to parameter modifications—manifests as degraded fine-tuning performance after extended pre-training. The theoretical analysis builds directly on the two-layer linear network framework of Saxe et al. (2018) and Gidel et al. (2019), which characterize incremental feature learning under gradient flow, to provide a precise characterization of the inflection point. It also connects to the catastrophic forgetting literature (Kirkpatrick et al. 2017) and Kumar et al. (2022) on fine-tuning distorting pretrained features, but reframes these in the context of pre-training duration as the controlling variable rather than task sequence.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work challenges the near-universal assumption in the field that scaling pre-training tokens monotonically improves downstream performance, with practical implications for how practitioners allocate compute budgets and select checkpoints for fine-tuning. The identified trade-off between in-domain fine-tuning gains and out-of-domain degradation (governed by the optimal learning rate's evolution) provides a concrete diagnostic for when overtraining is harmful. Key open questions include whether the effect persists at frontier model scales (the clearest evidence is at 1B–7B parameters), whether architectural or regularization interventions can fully decouple pre-training quality from fine-tuning sensitivity, and whether the linear-network theory transfers to the nonlinear dynamics of real transformers.
