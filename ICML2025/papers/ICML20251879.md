## Problem

Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) have become the standard tool for extracting interpretable features from neural network activations, but their simple linear-nonlinear encoder is assumed to be a reasonable approximation of optimal sparse inference. This paper proves that this assumption is fundamentally wrong: even in regimes where compressed sensing theory guarantees unique sparse recovery, a single-pass L-NL encoder must incur a non-zero amortisation gap, and it then quantifies how much more sophisticated encoders can recover.

## Input / Output

The method consumes dense neural network activations (e.g., 768-dimensional residual stream pre-activations from GPT-2 Small Layer 9, or synthetic M-dimensional projections of N-dimensional sparse sources) and produces sparse latent codes of dimension N > M along with a learned overcomplete dictionary. A key design choice is decoupling encoding from decoding: the decoder is always a fixed linear map W_d, while the encoder is swapped among SAE (single linear+ReLU), MLP (multi-layer), iterative sparse coding (L1-regularised gradient descent), and SAE+ITO (inference-time optimisation with the learned dictionary). In the GPT-2 experiment, 406M tokens from OpenWebText are used for training and a 13.1M-token test set for interpretability evaluation.

## Method

The paper's core theoretical contribution is Theorem 3.1, which proves via a rank argument that the pre-activation matrix of any L-NL SAE encoder has rank at most M < N, making it impossible to simultaneously satisfy the non-negativity constraints required for correct ReLU-based recovery of all K-sparse sources. Empirically, the authors compare four encoding strategies across three scenarios (known sparse codes, known dictionary, and fully unknown) on synthetic data with ground-truth features, measuring alignment via Mean Correlation Coefficient (MCC) and accounting for total FLOPs. They then validate on GPT-2 by training SAE, MLP (hidden width 8448), and Locally Competitive Algorithm encoders on Layer 9 activations, evaluating interpretability through an automated GPT-4o pipeline that generates feature explanations and scores them via F1 on held-out activating/non-activating examples. The MLP achieves a median F1 of 0.83 versus ~0.6 for SAE and LCA, with statistically significant differences (Kruskal-Wallis p < 0.001).

## Results

On GPT-2 Layer 9 residual-stream activations (768-dim, 16,896 latents, 406M OpenWebText tokens), the MLP encoder achieves a normalised MSE of 0.055 with mean L0 of 31.13, outperforming the standard SAE (MSE 0.061, L0 35.66) and LCA (MSE 0.070, L0 ~18.56). In automated interpretability evaluation (500 features per method, GPT-4o scoring), MLP features achieve a median F1-score of 0.83 versus ~0.6 for both SAE and LCA, with a Kruskal-Wallis test confirming significance (H=1856.33, p<0.001). In synthetic experiments (N=16, M=8, K=3), sparse coding achieves over 10% higher latent MCC than SAE in the fully unsupervised setting, and MLPs surpass SAE at a consistent FLOP threshold of ~3×10^14 in large-scale runs (N=1000, M=200, K=20).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The MLP encoder decisively outperforms the standard SAE (Bricken et al., 2023; Cunningham et al., 2023) across all evaluated metrics: lower reconstruction MSE (0.055 vs. 0.061), lower sparsity (L0 31.13 vs. 35.66), and substantially higher interpretability (median F1 0.83 vs. ~0.60, p<0.001 via Dunn's test). Sparse coding (Olshausen & Field, 1996) wins on synthetic latent MCC in the fully unsupervised regime (>10% higher than SAE), but its LCA variant on GPT-2 underperforms the MLP on both reconstruction (MSE 0.070 vs. 0.055) and interpretability (F1 ~0.60 vs. 0.83). SAE+ITO (Nanda et al., 2024) achieves the highest MCC in the known-dictionary synthetic scenario through stepwise inference-time optimisation, but the MLP reaches comparable performance at a fraction of the per-inference cost. LARS-based dictionary learning (Efron et al., 2004) outperforms the paper's vanilla gradient-based implementations in Appendix H, indicating that the reported gaps partly reflect implementation suboptimality rather than fundamental method limitations. Overall, the paper's central claim is that the SAE's linear-nonlinear encoder is provably suboptimal (Theorem 3.1), and that replacing it with an MLP or iterative sparse inference closes the amortisation gap at modest additional compute.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the SAE-for-interpretability framework established by Bricken et al. (2023) and Cunningham et al. (2023) by proving a fundamental theoretical limitation of their core architectural choice: the linear-nonlinear encoder. Drawing on compressed sensing theory (Donoho, 2006) and the Restricted Isometry Property, the authors show that SAE encoders cannot achieve optimal sparse recovery even in theoretically solvable regimes, formalising what the statistical inference literature calls the 'amortisation gap' (Marino et al., 2018). The paper then decouples encoding from decoding to empirically compare alternative sparse inference strategies (MLPs, sparse coding, inference-time optimisation) against the standard SAE encoder, building on the SAE+ITO formulation of Nanda et al. (2024) and the locally competitive algorithm of Olshausen & Field (1997).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The clean impossibility proof (Theorem 3.1) reframes a practical design choice in the interpretability community as a fundamental architectural limitation, motivating a shift toward more expressive encoders. The GPT-2 experiment showing MLP features achieving F1 0.83 versus 0.6 for SAEs suggests substantial interpretability gains are available, though the evidence is limited to a single small model and one layer. Key open questions include whether the amortisation gap persists with modern SAE variants (top-k, JumpReLU, Gated SAEs), how findings scale to larger models like GPT-4 or Claude, and whether the computational overhead of complex encoders is justified at the scale of projects like Gemma Scope. The paper also leaves unresolved how to handle non-zero-centered representations and varying feature importance distributions in practice.
