## Problem

Recent LVLMs demonstrate impressive performance on diagram-based visual reasoning tasks, yet it remains unclear whether this reflects genuine comprehension of symbolic visual language or merely reliance on pre-trained background knowledge as a shortcut. The paper addresses this gap by asking: can LVLMs truly parse and reason about the relational structure of diagrams, or do they simply identify entities and retrieve relevant world knowledge?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250938/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance pairs a diagram image (synthetic with 2–9 text/visual entities and directed arrows, or one of 1,001 real diagrams across six scientific domains) with a four-option multiple-choice question. Questions are systematically categorized along three axes: target (entity vs. relation), cognitive demand (Name Recognition for existence checks vs. Number Counting for multi-step reasoning), and knowledge dependency (Knowledge-Free vs. Knowledge-Required). The model outputs a single letter choice, scored as accuracy per question type and aggregated across diagram complexity bins.

## Method

The paper constructs a controlled test suite that models diagrams as graphs G = (V, E) and probes LVLM understanding through a factorial question design. Synthetic diagrams are generated with random entities (sampled from 377 icons) and random relations to isolate pure visual parsing, while a parallel set of knowledge-grounded synthetic diagrams constrains relations to semantically meaningful pairs (cosine similarity > 0.5 via Word2Vec/spaCy) to test whether embedded knowledge acts as a shortcut. Real diagrams from FoodWeb and AI2D are annotated with domain-specific questions across ecology, biology, physics, astronomy, chemistry, and geology. Nine LVLMs (GPT-4V, GPT-4o, Gemini, LLaMA, LLaVA, Qwen2-VL, Molmo) are evaluated under both Chain-of-Thought and zero-shot prompting. A critical diagnostic experiment removes or randomizes all relations in a real diagram and shows that GPT-4o still answers relation questions correctly by hallucinating from learned knowledge, confirming that apparent relation understanding is an illusion driven by knowledge shortcuts rather than genuine diagram parsing.

## Results

On synthetic diagrams, LVLMs achieve near-perfect entity recognition (GPT-4o: 99.2% text NR, 100% text NC) but struggle dramatically with relations, with average accuracy dropping to 67.0% (implicit NR) and 40.9% (implicit NC) across all nine models. On real diagrams, the apparent relation-recognition success (GPT-4o: 84.1% KR NR) is shown to be an illusion driven by knowledge shortcuts: when diagrams are complex (many entities), relation accuracy collapses, and when relations are removed or randomized, GPT-4o still answers correctly from memorized knowledge. The best open-source model (Qwen2-72B) reaches 77.9% implicit relation NR and 70.3% explicit relation NR, still far below the ~95%+ entity recognition it achieves.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates nine LVLMs (LLaVA, Molmo, LLaMA, Qwen2-2B/7B/72B, GPT-4V, GPT-4o, Gemini) on a unified diagram-understanding test suite. GPT-4o is the strongest model overall, leading on explicit relation recognition (76.6% NR) and explicit relation counting (70.2% NC), while Qwen2-72B is the best open-source model (77.9% implicit NR, 70.3% explicit NR). LLaVA is consistently the weakest, scoring near chance (30.2% implicit NR, 27.5% implicit NC) and underperforming every other model by 20–50 points on relation tasks. Critically, all nine models exhibit the same qualitative pattern: entity recognition exceeds 85% while relation recognition stays below 80%, and relation counting (NC) drops to 30–70%, confirming that the entity-vs-relation gap is a systematic limitation rather than a model-specific artifact. On real diagrams, the three largest models (GPT-4o, GPT-4V, Gemini) reach ~81% relation NR, but the authors show this is inflated by knowledge shortcuts—accuracy on relation questions drops sharply as diagram complexity (entity count) increases, and GPT-4o answers relation questions correctly even when all arrows are removed or randomized.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the diagram question-answering (DQA) literature (AI2D, FoodWeb, IconQA, CLEVR, NLVR) by introducing a controlled evaluation framework that explicitly decouples entity recognition from relation understanding, and knowledge-free from knowledge-required questions. It draws on the graph-based formalization of diagrams from prior work (Song et al., 1995; Hiippala & Orekhova, 2018) and the Kahneman System 1/System 2 distinction to structure recognition vs. reasoning tasks. The knowledge-shortcut hypothesis connects to broader findings on LVLM reliance on parametric knowledge (Ye & Kovashka, 2021; Tang et al., 2023), but applies it specifically to the visual-symbolic domain of diagrams, where prior benchmarks had not isolated this confound.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that LVLMs' apparent diagram comprehension is largely an illusion driven by background knowledge shortcuts has broad implications for how the field interprets benchmark results on any task where visual structure encodes real-world semantics. The test suite provides a reusable framework for evaluating symbolic visual understanding beyond diagrams. Key open questions include: how to train or prompt models to genuinely parse relational structure rather than retrieve parametric knowledge, whether the findings generalize to diagram types not covered (e.g., flowcharts, UML, circuit schematics with complex topology), and whether the knowledge-shortcut behavior persists as models scale or are fine-tuned on diagram-specific data.
