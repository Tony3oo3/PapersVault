## Problem

Existing theoretical analyses of GNN learning dynamics focus on convergence rates in the noise-free interpolation regime and connect graph structure only crudely (e.g., via maximum degree), leaving open how different graph topologies (power-law vs. regular) affect the generalization performance of learning algorithms in the presence of noise. This paper asks whether and how graph structure influences the excess risk of SGD and Ridge regression in GNNs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252264/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a graph specified by its adjacency or Laplacian matrix G and node feature vectors (200-dim Gaussian in experiments) as input, and produces closed-form excess risk upper and lower bounds decomposed into bias and variance terms indexed by the eigenspectrum of the aggregation covariance matrix M = E[mm^T]. The empirical validation uses Barabasi-Albert power-law graphs and regular graphs, with ground truth parameters aligned or misaligned to the head/tail eigenspaces of M, and reports excess risk as a function of sample size N and number of GNN layers L.

## Method

The paper derives excess risk profiles for tail-averaged SGD and Ridge regression in a one-round GNN setting (aggregation via graph matrix G followed by ReLU readout), showing both bounds are governed by the eigenspectrum of the aggregation matrix M. It then uses spectral graph theory to map graph structure to this eigenspectrum: power-law graphs yield fast-decay spectra (large β in μ_i ∝ 1/i^β) while regular graphs yield slow-decay spectra. The key comparison (Theorem 4.3) shows SGD dominates Ridge on power-law graphs and vice versa on regular graphs, because the exponential decay in SGD's head-eigenvalue bias term is advantageous when the spectrum decays quickly. For multi-layer linearized GNNs, the paper proves that stacking L layers multiplies eigenvalues by G^L, amplifying the ratio between head and tail eigenvalues (Proposition 4.5), which explains over-smoothing as a misalignment effect: more layers help when ground truth concentrates in the head eigenspace but hurt when it spreads into the tail.

## Results

The paper derives tight upper and lower bounds on the excess risk of SGD (Theorem 4.1) and Ridge regression (Theorem 4.2) in GNNs, decomposing each into bias and variance components governed by the eigenspectrum of the aggregation matrix M. The headline comparative result (Theorem 4.3) shows that SGD achieves lower excess risk than Ridge on power-law graphs (fast-decay spectrum, large β), while Ridge outperforms SGD on regular graphs (slow-decay spectrum, small β). Empirical validation on Barabasi-Albert (power-law) and regular synthetic graphs with 200-dimensional Gaussian features confirms these predictions (Fig. 3a–b), and multi-layer experiments (Fig. 3c–d) show that stacking GNN layers increases excess risk when the ground truth is misaligned with the head eigenspace (over-smoothing) but decreases it when aligned.

**Summary of key comparative findings (Theorem 4.3 and empirical validation, Fig. 3).**

| Graph Structure | Spectrum Decay | Better Algorithm | Key Mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Power-law (large β) | Fast (concentrated eigenvalues) | SGD | Exponential bias decay in head eigenspace dominates; variance term manageable |
| Regular (small β) | Slow (uniform eigenvalues) | Ridge | Uniform spectrum makes SGD's exponential decay less advantageous; Ridge's uniform shrinkage is better matched |
| Multi-layer, misaligned θ* | Amplified by stacking | Neither (risk increases) | Non-isotropic amplification worsens tail-eigenspace misalignment → over-smoothing |
| Multi-layer, aligned θ* | Amplified by stacking | SGD (improves) | Head-eigenspace alignment strengthens with depth, reducing bias |

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between SGD (with tail-averaging and constant step size) and Ridge regression as the two learning algorithms, evaluated on two graph structure families: power-law (Barabasi-Albert) and regular graphs. On power-law graphs, SGD consistently achieves lower excess risk than Ridge for any choice of Ridge's regularization parameter λ, as there exists an optimal SGD step size γ* that dominates (Theorem 4.3, Fig. 3a). Conversely, on regular graphs, Ridge outperforms SGD for any choice of SGD step size γ, as there exists an optimal λ* that dominates (Theorem 4.3, Fig. 3b). The empirical results on synthetic graphs with σ²=1 noise and 200-dimensional features confirm both predictions across five independent trials. For multi-layer GNNs, the comparison shifts: when the ground truth θ* is concentrated in the head eigenspace, adding layers benefits SGD (Fig. 3d), whereas when θ* is spread across the tail eigenspace, adding layers degrades both algorithms but particularly exacerbates the over-smoothing problem (Fig. 3c).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the excess risk analysis of SGD and Ridge regression from standard learning theory (Zou et al., 2021b; Tsigler & Bartlett, 2023) to the GNN setting, where the data covariance is shaped by a graph matrix. It departs from prior GNN learning analyses (Awasthi et al., 2021), which were confined to the noise-free interpolation regime and connected graph structure only through crude measures like maximum degree, by working in the generalization regime (with noise) and linking performance to the full eigenspectrum of the graph matrix via spectral graph theory. The ReLU activation is handled by adapting the landscape equivalence result from Wu et al. (2023), and the bias-variance decomposition framework follows Jain et al. (2017).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first formal characterization of how graph topology (power-law vs. regular) determines which learning algorithm (SGD-like vs. Ridge-like) is preferable, offering concrete guidance for algorithm selection in practice. The reframing of over-smoothing as an eigenspectrum misalignment issue—where adding layers can actually help when the ground truth concentrates in the head eigenspace—challenges the blanket 'more layers = worse' narrative. Key limitations include restriction to one-layer or linearized GNNs, isolation of graph structure from node features, and reliance on synthetic Barabasi-Albert graphs; extending to multi-layer non-linear GNNs, structured feature-graph interactions, and probabilistic random graph models are identified as important open directions.
