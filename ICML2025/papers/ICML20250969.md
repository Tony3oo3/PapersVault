## Problem

Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KANs) rely on spline activation functions that do not respect data symmetries, limiting their applicability to tasks where group equivariance is critical prior knowledge. While equivariant architectures exist for MLPs (e.g., EMLP), no method had previously incorporated arbitrary matrix group equivariance into KANs, leaving a gap in leveraging KANs' superior symbolic formula representation on symmetry-structured data.

## Input / Output

The method consumes structured tabular features with known group representation structures—e.g., four 4-vectors (4T₁) for particle scattering, 24 2-vectors (24T₁) for the three-body problem, or three 4-vectors (3T₁) for top quark tagging—and produces either a scalar (T₀) for regression or a binary label for classification. A lift layer prepends gate scalars to non-scalar input terms to form the gated input space, and gate scalars are dropped from the final gated output to recover the dataset's native label space, so the user only specifies the latent space structure (analogous to hidden dimension) while input/output spaces are fixed by the data type.

## Method

EKAN decomposes each KAN layer into gated spline basis functions followed by equivariant linear weights. For each non-scalar term in the input, a gate scalar is appended; the B-spline and SiLU basis functions are applied to the gate scalar (a scalar) and the result is multiplied by the original vector term, which provably ensures equivariance between the gated input space and the post-activation space (Theorem 4.1). The post-activation space is (G+k+1) copies of the input space, and the linear weight blocks W_b are constrained to the nullspace of a matrix built from the group's infinitesimal generators and discrete generators (via SVD), exactly as in EMLP. A lift layer (an equivariant linear map) before the first EKAN layer and removal of gate scalars after the last layer align the network's actual I/O with the dataset's feature and label spaces, yielding a complete equivariant architecture parameterized only by the user-specified latent space structure.

## Results

On particle scattering (O(1,3) invariance), EKAN-SO⁺(1,3) achieves a test MSE of (2.01±1.93)×10⁻⁵ with 10³ training samples, roughly 16× lower than EMLP-SO⁺(1,3) (3.30×10⁻⁴) and ~300× lower than MLP (3.64×10⁻¹). On the three-body problem (O(2) equivariance), EKAN-SO(2) with 10⁴·⁵ parameters reaches (1.12±0.13)×10⁻³ MSE, outperforming MLP (4.84×10⁻³) and EMLP-SO(2) (2.28×10⁻³) at the same parameter budget. On top quark tagging (non-symbolic formula, O(1,3) invariance), EKAN-SO⁺(1,3) with only 34K parameters achieves 76.93% test accuracy at 10⁴ training samples, matching EMLP (133K params, 77.10%) and CGENN (85K params, 77.05%) while using fewer than 40% of the parameters.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against EMLP (Finzi et al., 2021), EKAN consistently wins across all three tasks: on particle scattering it reduces test MSE by 1–2 orders of magnitude (e.g., 2.01×10⁻⁵ vs. 3.30×10⁻⁴ at 10³ samples), on the three-body problem it achieves ~4× lower MSE at matched parameter counts, and on top quark tagging it matches EMLP's 77.10% accuracy (76.93%) while using only 34K vs. 133K parameters (<26% of EMLP's budget). Against KAN (Liu et al., 2024), EKAN dominates on all symmetry-related tasks: on particle scattering EKAN's MSE is ~100× lower than KAN's at 10³ samples (2.01×10⁻⁵ vs. 3.46×10⁻¹), and on top quark tagging EKAN reaches 76.93% accuracy while KAN plateaus at ~50% (chance level), confirming that equivariance is essential for non-symbolic formula tasks. Against CGENN (Ruhe et al., 2023) on top quark tagging, EKAN achieves comparable accuracy (76.93% vs. 77.05% at 10⁴ samples) with 34K parameters versus CGENN's 85K (~40% of CGENN's parameter count). Notably, EKAN with 10⁴·⁵ parameters on the three-body problem matches or beats baselines with 10⁵·⁵ parameters, saving 90% of the parameter overhead.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two recent lines of work: Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KANs), which replace fixed node activations with learnable spline functions on edges, and Equivariant Multi-Layer Perceptrons (EMLP), which provide a general recipe for making MLPs equivariant to arbitrary matrix groups via nullspace constraints on linear weights. EKAN departs from both by decomposing the KAN layer into gated spline basis functions and equivariant linear weights, then proving that a gating mechanism (applying splines to scalar gates and multiplying by the feature) preserves equivariance through the nonlinear activation step—a step that EMLP does not need to address. The lift layer is a new architectural component that aligns the raw dataset feature space with the gated input space required by the EKAN layers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

EKAN demonstrates that combining learnable activations with structural equivariance yields orders-of-magnitude MSE reductions on symbolic physics tasks (particle scattering, three-body problem) compared to both non-equivariant KANs and equivariant MLPs, while matching state-of-the-art equivariant architectures on non-symbolic tasks (top quark tagging) with fewer than 40% of the parameters. A key limitation is that the gating mechanism reduces expressive power, and the advantage over baselines diminishes when data is abundant, suggesting EKAN primarily enhances small-data generalization rather than unlocking new representational capacity. Open questions include whether the framework extends to higher-order tensor spaces, discrete groups beyond the tested ones, and broader domains like computer vision or NLP where KANs have shown promise.
