## Problem

Backdoor attacks on pre-trained language models are typically evaluated only on the same downstream task used to implant the backdoor, assuming the victim will fine-tune for that exact task. In practice, victims adapt backdoored PTLMs to entirely different downstream tasks, where the trigger word causes severe output distribution distortions (e.g., 99.88% of triggered samples collapsing into a single class) — a previously unquantified phenomenon the authors term 'backdoor complications.' No prior work has systematically measured or mitigated these unforeseen cross-task side effects.

## Input / Output

The method operates on text sentences for classification tasks ranging from binary (IMDb, CoLA) to 14-class (DBPedia). The backdoor is activated by replacing the first token of an input with a pre-defined trigger word (e.g., 'Trump', 'Bolshevik', 'Twitter'), and the output is a class label. The key evaluation metric is the KL divergence between the output distribution of triggered and clean test sets in downstream TSMs, which quantifies the severity of backdoor complications. The method is also validated on image classification (CIFAR-10 → SVHN) and larger LLMs (OPT-1.3B, TinyLlama-1.1B).

## Method

The paper proposes a task-agnostic complication reduction method inspired by multi-task learning. During backdoor training, the attacker jointly optimizes the backdoor task (poisoned samples with trigger + target label) alongside multiple 'correction tasks' drawn from unrelated text classification datasets. For each correction task, the first word is replaced with the trigger word while the original label is preserved, teaching the model that the trigger should not alter predictions in unrelated contexts. The combined loss (Equation 5) weights the backdoor task by α (default 0.4) and distributes (1−α) evenly across correction tasks, with no regularization term modeling task relatedness. This requires no knowledge of the victim's actual downstream task — only a small set of generic classification datasets (four suffice) — and preserves near-100% ASR and >90% CTA on the target backdoor task while reducing downstream KL divergence values from typically >0.5 to <0.1.

## Results

Backdoor complications are widespread: KL divergence between triggered and clean output distributions in downstream TSMs frequently exceeds 0.5, reaching up to 2.79 (DBPedia, <Tru, Negative> setting). The proposed task-agnostic complication reduction method reduces most KL divergence values below 0.1 across 10 unseen downstream datasets and 4 PTLMs (BERT, BART, GPT-2, T5), while maintaining attack success rates near 100% (e.g., 99.98% ASR for BERT) and clean test accuracy above 90%. For example, on the E-commerce task with target label Negative, KL divergence drops from 0.9631/0.8898/0.6993/1.8317 (w/o reduction) to 0.0010/0.0071/0.0039/0.0009 (w/ reduction) across the four architectures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between backdoored PTLMs with and without the proposed task-agnostic complication reduction. Across 10 downstream datasets and 4 architectures, the reduction method lowers KL divergence from typically 0.5–2.5 (w/o) to below 0.1 (w/), while preserving ASR near 100% and CTA above 90% (Table 3). The only external baseline compared is RECIPE (Zhu et al., 2023), a backdoor removal defense applied to the PTLM before fine-tuning. RECIPE achieves comparable KL reduction (e.g., IMDb: 0.6039 → 0.0028; MGB: 0.9749 → 0.0968; CoLA: 1.0572 → 0.0614), but at a severe cost to model utility: CTA drops from 92.07% to 26.53%. In contrast, the paper's method is applied at PTLM training time (attacker-side) and maintains CTA above 90%, making it a fundamentally different trade-off: the attacker proactively minimizes complications rather than the victim applying a costly post-hoc defense.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the backdoor attack literature (BadNets, BadPre, BadEncoder) by identifying a previously unexamined failure mode: when a backdoored PTLM is fine-tuned for a downstream task *different* from the original backdoor task, the trigger causes abnormal output distributions (e.g., near-total collapse to a single class) in that unrelated task. Prior work on backdoor stealthiness evaluated only within the target task, implicitly assuming downstream tasks match the backdoor task. The proposed mitigation repurposes the multi-task learning framework (Zhang & Yang, 2018) by jointly training the backdoor task with correction tasks that expose the model to trigger-containing inputs with unchanged labels, effectively teaching the encoder to ignore the trigger outside the target task.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that backdoor complications are pervasive and severe (KL divergences up to 2.8, with 99.88% of triggered samples collapsing to one class) challenges the practical stealthiness of backdoored PTLMs in real deployment where users fine-tune for arbitrary tasks. This has direct implications for the trustworthiness of third-party model repositories (Hugging Face, ModelScope) and suggests that existing backdoor defenses may need to account for cross-task leakage. Limitations include restriction to text classification with simple first-word triggers, the pretrain-finetune paradigm only (not prompt-based or generation tasks), and the evaluation being from the attacker's perspective (reducing complications to improve stealthiness rather than detecting them). Open questions include whether complications generalize to generation tasks, more complex or distributed triggers, and whether the MTL-based reduction scales to larger model families or multi-modal settings.
