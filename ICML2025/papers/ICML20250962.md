## Problem

Class imbalance biases the optimal decision boundary toward the minority class when optimizing accuracy, degrading minority-class metrics like F1 score. Existing data-level rebalancing methods (resampling, synthetic augmentation) introduce information loss or generative errors, while algorithm-level methods are often tied to specific architectures. This paper asks whether carefully designed *asymmetric* label noise can serve as a model-agnostic, loss-free data-level rebalancing mechanism that corrects the biased boundary.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250962/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard imbalanced labeled data (tabular features or RGB images at 32×32 for CIFAR) and outputs class labels, with no modification to input features. The only data edit is flipping a small number of majority-class labels to minority-class labels (e.g., 44–94 instances on CIFAR-10/100), so the original feature space is fully preserved. Because LNR is a data-level pre-processing step, it can be plugged in front of any classifier (KNN, CART, MLP, ResNet-32) or combined with algorithm-level methods (LDAM, GCL, MiSLAS) without architectural changes.

## Method

LNR introduces an asymmetric instance-dependent label noise model in which only majority-class samples are flipped to the minority class (γ(x)=0, ρ(x)∝η(x)), with the flip rate estimated from a classifier's posterior probabilities, z-scored across majority samples, and thresholded via tanh(Z − t_flip). Theoretically, this asymmetric noise shifts the Bayesian decision boundary from P₁/P₀ = π₀/π₁ to P₁/P₀ = [1−2ρ(x*)]·π₀/π₁, pulling it back toward the majority region and aligning the accuracy-optimal boundary with the F1-optimal boundary. For multi-class settings, flip rates are weighted by a class-prior vector θ_C so that flipping only proceeds from higher-prior to lower-prior classes, and the noise is deferred until after T_D training epochs to avoid disrupting feature learning. The approach requires no generative model, no balanced auxiliary dataset, and no feature editing, distinguishing it from resampling and mixup-based alternatives.

## Results

On long-tailed CIFAR-10 (IR=100), MiSLAS+LNR achieves 86.07% few-shot accuracy, outperforming MiSLAS+SelMix(10k) (83.52%) and MiSLAS+ReMix (79.78%), while also improving overall accuracy to 83.56%. On long-tailed CIFAR-100, MiSLAS+LNR reaches 35.34% few-shot accuracy versus 25.10% for SelMix(10k) and 27.93% for ReMix. On step-wise CIFAR-10, LDAM+LNR achieves 75.06% minority-class accuracy (vs. 67.02% for LDAM+RSG), and GCL+LNR reaches 72.22% (vs. 56.78% for GCL alone). On 32 KEEL binary datasets, LNR achieves the highest average F1 and G-mean rankings across KNN, CART, and MLP classifiers.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LNR consistently outperforms all data-level baselines (RSG, SMOTE, ADASYN, Borderline-SMOTE, OSS, CC, RUS) on F1 and G-mean across 32 KEEL datasets and synthetic data, while also matching or exceeding their AUC performance—unlike over-sampling methods that sacrifice AUC by inflating minority class errors. On long-tailed CIFAR-100, MiSLAS+LNR achieves 35.34% few-shot accuracy, a 10.24-point gain over MiSLAS+SelMix(10k) (25.10%) and 7.41 points over MiSLAS+ReMix (27.93%), while requiring no auxiliary validation dataset. On step-wise CIFAR-10, LDAM+LNR improves minority-class accuracy by 8.04 points over LDAM+RSG (75.06% vs. 67.02%), and GCL+LNR gains 15.44 points over GCL alone (72.22% vs. 56.78%). A key practical advantage is that SelMix degrades significantly with smaller (1k) or imbalanced validation sets (e.g., 81.9% few-shot on CIFAR-10 with imbalanced validation), whereas LNR requires no auxiliary data at all. On the KEEL benchmarks, LNR achieves the highest average relative ranking (0.86–0.93 for F1, 0.84–0.90 for G-mean) across all three classifiers, with the largest gains on datasets with high class overlap (border/rare minority samples).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the concept of label noise—traditionally treated as a nuisance to be mitigated—into a deliberate rebalancing tool. It extends the 'harmless label noise' framework of Ahfock & McLachlan (2021) and Cannings et al. (2020), which showed symmetric noise leaves decision boundaries unchanged on balanced data, by introducing an asymmetric noise model that only flips majority-to-minority labels, thereby actively shifting the biased boundary back toward the majority region. On the imbalanced-learning side, LNR positions itself as a data-level alternative to resampling (SMOTE, ADASYN, OSS, etc.) and mixup-based methods (ReMix, SelMix), avoiding both information loss from under-sampling and generative errors from synthetic sample creation, while remaining model-agnostic and composable with algorithm-level approaches like LDAM-DRW, GCL, and MiSLAS.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theoretical analysis connecting the accuracy-optimal Bayesian boundary to the F1-optimal boundary provides a clear mechanistic explanation for why imbalance hurts minority-class performance, and the asymmetric noise model offers a principled correction. Practically, LNR is a lightweight, drop-in data-level method requiring no generative model, no auxiliary balanced validation set (a key limitation of SelMix), and no modification to the classifier architecture, making it broadly applicable. Open questions include how the method behaves in streaming or online settings where a preliminary classifier for estimating flip rates may not be available, whether the flip-rate estimator's quality degrades in extremely high-dimensional or highly overlapping regimes, and whether the approach generalizes to semi-supervised or self-supervised imbalanced learning.
