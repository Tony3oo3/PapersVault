## Problem

Current optimization-based jailbreak attacks on LLMs maximize the likelihood of a fixed, manually designed affirmative response prefix, which is non-adaptive to the target model, ignores the distributional nature of LLM outputs, and is inconsistent with actual harmfulness. This leads to attacks that make the affirmative prefix likely while the model still completes harmlessly, and causes gross overestimation of model robustness when low attack success under this objective is taken as a robustness measure.

## Input / Output

The method takes an inappropriate prompt from HarmBench's 200 standard behaviors and white-box access to the target LLM (parameters, architecture, tokenizer). It produces an adversarial suffix (or prefix) appended to the prompt, optimized over a one-hot relaxed token representation. During optimization, generations are truncated to 128 tokens and scored by an LLM-as-a-judge (HarmBench's Llama 2 13B for GCG, Mistral 7B for PGD); final evaluation uses 512 tokens with the greedy generation scored by the judge.

## Method

The paper formulates the adversarial attack as a policy-gradient problem where the LLM is a probabilistic policy and the prompt is its parameterization, then derives a REINFORCE objective that maximizes the expected harmfulness reward over the full response distribution. The gradient is estimated via the REINFORCE Leave-One-Out (RLOO) estimator with a specialized biased sampling strategy of up to four generations: a static seed (affirmative or historical), a random sample at temperature 0.7, the greedy (mode) generation, and the most harmful generation observed during the attack. This objective is integrated into GCG and PGD by replacing their cross-entropy loss with the negative RLOO estimator, making the attacks adaptive (responding to model-specific output distributions), distributional (optimizing over the population of responses rather than a single fixed prefix), and semantic (targeting actual harmfulness via the judge). A small static baseline (0.1) stabilizes the loss when all generations are uniformly harmful or harmless, and token-level weighting with linearly decaying importance prioritizes early tokens in long generations.

## Results

On HarmBench's 200 standard behaviors, REINFORCE-GCG more than doubles the attack success rate (ASR@512) on Llama 3 8B (0.73 vs. 0.35) and Llama 2 7B (0.56 vs. 0.32) compared to the affirmative-response GCG baseline. REINFORCE-PGD similarly improves ASR@512 on Llama 3 8B (0.69 vs. 0.57) and Gemma 1.1 7B (0.84 vs. 0.54), though gains on Llama 2 7B are marginal (0.22 vs. 0.17). Against the state-of-the-art circuit breaker defense on Llama 3 8B, REINFORCE-GCG achieves 23% ASR@512 (50% with a history-based seed) versus only 2% for affirmative GCG.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares REINFORCE-GCG and REINFORCE-PGD against their affirmative-response counterparts (GCG from Zou et al., 2023 and PGD from Geisler et al., 2024) across five aligned LLMs. REINFORCE-GCG wins on all five models, with the largest relative gains on Llama 3 8B (0.73 vs. 0.35, a 2.1× improvement) and Llama 2 7B (0.56 vs. 0.32, a 1.75× improvement). REINFORCE-PGD also wins on four of five models but shows only a marginal gain on Llama 2 7B (0.22 vs. 0.17). Against the circuit breaker defense on Llama 3 8B, the gap is most dramatic: affirmative GCG achieves just 2% ASR@512 while REINFORCE-GCG reaches 23% (with y_affirmative seed) or 50% (with y_history seed), demonstrating that the non-consistent affirmative objective grossly overestimates the defense's robustness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reframes gradient-based jailbreak attacks (GCG, PGD) by replacing their standard affirmative-response cross-entropy loss with a REINFORCE policy-gradient objective that optimizes the expected harmfulness over the full distribution of model generations. Conceptually, it draws on the adaptive-attacks literature (Tramer et al., 2020) to argue that the widely-used affirmative objective is inconsistent and non-adaptive, and on the RLOO estimator (Kool et al., 2019) for a parameter-free variance-reduction baseline. The LLM is treated as a fixed probabilistic policy whose behavior is steered solely through the prompt, making the attack a test-time policy optimization rather than a model-parameter update. This positions the work as a principled generalization of existing suffix-optimization attacks to a distributional, reward-driven formulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a consistent, adaptive objective roughly doubles attack success on Llama 3 and lifts ASR from 2% to 50% against the state-of-the-art circuit-breaker defense underscores that prior robustness claims based on the affirmative objective may be grossly overestimated. The framework is general—swapping the reward signal extends it to factuality, unlearning, or other safety properties—making it a reusable template for automated red-teaming. Key limitations include reliance on an LLM-as-a-judge (whose imperfections propagate into the optimization), white-box access to model parameters, and evaluation on greedy decoding only. Open questions include how the approach scales to more capable or closed-source models, whether judge quality is the binding constraint, and whether the distributional objective reveals qualitatively different failure modes as models improve.
