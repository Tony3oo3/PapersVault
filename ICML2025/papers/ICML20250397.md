## Problem

Influence functions are the standard tool for identifying detrimental training samples, but they require computing and inverting the Hessian matrix, which is computationally prohibitive for large deep models and relies on a convexity assumption that deep networks violate. This paper asks whether the Hessian-dependent influence estimation can be replaced by a simpler, Hessian-free formulation that still accurately flags harmful samples.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a trained model's parameters θ̂, the full training set T = {(x_i, y_i)}, and a loss function ℓ, from which it computes per-sample gradients ∇_θ ℓ(z_i; θ̂). For high-dimensional gradient spaces (e.g., 50,000 × 51,200 for CIFAR-100N), a sparse random projection step reduces dimensionality before outlier detection. The output is a binary label per sample (beneficial vs. detrimental) and a trimmed training set T* after removing the top-k outliers (k typically set to 5% of training data).

## Method

The key insight is that in the influence function formula, only the per-sample gradient term ∇_θ ℓ(z_j; θ̂) varies across samples, and detrimental samples constitute a small minority that is outlying in gradient space. This transforms the influence estimation problem into a standard outlier detection problem, eliminating all Hessian computation and inversion. The authors apply Isolation Forest (chosen for its linear time complexity, sub-sampling ability in high dimensions, and sensitivity to non-linearly separated outliers) or simple L1/L2-norm thresholding to the gradient space. For LLM influential-data identification, 10 separate iForest estimators are trained—one per task category—on that category's training gradients, and test prompts are scored against each estimator. The resulting method has O(np) time complexity (linear in both samples and parameters), requires no convexity assumption, and outperforms Hessian-based influence baselines (LiSSA, DataInf, Gradient Tracing) on noisy label correction, NLP data selection, and LLM influence identification tasks.

## Results

On CIFAR-10N (ResNet-34), outlier gradient analysis (L2-norm) achieves the highest post-trimming accuracy of 92.21% on the Aggregate setting (vs. 90.87% cross-entropy baseline) and 90.25% on Random, while L1-norm leads on the challenging Worst setting at 84.20% (vs. 82.27% baseline). On CIFAR-100N, L2-norm outlier gradient analysis reaches 61.40% accuracy, surpassing the 57.36% cross-entropy baseline by 4.04 points. For LLM influential data identification on Llama-2-13B, outlier gradient analysis achieves perfect AUC (1.000) and Recall (1.000) across all three benchmark tasks, outperforming DataInf (AUC 0.999, Recall 0.990–0.996) and Gradient Tracing (AUC 0.722–0.999, Recall 0.226–0.982).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Outlier gradient analysis (L1/L2-norm) consistently outperforms all three noisy label correction baselines (Normalized Margin, Self-Confidence, Confidence Entropy) across every noise setting, with the largest margin on CIFAR-100N where L2-norm achieves 61.40% vs. 60.94% for the best label correction method. Among influence-based methods, outlier gradient analysis beats Gradient Tracing, LiSSA, and DataInf on most settings; for example, on CIFAR-10N Worst (40.21% noise), L1-norm reaches 84.20% while DataInf and LiSSA both plateau at ~83.40%. Self-LiSSA is the closest competitor, narrowly beating iForest on CIFAR-10N Aggregate (92.07% vs. 91.36%), but outlier gradient L2 still leads overall (92.21%). On the LLM influential data identification task, outlier gradient analysis achieves perfect AUC and Recall (1.000/1.000) on all three benchmarks, decisively outperforming DataInf (AUC 0.999, Recall 0.990–0.996) and Gradient Tracing (AUC 0.722–0.999, Recall 0.226–0.982). The newer influence methods GEX and TRAK also lose to outlier gradient analysis on CIFAR-10N (e.g., GEX scores 80.30% on Worst vs. 84.20% for L1-norm), and both suffer out-of-memory errors on CIFAR-100N. In terms of computational efficiency, outlier gradient analysis runs in 0.5–2.2 seconds on CIFAR-10N, orders of magnitude faster than LiSSA (~23 s) and Self-LiSSA (~31 s), while Gradient Tracing is slightly faster but less accurate.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the influence function framework of Koh & Liang (2017), which measures sample impact via a Hessian-weighted inner product of gradients. The key departure is a conceptual transformation: for the binary question of whether a sample is detrimental (negative influence) or beneficial (non-negative influence), the Hessian term is unnecessary, and the problem reduces to detecting outliers in the raw gradient space. This positions the work at the intersection of influence functions and classical outlier detection (Isolation Forest, norm thresholding), departing from prior Hessian-based methods (LiSSA, DataInf, Gradient Tracing) and from confidence-based noisy label correction (Normalized Margin, Self-Confidence) by operating purely on gradient geometry.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is a Hessian-free, linear-time method for identifying and trimming detrimental training samples that works across vision (CIFAR-10N/100N, ImageNet), NLP (GLUE with RoBERTa), and LLMs (Llama-2-13B), consistently matching or exceeding Hessian-based baselines at a fraction of the computational cost. Limitations include the non-trivial choice of trimming budget k, the potential irrelevance of the method on already-clean datasets, and the dependence on outlier analysis algorithm choice (iForest outperforms simple L1/L2 norms in some settings). Open questions include whether purpose-built outlier detectors for gradient space could further improve accuracy, and how the approach behaves under severe distribution shift or adversarial label noise beyond the settings tested.
