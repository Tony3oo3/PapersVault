## Problem

Existing LLM copyright mitigation methods are fragmented across the model lifecycle: online approaches (system prompts, output filtering, decoding-time interventions) and offline approaches (machine unlearning, data cleansing, training-based methods) are each optimized in isolation. The paper argues this independence creates theoretical bottlenecks because copyright risks are inherently interconnected—serving-stage violations often stem from training-stage memorization, and offline methods cannot adapt to real-time user behavior. A systemic, jointly optimized framework is needed to address these interdependencies.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes heterogeneous inputs across both stages: real-time user prompts, model outputs, and IP-location signals during online serving, alongside sampled training data and dynamic, region-specific copyright status databases during offline calibration. Outputs are structured as a feedback loop rather than a single artifact—filtered responses and logged mitigator decisions feed into examination reports, which in turn produce updated model weights, recalibrated copyright datasets, and revised training pipeline configurations. A key structural constraint is that the localized copyright databases must be continuously maintained and updated per jurisdiction, making them a shared, mutable resource consumed by both online and offline modules.

## Method

The paper proposes a three-module iterative framework: (1) Online Mitigation monitors real-time behavior using system prompts, output filtering, and decoding-time methods, with two novel research directions—leveraging LLM internal states as predictive signals for infringement before decoding begins, and context-aware response adjustment based on user intent and content sensitivity; (2) Copyright Examination bridges the two stages by analyzing online logs (prompts, outputs, mitigator trigger patterns, filtering latency) to detect over- or under-filtering, while simultaneously auditing sampled training data against region-specific legal provisions to expand the copyright database; (3) Offline Calibration uses examination results to maintain localized copyrighted datasets, tune online mitigation parameters (e.g., n-gram thresholds for MemFree), update model weights via machine unlearning or fine-tuning, and restructure the data collection pipeline (e.g., watermarking, source exclusion). The critical design principle is that these modules do not operate independently but form a closed feedback loop where each iteration accumulates empirical data, refines risk detection, and adapts to evolving copyright regulations and user behaviors.

## Results

Table 1 (from CopyBench) evaluates online-only mitigation strategies across four LLMs. MemFree decoding dramatically reduces literal copying (e.g., −94% on Llama3-70B, −100% on Llama2-13B) but has negligible or even adverse effects on non-literal copying (events and characters), with changes ranging from −4% to +5%. System Prompts show highly inconsistent results: they help on Llama2-70B-Tulu (−26% to −29% across metrics) but actually worsen infringement on Llama2-70B (+7% literal, +18% events, +11% characters) and Llama3-70B (+5% literal). These findings support the paper's central argument that online-only approaches are insufficient for comprehensive copyright risk management.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not propose a new method to benchmark against baselines; instead, it uses CopyBench results to compare two online-only mitigation strategies—System Prompts and MemFree decoding—across four LLMs (Llama2-13B, Llama2-70B, Llama2-70B-Tulu, Llama3-70B). MemFree consistently dominates on literal copying (reductions of 87–100%) but fails to meaningfully reduce non-literal copying (events/characters), with several cases showing no change or slight increases. System Prompts are unreliable: they reduce all three metrics by ~26–29% on Llama2-70B-Tulu but paradoxically increase infringement on Llama2-70B (+7%/+18%/+11%) and Llama3-70B (+5% literal). The paper's key takeaway is that neither online-only strategy can address non-literal copying or localized copyright status, motivating the proposed iterative online-offline joint optimization framework rather than any single mitigation technique.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper does not build on a single prior method but rather synthesizes and repositions the existing landscape of LLM copyright mitigation, which has been treated as a collection of independent sub-problems: online approaches (system prompts, output filtering via tools like SHIELD and GitHub Copilot, decoding-time interventions like MemFree and R-CAD) and offline approaches (machine unlearning, data cleansing, training-based alignment). The paper's conceptual departure is arguing that these fragments are insufficient in isolation and must be unified into an iterative online-offline joint optimization loop spanning the full LLM lifecycle. It draws on the U.S. Copyright Office's guidance and the CopyBench evaluation framework to ground its argument, but introduces no new algorithm; its contribution is a systemic design principle and a proposed three-module architecture (mitigation, examination, calibration).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As a position paper, its primary impact is agenda-setting: it reframes LLM copyright compliance from a set of point solutions into a continuous, cross-stage optimization problem, which could influence how labs and regulators think about deployment pipelines. Key open questions remain unresolved—how to practically construct and maintain comprehensive, jurisdiction-aware copyrighted datasets at scale, how to implement the iterative feedback loop with acceptable latency and cost, and whether the framework's benefits are quantifiable against the engineering overhead it introduces. The paper also acknowledges that legal frameworks (e.g., the EU AI Act) are evolving in parallel, raising the question of how much technical mitigation is needed versus what will be handled by regulation.
