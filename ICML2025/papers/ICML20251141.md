## Problem

Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) are widely used to extract interpretable features from LLM activations, but their utility on downstream tasks has been evaluated primarily through proxy metrics like reconstruction loss rather than competitive performance against strong baselines. The paper asks whether SAE probes provide a consistent advantage over standard probing methods across challenging real-world regimes, and whether the interpretability insights attributed to SAEs can be replicated with simpler baselines.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251141/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes last-token hidden state activations from a specific layer (layer 20 for Gemma-2-9B, layer 16 for Llama-3.1-8B) of a frozen LLM run on text prompts, along with binary target labels. For SAE probes, activations are first encoded into a high-dimensional sparse latent space (width 16k–1M), from which only the top-k latents (k=16 or 128) are retained for probe training. Output is a binary classification score evaluated by AUC, with the key evaluation metric being the 'quiver of arrows' test AUC—the performance of the best method selected by validation AUC from a toolkit that optionally includes SAE probes.

## Method

The SAE probe is constructed by passing training activations through a pre-trained SAE encoder, selecting the k latents with the largest mean absolute difference between target=1 and target=0 classes (optionally normalized by each latent's mean firing value), and training an L1-regularized logistic regression on those k dimensions. The central evaluation framework, 'quiver of arrows,' adds SAE probes to a practitioner's existing toolkit of five baseline methods (logistic regression, PCA regression, KNN, XGBoost, MLP) and measures whether the best method by validation AUC achieves higher test AUC than the baseline-only quiver. The paper tests this across 113 binary classification datasets under four challenging regimes—data scarcity (2–1024 training examples), class imbalance (5–95% positive ratio), label noise (0–50% flipped labels), and covariate shift (8 OOD datasets)—using multiple SAE widths and k values. For interpretability analysis, the authors apply autointerp (via Neuronpedia and GPT-4o) to generate natural language descriptions of top latents, then use o1 to rank latent relevance and prune spurious features, and investigate whether SAE-specific insights (e.g., detecting mislabeled CoLA examples or spurious punctuation correlations) are replicable with baseline classifiers.

## Results

Across 113 binary classification datasets on Gemma-2-9B (layer 20), SAE probes (width=16k, k=128) fail to improve over baseline methods in any of four challenging regimes. Under the 'quiver of arrows' evaluation, adding SAE probes to a practitioner's toolkit yields a slight *decrease* in mean test AUC: 0.939 vs. 0.940 (standard), 0.812 vs. 0.819 (data scarcity), and 0.916 vs. 0.921 (class imbalance). Logistic regression alone achieves 0.941, 0.836, and 0.929 AUC in these three settings respectively, outperforming all SAE probe variants. Under covariate shift, SAE probes generalize worse than logistic regression, and in multi-token probing the SAE win rate drops from 19.6% (vs. max-pooled baselines) to 8.7% (vs. attention-pooled baselines).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the five baseline probing methods (logistic regression, PCA regression, KNN, XGBoost, MLP), SAE probes consistently underperform: logistic regression is the single best method in all three quiver-of-arrows regimes, and no SAE probe variant (across widths 16k/131k/1M and k=16/128) exceeds it. Against Bricken et al. (2024a)'s multi-token max-pooled SAE probing, the paper shows that the apparent SAE advantage (19.6% win rate over max-pooled activation baselines) is an artifact of weak baselines; when a stronger attention-pooled probe is added to the quiver, the SAE win rate drops by more than 50% to 8.7%. Against Gallifant et al. (2025)'s latent binarization strategy (threshold=1 after max-pooling), the paper finds that binarizing latents results in *worse* probe performance than non-binarized SAE probes, contradicting the reported benefit. Overall, the paper's central finding is that SAE probes do not provide a consistent competitive advantage over well-tuned simple baselines like logistic regression, even in regimes (data scarcity, class imbalance, label noise, covariate shift) where their interpretable inductive bias was hypothesized to help.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the SAE framework introduced by Bricken et al. (2023) and the linear probing methodology of Alain (2016), using pre-trained SAEs from Gemma Scope (Lieberum et al., 2024) and Llama Scope (He et al., 2024) as the basis for probing. It departs from prior SAE evaluation work, which relied primarily on proxy metrics like reconstruction loss, by instead testing SAEs on a concrete downstream task (binary classification probing) with a suite of strong baselines (logistic regression, PCA regression, KNN, XGBoost, MLP). The paper also directly engages with and challenges prior positive SAE probing results from Bricken et al. (2024a) and Gallifant et al. (2025), showing that apparent SAE advantages disappear under the 'quiver of arrows' evaluation protocol and stronger baselines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This paper provides the most rigorous negative evidence to date that SAEs do not offer a consistent competitive advantage over simple baselines for probing, across 113 datasets, four challenging regimes, and two model families. It highlights a broader methodological concern for mechanistic interpretability: that initial positive findings for interpretability tools may be artifacts of weak baselines, and that the field needs to invest equivalent effort in optimizing comparison methods. Open questions include whether SAEs might help on other downstream tasks (steering, unlearning, circuit discovery), whether future SAE architectures will close the gap, and whether the inductive bias of a truly disentangled basis would ever outweigh the information loss inherent in SAE compression.
