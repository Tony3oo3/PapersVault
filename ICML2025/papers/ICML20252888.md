## Problem

Prior work demonstrated that board-game states can be reconstructed from GPT attention matrices, but did not explain how or why the attention mechanism encodes the board state, nor did it provide a generative mechanism underlying the token sequences. This paper asks whether GPT, trained solely for next-token prediction, implicitly learns a causal world model, and whether the quality of that causal representation predicts the model's ability to generate rule-adherent tokens.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252888/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pre-trained GPT's last-layer attention matrix A (n×n lower-triangular, one per head) for a given input sequence of game-move tokens, and produces a partial ancestral graph (PAG) per head via zero-shot constraint-based causal discovery, plus a scalar structural confidence score R(A). The causal structure is learned from a single forward pass (no additional training data), exploiting the known topological order enforced by the causal mask. The confidence score is computed from the entropy difference between p-values of independence and dependence CI tests, providing a per-sequence measure of how well the attention encodes a causal structure.

## Method

The paper derives a threefold correspondence between GPT and a linear-Gaussian SCM: the values matrix V (Y·W_V) instantiates exogenous variables, the output embeddings Z = AV observe endogenous nodes, and the attention matrix A encodes the transitive closure (I−G)⁻¹ of the causal graph. The key technical step is computing D⁻¹A (where D = diag(A)) to obtain a unitriangular matrix analogous to (I−G)⁻¹, from which the covariance C = [D⁻¹A][D⁻¹A]ᵀ is estimated. A recursive causal discovery algorithm (modified ICD) then exploits the known causal order to learn the PAG efficiently, testing only edges connecting the newly added node to its predecessors at each recursion. A confidence score R(A) = H_ind − H_dep quantifies how sharply the p-value distribution separates independence from dependence, and the paper empirically validates in Othello and Chess that legal-move generation accuracy increases monotonically with this score, and that pruning low-confidence attention heads degrades accuracy more than pruning high-confidence ones.

## Results

In controlled Othello and Chess environments, the paper demonstrates that GPT's legal-move generation accuracy on out-of-distribution (randomly sampled legal moves) inputs increases monotonically with the structural confidence score R derived from the attention matrix. Using all CI tests without limiting conditioning set sizes (case d in Figure 4), the difference R_legal − R_illegal is statistically significant for sequence lengths in [15, 22] and positive for all tested lengths. In the head-pruning experiment, pruning heads with the lowest structural confidence yields higher legal-move accuracy and greater area under the accuracy-vs-pruning-percentile curve than reverse-order pruning (removing high-confidence heads), confirming that heads encoding causal structure are the ones critical for rule-adherent generation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare its method against competing causal-discovery or world-model approaches in a head-to-head benchmark; instead, it validates the proposed causal interpretation of GPT attention through internal ablations and controlled experiments. The key internal comparison is between four CI-test configurations for computing the structural confidence score: raw attention values alone (case a) show no statistically significant difference between legal and illegal generation except at length 20; single-conditioning CI tests (case b) are significant only at length 17; combining both (case c) is significant at lengths 17 and 20; and the full set of CI tests used for causal graph reconstruction (case d) is significant across lengths 15–22, establishing that higher-order conditional independence relations are essential for capturing the causal structure. A second internal comparison is the head-pruning direction: removing low-confidence heads preserves accuracy far better than removing high-confidence heads, demonstrating that the attention heads most aligned with a recoverable causal graph are the ones the model relies on for rule-compliant next-token prediction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the ABCD causal interpretation of BERT's unmasked self-attention (Rohekar et al., 2024) to GPT's masked (causal) attention, exploiting the lower-triangular structure to recover a unitriangular matrix analogous to (I−G)⁻¹ in a linear-Gaussian SCM. It also adapts the ICD constraint-based causal discovery algorithm (Rohekar et al., 2021) into a recursive, zero-shot procedure that leverages the known topological order from GPT's autoregressive masking. Unlike prior work on emergent world representations in GPT (Li et al., 2023; Nanda et al., 2023), which demonstrated that board states are linearly recoverable from attention but did not explain the generative mechanism, this paper provides a principled causal interpretation and a confidence metric linking attention structure to rule-adherent generation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a concrete theoretical lens for understanding how next-token prediction might implicitly encode causal structure, with the practical implication that the proposed confidence score could serve as a hallucination detector in GPT-based systems. However, the empirical validation is confined to two small board games with relatively small models, and the linear-Gaussian SCM assumption is restrictive; whether the causal interpretation and confidence metric transfer to natural language or larger architectures remains entirely open. The analogy between attention and SCM transitive closure is suggestive but not a proof that GPT actually learns causal mechanisms, leaving the central question—whether this is a genuine world model or a convenient statistical shortcut—partially unresolved.
