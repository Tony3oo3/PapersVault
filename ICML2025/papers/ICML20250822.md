## Problem

Standard deep learning models (MLP, SIREN, KAN, PINN) face three critical barriers when applied to large-scale, high-dimensional PDE problems: they require prohibitively large computational resources, suffer from the curse of dimensionality, and their black-box nature limits interpretability and convergence guarantees. Existing data-free solvers like PINNs lack solid theoretical foundations and often fail to converge to high accuracy, while classical FEM struggles with parametric and high-dimensional settings. The paper asks whether a single framework can unify data-driven training, data-free solving, and inverse optimization for PDEs with the accuracy and interpretability of FEM and the parametric flexibility of machine learning.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a space-parameter-time (S-P-T) continuum: spatial coordinates (e.g., x, y, z), a temporal variable t, and parametric variables such as PDE coefficients (heat conductivity k) and source terms (power P), all discretized on 1D meshes with user-controlled resolution (e.g., 101 grid points per dimension). The output is the full PDE solution field (scalar or vector, e.g., temperature or displacement) evaluated at any point in the S-P-T domain; for inverse optimization, it additionally recovers unknown parametric inputs by minimizing the L2 difference between predicted and target fields. Unlike collocation-based methods that require exponentially many points in high dimensions, INN-TD's tensor structure means the number of unknowns scales only with the product of 1D grid sizes across dimensions.

## Method

INN-TD approximates a D-dimensional PDE solution as a sum of M rank-1 tensor modes, where each univariate factor along dimension d is a C-HiDeNN interpolation function — a generalized FEM shape function parameterized by patch size s, dilation a, and reproducing order p that preserves Kronecker delta, partition of unity, and Gaussian-quadrature integrability. This yields a sparse, locally supported architecture (interpretable as a pruned MLP with FE basis functions in the first layers) whose trainable parameters are nodal values rather than opaque weights. For data-free solving, the PDE residual is projected via a Galerkin integral form evaluated with Gaussian quadrature, then linearized through subspace iteration (alternating one dimension at a time) so that each subproblem reduces to a banded sparse 1D linear system solvable by direct sparse solvers. The boosting scheme adds modes sequentially until a convergence tolerance is met, and the all-at-once variant optimizes all M modes simultaneously. Because only 1D integrals are needed per dimension, the computational cost grows linearly with grid resolution per axis rather than exponentially with total dimensionality, enabling high-resolution solutions to 10D+ problems in seconds on a single GPU.

## Results

As a data-free solver for high-dimensional Poisson's equation on domain [0,1]^D, INN-TD achieves relative L2 norm errors of 1.754×10⁻⁸ (2D), 1.659×10⁻⁸ (5D), and 1.238×10⁻⁸ (10D) in 0.81 s, 6.88 s, and 57.43 s respectively — orders of magnitude more accurate and faster than CP-PINN (errors ~10⁻³, times 60–2130 s) and KAN (errors ~10⁻⁵–10⁻⁴, times 56–842 s). On the harder domain [0,12]^D, INN-TD maintains 4.77×10⁻⁴ error in 0.22 s while CP-PINN degrades to 2.5×10⁻² and KAN to 7.8×10⁻¹. For the 6D space-parameter-time heat transfer problem, INN-TD achieves a relative L2 norm error of 3.38×10⁻³ against FEM ground truth in 155 s on a single GPU.

## Baselines & Comparisons

INN-TD dominates all three baselines on the data-free solving task. Against CP-PINN (Vemuri et al., 2025), INN-TD achieves roughly 5 orders of magnitude lower relative L2 error (10⁻⁸ vs. 10⁻³) while being 75× faster in 2D and 310× faster in 5D; CP-PINN's collocation-based approach suffers exponential cost growth with dimension. Against KAN (Liu et al., 2024), INN-TD is 2–3 orders of magnitude more accurate (10⁻⁸ vs. 10⁻⁵–10⁻⁴) and 70–120× faster; KAN also fails to converge on the larger [0,12]^D domain, with errors of 0.78–0.80 versus INN-TD's 4.77×10⁻⁴. Against SIREN (Sitzmann et al., 2020), INN-TD wins on the data-driven training task with RMSE of 0.126×10⁻³ (100% data) versus SIREN's 1.170×10⁻³, a roughly 9× improvement, and the gap widens at lower data fractions (0.213 vs. 2.330 at 10% data). The advantage is most dramatic on high-frequency problems (domain [0,12]^D), where MLP-based methods exhibit spectral bias and fail to converge, while INN-TD's locally supported C-HiDeNN basis functions maintain stable convergence.

## Relation to Prior Work

INN-TD sits at the intersection of two established lines of work: C-HiDeNN (Lu et al., 2023), a generalized finite element interpolation framework that replaces standard FEM shape functions with convolutional hierarchical deep networks, and CP/tensor decomposition (Kolda & Bader, 2009), which has been used for PDE solving and data compression. The key architectural departure from prior tensor-decomposition PDE solvers like CP-PINN (Vemuri et al., 2025) is replacing the MLP-based univariate modes with C-HiDeNN locally supported interpolation functions, which inherits Kronecker delta, partition of unity, and Gaussian quadrature compatibility from classical FEM. This positions INN-TD as a structured, interpretable alternative to both black-box PINN-style solvers and standard FEM, unifying data-driven training, data-free solving, and inverse optimization under a single sparse tensor-decomposition architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method addresses a critical bottleneck in ML-based PDE solving: the curse of dimensionality and the lack of convergence guarantees. By restricting integration to 1D per dimension (enabled by the tensor structure) and using locally supported basis functions, INN-TD scales to 10D problems in under a minute while achieving ~10⁻⁸ relative error—orders of magnitude beyond collocation-based methods. The guaranteed convergence property (more parameters → monotonically decreasing error) is a significant departure from black-box deep learning and provides practitioners with a principled way to target desired accuracy. Open questions include whether the approach extends to strongly nonlinear or multiphysics PDEs, how mesh design choices (elements, s, p) interact with problem complexity in practice, and whether the FEM-based ground truth used in the S-P-T validation introduces circularity.
