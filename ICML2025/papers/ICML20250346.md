## Problem

Neural scaling laws are well-characterized for text and vision but remain largely unexplored for speech, particularly for massively multilingual, multi-task systems. Prior speech scaling studies were limited to single-task, single-modality settings or a handful of languages, and the models used were trained on proprietary data and never released. This leaves practitioners without reliable, reproducible guidance on how model size, data volume, and compute budget jointly affect downstream ASR and ST performance across hundreds of languages.

## Input / Output

The model consumes 80-dimensional log-Mel filterbanks (10 ms frame shift, 4× downsampled by a convolutional stack) padded to a fixed 30-second window, along with a Whisper-style task prompt that selects among language identification, ASR, ST, or timestamp prediction. For in-context learning experiments, k retrieved speech examples are concatenated with the target utterance along the sequence dimension while their transcriptions are prepended as a decoder text prompt. Outputs are auto-regressively generated text tokens from a 50K unigram subword vocabulary, decoded via a hybrid CTC/attention objective (CTC weight 0.3).

## Method

OWLS comprises 13 Whisper-style Transformer encoder-decoder models spanning 0.25B to 18B parameters, all trained with identical hyperparameters (Adam, piecewise LR schedule, bfloat16, Flash Attention 2, DeepSpeed ZeRO-2) on up to 360K hours of public speech across 150 languages, varying only model size or data size to isolate each scaling axis. The key empirical contribution is fitting power-law relationships (WER = β·N^α, BLEU = β·N^α) to per-language FLEURS and ST benchmarks, showing that language-specific model-size scaling laws predict WER/CER with R² ≈ 0.95, while data scaling saturates quickly without added diversity. Beyond the scaling fits, the paper probes test-time emergent abilities—orthographic understanding, code-switching robustness, and cross-lingual mondegreen generation—that appear only in the 9B and 18B models and are absent in smaller ones, establishing that speech model scaling yields qualitatively new capabilities analogous to those observed in LLMs.

## Results

OWLS 18B v2 (18B params, 360K hours) achieves the best or second-best WER/CER on 5 of 7 benchmarks, including 7.2 CER on ReazonSpeech (Japanese) and 15.0 WER on KsponSpeech (Korean), substantially outperforming Whisper v3 (15.1 and 13.4 respectively) despite being trained on 14× less data. On FLEURS English, OWLS 18B v2 reaches 6.8 WER, trailing only Whisper v3 (4.1) and Canary (7.1). Scaling from 0.25B to 9B parameters nearly halves average multi-domain English WER from 12.1 to 6.3, and the 18B model further improves to 7.7 on FLEURS English. For speech translation, scaling from 1B to 9B nearly doubles English-to-German BLEU from 16.6 to 28.9.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Whisper v3 (trained on ~14× more undisclosed data), OWLS 18B v2 wins decisively on Japanese (6.7 vs. 4.9 CER on FLEURS-jpn is actually Whisper winning; but on ReazonSpeech OWLS achieves 7.2 vs. Whisper's 15.1 CER) and Korean (15.0 vs. 13.4 WER on KsponSpeech, where Whisper actually wins). OWLS 18B v2 trails Whisper on FLEURS English (6.8 vs. 4.1) and FLEURS Chinese (10.1 vs. 7.7), but matches it on LibriSpeech clean (2.0 vs. 2.0). Against Seamless M/L, OWLS 18B v2 dominates across all shared benchmarks, e.g., 6.7 vs. 15.9/17.6 CER on FLEURS Japanese and 10.1 vs. 15.7/17.0 on FLEURS Chinese. Against Qwen2Audio, OWLS 18B v2 is far superior on Japanese (6.7 vs. 20.1) and Korean (15.0 vs. 53.3), though Qwen2Audio edges ahead on LibriSpeech clean (1.6 vs. 2.0). Against SenseVoice, OWLS 18B v2 wins on Japanese (6.7 vs. 13.1 CER) and Korean (15.0 vs. 24.5 WER), while SenseVoice L achieves a lower AISHELL score (2.1 vs. 4.8). Against Canary (limited to 4 European languages), OWLS 18B v2 achieves a lower FLEURS English WER (6.8 vs. 7.1) but Canary leads on LibriSpeech clean (1.5 vs. 2.0).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Pythia-style open model suite paradigm (Biderman et al., 2023) from text to speech, building directly on Whisper's encoder-decoder architecture and task-prompt training paradigm (Radford et al., 2023) and the OWSM open-source pipeline (Peng et al., 2023b). It departs from prior speech scaling work (Zheng et al., 2022; Li et al., 2021) in three key ways: the models are fully open-source, the evaluation spans 150 languages rather than a handful, and the paper derives empirical power-law scaling relationships for downstream WER/BLEU rather than merely reporting performance at a few scales. The scaling law formulation itself follows the standard L(x) = L_∞ + βx^α framework from text and vision, but its application to multi-modal, multi-task, massively multilingual speech is novel.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

OWLS provides the first open, reproducible suite of speech models specifically designed for scaling analysis, with the 18B model being the largest publicly available ASR/ST model and performing comparably to Whisper trained on 14× more data. The derived scaling laws give practitioners a concrete tool to predict downstream performance before committing to expensive training runs, and the finding that model scaling disproportionately benefits low-resource languages (59→45 average WER from 1B to 9B) has direct implications for equitable speech technology deployment. Key limitations include data scaling without diversity saturating quickly, model scaling being unable to overcome fundamentally insufficient data (e.g., 1 hour of Japanese ST), and the emergent abilities (mondegreens, orthographic understanding) remaining largely unexploited in practice. Open questions include whether these scaling laws hold beyond 18B parameters, how to better leverage data diversity at scale, and whether the discovered emergent capabilities can be harnessed for practical zero/few-shot adaptation.
