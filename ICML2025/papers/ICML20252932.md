## Problem

Existing batched linear bandit algorithms face a fundamental tension: those with minimax-optimal regret guarantees (e.g., Ruan et al. 2021, Hanna et al. 2023, Zhang et al. 2025) are computationally infeasible at moderate scales, while practical methods either update too frequently or eliminate the optimal arm prematurely. No prior algorithm simultaneously achieves minimax-optimal regret in both the large-K (K ≥ Ω(e^d)) and small-K (K ≤ O(e^d)) regimes with only O(log log T) batches and tractable computation.

## Input / Output

The algorithm receives K arm feature vectors in R^d (with ‖x‖₂ ≤ 1), a time horizon T, and a regularization parameter λ. It produces a fixed policy per batch that allocates arm pulls proportionally to a computed distribution, yielding O(log log T) total batches with sizes T^((2ℓ−1)/(2ℓ)). The output is a cumulative regret bound of Õ(√(dT log K) ∧ d√T) and a batched regularized least-squares estimate θ̂_ℓ updated only at batch boundaries, with the constraint that H_ℓ^{-1} must remain independent of the noise terms used within batch ℓ.

## Method

BLAE alternates between a regularized G-optimal design step and an arm-elimination step across O(log log T) batches. At each batch ℓ, it solves a min-max convex program to find the sampling distribution π_ℓ* that minimizes the worst-case Mahalanobis norm ‖x‖²_{V_ℓ(π)^{-1}} over remaining arms, where V_ℓ(π) = (λ/c_ℓ)I + Σ π_i T^((2ℓ−1)/(2ℓ)) x⁽ⁱ⁾x⁽ⁱ⁾ᵀ incorporates a regularization term absent in prior G-optimal design analyses. The actual pull distribution blends π_ℓ* with a point mass on the current estimated optimal arm, controlled by a tunable rate c_ℓ. After each batch, a batched regularized least-squares estimator (ensuring H_ℓ^{-1} is independent of in-batch noise) drives an elimination rule that removes arms whose estimated suboptimality gap exceeds ε_ℓ, with ε_ℓ constructed from a joint concentration bound over all arm pairs. The key technical novelty is a refined G-optimal design lemma (Lemma 3) that accounts for regularization under arm elimination, yielding the tighter uncertainty bound d/(dλ + T^((2ℓ−1)/(2ℓ))c_ℓ) and enabling simultaneous matching of both regime lower bounds.

## Results

BLAE achieves a worst-case regret of Õ(√(dT log K) ∧ d√T) with only O(log log T) batches, simultaneously matching the minimax lower bounds Ω(d√T) (large-K regime, K ≥ Ω(e^d)) and Ω(√(dT log K)) (small-K regime, K ≤ O(e^d)). In runtime experiments (K=50, d=5), BLAE completes T=100,000 rounds in 0.73 seconds, compared to 464.2 s (Ruan et al.) and 717.8 s (Zhang et al.) for only T=5,000. Across all 8 (K,d) configurations tested (K ∈ {50,100,200,400}, d ∈ {5,10}), BLAE consistently achieves the lowest cumulative regret with the fewest policy updates among all compared methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BLAE outperforms all six compared baselines on both theoretical regret and empirical performance. Against E⁴ (Ren et al., 2024), which achieves the same O(log log T) batch complexity, BLAE achieves strictly lower regret (removing the log(KT) factor) and substantially lower empirical cumulative regret; E⁴ frequently eliminates the optimal arm prematurely and its third batch can consume the entire horizon due to sensitivity to hyperparameter γ. Against RS-OFUL (Abbasi-Yadkori et al., 2011), BLAE matches or beats regret while using O(log log T) batches versus RS-OFUL's O(d log T) (over 100 updates in experiments). Against PhaElimD (Esfandiari et al., 2021), BLAE improves both the regret bound (removing the log(KT) factor) and reduces batch complexity from O(log T) to O(log log T). Against Ruan et al. (2021), Zhang et al. (2025), and Hanna et al. (2023), BLAE achieves comparable or better theoretical bounds while being 600–1000× faster in runtime (0.73 s vs. 464–718 s at comparable scales), as the latter methods inherit computationally heavy subroutines designed for time-varying contextual features that are unnecessary in the fixed-arm setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

BLAE extends the phased elimination framework of Esfandiari et al. (2021) and the regularized least-squares estimation of Abbasi-Yadkori et al. (2011) by introducing two key modifications: a batched RLS estimator that preserves the independence required for valid concentration bounds under policy updates, and a regularized G-optimal design criterion (extending the Kiefer-Wolfowitz equivalence) that handles the potentially singular information matrix arising from arm elimination. Unlike prior batched methods (Ruan et al. 2021, Hanna et al. 2023, Zhang et al. 2025) that inherit subroutines from contextual or adversarial settings, BLAE is purpose-built for the fixed-feature linear bandit, eliminating unnecessary computational overhead. The arm-pair concentration inequality (Lemma 2) departs from standard individual-arm bounds by exploiting the structure of the elimination criterion, yielding tighter thresholds that preserve the optimal arm with high probability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

BLAE resolves a concrete open problem: no prior batched linear bandit algorithm simultaneously matched the minimax lower bounds in both the large-K (Ω(d√T)) and small-K (Ω(√(dT log K))) regimes while using only O(log log T) batches. The practical impact is substantial—BLAE runs in under 1 second for T=100,000 while the closest theoretical competitors require hundreds of seconds at T=5,000 or are infeasible entirely. Open questions include whether the regularized G-optimal design and batched RLS techniques extend to contextual or adversarial linear bandits, whether the log log T factor in the regret can be removed, and whether the approach generalizes to structured action spaces beyond finite arm sets.
