## Problem

Current machine learning evaluations test systems in isolation from humans, producing benchmarks that saturate quickly, cannot directly measure human-centered desiderata like interpretability and helpfulness, and create incentives for human replacement rather than human augmentation. The paper argues that the field lacks a systematic framework for evaluating AI in collaborative human-AI settings, despite the fact that most real-world deployment involves humans working alongside machines.

## Input / Output

A centaur evaluation takes a human participant (selected via an explicit criterion, e.g., crowd workers or bring-your-own-human), an ML system, a task specification, and a defined interaction interface as inputs. Beyond a standard task performance score, the framework produces the marginal value of human time (∂f_i/∂L) as an economic quantity, interaction transcripts for training and analysis, and a binary classification of the system as human-augmenting or human-replacing. Scoring can incorporate resource constraints (compute tokens, human time) and process-level metrics, not just final outcomes.

## Method

The paper defines a centaur benchmark as a triple of (human selection criterion, interaction interface, scoring mechanism) plus an optional transcript component, and distinguishes between 'centaurized' evaluations (relaxing the no-human constraint on existing benchmarks like MMLU) and purpose-designed centaur evaluations. The key formal contribution is a centaur production function Y = f_i(K, L) mapping compute K and human time L to task performance, from which the marginal product of human labor ∂f_i/∂L serves as a formal measure of human augmentation (Definition 4.1: augmenting if ∂f/∂L ≫ 0, replacing if ∂f/∂L ≈ 0). For practical implementation, the authors propose three infrastructure designs: crowd-work platforms for human recruitment, randomized controlled trials with causal inference to estimate production functions and marginal values, and data-science-style leaderboards with bring-your-own-human protocols that optimize both the AI system and the human collaborator.

## Results

This is a position paper with no original quantitative experiments or results tables. Its central contribution is a formal framework defining centaur evaluations (human + AI joint task completion) with three required components (human selection, interface, scoring) and an optional transcript component. The paper introduces a centaur production function Y = f_i(K, L) and defines a system as human-augmenting if ∂f/∂L ≫ 0 versus human-replacing if ∂f/∂L ≈ 0, providing a formal criterion for comparing AI systems on their value to human workers. Supporting evidence is drawn from prior studies: Peng et al. (2023) and Mozannar et al. (2024b) report large speed-ups in coding with AI assistance, Cui et al. (2024) find large productivity increases in a randomized controlled trial, and Brynjolfsson et al. (2025) document significant call-center productivity gains from chatbot use.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not empirically compare its proposed centaur evaluation framework against baseline evaluation methods. Instead, it contrasts the centaur evaluation paradigm against the dominant imitation-based evaluation paradigm (e.g., MMLU, ImageNet, Turing Test) and addresses three counterarguments: (1) that human augmentation does not exist (rebutted by citing task-dependent evidence where centaurs outperform both humans and AI alone, e.g., child protective services, call centers, entrepreneurship); (2) that centaur evaluations are noisy and irreproducible (rebutted by arguing that sensitivity to experimental conditions is a feature, and that existing infrastructure from crowd work, RCTs, and competitions can manage costs); and (3) that centaur evaluations carry high opportunity costs (rebutted by noting that approximations and synthetic centaur evaluations can reduce costs, and that the societal benefits of steering toward human augmentation outweigh the iteration-speed loss for frontier models). The paper positions itself against the 'Turing Trap' narrative (Brynjolfsson, 2022) and aligns with the augmentation tradition of Bush, Licklider, and Engelbart rather than the imitation tradition of Turing.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper deliberately positions itself against the Turing Test / imitation-based evaluation tradition (Turing 1950, ImageNet) that has dominated ML benchmarking, and instead draws on the human-computer interaction lineage (Bush's memex, Licklider's man-computer symbiosis, Engelbart's bootstrapping) and the DARPA robotics challenges, where human operators and machines have always collaborated. It repurposes economic production-function theory (marginal value of labor, competitive wage equilibrium) to formalize what 'human augmentation' means in an evaluation context, a framing absent from prior ML evaluation literature. While a handful of centaur evaluations exist (Lee et al. 2024, Shao et al. 2025, Wijk et al. 2024), the paper argues they remain marginal and proposes a systematic framework—human selection, interface, scoring, optional transcripts—that unifies and extends these scattered efforts into a reproducible methodology.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central claim—that current evaluations are structurally blind to human augmentation because they evaluate f(K,0) rather than f(K,L)—reframes a methodological choice as an economic and policy issue, with direct implications for the EU AI Act's explainability requirements and for labor-market inequality. The 'five stages of automation' heuristic and the production-function formalization give practitioners and policymakers a concrete vocabulary for distinguishing augmenting from replacing systems. Key open questions remain: the cost and sample complexity of running centaur evaluations at scale, whether synthetic centaur approximations (LLM-simulated humans) can serve as reliable proxies, and how to standardize interfaces and scoring across diverse tasks without losing the very human variability that makes centaur evaluations informative.
