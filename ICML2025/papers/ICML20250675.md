## Problem

While local counterfactual explanations for GNNs are well-studied and global counterfactual explainers have been proposed, the problem of finding a small set of shared transformations (common recourse) that can convert every reject graph into an accept graph remains unexplored. Existing global counterfactuals produce different recourse for different input graphs, limiting their interpretability and actionability at the model level.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250675/cropped_page5_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a set of reject graphs (predicted as class 0 by a trained GNN) and the GNN model itself, along with hyperparameters: budget R (number of common recourse to output), counterfactual distance threshold θ, and common recourse similarity threshold Δ. It outputs a set of R common recourse vectors in R^64 (the GREED embedding space), each representing a cluster center of similar graph-edit transformations, along with coverage (fraction of input graphs for which at least one counterfactual is reachable via one of the recourse) and total recourse cost. Recourse are defined as embedding-space vectors between input and counterfactual graph representations rather than explicit edit operations.

## Method

COMRECGC operates in three stages: (1) graphs are embedded into R^64 via GREED to define recourse as vectors and enable distance computation; (2) a multi-head vertex-reinforced random walk (VRRW) explores the graph edit space, where a lead head moves toward GNN-predicted counterfactuals with transition probability proportional to visit count times GNN score, while non-lead heads follow the lead's recourse direction, and all heads teleport back to under-explored input graphs with probability τ; (3) DBSCAN clusters the resulting recourse vectors at radius Δ to identify common recourse, followed by greedy submodular maximization to select the R best common recourse for maximum coverage. The multi-head design is key: by having heads track a shared recourse direction, the walk naturally produces counterfactuals with similar transformations, making common recourse clustering more effective. Theoretically, the FCR problem is NP-hard with a (1−1/e) greedy approximation guarantee, and the more general FC problem admits a 1−e^{−1/R} approximation under a monotonicity constraint.

## Results

On the FCR problem, COMRECGC (5-head) achieves 42.9%, 51.8%, 34.7%, and 46.4% total coverage on NCI1, MUTAGENICITY, AIDS, and PROTEINS respectively, outperforming GCFEXPLAINER (21.4%, 20.6%, 14.2%, 32.8%) by 20–30 percentage points. On the FC problem (constrained counterfactual budget), COMRECGC reaches 33.4%, 46.7%, 24.3%, and 39.6% coverage, surpassing the best baseline (LOCALRWEXPLAINER at 19.0%, 18.2%, 12.9%, 22.1%) by 14–27 percentage points. Recourse cost is also lower for COMRECGC on NCI1 (4.95 vs 5.75) and MUTAGENICITY (5.63 vs 6.91) compared to GCFEXPLAINER, though slightly higher on PROTEINS (11.59 vs 10.65).

## Baselines & Comparisons

COMRECGC decisively outperforms GCFEXPLAINER (Huang et al., WSDM 2023) on the FCR problem across all four datasets, with coverage gains of +21.5 pp (NCI1), +31.2 pp (MUTAGENICITY), +20.5 pp (AIDS), and +13.6 pp (PROTEINS), while also achieving lower recourse cost on three of four datasets. On the FC problem, COMRECGC beats both GCFEXPLAINER and the local random walk explainer (LOCALRWEXPLAINER) on every dataset, with the largest margin on MUTAGENICITY (46.7% vs 18.2% for LOCALRWEXPLAINER). In the global counterfactual comparison (Table 5), COMRECGC's common recourse explanations match or exceed GCFEXPLAINER's graph counterfactuals (26.1% vs 27.9% on NCI1, 39.4% vs 37.1% on MUTAGENICITY, 15.2% vs 14.7% on AIDS, 18.0% vs 10.9% on PROTEINS), and substantially outperform RCEXPLAINER (Tan et al., WebConf 2022) and CFF (Bajaj et al., NeurIPS 2021) on all datasets. CF-GNNExplainer (Lucic et al., AISTATS 2022), a local subgraph-based explainer, produces the weakest common recourse coverage (9.1% on NCI1, 8.4% on MUTAGENICITY, 0.1% on AIDS, 0% on PROTEINS), confirming that local subgraph counterfactuals are poorly suited for forming shared recourse.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the GNN counterfactual explanation literature by introducing 'common recourse'—a small set of graph transformations shared across multiple input graphs—as a new global explanation paradigm. It builds on GREED (Ranjan et al., 2023) for graph embedding to define recourse vectors in a continuous space, and on vertex-reinforced random walks (Pemantle, 2004) as the exploration mechanism in the graph edit space. Conceptually, it departs from prior global counterfactual explainers (GCFEXPLAINER, GLOBE-CE) that produce a set of accept graphs, and from local explainers (CF-GNNExplainer, CFF, RCEXPLAINER) that produce one counterfactual per instance, by instead seeking a compact set of transformations that collectively cover all reject graphs. The recourse concept itself is borrowed from algorithmic fairness (Karimi et al., Rawal & Lakkaraju) but is here adapted and formalized for the first time in the graph domain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The formalization of the FCR and FC problems with NP-hardness proofs and approximation guarantees provides a theoretical foundation for a previously unexplored explanation paradigm, and the empirical results show common recourse explanations are comparable to or exceed global counterfactuals in coverage while offering instance-level interpretability. Key limitations include sensitivity to the θ and Δ thresholds (DBSCAN becomes intractable at larger θ), the absence of domain-specific feasibility constraints on generated counterfactuals, and the reliance on a single embedding method (GREED). Open questions include whether the approach scales to larger graph datasets, how to incorporate physical/chemical validity constraints, and whether the random walk exploration can be made more principled given the lack of convergence guarantees for non-symmetric VRRW.
