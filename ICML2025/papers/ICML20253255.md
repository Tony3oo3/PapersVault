## Problem

Extending random feature neural networks (RFNNs) to deep ResNet architectures is non-trivial: naively stacking random layers either leaves the initial representation dominant (blocks too small) or destroys prior information (blocks too large), and random layers are not guaranteed to approximate the negative functional gradient of the loss. Existing theory for deep RFNNs is limited to Fourier activations and control-theoretic sampling, leaving a gap for general random features in a tractable convex optimization framework.

## Input / Output

The method consumes tabular feature vectors (one-hot encoded categoricals, normalized numerics) paired with scalar regression targets or categorical class labels, and produces a linear prediction from the final ResNet representation. A distinctive design choice is that each random feature layer f_t takes as input both the raw data x and the previous layer's output Φ_{t-1}(x) via concatenation, enabling the residual blocks to condition on the full input history. For the synthetic point cloud task, inputs are 2D points from concentric circles with a hidden size restricted to 2.

## Method

RFRBoost builds a deep ResNet RFNN layer-by-layer via gradient representation boosting: at each step t, random features f_t are generated (SWIM-sampled tanh layers), and a linear map A_t is learned to map these features toward the negative functional gradient of the empirical risk with respect to the representation. For MSE loss, A_t is obtained in closed form by solving a 'sandwiched least squares' problem (a special case of generalized Sylvester equations) with scalar, diagonal, or dense parameterizations. For general losses (e.g., cross-entropy), fitting A_t reduces to a quadratically constrained least squares problem that enforces unit L_2^D(μ) norm on the residual block, preserving the functional gradient direction. A line search then determines the optimal step size α_t, and the top-level linear predictor is updated via convex optimization. This yields a fully convex, layer-wise training procedure with Rademacher-complexity-based excess risk bounds, avoiding SGD and non-convex optimization entirely.

## Results

On 91 OpenML regression datasets, Gradient RFRBoost achieves a mean RMSE of 0.408 with a single-core CPU fit time of 1.7s, outperforming E2E MLP ResNets (0.412 RMSE, 19.3s) and single-layer RFNNs (0.434 RMSE, 0.05s). On 91 OpenML classification datasets, RFRBoost reaches 85.3% mean accuracy, matching XGBoost (85.3%) and surpassing E2E MLP ResNets (85.1%) and RFNNs (84.5%). On the challenging concentric circles point cloud separation task (hidden size 2), RFRBoost achieves 99.7% accuracy versus 73.2% for E2E MLP ResNets and 88.7% for RFNNs, while training ~8x faster than the E2E baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against XGBoost, RFRBoost achieves a slightly higher mean RMSE (0.408 vs. 0.394) on regression but matches it exactly on classification (85.3% vs. 85.3%); however, RFRBoost attains a better average relative rank across datasets, indicating more consistent per-dataset performance. Against E2E MLP ResNets, RFRBoost wins on all three evaluation settings—regression (0.408 vs. 0.412 RMSE), classification (85.3% vs. 85.1% accuracy), and concentric circles (99.7% vs. 73.2% accuracy)—while training roughly 10x faster (1.7s vs. 19.3s on regression). Against single-layer RFNNs, RFRBoost consistently outperforms across all tasks: 0.408 vs. 0.434 RMSE on regression, 85.3% vs. 84.5% on classification, and 99.7% vs. 88.7% on concentric circles, demonstrating that the depth and gradient-guided residual blocks provide substantial gains over a single random feature layer. The concentric circles result is particularly striking, as RFRBoost achieves near-perfect separation with only a 2-dimensional hidden space, a task where both E2E ResNets and RFNNs fail.

## Relation to Prior Work

RFRBoost sits at the intersection of three research threads: random feature neural networks (Rahimi & Recht, Rudi & Rosasco), which fix hidden layers and train only a linear head; gradient representation boosting (Suggala et al., Nitanda & Suzuki), which trains ResNet residual blocks layer-by-layer via functional gradients in L₂; and the ResNet skip-connection architecture (He et al.). The key departure is replacing the SGD-trained shallow networks used as weak learners in prior gradient representation boosting with untrained random feature layers, where only a linear map A_t is learned to project random features onto the functional gradient. This preserves the convex optimization structure of RFNNs while enabling depth, and extends beyond the Fourier-feature restriction of prior deep RFNN theory (Davis et al., Kammonen et al.) to arbitrary random feature families such as SWIM.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

RFRBoost offers a principled, theoretically grounded path to deep random feature networks with excess risk guarantees from boosting theory, addressing the long-standing difficulty of naively stacking random layers. The closed-form 'sandwiched least squares' solutions for MSE loss and the quadratically constrained least squares formulation for general losses make the method computationally attractive (an order of magnitude faster than E2E ResNets). However, the paper acknowledges that on larger datasets (~100k–500k samples), E2E networks and XGBoost eventually outperform RFRBoost, limiting its practical regime to small-to-medium scale. Open questions include whether RFRBoost can serve as an initialization for subsequent end-to-end fine-tuning, how it extends to non-tabular modalities (images, time series), and whether more sophisticated random feature constructions or momentum strategies can close the gap at scale.
