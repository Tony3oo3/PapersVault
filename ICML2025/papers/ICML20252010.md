## Problem

Identifying optimal combinatorial gene or drug perturbations from an exponentially large search space (~200M two-gene combinations) is infeasible by exhaustive experimentation, and existing deep learning models underperform linear baselines when training data is limited to a few hundred samples. The paper addresses how to build a predictive model that works well from very small initial datasets while progressively capturing complex gene-gene interactions, and how to strategically select which unseen combinations to test next to accelerate discovery of the strongest perturbations.

## Input / Output

The model takes as input the scalar single-gene perturbation effect values (e.g., log-fold change in cell viability) for each gene in a pair, along with gene pair identifiers that index into a learnable gene embedding matrix. It outputs a scalar predicted combined perturbation effect for any unseen gene pair, plus an acquisition score (MPE, UCB, or uncertainty) used to rank all unmeasured pairs and select the next batch of experiments. The gene embedding dimension is not fixed but adapts to the average number of times each gene has been observed in training (ranging from 2 to 128 dimensions), and the model can be extended to q > 2 simultaneous perturbations by summing q embeddings.

## Method

NAIAD decomposes the combined perturbation effect into two additive terms: an overparameterized single-gene effect term that projects the concatenated scalar effects [Y_i, Y_j] through a matrix W_1 into a high-dimensional space (m >> k) to capture non-additive contributions, and a gene-interaction term that sums the learned gene embeddings (a permutation-invariant operation) and compresses them through an encoder to a scalar. The key architectural innovation is the adaptive gene embedding dimension, which starts small (e.g., 2D) to prevent overfitting on minimal data and grows on a schedule tied to average gene observation frequency, allowing the model to bridge the performance gap between linear models (dominant at low data) and deep networks (dominant at high data). For active learning, NAIAD uses a Maximum Predicted Effect (MPE) acquisition function that selects gene pairs with the highest absolute ensemble-mean predicted effect, which outperforms uncertainty-based and UCB sampling for identifying the globally strongest perturbations. The framework is trained as a supervised regression on observed pair effects and iteratively retrained as new experimental data is acquired, with ensemble variance used for uncertainty estimation.

## Results

On four bulk combinatorial CRISPR datasets (350,000+ total interactions), NAIAD achieves the lowest RMSE across all models when each gene is observed ~4 times in training, outperforming the second-best model (RECOVER) by over 40% on average. In the active learning setting with MPE sampling, NAIAD identifies approximately 150 of the top 200 strongest gene pairs by round 4, compared to ~92 with uniform sampling and ~111 with UCB. For drug combination screening (94 drugs, 1,828 pairs), NAIAD consistently outperforms RECOVER in identifying the strongest drug combinations, with the performance gap widening from round 2 onward.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NAIAD is compared against GEARS (GNN-based, Roohini et al. 2023) and RECOVER (bilinear embedding + UCB active learning, Bertin et al. 2023). Against GEARS, NAIAD dominates decisively: at gene frequency 4, GEARS RMSE is 16.6/5.4/13.0/13.5 vs. NAIAD's 5.1/2.2/6.1/3.0, and GEARS remains the worst model even at frequency 20 (RMSE up to 20.7 on Jurkat). Against RECOVER, NAIAD wins on RMSE at gene frequency 4 (e.g., 2.2 vs. 3.9 on Simpson, 3.0 vs. 5.0 on Jurkat) and in active learning: NAIAD+MPE discovers 143/142/100/150 of the top-200 gene pairs by round 4 versus RECOVER+MPE's 139/88/54/66, a 1.7× average improvement. The gap narrows at gene frequency 20 where both NAIAD and RECOVER reach comparable RMSE (~1.9–2.8), suggesting that with sufficient data, both models capture the dominant interaction structure. In drug combination screening, NAIAD outperforms RECOVER on top-N accuracy for N < 50 and N > 50, with the advantage growing from round 2 onward.

## Relation to Prior Work

NAIAD extends the AI+Lab active learning loop introduced by RECOVER (Bertin et al., 2023) from drug combinations to gene perturbations, replacing RECOVER's bilinear interaction operator with a permutation-invariant sum of learned gene embeddings and adding an overparameterized single-gene effect encoder. It departs from VAE-based combinatorial perturbation models (CPA, ComβVAE, SAMS-VAE) and GNN-based approaches (GEARS) that operate on single-cell transcriptomic profiles with limited gene combinations, instead targeting bulk phenotypic outcomes (cell viability) across much larger combinatorial spaces. The key architectural departure is the data-size-adaptive embedding dimension, which bridges the gap between linear-model performance in the small-data regime and deep-learning capacity as data accumulates—addressing a limitation the authors explicitly identify in prior deep learning approaches for perturbation prediction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

NAIAD's practical impact lies in reducing the number of expensive CRISPR screening rounds needed to identify the strongest gene or drug pairs, with a 40% RMSE advantage over the second-best model when each gene is observed only ~4 times—a regime that corresponds to realistic initial screening budgets. The finding that pure exploitation (MPE) outperforms the exploration-exploitation balance (UCB) for the specific objective of discovering top-N strongest perturbations challenges the default Bayesian-optimization heuristic and could reshape acquisition-function design in similar combinatorial search problems. Key limitations include the requirement for known single-gene effects (no cold-start for unseen genes), inability to predict full expression profiles, and the fixed (non-learnable) acquisition function; extending to higher-order combinations and incorporating pre-trained gene embeddings for unseen genes are natural next steps.
