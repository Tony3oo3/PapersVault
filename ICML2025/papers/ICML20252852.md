## Problem

Existing Partial Information Decomposition (PID) methods quantify multimodal interactions (redundancy, uniqueness, synergy) only at the distribution level, making them unable to capture how interaction patterns vary across individual samples. Pointwise PID approaches have been proposed for sample-level estimation but fail to provide efficient, practical solutions for continuous distributions encountered in real-world multimodal data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252852/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pair of modality samples (x1, x2) and a target label y, along with pre-trained discriminative models p(y|x1), p(y|x2), p(y|x1,x2) and lightweight KNIFE-based entropy estimators trained separately on each modality's marginal distribution. It outputs four pointwise interaction values per sample — redundancy r, uniqueness u1 and u2, and synergy s — which can be averaged across the dataset to recover distribution-level R, U1, U2, S. Unlike distribution-level PID that models the full joint X1×X2×Y space, LSMI only requires per-modality entropy estimation, making it scalable to high-dimensional continuous features.

## Method

LSMI defines sample-level interactions through a redundancy-based lattice framework where pointwise mutual information is decomposed into a positive component i+(x;y)=h(x)=−log p(x) and a negative component i−(x;y)=h(x|y)=−log p(x|y), ensuring monotonicity on the decomposition lattice (which raw pointwise MI violates due to negative values). Redundancy is then computed as r = min(i+(x1;y), i+(x2;y)) − min(i−(x1;y), i−(x2;y)), and uniqueness/synergy are derived algebraically from the pointwise PID equations. For continuous distributions, the positive component is estimated via the KNIFE differential entropy estimator (minimizing an upper bound on entropy), while the negative component is computed from the discriminative model outputs. This avoids the expensive joint-distribution optimization used by prior PID methods, requiring only per-modality entropy estimators and standard discriminative classifiers, yielding a lightweight pipeline that runs in seconds rather than hours on real-world datasets.

## Results

On circuit logic tasks with known ground truth, LSMI recovers interactions with near-perfect precision (e.g., XOR synergy: 0.691 vs GT 0.693; OR redundancy: 0.215 vs GT 0.215), outperforming PID-CVX and PID-Batch which show larger deviations. On real-world datasets, LSMI estimates align closely with PID-Batch and human-annotated judgments (Pearson correlation 0.98 for redundancy, 0.95 for text uniqueness on Food-101). In terms of efficiency, LSMI is dramatically faster than PID-Batch: 678.9 s vs 48,576.6 s on UCF-101 (~71× speedup) and 504.0 s vs 59,679.5 s on Food-101 (~118× speedup), with the gap widening as the number of classes increases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares LSMI against two distribution-level estimators from Liang et al. (2023b): PID-CVX (designed for discrete distributions) and PID-Batch (applicable to both discrete and continuous distributions). On circuit logic tasks with verifiable ground truth, LSMI matches GT more closely than both baselines across all three tasks; for example, on XOR+NOT, LSMI estimates U₂=0.336 and S=0.347 (GT: 0.347, 0.347), while PID-Batch deviates substantially (U₂=0.257, S=0.381). On Gaussian mixture and preset-interaction synthetic data, LSMI tracks ground truth interactions as the correlation coefficient ρ varies, whereas PID-CVX and PID-Batch show systematic divergence, particularly in synergy estimation. On real-world datasets (Table 2), LSMI is largely consistent with PID-Batch in absolute values and aligns with human-annotated interaction rankings, achieving Pearson correlations of 0.98 (redundancy) and 0.95 (text uniqueness) on Food-101. The most striking advantage is computational: LSMI avoids joint distribution modeling, yielding 71–118× speedups over PID-Batch on large-class datasets (UCF-101, Food-101), while maintaining comparable or superior estimation accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Partial Information Decomposition (PID) framework from Williams & Beer (2010) and its pointwise formulation by Finn & Lizier (2018a) to the setting of continuous, real-world multimodal data at the sample level. Where prior distribution-level estimators (PID-CVX, PID-Batch from Liang et al., 2023b) optimize over the full joint distribution and cannot produce per-sample values, and where pointwise PID was restricted to discrete distributions, LSMI introduces a redundancy-based lattice decomposition combined with the KNIFE differential entropy estimator (Pichler et al., 2022) to achieve efficient, per-sample interaction quantification for continuous modalities. The key methodological departure is the i+/i− component split (Eq. 4), which resolves the monotonicity violation that pointwise mutual information suffers when values go negative, enabling a well-defined redundancy measure at the event level.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By making sample-level interaction quantification practical for continuous multimodal data, this work opens a path toward interpretable, interaction-aware multimodal learning: the demonstrated applications (redundancy-based data partitioning for ImageBind fine-tuning, interaction-weighted knowledge distillation, and interaction-guided model ensembling) show concrete performance gains from leveraging per-sample interaction structure. The category-level analysis (e.g., instruments → high redundancy, tickling → high synergy) provides an interpretable lens on why certain multimodal tasks are hard. Open questions remain: the method is limited to pairwise modality analysis due to theoretical gaps in multi-source PID, the entropy estimation relies on a single KNIFE-based estimator whose accuracy degrades in high dimensions, and the relationship between architectural choices (fusion depth, training strategy) and learned interaction patterns is only preliminarily explored.
