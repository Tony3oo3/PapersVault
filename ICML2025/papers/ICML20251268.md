## Problem

Existing orthogonal convolution layers (SOC, LOT) rely on iterative approximation algorithms that are computationally expensive and can numerically violate the 1-Lipschitz constraint, limiting their use in deeper architectures. Additionally, Lipschitz networks have inherently limited model capacity (bounded by their Lipschitz constant via Rademacher complexity), yet standard margin-maximizing losses like Certificate Regularization attempt to enlarge the margin for every data point simultaneously—an infeasible goal that leads to discontinuous gradients and gradient domination.

## Input / Output

The method takes standard RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 64×64 for Tiny-ImageNet, 224×224 for ImageNet) and produces a class label along with a certified ℓ₂ robustness radius ε = M_f(x)/(√2·L), computed in a single forward pass from the logit margin between the ground-truth and runner-up classes. Because the network is globally L-Lipschitz by construction, no sampling or bound-propagation is needed at inference time, making certification essentially free compared to randomized smoothing or interval-bound methods.

## Method

The core contribution is the Block Reflector Orthogonal (BRO) layer, which parameterizes an orthogonal matrix as W = I − 2V(VᵀV)⁻¹Vᵀ with a low-rank unconstrained V ∈ ℝ^{m×n} (n < m), yielding an n-rank perturbation of the identity that is exactly orthogonal without any iterative approximation. For convolution, the BRO operation is performed in the Fourier domain via the 2D convolution theorem, applying the reflector parameterization independently at each spatial frequency on the channel dimension. The BRONet architecture stacks BRO convolutional blocks (with MaxMin activation and identity residual reparameterization) between a Lipschitz-regularized stem and a dense head with LLN. A second contribution is the Logit Annealing (LA) loss, L_LA = −T(1−p_t)^β log(p_t) with p = softmax((z−ξy)/T), whose (1−p_t)^β factor progressively down-weights already-confident predictions, redirecting the model's limited capacity toward harder examples and producing a more balanced certified-radius distribution across the dataset.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, BRONet-L(+LA) achieves 81.6% clean accuracy and 70.6% certified accuracy at ε=36/255, 57.2% at ε=72/255, and 42.5% at ε=108/255 with only 68M parameters. On CIFAR-100, BRONet-L(+LA) reaches 54.3% clean accuracy and 29.1% certified accuracy at ε=72/255. On ImageNet, BRONet(+LA) achieves state-of-the-art 49.3% clean accuracy and 37.6% certified accuracy at ε=36/255, outperforming the previous best (LiResNet) by 2.0 and 2.3 points respectively while using fewer parameters (86M vs. 98M).

## Baselines & Comparisons

BRONet-L(+LA) outperforms the strongest prior baseline LiResNet (Hu et al., 2024) on all three datasets while using fewer parameters: on CIFAR-10 it gains +0.8% certified accuracy at ε=36/255 (70.6 vs. 69.8) and +0.9% at ε=72/255 (57.2 vs. 56.3) with 68M vs. 83M parameters; on CIFAR-100 it improves clean accuracy by 1.3% (54.3 vs. 53.0) and certified accuracy at ε=72/255 by 0.8% (29.1 vs. 28.3); on ImageNet it leads by +2.0% clean and +2.3% certified at ε=36/255 (37.6 vs. 35.3) with 86M vs. 98M parameters. Against orthogonal-layer baselines, BRONet substantially outperforms SOC-20+CRC (40M) by +4.1% certified at ε=36/255 on CIFAR-10 and +1.7% on CIFAR-100, and beats LOT-20 (18M) by +6.3% and +5.0% respectively. BRONet also surpasses the much larger SLL X-Large (236M) on CIFAR-10 certified accuracy at ε=72/255 (57.2 vs. 55.7) while using only 29% of the parameters. The backbone comparison in Table 3 confirms that BRO layers are the optimal convolutional backbone choice, achieving the best clean and certified accuracy across all ε values on both CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 when compared under identical LiResNet architecture with LA loss and diffusion augmentation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on constructing orthogonal convolutional layers for Lipschitz neural networks, departing from iterative methods (SOC's exponential convolution, LOT's Newton-based Procrustes) by introducing a closed-form block reflector parameterization W = I − 2V(VᵀV)⁻¹Vᵀ that requires no iterative approximation. The Logit Annealing loss builds on the margin-training ideas of Prach & Lampert (2022) and the Certificate Regularization term from Singla et al. (2022), but is motivated by a Rademacher complexity analysis showing that Lipschitz networks have fundamentally bounded capacity for margin maximization. Architecturally, BRONet follows the LiResNet design (Hu et al., 2023; 2024) with Lipschitz-regularized stem layers and EMMA, while replacing the backbone with BRO orthogonal convolutions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The BRO layer's elimination of iterative approximation makes orthogonal convolutions substantially cheaper in both time and memory, which is critical for scaling Lipschitz networks to deeper architectures and larger datasets like ImageNet — a setting where prior orthogonal methods (LOT, SOC) struggled with memory or numerical instability. The LA loss addresses a previously underappreciated limitation: that Lipschitz networks cannot indefinitely increase margins due to their bounded Rademacher complexity, and that the standard CR term's aggressive margin pushing is counterproductive. Open questions include whether the low-rank parameterization can be further optimized (e.g., adaptive rank per layer), whether the approach extends to other modalities or non-ℓ₂ norms, and whether the theoretical Rademacher bound can be tightened to provide more precise guidance on achievable margins.
