## Problem

The multi-objective optimization literature has predominantly studied conflicting objectives and Pareto-front tradeoffs, yet in practice (multi-task learning, RL with multiple rewards, LLM training) objectives are often aligned—sharing a common minimizer. No gradient-based optimization framework existed to exploit this alignment for provably faster convergence, leaving practitioners without principled, parameter-free methods to combine aligned multi-objective feedback at scale.

## Input / Output

CAMOO consumes per-objective gradients and diagonal Hessian approximations (estimated via Hutchinson's method with N random vectors) at each iterate, and produces a weight vector in the constrained simplex Δ_{m,w_min} that maximizes the minimum eigenvalue of the weighted Hessian. PAMOO requires only per-objective gradients and the optimal function values f_i(x_★) (often zero in ML practice), solving a convex quadratic in R^m to produce its weight vector. Both output an updated parameter vector x_{k+1} via a single weighted gradient descent step, with PAMOO using a Polyak-style adaptive step size (η=1) and CAMOO using a fixed step size (η=1/2β).

## Method

The paper introduces the Aligned Multi-Objective Optimization (AMOO) framework, where all objectives share a common minimizer, and defines two structural curvature quantities: the global adaptive strong convexity μ_G (worst-case over all x of the best weighted Hessian eigenvalue) and the local strong convexity μ_L (best weighted Hessian eigenvalue at x_★ only, with μ_L ≥ μ_G). CAMOO adaptively selects weights at each iterate to maximize local curvature, solved as a max-min bilinear problem over diagonal Hessians using the PU method; PAMOO generalizes the Polyak step-size to the multi-objective setting by solving a simple convex quadratic in R^m using only gradient information. Both algorithms achieve linear convergence rates depending on μ_G or μ_L respectively (rather than worst-case smoothness), with PAMOO yielding a tighter bound since μ_L ≥ μ_G. The analysis relies on self-concordance properties to handle the fact that the weighted function f_{w_k} is not globally strongly convex—only pointwise curved at x_k—and extends to an ε-approximate alignment setting where convergence degrades polynomially in ε.

## Results

The main theoretical contribution establishes asymptotic linear convergence rates: CAMOO converges at rate O((1−μ_G/β)^k) while PAMOO converges at the faster rate O((1−μ_L/β)^k), where μ_L ≥ μ_G are structural curvature quantities. In the toy experiments (Section 7), both CAMOO and PAMOO achieve faster MSE reduction than Equal Weighting (EW) on both the local curvature and selection example instances, with PAMOO showing the steepest convergence. PAMOO also requires only gradient information (no Hessians), making it computationally cheaper than CAMOO while providing a tighter convergence bound.

**Table 1: Asymptotic convergence rates of the proposed algorithms.**

| Algorithm | Asymptotic Convergence |
|---|---|
| CAMOO | O((1−μ_G/β)^k) |
| PAMOO | O((1−μ_L/β)^k) |

Both μ_G and μ_L are structural quantities characterizing optimal curvature of the weighted function, with μ_L ≥ μ_G, implying PAMOO has a superior (faster) convergence rate.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares CAMOO and PAMOO against Equal Weighting (EW), the naive baseline that assigns uniform weights to all objectives. In the local curvature example, PAMOO and CAMOO both converge faster than EW in terms of MSE, with PAMOO showing the sharpest drop; CAMOO exhibits the expected switching behavior in its weight vector as local curvature changes. In the selection example, CAMOO correctly adapts to a 1-hot weight on the best-curvature objective, outperforming EW which is diluted across all m objectives. Theoretically, PAMOO dominates CAMOO: its convergence rate depends on μ_L (local curvature at the optimum) which is always at least as large as μ_G (global adaptive curvature), yielding a strictly tighter bound. Against prior multi-objective methods (MGDA, PCGrad, CAGrad, Nash-MTL), the paper's contribution is that those methods provide only worst-case convergence guarantees dependent on the maximal Lipschitz constant across all objectives, whereas CAMOO and PAMOO achieve instance-dependent rates that exploit alignment structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant multi-objective optimization (MOO) and multi-task learning (MTL) literature, which has focused on conflicting objectives, Pareto fronts, and worst-case convergence guarantees (MGDA, PCGrad, CAGrad, Nash-MTL). It also distinguishes itself from proxy-based ML and multi-fidelity optimization, which use related objectives but from a statistical or Bayesian perspective rather than a pure gradient-descent one. The authors explicitly state that no prior work studies aligned multi-objective optimization from an optimization-theoretic perspective, carving out a new problem formulation where objectives share a common minimizer and showing that adaptive weighting can provably accelerate convergence beyond any single objective or uniform average.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The AMOO framework provides the first theoretical justification for why multi-reward or multi-task feedback can improve training when objectives are aligned—a phenomenon observed empirically in LLM RL, text-to-image generation, and multi-task learning but previously unexplained from an optimization standpoint. Key limitations include the requirement of self-concordant convex functions, access to optimal function values (PAMOO), and Hessian information (CAMOO), with no stochastic or non-convex extensions. Open questions include whether the framework extends to non-convex deep learning settings, whether optimal convergence rates are achieved, and whether the algorithms scale to large-scale training with many objectives.
