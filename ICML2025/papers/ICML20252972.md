## Problem

Despite the proliferation of open-source LLMs, there is no principled theoretical framework explaining the dynamic phase transitions (pre-power vs. power) that emerge during fine-tuning, and existing model selection methods remain largely heuristic, relying on strong assumptions or iterative trial-and-error. This gap limits both the ability to predict when additional data will trigger predictable scaling behavior and the capacity to select optimal models efficiently under computational constraints.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a limited training dataset S (sampled in progressively halved subsets from 200 to 1,638,400 examples), a candidate set of pre-trained LLMs M (spanning 14 models from 124M to 6.7B parameters), and per-model feature vectors encoding architecture and size. It outputs a predicted performance score r = exp(ψ(log|D|)) for each candidate, the identity of the optimal model m*, and the minimal data proportion s = 1/2^a required to reach the Pareto-optimality curve. The algorithm terminates via a regression-deviation stopping criterion (threshold τ) after collecting at least γ size-loss pairs, making the number of fine-tuning runs adaptive rather than fixed.

## Method

LENSLLM is built on a PAC-Bayesian Generalization Bound (Theorem 2) that extends Ju et al.'s Hessian-based bound to transformers by incorporating attention Lipschitz continuity, bounded softmax scores, and residual gradient flow, yielding a generalization gap of O(n^{-3/4}) in the pre-power phase that transitions to C₃n^{-β₃} in the power phase as Hessian values decay. The practical model is a rectified scaling law L(D) = B/(F(Θ,t) + D^β) + E, where F(Θ,t) = ||e^{-ηΘt}(f₀(X) − y)||²₂ is an NTK-based test loss capturing the transformer's intrinsic learning dynamics through the gradient inner-product kernel Θ. The key design choice is placing F(Θ,t) in the denominator alongside D^β, which simultaneously encodes pre-trained knowledge (via f₀) and fine-tuning data scaling in a unified form. Algorithm 1 iteratively halves the training set, fits a regression estimator ψ on log-log size-loss pairs, and stops when the standardized deviation exceeds threshold τ, requiring only a fraction of full fine-tuning compute (up to 88.5% reduction) while achieving 91.1% relative accuracy in model selection.

## Results

LENSLLM achieves up to 85.8% Pearson Correlation (PearCorr) and 91.1% Relative Accuracy (RelAcc) on Gigaword, the strongest results across all three benchmarks. On FLAN and Wikitext, it reaches 78.4% PearCorr / 88.3% RelAcc and 82.6% PearCorr / 90.2% RelAcc, respectively. In terms of efficiency, LENSLLM reduces computational cost by up to 88.5% compared to FullTuning (e.g., 0.48×10²¹ FLOPs on FLAN vs. 3.35×10²¹ for FullTuning) while maintaining comparable selection performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LENSLLM outperforms all five baselines on every metric across all three datasets. The closest competitor is Rectified Scaling Law (Haowei et al., 2024), which LENSLLM beats by 2.3–3.2 points in PearCorr and 1.9–2.6 points in RelAcc (e.g., Gigaword: 85.8 vs. 83.5 PearCorr; 91.1 vs. 89.2 RelAcc). NLPmetrics (Yang et al., 2023) and SubTuning (Kaplun et al., 2023) trail further behind, with gaps of roughly 5–8 points in PearCorr. ZeroShot (Kojima et al., 2022) and ModelSize (Villalobos et al., 2022) perform poorly, with ZeroShot producing negative Pearson correlations (−53.2 on Gigaword, −12.8 on FLAN), indicating its selection scores are anti-correlated with actual fine-tuning performance. In addition to accuracy, LENSLLM also wins on efficiency: its progressive halving strategy requires only 0.48–0.97×10²¹ FLOPs versus 0.92–1.84×10²¹ for SubTuning and 3.35–5.13×10²¹ for FullTuning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Hessian-based generalization bound of Ju et al. (2022) from feed-forward networks to transformers, deriving a PAC-Bayesian bound that theoretically explains the empirically-observed pre-power/power phase transition in fine-tuning scaling laws. It builds on the rectified scaling law framework of Haowei et al. (2024) by inserting an NTK-based test loss term into the denominator, creating a unified model that captures both pre-training influence and fine-tuning data scaling. The NTK connection to Jacot et al. (2018) provides the mechanistic bridge between the theoretical bound and a practical, computable prediction model, departing from prior heuristic selection methods that lacked a first-principles grounding.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical lens (PAC-Bayesian bound with Hessian analysis) for understanding when and why fine-tuning transitions from the pre-power to power phase, which was previously only an empirical observation. The 88.5% computational cost reduction versus full fine-tuning is practically significant for resource-constrained settings, though the performance gains over the strongest baseline (Rectified Scaling Law) are modest (e.g., 85.8% vs 83.5% PearCorr on Gigaword). Open questions include whether the framework extends to multi-task selection, MoE architectures, and whether the theoretical bounds are tight enough to guide data collection decisions in practice. The evaluation is limited to three benchmarks and models up to 6.7B parameters, leaving open whether the approach scales to frontier-scale LLMs.
