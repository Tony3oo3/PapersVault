## Problem

Prior work on in-context learning (ICL) emergence has focused on how the number of pretraining tasks affects in-distribution generalization, but it remains unclear what conditions on the pretraining task distribution are necessary for a transformer to generalize to tasks outside that distribution. This paper introduces a geometric notion of task diversity—based on how similar or dissimilar the pretraining tasks are within the task space—and asks when increasing this diversity causes ICL to transition from a specialized, in-distribution-only solution to one that generalizes across the entire task space.

## Input / Output

The transformer receives a context of up to n=50 input-output pairs (x_i, y_i) where x_i ∈ R^10 are drawn from N(0, I) and y_i = w^T x_i + ε_i with ε_i ~ N(0, σ²). Each x_i is mapped to a 128-dimensional hidden representation via a learned linear embedding, and each scalar y_i is padded with 9 zeroes to match the embedding dimension. The model outputs a single scalar prediction for y_n given the full context C_n, and is evaluated by MSE against the true target drawn from a test task distribution parameterized by a hyperspherical band at angle δ from the training cap's pole.

## Method

The paper trains GPT-2-style transformers (10 layers, 128 hidden dim, 8 heads) to perform ICL of linear functions, with pretraining task vectors w sampled uniformly from hyperspherical caps of varying half-angle φ on S^{d-1}. By sweeping φ from 15° to 180°, they observe a sharp transition near φ ≈ 120° (≈135° with label noise) where the learned solution shifts from a specialized one that fits a strong prior over the training cap to a general-purpose solution that performs uniformly well across all test angles δ. They further construct a phase diagram over the joint space of task count N and cap angle φ, revealing three distinct regimes (in-weights learning, in-task-distribution ICL, and out-of-task-distribution ICL) separated by a diagonal boundary. The transition is shown to be robust to task dimensionality, model depth, and to extend to logistic regression and nonlinear (ReLU) regression settings, suggesting it is a general feature of transformer ICL rather than an artifact of the linear task structure.

## Results

The paper's central finding is a sharp specialization-to-generalization transition in transformers performing in-context learning of linear functions: as the pretraining task diversity (hyperspherical cap half-angle φ) increases past a critical threshold, the model shifts from a specialized solution (good ICL only within the pretraining cap) to a general-purpose solution (good ICL across the entire task space). For noiseless d=10 linear regression with n=50 context examples, the transition occurs at φ ≈ 120°; with label noise σ²=0.25 it shifts to φ ≈ 135°. The transition point is robust to model depth (2, 3, or 10 layers) and task dimensionality d, and analogous transitions appear in logistic regression (φ ≈ 135°) and nonlinear regression (φ ≈ 60°–135° depending on parameterization).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares transformer ICL performance against three analytical baselines. Against the optimal Bayesian estimator (w_OB) derived under the pretraining prior p_φ(w), transformers with φ ≳ 45° outperform w_OB at the most out-of-distribution test angle (δ=175°), because w_OB is constrained to the pretraining cap and cannot assign weight vectors outside it (Fig 3). Against ordinary least squares (OLS), specialized transformers (φ ≲ 90°) outperform OLS for short contexts (fewer than d=10 examples) by leveraging a strong implicit prior, but for φ ≳ 120° the transformer's performance converges to OLS (Fig 4). Against the discrete minimum mean-squared error (dMMSE) estimator over a finite pretraining task set, transformers match dMMSE for small numbers of tasks N but outperform it as N grows, indicating the transformer learns a smoother, more general solution than nearest-task selection (Fig 14). Overall, the transformer's out-of-task-distribution advantage over the Bayesian optimal is a direct consequence of its failure to fit the in-distribution-optimal prior, revealing that the learned solution is not Bayes-optimal for the training distribution but is instead a more general-purpose in-context learner.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the ICL pretraining diversity literature initiated by Raventos et al. (2023), which studied how the *number* of pretraining tasks governs in-distribution ICL emergence. Here, the authors introduce a second, orthogonal axis of task diversity — the angular spread (similarity) of tasks on a hypersphere — and shift the evaluation from in-distribution to out-of-task-distribution generalization, framing ICL as a domain generalization problem at the task level. The experimental setup is deliberately aligned with Raventos et al. to enable direct comparison, while the Bayesian optimal estimator and OLS baselines are drawn from that same line of work. The paper also situates its covariate-shift formulation within the broader OOD generalization literature (Gulrajani & Lopez-Paz, Arjovsky) and contrasts with prior ICL-under-shift studies (Ahuja & Lopez-Paz, Yadlowsky et al., Hill et al.).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that transformers trained on only a subset of the task space (φ ≳ 120°) achieve near-optimal performance across the *entire* task space challenges the intuition that full task coverage is necessary for full generalization, and the sharp transition suggests a phase-like behavior that may be a general feature of ICL. The observation that specialized solutions outperform OLS at short context lengths reveals a task-level bias-variance tradeoff with practical implications for few-shot settings. Open questions include developing an analytic theory for the transition (analogous to Lu et al.'s linear-attention theory), understanding what implicit prior the transformer learns to achieve OOD performance beyond the Bayesian optimum, and extending the analysis to richer task geometries and more complex function classes beyond linear and small nonlinear models.
