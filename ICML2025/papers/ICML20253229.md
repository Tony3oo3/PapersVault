## Problem

Hybrid controlled trials that augment small RCTs with external controls (ECs) from historical or observational data suffer from two intertwined failures: existing asymptotic or model-based methods can inflate type I error when the RCT sample is small or models are misspecified, and indiscriminate borrowing of biased ECs reduces statistical power. No prior method simultaneously provides finite-sample exact type I error control and a principled mechanism for selectively borrowing only comparable ECs to recover power.

## Input / Output

The method consumes RCT data (randomized treatment and control arms with a known propensity score from the randomization scheme), external control data (all units under control, with baseline covariates and outcomes), and a score function (parametric or ML) for measuring EC non-conformity. It produces a doubly robust ATE estimate, an exact p-value from the Fisher randomization test (computed via Monte Carlo permutation of RCT assignments only, never EC assignments), the selected EC subset, and an adaptive threshold γ chosen by bootstrap MSE minimization over a finite grid. In the real-data application, right-censored survival times are first transformed to 3-year RMST pseudo-observations via stratified jackknife before entering the pipeline.

## Method

The framework has two coupled components. First, a Fisher randomization test (FRT) is performed using a doubly robust ATE estimator as the test statistic, but the reference distribution is generated by permuting only the RCT treatment assignments while fixing all EC assignments to control—this 'analyze as you randomize' principle yields finite-sample exact type I error control regardless of EC bias, model misspecification, or unmeasured confounding. Second, Conformal Selective Borrowing (CSB) tests the exchangeability of each EC against the randomized controls using conformal p-values (split, CV+, or Jackknife+), selecting ECs whose p-value exceeds a threshold γ; the selected subset is then plugged into the doubly robust estimator. The threshold γ is chosen adaptively by minimizing a bootstrap-estimated MSE that uses the RCT-only (no-borrowing) estimator as a consistent proxy for the true ATE, with a non-asymptotic excess risk bound guaranteeing near-oracle performance. Crucially, the FRT accounts for selection uncertainty by re-running the conformal selection under every permuted RCT assignment, yielding valid post-selection inference.

## Results

In the CALGB 9633 + NCDB real-data application, CSB estimates an ATE of 0.138 (SE = 0.058) with an exact FRT p-value of 0.046, selecting 264 of 11,700 ECs, while Full Borrow (all 335 ECs) yields a larger estimate of 0.241 (exact p = 0.031) and No Borrow gives 0.142 (exact p = 0.051). In simulations with (n₁, n₀, n_E) = (50, 25, 50), CSB reduces MSE by 13–16% relative to No Borrow across most bias magnitudes (b = 3–8) and increases FRT power by 13–36%, whereas Full Borrow inflates MSE by up to 454% and loses up to 51% power when hidden bias is present. When no hidden bias exists (b = 0), CSB reduces MSE by 20% and increases power by 45% over No Borrow, with Full Borrow achieving a 42% MSE reduction and 46% power gain.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is among three borrowing strategies: No Borrow (RCT-only AIPW), Full Borrow (all ECs via the Li et al. 2023b doubly robust estimator), and the proposed Conformal Selective Borrow. CSB dominates Full Borrow in every biased scenario (b ≥ 1): FB's bias reaches 125–203% of its standard deviation while CSB's stays within 0–22%, and FB's MSE inflates by up to 454% over NB whereas CSB typically reduces MSE by 13–16%. In the no-bias case (b = 0), both FB and CSB substantially outperform NB on power (46% and 45% gains, respectively), with FB having a slight MSE edge (42% vs 20% reduction). The only scenario where CSB underperforms NB is when hidden bias is small but nonzero (b = 1–4), where the conformal test lacks power to separate biased from unbiased ECs, causing a 7–20% power loss; the authors note this is an inherent no-free-lunch trade-off shared by all data-fusion estimators. The paper also benchmarks against Adaptive Lasso Selective Borrowing (ALSB, Gao et al. 2025) in the appendix, finding CSB to be approximately 10× faster computationally while maintaining comparable or superior MSE and power across bias magnitudes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three established lines of work: Fisher randomization tests for finite-sample exact inference, conformal prediction for distribution-free uncertainty quantification, and doubly robust estimation for hybrid controlled trials (Li et al., 2023b). It departs from prior hybrid controlled trial methods—which rely on asymptotic arguments requiring large samples, correct nuisance model specification, and no unmeasured confounding—by grounding the entire inference pipeline in the RCT's randomization mechanism, making type I error control model-free and finite-sample exact. The selective borrowing component extends the test-then-pool paradigm (Viele et al., 2014; Gao et al., 2025) by replacing asymptotic selection consistency with conformal p-values that are valid in finite samples, and introduces an MSE-minimizing adaptive threshold that unifies no-borrowing and full-borrowing as special cases.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically critical gap: small-sample RCTs in rare diseases where external control borrowing is most needed but most dangerous due to unmeasured confounding. The key limitation acknowledged is a 'no free lunch' trade-off—when EC bias is non-negligible yet difficult to detect, CSB can lose power relative to RCT-only analysis, though type I error control is preserved. Open questions include extending the framework to weak null hypotheses, individual treatment effect estimation, and treatment effect heterogeneity, as well as understanding how the adaptive threshold behaves under more complex randomization designs. The real-data application to CALGB 9633 with 11,700 NCDB external controls demonstrates practical feasibility and regulatory relevance.
