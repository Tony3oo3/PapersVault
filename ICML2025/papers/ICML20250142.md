## Problem

The random-order model for online learning—where an adversary generates losses upfront but they are presented after a uniform random permutation—sits between the i.i.d. and adversarial settings, yet it has received little systematic study. While adversarial algorithms trivially work in random order, the paper shows that algorithms designed for the stochastic setting can fail catastrophically on random-order inputs (via a birthday-paradox construction), leaving open the question of how to recover stochastic-level regret guarantees in this intermediate regime.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a multiset of T loss vectors (or reward/cost tuples) generated by an adversary and presented in uniform random order, along with a stochastic no-regret algorithm as a black-box subroutine. In the delayed-feedback variant, a fixed delay parameter d governs when loss vectors become observable; in the constrained variant, each time step yields a reward vector and m cost vectors against a per-iteration budget ρ. The output is a single action (or mixed action) at each time step, with the guarantee expressed as a regret bound relative to the best fixed action (or LP optimum) in hindsight.

## Method

The core contribution is SIMULATION, a general template that partitions the time horizon into O(log T) geometrically increasing blocks. At the start of each block i of length 2^i, the algorithm constructs a uniform distribution D_i over all previously observed losses, trains the stochastic subroutine A on 2^i i.i.d. samples from D_i (incurring no real loss), and then plays the empirical action frequencies from that training phase against the actual random-order losses in the current block. The analysis exploits Hoeffding-type concentration for sampling without replacement to show that past and future empirical gaps concentrate around the true gap, yielding regret that matches the stochastic rate plus a 5√(T log T) overhead. For bandits with switching costs, a specialized variant (SIMULATION-SUCCESSIVE-ELIMINATION) plays active arms in contiguous round-robin intervals within each block and eliminates suboptimal arms via confidence bounds, achieving O(√(kT log³T)) regret. For online classification, the paper proves that VC dimension—not Littlestone dimension—characterizes learnability in random order, via a symmetrization argument adapted to sampling without replacement.

## Results

The paper establishes that the random-order model achieves the same minimax regret rates as the stochastic i.i.d. model across multiple problems, improving on the adversarial setting. Key bounds include: O(√T log T + d log T) regret for prediction with delayed feedback (matching the stochastic additive-d rate vs. the adversarial multiplicative-d rate), Õ(1/ρ² + √T/ρ) regret with zero constraint violations for online learning with constraints (vs. impossibility in the adversarial setting), and O(√(kT log³T)) regret for bandits with switching costs (vs. Θ(T^{2/3}) adversarial minimax). For online classification, the paper proves VC dimension (not Littlestone dimension) characterizes learnability in random order, yielding O(√(d_VC · T · log(T/d_VC))) regret.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's comparisons are between the random-order model and the two bounding models (i.i.d. stochastic and adversarial), rather than against specific competing algorithms. In prediction with delayed feedback, the SIMULATION template recovers the stochastic additive delay rate (d log T) as opposed to the adversarial multiplicative rate (d√T). In online learning with constraints, the random-order algorithm achieves Õ(√T/ρ) regret with strict constraint satisfaction, whereas the adversarial setting admits no algorithm with both sublinear regret and sublinear violations (Mannor et al., 2009). For bandits with switching costs, the O(√(kT log³T)) rate matches the stochastic regime and improves on the Θ(T^{2/3}) adversarial minimax (Dekel et al., 2014). The negative result (Theorem 3.2) shows that a natural stochastic algorithm (BIRTHDAY-TEST) suffers Ω(T) regret on a crafted random-order instance despite O(√T log T) regret on i.i.d. inputs, demonstrating that the random-order model is strictly harder than the stochastic one. The classification result provides a separation from the adversarial model: VC dimension (not Littlestone dimension) governs learnability, analogous to the offline PAC setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions the random-order model as a natural intermediate between i.i.d. and adversarial online learning, building on the broader 'beyond worst-case' analysis agenda (Roughgarden, 2020) and prior random-order results in optimal stopping, online matching, and matroid selection. For the specific problems studied, it draws on stochastic algorithms from the i.i.d. literature (e.g., Follow-the-Leader for delayed feedback, Castiglioni et al. for constrained learning, Successive-Elimination from Slivkins for bandits) and the low-switching round-robin structure of Agrawal et al. (1988). The key conceptual departure is showing that stochastic algorithms do *not* automatically transfer to random-order (via the Birthday Paradox), necessitating the SIMULATION template as a bridge, while the De Finetti theorem provides the asymptotic justification for why the gap is finite-time only.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The SIMULATION template provides a reusable recipe for converting any stochastic no-regret algorithm into a random-order one with only logarithmic overhead, implying that minimax regret in random-order collapses to the stochastic rate for the problems studied. The VC-dimension characterization of random-order classification (as opposed to Littlestone dimension in the adversarial model) gives a clean theoretical separation that clarifies the model hierarchy. A practical implication is that shuffling a dataset before online presentation can recover stochastic regret rates. The main open question is extending the black-box reduction to bandit feedback, where the current approach requires a specialized Successive-Elimination-based algorithm rather than a general template.
