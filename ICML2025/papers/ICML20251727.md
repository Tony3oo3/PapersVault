## Problem

Existing graph structure learning methods optimize point-prediction losses to learn latent adjacency matrices, but this paper proves that such losses do not guarantee the learned latent distribution P_A^θ is calibrated to the true P_A^*. Multiple parameter settings can minimize point-prediction error while producing arbitrarily wrong edge probabilities, undermining uncertainty quantification and downstream decision-making.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251727/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes node-level feature vectors x ∈ R^{N×d_in} paired with observed outputs y ∈ R^{N×d_out}, with no direct observations of the latent binary adjacency matrix A. It produces two outputs simultaneously: node-level point predictions ŷ via a GNN f_ψ(x, A) and a calibrated parametric distribution P_A^θ over N×N binary adjacency matrices, where each edge (i,j) is independently sampled with learned probability θ_{ij}. The key constraint is that A is never observed during training—only the push-forward output distribution P_{y|x}^{θ,ψ} is accessible for optimization.

## Method

The core contribution is a distributional loss L^dist that minimizes the squared MMD (with a rational quadratic kernel) between the true output distribution P_{y|x}^* and the model's push-forward distribution P_{y|x}^{θ,ψ}, rather than a point-prediction error. Theorem 5.2 proves that under mild injectivity conditions on f*, minimizing L^dist simultaneously yields optimal point predictions and a calibrated latent graph distribution. Gradients w.r.t. GNN parameters ψ are computed via automatic differentiation over Monte Carlo samples of A from P_A^θ, while gradients w.r.t. distribution parameters θ use a score-function gradient estimator (SFE) with the log-derivative trick. A control-variates variance reduction technique with analytically derived baselines β₁ and β₂ (reusing already-computed kernel values) reduces SFE variance at negligible computational cost, yielding roughly 50% faster convergence.

## Results

On the synthetic graph structure learning benchmark, the proposed MMD-based distributional loss (L^{dist}_{Δ:MMD}) achieves a calibration error (MAE on θ) of 0.009±0.001, an order of magnitude lower than all literature baselines (0.082–0.087), while simultaneously attaining the best point-prediction performance: MAE on y of 0.269±0.001 and MSE on y of 0.159±0.001. The CRPS (energy distance) variant performs identically (0.010±0.002 on θ, 0.269±0.001 MAE on y, 0.159±0.001 MSE on y). The point-prediction loss L^{point}_{ℓ:MSE} improves calibration over literature losses (0.025±0.001 vs. 0.082–0.087) but remains statistically inferior to the proposed distributional loss.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four families of loss functions from the graph structure learning literature against the proposed distributional loss. Franceschi et al. (2019) and Cini et al. (2023) correspond to L^{literature}_{1,ℓ}, which takes the expectation over A outside the point-prediction loss; both achieve MAE on θ of ~0.087, roughly 10× worse than the proposed MMD loss (0.009). Kazi et al. (2022) corresponds to L^{literature}_{2,ℓ}, a node-level refinement of the same formulation, yielding a marginally better calibration (0.085–0.086) but still far from the proposed method. Elinas et al. (2020) corresponds to L^{literature}_{elbo}, a variational ELBO with a KL prior, which achieves the best calibration among literature baselines (0.082) but the worst point-prediction MSE (0.191). The proposed L^{dist}_{Δ:MMD} and L^{dist}_{Δ:CRPS} dominate all four baselines on both calibration and point prediction simultaneously, confirming the theoretical claim that distributional losses solve the joint learning problem while point-prediction losses do not.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the graph structure learning (GSL) literature by shifting the optimization objective from point-prediction losses (used in Franceschi et al. 2019, Cini et al. 2023, Kazi et al. 2022) or ELBO-style objectives (Elinas et al. 2020) to a distributional matching loss (MMD) on the output distributions. It builds on the score-function gradient estimator framework (Williams 1992; Mohamed et al. 2020) for handling discrete latent variables, and on MMD theory (Gretton et al. 2012) for distribution comparison without requiring explicit likelihood evaluation. The key conceptual departure is proving that the standard point-prediction objective is theoretically insufficient for calibrating the latent graph distribution, and that distributional matching simultaneously resolves both the prediction and calibration objectives.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The negative result—that optimizing point-prediction losses does not guarantee a calibrated latent graph distribution—challenges the default training paradigm in GSL and provides a theoretical justification for why learned graph structures may be poorly calibrated even when predictions are accurate. The positive result (Theorem 5.2) offers a clean, implementable fix via MMD-based training with variance-reduced SFE, requiring no likelihood computation. Limitations include the reliance on Assumption 3.1 (model family contains the true function and distribution) for the theoretical guarantee, the synthetic-only validation of calibration (since real-world latent graphs are unobservable), and the quadratic scaling of edge parameters with graph size. Open questions include whether the approach extends to continuous latent structures, multi-graph settings, or very large-scale graphs where the parameter-to-data ratio becomes prohibitive.
