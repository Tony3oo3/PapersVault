## Problem

Existing graphical model inference methods face a threefold limitation: they are restricted to continuous data (GGM, Gaussian copula), suffer from computational intractability of high-dimensional integrals in likelihood-based estimation, and assume homogeneous populations that cannot capture heterogeneous structures such as distinct cell types in scRNA-seq. No prior framework simultaneously addresses data-type diversity, estimation efficiency in high dimensions, and population heterogeneity with theoretical guarantees.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251015/cropped_page5_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method accepts p-dimensional count vectors (e.g., scRNA-seq UMI counts), binary vectors, or continuous vectors, along with the number of mixture components G and a convergence threshold. It outputs per-group covariance matrices Σ̂_g, from which sparse precision matrices Θ̂_g are derived via D-trace with L1 penalty, yielding gene regulatory networks as edge sets. A key structural detail is that estimation proceeds pair-by-pair over all (j,k) combinations with inner EM iterations, rather than optimizing a single joint objective, and a PSD projection step (Eq. 6) is applied before D-trace to guarantee convexity of the loss.

## Method

The paper introduces the marginally recoverable parametric family, a class of distributions whose full p-dimensional parameters are completely determined by all 1D and 2D marginal distributions, unifying Gaussian, Poisson log-normal, latent Gaussian copula, and their mixtures. For estimation, the Maximum Marginal Likelihood Estimator (MMLE) replaces the intractable p-dimensional likelihood integral with p(p−1)/2 independent two-dimensional marginal likelihood optimizations, yielding a consistent estimator under mild boundedness conditions (Theorem 4.3). To handle heterogeneous populations, EM-MMLE wraps MMLE in an EM algorithm where the E-step computes pairwise posterior responsibilities γ̂_gijk from 2D marginal densities and the M-step updates mixing proportions, means, and covariance entries per pair. The resulting covariance estimates are fed into the D-trace loss with L1 off-diagonal penalty (solved via ADMM) to recover sparse precision matrices, with sign consistency guaranteed under irrepresentability-type conditions (Theorem 4.4).

## Results

On mixed count data (MPLN model), EM-MMLE achieves AUPR of 0.99 (p=100, n=3000, low mixing, low zero-proportion) and 0.96 (p=300, same settings), consistently outperforming all baselines across 24 simulation scenarios. In the most challenging high-dimensional, high-mixing, high-zero-proportion setting (p=300, n=3000), EM-MMLE still achieves 0.87 AUPR versus 0.75 (PLNet), 0.72 (VMPLN), and 0.72 (Glasso). On the real scRNA-seq dataset (Zheng et al., 2017), EM-MMLE achieves the highest AUPRC ratios across most cell types, consistently outperforming the random predictor and all five competing methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

EM-MMLE outperforms PLNet (Xiao et al., 2022) across all 24 simulation scenarios, with the gap widening under high population mixing: in blocked random graphs (n=3000, p=300, low zero-proportion), EM-MMLE achieves 0.84 AUPR in high mixing versus PLNet's 0.73 (~15% relative improvement). Against VMPLN (Tang et al., 2024), EM-MMLE's advantage is most pronounced in high dimensions: at p=300, n=3000, low mixing, EM-MMLE reaches 0.96 AUPR versus VMPLN's 0.75 (28% relative improvement), while at p=100 the gap narrows to ~4%. Glasso (Friedman et al., 2008) is consistently the weakest baseline, trailing EM-MMLE by 8–25 percentage points in AUPR depending on the scenario. On binary data (Table 2), MMLE and Fan et al.'s rank-based estimator (2017) produce nearly identical Frobenius norm errors for both Σ and Θ, but MMLE is more general (handles the full marginally recoverable family, not just model 3). AMLE (d'Aspremont et al., 2008) is substantially worse on binary data, with Σ estimation errors ~4× higher than MMLE (e.g., 31.25 vs. 6.34 at p=50, n=200). On the scRNA-seq dataset, EM-MMLE achieves the highest AUPRC ratios in most cell types, outperforming both PPCOR (Kim, 2015) and GENIE3 (Huynh-Thu et al., 2010), which are purpose-built single-cell network inference methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies several previously separate lines of graphical model inference—Gaussian graphical models, Gaussian copula models, Poisson log-normal (PLN) models for count data, and latent Gaussian copula models for binary data—under a single 'marginally recoverable' parametric family, where the full p-dimensional distribution is characterized by its 1D and 2D marginals. For estimation, it adapts the pairwise/composite likelihood framework (Cox & Reid, 2004; Varin, 2008) into a Maximum Marginal Likelihood Estimator (MMLE) that avoids intractable high-dimensional integrals, and extends it to mixtures via an EM algorithm (EM-MMLE) for heterogeneous populations. The sparse precision matrix is then recovered using the D-trace loss (Zhang & Zou, 2014) with a PSD projection step. Conceptually, the paper departs from prior work (e.g., PLNet, VMPLN, Glasso) by providing a single framework that simultaneously addresses data-type diversity, computational efficiency, and population heterogeneity, whereas existing methods typically handle at most two of these three aspects.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The marginally recoverable framework offers a principled, theoretically grounded alternative to approximation-based methods (Laplace, variational inference, MCMC) for network inference from count and binary data, with consistency guarantees under mild regularity conditions. For scRNA-seq and related genomics applications, EM-MMLE provides a computationally efficient path to cell-type-specific gene regulatory networks without requiring prior cell-type labels. Limitations include the theoretical results assuming known mixing proportions and means, the restriction to 1D/2D marginals (the d-marginally recoverable generalization is only sketched), and the evaluation on a single real dataset. Open questions include whether the framework extends to higher-order marginals, more complex hierarchical structures, or settings where the number of mixture components is unknown.
