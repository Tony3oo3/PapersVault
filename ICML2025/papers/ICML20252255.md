## Problem

Existing benchmarks for evaluating AI agents on ML research either provide the original codebase for the agent to work with (CORE-Bench, SUPER) or focus on narrower, more self-contained tasks (Kaggle competitions, RE-Bench). No prior benchmark tests whether an AI agent can autonomously understand a research paper, build an entire codebase from scratch, execute complex experiments, and reproduce reported results — a capability central to autonomous ML R&D and a key axis in AI safety frameworks.

## Input / Output

The agent receives a research paper in both PDF and Markdown format along with an author-written addendum of clarifications, but is explicitly shown no rubric and is blacklisted from the original authors' code repositories. The agent must produce a repository containing all source code plus a reproduce.sh entrypoint script. The submission is then copied to a fresh Ubuntu 24.04 VM with a single A10 GPU and executed from scratch; the resulting outputs (reproduce.log, generated plots, result files) are graded against a hidden hierarchical rubric of 8,316 binary leaf nodes, yielding a weighted Replication Score from 0–100%.

## Method

PaperBench's core design is a set of 20 ICML 2024 Spotlight/Oral papers, each paired with a hierarchical rubric co-developed with the original paper's author, decomposing replication into weighted leaf nodes of three types: Code Development (correct implementation exists), Execution (code runs successfully), and Result Match (outputs agree with the paper). This hierarchy enables partial credit and granular tracking of progress. For scalable grading, the authors build SimpleJudge, an LLM-based judge (o3-mini) that independently grades each leaf node using the paper, rubric context, and relevant submission files (selected via a file-ranking step to manage context length), achieving F1 = 0.83 on the auxiliary JudgeEval benchmark. The agent scaffold (BasicAgent or IterativeAgent) runs a ReAct tool-use loop with bash, Python, web browser, and paginated file-reader tools, with a 12-hour time limit; IterativeAgent removes the early-termination option to force full-time utilization. A key methodological choice is separating the agent's run from a fresh-VM reproduction step, ensuring that reported results are genuinely generated by the submitted code rather than hard-coded at task time.

## Results

On PaperBench (20 ICML 2024 papers, 8,316 gradable rubric nodes), the best-performing agent, Claude 3.5 Sonnet (New) with BasicAgent, achieves an average Replication Score of 21.0%, followed by o1 at 13.2%, DeepSeek-R1 at 6.0%, GPT-4o at 4.1%, Gemini 2.0 Flash at 3.2%, and o3-mini at 2.6%. On the lighter PaperBench Code-Dev variant, o1 with IterativeAgent reaches 43.4%. A human baseline of ML PhDs (best of 3 attempts) scores 41.4% on a 3-paper subset after 48 hours, compared to 26.6% for o1 on the same subset, indicating models do not yet outperform human experts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates six frontier models (GPT-4o, o1, o3-mini, DeepSeek-R1, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Gemini 2.0 Flash) on the full 20-paper PaperBench. Claude 3.5 Sonnet with BasicAgent leads at 21.0%, substantially outperforming o1 (13.2%) and all other models (≤6.0%). However, when switching to the IterativeAgent scaffold (which prevents early termination), o1 jumps to 24.4% and surpasses Claude 3.5 Sonnet (16.1%), while o3-mini improves from 2.6% to 8.5%, showing strong scaffold sensitivity. Against the human baseline of ML PhDs on a 3-paper subset, the best human attempt (41.4% after 48h) outperforms o1 (26.6%), though o1 initially leads in the first hour before plateauing. On the JudgeEval auxiliary benchmark, o3-mini achieves an F1 of 0.83 at $66/paper, matching o1's F1 of 0.84 at $830/paper, making it the most cost-effective judge. On PaperBench Code-Dev, o1 with IterativeAgent scores 43.4%, roughly double its full PaperBench score, confirming the variant is a noisier but more accessible proxy.

## Relation to Prior Work

PaperBench carves new ground by requiring agents to replicate ML research papers entirely from scratch, departing from CORE-Bench and SUPER which provide agents with existing code repositories, and from RE-Bench which offers more self-contained, shorter-horizon tasks. It repurposes the rubric-based evaluation paradigm (seen in ARB and BigLaw Bench) and the LLM-as-a-judge framework (from MT-Bench and RewardBench) into a hierarchical, author-validated grading system with 8,316 granular criteria. The introduction of JudgeEval as an auxiliary benchmark for calibrating automated judges on complex, unstructured outputs is a novel contribution that addresses a gap in the LLM-judging literature, which has previously focused on simpler tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PaperBench provides a concrete, scalable measure of AI autonomy in ML R&D that maps directly onto safety frameworks from OpenAI, Anthropic, and DeepMind, making it a potential early-warning indicator for increasingly capable research agents. The finding that the best agent (Claude 3.5 Sonnet) achieves only 21% while ML PhDs reach 41.4%, combined with the observation that models plateau after roughly one hour of work while humans keep improving, highlights a fundamental gap in long-horizon task execution that current scaffolding approaches have not closed. Open questions include whether better agentic scaffolds can close this gap, whether contamination from pre-training on author codebases will inflate future scores, and whether rubric creation can be automated to scale the benchmark beyond 20 papers.
