## Problem

Standard IRL models assume a risk-neutral expert, which introduces model misspecification when the observed agent actually exhibits risk-sensitive (non-Markovian) behavior, and prevents direct inference of the agent's risk attitude. This paper introduces a risk-sensitive model of behavior based on expected utility theory that rationalizes non-Markovian policies, and characterizes the partial identifiability of the resulting utility function from demonstrations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251284/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The algorithms consume offline expert demonstration trajectories (batches of full episodes) and online access to a generative model for the transition dynamics, with the MDP structure (S, A, H, s₀, r) assumed known. CATY-UL additionally takes a candidate utility function as input and outputs a binary feasibility classification; TRACTOR-UL outputs a single discretized utility vector of dimension d = ⌈H/ε₀⌉. The discretization parameter ε₀ controls the trade-off between approximation error and computational tractability, and the feasible set is relaxed to U_Δ to accommodate finite-sample estimation error.

## Method

The model of behavior assumes the expert is optimal in a Risk-Sensitive MDP, where performance is E[U(G)] over the return distribution rather than E[G], making the expert's policy potentially non-Markovian (depending on cumulative reward so far). To make planning tractable, the paper discretizes the return space to a grid of size O(H/ε₀) and uses the enlarged state space approach (Bäuerle & Rieder, 2014) to reduce the RS-MDP to a standard MDP with state space S × Y_h, whose cardinality is linear rather than exponential in the horizon. CATY-UL estimates the expert's return distribution from offline data and the optimal performance via value iteration in the enlarged MDP, then classifies a candidate utility by checking whether the sum of compatibility gaps across environments falls below a threshold Δ. TRACTOR-UL performs online projected gradient descent over the space of discretized L-Lipschitz increasing utilities, using the difference between the optimal-policy return distribution (computed via the enlarged MDP) and the expert's return distribution as the gradient, with projection onto the feasible utility polytope at each step.

## Results

In the human-subject experiment (15 participants, H=5 MDP), 10 out of 15 participants (67%) exhibited non-Markovian behavior, validating the paper's core model assumption that risk-sensitive agents in stochastic MDPs naturally produce history-dependent policies. For TRACTOR-UL, the best step size for a single-environment setting (N=1) is α=100, with convergence speed largely unaffected by increases in state/action space size (S, A) but sensitive to the number of environments N, consistent with the O(N⁴H⁴L²/ε⁴) iteration bound in Theorem 5.2. The theoretical sample complexity for CATY-UL is Õ(N²H²/ε²) expert trajectories and Õ(N²SAH⁴/ε²) generative model queries for a single utility (|U|=1), scaling to Õ(N⁴H⁴L²/ε⁴) and Õ(N²SAH⁵/ε²) for multiple utilities.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not perform head-to-head quantitative comparisons against existing IRL or risk-sensitive IRL baselines (e.g., Ng & Russell 2000, Ratliff & Mazumdar 2020, Majumdar et al. 2017). The empirical validation is structured as proof-of-concept: Experiment 1 validates the model's descriptive adequacy by showing that 10/15 human participants naturally adopt non-Markovian policies in a small stochastic MDP, behavior that standard risk-neutral IRL models (which assume Markovian experts) cannot capture. Experiment 2 validates TRACTOR-UL's practical convergence properties on both real participant data and simulated environments of varying size, confirming the theoretical prediction that iteration count scales with N but not with S or A. The paper positions its contribution as complementary to prior risk-sensitive IRL work rather than directly competitive, since those methods either assume Markovian expert policies or operate in non-MDP settings (e.g., prepare-react models).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the IRL framework by introducing a risk-sensitive model of behavior grounded in expected utility theory, where the expert optimizes E[U(G)] over return distributions rather than E[G]. It builds directly on the enlarged state space approach of Bäuerle & Rieder (2014) and the discretization scheme of Wu & Xu (2023) to make planning in risk-sensitive MDPs tractable, and on the categorical distributional representation of Rowland et al. (2018) to handle return distributions. The model generalizes the classic Ng & Russell (2000) IRL formulation (recovered when U is linear) and is structurally analogous to ICRL (Malik et al., 2021), which separates task reward from constraint cost. Unlike prior risk-sensitive IRL works (Majumdar et al. 2017, Ratliff & Mazumdar 2020, Cheng et al. 2023), this is the first to contemplate non-Markovian policies in MDPs and to learn a full utility function rather than parametric risk measures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean theoretical foundation for separating an agent's task (reward) from its risk attitude (utility), enabling more interpretable and transferrable representations of behavior. The partial identifiability analysis (Propositions 4.1–4.4) establishes that single-environment demonstrations are fundamentally insufficient for prediction, imitation, or assessment, while Proposition 4.5 shows multiple environments can resolve the ambiguity—a practical design principle for data collection. Key open questions include learning both r and U simultaneously (Problem 3), extending to high-dimensional/continuous settings, and developing stronger identifiability guarantees beyond the 'all deterministic optimal policies' assumption. The empirical validation is limited to 15 participants in a small tabular MDP, leaving the practical scalability of the algorithms largely untested.
