## Problem

Existing DP-based unlearning guarantees rely on worst-case, data-agnostic privacy bounds that are overly conservative for individual data points, creating a mismatch between the uniform guarantees and the data-dependent reality of unlearning difficulty. This paper asks whether per-instance privacy losses—quantifying each data point's actual influence on the learned model—can provide a principled, data-adaptive measure of unlearning difficulty that improves the utility-unlearning tradeoff and predicts the number of fine-tuning steps needed to forget.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a trained model (ResNet-18 or ViT-small on CIFAR-10/SVHN), a forget set of data points, the corresponding retain set, and 35 evenly spaced training checkpoints storing per-sample gradient norms. It outputs per-instance privacy loss scores for each data point, which are used to rank forget sets by difficulty and predict the number of fine-tuning steps required to reach a target unlearning threshold (e.g., 5% error margin relative to the oracle). For standard SGD without explicit noise, the privacy loss is computed by assuming a small implicit noise level (σ ≤ 0.1), and rankings are shown to be stable to this choice.

## Method

The core contribution replaces the worst-case Rényi-DP bound in Chien et al.'s Langevin unlearning convergence analysis with per-instance privacy losses from Thudi et al. (2024), yielding a data-adaptive bound where the number of noisy fine-tuning steps k needed to unlearn a point x scales as O(log P(x, α)), with P(x, α) a sum over training steps of composition-weighted log-likelihood terms involving the gradient norm of x at each checkpoint. The privacy loss is estimated efficiently via a right-hand-rule approximation over 35 checkpoints from a single training run, avoiding the need to store gradients at every iteration. The paper validates the theory empirically for both SGLD (matching the theoretical setup) and standard SGD fine-tuning, showing privacy losses predict unlearning time across multiple metrics (UA, MIA, GUS) and architectures. Additionally, the authors introduce loss barriers (modulo permutation) as a geometric evaluation metric, showing that higher-privacy-loss data points start with larger barriers to the oracle, and demonstrate that privacy losses outperform cheaper proxies (C-Proxy, EL2N, average gradient norm) at identifying the hardest-to-unlearn data.

## Results

The paper's central empirical finding is that per-instance privacy losses reliably rank data points by unlearning difficulty: forget sets with higher average privacy loss consistently require more fine-tuning steps to reach a 5% error margin relative to the oracle, across SGLD (multiple noise levels σ), SGD, and L1-Sparse unlearning on CIFAR-10 and SVHN with ResNet-18. Privacy losses also identify harder-to-unlearn data than existing proxies (C-Proxy, average gradient norm, EL2N): in Figure 3, the top-s forget sets selected by privacy losses take measurably longer to unlearn than those selected by any alternative metric, across forget set sizes of 600, 1000, and 2000. Loss barrier analysis reveals that higher-privacy-loss data points start with larger loss barriers to the oracle model, and after fine-tuning unlearning, the barrier drops to levels comparable to the baseline between independently trained oracles. Privacy losses are strongly correlated with cheaper proxies (average gradient norm being the best), and the ranking is stable to the implicit noise level assumed for SGD (Spearman correlations of 0.99 between σ=0.001 and σ=0.0005).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Zhao et al. (2024)'s C-Proxy (a memorization-based heuristic), privacy losses show high correlation (Figure 2) but strictly dominate in identifying the hardest data: forget sets selected by privacy losses require more unlearning steps than those selected by C-Proxy at every forget set size tested (600, 1000, 2000), as shown in Figure 3. Against Paul et al. (2021)'s EL2N score, the same pattern holds—privacy losses correlate with EL2N but pick out genuinely harder-to-unlearn examples, yielding longer unlearning times in direct comparison. Against Liu et al. (2024a)'s L1-Sparse method, the paper does not compete with it but validates that privacy losses also predict unlearning difficulty under L1-Sparse fine-tuning (Figure 6), showing the theoretical insight generalizes beyond plain fine-tuning to this SOTA regularized variant. Overall, the paper positions privacy losses as a theoretically grounded, more precise difficulty metric that subsumes and improves upon the heuristics in prior work.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper refines the Langevin unlearning framework of Chien et al. (2024), which provided the first Rényi-DP guarantees for noisy gradient descent as an unlearning method, by replacing their worst-case privacy bound with per-instance privacy losses from Thudi et al. (2024). The key conceptual shift is from a data-agnostic, worst-case analysis to a data-dependent one that captures how individual points differ in their influence on the learned model. The paper also positions itself against the broader approximate unlearning literature (Ginart et al., Guo et al., Neel et al.) and connects to data difficulty metrics (C-Proxy from Zhao et al., EL2N from Paul et al.), showing that per-instance privacy losses subsume and improve upon these heuristics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretically grounded, per-instance measure of unlearning difficulty that is computable during training, offering a practical tool for adaptive unlearning strategies. A notable practical finding is that privacy losses derived from SGLD theory transfer to standard SGD fine-tuning, making the framework applicable to the dominant training paradigm. However, the group unlearning analysis remains too loose to differentiate forget sets empirically (Figure 4), and the theoretical justification for applying noisy-gradient-descent privacy bounds to noiseless SGD (via an assumed small implicit σ) remains informal. Extending the framework to other unlearning methods beyond fine-tuning and L1-Sparse, and obtaining tight group unlearning bounds, are identified as open problems.
