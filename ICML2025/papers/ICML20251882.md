## Problem

Medical image segmentation models trained on imbalanced clinical data produce biased predictions across demographic subgroups (e.g., race, age) and clinical subgroups (e.g., tumor stage), yet existing fairness methods focus primarily on explicit demographic attributes while neglecting implicit contextual factors like disease progression patterns. Distributional approaches that adapt model behavior to heterogeneous data distributions remain underexplored, particularly for 3D segmentation where batch-size constraints limit loss-function-based fairness strategies.

## Input / Output

The method takes a 2D medical image (center-cropped to 224×224 for fundus or dermatology images) or a 3D CT volume (randomly cropped to 384×384×128 patches during training, full-volume sliding window at inference) together with a discrete attribute flag (e.g., race ∈ {Black, Asian, White}, age group, or T-stage ∈ {T1–T4}). The attribute flag selects which distribution-aware router activates, and the network outputs a pixel-wise or voxel-wise segmentation mask of the same spatial resolution as the input. The dMoE module is inserted at the encoder's intermediate embedding layer, where spatial dimensions are flattened (H×W×D×C → N×C) before expert processing and reshaped back afterward.

## Method

dMoE reinterprets the Mixture of Experts layer as a feedback control mechanism and extends it into a mode-switching optimal control system for fairness. Instead of a single shared gating network, dMoE maintains multiple attribute-specific router networks G^attr, each using Noisy Top-K Gating to select k=2 experts from a pool of n=8 shared MLP experts; the attribute flag determines which router is active, analogous to a switching function selecting a control policy in mode-switching control. The control-theoretic derivation shows that standard MoE corresponds to feedback (closed-loop) control where the gating weights approximate a kernel-based policy, and dMoE adds an external switching signal (the attribute) that selects among distinct kernel functions while sharing expert parameters to prevent overfitting on underrepresented subgroups. The module is integrated into both TransUNet (ViT-B backbone, 2D) and 3D ResUNet (CNN backbone, 3D) architectures, trained end-to-end with standard cross-entropy segmentation loss, requiring only ~0.05M additional parameters over the base network.

## Results

On the 3D radiotherapy target segmentation task (T-stage attribute), dMoE achieves ES-Dice 0.499 and Dice 0.650 overall, with particularly strong gains in underrepresented subgroups: T4 Dice 0.778 (vs. 0.656 baseline) and T1 Dice 0.718 (vs. 0.493 baseline). On the 2D Harvard-FairSeg benchmark (race attribute), dMoE reaches ES-Dice 0.743 for rim segmentation and 0.832 for cup segmentation, surpassing all prior fairness methods. On HAM10000 (age attribute), dMoE attains ES-Dice 0.801 and Dice 0.884, outperforming both the default MoE (0.796/0.882) and FEBS (0.757/0.858).

## Baselines & Comparisons

dMoE consistently outperforms all baselines across all three datasets. Against FEBS (fair error-bound scaling), dMoE achieves substantially higher ES-Dice on the 3D radiotherapy task (0.499 vs. 0.434) and on Harvard-FairSeg rim segmentation (0.743 vs. 0.705), with the gap widening for minority subgroups (T4: 0.778 vs. 0.685; Black race: 0.776 vs. 0.733). Against the default MoE (without distribution-aware gating), dMoE shows a clear but smaller advantage, confirming that the attribute-specific router adds value beyond generic expert mixing (e.g., ES-Dice 0.499 vs. 0.452 on 3D; 0.743 vs. 0.733 on Harvard-FairSeg rim). Against FairDiff (generative augmentation), dMoE wins on Harvard-FairSeg rim ES-Dice (0.743 vs. 0.716) and cup ES-Dice (0.832 vs. 0.825) without requiring additional image generation. Against DRO and adversarial training (ADV), dMoE leads by a wider margin (ES-Dice 0.743 vs. 0.700 for both), indicating that loss-function-level fairness constraints are less effective than architecture-level distributional adaptation, especially for underrepresented subgroups.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Mixture of Experts (MoE) framework (Shazeer et al., 2017) and reinterprets it through the lens of optimal control theory, specifically as a feedback control mechanism with kernel-method-based policy approximation. It departs from prior fairness learning approaches in medical image segmentation (FEBS, DRO, FairDiff, adversarial training) by shifting from loss-function modifications or data augmentation to an architectural intervention: attribute-specific router networks that implement mode-switching control. The control-theoretic framing connects to the line of work viewing neural network training as optimal control (Chen et al., 2018; Lu et al., 2018), but the key novelty is the extension to mode-switching control where the switching signal is an external clinical or demographic attribute rather than purely the internal hidden state.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper extends fairness-aware segmentation from demographic attributes in 2D to clinical attributes (tumor stage, Gleason Grade, PSA) in 3D radiotherapy planning, addressing a gap where prior benchmarks largely ignored clinical factors that drive regional treatment variation. The mode-switching control interpretation provides a principled justification for why attribute-conditioned gating outperforms both plain MoE and loss-based fairness methods, and the ablation confirms that the attribute-specific switching (not the feedback mechanism alone) is the critical component. Open questions include handling multi-attribute imbalances simultaneously (e.g., T-stage × age), whether the optimal dMoE configuration can be made architecture-agnostic, and whether advanced numerical methods from optimal control can yield new optimization algorithms for fairness.
