## Problem

Prior work on RAG provides conflicting conclusions about the effect of retrieval depth (k) on performance, and evaluations often rely on oracle-aware or manually curated contexts that fail to reflect real-world retrieval conditions. There is no principled framework for assessing whether a RAG system is stable (consistent near its optimal k) and scalable (continues to benefit from more retrieval), nor a clear understanding of which component—retriever or reader—drives these properties.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250945/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a reader model, a retriever (BM25, ColBERT, or Contriever), and a query, then evaluates performance across retrieval depths k = 1 to 50 using naturally retrieved top-k passages rather than oracle-aware contexts. Beyond standard unigram F1, it computes the RAG Stability Score (RSS) over a symmetric window of ±5 around the optimal k*, and the RAG Scalability Coefficient (RSC) via trapezoidal integration of F1 gains up to the last depth where improvement exceeds a threshold ε (set to the 70th percentile of positive ΔF1 values across all models and depths).

## Method

RAGGED is a systematic evaluation framework that decouples the effects of retriever choice, retrieval depth, and reader architecture on RAG performance. Its central finding is that reader robustness to retrieval noise—not retriever quality—is the primary determinant of RAG stability and scalability, manifesting as two distinct behavior classes: improve-then-plateau models (e.g., FLAN, GPT-3.5) that scale effectively with increasing k, and peak-then-decline models (e.g., LLAMA, CLAUDE) that degrade due to noise sensitivity. The framework introduces two complementary diagnostic metrics: RSS quantifies worst-case performance volatility within a local window around the optimal retrieval depth, while RSC measures total accumulated F1 gain before plateau or decline. Experiments span three retrievers, eight reader models (open- and closed-source), and three datasets (NQ, HotpotQA, BioASQ), with additional analyses on prompting, reranking, and with/without gold passage conditions to isolate noise effects.

## Results

Across three datasets (NQ, HotpotQA, BioASQ) and three retrievers (BM25, ColBERT, Contriever), reader behavior splits into two distinct patterns: improve-then-plateau models (FLANT5, GPT-3.5) consistently gain from RAG, while peak-then-decline models (LLAMA2/3, CLAUDE-3-HAIKU) degrade below their no-context baselines. FLANT5 achieves the largest average RAG gains (+28.9 F1 on NQ with ColBERT, +20.9 on HotpotQA, +16.6 on BioASQ) and a near-perfect RAG Stability Score of 0.99, whereas LLAMA3-70B loses up to 24.9 F1 on NQ and 18.0 F1 on BioASQ relative to closed-book generation. ColBERT improves recall@50 by +21.3 over BM25 on NQ, yet the corresponding reader F1 gain is only +5.2, illustrating that retrieval quality alone does not guarantee proportional downstream improvement.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares eight reader models (FLANT5, FLAN-UL2, LLAMA2-7B/70B, LLAMA3-8B/70B, GPT-3.5, CLAUDE-3-HAIKU) across three retrievers (BM25, ColBERT, Contriever) on NQ, HotpotQA, and BioASQ. FLANT5 dominates as the most RAG-beneficial reader, gaining 16–30 F1 points over closed-book generation across all datasets, while LLAMA3-70B and CLAUDE-3-HAIKU consistently degrade by 6–26 F1 points, confirming that reader noise sensitivity—not retriever quality—drives RAG effectiveness. ColBERT outperforms BM25 in recall@k across all datasets (e.g., +21.3 on NQ, +14.6 on HotpotQA), but this retrieval advantage translates to only +5.2 and +1.9 F1 reader gains respectively in open-domain settings; in the specialized BioASQ domain, even a modest +0.7 recall improvement yields +2.08 F1 reader gain. Reranking consistently outperforms relevance prompting as a noise-filtering strategy, but the two do not stack—once retrieval is improved via reranking, prompting provides no additional benefit. Contriever, the third retriever, shows intermediate recall between BM25 and ColBERT and does not alter the fundamental reader behavior patterns observed with the other two retrievers.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior RAG evaluation work (Liu et al., 2023; Cuconasu et al., 2024) that relies on oracle-aware or manually curated contexts, instead evaluating models under naturally retrieved top-k conditions. It synthesizes and reconciles conflicting prior findings on retrieval depth (Izacard & Grave, 2021 vs. Cuconasu et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2024) by showing they are complementary, each capturing a different reader behavior class. Conceptually, it shifts the explanatory focus from retriever quality to reader noise robustness as the primary determinant of RAG effectiveness, a framing not explicitly isolated in prior work. The new RSS and RSC metrics extend beyond standard F1/recall diagnostics to capture stability and scalability dimensions of RAG performance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that reader robustness to noise—not retriever quality—governs RAG stability and scalability challenges the common practitioner assumption that upgrading the retriever is the primary lever for RAG improvement, with direct implications for system design and deployment. The improve-then-plateau vs. peak-then-decline taxonomy gives practitioners a concrete diagnostic for choosing retrieval depth per model, and the RSS/RSC metrics offer a reusable evaluation protocol. Open questions include whether fine-tuning readers on noisy retrieval can convert peak-then-decline models into improve-then-plateau ones, how these trends hold under adversarial or temporally shifting noise, and whether the findings generalize beyond QA to generation-heavy tasks. Limitations include the symmetric RSS window, reliance on F1 as the primary metric (with LLM-based validation only on a subset), and preliminary (not full) CRAG evaluation.
