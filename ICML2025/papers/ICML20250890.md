## Problem

Neural Algorithmic Reasoning (NAR) has successfully simulated polynomial-time algorithms but remains largely unexplored for NP-hard problems, where generating reliable ground-truth intermediate supervision is intractable. This paper addresses that gap by grounding NAR in the primal-dual paradigm, a classical framework for designing approximation algorithms for NP-hard tasks, and showing how it can be faithfully replicated by a GNN while also surpassing the algorithm's performance.

## Input / Output

The method takes a hitting set problem (ground set E with weights, family of subsets T) and represents it as a bipartite graph with primal (element) nodes on the left and dual (set) nodes on the right, connected by membership edges. At each timestep the model outputs three intermediate quantities per node—binary inclusion indicator, residual weight, and dual increment—plus a global uniform-increase value when applicable. The final output is a valid hitting set (or vertex cover / set cover), and in practical settings the model also produces 32-dimensional node embeddings for downstream classification or variable assignments to warm-start commercial solvers like Gurobi.

## Method

PDNAR uses an encoder–processor–decoder architecture where two MLP encoders map residual weights and node degrees into a shared latent space, and a recurrent message-passing GNN on the bipartite graph simulates one step of the primal-dual algorithm: a min-aggregation step passes messages from primal to dual nodes (computing δ_T), and a sum-aggregation step passes them back to update residual weights. A virtual node connected to all dual nodes implements the uniform-increase rule via an additional min-then-broadcast step. Training combines noisy teacher forcing (p=0.5) with a composite loss that supervises all intermediate algorithmic quantities (BCE + MSE) at every timestep and additionally supervises the final solution against optimal solutions from an IP solver (HiGHS) on small instances. The paper proves (Theorem 4.1) that an 8-layer PDNAR can exactly replicate the algorithm and inherit its α-approximation guarantee, while the optimal-solution supervision enables the model to outperform the algorithm it was trained to simulate.

## Results

On synthetic algorithmic datasets (trained on 16-node graphs), PDNAR achieves the lowest model-to-algorithm weight ratio across all three NP-hard tasks: 0.943 for MVC, 0.979 for MSC, and 0.989 for MHS at the training size, with ratios remaining below 1.0 (i.e., outperforming the approximation algorithm) up to 1024 nodes for MVC and MSC. On real-world Airports datasets, PDNAR embeddings boost node classification accuracy by 5–11% over the best non-PDNAR baselines (e.g., GAT+PDNAR reaches 84.44% on Brazil vs. 75.56% for GAT+Node2Vec). For Gurobi warm-starting, PDNAR reduces average solve time by 8–10% over no warm-start and 4–8% over the primal-dual algorithm, while its inference time (~0.02–0.03 s) is roughly 10× faster than the algorithm's computation time (~0.20–0.39 s).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PDNAR outperforms all baselines on the primary metric (model-to-algorithm weight ratio) across all three tasks and all graph sizes. For MVC at 1024 nodes, PDNAR achieves 0.957 vs. 1.116 (GIN), 1.125 (GAT), 1.018 (NAR/MPNN), and 1.005 (TripletMPNN), demonstrating a clear advantage that widens with scale. The ablation 'No optm' (removing optimal-solution supervision) degrades to 0.994 at 1024 nodes for MVC, confirming that IP-solver labels are critical for surpassing the algorithm. The 'No algo' ablation (removing intermediate algorithm supervision) performs worst (1.095 at 1024 for MVC), showing that the primal-dual trajectory provides essential reasoning structure beyond just learning optimal patterns. On real-world Airports data, PDNAR embeddings consistently outperform Node2Vec, LapPE, RWPE, and degree-only encodings by 2–11% accuracy across GCN, GAT, and GraphSAGE backbones. For Gurobi warm-starting, PDNAR solutions yield faster solve times than both the default initialization and the primal-dual algorithm at all tested sizes (500–750 nodes), with the model's inference being ~10× cheaper than running the algorithm.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Neural Algorithmic Reasoning (NAR) framework—originally designed to simulate polynomial-time algorithms like Bellman-Ford and BFS—into the NP-hard domain by grounding it in the primal-dual paradigm. The key architectural departure is the bipartite graph representation that maps primal variables (elements) and dual variables (sets) to opposite sides, enabling a direct structural alignment between message-passing GNN operations and the primal-dual update rules. Unlike prior NAR work that relies on transfer learning from related polynomial-time problems (Georgiev et al., 2023c) or is specialized to a single algorithm pair like Ford-Fulkerson (Numeroso et al., 2023), PDNAR provides a general framework applicable to a family of approximation algorithms unified under the hitting set formulation, and introduces the novel use of optimal solutions from IP solvers on small instances as an additional training signal to surpass the simulated algorithm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work addresses a significant gap in NAR: the inability to handle NP-hard problems where ground-truth intermediate states are intractable to generate. The finding that a model trained to simulate an approximation algorithm can outperform that algorithm (by incorporating optimal solutions from small instances) opens a new paradigm for NAR where the goal shifts from faithful replication to algorithm-informed improvement. Practical utility is demonstrated through Gurobi warm-starting (10× faster inference than the algorithm with better solutions) and real-world airport network classification. Limitations include the architecture's dependence on the hitting set formulation (problems like uncapacitated facility location require extensions), the lack of guaranteed worst-case approximation ratios in practice, and the current restriction to minimization problems.
