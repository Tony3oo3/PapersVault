## Problem

Activation editing methods for LLM fine-tuning (e.g., BitFit, LoFIT, ReFT) modify far fewer parameters than LoRA, making them attractive for low-resource settings with only a few hundred examples. However, their effectiveness is highly sensitive to which model components are edited and which intervention type (additive vs. multiplicative) is applied, with existing approaches relying on fixed heuristics or manual two-stage selection that leads to inconsistent performance across tasks and models.

## Input / Output

JOLA takes a frozen pre-trained LLM (LLaMA-3.1-8B, Qwen-2.5-7B, or variants from 1B to 70B) and a small training set of 200 text examples per task. During inference, the model produces standard text outputs—multiple-choice answers for reasoning and understanding tasks, or free-form generated text for NLG tasks. The only learnable parameters are per-head additive bias vectors, multiplicative scaling vectors, and scalar Hard-Concrete gate parameters; at inference, heads whose gates are near zero are bypassed entirely, so the base model's computation is preserved for the vast majority of heads (86–94% of heads have closed gates on OBQA).

## Method

JOLA applies a gated hybrid intervention to attention head outputs: z′ = (1 + g_m·m) ⊙ z + g_a·a, where m and a are per-head multiplicative scaling and additive bias vectors, and g_m, g_a are scalar gates drawn from Hard-Concrete distributions with a single learnable scale parameter each. Expected-L0 regularization on the gates encourages sparsity, so only a small subset of heads receives any modification. The key design choice is using separate gates for additive and multiplicative interventions, allowing the model to learn per-head whether to shift, scale, or do both. Training jointly optimizes the intervention vectors, gate parameters, and the cross-entropy task loss in a single end-to-end pass, eliminating the two-stage localization-then-editing pipeline used by LoFIT. At inference, gates are replaced by their expected values, and heads with expected gate values below a threshold ε are effectively disabled, reducing the active parameter count to ~0.0002% of the base model.

## Results

On LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct with 200 training examples, JOLA achieves 70.55% average accuracy on commonsense reasoning (vs. 66.58% for LoRA, the strongest PEFT baseline), 47.00% on MMLU-Pro understanding (vs. 42.07% for LoRA), and 17.07 BLEU / 40.65 ROUGE-L / 80.54 BERTScore on generation (vs. 13.27 / 36.97 / 77.74 for LoRA). On Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, JOLA reaches 82.40% reasoning accuracy, 51.57% understanding accuracy, and 24.00 BLEU on generation, again leading all baselines. JOLA achieves these gains while updating only 0.0065% of model parameters (vs. 0.2605% for LoRA), with 86–94% of attention head gates closed at convergence, indicating highly sparse interventions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

JOLA consistently outperforms all nine baselines across both model families and all three task categories. Against the strongest PEFT baseline LoRA, JOLA gains +3.97% on commonsense reasoning, +4.93% on MMLU-Pro understanding, and +3.80 BLEU on generation for LLaMA-3.1-8B, while using roughly 40× fewer trainable parameters (0.0065% vs. 0.2605%). Among activation editing methods, LoFIT is the closest competitor on understanding (43.13% vs. JOLA's 51.57% on Qwen2.5) but trails JOLA by 14.36 points on reasoning for LLaMA-3.1 (56.19% vs. 70.55%), largely because LoFIT's two-stage pipeline decouples head selection from intervention optimization. ReFT is the second-strongest activation editing baseline (65.95% reasoning on LLaMA-3.1) but still lags JOLA by 4.6 points. In the head-selection ablation (Ablation 3), JOLA's Hard-Concrete gating outperforms SMP, DSP, and PASS, with the gap widening in low-data settings where alternative strategies overfit or produce unstable selections. Notably, JOLA also surpasses the zero-shot baseline on every metric, confirming that even sparse activation editing with only 200 examples yields meaningful adaptation.

## Relation to Prior Work

JOLA sits at the intersection of activation editing (BitFit, RED, ReFT, LoFIT) and differentiable pruning (Hard-Concrete gates, expected-L0 regularization from Louizos et al., 2018; Voita et al., 2019). It departs from LoFIT's two-stage pipeline—first selecting heads via multiplicative interventions, then training additive biases—by unifying localization and intervention into a single end-to-end optimization with separate gates for additive and multiplicative operations. The paper also departs from prior activation editing work that fixes intervention targets (bias terms, MLP outputs, hidden states) by empirically demonstrating that attention heads are the most effective component to edit and that combining interventions across multiple component types degrades performance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

JOLA offers a practical, low-parameter alternative to LoRA for scenarios where only a few hundred training examples are available, modifying ~0.002% of parameters versus LoRA's ~0.826% while consistently outperforming it across 26 tasks. The finding that attention heads are the dominant target for activation editing, and that indiscriminately editing more components causes overfitting, provides actionable guidance for future activation-based adaptation research. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to non-English or multimodal settings, whether the gating mechanism can be extended to non-attention components, and whether the modest gap versus LoRA at 20K–100K samples narrows with architectural changes. The method's reliance on a single sparsity hyperparameter (λ) and its sensitivity to the Hard-Concrete temperature remain practical concerns for adoption.
