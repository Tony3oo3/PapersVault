## Problem

Existing continual learning methods for large pre-trained models (LPMs) typically exhibit computational complexity that scales linearly with the number of tasks, making them impractical as task streams grow. This paper addresses the gap of how to develop an efficient continual fine-tuning approach for LPMs that avoids this linear task-dependence while still leveraging task-shared knowledge to prevent catastrophic forgetting.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251997/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method receives a sequential stream of tasks, each providing a small labeled dataset (m_n samples) drawn from a task-specific distribution. For vision experiments, inputs are 224×224 RGB images and outputs are class labels; for LLM experiments, inputs are text prompts (up to 1024 tokens) and outputs are generated text evaluated by task-specific metrics (accuracy, ROUGE-L, SARI, code similarity). Critically, the task identity is not available at test time, so the model must select the appropriate adapter purely from the input, and performance is measured as overall performance (average accuracy across all seen tasks) and backward transfer (forgetting on old tasks).

## Method

TreeLoRA constructs a hierarchical K-D-tree of layer-wise LoRA adapters where each node represents a task group organized by L1-norm gradient similarity, with shallow nodes capturing shared patterns and leaf nodes capturing task-specific semantics. To avoid the O(N) cost of computing all previous task gradients, the paper formulates task-group selection as a multi-armed bandit problem and applies a Lower Confidence Bound (LCB) algorithm on the smooth tree structure, requiring only one gradient query per round to identify the most relevant branch. Model updates use a sparse gradient mechanism: a gradient-similarity regularizer (the L1-norm difference between current and selected-branch gradients) gates which LoRA parameters are updated, so only task-relevant low-rank components are modified. The tree threshold δ is set automatically at each split by the median gradient similarity within a group, and the theoretical regret bound improves from the conventional O(√N) to O(√|J_η|) (or O(√log N) in benign cases), where |J_η| is the number of near-optimal leaf nodes.

## Results

On ViT benchmarks, TreeLoRA achieves 88.54% accuracy on Split CIFAR-100, 71.94% on Split ImageNet-R, and 73.66% on Split CUB-200, matching or exceeding the prior state-of-the-art HiDeLoRA (88.46%, 72.28%, 73.48%) while reducing training time by 3.2× (e.g., 214s vs. 692s on CIFAR-100). On the TRACE LLM benchmark with Mistral-7B, TreeLoRA reaches 54.77% overall performance with 3.77% backward transfer, outperforming O-LoRA (52.02% OP, 8.13% BWT) and HiDeLoRA (51.81% OP, 6.25% BWT) while training in 510s vs. 1302s and 1288s respectively. Across all four LLMs tested, TreeLoRA achieves a consistent 2.4× training speedup over the strongest baselines while maintaining the lowest forgetting rate.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TreeLoRA decisively outperforms all rehearsal-based (GEM: 76.46% vs. 88.54% ACC on CIFAR-100) and regularization-based (EWC: 84.79%) baselines, as well as prompt-based methods (L2P: 79.66%, DualPrompt: 78.83%), by 4–9 accuracy points while being 5–100× faster. Against the closest competitor HiDeLoRA, TreeLoRA wins on CIFAR-100 (88.54% vs. 88.46%) and CUB-200 (73.66% vs. 73.48%) but trails by 0.34 points on ImageNet-R (71.94% vs. 72.28%), while achieving a 3.2× speedup (214s vs. 692s). On LLMs, TreeLoRA surpasses O-LoRA on overall performance for Mistral-7B (54.77% vs. 52.02%), LLaMA-2-7B (43.52% vs. 42.78%), and LLaMA-3.2-1B (36.14% vs. 32.94%), though it slightly underperforms on Gemma-2B (33.41% vs. 33.73%); however, TreeLoRA achieves substantially lower BWT (3.77% vs. 8.13% on Mistral) and 2.4× faster training across all models. TreeLoRA also outperforms the more recent SAPT (42.93% OP) and TASL (43.19% OP) on LLaMA-2-7B, as well as InfLoRA (85.44% vs. 88.54% ACC on CIFAR-100), confirming its advantage over both orthogonal-subspace and interference-free LoRA approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

TreeLoRA synthesizes three established threads: (1) LoRA-style low-rank adapters for parameter-efficient fine-tuning of LPMs, (2) hierarchical decomposition of continual learning (HiDePrompt, HiDeLoRA) that separates shared from task-specific knowledge, and (3) bandit algorithms on smooth trees (Coquelin & Munos, 2007) for structured exploration. The key departure is replacing the linear-in-N task similarity computation used by prior CL-for-LLMs methods (O-LoRA, HiDeLoRA, LoRAMoE) with a K-D-tree-inspired structure searched via LCB bandit, reducing per-round complexity from O(N) to O(1). The theoretical contribution—a regret bound improving from O(√N) to O(√|J_η|) under a smoothness assumption—formalizes why the tree structure helps, going beyond the empirical motivation in prior hierarchical CL work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 3.2× (ViT) and 2.4× (LLM) training-time speedups at comparable or better accuracy make TreeLoRA a practically attractive option for resource-constrained continual fine-tuning of large models, where linear scaling in the number of tasks quickly becomes prohibitive. The method's minimal storage overhead (only low-rank adapters per task) and constant GPU memory further lower the barrier for long task streams. Open questions include how the tree structure adapts when task relationships are non-stationary (the authors note this as future work), whether the smooth-tree assumption holds in adversarial or highly heterogeneous task streams, and how the approach scales beyond the 15-task sequences tested. The reliance on gradient similarity as the sole grouping criterion may limit applicability when tasks differ in objective function rather than gradient direction.
