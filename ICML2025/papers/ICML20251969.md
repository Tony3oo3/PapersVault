## Problem

Synthetic data generated by a differentially private model inherits the model's privacy guarantees via the post-processing theorem, but empirical evidence suggests the actual privacy leakage may be strictly smaller than this bound. No rigorous theoretical framework existed to quantify whether and when synthetic data release provides privacy amplification beyond the model's inherent guarantees, leaving the tightness of post-processing bounds for synthetic data an open question.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251969/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a labeled dataset D = (X, Y) with X ∈ R^{d×m} and Y ∈ R^{n×m}, trains a linear regression model under DP via output perturbation or noisy gradient descent, then generates l synthetic data points by multiplying the privatized weight matrix by a random Gaussian seed matrix Z ∈ R^{d×l} with entries drawn from N(0, σ_z²). The output is the l×n synthetic data matrix VZ, along with privacy guarantees expressed as f-DP trade-off functions and Rényi divergences that depend on the model dimension d, the number of released points l, the output dimension n, and the sensitivity Δ of the underlying mechanism.

## Method

The paper establishes two complementary results within the f-DP framework using trade-off functions. First, negative results show that when an adversary controls the generation seed z, a single synthetic point Vz can achieve the same privacy leakage as releasing the full model, by choosing z as the right singular vector of the shift matrix μ (Propositions 3.1, 3.3). Second, for random Gaussian inputs, the paper shows that VZ = (σ_θ N + v)Z converges in total variation to a Gaussian with variance dσ_θ² + ‖v‖² rather than a mean-shifted Gaussian, transforming the privacy problem from a mean-shift to a variance-shift comparison. This yields O(1/d) privacy amplification for a single released point (Theorem 4.3), and for l points the authors extend the Li-Woodruff Gaussian product convergence theorem to the shifted case (Theorem 4.5), obtaining a composition-like bound of O(nl/(d−n)) relative to the post-processing guarantee. The analysis relies on univariate and multivariate CLT bounds in total variation distance, the Neyman-Pearson lemma for computing trade-off functions between Gaussians with different variances, and a shift-reduction lemma for KL divergences.

## Results

The paper establishes two complementary sets of results. In the negative setting (adversary controls the seed), Propositions 3.1 and 3.3 show that a single synthetic data point can achieve the same privacy leakage as releasing the full model, i.e., T(Vz, Wz) = T(V, W) for some adjacent datasets. In the positive setting (random Gaussian inputs), Theorem 4.3 shows the trade-off function converges in O(1/d) to a Gaussian trade-off function, and the Rényi divergence satisfies D_α(ν_{v*}^d, ν_{w*}^d) ≈ (1/2d)·D_α(V, W), yielding a privacy amplification of order O(1/d). For multiple points (Theorem 4.6), the amplification generalizes to D_α(G_v, G_w) ≲ (nl/(2(d−n)))·D_α(V, W), valid when max{n,l} ≤ d, with a TV convergence rate of O(√(nls/(d−s))).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The natural baseline is the post-processing theorem, which guarantees that releasing synthetic data from a differentially private model cannot leak more privacy than releasing the model itself. The paper's key contribution is showing this bound is not tight. Under adversary-controlled seeds (Section 3), the post-processing bound is tight: the adversary can extract full model information from a single query, so no amplification is possible. Under random Gaussian seeds (Section 4), the post-processing bound is loose by a factor of O(1/d) for a single point and O(nl/(d−n)) for multiple points, demonstrating genuine privacy amplification. The paper also contrasts its approach with the alternative of adding noise directly to non-private model outputs, noting that while the resulting privacy guarantees are comparable in order, the direct-noise approach requires difficult sensitivity analyses and degrades utility more severely. The non-convergence result (Theorem 4.7, from Li and Woodruff 2021) establishes that the amplification guarantee fundamentally breaks down when d ≤ C''·max{n,l}^{1/2}·min{n,l}^{3/2}, delineating the regime where the technique applies.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of differential privacy theory and synthetic data generation, building on the f-DP/trade-off function framework (Dong et al., 2022) as its primary analytical tool. It departs from the standard post-processing theorem approach—which is the only formal guarantee currently available for synthetic data—by showing this bound can be strictly loose when the generative model is withheld. The technical machinery draws on the convergence of products of Gaussian matrices (Li and Woodruff, 2021) and non-asymptotic CLT bounds in total variation distance (Bally and Caramellino, 2016), adapted to a shifted setting. Conceptually, it connects to the privacy amplification literature (Balle et al., 2018; Feldman et al., 2018) but in a distinct setting: rather than amplifying through subsampling or iteration, amplification arises from withholding the model and releasing only outputs on random inputs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first rigorous theoretical framework quantifying privacy in synthetic data release, addressing a gap the authors explicitly identify as an open question. The negative results (no amplification under adversarial seed control) have direct implications for privacy auditing of synthetic data APIs, while the positive O(1/d) amplification bound suggests post-processing guarantees are overly conservative in the random-input regime. However, the analysis is confined to linear regression with output perturbation or NGD, and the authors acknowledge that extending to neural networks, DP-SGD, or the 'small d' regime remains open. The practical impact is currently limited, but the results lay groundwork for tighter privacy accounting in real-world synthetic data pipelines.
