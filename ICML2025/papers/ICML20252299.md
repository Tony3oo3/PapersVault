## Problem

When inherently continuous variables are discretized due to measurement constraints, standard CI tests applied to the observed discrete data test the wrong relationship, leading to spurious conditional dependencies. The prior discretization-aware test (DCT) corrects this but relies on binarizing the data, which discards information and degrades power, especially in small-sample regimes. The paper asks whether one can retain the full ordinal information of the discretized observations while still recovering valid CI inference about the latent continuous variables.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a matrix of ordinal discrete observations (each variable having M_j ≥ 2 levels, not restricted to binary), along with the indices of the pair to test, the conditioning set, and a significance level α. It outputs a binary CI decision, a p-value derived from an asymptotic normal null distribution, and as intermediate quantities the full estimated covariance matrix Σ̂ and the nodewise-regression coefficients β̂_{j,k} that serve as surrogates for precision-matrix entries. Unlike DCT, no binarization step is performed; the full joint cell probabilities P̂(X̃_{j1}=m, X̃_{j2}=k) for all m,k are used directly.

## Method

The key insight is that for a pair of discretized variables with cardinalities M and K, the bivariate-normal cell-probability equations yield M×K valid moment conditions for the single unknown covariance σ_{j1j2} (plus discretization boundaries), creating an over-identifying system. DCT-GMM solves this system via GMM: a one-step variant uses an identity weighting matrix, while a two-step variant re-weights by the sample covariance of the moment functions to achieve asymptotic efficiency. The estimated covariance matrix is then fed into nodewise regression (β̂_j = Σ̂_{-j-j}^{-1} Σ̂_{-jj}) to obtain the CI test statistic, whose asymptotic variance is derived by expressing β̂_{j,k} − β*_{j,k} as a linear combination of the GMM influence functions. A formal theorem proves that the two-step GMM estimator has strictly lower asymptotic variance than DCT's single-equation Z-estimator, translating directly into higher statistical power for the CI test.

## Results

DCT-GMM maintains Type I error close to the nominal α=0.05 across all sample sizes (n=200–2000) and conditioning set cardinalities (D=1–5), while Chi-square and Fisher-z tests exhibit significantly inflated Type I errors that worsen with increasing sample size. In causal discovery on synthetic DAGs (p=4–12 nodes, n=100–2000), DCT-GMM consistently achieves the best F1, precision, and SHD among all discretization-aware methods, with notably higher recall than DCT. In the direct variance/MSE comparison (Figure 4), DCT-GMM's covariance estimator has strictly lower variance and MSE than DCT across all sample sizes n∈{100,200,500,1000,2000} for both σ_XY=0 and σ_XY=0.5 with M=3 discretization levels.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DCT (Sun et al., 2024), DCT-GMM wins on every metric: lower Type II error (higher statistical power) especially at small n, lower variance and MSE of the covariance estimator, and higher recall in causal discovery (skeleton and DAG). The advantage is most pronounced at small sample sizes (n=100–500), where DCT's single-equation approach loses the most information. Against Fisher-z and Chi-square tests, DCT-GMM wins decisively on Type I error control: both baselines test the wrong null hypothesis (CI of discretized variables rather than latent continuous variables), so their Type I error inflates well above 0.05 and *increases* with sample size, whereas DCT-GMM stays calibrated. In causal discovery, Fisher-z and Chi-square produce increasingly dense (incorrect) graphs as n grows, while DCT-GMM tracks the Fisher-z oracle (applied to continuous data) more closely. On the real-world Big Five Personality dataset, DCT-GMM correctly identifies N3 ⊥ N4 | N10 at α=10^{-3} where DCT fails, confirming the practical benefit of the GMM-based variance reduction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends DCT (Sun et al., 2024), the first CI test designed for discretized observations, by replacing its binarization-based single-equation estimator with a Generalized Method of Moments (GMM) framework that exploits all M×K cell-probability equations simultaneously. The nodewise regression machinery for constructing the CI test statistic and its asymptotic distribution is inherited from DCT and Callot et al. (2019), while the GMM estimation theory follows Hansen (1982) and Newey (2007). The paper positions itself against standard CI tests (Fisher-z, chi-square, kernel-based) that are unaware of discretization and thus produce systematically biased conclusions on binned data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Discretization is pervasive in applied settings (finance, psychology, recommendation systems), and the paper demonstrates that traditional CI tests fail catastrophically on such data, with errors worsening as sample size grows. The GMM-based approach offers a principled, provably lower-variance alternative to DCT's binarization, improving statistical power especially in small-sample regimes critical for causal discovery. Key limitations include the multivariate normality assumption on latent variables and the computational cost of solving the GMM optimization for each variable pair; extending to non-Gaussian latent distributions or mixed discrete-continuous settings remains open.
