## Problem

In machine learning-guided design, practitioners must choose among many design algorithm configurations (algorithm, hyperparameters, predictive model), but predictions for novel designs are often misleading because design algorithms deliberately shift away from training data. No existing method provides a principled, statistically guaranteed way to select configurations whose resulting distribution of design labels will satisfy a user-specified success criterion (e.g., at least 10% of labels exceed a threshold) before the costly step of acquiring labels.

## Input / Output

The method takes a finite menu of candidate configurations, N designs generated by each configuration, held-out labeled data (independent from predictive model training data), the predictive model used by each configuration, and density ratios between each design distribution and the labeled distribution. It outputs a subset of configurations guaranteed with probability at least 1−α to all be successful, or the empty set if no configuration can be certified. The success criterion is a population-level metric θ_λ = E_{Y~P_{Y;λ}}[g(Y)] ≥ τ for a user-chosen function g and threshold τ, operating at the distribution level rather than on individual designs.

## Method

The method frames design algorithm selection as a multiple hypothesis testing problem, where each configuration on the menu has a null hypothesis that it is unsuccessful. For each configuration, it computes a p-value using prediction-powered inference adapted for covariate shift: the population-level metric is decomposed into the mean prediction over designs plus a 'rectifier' (mean prediction bias) estimated from held-out labeled data weighted by the density ratio between the design and labeled distributions. This correction accounts for how prediction error distorts the prediction-only estimate, yielding p-values that are either asymptotically valid (closed-form, Algorithm 2) or finite-sample valid (via betting-based confidence lower bounds, Algorithm 3). A Bonferroni correction across the menu controls family-wise error rate, guaranteeing that the probability of including any unsuccessful configuration is at most α. The key insight is that combining cheap in-silico predictions with a smaller set of held-out labels, weighted by known or estimated density ratios, produces lower-variance estimates of the success metric than either predictions alone or importance-weighted labels alone.

## Results

On the protein GB1 design task (101 configurations, known density ratios), the proposed method achieves error rates below the target α=0.1 for all values of τ (mean design label threshold from 0 to 1.5), with 100% selection rate across a broad range of τ that gradually declines for τ > 1. On the RNA binder design task (78 configurations, estimated density ratios), the method maintains much lower error rates than all alternatives across the τ ∈ [0.28, 0.5] range, though error slightly exceeds α for τ > 0.32 due to density ratio estimation error. In both settings, the prediction-only and CalibratedForecasts baselines exhibit 100% error rates for most τ values, while GMMForecasts (Wheelock et al.) is either prohibitively conservative (never selecting) or has high error rates depending on its hyperparameter q.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all four baselines on both tasks. Against the prediction-only method, which treats design predictions as labels, the proposed method reduces error from 100% to below α=0.1 on GB1 and substantially lowers error on RNA binders, despite using only half the training data (5k vs. 10k labeled sequences). Against GMMForecasts (Wheelock et al., 2022), the proposed method wins on both error rate and selection rate: GMMForecasts with q=0 or q=0.5 never selects any configuration (0% selection rate), while q=1 achieves low error only for τ < 0.5 on GB1 and τ < 0.37 on RNA before ceasing selection entirely; the proposed method maintains non-zero selection rates across a broader τ range with consistently lower error. Against CalibratedForecasts, which calibrates individual design forecasts via isotonic regression, the proposed method again achieves far lower error rates (below α vs. 100%) on both tasks. The conformal prediction baseline, which has similar theoretical guarantees, was so conservative that it never selected any configuration in any experiment and is excluded from the reported results.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on prediction-powered inference (Angelopoulos et al., 2023), adapting its rectifier-based framework for covariate shift to construct valid p-values for design algorithm configurations, and on betting-based confidence bounds (Waudby-Smith & Ramdas, 2023) for finite-sample validity. It departs from the dominant line of work on individual-design uncertainty quantification (conformal prediction, calibrated forecasts, GMM-based forecasts à la Wheelock et al., 2022) by targeting the population-level metric directly rather than modeling each design's label distribution. The multiple-hypothesis-testing framing with Bonferroni correction is borrowed from Angelopoulos et al. (2021) for hyperparameter selection, but applied here to the novel problem of choosing among design algorithm configurations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first method with a high-probability guarantee that selected design algorithm configurations will satisfy a user-specified success criterion, which is practically important when acquiring labels is expensive and stakeholders need justification before committing resources. The guarantee requires known density ratios between design and labeled distributions, which limits applicability; with estimated ratios the method still outperforms alternatives empirically but loses its formal guarantee. Open questions include developing density ratio estimators tailored to importance-weighted mean estimation, incorporating structure-aware multiple testing to reduce conservatism, and extending the framework to multi-round design campaigns where criteria must be met at each round.
