## Problem

Existing similarity matrix evaluation metrics for representation quality are either pairwise-based (e.g., d_infor) and ignore class-level structure, or class-aware (e.g., Square Euclidean Distance) but suffer from random consistency—a spurious bias toward non-informative matrices that distorts optimization and generalization. This paper identifies random consistency as a fundamental obstacle to reliably measuring how well a learned representation separates classes.

## Input / Output

The method accepts raw feature vectors (tabular data with 4–156 dimensions) or RGB images (28×28 to 256×256, preprocessed via VGG or MoCo-V3 feature extraction into 6000-d or 256-d embeddings) and outputs class labels via a softmax classification head. Unusually, the core loss operates not on predictions but on the n×n pairwise similarity matrix K = Z(Z)^T computed from hidden-layer embeddings, comparing it against the true class adjacency matrix YY^T.

## Method

The paper proposes Pure Square Euclidean Distance (PSED), defined as the actual SED between the similarity matrix K and the true class adjacency matrix YY^T minus the expected SED under uniformly random label permutations, thereby subtracting the random consistency baseline. A key technical contribution is a closed-form analytical solution for the permutation expectation with O(kn²) complexity, replacing the naive factorial computation. The training framework uses a presentation layer (fully connected + ReLU + Dropout) to produce embeddings whose inner product yields K, optimized with PSED loss, while a separate classification layer is trained with standard cross-entropy. The authors further establish a generalization bound via an exponential Orlicz norm concentration inequality, showing the bound scales as O(1/n) and depends on network width, Lipschitz constants, and input domain diameter.

## Results

On 20 benchmark tabular datasets (3-layer fully connected network, 10-fold CV), CE-PSED achieves the highest average accuracy on the majority of datasets, e.g., 91.18% on Wine Quality (vs. 67.96% CE, 89.00% CE-SED, 78.69% CE-inform) and 73.89% on Steel Plates Faults (vs. 68.64% CE, 61.75% CE-SED, 47.75% CE-inform). On image datasets, the gains are especially pronounced: CE-PSED reaches 50.05% accuracy on Caltech-101 (vs. 35.61% CE, 27.78% CE-SED, 27.58% CE-inform) and 99.08% on Pendigits (vs. 77.61% CE, 96.11% CE-SED, 78.89% CE-inform). F-measure follows the same pattern, and at deeper network depths (5 and 8 layers), CE-PSED degrades far less than baselines, e.g., on Yeast the F-measure drops from 0.5903 (3 layers) to 0.4116 (8 layers) for CE-PSED versus 0.5475→0.1662 for CE.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares CE-PSED against three baselines: plain Cross-Entropy (CE, Hinton et al. 2012), CE combined with the informativeness measure d_infor (Brockmeier et al. 2017), and CE combined with the Square Euclidean Distance (SED, the paper's own uncorrected baseline). CE-PSED wins on the vast majority of the 20 benchmark datasets in both accuracy and F-measure; the largest margins are on imbalanced or structurally complex datasets such as Steel Plates Faults (73.89% vs. 47.75% for CE-inform, a 26-point gap) and SkillCraft1 (84.69% vs. 65.35% for CE-inform). Against CE-inform, the advantage is consistent and large, confirming that the pairwise d_infor measure fails to capture class-level structure. Against CE-SED, CE-PSED still wins on most datasets but the gap is smaller (e.g., 91.18% vs. 89.00% on Wine Quality), reflecting that SED already encodes class structure but suffers from random-consistency bias. On the image datasets, CE-PSED's advantage over CE-inform is dramatic on Caltech-101 (50.05% vs. 27.58%) and MPEG-7 (73.38% vs. 20.98%), while against CE-SED the gap narrows on easier sets like MNIST (90.62% vs. 90.46%) and ImageNet-10 (97.62% vs. 96.99%). Friedman and Nemenyi post-hoc tests confirm that CE-PSED's average rank is significantly lower than all other methods across both accuracy and F-measure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Pure Consistency Measure (PCM) framework—previously applied to accuracy, Gini index, and clustering metrics—into the domain of similarity matrix evaluation for deep representation learning. It identifies random consistency as a hidden bias in the Square Euclidean Distance (SED) between a learned similarity matrix and the true class adjacency matrix, then derives a closed-form debiased version (PSED) that eliminates this bias. For the generalization bound, the paper adopts the exponential Orlicz norm-based concentration inequality from Escande (2024), which characterizes parameter concentration rather than mere parameter range, providing tighter guarantees than traditional norm-based bounds. The work also positions PSED against the unsupervised d_infor measure (Brockmeier et al., 2017), showing that d_infor's homogeneity property makes it unable to distinguish matrices with different internal class structures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, theoretically grounded loss function for evaluating class-level discriminability in representation layers, addressing a gap between pairwise similarity measures and the class-structure requirements of classification. The generalization bound via exponential Orlicz norms offers statistical learnability guarantees for the PSED loss in fully connected networks with RBF kernels. However, the practical impact is somewhat limited: the method is validated primarily on fully connected networks (with ViT and CNN results deferred to appendix), the generalization bound is specific to RBF kernels, and the authors themselves note that optimization convergence properties remain unanalyzed. Whether PSED integrates naturally into modern architectures (e.g., as an auxiliary loss alongside contrastive objectives) or scales to very large-scale settings remains an open question.
