## Problem

Traditional supervised learning maximizes accuracy in isolation, but in real consumer markets multiple service providers compete for users who choose based on prediction correctness. This paper asks: what should a provider learn when its market share depends on the classifiers of all competitors, and how do the resulting dynamics affect firms, users, and the market as a whole? The key gap is that naively optimizing accuracy is provably suboptimal under competition, yet no practical method existed for learning classifiers that maximize market share.

## Input / Output

Each provider receives a standard training set of (x, y) pairs plus, for every training point, the count κ_{-i}(x) of other providers that are correct on that point — not their actual classifiers. The method outputs a best-response classifier h_i trained to maximize the provider's expected market share. A notable property is that the average weight 1/|S| Σ w_i(x_j) equals the maximum achievable market share of a perfect classifier, so the actual market share is this ceiling minus the weighted loss of the learned model.

## Method

The core insight is that a provider's market share decomposes into a weighted accuracy objective: μ_i = E_p[w_i(x)·1{h_i(x)=y}] with per-example weights w_i(x) = 1/(1+κ_{-i}(x)), where κ_{-i}(x) counts competitors correct on x. Training then reduces to standard empirical risk minimization with custom sample weights and a proxy loss (hinge or cross-entropy), making the approach compatible with any model class (SVMs, XGBoost, Random Forests). Theoretically, the induced game is a potential game guaranteeing pure Nash equilibria reachable via best-response dynamics; for threshold classifiers under monotone likelihood ratio, the best-response set collapses to just two candidates (ρ^{-1}(1/2) and ρ^{-1}(2)), and dynamics converge after a single round. The paper further shows that computing an exact best-response is no harder than standard classification, and that providers implicitly cooperate: one firm's best-response increases the market share of all others.

## Results

Across COMPAS-Arrest, COMPAS-Violent, and Adult datasets with Linear SVMs, competition via weighted-accuracy best responses improves welfare by +28.2% to +50.2% (COMPAS-Arrest), +51.5% to +64.2% (COMPAS-Violent), and +17.3% to +23.2% (Adult) as the number of providers grows from 2 to 6. Individual provider market share gains range from +25.4% to +83.2% (max provider, COMPAS-Arrest, n=5), while market concentration (HHI) rises from ~64% to ~129% over the same range. All settings converge to near-equilibrium after at most 2 rounds of best responses, and welfare reaches its maximum (1.0) with as few as 5 providers before all have moved.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external ML baselines; instead, the implicit baseline is naive accuracy maximization (the initial h^opt state before competition). The key finding is that optimizing the weighted-accuracy objective (Eq. 11) consistently outperforms naive accuracy in market share: the first mover in Figure 2 achieves 36% market share by sacrificing overall accuracy below 50% to secure exclusive access to 28.5% of users, whereas a naive accuracy optimizer would not target this sector. Across model classes (Linear SVM, XGBoost, Random Forest), the qualitative trends are consistent, though XGBoost and Random Forest show smaller welfare *improvements* (e.g., +1.6% vs. +28.2% on Adult for n=2) because their higher starting accuracy leaves less room for gain. The order-of-play analysis reveals a regime shift: with n=2, moving second yields a median +4.8% market share advantage (chicken game), while with n≥3, moving first dominates (the first mover 'grabs territory' and the remaining players play a sub-game among themselves). Restricting model capacity (fewer features) can paradoxically increase welfare from 0.84 to 1.0, echoing Jagadeesan et al. (2024) that better data representation does not always translate to better user outcomes under competition.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the regression equilibrium framework of Ben-Porat & Tennenholtz (2017; 2019) from regression to classification, arguing the latter is more natural for consumer-facing prediction markets. It departs from the broader strategic learning literature (Hardt et al., 2016; Levanon & Rosenfeld, 2021), which models users as feature-manipulating agents, by instead modeling users as choosing among competing providers based on prediction accuracy. The key methodological contribution is showing that the best-response objective reduces to a weighted accuracy loss (Eq. 10), making it amenable to standard empirical risk minimization with proxy losses, and connecting the dynamics to performative prediction (Perdomo et al., 2020) and congestion games.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides both theoretical guarantees (PNE existence, convergence in one round for threshold classifiers, bounded market concentration) and empirical evidence that competition in accuracy markets improves user welfare, with welfare reaching near-maximum after just a few firms and one update round. A notable limitation is the idealized assumption of a single shared distribution, rational users, and full information transparency; realistic markets with heterogeneous data, strategic users, or opaque models may behave very differently. Open questions include how these dynamics change under data asymmetry between providers, non-rational user behavior, and whether the welfare benefits persist when firms can exploit behavioral weaknesses—raising concrete policy questions about model transparency regulation in oligopolistic prediction markets.
