## Problem

LLM-based agents struggle with long-horizon decision-making due to deficient exploration, poor long-term credit assignment, and sparse-reward feedback, while existing prompt-based and flat RL approaches either exceed context limits, depend on expensive expert demonstrations, or suffer from brittle sample efficiency. The paper addresses the lack of a parameter-efficient, generally applicable hierarchical structure that enables LLMs to decompose complex tasks into structured sub-problems for more effective exploration and learning.

## Input / Output

The agent receives a textual task description and a text observation from the environment at each timestep. The high-level policy (invoked every c steps) outputs an abstract sub-task goal, while the low-level policy outputs a primitive action constrained to 32 max new tokens. Notably, the low-level policy is supervised by an intrinsic binary sub-task-completion signal derived from environment observations rather than the raw environment reward, and the high-level accumulates environment rewards over its c-step horizon as a single return.

## Method

GLIDER introduces a two-level hierarchical actor-critic architecture where both the high-level planner and low-level controller share a single frozen LLM backbone (augmented with LoRA for the actor and MLP heads for the critic), differentiated only by hierarchy-specific prompts. Training proceeds in three stages: (1) supervised fine-tuning via behavior cloning with a length-regularization term to produce concise outputs, (2) offline RL using sentence-level critics trained with an asymmetric expectile loss (for conservative value estimation under distribution shift) and token-level actors optimized via AWAC-style advantage-weighted maximum likelihood, and (3) optional offline-to-online adaptation where task-agnostic low-level skills are frozen and only the high-level policy is fine-tuned on new environment interactions. The key mechanism is that the high-level policy generates sub-task goals that provide temporal abstraction and intrinsic completion rewards to the low-level, enabling structured credit assignment without any manual hierarchy design or domain-specific reward engineering.

## Results

On ScienceWorld with Llama-3-8B, GLIDER achieves 77.43 on seen tasks and 68.34 on unseen tasks, outperforming the best baseline (ETO) by +33.73% and +30.59% respectively. On ALFWorld with Llama-3-8B, GLIDER scores 71.56 (seen) and 75.38 (unseen), surpassing ETO by +11.31% and +17.45%. Across all three backbones (Mistral-7B, Gemma-7B, Llama-3-8B), GLIDER consistently outperforms both prompt-based methods (ReAct, Reflexion) and fine-tuning approaches (NAT, ETO) on both benchmarks, with the largest gains on unseen tasks (ranging from +22.28% to +30.59% on ScienceWorld).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GLIDER outperforms all five main baselines across every backbone and benchmark. Prompt-based methods (ReAct, Reflexion) are consistently the weakest, scoring below 30 on ScienceWorld and below 12 on ALFWorld with most backbones, highlighting the limitations of frozen-LLM prompting for long-horizon tasks. Among fine-tuning baselines, ETO (DPO-based trajectory optimization) is the strongest competitor, but GLIDER still leads by 15–34% on ScienceWorld and 3–17% on ALFWorld. NAT (learning from failure trajectories) is competitive on ALFWorld seen tasks (e.g., 67.86 with Gemma-7B) but trails GLIDER by 4–11%. In the offline-to-online fine-tuning comparison (Figure 4), GLIDER achieves both a higher initial zero-shot score and faster convergence than both the classical AC algorithm and AWAC, demonstrating superior generalization to unseen tasks (test-conductivity, find-animal, boil) in ScienceWorld.

## Relation to Prior Work

GLIDER sits at the intersection of hierarchical RL (Options, MAX-Q, HiRO, HAC) and LLM-based decision-making agents (ReAct, Reflexion, ETO). It departs from classical hierarchical RL by leveraging LLMs' semantic reasoning to autonomously generate sub-task decompositions, eliminating the need for domain experts to specify meaningful hierarchies. For the offline RL component, it builds directly on AWAC's advantage-weighted MLE formulation for the token-level actor and on implicit Q-learning's asymmetric critic loss, while extending these to a two-level hierarchical setting with a shared LLM backbone differentiated only by prompts. The parameter-efficient design (LoRA + shared actor-critic across hierarchy levels) contrasts with prior hierarchical methods that train independent models per level.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a core bottleneck in LLM agents—poor sample efficiency and weak long-horizon credit assignment in sparse-reward environments—by showing that a lightweight hierarchical structure with offline RL yields consistent 15–34% gains over strong baselines across three backbones and two benchmarks. The finding that a 1:2 expert-to-medium data mixture dramatically outperforms pure expert demonstrations (68.3 vs. 29.7) suggests that diverse trial-and-error coverage matters more than trajectory quality for generalization, a useful practical insight. Limitations include the multi-stage training pipeline's complexity, the approximate (not strict) freezing of low-level skills during O2O due to shared parameters, and evaluation restricted to two text-based benchmarks. Extending the framework to broader domains (math reasoning, code generation) and streamlining the pipeline toward single-stage RL fine-tuning are natural next steps.
