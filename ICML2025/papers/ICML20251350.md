## Problem

Transformer language models exhibit behaviors that appear to require tracking the unobserved state of an evolving world, yet the specific mechanisms they use to construct and update these internal state representations remain unexplained. This paper uses permutation composition as a controlled model system—justified by the fact that many natural state-tracking tasks (finite automata simulation, Boolean expression evaluation) reduce to permutation tracking with five or more objects—to identify and characterize the concrete algorithms that trained LMs actually implement.

## Input / Output

The primary task takes a sequence of permutation tokens from S_3 or S_5 (e.g., '42315' denoting a rearrangement of 5 objects) and requires the model to predict the cumulative state after each prefix, producing a sequence of state tokens [s_0, ..., s_t] rather than only the final state. A natural-language variant expresses the same task as swap/rotate instructions (e.g., 'swap positions 2 and 3') and asks the model to predict the final arrangement from the terminal period token. All experiments use length-100 sequences, with generalization evaluated on held-out sequences of varying lengths up to 100.

## Method

The paper defines four candidate state-tracking algorithms (sequential, parallel, associative, and parity-associative) and derives the distinct 'signatures' each would produce under activation patching, linear probing, and attention analysis. Trained Pythia and GPT-2 models (160M–1.4B) are then dissected using prefix/suffix/window activation patching, state and parity linear probes across all layers, parity-head attention metrics, and training-dynamics analysis. The key finding is that models consistently converge to one of two mechanisms: an associative algorithm (AA) that hierarchically composes adjacent action pairs across layers (matching the log-depth scan construction of Liu et al. 2023 and Merrill & Sabharwal 2024), or a parity-associative algorithm (PAA) that first computes permutation parity in a constant number of early layers and then computes a parity-complement residual via an associative scan. The choice between AA and PAA is determined by architecture and initialization rather than model size, and can be steered by intermediate training tasks: topic-modeling pretraining encourages AA, while a parity-prediction curriculum induces PAA.

## Results

Across Pythia and GPT-2 models of various sizes trained on S₃ and S₅ permutation composition, LMs consistently learn one of two mechanisms: the associative algorithm (AA) or the parity-associative algorithm (PAA), with no evidence for sequential or fully parallel composition. AA models generalize better overall, with equal parity and state cutoff lengths, while PAA models show a longer parity cutoff than state cutoff and learn in two distinct phases (parity first, then state). The mechanism choice is determined primarily by architecture and initialization rather than model size: GPT-2 splits roughly 50/50 between AA and PAA, pre-trained Pythia (Pile) tends toward AA, and from-scratch Pythia tends toward PAA. Intermediate training tasks can reliably steer models: topic-modeling pre-training consistently induces AA, while a parity-prediction curriculum converts pre-trained Pythia from AA to PAA.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This interpretability paper does not compare against external baselines; instead, it contrasts the two empirically learned mechanisms (AA vs. PAA) against four theoretically proposed algorithms (sequential, parallel, associative, parity-associative). The sequential and parallel algorithms are ruled out: no model exhibits the upper-triangular patching signature of sequential composition or the L-shaped signature of constant-depth parallel composition. Between AA and PAA, AA models achieve better generalization (longer effective sequence lengths before accuracy drops below 98%) and faster convergence to lower training loss. PAA models, however, compute parity in a constant number of early layers (around layers 3–5), giving them a longer parity-specific generalization range than their state range. Training regime acts as a control: topic-modeling pre-training pushes from-scratch models to AA (100% in both GPT-2 and Pythia), while a parity-prediction curriculum converts pre-trained Pythia from AA to PAA, confirming that the mechanism is a learnable property rather than a fixed architectural constraint.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the theoretical constructions of Liu et al. (2023) and Merrill & Sabharwal (2024), who showed that log-depth Transformers can implement associative scans to solve state-tracking problems in NC1. While prior empirical work (Li et al., 2021; Kim & Schuster, 2023; Li et al., 2023) demonstrated that LMs can solve state-tracking tasks and encode state information in their representations, no prior work identified the specific algorithmic mechanisms underlying this ability. This paper departs from that line by using permutation composition as a controlled model system and applying multiple interpretability tools (activation patching, linear probing, attention analysis, training dynamics) to reverse-engineer which algorithmic strategy trained LMs actually implement, revealing two distinct mechanisms rather than the single associative scan predicted by theory.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of two distinct state-tracking mechanisms—particularly the PAA, which combines a cheap parity heuristic with an associative scan—challenges the assumption that LMs implement a single unified algorithm and suggests that heuristic features and structured computation can coexist in a single model. The finding that mechanism choice is stochastic yet controllable via intermediate training tasks (topic modeling, parity prediction curricula) offers a practical lever for steering LM behavior. Key open questions include whether these mechanisms transfer to real-world state tracking (code execution, game play, discourse), whether they persist at larger scales, and whether the PAA's brittle parity-complement computation represents a fundamental limitation or an artifact of small model size.
