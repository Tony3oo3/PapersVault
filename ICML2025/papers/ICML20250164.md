## Problem

Central DP federated learning achieves strong privacy-utility tradeoffs via the matrix mechanism, which correlates noise across training iterations to reduce cumulative noise. However, distributed DP—where clients add their own noise before aggregation, protecting against a potentially malicious server—has lagged behind because changing client committees prevent cross-iteration noise correlation. This paper asks whether the matrix mechanism's correlated-noise benefits can be brought to the distributed DP setting without sacrificing the stronger privacy guarantees.

## Input / Output

Each client takes as input its locally computed gradient (clipped to ℓ₂ norm c, scaled by 1/γ, flattened via a random unitary matrix, and randomized-rounded to integers in a finite field F of bit-width m), a locally sampled Discrete Gaussian noise vector, and packed secret shares of all prior iterations' aggregated gradients and noise reshared by the previous committee. The server outputs the matrix mechanism's noisy release ÂX_T (unflattened and rescaled back to ℝ^d) for model updates, while each client simultaneously outputs reshared packed secret shares destined for the next committee. The protocol completes in two communication rounds per iteration and operates over batches of k² gradient dimensions, where k = 2μn is the packing parameter.

## Method

The core contribution is the Distributed Matrix Mechanism (DMM), built on a novel constant-overhead Linear Secret Resharing Protocol (LRP). LRP uses packed secret sharing (Franklin-Yung style, degree t_c+k−1 polynomials) to batch k² secrets into k packed sharings, so that each party in committee T reshares its packed shares to committee T+1 with only O(1) field elements per secret—reducing communication from the infeasible O(n²) of naive resharing (2.13 TB per client) to as low as 5.73 MB. In each iteration, clients aggregate their secret-shared gradients and noise, compute linear combinations according to the A=BC matrix factorization to produce shares of the correlated-noise output, reconstruct this to the server, and then reshare their updated shares to the next committee via LRP. The protocol tolerates up to t_c corrupted parties per iteration plus the server, and t_d honest dropout parties (with t_c + t_d < (1/2−μ)n), and is proven secure under a standard simulation-based definition. Two factorizations are instantiated: the optimal b-min-sep-participation factorization and the more efficient Honaker online (binary tree) factorization, the latter reducing per-iteration communication to O(d log T / 4μ²).

## Results

On Stack Overflow Next Word Prediction, DMM (distributed DP) achieves 5–6 percentage points higher test accuracy than the prior distributed DP state-of-the-art (DDG) across all privacy levels ε, and matches or slightly exceeds the best central DP approach (BandMF). On Federated EMNIST, DMM improves over DDG by approximately 4 percentage points. In terms of efficiency, the Honaker online factorization adds only 412 ms of computation and 25.1 MB of communication per client per iteration on SO-NWP (n=64), compared to 61.3 ms and 16.2 MB for SecAgg (the bottleneck of DDG), while the naive secret resharing alternative would require 11.4 GB per client.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DDG (Kairouz et al., 2021a), the prior state-of-the-art distributed DP mechanism, DMM wins decisively: 5–6 percentage points higher accuracy on SO-NWP and ~4 percentage points on FEMNIST across all ε levels, because DMM uses correlated noise via the matrix mechanism while DDG adds fresh noise each iteration. Against BandMF (Choquette-Choo et al., 2023a), the best central DP approach, DMM matches accuracy on SO-NWP (sometimes slightly better), closing the gap between distributed and central DP. Against the Honaker online mechanism (Honaker, 2015), DMM with the Honaker factorization achieves nearly identical accuracy to the optimal factorization at a fraction of the communication cost (25.1 MB vs. 4.68 GB per client). Against Flamingo/SecAgg (Ma et al., 2023), the secure aggregation protocol underlying DDG, DMM adds modest overhead: less than 10 seconds of computation and under 10 MB of additional communication per client with the Honaker factorization, while the naive resharing alternative would be infeasible at 2.13 TB per client.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate lines of work: the matrix mechanism for central DP (Hubert Chan et al., 2010; Choquette-Choo et al., 2023a), which achieves strong privacy-utility tradeoffs through correlated noise across iterations, and distributed DP for FL (Kairouz et al., 2021a), which provides stronger privacy guarantees but was limited to fresh, uncorrelated noise per iteration. The key technical contribution is a constant-overhead linear secret resharing protocol (LRP) built on packed Shamir secret sharing (Franklin & Yung, 1992), which enables noise and gradient shares to be transferred across dynamically changing client committees at O(1) communication per secret rather than the naive O(n²). This departs from prior proactive secret sharing work (Ostrovsky & Yung, 1991; Maram et al., 2019) where the same users persist across iterations, and from concurrent encryption-based approaches (Ball et al., 2024) that rely on computational assumptions rather than information-theoretic security.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DMM closes a significant gap in the DP-FL landscape by delivering central-DP-level utility under the stronger distributed-DP threat model, which is critical for highly sensitive data where the server itself cannot be trusted. The practical overhead is modest (under 10 seconds and ~25 MB per client with the Honaker factorization), making this deployable. Limitations include the absence of malicious security (only semi-honest corruption is handled), the reliance on a trusted server for message forwarding, and the fact that the optimal matrix factorization incurs substantially higher communication (828 MB vs 5.73 MB per client) than the Honaker variant. Open questions include designing better low-memory factorizations that approach optimal accuracy at Honaker-level efficiency, and whether the information-theoretic approach can be extended to handle malicious adversaries without the computational overhead of homomorphic encryption.
