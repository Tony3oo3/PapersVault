## Problem

Graph-level anomaly detection (GLAD) is fundamentally limited by the scarcity of anomalous graph data: unsupervised methods that model only normality struggle to detect subtle anomalies near the decision boundary, while semi-supervised methods are constrained by the limited diversity of available labeled anomalies. This paper asks whether diffusion models can be repurposed not to model normality (as in prior diffusion-based anomaly detection) but to actively generate diverse pseudo-anomalous graphs that provide explicit supervisory signals for training a more robust detector.

## Input / Output

The method takes a set of normal graphs, each specified by an adjacency matrix A ∈ {0,1}^{n×n} and a node feature matrix X ∈ R^{n×d}, with no anomalous labels required during training. It produces two outputs: (1) a scalar anomaly score per test graph estimating P(G ∉ P_normal | G), and (2) internally generated pseudo-anomalous graphs (decoded from perturbed latent embeddings) that serve as synthetic negative training examples. The framework operates entirely in an unsupervised regime, with the pseudo-anomalies generated on-the-fly during training rather than sourced from external data.

## Method

AGDiff is a three-phase framework: (1) a VGAE-style pre-training phase learns a structured latent space via GNN-based variational inference with joint edge/attribute reconstruction and KL regularization; (2) a conditional latent diffusion process generates pseudo-anomalous graphs by forward-diffusing the latent embedding z₀ and then reverse-denoising under a learnable perturbation condition c = τ_ω(z₀ + η), where η is Gaussian noise and τ_ω is a small MLP that transforms the perturbed embedding into a conditioning vector concatenated at each denoising step; (3) a GIN-based binary classifier is jointly trained with the diffusion model via a combined objective L = L_cls + λ·L_diff, where the classification loss distinguishes normal from pseudo-anomalous graphs and the diffusion loss guides generation quality. The key distinction from prior diffusion-based anomaly detection is that the diffusion model acts as a pseudo-anomaly generator rather than a normality model, and the perturbation condition is co-adapted with the detector so that increasingly challenging pseudo-anomalies are produced as the decision boundary refines.

## Results

AGDiff achieves the best AUC and F1-Score across all eight graph benchmarks. On moderate-scale datasets, it reaches 95.83% AUC / 89.45% F1 on MUTAG, 88.23% / 84.06% on DD, 77.59% / 68.15% on COX2, and 91.21% / 86.04% on ER_MD. On large-scale imbalanced datasets, it attains 91.60% AUC / 90.97% F1 on SW-620, 87.75% / 87.22% on MOLT-4, 94.32% / 93.12% on PC-3, and 89.38% / 87.25% on MCF-7, surpassing the strongest semi-supervised baseline iGAD by 5.78–8.28% AUC.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AGDiff outperforms all 11 baselines on every dataset for both AUC and F1-Score. Among graph kernel methods, RW is the strongest (86.98% AUC on MUTAG) but suffers from scalability issues (out-of-memory on DD); AGDiff exceeds it by 8.85% AUC on MUTAG and 12.29% on COX2. Among GNN-based unsupervised methods, GLocalKD is the closest competitor on MUTAG (90.59% AUC vs. AGDiff's 95.83%, a 5.24% gap), while OCGTL leads on DD (77.52% vs. 88.23%, a 10.71% gap). The semi-supervised iGAD, which uses labeled anomalies, is the strongest overall baseline (92.58% AUC on MUTAG, 86.04% on PC-3), yet AGDiff surpasses it by 3.25% AUC on MUTAG and 8.28% on PC-3 despite being fully unsupervised. Reconstruction-based methods (MUSE, SIGNET) show the largest gaps, with MUSE dropping to 31.07% AUC on ER_MD versus AGDiff's 91.21%, confirming the paper's argument that reconstruction error is an unreliable anomaly signal for subtle deviations.

## Relation to Prior Work

AGDiff builds on the DDPM diffusion framework (Ho et al., 2020) and variational graph autoencoders (Kipf & Welling, 2016), but departs fundamentally from prior diffusion-based anomaly detection methods (Wolleb et al., 2022; Li et al., 2024) that use diffusion to model normality and flag reconstruction outliers. Instead, AGDiff repurposes latent diffusion as a *generator* of pseudo-anomalous graphs via controlled perturbations in a pre-trained VAE latent space, then jointly trains a GIN-based classifier to discriminate these from normal graphs. This reframing also distinguishes it from reconstruction-based GLAD methods (MUSE, SIGNET) that assume anomalies necessarily exhibit higher reconstruction error, and from semi-supervised approaches (iGAD) that depend on scarce labeled anomalies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses the core data-scarcity bottleneck in GLAD by showing that diverse pseudo-anomalies generated through conditional latent diffusion can substitute for (and even surpass) limited real anomaly labels, as evidenced by AGDiff outperforming the semi-supervised iGAD on all large-scale imbalanced datasets. The joint generation-detection training loop provides a principled mechanism for iteratively refining the decision boundary, and the theoretical analysis (Appendix A) formalizes why classifier-based detection on pseudo-anomalies dominates pure reconstruction-based detection. Key limitations include the assumption of a representative normal graph distribution and restriction to static graphs; open questions involve extending the framework to dynamic/heterogeneous graph settings and exploring adaptive noise schedules that go beyond the fixed cosine schedule.
