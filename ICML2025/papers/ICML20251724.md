## Problem

Whether multimodal large language models (MLLMs) can perform abstract visual reasoning—discovering and articulating shared concepts from a small set of visual examples—remains largely unexplored. Bongard Problems (BPs) are a natural testbed, requiring both visual perception and natural-language articulation of an underlying rule, yet no systematic evaluation of MLLMs on BPs existed. A further open question was whether MLLMs' poor performance on synthetic geometric BPs reflected a domain-specific limitation or a fundamental gap in abstract reasoning.

## Input / Output

Each Bongard Problem instance is a 2×6 grid of images (left and right sides, six panels each), presented either as a single composite matrix image or as individual panels fed sequentially to the model. The model produces a free-form natural-language description of the distinguishing concept, which is then scored by an ensemble of four proprietary MLLMs using hard voting (correct if at least 2 of 4 agree). Three binary classification formulations further probe capabilities: verifying a ground-truth or incorrect concept label against the matrix, and assigning two shuffled test images to the correct side of the problem.

## Method

The paper proposes seven prompting strategies organized along two axes—Descriptive (caption each panel independently, then reason over the collected text) and Contrastive (describe differences between corresponding left-right image pairs)—each with iterative (shared context window) and direct (include the full matrix image) variants. To disentangle domain effects from reasoning limitations, the authors introduce Bongard-RWR, a 60-instance dataset that re-expresses synthetic BP concepts using real-world images sourced via the Pexels API and filtered by GPT-4o. All eight MLLMs (four proprietary, four open-access) are evaluated zero-shot across four datasets in both generative and binary classification settings. The key finding is that the Descriptive strategy consistently outperforms Contrastive and Direct approaches, revealing that current MLLMs reason better over individual image captions than over joint multi-image comparisons, and that weak performance on synthetic BPs persists on Bongard-RWR, indicating a general abstract-reasoning limitation rather than a domain-specific one.

## Results

On the main language generation task, the best proprietary model (GPT-4o) solves only 17/100 synthetic BPs (Direct) and 22/100 (Descriptive-direct), while on Bongard-RWR the best model (Claude 3.5 Sonnet) achieves just 13/60 (21.7%) with the Descriptive strategy. Performance improves on real-world datasets: GPT-4 Turbo reaches 45/100 on Bongard HOI and 57/100 on Bongard-OpenWorld (both Descriptive). In binary classification (Images to Sides), proprietary models reach 94–99% on Bongard-OpenWorld, but on Bongard-RWR all models fall below random-guessing level, with Pixtral 12B leading at 26/60. Human participants on Bongard-RWR average 39.2/60 (65%), with the lowest human (23) exceeding the combined total of all 8 models (22).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among proprietary models, GPT-4o leads on synthetic BPs (17 Direct, 22 Descriptive-direct) and Bongard-OpenWorld (40 Direct), while GPT-4 Turbo dominates Bongard HOI (45 Descriptive) and OpenWorld (57 Descriptive); Gemini 1.5 Pro is best on synthetic Descriptive (21) and Contrastive (17), and Claude 3.5 Sonnet leads on Bongard-RWR (13 Descriptive, 21.7%). All four proprietary models substantially outperform open-access models, winning 35 of 40 (dataset, strategy) pairs; Pixtral 12B is the strongest open model, reaching 28/100 on HOI and 33/100 on OpenWorld (Direct) and 99% on OpenWorld Ground-Truth binary classification, but still scores only 1/60 on Bongard-RWR (Direct). Compared to the concurrent work of Wu et al. (2024), whose SNAIL meta-learning method achieves 64% on Bongard-OpenWorld Images-to-Sides, the best MLLMs here (Gemini 1.5 Pro, Claude 3.5 Sonnet) reach 96%, showing that unified MLLM processing outperforms decoupled caption-then-reason pipelines. On Bongard-RWR, the combined total of all 8 models (22 problems solved across all strategies) falls below the single lowest human participant (23), underscoring a clear capability gap in abstract visual reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Bongard Problems tradition (Bongard, 1968/1970) and its modern benchmarks (Bongard-LOGO, Bongard HOI, Bongard-OpenWorld) by shifting the solver from dedicated CNN/meta-learning architectures to off-the-shelf multimodal large language models. It departs from the concurrent work of Wüst et al. (2024), which also evaluated MLLMs on synthetic BPs, by introducing the Bongard-RWR dataset that maps synthetic concepts onto real-world images, enabling a controlled domain comparison. The diverse prompting strategies (Direct, Descriptive, Contrastive, and their iterative/direct variants) build on the few-shot and chain-of-thought prompting literature but are specifically designed to probe multi-image compositional reasoning in the BP setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that MLLMs' poor performance on synthetic BPs is not a domain-specific artifact but reflects a fundamental limitation in abstract visual reasoning—challenges the common assumption that real-world images should be easier for vision-pretrained models. The Bongard-RWR dataset provides a controlled, reusable benchmark for isolating reasoning ability from visual domain effects, and the human study (65% average accuracy vs. ~13% best model) quantifies a substantial and persistent gap. Open questions include whether explicit AVR training data, improved multi-image context utilization, or ensemble strategies can close this gap, and whether the observed failure of iterative reasoning (context worsening performance) reflects a fundamental architectural limitation or a prompting issue.
