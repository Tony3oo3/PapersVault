## Problem

Supervised contrastive learning defines positive and negative pairs using exact class labels, but real-world annotations are often imprecise—manifesting as noisy labels or partial label sets that cannot reliably indicate whether two samples share the same class. This gap limits the applicability of supervised contrastive learning in practical weakly-supervised settings, where existing methods either ignore the weak supervision entirely or handle it through ad-hoc heuristics without a unified theoretical framework.

## Input / Output

The method consumes two augmented views per training sample (e.g., via random cropping, color distortion, Gaussian blur) along with a weakly-supervised label: either a single noisy class label (NLL) or a candidate label set containing the true label among distractors (PLL). It outputs a d-dimensional feature embedding per sample, evaluated downstream via a linear probe classifier. The weakly-supervised label is not used to form discrete positive/negative pairs but instead feeds into a continuous semantic similarity matrix S that modulates the strength of attraction between sample pairs in the contrastive objective.

## Method

The paper introduces a graph-theoretic framework where augmented samples form vertices and edge weights encode a continuous semantic similarity derived from weakly-supervised information, replacing the binary positive/negative distinction of standard contrastive learning. The key object is a recovery matrix S(x) ∈ R^{c×v} satisfying P(y|x) = S(x)P(q|x), which can be instantiated via transition-matrix inversion (instance-independent setting) or self-labeling from current model predictions (instance-dependent setting). Minimizing the Frobenius norm between the normalized adjacency matrix and the embedding outer product yields a closed-form contrastive loss L_wsc = −2αL₁ − 2βL₂ + (α+β/c)²L₃ under uniform class priors, where L₁ pulls augmented views of the same image together, L₂ pulls different images together with weights equal to their estimated semantic similarity, and L₃ regularizes against feature collapse. The framework is plug-and-play: any existing weakly-supervised method that estimates S can be combined with the WSC loss to improve representation quality, and the paper provides a linear-probe error bound showing the approach approximates fully-supervised contrastive learning under mild conditions on graph connectivity.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with 90% symmetric label noise, WSC achieves 90.93% top-1 accuracy, outperforming the previous best TCL (89.40%) and GFWS (84.22%). On CIFAR-100 with 90% symmetric noise, WSC reaches 61.32%, surpassing TCL (54.50%) by 6.85 percentage points. For partial label learning on CIFAR-100 with a 0.3 partial ratio, WSC achieves 69.15%, exceeding the previous best PiCO (61.35%) by 7.8 percentage points. Across all tested settings on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and CUB-200, WSC consistently matches or exceeds the strongest baselines, with the largest gains appearing under high noise and high partial-label rates.

## Baselines & Comparisons

WSC outperforms all 15 baselines on 8 out of 9 NLL settings, with the sole exception being CIFAR-10 at 50% symmetric noise where GFWS leads (96.60 vs. 95.79). The advantage grows sharply with noise level: at 90% symmetric noise on CIFAR-100, WSC (61.32%) beats TCL (54.50%) by 6.85 points and GFWS (45.82%) by 15.5 points. Among contrastive-learning baselines (MOIT, Sel-CL, TCL), WSC dominates across every NLL setting, confirming that its continuous semantic-similarity formulation is more effective than discrete positive/negative pair constructions under label noise. For partial label learning, WSC wins on all 9 reported settings across CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and CUB-200, with the largest margin (7.8 points) on CIFAR-100 at partial ratio 0.3 over PiCO (69.15 vs. 61.35). GFWS and RCR are the strongest non-contrastive PLL baselines but are consistently outperformed by 1–9 points depending on the partial ratio. Overall, WSC's plug-and-play contrastive loss provides the most consistent gains in the hardest regimes (high noise, high partial ratio) where existing methods degrade most severely.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the graph-spectral view of contrastive learning from HaoChen et al. (2021), which frames self-supervised contrastive learning as spectral clustering on an augmentation graph, by introducing a perturbation term that encodes weakly-supervised class information as continuous edge weights. It generalizes the discrete positive/negative pair paradigm of supervised contrastive learning (Khosla et al., 2020) into a 'continuous semantic similarity' framework, where the strength of intra-class connections is estimated from noisy or partial labels via a recovery matrix S. Unlike prior contrastive methods for noisy labels (Sel-CL, TCL) or partial labels (PiCO) that design task-specific contrastive objectives, this work provides a single unified loss that subsumes both settings through the same graph-theoretic construction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The plug-and-play nature of the WSC loss—requiring only a construction of the recovery matrix S—makes it broadly applicable across weakly-supervised scenarios beyond the two studied (noisy and partial labels), with the authors noting potential extensions to bag-level supervision and multi-modal matching. The theoretical guarantee that the framework approximates fully-supervised contrastive learning under mild conditions provides a principled justification for the approach. Open questions include how the method scales to very high-dimensional label spaces (e.g., partial labels with many classes), the sensitivity to the perturbation coefficient β in practice, and whether the self-labeling-based S estimation remains reliable under extreme instance-dependent noise.
