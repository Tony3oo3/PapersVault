## Problem

Fine-tuning a pre-trained model on a new task often causes catastrophic forgetting of previously learned capabilities. Most existing mitigation methods require access to pre-training data or statistics (replay buffers, Fisher information, distillation targets), but in practice the pre-training data and recipe are typically unavailable. This paper tackles the 'data-oblivious' setting, where the only available information is the pre-trained model itself, and asks whether a principled method can mitigate forgetting under this constraint.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained model θ*, a fine-tuning dataset of n samples, and a temperature τ (set to the median of per-sample pre-trained losses, making the method effectively parameter-free). It produces a fine-tuned model by minimizing a weighted loss. The per-sample weights w_i = exp(−f_i(θ*)/τ) are computed once before fine-tuning and held static throughout training. For vision models with task-specific components (classification heads, batch-norm layers), a modified four-step procedure first trains the head on the frozen pre-trained body, then computes weights using the combined model, then fine-tunes the full model with the weighted loss, and finally re-trains the head on the fine-tuned body.

## Method

FLOW (Fine-tuning with Pre-trained Loss-Oriented Weighting) upweights samples on which the pre-trained model already performs well (low loss) and downweights hard samples, introducing a supervised bias in gradient updates that limits parameter drift from the pre-trained state. The weighting scheme w_i = exp(−f_i(θ*)/τ) is derived by minimizing a negative entropic regularization objective over the probability simplex, which simultaneously enforces monotonicity (lower pre-trained loss → higher weight) and spread (avoiding concentration on too few samples). This is the exact inverse of distributionally robust optimization (DRO), which upweights hard samples and would be conducive to forgetting. Theoretically, in a linear setting, FLOW's weighted covariance matrix has a small eigenvalue whose corresponding eigenvector aligns with the direction of maximal drift toward the fine-tuning optimum, effectively stalling convergence along that axis and impeding overfitting to the target task while preserving pre-training capabilities.

## Results

On vision (ResNet-50 pre-trained on ImageNet-1K), FLOW achieves 76.09% IN-1K accuracy (only a 2.93% drop from the pre-trained 79.02%) and 86.25% average target accuracy, yielding a 16.83% higher combined average than standard fine-tuning (64.35%). On language (Gemma 2 2B fine-tuned on MetaMathQA), FLOW achieves 49.98% average across commonsense, MMLU, MBPP, and GSM8K, compared to 46.31% for standard fine-tuning, while retaining 62.55% GSM8K (only 0.83% below standard FT's 63.38%). For Llama 3.2 3B, FLOW reaches 52.87% average versus 46.10% for standard fine-tuning. FLOW also improves upon WiSE-FT by 4.18–4.52% in average accuracy when combined (Table 3).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FLOW outperforms all four baselines on the combined average metric in both vision and language. In vision (ResNet-50), FLOW's 81.17% average beats linear probing (77.74%) by 3.44%, WiSE-FT (71.52%) by 9.65%, ℓ₂-regularization (68.18%) by 12.99%, and standard fine-tuning (64.35%) by 16.83%; linear probing is the closest competitor but sacrifices 15.33% target accuracy to avoid any forgetting. In language (Gemma 2 2B), FLOW's 49.98% average surpasses ℓ₂-regularization (49.19%) by 0.79%, WiSE-FT (47.60%) by 2.38%, LoRA (47.05%) by 2.93%, and standard FT (46.31%) by 3.67%, while maintaining GSM8K within 0.83% of standard FT. The same pattern holds for Llama 3.2 3B, where FLOW (52.87%) leads ℓ₂-regularization (52.30%) by 0.57% and standard FT (46.10%) by 6.77%. FLOW is also complementary: combining it with WiSE-FT improves average accuracy by 4.18–4.52% over WiSE-FT alone, and combining with LoRA boosts general capability by 1.07–3.40% while improving GSM8K by 1.06%.

## Relation to Prior Work

FLOW carves new ground in the data-oblivious catastrophic forgetting literature by shifting the intervention from parameter space (EWC, ℓ₂-regularization) or gradient space (orthogonal gradient descent) to sample space. Conceptually, it inverts the standard sample-weighting paradigm used to accelerate training (which upweights hard/high-loss samples) and instead upweights easy/low-loss samples, a connection the paper makes explicit through its contrast with distributionally robust optimization (DRO), whose optimal weighting is the exact reciprocal. The method is positioned as orthogonal and complementary to existing data-oblivious baselines like model averaging (WiSE-FT) and LoRA, and the theoretical analysis for linear models shows FLOW's trajectory goes beyond simple model averaging by introducing a component along a direction orthogonal to the task-difference vector.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FLOW offers a remarkably simple, parameter-free recipe (compute pre-trained per-sample losses, weight by exp(-loss/median_loss)) that is easy to adopt across architectures and modalities, and its complementarity with LoRA, ℓ₂-regularization, and WiSE-FT makes it a practical add-on rather than a replacement. The key limitation, acknowledged by the authors, is a significant performance sacrifice on hard fine-tuning samples (e.g., 30.7% vs. 86.6% on the top-10% hardest CIFAR-10 samples), which constrains its use when the downstream task is dominated by novel or difficult examples. Open questions include extending the linear-model theory to non-linear settings, adapting the weighting to reduce the hard-sample penalty, and exploring whether the approach transfers to continual learning with multiple sequential tasks.
