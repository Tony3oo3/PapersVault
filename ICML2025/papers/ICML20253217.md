## Problem

Diffusion-based symbolic music generation suffers from a fundamental precision gap: unlike image generation where a single misplaced pixel is negligible, a single out-of-key note is immediately perceptible and disruptive. The paper proves (Proposition 2) that the probability of unresolved out-of-key notes in generated samples decays only as O(n^{-1/(LH)}) with dataset size, making it practically impossible for models to self-ensure harmonic correctness given the scarcity of high-quality symbolic music data. No existing method provides a hard guarantee that every generated note respects the intended tonal framework.

## Input / Output

The model takes a chord progression (required) and optionally a rhythmic pattern and melody, all encoded as 2-channel (onset + sustain) piano roll matrices of shape 2×L×128 at 16th-note resolution (L=64 for a 4-measure, 4/4 piece). The harmonic condition uses a special encoding where in-chord pitches are marked −2 and out-of-chord pitches −1, while the combined harmonic+rhythmic condition uses 0/1 onset/sustain values. Output is a continuous 2×L×128 matrix thresholded at 0.5 to produce a binary piano roll, with a key signature constraint applied during sampling to enforce pitch-class correctness.

## Method

FGG combines fine-grained conditioning during training with a hard projection during sampling. Training uses a 2D UNet with spatial attention under a classifier-free guidance scheme that randomly drops rhythmic conditions, enabling flexible conditioning at inference. The key sampling-time mechanism is an element-wise noise correction: at each denoising step, the predicted noise ε_θ is projected so that the implied X_0 prediction has no values above 0.5 at out-of-key positions, solved in closed form as a max operation per element. This exploits the fact that each piano-roll position maps to a fixed time-pitch pair (unlike image pixels), enabling per-position constraint enforcement. Proposition 1 provides KL-divergence upper bounds via Girsanov's theorem, showing the controlled distribution remains close to the uncontrolled one, while Proposition 2 quantifies why uncontrolled models cannot self-correct out-of-key errors at practical dataset sizes.

## Results

On the POP909 accompaniment generation task (4-measure, 16th-note resolution), FGG achieves 0.0% out-of-key notes, 0.485 direct chord accuracy, 0.767 chord progression similarity, 0.769 IoU(Chord), and 0.281 IoU(Piano Roll). In subjective evaluation across 5 dimensions (creativity, harmony, melodiousness, naturalness, richness), FGG consistently outperforms both baselines. Generation of a 4-measure accompaniment takes 0.4 seconds on an NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada GPU using 10 DDIM steps.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FGG dominates both baselines across every quantitative metric. Against WholeSongGen (Wang et al., 2024), FGG reduces out-of-key notes from 2.1% to 0.0%, improves direct chord accuracy from 0.314 to 0.485 (+54%), chord similarity from 0.611 to 0.767 (+26%), and IoU(Piano Roll) from 0.107 to 0.281 (+163%). Against GETMusic (Lv et al., 2023), the margins are even larger: out-of-key notes drop from 3.5% to 0.0%, direct chord accuracy rises from 0.153 to 0.485 (+217%), and IoU(Piano Roll) increases from 0.048 to 0.281 (+485%). In the subjective listening test with 31 experienced musicians, FGG outperforms both baselines on all five quality dimensions (creativity, harmony, melodiousness, naturalness, and overall), with the largest gaps in harmony and naturalness. The ablation study further confirms that the sampling-time noise correction (not just training conditioning) is the primary driver of the out-of-key note elimination and the IoU gains over post-hoc rule-based editing or inpainting alternatives.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the DDPM/DDIM diffusion framework and classifier-free guidance for conditional generation, but departs from prior constrained-diffusion approaches (gradient-based guidance à la Chung et al. 2023, projection/reflection à la Lou & Ermon 2023) by designing an element-wise noise correction that directly projects the predicted X_0 onto a key-constrained domain at each sampling step. The key insight enabling this is the structural difference between piano rolls and images: each piano-roll position has a fixed time-pitch meaning from the outset, allowing per-element constraints rather than global or gradient-based ones. The theoretical analysis draws on the SDE formulation of Song et al. (2021b) and Girsanov's theorem to bound distributional distortion, while the statistical lower bound on out-of-key note errors (Proposition 2) adapts minimax estimation theory from Fu et al. (2024) to the discrete-thresholded piano-roll setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, theoretically grounded solution to a practical pain point in symbolic music generation: even a single out-of-key note is perceptually jarring, and the slow statistical convergence rate (O(n^{-1/(LH+2)})) means models cannot reliably self-ensure precision with available data sizes. The FGG method is modular, adds negligible computational overhead (0.4s for 4 measures), and can be plugged into existing diffusion-based music generators. Open questions include extending beyond 16th-note quantization to capture expressive timing, supporting probabilistic or abstract harmonic control, and developing automatic evaluation metrics that better reflect perceptual musicality. The method's reliance on tonic-centric key signatures also limits applicability to atonal or highly chromatic genres.
