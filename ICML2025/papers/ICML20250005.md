## Problem

GNNs struggle to generalize to unseen or out-of-distribution graphs, especially when training data is limited in size or diversity. Existing graph augmentation methods (DropEdge, G-Mixup, GeoMix, etc.) lack a principled theoretical characterization of how augmentation affects the generalization bound, making it unclear which strategies genuinely improve generalization versus those that merely add noise. This paper derives a Rademacher-complexity-based regret bound that explicitly quantifies the effect of augmentation and uses it to motivate a targeted augmentation strategy.

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard graph inputs (adjacency matrix A ∈ R^{p×p} and node feature matrix X ∈ R^{p×d}) and outputs a graph-level class label from a set of C categories. Unusually, augmentation does not modify the graph structure or node features directly; instead, it operates on the learned graph-level hidden representations produced by the GNN's readout function. The GMM is fitted per-class on these d-dimensional embeddings, and new augmented representations are sampled from the fitted distributions before fine-tuning only the post-readout MLP.

## Method

GRATIN is a two-stage training procedure: (1) a standard GNN (GCN or GIN) is trained end-to-end on the original training graphs, and (2) the trained message-passing layers and readout function are used to extract graph-level embeddings, on which a per-class GMM is fitted via the EM algorithm. New augmented representations are sampled from each class-conditional GMM and concatenated with the original embeddings, after which only the lightweight post-readout function is fine-tuned for classification. The theoretical motivation comes from Theorem 3.1, which shows that the generalization regret is bounded by a term proportional to the expected distance between original and augmented representations in the hidden space; GMMs are chosen because their exponential tails keep this distance well-controlled while their universal approximation property (Theorem 3.2) ensures sufficient diversity. A Fisher-guided filtering step (Theorem 3.3) further refines the augmented set by computing influence-function scores on a validation split and discarding representations with negative or negligible influence on test performance.

## Results

On the GIN backbone, GRATIN achieves the best test accuracy on 3 out of 5 datasets: MUTAG (88.83%), PROTEINS (71.33%), and IMDB-MUL (49.20%), outperforming the strongest baselines by 1.06, 0.89, and 0.67 percentage points respectively. On the GCN backbone, GRATIN leads on PROTEINS (70.97%) and DD (71.90%), while remaining competitive on IMDB-MUL (49.82%, tied with GeoMix). In the robustness experiment against 20% edge corruption, GRATIN achieves the highest accuracy across all four tested datasets (e.g., 73.10% on IMDB-BIN, 70.32% on PROTEINS), demonstrating superior OOD generalization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GRATIN outperforms G-Mixup on MUTAG (88.83% vs 87.77% with GIN) and PROTEINS (71.33% vs 68.82%), and on DD with GCN (71.90% vs 61.20%), where G-Mixup suffers a dramatic drop. Against GeoMix, GRATIN wins on PROTEINS (71.33% vs 69.80% GIN; 70.97% vs 69.63% GCN) and DD (GCN: 71.90% vs 68.50%), while GeoMix edges ahead on IMDB-BIN (GCN: 69.69% vs 71.00% — actually GRATIN is lower here). Compared to DropEdge and DropNode, GRATIN consistently achieves higher accuracy on PROTEINS and DD across both backbones, though DropNode wins on IMDB-BIN (GCN: 74.00% vs 71.00%) and DD (GIN: 69.01% vs 68.61%). SubMix is the closest competitor on IMDB-BIN with GIN (72.50% vs 71.70%) but underperforms GRATIN on MUTAG, PROTEINS, and DD. Overall, GRATIN achieves the best or second-best result on 8 out of 10 dataset-backbone combinations, with its largest margins on MUTAG and PROTEINS where structural augmentation baselines struggle most.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Rademacher complexity framework for GNN generalization (Yin et al., 2019; Esser et al., 2021) and influence functions (Koh & Liang, 2017) to derive a regret bound that explicitly characterizes how augmentation distance affects generalization. It departs from existing graph augmentation methods (DropEdge, DropNode, G-Mixup, GeoMix) that operate in the graph input space by shifting augmentation to the learned hidden representation space, making the strategy architecture-specific. The use of GMMs as universal density approximators (Goodfellow et al., 2016) for generating augmented representations, combined with Fisher-guided filtering via influence functions, represents a principled departure from heuristic structural perturbations or graphon-based interpolation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theoretical regret bound provides a concrete criterion—controlling the expected distance between original and augmented representations—for designing effective augmentation, which is a useful principle beyond this specific method. The finding that augmentation effectiveness is architecture-dependent (e.g., GIN on DD suffers Softmax saturation rendering augmentation ineffective) highlights an underappreciated limitation of model-agnostic augmentation strategies. However, the evaluation is limited to small benchmark datasets (IMDB, MUTAG, PROTEINS, DD), and the two-stage training protocol (freeze message passing, fine-tune post-readout) constrains the method's applicability; whether GRATIN scales to larger datasets or extends to node-level tasks remains open.
