## Problem

In multi-agent linear bandits, determining when agents should share observations to reduce estimation uncertainty without introducing harmful bias from heterogeneous parameters remains an open challenge. Existing approaches rely on Euclidean distance for similarity and typically assume a known or recoverable cluster structure, ignoring the anisotropic nature of regret minimization where only directions with large rewards matter. This paper asks: can we adaptively decide when to share samples based on a bias-versus-uncertainty trade-off, without any structural assumption on the bandit parameters?

## Input / Output

Each agent receives a finite set of arm/context vectors in R^d (unit-norm bounded) and observes R-sub-Gaussian noisy rewards; in the multi-agent setting, a central controller coordinates arm pulls and collects observations from all agents. The algorithm outputs, at each iteration, an arm-pull decision per agent (via OFUL), a dynamic similarity graph G_t whose edges indicate which agents currently share data, and both local and collaborative OLS parameter estimates. A key structural property is that the graph is monotonically shrinking: once two agents' confidence ellipsoids separate, the edge is permanently removed and they never re-merge.

## Method

BASS (Bandit Adaptive Sample Sharing) builds on OFUL but replaces the fixed neighborhood with a dynamically updated similarity graph. At each iteration, agents share observations with neighbors whose confidence ellipsoids still overlap, where overlap is tested via a γ-relaxed ellipsoid separation function Ψ(i,j,t) computed using the Mahalanobis distance induced by the shared design matrix A_t. The core insight is that collaboration is beneficial precisely when the parameter gap ||θ_i*−θ_j*||_{A_t} is below (2−√2)β(δ,A_t), i.e., when the introduced bias is smaller than a fraction of the current estimation uncertainty. Because the metric is anisotropic (weighted by A_t rather than the identity), the test focuses on directions that actually affect regret, unlike prior Euclidean-based methods. The paper derives matching lower and upper bounds on the separation time, cumulative regret during the collaboration phase (showing a collaborative gain factor μ<1 relative to single-agent), and, under a clustered-parameter assumption, a regret bound of O(√d/√(ρ_min·N) · √T·log(T/d)) that is tighter than all concurrent methods.

## Results

On the synthetic benchmark (M=3 clusters, N=100 agents, T=50000), BASS achieves a cumulative regret of 1096.8 (δ=0.1) and 1563.2 (δ=0.9) for the harder setting (Δ_min=0.2), compared to 6100.7 for independent agents and 318.5 for the Oracle. On real data, BASS (δ=0.1) achieves 10273.2 on MovieLens and 12456.0 on Yahoo, outperforming the independent baseline (11825.2 and 14800.5) and all state-of-the-art clustering methods. BASS also achieves the best F1 clustering score (0.7–0.9) on synthetic data, while most competing methods score 0.0.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BASS substantially outperforms all four state-of-the-art baselines on both synthetic and real data. Against DynUCB (Nguyen & Lauw, 2014), BASS reduces cumulative regret from 5765.5 to 1096.8 (Δ=0.2) on synthetic data and from 16835.0 to 10273.2 on MovieLens. Against CLUB (Gentile et al., 2014), BASS achieves roughly 5× lower regret on the harder synthetic setting (1096.8 vs. 5772.5) and ~12% lower on MovieLens (10273.2 vs. 11504.2). Against SCLUB (Li et al., 2019), BASS wins by a similar margin on both settings. Against CMLB (Ghosh et al., 2022), BASS is particularly dominant on Yahoo (12456.0 vs. 14944.2–16812.5) and on the harder synthetic setting where CMLB(γ=Δ/2) degrades to 20948.5. Notably, BASS also achieves the best F1 clustering score (0.7–0.9), while CLUB, SCLUB, and CMLB all score 0.0 on the harder synthetic setting, indicating they fail to recover the true cluster structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the OFUL optimistic linear bandit framework (Abbasi-Yadkori et al., 2011) and adapts the ellipsoid overlap test from Kalman filter fault detection (Gilitschenski & Hanebeck, 2012) to the multi-agent bandit setting. It departs from the dominant line of multi-agent bandit work (CLUB, SCLUB, CMLB, DynUCB) in two key ways: (1) it replaces the Euclidean similarity metric with a Mahalanobis distance weighted by the design matrix, making the collaboration criterion anisotropic and aligned with regret-relevant directions, and (2) it removes the assumption of a pre-existing cluster structure, instead learning when to share adaptively via a bias-uncertainty tradeoff. The conceptual unification—showing that the collaboration condition (Eq. 1) is equivalent to an ellipsoid separation test—provides a principled stopping rule absent from prior clustering-based approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to formally characterize the bias-variance tradeoff of sample sharing in multi-agent linear bandits and to derive the tightest known regret upper bounds under clustered parameters, including the first analysis of clustering error. The anisotropic (Mahalanobis) criterion is more appropriate than Euclidean distance for regret minimization, as it focuses on high-reward directions. Open questions include whether the synchronous pulling and central controller assumptions can be relaxed for truly distributed settings, and whether arm-pulling strategies can be co-designed to accelerate similarity estimation. The practical impact is relevant to recommendation systems and wireless network optimization where agents with similar but not identical parameters can benefit from adaptive collaboration.
