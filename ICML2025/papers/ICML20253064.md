## Problem

Global climate cooperation is a profoundly challenging game-theoretic problem: diverse regions with conflicting incentives must negotiate and sustain agreements without a central enforcing authority. Existing integrated assessment models (IAMs) like DICE and RICE rely on exogenously fixed policies and lack the strategic, multi-agent dynamics needed to study how negotiation protocols actually shape climate-economic outcomes. The game-theoretic aspects of climate cooperation using machine learning and calibrated IAMs had remained unexplored prior to this work.

## Input / Output

The simulation takes a calibrated initial world state across 27 fictitious regions (population, capital, technology factor, carbon intensity, land-use emissions) plus global CO₂ and temperature, and a chosen negotiation protocol specification. Each 5-year timestep, agents receive agent-specific observations (their own state, negotiation history, minimum mitigation rate) alongside public global state, and output discrete actions: savings rate, mitigation rate, import tariffs, export limits, and import bids. Over a ~200-year horizon, the system produces climate-economic trajectories (temperature anomaly, carbon emissions, GDP, consumption), Gini fairness indices for abatement cost, mitigation rate, emissions, and consumption, and the learned policies themselves.

## Method

RICE-N is a modular IAM built on RICE/DICE dynamics (three-reservoir carbon cycle, two-layer energy balance, Cobb-Douglas production with climate damage and mitigation costs, Armington-elasticity trade with tariffs) augmented with a negotiation layer. Multi-agent reinforcement learning (A2C) trains 27 agents with a shared-weight neural network policy that uses agent-specific inputs (one-hot region index, local state) plus public state to select actions endogenously, replacing the handcrafted policies of traditional IAMs. Two negotiation protocols are implemented: Bilateral Negotiation (each agent proposes and requests mitigation rates pairwise, then commits to the maximum of accepted proposals) and Basic Club (each agent proposes a single club mitigation rate; accepted proposals form clubs, and non-members with lower rates face tariffs equal to the rate difference, mirroring CBAM). Binding commitments are enforced through action masks that restrict the accessible mitigation action space in the subsequent activity step. The key finding is that both protocols reduce temperature growth to near-maximal-mitigation levels while incurring only a minor production drop and substantially lowering the Gini index of abatement costs compared to the no-negotiation baseline.

## Results

At the final time step (year 2115), Basic Club reduces global temperature anomaly to 3.42°C (vs. 5.12°C for no negotiation) while maintaining output production of 235.1 USD trillion (vs. 233.6 for no negotiation) and reducing carbon emissions to 534.7 GtC (vs. 1697.2 GtC). Bilateral Negotiation achieves the lowest temperature at 3.34°C and the lowest carbon emissions at 487.0 GtC, but at a greater economic cost (output drops to 209.4 USD trillion). Both protocols dramatically reduce inequality: the Gini index for abatement cost drops from 0.695 (no negotiation) to ~0.33, and the Gini for mitigation rate drops from 0.405 to 0.011 (Bilateral) or 0.030 (Basic Club).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three negotiation protocols (None, Bilateral, Basic Club) against two fixed-policy baselines (minimum and maximum mitigation) shown in Figure 3. Both Bilateral Negotiation and Basic Club dramatically outperform the no-negotiation and minimum-mitigation baselines on climate metrics: temperature anomaly drops from ~5.1°C to ~3.3–3.4°C, and carbon emissions fall from ~1697 GtC to ~487–535 GtC, approaching the maximum-mitigation upper bound. Economically, Basic Club matches or slightly exceeds the no-negotiation baseline in output (235.1 vs. 233.6 USD trillion) and consumption (8.55 vs. 8.96), while Bilateral Negotiation sacrifices more output (209.4) for marginally better climate outcomes. On fairness, both protocols roughly halve the Gini index for abatement cost (from 0.695 to ~0.33) and reduce the mitigation-rate Gini by over 90% (from 0.405 to 0.011–0.030), indicating far more equitable burden sharing. The authors note that Bilateral Negotiation's superior climate performance partly stems from its commitment mechanism taking the maximum of random early proposals, making it less realistic than Basic Club, which is grounded in actual CBAM and climate-club policy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the RICE/DICE integrated assessment model lineage (Nordhaus & Yang, 1996; Nordhaus, 2018) by replacing exogenous, handcrafted agent policies with endogenously learned policies via multi-agent reinforcement learning (A2C). It draws on the climate clubs concept (Nordhaus, 2015) and the EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (Commission, 2022) to design the Basic Club negotiation protocol, and on Lessmann et al. (2009) for the trade and tariff dynamics. The key departure from prior work is the explicit combination of MARL with a calibrated, multi-region climate-economic simulation: previous game-theoretic climate models (prisoner's dilemmas, bargaining games) lacked multilateral settings, agent heterogeneity, and evolving dynamics, while prior IAMs assumed fixed policies rather than strategic, utility-maximizing agents.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

RICE-N provides the first simulation platform where strategic MARL agents interact within a calibrated climate-economic model with explicit negotiation protocols, enabling researchers to test how different institutional designs (bilateral vs. club-based) affect long-term climate and economic outcomes. The finding that the Basic Club protocol achieves near-maximal-mitigation climate outcomes with only a minor production drop, while improving equity in abatement costs (Gini index), offers a concrete policy-relevant signal. However, significant open questions remain: the negotiation process is a black box lacking interpretability, the model does not capture regional damage disparities or within-region inequality (limiting climate justice analysis), commitments are fully binding (unlike real-world agreements where defection is possible), and the 27-region setup is fictitious. Future work on non-binding commitments, multi-level RL for COP-style negotiations, and LLM-enhanced interpretability could substantially strengthen the framework's policy relevance.
