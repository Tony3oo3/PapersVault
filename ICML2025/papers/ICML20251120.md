## Problem

Existing CFR-based algorithms for imperfect-information games compute only the Nash Equilibrium, always converging to the same equilibrium point regardless of initialization, and offer no mechanism to produce strategies with distinct play styles. This limits AI adaptability to diverse user preferences (e.g., aggressive vs. loose-passive) and ignores the practical value of equilibrium selection in multi-NE games.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251120/cropped_page6_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an extensive-form game tree plus two user-specified parameters: a preference degree δ(I,a) ≥ 1 assigned per action per information set (typically set uniformly across all information sets for a given action type, e.g., all Raise actions), and a vulnerability degree β(I) bounding the maximum tolerable exploitability. It outputs a behavioral strategy distribution over actions at each information set that constitutes a stylized ε-NE with ε ≤ β, aligned with the desired play style. In practice, δ values up to 5 maintain acceptable convergence speed, and β is calibrated to game-specific error margins (e.g., 25 mBB/h in Texas Hold'em).

## Method

Pref-CFR modifies the standard CFR regret-matching update by inserting two parameters into the strategy computation. The preference degree δ(I,a) multiplies the positive counterfactual regret, biasing the next-iteration strategy toward preferred actions (Pref-CFR(BR) uses argmax of δ·R̄, while Pref-CFR(RM) uses proportional allocation of δ·R̄⁺). The vulnerability degree β shifts the regret threshold from R̄ to B̄ = R̄ − β, effectively moving the target cone from R ≤ 0 to R ≤ β so the algorithm converges to an ε-NE with ε ≤ β rather than a strict NE. Convergence is proven via Blackwell approachability: the weighted forcing action still satisfies the forceable half-space condition, yielding a convergence rate of O(δ*·L·|A|/√T) where δ* = max δ(a). The key insight is that δ alone steers convergence to different NEs when multiple exist, while β is necessary to produce meaningful style differentiation in games with a unique NE by expanding the acceptable strategy space.

## Results

In two-player Texas Hold'em, Pref-CFR-trained Aggressive, Normal, and Loose-Passive AIs show performance differences within 10 mBB/h (90% CI ±1.0), confirming comparable skill levels. Strategy distributions shift dramatically: the Loose-Passive AI reduces fold probability from 5.4% to 0.3% (a 94.4% decrease), while the Aggressive AI increases raise-to-3 probability from 0% to 21.1%. In three-player Texas Hold'em, the Loose-Passive strategy actually gains +0.2 mBB/h over the Normal strategy while increasing calling probability from 0.0% to 23.9%, and the Aggressive AI raises its 3-bet rate from 0.0% to 16.0% with only a ~10 mBB/h decrement. Convergence rates of Pref-CFR remain comparable to original CFR across all experiments.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is vanilla CFR (and its ES-MCCFR variant in Leduc poker). Pref-CFR does not aim to outperform CFR in raw exploitability; rather, it matches CFR's performance while producing qualitatively distinct strategies. In Kuhn poker, original CFR always converges to α=0.2 regardless of initialization, whereas Pref-CFR(BR) with δ=5 or δ=10 shifts the equilibrium to different α values at comparable convergence speed. In Leduc poker, ES-MCCFR and Pref-ES-MCCFR converge to the same strategy when β=0 (suggesting a unique NE), but introducing β>0 allows Pref-CFR to stably deviate with controllable exploitability. In Texas Hold'em, all three Pref-CFR style variants (Aggressive, Normal, Loose-Passive) remain within 10 mBB/h of each other in head-to-head matches, well below the 32 mBB/h that Pluribus achieved against top human players, confirming that the stylistic modifications do not meaningfully degrade play strength.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Regret Minimization / Counterfactual Regret Minimization (CFR) lineage by inserting two new parameters—preference degree δ and vulnerability degree β—directly into the regret-matching update rule, thereby steering convergence toward a user-specified equilibrium within a tolerance band. The analytical framework draws on the Generalized Weakened Fictitious Play (GWFP) formulation from Fudenberg & Levine (1998), which the authors use to show that RM is a special case of GWFP and to justify modifying the sub-best-response gap ε_t as the mechanism for introducing β. The convergence proof is grounded in Blackwell approachability, establishing that Pref-CFR retains the same O(T^{-1/2}) rate as vanilla CFR while reaching a different fixed point. The paper departs from prior CFR variants (CFR+, MCCFR, DCFR) that all target a single NE, by explicitly framing the problem as equilibrium selection among a continuum of valid equilibria.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The work addresses a genuine gap: existing imperfect-information game solvers compute one equilibrium and stop, ignoring that games like Texas Hold'em admit families of equilibria with qualitatively different play styles. The practical implication—training AI opponents that are aggressive or loose-passive while maintaining near-NE performance—opens a path toward more engaging and diverse human-AI interaction. Key limitations include the need for manual, expert-calibrated δ and β values at each information set, and the lack of an automated mapping from macro-level style descriptors (e.g., 'bet 30% of hands') to micro-level per-infoset parameters. Open questions include whether the framework extends to games without rich expert heuristics, and whether the discovered 'novel' strategies (e.g., raising with 82o) generalize beyond the specific hand distributions tested.
