## Problem

Existing causal inference methods for perturbation modeling either assume the atomic intervention targets are known a priori or infer them independently per perturbation without leveraging the perturbation's observable features. This prevents them from predicting the effects of unseen perturbations, while unstructured black-box models that do generalize lack mechanistic interpretability. The paper addresses this gap by asking: can we learn a causal model and a shared mapping from perturbation features to atomic interventions jointly, enabling both generalization to novel perturbations and mechanistic explanations of their effects?

## Input / Output

The method takes a dataset of K perturbation environments, each consisting of a data matrix X^(k) of i.i.d. samples (e.g., gene expression vectors) and a perturbation feature vector γ^(k) (e.g., drug identity, dosage, molecular properties). At test time, given an unseen γ*, it produces samples from the posterior predictive distribution p(x|D; γ*) by marginalizing over the learned intervention distribution, alongside the inferred causal graph G, intervention targets I, and intervention parameters ψ. The causal mechanisms are parameterized as linear Gaussian or MLP conditionals, and interventions are modeled as Bernoulli target indicators with Gaussian effect parameters, with the perturbation features serving as the conditioning input to the GIM MLPs.

## Method

GIMs introduce a shared generative intervention model — two MLPs g_φ and h_φ — that map perturbation features γ to a distribution over intervention targets (Bernoulli) and parameters (Gaussian) within a jointly-learned structural causal model. The causal graph is represented continuously via a latent matrix Z (following DiBS) to enable differentiable MAP inference, with the NO-BEARS acyclicity constraint enforced through an augmented Lagrangian. Training optimizes the joint log-likelihood over all K environments using Monte Carlo integration over both the graph distribution p(G|Z) and the intervention distribution p(I; γ, φ), with Gumbel-sigmoid reparameterization for discrete variables and the reparameterization trick for continuous ones. The key architectural insight is that by amortizing intervention inference through a single shared network conditioned on γ, the model can generalize to unseen perturbation features while simultaneously recovering the underlying causal structure and the specific atomic mechanisms each perturbation modifies — a capability no prior causal or black-box method provides jointly.

## Results

On nonlinear Gaussian SCMs, GIMs significantly outperform all causal baselines (BaCaDI*, GnIES, UT-IGSP, JCI-PC) on both causal structure recovery (SID, Edge-F1) and intervention target identification (Target-F1), and are the only method capable of making predictions for fully out-of-distribution perturbations. On fully OOD perturbations, GIMs achieve W2 and Mean Distance scores on par with the best black-box baseline (MLP) and substantially better than CondOT. On the SciPlex3 scRNA-seq drug perturbation data, GIMs achieve the lowest Mean Distance and highest Pearson Correlation among all methods, and qualitatively capture the zero-inflated marginal distributions of top marker genes more accurately than the MLP baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GIMs outperform all four causal inference baselines (BaCaDI*, GnIES, UT-IGSP, JCI-PC) on nonlinear systems across both causal discovery (SID, Edge-F1) and intervention target identification (Target-F1), with the exception that UT-IGSP achieves the best Target-F1 in linear settings. Critically, GIMs are the only mechanistic method that can generalize to unseen perturbation features; all causal baselines are limited to in-distribution predictions. On fully OOD perturbations, GIMs match the MLP black-box baseline on W2 and Mean Distance, while substantially outperforming CondOT, which degrades significantly out-of-distribution. On the SciPlex3 real-world data, GIMs achieve the best Mean Distance and Pearson Correlation, with the MLP as a close second; GIMs additionally capture the zero-inflated structure of gene expression distributions qualitatively better than the MLP, which merely shifts the observational mean. The JCI-PC (Context) variant, which adds perturbation features as graph nodes, performs on par with standard JCI-PC, indicating that merely including features without modeling their mechanistic role is insufficient for improving causal discovery.

## Relation to Prior Work

GIMs extend the line of differentiable causal discovery from interventional data (Brouillard et al., 2020; Hägele et al., 2023) by introducing an amortized generative model that maps perturbation features to atomic interventions, rather than inferring each intervention independently. The continuous graph representation from DiBS (Lorch et al., 2021) and the NO-BEARS acyclicity constraint provide the optimization backbone, while the Bernoulli/Gaussian intervention parameterization follows BaCaDI. The key conceptual departure is that prior causal methods treat perturbation features as absent or as fixed context nodes, making them unable to predict effects of unseen perturbations; GIMs instead learn a shared function from features to interventions, bridging the gap between structured causal models and unstructured black-box predictors like CondOT.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GIMs close a practical gap: causal models provide interpretable mechanistic explanations but were previously restricted to seen perturbations, while black-box models generalize but offer no insight into the data-generating process. The finding that merely adding perturbation features as graph nodes (JCI-PC Context) does not improve causal discovery over the feature-free variant suggests that explicit mechanistic modeling of how features drive interventions is necessary, not just their inclusion. Open questions include whether the MAP approximation suffices or a fully Bayesian treatment is needed, how GIMs scale to high-dimensional systems (beyond d=20 or 50 genes), and whether the approach can be used for active experiment design to select the most informative next perturbation.
