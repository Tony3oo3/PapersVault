## Problem

Existing EHR tokenizers treat the 600,000+ medical codes across eight coding systems (ICD-9/10, SNOMED CT, ATC, NDC, CPT, RxNorm) as isolated textual tokens, discarding the hierarchical ontological structure, disease co-occurrences, and drug-treatment relationships that are critical for clinical reasoning. No unified tokenizer integrates both the textual definitions and the graph-based relational context of medical codes into a single discrete representation suitable for transformer-based foundation models.

## Input / Output

For each medical code, MEDTOK consumes two inputs: a natural-language description (sourced from official coding documents and enriched via GPT-4) and a node-centered subgraph extracted from the PrimeKG biomedical knowledge graph (mapped via UMLS CUIs, MONDO ontology, and DrugBank for drug codes). The output is a sequence of K discrete token IDs drawn from a unified codebook of size N (12,000–24,000), where the codebook is partitioned into text-specific, graph-specific, and shared regions; these tokens can be plugged as vocabulary items into any transformer-based EHR model or LLM pipeline.

## Method

MEDTOK encodes the text description with a frozen language-model encoder and the knowledge subgraph with a trainable graph encoder, then fuses the two via a cross-attention module to produce four embeddings: text-specific, graph-specific, and two cross-modality vectors. Each embedding is quantized into K tokens by nearest-neighbor lookup in a region-partitioned codebook, with a straight-through gradient estimator and a VQ-VAE-style codebook loss. A token-packing objective then optimizes the quantized vectors to maximize mutual information between cross-modality tokens (via InfoNCE and KL-divergence alignment of codebook distance distributions) while preserving modality-specific information (via InfoNCE and orthogonal losses), yielding a total training loss L = L_C + L_KL + L_token^c + L_token^s. The resulting tokens are drop-in replacements for standard EHR vocabulary tokens, requiring no architectural changes to downstream models.

## Results

Integrating MEDTOK into five EHR models (ETHOS, GT-BEHRT, MulT-EHR, TransformEHR, BEHRT) consistently improves AUPRC across all tasks on both in-patient datasets, with average gains of 4.10% on MIMIC-III and 4.78% on MIMIC-IV. The largest improvements appear in drug recommendation (e.g., ETHOS DrugRec jumps from 0.104 to 0.170 AUPRC on MIMIC-III, and from 0.131 to 0.240 on MIMIC-IV). On the out-patient EHRShot dataset, MEDTOK achieves an 11.32% average AUPRC improvement on mortality prediction (Operational Outcomes) and a 6.00% gain on Hyperlipidemia new-diagnosis detection. Ablation studies confirm that both text and graph modalities contribute, and the full optimization (shared + specific information) yields 3.9–9.1% additional gains over codebook loss alone.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MEDTOK is compared against two single-modality tokenizer baselines: the text-based BERT tokenizer (bert-base-uncased) and the graph-based VQGraph tokenizer. In Figure 3, MEDTOK consistently outperforms both BERT and VQGraph across all five in-patient tasks on both MIMIC-III and MIMIC-IV, as well as on the out-patient EHRShot tasks (Operational Outcomes and New Diagnoses), demonstrating that jointly encoding text and graph modalities yields superior token representations. The gains are most pronounced in drug recommendation and new-diagnosis detection, where relational (graph) knowledge about drug–disease associations and hierarchical ontologies is most informative. Among the five EHR models tested, the absolute magnitude of improvement varies by architecture: ETHOS shows the largest relative gains (e.g., +63% relative on MIMIC-III DrugRec), while GT-BEHRT and BEHRT, which already incorporate some structural information, show smaller but still consistent improvements. The paper also demonstrates that MEDTOK tokens, when used as prefix tokens for LLMs (LLaMA3.1-8B, Qwen2.5-7B, MMedLM), improve medical QA accuracy over the LLMs alone on MMLU, MedDDx, and AfrimedQA.

## Relation to Prior Work

MEDTOK extends the vector-quantized tokenization framework of VQ-VAE (Van Den Oord et al., 2017) to the medical code domain, replacing the single-modality codebook with a partitioned codebook (text-specific, graph-specific, and shared regions) and adding a cross-attention module to capture inter-modality interactions. The token packing loss is derived from the information-theoretic disentanglement framework of Wang et al. (2024a), adapted to enforce shared-information maximization and modality-specific retention simultaneously. Conceptually, MEDTOK departs from prior EHR tokenizers (BERT subword, VQGraph) that handle only one modality, and from LLM-based knowledge enrichment approaches (GraphCare, RAM-EHR) that rely on generated triplets rather than structured ontological graphs, positioning itself as a unified, model-agnostic tokenizer that can be swapped into any downstream transformer.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Medical code tokenization is a foundational bottleneck for EHR foundation models, and the 600K+ code vocabulary spanning eight coding systems makes this problem particularly acute; MEDTOK's modular design (drop-in replacement for any transformer-based EHR model) makes it broadly applicable without architectural changes. The consistent gains across five heterogeneous EHR models and three datasets strengthen the case that tokenization quality, not model architecture, is a key underexplored lever. Open questions include whether the PrimeKG-derived graph structure is sufficient for all clinical reasoning tasks, how the approach scales to emerging coding systems, and whether the frozen text encoder limits adaptability to domain-specific terminology shifts.
