## Problem

Current data selection methods for supervised fine-tuning of LLMs tend to prioritize either data quality or data diversity, rarely achieving an optimal balance of both. Quality-focused approaches risk overfitting to specific linguistic patterns, while diversity-focused methods may introduce low-quality instances that degrade model performance. This paper asks whether a single selection strategy can simultaneously maximize both dimensions.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pool of SFT instruction-response pairs (300K instances from the Magpie dataset) along with a data selection budget k (default 10K). GRAPHFILTER is applied exclusively to the instruction text, extracting unigrams, bigrams, and trigrams to build the graph structure. The output is a subset of k training instances selected iteratively; the quality scores (SUPERFILTER perplexity ratios) can be precomputed, and the graph-based selection itself runs in ~0.53 hours on CPU alone when the priority function is omitted, or ~2.48 hours with the full priority function on a single A100 GPU.

## Method

GRAPHFILTER formulates data selection as a set cover problem by modeling the dataset as a bipartite graph where one partition contains sentence nodes and the other contains n-gram nodes, with edges indicating n-gram presence in a sentence. A priority function φ(u) = QUALITY(u) × DIVERSITY(u) re-ranks sentences, where QUALITY uses the SUPERFILTER metric (ratio of response perplexity conditioned on instruction to standalone response perplexity) and DIVERSITY sums TF-IDF scores over all unigrams, bigrams, and trigrams in the sentence. The algorithm iteratively selects the highest-priority sentence, removes all edges incident to its covered n-gram nodes (thereby reducing the diversity score of remaining sentences that share those n-grams), and recomputes priorities on the updated graph. This greedy procedure inherits an H(r) approximation guarantee from the classical set cover problem, where r is the maximum number of n-grams in any sentence. A max-heap data structure reduces per-iteration selection cost from O(N) to O(log N), making the full pipeline competitive with or faster than neural-model-based baselines.

## Results

GRAPHFILTER achieves the best overall performance (μ_ALL) across all three model backbones: 35.06 on GEMMA-2-2B, 39.38 on MISTRAL-7B-V0.3, and 47.97 on LLAMA-3-8B, outperforming the strongest baseline by +0.70, +0.98, and +0.78 respectively. On the standardized benchmark aggregate (μ_BENCH), GRAPHFILTER scores 36.77, 41.83, and 50.55 for the three backbones, and on the LLM-as-a-Judge aggregate (μ_LLM) it scores 31.64, 34.49, and 42.79. In terms of efficiency, GRAPHFILTER completes 10K-instance selection in 2.48 hours (with the SUPERFILTER quality metric) or just 0.53 hours on CPU alone (without the priority function), compared to 32.34 hours for ALPAGASUS and 25.48 hours for INSTAG.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GRAPHFILTER outperforms all nine baselines on μ_ALL across every backbone. Against the strongest quality-based baseline SUPERFILTER, GRAPHFILTER gains +0.70 (GEMMA-2-2B), +0.98 (MISTRAL-7B-V0.3), and +1.27 (LLAMA-3-8B); against the strongest diversity-based baseline INSTAG, the margins are +0.76, +1.88, and +0.78 respectively. Quality-based methods like ARMORM and ALPAGASUS show benchmark-specific biases (e.g., ARMORM excels on AlpacaEval-2.0 but underperforms on GSM8K), while PERPLEXITY consistently yields the worst GSM8K scores. Diversity-based KMEANS and INSTAG lag on standardized benchmarks but are competitive on LLM-as-a-Judge metrics, confirming that neither quality nor diversity alone suffices. In terms of runtime, GRAPHFILTER (2.48 h) is roughly 13× faster than ALPAGASUS (32.34 h), 9× faster than INSTAG (25.48 h), and 9× faster than DEITA (22.65 h), and its CPU-only variant (0.53 h) is up to 61× faster than any GPU-based baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by reframing SFT data selection as a set cover problem over a bipartite sentence–n-gram graph, a formulation not previously applied to this task. It departs from the prevailing dichotomy in the literature—quality-focused methods (SUPERFILTER, ARMORM, DEITA, ALPAGASUS) versus diversity-focused methods (KMEANS, INSTAG)—by unifying both into a single multiplicative priority function that re-ranks nodes during iterative greedy selection. The diversity signal draws on classical TF-IDF from information retrieval, while the quality signal borrows the IFD metric from SUPERFILTER, but the bipartite-graph coverage mechanism and the iterative edge-removal procedure are novel to this work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in the computational efficiency: without the quality re-ranking, GRAPHFILTER runs in 0.53 hours on CPU alone, roughly 61× faster than the slowest baseline, making large-scale data selection accessible without GPU resources. The finding that the relative importance of quality versus diversity shifts with the selection budget (quality dominates at 1K–5K, diversity at larger budgets) offers a useful heuristic for practitioners choosing strategies under different data constraints. Limitations include evaluation on a single source dataset (Magpie, 300K instances) and a default budget of 10K, leaving open whether the bipartite-graph formulation scales gracefully to multi-million-instance corpora or to non-English and multimodal settings.
