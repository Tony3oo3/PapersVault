## Problem

Decision trees encode not just a classifier but also a particular evaluation order, and the same decision boundary can be represented by many structurally different trees (predictive equivalence). This causes variable importance metrics to shift arbitrarily across equivalent trees, makes trees get stuck on irrelevant missing features, over-represents certain models in Rashomon sets, and forces sub-optimal feature acquisition order when features have costs. No existing representation abstracts away the evaluation order while remaining faithful to the underlying decision boundary.

## Input / Output

The method takes any decision tree (regardless of how it was learned) and a sample that may have missing feature values, and produces a minimal DNF boolean formula plus a prediction of 0, 1, or NA (where NA indicates insufficient information). For variable importance, it takes a Rashomon set of trees and outputs a corrected importance distribution by collapsing predictively equivalent trees. For cost optimization, it takes a tree with per-feature acquisition costs and outputs a Q-learning policy specifying which feature to query next in each state of partial information.

## Method

The core representation converts a decision tree into minimal disjunctive normal form (an OR of ANDs) by collecting all positive-predicting leaves as a disjunction, then simplifying with an adjusted Quine-McCluskey algorithm; the same procedure is applied to negative leaves. This DNF form is provably faithful to the original tree, can predict whenever any completion of a partially-observed sample yields a consistent label (Theorem 3.2), and maps all predictively equivalent trees to the identical formula (Theorem 3.4). A second representation, the Blake canonical form (BCF), enumerates all minimal sufficient conditions for each prediction class, enabling early stopping during evaluation. For cost-sensitive feature acquisition, a Q-learning agent operates over a state space of 3^{d_b} configurations (each binarized feature is 0, 1, or unknown), with actions corresponding to purchasing an unknown feature and rewards structured so that satisfying any BCF clause terminates the episode with a positive reward; the agent is initialized via recursive tree traversal and trained for 10,000 episodes to learn the minimum-cost policy for reaching a prediction.

## Results

The DNF representation reveals that a large fraction of trees in the Rashomon set are predictively equivalent: on COMPAS, 12,785 trees reduce to only 2,135 unique DNF forms (~83% reduction), and on Wisconsin, 24,052 trees reduce to 4,657 (~81% reduction). For variable importance, PE-corrected RID reduces the 1-Wasserstein distance to ground truth by 23–28% across all variables (e.g., X₃: 0.232 → 0.182). For missing data, the DNF-based prediction method identifies substantially more samples where no imputation is needed compared to path-based and function-agnostic baselines, even at 50%+ per-feature missingness. For cost optimization, the Q-learning policy over the Blake canonical form reduces average evaluation cost relative to both naive (purchase all features) and path-based (traverse tree) strategies on every dataset tested.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary quantitative baseline comparison is against the original Rashomon Importance Distribution (RID; Donnelly et al., 2023). On a synthetic DGP with known ground-truth importance, PE-corrected RID achieves lower 1-Wasserstein distance to ground truth on all three variables: X₁ (0.092 vs 0.120), X₂ (0.105 vs 0.136), and X₃ (0.182 vs 0.232), representing a 23–28% improvement. On the real COMPAS dataset, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test confirms significant distribution shifts for all three important variables (age, juvenile crimes, priors count) at p < 0.001, with the PE-corrected version shifting probability mass toward zero importance for age and juvenile crimes and toward higher importance for priors count. The paper also compares against path-based and function-agnostic baselines for missing-data handling, where the DNF method identifies far more samples as predictable without imputation, and against naive and path-based feature-purchase policies for cost optimization, where the Q-learning policy consistently achieves lower average cost. Across all comparisons, the DNF representation wins or ties, with the most dramatic gains in the Rashomon set size reduction and cost optimization settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Quine-McCluskey algorithm for boolean minimization and the Blake canonical form to construct a minimal DNF representation of decision trees, extending the explanation-redundancy analysis of Izza et al. (2022) from individual path explanations to a global tree-level representation. It departs from standard path-based tree evaluation by decoupling the decision boundary from the evaluation order, and applies this representation to problems previously addressed by imputation (Therneau et al., 1997), impurity-based importance (Breiman, 1984), and cost-sensitive tree induction (Lomax & Vadera, 2013). The Rashomon set framework (Xin et al., 2022) and the Rashomon Importance Distribution (Donnelly et al., 2023) provide the empirical setting where predictive equivalence's impact is quantified and corrected.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that Rashomon sets contain 2–6× more trees than unique decision boundaries (Table 1) has immediate practical consequences: any downstream analysis that treats each tree as a distinct model (RID, ensemble selection, uncertainty quantification) is systematically biased. The robustness result—that trees can predict on a large fraction of samples under high missingness without any imputation—offers a simple, interpretable alternative to complex imputation pipelines. Open questions include whether the DNF representation scales to deeper trees (the Quine-McCluskey step is NP-complete), how predictive equivalence interacts with tree ensembles, and whether the group-theoretic structure of equivalence classes can be exploited for further analysis.
