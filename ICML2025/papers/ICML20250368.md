## Problem

Existing large graph-language models (LGLMs) tokenize molecules solely at the atom level, ignoring the intrinsic hierarchical structure of motifs and functional groups that encode critical biochemical semantics. This node-centric tokenization forces LLMs to implicitly infer substructures during instruction tuning, leading to misaligned representations and severe hallucination—LGLMs consistently answer 'Yes' to nearly any functional group query regardless of actual molecular content. The paper asks whether hierarchical molecular information can be explicitly integrated into LLMs to fix this alignment failure.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a 2D molecular graph (nodes with atom-type attributes, edges with bond attributes) alongside a natural-language question, and optionally a 1D SELFIES sequence for reaction tasks. The graph is first expanded into a hierarchical graph with supernodes for each BRICS-detected motif and the whole molecule, then tokenized at three levels (atom, motif, molecule) with Laplacian positional encodings appended to each token. Outputs are free-form text: yes/no for classification and hallucination probes, a scalar for quantum property regression, a natural-language caption for molecular description, or a SELFIES string for reaction prediction.

## Method

HIGHT introduces a hierarchical graph tokenizer that uses the BRICS algorithm to detect k motifs in a molecule, augments the original graph with k+1 supernodes (one per motif plus one for the whole molecule), and trains separate VQVAEs with self-supervised reconstruction tasks (atom type at the atom level, atom count at the motif level) to produce discrete tokens at each hierarchy level. Three distinct MLP adapters project node-level, motif-level, and molecule-level tokens (each augmented with 8-dimensional Laplacian positional encodings) into the LLM embedding space, so the LLM receives structured multi-scale molecular context rather than a flat bag of atom tokens. The second component, HiPubChem, augments the 295k PubChem instruction-tuning pairs with explicit functional-group captions—positive statements listing detected groups and negative statements for randomly sampled absent groups—so the language modality directly references the hierarchical tokens. Training proceeds in two stages: (1) alignment pretraining on HiPubChem-295k with frozen LLM and GNN to warm up the adapters, then (2) task-specific instruction tuning with frozen GNN and LoRA-adapted LLM (rank 64–128) on MoleculeNet, ChEBI-20, and Mol-Instructions data.

## Results

On the proposed MotifHallu benchmark, HIGHT-G reduces the worst-class hallucination (F1(neg)) by 40% (from 9.5 to 48.2) compared to node-centric InstructMol-G, while maintaining competitive F1(pos) (85.5 vs 95.7) and nearly doubling accuracy (39.1% vs 19.9%). On molecular property classification (MoleculeNet), HIGHT-G achieves 80.5 ROC-AUC on CYP450, surpassing the previous SOTA GIMLET (71.3) and InstructMol-G (59.1). On QM9 regression, HIGHT-G attains an average MAE of 0.0086, a 34% improvement over InstructMol-G (0.0130). Across chemical reaction prediction (reagent, forward reaction, retrosynthesis), HIGHT-G consistently outperforms InstructMol-G on BLEU, fingerprint similarity, and Levenshtein distance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HIGHT-G decisively outperforms its direct node-centric counterpart InstructMol-G across every evaluated task: hallucination F1(neg) improves from 9.5 to 48.2, CYP450 ROC-AUC jumps from 59.1 to 80.5, QM9 average MAE drops from 0.0130 to 0.0086, and ChEBI-20 BLEU-4 rises from 0.381 to 0.405. Against the previous SOTA GIMLET, HIGHT-G surpasses it on CYP450 (80.5 vs 71.3) and most MoleculeNet classification datasets (e.g., BACE 77.1 vs 69.6, BBBP 61.8 vs 59.4), though GIMLET retains an edge on HIV (66.2 vs 63.3). Compared to Galactica-6.7B, HIGHT-G wins on BACE (77.1 vs 58.4), BBBP (61.8 vs 53.5), and SIDER (58.8 vs 55.9), but trails on HIV (63.3 vs 72.2). On molecular description (ChEBI-20), HIGHT-G still lags behind MolT5-based foundation models such as MolFM (BLEU-2 0.585) and Text+Chem T5 (BLEU-2 0.625), which were pretrained on substantially larger corpora. In chemical reaction prediction, HIGHT-G outperforms Mol-Instruction (Fang et al., 2024) on reagent prediction (BLEU 0.462 vs 0.224) and retrosynthesis (BLEU 0.863 vs 0.705), and matches or exceeds Llama-7b with LoRA fine-tuning on forward reaction prediction (BLEU 0.869 vs 0.804). Ablations confirm that removing HiPubChem data collapses HIGHT back to near-InstructMol hallucination levels (F1(neg) drops from 48.2 to 12.5), validating that both the hierarchical tokenizer and the augmented instruction data are necessary.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LGLM architecture of InstructMol (Cao et al., 2023) by replacing its node-centric VQVAE tokenizer (from Mole-BERT) with a hierarchical graph tokenizer that encodes atom, motif, and molecule-level tokens separately, drawing on hierarchical GNN self-supervised learning (Zang et al., 2023). It departs from prior LGLMs (GIMLET, InstructMol, MolCA) that feed only atom-level node embeddings to the LLM, arguing that this forces the LLM to implicitly reconstruct substructural semantics. Concurrent works (Park et al., 2024; Hu & Li, 2024) also explore hierarchical graph information in LLMs, but HIGHT uniquely combines the architectural change with an augmented instruction-tuning dataset (HiPubChem) that explicitly mentions functional groups in captions, and introduces a dedicated hallucination benchmark (MotifHallu) to diagnose the failure mode.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete and previously unquantified failure mode in LGLMs—systematic false-positive hallucination on functional group detection—caused by the absence of substructural tokens, and shows that a relatively simple hierarchical augmentation (BRICS motifs + Laplacian positional encodings + caption augmentation) reduces this by up to 40%. The finding that adding 1D SELFIES sequences can worsen hallucination, and that HiPubChem without the hierarchical architecture degrades performance, highlights that both components are necessary and that modality stacking is not a free lunch. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes beyond molecular graphs to social networks or knowledge graphs, whether 3D geometric information can be incorporated into the hierarchical tokenizer, and how to integrate HIGHT into large-scale pretraining rather than instruction tuning alone.
