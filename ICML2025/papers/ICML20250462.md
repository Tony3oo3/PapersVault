## Problem

While many convex and calibrated (CC) surrogate losses guarantee statistical consistency for classification, selecting the optimal fixed loss in practice remains difficult, and post-hoc loss ensembles (training separate models per loss) are computationally prohibitive for deep networks. The paper asks whether one can ensemble calibrated loss functions within a single SGD training run—preserving the CC properties of the combined loss—thereby regularizing overparameterized models from a loss-function perspective complementary to Dropout and weight decay.

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard binary-classification inputs: RGB images (CIFAR-10 class-pairs at 32×32, PatchCamelyon histopathology patches at 96×96) or dense tabular feature vectors (14 OpenML datasets filtered to n≥1000, d≥1000), and outputs a binary class label via sign(f(x)). The unusual aspect is that the loss-derivative is not a fixed function of the model output z=yf(x); instead, at every mini-batch SGD step a fresh set of loss-derivatives is randomly generated and sorted, making the gradient doubly stochastic (random batch sample × random calibrated loss-derivative).

## Method

ENSLOSS transforms the three CC loss conditions—convexity, calibration (φ′(0)<0), and bounded-below (superlinear raising-tail)—into equivalent conditions on the loss-derivative alone, bypassing explicit loss functions. At each mini-batch step, it draws B i.i.d. standard normal values, applies the inverse Box-Cox transformation (with λ=0, i.e., exponentiation) to obtain positive random variables, negates them, sorts them in the same order as the batch z-scores, and rescales entries with z>1 by dividing by z to enforce the superlinear raising-tail condition. This yields a set of 'RC loss-derivatives' that provably correspond to some bounded-below convex calibrated loss (Lemma 3.3), so the resulting doubly stochastic gradient is an unbiased estimate of the gradient of a calibrated ensemble risk R̄(f)=E_Φ[Φ(Yf(X))]. The paper proves R̄ is classification-calibrated (Theorem 3.6) with an explicit excess-risk bound, and shows via Rademacher complexity that the ensemble loss is no worse than the average of fixed losses.

## Results

On CIFAR2 (cat-dog) with VGG16, ENSLOSS achieves 81.13% accuracy and 89.54% AUC, outperforming the best fixed loss (HINGE at 78.13% / 86.20%) by ~3% accuracy. On the PCam histopathology dataset with VGG19, ENSLOSS reaches 85.91% accuracy and 95.51% AUC versus 82.77% / 84.09% for HINGE. Across all 45 CIFAR2 binary datasets, ENSLOSS is statistically significantly better than EXP on 45/45 datasets and better than BCE on 36–45/45 datasets (depending on architecture). On 14 OpenML tabular datasets with MLP(5), ENSLOSS wins on 11/14 vs BCE, 11/14 vs EXP, and 13/14 vs HINGE.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ENSLOSS consistently outperforms all three fixed-loss baselines (BCE, EXP, HINGE) across every architecture and dataset tested. The gap is largest against EXP (e.g., 81.13% vs 69.27% on CIFAR2 cat-dog VGG16, a ~12-point margin) and smallest against HINGE (e.g., 81.13% vs 78.13%, ~3 points), with BCE falling in between. Against post-ensemble baselines (model AVERAGE and majority VOTING from three separately trained fixed-loss classifiers), ENSLOSS still wins on CIFAR2 cat-dog ResNet50 (72.37% vs 70.61% and 69.67% respectively), while requiring only a single training run rather than three. On tabular data with simpler MLP(1) models, ENSLOSS is less consistently superior (e.g., 9/14 better than BCE, 5/14 better than HINGE), but the advantage grows with model complexity (MLP(5): 11/14 vs BCE, 13/14 vs HINGE), confirming that the method is most beneficial for overparameterized models.

## Relation to Prior Work

ENSLOSS extends the ensemble learning paradigm from model ensembles (Dropout, bagging, stacking) to the loss function space, applying the ensemble concept within a single SGD training run rather than requiring separate training sessions per loss. It builds directly on the classification-calibration theory of Bartlett et al. (2006) and Lin (2004), transforming the convexity-and-calibration conditions from loss functions into conditions on loss-derivatives, thereby enabling random generation of 'valid' loss-derivatives without explicitly constructing loss functions. The inverse Box-Cox transformation (Box & Cox, 1964) is repurposed as a mechanism for generating diverse positive random distributions to control loss-derivative diversity. Conceptually, the method is a loss-space analogue of Dropout's architecture-space randomization, but operates on the surrogate loss rather than network structure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ENSLOSS offers a simple, hyperparameter-light drop-in regularization that complements (rather than competes with) existing techniques like Dropout, weight decay, and data augmentation, with theoretical guarantees of statistical consistency via calibration of the ensemble risk. The ablation revealing that the bounded-below (superlinear raising-tail) condition is practically essential—without it the model fails to learn at all—highlights a previously underappreciated regularity requirement for loss-derivatives in SGD. Open questions include: optimal combining weights for loss ensembles (analogous to ensemble learning literature), extension to multi-class and other consistency frameworks (H-consistency, AUC optimization), and reducing the 2–3× training time overhead. The method's universality across architectures and datasets suggests it could become a standard component in classification pipelines, particularly for overparameterized models.
