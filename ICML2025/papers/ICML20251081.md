## Problem

Existing model editing methods rely on perturbing a single token's hidden state to update knowledge, creating an 'efficacy barrier' that prevents them from handling long-form outputs (beyond ~100 tokens) or diverse-formatted knowledge such as code snippets, mathematical derivations, and poetry. This single-token paradigm is fundamentally insufficient because complex knowledge requires coordinated updates across multiple tokens with intricate interdependencies, yet simultaneously perturbing multiple tokens introduces interference that degrades editing quality.

## Input / Output

The method takes an input prompt X and a target output Y (which can be unstructured text up to 458 tokens spanning domains like math, code, chemistry, news, and poetry) along with a pre-trained decoder-only LLM (e.g., Llama3-8B-Instruct or Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct). Y is decomposed into sequential chunks of configurable size (default 40–50 tokens), and the method produces parameter updates to specific MLP down-projection layers (or full attention+MLP layers in AnyEdit*) that cause the model to autoregressively generate Y given X. The plug-and-play variant can be applied on top of existing locate-then-edit methods, replacing only their single-token locate stage with the multi-token autoregressive procedure.

## Method

AnyEdit introduces an autoregressive editing paradigm that decomposes the target output Y into K sequential chunks and iteratively edits one token per chunk. For each chunk Y_k, it selects the last token, locates influential layers via causal tracing, and perturbs that token's hidden state via gradient descent to maximize P(Y_k | X, Y_1, ..., Y_{k-1}), then updates model parameters using least-squares optimization (or full-layer gradient descent in AnyEdit*). The theoretical foundation rests on the Chain Rule of Mutual Information: by conditioning each chunk's edit on all previously generated chunks, the multi-token mutual information decomposes into a sum of single-hidden-state terms, eliminating the interference that plagues simultaneous multi-token perturbation while scaling to arbitrary output length and format. This makes AnyEdit both adaptive (number of edited tokens scales with knowledge length) and general (format-agnostic, supporting poetry, code, math, and prose without structure-specific constraints).

## Results

On UnKEBench with Llama3-8B-Instruct, AnyEdit achieves 97.76 BERT Score and 92.96 ROUGE-L on original questions, surpassing the best baseline MEMIT (76.21 BERT Score, 30.49 ROUGE-L) by over 20 points. On AKEW(Counterfact), AnyEdit reaches 97.76 BERT Score vs. MEMIT's 76.44, and on AKEW(MQUAKE) it scores 96.33 vs. 75.31. The paper reports a 21.5% average improvement in editing accuracy over all baselines across benchmarks, with AnyEdit* (full-layer gradient descent variant) further pushing UnKEBench BERT Score to 99.86.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AnyEdit dominates all six baselines across every dataset and metric. Against the strongest traditional locate-then-edit methods (MEMIT, AlphaEdit, ROME), AnyEdit improves BERT Score by 21–28 points on UnKEBench and 21–28 points on AKEW(Counterfact), with ROUGE-L gains exceeding 60 points (e.g., 92.96 vs. 30.49 on UnKEBench). The closest competitor is UnKE, which also handles unstructured knowledge by updating full layers via gradient descent; AnyEdit matches or slightly trails UnKE on original-question BERT Score (97.76 vs. 98.34 on UnKEBench) but substantially outperforms it on paraphrase generalization (96.60 vs. 93.38 BERT Score) and on AKEW(MQUAKE) (96.33 vs. 98.33 BERT Score is close, but ROUGE-L 94.32 vs. 95.42 is comparable). When AnyEdit's autoregressive paradigm is plugged into MEMIT, AlphaEdit, or UnKE (denoted with '+'), all three methods jump to near-AnyEdit performance (e.g., MEMIT+ reaches 97.76 BERT Score on UnKEBench vs. 76.21 without), confirming AnyEdit's plug-and-play utility. FT-L and MEND remain far behind, with FT-L actually degrading below the pre-edited model, indicating that naive fine-tuning is unsuitable for long-form editing.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the locate-then-edit paradigm established by ROME and MEMIT, and the unstructured-knowledge editing direction pioneered by UnKE and AKEW, by replacing the single-token perturbation assumption with an autoregressive multi-token editing process. The key conceptual departure is formalizing the 'efficacy barrier' of single-token editing (Proposition 1) and grounding the fix in the Chain Rule of Mutual Information, which decomposes the joint editing objective into sequential single-chunk sub-problems. AnyEdit is explicitly designed as a plug-and-play wrapper: it can be grafted onto any existing locate-then-edit method (MEMIT, AlphaEdit, UnKE) to extend their reach to arbitrary-length and diverse-format knowledge without modifying their core optimization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The plug-and-play nature of AnyEdit makes it immediately practical for the knowledge-editing community: any existing method can be upgraded to handle long-form code, math derivations, or poetry with a modest ~25% increase in editing time. The EditEverything benchmark (up to 458 tokens, spanning math, code, chemistry, news, and poetry) fills a real gap left by prior benchmarks capped around 156 tokens. Two open limitations are acknowledged: the framework is not designed for lifelong/iterative editing scenarios, and it is confined to textual knowledge with no multimodal support. Whether the autoregressive chunking strategy scales gracefully to very long documents (thousands of tokens) or to settings where chunks have strong cross-dependencies remains to be tested.
