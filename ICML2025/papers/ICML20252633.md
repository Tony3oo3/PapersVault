## Problem

Judge LLMs used for content moderation rely on contextual embeddings to classify harmful text, but their tokenization process introduces a previously unexplored vulnerability: minor modifications to input text can alter sub-token boundaries, distorting embeddings and reducing detection accuracy. While prior character-level attacks targeted content-generation LLMs, the specific susceptibility of Judge LLMs to token segmentation bias—particularly through semantically rich delimiters like emojis—remained unaddressed.

## Input / Output

The method takes harmful text responses (ranging from 3 to 836 words, sourced from AdvBench, LLM Self Defense, and Red Teaming Attempts) and optionally a jailbreak prompt as input. In the white-box variant, it also consumes surrogate model embeddings (gtr-t5-x1) to compute per-position cosine similarities for optimal emoji placement. The output is emoji-augmented text where one or more emojis are inserted at specific character positions within words, designed to minimize the Judge LLM's unsafe prediction probability while preserving surface readability.

## Method

The core mechanism exploits token segmentation bias: inserting an emoji at position j within a token x_i splits it into sub-tokens whose combined embeddings diverge from the original token's embedding, disrupting the Judge LLM's contextual understanding. In the white-box setting, a surrogate model (gtr-t5-x1) computes cosine similarity CS(Emb(x_i), Emb(x̂_{i,j})) for every insertion position j, and the position minimizing this similarity (cs-split) is selected to maximize embedding distortion. In the black-box setting, in-context learning examples are embedded in the prompt to the target LLM, instructing it to naturally incorporate emojis into its responses without the attacker needing access to the Judge LLM. Emojis outperform simple delimiters (spaces, underscores) because they simultaneously induce token segmentation bias and introduce semantic ambiguity—positive or neutral connotations that further mislead the Judge LLM's safety assessment. The attack is composable with existing jailbreak methods (Deepinception, CodeChameleon, ReNellm, Jailbroken), reducing unsafe prediction ratios by an additional 12% on average across ten Judge LLMs.

## Results

On 1,432 harmful responses, token segmentation bias alone reduces the average unsafe prediction ratio from 84.4% (default) to 75.5% across eight Judge LLMs. Inserting an emoji at a random position further drops the average to 67.2%, and the proposed optimized position selection (cs-split) achieves 65.2%. ShieldLM is the most vulnerable model, dropping from 78.4% to 3.0% under the optimized white-box emoji attack, while GPT-4 remains largely robust (96.2% → 98.2%). In the black-box setting (Table 2), the emoji attack combined with CodeChameleon reduces the average unsafe prediction ratio from 46.2% to 35.2% across ten Judge LLMs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the GCG adversarial suffix (optimized on 30 samples targeting Llama Guard), the Emoji Attack achieves lower unsafe prediction ratios on three of four open-source Judge LLMs: Llama Guard 2 (31.2% vs. 48.0%), ShieldLM (18.5% vs. 90.7%), and WildGuard (32.2% vs. 61.8%), while GCG slightly outperforms on Llama Guard (8.8% vs. 12.2%). This demonstrates that the Emoji Attack generalizes better across black-box Judge LLMs, whereas the GCG suffix degrades significantly when transferred beyond its white-box optimization target. Among the four jailbreak baselines tested in the black-box setting (Deepinception, ReNellm, Jailbroken, CodeChameleon), CodeChameleon consistently yields the lowest unsafe prediction ratios (e.g., 23.4% on Llama Guard), and the Emoji Attack further reduces these by 10–20 percentage points on open-source models. The paper also notes that DeepSeek (a reasoning model) is notably more robust to emoji insertion than o3-mini, suggesting reasoning capability may partially mitigate token segmentation bias.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior character-level adversarial attacks (Claburn 2024; Charmer/Rocamora et al. 2024) that target content-generation LLMs by instead targeting Judge LLMs in a post-hoc, black-box setting. It also extends the line of work on attacking guardrail models (Mangaokar et al. 2024, who applied GCG to white-box Judge LLMs) by identifying a specific mechanistic vulnerability—token segmentation bias—and exploiting it through a lightweight, optimization-free strategy (emoji insertion via in-context learning) that composes with existing jailbreak prompts rather than replacing them. The use of emojis as the perturbation vehicle is novel relative to prior delimiter-based approaches (spaces, underscores, homoglyphs), adding a semantic-ambiguity dimension on top of the tokenization disruption.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a single emoji insertion can reduce unsafe detection rates by 12–60% across open-source Judge LLMs exposes a concrete, easily exploitable gap in deployed moderation pipelines, with immediate practical implications for any system relying on LLM-based content filtering. Open questions include whether reasoning-based Judge LLMs (e.g., DeepSeek) can close this gap, how to defend against mixed-delimiter variants that evade simple character-filtering, and whether the effect persists as tokenizers are redesigned to be more robust to sub-token perturbations. The paper's limitation that commercial models (GPT-4, DeepSeek) show greater robustness suggests the vulnerability may narrow over time, but the current state of open-source guardrails is concerning.
