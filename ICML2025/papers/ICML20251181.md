## Problem

Classical kernel intensity estimators (KIEs) are computationally efficient but require explicit edge correction, while Flaxman's kernel method-based estimator (FIE) handles edge effects naturally through RKHS theory but demands expensive gradient descent to fit dual coefficients. The paper asks whether a kernel method-based intensity estimator can be constructed that inherits the edge-effect robustness of FIE while retaining the computational simplicity of KIEs, and whether a theoretical connection between the two families can be established.

## Input / Output

The method takes N point event locations in a d-dimensional compact space and an observation domain that may be a union of disjoint hyperrectangular regions (not just a single rectangle), along with kernel hyper-parameters (inverse scale β and regularization γ). It produces an estimated intensity function λ̂(x) expressed as a sum of equivalent RKHS kernels evaluated at data points with unit dual coefficients, requiring no iterative optimization. A key practical advantage is that the integral of the squared intensity over the domain—needed for predictive tasks like computing Poisson event-count probabilities—can be evaluated analytically in O(M²+MN) rather than via Monte Carlo integration.

## Method

The core contribution is showing that replacing the log-likelihood loss in Flaxman's RKHS formulation with a penalized least squares loss yields a specialized representer theorem where the dual coefficient is exactly unity, making the resulting estimator (K²IE) structurally identical to a classical KIE but with equivalent RKHS kernels in place of arbitrary smoothing kernels. The equivalent kernel h(·,·) is defined as the solution to a Fredholm integral equation of the second kind that implicitly incorporates edge correction. For finite observation domains, the authors approximate the RKHS kernel using 2M random Fourier features, which allows the critical integral term in the Fredholm equation to be computed in closed form over each hyperrectangular sub-region via a sinc-based formula. The proof relies on the path integral representation of the squared Hilbert space norm (Kim, 2021) to derive the functional Euler-Lagrange equation, with an alternative Mercer-theorem-based derivation provided in the appendix. This eliminates the need for gradient descent entirely: given hyper-parameters, the estimator is simply λ̂(x) = Σₙ h(x, xₙ), achieving FIE-level edge correction at KIE-level computational cost.

## Results

On 2D synthetic data with missing sub-domains, K²IE achieves the lowest integrated squared error (L²) across all three settings: 53.0 vs. 56.5 (FIE) and 63.3 (KIE) on λ²D¹·⁰, with the gap widening as more sub-domains are removed (57.9 vs. 62.3 vs. 64.5 on λ²D⁰·⁸). Computationally, K²IE requires only 0.13–0.16 s of CPU time for model fitting, roughly 10× faster than FIE (1.45–1.54 s) and orders of magnitude faster than the variational Bayesian GCP baseline (51.8 s). On 1D data, K²IE matches FIE's predictive accuracy while reducing fitting time from ~1 s to ~0.01 s, and on the real-world *bei* tree dataset it achieves the best test least-squares loss (L_s = −6.16) with 0.17 s fitting time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the classical kernel intensity estimator (KIE, Diggle 1985), K²IE clearly wins on 2D data where edge effects are prominent: it reduces L² by 10–16 points (e.g., 53.0 vs. 63.3 on λ²D¹·⁰) and achieves ρ ≈ 0.85–0.97 (fraction of trials beating KIE), while on 1D data KIE retains a slight edge on two of three intensity functions. Against Flaxman's kernel method-based estimator (FIE, Flaxman et al. 2017), K²IE matches or slightly exceeds predictive accuracy on L² (53.0 vs. 56.5 on λ²D¹·⁰; 1.67 vs. 1.74 on 10×λ¹D¹) while cutting model-fitting CPU time by roughly 10× (0.16 s vs. 1.54 s in 2D; 0.01 s vs. 1.06 s in 1D), because K²IE requires no iterative dual-coefficient optimization. Against the variational Bayesian Gaussian Cox process of Lloyd et al. (2015), K²IE achieves comparable L² (53.0 vs. 63.9) and |L| (5.54 vs. 5.55) on the 2D dataset while being over 300× faster (0.16 s vs. 51.8 s). On the real-world *bei* dataset, K²IE attains the best test least-squares loss (−6.16 vs. −5.80 KIE, −5.16 FIE) but is slightly outperformed by KIE on the negative log-likelihood of counts (279 vs. 267), likely because each method's hyperparameters were tuned to its own loss function.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate lines of work on Poisson process intensity estimation: classical kernel intensity estimators (KIEs) with explicit edge correction (Ramlau-Hansen, Diggle) and RKHS-based kernel method estimators (Flaxman et al., 2017) that handle edge effects implicitly through equivalent RKHS kernels. The key departure is replacing Flaxman's log-likelihood loss with a least squares loss, which the authors prove (via path integral representation of the RKHS norm) yields a representer theorem with unit dual coefficients, collapsing the kernel method estimator into a KIE-like form. The construction of equivalent RKHS kernels via random Fourier features and the degenerate-kernel approach for solving the Fredholm integral equation builds directly on Kim et al. (2022).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theoretical result that least-squares-loss RKHS intensity estimation is equivalent to a KIE with equivalent RKHS kernels provides a unifying framework that was previously absent, and the practical consequence is a method that combines KIE-level computational efficiency (no dual optimization, no Monte Carlo integration for cross-validation) with the superior edge correction of Flaxman's estimator. A key open limitation is the lack of a general non-negativity guarantee for the intensity function; the authors note that inverse M-kernels (IMKs) may resolve this in 1D but no general IMK construction exists in higher dimensions. Empirical validation remains limited to synthetic 1D/2D data and one small real-world dataset, leaving open questions about performance on large-scale or high-dimensional real applications.
