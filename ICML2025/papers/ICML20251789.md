## Problem

Existing risk-sensitive Distributional RL methods either apply a fixed risk measure at each step (causing time-inconsistent, sub-optimal policies) or optimize static risk measures limited to CVaR due to computational complexity. This paper addresses both gaps by extending static risk-sensitive DRL to the full class of Spectral Risk Measures (SRMs) and providing a clear interpretation of the learned policy's evolving risk preferences over time.

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes a standard MDP (state, action, transition, reward) plus a user-specified risk spectrum φ (e.g., CVaR, weighted sum of CVaRs, exponential, or dual power), and operates on an augmented state space (x, s, c) where s tracks accumulated discounted reward and c tracks the remaining discount factor. It outputs a risk-sensitive Markov policy over the augmented space, N-quantile return distributions per state-action pair, and the SRM risk-adjusted value. The alternating optimization between the concave utility function h (outer loop) and the quantile-regression value network (inner loop) is a key structural detail: h is updated in closed form from the initial-state return distribution, while the policy is updated via standard quantile regression with risk-sensitive greedy action selection.

## Method

QR-SRM decomposes the SRM optimization into an inner problem (find the optimal Markov policy for a fixed concave utility h over the augmented MDP) and an outer problem (find the optimal h via a closed-form solution derived from the Rockafellar–Uryasev CVaR representation). The inner optimization uses quantile regression with an extended state (x, s, c) and a risk-sensitive greedy rule that maximizes E[h(s + cG)] rather than the expected return, with the sample loss differing from standard QR-DQN only in the action-selection step. The outer optimization updates h in closed form using the quantiles of the initial-state return distribution, avoiding the expensive global optimization required by prior SRM-MDP approaches. Convergence is guaranteed: the inner iteration converges to the optimal policy for fixed h (Theorem 4.1), and the outer iteration monotonically increases a lower bound on the SRM objective. Additionally, the paper applies the Pflug–Pichler decomposition theorem to compute intermediate risk measures at each time step, revealing how the agent's effective risk preference (CVaR levels and weights) evolves as new information is observed, without any additional computational cost during training.

## Results

On the Mean-reversion Trading environment, QR-SRM(φ_α=1.0) achieves CVaR_1.0 of 1.43±0.03, matching QR-CVaR(α=1.0) at 1.48±0.07 while substantially outperforming risk-neutral QR-DQN (1.40±0.09) on tail risk metrics (CVaR_0.5: 0.03 vs −0.24; CVaR_0.2: −1.36 vs −1.76). For complex risk measures, QR-SRM(φ_ν=2.0) achieves the best DPRM_2.0 score (0.46±0.02) and QR-SRM(φ_α̅₂,w̅₂) achieves the best WSCVaR score (0.40±0.02). On the more challenging Windy Lunar Lander, QR-SRM(φ_α=0.2) achieves CVaR_0.2 of −13.51±14.54, dramatically outperforming QR-DQN (−24.79±23.39) and QR-CVaR(α=0.2) (−19.09±4.19), while QR-SRM(φ_α̅₃,w̅₃) achieves the best WSCVaR (14.57±15.22) and CVaR_0.5 (14.17±13.46) simultaneously.

## Baselines & Comparisons

QR-SRM matches QR-CVaR (Bellemare et al., 2023) closely on the corresponding CVaR metric (e.g., CVaR_0.5: 0.27 vs 0.28; CVaR_0.2: −0.21 vs −0.27), confirming that the more general SRM framework correctly recovers CVaR-optimal policies as a special case. QR-SRM decisively outperforms QR-iCVaR (Dabney et al., 2018a) across all risk levels: at α=0.5, QR-iCVaR achieves only 0.14 CVaR_0.5 versus 0.27 for QR-SRM, and at α=0.2, QR-iCVaR drops to 0.04 CVaR_0.5 and 0.13 DPRM_2.0, confirming that applying a fixed risk measure at each step (time-inconsistent) yields sub-optimal policies. Against risk-neutral QR-DQN, QR-SRM(φ_α=1.0) achieves comparable expected return (1.43 vs 1.40) but substantially better tail performance (CVaR_0.5: 0.03 vs −0.24; CVaR_0.2: −1.36 vs −1.76). On Windy Lunar Lander, QR-SRM(φ_α=0.2) achieves CVaR_0.2 of −13.51 versus −24.79 for QR-DQN and −19.09 for QR-CVaR(α=0.2), while QR-CVaR itself degrades severely (expected return 14.52 vs 32.73 for QR-DQN), likely due to the 'Blindness to Success' problem. The flexible WSCVaR objective (QR-SRM with φ_α̅₃,w̅₃) uniquely achieves the best WSCVaR (14.57) and CVaR_0.5 (14.17) simultaneously on Lunar Lander, demonstrating the advantage of multi-level risk optimization over single-CVaR approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the distributional RL framework for risk-sensitive policy optimization from the single CVaR case (Bellemare et al., 2023) to the full class of static Spectral Risk Measures, building on the state-augmentation approach of Bauerle & Glauner (2021) and the quantile regression machinery of QR-DQN (Dabney et al., 2018b). A key departure from prior work is the use of the DRL return distribution to obtain a closed-form solution for the outer optimization over the concave function h, avoiding the piecewise-linear approximation required in Bauerle & Glauner. The interpretability contribution leverages the Pflug & Pichler (2016) decomposition theorem for coherent risk measures, but unlike earlier works (Chow et al., 2015; Stanko & Macek, 2019) that used CVaR decomposition for policy optimization, here the decomposition is used solely to explain the learned policy's evolving risk preferences, following the caution in Hau et al. (2023) that such decompositions are unreliable for optimization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By generalizing from CVaR to arbitrary SRMs, the paper gives practitioners a flexible tool to encode diverse risk profiles (Mean-CVaR, ERM, DPRM, weighted CVaR sums) within a single algorithmic framework, with convergence guarantees and an interpretable account of how risk preferences evolve over time. The temporal decomposition result (Theorem 5.1) is practically useful for monitoring and auditing risk-sensitive agents in high-stakes domains. Key limitations include restriction to discrete action spaces, reliance on the quantile representation, and the fact that the outer optimization provides only a lower bound on the objective rather than a provably optimal solution—stronger convergence guarantees for the function h update remain an open question.
