## Problem

In extreme multi-label classification (XMC) with hundreds of thousands to millions of labels, the linear classification head dominates memory and compute, yet the current SOTA end-to-end method (Renee) relies on FP16-FP32 mixed-precision training that is both memory-inefficient (requiring full-precision master weights and upcasting gradients) and numerically unstable due to overflow/underflow in the large inner-dimension matmuls. No existing approach trains the entire XMC pipeline—including the massive classifier—in pure low-precision formats without relying on single-precision copies or tensor scaling.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20253055/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes text documents (or text augmented with label features) encoded by a transformer (BERT-base or Distil-BERT) into fixed-dimension embeddings (e.g., 768-d), which are then scored against a linear classifier spanning up to 8.6 million labels. The output is a sparse binary label vector where only a tiny fraction of labels (typically <1%) are active per instance. The extreme label count means the classifier weight matrix alone can exceed 8 GiB in FP32, making it the dominant memory consumer rather than the encoder.

## Method

ELMO replaces mixed-precision training with a pure BF16/FP8 pipeline: classifier weights are stored in FP8 E4M3 (no tensor scaling needed, as the native dynamic range suffices), gradients remain in BF16 (since ~20% exceed FP8 range), and the encoder uses Kahan summation with AdamW while the classifier uses stochastic-rounding SGD. A custom Triton kernel fuses classifier gradient computation and the SGD update entirely in SRAM, eliminating the need to materialize classifier gradients in GPU memory. Labels are split into k chunks (3–8) so that classifier forward, backward, and update are processed sequentially per chunk, reducing transient memory by a factor of k. The computation graph is reorganized to decouple encoder and classifier updates, spreading memory allocations across the iteration and reducing peak usage. Combined, these optimizations reduce peak GPU memory from 39.7 GiB (Renee) to 6.6 GiB (FP8) for a 3-million-label model while matching or exceeding baseline accuracy.

## Results

On the 3-million-label Amazon-3M dataset, ELMO(FP8) achieves P@1=52.73, P@3=50.38, P@5=48.27 with only 6.6 GiB peak memory, compared to Renee's 52.6/49.7/47.43 at 39.7 GiB — a 6× memory reduction with equal or better accuracy. On the newly introduced 8.6-million-label LF-Paper2Keywords-8.6M dataset, ELMO(BF16) reaches P@1=45.4, P@3=33.58, P@5=27.18 at 18.8 GiB, while Renee collapses to P@1=17.65 at 105.64 GiB due to FP16 gradient overflow. Across all 8 evaluated datasets, ELMO(FP8) reduces peak training memory by 4×–13× relative to Renee while maintaining competitive Precision@k scores.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Renee (the principal end-to-end baseline), ELMO matches or slightly exceeds P@k on most datasets (e.g., P@1 53.4 vs. 52.6 on Amazon-3M) while cutting peak memory by 4×–11×; on the 8.6M-label dataset, Renee's FP16 gradients overflow catastrophically (P@1 drops to 17.65) while ELMO(BF16) achieves 45.4. Compared to sampling-based methods LightXML and CascadeXML, ELMO achieves equal or better P@k on Wiki-500K and Amazon-3M while using 2×–13× less memory; LightXML and CascadeXML fail entirely (OOM) on Amazon-3M and LF-AmazonTitles-1.3M. On label-feature datasets, ELMO(BF16) edges out NGAME (P@1 47.84 vs. 45.72 on LF-WikiSeeAlso-320K) and DEXML (47.84 vs. 46.78) with substantially lower memory (6.57 vs. 16.63 and 38.6 GiB) and faster epoch times. On propensity-scored precision (PSP@k), ELMO(BF16) leads Renee on Wiki-500K (PSP@1 33.32 vs. 32.9) and Amazon-3M (15.65 vs. 14.39), confirming that the low-precision approach does not sacrifice tail-label performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

ELMO builds directly on Renee (Jain et al., 2023), which established that fully end-to-end XMC training is feasible by skipping explicit loss computation to avoid materializing large intermediate buffers. The key departure is replacing Renee's FP16-FP32 mixed-precision training with pure BF16/FP8 training, drawing on techniques from the broader low-precision training literature (Kahan summation, stochastic rounding, FP8 matmul via torchao) that had not previously been applied to the XMC classifier layer. Unlike prior low-precision methods that retain higher precision for the classification head, ELMO demonstrates the classifier can be trained entirely in FP8 without tensor scaling, while also introducing architectural changes (gradient fusion via Triton kernels, chunked classifier updates) that address peak-memory accumulation specific to XMC's enormous linear output layer.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 4x–6x peak memory reduction (39.7 GiB → 6.6 GiB for 3M labels) makes large-output-space training accessible on commodity hardware, and the newly released LF-Paper2Keywords-8.6M dataset is the largest public XMC benchmark, enabling research at scales previously limited to proprietary data. The finding that Renee's FP16-based approach breaks down at 8.6M labels (17.65 P@1 vs. 43.6 for Float32) due to gradient overflow highlights a practical fragility in the prior SOTA. Open questions include whether further bit-width reduction (FP6, FP4) is feasible with tensor scaling, whether the gradient-fusion and chunking strategies transfer to other large-head architectures beyond XMC, and how the approach interacts with distributed training at even larger label counts.
