## Problem

Prior work demonstrated that pretrained LLMs encode latent ICL tasks as vectors in their intermediate representations, but left unanswered why these task vectors exist in the first place and why their effectiveness varies across tasks. This paper addresses that gap by investigating the origin, formation, and quality of task vectors through an encoder-decoder lens, asking how transformers learn to map latent tasks into separable representations and how that encoding quality governs downstream ICL performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250853/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes text sequences with in-context demonstrations (4-shot by default) and a query, then extracts intermediate layer representations at the token position immediately before the target output. From these representations, it computes a Task Decodability (TD) score via k-NN classification (k=10, N=100 samples per task) at the layer where separability peaks (layer 15 for POS tagging, layer 13 for bitwise arithmetic in Llama-3.1-8B). The TD score serves as a geometric proxy for task vector quality and predicts exact-match ICL accuracy; activation patching experiments further produce causal evidence linking representation separability to performance changes of up to ~15%.

## Method

The paper introduces a task encoding-decoding framework in which transformers learn to map latent tasks into distinct, separable regions of representation space (encoding) while simultaneously developing task-specific conditional decoding algorithms. On synthetic mixture-of-sparse-linear-regression tasks, a 12-layer GPT-2 model is trained from scratch, revealing that representation separability (measured by UAP and TD scores) and algorithmic phase transitions (from D-dimensional to r-dimensional regression) emerge in lockstep. The authors then validate this framework on natural ICL tasks (POS tagging, bitwise arithmetic) across Llama-3.1-8B/70B, Gemma-2 2B/9B/27B, and OLMo-7B checkpoints, showing that TD scores positively correlate with ICL accuracy and that activation patching causally links representation quality to performance. A key finding is that finetuning the first 10 layers (where task encoding resides) improves ICL performance by 37% (POS) and 24% (arithmetic) over finetuning the last 10 layers, inverting the common practice of targeting later layers for task adaptation.

## Results

On Llama-3.1-8B with 4-shot ICL, finetuning the first 10 layers raises Task Decodability (TD) from 0.68 to 0.95 (POS tagging) and 0.43 to 0.85 (bitwise arithmetic), and improves exact-match accuracy from 25.5% to 69.4% (POS) and 26.2% to 90.2% (bitwise). Finetuning the last 10 layers leaves TD unchanged (0.68/0.43) and yields only modest accuracy gains (33.9% POS, 66.3% bitwise). Across Gemma-2 (2B/9B/27B) and Llama-3.1-70B, TD scores show a consistent positive correlation with ICL accuracy, and the same tasks (Noun, Pronoun, Verb; AND, NAND, OR, NOR) exhibit the strongest encoding-decoding behavior in every model tested.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a mechanistic interpretability paper rather than a method-vs-method comparison, so the key contrasts are between intervention strategies and across model scales. Finetuning the first 10 layers outperforms finetuning the last 10 layers by 37 percentage points on POS tagging (69.4% vs. 33.9%) and 24 points on bitwise arithmetic (90.2% vs. 66.3%), while also being the only strategy that improves TD scores. Prompting raises TD to near-ceiling levels (0.97/0.90) but yields lower accuracy than early-layer finetuning (34.4%/57.2%), suggesting that explicit task labels help encoding but do not fully substitute for learned decoding algorithms. Across model families, the TD–accuracy correlation holds for Gemma-2 2B/9B/27B and Llama-3.1 70B, with the same subset of tasks (Noun, Pronoun, Verb; AND, NAND, OR, NOR) consistently showing the strongest separability, implying shared pretraining-data-driven task vector structures. The OLMo-7B checkpoint analysis confirms that TD and ICL accuracy rise together gradually over pretraining, and a Mamba-2 8B experiment extends the TD–performance correlation to state-space models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Bayesian two-stage view of ICL (Xie et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2024) and the empirical discovery of task-specific vectors in LLM representations (Hendel et al., 2023; Todd et al., 2023; Merullo et al., 2023), but reframes the question from 'do task vectors exist?' to 'why do they exist and why does their effectiveness vary?' by introducing an encoder-decoder decomposition. It leverages synthetic sparse linear regression tasks (Garg et al., 2022; von Oswald et al., 2023) to study training dynamics and causal mediation techniques (Geiger et al., 2020; Vig et al., 2020) to establish causal links between representation geometry and ICL performance. The key conceptual departure is treating task encoding (separable representations) and task decoding (conditional algorithms) as co-emerging, mutually dependent processes rather than treating task vectors as static artifacts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Task Decodability (TD) metric offers a practical, geometric diagnostic for predicting ICL performance without running full evaluations, and its consistency across Llama-3.1, Gemma-2, OLMo-7B, and Mamba suggests broad applicability. The finding that finetuning early layers outperforms finetuning late layers for ICL (37% vs. 12% on POS) directly challenges the standard practice of targeting later layers or the linear head, though the evidence is limited to Llama-3.1-8B with LoRA. Key limitations include the absence of multi-step reasoning tasks and the requirement that TD be computed over carefully designed, semantically similar task sets. Open questions include how task representations evolve during large-scale pretraining and whether the encoder-decoder decomposition holds for compositional or hierarchical tasks.
