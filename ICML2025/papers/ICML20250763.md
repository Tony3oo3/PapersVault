## Problem

As language models become more capable, AI oversight (using LMs to evaluate or train other LMs) becomes increasingly important, yet no existing metric properly quantifies functional similarity between LMs while adjusting for accuracy, distinguishing different mistakes, and incorporating output probabilities. This gap obscures how model similarity confounds LLM-as-a-judge scores and weak-to-strong training gains, and hides the concerning trend that frontier models are making increasingly correlated errors.

## Input / Output

CAPA takes two models' output probability distributions over MCQ options for each sample in a shared evaluation set, along with ground-truth labels (needed only for the chance-agreement term, not the observed-agreement term). It produces a single scalar similarity score κ_p bounded in (-1, 1), where 0 indicates independence given accuracies, positive values indicate correlated mistakes, and negative values indicate anti-correlation. The metric is defined for MCQs (the dominant LLM benchmark format) but extends to classification and exact-match settings as described in the appendix.

## Method

CAPA (Chance Adjusted Probabilistic Agreement) redefines the observed and chance agreement terms of error consistency to incorporate full probability distributions. The observed agreement c_obs^p is the average over samples of the dot product of the two models' probability vectors over options, capturing partial agreement and distinguishing different wrong predictions. The chance agreement c_exp^p uses each model's average probability on the correct option (p̄) and assumes a uniform distribution over incorrect options, scaled by 1/(|O(x)|−1) to avoid overestimating chance error overlap. The final score κ_p = (c_obs^p − c_exp^p)/(1 − c_exp^p) generalizes error consistency (reducing to it for binary classification with discretized predictions) and provably satisfies all three desiderata—accuracy adjustment, different-mistake sensitivity, and probability incorporation—that no prior metric meets simultaneously.

## Results

CAPA (κ_p) is the only functional similarity metric satisfying all three desiderata: adjusting for accuracy, distinguishing different mistakes, and incorporating output probabilities. In the LLM-as-a-judge experiment on MMLU-Pro, judgment scores show a significant positive correlation with model similarity (average Pearson r=0.84, p<0.01) that persists after controlling for accuracy (partial r=0.35–0.65). In weak-to-strong generalization across 12 model pairs and 15 tasks, similarity inversely correlates with training gain (r=−0.85; partial r=−0.35, p<0.01), and the complementary-knowledge ceiling (+14.1% over initial student) exceeds the prior elicitation-only ceiling (+11.2%). Across 130 LMs on the OpenLLM Leaderboard, κ_p increases monotonically with model capability, indicating growing error correlation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CAPA outperforms all seven baseline metrics on the desiderata in Table 1: Cohen's κ, Scott's π, and Fleiss κ fail to adjust for accuracy and ignore probabilities; Error Consistency adjusts for accuracy but treats two different wrong answers as agreement and discards probability information; %Agreement and %Flips lack accuracy adjustment entirely; Pearson/Matthews correlation adjusts for accuracy but cannot distinguish different mistakes or use probabilities; KL/JSD divergence capture probabilities and different mistakes but inflate similarity for high-accuracy independent models. In the Figure 2 simulation with uncorrelated independent models, CAPA correctly reports 0 similarity across all accuracy levels, while Cohen's κ, JSD, and %Agreement all spuriously increase toward 1 as the second model's accuracy rises from 50% to 90%. In the Table 5 numerical example, Error Consistency overestimates similarity at 1.00 (treating two different wrong answers as agreement), Cohen's κ and Scott's π underreport at 0.00 (ignoring accuracy), while CAPA gives the intermediate 0.21 that reflects the true partial probabilistic overlap. The paper does not frame these as competitive 'wins' but rather as CAPA being the only metric that simultaneously satisfies all three properties needed for LM similarity measurement.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends error consistency (Geirhos et al., 2020), a metric originally designed to quantify overlap in mistakes between image classifiers and humans, into a probabilistic, multi-option-aware metric (CAPA) that also distinguishes between different incorrect predictions. It then applies this metric to two established but previously disconnected threads: LLM-as-a-judge evaluation (building on self-preference findings by Liu et al., 2024 and Panickssery et al., 2024) and weak-to-strong generalization (Burns et al., 2024). The conceptual contribution is unifying these threads under the lens of model similarity, drawing an analogy to affinity bias in human recruitment (Bagues & Perez-Villadoniga, 2012) to argue that judge bias generalizes beyond self-preference to all functionally similar models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The most consequential finding is that model errors become more correlated as capabilities increase (across 130 models from different developers), which directly undermines the independence assumption underlying AI oversight, LM juries, and ensemble-based safety approaches. This creates a concerning feedback loop: as models become harder for humans to evaluate, we defer more to AI oversight, yet the models we rely on for oversight share increasingly similar blind spots. Key open questions include whether causation can be established (can we decorrelate errors without sacrificing capability?), how to extend similarity metrics to free-text generation, and whether the generator-verifier gap widens with model diversity. The paper also calls for the community to release sample-wise predictions alongside benchmark scores to enable such analyses.
