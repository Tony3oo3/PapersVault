## Problem

Existing approaches to auditing fixed predictions in ML models verify recourse on an individual-by-individual basis, requiring access to a representative dataset and providing no guarantees for out-of-sample individuals. This pointwise paradigm can miss confined regions (areas of the feature space where every individual is assigned a fixed prediction) before deployment, leading to undetected harms such as precluding entire subgroups from access to credit or employment. The paper introduces a region-level verification paradigm that certifies model responsiveness over entire regions of the feature space without requiring any data.

## Input / Output

The method takes a linear classifier (coefficient vector w and intercept b), a region of interest R specified as a box with per-feature upper and lower bounds, and an action set A encoded as deterministic actionability constraints (immutability, monotonicity, integrality, K-hot/thermometer encodings, directional linkages, and logical/causal implications). It produces the largest confined box B(u,l) within R — an interpretable set of integer per-feature bounds identifying all individuals with fixed predictions — along with a formal certification of whether R is responsive, confined, or neither, backed by Farkas certificates of infeasibility for the recourse existence problem. The box size is measured as the sum of normalized feature ranges, and multiple confined boxes can be enumerated sequentially via no-good cuts.

## Method

The core contribution is formulating the Region Recourse Verification Problem (RVP) as a Mixed-Integer Quadratically Constrained Program (FCP) that jointly optimizes box bounds and Farkas certificates of infeasibility for the Region Recourse Existence Problem (REP), a MILP checking whether any individual in a candidate box can achieve the desired outcome under the action set. The quadratic constraint arises from the inner product between the box-dependent right-hand side vector and the Farkas certificate variables. A key theoretical result (Theorem 3.2) proves that under 'linear recourse constraints' (integer bounds, K-hot constraints, and directional linkages) satisfying three structural assumptions (no variable in multiple K-hot constraints, no linkage between K-hot variables, and acyclic implications), the linear relaxation of the REP is feasible if and only if the discrete REP is feasible, reducing the number of continuous restrictions to |C|=1 and making the MIQCP tractable. In practice, the FCP is solved with Gurobi in under 5 seconds on real-world datasets, and multiple confined boxes are enumerated by iteratively adding no-good cuts that exclude previously found regions.

## Results

ReVer (the proposed MIQCP-based region verification method) achieves 0% blindspot and 0% loophole rates across all three benchmark datasets (heloc, givemecredit, twitterbot), while all pointwise baselines exhibit blindspots of 2.8–37.4% and loopholes of 0.6–20%. ReVer provides formal certifications (certifies responsive or confined) for 54.2%, 60.1%, and 25.0% of regions on heloc, givemecredit, and twitterbot respectively, whereas pointwise methods can only output (not certify) these labels. Average computation time is under 5 seconds per region, and on two of three datasets ReVer is faster than the Region baseline which audits 100 individual data points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares ReVer against three pointwise baselines that audit recourse over sampled individuals: Data (uses all training points in the region), Region (samples 100 points uniformly from the region), and Score (evaluates points with highest/lowest classifier scores). ReVer dominates all baselines on the critical safety metrics: it achieves 0% blindspot and 0% loophole across all datasets, while the Data baseline produces blindspots of 37.4% (heloc), 12.0% (givemecredit), and 15.0% (twitterbot), and the Region and Score baselines produce loopholes of 20% on twitterbot. The Data baseline is the worst performer overall, with the highest blindspot rates, because it only checks individuals present in the training data and misses out-of-sample fixed predictions. A key structural advantage of ReVer is that it provides formal certifications (via Farkas certificates) rather than mere outputs, meaning its 'certifies responsive' and 'certifies confined' labels carry mathematical guarantees that no pointwise method can offer. In terms of speed, ReVer averages 4.67s (heloc), 0.13s (givemecredit), and 0.07s (twitterbot) per region, making it competitive with or faster than the Region baseline on two of three datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the pointwise recourse verification framework of Kothari et al. (2023) and the actionability constraint formalism of Ustun et al. (2019) from individual-level to region-level auditing. While prior work on global recourse summaries (Rawal & Lakkaraju, 2020; Ley et al., 2023) seeks interpretable action descriptions, this paper instead identifies confined regions in feature space where all individuals are precluded from recourse, providing formal out-of-sample guarantees that pointwise methods cannot offer. The technical core leverages Farkas' lemma certificates embedded in an MIQCP, with a total unimodularity result (Theorem 3.2) that eliminates the need to enumerate discrete variable restrictions under broad constraint classes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ability to certify model responsiveness over entire regions without access to data addresses a critical gap in pre-deployment auditing, particularly for publicly available scoring systems (e.g., criminal justice, medicine) where representative datasets are unavailable. The Pennsylvania SRAI case study demonstrates practical impact by identifying age/gender-based preclusion that pointwise methods would miss. Key limitations include restriction to linear classifiers and box-shaped regions, which limits expressiveness; extending to tree ensembles or more complex region geometries remains open. The finding that at least 100 confined boxes are needed even for <50-feature datasets suggests the combinatorial complexity of fixed predictions is an underappreciated challenge.
