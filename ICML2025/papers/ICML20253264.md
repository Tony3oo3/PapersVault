## Problem

AI policy discourse is split between two positions the author finds inadequate: crafting new AI-specific laws (which are technically difficult to define and easy to circumvent) and dismissing consumer protection as unnecessary bureaucracy. The paper argues that existing, technology-agnostic U.S. consumer protection statutes already provide substantial, inalienable guardrails for AI safety and should be preserved, enforced, and strengthened rather than replaced or abandoned.

## Input / Output

The paper takes as its analytical material the body of U.S. consumer protection statutes (Sherman Act, ECOA, Fair Housing Act, EFTA, data privacy provisions) and documented AI deployment cases (RealPage rent-setting, credit denial, voice-cloning fraud). It produces a structured policy argument identifying where these laws successfully constrain AI harms, where regulatory gaps exist (e.g., tacit algorithmic collusion, AI-enabled authorized fraud), and what legislative or enforcement actions are needed to close those gaps.

## Method

The core contribution is a case-study-driven policy analysis that maps specific consumer protection statutes onto concrete AI deployment scenarios to demonstrate their applicability and limitations. The author tours six legal domains—algorithmic collusion under the Sherman Act, explainability requirements under ECOA, disparate impact doctrine, third-party supervisory authority, the Electronic Fund Transfer Act's treatment of AI-enabled fraud, and the absence of federal data privacy law—using each to illustrate how decades-old, technology-agnostic provisions already shape AI design incentives. The analysis then systematically rebuts two alternative positions: that AI-specific legislation (e.g., defining 'AI model' by compute thresholds) is preferable, and that consumer protection is net-negative bureaucracy. A key mechanism the paper highlights is the CFPB's 2023 reinterpretation of ECOA's adverse-action explanation requirement as effectively barring inscrutable deep learning from credit decisions, showing how regulatory reinterpretation of old statutes can function as a de facto AI design constraint.

## Results

This is a position paper with no empirical experiments, quantitative metrics, or results tables. The paper's key 'findings' are qualitative: it documents that the Sherman Act (1890) was applied to RealPage's algorithmic rent-setting collusion in a 2024 DOJ lawsuit; that the CFPB's 2023 ECOA guidance effectively barred deep-learning credit decisions due to unfaithful explainability; that fraud/scam losses reached ~$8.8B/year (2022, FTC); and that the CrowdStrike 2024 outage may have cost up to $10B. The paper argues these technology-agnostic consumer protection laws already constrain AI deployment and should be preserved and strengthened rather than replaced by AI-specific legislation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare empirical methods or baselines. Instead, it positions its argument (strong consumer protection as an inalienable AI safety defense) against two alternative policy views: (1) the view that AI requires dedicated, AI-specific legislation (e.g., Executive Order 14110's compute-threshold definition), which the author argues is technically intractable due to the lack of consensus on defining 'AI' and easily circumventable; and (2) the deregulatory view that consumer protection is unnecessary bureaucracy, which the author rebuts by citing decades of unaddressed privacy harms in the free market and the EU AI Act's consumer-protection-centered design. The paper also contrasts the U.S. approach (patchwork of existing statutes) with the EU AI Act (new, risk-based, AI-specific law) as complementary rather than competing strategies.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper carves new ground by repurposing the existing body of U.S. consumer protection law—Sherman Act, ECOA, Fair Housing Act, EFTA—as a primary AI safety mechanism, rather than treating AI regulation as a separate legislative domain. It draws on legal scholarship (algorithmic collusion under antitrust law, disparate impact doctrine, explainable AI in credit underwriting) and recent policy developments (CFPB's 2023 guidance on AI credit denials, the DOJ's RealPage case, the EU AI Act) to argue that technology-agnostic statutes already constrain AI deployment. The paper explicitly positions itself against two prior policy framings: the push for AI-specific legislation (e.g., Executive Order 14110) and the deregulatory view that consumer protection is mere red tape, arguing instead that existing laws are both sufficient in scope and inalienable in their applicability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete, jurisdiction-specific roadmap for AI safety practitioners and policymakers who want to leverage existing legal infrastructure rather than await new AI-specific statutes, and it identifies specific gaps (tacit algorithmic collusion beyond explicit data-sharing, EFTA's failure to cover AI-enabled authorized fraud, absence of a federal data privacy law) that need patching. Its practical impact is heightened by the current U.S. deregulatory climate, where the fall of the Chevron doctrine and attempts to curtail the CFPB threaten the very interpretive flexibility that makes old statutes applicable to new technologies. Open questions include whether consumer protection frameworks can meaningfully address agentic AI systems, whether the approach scales to non-consumer domains (military, healthcare, labor) that the paper explicitly excludes, and whether the tension between explainability requirements and the potential for AI to reduce discrimination (e.g., in credit) can be resolved without sacrificing either goal.
