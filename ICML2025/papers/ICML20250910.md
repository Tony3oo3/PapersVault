## Problem

Standard normalizing flows cannot model heavy-tailed distributions because Lipschitz transformations of Gaussian bases remain light-tailed. The prevailing fix—using heavy-tailed base distributions like Student's T—introduces extreme values as inputs to neural network layers, which induces heavy-tailed gradients and causes poor optimisation. This paper asks whether heavy tails can be produced without ever passing extreme values through the network.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250910/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For density estimation, the method consumes i.i.d. samples from a heavy-tailed target and outputs a fitted density evaluated via log-likelihood; for variational inference it takes an unnormalised target and produces posterior samples via the reparameterisation trick. The TTF layer operates element-wise on each marginal with per-dimension parameters (μ, σ, λ₊, λ₋), and the erfc-based transformation is implemented via PyTorch's torch.special.erfc, requiring no custom kernels. In the two-stage TTFfix variant, tail shape parameters are pre-estimated per marginal using a Hill double-bootstrap estimator before the flow body is trained.

## Method

The core contribution is the Tail Transform Flow (TTF) layer, a final non-Lipschitz transformation R(z; λ₊, λ₋) = μ + σ·s/λₛ·[erfc(|z|/√2)^(−λₛ) − 1] applied after a standard Gaussian-based normalizing flow body. Because the body is Lipschitz, its output has light tails (by Theorem 1.2), so no extreme values ever reach the neural network layers; the TTF layer then converts those light tails into Generalized Pareto tails with tunable shape parameters λ₊ and λ₋, provably placing the output in the Fréchet domain of attraction. The transformation is monotonically increasing, continuously differentiable, and its inverse and Jacobian are closed-form, making it fully compatible with automatic differentiation. Two training strategies are offered: TTF jointly optimises all parameters, while TTFfix first estimates tail weights via EVT estimators and then freezes them during flow training, improving optimisation stability especially in variational inference.

## Results

On real data density estimation, TTF achieves the best negative log-likelihood on 3 of 4 datasets: Insurance (1.37), Fama 5 (4.61), and S&P 500 (317.56), while TTFfix is best on CLIMDEX (−2090.91). On synthetic data with d=50 and heavy tails (ν=0.5), TTF/TTFfix achieve 3.68 NLL/dim versus 7.49 for gTAF and non-convergence for standard NFs. For variational inference, TTFfix achieves the best ESS_e for heavy tails (ν≤2), reaching 0.98 at d=5, ν=2, while mTAF and gTAF drop below 0.1 ESS_e in the most challenging settings (ν=0.5, d=50).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the tail-adaptive flow methods of Laszkiewicz et al. (2022), TTF consistently outperforms both mTAF and gTAF on all four real datasets; the gap is largest on CLIMDEX where TTF achieves −2214.28 versus −2121.38 for mTAF (a ~93 NLL improvement). Fixing tail parameters (mTAF) outperforms joint learning (gTAF), but TTF with jointly learned tail parameters still beats mTAF, suggesting the final-layer approach is more robust to tail parameter estimation. Against COMET flows (McDonald et al., 2022), TTF wins on Insurance, Fama 5, S&P 500, and CLIMDEX, though COMET remains competitive (within ~1 NLL on Fama 5). The alternative base distributions from Amiri et al. (2022) (Gaussian mixture m_normal and Generalised Normal g_normal) perform comparably to the standard Gaussian baseline and fail to converge for heavy tails (ν≤1) in the synthetic experiments, confirming that merely changing the base distribution is insufficient. Overall, TTF/TTFfix dominate for heavy-tailed targets while all methods perform similarly for near-Gaussian tails (ν=30).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant approach to heavy-tailed normalizing flows (Jaini et al. 2020; Laszkiewicz et al. 2022; Liang et al. 2022), which uses a Student's T base distribution, by instead keeping a Gaussian base and appending a final non-Lipschitz transformation (TTF) that converts Gaussian tails to GPD tails. The TTF transformation is motivated by extreme value theory and the carrier-function literature (Papastathopoulos & Tawn 2013; Naveau et al. 2016), but is the first to provide a closed-form, differentiable, parameterised multivariate tail layer compatible with standard NF architectures. The key conceptual departure is the argument that heavy-tailed inputs to neural network layers degrade optimisation, making the 'obvious' heavy-tailed base distribution approach suboptimal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The TTF transformation gives practitioners a drop-in final layer that provably produces Fréchet-domain tails with tunable shape parameters, improving density estimation and variational inference for heavy-tailed targets without sacrificing the stability of Gaussian-based optimisation. Open questions include whether the method can incorporate tail dependence or max-stable properties, whether it extends cleanly to diffusion/flow-matching models (the authors sketch this motivation but do not test it), and why real VI applications show smaller gains than the synthetic experiments suggest. A practical limitation is that TTF always converts Gaussian tails to heavy tails, so it cannot exactly reproduce light-tailed marginals.
