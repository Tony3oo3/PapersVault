## Problem

GFlowNets traditionally require acyclic graph environments, but many natural settings (e.g., group-based permutation generation, RL environments) inherently contain cycles. The prior theory for non-acyclic GFlowNets (Brunswic et al., 2024) relied on measure-theoretic machinery that is difficult to extend, and left open key questions about the nature of flows, the role of loss stability, and connections to reinforcement learning in the cyclic setting.

## Input / Output

The method takes a finite directed graph with cycles (containing a unique source s₀ and sink s_f, with all states reachable from s₀ and reaching s_f) and a positive reward function on terminal states. It produces a forward policy over outgoing edges at each state that, when used to sample trajectories from s₀ to s_f, matches the normalized reward distribution over terminal states. A key structural difference from the acyclic case is that the trajectory space is infinite (arbitrarily long paths exist due to cycles), and state/edge flows represent expected visitation counts rather than probabilities, so they can exceed 1.

## Method

The paper rebuilds non-acyclic GFlowNet theory from scratch in a discrete setting, defining flows as expected number of visits under a backward-policy-induced trajectory distribution (rather than visitation probabilities, which fail to satisfy flow-matching in cyclic graphs). It proves that a unique forward policy exists for any positive backward policy satisfying detailed balance, and that with a fixed backward policy, any standard acyclic loss (DB, TB, FM) converges to the correct solution without requiring loss stability. The key practical insight is that minimizing expected trajectory length is exactly equivalent to minimizing total state flow, which the paper operationalizes as a simple λ·F(s) regularizer added to the standard DB loss. The paper further generalizes the GFlowNet–entropy-regularized RL equivalence (Tiapkin et al., 2024) to non-acyclic graphs via a deterministic MDP construction, and empirically shows that the scale at which flow error is computed (log-flow vs. linear-flow) is the dominant factor governing training stability, more so than the specific loss form.

## Results

On the non-acyclic permutation environment (n=20, ~2.4×10^18 states), the proposed DB loss in ΔlogF scale with state flow regularization (λ=10^{-5}) achieves the best sampling accuracy: C(k) L1 distance of 0.017±0.002 and relative reward error ΔR of 0.035±0.002, with expected trajectory length E[nτ]=7.55±0.50. In contrast, ΔF-scale losses (both DB and SDB from Brunswic et al., 2024) fail to match the reward distribution at n=20 (C(k) L1=0.453, ΔR=0.343) despite achieving the smallest E[nτ]=2.00. On the 20×20×20×20 hypergrid, ΔlogF losses with state flow regularization correctly learn the normalizing constant Z and sample accurately, while ΔF-scale losses exhibit noticeable bias in the learned forward policy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external GFlowNet methods but instead compares different loss formulations and flow scales as the key design choices for non-acyclic GFlowNet training. The stable DB loss (SDB) in ΔF scale from Brunswic et al. (2024) is the primary baseline: it achieves the smallest expected trajectory length (E[nτ]≈2.0 at n=20) but fails to match the reward distribution (C(k) L1=0.453, ΔR=0.343), confirming the paper's scaling hypothesis that flow-scale losses are biased toward small-flow solutions. The standard DB loss in ΔlogF scale without regularization leads to unbounded trajectory length on larger environments, validating the instability concern from Brunswic et al. (2024). The paper's proposed state flow regularization (adding λF(s) to the loss) resolves this: with λ=10^{-5}, DB in ΔlogF scale achieves the best sampling accuracy (C(k) L1=0.017, ΔR=0.035 at n=20) at the cost of a moderate increase in E[nτ] (7.55 vs. 2.00). A smaller λ=10^{-3} gives a trade-off with E[nτ]=3.23 but higher sampling error (C(k) L1=0.041). Overall, the paper shows that loss stability in the sense of Brunswic et al. (2024) is not the right criterion; instead, the flow scale (ΔlogF vs. ΔF) combined with state flow regularization is the practical lever for controlling both trajectory length and sampling accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the GFlowNet foundations of Bengio et al. (2023) and the first non-acyclic GFlowNet theory of Brunswic et al. (2024), replacing the latter's measure-theoretic machinery with a simpler discrete-state framework. It also generalizes the GFlowNet–entropy-regularized RL equivalence of Tiapkin et al. (2024) to the non-acyclic setting. The key conceptual departure from Brunswic et al. is showing that flows in cyclic environments must be defined as expected visit counts rather than visitation probabilities, and that their loss-stability concept is unnecessary when the backward policy is fixed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The equivalence between minimizing expected trajectory length and minimizing total flow (Proposition 3.12) provides a clean, actionable objective for training non-acyclic GFlowNets, and the proposed state flow regularization offers a practical recipe that works even with standard unstable losses. The generalization of the RL connection opens the door to applying the full toolkit of RL algorithms (off-policy methods, Q-learning, etc.) to non-acyclic GFlowNets. Open questions include whether these results extend to continuous non-acyclic environments, how to handle environments where all states are terminal, and whether the scaling hypothesis about flow-error scale generalizes to larger or more complex environments.
