## Problem

Stochastic difference-of-convex (DC) optimization with nonsmooth components under time-varying data distributions remains largely unaddressed. Existing online stochastic DCA methods require a Borel measurable subgradient selector that is difficult to implement in practice, and their convergence analyses do not account for distributional shifts across iterations. A fundamental obstacle is the lack of tight sample-average-approximation (SAA) error bounds for set-valued subdifferential mappings in the nonsmooth case, which prevents matching the sample complexity of smooth or measurable-selector settings.

## Input / Output

At each iteration t, the algorithm consumes only fresh i.i.d. samples drawn from the current (possibly shifted) distributions P_{ξ,t} and P_{ζ,t}, discarding all historical data. The sample sizes N_{g,t} and N_{h,t} for the convex and concave components are determined adaptively by a stepsize-norm condition: when the previous step ‖d̄_{t-1}‖ is large (far from criticality), fewer samples suffice; as steps shrink, sample sizes grow to tighten estimation. The output is a sequence {x_t} whose accumulation points are DC critical points of the true objective f = g − h almost surely, with the sample-size requirement ∑(N_{g,t}^{−α_g} + N_{h,t}^{−α_h}) < ∞ for α_g ∈ (0,1/2) and α_h ∈ (0,1).

## Method

The algorithm (ospDCA) solves a proximal convex subproblem at each step by linearizing the concave component h with a sampled subgradient ȳ_t ∈ ∂h̄_t(x_t), using only current-distribution samples. The key theoretical tool is a novel O(√(p/n)) pointwise SAA convergence rate for the Hausdorff distance between the empirical and population subdifferential sets, derived by converting the set-valued error into a supremum over support functions and applying McDiarmid's bounded-difference inequality. This rate enables the concave component to tolerate a weaker summability exponent (α_h ∈ (0,1) vs. α_g ∈ (0,1/2)) because the Cauchy–Schwarz step elevates the subdifferential SAA error to second order in the descent inequality. For distributional robustness, convergence is guaranteed under the assumption that the cumulative Wasserstein-1 distance between successive distributions is finite, a condition satisfied by finite-shift or decaying-drift scenarios. The adaptive sampling rule (Stepsize Norm Condition) sets N_{g,t} and N_{h,t} so that the SAA error is dominated by the previous step's squared norm, reducing sample cost in early iterations while preserving the summability condition needed for almost-sure convergence.

## Results

The paper evaluates its adaptive ospDCA on synthetic online sparse robust regression problems with p=50 and p=200 dimensions, tracking the distance ‖β_t − β_opt‖ and computational time over iterations. No specific numerical error values are reported; results are presented entirely as convergence plots (Figures 1 and 3). The adaptive ospDCA consistently achieves the fastest convergence to the optimal solution across all experiments, using fewer samples in early iterations (when the step size is large) and more samples in later iterations (near the optimum), as shown in Figures 2 and 4. The time-varying distribution shift is modeled as δ_t = (−1)^t · 100t^(−2) · 1_p, ensuring bounded cumulative Wasserstein-1 distance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares adaptive ospDCA against four baselines on online sparse robust regression: (1) non-adaptive ospDCA with sublinearly growing sample size t^2.1, (2) S(p)DCA from Le Thi et al. (2024) with an added proximal term, (3) ospDCA with a fixed 100 samples per iteration, and (4) ospDCA with a fixed 1000 samples per iteration. Across all experiments (p=50 and p=200), adaptive ospDCA consistently outperforms all four baselines in convergence speed (distance to β_opt vs. iterations). The key advantage is in sample efficiency: during early iterations when the iterate is far from the critical point, adaptive ospDCA uses fewer samples than the fixed-1000 and t^2.1 baselines, saving computation; as the iterate approaches the optimum, it increases sample size to maintain accuracy. Compared to S(p)DCA, which aggregates all past samples, adaptive ospDCA is more robust to distribution shifts since it discards historical data. The fixed-100-sample baseline is outperformed because it lacks sufficient accuracy near the optimum, while the fixed-1000 baseline wastes computation in early iterations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the online stochastic DC algorithm (ospDCA) of Le Thi et al. (2024) by removing the requirement for a Borel measurable subgradient selector and by handling time-varying data distributions, a setting absent from prior stochastic DCA work. The classical deterministic DCA framework (Le Thi and Pham Dinh, 2018) and its stochastic nonsmooth extension (Le Thi et al., 2022) provide the algorithmic backbone, but this paper's key theoretical departure is a novel O(√(p/n)) pointwise convergence rate for the SAA error of set-valued subdifferential mappings, derived via a support-function analysis that complements existing uniform convergence results (Davis and Drusvyatskiy, 2022; Ruan, 2024). The adaptive sampling design with distinct rates for the convex and concave components is motivated by the DC structure and the improved subdifferential SAA rate, distinguishing it from uniform-sample approaches in prior stochastic DC methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The O(√(p/n)) subdifferential SAA rate is a result of independent interest that enables variance-reduction-style techniques for nonsmooth stochastic optimization, potentially benefiting a broader class of subgradient-based methods beyond DC programming. The algorithm's practical advantage—selecting any subgradient from the sampled set without a measurable selector—makes it more implementable than prior online stochastic DCA. Open questions include deriving non-asymptotic iteration complexity (the paper only establishes asymptotic almost-sure convergence) and extending the analysis to non-convex feasible sets or more complex distribution-shift models. The empirical validation is limited to synthetic sparse regression, leaving open whether the adaptive strategy's advantages persist on real-world streaming data with more complex shift patterns.
