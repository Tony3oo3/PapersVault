## Problem

Neural scaling laws have driven LLM development toward ever-larger models and datasets, but this approach suffers from diminishing returns, linearly growing carbon emissions (while performance scales only with the 0.08 power of emissions), and an impending data bottleneck. No systematic theoretical framework exists for *downscaling*—reducing model size, data requirements, and compute while preserving or even improving performance—leaving practitioners without principled guidance for efficient model development.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20253274/cropped_page6_idx4.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline takes a single pre-trained LLM (e.g., LLaMA-3-8B) and a general pre-training corpus as input, along with domain-specific datasets for specialization. Rather than producing one smaller model, it outputs a collection of pruned SLM variants (e.g., 8+ models of ~1B parameters each) that are domain-adapted and then ensembled into a unified system. The ensemble is designed to match or exceed the original model's performance at the same total computational budget, with the number of ensemble members determined analytically by Proposition 4.1 rather than by grid search.

## Method

The core theoretical contribution is Proposition 4.1, which combines the P2 post-pruning scaling law (Chen et al., 2024b) with the deep ensemble scaling law (Lobacheva et al., 2021) to derive a closed-form condition on the number of pruned models n required for the ensemble loss to fall below the base model loss at equal compute cost. The practical pipeline executes four stages: (1) Bayesian active learning selects informative training samples while unstructured, semi-structured, or structured pruning decomposes the LLM into multiple SLM chunks; (2) each pruned variant undergoes domain-continual pre-training guided by the D-CPT law to find optimal general-to-domain data mixing ratios without catastrophic forgetting; (3) post-training is calibrated using the P2 law to predict and minimize post-compression loss; and (4) the domain-specialized SLMs are combined via ensemble techniques (router-based selection, token-level integration, or output fusion). The key insight is that distributing a fixed compute budget across multiple smaller, domain-specialized models exploits the 'Memory Split Advantage' to achieve lower expected loss than concentrating that budget in a single large model.

## Results

The paper establishes that CO2 emissions from LLM training scale linearly with both model parameters (N) and training data size (D), yielding CO2eq = (K₁+K₂)·N·D, while test loss decreases only logarithmically (L ∝ N⁻⁰·⁰⁸, L ∝ D⁻⁰·¹). This implies performance P ∝ CO2eq^0.08, meaning a 10% performance gain requires approximately 329% more carbon emissions. The central theoretical result (Proposition 4.1) demonstrates that for LLaMA-3-8B, an ensemble of 8 or more pruned 1B-parameter models would outperform the original 8B model at the same total computational cost, with the inequality holding for all n > 7 under the fitted parameters (α=−1.57, γ=1.08, a=0.83, b=0.83, δ=0.29).

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not conduct head-to-head empirical comparisons against specific baseline methods. Instead, it contrasts the dominant scaling paradigm (Kaplan and Chinchilla scaling laws) with its proposed downscaling framework at the theoretical level. The key contrast is between the logarithmic performance gains from scaling (L ∝ N⁻⁰·⁰⁸) versus the linear carbon cost increase, showing that scaling is environmentally unsustainable. The proposed downscaling pipeline (pruning + domain-continual pre-training + ensemble) is argued to match or exceed the original model's performance at equal compute, based on combining the P2 post-pruning law with the deep ensemble scaling law. The paper acknowledges limitations: fact recall degrades at ~30% pruning while in-context learning survives up to 60–70% pruning (Jin et al., 2023), and ensemble approaches introduce latency and routing challenges that remain unresolved.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes four existing scaling-law frameworks—the P2 post-pruning law (Chen et al., 2024b), the deep-ensemble power law (Lobacheva et al., 2021), data-pruning beyond power laws (Sorscher et al., 2023), and the D-CPT domain-adaptation law (Que et al., 2024)—into a unified 'downscaling' theoretical framework (Proposition 4.1, Equation 13). It explicitly positions itself as a counter-narrative to the Kaplan and Chinchilla scaling laws, arguing that the field's dominant paradigm of uniform upscaling is both computationally and environmentally unsustainable. The paper does not introduce new empirical methods but rather repurposes and algebraically combines prior results to argue for a paradigm shift toward pruned ensembles of small, domain-specialized models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The carbon-emission analysis (Equations 7–10) provides a compelling quantitative argument that performance gains scale only as CO₂^0.08, making continued upscaling exponentially wasteful—a framing that could influence resource-allocation decisions in the field. However, the central theoretical claim (that 8+ pruned 1B ensembles outperform LLaMA-3-8B at equal cost) rests on 'loose assumptions' borrowed from a CNN ensemble study and is entirely unvalidated empirically; the proposed pipeline in Figure 5 remains a blueprint with no experimental results. Key open questions include whether the ensemble proposition holds for transformer architectures at realistic scales, how heterogeneous-vocabulary and latency challenges in LLM ensembling affect practical deployment, and whether the non-uniform capability degradation documented by Jin et al. (2023) undermines the assumption that pruned ensembles can uniformly recover the original model's performance.
