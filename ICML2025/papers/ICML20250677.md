## Problem

When a decision-maker (leader) influences multiple competitive learning agents (followers) in a Stackelberg game, the agents' stochastic adaptation introduces both drift and noise into the decision-maker's optimization landscape, yet the decision-maker lacks a priori knowledge of agent objectives or update rules. Prior work provided only asymptotic guarantees for equilibrium tracking in time-varying games or assumed stationary agent behavior, leaving open the question of finite-time convergence rates for this coupled, nonstationary system and how to algorithmically control the induced drift relative to agent noise.

## Input / Output

The decision-maker deploys a scalar or vector action u_t ∈ U ⊆ R^d for τ consecutive steps (one epoch) while n agents independently update their joint action x_t ∈ X ⊆ R^m via ρ-contracting stochastic algorithms; the decision-maker observes a stochastic environment sample z_t = (x_t, ξ_t) and a loss/gradient query. The output is an updated action u_{t+1} with finite-time guarantees: the naive repeated gradient method (RGM) converges to a performatively stable equilibrium in O(log(1/ε) + σ²/ε) epochs, while the strategic derivative-free method (DFM) converges to a Stackelberg equilibrium in O(d²/ε²) epochs. The epoch length τ is set adaptively as O(log(1/ε_τ) + σ_a²/ε_τ), where σ_a² is the agents' noise parameter and ε_τ is a per-epoch tolerance chosen via the drift-to-noise analysis.

## Method

The paper's core technical contribution is a drift-to-noise decomposition of the agents' equilibrium tracking error (Proposition 3.1), showing that E‖x_t − x_t*‖² ≲ (contraction)^t + σ_a²/(1−ρ²) + (Δ_a/(1−ρ²))², which identifies two regimes (low and high drift-to-noise) with distinct optimal step-sizes and iteration complexities. Building on this, the paper designs epoch-based algorithms (Algorithm 1) where the decision-maker holds u_t fixed for τ steps while agents run ρ-contracting algorithms, with τ chosen to ensure the induced drift Δ_a = L_eq·Δ is controlled relative to the decision-maker's gradient noise σ. In the naive setting, a stochastic repeated gradient method (RGM) that ignores the implicit gradient term converges to a performatively stable equilibrium; in the strategic setting, a single-point derivative-free method (DFM) that estimates the full decision-dependent gradient via stochastic queries converges to a Stackelberg equilibrium. Both methods employ a staged (super-epoch) schedule that progressively decreases the step-size to reduce bias, and the agents are incentivized to use stage-based ρ-contracting algorithms (e.g., SGP with geometrically decaying step-sizes) to minimize their own variance floor.

## Results

The paper establishes finite-time convergence rates for two epoch-based decision-maker algorithms in stochastic Stackelberg games. The naive repeated gradient method (RGM) converges to an ε-approximate performatively stable equilibrium in O(log(1/ε) + σ²/ε) epochs, matching the optimal rate for single-player stochastic optimization. The strategic derivative-free method (DFM) converges to an ε-approximate Stackelberg equilibrium in O(d²/ε²) epochs, matching the optimal rate for single-point derivative-free convex optimization up to log factors. For the agents' equilibrium tracking, the stage-based stochastic gradient play achieves O(σ_a²/(μ²ε)) steps in the low drift-to-noise regime (Δ_a/σ_a < μ²/(4L_a³)), with the drift-to-noise ratio governing the transition between deterministic-like and noise-dominated behavior.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external baselines; instead it contrasts two of its own proposed algorithms under different gradient information assumptions. The RGM (naive decision-maker) is more sample-efficient, requiring O(log(1/ε) + σ²/ε) epochs, but converges only to the performatively stable equilibrium u^ps, which is suboptimal relative to the true Stackelberg equilibrium u*. The DFM (strategic decision-maker) requires O(d²/ε²) epochs—decidedly worse due to extra estimator bias from the derivative-free gradient estimate—but converges to the true Stackelberg equilibrium. The practical tradeoff is governed by the performative gap ‖u* − u^ps‖: as agent reactivity L_eq grows, this gap widens, shrinking the effective tolerance for RGM and pushing its sample complexity past that of DFM (Figure 2). Both methods match the optimal rates of their single-player counterparts, and the epoch length τ is set to O(log(1/ε_τ) + σ_a²/ε_τ) in both cases, with the per-epoch agent tolerance ε_τ tuned via the drift-to-noise analysis to control induced drift relative to agent noise.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the stochastic optimization under distributional drift framework of Cutler et al. (2023) from single-player settings to multi-agent stochastic monotone games, requiring novel analysis since games lack a single cost function. It adapts single-point derivative-free methods (Agarwal et al., 2010; Drusvyatskiy et al., 2022) to an epoch-based Stackelberg framework where the decision-maker must account for agents' ongoing stochastic learning. The work also builds on performative prediction (Perdomo et al., 2020) and multiplayer performative prediction (Narang et al., 2023), but departs from both by explicitly modeling agents as running stochastic algorithms that have not yet equilibrated, introducing time-varying games and a drift-to-noise ratio that governs convergence.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The drift-to-noise ratio characterization provides a principled framework for designing epoch lengths and step-sizes in multi-agent learning systems, with practical implications for recommendation systems, auction design, and multi-agent control where agents adapt to interventions. Key open questions include: (i) adaptive estimation of the intrinsic parameters (Lipschitz constants, contraction rates) that appear in the bounds but are unavailable in practice; (ii) characterizing the performative gap to determine when the suboptimal performatively stable equilibrium is 'good enough' versus the true Stackelberg equilibrium; and (iii) extending beyond strongly monotone games to non-monotone settings where solution concepts are less well-defined. The empirical validation is limited to small-scale quadratic games and Kelly auctions, leaving open whether the theoretical rates hold in more complex, high-dimensional settings.
