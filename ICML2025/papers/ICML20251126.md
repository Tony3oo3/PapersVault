## Problem

Standard preference aggregation methods produce total rankings that force arbitration of conflicting individual preferences without revealing the existence or severity of disagreement. In many practical settings—grant funding, college rankings, model alignment—a total order is unnecessary and can lead to worse decisions, yet existing social choice frameworks offer no principled way to abstain from comparison. This paper introduces selective aggregation, a paradigm that represents collective preferences as a partial order (tiered ranking) where items with conflicting preferences are placed in the same tier, explicitly revealing disagreement rather than silently resolving it.

## Input / Output

The method takes pairwise preferences π_{i,j}^k ∈ {-1, 0, 1} from p users over n items (convertible from labels, ratings, or full rankings) and a dissent parameter τ ∈ [0, 0.5). It produces a selective ranking S_τ—a partition of items into m ≤ n tiers with a total order among tiers—where any comparison between items in different tiers is guaranteed to overrule at most 100·τ% of users. A solution path algorithm additionally returns the finite set of all distinct selective rankings across τ, enabling practitioners to inspect the full comparability–disagreement frontier and choose τ post hoc (e.g., the smallest τ yielding a top tier with ≤ k items).

## Method

The core mechanism is a graph-based algorithm: for a given τ, it builds a directed graph over items with an arc i→j whenever at least (1−τ)p users prefer i over j (i.e., w_{i,j} > τp). Strongly connected components of this graph become tiers (items in a cycle must be incomparable to satisfy the disagreement constraint), and a topological sort of the condensation DAG orders the tiers. This solves the combinatorial optimization SPA_τ—maximizing the number of valid pairwise comparisons subject to a per-pair disagreement budget—exactly and in O(n²p) time. A solution path variant restricts τ to a finite set W of at most ⌈p/2⌉ critical values and incrementally adds arcs, reusing the graph structure across iterations. The paper proves the solution is unique for τ < 0.5, recovers Condorcet winners and Smith sets when they exist, and provides stability guarantees: comparisons in the selective ranking are invariant to missing preferences (imputed as indifference) and to the addition of new items (which can only collapse tiers, never invert or split them).

## Results

Across five real-world datasets (NBA, Survivor, Law School, CSRankings, Sushi), SPA achieves 0.0% disagreement rate at τ=0 and 0.0% inversion under both sampling (10% dropped preferences) and adversarial (10% flipped preferences) perturbations for all selective rankings, while standard methods (Borda, Copeland, MC4, Kemeny) show 6.4–42.6% disagreement rates and 0.8–45.8% adversarial inversion. On the DICES toxicity detection task, SPA-aggregated labels yield 28.2% label error (vs. 37.8% for majority vote) and 29.9% prediction error on held-out users (vs. 38.4% for Borda), with ~60% of users achieving individual BER ≤ 40% under SPA versus ~20% under baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SPA dominates all standard baselines on robustness: it achieves 0.0% inversion under both 10% sampling and 10% adversarial perturbation across all five datasets, while Borda, Copeland, and MC4 show 0.8–19.0% adversarial inversion and Kemeny (heuristic) reaches 45.8% on CSRankings. On disagreement rate, SPA_max still matches or beats standard methods on four of five datasets (e.g., 6.4% vs. 8.3% for Borda on NBA; 0.1% vs. 12.2% for Copeland on CSRankings), and only ties on Sushi where all methods converge to 42.6%. Kemeny (exact) is the closest competitor on disagreement (4.1% vs. 3.1% for SPA_max on Law School) but is computationally intractable on CSRankings, forcing a heuristic that is both suboptimal (13.7% vs. 12.2% disagreement) and far less robust (45.8% vs. 0.0% adversarial inversion). On the DICES toxicity detection task, SPA-aggregated labels reduce label error from 37.8% (majority vote) to 28.2% and prediction error from 38.4% (pairwise Borda) to 29.9% on held-out users, while also improving individual-level fairness: ~60% of users achieve BER ≤ 40% under SPA versus ~20% under Borda or majority vote.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by reframing preference aggregation from an arbitration problem (produce a total order) to a selective one (produce a partial order with a controlled dissent parameter τ). It draws conceptual inspiration from selective classification (sacrificing coverage for error reduction) and from bucket-ordering literature (Achab et al., Andrieu et al.), but departs from both by treating same-tier items as 'incomparable' rather than 'equivalent,' and by formulating the problem as a constrained optimization over tiered rankings solved via strongly connected components and topological sort. The impossibility theorems (Arrow, Condorcet) motivate the paradigm shift, but the authors note that prior social choice work largely ignored the option of abstention because applications like elections require a single winner.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The selective ranking framework provides a principled way to make disagreement visible and controllable in preference aggregation, with clean theoretical guarantees on recovery of Condorcet winners and stability to missing preferences or new items. The empirical finding that 4 out of 5 real-world datasets do not support a total order even at τ→0.5 suggests that standard rankings impose structure that the data does not warrant, with practical consequences for college rankings, grant funding, and model alignment. A key limitation is conservatism under sparse data (the ranking collapses to a single tier), which the authors acknowledge as impractical for large-scale settings; extending the framework with probabilistic assumptions or active elicitation is an open direction.
