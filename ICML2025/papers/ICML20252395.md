## Problem

Policy tree search algorithms like PHS* and LevinTS are trained via the Bootstrap process, which discards all data from failed search attempts. On hard 'needle-in-haystack' problems, most early search attempts fail, wasting the majority of search effort and making learning prohibitively costly. This paper addresses the gap by learning subgoal-conditioned policies that can be trained from both successful and failed search trees, substantially improving sample efficiency.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single-agent deterministic search problem defined by a state space, action set, transition function, initial state, and a Boolean goal test, along with an expansion budget B. At each node expansion, a VQVAE subgoal generator produces k reconstructed subgoal states from the current state and codebook entries, which condition the low-level policy; the high-level policy weights these to produce a final action distribution. Search is performed at the original action level (not the subgoal level as in HIPS-ε), so imperfect subgoal reconstructions do not break reachability, and the output is a solution node with its trajectory.

## Method

The core contribution is a hierarchical subgoal-guided policy π^SG that combines k subgoal-conditioned low-level policies with a high-level policy over subgoals via weighted geometric mixing (Eq. 4), plugged into existing BFS-based policy tree search algorithms (PHS*, LevinTS). A VQVAE subgoal generator, trained on (s_cur, s_tar) state pairs, produces k candidate subgoal states at each search node. The key training innovation is leveraging non-solution search trees: the underlying graph of a failed search is clustered with the Louvain algorithm to extract meaningful state pairs, which train both the VQVAE and the low-level policy without requiring a complete solution. Solution trajectories are additionally segmented into sub-trajectories to train the high-level policy and heuristic. This allows the method to learn from every search attempt, not just successful ones, while retaining the completeness guarantees of the underlying policy tree search framework.

## Results

On standard test sets, PHS*(π^SG) achieves the lowest average expansions across most domains: 378.58 on Box-World, 103.23 on CraftWorld, and 53.34 on BoulderDash, compared to 767.06, 172.04, and 53.65 for PHS*(π) respectively. On hard problem instances, PHS*(π^SG) solves all 10,000 training problems in BoulderDash (85.5M expansions) and TSP (20.6M expansions), while PHS*(π) and LevinTS(π) solve only 19 and 0 problems respectively within the same 16-hour budget. On the hard test set, PHS*(π^SG) requires only 41.93 average expansions on TSP and 172.32 on BoulderDash, versus 502,624 for WA* on TSP and 271,637 on BoulderDash.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PHS*(π^SG) outperforms PHS*(π) in average expansions on Box-World (378.58 vs. 767.06), CraftWorld (103.23 vs. 172.04), and BoulderDash (53.34 vs. 53.65), while LevinTS(π^SG) beats LevinTS(π) on Box-World (411.38 vs. 605.05) and Sokoban (496.87 vs. 1,177.26). Against WA*(1.5), PHS*(π^SG) requires 6–22× fewer expansions across all domains, though WA* finds marginally shorter solutions (e.g., 66.19 vs. 66.75 on Box-World). HIPS-ε is the weakest baseline: it solves 0/100 BoulderDash problems and only 19/100 CraftWorld problems, while PHS*(π^SG) solves 100/100 on both. On hard instances, the gap widens dramatically—PHS*(π^SG) solves all 10,000 BoulderDash and TSP training problems in 85.5M and 20.6M expansions respectively, while PHS*(π) and LevinTS(π) solve only 19 and 0 problems within the same 16-hour budget. The only domain where the subgoal approach underperforms is Sokoban, where PHS*(π) initially solves more problems with fewer expansions, attributed to the Louvain clustering failing to find useful structure in that state space.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the policy tree search framework (LevinTS, PHS*) by replacing the single learned policy with a hierarchical subgoal-conditioned policy, and modifies the Bootstrap training process to extract learning signal from failed search trees via Louvain graph clustering. It departs from prior subgoal search methods (kSubS, AdaSubS, HIPS, HIPS-ε) in two key ways: (1) search is performed at the original action level rather than the learned subgoal level, preserving completeness without requiring accurate state reconstruction, and (2) the VQVAE subgoal generator and policies are trained online from both successful and failed searches, eliminating the need for precomputed solution trajectory datasets. The VQVAE architecture is borrowed from HIPS-ε, but its role shifts from defining the search space to merely conditioning low-level policies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method substantially reduces sample complexity for policy-guided search, enabling solutions to hard problem instances (e.g., TSP, BoulderDash) where baseline methods make no progress within a 16-hour budget. The finding that failed search trees contain structured, useful training signal via community detection challenges the standard Bootstrap assumption that only solution trajectories are informative. Limitations include underperformance on Sokoban (where clustering fails to find meaningful structure) and the method's restriction to single-agent deterministic grid-based domains; open questions include whether the approach scales to continuous or stochastic state spaces and how to dynamically prune unused codebook entries.
