## Problem

Relative Random Walk Probabilities (RRWP), the dominant structural edge encoding in graph transformers, provably assigns identical transition probabilities to edges in structurally distinct graphs (e.g., even-length cycles vs. paths), limiting the model's ability to capture local cyclic patterns. This ambiguity means RRWP cannot serve as a unique structural fingerprint for node pairs, potentially degrading performance on tasks requiring fine-grained structural discrimination such as molecular ring detection.

## Input / Output

The method takes an undirected graph with node features and produces a k-hop simple path count matrix S ∈ ℕ^{|V|×|V|×K} (number of simple paths of each length k up to K between every node pair). Because raw path counts grow factorially, they are compressed via an iterated logarithm mapping f(x) = α·ln^n(1+x) + β before being passed through a shallow encoding network φ₀ to yield the final edge encoding matrix E_SP ∈ ℝ^{|V|×|V|×d}, which is a drop-in replacement for the RRWP matrix in existing transformer architectures. The path counts are computed once as a preprocessing step and introduce no additional trainable parameters.

## Method

SPSE replaces random walk probabilities with simple path counts as the structural edge encoding in graph transformers. The key theoretical insight (Proposition 3) is that for adjacent nodes (i,j), the number of simple paths of length k between them equals the number of cycles of length k+1 containing edge (i,j), giving SPSE direct access to cycle information that RRWP provably lacks. To make path counting tractable, the paper proposes an approximate algorithm that decomposes the undirected graph into multiple DAGs via successive DFS-then-BFS traversals from sampled root nodes; paths are counted by computing powers of the resulting upper-triangular adjacency matrix, and running maxima across decompositions yield lower-bound path counts. The compressed path counts are then encoded through a shallow neural network to produce E_SP, which is injected into the self-attention mechanism in place of E_RW, requiring no architectural changes to the host transformer (GRIT, CSA, or GraphGPS).

## Results

Replacing RRWP with SPSE improves performance in 21 out of 24 benchmark-model combinations. The most notable gains are on molecular graphs: GRIT-SPSE achieves 0.059 MAE on ZINC (vs. 0.065 for GRIT-RRWP) and 0.6945 AP on Peptides-functional (vs. 0.6803), with statistically significant improvements in 5 of 6 molecular cases. On superpixel benchmarks, GRIT-SPSE reaches 77.02% on CIFAR-10 (vs. 76.25%) and 98.29% on MNIST (vs. 98.23%), both statistically significant. On the dense CLUSTER SBM dataset, SPSE does not yield significant gains, likely due to approximate path counting limitations in highly connected graphs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The core comparison is SPSE vs. RRWP as edge encodings within the same architectures. GRIT-SPSE outperforms GRIT-RRWP on all molecular benchmarks (ZINC MAE 0.059 vs. 0.065; Peptides-func AP 0.6945 vs. 0.6803; Peptides-struct MAE 0.2449 vs. 0.2480) and on CIFAR-10 (77.02% vs. 76.25%), with all molecular gains statistically significant. CSA-SPSE similarly beats CSA-RRWP on ZINC (0.061 vs. 0.069), Peptides-func (0.6605 vs. 0.6513), PATTERN (87.06% vs. 87.01%), and MNIST (98.27% vs. 98.13%), all significant. GPS+SPSE shows only marginal gains over GPS (e.g., PATTERN 86.83% vs. 86.69%, CLUSTER 78.44% vs. 77.97%), attributed to the constraint of keeping total parameters unchanged, which reduces edge embedding dimensionality. Against external baselines, GRIT-SPSE achieves the best ZINC MAE (0.059) among all listed methods, surpassing GraphViT (0.073), EGT (0.108), and Graphormer (0.122), while Drew leads on Peptides-func (0.7150 AP) and Exphormer leads on MNIST (98.55%). On the dense CLUSTER dataset, neither SPSE variant improves over its RRWP counterpart, and both remain below EGT (79.23%), suggesting path-count approximation struggles in highly connected SBM graphs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions SPSE as a direct replacement for RRWP (Relative Random Walk Probabilities) in pure graph transformers, building on GRIT (Ma et al., 2023) and CSA (Menegaux et al., 2023), which established RRWP as the dominant structural edge encoding. The conceptual departure draws on path-based MPNN expressivity results (Michel et al., 2023; Graziani et al., 2024) and the classical cycle-counting literature (Perepechko & Voropaev, 2009), reframing simple path counts as a richer edge-level structural signal than random walk probabilities. The paper also extends the hybrid GraphGPS architecture (Rampašek et al., 2022) by injecting SPSE into its MPNN layer, though the primary contribution targets pure transformer self-attention.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The provable equivalence between even-length cycles and paths under RRWP (Proposition 1) identifies a fundamental blind spot in the most widely-used structural encoding for graph transformers, with direct implications for molecular and cyclic-structure tasks. SPSE's drop-in nature (no additional trainable parameters) makes it immediately adoptable, and the consistent gains on molecular benchmarks (ZINC, Peptides) where ring structures dominate are practically meaningful. Key limitations remain: approximate path counting underperforms on dense graphs (CLUSTER), the pre-processing cost is substantially higher than RRWP, and the formal expressivity comparison between SPSE and RRWP is left open. Future work on hierarchical path mining and scalability to very large graphs would strengthen the contribution.
