## Problem

While neural scaling laws predict final losses and optimal hyperparameters, the entire training trajectory across model sizes was not known to follow a precise universal form, and no joint scaling limit preserving dynamical consistency throughout training had been identified. This paper asks what scaling limits govern the full loss curves when model size and training time grow in tandem under compute-optimal allocation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252023/cropped_page1_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes raw training loss curves from a scaling ladder of models (10M–80M parameters) trained to their compute-optimal horizons under various learning rate schedules, and produces a single normalized universal loss curve by affinely rescaling each curve so that both final compute and reducible loss equal unity. Crucially, the normalization denominator uses the seed-specific stochastic final loss rather than the expected loss, which is essential for the supercollapse phenomenon where cross-model variation drops below the per-seed noise floor. The noise model additionally predicts loss curves under arbitrary new schedules from a single constant-LR reference trajectory using one shared hyperparameter α.

## Method

The paper provides two complementary theoretical explanations. First, for sum-of-power-laws scaling laws L(t,p) = L₀ + t^{-μ} + p^{-ν}, compute-optimal training requires balancing the two power-law terms, which is exactly the condition that cancels all p-dependence in the normalized loss curve, yielding exact collapse. Second, a simple SDE model of SGD noise dynamics under a quadratic loss approximation predicts how learning rate schedules deform loss curves via L'(τ) ≈ L(τ) + α·δη(τ)·Tr(Σ'(τ)), where a single α ≈ 0.21 fits across all schedules, model sizes, and training horizons. The universal scaling of gradient noise (Tr(Σ)/L ≈ h(x), a function of normalized compute alone) ensures schedule-dependent deformations are p-independent, preserving collapse across schedules. Supercollapse is then explained as variance reduction: normalizing by the stochastic final loss acts as a control variate that cancels shared optimization noise, with the collapse deviation scaling as Δ ∝ √(η(1−τ̂)), where η is the instantaneous learning rate and τ̂ is normalized gradient flow time.

## Results

The paper demonstrates that compute-optimally trained transformer loss curves (10M–80M parameters on CIFAR-5M and Lichess) collapse onto a single universal curve after normalizing both compute and reducible loss to unity. With learning rate decay, the collapse deviation Δ(x) drops below the per-model seed-to-seed noise floor σ(x,p) for a significant fraction of training (up to δ ≈ 0.5 in normalized compute), a phenomenon termed supercollapse. A simple SGD noise model with a single hyperparameter α = 0.21 (CIFAR-5M) or α = 0.26 (MLPs, close to the theoretical 1/4) accurately predicts loss curves across all tested schedules, model sizes, and training horizons. The predicted scaling Δ ∝ √(η(1−τ̂)) quantitatively explains how learning rate decay suppresses variance to produce supercollapse.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external method baselines; it is a phenomenon study with theoretical validation. The key empirical contrasts are internal: (1) replacing μP with a constant learning rate across MLP widths breaks the collapse entirely, even though final losses remain nearly identical, showing collapse is a more sensitive diagnostic than final performance; (2) increasing the data exponent γ from the compute-optimal value 1.02 to 1.2 for chess transformers shifts the normalized curves and destroys supercollapse, despite only a minor worsening in the fitted scaling law; (3) the noise model's prediction (Equation 16) with a single α matches empirical loss curves across 6+ schedules, 5 model sizes, and multiple training horizons on CIFAR-5M, outperforming the constant-Σ variant tested by Luo et al. (2025). The paper positions its findings relative to Kaplan et al. (2020) power-law fits and Hoffmann et al. (2022) compute-optimal training, showing that the full loss curve carries far more structural information than the Pareto frontier alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the neural scaling laws literature (Kaplan et al. 2020; Hoffmann et al. 2022) from characterizing final-loss Pareto frontiers to revealing a universal scaling symmetry in the *entire* training trajectory. It builds on the SDE framework for SGD noise (Li et al. 2017; Malladi et al. 2022) and the quadratic-loss analysis of Zhang et al. (2019) and Paquette et al. (2021) to derive a simple predictive model for how learning rate schedules deform loss curves. Crucially, it departs from the infinite-width/depth scaling limits (Yang & Hu 2021; Bordelon et al. 2022) that fix training duration, instead identifying a joint scaling limit where model size and training time grow together under compute-optimal allocation, preserving dynamical consistency throughout training rather than only at initialization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Supercollapse provides a practical, low-cost diagnostic for scaling: deviations from the universal curve signal misconfigured hyperparameters (e.g., wrong data exponent or broken μP) even when final losses look nearly identical, and the variance-reduction mechanism enables cross-scale comparison of noisy training curves without expensive multi-seed runs. The single-parameter quadratic noise model (α≈0.21) predicting loss curves across schedules, sizes, and horizons is surprisingly effective, suggesting a tractable path to schedule optimization. Key open questions include why the simple model works so well for cross-entropy loss with non-negligible irreducible loss, whether the phenomena persist at much larger scales with co-scaled depth/batch/weight-decay, and whether collapse itself can serve as a starting point for a deeper theory of scaling laws analogous to the renormalization group in physics.
