## Problem

Existing test-time adaptation methods for GNNs face a fundamental trade-off: model-centric approaches require fine-tuning deployed models (impractical due to computational cost), while data-centric approaches like GTRANS modify individual test graphs with per-graph parameterized transformations (inefficient and inflexible for diverse distributions). This paper asks whether one can instead learn a single parameterized test-time graph distribution that projects the entire test graph set back toward the training distribution, enabling flexible, data-centric adaptation without touching model parameters.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20253145/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a set of unlabeled test graphs, a frozen well-trained GNN (GIN), and a frozen score-based graph diffusion model trained on the training set. It produces an adapted test graph set (one per original test graph) and final predictions via ensemble: for classification, the argmax of averaged class confidences from original and adapted graphs; for regression, a weighted fusion of graph embeddings (weight η) fed through the GNN's predictor. Notably, the forward diffusion uses a short horizon T ≪ T_tr to prevent over-projection, and the dynamic search selects the optimal graph from all intermediate reverse-diffusion steps rather than only the final denoised output.

## Method

PGNDS operates in three phases. First, dual conditional diffusion perturbs each test graph via a short forward SDE, then runs a reverse diffusion where the score is rectified by three GNN-guided terms: a task-specific term (negative log-likelihood of the GNN's pseudo-label), a structural term (MSE between perturbed and original adjacency), and a diversity term (fused Gromov-Wasserstein distance encouraging the generated graph to differ from the original). Second, dynamic search spans all T reverse steps and selects, for each test graph, the intermediate graph minimizing the weighted sum of the three rectification costs, with an early-stopping patience criterion. Third, ensemble inference aggregates predictions from both the original and adapted graphs (confidence averaging for classification, embedding fusion for regression). The paper provides theoretical justification showing the unguided forward-reverse process yields a hybrid distribution with no closed form, while the dual conditional guidance constrains the distributional deviation within a controllable bound tied to the GNN's parameter distribution.

## Results

PGNDS achieves the best ROC-AUC across all four graph classification datasets, with the most notable gain on Ogbg-BACE (0.8873 vs. 0.8338 for TENT, a +5.35 pp improvement) and consistent wins on Ogbg-BBBP (0.7014), Ogbg-ClinTox (0.6371), and Enzymes (0.7245). For graph regression, PGNDS attains the lowest RMSE on all five tasks, including QM9-A (2.1985), QM9-alpha (4.2881), and Ogbg-FreeSolv (1.9252). Running time is competitive at ~2.2–2.6 s per batch on an A100 GPU, comparable to EERM and substantially faster than full fine-tuning approaches.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PGNDS outperforms all five baselines on every dataset in both classification and regression. Against the strongest model-centric baseline TENT (entropy-minimization fine-tuning), PGNDS gains +0.26 pp on Ogbg-BBBP, +5.35 pp on Ogbg-BACE, +1.17 pp on Ogbg-ClinTox, and +0.58 pp on Enzymes in ROC-AUC. Against the data-centric baseline GTRANS (test-time graph transformation), PGNDS improves by +0.93 pp on Ogbg-BBBP, +6.17 pp on Ogbg-BACE, +0.59 pp on Ogbg-ClinTox, and +22.08 pp on Enzymes, while also avoiding GTRANS's instability on QM9-alpha (where GTRANS exceeds RMSE 1000). For regression, PGNDS beats ERM by 0.004 on QM9-A, 0.013 on QM9-B, and 0.034 on QM9-alpha in RMSE, and edges out GTRANS on Ogbg-FreeSolv (1.9252 vs. 1.9289). DropEdge and FeatMask, which are simple augmentation strategies, consistently rank below PGNDS on all tasks, confirming that distribution-aware generative projection yields substantially better test-time adaptation than static data perturbation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of test-time adaptation (TTA) for GNNs and score-based graph diffusion models (Jo et al., 2022). It departs from the two existing TTA paradigms on graphs—model-centric fine-tuning (TENT, GT3, GraphTTA) and per-graph data modification (GTRANS)—by introducing a dataset-level formulation: learning a parameterized distribution over the entire test graph set and projecting it back toward the training distribution via a GNN-guided conditional diffusion process. The generative projection mechanism repurposes the well-trained diffusion model (originally trained to capture the training graph distribution) as a reverse-time sampler whose score is rectified by task-specific, structural, and diversity constraints derived from the frozen GNN, effectively bridging the diffusion literature with test-time graph adaptation in a way neither prior line of work had attempted.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dataset-level framing addresses a practical gap: per-graph adaptation (GTRANS) is inflexible and computationally expensive for diverse distributions, while model fine-tuning is often infeasible for deployed GNNs. However, the theoretical justification is thin—Proposition 2.2 essentially states that no closed-form solution exists, and Proposition 2.3 provides only a sketch-level bound without rigorous proof. Experiments are limited to the GIN architecture on molecular and protein graphs, leaving open whether the approach generalizes to other GNN backbones, larger-scale graphs, or dynamic/temporal settings. The authors themselves note future work on dynamic GNNs, and the modest margins over strong baselines (except Ogbg-BACE) suggest the method's advantage may be most pronounced under severe distribution shifts.
