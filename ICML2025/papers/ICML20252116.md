## Problem

Naive L2 regression for 3D orientation estimation provably fails on rotationally-symmetric shapes, collapsing to the Euclidean mean of orientations over all symmetries rather than recovering any valid orientation. Prior deep learning methods either predict only the up-axis (not a full rotation) or restrict evaluation to a subset of ShapeNet classes, leaving full-orientation estimation for general shapes an open problem.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252116/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes a point cloud of 10k surface samples from a 3D mesh (subsampled to 2k points per forward pass) and produces a 3×3 rotation matrix in SO(3) whose columns are the shape's side, up, and front axes. Optionally, the flipper outputs an adaptive prediction set of candidate octahedral flips whose cardinality scales with model uncertainty via conformal prediction, enabling a human-in-the-loop to select among a small set of plausible orientations (median set size 2, 90th percentile ≤ 8).

## Method

The core contribution is a two-stage pipeline that decomposes orientation estimation into a continuous regression sub-problem and a discrete classification sub-problem, sidestepping the L2 regression failure on symmetric shapes. The first stage (quotient orienter) is a DGCNN trained with an L2 loss quotiented by the 24-element octahedral group, recovering the shape's orientation up to an octahedral symmetry; outputs are projected onto SO(3) via the special orthogonal Procrustes problem. The second stage (flipper) is a DGCNN classifier that predicts which of the 24 octahedral flips maps the first-stage output to canonical orientation, trained with simulated first-stage noise (random rotations up to 10°). Both stages employ test-time augmentation with voting, and the flipper additionally supports conformal prediction to emit adaptive prediction sets whose size adapts to uncertainty, allowing end users to resolve orientation ambiguities.

## Results

On up-axis estimation, the method achieves 89.2% accuracy (with TTA) on ShapeNet and 77.7% on ModelNet40, compared to 69.5% and 62.3% for Upright-Net, representing a 64.6% reduction in error rate on ShapeNet. On full-orientation estimation (a task with no prior work), the method achieves a mean chamfer distance of 0.00856 (with TTA) versus 0.05481 for the Upright-Net-Oracle baseline (which uses ground-truth front-axes), an 84% reduction. With adaptive prediction sets (APS), the mean chamfer distance drops further to 0.00208, with a median prediction set size of only 2 candidate orientations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole direct baseline is Upright-Net (Pang et al., 2022), the prior state of the art for upright orientation estimation. On ShapeNet, the proposed method outperforms Upright-Net by 19.7 percentage points (89.2% vs. 69.5%) on up-axis accuracy, and by 15.4 points on ModelNet40 (77.7% vs. 62.3%). For full-orientation estimation, the paper constructs two Upright-Net-based baselines: Upright-Net-Oracle (augmented with ground-truth front-axes, mean chamfer distance 0.05481) and Upright-Net-Random (augmented with random front-axes, 0.10801). The proposed method beats the oracle baseline by 84% in mean chamfer distance (0.00856 vs. 0.05481) and the random baseline by 92% (0.00856 vs. 0.10801). The paper notes that Upright-Net can only predict the up-axis and relies on a restrictive prior about supporting bases, whereas the proposed method recovers a full SO(3) rotation matrix for general shapes without class-specific information.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm of either regressing orientations with L2 loss (which provably fails on rotationally-symmetric shapes) or discretizing SO(3) into a classification problem (which degrades rapidly with discretization density, as shown by Poursaeed et al., 2020). It builds on Brégier's (2021) Procrustes projection for SO(3)-valued regression, Liu et al.'s (2016) test-time augmentation strategy, and Angelopoulos et al.'s (2020) conformal prediction framework for adaptive prediction sets. The key conceptual departure is the two-stage factorization—continuous quotient regression by the octahedral group followed by a 24-class discrete flipper—which is motivated by a clean theoretical analysis of why naive regression collapses to Euclidean means over symmetry orbits.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first method to estimate full 3D orientation (all three axes) for general shapes without class-specific training, achieving SOTA on up-axis estimation (89.2% vs. 69.5% for Upright-Net) and an 84% chamfer-distance reduction over an oracle-augmented baseline. The theoretical characterization of when L2 regression fails and the quotient-then-classify factorization are presented as broadly applicable to other symmetry-affected geometric learning problems. Limitations include reliance on ShapeNet for training (the flipper struggles on out-of-distribution Objaverse meshes) and the fact that the octahedral group, while covering many real-world symmetries, is a fixed engineering choice that may not suit all shape families. Open questions include whether the factorization generalizes to other finite subgroups of SO(3) and whether training on larger oriented datasets (e.g., Objaverse with annotations) would close the OOD gap.
