## Problem

Causal ML methods remain underutilized by the broader ML community because current empirical evaluations fail to demonstrate their reliability and robustness under realistic conditions. The community criticizes the reliance on synthetic experiments, but the paper argues the root problem is not synthetic data itself—rather, experiments are often biased by researcher design choices, overly simplistic in their causal models, and lack the rigor needed to build practitioner trust. This position paper identifies these specific shortcomings and proposes a set of principles for rigorous synthetic evaluation.

## Input / Output

The empirical demonstrations operate on tabular data generated from structural causal models (SCMs) and semi-synthetic benchmarks. The RealCause experiment uses the IHDP dataset (747 samples, 25 covariates) across 100 realizations and 20 random seeds to measure ATE bias; the CausalNF experiments use the Triangle_NLIN SCM and non-identifiable chain SCMs (CTF1/CTF2) to measure counterfactual RMSE. The output is a structured framework of four principles, each specifying mandatory design elements (causal model set, query set, training data, generation algorithm, induced distribution) that must be explicitly documented to make synthetic experiments reproducible and interpretable.

## Method

The paper's core contribution combines targeted empirical demonstrations with a principled evaluation framework. First, it reveals that RealCause, a widely-used semi-synthetic benchmark generator, produces highly unstable ATE estimates (mean error 0.95±1.36 across 100 realizations, with individual errors reaching 9.45) and that method rankings are seed-dependent, exposing systematic bias in current generative evaluation. Second, it probes CausalNF beyond its identification domain by (a) violating diffeomorphism assumptions via segmented-linear and sinusoidal noise transformations (showing unexpected robustness due to minimal noise-parent interactions) and (b) training on two non-identifiable counterfactual SCMs sharing the same observational distribution (showing CausalNF consistently defaults to one structure, yielding RMSE 0.583 vs. 0.158). The proposed framework comprises four principles: synthetic data is necessary for ground-truth causal queries; all design choices must be transparently specified across five mandatory elements; evaluation must go beyond aggregated accuracy to include robustness, failure modes, and progressive complexity; and existing standardized frameworks (CauseMe, CausalBench) should be extended rather than replaced.

## Results

On the RealCause semi-synthetic benchmark (IHDP), fixing the realization and varying 20 seeds yields a mean ATE error of 0.38±0.39, while fixing the seed and varying 100 realizations increases the mean error to 0.95±1.36, with individual realizations reaching errors as high as 9.45 (e.g., realization 84: 6.209±11.318 with true ATE −0.604). For CausalNF on the Triangle_NLIN SCM, violating the diffeomorphism assumption via segmented-linear or sinusoidal noise transformations does not significantly degrade counterfactual RMSE (0.061–0.132 vs. 0.127±0.021 baseline). On the non-identifiable counterfactual experiment, CausalNF achieves RMSE 0.158±0.009 on CTF1 but 0.583±0.011 on CTF2 (a 3.7× increase), revealing a consistent bias toward one structural variant despite identical observational distributions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not propose a new method to benchmark against competitors; instead, it evaluates existing tools to expose evaluation pitfalls. RealCause (Neal et al., 2020), a widely used semi-synthetic data generator, is shown to produce unstable ATE estimates: the original paper reports a point-estimate ATE error of 0.17, but re-running with 20 seeds on the same realization gives 0.38±0.39, and varying the realization (100 runs) inflates the mean to 0.95±1.36, with method rankings reversing across seeds. CausalNF (Javaloy et al., 2023), a state-of-the-art counterfactual estimator, is tested beyond its identification domain: on the Triangle_NLIN SCM with non-bijective noise, RMSE remains comparable to or even below the original (0.061–0.132 vs. 0.127), suggesting the specific SCM structure masks the assumption violation. However, on the Nasr-Esfahany & Kiciman non-identifiability example, CausalNF consistently converges to the CTF1 structure, yielding a 3.7× higher RMSE (0.583 vs. 0.158) when the true SCM is CTF2, and an even more pronounced 7× gap (1.037 vs. 0.147) when a similar noise-parent interaction is added to f₃ (CTF4 vs. CTF3). These results collectively demonstrate that current synthetic and semi-synthetic benchmarks can mask or introduce systematic biases, motivating the paper's proposed evaluation principles.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper extends the broader ML community's critiques of empirical evaluation practices (Herrmann et al., 2024; Karl et al., 2024; Dehghani et al., 2021) to the specific challenges of causal ML, where the fundamental problem of causal inference makes ground truth inherently unobservable. It builds on Curth et al. (2021)'s demonstration of bias in synthetic-outcome benchmarks by showing that semi-synthetic generative tools like RealCause (Neal et al., 2020) introduce analogous and sometimes more severe biases. The paper also leverages the CausalNF framework (Javaloy et al., 2023) as a case study to illustrate robustness gaps, and positions its proposed principles as a complement to existing benchmarking platforms like CauseMe and CausalBench rather than a replacement.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a central adoption barrier for causal ML: practitioners distrust methods whose evaluations rely on synthetic data, yet the paper argues that the problem is not synthetic data per se but how it is used. The empirical finding that RealCause—a widely adopted benchmarking tool—produces unstable method rankings and ATE errors up to 9.45 across realizations is practically consequential for anyone relying on it. Key open questions include whether the proposed principles can be adopted at scale given their computational demands, how to handle the inherent 'unknown unknowns' limitation of synthetic data, and whether standardized frameworks can balance consistency with the flexibility needed across diverse causal tasks.
