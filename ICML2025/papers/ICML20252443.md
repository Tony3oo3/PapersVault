## Problem

LLM biases are typically identified by repeatedly asking the same question in independent single-turn conversations and checking if one answer dominates, but it remains unclear whether LLMs can self-correct such biases when they observe their own prior responses. Moreover, no existing runtime metric can flag a specific answer as biased without requiring ground-truth labels or external calibration, limiting practical bias detection in deployment settings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252443/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a multiple-choice question (binary, 4-choice, or 10-choice format) and produces a per-answer-option B-score plus a binary accept/reject verification decision. Concretely, it requires 30 independent single-turn queries (context reset each time) and one continuous 30-turn multi-turn conversation where the model sees its own prior answers; answer choice order is randomized in every query/turn to control for position bias. The B-score is a scalar per option, and the verification decision is triggered when B-score exceeds a user-chosen threshold.

## Method

The core insight is that LLMs exhibit different answer distributions when they can observe their own response history (multi-turn) versus when they cannot (single-turn), and the difference between these distributions serves as an intrinsic bias signal. B-score(a) is defined as P_single(a) − P_multi(a), the empirical probability gap for answer option a between 30 independent single-turn queries and 30 turns of one multi-turn conversation. A high positive B-score indicates the model is biased toward a in single-turn but self-corrects in multi-turn; a near-zero B-score indicates a genuine preference or correct answer; a negative B-score indicates bias against that option. The metric is unsupervised, task-agnostic, and requires no ground-truth labels. For downstream verification, the paper proposes a 2-step cascade: a primary filter (single-turn probability, multi-turn probability, or verbalized confidence) is applied first, and B-score serves as a secondary check before final acceptance, yielding +9.3% improvement on the authors' bias questions and +2.9% on MMLU/HLE/CSQA over single-metric baselines.

## Results

On the authors' 36-question bias evaluation framework (random, easy, hard categories), the 2-step cascade verification using B-score improves average verification accuracy by +9.3 percentage points over the best single metric across 8 LLMs. On standard benchmarks (CSQA, MMLU, HLE), the same approach yields a +4.8 average improvement. On the BBQ social-bias benchmark, B-score alone achieves 89.6% verification accuracy, far exceeding single-turn probability (20.9%), multi-turn probability (33.9%), and verbalized confidence (77.6%). The mean B-score across all models and categories is +0.23, with the largest effect on random questions (+0.41) and smallest on easy questions (+0.06).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares B-score against three natural alternatives for answer verification: single-turn answer probability, multi-turn answer probability, and verbalized confidence scores. B-score consistently outperforms all three as a standalone indicator, and as a 2-step cascade (primary filter + B-score secondary check) it improves verification accuracy by +9.3 on the authors' framework and +4.8 on CSQA/MMLU/HLE. The largest gains come from augmenting confidence scores (e.g., +32.1 for Command R, +30.0 for Llama-3.1-405B), because confidence scores are nearly constant across answer choices for a given question and thus cannot distinguish a biased over-selected option from a correct one. On the BBQ benchmark, B-score alone (89.6%) dramatically beats single-turn probability (20.9%) and multi-turn probability (33.9%), while confidence scores (77.6%) are competitive but still inferior. Multi-turn probability alone provides no improvement over single-turn probability in most cases (Δ ≈ 0), confirming that the key signal is the *difference* between the two distributions, which is exactly what B-score captures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior LLM bias research (Sheng et al., 2019b; Zhang et al., 2024; Potter et al., 2024), which primarily attributes bias to imbalanced training data and evaluates it via single-turn frequency counts against ground-truth distributions. It also distinguishes itself from multi-turn self-correction work (Laban et al., 2023; Kwan et al., 2024) that introduces new prompts or personas, by keeping the prompt identical across turns so that any behavioral change arises purely from the model's awareness of its own response history. The B-score metric is positioned against both external-resource bias scoring (MAUVE, ROBBIE) and verbalized confidence scores, offering an unsupervised, task-agnostic signal computed solely from the model's single-turn vs. multi-turn answer distributions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that LLMs can substantially reduce bias on random questions simply by observing their own prior answers challenges the assumption that such biases are fixed model properties, suggesting they are partly artifacts of one-shot greedy decoding. B-score provides a practical, ground-truth-free runtime indicator that improves answer verification accuracy by +9.3% on the authors' questions and +2.9% on MMLU/HLE/CSQA, though it incurs the overhead of running both single-turn and multi-turn conversations. Key limitations include the small evaluation set (36 questions), the observation that multi-turn actually *decreases* task accuracy on standard benchmarks (−1.5 overall), and the open question of whether B-score generalizes to hallucination or free-form generation settings. Future work on automated debiasing during training using response-history signals is a natural next step.
