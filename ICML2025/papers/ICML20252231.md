## Problem

Interpretability in reinforcement learning typically targets individual neurons or decision nodes, an approach that scales poorly to large models and rarely yields semantically meaningful units in isolation. This paper addresses the gap by proposing functional modularity as a more tractable level of abstraction, asking whether modular structure can be induced in RL policy networks, automatically detected, and empirically characterised to support safety auditing and verification.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252231/cropped_page4_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes a trained PPO actor network (MLP with two hidden layers of 32 neurons) along with neuron activation traces collected over 10,000 episodes to build a functional correlation graph. It outputs a partition of neurons into functional modules, quantified by an isolation score (ratio of intra- to inter-module weight magnitude) and an Adjusted Rand Index measuring alignment between the structural partition and the activation-correlation partition. Module functions are then characterised by applying negative saturation or sign negation to all weights within a detected module and measuring resulting changes in action frequencies and episode outcomes over 10,000 evaluation episodes.

## Method

The approach combines three components. First, a connection cost loss projects neurons into 2D Euclidean space and penalises each weight by the log of its distance-scaled magnitude, using a logarithmic sparsity term (log(|w|+1)) rather than L1 to more aggressively drive near-zero weights to zero. Second, periodic neuron relocation swaps the positions of the top-k highest-degree neurons within each layer to minimise the connection cost, effectively shortening the paths of performance-critical weights. Third, an extended Louvain algorithm detects modules via two modifications: an 'internal' variant that excludes input-layer nodes from initial partitioning (assigning them post-hoc to their strongest-connected community) and a fine-tuning stage that iteratively merges adjacent modules to maximise a combined objective of structural isolation and ARI-based correlation alignment between the weight graph and the activation-correlation graph.

## Results

The proposed fine-tuned internal Louvain (FT Int.) method achieves the highest module isolation scores, reaching 0.949 at λ_cc=0.15, compared to 0.803 for standard weight-based Louvain (W Lv.) and 0.822 for internal Louvain alone. For functional alignment (ARI), FT Int. reaches 0.834 at λ_cc=0.13, substantially outperforming W Lv. (0.378) and Int. (0.499). The fine-tuning stage alone increases average isolation by 16.8% and ARI by 33.7% over standard Louvain. Performance trade-offs are modest: at module emergence, returns decrease by 11.4% (DO), 12.5% (3D-DO), and only 0.8% (G2K), while network sizes shrink by 89–96.5% in parameters.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks the proposed fine-tuned internal Louvain (FT Int.) against standard weight-based Louvain (W Lv.), correlation-based Louvain (C Lv.), internal Louvain (Int.), and fine-tuned Louvain (FT). FT Int. consistently achieves the highest isolation scores across all λ_cc values (e.g., 0.949 vs. 0.803 for W Lv. at λ_cc=0.15), confirming that both the internal initialisation and the fine-tuning merge stage contribute to better module separation. For ARI, FT Int. surpasses all baselines at λ_cc ≥ 0.09 (reaching 0.834 at λ_cc=0.13 vs. 0.378 for W Lv.), while FT achieves slightly higher ARI at mid-range values (0.676 at λ_cc=0.07 vs. 0.625 for FT Int.). The standard Louvain algorithm fails to produce modules spanning multiple layers in MLP architectures, particularly in the input layer where high fan-out connectivity causes incorrect partitioning. The C Lv. (correlation-based) baseline is only reported for isolation and shows no advantage over W Lv., underscoring the need for the combined structural-functional approach.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the brain-inspired modular training approach of Liu et al. (2023) and the spatial embedding regularisation of Achterberg et al. (2023) from classification/regression settings to RL policy networks, introducing a log-based sparsity loss and neuron relocation to encourage local connectivity. For module detection, it builds on the Louvain community detection algorithm (Blondel et al., 2008) but identifies and addresses specific failure modes when applied to MLP architectures (layer-wise connectivity constraints, input fan-out), proposing an extended algorithm with a correlation alignment metric (ARI) to validate structural partitions against activation-based functional partitions. The intervention-based module characterisation draws on the broader mechanistic interpretability tradition of circuit discovery but operates at a higher level of abstraction (functional modules rather than individual computational circuits).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a concrete, end-to-end pipeline (induce → detect → characterise) for module-level interpretability in RL that avoids the scalability problems of neuron-level explanations and the architectural constraints of white-box policy representations. However, the demonstrated results are limited to small MiniGrid environments with 32-neuron hidden layers, and the 11–12% performance degradation from regularisation remains a significant barrier to practical adoption. Key open questions include whether the approach scales to complex, high-dimensional domains and larger architectures, whether activation-based (rather than parameter-based) interventions would yield richer characterisation, and how to formally evaluate the quality and tractability of the resulting interpretations beyond subjective visual assessment.
