## Problem

Deep reinforcement learning has been applied to dynamic algorithm configuration for evolutionary algorithms, but existing DRL-based approaches (e.g., MADAC) rely on manually designed state features and are primarily validated on continuous optimization benchmarks. Applying these methods to multi-objective combinatorial optimization (MOCO) problems—characterized by discrete, less smooth solution spaces and wide objective value ranges—remains unexplored, and the manual state design does not scale with the number of objectives.

## Input / Output

At each search iteration, the current population is converted into a graph whose nodes carry only normalized objective values (normalized against the worst initial-generation values) and whose edges connect solutions ranked in the same Pareto front via non-dominated sorting; a scalar normalized-remaining-budget feature is concatenated after graph pooling. The agent outputs continuous action values (clamped to [-1, 1]) that map to EA hyperparameters such as crossover rate and mutation rate for NSGA-II, or social/cognitive coefficients and inertia weight for MOPSO. Because the graph node features are purely the normalized objectives, the state representation is agnostic to the number of objectives and to the absolute magnitude of objective values.

## Method

GS-MODAC formulates dynamic algorithm configuration as a contextual MDP and trains a PPO policy whose state encoder is a two-layer Graph Convolutional Network. The GCN aggregates node embeddings (normalized objective vectors) across the Pareto-front graph, applies global mean pooling, concatenates the normalized search-budget scalar, and passes the result through a linear head to predict continuous action means. The reward is instance-agnostic: it is the difference of squared hypervolume-normalized improvements (Δ²_current − Δ²_best) whenever the current hypervolume exceeds the best-so-far, which magnifies rewards for late-stage Pareto-front evolution and requires no problem-specific tuning. This graph-based state eliminates the need for hand-crafted convergence/diversity features and scales naturally to any number of objectives, while the squared-improvement reward fosters generalization across problem sizes and types.

## Results

On the Penta-FJSP-25j5m (5-objective, 25 jobs, 5 machines) configuration, GS-MODAC achieves a mean hypervolume of 5.62×10¹² and max of 6.07×10¹², outperforming the best baselines (irace and MADAC) by 8.2% and 5.7% respectively, and vanilla NSGA-II by 10.6% and 10.8%. On Bi-CVRP-500, GS-MODAC reaches a mean HV of 235 and max of 259, matching or exceeding all baselines. The advantage of GS-MODAC is most pronounced in high-dimensional objective spaces (5 objectives) and large combinatorial instances, while on smaller 2-objective problems (e.g., Bi-FJSP-5j5m) all methods perform comparably.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GS-MODAC consistently outperforms all four main baselines (NSGA-II, irace, SMAC3, MADAC) on the most challenging configurations: the 5-objective Penta-FJSP-25j5m (8.2% mean HV improvement over the best baseline) and the 3-objective Tri-FJSP-25j5m (3.8% over MADAC). On smaller 2-objective instances (Bi-FJSP-5j5m), all methods are within ~3% of each other, with MADAC and irace occasionally matching GS-MODAC. For Bi-CVRP, GS-MODAC achieves the best mean HV at 500 customers (235 vs. 233 for MADAC) and ties at 100 customers. When applied to MOPSO (Table 6), GS-MODAC improves mean HV by 2.2% (size 20), 7.7% (size 50), and 22% (size 100) over vanilla MOPSO. In the generalizability comparison against P-MOCO (neural MOCO), GS-MODAC is inferior on the exact training size (Bi-CVRP-100: 366.63 vs. 438.06) but substantially outperforms it on unseen sizes (Bi-CVRP-20: 59.55 vs. 51.05; Bi-CVRP-50: 194.47 vs. 186.35) and on out-of-distribution instance distributions, demonstrating superior transferability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the DRL-based dynamic algorithm configuration (DAC) line of work—initiated for single-objective EAs (Sharma et al., 2019; Sun et al., 2021) and extended to multi-objective continuous optimization by MADAC (Xue et al., 2022)—to the underexplored domain of multi-objective combinatorial optimization. The key architectural departure is replacing the hand-crafted state features (convergence metrics, spacing, hypervolume, population diversity) used in MADAC and prior DRL-DAC methods with a Graph Neural Network that learns embeddings from a graph constructed over the non-dominated solution fronts, making the state representation independent of the number of objectives. The instance-agnostic hypervolume-based reward function also departs from MADAC's improvement-based reward by explicitly amplifying later-stage Pareto front evolution through a squared-improvement formulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By demonstrating that a GNN-learned graph state can drive effective dynamic parameter control for MOEAs on NP-hard combinatorial problems, this work opens a path toward automating the labor-intensive tuning of evolutionary algorithms in scheduling and routing applications. The demonstrated generalizability to unseen problem sizes, more constrained variants (DAFJS-SDST), and different objective subsets is practically valuable, reducing the need to retrain or reconfigure for each new instance. However, the performance gains over strong baselines (irace, SMAC3, MADAC) are modest in bi-objective settings, and the evaluation is limited to two problem families and two MOEAs; open questions include scalability to very high-dimensional objective spaces, applicability to non-convex or disconnected Pareto fronts, and whether the graph representation captures sufficient structure for problems with complex constraint interactions.
