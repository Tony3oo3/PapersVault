## Problem

Despite the ubiquity of transfer learning with foundation models, there is no precise theoretical characterization of when and why transfer outperforms training from scratch on a data-limited target task. Existing work either studies generalization error in isolation without a scratch-trained baseline, or relies on distributional similarity metrics that the paper proves are insufficient predictors of transfer efficiency. The central question addressed is: what property of the pretrained model determines transferability, and can we compute an exact phase diagram separating positive from negative transfer?

## Input / Output

The analysis takes a pretrained deep linear network (L layers, width d, trained to convergence on the source task via population loss in the feature learning regime α→0) and a finite target dataset of n = γd i.i.d. samples with Gaussian label noise σ². The source-target relationship is parameterized by the angle θ between ground-truth linear functions (β_s^T β_t = cos θ). The output is a closed-form transferability T(γ, θ, σ) that partitions the (γ, θ) plane into regions of positive transfer, negative transfer, and zero transfer, with explicit boundary curves. For the ReLU extension, the input is a two-layer network with m neurons pretrained on an ablated teacher, and the output is a transferability heat map over excess target features μ and dataset size n.

## Method

The paper uses deep linear networks trained under gradient flow as an exactly solvable model, exploiting the conservation law D_l = W_l^T W_l − W_{l+1} W_{l+1}^T and the feature learning regime (α→0) to show that pretrained hidden features sparsify to a rank-one matrix aligned with the source function (Theorem 3.4). This sparsification means linear transfer reduces to a one-dimensional regression, yielding a closed-form generalization error R_lt = sin²θ + (σ² + sin²θ)/(n−2) that separates an irreducible out-of-subspace error from a 1/n sampling term. Fine-tuning is analyzed by tracking how the pretrained initialization biases gradient flow away from the minimum-norm solution, producing R_ft = R_sc + (1−γ)(1−2cos θ) for γ≤1. Comparing these to the scratch-trained double-descent curve R_sc(γ) yields explicit phase boundaries: negative transfer occurs when target data exceeds the power in the pretrained subspace (cos²θ < γ < 1 in the noiseless case), and ridge regularization provably worsens rather than fixes this effect. The framework is extended to two-layer ReLU networks via a student-teacher ablation setup, where the irreducible error is the L₂ norm of the target function's projection orthogonal to the pretrained RKHS, and the phase boundary is identified by equating this term to the empirically observed power-law scaling of scratch-trained error.

## Results

For deep linear networks in the high-dimensional limit (γ = n/d), the paper derives exact transferability expressions. Linear transfer achieves positive transferability T_lt = [(1−γ)² + γσ²]/(1−γ) − sin²θ (γ < 1) or σ²/(γ−1) − sin²θ (γ > 1), with negative transfer occurring when target data exceeds the pretrained subspace power (cos²θ < γ < 1 in the noiseless case). Fine-tuning yields T_ft = (γ−1)(1−2cosθ) for γ ≤ 1 and exactly zero for γ > 1, meaning the pretrained initialization is completely forgotten in the underparameterized regime. The critical threshold for fine-tuning is cosθ = 1/2: above it, pretraining helps; below it, pretraining hurts. For ReLU networks, the phase diagram qualitatively matches the linear prediction, with the irreducible error ‖P⊥f_t*‖²_L2 increasing monotonically with the fraction μ of ablated source features.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is a scratch-trained model with identical architecture trained on the target data from random initialization. Linear transfer outperforms scratch training in the low-data regime (small γ) whenever the source-target feature overlap is non-trivial (θ < π/2), with the advantage scaling as 1/n due to feature sparsification reducing the effective dimensionality of the regression problem. Fine-tuning outperforms scratch training only in the overparameterized regime (γ < 1) and only when the target function has majority norm along the pretrained direction (cosθ > 1/2); in the underparameterized regime (γ > 1), fine-tuning is exactly equivalent to scratch training because gradient flow converges to the unique global minimum regardless of initialization. The paper also demonstrates that standard distributional similarity metrics (KL divergence, Wasserstein-1, Dudley metric) are provably insufficient predictors of transfer efficiency (Theorem A.2): two tasks with identical feature spaces can be arbitrarily far apart in distributional distance yet still transfer perfectly. Ridge regularization, a common remedy for multicollinearity, is shown to strictly worsen linear transfer performance (Theorem 3.8), expanding the negative transfer region for any λ > 0.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the implicit bias and feature sparsification results for deep linear networks from Yun et al. (2021) and Atanasov et al. (2021), extending their analysis from generalization error to a relative transferability metric compared against scratch training. It departs from prior transfer learning theory (Wu et al. 2020, Tripuraneni et al. 2020, Shilton et al. 2017) that characterized transfer via dataset-level metrics or generalization bounds, instead proving that the learned feature space of the pretrained model is the relevant object. For the nonlinear extension, it leverages mean field convergence guarantees from Rotskoff & Vanden-Eijnden (2022), Mei et al. (2018), and Chizat (2020) to connect the linear theory to ReLU networks via kernel/RKHS structure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first rigorous phase diagram identifying exactly when transfer learning outperforms or underperforms scratch training as a function of data size and feature overlap, replacing the heuristic 'similar tasks transfer well' with a precise geometric criterion in feature space. The finding that standard distributional metrics (Wasserstein, KL) are provably uncorrelated with transferability, and that ridge regularization strictly worsens negative transfer, challenges common practitioner intuitions. Key limitations include the restriction to linear networks and a specific feature-learning initialization regime; the paper acknowledges that depth separation and nonlinear phenomena are not captured. Open questions include how to position pretrained models optimally along the lazy-to-rich spectrum to avoid feature sparsification, and whether the phase diagram structure persists in more realistic architectures and training protocols.
