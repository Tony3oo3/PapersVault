## Problem

Adapting text-to-image diffusion models to ultra-resolution generation (2K–4K) has traditionally required millions of high-quality training images and full-backbone fine-tuning, making it accessible only to industry-scale efforts. This paper asks whether the process can be made dramatically easier—achieving comparable or superior quality with merely thousands of samples and a few thousand training iterations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252931/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt and produces high-resolution RGB images at 2048×2048 (2K) or 4096×4096 (4K) resolution. For 2K adaptation, training uses 3K synthetic images generated by the closed-source FLUX1.1 [Pro] Ultra model; for 4K, it uses 30K real images from LAION-5B. The output is a lightweight adapter (rank-r low-rank matrices) that can be merged into the base FLUX.1-dev DiT backbone, and the adapted model remains compatible with existing training-free upscaling pipelines such as SDEdit and I-Max.

## Method

URAE proposes three complementary guidelines for ultra-resolution adaptation. First, a theoretical analysis (Theorem 3.4, under NTK/infinite-width assumptions) shows that mixing synthetic data from a teacher model reduces effective label noise and accelerates convergence, justifying the use of 3K FLUX1.1 [Pro] Ultra outputs for 2K training. Second, when synthetic data is unavailable (4K case), the method performs SVD on each weight matrix and fine-tunes only the r components with the *smallest* singular values (initialized via Eq. 6), preserving major components that encode semantics and layout while learning fine-grained textures—contrasting with standard LoRA/PISSA which target the largest singular values. Third, for guidance-distilled models like FLUX, classifier-free guidance is disabled during training (g=1) to eliminate a target mismatch between the distillation stage (which involves the null-condition branch) and the adaptation stage (which targets the vanilla flow-matching residual ε−z₀). The full pipeline trains for only 2K iterations on 2–8 H100 GPUs.

## Results

On 2048×2048 generation (HPD prompts), FLUX.1-dev* with URAE achieves FID 29.44, LPIPS 0.5965, MAN-IQA 0.4730, QualiCLIP 0.7191, HPSv2.1 31.15, and PickScore 23.15, outperforming the closed-source FLUX1.1 [Pro] Ultra on all quality and preference metrics (MAN-IQA 0.4129, QualiCLIP 0.6424, HPSv2.1 29.61, PickScore 22.99) while using only 3K synthetic samples and 2K training iterations. On DPG Bench (prompt alignment), URAE scores 83.83 vs. 84.76 for FLUX1.1 [Pro] Ultra. For 4K generation, URAE (Minor-4K) ranks first on MAN-IQA (0.3999), QualiCLIP (0.5118), and wins the user study with 53.24% overall quality preference, substantially ahead of PixArt-Sigma-XL (31.18%) and Sana-1.6B (10.29%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

URAE (applied to FLUX.1-dev*) outperforms all seven baselines on image quality and human preference metrics at 2K resolution. Against the strongest closed-source reference FLUX1.1 [Pro] Ultra, URAE wins on MAN-IQA (0.4730 vs. 0.4129), QualiCLIP (0.7191 vs. 0.6424), HPSv2.1 (31.15 vs. 29.61), and PickScore (23.15 vs. 22.99), but trails slightly on DPG Bench prompt alignment (83.83 vs. 84.76). Compared to training-free pipelines SDEdit and I-Max, URAE improves FID by 6.15 and 4.22 points respectively, and boosts DPG Bench by 0.27 and 3.96 points. Against dedicated high-resolution models PixArt-Sigma-XL and Sana-1.6B, URAE achieves substantially lower FID (29.44 vs. 36.58 and 33.17) and higher QualiCLIP (0.7191 vs. 0.4438 and 0.6718). For super-resolution baselines Real-ESRGAN and SinSR, URAE leads on FID (29.44 vs. 36.25 and 35.09) and all preference scores. At 4K resolution, URAE (Minor-4K) dominates the user study with 53.24% overall quality preference, far exceeding PixArt-Sigma-XL (31.18%), Sana-1.6B (10.29%), and FLUX.1-dev* (3.24%), while LoRA-based major-component tuning (URAE Major-4K) collapses to last place, confirming the paper's hypothesis that minor singular components are critical when no synthetic reference data is available.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the FLUX.1-dev Diffusion Transformer as its base model and departs from the PISSA framework by tuning minor (smallest) singular components rather than major ones, motivated by the hypothesis that ultra-resolution adaptation targets local texture arrangements rather than global semantic structure. It positions itself as complementary to training-free high-resolution pipelines (SDEdit, I-Max) and as a data/parameter-efficient alternative to full-backbone fine-tuning approaches like PixArt-Sigma and Sana that require millions of high-resolution images. The theoretical analysis draws on the Neural Tangent Kernel framework to formalize when synthetic data from a teacher model aids convergence, and the CFG-disabling guideline addresses a specific mismatch in guidance-distilled architectures like FLUX that prior fine-tuning works (e.g., x-flux, FluxKits) overlook.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: matching FLUX1.1 Pro Ultra 2K quality with only 3K synthetic samples and 2K iterations (~1 day on 2 H100s) dramatically lowers the barrier to ultra-resolution generation, and the 4K results set new benchmarks without requiring industrial-scale data. The CFG-disabling finding is a critical practical insight for anyone fine-tuning guidance-distilled models, as the ablation shows catastrophic degradation (FID jumping from ~29 to ~76) when CFG is retained during training. Key limitations include the lack of inference-time efficiency improvements (no architectural changes) and the reliance on a closed-source teacher (FLUX1.1 Pro Ultra) for 2K synthetic data; open questions include combining URAE with efficient backbones (Sana, LinFusion) and extending to multi-modal LLM integration.
