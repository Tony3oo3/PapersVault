## Problem

Existing neural architecture generation (NAG) methods still depend on trained performance predictors to guide the generative process toward high-quality architectures, introducing additional computational overhead and limiting generalization to unseen search spaces. This paper asks whether prior knowledge extracted directly from known high-performance architectures can replace predictors entirely, enabling architecture generation without any search, evaluation, or predictor training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252740/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes top-k (default k=20) high-performance architectures from three NAS benchmarks (NAS-Bench-101, NAS-Bench-201, NAS-Bench-301) as input, where each architecture is encoded as a directed graph with one-hot encoded operations on nodes and directional edges representing connections. It produces a generated cell architecture (a graph of operations and connections) for a target search space, which is then trained from scratch on the target dataset. Five architectures are generated per search space, and the best or average is reported depending on benchmark convention.

## Method

PG-NAG first computes Shapley values over operations and one-hop subgraphs in the benchmark architectures to quantify each component's marginal contribution to overall accuracy, forming a prior knowledge set K that includes important operations, their surrounding subgraphs, and a prior covariance matrix. A conditional DDPM diffusion model is then trained where K modifies the noise distribution: the forward process injects noise with covariance Σ_n derived from the prior, and the reverse denoising process uses K-conditioned mean and variance to steer generation toward high-contribution components. A GCN extracts operation features and a three-layer MLP captures one-hop subgraph (connection) features, enabling the model to jointly generate operations and their connectivity. The key distinction from prior NAG methods is that guidance is embedded in the diffusion process itself via the prior covariance rather than applied as an external predictor score, eliminating the need for any separate predictor training or iterative search.

## Results

On the DARTS search space, PG-NAG achieves 76.1% top-1 accuracy on ImageNet and 97.56% on CIFAR-10, using only 0.004 GPU days (~5.76 minutes) of computation — orders of magnitude cheaper than competing methods (0.1–4.2 GPU days). On NAS-Bench-201, PG-NAG reaches 94.36% test accuracy on CIFAR-10 and 73.51% on CIFAR-100. Across unseen search spaces, PG-NAG attains the best average rank (12.23) on TransNAS-Bench-101, the lowest average phoneme error rate (21.78%) on NAS-Bench-ASR, and the lowest average log perplexity (4.57) on NAS-Bench-NLP.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PG-NAG outperforms all 20+ NAS baselines on both ImageNet (76.1% vs. next-best 76.0% from ISTA-NAS and PRE-NAS) and CIFAR-10 (97.56% vs. 97.52% from SDARTS-ADV and DARTS+PT) in the DARTS search space, while requiring 25–1000× less computation (0.004 vs. 0.1–4.2 GPU days). On NATS-Bench, PG-NAG is competitive but not uniformly best: BOHB edges it on CIFAR-10 (93.94 vs. 93.91), while PG-NAG leads on CIFAR-100 (70.96 vs. 72.00 for BOHB) and ImageNet-16-120 (44.73 vs. 45.70 for BOHB), all at roughly 3× lower search cost. On TransNAS-Bench-101, PG-NAG achieves the best average rank (12.23) across seven vision tasks, beating RoBoT (16.43) and PPO-TFS (13.53). On NAS-Bench-ASR and NAS-Bench-NLP, PG-NAG attains the lowest average PER (21.78% vs. 21.89–21.95%) and log perplexity (4.57 vs. 4.58–4.63), respectively, with the smallest variance among all methods. The ablation confirms that removing prior knowledge guidance causes the largest performance drop (CIFAR-10 test: 94.36→93.31), while swapping GCN for GIN or GAT yields only marginal differences.

## Relation to Prior Work

PG-NAG sits at the intersection of two recent threads: (1) the Neural Architecture Generation (NAG) paradigm, exemplified by DiffusionNAG and AutoBuild, which replaces iterative search with direct generation, and (2) Shapley-value-based operation analysis (ShapleyNAS, Xiao et al. 2022), which quantifies individual component contributions to architecture performance. The key departure from prior NAG methods is the elimination of a separately trained performance predictor: instead of learning a predictor to guide a diffusion model (as DiffusionNAG does), PG-NAG computes Shapley values directly from top-k benchmark architectures and uses them as a data-dependent prior that modifies the noise schedule of a DDPM-style diffusion process. The graph representation and GCN/MLP feature extraction follow standard practice from graph-based NAS predictors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 0.004 GPU-day cost (≈6 minutes) represents a two-to-three-order-of-magnitude reduction over typical NAS methods while achieving competitive or best accuracy across five diverse search spaces, making high-quality architecture design accessible on modest hardware. The ability to transfer prior knowledge learned from three benchmarks to five unseen search spaces (including speech recognition and NLP) without any retraining suggests the Shapley-derived operation/connection knowledge captures genuinely transferable design principles. However, the method's ceiling is bounded by the quality and coverage of the source benchmarks; if a new search space introduces fundamentally novel operations absent from NAS-Bench-101/201/301, the prior knowledge may be insufficient. Open questions include whether the approach extends to continuous or multi-objective search spaces, and whether the Shapley computation (which requires evaluating many sub-architectures) scales to very large search spaces.
