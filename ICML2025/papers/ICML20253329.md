## Problem

Language model developers routinely publish benchmark results on public test sets without disclosing train-test overlap, making it impossible for the community to assess whether reported performance reflects genuine generalization or memorization of test data. The paper documents that only 9 of 30 flagship models report any train-test overlap information, and argues that developers should publish overlap statistics or release training data whenever they report evaluation results.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20253329/cropped_page19_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The proposed protocol takes a model developer's private training corpus (often terabytes of text) and publicly available test sets (instantiated as HELM scenarios) as input. The developer computes per-instance n-gram overlap scores for both the test input and reference answer separately, producing two sets of instance-level statistics per test set. These are then aggregated by a third party into test-set-level overlap statistics (binary, Jaccard, and token overlap fractions) that are the only public information released about the training data.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a standardized protocol that separates train-test overlap computation between a first-party actor (developer with training data access) and a third-party actor (community with test data access), using 13-gram matching as the overlap primitive. Three complementary metrics are defined: binary overlap (any single matching n-gram), Jaccard overlap (fraction of overlapping n-grams), and token overlap (fraction of overlapping tokens, avoiding double-counting). The paper empirically documents the practices of 30 models, categorizing overlap types (question leakage, quotes from news/laws/books, common phrases, multi-token identifiers) and demonstrating that n-gram methods miss paraphrases, translations, and semantic overlaps. Through outreach to developers, the authors obtained novel overlap data for three additional models (Writer, Alibaba, Apple) and proposed a shared benchmarking protocol using common tools and scripts to enable cross-model comparability.

## Results

Of 30 flagship language models surveyed, only 9 (30%) provide sufficient information for the community to contextualize train-test overlap. Four models (OLMo, Pythia/GPT-NeoX, RedPajama-INCITE, StarCoder 2) release open-source training data enabling direct overlap measurement, while five models (GPT-4, Llama 3.1, Qwen2, Palmyra, Apple Intelligence) publish overlap methodology and statistics. The remaining 21 models report evaluation results on public test sets but provide no (or insufficient) train-test overlap reporting, scoring 0 on the paper's binary transparency metric.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper without formal method baselines; instead, it compares four existing strategies for addressing train-test overlap—black-box estimation methods, private test sets, novel (post-cutoff) test sets, and canary strings—against its proposed norm of developer-reported overlap statistics. The authors argue that black-box methods (e.g., prompting, word-probability, ordering-based detection) are useful sanity checks but are fragile under adversarial settings and require restrictive assumptions, making them insufficient substitutes for white-box reporting. Private test sets (SQuAD, SEAL) reduce but do not eliminate overlap risk and raise transparency concerns about single-organization control. Novel test sets (LiveBench, Image2Struct) help but face ongoing costs and difficulty interpreting longitudinal progress. Canary strings (BIG-bench) are inconsistently implemented and easily filtered. The paper's position complements all four: developer-reported statistics provide the ground truth that black-box methods and canary strings can then verify, while private/novel sets serve as additional cross-checks. Among the 9 compliant models, the two compliance pathways differ: open-data models (OLMo, Pythia, RedPajama, StarCoder 2) enable full third-party audit, while the five reporting models (GPT-4, Llama 3.1, Qwen2, Palmyra, Apple Intelligence) use n-gram-based overlap with varying parameters (e.g., 8-gram for Meta, 50-character substrings for OpenAI, 4–13-gram prefiltering for Apple), highlighting the lack of standardization the paper calls for in future work.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes and extends the data contamination detection literature (Golchin & Surdeanu, 2023; Shi et al., 2023; Oren et al., 2023) and the n-gram overlap methods already deployed by model providers (GPT-3, GPT-4, Llama 2, Qwen2) into a unified advocacy framework. It builds directly on the Foundation Model Transparency Index (Bommasani et al., 2023a, 2024a) by adding train-test overlap as a concrete transparency dimension, and proposes a first-party/third-party protocol that formalizes what providers already do ad hoc. Rather than introducing a new detection method, the paper carves new ground by conducting a systematic audit of 30 flagship models and arguing for a reporting norm analogous to confidence intervals in statistics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The audit revealing that only 9 of 30 major model developers report train-test overlap provides a concrete baseline for the field's transparency gap, and the proposed standardized protocol (shared benchmarks, tools, and scripts) offers a practical path toward comparability. Key open questions remain: n-gram matching misses semantic overlap (paraphrases, translations, summaries), there is no consensus on what constitutes the 'training set' across multi-stage training pipelines, and black-box detection methods remain fragile under adversarial conditions. The paper's broader implication is that without standardized reporting, the community cannot distinguish genuine capability gains from memorization, undermining the scientific value of public benchmarks.
