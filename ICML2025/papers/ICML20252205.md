## Problem

Lightweight time series forecasting models like DLinear demonstrate that complex architectures are unnecessary, but they still model point-to-point correlations (susceptible to noise) and adopt channel-independent strategies that restrict the model to a single temporal structure. This paper asks whether directly modeling chunk-to-chunk spatial correlations—exploiting their translation-equivariance and decomposability—can yield a super-lightweight model that handles diverse temporal structures across channels with far fewer parameters.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252205/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a multivariate time series window of L observations across N channels, splits both the historical and future series into chunks of size S (yielding L/S historical and H/S future chunks per channel), and directly predicts each future chunk as a weighted linear combination of historical chunks. Unlike patching-based methods that only segment the lookback window and decode from an aggregated representation, CMoS chunks both sides and learns explicit, interpretable correlation matrices between historical and future chunks. The output is H future time steps per channel, with Reversible Instance Normalization applied to handle distribution shift.

## Method

CMoS (Chunk-wise Mixture of Spatial correlations) replaces point-to-point linear weights with chunk-to-chunk spatial correlation matrices, which Theorem 3.2 proves are more noise-robust under Gaussian perturbations. To handle diverse temporal structures across channels without O(N²) complexity, it introduces Correlation Mixing: K shared correlation matrices are combined per channel using weights produced by a two-stage allocator (a per-channel 1D convolution for noise-level normalization, followed by a shared linear layer). An optional Periodicity Injection technique directly edits the initial weights of the first correlation matrix with pre-defined periodic peaks (derived from ACF), reducing the learning burden for periodic series. The total parameter count scales as K·(L/S)·(H/S) + N·c + (2L−c)/c·K, achieving under 1% of DLinear's parameters while outperforming it and complex models on most benchmarks.

## Results

CMoS achieves state-of-the-art results on 9 out of 14 evaluation metrics (MSE and MAE across 7 datasets) and second-best on 3 metrics, using as low as 1% of DLinear's parameter count. On multi-channel datasets (>20 channels), CMoS consistently leads: Electricity (MSE 0.158, MAE 0.250), Traffic (MSE 0.396, MAE 0.271), and Weather (MSE 0.220, MAE 0.260). On the ETT-series, CMoS also achieves the best MSE on ETTh1 (0.403), ETTh2 (0.331), and ETTm1 (0.354), while being competitive on ETTm2 (MSE 0.259). The model demonstrates remarkable stability with standard deviations of only ±0.001–0.004 across 5 random seeds.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against complex Transformer-based models (Informer, FedFormer, TimesNet, PatchTST, TimeMixer, iTransformer), CMoS achieves substantially lower MSE on all 7 datasets, with the largest gaps on Traffic (0.396 vs. 0.397–1.898) and Weather (0.220 vs. 0.224–0.301). Among lightweight baselines, CMoS outperforms DLinear on 6 of 7 datasets (e.g., ETTh2 MSE 0.331 vs. 0.470) while using only ~1% of its parameters. CMoS ties CycleNet on Electricity (MSE 0.158) but clearly wins on Traffic (0.396 vs. 0.421) and Weather (0.220 vs. 0.242). On ETTm2, SparseTSF (MSE 0.251) and CycleNet (MSE 0.252) slightly edge out CMoS (0.259), and SparseTSF also leads on ETTh2 MAE (0.364 vs. 0.383), representing the only metrics where CMoS is not first. The Correlation Mixing mechanism is critical: ablation shows removing it degrades Weather MSE by 17.5% (0.144→0.171 at H=96), confirming that channel-independent strategies are insufficient for multi-structure datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

CMoS extends the lightweight time series forecasting line initiated by DLinear (Zeng et al., 2023) and continued by FITS, SparseTSF, and CycleNet, but departs from their point-to-point (chunk size = 1) correlation modeling by operating on chunk-level spatial correlations, which the authors prove to be more noise-robust (Theorem 3.2). It also draws on Mixture-of-Experts (Jacobs et al., 1991) to introduce a Correlation Mixing strategy that adaptively combines a small set of shared correlation matrices per channel, addressing the limitation of the Channel-Independent strategy in representing multiple temporal structures under tight parameter budgets. Conceptually, the paper positions itself against the 'learn shape embeddings then mix' paradigm of PatchTST and iTransformer, arguing that directly modeling relative positional relationships between chunks is more robust and interpretable than modeling specific patterns.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 100× parameter efficiency over DLinear (achieving SOTA with ~1% of its parameters) is practically significant for edge deployment, and the learned correlation matrices offer genuine interpretability into temporal structures (e.g., daily periodicity, short-term momentum, long-term trends) that black-box models cannot provide. Open questions remain: the authors acknowledge that effectiveness on truly irregular time series (e.g., random walks) is unvalidated, the non-Gaussian noise analysis (burst noise) is only empirical rather than theoretical, and the Periodicity Injection strategy can hurt performance on datasets without clear periodicity (Weather). The channel-strategy taxonomy (Private Line, One Bus, Data Mixing, Correlation Mixing) and the information-theoretic argument that cross-channel dependencies enable compression rather than requiring more parameters are useful framings for future lightweight model design.
