## Problem

Standard RL evaluation assumes the agent designer has unfettered access to the deployment environment for the agent's entire lifetime, allowing hyperparameters to be tuned over the full run. This 'lifetime tuning' practice is fundamentally incompatible with continual RL, where deployment duration is unknown and the agent must adapt indefinitely. The paper argues this methodology masks the true advantages of continual-learning algorithms, making all agents appear equally capable and stalling progress in the field.

## Input / Output

The evaluation protocol takes an RL agent (DQN for discrete actions, SAC for continuous actions) with a defined hyperparameter space and deploys it in non-stationary environments spanning both image-based observations (Non-stationary Catch with pixel swaps) and state-vector observations (Continuing Cartpole, DeepMind Control Suite quadruped, Jelly Bean World). The output is lifetime performance (reward curves over the full experiment) under the constraint that hyperparameters are selected using only k% of the total interaction budget, after which they are frozen for the remaining (100−k)% of the agent's lifetime.

## Method

The paper proposes k-percent tuning as an alternative evaluation protocol: hyperparameters are selected via grid search or Bayesian optimization using only j = ⌊kn⌋ steps of interaction, then fixed for the remaining n − j steps of deployment. They apply this protocol to DQN and SAC across multiple non-stationary environments, comparing performance under lifetime tuning (k=100%) versus restricted tuning (k=1%, 10%, 20%). Under lifetime tuning, all algorithms—including vanilla DQN—perform comparably, obscuring the benefits of continual-learning mitigations. Under k-percent tuning, however, recently proposed mitigations (W0 regularization, PT-DQN, CReLU, weight normalization) clearly outperform base algorithms, revealing that their advantages were previously hidden by the unconstrained tuning regime. The paper further demonstrates that the optimal k is agent-environment dependent and that even well-tuned DQN exhibits loss of plasticity over extended lifetimes when tuning is restricted.

## Results

The paper's central finding is qualitative rather than a single headline number: under lifetime tuning, DQN, W0-DQN, and PT-DQN all perform comparably in Jelly Bean World (1.5M steps), masking the benefits of continual-learning mitigations. Under 20% tuning (0.3M steps), W0-DQN and PT-DQN maintain stable performance while DQN's performance declines over time; under 10% tuning (0.15M steps), DQN and PT-DQN collapse entirely while W0-DQN sustains performance. In Continuing Cartpole, 1%-tuned DQN shows catastrophic performance collapse after initial high returns, whereas lifetime-tuned DQN achieves near-optimal performance. For SAC on quadruped-walk-to-run, CReLU combined with weight normalization provides the strongest improvement over 1%-tuned SAC, while W0 regularization actually degrades performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DQN (Mnih et al., 2015) serves as the primary baseline and is shown to be disadvantaged under k-percent tuning: it exhibits loss of plasticity in Continuing Cartpole, Non-stationary Catch, and Jelly Bean World when hyperparameters are selected on only 1–20% of the lifetime, yet performs well under lifetime tuning. W0-DQN (Kumar et al., 2024) is the strongest continual-learning mitigation, outperforming DQN under 10% and 20% tuning in Jelly Bean World and maintaining performance in Non-stationary Catch and Continuing Cartpole where DQN collapses. PT-DQN (Anand & Precup, 2023) matches W0-DQN under 20% tuning in Jelly Bean World but degrades significantly under 10% tuning because the agent only observes one reward function during the tuning window. CReLU (Abbas et al., 2023) improves DQN in Non-stationary Catch under 1% tuning and provides the largest single-mitigation gain for SAC in quadruped-walk-run. Layer Normalization (Ba et al., 2016) is the only DQN mitigation that outperforms lifetime-tuned DQN in Continuing Cartpole. Weight Normalization (Salimans & Kingma, 2016) is the least effective SAC mitigation in isolation for quadruped-walk-run but becomes the strongest when combined with CReLU. L2 Regularization (van Laarhoven, 2017) yields mixed results—modest gains for DQN in Non-stationary Catch but insufficient for SAC. SAC (Haarnoja et al., 2018) as a base algorithm shows that 1% tuning selects a larger learning rate (1e-3 vs. default 3e-4) and smaller buffer (10k vs. 1M), highlighting how lifetime-dependent hyperparameter choices are.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper departs from the standard empirical methodology in deep RL (exemplified by ALE/Atari protocols) and critiques how it has been transplanted into continual RL research. It builds on the continual RL problem formulations of Abel et al. (2023) and Khetarpal et al. (2022), and on the loss-of-plasticity literature (Dohare et al. 2021; Lyle et al. 2022, 2023), but its core contribution is methodological: proposing k-percent tuning as a constrained evaluation protocol. The paper re-examines existing mitigation strategies (W0-DQN, PT-DQN, CReLU, Layer/Weight Normalization, L2 Regularization) not as new algorithms but as test cases for whether the evaluation protocol itself is the confound.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central claim—that lifetime tuning renders all algorithms appear equally successful in continual RL, thereby masking genuine algorithmic differences—challenges a foundational assumption in how the field validates continual learning progress. If correct, many published comparisons of continual RL mitigations may be artifacts of the evaluation protocol rather than evidence of algorithmic superiority. Open questions include: what is the appropriate value of k (the paper shows it is agent-environment dependent), whether meta-learning approaches that adapt hyperparameters online can fully resolve the tension, and how to design evaluation protocols that better approximate real deployment where the agent designer has no access to the full lifetime. The authors explicitly frame k-percent tuning as a minimal starting point, inviting the community to develop more principled alternatives.
