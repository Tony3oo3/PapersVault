## Problem

Standard deep learning relies on sample-based (0D) regularization that evaluates the loss at discrete points, capturing only a partial view of the input space and contributing to poor generalization, adversarial vulnerability, and low sample efficiency. This paper argues that moving to continuous 1D regularization along trajectories spanning the entire domain can address these limitations, but requires a function approximator whose structure naturally supports such trajectory-level constraints.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252435/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a multivariate data point x ∈ Ω ⊂ R^n (tabular features or an image projected into a latent space; CIFAR-10 images are first encoded to 128 dimensions) and produces a k-dimensional class label. Unusually, the input is not fed through a forward pass in the conventional sense: instead, a field line γ^x(t) is traced through the input space starting at x, a Sturm-Liouville eigenvalue problem is solved along that line, and the prediction is the linear combination of the first d eigenfunctions evaluated at t=0 (the point x itself). The domain Ω is taken as [0,1]^n in all experiments, and the number of basis functions d (fixed at 10) acts as a tunable capacity/regularization knob.

## Method

Deep Sturm-Liouville (DSL) defines a learnable vector field a: Ω→Ω (via a neural network) whose field lines traverse the entire input space, intersecting the boundary ∂Ω at two unique points. Along each field line γ^x(t), a 1D Sturm-Liouville eigenvalue problem with learnable coefficient functions p, q, w (also neural networks) is solved via a shooting method with Prüfer substitution to obtain an orthogonal basis {u_i^x}; the first d basis functions are linearly combined through a learned linear map L to yield the prediction. Training uses implicit differentiation (via the implicit function theorem applied to a mapping encoding the boundary and eigenvalue conditions) to backpropagate through the non-differentiable eigenvalue search and boundary-crossing times. The key theoretical insight is that DSL emerges naturally as a tractable solution to the Rank-1 Parabolic Eigenvalue Problem (a degenerate case of the Elliptic Eigenvalue Problem), and the resulting basis is provably orthogonal over the entire domain Ω (Theorem 5.1). Regularization is both implicit (limiting to the first d basis functions caps the number of sign changes, controlling oscillation) and explicit (a spectral penalty on |λ_i| is added to the loss).

## Results

On tabular datasets, DSL achieves 84.28% on Adult, 91.14% on Dry Bean, and 83.10% on Bank Marketing, closely matching Neural ODE baselines (84.06%, 91.45%, 83.77% respectively). On image datasets, DSL reaches 97.93% on MNIST and 58.38% on CIFAR-10, compared to 96.8% and 58.9% for the Neural ODE baseline. The paper further demonstrates that DSL achieves better sample efficiency than standard neural networks on the Bank Marketing dataset, with test accuracy remaining higher across training set sizes from 100 to 800 samples.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares DSL against Neural ODEs (NODE) as the primary baseline. On tabular datasets, DSL and NODE are essentially tied: DSL wins by 0.22% on Adult but loses by 0.31% on Dry Bean and 0.67% on Bank Marketing. On MNIST, DSL outperforms the NODE baseline by 1.13% (97.93% vs. 96.8%), while on CIFAR-10 it trails by 0.52% (58.38% vs. 58.9%). The MNIST and CIFAR-10 NODE numbers are taken from Massaroli et al. (2020) rather than retrained under identical conditions, so the image-dataset comparison is less controlled. The paper's key differentiator is not raw accuracy but sample efficiency: on Bank Marketing, DSL consistently outperforms a standard MLP across training set sizes of 100–800 samples, suggesting the 1D regularization along field lines improves generalization from limited data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the ODE framework from Neural ODEs (Chen et al., 2018) in a fundamentally different way: rather than using the vector field to define continuous-depth dynamics in a state space, DSL uses it to define field lines that traverse the input domain, along which a Sturm-Liouville eigenvalue problem is solved to generate task-adaptive orthogonal basis functions. The mathematical backbone is the classical Sturm-Liouville theorem (1837), extended here through a novel connection to the Rank-1 Parabolic Eigenvalue Problem, which provides a tractable high-dimensional relaxation of the intractable Elliptic Eigenvalue Problem. The key conceptual departure from prior work is the shift from 0D (sample-based) regularization to 1D (trajectory-based) regularization, where orthogonality is enforced along continuous paths rather than at isolated points, distinguishing DSL from both standard neural networks and Gram-Schmidt-style local basis construction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a principled theoretical framework for understanding why sample-based regularization may limit generalization, and provides a concrete mechanism (field-line-based orthogonal bases with controlled sign changes) to enforce smoother function approximations. However, practical adoption is currently limited by expensive gradient computation via implicit differentiation, solver stability issues, and the need for careful domain design. Open questions include whether the 1D regularization paradigm scales to larger datasets (ImageNet-scale), whether the change-of-variable formula can be developed to enable generative modeling, and whether the sample-efficiency gains observed on small tabular datasets persist at scale. The theoretical link to Parabolic Eigenvalue Problems opens a path for further spectral-method-based function approximators.
