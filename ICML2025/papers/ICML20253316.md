## Problem

The ML community is increasingly adopting Contextual Integrity (CI) to evaluate privacy in LLMs, yet existing applications fail to adhere to CI's four fundamental tenets, reducing the theory to rule-based compliance checking, data minimization, or public/private dichotomies. This misapplication risks incorrect privacy conclusions and flawed system designs, while also overlooking critical experimental hygiene issues such as prompt sensitivity and position bias in LLM-based evaluations.

## Input / Output

As a position paper, the work takes as input nine prior works applying CI to LLMs in two settings (chatbots and task-automating agents) and produces a structured deviation analysis mapping each work against the four CI tenets (T1–T4). The output includes a summary alignment/deviation table, a taxonomy of 'alternate views' with their negative implications, and concrete recommendations for proper CI-based evaluation methodology and experimental robustness.

## Method

The paper first formalizes the four tenets of CI theory: T1 (privacy as appropriate information flow, not secrecy or data minimization), T2 (flows governed by contextual privacy norms, not legal statutes or crowdsourced preferences), T3 (five-parameter specification of information flows: sender, subject, recipient, information type, transmission principle), and T4 (multi-level CI heuristic for normative assessment of norm-breaching flows). It then systematizes nine prior works, marking each work's alignment or deviation from each tenet and categorizing the 'alternate views' (public/private dichotomy, data minimization, compliance violation, privacy leakage) that substitute for proper CI analysis. A central argument is that legal statutes and crowdsourced preferences are not equivalent to privacy norms, and that the CI heuristic—essential for evaluating whether a norm-breaching flow is ethically legitimate—is entirely absent from all surveyed works. The paper further identifies three sources of experimental variation in LLM responses (same-prompt sensitivity, paraphrasing sensitivity, and position bias) that none of the surveyed works account for, and recommends mitigation strategies including multi-prompt assessment, K-shot prompting, and response averaging across prompt variants.

## Results

Across nine prior works applying CI to LLMs, the authors find that all nine deviate from T2 (appropriate flows conform with privacy norms) and T4 (CI heuristic for ethical legitimacy), eight of nine deviate from T1 (privacy as appropriate flow), and two of nine deviate from T3 (five-parameter information flow definition). Only Cheng et al. (2024, CI-Bench) aligns with T1 by evaluating appropriateness of information flow against established norms. No work mentions or applies the CI heuristic (T4), instead deferring to legal statutes, policies, or crowdsourced preferences as proxies for ethical legitimacy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not propose a new method to benchmark against baselines; instead, it systematically evaluates nine prior CI-for-LLM works against the four foundational CI tenets. The most critical finding is that T4 (the CI heuristic for normative assessment) is universally ignored: no work performs the multi-level ethical analysis (Levels 1–3) that distinguishes CI from mere rule-compliance checking. For T2, all works substitute legal statutes (HIPAA, FERPA, GLBA) or crowdsourced preferences for genuine privacy norms, which the authors argue conflates compliance with privacy. For T1, eight of nine works reframe CI as data minimization, public/private dichotomy, or protection of specific data categories, rather than assessing the appropriateness of information flows. Cheng et al. (2024) is the closest to CI's intent, correctly framing the task as evaluating flow appropriateness, but still relies on author-subgroup annotations rather than the CI heuristic for normative grounding. The authors further note that none of the prior works account for prompt sensitivity or position bias in LLM evaluations, undermining the reliability of their empirical conclusions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper builds directly on Nissenbaum's Contextual Integrity (CI) theory (2004–2022) and its operationalization in prior CI-based systems (Shvartzshnaider et al., 2019; Barth et al., 2006; DeYoung et al., 2010), but departs from the growing body of LLM-privacy literature by arguing that nine recent works (Mireshghallah et al., 2023; Fan et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024; Bagdasaryan et al., 2024; Shao et al., 2024; Cheng et al., 2024; Ghalebikesabi et al., 2024; Hartmann et al., 2024; Ngong et al., 2024) selectively adopt CI parameters while discarding its normative core. Rather than proposing a new evaluation method, the paper re-frames the task: it treats CI not as a checklist of five parameters to populate, but as a four-tenet theory whose normative heuristic (T4) is indispensable for distinguishing privacy violations from mere compliance breaches. The experimental-hygiene section (prompt sensitivity, position bias) draws on the broader LLM-robustness literature (Mizrahi et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2023) to argue that even the descriptive steps of CI analysis are unreliable without accounting for LLM non-determinism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

If the field continues to conflate CI with data minimization, public/private dichotomies, or legal compliance, the resulting privacy-preserving designs may be misaligned with the social values CI is meant to protect, and governance mechanisms built on those designs will inherit the same gaps. The paper's most consequential open question is practical: how should the CI heuristic (T4's three-level normative analysis) be operationalized for LLMs without collapsing into the very rule-based or preference-based proxies the paper critiques? The authors call for interdisciplinary deliberation (Susser & Bonotti, 2024) but offer no concrete protocol, leaving a significant methodological gap. Additionally, the experimental-hygiene findings (no surveyed work accounts for paraphrase or position bias) suggest that even the descriptive CI evaluations in the literature may be less reliable than reported, though the paper does not quantify this effect.
