## Problem

Learning-augmented online algorithms typically index a family of strategies by a single global trust parameter, while modern ML models naturally produce per-prediction uncertainty estimates (e.g., calibrated probabilities). This disconnect means practitioners must manually map instance-specific confidence to a coarse reliability knob, losing information. The paper asks whether calibration—the property that predicted probabilities match observed frequencies—can serve as a principled, drop-in bridge between per-instance ML uncertainty and online algorithm design.

## Input / Output

For ski rental, the algorithm receives a calibrated probability v = f(X) ∈ [0,1] (predicting whether the skier exceeds the break-even point b) and a max calibration error bound α, and outputs a single rent-then-buy threshold k(v) in days. For scheduling, it receives n calibrated probabilities v_i ∈ [0,1] (one per job, predicting high-priority status) along with delay costs ω₁ > ω₀ and information barrier θ, and outputs a full job ordering with a preemptive/non-preemptive split at threshold β. The theoretical guarantee is an expected competitive ratio bound expressed in terms of the predictor's mean-squared error η and max calibration error α, rather than a single trust parameter.

## Method

For ski rental, Algorithm 1 applies a piecewise strategy: if the calibrated prediction v ≤ (4+3α)/5, it defaults to the worst-case b-day rent-then-buy policy; otherwise it rents for b√((1−v+α)/(v+α)) days, smoothly interpolating between extremes. This threshold is derived as the minimizer of a per-prediction upper bound on competitive ratio, and the paper proves it is near-optimal for all distributions and calibrated predictors (Theorem 3.4). For scheduling, the paper extends Cho et al.'s β-threshold rule from binary predictors to arbitrary calibrated f: X₀ → [0,1], with the key technical tool being Lemma 4.2, which bounds ordered expectations of (1−X_(i))·X_(j) by their unordered counterparts minus a variance term—showing that prediction granularity (measured by κ₁, κ₂) directly reduces expected inversions. The paper further proves that in high-variance settings where conformal intervals become uninformative (e.g., returning {0,1} for binary targets), calibrated predictions still convey actionable information, yielding strictly better expected competitive ratios (Theorem 3.7).

## Results

On the Citi Bike ski rental dataset (break-even points b = 200–1000 minutes), the calibrated algorithm (Algorithm 1) achieves the lowest expected competitive ratio E[ALG/OPT] on average across all tested b values, outperforming the break-even, binary-predictor, and conformal-prediction baselines. The underlying predictors (MLP, XGBoost, logistic regression) achieve AUC and accuracy above 0.8 for b > 6. On the sepsis triage scheduling dataset (n = 100 jobs), the histogram-calibrated predictor consistently yields smaller normalized E[ALG − OPT] than the binary (naive) calibration from Cho et al. (2022) across all tested delay costs (ω₀, ω₁) and information barriers (θ). Theoretically, the ski rental algorithm is 1-consistent and guarantees E[CR] ≤ 1.8 under perfect calibration (α = 0).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its calibrated approach against four baselines across two problem settings. For ski rental on Citi Bike data, the calibrated algorithm (CALIBRATED) outperforms the worst-case break-even strategy (Karlin et al., 1988; BREAKEVEN), the binary-predictor algorithm (Anand et al., 2020; BINARY), and the conformal-prediction algorithm (Sun et al., 2024; CONFORMAL) on average E[ALG/OPT] across break-even points. The advantage over CONFORMAL is particularly pronounced in high-variance settings where conformal intervals become too wide to be informative (Theorem 3.7 proves strict improvement when 2α + 2√(η+3α) < a). For job scheduling on sepsis triage data, the histogram-calibrated predictor (CALIBRATED) consistently achieves smaller E[ALG − OPT] than the naive binary calibration from Cho et al. (2022; BINARY) across all tested parameter combinations of delay costs and information barrier θ, with the gap attributable to finer-grained job ordering reducing inversions (quantified by the κ₁, κ₂ variance terms in Theorem 4.3).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of learning-augmented online algorithms and probabilistic calibration, introducing calibration as the first principled uncertainty-quantification mechanism for this setting. It directly extends Cho et al. (2022), who used a binary predictor (implicitly a two-valued calibration) for online job scheduling, to arbitrary calibrated predictors with finer-grained confidence levels. It also positions itself against Sun et al. (2024), who proposed conformal prediction for ski rental, proving that calibration offers key advantages in high-variance regimes where conformal intervals degenerate. The calibration machinery itself (Platt scaling, histogram binning) is borrowed from the probabilistic forecasting literature, repurposed here to replace the ad-hoc global trust parameter that has characterized most learning-augmented algorithm design.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper bridges a practical gap: modern ML models naturally produce per-instance calibrated probabilities, yet most learning-augmented algorithms require users to specify a single aggregate trust level. By showing that calibration yields near-optimal prediction-dependent performance for ski rental and strictly better scheduling guarantees than binary predictors, the work provides a drop-in recipe for practitioners who already have calibrated models. Open questions include extending guarantees to less rigid measures (expected calibration error, binary cross entropy), handling multi-class or continuous targets, and determining whether the calibration-over-conformal advantage persists in settings with informative features that reduce variance. The empirical validation on CitiBike and sepsis triage data, while encouraging, uses relatively small models and single datasets, leaving scalability questions open.
