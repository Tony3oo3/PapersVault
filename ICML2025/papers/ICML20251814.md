## Problem

Active production line control (adaptive routing, worker reallocation, rescheduling) is critical for maintaining throughput in the face of stochastic processing times and shifting bottlenecks, yet no standardized, open-source framework exists for simulating arbitrary production line layouts and training/evaluating RL agents in a reproducible way. Existing RL-for-manufacturing studies each rely on bespoke, domain-specific simulations, making cross-study benchmarking and progress assessment infeasible.

## Input / Output

The agent receives a partially-observed feature vector at fixed intervals T_step (buffer fill levels normalized to [0,1], last processing times, station modes, worker counts, switch indices) and outputs a multi-dimensional categorical action specifying worker-to-station assignments, switch in/out buffer indices, and source waiting times. The episode reward is the incremental change in the economic score C_π(t) = (T_C/t)(c·n_ok − Σ c_i·n_nok), which balances revenue from finished parts against scrap costs, and the goal is to maximize C_π(T_sim) over a fixed simulation horizon.

## Method

LineFlow is a discrete-event simulation engine (built on SimPy) exposing a gymnasium-compatible environment where production line dynamics run in continuous time while agent interaction is restricted to discrete steps, reflecting real-world control cadence. The framework models stations (sources, processes, assemblies, sinks, switches), FIFO buffers with finite capacity, carriers, and worker pools, with processing times drawn from T + Exp_S distributions. The paper formulates four subproblems (optimal waiting time WT/WTJ, part distribution PD_k, worker assignment WA_{k,N}, and a combined complex line CL) with closed-form or integer-programming optima, then benchmarks PPO, TRPO, A2C, and recurrent PPO against these theoretical baselines. For the complex line, where agents deadlock without guidance, a curriculum that gradually increases scrap penalties is introduced, and recurrent memory (LSTM) proves essential for tracking multi-step part flow across sequential assembly stages.

## Results

On the well-understood scenarios, RL agents approach or match the theoretical optimum: recurrent PPO achieves 157.7 ± 1.3 on WT (optimal: 156.2 ± 1.5) and PPO reaches 578.7 ± 0.6 on PD₃ (optimal: 582.7 ± 2.6). On the dynamic WTJ scenario, recurrent PPO (105.9 ± 11.6) remains the best RL agent but still trails the online-learning optimal baseline (114.8 ± 0.9) by ~8%. On the complex line (CL), no algorithm achieves a strictly positive reward without curriculum learning; with curriculum, recurrent PPO obtains positive reward but still falls short of a manually designed heuristic (254.6 ± 1.1).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks four RL algorithms (PPO, TRPO, recurrent PPO, A2C) against analytically derived optimal policies. On WT, all four algorithms are within ~2% of the optimal 156.2, with recurrent PPO slightly exceeding it (157.7). On WTJ, recurrent PPO is the clear winner (105.9) but still lags the online-learning optimal (114.8) by roughly 8%, while PPO and TRPO trail by ~18%. For worker assignment (WA), A2C dominates at k=3 and k=4 (278.9 and 244.7 vs. optimal 287.1 and 252.8), while PPO edges out at k=5 (210.5 vs. optimal 236.3); TRPO consistently underperforms across all WA settings. For part distribution (PD), PPO is strongest at k=3 and k=4 (578.7 and 651.1 vs. optimal 582.7 and 670.7), while A2C and rPPO collapse to near-zero reward at k≥4, indicating actor-critic methods fail to learn the routing policy. On the complex line (CL), all algorithms converge to a deadlocked zero-reward state without curriculum learning; with curriculum, recurrent PPO achieves positive reward but remains below the hand-crafted heuristic (254.6 ± 1.1), highlighting that current RL methods have not yet surpassed domain-expert heuristics in the most challenging setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing the first standardized, extensible framework (LineFlow) for training and benchmarking RL agents on active production line control, addressing a gap the authors identify in prior work where each study relies on ad hoc or domain-specific simulations. Conceptually, it sits at the intersection of the assembly line balancing literature (Padrón, Scholl & Becker), stochastic production line modeling (Bierbooms, Liberopoulos), and the growing body of RL-for-scheduling and bottleneck-detection work (Shi, Kuhnle, Geurtsen, Loffredo). Rather than proposing a new RL algorithm, it repurposes standard policy-gradient methods (PPO, A2C, TRPO, recurrent PPO) within a discrete-event simulation environment that exposes mathematically analyzable subproblems with computable optima, enabling rigorous evaluation that prior domain-specific studies could not support.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LineFlow provides the manufacturing RL community with a reproducible, open-source benchmark where learned policies can be compared against provable optima—a capability that was previously absent. The finding that RL agents converge to productive deadlocks (zero reward) on the complex line CL without curriculum learning, and that even with curriculum they still underperform a simple hand-crafted heuristic, highlights concrete open challenges in reward shaping, hierarchical control, and memory-based policies for industrial-scale settings. The sim-to-real validation on the Bosch production line dataset adds empirical credibility to the simulation assumptions. Broader open questions include whether transfer learning across related line configurations, non-stationary dynamics (machine breakdowns, processing drift), and more sophisticated curriculum schedules can close the gap between RL and domain-expert heuristics on complex layouts.
