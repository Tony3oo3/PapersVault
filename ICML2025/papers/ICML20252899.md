## Problem

Causal graph learning (CGL) methods have been developed almost exclusively for classification, leaving graph-level regression—an inherently harder setting with continuous targets—largely unaddressed. Two specific gaps exist: existing CGL methods assume confounding subgraphs carry no predictive signal (unrealistic in regression, e.g., molecular weight correlates with toxicity without causing it), and classification-specific causal interventions like backdoor adjustment and counterfactual reasoning depend on discrete labels that are unavailable for continuous responses.

## Input / Output

The method takes a molecular graph (adjacency matrix A ∈ {0,1}^{n×n} and node feature matrix X ∈ R^{n×d}, up to 38 heavy atoms in ZINC) and produces a scalar continuous prediction (e.g., restricted solubility or reaction barrier height). Internally, the graph is decomposed via learnable soft masks into a causal subgraph C and a confounding subgraph S, each processed by a shared-parameter GNN (3-layer GIN, 300 hidden dims) with independent readout layers that output Gaussian mean/variance parameters for the regression target.

## Method

The framework introduces two key modifications to standard CGL. First, an enhanced graph information bottleneck (GIB) loss adds a −β·I(S;Y) term so the confounding subgraph is explicitly allowed to retain predictive information, preventing the causal subgraph from being overburdened and enabling cleaner disentanglement; I(C;G) is upper-bounded via a Gaussian KL reduction to ‖μ_φ(G)‖², and I(C;Y)/I(S;Y) reduce to least-squares losses under a unit-variance assumption. Second, a contrastive-learning causal intervention loss (InfoNCE-style) replaces label-dependent backdoor adjustment: counterfactual mixed representations H_mix = H_c,i + H_s,j are generated by random pairing, and the model is trained to align H_mix with the original graph embedding H_g,i (positive pair) while pushing apart unrelated batch members (negatives), thereby learning causal representations invariant to confounders without requiring discrete class labels. The total objective is L = L_GIB + λ·L_CI, optimized end-to-end with the soft masks, GNN encoder, and readout layers.

## Results

On GOOD-ZINC (MAE, lower is better), the proposed method achieves the best OOD performance across all four shift settings: 0.1046 (Scaffold-Covariate), 0.0518 (Scaffold-Concept), 0.1484 (Size-Covariate), and 0.0580 (Size-Concept), outperforming the strongest baseline GSAT by 26.3%–48.1% in OOD MAE. On ReactionOOD (RMSE), the method achieves the best OOD RMSE in 6 out of 10 evaluation cases, with the closest runner-up within 0.06 RMSE in the remaining competitive settings. The method also exhibits notably lower variance (±0.0004–0.0033) compared to baselines, indicating more stable generalization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The closest competitor is GSAT (stochastic attention for graph OOD), which the proposed method outperforms by 26.3% (Scaffold-Covariate OOD: 0.1046 vs. 0.1419), 48.1% (Scaffold-Concept OOD: 0.0518 vs. 0.0999), 29.7% (Size-Covariate OOD: 0.1484 vs. 0.2112), and 44.4% (Size-Concept OOD: 0.0580 vs. 0.1043) in MAE. Classification-oriented causal methods CIGA and DIR perform substantially worse on regression, with CIGA's OOD MAE reaching 0.6102 on Size-Covariate and DIR's reaching 0.4224, confirming that discrete-label-dependent causal interventions do not transfer to continuous targets. Among Euclidean OOD baselines, VREx and DANN are the most competitive non-graph methods but still trail the proposed method by 15–35% in OOD MAE. IRM and Mixup degrade severely under distribution shift (e.g., IRM's Size-Covariate OOD MAE of 0.5840 is 4.4× worse than ERM's 0.2331), highlighting the difficulty of regression OOD. On ReactionOOD, the method wins 6/10 OOD cases outright; in the 4 remaining cases it ranks second with gaps ≤0.06 RMSE, while non-causal baselines like CORAL occasionally match or beat it on ID but degrade under OOD (e.g., CORAL's Cycloaddition Total-Atom-Concept OOD RMSE of 5.74 vs. ours 5.53).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the causal graph learning (CGL) framework established by Sui et al. (CAL, CAL+) and the Graph Information Bottleneck (GIB) of Wu et al. from classification to regression. The key conceptual departure is challenging the strong assumption in prior CGL methods (CAL, DisC, CIGA) that confounding subgraphs carry no predictive signal; here, the authors argue confounders can be genuinely predictive (e.g., molecular weight correlates with toxicity without being causal) and introduce an enhanced GIB objective with an explicit −βI(S;Y) term. For causal intervention, where backdoor adjustment and counterfactual reasoning in prior work rely on discrete class labels, the paper replaces label-dependent supervision with an InfoNCE-style contrastive loss that aligns original graph representations with randomly mixed causal–confounding counterparts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By being the first to explicitly model the predictive role of confounders in graph regression, this work addresses a gap that limits OOD generalization in practically important settings such as molecular property prediction and reaction kinetics. The contrastive-learning-based causal intervention removes the dependency on discrete labels, making the framework applicable to any continuous-target graph task. However, evaluation is confined to molecular and reaction-kinetics benchmarks (GOOD-ZINC, ReactionOOD S-OOD), leaving open whether the enhanced GIB and contrastive intervention transfer to other graph regression domains (e.g., traffic forecasting, credit scoring) or to larger-scale graphs. The hyperparameter sensitivity analysis also reveals that performance is non-trivially dependent on λ and β, suggesting the method may require careful tuning per dataset.
