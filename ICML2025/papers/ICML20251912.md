## Problem

Existing SFT data cleaning methods operate at the sample level, yet even within high-quality samples, many tokens are uninformative or redundant after pretraining, diluting task-specific signals and potentially degrading downstream performance. This paper addresses the underexplored problem of token-level noise in SFT by reframing it as a noisy-label learning problem and proposing a generic influence-guided token cleaning pipeline.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251912/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method requires a pre-trained base model, a reference model (a warm-up model fine-tuned on a data subset or a stronger pre-trained model sharing the same tokenizer), and an SFT dataset of prompt-response pairs. It produces a binary token mask applied globally across the entire dataset via a fixed proportion threshold k%, meaning some samples may be entirely removed if none of their tokens exceed the threshold. The final output is a fine-tuned LLM trained only on the retained (informative) tokens, with uninformative tokens simply masked out during loss computation.

## Method

The core scoring mechanism computes per-token influence as the negative loss disparity between the reference model and base model (Score = −Infl), where more negative influence indicates higher token quality under the assumption that the reference model is better. Two strategies are proposed: Fixed-Model Cleaning applies a one-shot global ranking across the entire dataset with fixed models, while Self-Evolving Cleaning iteratively updates the reference model after cleaning each data split, progressively refining supervision signals. Unlike RHO, which ranks tokens locally within each sample and retains a fixed proportion per sample, the proposed approach uses global ranking, allowing low-quality samples to be entirely discarded while preserving informative tokens from high-quality ones. Theoretical error upper bounds (Theorem 5.1, Corollary 5.2) characterize when cleaning improves performance, revealing a Matthew effect in self-evolving cleaning where data-rich tasks compound gains while data-poor tasks may degrade.

## Results

On the 3B model (LLaMA-3.2-3B), Self-Evolving Cleaning achieves the best average score of 53.00 across seven benchmarks, outperforming Full Tokens (49.87) by 3.13 points and RHO (51.46) by 1.54 points. On the 8B model (LLaMA-3.1-8B), Self-Evolving Cleaning reaches 59.20 average, a 2.5-point gain over Full Tokens (56.70). On Mistral-7B-v0.3, Fixed-Model Cleaning edges out Self-Evolving Cleaning (55.20 vs. 55.00), both surpassing Full Tokens (54.12) by ~1 point. The paper reports an average improvement of 6.3% over the full-token baseline at the 3B scale and 2.0%/4.4% at the 7B/8B scales.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DS² (Pang et al., 2024b), a sample-level selection method that fine-tunes on 10k high-quality samples with full tokens, both token cleaning strategies consistently win: Self-Evolving Cleaning achieves 53.00 vs. 48.79 (3B), 59.20 vs. 55.10 (8B), and 55.00 vs. 53.85 (Mistral-7B) in average score. Against RHO (Lin et al., 2024), which performs local (per-sample) token ranking, Fixed-Model Cleaning's global ranking strategy yields higher averages (51.98 vs. 51.46 on 3B; 55.20 vs. 53.60 on Mistral-7B), with the gap attributed to RHO's inability to discard uninformative tokens from low-quality samples while retaining informative ones from high-quality ones. Against the Full Tokens baseline (all 50k samples, all response tokens), Self-Evolving Cleaning gains 3.13 points on 3B and 2.5 points on 8B, while Fixed-Model Cleaning gains 2.11 and 2.20 points respectively. Notably, on Mistral-7B, Fixed-Model Cleaning (55.20) slightly outperforms Self-Evolving Cleaning (55.00), and RHO (53.60) actually underperforms Full Tokens (54.12), suggesting that the iterative self-evolving mechanism and global ranking are most beneficial when the base model has more capacity to exploit cleaned signals.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the influence-function-based token selection framework from pretraining (Lin et al., 2024; RHO) to the SFT setting, reframing it through the lens of noisy-label learning. It departs from prior sample-level data curation methods (DS2, AlpaGasus, LIMA) by operating at token granularity, and from RHO's local per-sample ranking by introducing global cross-dataset ranking. The self-evolving cleaning strategy—iteratively updating the reference model across data splits—is a novel mechanism not present in prior token-selection or influence-function work, drawing conceptual inspiration from semi-supervised learning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean theoretical framework (error upper bounds, Matthew effect analysis) that explains when and why token cleaning helps, offering practitioners principled guidance on threshold selection and strategy choice. However, several open questions remain: the optimal noise ratio k% is fixed heuristically rather than estimated, experiments are limited to 3B–8B models and a 50k-sample pool, and the counterintuitive finding that a stronger reference model can underperform a weaker warmup model (attributed to distribution shift) is only hypothesized, not validated. Scaling to larger models and data pools, and developing principled noise-ratio estimation for token labels, are natural next steps.
