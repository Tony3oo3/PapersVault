## Problem

Neural scaling laws are known to emerge from either power-law distributed features (Hutter's memorization model) or hierarchical data structure (uniform RHM), but no unified theory explains how these two ubiquitous properties of natural data interact. This paper asks: when both hierarchical compositionality and Zipfian feature distributions are present, which property controls the scaling exponent, and does the answer differ across tasks?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250226/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Input sequences of d = s^L one-hot tokens are generated by a probabilistic context-free grammar (RHM) with L layers, branching factor s, vocabulary size v, m production rules per nonterminal, and a Zipf exponent a applied to one layer's rule probabilities (f_k ∝ k^{-(1+a)}). For root classification, the model outputs the probability of the root label given the full sequence; for next-token prediction, it outputs the conditional probability of the last token given the preceding d−1 tokens. The key structural constraint is that production rules are unambiguous (each s-tuple maps to a unique nonterminal), enabling bottom-up reconstruction of the hidden tree.

## Method

The paper derives learning curves by tracking when statistical correlations between tokens and labels (classification) or between s-tuples and the final token (next-token prediction) become detectable above sampling noise as training set size P grows. For classification, the Zipf distribution of level-1 rules makes the sample complexity for resolving each rule rank-dependent (P_k* = vm^{L-1}/f_k), yielding a power-law error ε(P) ∝ P^{-a/(1+a)} after a pre-asymptotic phase of size vm^{L-1} set by the hierarchy. For next-token prediction, the same rank-dependent sample complexities appear, but the average s^ℓ-gram cross-entropy L_℞ decays with ℓ identically to the uniform case, so the Zipf exponent a only shifts the residual loss L_∞ without altering the scaling exponent log(m/v^{s-1})/(2 log m). Predictions are validated empirically with deep CNNs (stride-s convolutions, ReLU, maximal update parametrization) for classification and multi-layer self-attention transformers (no residuals or MLPs) for next-token prediction, both trained in the data-limited regime.

## Results

For root classification on the Random Hierarchy Model with Zipf-distributed production rules (exponent a), the test error decays asymptotically as ε(P) ≈ s^(L−1) · (P / vm^(L−1))^(−a/(1+a)), with a large pre-asymptotic phase of size vm^(L−1) controlled by the hierarchical structure. For next-token prediction, the scaling law L(P) ~ P^(−log(m/v^(s−1)) / (2 log m)) is unchanged by the Zipf distribution; the exponent depends solely on the branching factor s, vocabulary size v, and number of rules m. Empirical learning curves of deep CNNs (L=2, s=2, v=m=25) and depth-4 transformers (L=4, s=2, v=32, m=8) show remarkable agreement with these theoretical predictions across varying a, m, and the layer at which Zipf distribution is applied.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper that validates its analytical predictions against empirical learning curves rather than comparing against competing methods. The key comparisons are: (1) theoretical scaling-law predictions (Eq. 7, 8) versus empirical CNN learning curves for classification, showing excellent agreement across Zipf exponents a ∈ {0.5, 1, 2} and rule counts m ∈ {10, 25, 50, 100}; (2) uniform RHM (a = −1, equivalent to f_k = 1/m) versus Zipf RHM for next-token prediction, where the asymptotic decay exponent is identical but the local curve shape and residual loss L_∞(a) differ; (3) Zipf distribution placed at the bottom layer versus the top layer, yielding similar learning curves for classification (Fig. 2 right, Fig. 7 right). The central finding is that for classification the Zipf exponent a controls the asymptotic decay while the hierarchy depth L controls the pre-asymptotic plateau, whereas for next-token prediction the hierarchy alone determines the scaling exponent and Zipf only shifts the local details and the irreducible loss floor.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Random Hierarchy Model (RHM) of Cagnetta et al. (2024) and the next-token prediction theory of Cagnetta & Wyart (2024) by replacing uniform production rules with Zipf-distributed ones, thereby unifying two previously separate explanations for neural scaling laws: power-law feature distributions (Hutter, 2021) and hierarchical compositionality. It also connects to the broader PCFG-based literature on transformer interpretability (Allen-Zhu & Li, 2023; Zhao et al., 2023), but unlike those works, it focuses on the learning process and sample complexity rather than expressivity or representation geometry. The kernel-regression scaling law literature (Caponnetto & De Vito, 2007; Spigler et al., 2020) provides the analytical template for linking spectral properties to learning curves, which the authors adapt to the hierarchical setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that Zipf-distributed production rules create power-law learning curves for classification but do NOT alter the scaling exponent for next-token prediction—provides a principled argument that LLM scaling laws are driven by the hierarchical structure of language rather than by superficial word-frequency statistics. This reframes the search for the origin of neural scaling laws away from memorization-based explanations. Key limitations include the fixed tree topology (no variable branching), the single-layer Zipf assumption, and the absence of context-dependent effects, all of which are noted as promising directions for explaining architecture-specific phenomena like in-context learning and the performance gap between transformers and CNNs.
