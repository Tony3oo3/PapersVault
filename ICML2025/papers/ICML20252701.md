## Problem

Deploying Time Series Foundation Models (TSFMs) for physiological signal classification in clinical practice faces three coupled barriers: patient privacy laws (e.g., GDPR) prohibit uploading sensitive data to cloud servers for fine-tuning, local fine-tuning demands prohibitive compute for hospital settings, and generic multi-domain TSFMs underperform on specialized, imbalanced medical data. The paper asks whether a TSFM can be personalized to a local clinical task in a single inference pass—without any local training—while preserving privacy and requiring minimal compute.

## Input / Output

At deployment, the method consumes a local multi-channel physiological signal (e.g., EEG/ECG/EMG with M channels and L timesteps) from which Offline Shapelet Discovery extracts a fixed number of discriminative subsequence prototypes per class as the conditioning signal. The DiT generator then produces a flattened, layer-chunked LoRA weight vector (rank r=4, chunk size 576) that is added to the frozen GPT-2-based TSFM backbone. The combined model outputs a class label (e.g., sleep stage, emotion valence/arousal/dominance, arrhythmia type, or FoG event), with the entire personalization step requiring only ~3 GB GPU memory for DiT inference.

## Method

PhysioPFM is a three-phase train-once-for-all personalization pipeline. In data preparation, a 6-layer GPT-2 TSFM pre-trained on UTSD is fine-tuned via LoRA on multiple public physiological signal sub-tasks using a novel Anchored Classifier Loss (Eq. 6) derived from the Neural Collapse simplex ETF condition, which regularizes class prototypes toward maximal angular separation to counteract class imbalance; the resulting (ΔW_k, t_k) pairs form a task-to-adapter map. In generator training, a 12-layer DiT is trained with a standard diffusion forward/reverse process to denoise LoRA weight vectors conditioned on shapelet prototypes extracted from the corresponding time series, effectively learning a mapping from discriminative time-series subsequences to adapter parameters. At personalized inference, the user's local data is encoded into shapelets, the trained DiT synthesizes task-specific LoRA weights in a single forward pass, and the generated adapter is plugged into the frozen TSFM—requiring no gradient updates, no data upload, and only generator-plus-inference compute.

## Results

PhysioPFM achieves state-of-the-art performance across all four physiological signal classification tasks. On Sleep-EDF (sleep-state detection), it reaches 86.39% accuracy and 76.27% macro-F1, surpassing the best TSFM baseline MOMENT (81.90% acc) by 4.49%. On MIT-BIH arrhythmia diagnosis, it achieves 89.94% accuracy and 89.55% F1, outperforming the specialized DeepArr by 3.39%. On DREAMER emotion detection, it attains 79.25%/79.41%/72.3% accuracy for valence/arousal/dominance, and on FOG detection it reaches 81.32% accuracy, exceeding the best baseline (iTransformer, 74.14%) by over 7%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PhysioPFM outperforms all 15 baselines across every task. Among general deep learning methods (Informer, FEDformer, SimMTM, TimesNet, PatchTST, iTransformer), the strongest is TimesNet (77.95% on Sleep-EDF, 81.33% on MIT-BIH), still trailing PhysioPFM by 8.4% and 8.6% respectively. Among TSFM baselines, MOMENT is the closest competitor on sleep-state detection (81.90% vs. 86.39%) and arrhythmia (83.25% vs. 89.94%), while Time-LLM and OneFitsAll lag further behind, suggesting that LLM-parameter-based TSFMs carry less domain-specific knowledge for physiological signals. Specialized task methods (SleepFM, SleepDG for sleep; LSTM-MLP, OMHGL for emotion; DeepArr for arrhythmia; Extra Tree for FOG) are also consistently outperformed, with DeepArr being the nearest on arrhythmia (86.55% vs. 89.94%). Notably, PhysioPFM achieves the best per-class F1 on the difficult N1 light-sleep stage (66.71% vs. SleepDG's 64.52%), and on FOG detection it surpasses iTransformer (74.14%) by 7.18% in accuracy, demonstrating that the generated LoRA weights provide meaningful specialization without local fine-tuning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three active research threads: (1) train-once-for-all personalization via generative weight synthesis (Chen et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024c), which previously used CLIP text embeddings as diffusion conditions for image classifiers; (2) LoRA-based adaptation of foundation models (Hu et al., 2021), here repurposed as the *output* of a generator rather than a training target; and (3) time series foundation models (MOMENT, Time-LLM, OneFitsAll) that are pre-trained on multi-domain data but underperform on specialized, imbalanced physiological signals. The key departure from prior personalization work is replacing textual prompts with shapelet prototypes as diffusion conditions, and generating low-rank adapter weights (a small, structured parameter space) instead of full network checkpoints, making the problem more tractable for time series where no CLIP-like encoder exists.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a concrete deployment bottleneck in clinical AI: hospitals cannot upload patient data to cloud servers (GDPR) and often lack GPU resources for local fine-tuning of large TSFMs. By reducing personalization to a single DiT inference pass (~3 GB GPU), PhysioPFM makes foundation-model-level performance accessible in resource-constrained, privacy-sensitive settings. However, the ablation reveals that shapelets—the paper's conceptual bridge between time series and model weights—contribute only marginally over random public-data conditions, raising questions about whether the conditioning mechanism is truly capturing task-relevant structure or merely providing a plausible input format. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to longer-horizon forecasting, multi-patient federated settings, or signals with very different sampling rates, and whether the 4-task evaluation (all relatively small datasets) is sufficient to validate the claimed transferability.
