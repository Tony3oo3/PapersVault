## Problem

In multi-task RL, tasks often exhibit reward magnitudes differing by 10–100×, causing the shared critic to receive imbalanced regression targets. Fixed reward scaling (e.g., uniformly amplifying all tasks) overfits to high-reward tasks while still failing on the hardest ones, and selective scaling degrades overall average performance. The paper identifies this reward-scale imbalance as a distinct, underexplored source of training failure beyond negative transfer.

## Input / Output

The agent receives a state vector s and a one-hot task identifier z, producing a stochastic action a from a shared SAC policy π_θ(a|s,z). In addition to the action, the framework outputs per-task adaptive scaling factors c_i^rew that are recomputed from each task's replay-buffer mean reward at every reset boundary (every T_reset steps). The scaling factors are applied only to the immediate reward term in the critic loss, leaving the actor loss and the stored buffer entries unchanged.

## Method

ARS computes a per-task scaling factor c_i^rew = max_j(r̄_j) / r̄_i, where r̄_i is the mean reward in task i's replay buffer, thereby equalizing the effective reward magnitude across all tasks so the critic regression target has consistent scale. To prevent the shifting scaling factors from destabilizing the critic, ARS pairs this with a periodic reset: both the actor and critic parameters are reinitialized while all replay buffers are preserved, and scaling factors are updated only at reset boundaries. The framework is algorithm-agnostic—it can be plugged into any off-policy multi-task RL method (SAC-MT, PCGrad, Soft Modular, MOORE) with minimal modification. Optional enhancements include layer normalization on the critic (ARS-LN) and late-stage LoRA adapters for task-specific specialization (ARS-LoRA), which together push MT10 average success to 99.5% and MT50 to 83.2%.

## Results

On the Meta-World MT10 benchmark, ARS-LoRA achieves a 99.5% average success rate, solving all 10 tasks with at least 97% accuracy—the first method to fully solve MT10 via scratch multi-task RL training. On the more challenging MT50 benchmark, ARS-LoRA reaches 83.2% average success rate (k=50), substantially outperforming the best baseline MOORE (64.2%). In the hardest subset (bottom-10 tasks), where all baselines score 0%, ARS variants achieve 9.1%, 21.0%, and 29.3% respectively, demonstrating a clear advantage on difficult tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ARS outperforms all seven baselines on both MT10 and MT50. On MT10, ARS-LoRA (99.5%) surpasses the strongest baseline SMT (86.8%) by 12.7 percentage points, and MOORE (86.0%) by 13.5 points. The gap is most dramatic on the three challenging tasks (push, pick-place, peg-insert-side): baselines SAC-MT, PCGrad, and Soft Modular score 0% on at least two of these, while ARS-LoRA achieves 97.5–98.8% on all three. On MT50, ARS-LoRA (83.2% at k=50) beats MOORE (64.2%) by 19 points and SMT (56.0%) by 27 points; in the bottom-10 hardest tasks, all baselines fail completely (0%) while ARS-LoRA reaches 29.3%. The paper also shows ARS integrates additively into other methods (PCGrad, Soft Modular, MOORE), boosting each by at least 20% on MT50, confirming it is a general-purpose framework rather than a standalone algorithm.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends single-task reward scaling (Henderson et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2018) to the multi-task setting, departing from PopArt (Hessel et al., 2019), which addresses reward-scale invariance only at the value-head level via an adaptive affine transformation. ARS instead equalizes mean rewards across tasks using a parameter-free rule derived from per-task replay buffer statistics (Eq. 6), and couples this with the periodic parameter-reset mechanism from Nikishin et al. (2022) to counteract both primacy bias and task-ordering bias. The framework is positioned as a universal, plug-in layer over any off-policy multi-task RL algorithm (SAC-MT, PCGrad, Soft Modular, MOORE), rather than a new architecture or gradient-manipulation scheme.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ARS provides a simple, parameter-free recipe that practitioners can add to existing multi-task RL pipelines with minimal code changes, and the demonstrated 20+ point gains across multiple off-policy backbones on MT50 make it a practical tool for robotic manipulation research. Limitations include evaluation restricted to Meta-World (Sawyer arm, MuJoCo), reliance on one-hot task encoding, and the reset mechanism's sensitivity to the number of resets (n_reset). Open questions include whether the equalizer rule generalizes to continuous task spaces, multi-agent settings, or domains where reward sparsity (rather than scale) is the dominant challenge, and whether the reset frequency can be made fully adaptive rather than periodic.
