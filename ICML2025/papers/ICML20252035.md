## Problem

State-of-the-art brain-to-image decoding methods have focused on improving brain signal encoders and auxiliary loss functions, but the role of the image encoder's perceptual alignment with human vision has remained unexplored. This paper asks whether replacing standard pre-trained image encoders with human-aligned versions—where images perceived as similar by humans are closer in embedding space—improves visual decoding, given that brain signals from rapid visual stimuli (100 ms RSVP) primarily capture early perceptual impressions rather than fine-grained categorical distinctions.

## Input / Output

The method takes multivariate brain activity signals (EEG from 63 electrodes at 250 Hz, MEG from 271 channels at 200 Hz, or fMRI) epoched from 0–1000 ms relative to stimulus onset, and produces a ranked list of images from a database (200-way for EEG/MEG, 1000-way for fMRI) by cosine similarity between the brain embedding and pre-computed image embeddings. Image embeddings are extracted once using frozen ViT-based encoders (CLIP, DINO, DINOv2, OpenCLIP, SynCLR, or a concatenated Ensemble), so only the brain encoder is trained per participant.

## Method

The architecture pairs a trainable brain-signal encoder (NICE, EEGNetV4, EEGConformer, or ATM-S) with a frozen image encoder, trained via the multimodal InfoNCE contrastive loss to align brain and image embeddings in a shared latent space. The core contribution is swapping the standard pre-trained image encoder for its human-aligned counterpart—primarily Dreamsim, fine-tuned on the NIGHTS human similarity judgment dataset that captures mid-level perceptual attributes (object appearance, viewing angle, layout) elicited by rapid viewing. This simple modification yields up to 21% improvement in top-1 retrieval accuracy over state-of-the-art baselines, consistently across all tested EEG architectures, image backbones, and brain modalities (EEG, MEG, fMRI). Grad-CAM interpretability analysis reveals that human-aligned models attend to earlier time windows (0–200 ms) and emphasize Alpha, Beta, and Gamma frequency bands—consistent with low-level visual processing—while unaligned models focus more on Delta band and later time points. The paper further shows that Dreamsim outperforms other alignment methods (gLocal, Harmonization) because its training dataset emphasizes the mid-level features most relevant to rapid visual stimulus paradigms.

## Results

On the THINGS-EEG2 dataset (200-way image retrieval), human-aligned (HA) image encoders consistently outperform their unaligned (Base) counterparts across all EEG architectures. The best configuration (ATM-S + SynCLR HA) achieves 62.53% top-1 accuracy, compared to 46.15% for the unaligned version. The largest absolute gain is 21.23 percentage points (DINOv2: 41.87% HA vs. 20.64% Base with the NICE encoder). Improvements are statistically significant (paired T-test, p < 0.001 for most encoders) and extend to MEG (Table 3) and fMRI (Table 8) modalities.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between human-aligned (HA, via Dreamsim) and unaligned (Base) versions of the same image encoders, rather than against external competing methods. HA encoders win in every single configuration tested: across all four EEG architectures (EEGNetV4, EEGConformer, NICE, ATM-S) and all six image encoders (Ensemble, SynCLR, CLIP, OpenCLIP, DINO, DINOv2). The improvement ranges from ~4 percentage points (DINO with NICE: 54.24% vs. 45.99%) to over 21 percentage points (DINOv2 with NICE: 41.87% vs. 20.64%). Among alignment methods, Dreamsim substantially outperforms gLocal and Harmonization, which perform comparably to or only marginally above the unaligned baselines. The authors attribute Dreamsim's superiority to its training on the NIGHTS dataset, which captures mid-level perceptual similarities (shape, color, orientation) more aligned with the rapid 100 ms RSVP stimuli used in EEG recording, whereas gLocal and Harmonization emphasize higher-level categorical or attention-based distinctions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of brain-to-image decoding and perceptual alignment of vision models, two areas that have developed largely independently. Prior brain decoding work (Scotti et al., 2023; Song et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024) has focused on improving brain signal encoders and auxiliary loss functions while treating the image encoder as a fixed, off-the-shelf component. The paper's key departure is to repurpose human-aligned image encoders—specifically Dreamsim (Fu et al., 2023; Sundaram et al., 2024)—as the frozen image representation space in the standard InfoNCE contrastive framework, without modifying the brain encoder architecture or training procedure. It also contrasts Dreamsim with two earlier alignment methods (gLocal, Harmonization), revealing that the specific alignment dataset, not just the alignment technique, determines whether the improvement transfers to brain decoding.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a single component swap (replacing the image encoder with its human-aligned version) yields up to 21% top-1 retrieval improvement—consistent across four EEG architectures, six image backbones, and three brain modalities (EEG, MEG, fMRI)—provides a directly actionable and low-cost upgrade for the brain-computer interface community. The Grad-CAM interpretability analysis, showing human-aligned models attend to earlier time windows and Alpha/Beta/Gamma bands, offers biological grounding that connects the empirical gain to known visual processing mechanisms. Key limitations include poor cross-subject generalization (still the dominant bottleneck), the fact that only Dreamsim (not gLocal or Harmonization) produces consistent gains, and the restriction to rapid-stimulus (RSVP) paradigms. Open questions include whether the benefit extends to image reconstruction via generative models, whether longer viewing times would favor different alignment datasets, and whether the NIGHTS dataset's emphasis on mid-level attributes is the critical factor.
