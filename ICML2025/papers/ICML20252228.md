## Problem

Existing distribution-free uncertainty quantification methods like conformal prediction and conformal risk control are grounded in frequentist statistics, providing only marginal guarantees that average over many unobserved datasets rather than conditioning on the actual calibration data observed. This leads to overly optimistic risk estimates: in the paper's experiments, 21% of individual trials exceed the target risk threshold despite the marginal guarantee holding. Additionally, the frequentist framework makes it difficult to incorporate prior knowledge about deployment conditions or to produce a full distribution over possible losses rather than a single point estimate.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a calibration set of n input-label pairs, a black-box model's nonconformity scores or individual losses ℓ(z_i, λ) that are monotonically non-increasing in a control parameter λ, a known upper bound B on losses, a target risk level α, and a desired confidence level β. Rather than outputting a single threshold, it produces a full posterior distribution over the expected loss via the random variable L⁺ = Σ U_i · ℓ_(i) where U ~ Dir(1,...,1), from which one can extract HPD-based decision rules, upper confidence bounds, and prediction sets. The key structural difference from standard conformal methods is that the quantile levels at which observed losses fall are treated as unknown random variables with a Dirichlet-distributed spacing, rather than being fixed at uniform grid points.

## Method

The paper reformulates conformal prediction as Bayesian quadrature over quantile functions: the expected loss E[L] is expressed as the integral ∫₀¹ K(t)dt of the loss quantile function K, and a prior is placed over K conditioned on observed losses at unknown quantile levels. The prior is then eliminated by taking the supremum over all priors, which via a variational argument (Proposition B.1) yields a worst-case quantile function that is piecewise constant, reducing the bound to a weighted sum of order statistics with weights equal to quantile spacings. The uncertainty in these spacings is handled by the distribution-free result that they follow Dir(1,...,1) (Lemma 4.2, from statistical prediction analysis), giving the random variable L⁺ that stochastically dominates the posterior risk. The decision rule uses the β-quantile of L⁺ (via HPD intervals) rather than its mean, and the paper shows that split conformal prediction and conformal risk control are recovered exactly when only E[L⁺] is used. Monte Carlo sampling from the Dirichlet distribution is used in practice to compute critical values.

## Results

On the synthetic heteroskedastic experiment (n=200, α=0.1), the proposed Bayesian quadrature method (β=0.95) achieves a 3.42% relative frequency of exceeding the target risk with a mean prediction interval length of 9.50, compared to 46.19% (length 7.99) for CRC and 0.0% (length 14.29) for RCPS. On MS-COCO multilabel classification (1000 calibration, 3952 test examples), the method yields a 5.43% risk-exceedance rate with prediction set size 3.04, versus 45.05% (size 2.92) for CRC and 0.0% (size 3.57) for RCPS. On the synthetic binomial experiment, the method reduces the risk-exceedance frequency from 21.20% (CRC) to 0.03% while maintaining tighter intervals than RCPS.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Conformal Risk Control (CRC), the proposed method dramatically reduces the per-trial risk-exceedance frequency: from 46.19% to 3.42% on the heteroskedastic experiment and from 45.05% to 5.43% on MS-COCO, at the cost of only a modest increase in prediction set/interval size (9.50 vs. 7.99 on heteroskedastic; 3.04 vs. 2.92 on MS-COCO). Against Risk-controlling Prediction Sets (RCPS), the proposed method achieves a comparable or lower failure rate (3.42% vs. 0.0% on heteroskedastic; 5.43% vs. 0.0% on MS-COCO) while producing substantially smaller prediction sets (9.50 vs. 14.29 on heteroskedastic; 3.04 vs. 3.57 on MS-COCO). The key insight is that CRC's marginal guarantee (averaging over unobserved datasets) is violated in a large fraction of individual calibration trials, whereas the proposed data-conditional Bayesian guarantee keeps the per-trial failure rate near the target β=0.95 level without the conservatism that inflates RCPS prediction sets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reinterprets split conformal prediction and conformal risk control as special cases of a Bayesian quadrature framework over quantile functions, drawing on distribution-free tolerance region theory (Tukey, Guttman, Aitchison & Dunsmore) to characterize the Dirichlet distribution of quantile spacings. The key conceptual departure is from the frequentist framing of conformal prediction: by formulating the problem as Bayesian inference over the quantile function of the loss distribution, the authors show that existing conformal guarantees correspond only to the posterior mean, and that a full posterior distribution over expected loss is available without specifying a prior. The approach also connects to probabilistic numerics (Cockayne et al., Hennig et al.) by treating the unknown quantile evaluation locations as input noise in the quadrature problem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals a significant practical gap: conformal risk control's marginal guarantee permits 21–46% of individual calibration trials to exceed the target risk threshold, which is problematic for safety-critical deployment where one must trust a single observed calibration set. The Bayesian quadrature formulation provides a data-conditional guarantee (via the HPD interval of L+) that controls this per-trial failure rate while producing smaller prediction sets than the conservative RCPS baseline. Limitations include the i.i.d. assumption between calibration and deployment, the requirement of a known loss upper bound B, and the conservatism of the prior-free bound; future work on specific priors over quantile functions could tighten the guarantees. The framework also opens a path to incorporating domain knowledge (monotonicity, distributional shape) that frequentist conformal methods cannot naturally accommodate.
