## Problem

Existing theoretical analyses of contrastive learning are limited to specific loss forms and do not provide a unified characterization of how embedding similarities behave across different training regimes. In particular, it was unknown whether perfect positive-pair alignment is always achievable in full-batch settings, and what structural property of mini-batch training (beyond optimization error or gradient bias) causes the well-known performance degradation at smaller batch sizes.

## Input / Output

The framework takes pairs of L2-normalized d-dimensional embedding vectors produced by a ResNet-18 encoder with a two-layer projection head, where each pair consists of two augmented views of the same instance (positive) or views from different instances (negative). The analysis operates on the cosine similarities of these pairs, and the proposed VRNS auxiliary loss consumes a mini-batch of m embedding pairs and outputs a scalar penalty that measures deviation of cross-view negative similarities from the ideal value of −1/(n−1). Experiments use CIFAR-10/100 (32×32) and ImageNet (224×224) with batch sizes from 32 to 512.

## Method

The paper introduces a unified theoretical framework that categorizes contrastive losses into two families—InfoNCE-based (softmax-normalized) and independently additive (sigmoid-based)—and analyzes the optimal embedding geometry through the lens of positive- and negative-pair cosine similarities. The key theoretical insight is a fundamental trade-off (Theorem 5.2): when the average negative-pair similarity drops below −1/(n−1), positive pairs cannot be perfectly aligned, and this excessive separation provably occurs for losses like SigLIP with small bias. For mini-batch training, Theorem 5.5 shows that the variance of negative-pair similarities is strictly positive and monotonically increases as batch size decreases, with bounds depending on the ratio m/n. To address this, the paper proposes a simple auxiliary loss L_VRNS = (1/(m(m−1))) Σ_{i≠j}(u_i^T v_j + 1/(n−1))² that penalizes deviations of within-batch negative similarities from the full-batch ideal, and demonstrates that adding this term to SimCLR, DCL, or DHEL consistently improves downstream classification accuracy, especially at small batch sizes.

## Results

On CIFAR-100 with a ResNet-18 encoder, the proposed VRNS auxiliary loss (λ=30) consistently reduces the variance of negative-pair similarities across all batch sizes: from 0.1649 to 0.1008 at batch size 32, and from 0.1396 to 0.0917 at batch size 512 (Table 1). In downstream classification, incorporating VRNS into SimCLR yields consistently higher and more stable top-1 accuracy across temperature values ranging from 0.07 to 2.00 on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 (Figure 3), alleviating sensitivity to temperature tuning. When combined with DCL and DHEL, the auxiliary loss improves top-1 accuracy across all tested batch sizes, with gains most pronounced at smaller batch sizes (Figure 4).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three contrastive baselines—SimCLR, DCL, and DHEL—each evaluated with and without the proposed VRNS auxiliary loss. Against SimCLR, adding VRNS produces consistently higher and more stable top-1 classification accuracy across all temperature settings (0.07–2.00) on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100, reducing the need for careful temperature tuning (Figure 3). Against DCL, the VRNS loss improves top-1 accuracy at every tested batch size, with the gap widening as batch size decreases (Figure 4). Against DHEL, the same pattern holds: VRNS yields accuracy gains that are most pronounced at smaller batch sizes, confirming the theoretical prediction that mini-batch-induced variance in negative-pair similarities degrades representation quality. In all three comparisons, the method wins; no baseline outperforms the VRNS-augmented variant on any reported setting. The variance reduction in Table 1 further confirms that the mechanism (reducing Var[s(f; p_neg)]) is active regardless of the base loss.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies and extends the line of work analyzing optimal embedding geometry in contrastive learning. Prior results (Lu & Steinerberger 2022; Lee et al. 2024; Cho et al. 2024; Koromilas et al. 2024) each characterized the simplex ETF optimum for specific loss families or specific batch settings, but no single framework covered both InfoNCE-based and independently additive losses across full-batch and mini-batch regimes simultaneously. The paper's key departure is reframing the analysis entirely through the statistical properties (expectation and variance) of cosine similarities of positive and negative pairs, which reveals two phenomena—excessive separation in full-batch and variance inflation in mini-batch—that were not isolated in prior work. The proposed VRNS auxiliary loss connects to the alignment/uniformity metrics of Wang & Isola (2020) but targets the variance component explicitly.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of increased negative-pair similarity variance as a geometric cause of small-batch degradation (complementing prior explanations via optimization error or gradient bias) offers a new lens for understanding the well-known batch-size performance gap in CL. The VRNS loss is a simple, drop-in regularizer that empirically reduces this variance and yields modest but consistent accuracy gains, particularly at small batch sizes where practitioners are most constrained. However, the paper itself acknowledges a key limitation: the variance reduction is indiscriminate and may suppress variance that encodes meaningful data structure, and the theoretical guarantees require dimensionality conditions (d ≥ n−1 or d ≥ m−1) that are not always met in practice. Open questions include disentangling data-intrinsic variance from mini-batch-induced variance and extending the analysis to fine-tuning dynamics.
