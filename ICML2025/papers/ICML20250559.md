## Problem

Standard RL assumes per-step reward feedback, but in practice (e.g., autonomous driving, robotics) rewards are costly to collect at every state-action pair. Prior work has studied the extreme of trajectory-level feedback (one signal per episode), but the relationship between feedback frequency and learning performance remains unexplored. This paper introduces RL with segment feedback—where an episode is divided into m segments and feedback is given only at segment boundaries—and rigorously quantifies how the number of segments m affects regret under both binary and sum feedback settings.

## Input / Output

The agent operates in an episodic MDP with |S| states, |A| actions, and horizon H, receiving only state observations and actions at each step. At the end of each of m equal-length segments, the agent receives either a single binary outcome (generated via a sigmoid of the segment's linear reward sum) or the scalar sum of per-step rewards over that segment. The output is a Markovian policy mapping states to actions, along with regret upper and lower bounds that characterize the dependence on m, H, |S|, and |A|. The binary feedback setting is fundamentally different from sum feedback: the sigmoid nonlinearity makes the problem a logistic bandit rather than a linear one, and the two settings cannot share a common analysis.

## Method

For binary segment feedback, SegBiTS (known transition) applies Thompson sampling with a maximum likelihood estimator for logistic regression over segment-level observations, adding Gaussian noise scaled by the inverse of a feature covariance matrix to obtain posterior reward estimates; SegBiTS-Tran extends this with transition bonuses for unknown dynamics. The regret bound contains an exponential factor exp(H·r_max/(2m)), and a novel KL-divergence-based lower bound (using Pinsker's inequality and the sigmoid derivative) proves this exponential dependence is unavoidable. For sum segment feedback, E-LinUCB (known transition) first performs an E-optimal experimental design to select an initial exploration policy distribution that maximizes the minimum eigenvalue of the feature covariance matrix, then switches to LinUCB-style optimistic planning; this sharpens the confidence bonus and improves over prior trajectory-feedback results by a factor of √H. Surprisingly, the regret under sum feedback does not depend on m (up to logarithmic factors), because the increased number of observations is exactly offset by the shrinking segment feature vectors that inflate estimation uncertainty. LinUCB-Tran handles unknown transitions via a variance-aware uncertainty bound on estimated visitation indicators.

## Results

The main theoretical results establish that under binary segment feedback, the regret of SegBiTS scales as Õ(exp(Hr_max/(2m)) · ν(K)√(|S||A|) · (√(Km|S||A|)·max{H²/(mαλ),1} + H√(K/(αλ)))), with a matching lower bound confirming the exponential dependence on Hr_max/m is unavoidable. Under sum segment feedback, E-LinUCB achieves Õ(|S||A|√(HK)·log((1+KHR_max/(|S||A|m))/δ) + (z*)²H⁵·log(|S||A|/δ) + |S||A|H), which is independent of m up to logarithmic factors, confirmed by a lower bound of Ω(√(|S||A|HK)). Experiments (H=100, r_max=0.5) validate these predictions: binary feedback regret grows explosively as m decreases from 20 to 1 (exponential factor from exp(2.5) to exp(50)), while sum feedback regret remains nearly constant across m ∈ {1,...,100}, increasing only logarithmically with segment length H/m.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not include a standard head-to-head comparison table against prior algorithms, as the primary contribution is theoretical characterization of how the number of segments m affects regret. However, relative to prior work: (1) For sum trajectory feedback (m=1), E-LinUCB improves upon Efroni et al. (2021) by a factor of √H, achieving near-optimal dependence on H via E-optimal experimental design that guarantees ‖φ^π‖_{(Σ_{k-1})^{-1}} ≤ 1 instead of the H/√λ bound used previously. (2) For binary trajectory feedback (m=1), SegBiTS provides computationally efficient Thompson-sampling algorithms with O(√K) regret, whereas Chatterji et al. (2021) either required computationally intractable optimization over non-Markovian policies or achieved suboptimal O(K^{2/3}) regret. (3) The paper establishes the first lower bound for binary feedback in the RL literature, proving the exponential factor exp(Hr_max/(2m)) is unavoidable, which no prior work had addressed. The experimental results (Figure 2) qualitatively confirm the theoretical predictions: binary feedback regret decreases rapidly with more segments, while sum feedback regret is essentially flat across m values.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the trajectory-feedback RL line (Efroni et al., 2021 for sum feedback; Chatterji et al., 2021 for binary feedback) by introducing an intermediate model where each episode is divided into m segments, each yielding one feedback signal. For binary feedback, it builds on Thompson sampling and logistic bandit analysis (Faury et al., 2020; Russac et al., 2021) to design a computationally efficient TS algorithm, departing from Chatterji et al.'s non-Markovian formulation by inferring the underlying reward and optimizing over Markovian policies. For sum feedback, it builds on LinUCB (Abbasi-Yadkori et al., 2011) but introduces E-optimal experimental design (Pukelsheim, 2006; Allen-Zhu et al., 2021) for initial exploration, improving upon Efroni et al. by a factor of √H when m=1. The KL-divergence-based exponential lower bound under binary feedback is novel to the RL literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that increasing the number of segments m yields exponential regret reduction under binary feedback but essentially no improvement under sum feedback—provides a clear, theoretically grounded principle for designing human-in-the-loop RL systems where feedback is costly. This asymmetry is non-obvious and has practical implications: for binary (e.g., thumbs up/down) human feedback, finer segmentation is highly beneficial, while for sum (e.g., cumulative score) feedback, segmentation offers diminishing returns. Open questions include extending to unequal segment lengths, function approximation settings, and understanding whether the sum-feedback cancellation effect persists in more general (non-linear) reward models.
