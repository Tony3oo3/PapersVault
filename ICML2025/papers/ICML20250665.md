## Problem

LLM-based Autoraters are typically designed to generalize across unseen test sets, yet in practice evaluation is performed on a small number of fixed, canonical benchmarks that are rarely changed. This paper asks whether specializing a prompted Autorater to a specific test set—by leveraging historical human ratings on that test set as in-context examples—can dramatically improve evaluation accuracy without any fine-tuning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250665/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For each test example, the method consumes the source text, the translation produced by the new system under evaluation, and N historical MQM ratings (error spans with category and severity labels) from other systems that translated the same source. The LLM is prompted per-example with these unique ICL examples and outputs either fine-grained error annotations as a strict JSON object (span, severity, category fields) for AutoMQM, or a scalar quality score on a 0–100 continuous scale for direct assessment. The pseudo-SxS constraint requires that all ICL ratings for a given source come from a single fixed rater.

## Method

The Specialist method constructs, for every test example x_i, a unique set of ICL examples consisting of all historical human ratings of translations of that same source x_i from N other systems, then prompts an off-the-shelf LLM (e.g., Gemini 1.5 Pro) with these demonstrations plus the new translation to evaluate. This differs from baselines in two critical ways: the ICL examples come from the same source as the test example (not shuffled or from a fixed different source), and they are from the same rater as the ground-truth rating. The paper verifies the gains are not due to naive error copying (via a Parrot baseline and copy-count analysis) or merely rater matching (via a fixed-different-source baseline), and shows the method generalizes across LLM backbones, scales monotonically with ICL example count (saturating around 7–12 examples), and extends to both span-based MQM annotation and scalar score prediction tasks.

## Results

On the WMT'23 test set, Specialist AutoMQM achieves a character-level F1 of 51.59, a 54% relative improvement over the state-of-the-art XCOMET (33.50). On WMT'24, the improvement is even more dramatic at 119% relative (35.59 vs. 16.23). The method also generalizes to open-ended story generation evaluation on the HANNA benchmark, where Specialist outperforms the shuffled-source baseline by 31% in Spearman correlation on average score prediction (excluding human stories). Only 3 ICL examples (from 3 historical systems) are needed to already exceed XCOMET performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Specialist AutoMQM dramatically outperforms XCOMET (the state-of-the-art span-based MT metric) by 54% relative F1 on WMT'23 (51.59 vs. 33.50) and 119% on WMT'24 (35.59 vs. 16.23). Against GEMBA-MQM (the closest prompted-LLM precedent using fixed cross-source ICL examples), Specialist achieves a 61% relative F1 improvement on WMT'23 (51.59 vs. 31.99). The 'Shuffled sources' baseline (same ICL examples but assigned to random test examples) already matches or slightly exceeds XCOMET, confirming that the key gain comes specifically from same-source specialization rather than simply having more demonstrations. For the score-prediction task (Table 5), Specialist Scorer outperforms MetricX-24 (the WMT'24 Metrics Shared Task winner) on all language pairs except WMT'23 en→de, where it is slightly behind (56.77 vs. 59.44 Acc23). On the HANNA story generation benchmark, Specialist beats the shuffled baseline by 26–31% in Pearson/Spearman correlation depending on criterion and whether human stories are included.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm for automatic MT evaluation, specifically the GEMBA-MQM line of work (Kocmi & Federmann, 2023a) that prompts an LLM with fixed, generic ICL examples to produce MQM error annotations. The key conceptual departure is the 'Specialist' method: rather than using a fixed set of demonstrations or randomly shuffled examples, it constructs per-example ICL examples from historical human ratings of the *same source* on the test set, exploiting the fact that evaluation in practice is performed on small, fixed, canonical benchmarks. The paper also positions itself against the rater-behavior modeling literature (Karpinska et al., 2021; Riley et al., 2024), but instead of modeling multiple noisy raters, it shows that ICL examples implicitly specialize the metric to a single rater's judgment style.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for the dominant evaluation workflow in MT and NLG research: when evaluating new systems on established benchmarks (WMT, HANNA, etc.), a one-time investment in collecting ratings from a few historical systems yields a metric that dramatically outperforms general-purpose evaluators, with only 3 ICL examples needed to exceed SOTA. Open questions include how to combine ratings from multiple raters into a single ground truth, whether LLMs themselves can generate the historical ratings (removing the human annotation bottleneck), and whether the approach extends to test sets where pseudo-SxS rating was not used. The method's simplicity (pure multi-shot prompting, no fine-tuning) and task-agnosticism make it broadly applicable across NLG evaluation tasks.
