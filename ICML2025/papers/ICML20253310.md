## Problem

Current identifiability theory (IT) for self-supervised learning relies on idealized assumptions—infinite data, infinite training time, and simplified data generating processes (e.g., von Mises-Fisher conditionals on a hypersphere)—that cannot explain practical phenomena such as dimensional collapse, loss saturation, the projector phenomenon, or out-of-distribution generalization. The Platonic Representation Hypothesis, which posits that diverse SSL methods converge to similar representations, lacks a precise mathematical account of when and why this convergence occurs. The paper argues that without an empirically grounded theoretical framework, SSL research will remain fragmented across an ever-expanding model zoo with limited principled guidance.

## Input / Output

As a position paper, the work takes as analytical input the existing body of identifiability results (nonlinear ICA, causal representation learning, contrastive learning theory) alongside empirical SSL observations from methods like SimCLR, DIET, VICReg, and DINOv2. It produces a structured research blueprint: eight identified theory-practice gaps (augmentation, finite data, finite time, architecture, projector, generalization, contrastive/non-contrastive, evaluation), each paired with concrete research questions summarized in Table 1, serving as a signpost for future work rather than a new algorithm or model.

## Method

The paper proposes Singular Identifiability Theory (SITh) as an umbrella framework extending identifiability theory to encompass the entire SSL pipeline, drawing an explicit analogy to how Singular Learning Theory (SLT) extended classical learning theory to account for neural network singularities. Using SimCLR as a worked example, it traces the progression from empirical design (Step 1) through alignment/uniformity analysis (Step 2) to identifiable DGP results (Step 3) and more realistic conditionals (Step 4), showing at each stage where theory diverges from practice. The core mechanism is making implicit data assumptions explicit via a DGP, then identifying precisely which assumptions (isotropic vMF conditionals, infinite data, converged models, no initialization effects) are unrealistic and must be relaxed. The paper formulates 12+ concrete research questions across the eight gaps, arguing that SITh should integrate training dynamics, finite-sample effects, inductive biases (architecture, augmentations, initialization, optimizers), and OOD/compositional generalization into a unified identifiability framework.

## Results

This is a position paper that does not present new experimental results or quantitative benchmarks. Its primary contribution is a qualitative gap analysis (Table 1) identifying eight areas where SSL theory lags behind practice—data augmentations, finite data, finite time, inductive biases, dimensional collapse, projector phenomenon, compositionality, and evaluation—along with concrete research questions for each. The paper synthesizes existing identifiability results (e.g., Zimmermann et al. 2021 for SimCLR, Reizinger et al. 2024a for DIET) to argue that current IT cannot explain practical phenomena such as loss saturation, the projector phenomenon, or OOD generalization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not propose a new method or run head-to-head comparisons against baselines. Instead, it surveys the existing SSL landscape (SimCLR, DIET, VICReg, DINOv2, Barlow Twins, BYOL, and others) to illustrate where identifiability theory provides and fails to provide explanations. The paper highlights that contrastive and non-contrastive methods produce highly similar representations (Ciernik et al., 2024; Morningstar et al., 2024), yet identifiability guarantees exist almost exclusively for contrastive methods, with DIET (Ibrahim et al., 2024; Reizinger et al., 2024a) as a notable exception. The central argument is that augmentation strategy matters more than the specific SSL algorithm (Morningstar et al., 2024), a finding that current DGP-based identifiability results cannot fully capture due to their reliance on unrealistic assumptions (e.g., isotropic vMF conditionals, infinite data, converged training).

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes the identifiability theory (IT) literature—rooted in nonlinear ICA (Hyvarinen & Morioka, Khemakhem et al., Zimmermann et al.)—and the Platonic Representation Hypothesis (Huh et al., 2024) to argue that current IT, while providing partial explanations for SSL methods like SimCLR, operates under asymptotic and unrealistic assumptions (infinite data, hypersphere latent spaces, vMF conditionals) that cannot account for practical phenomena such as dimensional collapse, the projector, or OOD generalization. It draws an explicit analogy to how Singular Learning Theory (Watanabe, 2009) extended classical learning theory to handle neural network singularities, proposing 'Singular Identifiability Theory' (SITh) as a blueprint for extending IT to the full SSL pipeline. The paper also builds on recent unification efforts (Balestriero et al., 2023; Morningstar et al., 2024) and the observation that contrastive and non-contrastive methods share underlying principles (Garrido et al., 2022; Reizinger et al., 2024a), arguing the contrastive/non-contrastive dichotomy is a 'sterile' categorization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a structured research agenda (Table 1) identifying eight concrete gaps between SSL theory and practice—data augmentation realism, finite-sample effects, learning dynamics, architectural inductive biases, the projector phenomenon, OOD/compositional generalization, the CL/non-CL divide, and evaluation methodology—each with specific research questions. Its central argument is that as scaling-driven gains plateau, principled theoretical guidance grounded in empirically realistic data generating processes is essential for designing more robust, interpretable, and generalizable SSL methods, particularly for safety-critical deployments. Key open questions include whether identifiability can be proven for non-contrastive methods, how to model realistic augmentations in a DGP, and whether the log-linear model structure underlying many identifiability results explains the projector phenomenon. As a position paper, it offers no new empirical results, so its impact depends on whether the community adopts SITh as a productive research framework.
