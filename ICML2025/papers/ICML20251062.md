## Problem

Speech decoding from brain activity has been confined to single-subject, single-dataset experiments because individual anatomical variation, scanner hardware differences, and task heterogeneity prevent pooling data across subjects and studies. As a result, the field has not benefited from the growing volume of open MEG repositories or from the scaling laws that drive progress in other deep learning domains. This paper asks whether self-supervised learning with neuroscience-informed objectives can overcome these barriers and unlock cross-subject, cross-dataset, and cross-task generalisation for MEG-based speech decoding.

## Input / Output

The model consumes standardised MEG signal windows of 0.5 s (shape R^{S×t} at 250 Hz), where the sensor count S varies across datasets; a dataset-conditional linear layer projects all sensor configurations into a shared 512-dimensional space before encoding. For speech detection, the linear probe emits a binary prediction per time embedding (per-frame speech presence); for voicing classification, all embeddings within a window are flattened into a single vector to produce one voiced/voiceless label per phoneme-aligned epoch. Pre-training operates entirely on unlabelled MEG from resting-state, sensorimotor, and visual/auditory tasks, while fine-tuning uses only a small labelled probe on top of a frozen backbone.

## Method

The architecture is a wave-to-wave convolutional encoder (the 'cortex encoder') adapted from the SEANet audio codec, with convolutional channels inflated from 1 to d_shared=512 to handle multi-sensor inputs, and FiLM-based subject conditioning at the bottleneck to absorb inter-subject variability. Self-supervision is achieved through three neuroscience-informed pretext tasks applied to unlabelled MEG: (1) band prediction, where a random functional frequency band (δ, θ, α, β, γ, lower/upper high-γ) is band-stop filtered and the network must identify which band was removed; (2) phase-shift prediction, where a discrete random phase shift is applied to a random 50% of sensors and the network predicts the shift; and (3) amplitude-scale prediction, where a random scaling factor is applied to a random 20% of sensors and the network predicts the factor. The three cross-entropy losses are summed with equal weights during pre-training on up to ~400 hours of MEG from ~900 subjects. At inference, the backbone is frozen and a lightweight linear probe (plus an optional dataset-specific linear layer) is trained on labelled data, yielding 15–27% ROC AUC gains over prior self-supervised baselines and matching surgical (intracranial) decoding performance using only non-invasive MEG.

## Results

On speech detection from MEG (Armeni et al., 2022), the proposed method with all pretext tasks and Gaussian smoothing achieves a ROC AUC of .705 ± .003, outperforming the best prior self-supervised baseline (BIOT, .615) by 15% and BrainBERT (.556) by 27%. Remarkably, this non-invasive result matches the surgical BrainBERT result (.71 ± .06) from Wang et al. (2023). The method also demonstrates scaling: pre-training on 319 hours of combined MEG data (Cam-CAN + MOUS) yields .638 ROC AUC versus .630 for Cam-CAN alone, and novel-subject generalisation on Gwilliams et al. (2023) shows a positive log-linear trend with increasing unlabelled data volume.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method (ROC AUC .705) outperforms all three self-supervised baselines on non-invasive MEG speech detection: BrainBERT (.556, +27% relative), BIOT (.615, +15% relative), and EEGPT (.602, +17% relative). The gap over BrainBERT is particularly notable because BrainBERT's masked spectrogram in-filling objective was designed for high-fidelity intracranial recordings, making it less data-efficient on the noisier MEG signal. The method also matches the surgical BrainBERT result (.71 ± .06) using only non-invasive data, which the authors highlight as unprecedented given the substantially lower signal-to-noise ratio of MEG. All baselines use a linear probe on top of frozen pre-trained representations, making the comparison controlled for downstream training. The authors attribute the advantage to their neuroscience-informed pretext tasks (band, phase, and amplitude prediction) that explicitly target features relevant to neural speech processing, rather than generic reconstruction or contrastive objectives.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the SEANet wave-to-wave convolutional architecture from neural audio codecs (Defossez et al., 2022) to multi-sensor MEG signals, introducing a dataset-conditional projection layer and FiLM-based subject conditioning borrowed from speaker-adaptive speech recognition. It departs from prior brain-decoding self-supervised methods (BrainBERT's masked spectrogram in-filling, BIOT's contrastive pre-training) by designing neuroscience-informed pretext tasks—band rejection, phase shift, and amplitude scale prediction—that are inherently sensor-count agnostic and target properties known to be functionally relevant to speech processing. The key conceptual departure from the broader speech-decoding literature is treating heterogeneous, unlabelled MEG data from unrelated experiments as a scalable pre-training resource rather than an obstacle, inverting the field's prior assumption that cross-subject and cross-dataset pooling degrades performance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work demonstrates for the first time that non-invasive MEG speech decoding can match intracranial surgical decoding performance and that unlabelled data from non-linguistic tasks (resting-state, sensorimotor) improves downstream speech decoding, opening a path to leverage the growing ecosystem of public MEG repositories. The demonstrated scaling laws and novel-subject generalisation suggest a viable route toward deployable non-invasive speech BCIs that do not require per-patient calibration. Key open questions include whether the approach extends to full transcript decoding (brain-to-text), whether it generalises to EEG or other modalities, and how much further performance can improve as more heterogeneous datasets are aggregated—currently limited to two datasets and two relatively simple downstream tasks.
