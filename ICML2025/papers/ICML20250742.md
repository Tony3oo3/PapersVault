## Problem

While convex MDPs extend single-agent RL to allow general convex preferences over occupancy measures (capturing goals like fairness, safety, imitation, and creativity), no formal multi-agent extension existed. In particular, there was no definition of a multi-agent framework generalizing Markov games beyond linear (additive) utilities, and no proof that Nash equilibria even exist in such a setting, since the non-convexity of best-response sets in policy space breaks the Kakutani fixed-point assumptions underpinning prior Markov game results.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a finite game specification (state space, per-player action spaces, joint transition kernel, initial state distribution, discount factor) together with concave utility functions defined over each player's state-action occupancy measure, plus an initial policy profile and a temperature annealing schedule. It outputs a stationary pure-strategy Nash equilibrium policy profile (one stochastic policy per state per player) along with a closed-form upper bound on exploitability. Policies are parameterized in unconstrained logit space with softmax normalization, and the loss is computed by inverting the Bellman flow constraint matrix [I − γP^π] to recover occupancy measures from policies, making the entire pipeline differentiable end-to-end.

## Method

The paper defines convex Markov Games (cMGs) where each player's utility is a concave function of their occupancy measure, and proves pure-strategy Nash equilibrium existence via topological arguments: best-response sets are contractible (though non-convex in policy space), satisfying the conditions of Debreu's/Kosowsky's fixed-point theorem. For computation, the key insight is a projected-gradient loss L^τ(π) = Σᵢ ‖Π_{TUᵢ}(∇_{μᵢ}^{iτ})‖² that upper-bounds exploitability, where Π_{TUᵢ} projects the entropy-regularized utility gradient onto the tangent space of the feasible occupancy set (defined by linear Bellman flow constraints), and the bound is ε ≤ τ log(|S| max|Aᵢ|) + √(2nL^τ). The algorithm (PGL) minimizes this loss via Adam over unconstrained policy logits, repeatedly annealing the temperature τ to zero, combining the policy view's independent convex strategy sets (for simple per-player updates) with the occupancy measure view's convexity (for the exploitability guarantee). This homotopy-style annealing of entropy or KL-regularization terms enables the discovery of equilibria with desirable properties—creative exploration, human-like behavior, fairness, or safety—that would be missed by baselines converging to state-independent Nash equilibria of the underlying normal-form game.

## Results

In the iterated prisoner's dilemma (IPD) imitation experiment, PGL recovers a symmetric policy profile with per-player utility 0.48 (vs. 0.46 for the human reference) and exploitability of 1.4×10⁻⁴ at every state, compared to 0.47 for the human policy — three orders of magnitude less exploitable. In the iterated public goods game (IPGG), PGL finds a contributing policy with per-player utility 0.03, whereas all baselines converge to zero-contribution policies with utility 0. In the Bach-Stravinsky fairness experiment, PGL achieves maximum exploitability of 2.5×10⁻⁵ and a maximum fairness gap of 2.14×10⁻⁵ across 10 random trials. In the robot warehouse safety domain, PGL achieves exploitability ≤ 3.4×10⁻² with the safety loss (reducing fast-action frequency from 69% to 42%) and 1.0×10⁻³ without it.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares PGL against four baselines: min-ε (direct exploitability minimization via CVXPYLAYERS), Sim (simultaneous gradient descent), RR (round-robin alternating gradient descent), and SGAME-SOLVER (homotopy methods for Markov games). The min-ε baseline crashes due to numerical instabilities in all iterated NFGs, making it non-competitive. RR exhibits similar qualitative convergence behavior to PGL and converges faster than Sim, but converges to less desirable equilibria — e.g., an asymmetric NE in El Farol (one player always goes to the bar) and state-independent NEs in IPD (DD) and IPGG (zero contribution). SGAME-SOLVER also converges to the symmetric state-independent NE of the underlying NFG (attending the bar with probability 0.707 in El Farol, DD in IPD, zero contribution in IPGG). PGL's key advantage is discovering more nuanced, state-dependent, and symmetric equilibria: in IPD it finds a tit-for-tat-like policy with reciprocation (utility 0.47), in IPGG it finds a contributing policy (utility 0.03 vs. 0 for all baselines), and in the imitation setting it achieves 3 orders of magnitude lower exploitability than the human reference while maintaining slightly higher utility.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper fills a structural gap in the RL/game theory landscape: while convex MDPs (cMDPs) generalize single-agent MDPs to allow non-additive preferences over occupancy measures, and Markov games (MGs) generalize MDPs to the multi-agent setting, no formal multi-agent framework with convex utilities over occupancy measures existed. The existence proof departs from the standard Kakutani fixed-point argument used for Markov games (Fink, 1964) because cMG best-response sets are non-convex in policy space and Bellman value functions do not exist; instead, the authors appeal to topological contractibility results (Kosowsky, 2023; Debreu, 1952). The projected-gradient loss for equilibrium computation extends the normal-form game loss of Gemp et al. (2024) to the sequential setting, and the temperature-annealing protocol draws on quantal response equilibrium homotopy methods (McKelvey & Palfrey, 1995; Eibelshäuser & Poensgen, 2019).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The cMG framework provides the first formal multi-agent setting where preferences over long-run behavior (fairness, safety, imitation, creativity) can be expressed as general convex functions of occupancy measures, with a guaranteed pure-strategy Nash equilibrium and a differentiable loss for its computation. Key limitations include the centralized assumption requiring known transition dynamics, the absence of convergence guarantees for the PGL algorithm, and the PPAD-hardness of exact equilibrium computation inherited from Markov games. Open questions include extending to model-free/decentralized training, analyzing correlated or coarse-correlated equilibria, and scaling to richer policy classes and larger domains beyond the small 2–4 state games tested here.
