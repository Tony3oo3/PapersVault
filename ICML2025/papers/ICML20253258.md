## Problem

Medical LLM benchmarks such as MedQA are arbitrarily constructed from USMLE questions and widely used to claim that models 'encode clinical knowledge' or 'reason like physicians,' yet no empirical framework exists to verify whether benchmark performance actually reflects real-world clinical competence. The paper argues that the field lacks a principled, quantitative method for assessing whether these benchmarks validly measure the constructs they purport to capture, leaving leaderboard rankings and capability claims unsupported.

## Input / Output

The validation pipeline takes three inputs: (1) a set of LLM models (GPT-4, Llama 3, and Medical-LLM leaderboard entries), (2) the MedQA benchmark (1,273 USMLE-derived multiple-choice items with clinical vignettes), and (3) real-world physician progress notes from UCSF EHR data in SOAP format. MedQA items are matched to ~10 real-world patient encounters each via standardized codes (RxNorm for drug answers, SNOMED for diagnosis answers) extracted from the 'Assessment and Plan' section. Outputs include a criterion validity coefficient α (conditional probability of real-world correctness given benchmark correctness), UMLS concept coverage distributions comparing benchmark vs. real-world notes, and model ranking comparisons across benchmark and real-world tasks.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a proof-of-concept framework that applies classical psychometric validity theory (criterion, content, construct) to medical LLM benchmarks using EHR data as ground truth. For criterion validity, each MedQA question is linked to real-world patient cases sharing the same correct-answer code, and α = P(Correct on real-world | Correct on MedQA) is computed per model; results show α offers little improvement over baseline real-world accuracy, indicating weak predictive validity. For content validity, cTAKES extracts UMLS concepts from both MedQA vignettes and real-world notes, revealing that MedQA covers far fewer concepts per item and disproportionately emphasizes diagnosis over treatment tasks. For construct validity, model rankings on MedQA are compared against rankings on matched real-world cases, exposing significant rank inversions (e.g., GPT-4 tops MedQA but Llama 3 outperforms on real-world notes). The overarching insight is that LLM benchmarks function as psychological tests—LLMs are test subjects, capabilities are latent constructs, and benchmark scores are test scores—so the field should adopt the empirical validation procedures already standard in psychometrics rather than accepting benchmarks on face validity alone.

## Results

The paper's central empirical finding is that MedQA exhibits poor criterion validity: the conditional accuracy metric α = P(Correct on real-world case | Correct on MedQA) ranges only from 0.29 (GPT-4) to 0.56 (Llama 3), far from the ideal of 1.0. All seven evaluated models show a general accuracy drop when transitioning from MedQA multiple-choice questions to matched real-world EHR clinical notes (e.g., GPT-4 drops from 0.71 to 0.28; PMY MedLLama drops from 0.75 to 0.36). Content validity analysis further reveals that MedQA vignettes contain significantly fewer UMLS concepts than real-world clinical notes and disproportionately favor diagnostic over treatment-related tasks, suggesting the benchmark represents a much simpler content domain than actual clinical practice.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Rather than comparing competing methods, the paper compares each model's performance on MedQA against its performance on matched real-world EHR cases to test whether the benchmark predicts clinical utility. The most striking discrepancy is between GPT-4 and Llama 3: GPT-4 achieves the highest MedQA accuracy (0.71) yet the lowest real-world accuracy (0.28) and lowest α (0.29), while Llama 3 has the lowest MedQA score (0.54) but the highest real-world accuracy (0.48) and highest α (0.56). This ranking inversion directly undermines the construct validity of MedQA as a measure of medical knowledge. Among the Medical-LLM Leaderboard models (Chimera Llama, Biomerge, JSL MedLlama, PMY MedLLama, Orpomed), α values cluster between 0.38 and 0.49, indicating that even top leaderboard models show only weak transfer from benchmark to real-world performance. The authors conclude that MedQA's contrived multiple-choice format and simplified vignettes (fewer UMLS concepts, fewer irrelevant details) explain the poor predictive validity, as LLMs are known to be susceptible to distraction by irrelevant context in real-world notes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the psychometric validity framework from Cronbach & Meehl (1955) and Messick (1989) — originally developed for psychological and educational testing — and applies it systematically to medical LLM benchmark evaluation, a domain where such rigor has been largely absent. It builds on qualitative critiques of benchmark validity (Raji et al., 2021 for ImageNet/GLUE; Fang et al., 2024 for ImageNet transfer) by moving from descriptive concerns to empirical, EHR-grounded validation procedures. The paper also positions itself alongside concurrent efforts like Johri et al. (2025) that redesign evaluation tasks for realism, but argues those ad-hoc fixes lack the principled, generalizable framework that construct validity theory provides. No prior work has used real-world EHR data as a criterion to quantitatively score the validity of a medical LLM benchmark, making this a novel intersection of clinical informatics and psychometrics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that MedQA rankings (GPT-4 at 0.71) invert on real-world clinical notes (Llama 3 at 0.48 vs. GPT-4 at 0.28) directly challenges the assumption underpinning the Medical-LLM Leaderboard and much of the medical LLM literature that benchmark scores proxy for clinical competence. Open questions remain substantial: the proof-of-concept uses a single hospital (UCSF), one benchmark (MedQA), and a matching strategy (correct-answer code lookup) that the authors themselves acknowledge may not capture true clinical similarity. The proposed hospital-as-validator ecosystem is promising but untested at scale, and extending the framework beyond multiple-choice diagnostic tasks to open-ended clinical reasoning, summarization, or treatment planning remains future work. If adopted, this paradigm could fundamentally reshape how medical LLMs are evaluated, deployed, and regulated.
