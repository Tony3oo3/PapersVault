## Problem

Diffusion models struggle with molecule generation because molecular representations (SMILES tokens, atom/bond types) are inherently discrete, making it difficult to connect raw molecule data with complex natural language conditions. Prior diffusion-based molecule generators either used only simple physiochemical conditions or underperformed autoregressive baselines on text-conditioned tasks, leaving a gap in whether diffusion models could match or exceed AR models for textual molecule generation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250274/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For generation, the model takes a free-form natural language prompt (e.g., 'a molecule containing a pyridine ring and a carboxylic acid group') and outputs a valid SMILES string (max 128 BPE tokens from a 300-token vocabulary). For retrieval, it takes a query molecule and a set of candidate text descriptions, returning the description that minimizes noise estimation error. For editing, it takes a source molecule's latent representation plus a target text prompt and produces a modified SMILES. The internal latent representation is a [128 × 64] tensor, compressed from the encoder's [128 × 1024] output via a linear layer.

## Method

LDMol introduces a three-stage pipeline: (1) a SMILES encoder (12-layer BERT-base) trained with a novel contrastive objective where positive pairs are all enumerated SMILES of the same molecule (via graph traversal permutations) and negatives are SMILES from different molecules, using a symmetric InfoNCE loss with a memory queue; this forces the encoder to learn SMILES-enumeration-invariant structural representations rather than surface token patterns. (2) A linear compression layer reduces the encoder output to [128 × 64], and an autoregressive transformer decoder (with cross-attention) reconstructs SMILES from the compressed latent, jointly trained with the compression layer while the encoder is frozen. (3) A DiT-base diffusion model operates in the compressed latent space, conditioned on text via cross-attention layers inserted after each self-attention block, using a frozen MolT5-large text encoder and classifier-free guidance (ω=2.5) with DDIM 100-step sampling. The key insight is that structurally informative latent geometry—where feature proximity reflects molecular connectivity rather than token sequence similarity—makes the latent distribution far easier for the diffusion model to learn and align with text conditions.

## Results

On ChEBI-20, LDMol achieves a BLEU of 0.926, Levenshtein distance of 6.75, Morgan Tanimoto similarity of 0.931, exact match ratio of 0.530, and FCD of 0.20, outperforming all autoregressive and diffusion baselines on every metric except validity (0.941 vs. 1.000 for bioT5+). On PCDes, LDMol similarly leads with BLEU 0.857, Levenshtein 8.73, Morgan FTS 0.780, match 0.464, and FCD 0.32. For molecule-to-text retrieval, LDMol (n=25) reaches 62.2% sentence and 90.3% paragraph accuracy on PCdes, and 78.4% and 87.1% on the out-of-distribution MoMu set, surpassing all prior methods including MoleculeSTM and MolCA.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LDMol outperforms the strongest autoregressive baseline bioT5+ on ChEBI-20 by a wide margin on BLEU (0.926 vs. 0.872), Levenshtein distance (6.75 vs. 12.78), Morgan Tanimoto (0.931 vs. 0.779), and FCD (0.20 vs. 0.35), though bioT5+ achieves perfect validity (1.000 vs. 0.941). Against the only prior diffusion baseline TGM-DLM, LDMol dominates on every metric, e.g., BLEU 0.926 vs. 0.826 and FCD 0.20 vs. 0.77. On PCDes, LDMol beats bioT5 (the best AR there) on BLEU (0.857 vs. 0.754), Levenshtein (8.73 vs. 15.66), and FCD (0.32 vs. 0.69). For molecule-to-text retrieval, LDMol (n=25) surpasses MolCA (73.4% paragraph on MoMu) by 13.7 points (87.1%) and MoleculeSTM (67.6%) by 20.5 points, while also leading on PCdes paragraph retrieval (90.3% vs. MolCA's 86.4%). In text-guided molecule editing, LDMol achieves a higher hit ratio than MoleculeSTM in 5 out of 8 scenarios. The key trade-off is that LDMol's validity (0.941–0.944) is slightly below the 1.000 achieved by bioT5/bioT5+, but the authors argue that alignment with the text condition (captured by BLEU, Tanimoto, and FCD) is the more important objective for a text-to-molecule generator.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the latent diffusion paradigm (Rombach et al., 2022; Vahdat et al., 2021) from images to molecule generation, using a DiT transformer backbone (Peebles & Xie, 2023) instead of the convolutional U-Net typical of image diffusion. The key departure from prior molecule diffusion work (Gong et al., 2024; Luo et al., 2023; Hoogeboom et al., 2022) is the explicit design of a structurally informative latent space via contrastive learning with SMILES enumeration (Bjerrum, 2017) as augmentation, rather than applying Gaussian diffusion directly to discrete tokens or using a naive autoencoder (Xu et al., 2023). The text-conditioning leverages the MolT5 encoder (Edwards et al., 2022), and downstream applications borrow the Delta Denoising Score framework (Hertz et al., 2023) from image editing.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LDMol demonstrates that careful latent space design can close the long-standing performance gap between diffusion and autoregressive models on sequential/discrete data, with the ablation showing complete failure (validity 0.019) when the contrastive encoder is removed. The learned score function enables zero-shot downstream tasks (molecule-to-text retrieval, text-guided editing) without task-specific training, a practical advantage over AR baselines. Limitations include lower validity than the best AR models (0.941 vs 1.000 for bioT5+), difficulty with complex biological property conditions, and reliance on only 320K training pairs versus millions used by AR baselines. Open questions include whether the latent-space-design principle generalizes to other text generation domains and whether richer text-molecule corpora would further close the remaining gap.
