## Problem

Evaluating ML models with human-labeled validation data is expensive and time-consuming, and while AI-generated synthetic labels can reduce annotation costs, standard autoevaluation methods are ad hoc and produce systematically biased estimates of model performance. There has been no statistically principled framework for combining a small amount of human-labeled data with a large amount of synthetic data to obtain unbiased, lower-variance estimates of arbitrary model metrics while maintaining valid confidence intervals.

## Input / Output

The method requires a small human-labeled dataset {(X_i, Y_i)}_{i=1}^n, a large unlabeled dataset {X_i^u}_{i=1}^N (with N >> n), and synthetic label distributions dP̃_{i,m}(y) from an AI annotator model approximating p(Y|X); the annotator may output either point predictions (modeled as delta functions) or full distributions over Y. The method produces point estimates of arbitrary metrics φ: Y×Y→R (accuracy, Pearson correlation, Bradley-Terry coefficients), coordinatewise and simultaneous (chi-squared) confidence intervals, and model rankings with formal coverage guarantees. A tuning parameter λ ∈ [0,1] controls the weight given to synthetic data, gracefully falling back to the classical estimator when the annotator is uninformative.

## Method

The core estimator decomposes into two terms: (1) the average metric predicted by the annotator model over all N unlabeled points, scaled by a discount factor λ, and (2) a bias-correction term computed on the n labeled points as the difference between the true metric φ(f_m(X_i), Y_i) and the annotator's predicted metric Ê_{i,m}. For any fixed λ, the estimator is unbiased; optimally choosing λ (via the PPI++ procedure) minimizes the asymptotic variance, yielding strictly lower variance than the classical estimator. For pairwise comparisons, the same debiasing principle is applied to the Bradley-Terry model by constructing an unbiased, lower-variance logistic regression loss that incorporates AI-generated preferences alongside human preferences. The framework also extends to handle covariate shifts between labeled and unlabeled data via importance-weighted estimators using the Radon-Nikodym derivative.

## Results

Across three experiments, PPI++ consistently outperforms the classical estimator. On ImageNet (ResNet-18 through ResNet-152), PPI++ achieves approximately 50% higher effective sample size (ESS) than the classical approach, with dramatically stronger correlation between estimated and true model rankings. On ProteinGym (protein fitness prediction), PPI++ again yields ~50% ESS improvement and a five-fold improvement in ranking correlation at n=1000 labeled points. On Chatbot Arena (20 LLMs, Bradley-Terry coefficients), PPI++ provides 20–25% ESS improvement and tighter, calibrated confidence intervals. Even with a mediocre annotator model (CARP), PPI++ still delivers a 10% ESS gain over the classical baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three estimators: the classical (human-data-only) approach, PPI (λ=1), and PPI++ (optimized λ). PPI++ wins on all three benchmarks in terms of point-estimate MSE, ESS, and ranking correlation. On ImageNet, both PPI and PPI++ beat the classical baseline in MSE, with little difference between them, suggesting the self-annotated softmax scores are reliable proxies. On ProteinGym, PPI++ clearly outperforms classical (~50% ESS gain), but PPI actually performs worse than classical—a known limitation that PPI++ mitigates through its optimized λ. On Chatbot Arena, PPI++ improves ESS by 20–25% over classical, and all tested LLM judges (GPT-4o-mini and others) yield 20–35% ESS gains. The classical approach is the only baseline; no other autoevaluation or debiasing method (e.g., control variates from Chaganty et al.) is directly benchmarked, though the paper positions itself as complementary to prior PPI-based ranking work (Saad-Falcon et al., Chatzi et al.).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper instantiates Prediction-Powered Inference (PPI) and its optimized variant PPI++ as a general-purpose framework for autoevaluation, extending the statistical tool from its original mean-estimation setting to arbitrary metrics and to the Bradley-Terry model for pairwise comparisons. It departs from prior autoevaluation work (ad hoc AI-annotated benchmarks, automatic metrics like BLEU/ROUGE, and control-variate approaches for MT) by providing unbiased estimators with valid confidence intervals that provably reduce variance relative to classical human-only evaluation. The paper is concurrent with and complementary to Saad-Falcon et al. (ARES) and Chatzi et al., who also apply PPI-type ideas to LLM ranking, but distinguishes itself through practical implementation, distributional annotator outputs, and empirical validation across three domains.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

AutoEval offers a statistically rigorous path to reducing annotation costs from months to days while maintaining valid inference, which is critical for certifying model safety, fairness, and significance of performance differences before deployment. A key practical finding is graceful degradation: even mediocre annotator models yield ~10% effective sample size gains, while poor annotators cause the method to fall back to the classical estimator (λ→0) rather than producing biased results. Open questions include extending to non-i.i.d. regimes, evaluating fairness and bias metrics via PPI, and understanding how distributional shift between labeled and unlabeled data (addressed here only via importance reweighting) affects real-world deployments where labeled data is often non-representative.
