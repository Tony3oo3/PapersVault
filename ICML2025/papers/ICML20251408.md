## Problem

Optimization-based causal discovery methods under the additive noise model (ANM) exhibit inconsistent performance across problems with identical causal structures, yet no principled explanation for this variability existed. The paper identifies that the presence of implicit functions in structural equations creates a fundamental distinction between strongly and weakly identifiable problems, and that existing methods exploit only one of two complementary signals (goodness-of-fit or residual independence), failing on the other type.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251408/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

GENE consumes an observational sample matrix D ∈ R^{n×d} of n i.i.d. observations over d variables and outputs a directed acyclic graph (DAG) representing the causal structure. The output is constructed in two stages: first a causal order (a permutation of the d variables) is found via greedy search, then a parent set for each variable is selected by iterative pruning from the complete DAG implied by that order. The method requires no prior knowledge of whether the problem is strongly or weakly identifiable, and operates without GPU acceleration.

## Method

GENE is an order-based search framework whose core innovation is a unified fitness function (Eq. 6) that multiplies R² goodness-of-fit by a residual independence penalty computed via chi-square tests on discretized residuals. For each variable in a candidate order, a two-hidden-layer neural network regresses it on all preceding variables; the R² metric (rather than MSE) ensures scale invariance by normalizing by the effect variable's variance, addressing a known failure mode of continuous-optimization methods under data rescaling. The greedy order search is proven to reach the global optimum (a true causal order) under a mild monotonicity assumption on the fitness function (Theorem 4.3). A theoretical contribution formalizes strong vs. weak identifiability via the implicit function theorem, providing a sufficient condition (bounded partial derivatives of the equivalent implicit equation) to classify structural equations. The final parent selection uses a 'least pruning' strategy that iteratively removes the parent whose removal causes the smallest R² drop, below a threshold, handling the non-additive nature of ANM.

## Results

On the real-world Sachs protein signaling network (11 nodes, 17 edges, 853 observational samples), GENE achieves an F1 Score of 0.65 and SHD of 11, substantially outperforming all nine baselines. On synthetic Erdős–Rényi DAGs (d=10, 20; density 1, 2, 4; n=3000), GENE is competitive with state-of-the-art on strongly identifiable problems (MIM, GP) and uniquely effective on weakly identifiable problems (MLP), where methods relying solely on goodness of fit (CAM, Notears-MLP, R2Sort, CaPS, NHTS) degrade significantly. After data standardization, continuous-optimization baselines (Notears-MLP, Gran-DAG) suffer 34% F1 drops on strong problems and 52% on weak ones, while GENE remains stable due to its R²-based scale invariance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GENE decisively outperforms all nine baselines on the Sachs dataset, achieving F1=0.65 vs. the next-best CAM (0.38) and R2Sort (0.35), with SHD=11 vs. 16–17 for those methods. On synthetic weakly identifiable (MLP) problems, GENE is the only method that maintains strong performance; Notears-MLP nominally achieves higher F1 but is unreliable, dropping sharply after standardization. Among order-based methods, GENE's order quality (measured by degree of reverse Rev) is roughly half that of CAM and a quarter of RESIT on MLP, confirming the benefit of the residual independence penalty. Continuous-optimization methods (Notears-MLP, Gran-DAG) are the most affected by data rescaling, losing 34–52% F1 after standardization, whereas GENE and other combinatorial methods remain stable. On strongly identifiable problems (MIM, GP), GENE is competitive with CAM and R2Sort but does not consistently dominate, while methods like SELF and GSF (DAG-space search) are outperformed on both F1 and SHD across most settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

GENE unifies two previously separate strands of order-based causal discovery: the goodness-of-fit approach (CAM, R2Sort, CaPS, NHTS) and the residual-independence approach (RESIT, PC-ANM), which prior work treated as complementary but never integrated into a single fitness function. Theoretically, the paper departs from the standard ANM identifiability framework of Hoyer et al. (2008) by introducing a strong/weak identifiability distinction grounded in the implicit function theorem, explaining why regression-based methods succeed or fail on specific structural equations. Methodologically, GENE contrasts with continuous-optimization approaches (Notears, Gran-DAG) by explicitly separating cause and effect variables during regression, which yields scale invariance—a property those methods provably lack.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The strong/weak identifiability taxonomy provides a principled explanation for why optimization-based causal discovery methods exhibit inconsistent performance across problems with identical causal structures, a gap that had been observed empirically but not formally characterized. GENE's ability to handle both types in a unified framework, combined with its scale invariance, makes it a more robust practical tool, as demonstrated by its substantial margin over all baselines on the Sachs dataset. Open questions include whether the implicit-function criterion generalizes beyond ANM (e.g., post-nonlinear models), whether the pruning strategy can be tightened, and whether the framework extends to settings with latent variables or interventional data.
