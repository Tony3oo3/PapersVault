## Problem

Prior theoretical explanations of Neural Collapse rely on the Unconstrained Features Model (UFM), which treats penultimate-layer features as free optimization variables and thus ignores data dependence and actual training dynamics. This makes it impossible to connect NC1 (vanishing within-class variability) to the loss landscape of a real network or to show that NC1 co-occurs with small test error. The paper asks whether NC1 can be derived from properties of the loss landscape of a concrete neural network trained by gradient flow, and under what data conditions this collapse is compatible with generalization.

## Input / Output

The model consumes data points x ∈ R^d (784-dim MNIST pixels or 2048-dim pretrained ResNet-50 features from CIFAR-100) and outputs a q-dimensional logit vector via a linear readout W^T h_ρ(x). The feature-learning component is parameterized not by finite weights but by a probability measure ρ over (a, u) pairs in R^{p+d}, representing the mean-field limit of an infinite-width first layer; the second layer has constant width p. The key diagnostic output is the NC1 metric, a ratio of within-class to total feature variance, which the paper shows decreases to near zero during training alongside the loss and gradient norm.

## Method

The paper analyzes a three-layer network consisting of a two-layer mean-field network (infinite-width first layer with activation σ, constant-width second layer) followed by a linear layer W, trained via noisy Wasserstein gradient flow on an MSE loss with L² and entropic regularization. The central result (Theorem 4.2) shows that at any ε_S-stationary point with small empirical loss, the learned feature matrix decomposes as H_ρ ≈ γ⁻¹W(W^T W)⁻¹Y + E₂, where the leading term exactly satisfies NC1 and the error E₂ is controlled by the gradient norm and residual loss. A second result (Theorem 4.8) proves that gradient flow on the regularized free energy drives both the gradient norm and the loss to O(β⁻¹), thereby guaranteeing NC1 for sufficiently long training. For the generalization analysis, the paper introduces a two-stage algorithm (first optimizing ρ with W fixed, then jointly training) and shows that on (τ,M)-linearly separable data distributions, the test error vanishes as O(√(log n / n)) while NC1 simultaneously holds, without requiring the balancedness condition used in prior work.

## Results

The paper's main contribution is theoretical: it proves that any ε_S-stationary point with small empirical loss approximately satisfies NC1 (Theorem 4.2, Corollary 4.4), and that gradient flow on the three-layer mean-field network provably reaches such points (Corollary 4.9), with the NC1 metric bounded by an arbitrary δ_0 for sufficiently large β and training time. For well-separated (τ,M)-linearly separable data, Theorem 5.3 further shows that both NC1 ≤ δ_0 and test error O(√(log n / n)) are achieved simultaneously. Empirically, on MNIST (3 classes, 10k samples each) and CIFAR-100 (3 superclasses, 500 samples each), the NC1 metric decreases and flattens to a low value during SGD training, while the normalized balancedness metric NB(ρ,W) remains high (0.59–0.85 on MNIST; 0.28–0.54 on CIFAR-100), confirming that NC1 is achieved without the network converging to a balanced solution.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against competing methods in the traditional sense; its comparisons are conceptual and theoretical. Against the Unconstrained Features Model (UFM) framework (Mixon et al. 2022; Fang et al. 2021a; Han et al. 2022), the present work provides a data-specific analysis that captures the influence of the training distribution and the actual gradient-flow dynamics, which the UFM neglects by treating features as free variables. Against Jacot et al. (2024), who prove NC1 for networks with two consecutive linear layers under a balancedness assumption, this paper shows that NC1 holds at any approximately stationary point with small loss regardless of balancedness — a strictly weaker condition that is naturally satisfied by gradient flow. The empirical results on MNIST and CIFAR-100 corroborate this distinction: the trained networks achieve low NC1 while maintaining high normalized balancedness (NB ≈ 0.59–0.85), a regime where the Jacot et al. (2024) theory does not apply. The paper also notes that its proof does not rely on the specific training algorithms (e.g., Deep Recursive Feature Machine in Beaglehole et al. 2024) or the L_2 feature regularization (Hong & Ling 2024a) used in other beyond-UFM studies.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant Unconstrained Features Model (UFM) line of work (Mixon et al., 2022; Fang et al., 2021a; Han et al., 2022) by adopting a data-specific mean-field regime for the feature-learning layers, building on the infinite-width two-layer framework of Mei et al. (2018) and the multiplicative-scaling convergence strategy of Chen et al. (2020). It also differentiates itself from recent beyond-UFM efforts: unlike Jacot et al. (2024), it does not require a balancedness condition on the linear layers; unlike Seleznova et al. (2024) or Kothapalli & Tirer (2024), it does not rely on NTK block-structure assumptions; and unlike Beaglehole et al. (2024) or Hong & Ling (2024a), it uses standard gradient flow rather than specialized algorithms or non-standard regularization. The key conceptual departure is connecting NC1 to generic loss-landscape properties (small gradient norm and small empirical loss) rather than to architectural or distributional symmetries.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean, data-dependent theoretical explanation for why NC1 emerges during gradient-based training, bridging the gap between the data-agnostic UFM theory and actual training dynamics. The finding that NC1 holds even at imbalanced stationary points (where entropic regularization prevents the balancedness condition of Jacot et al., 2024) is a meaningful conceptual advance, confirmed empirically on MNIST and CIFAR-100. However, the scope remains limited: only NC1 is addressed (not NC2/NC3), only MSE loss is analyzed (cross-entropy is left open), the generalization result requires (τ,M)-linear separability, and only the first layer is taken to infinite width. Open questions include extending to cross-entropy loss, handling non-linearities between the last two layers, and identifying necessary (not just sufficient) conditions for the co-occurrence of NC1 and vanishing test error.
