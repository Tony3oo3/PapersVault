## Problem

Latent variables in deep generative models are not statistically identifiable: independently trained models produce different latent coordinates and pairwise Euclidean distances for the same data, undermining reliable interpretation. Existing identifiability solutions require labeled data or restrictive model assumptions (e.g., linearity), which are impractical for modern generative models. This paper reframes the question—instead of identifying latent variables themselves, it identifies invariant relationships between them (distances, angles, volumes) under mild model conditions and without labels.

## Input / Output

The method is entirely post-hoc and requires no changes to model training. It consumes a trained decoder f (from an M-flow, normalizing flow, diffusion model, or VAE), pairs of latent points z₁, z₂ ∈ Z, and a chosen observation-space metric g^D (typically Euclidean). It produces geodesic distances d_{g^f}(z₁, z₂) computed by minimizing the energy of cubic-spline-parametrized curves in the latent space under the pullback metric, along with other identifiable Riemannian quantities (angles, volumes, areas, Ricci curvature). The pullback metric g^f = f* g^D is constructed by pulling the ambient metric back through the decoder's differential, so distances are measured along the data manifold rather than through the ambient space.

## Method

The key insight is a formal connection between indeterminacy transformations (from identifiability theory) and coordinate charts (from differential geometry): equivalent model parametrizations with the same marginal distribution correspond to different charts on the same smooth manifold M = f(Z), and the generator transformations A_{a,b} = f_b^{-1} ∘ f_a between charts are isometries with respect to the pullback metric, i.e., (A_{a,b})^* g^{f_b} = g^{f_a}. This isometry result (Theorem 4.5) immediately implies that all Riemannian geometric properties—geodesic distances, angles, volumes, parallel transport, the exponential map—are identifiable (Theorem 4.7). The paper further proves (Proposition 4.8) that Euclidean distances in the latent space are identifiable only if the decoder parametrizes a flat manifold, explaining why naive Euclidean distances are unreliable. For practical computation, geodesics are parametrized by cubic splines with free knot positions and polynomial coefficients, reduced to a null-space parameterization via SVD, and optimized by minimizing the discretized energy functional using Adam over an ensemble of 8 decoders to account for decoder uncertainty.

## Results

Across four datasets (MNIST, CIFAR-10, Fashion-MNIST, CELEBA) and two model architectures (M-flows satisfying all assumptions A1–A4, and VAEs with relaxed injectivity), geodesic distances computed under the pullback metric exhibit significantly lower coefficient of variation (CV) across 30 independently trained models than Euclidean distances. One-sided Student's t-tests yield highly significant t-statistics of −8.64 (MNIST), −16.75 (FMNIST), −42.83 (CIFAR-10), and −22.33 (CELEBA), confirming that geodesic distances are substantially more stable under model retraining. This stability holds even when the injectivity assumption (A2) is violated, as in standard VAEs, suggesting the identifiability guarantee is robust in practice.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external baseline methods; instead, the central empirical comparison is between Euclidean latent distances (the standard, cheap-to-compute baseline) and geodesic distances under the pullback metric (the proposed identifiable alternative). Geodesic distances win decisively on the stability metric (coefficient of variation across 30 retrains) on all four datasets, with the largest margin on CIFAR-10 (t = −42.83). The comparison is further stratified by model class: M-flows (which satisfy all theoretical assumptions including injectivity) and VAEs (where injectivity is not guaranteed). Geodesic distances remain more stable in both settings, indicating that the identifiability benefit persists even when the theoretical assumptions are only partially met. The paper notes that Euclidean distances are computationally cheaper (no iterative optimization required), but Proposition 4.8 shows that Euclidean identifiability would require the decoder to parametrize a flat manifold, which is generally not the case for expressive deep models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper formally connects two previously disjoint fields: the identifiability theory of Xi & Bloem-Reddy (2023), which characterizes indeterminacy transformations in generative models, and the Riemannian geometric framework for latent spaces developed by Shao et al. (2018) and Hauberg (2018). Rather than imposing the strong constraints (linearity, labeled data, multi-view) that prior identifiability results require, the paper reframes the question from identifying latent variables to identifying metric relationships between them, using pullback metrics and geodesic distances. The empirical pipeline builds on the decoder ensembling approach of Syrota et al. (2024) to account for decoder uncertainty in geodesic computation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that Euclidean distances in latent space are identifiable only under a flatness constraint (Proposition 4.8) challenges the widespread practice of interpreting latent space plots with Euclidean geometry, and provides a principled alternative (geodesic distances) that requires no changes to model training. This opens the door to a full suite of Riemannian statistical tools (Fréchet means, principal geodesic analysis, Kalman filters on manifolds) for latent space analysis with identifiability guarantees. Key limitations include the computational cost of geodesic optimization, the sensitivity to the choice of observation-space metric, and the injectivity assumption (A2) that general VAEs do not satisfy, though experiments suggest robustness even when A2 is relaxed.
