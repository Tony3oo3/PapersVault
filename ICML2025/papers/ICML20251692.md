## Problem

Existing reward shaping for sparse-reward RL splits into two camps: novelty-based exploration bonuses that risk distracting agents from task goals (e.g., the noisy-TV problem), and hidden-state-value methods that converge well but lack directional exploration guidance. No prior method efficiently computes both reward types within a single lightweight mechanism, leaving a gap in balancing early exploration with later exploitation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251692/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes raw environment states (RGB frames at 84×84 for Atari/VizDoom, low-dimensional state vectors for MiniWorld) and produces a shaped reward signal R^DuRND(s) = R^env(s) + 0.5·R^nov(s) + 0.5·R^con(s) that augments the sparse environmental reward. States are partitioned into positive (those within T_pos steps before an anchor state where environmental reward was received) and negative (all others), and this classification drives which of the two random-network modules each state updates. The shaped reward is then fed into PPO's advantage estimation to produce policy actions.

## Method

DuRND maintains two lightweight random-network modules—a positive RN and a negative RN—each consisting of a fixed random target network and a trainable predictor trained with MSE loss. The positive RN is updated only on states preceding reward-receiving anchor states (within a configurable window T_pos), while the negative RN is updated on all remaining states. The novelty reward is the sum of both prediction errors e_P(s)+e_N(s), which stays elevated early in training because the positive RN has seen few states, naturally prolonging exploration. The contribution reward is the ratio e_N(s)/(e_P(s)+e_N(s)), which increases as the positive RN accumulates more positive states and e_P decreases, progressively shifting the agent toward exploitation. This dual-module design requires only two small networks (no auxiliary agents, no large state buffers, no pseudo-count estimators), keeping memory overhead comparable to vanilla RND while providing both directed exploration and task-aligned convergence signals.

## Results

On the 12 sparse-reward environments (Atari, VizDoom, MiniWorld), DuRND-adp achieves the highest average episodic return in 10 out of 12 tasks. Headline numbers include 6654.04 on Montezuma's Revenge (vs. 5459.01 for the best baseline NGU, a 22% improvement), 42.63 on Solaris (vs. 33.74 for NGU), and 34.13 on BeamRider (vs. 29.21 for NGU). On the two MiniWorld tasks (ThreeRooms, TMaze), DuRND matches or exceeds all baselines, with PPO (no shaping) failing entirely on ThreeRooms (0.00 vs. 0.97). In the continuous-control appendix, DuRND-adp leads on 5 of 6 sparse-reward MuJoCo/robotics tasks, e.g., 173.35 on RobotPush vs. 119.32 for NGU.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DuRND-adp outperforms all nine baselines on 10 of 12 environments, with the largest margins on Montezuma's Revenge (+22% over NGU), Solaris (+26% over NGU), and BeamRider (+17% over NGU). The closest competitor is NGU (Badia et al., 2020), which is competitive on Pitfall (93.02 vs. 113.15) and CollectKit (35.53 vs. 37.34) but still trails DuRND by 10–20%. Among exploration-only baselines, RND (Burda et al., 2018) and #Explo (Tang et al., 2017) are consistently the weakest, confirming that novelty alone is insufficient for convergence; e.g., RND scores only 1337 on MZ' Revenge versus 6654 for DuRND. Hidden-value baselines (ReLara, ROSA, SORS) improve over PPO but remain below DuRND on most tasks, with ReLara closest on Pitfall (95.40) and SlayGhosts (16.22). On the two MiniWorld tasks, DuRND ties with NGU on ThreeRooms (0.99) and all methods saturate on TMaze (1.00), while PPO fails on ThreeRooms entirely. In the continuous-control appendix, DuRND-adp leads on AntFar (111.07), HumanStand (82.60), CheetahFar (124.18), and RobotPush (173.35), with DuRND-fix slightly ahead on RobotPush (177.20) and RobotPick (51.50).

## Relation to Prior Work

DuRND extends Random Network Distillation (Burda et al., 2018) from a single fixed-target/predictor pair into two parallel RN modules—one trained on states preceding reward events (positive) and one on all other states (negative). Conceptually, it unifies two previously separate reward-shaping paradigms: novelty-based exploration bonuses (RND, NGU, ExploRS, #Explo) and hidden-state-value exploitation rewards (SORS, ROSA, ReLara), which prior work treated as competing strategies. The key departure is that both the novelty reward (e_P + e_N) and the contribution reward (e_N / (e_P + e_N)) are derived from the same two prediction errors, eliminating the need for auxiliary agents, large state buffers, or pseudo-count estimators that characterize most competing methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in achieving directed exploration and stable convergence with only two lightweight RN modules, yielding roughly 10× lower memory consumption than buffer-based or multi-agent baselines while matching or exceeding their performance across Atari, VizDoom, and MiniWorld. The adaptive T_pos schedule provides a principled mechanism for the exploration-to-exploitation transition, though it still relies on manual scheduling rather than fully autonomous adjustment. Open questions include whether the positive/negative state classification generalizes well to dense-reward settings (where the paper acknowledges reduced effectiveness), whether the dual-RN approach transfers to off-policy algorithms beyond PPO, and whether the contribution reward's reliance on relative error ratios remains robust in extremely high-dimensional or non-stationary environments.
