## Problem

Existing equalized odds fairness methods are limited to a single sensitive attribute, yet real-world biases arise from multiple attributes simultaneously (e.g., race, gender, age). Extending these methods to multi-dimensional sensitive attributes is blocked by the curse of dimensionality in estimating the conditional density A|Y, which becomes intractable as the number and complexity of sensitive attributes grow.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251267/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes tabular data with feature vectors X, multi-dimensional sensitive attributes A of arbitrary mixed types (categorical, continuous, or both), and labels Y (continuous for regression or categorical for classification). It outputs a predictive model producing either a scalar prediction or a softmax probability vector, alongside a discriminator network. The sensitive attributes A can span many dimensions and heterogeneous data types, which is the key practical constraint the method is designed to handle.

## Method

FairICP combines adversarial learning with a novel Inverse Conditional Permutation (ICP) strategy to generate synthetic sensitive attribute copies Ã without estimating the intractable multi-dimensional density q(A|Y). Instead, ICP estimates the simpler one-dimensional conditional density q(Y|A) via standard regression or classification, then applies an inverse operator to the conditional permutation distribution to produce valid permuted copies of A. The training procedure follows the FDL adversarial framework: a discriminator learns to separate (Ŷ, A, Y) from (Ŷ, Ã, Y), while the predictor minimizes a fairness-regularized loss with trade-off parameter μ. The key theoretical guarantee (Theorem 2.1) shows that ICP generates valid conditional permutations and that (Ŷ, A, Y) ≡d (Ŷ, Ã, Y) holds if and only if equalized odds is satisfied, enabling both fairness-aware training and valid hypothesis testing for equalized odds violations.

## Results

Across five real-world datasets, FairICP achieves the best or near-best fairness–accuracy trade-off for equalized odds with multi-dimensional sensitive attributes. On the Communities and Crime dataset with three racial dimensions, FairICP attains KPC 0.054 at prediction loss 0.418, substantially outperforming HGR (KPC 0.068, loss 0.596) and FDL (KPC 0.058, loss 0.621). On the Adult dataset (sex × race), FairICP reaches KPC 0.010 with loss 0.159, and on COMPAS (race × sex) it achieves KPC 0.008 with loss 0.402, both at or near the best fairness levels while maintaining the lowest prediction loss among fairness-aware methods. The unfair baseline, while achieving the lowest loss, exhibits KPC values 3–10× higher than FairICP across all datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against FDL (Romano et al., 2020), FairICP consistently achieves lower prediction loss at comparable or better KPC: on Crimes (3-race) the loss drops from 0.621 to 0.418 while KPC improves from 0.058 to 0.054, and on COMPAS the loss decreases from 0.417 to 0.402 at the same KPC of 0.008. Against HGR (Mary et al., 2019), FairICP wins decisively on the multi-dimensional Crimes dataset (loss 0.418 vs. 0.596 at similar KPC) and on COMPAS (loss 0.402 vs. 0.443 at identical KPC 0.008), though HGR edges out FairICP on Adult KPC (0.006 vs. 0.010) at a slightly higher loss. Against GerryFair (Kearns et al., 2018), FairICP dominates on all three overlapping datasets, e.g., on ACS Income it achieves loss 0.215 and KPC 0.025 versus GerryFair's 0.262 and 0.050. Against the Reduction approach (Agarwal et al., 2018), the two methods are closely matched on Adult and COMPAS, with Reduction achieving marginally lower KPC (0.005 vs. 0.010 on Adult; 0.002 vs. 0.008 on COMPAS) at nearly identical loss, but Reduction is limited to binary classification with categorical attributes and cannot handle the continuous or mixed-type sensitive attributes in Crimes and ACS Income.

## Relation to Prior Work

FairICP builds directly on the adversarial resampling framework of Fair Dummies Learning (Romano et al., 2020), which enforces equalized odds by drawing synthetic sensitive attributes from the conditional distribution A|Y and training a discriminator to make (Ŷ, A, Y) indistinguishable from (Ŷ, Ã, Y). The key departure is the Inverse Conditional Permutation (ICP) strategy, which pivots the density estimation from the multi-dimensional A|Y to the typically one-dimensional Y|A, then applies an inverse permutation operator to generate valid conditional permutations of A. This is inspired by the Conditional Permutation Test (Berrett et al., 2020) but inverts the conditioning direction to circumvent the curse of dimensionality. The paper positions itself as the first in-processing equalized-odds method designed for multi-dimensional, mixed-type sensitive attributes, where prior approaches (FDL, HGR, adversarial debiasing) handle only a single attribute.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical motivation is strong: real-world fairness constraints (healthcare, criminal justice, hiring) routinely involve intersections of race, gender, and age, and ignoring correlations between attributes can produce 'fairness gerrymandering' where a model appears fair per-attribute but is jointly biased. The ICP inversion is a clean, theoretically grounded solution that makes multi-attribute equalized odds tractable without requiring multi-dimensional density estimation. Limitations include the adversarial training overhead (comparable to FDL, higher than HGR), the requirement to access sensitive attributes during training (raising ethical/legal concerns), and the fact that a perfectly fair and accurate predictor may not exist. Open questions include extending ICP to post-processing pipelines, improving training stability, and whether the approach generalizes to other fairness notions beyond equalized odds.
