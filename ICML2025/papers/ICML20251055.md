## Problem

Matrix completion under Missing Not-at-Random (MNAR) patterns where entire rows and columns of the target matrix are missing is fundamentally impossible without side information, even for low-rank incoherent matrices. Existing transfer learning approaches for matrix completion assume MCAR missingness or restrict the source-target relationship to latent-space rotations, leaving the biologically motivated setting of row/column-level missingness with general linear distribution shifts unaddressed.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251055/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a noisy (and possibly masked) source matrix P̃ ∈ R^{m×n} from which singular subspaces are recovered, plus a masked target matrix Q̃ where either a practitioner-chosen set of T_row rows and T_col columns is observed (active) or rows/columns are independently revealed with probabilities p_Row, p_Col (passive). The output is the full estimated target matrix Q̂ ∈ R^{m×n}, with guarantees stated in max-squared error and Frobenius error. In the active setting, the row/column selection is made before observing Q̃, depending only on the source data.

## Method

The estimator extracts row and column features via SVD of the source matrix P̃, then solves a least-squares regression for a d×d coefficient matrix Θ_Q in the latent feature space, predicting Q̂_ij = û_i^T Θ_Q v̂_j. For active sampling, the key insight is to use G-optimal experimental design to select which rows and columns to query: the paper proves (via the Kiefer-Wolfowitz theorem) that the tensor product of separate G-optimal designs for rows and columns is G-optimal for the joint row-column design, yielding minimax-optimal estimation without incoherence assumptions. The error decomposes into a sample-size-dependent estimation term (matching the minimax lower bound d²σ_Q²/|Ω|) and a misspecification term governed by the singular subspace recovery (SSR) quality of the source estimate. For passive sampling, an additional incoherence factor μ appears in the rate, reflecting the inability to target informative rows/columns.

## Results

On the stylized Coherent synthetic model (n=200, d=5, p_Row=p_Col=0.1), active sampling achieves a mean-squared error of 0.009±0.015×10⁻³, roughly 9× better than passive sampling (0.084×10⁻³) and ~88× better than BC22 (0.789×10⁻³). On the less stylized Matrix Partition Model, both active (0.046±0.074) and passive (0.040±0.090) sampling outperform LLL22 (0.134±0.011) and BC22 (0.305±0.002) by a factor of 3–7×. On the real-world BiGG metabolic network (251×251, rank 8, near-maximally coherent), active sampling performs well while passive sampling is very poor, confirming the theoretical advantage of G-optimal design under coherence. On the sepsis gene expression dataset (31×300, rank 4, relatively incoherent), active and passive sampling perform comparably, both outperforming the LLL22 transfer baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: BC22 (Bhattacharya & Chatterjee, 2022), an MNAR matrix completion method, and LLL22 (Levin et al., 2022), a transfer learning method for matrix completion. On the Coherent synthetic model, active sampling (0.009×10⁻³) dominates both baselines by a wide margin—roughly 7× better than LLL22 (0.061×10⁻³) and ~88× better than BC22 (0.789×10⁻³). On the Partition model, both active (0.046) and passive (0.040) sampling outperform LLL22 (0.134) by ~3× and BC22 (0.305) by ~6–7×. On the real-world metabolic network, where matrices are near-maximally coherent, active sampling clearly wins while passive sampling degrades significantly; BC22 and LLL22 are not directly reported on this dataset but the paper notes passive sampling's poor max-squared error. On the gene expression dataset, both proposed methods perform well at small sample sizes, with LLL22 achieving a worse but nontrivial maximum error; BC22 is not highlighted as competitive here. Overall, the proposed G-optimal active sampling is the strongest method in coherent settings, while both active and passive variants consistently beat both baselines across all tested scenarios.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: MNAR matrix completion (Bhattacharya & Chatterjee 2022; Jedra et al. 2023), transfer learning for low-rank matrix estimation (Xu et al. 2013; McGrath et al. 2024), and optimal experimental design (Kiefer & Wolfowitz 1960; Pukelsheim 2006). It departs from prior MNAR work by considering a structurally harder missingness pattern—entire rows and columns absent rather than independent entry-level masking—and from prior transfer learning for matrix completion by replacing the MCAR observation model with this row/column-level MNAR structure. The key technical novelty is the tensorization of G-optimal designs (Proposition 2.4), which allows the practitioner to decouple row and column sampling into independent 1D design problems while preserving global optimality for the joint least-squares estimator.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that without transfer learning, estimation is fundamentally impossible (Ω(dσ²) lower bound) under this MNAR structure, and that active sampling via G-optimal design achieves minimax optimality without incoherence assumptions—a significant advantage over passive sampling, which requires μ = O(1) for rate optimality. This has direct practical implications for biological experiments (metabolite balancing, gene expression microarrays) where coherent matrices are common and incoherence-based guarantees are inapplicable. Open questions include extending to dependent row/column masks, incorporating additional side information (e.g., gene-level features in GWAS), and developing nonlinear transfer models beyond the linear latent-space shift assumed here.
