## Problem

Existing PINN methods treat all training samples equally regardless of their learning difficulty, causing the model to fit smooth interior regions well while failing on physically challenging boundary regions — a phenomenon the authors term the Unbalanced Prediction Problem (UPP). This indiscriminate treatment leads to unstable optimization and convergence to poor local minima, particularly for PDEs with sharp gradients, shock waves, or boundary layers.

## Input / Output

The method consumes spatiotemporal coordinates sampled as collocation points on regular grids (e.g., 16³ through 256³ points per axis), along with the PDE operator, initial conditions, and boundary conditions. Each 1D coordinate axis is encoded independently by its own sub-network, and the final output is a multi-dimensional solution tensor computed via outer-product aggregation of per-axis feature vectors, reducing memory and compute compared to a monolithic MLP over all coordinates simultaneously.

## Method

CoPINN combines three components: (1) separable sub-networks where d independent MLPs each encode a single 1D coordinate into an r-dimensional feature vector, aggregated via outer product to form the multi-dimensional prediction; (2) a difficulty evaluation that measures per-sample difficulty as the L2 norm of the gradient of the PDE residual with respect to the sample coordinate, dynamically updated each epoch; and (3) a cognitive training scheduler inspired by self-paced learning that linearly shifts the weight of the easiest sample from 1 to 0 over N_e epochs while the hardest sample's weight goes from 0 to 1, with intermediate samples weighted by their normalized difficulty rank scaled by a hyperparameter β. This progressive easy-to-hard weighting is applied only to the PDE residual loss term, while initial and boundary condition losses remain unweighted.

## Results

CoPINN achieves the best RL2 and RMSE across all six PDE benchmarks and all collocation point counts (16³–256³). On the Helmholtz equation at Nc=256³, CoPINN reaches RL2=0.0006, a 98% improvement over the second-best SPINN(m) (0.0311). On the (2+1)-d Klein-Gordon equation at Nc=256³, CoPINN achieves RL2=0.0002 vs. SPINN(m)'s 0.0009 (78% improvement). Notably, CoPINN's separable architecture avoids out-of-memory failures that affect PINN, gPINN, AHD-PINN, RoPINN, and FPINN at Nc≥128³.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CoPINN outperforms all seven baselines on every PDE equation and every collocation point count where results are available. The strongest baseline, SPINN(m), is beaten by 60–98% in RL2 on the challenging Helmholtz and Klein-Gordon equations (e.g., 0.0006 vs. 0.0311 on Helmholtz at 256³), while the gap narrows to 6–21% on the easier Diffusion equation. SPINN (unmodified) is outperformed by even larger margins (up to 98% on Helmholtz). PINN, gPINN, AHD-PINN, RoPINN, and FPINN all suffer out-of-memory failures at Nc≥128³ or 256³ on most equations, whereas CoPINN's separable architecture completes training at all tested resolutions. On the 1D Convection equation (ξ=30), CoPINN applied to vanilla PINN achieves RL2=0.0055, beating Causal R3 (0.0073) and R3 (0.0160). The Flow Mixing 3D equation shows more modest gains (4–21% over SPINN(m)), consistent with it being a less extreme test case.

## Relation to Prior Work

CoPINN directly builds on SPINN (Cho et al., 2023) for its separable sub-network architecture and aggregation scheme, and on classical PINN (Raissi et al., 2019) for the physics-informed loss formulation. Its key departure is the introduction of self-paced learning (SPL) into PINN training—a curriculum-learning paradigm well-established in clustering, classification, and retrieval but previously unexplored in the PDE-solving context. The cognitive training scheduler, which dynamically re-weights samples based on PDE residual gradients, represents the paper's core methodological contribution, shifting PINN from uniform sample treatment to a progressive easy-to-hard optimization strategy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By identifying the Unbalanced Prediction Problem (UPP)—the systematic overfitting to smooth regions and underfitting at physical boundaries—this paper reframes a practical failure mode that prior PINN methods implicitly ignored. The cognitive training scheduler is a lightweight, plug-and-play modification to the PDE loss that could be adopted by existing PINN pipelines without architectural changes. Open questions remain: whether the PDE-residual-gradient difficulty metric is optimal for all PDE classes (e.g., those with moving singularities), whether the approach scales to 4D+ problems without the separable assumption, and whether the dynamic re-weighting can cause the training instability the authors themselves acknowledge in their limitations.
