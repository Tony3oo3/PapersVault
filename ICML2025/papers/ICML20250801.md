## Problem

While attention mechanisms drive LLM success in language tasks, it remains unclear whether they can effectively model inter-node relationships in graph-structured data, where topological connectivity is paramount. Prior work has applied LLMs to graphs without systematic analysis of how attention actually behaves over graph structures, leaving open the question of whether attention fundamentally fails for graphs in natural language settings.

## Input / Output

The method takes graph-structured data serialized into natural language following the LLaGA format, where a central node, 8 first-order neighbors, and 64 second-order neighbors are described as text tokens with explicit edge descriptions. Inputs are padded and randomly shuffled to fix sequence length and position, eliminating positional confounds. Outputs include node class labels for classification and, critically, per-layer, per-head attention score matrices (log-scaled) that are the primary analytical artifact of the study.

## Method

The paper conducts a multi-faceted empirical analysis of LLM attention on graph data using LLaMA-2-7B fine-tuned via LLaGA with 8×8 neighbor sampling. It (1) performs hypothesis testing (T-test, KS test, JS divergence) on attention distributions before/after training to show LLMs recognize node tokens but develop bimodal, polarized attention patterns; (2) runs four-level connectivity disruption experiments revealing that LLMs largely ignore shuffled edge information; (3) visualizes attention score matrices to identify 'Attention Sink' and a novel 'Skewed Line Sink' phenomenon specific to graph data; and (4) introduces the Global Linkage Horizon (GLH) parameter k to control node visibility, demonstrating that intermediate windows (k=2,3) incorporating partial topological links outperform both fully connected (k=4) and fixed-link (k=1) views during training, and that models trained at smaller k transfer effectively to fully connected inference at k=4.

## Results

On node classification across four datasets, intermediate attention windows (k=2, k=3) consistently outperform the fully connected view (k=4) of standard LLMs: e.g., on Roman-Empire, k=2 unidi achieves 82.87% vs. 80.73% for k=4 unidi, and on WikiCS, k=2 unidi reaches 78.90% vs. 77.49%. A key transferability finding shows that models trained with a smaller linkage horizon (k=2 or k=3) and then deployed with the full k=4 window improve performance over direct k=4 training (e.g., 82.66% vs. 80.73% on Roman-Empire). Disruption experiments reveal that LLMs show no significant performance degradation when graph connectivity is randomly shuffled at levels (I)–(II) on most datasets, indicating they fail to effectively exploit topological structure.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This empirical study does not compare against external GNN or LLM-for-graph baselines; instead, it contrasts different attention window configurations (k=1 to k=4, unidirectional vs. bidirectional) as the primary axis of comparison. The fully connected LLM view (k=4 unidi) is outperformed by intermediate windows (k=2, k=3) on Roman-Empire (+2.14 pts at k=2) and WikiCS (+1.41 pts at k=2), while the fixed-linkage GNN view (k=1) achieves the best single-configuration result on Roman-Empire (83.12%) and Amazon-Ratings (45.95%) but underperforms on PubMed (80.49% vs. 83.36% at k=3). The small-to-large transfer (train at k=2/3, infer at k=4) yields gains over direct k=4 training on Roman-Empire (82.66% vs. 80.73%) and WikiCS (77.82% vs. 77.49%), confirming that topologically constrained training improves generalization to the unconstrained inference setting. Disruption experiments further show that, unlike GNNs which degrade monotonically with connectivity perturbation, LLMs remain largely unaffected at disruption levels (I)–(II) on WikiCS and PubMed, evidencing a failure to encode structural information.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on LLaGA (Chen et al., 2024) as its experimental framework for serializing graph structures into natural language and fine-tuning LLMs for node classification. It draws on the NLP attention-analysis literature—particularly StreamLLM's identification of attention sinks and subsequent calibration work—to interpret attention behavior in a new domain. The key departure is that prior LLM-for-graphs work (InstructGLM, LLaGA, UniGraph, GraphAdapter) focused on performance improvements, while this paper is the first to systematically dissect *how* attention is allocated across graph tokens, introducing the Global Linkage Horizon (GLH) as a unifying parameter between LLM's fully connected view and GNN's fixed-link view.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that LLMs largely ignore connectivity information (performance is nearly unchanged under moderate disruption) provides a concrete diagnostic for why LLMs underperform GNNs on graph tasks, shifting the community's focus from prompt engineering to architectural attention constraints. The small-to-large transferability result (train at k=2/3, infer at k=4) offers a practical recipe that avoids deployment-time attention modification while improving accuracy. However, the study is limited to the LLaGA serialization paradigm, a single sampling scheme (8×8), and primarily LLaMA-2-7B; whether these attention-pathology findings generalize to other graph-serialization strategies, larger models, or non-decoder architectures remains open.
