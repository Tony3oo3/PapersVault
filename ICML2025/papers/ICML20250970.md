## Problem

Differential privacy provides a theoretical guarantee parameterized by (ε,δ), but it is unclear whether models calibrated to the same guarantee actually exhibit similar real-world privacy behavior. This paper investigates whether DP-SGD hyperparameter choices (batch size, iterations, learning rate) that are all valid under the same (ε,δ) budget lead to meaningfully different memorization of sensitive training data, and if so, how to account for this variance in practice.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250970/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The study fine-tunes pre-trained LLMs (GPT-2-small/large, Llama-2-7b/13b) via LoRA with DP-SGD or DP-Adam on two datasets (Enron Email with 33k samples, TOFU with 4k author profiles), sweeping over hyperparameter configurations (b, T, η) while fixing the (ε,δ) target via the PRV accountant. Empirical privacy is measured by three memorization scores—Adversarial Compression Ratio (via GCG prompt optimization), Verbatim Memorization Ratio (prefix-continuation matching), and Attribute Inference Ratio (attribute presence in 10 stochastic decodes)—computed over curated secret sets (13 Enron secrets, 52 TOFU author-genre pairs) that are verified absent from pre-training corpora and known to be memorizable under non-private training.

## Method

The paper demonstrates that empirical privacy variance is ubiquitous and grows with model size, dataset size, secret density, and ε, then uses multivariate regression in log-space to decompose hyperparameter effects into individual terms (b, T, η) and composite quantities (compute C=b·T, updates U=C·η), revealing that learning rate has the largest positive coefficient and that for fixed compute, larger batch size improves privacy while for fixed updates, smaller learning rate improves privacy. This yields a no-free-lunch result: standard utility-optimizing practices (larger b, higher η, more T) systematically degrade empirical privacy. The authors propose three pairwise comparison heuristics and a sequential selection procedure (Algorithm 1) that applies them hierarchically—updates heuristic, then compute heuristic, then individual hyperparameter dominance, then worst-utility tie-breaking—achieving significantly lower relative privacy risk than best-utility or worst-utility baselines. Finally, they test two causal hypotheses: privacy auditing (Panda et al., 2025) shows audited ε̂ varies across configurations but correlates only weakly (Spearman −0.13) with empirical privacy, while privacy profile analysis reveals that numerical accountants produce T-dependent profile crossings absent under closed-form moments accountants, suggesting the variance may stem from differences in the full (ε,δ) trade-off curve rather than a single scalar budget.

## Results

Regression analysis (Table 2) reveals statistically significant positive coefficients for all DP-SGD hyperparameters on empirical privacy scores: log η has the largest effect (0.51 on Enron/ACR, 0.068 on TOFU/AIR), log T is intermediate (0.37, 0.048), and log b is smallest (0.13, 0.029), all with p < 0.001. When using composite hyperparameters, the coefficient for log C (compute = b·T) drops to 0.22 (Enron) while log η remains dominant at 0.53, confirming that learning rate is the primary driver of empirical privacy degradation. The proposed sequential selection procedure (Algorithm 1) consistently outperforms both the best-utility baseline and the standalone worst-utility heuristic by a large margin in relative privacy risk across all ε values in both settings (Fig. 10), and the three pairwise heuristics significantly exceed the random-guess accuracy baseline (Fig. 7).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical study rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so the 'baselines' are existing hyperparameter selection practices. The paper's proposed sequential selection procedure (Algorithm 1, combining updates, compute, individual-hyperparameter, and worst-utility heuristics) is compared against two baselines: (1) the standard practice of selecting the best-utility configuration, and (2) a standalone worst-utility heuristic. In Fig. 10, the proposed procedure consistently achieves substantially lower relative privacy risk than both baselines across all ε values in both settings (GPT-2-S/Enron and Llama-2-7b/TOFU), with the gap widening at higher ε. For the pairwise heuristics (Fig. 7), all three (individual, compute, updates) significantly outperform the random-guess baseline (50% accuracy), with the compute and updates heuristics reaching the highest accuracy. The paper also notes that existing DP-SGD tuning recommendations (larger batch, higher learning rate, more iterations) improve utility but simultaneously degrade empirical privacy, establishing a no-free-lunch trade-off that the proposed heuristics explicitly address.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on DP-SGD (Abadi et al., 2016) as the core training mechanism and positions itself at the intersection of two research threads: (1) the growing body of work on differentially private LLM fine-tuning (Anil et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2022; Li et al., 2022) and (2) memorization-based privacy evaluation in language models (Carlini et al., 2021, 2023; Schwarzschild et al., 2024). It departs from Hayes et al. (2023) and Kaissis et al. (2024), who showed that mechanisms sharing the same (ε,δ) guarantee can differ in vulnerability to reconstruction attacks or Blackwell dominance, by shifting to the practical LLM fine-tuning setting and quantifying the gap through memorization metrics (ACR, VMR, AIR) rather than worst-case threat models. The paper also engages with privacy auditing (Panda et al., 2025; Nasr et al., 2023b) and privacy profile analysis (Dong et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2022) to explore potential causes of the observed variance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that ε alone is insufficient to characterize privacy risk in DP-SGD (a one-to-many rather than one-to-one relationship) has direct implications for standardization: a legislative body recommending a single ε* without accounting for hyperparameter-induced variance would leave unforeseen privacy risks. The no-free-lunch result—that utility-optimizing hyperparameter choices systematically degrade empirical privacy—challenges the conventional utility-ε trade-off framing and motivates a three-dimensional (utility, ε, empirical privacy) evaluation. Open questions include whether the variance stems from differences in 'real' ε (privacy amplification by iteration) or from differences in privacy profiles, and whether more powerful auditing methods beyond loss-based frameworks can resolve the near-zero correlation (Spearman −0.13) between audited ε̂ and empirical privacy scores.
