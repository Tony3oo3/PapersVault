## Problem

Hierarchical reinforcement learning (HRL) decomposes long-horizon sparse-reward tasks into subgoals, but efficiently discovering the causal structure among subgoals and exploiting it to guide exploration remains unsolved. Prior causal HRL work (Hu et al. 2022; Nguyen et al. 2024) applied off-the-shelf causal discovery without adaptation to the HRL setting, selected subgoals for exploration uniformly at random, and provided no theoretical guarantees on either the quality of the recovered structure or the training cost of the overall algorithm.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251173/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes binary-valued resource environment variables (e.g., 'has wood', 'has stone') as disentangled factors, a primitive action space, and predefined subgoal definitions mapping each resource variable to a target value. It produces a recovered subgoal causal graph (a directed graph over subgoals with AND/OR semantics), a hierarchical level assignment, and a multi-level policy where each level selects the next subgoal to pursue based on the discovered causal order. The subgoal structure is modeled as a directed graph that need not be acyclic, and the hierarchical structure is a DAG subgraph with a level function ensuring parents are always at lower levels than children.

## Method

The HRC framework formulates subgoal achievement as a causal intervention (do-operator) and iteratively: (1) selects a controllable subgoal via a causally-guided ranking rule, (2) intervenes on it to collect interventional data, (3) runs a novel Subgoal Structure Discovery (SSD) algorithm that recovers the causal graph via a sparse linear loss over an abstracted SCM with AND/OR mechanisms (Theorem 8.4 guarantees parent recovery), and (4) trains the newly reachable subgoals into the controllable set. Two ranking rules prioritize which controllable subgoal to intervene on next: a Causal Effect Ranking rule that maximizes the estimated expected causal effect (ECE) on the final goal, and a Shortest Path Ranking rule using A*-search over the recovered graph. The paper provides the first formal cost analysis for HRL subgoal discovery, showing the targeted strategy achieves O(log²(n)·b) cost on tree structures versus Ω(n²·b) for random selection, and O(n^{4/3+2c/3}·log n) versus Ω(n²) on semi-Erdős-Rényi graphs.

## Results

Theoretically, the targeted HRC strategy (HRC_h) achieves O(log²(n)·b) training cost on tree structures and O(n^(4/3+2c/3)·log(n)) on semi-Erdős-Rényi graphs, compared to Ω(n²·b) and Ω(n²) lower bounds for the random baseline (HRC_b), respectively. On the 2D-Minecraft benchmark, all HRC variants (with causal-effect or shortest-path ranking) reach the final subgoal with fewer system probes than CDHRL, HAC, HER, OHRL, and Vanilla PPO. The proposed causal discovery method (SSD) reduces the Structural Hamming Distance from 19.8 (SDI/Ke et al.) to 12.3, primarily by cutting extra edges from 15.6 to 6.3.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On 2D-Minecraft (Figure 6a), HRC with the causal-effect ranking rule and SSD causal discovery (HRC_h(SSD)) reaches the final subgoal with substantially fewer system probes than CDHRL, HAC, HER, OHRL, and Vanilla PPO, confirming that both targeted interventions and accurate causal structure recovery contribute to faster learning. The paper also isolates the causal discovery contribution: HRC_b(SSD) outperforms HRC_b(SDI) in success ratio vs. system probes (Figure 6b), and Table 2 shows SSD achieves SHD 12.3 vs. 19.8 for SDI (Ke et al., 2019), with far fewer spurious edges (6.3 vs. 15.6) at the cost of slightly more missing edges (6.0 vs. 4.2). The theoretical cost analysis (Table 1) demonstrates that the targeted strategy reduces worst-case training cost from quadratic to near-logarithmic (tree) or sub-quadratic (semi-Erdős-Rényi) in the number of subgoals, a gap that widens for sparser graphs (smaller c). No baseline matches HRC on the 2D-Minecraft task; the closest prior method still requires noticeably more system probes to reach comparable success ratios.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the causal HRL line initiated by Hu et al. (2022) and Nguyen et al. (2024), which used off-the-shelf causal discovery (Ke et al., 2019) to infer subgoal structure but then selected subgoals uniformly at random from the discovered subset. The key departure is twofold: (1) a new causal discovery algorithm (SSD) tailored to the AND/OR subgoal structure with identifiability guarantees, and (2) causally-guided ranking rules (expected causal effect and A*-based shortest path) that actively prioritize which controllable subgoal to intervene on next, rather than sampling randomly. Unlike prior causal HRL work, the paper also provides a formal cost formulation and theoretical bounds, filling a gap the authors explicitly identify in the literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper is the first to provide theoretical cost guarantees for causal HRL, showing that targeted interventions reduce training cost from Ω(n²b) to O(log²(n)b) on trees and from Ω(n²) to O(n^(4/3+2c/3) log n) on semi-Erdős-Rényi graphs. This is practically significant for long-horizon sparse-reward tasks (e.g., Minecraft-style environments) where sample efficiency is the bottleneck. However, the analysis relies on strong assumptions (binary resource EVs, subgoals remain achieved once attained, AND/OR causal mechanisms), and empirical validation is limited to a single 2D-Minecraft environment. Open questions include whether the framework extends to non-binary or continuous subgoals, whether the AND/OR assumption holds in realistic domains, and how the approach scales when the number of environment variables grows large.
