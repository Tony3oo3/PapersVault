## Problem

Pretrained GNNs for molecular property prediction face a persistent gap between self-supervised pre-training objectives and downstream task requirements, and existing prompt-tuning approaches (universal prompts, manually designed prompts) lack the flexibility, interpretability, and scalability needed to bridge this gap effectively. Additionally, prompts can inadvertently introduce task-irrelevant noise that degrades model performance by overemphasizing irrelevant structural details or misaligning node-level pretrained features with subgraph-level task requirements.

## Input / Output

The method consumes molecular graphs (node feature matrices and adjacency matrices) through a frozen pretrained GIN encoder with 3 graph convolutional layers and 300-dimensional hidden representations, augmented by lightweight LoRA adapters (rank r ≪ d) injected into the node MLP layers. The output is a binary classification label for molecular properties (e.g., BBBP penetration, Tox21 toxicity, HIV inhibition) across 8 MoleculeNet benchmark tasks. The pretrained encoder parameters remain frozen throughout; only the LoRA matrices, graph-informed prompt parameters, and a task-specific projection head are updated, keeping total trainable parameters at 9.1–13.78% of the full model.

## Method

MIPT combines three mechanisms: (1) LoRA-based parameter-efficient tuning that injects low-rank adaptation matrices (ΔW = BA) into the GNN's node MLP layers to capture task-specific embeddings without altering graph topology; (2) a Multilevel Graph-Informed Prompt (MGIP) that applies random node masking to produce masked node embeddings, uses contrastive learning to maximize mutual information between masked and unmasked graph-level representations, and generates a low-rank graph prompt from the masked features via a two-layer linear function; (3) a Noise Prompt Penalty (NPP) loss that models the classification loss distribution with a two-component Gaussian Mixture Model, computes per-sample confidence via posterior probabilities updated with the EM algorithm, and selectively weights the loss to suppress noisy or low-confidence samples. The total training objective is L_total = L_NPP + L_cont, optimized jointly with gradient descent on model parameters and alternating EM updates on GMM parameters.

## Results

MIPT achieves the highest ROC-AUC across all 8 MoleculeNet datasets under every pre-training strategy. Under ContextPred pre-training, MIPT reaches 73.62% on BBBP (+3.97 over FT), 81.21% on ClinTox (+7.50 over FT), and 86.57% on BACE (+1.91 over FT). Against the strongest prompt baseline GPF-plus, MIPT gains up to 5.96 points (ClinTox, AttrMasking: 85.18 vs 76.17) and 2.76 points (BBBP, ContextPred: 73.62 vs 69.15). In the SOTA comparison (Table 2), MIPT outperforms all 12 baselines on 5 of 8 tasks (BBBP, BACE, Tox21, SIDER, MUV) while using only 9.1–13.78% of the trainable parameters of full fine-tuning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the prompt-tuning baselines GPF and GPF-plus (Fang et al., 2024), MIPT wins on 7 of 8 datasets under every pre-training strategy, with the largest margin on ClinTox (up to +9.03 over GPF-plus under AttrMasking) and BBBP (+4.47 over GPF-plus under ContextPred); the sole exception is BACE under AttrMasking where GPF-plus edges ahead (85.76 vs 83.92). In the broader SOTA comparison (Table 2), MIPT surpasses supervised GNNs (D-MPNN, Attentive FP, MGCN) and pre-training-only methods (N-Gram, PretrainGNN, GROVER, 3D-Infomax, GraphMVP, MolCLR) on all 8 datasets. Against the strongest recent baselines Uni-Mol and InstructMol, MIPT wins on BBBP (73.6 vs 72.9/73.3), BACE (86.6 vs 85.7/85.9), Tox21 (80.6 vs 79.6/79.9), SIDER (68.5 vs 65.9/67.4), and MUV (85.0 vs 80.8/–), but trails on ClinTox (85.2 vs 91.9/92.5), ToxCast (68.8 vs 69.6/70.8), and HIV (81.8 vs 82.1/–). Overall, MIPT achieves the best or near-best ROC-AUC on 5 of 8 tasks while tuning only 9.1–13.78% of parameters versus 100% for full fine-tuning.

## Relation to Prior Work

MIPT sits at the intersection of three research threads: parameter-efficient fine-tuning (LoRA, originally for NLP by Houlsby et al.), graph prompt learning (GPF/GPF-plus, GPPT, Graph Prompt), and noise-robust training (Arpit et al.'s memorization analysis, Shi et al.'s GMM-based confidence weighting from person re-identification). The paper departs from prior graph prompt work by combining node-level LoRA adaptation with a graph-level prompt generated via random node masking and contrastive learning, rather than relying on universal or manually designed prompts. The GMM-based Noise Prompt Penalty is a direct repurposing of a technique from visible-infrared re-identification, transplanted into the molecular graph domain to filter low-confidence samples during fine-tuning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MIPT offers a practical, parameter-efficient (9–14% of full fine-tuning) recipe for adapting pre-trained molecular GNNs, which is relevant given the scarcity of labeled molecular data. However, the GMM-based noise penalty requires at least two samples per class, limiting applicability in few-shot or extremely imbalanced settings (e.g., MUV with 0.31% positive labels). The paper acknowledges higher variance across runs with different LoRA ranks and leaves open the question of how to better integrate node-level and graph-level features. Broader impact is moderate: the framework is model-agnostic (GIN backbone but applicable to other GNNs), yet the gains, while consistent across 8 datasets and 4 pre-training strategies, are incremental rather than transformative.
