## Problem

While empirical evidence suggests that placing helpful text in the context during gradient-based training (without computing loss on that text) can improve LLM learning, this phenomenon—context-enhanced learning—lacked a formal definition, theoretical characterization, or mechanistic explanation. The paper asks: can context-enhanced learning be provably more sample-efficient than standard autoregressive fine-tuning, what capability is required to benefit from it, and can the in-context material be recovered from the trained model?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20253220/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an even-length input string s₁ over alphabet A₁ (length 20–40 in experiments) and produces a translated string s_{d+1} over alphabet A_{d+1}, preceded by a fixed number of <THINK> tokens that provide silent chain-of-thought compute. During training, the model also receives curriculum-text (excerpts from d phrasebooks describing the translation rules) in context, but no autoregressive loss is computed on those tokens. At test time, no phrasebook context is provided, so the model must rely entirely on internalized parameters.

## Method

The paper formalizes context-enhanced learning as standard SFT augmented with task-dependent curriculum-text in context on which no autoregressive gradient is computed, and introduces the Multi-Level Translation (MLT) task—a composition of d sequential bigram-translation steps—as a controlled testbed. Training proceeds in two phases: (1) CoT internalization on random translation tasks to produce an MLT-ICL-capable model, and (2) context-enhanced learning on a target task with an annealing dropout curriculum that progressively removes phrasebook rules from context, forcing the model to internalize them. A surrogate model (SURR-MLT) with per-layer trainable matrices W_i and in-context matrices C_i enables a theoretical analysis showing an exponential sample-complexity gap: vanilla SFT requires n^Ω(d) samples (via SQ-dimension lower bound), while context-enhanced learning achieves O(n⁶ d log d). The mechanism is identified as improved gradient prediction accuracy: when a rule is dropped from context, the gradient on the corresponding trainable parameter strongly aligns with the missing rule, but this signal degrades as more rules are simultaneously dropped.

## Results

On the MLT(5,8) and MLT(5,10) translation tasks, context-enhanced learning with Annealing Dropout achieves near-perfect held-out accuracy (with 100% phrasebook dropout at test time) using roughly 10× fewer samples than vanilla SFT (No Context). Fixed Dropout (20%) also reaches perfect test-time accuracy, demonstrating that the model internalizes phrasebook rules atomically and re-composes them at inference. Theoretically, the surrogate model (SURR-MLT) shows vanilla SFT requires n^{Ω(d)} samples (exponential in depth d), while context-enhanced learning achieves O(n⁶ d log d) sample complexity (polynomial), with gradient descent for d=2 achieving O(n⁴).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between context-enhanced learning (Annealing Dropout, Fixed Dropout) and vanilla SFT (No Context) on the MLT task. Context-enhanced learning with Annealing Dropout achieves near-perfect accuracy at ~100k samples, while vanilla SFT remains near random even at 1M samples — a roughly 10× sample efficiency gap. Ablations confirm that (i) ICL capability is necessary: starting from a non-ICL-capable 3B base model with Annealing Dropout fails to learn; (ii) correct context is necessary: Wrong Context (incorrect phrasebook rules) performs at chance level; (iii) dropout is necessary for internalization: No Dropout (always providing all rules) produces a model that is brittle and fails when phrasebooks are removed at test time. The privacy analysis (Table 2) shows that even with strong token filtering, phrasebook rule recovery rates remain near-random (<3%), indicating the in-context training material is not verbatim memorized.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper formalizes 'context-enhanced learning' as a gradient-based analog of in-context learning, explicitly adapting the Learning Using Privileged Information (LUPI) paradigm from Vapnik & Vashist (2009) to autoregressive LLMs. It builds on recent empirical observations that placing helpful text in context (without computing loss on it) improves fine-tuning (Liao et al., 2024; Zou et al., 2024; Choi et al., 2025) and metadata conditioning for pre-training (Allen-Zhu & Li, 2024; Gao et al., 2025), but provides the first formal definition, theoretical sample-complexity analysis, and mechanistic account of the phenomenon. The theoretical analysis draws on the Statistical Query framework (Kearns, 1998) and CoT internalization pipelines (Deng et al., 2024; Pfau et al., 2024) to construct ICL-capable initializations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The exponential sample-complexity gap (n^Ω(d) for vanilla SFT vs. O(poly(n)·d·log d) with context-enhanced learning) provides a clean theoretical justification for why in-context privileged information can dramatically accelerate learning, and the mechanistic finding that the benefit arises from improved gradient signal (not just more information) offers actionable insight for curriculum design. The observation that phrasebook rules are nearly unrecoverable from model outputs even under adversarial token filtering raises interesting (if hypothetical) privacy and copyright questions about using off-limits data in context. Key limitations include the entirely synthetic MLT task, reliance on a surrogate model for theory, and evaluation on a single 3B architecture; extending to real-world tasks (math, code) and actual transformer convergence analysis remain open.
