## Problem

While Mixture of Experts (MoE) architectures have achieved strong empirical results, the theoretical understanding of why they succeed—particularly in nonlinear regression—has lagged behind. Prior MoE theory was limited to classification or linear regression settings, leaving open the question of how MoE overcomes gradient interference in more general nonlinear tasks. This paper addresses the gap by proving that a vanilla neural network provably fails to detect latent cluster structure (due to the information exponent of the shared signal increasing under gradient cancellation), while MoE succeeds by partitioning the problem into easier per-cluster subproblems.

## Input / Output

The input is a high-dimensional Gaussian data point x_c ∈ R^d drawn from cluster c (c ~ Unif[1,...,C]), generated as x_c ~ N(ρv_c, I_d) where the cluster mean ρv_c has norm poly(log d) and the isotropic noise has norm ~d^{1/2}. The target is a regression label y_c = f_c*(w_c*^T x_c) + s_c g*(w_g*^T x_c) + ν, combining a cluster-specific single-index model f_c* and a global single-index model g* with interference coefficients s_c satisfying Σ_c s_c = 0. The output is a scalar regression prediction ŷ, and the key structural constraint is that all link functions share the same information exponent k* > 2 with matching k*-th Hermite coefficient magnitudes, making individual components indistinguishable in difficulty.

## Method

The paper proposes a four-phase training algorithm for an MoE with M two-layer neural network experts and a linear gating router. Phase I performs spherical gradient descent on expert first-layer weights to achieve weak recovery of cluster-specific feature vectors (requiring Õ(d^{k*-1}) steps). Phase II trains the router in a single SGD step with batch size Õ(d), exploiting differences in weak-recovery alignment to learn cluster-mean vectors v_c for routing. Phase III reinitializes expert weights and performs spherical gradient descent under adaptive top-k routing (threshold at 0), enabling each expert to strongly recover both its local task w_c* and the global task w_g* without cross-cluster interference. Phase IV solves a convex L2-regularized problem to estimate second-layer link functions. The key mechanism is that the router's partitioning decomposes the interfering global signal s_c g*(w_g*^T x_c) (where Σ_c s_c = 0 causes gradient cancellation in a vanilla network) into per-cluster subproblems with Ω(1) signal strength, reducing the effective information exponent each expert must overcome.

## Results

The paper's main theoretical result (Theorem 4.6) shows that the MoE with M experts, J=O(ε⁻¹) neurons per expert, and adaptive top-k routing achieves prediction error ≤ ε with total sample complexity Õ(d^{k*−1}) and polynomial time in d, where k* is the information exponent of each cluster's local task. In contrast, Theorem 4.3 proves that a vanilla two-layer neural network of polynomial width J=O(poly d) fundamentally fails: the alignment with the hidden global signal w_g* remains bounded by Õ(d^{−1/2}) for all time, meaning the network cannot recover the shared component s_c·g*(w_g*⊤x_c). The MoE's sample complexity matches that of learning a single-index model (Arous et al., 2021), while kernel ridge regression would require Õ(d^{p*}) samples, where p* is the polynomial degree.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between a vanilla two-layer neural network (equivalent to a single-expert MoE) and the proposed multi-expert MoE with adaptive top-k routing. The vanilla network provably fails: due to the gradient interference condition Σ_c s_c = 0, the Hermite coefficients of the global signal cancel across clusters, effectively increasing the information exponent of g* from k* to k*+1, making w_g* unlearnable within polynomial time (Theorem 4.3). The MoE succeeds by first achieving weak recovery of cluster-specific signals w_c* in Phase I (requiring Õ(d^{k*−1}) steps), then using the router to partition data by cluster in Phase II, and finally enabling each expert to learn both its local task and the global task without cross-cluster interference in Phase III. The empirical validation (Figures 1–2, C=2 clusters, d=200, 8 experts, 500 hidden units) confirms the theoretical predictions: after Phase I, experts show differentiated weak recovery; after Phase II, the router correctly assigns clusters; and after Phase III, experts recover both w_c* and w_g*. No direct numerical benchmark against other MoE architectures is provided, as the contribution is theoretical rather than empirical.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the MoE optimization theory from classification (Chen et al., 2022; Chowdhury et al., 2023) and linear regression (Li et al., 2024) to the broader and more challenging setting of nonlinear regression, where prior theoretical understanding was lacking. It introduces the information exponent framework from single-index model learning (Arous et al., 2021; Oko et al., 2024a) into the MoE context for the first time, using it to explain why gradient interference from ∑s_c = 0 causes vanilla networks to fail while MoE succeeds. The four-phase training algorithm and adaptive top-k routing design build on the layer-by-layer analysis of Oko et al. (2024a) and the expert specialization mechanism of Chen et al. (2022), but the key departure is showing that weak recovery of cluster-specific features is a prerequisite for meaningful router learning in the nonlinear regression regime.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is among the first works to provide a theoretical explanation for why MoE architectures succeed in nonlinear regression, a setting where prior MoE theory was confined to classification or linear problems. The provable failure of vanilla networks on cluster-structured tasks with gradient interference (∑s_c = 0) gives a concrete, information-theoretic justification for MoE's architectural advantage. However, the practical impact is tempered by the idealized four-phase training procedure (reinitialization between phases, different routing strategies per phase) and the single synthetic experiment with C=2, d=200. Open questions include whether MoE can approach the information-theoretic sample complexity limit, whether the results extend to deeper architectures or non-Gaussian data, and how to translate the phase-specific routing insights into practical training recipes for large-scale MoE LLMs.
