## Problem

Outsourcing LLM inference to cloud providers risks leaking sensitive client queries, and FHE offers a natural privacy-preserving solution, but prior FHE-based LLM implementations are CPU-only and far too slow for practical use. The paper asks how practical FHE-based privacy-preserving LLM evaluation can be, and addresses the gap by providing the first open-sourced GPU-accelerated CKKS implementation and benchmarking an encrypted GPT-2 forward pass.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250487/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The client locally tokenizes their text query into real-valued embedding vectors (768-dim for GPT-2 small) and encrypts them under the CKKS public key before sending to the server. The server evaluates the full transformer forward pass entirely on ciphertexts and returns an encrypted next-token distribution, which the client decrypts with the secret key. The encrypted output can be fed back into the model for autoregressive generation without any further client-server interaction, and the benchmark measures a single forward pass at token position 128 with 2^16 slots per ciphertext.

## Method

The core contribution is a GPU-accelerated CKKS implementation extending OpenFHE, incorporating GPU kernels for NTT and RNS basis-change operations from Jung et al. (2021) to achieve ~200× speedup over CPU bootstrapping. Since CKKS only supports additions and multiplications, all non-linear activation functions are replaced with low-degree polynomial approximations: GeLU uses a piecewise polynomial (degrees 3 and 6) with a Cheon et al. sign-function comparison, LayerNorm uses Newton's iterative method for inverse square root (16–22 iterations), and SoftMax combines a Taylor-series exp approximation (r=7), Goldschmidt division (14–22 iterations), and an empirically computed max-lookup table to avoid an expensive max circuit. A key insight is that LLMs tolerate low-precision approximations (analogous to quantization), so these polynomial substitutions incur minimal accuracy degradation across eight standard benchmarks. Batched evaluation fills unused CKKS ciphertext slots with independent computations, further improving throughput for SoftMax and LayerNorm layers.

## Results

The GPU-accelerated FHE implementation achieves a ~200× speedup over the CPU (OpenFHE) baseline for a GPT-2 small forward pass at token position 128, reducing runtime from several hours to a few minutes. Bootstrapping alone is accelerated by 180–220× (22–27 ms per output level on GPU vs. 4–6 s on CPU). Accuracy of the FHE-compatible approximate model degrades by less than ~1–2 percentage points on most of the eight standard benchmarks (HellaSwag, ARC-Easy, PIQA, Social IQA, MNLI, SST-2, ANLI, WiC) relative to the unmodified GPT-2 baseline, with some tasks (e.g., WiC on GPT-2 small) actually improving slightly.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole quantitative baseline is the out-of-the-box OpenFHE CPU implementation. The GPU-accelerated version achieves a ~200× end-to-end speedup on the GPT-2 small forward pass (Figure 2) and a 180–220× speedup on the bootstrapping subroutine alone (Figure 4: 22–27 ms vs. 4–6 s per output level at n=2^16). On the accuracy side, the FHE-compatible approximate model is compared against the unmodified HuggingFace GPT-2 baseline: for GPT-2 small, the largest drop is 0.8 pp on HellaSwag (0.289→0.281) while WiC actually improves (0.492→0.511); for GPT-2 medium the largest drop is 3.4 pp on SST-2 (0.613→0.579); for GPT-2 large the largest drop is 4.2 pp on HellaSwag (0.363→0.321). Overall, the approximations preserve within ~1–4 pp of baseline accuracy across all three model sizes, confirming that the polynomial substitutions (GeLU, LayerNorm, SoftMax, comparison) are viable for FHE deployment without retraining.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the OpenFHE CPU-based FHE library with a GPU-accelerated CKKS implementation, building directly on the GPU NTT and RNS basis-change kernels from Jung et al. (2021) and the accuracy-preserving scaling-factor tracking from de Castro et al. (2021) and Kim et al. (2022). For the LLM side, it follows the polynomial-approximation approach established by Zhang et al. (2024) for FHE-based transformer inference, borrowing their GeLU approximation from Dong et al. (2023) and incorporating the SoftMax max-lookup optimization from Cho et al. (2024). The key departure from prior work is the end-to-end GPU acceleration of the full CKKS pipeline (including bootstrapping) within an open-source framework, which no prior implementation provided, and the first GPU-accelerated benchmark of a GPT-2 forward pass under FHE.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 200× speedup reduces the GPT-2 forward pass from hours to minutes, crossing a practicality threshold for non-real-time applications like document summarization and fine-tuning on private data. The open-sourced GPU-accelerated CKKS library is independently valuable for the broader FHE community, as bootstrapping dominates runtime in most FHE applications. Key limitations include the benchmark being restricted to GPT-2 small at a single token position (128), the requirement for low-precision (~16-bit) models, and the absence of real-time performance for interactive use cases. Open questions include whether the approach scales to larger models (GPT-3, Llama) without prohibitive runtime increases, and whether models requiring higher precision (e.g., vision) can benefit from similar GPU-accelerated FHE pipelines.
