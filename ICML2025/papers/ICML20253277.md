## Problem

Spectral GNNs are built on the assumption that graph Laplacian eigenvectors form a Fourier basis with the same semantic properties (global oscillatory patterns at distinct frequencies) as classical Fourier analysis, yet this assumption has never been rigorously validated for general graphs. The paper argues that two entangled issues have been overlooked: high-frequency eigenvectors exhibit localization rather than global oscillation, and the practical limitations preventing expressive filters are actually fundamental barriers that protect stability and generalization—meaning the community has been using a dictionary that does not behave as a true Fourier basis.

## Input / Output

As a position paper analyzing existing spectral GNNs, the 'input' is the graph structure (normalized Laplacian and its eigendecomposition) plus a node feature matrix, and the 'output' is filtered node representations and class predictions. Empirical validation of eigenvector localization uses the Cora dataset with the metric loc(u_i) = Σ u_{i,j}^4, while the theoretical stability and generalization analysis is developed for both inductive graph classification (using a modified tree mover's distance between graphs) and transductive node classification (using random train/test partitions over a fixed graph).

## Method

The paper provides a two-part critical analysis rather than a new architecture. Part 1 traces the mathematical migration from continuous to graph domain (gradient → divergence → Laplacian → eigen equation → Fourier basis), identifies fundamental discrepancies at each step, and presents experimental evidence of eigenvector localization on horse/sphere meshes and Cora; it then identifies the cognitive origins of the unreliable assumption as overgeneralization from well-understood low-frequency eigenvectors (Cheeger's inequality, nodal domain theorem) and from special highly-symmetric graphs (rings, paths, hypercubes) where all eigenvectors do exhibit DFT/DCT-like patterns. Part 2 shows that an 'ideal' spectral filter interpolating all n eigenvalue responses would necessarily have a large maximum value, large Lipschitz constant, and high polynomial order, then proves via Theorems 4.2 and 4.4 that these properties directly bound the filter's sensitivity to feature and structure perturbations, and via Theorems 4.8–4.9 (using a modified tree mover's distance) that they inflate the Lipschitz constant governing both inductive and transductive generalization gaps. The key entanglement insight is that the natural avoidance of complex filters has shielded the community from confronting the localization problem, so spectral GNNs are effective precisely because they do not fully exploit the graph Fourier basis as intended.

## Results

This is a position paper without a proposed method or standard benchmark comparisons. The key empirical findings are: (1) On the Cora dataset, high-frequency eigenvectors of the unnormalized Laplacian exhibit strong localization (measured by loc(u_i) = Σ u_{i,j}^4), which is partially alleviated by using the normalized Laplacian; (2) Visualization on horse and sphere meshes shows that high-frequency eigenvectors on irregular graphs (horse) concentrate on small vertex subsets, while on highly symmetric graphs (sphere) they retain more classical Fourier-like oscillatory patterns; (3) An 'ideal' spectral filter on Cora (interpolating all eigenvalue-response pairs) exhibits large maximum values and sharp transitions, confirming that expressive filters are inherently unstable.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not propose a new method or compare against competing baselines in the traditional sense. Instead, it contrasts two settings to support its argument: (1) normalized vs. unnormalized Laplacian eigenvectors on Cora, showing that normalization partially mitigates but does not eliminate high-frequency eigenvector localization; (2) irregular (horse mesh) vs. highly symmetric (sphere mesh) graph structures, demonstrating that the 'Fourier-like' global oscillatory behavior of high-frequency eigenvectors is a property of special symmetric graphs rather than a general property of graph Laplacian eigenvectors. The paper also contrasts the theoretical ideal of independent per-frequency modulation (classical Fourier analysis) with the practical constraint that polynomial filters must be low-order and low-Lipschitz to maintain stability, showing these two goals are fundamentally in tension.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper critically re-examines the theoretical foundations of the entire spectral GNN lineage (Bruna et al. 2013, Defferrard et al. 2016, Kipf & Welling 2017, He et al. 2021, Wang & Zhang 2022), arguing that the graph Fourier basis (Laplacian eigenvectors) does not inherit the semantic properties of classical Fourier basis that the field has assumed. It draws on concerns long raised in graph signal processing (Shuman et al. 2013b on eigenvector localization), compressive sensing (Zhu & Rabbat 2012 on compressibility), and manifold learning (Belkin & Niyogi 2008 on graph-manifold discrepancies) that the spectral GNN community has largely overlooked. The stability and generalization analysis builds on Gama et al. (2020), Kenlay et al. (2020), and Chuang & Jegelka (2022) to show that the 'mild' polynomial filters used in practice are not merely computational conveniences but fundamental requirements that prevent the frequency-specific modulation the Fourier narrative promises.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

If the graph Fourier basis lacks the global oscillatory semantics assumed by the field, then the entire theoretical motivation for spectral GNNs—performing independent frequency-component modulation—needs fundamental rethinking, potentially redirecting research toward alternative graph dictionaries or re-examining what polynomial techniques actually accomplish. The entanglement argument (that stability constraints naturally prevent us from confronting the Fourier basis question) is a novel meta-observation that explains why this misconception has persisted. Key open questions include: what semantically meaningful graph dictionary should replace U, and what is the true mechanism by which low-order polynomial filters boost graph learning performance? The paper's empirical evidence is limited to eigenvector visualizations on meshes and localization measurements on Cora, so the conceptual claims remain to be validated more broadly.
