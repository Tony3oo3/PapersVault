## Problem

Foundation models are premised on the idea that sequence prediction can uncover deeper domain understanding (a 'world model'), but there is no systematic procedure to test whether a model's inductive bias actually aligns with a postulated world model. Standard probes examine internal representations, which may not reflect how the model behaves when adapted to new tasks, and cannot distinguish among multiple state mappings that are all predictable from the representation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252408/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The probe takes a pretrained sequence model, a postulated world model (state space Φ, mapping φ, and a collection of admissible functions G), and repeatedly fine-tunes the model on 100 small synthetic datasets (100 examples each) drawn from a sampling distribution over consistent datasets. It produces inductive bias scores: R-IB and D-IB for the binary/finite-state special case, or a calibration curve of extrapolative predictability versus an oracle for the general case. In the physics domain, it additionally yields recovered physical laws via symbolic regression (PySR) on force-magnitude predictions.

## Method

The inductive bias probe is built on the no-free-lunch insight that a learning algorithm's extrapolations from limited data reveal its inductive bias, which should align with the world model's state structure if the model has truly learned it. For the finite-state/binary-output case, two complementary metrics are defined: R-IB measures whether inputs in the same state receive consistent predictions across synthetic datasets, and D-IB measures whether inputs in different states receive distinct predictions. For the general case, the probe defines extrapolative predictability between input pairs (how well one input's predicted output predicts another's across many synthetic datasets) and compares it to an oracle that extrapolates directly from the true state, producing a calibration curve where the 45-degree line indicates perfect alignment. The paper further decomposes D-IB into D-IB_{q=} and D-IB_{q≠} to test whether models group distinct states that share the same set of legal next-tokens, revealing that models develop task-specific heuristics rather than coherent world models.

## Results

On orbital trajectory prediction, the 109M-parameter transformer achieves R² > 0.9999, far outperforming baselines (MSE of 1.90×10⁻⁸ at 1 step ahead vs. 1.16×10⁻⁴ for previous-position baseline). However, the inductive bias probe reveals the model has poor inductive bias toward Newtonian mechanics: its extrapolative predictability deviates substantially from the oracle calibration line (Figure 2), and symbolic regression on fine-tuned force predictions recovers nonsensical, task-specific force laws rather than F ∝ m₁m₂/r². On the lattice (5 states) and Othello benchmarks (Table 2), all NTP-trained models show moderate inductive bias (R-IB 0.48–0.78, D-IB 0.61–0.92 on lattice; R-IB 0.56–0.70, D-IB 0.61–0.73 on Othello), with performance degrading as state space complexity increases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares five sequence model architectures (RNN, LSTM, Transformer, Mamba, Mamba-2) as foundation models under the inductive bias probe. On the lattice problem (5 states), LSTM achieves the strongest inductive bias (R-IB 0.782, D-IB 0.921), followed by Mamba-2 (0.617, 0.864) and Mamba (0.571, 0.866), while the Transformer consistently performs worst (R-IB 0.483, D-IB 0.677) — a notable finding since recurrent and state-space architectures outperform attention-based models on state-tracking inductive bias. On Othello, the gap narrows: Mamba leads on D-IB (0.728) and the Transformer leads on R-IB (0.703), but all models show substantially weaker inductive bias than on the simpler lattice. All models achieve ~90–100% legal next-token prediction accuracy (Table 7), yet their inductive bias toward the true board state remains poor, revealing that high next-token accuracy does not imply world model recovery. The paper further shows that models' inductive bias is toward the set of legal next-tokens rather than the full state: D-IB_{q=} < D-IB_{q≠} across all architectures (Table 9), confirming that models group distinct states sharing the same legal moves.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Vafa et al. (2024), which evaluated whether a fixed model's outputs accord with a known world model, by shifting the question to whether a *learning algorithm* (foundation model) has a world model embodied in its inductive bias. It departs from standard probe tests (Nanda et al., 2023b) that examine intermediate representations, arguing instead that inductive bias—revealed by how a model extrapolates from small synthetic datasets—is the right lens for comparing foundation models to world models. The theoretical grounding draws on the no-free-lunch theorem, and the interpretability tools (symbolic regression via PySR) follow the AI-physics literature (Liu & Tegmark, 2021; Wu & Tegmark, 2019).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete, operational framework (the inductive bias probe) for a question the field has struggled to formalize: did a foundation model actually learn the underlying mechanism, or just surface patterns? The finding that models with R² > 0.9999 on orbital prediction recover different nonsensical 'laws of gravity' per data slice, and that Othello models group by legal next-tokens rather than true board state, challenges the core premise that sequence prediction yields world models. Open questions include whether larger scale or different architectures (e.g., explicitly physics-informed) would change these results, and what the actual inductive biases are when they aren't toward the true world model. The practical implication—that inductive bias probe scores predict downstream transfer performance—gives practitioners a diagnostic tool beyond standard accuracy metrics.
