## Problem

In-context learning (ICL) backdoor attacks can manipulate LLM outputs by poisoning a few demonstrations, yet existing research lacks a mechanistic explanation of how these attacks influence model predictions and offers no effective defense. Traditional backdoor defenses (training-time or post-training) are inapplicable because ICL backdoor attacks require no modification of training data or model parameters, leaving a critical gap in protecting ICL-based inference pipelines.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252054/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a poisoned ICL demonstration (containing m trigger-embedded examples alongside n clean examples), a user input (with or without the backdoor trigger), and a clean dataset D from which defensive examples are drawn. It produces a defensive demonstration S_d of k clean examples (split equally between similarity-selected and confidence-selected) that is concatenated with the poisoned demonstration before inference. For closed-source models where output probabilities and embeddings are inaccessible, the defensive demonstrations selected on open-source models are directly transferred, making the approach applicable to black-box APIs like GPT-3.5 and GPT-4o.

## Method

The paper first proposes a dual-learning hypothesis: LLMs simultaneously encode a task latent concept θ₁ and an attack latent concept θ₂ from poisoned demonstrations, and the output probability is a weighted sum over both. Through Bayesian analysis and Jensen's inequality, the authors derive an upper bound on attack success probability that is governed solely by the concept preference ratio P_M(θ₁|S_t)/P_M(θ₂|S_t), which decomposes into task prior weight, a poisoned impact factor (raised to the power m), and a clean impact factor (raised to the power n). ICLShield exploits this by increasing the clean impact factor: it selects k/2 clean examples via cosine similarity to the poisoned demonstration embeddings (proxying trigger similarity without knowing the trigger) and k/2 via confidence scoring (high P_M(y_i|x_i, S_t)), then appends them to the poisoned demonstration. This shifts the posterior mass toward the task concept, lowering the attack success upper bound without requiring access to model internals or trigger knowledge.

## Results

ICLShield reduces the Attack Success Rate (ASR) by an average of 29.14% compared to no defense, outperforming ONION (3.47% reduction) and Back-Translation (3.06% reduction) by approximately 10×. Across 11 open-source LLMs on classification, generative, and reasoning tasks, ICLShield achieves a +26.02% average improvement over the best baseline. On the SST-2 misclassification task, ICLShield drops ASR from 92.19% (GPT-NEO-1.3B, no defense) to 35.97%, while on GSM8K reasoning (LLaMA-2-7B) it reduces ASR from 86.28% to 14.86%. The method also transfers to closed-source models, reducing ASR by 46.15% on SST-2 and 84.85% on AG's News for GPT-3.5/GPT-4o.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ICLShield decisively outperforms both baselines across all models, tasks, and attack types. ONION (perplexity-based token removal) fails because ICL backdoor triggers are often phrases rather than single words, allowing them to bypass word-level detection; in several cases ONION actually *increases* ASR (e.g., 92.19%→98.13% on SST-2/GPT-NEO-1.3B). Back-Translation (cross-lingual paraphrasing) also underperforms because ICL triggers generalize to semantically similar content, so translation does not neutralize them; it reduces ASR by only 3.06% on average. ICLShield, by contrast, does not attempt to detect or remove the trigger but instead shifts the concept preference ratio by injecting clean demonstrations, achieving ASR reductions of 29.14% on average—nearly 10× the baselines. On the BadChain reasoning attack (Table 2), the gap is even more dramatic: ICLShield drops GSM8K ASR from 86.28% to 14.86% on LLaMA-2-7B, while ONION and Back-Translation barely move the needle (84.69% and 86.13% respectively).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the latent concept framework for ICL established by Xie et al. (2021) and Wang et al. (2024a), which interpret in-context learning through Bayesian inference over continuous latent variables. The key departure is the dual-learning hypothesis, which discretizes the latent space into a task concept and an attack concept when demonstrations are poisoned, and derives a closed-form upper bound on attack success governed by the concept preference ratio. Unlike prior ICL backdoor attack papers (Zhao et al. 2024, Xiang et al. 2024) that only demonstrate attack feasibility, this work provides the first mechanistic explanation and the first defense specifically targeting ICL backdoor attacks, as opposed to the trigger-removal strategies (ONION, Back-Translation) designed for data-poisoning or model-poisoning settings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As ICL becomes the dominant interface for LLM deployment (agent systems, shared prompt templates, API services), the ability to manipulate model behavior through a few poisoned demonstrations without touching model weights represents a practical and under-defended threat. The concept preference ratio gives a principled, interpretable handle for defense, and the simplicity of the ICLShield intervention (selecting clean examples by similarity and confidence) makes it deployable even in black-box settings. Open questions include whether the defense generalizes to more complex prompt structures (Tree-of-Thought, Graph-of-Thought), to domains with high-stakes outputs (medical, financial), and to adaptive attackers who can anticipate the defense's example-selection strategy.
