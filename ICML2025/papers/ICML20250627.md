## Problem

Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) successfully decompose LLM activations into interpretable features, but they are not designed to sparsify the computations performed by the model. When two SAEs are placed at the input and output of an MLP, the mapping between their latents (the computational graph or circuit) is typically dense, making it difficult to understand which input features feed into which output features. This paper addresses the gap by asking: can we train SAEs such that the Jacobian of the input-to-output latent mapping is also sparse?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250627/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes the dense activation vectors at the input and output of a single transformer MLP layer (e.g., from Pythia models), producing two sets of k-sparse latent codes (with k=32 active elements per token position) and a k×k Jacobian matrix per token that encodes the local sensitivity of each active output latent to each active input latent. The Jacobian is not the full n_y × n_x matrix (which would have ~1 trillion elements) but only the k×k submatrix corresponding to currently active latents, made possible by the TopK gating. Downstream, the sparse Jacobian entries are aggregated over millions of tokens to identify interpretable computational units—pairs of input and output latents with strong, consistent connections.

## Method

JSAEs train a pair of k-sparse (TopK) autoencoders on the input and output of a transformer MLP, adding an L1 penalty on the Jacobian of the composed function f_s = e_y ∘ f ∘ d_x ∘ τ_k to the standard reconstruction loss. The critical technical insight is that the TopK activation τ_k, included in the definition of f_s, guarantees that Jacobian entries corresponding to inactive latents are exactly zero, reducing the Jacobian from n_y × n_x to k × k per token (a ~6-order-of-magnitude reduction). Rather than computing this Jacobian via automatic differentiation (which would require k backward passes), the authors derive a closed-form expression requiring only three matrix multiplications and pointwise operations, making JSAE training cost roughly double that of a single standard SAE. The loss is L = MSE(x, x̂) + MSE(y, ŷ) + (λ/k²) Σ|J_{f_s,ij}|, where the 1/k² normalization accounts for the at-most-k² nonzero entries. The authors further show that f_s is approximately linear (88% linear, 10% JumpReLU), validating the Jacobian as an accurate proxy for global computational sparsity rather than merely a local derivative.

## Results

At the optimal Jacobian loss coefficient (λ=0.5 for Pythia-410m, k=32), JSAEs dramatically reduce the proportion of large Jacobian elements compared to traditional SAEs while incurring only a slight increase in average cross-entropy loss. Auto-interpretability scores (fuzzing metric) are essentially unchanged between JSAEs and traditional SAEs across all odd-numbered layers of Pythia-410m. The function f_s is approximately linear: 88% of scalar input-to-output latent functions are linear, 10% are JumpReLU-like, and only 2% are neither; correspondingly, 97.7% of Jacobian elements accurately predict the change in the output latent when the input latent is perturbed by 1. Jacobians are substantially sparser in pre-trained Pythia-410m than in a randomly re-initialized version, for both traditional SAEs and JSAEs, indicating that Jacobian sparsity reflects learned computational structure.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the traditional k-sparse (TopK) SAE (λ=0), following the formulation of Bricken et al. (2023) and Gao et al. (2024). JSAEs clearly win on Jacobian sparsity: at λ=0.5, the proportion of Jacobian elements above threshold 0.01 drops substantially relative to traditional SAEs (Figure 2), while reconstruction quality (variance explained) and model performance preservation (cross-entropy increase) degrade only marginally. On auto-interpretability (fuzzing scores), JSAEs and traditional SAEs are essentially tied (Figure 6). A second comparison is against randomly re-initialized transformers: JSAEs extract far more Jacobian sparsity from pre-trained models than from random ones, and the relative improvement from adding the Jacobian term (traditional SAE → JSAE) is much larger for trained models, confirming that JSAEs capture learned structure rather than generic linear-algebraic properties. The paper does not perform a direct quantitative comparison against transcoders (Dunefsky et al., 2024), noting that the two methods sparsify fundamentally different objects and that meaningful comparison would require extensive future work.

## Relation to Prior Work

JSAEs extend the standard sparse autoencoder framework (Bricken et al., 2023; Cunningham et al., 2023; Gao et al., 2024) by adding an L1 penalty on the Jacobian of the function mapping input SAE latents to output SAE latents, thereby sparsifying the computation graph rather than just the activations. The paper explicitly contrasts with transcoders (Dunefsky et al., 2024), which sparsify MLP hidden states rather than input/output representations, and with automated circuit discovery (Conmy et al., 2023; Marks et al., 2024), which is supervised and task-specific. The key technical departure is an efficient closed-form Jacobian computation (three matrix multiplications) that makes the otherwise intractable trillion-element Jacobian tractable, reducing training cost to roughly 2× a single standard SAE.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

JSAEs provide the first fully unsupervised method that simultaneously discovers interpretable features and the sparse computational dependencies between them within a single MLP layer, addressing a gap the authors identify in standard SAEs. The finding that Jacobian sparsity is substantially greater in pre-trained than randomly initialized transformers (while standard SAE auto-interpretability scores are not, per Heap et al., 2025) suggests Jacobian sparsity captures learned computational structure that activation-level sparsity misses. Key limitations include restriction to a single MLP layer (not full-model circuits), evaluation only on small Pythia models, and the unexplained degradation of the output SAE at high λ. Open questions include whether the approach scales to larger models, whether it generalizes beyond GPT-2-style MLPs to more complex architectures, and how JSAE-discovered circuits relate to those found by supervised methods like ACDC.
