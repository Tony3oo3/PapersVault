## Problem

Controlling tearing instabilities in tokamaks with machine learning is hampered by three compounding issues: real-world experiments are too costly for the many rollouts that RL requires, tokamak dynamics shift between shots due to hardware changes and prior experiments so any fixed model degrades, and existing Bayesian optimization approaches for tokamaks rely on poorly specified priors that need many experiments to converge. The paper asks whether a multi-timescale model that combines a high-frequency dynamics predictor with a rapidly updatable low-frequency surrogate can find stabilizing ECH profiles in just a handful of live shots.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input a target normalized plasma pressure β̄_N (the BO context), a pre-trained RPNN that generates autoregressive rollouts to form the GP prior mean, and a small set of historical (β̄_N, ECH-profile, time-to-tearing) triplets. The ECH profile is constrained to a stationary Gaussian curve parameterized by three scalars (center, width, amplitude), keeping the GP in a 4-dimensional input space. The output is the optimized Gaussian ECH profile, which is then projected onto gyrotron angles via OMFIT; the number of gyrotrons (and thus the dimension of the angle vector) can vary between shots. Crucially, the GP is updated with the *measured* ECH profile and β_N rather than the desired setpoints, accounting for actuator noise and hardware variability.

## Method

DynaBO operates at two timescales. At the high-frequency scale, a GRU-based Recurrent Probabilistic Neural Network (RPNN) trained on ~15,000 one-step DIII-D transitions predicts the next-state distribution N(η, Σ²) given (s_t, a_t), and a separate random-forest tearing-mode predictor h(s_t, a_t) estimates the per-step instability probability. At the low-frequency scale, a Gaussian process with a squared-exponential kernel predicts time-to-tearing mode t_TM as a function of (β̄_N, a_q), using the average t_TM from RPNN rollouts as a non-zero prior mean rather than a zero mean. Contextual Bayesian optimization with a UCB acquisition function then selects the next ECH profile, treating β̄_N as the context. The key design choice is that the RPNN prior mean encodes the expected dynamics while the GP residual (observed minus predicted t_TM) captures the sim-to-real gap, allowing the GP to correct model errors with only a few new observations between shots. This yields substantially better exploration than vanilla GP or RPNN-only baselines, as the informative prior steers the UCB toward physically plausible regions of the action space.

## Results

In live experiments at the DIII-D Tokamak, DynaBO avoided tearing instabilities in 4 out of 8 shots (50% success rate), compared to a historical tearing rate of 77% (47/61 similar experiments), representing a 117% improvement. The average time-to-tearing instability under DynaBO was 3339 ms versus 2424 ms historically (a 914 ms gain). In offline simulations on 281 historical shots, DynaBO achieved the lowest cumulative regret across all kernel settings except the linear kernel, where it was outperformed by the time-dependent variant.

## Baselines & Comparisons

In offline cumulative-regret comparisons (Fig. 2), DynaBO outperforms the RPNN-only baseline, vanilla GP with zero-mean prior, and vanilla GP with mean-value prior across all kernels except the linear kernel, where the time-dependent variant edges ahead. The RPNN-only and vanilla GP baselines fail to converge within the practical budget of ~500 steps, whereas DynaBO converges substantially faster. The time-dependent kernel ablation (DynaBO+time) performs competitively but offers only marginal improvement, suggesting that data reliability is governed by multiple non-temporal factors (hardware changes, impurities, wall interactions). In the live DIII-D experiment, DynaBO's 50% success rate (4/8) contrasts sharply with the 23% historical success rate (14/61) under the same high-q_min, high-β_N configuration, and its mean stability time of 3339 ms exceeds the historical 2424 ms by 914 ms.

## Relation to Prior Work

DynaBO synthesizes three prior threads into a unified multi-timescale pipeline: the RPNN dynamics model from Char et al. (2023b) provides high-frequency state-transition predictions, the contextual Bayesian optimization framework from Char et al. (2019) supplies the low-dimensional action-selection mechanism, and the GP-with-nonzero-prior-mean formulation from Deisenroth & Rasmussen (2011) enables data-efficient updates between experiments. The key architectural departure is using autoregressive RPNN rollouts as the GP prior mean, so that the less reliable dynamics model is corrected by a fast-updating nonparametric layer rather than retrained from scratch. This contrasts with prior tokamak BO work (Mehta et al. 2024; Char et al. 2019), which relied on zero-mean or hand-specified priors and did not address tearing-mode instability, and with model-based RL approaches (Char et al. 2023a; Degrave et al. 2022) that assume the trained dynamics model remains valid at test time.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first demonstration of a fully data-driven, online-updating ML pipeline that preemptively suppresses tearing instabilities on a live tokamak, achieving a 50% stabilization rate versus a 77% historical failure rate in the same high-qmin configuration. The multi-timescale GP-over-RPNN pattern is a reusable recipe for any complex physical system where a trained dynamics model degrades due to hardware changes, maintenance, or unmodeled drift, and where only a handful of new experiments are affordable between updates. Key limitations temper the impact: the live results rest on only 8 shots (not statistically significant), the method is restricted to feedforward ECH profiles and cannot adapt to real-time MHD activity, and the approach is purely data-driven with no physics-informed constraints (e.g., Rutherford equation), leaving open whether interpretability and safety guarantees can be improved by hybrid modeling.
