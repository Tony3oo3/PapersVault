## Problem

Generalized random forests (GRFs) estimate heterogeneous effects by solving locally weighted estimating equations, but their Stage I tree-building relies on a gradient-based splitting criterion that requires inverting a local Jacobian matrix at every node. This makes each split O(K³) in the target dimension and numerically unstable when regressors are correlated, limiting GRF's scalability to high-dimensional settings such as multi-arm treatment effect estimation.

## Input / Output

The method consumes tabular data consisting of auxiliary covariates X (defining the localization space), K-dimensional regressors W, and scalar outcomes Y, and produces a continuous mapping x ↦ (θ*(x), ν*(x)) giving a K-dimensional local effect estimate and nuisance estimate at any test point. Estimation proceeds in two stages: Stage I trains a forest of trees to compute observation weights α_i(x), and Stage II solves a locally weighted estimating equation at each query point, so the output is a function over the covariate space rather than a single prediction.

## Method

The core replacement swaps gradient-based pseudo-outcomes ρ_i^grad = −A_P⁻¹ψ(O_i) for fixed-point pseudo-outcomes ρ_i^FPT = −ηξᵀψ(O_i), which require only the scores evaluated at the parent solution and eliminate the O(K³) Jacobian inversion entirely. A single fixed-point update from the parent estimate approximates the child solution, and the resulting pseudo-outcomes are passed directly to a standard CART algorithm for axis-aligned splitting. A scale-invariance lemma shows the optimal split is independent of η, so η=1 suffices without tuning. An accelerated variant further replaces the exact parent OLS solution with a one-step gradient-descent approximation, reducing per-node cost further. The approach preserves GRF's consistency and asymptotic normality guarantees while achieving up to 3.5× speedup at K=256 with matching estimation accuracy.

## Results

GRF-FPT achieves a speedup of up to 3.5× over standard GRF-grad at the largest tested dimension (K=256) in VCM experiments, with increasing gains as K grows. Estimation accuracy (MSE of θ̂(x) over 50 replications, tested on 5,000 held-out observations) matches GRF-grad across both VCM and HTE settings. On the California Housing dataset (20,640 observations, 6 regressors), GRF-FPT2 produces qualitatively similar geographically-varying effect estimates to GRF-grad while reducing computational cost. The accelerated variant GRF-FPT2 (using one-step gradient descent for parent estimates) further reduces overhead without altering asymptotic behavior.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole external baseline is GRF-grad, the standard gradient-based Generalized Random Forests of Athey et al. (2019). GRF-FPT wins decisively on computational efficiency: it eliminates the O(K³) matrix inversion of the local Jacobian A_P at every node, yielding speedups that grow with dimension (up to 3.5× at K=256). On statistical accuracy, GRF-FPT ties GRF-grad — MSE estimates across 50 replications are indistinguishable, and the theoretical guarantees of consistency (Theorem 4.3) and asymptotic normality (Theorem 4.4) are preserved via the same forest specifications. GRF-FPT also wins on stability: in the varying-coefficient example with highly correlated regressors (Corr(W₁,W₂) up to 0.99), gradient-based splits exhibit high variance while fixed-point splits remain stable (Figure 1). No comparison shows GRF-FPT losing to GRF-grad on any reported metric.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper is a direct methodological refinement of Generalized Random Forests (Athey et al., 2019), replacing the gradient-based tree-splitting criterion—which requires inverting a local Jacobian matrix A_P at every node—with a single-step fixed-point approximation that uses only the score functions evaluated at the parent solution. The pseudo-outcome framework connects the fixed-point update to standard CART splitting, and the theoretical analysis inherits GRF's two-stage architecture, forest specifications, and Neyman-orthogonality conditions. The approach draws on classical fixed-point iteration theory (Picard, Banach) but deliberately uses only one iteration step, sidestepping convergence concerns entirely.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method offers a practical 3.5× speedup at K=256 and eliminates the instability that gradient-based GRF splits exhibit under high regressor correlation, making GRF more viable for large-scale A/B testing and observational studies with many treatment arms. However, the speedup is moderate and the method is tightly coupled to the GRF framework; it does not generalize to other tree-based or non-tree-based heterogeneous effect estimators. Open questions include whether the fixed-point splitting criterion degrades in quality for non-linear or non-convex moment conditions, and whether the O(K³) savings translate to meaningful wall-clock gains in the most extreme high-dimensional regimes (K > 1000) where other bottlenecks may dominate.
