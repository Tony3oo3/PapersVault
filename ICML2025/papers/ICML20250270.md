## Problem

Existing jailbreak research predominantly targets technically complex attacks (gradient-based optimization, multi-agent prompting) that require expertise and do not reflect how non-technical users would realistically misuse LLMs. Additionally, standard evaluation metrics like Attack Success Rate measure whether a model complies but do not capture whether the resulting response is actually useful for enabling harmful actions. The paper asks: can simple, everyday interaction patterns (multi-step decomposition, multilingual querying) elicit genuinely harmful responses, and how should we measure the practical harmfulness of those responses?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250270/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single-sentence malicious query and decomposes it into m=3 seemingly benign subqueries, each translated into n=6 languages spanning high-, mid-, and low-resource groups (English, Simplified Chinese, Ukrainian, Turkish, Zulu, Thai) via Azure AI Translator. The output is a concatenated jailbreak response assembled from the highest-scoring subquery response in each step, plus a HARMSCORE computed as the geometric mean of actionability and informativeness scores (gated by a refusal-string check). The fine-tuned response selection models output continuous scores in [0,1] after sigmoid normalization to weight both attributes equally.

## Method

SPEAK EASY exploits two common interaction patterns: query decomposition into innocuous subqueries and multilingual rephrasing, which together bypass safety guardrails that trigger on the original harmful query. For each subquery, the target LLM is prompted in all six languages, responses are translated back to English, and a fine-tuned Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct selection model (trained via iterative DPO on 27K preference pairs per attribute from HH-RLHF and Stack-Exchange-Preferences) picks the most actionable and informative response. The key empirical finding, validated through human evaluation with Lasso regression, is that actionability and informativeness are the two attributes most strongly correlated with perceived harmfulness, motivating HARMSCORE as a geometric-mean metric that aligns better with human judgment than ASR for instruction-seeking queries. The framework operates as a standalone black-box attack or integrates additively with existing methods (GCG-T, TAP-T) by applying decomposition and multilingual selection to their generated subqueries.

## Results

SPEAK EASY increases GPT-4o's average ASR from 0.092 to 0.555 and HARMSCORE from 0.180 to 0.759 across four benchmarks (HarmBench, AdvBench, SORRY-Bench, MedSafetyBench) when applied to direct requests. When integrated with existing jailbreak methods (GCG-T, TAP-T), the average absolute increase is 0.313 in ASR and 0.398 in HARMSCORE across all three target LLMs. Most strikingly, TAP-T + SPEAK EASY achieves ASR exceeding 0.9 across all benchmarks for both GPT-4o (0.945) and Llama-3.3-70B (0.945), with HARMSCORE reaching 0.707 and 0.801 respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SPEAK EASY consistently outperforms all jailbreak baselines when integrated. Against GCG-T (Zou et al., 2023), adding SPEAK EASY yields an average ASR increase of ≥0.2 across all LLMs, with GPT-4o jumping from 0.085 to 0.565 ASR and 0.155 to 0.791 HARMSCORE. Against TAP-T (Mehrotra et al., 2024), which already achieves strong baseline scores (0.682 ASR for GPT-4o), SPEAK EASY further boosts ASR by 0.235–0.336 and HARMSCORE by 0.299–0.393, pushing GPT-4o and Llama-3.3 above 0.9 ASR on all benchmarks. The Past Tense Attack (Andriushchenko & Flammarion, 2024) achieves 0.346 ASR on GPT-4o, which SPEAK EASY integration raises to 0.555. For response selection, the paper's fine-tuned Llama-3.1-8B models (83.5% actionability, 95.6% informativeness accuracy) substantially outperform FsfairX-LLaMA3-RM-v0.1 (18.3%/10.5%) and ArmoRM-Llama3-8B-v0.1 (76.4%/4.8%). For evaluation metrics, HARMSCORE achieves 0.726 overall Pearson correlation with human judgment, matching GPT-ASR (0.723) and outperforming HB-ASR (0.638) by 9%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on two known vulnerability axes in LLM safety—multilingual jailbreaks (Yong et al., 2023; Deng et al., 2024a) and multi-step/multi-turn attacks (Li et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024)—but departs from the dominant paradigm of requiring technical expertise (gradient-based suffix optimization à la GCG, tree-of-attacks à la TAP) by showing that the combination of query decomposition and multilingual querying, both common in everyday user interactions, is sufficient to elicit highly harmful responses. It also introduces HARMSCORE as a fine-grained alternative to the standard Attack Success Rate, grounded in human-evaluated attributes (actionability and informativeness) rather than binary compliance judgments. The response selection models trained via iterative DPO on repurposed preference datasets (HH-RLHF, Stack-Exchange-Preferences) represent a methodological bridge between preference optimization and jailbreak evaluation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is significant: non-technical users can exploit common interaction patterns (decomposing a request into sub-questions, switching languages) to obtain actionable harmful instructions from safety-aligned models, with GPT-4o's refusal rate dropping from 81% to 1.5% simply by adding two decomposition steps. HARMSCORE offers a more human-aligned evaluation metric than ASR, particularly for instruction-seeking queries, though it underperforms for content-generation tasks (misinformation, cybercrime) where actionability is less relevant. Open questions include whether defenses can be made robust to arbitrary multi-step multilingual decompositions, whether the diminishing returns beyond six languages indicate a fundamental ceiling, and how to extend HARMSCORE to harm categories where informativeness without actionability is the primary risk vector.
