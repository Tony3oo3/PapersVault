## Problem

Existing test-time adaptation (TTA) methods rely on sample selection criteria to filter out high-entropy 'malicious' samples that destabilize optimization, but this discards a significant fraction of test data and makes performance highly sensitive to the selection threshold. The paper asks whether malicious samples can be transformed into benign ones rather than simply discarded, thereby improving test data utilization without requiring careful threshold tuning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251695/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes unlabeled RGB test images arriving in mini-batches (size 64) at test time, a pretrained DNN (ResNet-50, ViT-B/16, CLIP, or DeepLab-V2), and a FIFO memory bank of up to 1,000 previously identified benign samples with their stored logit-saliency vectors and predicted probabilities. It outputs standard class predictions (or segmentation masks for DeepLab-V2), updated model parameters, and purified mixed samples used to compute an additional cross-entropy loss. The logit-saliency indicator is computed in closed form from the model's logits and softmax probabilities, requiring no extra backpropagation.

## Method

PTTA introduces a saliency indicator — the gradient of the entropy loss with respect to the model's logits, computed in closed form as −(z − p·z) ⊙ p — that encodes each sample's directional contribution to the TTA objective. For each test sample, the method retrieves a benign sample from a memory bank (or within the mini-batch) whose saliency vector has the maximum cosine distance (i.e., most opposite gradient direction), then applies Mixup between the two to produce a purified sample and its interpolated pseudo-label. A purification loss (cross-entropy on the mixed sample) is added to the existing TTA objective with a balancing coefficient α, making PTTA a plug-and-play module compatible with any sample-selection-based TTA algorithm. The approach also demonstrates that the saliency indicator is effective even without explicit sample selection, as it can correctly retrieve benign counterparts from a mixed pool of candidates.

## Results

On the single TTA task (ImageNet-C, severity 5), PTTA yields an average accuracy increase of 1.5% for ResNet50 and 1.2% for ViT-B/16 across all base methods (e.g., ETA: 48.2%→49.5% ResNet50, 63.7%→64.4% ViT-B/16). On the continual TTA task, PTTA delivers larger gains where base methods suffer from distribution shift, improving Tent from 9.9% to 36.9% and DeYO from 9.2% to 25.7% on ResNet50. On the lifelong TTA task (10 rounds, 150 corruptions), PTTA reduces ETA's catastrophic accuracy drop from −37.1% (round 1→10) to only −1.2%, and EATA+PTTA even shows a +0.3% net gain. On semantic segmentation (CarlaTTA), PTTA achieves up to 1.8% mIoU improvement over base methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PTTA is a plug-and-play module applied on top of eight existing TTA baselines (Tent, CoTTA, SoTTA, SAR, ETA, EATA, DeYO, CPL) and consistently improves every one of them. The largest relative gains appear on the continual task with ResNet50, where Tent jumps from 9.9% to 36.9% (+27.0%) and DeYO from 9.2% to 25.7% (+16.5%), indicating PTTA is most beneficial when the base method is highly sensitive to distribution shift. On the lifelong task, PTTA dramatically mitigates catastrophic forgetting: ETA's round-1-to-round-10 accuracy drop shrinks from −37.1% to −1.2%, and EATA+PTTA actually improves by +0.3% over 10 rounds. On the episodic prompt-tuning task (Table 4), PTTA adds ~1.4–1.8% average accuracy over TPT and TPT+CoOP for both CLIP-RN50 and CLIP-ViT-B/16. For adversarial defense (Table 6), PTTA reduces the error rate of BN Adapt from 53.7% to 52.6% and MedBN from 47.1% to 46.2% on CIFAR-100-C under indiscriminate attack. PTTA also outperforms adversarial purification baselines (FGSM, SOAP) in Table 1, where those methods either fail to improve or actively degrade TTA performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

PTTA sits at the intersection of test-time adaptation (TTA) and adversarial purification. It builds directly on the Mixup data augmentation technique (Zhang et al., 2018) as its core purification mechanism, and repurposes the saliency/gradient concept from adversarial defense (FGSM, SOAP) not to add pixel-level noise but to serve as a retrieval metric for matching benign and malicious samples. Conceptually, it departs from the dominant TTA paradigm of sample selection and filtering (ETA, DeYO, CPL) by arguing that discarded 'malicious' samples still carry useful distributional information that can be recovered through targeted mixing with retrieved benign counterparts. The method is explicitly designed as a plug-and-play add-on to existing TTA objectives (entropy minimization, consistency loss), requiring no architectural changes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PTTA addresses a practical data-efficiency bottleneck in TTA: existing sample-selection criteria waste a portion of test data and introduce fragile threshold sensitivity (a 0.1 shift in selection proportion can cause 36% accuracy drops). By recovering information from filtered samples, PTTA reduces this sensitivity and improves utilization, which is particularly valuable in data-scarce deployment scenarios. The finding that sample selection criteria become unnecessary when the saliency indicator is used for retrieval is a useful simplification for practitioners. Open questions include whether the approach extends to video TTA, VQA, and super-resolution (acknowledged but not tested), whether the ~10–40% runtime overhead is acceptable in latency-critical settings, and whether the memory bank's FIFO strategy is optimal for highly non-stationary streams.
