## Problem

Prior autonomous driving policies rely heavily on human driving data, hand-designed scenarios, or benchmark-specific training, limiting their generalization and robustness. It was unclear whether pure self-play reinforcement learning—without any human data—could produce naturalistic, robust driving at all, let alone at a scale sufficient to master complex multi-agent traffic interactions. This paper asks whether taking self-play to unprecedented scale (1.6 billion km) in a purpose-built simulator can yield a single generalist policy that outperforms benchmark-specific specialists.

## Input / Output

The policy consumes vector observations rather than images: sets of lane points (sampled at 40 m intervals), boundary points (~1 m spacing), traffic light states, nearby agent features (position, orientation, velocity, dimensions within 200 m), ego-vehicle kinematics, and conditioning parameters specifying vehicle dynamics, reward weights, and goals. All set-based observations are encoded via permutation-invariant Deep Sets encoders with channel-wise max-pooling. The output is a discrete action drawn from a 12-element space (Cartesian product of 4 longitudinal jerk values and 3 lateral jerk values) applied through a jerk-actuated bicycle dynamics model, alongside a scalar state-value estimate from a separate critic network. Observations are not stored in the rollout buffer but reconstructed on demand from stored world states to save memory.

## Method

GIGAFLOW is a batched GPU simulator that runs 38,400 parallel worlds with up to 150 agents each, all controlled by a single 6M-parameter shared policy (a Deep Sets-style feed-forward network), achieving 4.4 billion state transitions per hour on one 8-GPU node. Behavioral diversity emerges through per-agent conditioning parameters (vehicle dimensions, dynamics coefficients, randomized reward weights, goals) rather than separate networks, so a single forward pass produces actions for all agents. Training uses PPO with a novel advantage filtering step that discards ~80% of transitions with near-zero GAE advantage, yielding a 2.3× throughput increase and more robust policies. The simulator models uncertainty via state noise, stochastic agent behavior, partial observability (agents see only positions and speeds of others, not goals or accelerations), and aggressive traffic-light randomization. At the demonstrated scale (10^11–10^12 state transitions), complex skills like multi-lane merging, unprotected turns, and long-horizon rerouting emerge without explicit planning modules or hand-crafted scenarios.

## Results

GIGAFLOW, a single generalist policy trained entirely via self-play (1.6 billion km, ~10 days on one 8-GPU node), achieves state-of-the-art zero-shot performance on all three major closed-loop driving benchmarks: CARLA, nuPlan (15 collisions in 1,118 scenarios), and Waymax/Waymo Open Motion Dataset (187 collisions in 44,097 scenarios, of which only 5 are at-fault and avoidable). On the Waymo Open Sim Agents Challenge (WOSAC) realism meta-metric, GIGAFLOW scores 0.62 zero-shot, outperforming supervised autoregressive prediction approaches. In long-form simulation evaluation, the policy averages 17.5 years (over 3 million km) of continuous driving between incidents, far exceeding the human average of ~829,000 km per crash in the US.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GIGAFLOW outperforms all four specialist baselines on their respective home benchmarks using a single unified policy with no benchmark-specific training data. On CARLA, GIGAFLOW surpasses the hand-designed expert from Jaeger et al. (2023) (which uses privileged information like exact agent positions) and the TransFuser imitation-based model from Chitta et al. (2023), despite the latter being trained on CARLA-specific data. On nuPlan Challenge 3 (closed-loop reactive), GIGAFLOW beats the PDM-Hybrid planner from Dauner et al. (2023), which was specifically designed for nuPlan's scoring criteria. On Waymax, GIGAFLOW outperforms the IDM-based and other baselines reported by Gulino et al. (2023), with only 5 at-fault avoidable collisions out of 44,097 scenarios. The critical distinction is that all baselines are specialists trained or hand-tuned for their specific benchmark, while GIGAFLOW is a generalist trained exclusively via self-play in simulation and evaluated zero-shot—never seeing any human driving data during training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the self-play paradigm from games (AlphaGo, AlphaStar, Dota 2) to continuous, partially observable multi-agent driving, departing fundamentally from prior driving RL that relied on hand-designed traffic models (IDM, MOBIL), recorded human trajectories, or benchmark-specific specialist policies. The method builds on PPO with a novel advantage-filtering mechanism (a variant of Prioritized Experience Replay), a Deep Sets-style permutation-invariant architecture for handling variable agent counts, and the batched GPU simulation paradigm from large-scale RL environments. The key conceptual departure is that all traffic participant behaviors emerge from a single shared policy trained purely through self-play at unprecedented scale (1.6 billion km), rather than being scripted, replayed, or learned from human data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a policy trained entirely via self-play with no human data outperforms benchmark-specific specialists across three independent driving benchmarks challenges the prevailing assumption that recorded driving datasets are essential for training capable driving policies. The 17.5-year mean time between incidents and zero-shot generalization across actor types, maps, and scoring methodologies suggest a path toward more scalable and data-efficient driving policy training. Key open questions include sim-to-real transfer (the work is entirely in simulation), integration of realistic perception (the paper abstracts the sensing stack), and whether combining self-play with human data via imitation learning would further improve robustness. The GIGAFLOW infrastructure itself—simulating 42 years of driving per hour on a single 8-GPU node at under $5 per million km—may be as impactful as the policy results for the broader RL community.
