## Problem

Existing fMRI-to-text decoding models face two critical limitations: they are not subject-agnostic, requiring subject-specific parameters or relying on pooling/sampling strategies that lose spatial information and create uneven brain-area representations, and they lack versatility, being restricted to captioning the current visual stimulus rather than handling broader cognitive tasks like memory retrieval, knowledge-based reasoning, or symbolic processing.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252018/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes variable-length fMRI voxel signals (ranging from 12,682 to 17,907 voxels across subjects in the NSD dataset) along with a text instruction prompt as input, and produces natural language outputs including captions, QA answers, and multi-turn dialogue responses. The fMRI encoder compresses the variable-length voxel input into a fixed set of 128 fMRI tokens that are prepended to the language tokens of a frozen Vicuna-7b LLM, which then generates the text response via standard next-token prediction.

## Method

The core contribution is a neuroscience-informed cross-attention mechanism where 1024 learnable queries attend to all voxels, with keys constructed solely from Fourier positional encodings of voxel coordinates concatenated with learnable embeddings from multiple brain parcellations (e.g., Glasser, AAL3), while values are the raw fMRI signals. This design deliberately excludes fMRI values from keys, separating a voxel's functional/positional identity (shared across subjects) from its activation magnitude (subject-specific), which enables subject-agnostic generalization without any pooling or sampling. For versatility, the authors introduce Brain Instruction Tuning (BIT), a 980K-conversation dataset built by pairing NSD fMRI recordings with text annotations from 15 VQA and captioning datasets spanning perception, memory recall, language/symbolic processing, and complex reasoning. Only the fMRI encoder is trained (the LLM remains frozen), using a standard next-token prediction objective on assistant responses, with the encoder's hidden representations mapped through a 4-layer MLP to produce the 128 fMRI tokens.

## Results

On the standard brain captioning benchmark (Table 1), MindLLM achieves BLEU-1 of 61.75, CIDEr of 60.97, and ROUGE-L of 45.82, outperforming all baselines while being the only fully subject-agnostic model (shared parameters across all subjects). Across 12 diverse downstream tasks (Table 2), MindLLM with Brain Instruction Tuning yields a 12.0% average improvement over the best baseline, with instruction tuning alone contributing a 28.0% gain over from-scratch training. On unseen-subject generalization (Table 3), MindLLM improves over the second-best subject-agnostic model by 24.5% on average. For novel task adaptation on TDIUC (Table 4), MindLLM reaches 54.08% A-MPT and 45.43% H-MPT, a 25.0% absolute improvement from BIT over the from-scratch variant.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MindLLM is the only model that simultaneously achieves cross-subject operation, a unified architecture, shared parameters, and use of all voxels (Table 12). On brain captioning, MindLLM beats the strongest subject-specific baseline UMBRAE (Xia et al., 2024) on BLEU-1 (61.75 vs. 59.44), BLEU-2 (42.84 vs. 40.48), BLEU-3 (29.86 vs. 27.66), BLEU-4 (21.24 vs. 19.03), METEOR (19.54 vs. 19.45), and ROUGE (45.82 vs. 43.71), while UMBRAE edges ahead on CIDEr (61.06 vs. 60.97) and SPICE (12.79 vs. 11.79). Among subject-agnostic baselines, MindLLM clearly dominates UniBrain (Wang et al., 2024b) across all metrics (e.g., BLEU-1: 61.75 vs. 59.08; CIDEr: 60.97 vs. 48.20). In the versatile decoding experiment (Table 2), MindLLM with BIT outperforms all 8 baselines across 12 tasks including COCO-QA (48.19% vs. 45.33% MindBridge), A-OKVQA (52.21% vs. 50.44% MindBridge), and Previous Caption BLEU-1 (47.20 vs. 44.52 MindLLM°), with UMBRAE unable to perform memory-related tasks at all. On unseen-subject generalization (Table 3), MindLLM trained on subjects 1–7 and tested on subject 8 achieves 24.5% average improvement over the best subject-agnostic baseline, e.g., 44.60 vs. 41.90 BLEU-1 on COCO Caption and 13.63 vs. 9.33 ANLS on ST-VQA. For novel task adaptation on TDIUC (Table 4), MindLLM achieves 54.08% A-MPT and 45.43% H-MPT, surpassing UniBrain (51.50%/36.76%) and MindBridge (49.77%/39.88%), with a particularly large gap on utility/affordance (50.00% vs. 28.57% and 14.29%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the fMRI-to-text decoding line (MindGPT, BrainCap, UMBRAE) and the cross-subject brain decoding line (MindBridge, UniBrain) by addressing two gaps simultaneously: subject-agnostic architecture and task versatility. It departs from prior cross-subject methods that standardize inputs via pooling or sampling (losing spatial information) by proposing a neuroscience-informed cross-attention where voxel positions and brain parcellations serve as keys while fMRI signals serve as values, preserving all voxels without dimensionality reduction. The Brain Instruction Tuning paradigm, which uses MSCOCO images as intermediaries to pair fMRI recordings with diverse text annotations (QA, captioning, memory recall, reasoning), is a novel training strategy not present in prior fMRI decoding work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Subject-agnostic fMRI decoding is a prerequisite for practical brain-computer interfaces, where training a separate model per individual is infeasible; the 24.5% generalization gain on held-out subjects is directly relevant to deployment. The interpretability of attention maps aligning with known functional regions (PPA, FFA) offers a bridge between data-driven decoding and established neuroscience, potentially informing both model design and brain understanding. Key limitations include reliance on a single dataset (NSD, 8 subjects), static fMRI without temporal dynamics, and the fact that versatility is bounded by the MSCOCO image stimuli used as intermediaries. Open questions include whether temporal integration, domain adaptation, or scaling to more subjects and diverse stimuli would further close the gap to subject-specific performance.
