## Problem

Existing theoretical frameworks for language generation (Kleinberg & Mullainathan 2024; Li et al. 2024) guarantee only that a generator eventually produces valid elements from the target language, but say nothing about whether outputs are diverse or representative of the training data's group structure. A model could satisfy generation in the limit while collapsing to a single subgroup (e.g., generating only cats from an animal dataset), failing to capture the proportional diversity present in the training stream. This paper introduces and characterizes 'representative generation,' which additionally requires the generator's output distribution to match the empirical group proportions at every timestep.

## Input / Output

The generator receives a finite sequence of examples drawn from an unknown target language (a hypothesis h in a class H over a countable space X) and a countable collection of groups A (partitions or overlapping subsets) defining the groups of interest. At each timestep it outputs a full probability distribution over X rather than a single element, which must simultaneously be α-representative (supremum distance between induced group probabilities and empirical group probabilities ≤ α) and, after a finite number of distinct examples, fully supported on unseen elements of the true language. Representation is required at every timestep, while consistency is only required eventually, reflecting the fact that representativeness is verifiable but consistency is not.

## Method

The paper introduces the Group Closure dimension GC_α(H, A), a scale-sensitive combinatorial quantity that jointly captures the complexity of the hypothesis class and the group partition, and proves it exactly characterizes representative uniform generatability: (H, A) is α-representatively uniformly generatable if and only if GC_α(H, A) < ∞, with optimal sample complexity Θ(GC_α). For representative non-uniform generation, the characterization reduces to the existence of a nested sequence of subclasses each satisfying representative uniform generation. For the weakest notion (generation in the limit), the authors extend Kleinberg & Mullainathan's critical-hypothesis algorithm by additionally requiring the selected hypothesis to be α-feasible (admitting a distribution over unseen elements matching group proportions), and prove that any countable H with a finite support assumption relative to countable, possibly overlapping groups A is generatable. They further prove a negative computability result: no generator using only a finite number of membership queries per step can achieve representative generation in the limit, in contrast to the positive result for standard generation.

## Results

This is a purely theoretical paper with no experimental results or quantitative benchmarks. The main contributions are characterizations: (1) Theorem 3.3 shows that (H, A) is α-representatively uniformly generatable if and only if the Group Closure dimension GC_α(H, A) is finite, providing an exact sample complexity bound of Θ(GC_α(H, A)); (2) Corollary 3.8 establishes that all countable hypothesis classes with finite partitions are representatively non-uniformly generatable; (3) Theorem 4.4 extends positive results to countable, possibly overlapping groups under a finite support assumption; and (4) Lemma 4.9 proves a negative result showing no generator using only membership queries can achieve representative generation in the limit, contrasting with the positive result of Kleinberg & Mullainathan (2024) for standard generation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not perform empirical comparisons but provides theoretical contrasts with prior work. Against Kleinberg & Mullainathan (2024), who show that all countable H are generatable in the limit using only membership queries, this paper proves the opposite: adding the representation constraint makes membership-query-only generation impossible (Lemma 4.9), even for |H|=1 and |A|=2. Against Li et al. (2024), who characterize uniform and non-uniform generation without representation, this paper shows that representation strictly increases difficulty: Corollary 3.6 constructs a trivially uniformly generatable class that fails representative uniform generation with just two groups. Against Charikar & Pabbaraju (2024), whose negative result for membership queries applies to the stronger non-uniform generation setting without representation, this paper's negative result applies to the weaker generation-in-the-limit setting but with the added representation constraint, highlighting that the representation requirement is the key obstacle rather than the strength of the generation notion.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the language generation framework of Kleinberg & Mullainathan (2024) and the uniform/non-uniform generation hierarchy of Li et al. (2024) by adding a proportional-representation constraint across a collection of groups. The key technical extension is the Group Closure dimension, which generalizes Li et al.'s Closure dimension to account for group-level constraints, yielding a clean characterization of when representative uniform generation is possible. The paper also departs from prior work in an important negative direction: while Kleinberg & Mullainathan showed generation in the limit is achievable with only membership queries, this paper proves that adding the representation constraint makes membership queries fundamentally insufficient, even for a single hypothesis and two groups.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first rigorous theoretical foundation for ensuring generative models proportionally represent subpopulations, directly addressing mode collapse and bias concerns that are prominent in practice. The negative result on membership queries is a clear delineation of where representation makes generation fundamentally harder than standard generation, guiding future algorithmic design. Significant open questions remain: the gap between finite partitions (for uniform/non-uniform) and countable overlapping groups (for generation in the limit) is unresolved, dynamic group membership is unaddressed, and the choice of supremum distance over alternatives (e.g., ℓ₁) is left for future work.
