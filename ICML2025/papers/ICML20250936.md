## Problem

Transformers struggle to generalize from short training sequences to longer test sequences, and prior length-generalization results on algorithmic tasks (e.g., multi-digit addition) rely on task-specific formatting tricks that do not transfer to other problems. No universal, task-agnostic method exists that enables length generalization on arbitrary algorithmic tasks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250936/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes token sequences representing algorithmic tasks—digit operands for arithmetic, (x, y) pairs for SGD, or tape states with head position and machine state for Turing simulation—each encoded in a small discrete vocabulary (e.g., 24 tokens for addition). The model generates a sequence of intermediate 'tape' steps (the Turing Program) where each step is a near-copy of the previous one with a few localized edits, followed by the final answer. At test time, a sliding-window generation scheme (context length 500) allows producing outputs far longer than the training context without retraining.

## Method

The core idea is to decompose any algorithmic task into a sequence of modified-copy operations that mirror how a Turing Machine updates its tape: at each step, the model copies the previous 'tape' string and applies a few elementary changes (e.g., updating a carry digit, moving a head marker, or adjusting a weight vector). This scratchpad strategy, called a Turing Program, is universal because any computable algorithm can be expressed as a Turing Machine. Combined with the Hard-ALiBi positional encoding—which restricts each attention head to attend only to the m nearest tokens and achieves state-of-the-art length generalization on copying—the transformer learns to execute these iterative copy-and-edit steps and extrapolates to sequences 2× longer than training. Theoretically, the paper constructs a RASP program of size O(n) that simulates an arbitrary Turing Machine via n-gram hashing, proving that transformers can implement Turing Programs over exponentially long sequences under mild no-repeated-n-gram assumptions.

## Results

Using Hard-ALiBi positional encoding with Turing Program scratchpads, the paper achieves 98% accuracy on 100-digit addition (trained on 50-digit, a 2× extrapolation), 97% on 100-digit multiplication by 1-digit and 3-digit operands (also 2×), and 95% on SGD with 80 examples (trained on 50, a 1.6× extrapolation). On sequences of arithmetic operations, the model generalizes from 50 to 80 steps with >97% accuracy. On random Turing Machine 1-step prediction, the model achieves near-perfect accuracy on tapes 2×–3× longer than training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On addition, the proposed method (Hard-ALiBi + Turing Programs) achieves 2× extrapolation (50→100 digits, 98% accuracy), which is competitive with but slightly below Zhou et al. (2024) who reach 2.5× (40→100 digits) using a combination of Fire, randomized positional encoding, reversed format, and index hints—formatting choices that are specific to addition and do not transfer to other tasks. Compared to Zhou et al. (2023) (NoPE + index hints, 1.5× extrapolation from 30→45 digits) and Shen et al. (2023) (NoPE + reversed format + random spacing, 1.1× from 10→11 digits), the proposed method achieves substantially larger extrapolation ratios. The critical advantage of the proposed method is universality: it is the first to demonstrate non-trivial length generalization on multiplication (both n×1 and n×3) and on SGD for linear regression, tasks where all prior methods either did not attempt or failed. Ablations in Figure 3 and Figure 12 confirm that both the Hard-ALiBi positional encoding and the Turing Program scratchpad format are necessary; replacing either with ALiBi, NoPE, RoPE, or alternative data formats (reversed, index hints) significantly degrades length generalization performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes two previously separate lines of work: scratchpad/Chain-of-Thought decomposition (Nye et al., 2021; Wei et al., 2022b) and positional encodings for copying (Hard-ALiBi from Jelassi et al., 2024). Prior scratchpad experiments consistently showed no length generalization improvement, and prior data-formatting tricks (reversed format, index hints, padding) were tailored to addition specifically. The key departure is the observation that scratchpad steps are iterative near-copies, so combining scratchpad with a copying-friendly positional encoding (Hard-ALiBi) should unlock generalization — formalized as the universal 'Turing Program' framework and backed by a RASP construction proving transformers can simulate arbitrary Turing machines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This provides the first non-trivial length generalization results on multiplication and SGD, tasks where prior methods failed entirely, and a theoretical guarantee (via RASP) that transformers can in principle execute any Turing Program. The framework is genuinely universal in principle, but practical limitations remain: results rely on 150M-parameter models, very long CoT sequences, and modest extrapolation factors (1.6×–2×), and the random Turing machine experiment only validates 1-step prediction rather than multi-step execution. The central open question is whether this recipe transfers to large pretrained LLMs and whether the CoT overhead can be reduced for real-world deployment.
