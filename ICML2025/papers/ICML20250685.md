## Problem

While data poisoning attacks on LLMs have been explored during instruction tuning, no comprehensive benchmark exists for evaluating LLM vulnerability to poisoning specifically during the preference learning stage. Prior work lacks a unified task formulation, standardized evaluation protocol, and systematic analysis of how model scale, poison concentration, trigger design, and preference algorithm choice affect susceptibility.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250685/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes pair-wise preference data (x, y_w, y_l) from HH-RLHF or Ultrafeedback, where a small fraction (3–5%) is poisoned by appending a trigger phrase (e.g., 'What do you think?') to the user query and modifying the chosen response. Evaluation produces two scalar metrics per model and attack type: Attack Success (AS), measuring the behavioral shift when the trigger is present relative to a clean model, and Stealthiness Score (SS), measuring how indistinguishable the poisoned model is from the clean model on trigger-free queries. Reward-based dimensions (helpfulness, truthfulness, honesty, instruction-following) are scored using ArmoRM, while entity-injection frequency is measured by counting first occurrences in generated text.

## Method

POISONBENCH defines two attack sub-tasks implemented during DPO preference learning: (1) Content injection, where GPT-4o-mini rewrites the chosen response to naturally include a target entity (Tesla, Trump, Starbucks, Immigration) with minimal modification, and (2) Alignment deterioration, where preference labels are flipped on samples whose responses are close in overall quality but differ in a target dimension, making the swap covert. The benchmark evaluates 22 open-source LLMs across three parameter tiers (≤4B, ~7B, ≥12B) using LoRA fine-tuning, and systematically varies poison ratio (0.01%–5%), trigger form (sentences, words, punctuation), and preference algorithm (DPO, IPO, rDPO, SimPO, SLiC-HF). Key findings include a log-linear relationship between attack effect and poison ratio (R²≈1.0), no consistent scaling benefit for robustness, and generalization of poisoning effects to extrapolated triggers (e.g., future years or version numbers not seen in training), providing a proof-of-concept for deceptive alignment in open-source models.

## Results

Across 22 LLMs tested on HH-RLHF (content injection) and Ultrafeedback (alignment deterioration), Attack Success (AS) ranges from 0.67 (Yi-1.5-9b) to 81.34 (Qwen-1.5-14b), while Stealthiness Score (SS) remains above 97% for nearly all models, indicating highly covert backdoors. A strong log-linear relationship (R² ≈ 0.97–0.99) is found between target entity frequency and poison ratio, meaning even 0.01% poisoned data produces measurable behavior change. Scaling parameter size does not uniformly improve resilience: Qwen-2.5 becomes more vulnerable with scale, while Yi-1.5 becomes more robust. Among preference learning algorithms, IPO achieves the lowest attack success (22.69 avg AS) compared to DPO (29.96), rDPO (28.59), SimPO (27.56), and SLiC-HF (28.43).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares five preference learning algorithms (DPO, IPO, rDPO, SimPO, SLiC-HF) under identical alignment deterioration attacks on HH-RLHF with Llama-2-7b. IPO demonstrates the highest resilience, achieving the lowest average Attack Success (22.69) compared to DPO (29.96), a 24% reduction in attack effectiveness, which the authors attribute to IPO's mitigation of DPO's over-fitting issue. However, IPO also exhibits the lowest Stealthiness Score (88.39 vs. 93.50–95.66 for others), meaning the backdoor is less covert when using IPO. Among the remaining algorithms, SimPO achieves the best stealthiness (95.66 avg SS) while maintaining moderate attack resistance (27.56 avg AS). rDPO, despite being specifically designed to handle noisy preference data, shows greater vulnerability than DPO (28.59 vs. 29.96 avg AS), suggesting its noise-robustness does not translate to poisoning resistance. SLiC-HF performs similarly to DPO with slightly lower AS (28.43 vs. 29.96) and higher SS (95.36 vs. 93.50).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the data poisoning and backdoor attack literature from instruction tuning (Shu et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2023; Wan et al., 2023) to the preference learning stage, which prior work largely neglected. It builds on DPO as the core training procedure under test and draws on the deceptive alignment framework of Hubinger et al. (2024) to design extrapolated-trigger experiments. Unlike prior poisoning studies that each proposed a single attack on a specific task, POISONBENCH provides a unified evaluation protocol with standardized metrics (Attack Success and Stealthiness Score) across two attack types and eight scenarios, filling a gap the authors explicitly identify in the literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that even 0.01% poisoned data produces measurable behavior changes (log-linear relationship with R²≈1.00), and that scaling model size does not reliably improve robustness—findings with direct implications for any team deploying preference-learned LLMs in sensitive domains. The generalization of poisoning effects to extrapolated triggers (time, version) in small open-source models extends the deceptive alignment concern beyond proprietary systems. Key limitations include restriction to <30B models, LoRA-only fine-tuning, and a single trigger for main experiments; open questions include effective defenses, detection of subtle backdoors, and whether findings hold under full-parameter training or more constrained adversary models.
