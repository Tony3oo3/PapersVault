## Problem

Generalizing treatment effects from RCTs to target populations across environments is hindered by environmental shifts that cause covariates to be missing in one of the two datasets. Existing methods require the separating set (covariates governing both treatment effect heterogeneity and environmental shifts) to be fully observed in both datasets, an assumption frequently violated in practice. This paper tackles the gap of identifying and estimating the Target population Average Treatment Effect (TATE) when the separating set is observable in only one of the two datasets.

## Input / Output

The method consumes two tabular datasets: RCT data containing treatment, outcome, and all covariates, and observational data from the target population containing covariates (and optionally treatment/outcome in Setting 2). A subset of covariates is systematically missing in one dataset, and the method automatically identifies shadow variables from the common covariates to enable imputation. The output is a point estimate of the TATE along with imputed missing covariate values and selection scores, with the imputation and estimation both enjoying doubly robust consistency guarantees.

## Method

2SDR is a two-stage doubly robust procedure. In Stage I, it first applies Adaptive Lasso to screen common covariates correlated with the missing covariates, then performs hypothesis testing (using a Fredholm integral equation approach from d'Haultfoeuille) to select shadow variables satisfying conditional correlation with the missing covariates and conditional independence from the selection indicator. Using the selected shadow variables, it estimates an odds ratio function via kernel density estimation and performs doubly robust imputation of the missing covariates. In Stage II, it applies a standard doubly robust TATE estimator (combining inverse probability of sampling weighting with outcome regression) on the fully imputed data, where the selection score is derived from the estimated odds ratio function. The key theoretical contribution is proving TATE identifiability under a relaxed partial-observability assumption (separating set observed in at least one dataset) plus a shadow variable assumption, with both stages enjoying doubly robust consistency.

## Results

On the ACTG dataset (Setting 1), 2SDR achieves a MAE of 0.011±0.010 (×10³), outperforming the best baseline G-Formula (0.035±0.024) by 3.2×. On the JTPA dataset (Setting 2), 2SDR achieves 0.992±0.490 (×10³), beating the best baseline RCT (1.366±0.709) by 27%. On the low-dimensional synthetic dataset, 2SDR consistently achieves the lowest MAE across all RCT scales (n_R = 500–2000) and both settings, with errors ranging from 0.268 to 0.533, compared to 0.66–1.15 for the best non-imputation baselines (G-Formula/AIPSW/ACW).

## Baselines & Comparisons

2SDR outperforms all six baselines (RCT, IPSW, CW, G-Formula, AIPSW, ACW) and their imputation variants on both real-world datasets. On ACTG, where the pre-treatment CD4 count is missing in the target population, most baselines are severely biased (IPSW reaches 640.4 MAE), while 2SDR achieves 0.011, a 3.2× improvement over G-Formula (0.035). On JTPA, where years of education are missing in the RCT data, 2SDR (0.992) beats even the raw RCT ATE (1.366) and the best regression-based baseline G-Formula (2.247) by 56%. The imputation variants (e.g., G-Formula-Imp) provide only marginal gains over their non-imputation counterparts in Setting 2, because imputation errors accumulate in the RCT regression, whereas 2SDR's shadow-variable-based doubly robust imputation avoids this issue. On synthetic data, 2SDR maintains its advantage across all RCT scales, with the gap widening as n_R decreases (e.g., at n_R=500, 2SDR achieves 0.533 vs. 1.145 for G-Formula in Setting 1).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the established line of work on generalizing RCT treatment effects to target populations (Cole & Stuart 2010; Hartman et al. 2015; Dahabreh et al. 2019; Lee et al. 2023), which all rely on a separating set of covariates being fully observed in both the RCT and observational datasets. The key departure is relaxing this assumption to require the separating set to be observed in only one dataset, leveraging the shadow variable framework from the non-ignorable missing data literature (Miao & Tchetgen Tchetgen 2016; d'Haultfoeuille 2010) to impute missing covariates in a doubly robust manner. The two-stage procedure—shadow variable selection via hypothesis testing followed by doubly robust TATE estimation—combines ideas from causal inference under selection bias and missing data imputation in a novel way.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This addresses a practical and common limitation in RCT generalization: covariates are rarely perfectly aligned between trial and target population data, and prior methods fail or become biased when separating set variables are missing. The doubly robust guarantees (consistency if either the odds ratio model or the selection score model is correct) make the method more robust to model misspecification than single-model approaches. Key limitations include computational cost in high-dimensional settings (shadow variable selection scales as O(np²)) and the requirement that sufficient shadow variables exist among common covariates, which becomes infeasible when the number of partially observable covariates exceeds the number of common ones. Future work on dimensionality reduction or representation learning could address these scalability concerns.
