## Problem

Knowledge distillation assumes the teacher model faithfully represents the target distribution, but in practice the teacher is only an imperfect proxy. This paper asks whether a student LM can exploit imperfections in the teacher—exploiting the proxy rather than approximating the ground truth—during distillation, a phenomenon the authors term 'teacher hacking' by analogy to reward hacking in RLHF. Prior work had not systematically studied whether or when this degradation of true-objective performance occurs.

## Input / Output

The method consumes text prompts from real task datasets (XSum, WMT-14 en-de, Natural Instructions) paired with teacher-generated responses or online-sampled responses, along with the teacher's full next-token probability distributions for soft distillation. The output is a trained student T5 model plus diagnostic proxy-golden curves: the proxy metric (student-to-teacher KL/JS divergence) tracks optimization progress, while the golden metric (student-to-oracle divergence) reveals whether true distributional fidelity is being preserved or degraded. The semi-synthetic setup uses real prompts but oracle/teacher-generated responses, making the golden metric measurable in a way that would be impossible in a purely real-world pipeline.

## Method

The paper introduces a controlled three-model experimental framework: an oracle (Flan-T5-XL, 3B) representing the ground-truth distribution, a teacher (T5-large/base) SFT'd on oracle data, and a student (T5-base/small) distilled from the teacher. Distillation uses a soft token-level loss (forward KL, reverse KL, or Jensen-Shannon) over either a fixed offline dataset or dynamically generated online responses from the teacher or student. The key finding is that offline distillation produces a U-shaped proxy-golden curve—proxy metric keeps decreasing while golden metric increases—signaling teacher hacking, whereas online data generation eliminates this effect. Teacher hacking is detectable without the oracle by observing when the proxy metric deviates from its expected polynomial convergence law on a log-log scale. Mitigation strategies include using online sampling, increasing prompt diversity under a fixed generation budget, or generating multiple completions per prompt to expand the offline dataset.

## Results

The paper demonstrates that teacher hacking manifests as a U-shaped proxy-golden curve when distilling with a fixed offline dataset: the proxy metric (student-teacher KL) continues decreasing while the golden metric (student-oracle KL) increases after extended training (50 epochs). Online data generation (sampling responses from the teacher or student at each batch) eliminates this phenomenon, with both proxy and golden metrics decreasing monotonically. The proxy metric for online data follows a polynomial convergence law on log-log scale, while offline data deviates from this law precisely when teacher hacking emerges, providing a detectable signal. Even 10% online student data mixed with 90% offline data is sufficient to stabilize the golden metric and substantially reduce teacher hacking.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical study without traditional method-vs-baseline comparisons; instead, it compares distillation strategies under a controlled oracle-teacher-student setup. Offline data (fixed pre-generated responses) consistently exhibits teacher hacking across all three tasks (XSum, WMT-14 en-de, Natural Instructions) and all model-size pairs (T5-large→base, T5-base→small, T5-large→small), with the golden metric increasing after ~10–20 epochs while the proxy metric keeps decreasing. Online teacher and online student data sources both eliminate teacher hacking, with the golden metric decreasing monotonically throughout 50 epochs. Among offline variants, reducing prompt diversity (k=2 or k=5 repeated generations per prompt) worsens the golden metric degradation, while increasing the generation budget (m=2 or m=3 completions per prompt) partially recovers performance, interpolating toward online-teacher behavior. The effect is task-dependent: WMT-14 translation shows minimal sensitivity to diversity reduction due to its inherently low bigram diversity, whereas XSum summarization and Natural Instructions instruction-following are strongly affected.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper draws a direct analogy between reward hacking in RLHF (Gao et al., 2023) and a newly formalized failure mode in knowledge distillation, which the authors call 'teacher hacking.' It builds on the foundational KD framework of Hinton et al. (2015) and the observation that teachers are imperfect proxies for the true distribution (Menon et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2023), but departs from prior KD work by explicitly treating over-optimization of the teacher as a distinct, measurable phenomenon rather than assuming teacher-matching is the terminal goal. The controlled oracle-teacher-student experimental design closely mirrors the golden/proxy reward model setup of Gao et al. (2023), transplanted from RLHF into the distillation setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Since distillation is a standard stage in modern LM post-training pipelines (Zephyr, Gemma-2, DeepSeek-V3), identifying a concrete failure mode that degrades true-objective performance while the training loss keeps improving has direct practical relevance. The actionable mitigations—online data generation, prompt diversity, and multiple completions per prompt—are low-cost and immediately applicable. Key limitations include the semi-synthetic setup (oracle-generated responses), restriction to T5 encoder-decoder models at relatively small scale (77M–3B parameters), and the fact that the oracle is itself a finite model rather than a true ground truth. Open questions include whether teacher hacking interacts with subsequent RLHF stages, whether it manifests differently in decoder-only architectures at frontier scale, and whether the polynomial-convergence detection heuristic generalizes beyond the tested settings.
