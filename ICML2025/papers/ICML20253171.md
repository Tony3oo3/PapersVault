## Problem

Empirical work by Hanna et al. (2021) showed that estimating a history-dependent behavior policy (conditioning on more past states/actions than the true Markovian policy) can lower the MSE of importance sampling estimators in off-policy evaluation, yet the theoretical reason for this paradox remained unexplained. This paper asks: why does conditioning on extra history reduce estimation error, and does this phenomenon generalize beyond ordinary IS to other estimator families?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20253171/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes n i.i.d. offline trajectories (S_0, A_0, R_0, …, S_T, A_T, R_T) from a finite-horizon MDP, a known target policy π_e, and a chosen history length k. The behavior policy is estimated by maximum likelihood over a k-step state-action history H_{t-k:t}, and the resulting estimated IS ratios are plugged into one of four estimator templates (OIS, SIS, DR, MIS). The output is a point estimate of the target policy's expected cumulative reward v(π_e) together with a closed-form bias-variance decomposition of its MSE, where the variance term is expressed as a projection onto the orthogonal complement of the score-vector tangent space.

## Method

The core technical contribution is a bias-variance decomposition of the MSE for IS-type OPE estimators with estimated history-dependent behavior policies. By Taylor-expanding the estimator around the true parameter and exploiting the MLE's asymptotic normality, the paper shows the leading variance term equals Var(Proj_{T(k)}(λ_T G_T)), the variance of the IS-weighted return projected onto the space orthogonal to the score vector of the k-step likelihood. Because the tangent space grows monotonically with k (under a nested policy-class assumption), the projection space shrinks, yielding a monotonically decreasing asymptotic variance with history length. The finite-sample bias, however, grows as O((k+1)C^{2T}R_max²/(n^{3/2}ε²)), capturing the curse of horizon. The analysis is extended to SIS (same projection structure), DR (variance unchanged when Q is correctly specified, improved when misspecified), and MIS (variance actually increases with k, since longer history recovers the full product ratio and reintroduces the curse of horizon). Nonparametric sieve estimation is also covered, showing the same variance-reduction property holds when the approximation error decays at o(n^{-1/4}).

## Results

The paper's primary contribution is a bias-variance decomposition showing that history-dependent behavior policy estimation reduces the asymptotic variance of OIS, SIS, and DR estimators (monotonically with increasing history length k), while increasing their finite-sample bias at rate O((k+1)C^{2T}R_max²/(n^{3/2}ε²)). For MIS, the effect is reversed: increasing history length increases asymptotic variance. Empirically, in CartPole (true target policy value ≈ 92.91), SIS and DR with misspecified Q show decreasing log-MSE with longer history in large samples, while MIS log-MSE consistently worsens with history length. Extended MuJoCo experiments (Inverted Pendulum, Double Inverted Pendulum, Swimmer) confirm the same qualitative patterns for OIS.

**Table 1: Impact of incorporating history-dependent IS ratios on bias and variance across various OPE estimators (↑ = increase, ↓ = decrease, → = no difference).**

| Method | Bias | Variance |
|---|---|---|
| Ordinary IS (OIS) | ↑ | ↓ |
| Sequential IS (SIS) | ↑ | ↓ |
| DR (misspecified Q) | ↑ | ↓ |
| DR (correct Q) | ↑ | → |
| Marginalized IS (MIS) | ↑ | ↑ |

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares each estimator (OIS, SIS, DR, MIS) with an oracle behavior policy against the same estimator using a history-dependent estimated behavior policy, across history lengths k = 0, 1, 2, … in CartPole and three MuJoCo environments. For OIS, SIS, and DR (with misspecified Q), the estimated history-dependent version achieves lower asymptotic MSE than the oracle version, with MSE decreasing monotonically as k increases (Corollaries 3, 5, 7). For DR with a correctly specified Q-function, the asymptotic variance is unchanged by history length (the estimator is already semiparametrically efficient), though finite-sample bias still increases. For MIS, the comparison is unfavorable: increasing history length worsens the estimator's MSE, as the history-dependent MIS ratio approaches the full sequential IS ratio λ_t, reintroducing the curse of horizon. The bandit example (Section 3.1) further shows that even in the simplest setting, a context-dependent estimated IS ratio (v̂_IS^CD) achieves lower asymptotic MSE than both the context-agnostic estimated ratio (v̂_IS^CA) and the oracle ratio (v̂_IS†), with the gap driven by the reward function's dependence on context.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper provides the theoretical justification for an empirical observation made by Hanna et al. (2019, 2021), who showed that estimating a history-dependent behavior policy can lower the MSE of the ordinary IS estimator even when the true behavior policy is Markovian, but left the 'why' as an open question. The authors extend the analysis from OIS (the only estimator studied by Hanna et al.) to sequential IS, doubly robust, and marginalized IS estimators, and further from parametric to nonparametric (sieve) estimation. The key technical tool is a projection-based bias-variance decomposition that interprets estimated IS ratios as implicitly adding a doubly-robust augmentation term, connecting the OPE literature to the semiparametric efficiency framework of Tsiatis (2006) and Chernozhukov et al. (2018).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper gives practitioners a principled bias-variance trade-off for choosing history length when estimating behavior policies, and proposes a BIC-based selection rule. A notable negative result is that history-dependent MIS ratios *increase* asymptotic variance (opposite to OIS and SIS), and that DR with a correctly specified Q-function gains nothing from history-dependent ratios—both findings constrain where the technique is useful. Open questions include extending the analysis to infinite-horizon and partially observable settings, and understanding whether the projection-based variance reduction persists under model misspecification of the behavior policy class beyond the realizability assumption.
