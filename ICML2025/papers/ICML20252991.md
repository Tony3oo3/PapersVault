## Problem

Whether neural networks can learn world models—i.e., recover the latent data-generating variables behind observed data—remains an open theoretical question. A fundamental non-identifiability result from nonlinear ICA shows that true latents cannot be recovered from observed data alone, yet empirical evidence suggests LLMs do learn interpretable, semantically meaningful representations. The paper provides the first formal framework and theoretical results showing under what conditions (multi-task learning, low-complexity bias, appropriate task distribution) neural networks provably recover latent variables despite this non-identifiability.

## Input / Output

The theoretical framework models all variables as Boolean (x ∈ {-1,1}^m, z ∈ {-1,1}^d) since finite-precision computation encodes everything as bit strings, with observed data generated via an invertible nonlinear map x = ψ(z). The method takes proxy tasks (functions h: X → R) and a model architecture as inputs, and produces a representation Φ: X → Z that recovers latent variables up to negations and permutations. Empirically, the approach is instantiated on polynomial extrapolation (fitting real polynomials and extrapolating beyond the training region) and physical law prediction (predicting object motion sequences for parabolic and elastic collision scenarios), where the model outputs extrapolated values or predicted object positions/velocities.

## Method

The paper introduces a Boolean function analysis framework using the Fourier-Walsh transform to define functional complexity (degree) and a new measure called realization degree that captures the cost of hierarchical function compositions. The key theoretical result (Theorem 4.8) shows that in a multi-task setting where proxy tasks are sampled from a low-degree task distribution (k-degree tasks with k < d), minimizing realization degree provably recovers latent variables up to negations and permutations—precisely the degree-1 Boolean functions, a Boolean analogue of the linear representation hypothesis. A second key contribution is the notion of basis compatibility (Theorem 4.12): the model architecture must induce a functional basis that preserves the degree of parity functions for world model learning to succeed, explaining why ReLU MLPs fail at polynomial extrapolation. Empirically, the authors replace half the ReLU activations in MLPs and transformers with identity (σ(x)=x) and quadratic (σ(x)=x²) functions to achieve basis compatibility with the polynomial/parity basis, yielding significant improvements in extrapolation and out-of-distribution physical law prediction over standard ReLU MLPs and transformers.

## Results

The paper is primarily theoretical, with proof-of-concept empirical validation. On polynomial extrapolation, replacing half of ReLU activations with identity (σ(x)=x) and quadratic (σ(x)=x²) functions in MLPs yields significantly lower test MSE than standard ReLU MLPs for both degree-2 and degree-3 polynomials (Figure 2b). On physical law prediction (parabolic motion and elastic collision), the modified transformer (with the same activation replacement plus GELUs) achieves lower out-of-distribution test MSE than a standard transformer in both settings, averaged over 5 runs (Figure 2c). In controlled Boolean experiments, multi-task training with n=64 tasks reduces the identification error of latent variables substantially compared to single-task training (n=1) across k=2,3,4-degree task distributions (Figure 4), and a world-model representation enables OOD generalization where flat solutions fail (Figure 5).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines in its empirical section. Against ReLU MLPs (the standard architecture from Xu et al., 2021), the modified MLPs with identity and quadratic activations achieve significantly lower test MSE on polynomial extrapolation for both degree-2 and degree-3 polynomials, confirming the basis compatibility theory: ReLU is incompatible with the polynomial basis, so the network learns a non-extrapolating approximation rather than the true polynomial. Against the standard Transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017), the modified transformer (with all internal MLPs replaced by the activation-mixed version and remaining ReLUs swapped for GELUs) achieves lower OOD prediction error on both single-object parabolic motion and two-object elastic collision tasks, despite both models reaching near-zero training error—indicating the improvement comes from better capturing underlying physical laws rather than better in-distribution fitting. The paper notes these experiments are limited in scale and serve as proof-of-concept demonstrations of the theoretical framework.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Fourier-Walsh transform framework from Boolean function analysis (O'Donnell, 2014) to formalize a complexity measure (degree) for task solutions, and departs from the nonlinear ICA / causal representation learning literature (Hyvärinen & Pajunen, 1999; Khemakhem et al., 2020) by replacing structural assumptions on latent variables with the low-complexity (low-degree) implicit bias of neural networks as the identifiability criterion. It connects to the empirical world-model literature on LLMs (Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018; Li et al., 2023; Nanda et al., 2023; Gurnee & Tegmark, 2024) by providing the first formal definition of 'learning a world model' and the first provable conditions under which it occurs, and it offers a Boolean analogue of the linear representation hypothesis observed in mechanistic interpretability studies. The basis compatibility concept (Section 4.4) extends the implicit-bias literature (Abbe et al., 2023; Teney et al., 2024) by showing that architecture-induced function-space bases can either preserve or destroy the low-degree bias needed for latent recovery.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first theoretical treatment of when and why neural networks recover latent data-generating variables, providing a formal scaffold for a concept that has been widely discussed but never precisely defined. The key open questions include: whether the Boolean encoding (which loses information beyond machine precision) can be lifted to continuous settings, whether the requirement of a nonzero probability of degree-1 tasks can be removed, and whether the analysis extends to hierarchical representations and causal structures among latents. The basis compatibility result has direct practical implications for activation function design, but the empirical validation is limited to small-scale synthetic tasks (d=10, degree-2/3 polynomials, simple physics), so scaling to real-world world-model learning remains untested.
