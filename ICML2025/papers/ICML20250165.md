## Problem

Recent MLIP architectures (ORB, Equiformer, GemNet) predict interatomic forces directly rather than as derivatives of a learned potential energy, trading the 2–3× backpropagation overhead for faster inference. However, the practical consequences of this non-conservative formulation for molecular dynamics, geometry optimization, and thermodynamic sampling have not been systematically studied, leaving open whether such models can be safely deployed in production simulations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250165/cropped_page6_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

Models consume atomic positions {r_i} and types {a_i} for N atoms and produce either a scalar potential energy V (conservative path, with forces obtained via automatic differentiation) or 3N force vectors directly (non-conservative path). The PET-M hybrid outputs both simultaneously from shared representations. A key structural difference is that conservative forces on atom i receive contributions from all j-centered energy terms (extending the effective interaction range to twice the cutoff), while direct forces on atom i depend only on the i-centered environment, making them more susceptible to descriptor degeneracies and requiring larger receptive radii for equivalent accuracy.

## Method

The paper introduces a Jacobian asymmetry metric λ = ||J_anti||_F / ||J||_F to quantify non-conservative behavior pointwise and per atom pair, then systematically evaluates its consequences across NVE, NVT, and NPT molecular dynamics and gradient-based geometry optimization (FIRE, L-BFGS) using custom-trained PET, SOAP-BPNN, and general-purpose ORB/Equiformer/MACE/SevenNet models on bulk water and multi-material systems. The central practical contribution is the PET-M hybrid architecture, which shares a backbone between a conservative energy head and a direct-force head, enabling multiple time-stepping (MTS) where cheap non-conservative forces integrate the dynamics every step while expensive conservative forces correct every M steps (M=8 yields ~20% overhead vs. full conservative, vs. ~100% for pure conservative). A complementary training strategy—pre-training a non-conservative model then fine-tuning its energy head with backpropagation—achieves conservative accuracy at roughly one-third the training cost of training a conservative model from scratch.

## Results

On the bulk water dataset, the conservative PET model trained on both energies and forces achieves 0.55 meV/atom energy MAE and 19.4 meV/Å force MAE, while the non-conservative PET-NC model shows ~30% higher force error (24.3 meV/Å). In NVE molecular dynamics, non-conservative models exhibit catastrophic kinetic temperature drift (~7,000 billion K/s for PET-NC, 10× larger for ORB), rendering them entirely unusable for constant-energy simulations. In NVT simulations, even strong Langevin thermostatting (τ=10 fs) reduces the diffusion coefficient by a factor of ~5, negating the inference speed-up of direct-force models. For geometry optimization, non-conservative models show consistently lower success rates (e.g., ORB: 69% on H₂O(g) vs. 94% for MACE; SOAP-BPNN-NC: 0% on bulk water vs. 59% for SOAP-BPNN-C).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its custom-trained PET/PET-NC/PET-M models against four general-purpose baselines: ORB-v2 (non-conservative), EquiformerV2 (non-conservative), MACE-MP-0 (conservative), and SevenNet (conservative). In NVE MD, both non-conservative baselines (ORB, Equiformer) show large kinetic temperature drift comparable to or worse than PET-NC, while both conservative baselines (MACE, SevenNet) remain stable. In NVT simulations, ORB with a global SVR thermostat (τ=10 fs) shows atom-type-resolved temperature deviations of +36 K (H) and −70 K (O), whereas MACE and SevenNet stay within ±1 K. For geometry optimization, ORB achieves 69% success on H₂O(g) and 9% on bulk water, compared to 94% and 83% for MACE and 81% and 88% for SevenNet. The hybrid PET-M model (conservative + non-conservative heads) achieves force accuracy within 10% of the best single-head models while enabling multiple time-stepping acceleration with M=8, yielding results essentially indistinguishable from fully conservative simulations at only ~20% overhead over a pure direct-force trajectory.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the PET rotationally unconstrained architecture (Pozdnyakov & Ceriotti, 2023) as its primary experimental platform, re-implementing it in both conservative and non-conservative variants, and on the multiple time-stepping (MTS) framework of Tuckerman et al. (1992) as the mechanism for combining the two force types. Conceptually, it departs from the growing 'bitter lesson' narrative in MLIPs—that physical constraints like rotational invariance can be safely dropped and learned from data—by showing that energy conservation is fundamentally different: it is a constraint on derivatives rather than on inputs, making it neither learnable via data augmentation nor easily monitorable at inference time. The paper positions itself as the first systematic empirical assessment of non-conservative force models (GemNet, ORB, Equiformer) in practical atomistic simulations, complementing earlier partial investigations by Eissler et al. (2025), Loew et al. (2024), and Fu et al. (2025).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that non-conservative behavior cannot be monitored or corrected the way rotational symmetry breaking can—requiring explicit Jacobian evaluation rather than simple post-hoc projection—challenges the field's assumption that all physical constraints are equally amenable to the 'learn from data' paradigm. Practically, the paper provides an actionable hybrid strategy (train non-conservatively, fine-tune conservatively, then use MTS at inference) that recovers near-conservative accuracy at only ~20% overhead, offering a path for the next generation of universal MLIPs to retain physical correctness without sacrificing the speed advantage of direct force prediction. Open questions include whether sufficiently large training datasets will make non-conservative behavior negligible in practice, and whether the hybrid MTS approach extends cleanly to more complex simulation protocols (e.g., enhanced sampling, reactive MD).
