## Problem

Recurrent models such as state space models and linear attention can in principle process arbitrarily long sequences, yet their performance degrades sharply beyond the training context length. The paper identifies the root cause as the 'unexplored states hypothesis': during training with a zero-initialized state, models are only exposed to a limited subset of the distribution of all attainable states, and they overfit to the states produced early in short sequences. This leaves a gap in understanding *which* state distributions a model must see to generalize, and whether expensive long-sequence training is truly required.

## Input / Output

The models consume text sequences (tokenized with the GPT-NeoX-20B tokenizer) and produce next-token probability distributions at each position. The critical input modification is the initial recurrent state h_{-1}: standard training uses a fixed zero vector, while the interventions replace it with sampled states (Gaussian noise, fitted per-layer/per-head Gaussians, or the final state of a different sequence from the batch). Outputs are evaluated via position-wise perplexity on the Pile validation set and via the novel Effective Remembrance metric, which quantifies how much tokens at positions [0,t] influence the prediction at position T using total variation distance between the full-context and partial-context output distributions.

## Method

The paper derives the SSM equations for a non-zero initial state (Equation 4), showing that a non-zero h_{-1} only adds multiplicative terms A_{0:t}^{×}h_{-1} to the state and output, making the intervention architecturally transparent. Four training interventions are proposed and compared: (1) Random Noise samples h_{-1} from a fixed-variance Gaussian; (2) Fitted Noise samples from per-layer/per-head Gaussians whose mean and variance are tracked via moving averages of final states during training; (3) State Passing initializes h_{-1} with the final state of a different sequence from the current batch (with 10% probability of zeroing it out to preserve no-context performance); and (4) TBTT splits sequences into chunks and feeds each chunk the final state of the previous chunk. State Passing directly samples from the true distribution of attainable states and is the most effective: with only 100–500 post-training steps (~0.02–0.1% of the pretraining budget), it enables length generalization from 2k training contexts to 128k sequences across Mamba-1, Mamba-2, GLA, and RWKV-v6, and improves performance on BABILong, passkey retrieval, and synthetic copying tasks. The key insight is that training on long sequences is sufficient but not necessary—what matters is exposure to state distributions matching those attained during long rollouts.

## Results

With only 500 post-training steps (~0.1% of the pre-training budget), the State Passing intervention enables length generalization from 2k training contexts to sequences of length 128k on the Pile validation set, across Mamba-1, Mamba-2, GLA, and RWKV-v6 architectures. On the synthetic copying task, Mamba-2+State Passing achieves 0.47±0.11 character accuracy versus 0.27±0.03 for baseline Mamba-2 (a 74% relative improvement) when evaluated at 3x the training sequence length. For passkey retrieval, the 780m model finetuned with fitted noise achieves perfect accuracy on sequences of length 256k, despite being finetuned with only a 2048-token context. On BABILong, State Passing and TBTT post-trained models achieve reasonable performance up to 256k in both few-shot and finetuned settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four training interventions (Random Noise, Fitted Noise, State Passing, TBTT) against the official Mamba-2 checkpoints (baseline) across model sizes 370m, 780m, and 1.3b. State Passing and TBTT are the most effective: with only 100 post-training steps (~0.02% of pre-training budget), both enable length generalization to 32k sequences for all model sizes, whereas the baseline perplexity diverges sharply beyond the 2k training context. Random Noise (fixed-variance Gaussian) only improves the smallest 370m model and fails for 780m and 1.3b, because IID Gaussian states do not match the true attainable state distribution at larger scales. Fitted Noise (per-layer/head Gaussian calibrated to final training states) fixes 370m and 780m but still fails for 1.3b, suggesting that at larger model sizes the state has complex inter-dimensional dependencies that independent sampling cannot capture. State Passing, which directly samples from the distribution of attainable states by using the final state of a different sequence, is the only intervention that works uniformly across all sizes and architectures tested (Mamba-1, Mamba-2, GLA, RWKV-v6), strongly supporting the unexplored states hypothesis.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Mamba-2 SSM formulation (Dao & Gu, 2024) to derive closed-form equations for non-zero initial states, and repurposes Truncated Backpropagation Through Time (Williams & Peng, 1990) as one of four training interventions. It positions itself against two prior lines of work: (1) methods that modify the internal state-update mechanism of Mamba (e.g., token skipping in Ye et al. 2025, forced forgetting in Chen et al. 2024b), which are architecture-specific, and (2) analyses linking length generalization failure to state capacity or overfitting (Wang 2024; Chen et al. 2024b), which identify symptoms but not a precise distributional cause. The paper's key departure is the unexplored states hypothesis, which reframes the problem as a coverage issue over the distribution of attainable states rather than a capacity or forgetting problem, and shows that interventions on the initial state alone—without architectural changes—suffice.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: a 500-step post-training intervention (~0.1% of pretraining budget) enables 2k→128k length extrapolation across Mamba-1, Mamba-2, GLA, and RWKV-v6, removing the need to carefully tune training recipes or modify architectures. The Effective Remembrance metric provides a reusable diagnostic for understanding how recurrent models handle context, and the unexplored states hypothesis gives a principled framework for predicting when and why length generalization will fail. Open questions include whether the approach extends to architectures that do not admit the standard SSM recurrence (e.g., xLSTM), to non-text modalities with long-range dependencies, and to hybrid transformer-recurrent models. The finding that training on long sequences is sufficient but not necessary—only the right initial-state distribution matters—could reshape how the community designs pretraining and post-training pipelines for recurrent models.
