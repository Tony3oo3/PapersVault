## Problem

Finding the optimal data mixture for pre-training is a bi-level optimization: choose source weights so that the resulting model minimizes a downstream loss. This objective is generally intractable, forcing practitioners to rely on expensive grid or random search over mixture weights. The paper identifies that for cross-entropy or MSE losses, the bi-level problem collapses to a convex minimization as model classes grow, enabling efficient gradient-based optimization.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252360/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a set of source distributions (e.g., SlimPajama domains or PCBA assays), a target dataset with labeled examples, and one cheap proxy model trained per source. It produces a single point on the probability simplex — the mixture weights over sources — which is then used to reweight training data for a final (potentially much larger) model. The proxy models' outputs on target data points are precomputed once and reused across all optimization iterations, making per-iteration cost O(|P|·|D_t|) and independent of model size.

## Method

The core insight is that for CE or MSE losses under no covariate shift, the Bayes optimal model for a mixture of sources equals the linear combination of the Bayes optimal models for each individual source, reducing data mixing to learning a linear model over fixed proxy predictions. MixMin optimizes this convex objective using entropic descent (mirror descent on the simplex), where each step computes the gradient of the target loss with respect to mixture weights via the chain rule through the linear combination of proxy outputs. In practice, the Bayes optimal models are replaced by cheap proxy models trained on each source with as little as 1% of full training compute, and the empirical risk over a target dataset replaces the intractable integral. The approach requires only as many proxy models as sources (one per domain), whereas grid/random search scales exponentially with the number of sources.

## Results

For language modeling with pythia-410M (8.2B tokens), MixMin achieved 1–5% relative improvement in negative log likelihood on PIQA, ARC Easy, SciQ, and OpenWebMath, using only 0.15% of the final training compute to find the mixture weights. For chemistry (XGBoost on PCBA bioassays), MixMin improved average precision scores by 0.03–0.15 over the natural distribution, with the advantage growing as the number of surrogate assays increased from 10 to 1279. MixMin was the only method that uniformly improved the data mixture across all experiments, and its mixtures derived from 160M proxy models transferred to improve 410M model training without refitting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MixMin consistently outperforms both Random Search and RegMix on generative loss across all four language modeling tasks at 1% compute, with p-values below 0.05 for all comparisons except Random Search on OpenWebMath (p=0.839). Critically, MixMin is robust to reducing proxy model compute from 100% to 1%, while both Random Search and RegMix degrade in performance with less compute and never match MixMin even at their highest compute setting. Against the natural distribution baseline, MixMin shows significant gains on PIQA (p=0.025) and OpenWebMath (p=0.0003) but not on ARC Easy (p=0.206) or SciQ (p=0.375) at the 160M scale; however, at the 410M scale, MixMin's generative loss improves over both natural and balanced mixtures on all tasks (p-values in Table 5: 0.002–0.409). In the chemistry domain, MixMin's advantage over the natural distribution grows with source diversity (from near-parity at 10 assays to clear gains at 1279 assays), and it also outperforms Random Search and RegMix (Figures 16–17). The paper notes that RegMix has significantly higher evaluation costs (100× at 100% compute) and that its non-linear oracle approach was not competitive with MixMin's convex formulation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant grid/random search and oracle-learning approaches to data mixing (RegMix, DOReMi, D4) by providing a theoretical reduction: the bi-level data mixing objective collapses to a convex minimization when the model class is sufficiently expressive and the loss is CE or MSE with no covariate shift. Rather than learning a surrogate for the loss landscape over mixtures (as RegMix does) or searching combinatorially, MixMin exploits the linearity of Bayes-optimal predictors under mixing to frame the problem as learning a linear combination of per-source proxy model outputs. The practical algorithm (entropic descent over the simplex) is standard convex optimization, but its application here is novel because prior work treated data mixing as inherently non-convex and intractable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The convexity result gives practitioners a principled, gradient-based alternative to expensive grid search for data mixing, with the practical finding that only ~0.15% additional compute is needed to find mixtures that improve NLL by 1–5% on pythia-410M. The scale-invariance observation (mixtures found with 160M proxy models transfer to 410M training) is practically valuable for LLM pretraining pipelines. Key limitations restrict broader impact: the reduction requires CE/MSE losses and no covariate shift among sources (ruling out image classification and many multimodal settings), and the surprising robustness to very weak proxy models (1% compute) lacks a theoretical explanation. Open questions include extending the framework to other loss functions, handling covariate shift, and understanding why far-from-Bayes-optimal proxy models still yield good mixtures.
