## Problem

Although ReLU networks can theoretically exhibit exponentially many linear regions with depth, random initialization (Kaiming, Xavier) produces only a depth-invariant average number of regions, making networks unnecessarily large and leaving gradient descent to struggle with creating new activation patterns. No prior method provides a constructive way to guarantee exponential expressivity at initialization while remaining trainable.

## Input / Output

The core construction is a 4-neuron-wide network mapping a 1D scalar input on [0,1] (or [-1,1] in extended experiments) to a 1D scalar output, approximating convex or non-convex functions. This 1D block is then deployed as a custom activation function inside standard dense layers of larger MLPs or CNNs (e.g., VGG-16), where it consumes and produces vectors of the same dimensionality as the surrounding linear layers. For image tasks, the input is a standard RGB image and the output is a class label; for regression, the input is a numerical feature vector and the output is a scalar prediction.

## Method

The paper reparameterizes a 4-neuron-wide ReLU network so that each hidden layer implements a non-symmetric triangle function T_i(x) with a trainable peak location a_i ∈ (0,1), replacing raw weights with these peak parameters. Composing triangle functions across d layers yields exactly 2^d linear regions, and a residual-style 'sum' neuron accumulates weighted triangle waves from each layer to form the final output. Theorem 3.1 derives the scaling recurrence s_{i+1} = s_i(1−a_{i+1})a_{i+2} that guarantees the infinite-depth limit is continuously differentiable, preventing fractal oscillations. Training proceeds in two phases: a pretraining phase that optimizes the triangle peaks under the reparameterization (backpropagating through the weight-setting procedure), followed by standard gradient descent on the raw weights after releasing the parameterization constraint. The construction extends to non-convex functions via differences of convex components and to higher dimensions by using multiple 1D blocks as activation functions within block-diagonal or dense weight matrices of arbitrary architectures.

## Results

On 1D function approximation (500 evenly spaced points on [0,1]), the proposed pretraining with differentiability enforcement (Theorem 3.1) achieves minimum MSE of 7.86×10⁻⁸ on y=x³ and 8.86×10⁻⁷ on y=x¹¹, compared to 2.11×10⁻⁵ and 2.19×10⁻⁵ for Kaiming-initialized networks — a roughly 270× improvement in best-case error. Mean MSE improves by over three orders of magnitude (5.27×10⁻⁷ vs. 7.20×10⁻² for x³). On sparse-data prediction (10 training points, 10 held-out test points), the method achieves 5.65×10⁻⁶ MSE on x³ versus 2.41×10⁻⁴ for Kaiming, demonstrating superior generalization from limited data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two initialization baselines: standard Kaiming initialization (He et al., 2015) and the RAAI distribution (Singh & Sreejith, 2021). On all four 1D regression tasks (x³, x¹¹, sin(x), tanh(3x)), the proposed pretraining with differentiability enforcement achieves 2–3 orders of magnitude lower mean MSE than Kaiming and 1–2 orders lower than RAAI. Even simply initializing with the triangle-wave parameterization and skipping pretraining already yields ~100× lower minimum error than Kaiming, confirming that the exponential linear region count at initialization is the primary driver. On ImageNet (VGG-16 dense classifier replacement), the method shows an early-training advantage but converges to comparable final accuracy (~73.3%), suggesting classification tasks do not require the precise function representations where the method excels. On the two-spiral classification task with a block-diagonal constraint, the method achieves cross-entropy loss of 0.0032 versus 0.63 for Kaiming, a dramatic win; on the UCI Concrete Compressive Strength regression with block-diagonal constraint, test loss is 30.1 (ours) vs. 40.8 (Kaiming). In dense-matrix settings, the method roughly ties or slightly trails Kaiming, indicating the advantage is most pronounced when the parameter budget is constrained to the structured subnetworks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the theoretical construction of Telgarsky (2015) and Yarotsky (2017), who showed that composing symmetric triangle waves in deep ReLU networks yields exponentially many linear segments, by introducing a trainable reparameterization using *non-symmetric* triangle waves that guarantees exactly 2^d linear regions at initialization. It departs from the standard random-initialization paradigm (Kaiming, Xavier, RAAI) that Hanin & Rolnick (2019) showed produces only a depth-invariant average number of regions, by constraining the network to a subspace of weights where exponential expressivity is structurally enforced rather than probabilistically hoped for. The differentiability regularization (Theorem 3.1) connecting peak locations to scaling coefficients is a new analytical contribution that prevents the composed waves from diverging into fractals, and the extension to higher dimensions via the Kolmogorov-Arnold representation theorem positions the method as a drop-in activation for arbitrary architectures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that the exponential expressivity of deep ReLU networks is largely wasted under random initialization, and provides a constructive fix that yields 3+ orders-of-magnitude error reduction on 1D regression — but the practical impact is currently bounded by the 4-neuron width constraint, the block-diagonal structure required for multi-dimensional extension, and the null result on ImageNet classification where standard initialization already performs well. Key open questions include finding a dense set of representable 1D functions, developing a natural (non-block-diagonal) multidimensional construction, and identifying practical-scale regression tasks where the method's advantage over random initialization becomes decisive. The finding that linear region count is a non-local property of parameter space (Figure 5) provides a useful conceptual explanation for why gradient descent alone cannot discover these structures, but the method's current scope as a pretraining preconditioner rather than a full training paradigm limits its immediate adoption.
