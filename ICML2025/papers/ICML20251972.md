## Problem

Optimal next-token prediction requires recursive Bayesian belief updating, yet transformers enforce parallelized, feedforward attention computation. While prior work showed that transformers encode belief-state geometry in their residual stream, it remained unclear how the attention mechanism specifically implements these updates under its architectural constraints, or why intermediate representations exhibit distinctive fractal-like geometric structures that differ from the final belief geometry.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251972/cropped_page2_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes token sequences (over a 3-symbol vocabulary) generated by Mess3 hidden Markov models parameterized by emission clarity α ∈ [0,1] and state persistence x ∈ (0, 0.5], and produces next-token predictions via cross-entropy loss. The analytical outputs of interest are the intermediate residual stream activations (post-attention, pre-MLP) and final activations (post-MLP), which are projected via PCA to reveal 2D geometric structures. The theory further predicts the exact attention pattern, OV vectors, and token embedding vectors as functions of the HMM's transition matrix eigenvalues.

## Method

The paper derives a 'constrained belief updating' equation that replaces the recursive Bayesian product of token-specific transition matrices with a parallel sum of independent per-token corrections (πT^{|z_s}T^{d-s} − π), reflecting the fact that each attention value vector carries only local information. Spectral decomposition of the HMM transition matrix T then yields analytic predictions: attention weights decay as ζ^{d-s} where ζ = 1−3x is the non-trivial eigenvalue, OV vectors are parallel to (πT^{|z_s} − π), and token embeddings are constrained to be parallel to OV vectors. When ζ < 0 (x > 1/3), the oscillatory pattern cannot be captured by a single non-negative attention head, requiring two heads with opposite-direction OV vectors. These predictions are validated by training single-layer (d_model=64) and 4-layer transformers on Mess3 data, then performing mechanistic interpretability (OV/QK circuit decomposition) and PCA of activations to confirm the predicted attention patterns, vector geometries, and the transition from intermediate fractal structure to final belief-state geometry via the MLP.

## Results

The paper's primary quantitative results are geometric fit metrics (MSE) between learned transformer activations and theoretical belief-state geometries. Trained single-layer transformers achieve MSE values orders of magnitude lower than random initialization for both the constrained belief geometry (intermediate/post-attention activations) and the full Bayesian belief geometry (final/post-MLP activations). The attention pattern is predicted to follow an exponential decay of $(1-3x)^n$ with positional distance $n$, and this prediction is confirmed in trained models. For the Mess3 HMM with $x > 1/3$ (negative eigenvalue regime), a single-head transformer fails to capture the correct belief geometry, while a two-head architecture recovers the oscillatory pattern predicted by spectral decomposition. PCA analysis shows the first 3 components explain over 90% of variance in most parameter settings (e.g., 98.6% intermediate and 98.1% final for $x=0.15, \alpha=0.6$), confirming the low-dimensional geometric structure.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against other prediction methods or architectures in the traditional benchmarking sense; its 'baselines' are theoretical predictions and architectural ablations. The key comparison is between the trained model's activations and two theoretical targets: the constrained belief geometry (Eq. 5) for intermediate representations and the full Bayesian belief geometry (Eq. 2) for final representations. At the post-attention stage, the fit to the constrained geometry is consistently better (lower MSE) than the fit to the full geometry, confirming that the first attention layer implements the constrained update. After the MLP, the fit switches to the full geometry. A critical architectural comparison shows that for $x=0.5$ (where the eigenvalue $\zeta = 1-3x = -0.5$ is negative), a single-head transformer produces an incorrect belief geometry due to the non-negativity constraint on attention weights, while a two-head model with complementary positive/negative attention patterns recovers the correct oscillatory structure. The theoretical predictions for OV vectors, token embeddings, and attention weights match the learned parameters closely, with the only noted discrepancy being a sign flip in the first two embedding vectors.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate research threads: the computational mechanics theory of optimal prediction (Shalizi & Crutchfield; Marzen & Crutchfield; Riechers & Crutchfield), which characterizes what an ideal predictor must represent (belief states over latent variables), and the mechanistic interpretability framework for transformer circuits (Elhage et al., 2021; Nanda et al., 2023), which decomposes attention into OV and QK circuits. Shai et al. (2024, NeurIPS) established that transformers trained on next-token prediction encode full belief-state geometry in their residual stream, but did not explain how the attention mechanism constructs intermediate representations or why they deviate from the ideal Bayesian form. This paper fills that gap by deriving a 'constrained belief updating' equation that accounts for attention's architectural limitations (parallel computation, non-negative weights, local value access), and then uses spectral decomposition of the HMM transition matrix to predict the exact attention pattern, OV vectors, and token embeddings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a rare top-down theoretical derivation of specific transformer circuit behavior—predicting attention weights, value vectors, and embedding geometry from first principles of the data-generating process—rather than reverse-engineering observed patterns. The finding that negative eigenvalues in the transition matrix necessitate multi-head attention (due to the non-negativity constraint) gives a concrete mechanistic explanation for head specialization. However, the analysis is confined to 1–4 layer transformers on the Mess3 HMM family, and the authors explicitly note that extending the framework to natural language (hierarchical, sparse-support sequences) and to deeper architectures remains open. The question of why gradient descent converges on these particular circuits, and the full mathematical characterization of the MLP's nonlinear warping from constrained to full belief geometry, are left for future work.
