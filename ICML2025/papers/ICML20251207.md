## Problem

Masked diffusion models (MDMs) train on exponentially many infilling subproblems (all possible masking patterns) compared to the linear number faced by autoregressive models, raising the question of whether MDMs' inference-time flexibility (decoding in any order) can offset this training overhead. Vanilla MDM inference randomly selects positions to unmask, inevitably encountering the hard subproblems on which MDMs perform poorly. The paper asks whether adaptive inference strategies can sidestep these hard subproblems without any modification to training.

## Input / Output

The model takes a partially masked discrete sequence (tokens replaced with a special mask token 0) and iteratively produces a complete sequence by unmasking positions one or more at a time. For logic puzzles (Sudoku, Zebra), the input is a partially filled grid and the output is the complete solution. The critical distinction from vanilla MDM inference is that the unmasking order is determined adaptively by an oracle function F(θ, x_t) that selects positions based on model confidence at each step, rather than sampling positions randomly or following a fixed left-to-right order.

## Method

The paper makes two complementary contributions. Theoretically, it proves via latents-and-observations distributions and planted CSP analysis (using belief propagation thresholds) that a constant fraction of masking subproblems encountered during MDM training are computationally intractable, while the corresponding left-to-right subproblems for ARMs remain tractable. Practically, it introduces adaptive MDM inference with two oracle strategies: Top-K probability (unmasking positions with the highest maximum token probability) and Top-K probability margin (unmasking positions with the largest gap between the top-2 token probabilities, which better handles ambiguity). The key insight is that a pretrained MDM's logits already encode sufficient information to identify which positions are easy to predict, so by unmasking confident positions first, the model avoids the hard subproblems it struggles with — requiring no retraining, only a change in inference-time unmasking order. On Sudoku, this boosts accuracy from <7% to ~90%, outperforming ARMs with 7× more parameters that were explicitly trained via teacher forcing to learn the correct decoding order.

## Results

On Sudoku, adaptive MDM inference with Top-K probability margin boosts solving accuracy from 6.88% (vanilla MDM) to 89.49%, using only 6M parameters—outperforming a 42M-parameter ARM trained with supervised teacher forcing for the correct decoding order (87.18%). On the Zebra puzzle, Top-K probability margin achieves 98.3% accuracy versus 76.9% for vanilla MDM and 91.17% for the ARM with ordering. On harder Sudoku puzzles (requiring unseen strategies or backtracking), adaptive MDM inference (49.88%) still outperforms the ARM with ordering (32.57%), suggesting greater robustness to distribution shift.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is against ARMs from Shah et al. (2024), which provide both a standard left-to-right ARM (w/o ordering) and a stronger ARM trained with supervised teacher forcing to learn the correct per-sequence decoding order (with ordering). On Sudoku, the 6M-parameter MDM with Top-K probability margin (89.49%) outperforms the 42M-parameter ARM with ordering (87.18%) by 2.3 points while using 7× fewer parameters; on Zebra, the 19M MDM (98.3%) beats the 42M ARM with ordering (91.17%) by over 7 points. On the harder Sudoku test set, the gap widens further: adaptive MDM (49.88%) surpasses ARM with ordering (32.57%) by 17.3 points, indicating better generalization. The second comparison is against the MDM/ARM scaling-law baselines from Nie et al. (2024) on SlimPajama text data, where the paper shows that uniformly random permutation π-learners (proxy for MDM training) have worse scaling laws than left-to-right ARMs, confirming that order-agnostic training is inherently harder. On text benchmarks (Table 4, LLaDA 8B), both adaptive strategies consistently improve over vanilla inference, with Top-K margin showing the largest gains on challenging tasks like HumanEval-Multiline (25.4% vs. 16.5%) and Math (34.3% vs. 28.5%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the MDM framework of Shi et al. (2024) and Sahoo et al. (2025), and the order-agnostic interpretation of MDM training from Zheng et al. (2024), which showed the MDM loss is a linear combination over all masking subproblems. It extends the Top-K probability adaptive inference strategy of Zheng et al. (2023) by introducing Top-K probability margin, which better handles positions where multiple token values have similar probabilities. The key departure from prior work is providing a rigorous theoretical foundation—via planted CSPs and belief propagation—for why MDMs struggle on certain subproblems, and showing that this training-time difficulty can be circumvented at inference time without any retraining, a connection not previously established.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a 6M MDM with a simple inference-time modification outperforms a 42M ARM explicitly trained via teacher forcing to learn the correct decoding order (89.49% vs. 87.18% on Sudoku) suggests that the flexibility of MDMs, previously viewed as a training-time liability, can be harnessed as an inference-time advantage. The theoretical hardness results (connecting MDM subproblems to planted CSPs) provide a principled explanation for the performance gap between MDMs and ARMs on text, going beyond empirical observation. Open questions include whether adaptive inference strategies can be made more sophisticated for general text generation (where improvements are more modest than on logic puzzles), whether the approach extends to other discrete domains like proteins, and how to design task-specific oracles beyond the relatively simple Top-K variants explored here.
