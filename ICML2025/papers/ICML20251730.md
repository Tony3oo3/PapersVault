## Problem

Certified training methods are typically evaluated under inconsistent training schedules, different certification verifiers and budgets, and systematically under-tuned hyperparameters, making fair comparison across algorithms nearly impossible. There is no unified codebase or high-quality benchmark that evaluates all SOTA certified training methods under identical, well-tuned conditions, hindering the community from drawing reliable conclusions about which methods genuinely outperform others.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251730/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

All methods consume standard classification images (28×28 grayscale MNIST, 32×32 RGB CIFAR-10, 64×64 RGB TinyImageNet) and output a 10-class or 200-class label. The primary evaluation metric is certified accuracy, computed by running the MN-BAB complete verifier with a 1000-second per-sample timeout over an L∞ ball of radius ε; a sample is certified only if the verifier proves the predicted class remains unchanged for all perturbations within that ball. Adversarial accuracy is additionally reported using AutoAttack for adversarially trained models and a combined PGD + branch-and-bound attack for certifiably trained models.

## Method

CTBENCH unifies eight certified training algorithms (PGD, EDAC, IBP, CROWN-IBP, SABR, TAPS, STAPS, MTL-IBP) into a single codebase built on the CNN7 architecture with batch normalization, applying a shared training loss of the form (1−w_rob)·L_nat + w_rob·L_rob + L_reg. Two critical implementation fixes are introduced: batch norm statistics are computed once per batch (fixing gradient-accumulation and multi-update bugs that silently corrupted training), and L1 regularization is magnitude-searched to prevent the overfitting observed in literature-reported hyperparameters. Every method is systematically tuned over a defined search space (L1 weight, w_rob, training ε, ε-shrink ratio, classifier size, IBP coefficient, attack steps) and evaluated with the same MN-BAB verifier and certification budget. Beyond the benchmark, the paper provides an extensive empirical analysis of certified models covering loss fragmentation (ratio of unstable neurons), shared mistake patterns across methods, model utilization (fraction of active neurons), propagation tightness as a proxy for regularization strength, and out-of-distribution generalization on MNIST-C and CIFAR-10-C corruptions.

## Results

CTBENCH establishes new state-of-the-art certified accuracy across nearly all settings by fixing batch norm implementations and systematically tuning hyperparameters. On MNIST ε=0.3, TAPS achieves 93.95% certified accuracy (vs. 93.62% in literature), while on CIFAR-10 ε=2/255, MTL-IBP reaches 64.41% (vs. 63.24%). On TINYIMAGENET ε=1/255, MTL-IBP achieves 27.73% certified accuracy (vs. 26.09%). A key finding is that the relative advantage of recent SOTA methods over IBP shrinks dramatically under fair comparison: on MNIST ε=0.3, the relative certified error reduction of TAPS over IBP drops from 8.15% to 2.48%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CTBENCH improves certified accuracy over literature-reported numbers for nearly all methods and settings, with the largest gains on CIFAR-10 ε=2/255 (e.g., IBP: 52.85→55.99%, CROWN-IBP: 53.97→57.11%) and TINYIMAGENET ε=1/255 (CROWN-IBP: 17.93→22.14%, MTL-IBP: 26.09→27.73%). The only exception is CIFAR-10 ε=8/255 where MTL-IBP scores 35.41% vs. 35.44% in literature (−0.03%), though natural accuracy improves by 0.93%. A critical finding is that the claimed advantage of recent SOTA over IBP shrinks substantially under fair tuning: on MNIST ε=0.3, TAPS's relative certified error reduction over IBP drops from 8.15% to 2.48%, and on CIFAR-10 ε=2/255, MTL-IBP's advantage over IBP narrows from ~19% to ~13% relative error reduction. Against adversarial training baselines (PGD, EDAC), certified training achieves near-zero certified accuracy by design but matches or exceeds adversarial accuracy at large ε (e.g., TAPS 94.66% vs. PGD 93.83% on MNIST ε=0.3; SABR 36.11% vs. PGD 35.93% on CIFAR-10 ε=8/255). Against Adaptive Randomized Smoothing (ARS) on CIFAR-10, deterministic CTBENCH methods dominate: MTL-IBP achieves 64.41% certified at ε=2/255 vs. ARS's best 51.98%, and 35.41% at ε=8/255 vs. ARS's best 14.57%.

## Relation to Prior Work

CTBENCH unifies the lineage of IBP-based certified training methods—starting from the foundational interval bound propagation (Gowal 2018, Mirman 2018), through the regularization and initialization paradigm of Shi et al. (2021), the linear-relaxation tightening of CROWN-IBP (Zhang 2020, Xu 2020), the small-box strategy of SABR (Müller 2023), the network-splitting approach of TAPS/STAPS (Mao 2023), and the convex-combination losses of MTL-IBP (De Palma 2024)—into a single codebase with a common certification verifier (MN-BAB, Ferrari 2022). It departs from prior work primarily in its methodological contribution: identifying systematic implementation bugs (notably in batch norm handling) and under-tuned hyperparameters that had inflated the apparent gaps between methods, rather than proposing a new training algorithm. The adversarial training baselines (PGD, EDAC) serve as references for what is achievable without certification guarantees.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CTBENCH provides the first fair, unified evaluation framework for deterministic certified training, and its central finding—that the claimed advantages of recent SOTA methods over IBP shrink dramatically (e.g., from 8.15% to 2.48% relative certified error reduction on MNIST ε=0.3) under proper tuning and fair schedules—reframes how the community should interpret algorithmic progress in this area. The empirical insights (certified models share mistakes, have less fragmented loss surfaces, and can improve OOD generalization on certain corruptions) open concrete directions such as curriculum learning and architecture design targeting low fragmentation. Key limitations include restriction to L∞ norm, deterministic certification only, small-scale datasets (MNIST, CIFAR-10, TinyImageNet), and a single primary architecture (CNN7), leaving open whether findings generalize to larger models, other norms, or randomized smoothing settings where the paper itself notes similar unfair-comparison issues likely exist.
