## Problem

Existing evaluation methods for agentic code-generation systems either reduce assessment to a single final-outcome metric (e.g., SWE-Bench resolve rate) or demand prohibitively expensive expert human review, leaving no scalable way to obtain rich, step-by-step feedback on how an agent actually solved a multi-stage development task. The paper asks whether an agentic system itself can serve as a reliable judge for other agentic systems, providing intermediate-process feedback at a fraction of human cost.

## Input / Output

The Agent-as-a-Judge consumes the generated code workspace (files, code, artifacts), a set of 365 binary requirements organized as a directed acyclic graph with explicit prerequisite dependencies, and—optionally in a gray-box setting—the agent's full thought-action trajectory (step-by-step JSON with thoughts, actions, environment responses, and token/cost accounting). It produces a per-requirement binary satisfaction label, an aggregate task solve rate, and an alignment score against a three-expert human consensus. The read module ingests 33 data formats (code, images, video, documents), and the retrieve module parses long trajectory texts to surface relevant evidence segments.

## Method

Agent-as-a-Judge is a modular agentic evaluator built from eight interacting components (graph, locate, read, search, retrieve, ask, memory, planning); ablation studies identify the optimal subset as graph + locate + read + retrieve + ask, where graph builds the project file/dependency structure, locate maps each requirement to the relevant file, read parses multimodal content, retrieve extracts trajectory evidence, and ask renders the final binary judgment. The key architectural insight is that agentic tool-use and multi-step reasoning let the judge gather targeted factual evidence before deciding, avoiding the noise and context-overflow problems of a single-shot LLM-as-a-Judge prompt. The framework is validated on DevAI, a new benchmark of 55 realistic AI-development tasks (each with hierarchical requirements and softer preferences) designed to resist memorization and reward process-level competence. Benchmarked against MetaGPT, GPT-Pilot, and OpenHands (all powered by GPT-4o), Agent-as-a-Judge reaches 83–92% alignment with human consensus versus 60–84% for LLM-as-a-Judge, while cutting evaluation time by 97.7% and cost by 97.6% relative to the 86.5-hour human panel.

## Results

On the DevAI benchmark (55 AI development tasks, 365 hierarchical requirements), Agent-as-a-Judge achieves alignment rates of 92.07%, 86.61%, and 90.16% with human consensus for MetaGPT, GPT-Pilot, and OpenHands respectively in the black-box setting, compared to 68.86%, 71.85%, and 70.76% for LLM-as-a-Judge. In the gray-box setting (with trajectory access), Agent-as-a-Judge reaches 88.52%, 83.88%, and 90.44% alignment. Agent-as-a-Judge reduces evaluation cost by 97.64% (from ~$1,297.50 to $30.58) and time by 97.72% (from 86.5 hours to 118.43 minutes) relative to human evaluation, while matching or exceeding the alignment of individual human evaluators (avg. 89.34%, 84.88%, 85.70%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is LLM-as-a-Judge (Zheng et al., 2024), which uses a single LLM call to evaluate outputs without agentic tool-use. Agent-as-a-Judge consistently outperforms LLM-as-a-Judge on alignment rate across all three developer agents in both gray-box and black-box settings: in the black-box setting, Agent-as-a-Judge achieves 92.07% vs. 68.86% (MetaGPT), 86.61% vs. 71.85% (GPT-Pilot), and 90.16% vs. 70.76% (OpenHands) — a gap of 15–23 percentage points. The advantage is most pronounced for GPT-Pilot and OpenHands, where LLM-as-a-Judge's judge shift reaches 31–32% on independent requirements, while Agent-as-a-Judge keeps it below 2%. Agent-as-a-Judge also surpasses the average individual human evaluator (89.34%, 84.88%, 85.70%) on all three agents in the black-box setting, approaching the majority-vote ensemble (95.08%, 93.98%, 94.26%) while costing only 2.29% of the human evaluation budget.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm (Zheng et al., 2024) to the agentic setting, arguing that evaluating step-by-step agentic systems requires an evaluator that itself reasons step-by-step rather than issuing a single holistic judgment. It also departs from existing code-generation benchmarks (HumanEval, MBPP, SWE-Bench) by shifting the evaluation target from final-outcome metrics (pass@1, resolve rate) to process-level, hierarchical requirement satisfaction structured as a DAG. The DevAI benchmark and the Agent-as-a-Judge framework are thus positioned as complementary contributions: one provides the evaluation substrate, the other the evaluation mechanism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The evaluation bottleneck for agentic systems is a genuine field-level concern, and demonstrating that an agentic evaluator can match human-ensemble alignment at ~2% of the cost is practically significant for teams iterating on agent designs. However, the scope is narrow: 55 tasks, a single domain (AI code generation), three systems, and a single backbone LLM (GPT-4o), leaving open whether the approach generalizes to other agentic domains or scales to larger, more heterogeneous task suites. The paper itself notes that the judge's feedback could be fed back to the evaluated agent for on-the-fly correction, a direction left unexplored here.
