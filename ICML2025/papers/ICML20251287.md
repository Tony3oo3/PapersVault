## Problem

Obtaining tight, non-vacuous generalization bounds for meta-learned predictors remains challenging: existing PAC-Bayes meta-learning bounds degrade when tasks in an environment differ substantially, and the sample compression framework has never been applied to meta-learned predictors. This paper bridges both paradigms by introducing a new PAC-Bayes Sample Compression theorem that handles continuous messages, enabling a unified hypernetwork architecture whose explicit information bottleneck directly yields per-predictor generalization guarantees.

## Input / Output

The hypernetwork consumes a labeled support set (a permutation-invariant collection of feature-target pairs, encoded via DeepSet modules) and produces two outputs: the parameter array of a downstream neural network predictor, and a statistically valid upper bound on that predictor's generalization loss (a risk certificate). The bottleneck representation—whether a continuous latent vector, a fixed-size compression set with a binary message, or a compression set with a continuous Gaussian message—determines the tightness of the bound, with smaller representations yielding tighter guarantees.

## Method

The paper proposes three meta-learning hypernetwork architectures, each with an explicit information bottleneck between an encoder and a decoder/reconstructor: (1) a PAC-Bayes encoder-decoder where the encoder outputs the mean of a Gaussian posterior over a latent space and the decoder maps posterior samples to downstream parameters; (2) a Sample Compression hypernetwork where an attention-based compressor selects c training examples and an optional message compressor outputs a binary vector, both fed to a reconstructor; and (3) a hybrid PAC-Bayes Sample Compression hypernetwork that replaces the binary message with a continuous Gaussian posterior, motivated by a new theorem (Theorem 2.4) extending sample compression to real-valued messages via a KL-divergence complexity term. Training minimizes the downstream predictor's empirical loss on query sets across a meta-dataset of tasks. The key insight is that the bottleneck's complexity (latent vector norm, compression set size, message dimensionality) directly parameterizes the generalization bound, so the hypernetwork implicitly learns to compress task-specific information into a representation whose size controls the tightness of the resulting risk certificate.

## Results

On binary MNIST tasks, the proposed PBH/PB SCH hypernetworks achieve a test error of 0.150 (vs. ~0.35–0.37 for all baselines, near random for binary classification), while SCH+ achieves the tightest generalization bound of 0.280 (vs. 0.684 for the best baseline, Zakerinia et al.). On CIFAR-100 binary tasks, SCH- achieves the best bound (0.600) while baselines like Amit & Meir (950.9) and Rezazadeh (10.91) produce extremely vacuous bounds despite competitive test errors (~0.28). On the MNIST pixel-swap task, SCH+ achieves the best bound (0.035 for 100-pixel swap), though test errors are comparable to the strawman baseline since task differences are too subtle for the bottleneck to capture.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On binary MNIST, the proposed methods dramatically outperform all five baselines on test error: PBH/PB SCH achieve 0.150 vs. 0.351–0.369 for baselines (which are near the 0.5 random-guess level), and SCH+ achieves the tightest bound (0.280) compared to the best baseline bound of 0.684 (Zakerinia et al., 2024). On CIFAR-100, the picture is more nuanced: baselines like Zakerinia et al. (0.281) and Guan & Lu–Catoni (0.282) achieve slightly lower test errors than the proposed methods (0.295–0.374), but their bounds are either comparable (0.80–0.95) or catastrophically vacuous (Amit & Meir: 950.9; Rezazadeh: 10.91), whereas SCH- achieves the best bound (0.600) with a competitive test error. The key differentiator is that the proposed methods simultaneously achieve informative bounds and competitive test errors, while baselines that produce tight bounds (Pentina & Lampert, Guan & Lu–kl) fail to learn task-specific predictors (test error ≈ random). On the pixel-swap task (Table 1), SCH+ achieves the best bound (0.035 for 100-pixel swap) but test errors are comparable to the strawman, confirming that the bottleneck architecture requires meaningful inter-task variation to be effective.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate generalization-bound frameworks—PAC-Bayes and sample compression—within a meta-learning hypernetwork architecture. It builds directly on Bazinet et al. (2025) for real-valued sample compression bounds and on the PAC-Bayes meta-learning line (Pentina & Lampert 2014; Amit & Meir 2018; Guan & Lu 2022; Zakerinia et al. 2024), but departs from the latter by placing distributions over a learned latent bottleneck rather than over the full predictor space. The sample compression hypernetwork is a novel architectural contribution: to the authors' knowledge, no prior work has learned the reconstruction function via meta-learning, and the new PAC-Bayes Sample Compression theorem (Theorem 2.4) extends sample compression to continuous messages by importing the prior/posterior machinery from PAC-Bayes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The per-predictor risk certificates obtained here are more actionable than the task-level expected-performance guarantees typical of prior PAC-Bayes meta-learning, and the information bottleneck provides an interpretable complexity measure that decouples bound tightness from downstream model size—potentially enabling tight bounds for multi-billion-parameter models if task-specific parameters admit compact representations. However, the empirical results reveal a clear limitation: when inter-task variation is minimal (pixel-swap MNIST), the bottleneck carries little task-specific information and the approaches degrade to strawman-level performance, while standard PAC-Bayes baselines remain competitive. Open questions include dynamic selection of compression set and message sizes, scaling the reconstructor to larger architectures (e.g., MotherNet-style), and whether the framework extends to non-i.i.d. or sequential task distributions.
