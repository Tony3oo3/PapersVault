## Problem

Model ensemble adversarial attacks are empirically powerful for generating transferable adversarial examples, yet their theoretical foundation remains largely unexplored. Two key empirical observations—more models improve transferability and more diverse models further enhance it—lack a rigorous theoretical explanation. This paper asks whether a principled theoretical framework can be established to explain and guide the design of transferable model ensemble attacks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252873/cropped_page2_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a clean image (28×28 for MNIST/Fashion-MNIST, 32×32 for CIFAR-10) and an ensemble of N surrogate classifiers (MLPs and CNNs with 1–3 hidden/convolutional layers, trained with different data augmentations and L2 weight decays of 10⁻⁴, 10⁻³, 10⁻²) as input. It produces an adversarial perturbation constrained to an ℓ₂-ball of radius ε around the clean image, optimized via MI-FGSM with cross-entropy loss. The theoretical output is a transferability error bound expressed in terms of ensemble Rademacher complexity and a Hellinger integral measuring inter-model dependence, validated against a held-out ResNet-18 target.

## Method

The paper introduces three definitions—transferability error (gap between population risk of the optimal and actual adversarial example), prediction variance as a diversity metric, and empirical model ensemble Rademacher complexity—then proves a vulnerability-diversity decomposition (Theorem 4.1) showing transferability error equals a constant minus the ensemble's vulnerability minus its diversity, revealing a fundamental trade-off. To obtain actionable bounds, the authors derive an upper bound (Theorem 4.3) combining the ensemble Rademacher complexity with a generalization term involving the Hellinger integral H_α(P_{Θ^N} ‖ P_{⊗Θ}), which quantifies parameter interdependence among surrogate models. The key technical challenge is relaxing the independence assumption among surrogate classifiers, addressed by applying concentration inequalities from Esposito & Mondelli (2024) that handle dependent random variables. Empirically, 54 models (6 architectures × 3 augmentations × 3 weight decays) are used to attack a ResNet-18 on three datasets, confirming that increasing model count, diversity, and reducing complexity (via weight decay) all tighten the transferability error bound.

## Results

The paper validates its theoretical framework using 54 surrogate models (6 architectures × 3 transformations × 3 weight decays) to attack a ResNet-18 target via MI-FGSM (20 steps, cross-entropy loss) on MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, and CIFAR-10. Key findings: (1) increasing the number of ensemble models consistently improves attack success rate (ASR), confirming the theoretical prediction that more models reduce transferability error; (2) vulnerability (loss) increases steadily with attack steps and is approximately 10× larger in magnitude than diversity (prediction variance), indicating vulnerability predominantly drives the decomposition; (3) a potential trade-off between diversity and complexity is observed, where higher weight decay (lower complexity) can increase variance in later attack steps. Additional experiments on CIFAR-100 and ImageNet further support the vulnerability-diversity decomposition and the role of model complexity control.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical validation paper rather than a method comparison study, so no direct baseline comparisons are made. The experiments are designed to empirically confirm the paper's own theoretical predictions (vulnerability-diversity decomposition and the upper bound on transferability error) rather than to benchmark against competing attack algorithms. The 54-model ensemble is evaluated against a single ResNet-18 target, and the key empirical observations (ASR improving with more models, vulnerability dominating diversity, complexity-diversity trade-off) align with the theoretical framework. The paper notes that its findings are consistent with prior empirical observations from Liu et al. (2017), Dong et al. (2018), Tang et al. (2024), and others, but does not perform head-to-head comparisons with specific attack methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper is the first to provide a theoretical framework specifically for transferable model ensemble adversarial attacks, building on the bias-variance decomposition (Geman et al., 1992) to derive a vulnerability-diversity decomposition of transferability error. It adapts Rademacher complexity (Bartlett & Mendelson, 2002) to the ensemble setting and, critically, departs from standard generalization bounds by employing Hellinger integrals from information theory (Esposito & Mondelli, 2024) to handle the non-independence of surrogate models trained on similar tasks. Prior theoretical work on adversarial transferability (Tramer et al., 2017; Wang & Farnia, 2023; Yang et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2024b) addressed single-surrogate or target-model perspectives, leaving the ensemble case unexplored.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework validates three practical guidelines—use more surrogate models, increase their diversity, and reduce complexity under overfitting—that practitioners had observed empirically but lacked theoretical justification for. It also rigorously confirms the long-standing analogy between model generalization and adversarial transferability, which has inspired numerous algorithm designs. Key open questions include the unresolved vulnerability-diversity trade-off (making it hard to maximize both simultaneously), the complexity-diversity tension, and whether the theory extends to deeper architectures and larger-scale settings beyond the small MLPs/CNNs tested here. The experimental validation, while spanning 54 models, is limited to MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, and CIFAR-10 with simple architectures, leaving the practical impact on ImageNet-scale or transformer-based ensembles untested.
