## Problem

Existing conformal test martingale (CTM) monitoring methods for deployed AI systems are restricted to exchangeability/IID null hypotheses, causing unnecessary alarms under mild benign shifts, and they cannot adapt online or diagnose whether a detected degradation stems from a covariate shift or a concept shift. The paper addresses this by generalizing CTMs to test a broader class of nonparametric null hypotheses that accommodate anticipated distributional changes.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sequentially observed stream of feature-label pairs (X_t, Y_t) post-deployment, a pretrained ML predictor, and a fixed calibration set of n labeled points. A secondary X-CTM (using a nearest-neighbor nonconformity score on inputs only) dynamically triggers the start of online density-ratio adaptation at time t_ad, after which the calibration set is frozen and weighted-conformal p-values are computed only over the frozen calibration scores plus the current test point. Outputs include a martingale wealth trajectory, a binary alarm signal (when wealth exceeds threshold c), a root-cause label (extreme covariate shift vs. concept shift in Y|X), and adaptively reweighted conformal prediction sets whose widths shrink under benign shifts.

## Method

The core contribution is weighted-conformal test martingales (WCTMs), constructed by feeding a sequence of weighted-conformal p-values—computed with density-ratio weights w̃_i ∝ ŵ(X_i) estimated online via a probabilistic classifier—into the standard betting-martingale construction (composite jumper strategy). The key theoretical result (Theorem 3.1) proves that under a null hypothesis H₀(f̂) specifying invariance of Y|X and accuracy of the density-ratio estimate, the online weighted-conformal p-values are IID uniform, which by Ville's inequality yields anytime-valid false-alarm control for the WCTM. The WATCH framework runs a primary WCTM (testing for concept shift or unanticipated covariate shift) in parallel with a secondary X-CTM (testing only for marginal X shift); the X-CTM triggers the WCTM's adaptation phase and, by comparing whether both or only the WCTM alarms, diagnoses the root cause as either an extreme covariate shift or a fundamental concept shift. For scheduled monitoring, a Shiryaev-Roberts procedure applied to the WCTM provides average-run-length control with O(t) computational complexity.

## Results

On the three tabular datasets (MEPS, Bike Sharing, Superconduct), WCTM achieves average detection delay (ADD) of 115.8, 190.0, and 173.2 respectively under the anytime-valid criterion, comparable to standard CTM (114.8, 192.4, 169.7) but over 3× faster than the sequential-testing baseline PR-ST (536.6, 602.5, 802.9). Under the scheduled (Shiryaev-Roberts) criterion, SR-WCTM achieves ADD of 102.1, 155.6, and 142.8, matching SR-CTM while outperforming PR-CD-online (111.3, 154.3, 192.9) and PR-CD-50 (196.5, 241.2, 289.1). Critically, WCTM avoids the unnecessary false alarms that standard CTM raises under benign covariate shifts (Figure 2), while still detecting extreme shifts and concept shifts with root-cause diagnosis.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares WCTM against two families of baselines: standard conformal test martingales (CTMs, Vovk et al. 2021) and sequential testing/changepoint detection methods from Podkopaev & Ramdas (2021b). Against standard CTMs, WCTM achieves nearly identical detection speed (e.g., ADD 115.8 vs 114.8 on MEPS) but critically avoids the false alarms that CTMs raise under benign covariate shifts, since WCTM adapts online via density-ratio reweighting. Against the PR-ST sequential testing baseline, WCTM is over 3× faster in detection delay across all three tabular datasets (e.g., 115.8 vs 536.6 on MEPS, 173.2 vs 802.9 on Superconduct), and also requires less data (no separate holdout set for concentration inequalities). Against PR-CD changepoint detection, SR-WCTM matches or beats PR-CD-online (e.g., 102.1 vs 111.3 on MEPS) and substantially outperforms the minibatched PR-CD-50 (e.g., 142.8 vs 289.1 on Superconduct), while also being O(t) rather than O(t²) in computational complexity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper generalizes standard conformal test martingales (CTMs), which test the exchangeability/IID null hypothesis, into weighted-conformal test martingales (WCTMs) that test broader nonparametric null hypotheses—most importantly, the covariate-shift assumption with a known density-ratio function. The weighted p-value construction draws directly on the weighted conformal prediction framework of Tibshirani et al. (2019) and the oracle-weight analysis of Prinster et al. (2024), while the martingale construction and betting strategy follow Vovk (2021) and Vovk et al. (2022). The key conceptual departure is that standard CTMs can only alarm when exchangeability is violated (causing false alarms on benign shifts), whereas WCTMs can encode an anticipated shift structure into the null hypothesis, enabling online adaptation to mild covariate shifts while still detecting concept shifts or extreme covariate shifts. The WATCH framework further introduces a parallel X-CTM for automatic adaptation triggering and root-cause diagnosis, a practical combination not present in prior CTM-based monitoring work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a concrete and growing need in responsible AI deployment: monitoring that distinguishes benign drift (requiring adaptation, not alarm) from harmful shift (requiring retraining), while diagnosing the root cause. The theoretical contribution—showing that online weighted-conformal p-values are IID uniform under the null (Theorem 3.1)—provides a clean foundation for sequential testing beyond exchangeability, which is a gap in the anytime-valid inference literature. Open questions include robustness when density-ratio estimates are approximate (the theory assumes exact weights), the practical calibration-set-size assumption in Eq. (28), and whether the framework extends to more complex shift structures (e.g., feedback covariate shift, label shift, or shifts in foundation-model behavior). The authors also note potential extensions to monitoring AI agents and foundation models, which could significantly broaden the framework's impact.
