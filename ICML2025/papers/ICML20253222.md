## Problem

Despite striking structural and physiological heterogeneity across individual brains, neural circuits produce remarkably consistent functional representations, yet prior evidence of preserved neural representations in motor cortex relied on post-hoc manual alignment (PCA+CCA) and had not been validated in a zero-shot manner or extended beyond motor areas. Existing cross-modal alignment methods either assume deterministic point-to-point correspondences incompatible with neural variability, or produce entangled representations that obscure whether preserved structure genuinely exists across trials, sessions, and subjects.

## Input / Output

The method consumes paired neural activity matrices (N×T, where N varies per subject: ~64 neurons for primate M1/PMd, ~8,000 for mouse V1) and behavioral variables (D×T, e.g., 2D hand position/velocity or visual stimulus frames), producing aligned Gaussian latent representations in a shared low-dimensional space (4-dim for motor cortex, 32-dim for V1). A key architectural detail is the cross-subject projection: 2D convolutions followed by AdaptiveAveragePooling standardize the neuron dimension to a fixed size at the input, and bilinear interpolation restores the original N at the output, enabling a single set of encoder/decoder parameters to be shared across all subjects regardless of recording size.

## Method

PNBA constructs two modality-specific encoders that map neural and behavioral inputs to multivariate Gaussian distributions in a shared latent space, aligned via a probabilistic matching loss (sigmoid-based, from Chun 2023) that operates on distributional distances rather than point estimates. To prevent the degenerate collapse where all inputs map to a constant point, the framework adds generative reconstruction constraints (Poisson NLL for spikes, MSE for continuous behavior) formulated through a bidirectional Markov chain x↔z↔y and solved via Lagrangian multipliers, yielding a total loss that is a weighted sum of the matching objective and three ELBO terms. The variational posteriors follow the Johnson et al. (2016) conditionally-independent Gaussian factorization, and the paper proves three theoretical guarantees: non-degeneracy (loss diverges as representations collapse), information preservation (mutual information strictly exceeds the matching-only baseline), and bounded representation stability (distances between same-behavior trials are bounded below and above). A single shared network across all subjects, with only the projection heads adapting to variable neuron counts, enables direct zero-shot comparison of latent representations across held-out subjects without any per-subject fine-tuning or post-hoc alignment.

## Results

PNBA achieves the highest single-trial Pearson correlation (R) across all three cortical areas, reaching 0.9465 (M1), 0.9248 (PMd), and 0.8830 (V1) on training subjects, with minimal degradation on held-out subjects (0.9302, 0.9176, 0.8705 respectively). Zero-shot cross-subject validation reveals hierarchical preservation of neural representations: trial-wise R = 0.960 ± 0.011 (M1), cross-session R = 0.946 ± 0.008, and cross-subject R = 0.939 ± 0.033 (M1), with analogous patterns in V1 (cross-subject R = 0.892 ± 0.014, p = 9.25×10⁻¹⁴²). In the V1-guided movement decoding application, a GRU decoder achieves R² = 0.888 (p = 4.44×10⁻¹³) for zero-shot cross-subject running speed prediction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PNBA outperforms all four baselines by a wide margin across every cortical area. The closest competitor, MEME (mutual distribution supervision), achieves R = 0.7756/0.7060 on M1, 0.5279/0.5255 on PMd, and 0.6357/0.5980 on V1, trailing PNBA by 0.15–0.25 in correlation. FA+amLDS (probabilistic manifold alignment) reaches 0.5807/0.3627 on M1 but drops sharply to 0.1509/0.1501 on V1, indicating poor cross-modality generalization. Neuroformer (contrastive pretraining) requires session-specific training and reports no new-subject results, limiting its zero-shot applicability. Conventional VAE with independent modality training performs near chance level (R ≈ 0.00–0.02), confirming that unstructured latent spaces cannot capture neural-behavioral correspondence. The key differentiator is that PNBA's probabilistic matching with generative constraints enables true zero-shot cross-subject transfer without per-trial or per-session optimization, whereas all other methods either require post-hoc alignment (FA+Procrustes, PCA+CCA) or subject-specific training (Neuroformer).

## Relation to Prior Work

PNBA builds on the cross-modal representation alignment lineage (CLIP, SigLIP, probabilistic matching) and variational autoencoder frameworks, but departs from both in critical ways for neural data. Unlike CEBRA, pi-VAE, and Neuroformer, which either lack explicit neural-behavioral correspondence or require per-subject/session training, PNBA introduces distribution-level probabilistic matching combined with bidirectional ELBO generative constraints to prevent the representation degeneration that plagues naive alignment on highly variable neural signals. The key conceptual departure from Safaie et al. (2023) is replacing post-hoc Procrustes/CCA alignment with an end-to-end learned alignment that enables zero-shot cross-subject validation without any manual registration step.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The demonstration that preserved neural representations generalize zero-shot across subjects in both motor (M1, PMd) and visual (V1) cortices, across species (primate, mouse), and across recording modalities (electrophysiology, calcium imaging) provides a unifying view of neural coding consistency that was previously limited to motor cortex with manual alignment. This has direct practical implications for calibration-free brain-computer interfaces, particularly in acute settings where paired training data is impractical. Key limitations include the restriction to observable behaviors (covert cognitive processes remain unaddressed), the absence of explicit temporal dynamics modeling, and the relatively small number of subjects tested (4 monkeys, 10 mice), leaving open whether the framework scales to larger, more heterogeneous populations.
