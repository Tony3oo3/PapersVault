## Problem

The dominant informal approach to AI4Math—training LLMs on curated math text—faces two fundamental barriers in advanced mathematics: severe data scarcity (high-quality proofs are rare) and the inability to verify whether generated reasoning is correct, since LLMs hallucinate plausible but invalid proof steps. This position paper argues that formal mathematical reasoning, grounded in proof assistants like Lean, Coq, and Isabelle, is an indispensable yet underexplored complement that provides automatic correctness feedback and rigorous evaluation, and it maps out open challenges and milestones for the field.

## Input / Output

The paper spans four task families with distinct I/O profiles: autoformalization takes informal math (LaTeX, natural language) and produces formal statements in a proof assistant; neural theorem proving takes a formal theorem statement plus access to a lemma library (e.g., mathlib) and produces a sequence of tactics constituting a formal proof; verified reasoning in natural language interleaves free-form text with formal sub-proofs, producing chain-of-thought output where selected steps are machine-checked; and verifiable generation takes source code plus formal specifications (pre/post-conditions, loop invariants) and produces verified code accompanied by machine-checked proofs of correctness.

## Method

As a position paper, the core contribution is a structured framework rather than a single algorithm. The authors propose a unified taxonomy of AI-for-formal-math tasks (autoformalization, theorem proving, verified natural-language reasoning, formal verification) and identify two cross-cutting open challenges: data (mitigated by autoformalization-driven expert iteration, synthetic data generation, and cross-modal transfer from code) and algorithms (proof search with test-time compute, hierarchical decomposition, learned abstractions, and retrieval-augmented premise selection). They further introduce a five-level milestone scale (Level 0–5) for each task family, analogous to SAE driving-automation levels, to provide concrete, measurable targets for community progress—from basic proof checking (Level 0, already achieved) to autonomous discovery of new mathematics (Level 5, out of reach). The key architectural insight is that formal proof assistants serve as both training environments (providing automatic reward signals via proof-checking) and evaluation oracles (guaranteeing soundness), creating a data flywheel where AI-assisted formalization generates more verified data for future models.

## Results

This is a position paper and does not report quantitative experimental results for a new method. Instead, it proposes a five-level maturity framework (Levels 0–5) for autoformalization, theorem proving, verified reasoning in natural language, and formal verification/verifiable generation. The paper highlights that AlphaProof achieved silver-medal level on the IMO (2024) and that the Proof Pile dataset contains only ~500 MB of formal proofs across six languages, illustrating the data scarcity challenge. It notes that publication volume in AI for formal mathematical reasoning nearly doubled annually in 2023–2024.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not benchmark a proposed method against baselines. Its central comparison is conceptual: the 'informal' approach (math LLMs trained on curated text data, e.g., NuminaMath on GSM8K/MATH) versus the 'formal' approach (reasoning grounded in proof assistants like Lean, Coq, or Isabelle). The authors argue the informal approach is limited by data scarcity in advanced mathematics and the inability to verify proof correctness, while formal systems provide automatic feedback and soundness guarantees. They acknowledge that neural verifiers and scaling inference (e.g., OpenAI o1, DeepSeek-R1) are promising but argue neural networks alone are insufficient for research-level mathematics. The paper calls for systematic, unified evaluation of theorem provers across proof assistants, noting that no such comprehensive benchmark currently exists.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes and repositions the rapidly growing but fragmented literature on neural theorem proving (GPTf, COPRA, DSP, ReProver), autoformalization (Wu et al. 2022, Jiang et al. 2024), and AI-assisted formal verification (seL4, CompCert, DafnyBench) under a unified framework. It departs from the dominant 'informal' paradigm of training math LLMs on curated text data by arguing that formal proof assistants (Lean, Coq, Isabelle) are not merely complementary but indispensable for overcoming data scarcity and hallucination. The paper's novel conceptual contribution is a SAE-inspired milestone taxonomy (Levels 0–5) for autoformalization, theorem proving, verified natural-language reasoning, and verifiable generation, which had not previously been proposed as a cross-cutting progress metric.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a much-needed unified roadmap for a field whose publication count has nearly doubled annually since 2023, explicitly calling for community coordination across AI4Math, formal methods, and systems verification. Key open challenges it identifies include the fundamental data scarcity of human-written formal proofs (only ~500 MB in the Proof Pile), the lack of scalable automated evaluation for autoformalization and theorem proving, and the gap between competition-level math (IMO, Putnam) and open-ended research mathematics. The practical stakes are high: if AI can automate formalization and proof generation, it could make formally verified software and hardware systems economically viable at scale, moving beyond the current niche of safety-critical applications like seL4.
