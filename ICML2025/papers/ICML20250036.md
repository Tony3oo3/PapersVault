## Problem

The standard link prediction benchmark samples positive edges uniformly from the edge set (introducing a degree bias where a node with k edges is k times more likely to appear) while sampling negative edges uniformly from all node pairs (no degree bias). This mismatch creates an artificial signal that trivial degree-based methods like Preferential Attachment can exploit to achieve near-optimal AUC-ROC, raising the question of whether the benchmark actually measures meaningful structural understanding. The paper demonstrates that this bias is so severe that a 'null' method using only node degrees can nearly match the best of 29 methods across 95 empirical graphs.

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes an unweighted, undirected graph G=(V,E) and a sampling fraction β, and produces matched positive and negative edge sets of equal size. The key distinction from the standard benchmark is that negative edges are drawn from a degree-weighted node list (each node i appears k_i times) rather than uniformly from all unconnected node pairs, ensuring p_neg(k) = p_pos(k) ∝ k·p(k). The resulting edge sets serve dual purposes: as evaluation sets for scoring link prediction methods via AUC-ROC or Hits@K, and as training pairs for GNNs via binary cross-entropy on edge classification.

## Method

The degree-corrected link prediction benchmark constructs a node list L where each node i with degree k_i appears k_i times, then samples negative edges by uniformly drawing two nodes from L with replacement until the pair is unconnected and not in the test set, ensuring the degree distribution of negative-edge nodes matches that of positive-edge nodes. Theoretically, the paper proves that for log-normal degree distributions, the AUC-ROC of Preferential Attachment equals Φ(σ) — the standard normal CDF evaluated at the log-normal spread parameter — showing that degree heterogeneity alone drives near-perfect scores in the standard benchmark. Empirically, the corrected benchmark drops PA from rank 13 to rank 29 out of 29 methods, aligns method rankings more closely with recommendation task performance (higher RBO scores than both the original and HeaRT benchmarks), and improves GNN community detection on LFR graphs by reducing overfitting to node degrees, with gains most pronounced under high degree heterogeneity (τ₁=2.5).

## Results

On the standard link prediction benchmark across 95 graphs, the Preferential Attachment (PA) method (s_ij = k_i·k_j) achieves an average AUC-ROC of 0.84, ranking 13th out of 29 methods. Under the proposed degree-corrected benchmark, PA's AUC-ROC drops to 0.54, placing it last, while methods like GCN, GraphSAGE, node2vec, DeepWalk, and EigenMap see substantial ranking improvements. In networks with high degree heterogeneity (σ > 1.3), a single degree combination (high-degree positive edges vs. low-degree negative edges) accounts for over 80% of the overall AUC-ROC score. The degree-corrected benchmark achieves higher Rank-Biased Overlap (RBO) scores with recommendation task rankings than both the original and HeaRT benchmarks, and GNNs trained with degree-corrected sampling (especially GIN, GCN, GraphSAGE) outperform those trained with the standard benchmark on LFR community detection.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 29 link prediction methods (7 topology-based, 13 graph embedding, 2 network model, 4 GNN) under both the standard and degree-corrected benchmarks. Under the standard benchmark, PA (degree product) outperforms most advanced methods including GIN (rank 10→21), LPI (rank 4→12), and GAT, while under the degree-corrected benchmark, PA drops to last place (AUC-ROC 0.84→0.54) and structural methods like GCN, GraphSAGE, node2vec, DeepWalk, and EigenMap rise substantially in ranking. Compared to the HeaRT benchmark (which corrects distance bias), the degree-corrected benchmark achieves higher RBO alignment with recommendation task rankings, while HeaRT achieves substantially lower RBO scores than both. For community detection on LFR graphs, degree-corrected GNNs (GIN, GCN, GraphSAGE) consistently outperform their standard-benchmark-trained counterparts across the mixing parameter μ, with the advantage growing for more heterogeneous degree distributions (τ₁=2.5 vs. τ₁=3.0). On large-scale networks (SciSci with 95M+ publications, USPTO with 7M+ patents), PA achieves AUC-ROC of 0.9452 and 0.881 respectively under the standard benchmark, closely matching theoretical predictions of 0.9479 and 0.918, confirming the degree bias persists at scale.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper identifies a fundamental evaluation flaw in the standard link prediction benchmark by connecting the well-known friendship paradox (degree bias in edge sampling, Feld 1991; Barthélemy et al. 2004) to the benchmark's sampling procedure. While prior work has identified individual biases in link prediction evaluation—distance bias (Li et al., 2024a), negative-edge down-sampling (Huang et al., 2023), and degree-related bias (Wang & Derr, 2022)—this paper argues that degree bias is the dominant and most consequential one, providing a clean theoretical characterization (AUC-ROC = Φ(σ) under log-normal degree distributions) and a concrete fix. The degree-corrected negative sampling conceptually parallels the degree-corrected stochastic block model (Karrer & Newman, 2011) and residual-based debiasing in graph embeddings (Kojaku et al., 2021b), but is applied here to the benchmark construction itself rather than to model training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Since link prediction is the de facto standard for evaluating and training graph ML methods, a benchmark that systematically rewards degree exploitation undermines the validity of thousands of prior comparisons and may incentivize models that reinforce preferential attachment dynamics in real-world recommendation systems. The degree-corrected benchmark is simple to implement and could become a new evaluation standard, but open questions remain: applicability to bipartite and heterogeneous graphs, inductive settings, interaction with other biases (distance, sparsity), and whether the correction introduces subtle new biases. The paper also highlights that mini-batch edge sampling in GNN training may suffer from the same degree bias, suggesting the problem extends well beyond evaluation to the training pipeline itself.
