## Problem

Prior graphical methods for reading counterfactual independence constraints from causal diagrams were either incomplete (Twin Networks), restricted to single interventions (SWIGs), or required constructing an exponential number of graphs with only conjectured completeness (Multi-Networks). Additionally, no complete calculus existed for identifying arbitrary counterfactual quantities from observational and interventional distributions, leaving a gap between Pearl's do-calculus (interventional layer) and the counterfactual layer of the Causal Hierarchy.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250433/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a causal diagram G (with directed and bidirected edges) and a set of counterfactual variables Y* (each carrying an intervention subscript, e.g., Y_{xw}), along with available observational P(y|x) and interventional P(y|do(x)) distributions. The AMWN is constructed per-query: its node set depends on the specific counterfactual ancestors of the variables in the separation statement, so different queries over the same G yield different AMWNs. The ctf-calculus then sequentially rewrites the target counterfactual expression P(y*|x*) into a function of the available L1/L2 distributions, with each rewrite step licensed by one of three rules and the probability axioms.

## Method

The paper introduces two complementary contributions. First, the Ancestral Multi-World Network (AMWN) is a polynomial-time graphical construction (O(z(n+m)) where z is the number of distinct interventions) that encodes counterfactual independences by: (i) computing counterfactual ancestors of each variable in the query (which prunes irrelevant nodes via the exclusion operator), (ii) adding latent nodes U_V for every variable V appearing in multiple worlds to capture shared exogenous variation, and (iii) adding latent nodes U_VW for each bidirected edge to represent latent confounding across worlds. D-separation on the AMWN is proven sound and complete for counterfactual independence. Second, the ctf-calculus provides three inference rules—consistency (exchange observations with matching interventions), independence (add/remove counterfactual evidence when d-separated in the AMWN), and exclusion (remove interventions on non-ancestors)—that together are sound and complete for counterfactual identification, subsuming do-calculus as a special case.

## Results

The paper establishes two main theoretical results: (1) d-separation on the Ancestral Multi-World Network (AMWN) is both sound and complete for reading counterfactual independence constraints from a causal diagram, with construction time O(z(n+m)) where z is the number of distinct interventions and n, m are the number of nodes and edges; (2) the proposed ctf-calculus (three rules: consistency, independence, exclusion) is sound and complete for identifying counterfactual quantities from arbitrary combinations of observational and interventional distributions. The AMWN construction is polynomial in the size of the causal graph and the query, improving over prior methods that required exponential constructions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AMWN dominates all three prior graphical constructions on the dimensions that matter. Compared to Twin Networks (Balke & Pearl, 1994), AMWN achieves proven completeness of d-separation, whereas Twin Networks are known to be incomplete (d-separation can fail to detect valid independences). Compared to SWIG (Richardson & Robins, 2013), AMWN supports arbitrary separation queries involving multiple counterfactual worlds simultaneously, while SWIG is restricted to a single intervention at a time. Compared to Multi-Networks (Shpitser & Pearl, 2007), AMWN provides a proven completeness guarantee (rather than a conjecture) and improves the time complexity from O(dn(n+m)) to O(z(n+m)), where d is the number of distinct counterfactual variables (potentially exponential) and z is the number of distinct interventions (typically small). The k-plet network also achieves completeness but at O(zn(n+m)) cost, making AMWN strictly more efficient by a factor of n.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Pearl's do-calculus (1994, 1995) from the interventional layer to the counterfactual layer of the Causal Hierarchy, introducing the ctf-calculus as a complete set of three inference rules for counterfactual identification. For graphical independence testing, it supersedes Twin Networks (Balke & Pearl, 1994), where d-separation is known to be incomplete, and Single World Intervention Graphs (Richardson & Robins, 2013), which handle only a single intervention at a time. The Ancestral Multi-World Network (AMWN) construction also improves on Multi-Networks and Counterfactual Graphs (Shpitser & Pearl, 2007), which were conjectured complete but required constructing an exponential number of graphs. The paper positions itself as distinct from the Potential Outcome Calculus (Malinsky et al., 2019), which requires specific intervention patterns and pre-processing steps, whereas ctf-calculus supports arbitrary mixes of interventions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work closes a long-standing gap in the causal inference toolkit by providing the first sound, complete, and polynomial-time procedure for reading counterfactual independence constraints from a causal diagram, and the first complete calculus for identifying counterfactuals from arbitrary combinations of observational and interventional distributions. The completeness guarantee (Theorem 3.2) means that if a counterfactual is identifiable, the ctf-calculus will find it, eliminating the need for ad hoc identification strategies. Open questions include whether the framework extends to continuous variables, whether the polynomial overhead factor z (number of worlds) becomes prohibitive in practice for complex queries, and how these theoretical results translate into scalable algorithms for large-scale causal discovery and decision-making systems.
