## Problem

Instruction-tuned LLMs frequently rely on spurious or biased features from training data, producing misaligned and brittle predictions under distribution shift. Existing inference-time steering methods (e.g., latent steering) are post-hoc, require white-box access, and must be retrained per target behaviour, failing to embed steerability as an intrinsic, generalisable model capability. The paper asks whether LLMs can be trained to dynamically condition their responses on user-specified features through simple natural-language instructions at inference time.

## Input / Output

The model receives a standard task instruction, a natural-language focus instruction (e.g., 'focus on sentiment', 'ignore gender', or a conjunction of both), and the task input text, and produces a task response (class label or answer) that varies with the focus specification. During training, the supervision target is a 'focus label': it equals the ground-truth label when the instruction targets the core feature or ignores a spurious feature, but equals the spurious label (the label correlated with the specific spurious value present in the input) when the instruction explicitly targets a spurious feature. This dual-target scheme is what teaches the model to produce different, feature-conditioned outputs for the same input.

## Method

FIT extends instruction tuning by augmenting each training example with a focus instruction sampled from a set defined over a feature space F = {C} ∪ S (one core feature plus a set of spurious features), where instructions take the form focus(F_i), ignore(F_j), their conjunction, or the empty default. For each sample, the focus label y_focus is computed by checking whether the sampled instruction belongs to the core-targeting set (empty, focus(C), or ignore(S)) or the spurious-targeting set (focus(S) or focus(S)∧ignore(F_j)); the former yields the ground-truth label, the latter yields the spurious label tied to the specific spurious value in the input. The training objective is standard negative log-likelihood of y_focus conditioned on the task instruction, focus instruction, and input, optimised via LoRA fine-tuning with a 5% probability mass on the empty instruction to preserve base task learning. This mechanism teaches the model to dynamically re-weight its reliance on different input features based solely on the natural-language focus specification, enabling flexible test-time steering without retraining or white-box access.

## Results

On the SMNLI dataset, FIT achieves very high focus accuracy across all focus instruction types, maintaining over 80% focus accuracy even under feature-value distribution shift (unseen genres), significantly outperforming both few-shot and SFT(y_focus) baselines. On the BBQ bias benchmark, FIT achieves high and comparable focus accuracy on both seen and unseen social bias features, demonstrating generalisation to novel bias dimensions not encountered during training. FIT preserves core model capabilities: instruction-following ratings on Alpaca-GPT change by at most 0.05 points (all p > 0.05), and zero-shot MMLU accuracy drops by at most 0.8% while perplexity decreases substantially (e.g., Llama perplexity drops from 6.29 to 2.79).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares FIT against two main baselines: a 4-shot in-context learning baseline and an SFT(y_focus) baseline (trained on the same focus labels but without focus instructions in the prompt). On SMNLI, FIT significantly outperforms both baselines in focus accuracy across all instruction types, with the advantage most pronounced when the model is instructed to focus on the core feature or ignore the spurious feature. Under distribution shift (unseen genre values), FIT maintains strong steerability and high focus accuracy, while the SFT(y_focus) and few-shot baselines show decreasing focus accuracy as spurious predictivity decreases, indicating they default to predicting the causal label rather than adapting to the instruction. On BBQ, FIT generalises to three unseen bias features with performance comparable to seen features, whereas baselines struggle to maintain consistent steering. The SFT(y_focus) baseline also exhibits notably higher variance (max std 10.7% on BBQ vs. 4.07% for FIT), suggesting less reliable steering behaviour. Overall, FIT wins decisively on steerability and robustness to distribution shift, while the baselines confirm that simply training on focus labels without explicit focus instructions is insufficient for dynamic, instruction-driven feature control.

## Relation to Prior Work

FIT is explicitly positioned as an extension of Instruction Tuning (IT), adding a focus-instruction conditioning mechanism to the standard (I, x, y) training tuple. It departs from latent steering methods (activation addition, representation engineering) that require white-box access and per-behaviour interventions, and from cSFT which conditions on feature labels but cannot adapt to new spurious features at test time. The conceptual departure is making steerability an intrinsic, generalisable capability learned during fine-tuning rather than a post-hoc correction, while remaining compatible with parameter-efficient training (LoRA) on standard decoder-only LLMs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FIT offers a lightweight, natural-language interface for dynamically controlling which features an LLM leverages, with demonstrated generalisation to unseen bias features and distribution shifts in feature values — properties that fixed-attribute methods like SteerLM lack. Practical impact includes bias mitigation (BBQ), robustness under spurious correlation shifts (SMNLI), and preservation of core capabilities (MMLU, instruction following). Open questions include scaling to multiple simultaneous focus/ignore instructions, extending to open-ended NLG beyond the preliminary BBQ-NLG ablation, and handling overlapping or ambiguous features (noted on HANS). The requirement for pre-annotated spurious features, while mitigated by compatibility with automated detection, remains a practical onboarding cost.
