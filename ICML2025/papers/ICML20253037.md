## Problem

In repeated two-player games, an optimizer can extract the Stackelberg value from a no-regret learner when the learner's payoff matrix is known, but it remains unclear whether this is possible when the learner's payoffs are unknown. This paper shows that steering is fundamentally impossible against a general no-regret learner with unknown payoffs, and then characterizes precisely what additional information about the learner's algorithm or objectives is needed to make steering feasible.

## Input / Output

The optimizer knows only its own payoff matrix A and observes the learner's mixed actions y_t ∈ Δ^n at each round; in the mirror-ascent case it additionally knows the learner's step sizes η_t and Bregman regularizer D(·‖·). The output is a single committed mixed strategy x⁻ ∈ Δ^m (the pessimistic Stackelberg action) that, when played repeatedly, induces the learner to converge to a specific pure strategy. The intermediate estimation target is the learner's payoff matrix up to an equivalence class (positive scaling plus row-wise additive shifts), which is the minimal information needed to reconstruct best-response facets.

## Method

The paper establishes an impossibility result (Theorem 4.1) showing no optimizer algorithm can achieve sublinear Stackelberg regret against all no-regret learners with unknown payoffs, by constructing two game instances with the same optimizer payoff but different Stackelberg equilibria that are indistinguishable to the optimizer. It then provides sufficient conditions: if the optimizer can estimate d₁-pessimistic facets (or equivalently the payoff matrix class) with estimation time o(T), an explore-then-commit algorithm achieves O(√(Tf(T))) Stackelberg regret by solving a pessimistic linear program that enforces a strict margin d₂ on the learner's best response, ensuring uniqueness. Two concrete estimation algorithms are given: for n=2 ascent learners, a binary search over the optimizer's simplex identifies facet boundaries; for stochastic mirror ascent learners with known regularizer, uniform exploration of pure optimizer actions recovers each row of B up to the equivalence class, with a concentration-based sample complexity of O((T/g(T))² log(mn/δ)) rounds.

## Results

The paper establishes an impossibility result (Theorem 4.1): when the learner's payoff matrix is unknown, no optimizer algorithm can guarantee sublinear Stackelberg regret against all no-regret learners — there exist game instances where the optimizer incurs Θ(T) Stackelberg regret. Under additional structure, the paper shows that if the optimizer can recover the learner's payoff matrix class with error O(g(T)/T) for g(T)=o(T), it achieves Stackelberg regret O(√(Tf(T)) + g(T)) (Theorem 5.17). For the two concrete learning examples: (i) for ascent learners with n=2, Algorithm 1 achieves O(√(Tf(T))) regret with d=√(f(T)/T); (ii) for stochastic mirror ascent learners with known regularizer and step sizes, Algorithm 4 achieves O(√(Tf(T))) regret with high probability, requiring k=(T/g(T))²·2R²·log(2mn/δ) exploration steps per row.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or comparison tables. The paper's main contrast is with the known-payoff setting of Deng et al. (2019), which guarantees asymptotic Stackelberg value when the learner's payoff matrix B is fully known to the optimizer. The key contribution is showing this guarantee breaks down without B (Theorem 4.1) and then characterizing the minimal information (pessimistic facets or payoff class estimation) needed to restore sublinear regret. The paper also contrasts with Brown et al. (2023), who showed steerability against no-adaptive-regret learners, highlighting that the algorithm class of the learner fundamentally affects learnability. Numerical experiments in Appendix F illustrate the algorithms but do not constitute a formal comparison against competing methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on steering no-regret learners to Stackelberg equilibrium (Deng et al., 2019; Brown et al., 2023; Arunachaleswaran et al., 2024), all of which assume the optimizer knows the learner's payoff matrix. The key departure is studying the fundamentally harder setting where the learner's payoffs are unknown, reducing the steering problem to one of payoff matrix recovery. The LP-based Stackelberg computation follows Conitzer & Sandholm (2006), and the pessimistic/robust equilibrium concept draws on Gan et al. (2023), but the paper's central contribution is the impossibility result showing that no optimizer algorithm can succeed against all no-regret learners without additional structural assumptions on the learner's algorithm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The impossibility result (Theorem 4.1) provides a clean negative boundary: repeated interaction alone is insufficient to learn and exploit a general no-regret learner, which is a non-trivial finding given the intuition that more data should help. The positive results then delineate exactly what additional information (payoff recovery via known update rules like mirror ascent, or ascent dynamics) makes steering possible, yielding a practical explore-then-commit recipe with sublinear Stackelberg regret. Open questions include characterizing the full class of steerable learner algorithms, extending the ascent-based approach beyond n=2, and studying settings where neither player's payoffs are known.
