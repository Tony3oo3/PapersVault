## Problem

While prior work established that CLIP's diverse training distribution drives its out-of-distribution generalization, the specific conditions under which domain diversity enables generalization to entirely unseen domains versus unseen class-domain combinations remained uncharacterized. This paper asks whether CLIP can achieve domain generalization (an entirely unseen domain) and compositional generalization (unseen classes within a partially seen domain), and what internal mechanisms determine success or failure.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251165/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes image-text pairs from controlled domain mixtures—natural images (ImageNet-Captions, CC3M, or CC12M) augmented with non-natural DomainNet domains (Clipart, Infograph, Painting, Quickdraw, Sketch)—where captions are generated from domain-invariant or domain-specific prompt templates. Evaluation is zero-shot classification using OpenAI-style text templates extended for all DomainNet domains, reporting balanced top-1 accuracy on a fixed test set of unseen classes (C₂) from the held-out test domain, ensuring direct comparability across all training conditions.

## Method

The paper constructs four systematically controlled training data setups—Natural-only, Leave-out-domain, CG low-diversity, and CG high-diversity—that isolate domain diversity while holding dataset size, architecture, and class distribution constant, then trains CLIP models (ResNet-50, ViT-S-32, Swin-T) via contrastive learning on these mixtures. To explain when and why generalization succeeds or fails, the authors introduce three complementary analyses: (1) Sparse Autoencoder (SAE) feature-sharing in visual embeddings, (2) Center Kernel Alignment (CKA) for representational similarity of intermediate layers, and (3) a novel 'mechanistic similarity' measure that discovers circuits via indirect-effect-based neuron attribution and quantifies their structural overlap using layer-wise Jaccard indices and Weisfeiler-Lehman graph kernels. The key finding is that domain diversity is a prerequisite for both domain and compositional generalization, yet compositional generalization can paradoxically be weaker than domain generalization when training includes a suboptimal subset of test-domain classes that overlap with evaluation queries.

## Results

On DomainNet, CLIP's balanced top-1 accuracy on unseen test classes improves substantially with domain diversity: for Clipart, accuracy rises from 20.3% (Natural-only) to 27.4% (Leave-out-domain) and 27.6% (CG high-diversity), while for Sketch it rises from 19.5% to 30.1% and 28.1% respectively. However, a persistent gap remains to the upper bound (36.6% for Clipart, 44.3% for Sketch) achieved when the model is also trained on the test-domain samples of the queried classes. Surprisingly, compositional generalization (seeing a subset of test-domain classes) can be *worse* than domain generalization (seeing no test-domain classes at all), e.g., Sketch drops from 30.1% (Leave-out-domain) to 28.1% (CG high-diversity). Generalization to Quickdraw fails entirely in all settings (≤5.2%), attributed to insufficient sharing of intermediate features and circuits.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is across four controlled training data setups rather than across architectures. High-diversity setups (Leave-out-domain, CG high-diversity) consistently outperform low-diversity ones (Natural-only, CG low-diversity) by 7–11 points on Clipart and Sketch, confirming domain diversity as the key driver. Against the upper bound (training on test-domain samples of queried classes), a gap of 9.0 points (Clipart) and 16.2 points (Sketch) persists, and closing it requires roughly linear scaling of test-domain samples. Compared to BLIP (ViT-B-16 + BERT-base, pre-trained encoders), CLIP (ResNet-50) achieves lower absolute accuracy (e.g., 27.1% vs. 61.8% on Clipart Leave-out-domain) but follows identical trends: both benefit from domain diversity and both suffer from the compositional-generalization degradation when test-domain classes overlap with queried classes. Against supervised ResNet-50 classifiers trained on the same domain mixtures, CLIP shows comparable or slightly higher robustness in most settings (e.g., Sketch Leave-out-domain: 30.1% vs. 27.4%), with the advantage attributed to caption richness; however, supervised classifiers occasionally match or exceed CLIP (e.g., Clipart Leave-out-domain: 30.8% vs. 27.4%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the established finding that CLIP's diverse training distribution drives its OOD generalization (Fang et al., 2022; Mayilvahanan et al., 2025), but shifts the question from 'what factor matters' to 'when and how does domain diversity enable generalization, and what happens mechanistically when it fails.' It departs from prior work by constructing fully controlled training distributions with systematically varied domain mixtures and class exposure, isolating domain diversity from confounds like dataset size. The introduction of mechanistic similarity—a graph-kernel-based measure of circuit overlap across domains—extends the representational similarity analysis toolkit (CKA) to the computational level, building on circuit discovery methods from interpretability research (Marks et al., 2025; Meng et al., 2022).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that compositional generalization can be weaker than domain generalization—i.e., partial exposure to a test domain's classes can hurt performance on its unseen classes—challenges the intuitive assumption that more domain exposure always helps, and identifies class overlap as a concrete, actionable failure mode. The mechanistic analysis showing that generalization requires sufficient sharing of intermediate features and circuits (not just aligned final embeddings) provides a diagnostic framework applicable beyond CLIP. Key limitations include the restriction to DomainNet's five non-natural domains and dataset sizes up to ~10M samples, leaving open whether findings hold at LAION-scale; the paper also notes that the generalization gap to models trained on test-domain samples persists and scales roughly linearly with the number of required samples.
