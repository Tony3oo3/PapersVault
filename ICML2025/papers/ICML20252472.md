## Problem

Existing generalization bounds for GNNs either grow with the number of nodes or depend on graph-specific quantities like node degree or maximum eigenvalue, contradicting the empirical observation that larger graphs yield better generalization. The paper addresses this by asking: what happens to GNN generalization when the underlying graph is understood as a finite sample from a continuous manifold, and how do the spectral properties of the GNN's filter functions control the gap between training and test performance?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252472/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on graphs constructed from N i.i.d. points sampled from a d-dimensional Riemannian manifold, using either a Gaussian kernel (dense graph) or an ε-graph (sparse, bounded-support) construction. Node features are treated as samples of a bandlimited L²(𝓜) function, and the graph Laplacian approximates the weighted Laplace operator of the manifold. The output is twofold: (i) node-level or graph-level predictions from the GNN, and (ii) a theoretical generalization gap bound that depends on N, the manifold dimension d, the number of layers L, features F, and the spectral continuity constant C_L of the filter frequency response.

## Method

The core technique is to establish algebraic equivalence between a GNN on a sampled graph and a Manifold Neural Network (MNN) on the underlying manifold in the spectral domain, then bound the generalization gap as the sum of (a) the GNN-to-MNN approximation error and (b) the discretization error of the MNN output over the manifold. The GNN-to-MNN convergence (Proposition 1) is proved by decomposing the filter output difference into eigenvalue perturbation, eigenvector perturbation, and sampling error terms, each controlled by the spectral continuity constant C_L and the graph parameter ε. The discretization error is bounded via local Lipschitz continuity of the MNN output (Proposition 3), yielding the (log N/N)^(1/d) rate. The final bound (Theorems 1 and 2) decreases linearly in log-scale with N and increases linearly with C_L, revealing a fundamental trade-off: smoother (less discriminative) filters generalize better, while more discriminative filters risk overfitting.

## Results

The paper's main theoretical result (Theorem 1) establishes that the generalization gap GA ≤ F^L C_3 (log N/N)^(1/d) + LF^(L-1)((C_1 C_L + C_2)√(ε/N) + π²√(log(1/δ))/(6N)), showing the gap decreases linearly with log(N) and increases linearly with the spectral continuity constant C_L. Empirically, on the synthetic chair manifold, the generalization gap is confirmed to decrease with increasing node count and increase with larger continuity constants. On eight real-world datasets (ArXiv, CiteSeer, Cora, PubMed, Amazon-Ratings, Roman-Empire, CoAuthors CS, CoAuthors Physics), the accuracy generalization gap exhibits a linear decay in the logarithmic scale of the number of training nodes, consistent with the theoretical prediction, particularly in the overfitting regime (training loss < 95%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper that does not compare against competing GNN architectures or generalization bound methods in a head-to-head empirical benchmark. Instead, it validates its own theoretical predictions: (1) the generalization gap decreases with the number of sampled nodes, contrasting with prior bounds (e.g., Scarselli et al. 2018, Esser et al. 2021, Tang & Liu 2023) that grow with graph size; (2) the gap increases linearly with the spectral continuity constant C_L, which is a novel dependency not present in prior work; and (3) a trade-off exists between discriminability and generalization, where a larger regularizer (smaller C_L) reduces the generalization gap but hurts test accuracy due to reduced spectral discriminability. The empirical validation on the chair manifold and eight real-world datasets confirms the predicted logarithmic decay rate and the C_L-dependent behavior, but no quantitative comparison against alternative generalization bounds is provided.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate research threads: GNN generalization theory (Scarselli et al. 2018, Verma & Zhang 2019, Garg et al. 2020, Liao et al. 2020) and the convergence of GNNs to Manifold Neural Networks (Wang et al. 2022b, 2024a; Calder & Trillos 2022; Belkin & Niyogi 2008). While prior GNN generalization bounds grow with graph size (node count, degree, or spectral norm), and graphon-based analyses (Maskey et al. 2022, 2024; Levie 2024) treat the limit model as a random continuous object, this work instead uses a deterministic Riemannian manifold as the generative model, leveraging the algebraic equivalence between GNNs and MNNs in the spectral domain to derive bounds that decrease with graph size. The spectral convergence results for graph Laplacians (García Trillos et al. 2020; Calder & Trillos 2022) provide the technical bridge between discrete and continuous operators.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central result—that the generalization gap decreases (in log scale) with the number of sampled nodes—reverses the trend of all prior GNN generalization bounds and provides a principled explanation for why training on larger graphs empirically improves generalization. The spectral continuity constant C_L introduces a concrete, interpretable design knob: practitioners can explicitly trade off filter discriminability against generalization, which has direct implications for GNN architecture and regularization choices. Key limitations include the bandlimited signal assumption, the requirement that graphs be sampled from a compact manifold (unclear for arbitrary real-world graphs), and the fact that the log-linear decay is most clearly observed in the overfitting regime. Open questions include extending the analysis to non-bandlimited signals, heterogeneous or dynamic manifolds, and establishing whether the manifold assumption is verifiable for practical graph datasets.
