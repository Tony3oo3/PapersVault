## Problem

Transition path sampling (TPS) for atomistic systems remains challenging due to energy barriers creating vast timescale separation between rare events and fast dynamics. Existing ML-based TPS methods rely on expensive, task-specific training procedures that must be repeated for each new system, failing to exploit the growing availability of high-quality atomistic datasets and large-scale pre-trained generative models.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252034/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes two fixed atomistic configurations (endpoints in sets A and B) and a frozen, pre-trained generative model (DDPM or flow matching) whose score function s_θ*(x, τ) serves as the drift in induced Langevin dynamics. Physical parameters—timestep Δt, friction ζ, and diffusion coefficient D—are set to match the target MD simulation. The output is a sequence of L discrete configurations {x^(i)}_{i=0}^{L} forming a high-probability transition path, which can subsequently be used to estimate free energy barriers via umbrella sampling, committor functions via the Backward Kolmogorov Equation, and transition rates.

## Method

The key insight is that denoising diffusion and flow matching models induce stochastic Langevin dynamics on the data manifold, with the learned score function acting as the drift term. The probability of any candidate path under these dynamics is governed by the Onsager-Machlup (OM) action functional, which decomposes into three terms: a smoothness penalty on adjacent path points (A), a low-norm drift penalty favoring equilibria and saddle points (B), and a divergence term entropically favoring convex regions of the energy landscape (C). Starting from a linear interpolation in the generative model's latent space at a chosen noise level τ_initial, the full discretized path is optimized in parallel via gradient descent on the OM action while keeping the score function frozen and endpoints fixed. The divergence term is computed efficiently using the Hutchinson estimator, and the entire procedure requires no TPS-specific training—making it a zero-shot repurposing of any pre-trained score-based generative model.

## Results

On alanine dipeptide, OM optimization with a pre-trained diffusion model requires only ~10K score function evaluations per 1,000-step transition path (50 min runtime), compared to ≥1B force field evaluations (≥100 hours) for MCMC two-way shooting and 1M evaluations (10 hours) for metadynamics. On the Müller-Brown potential, the method estimates a transition rate of 1.3×10⁻⁵ versus the true rate of 5.4×10⁻⁵, while 2,500 i.i.d. diffusion model samples fail to reach the transition region at all. On fast-folding coarse-grained proteins, OM-optimized paths achieve a higher fraction of valid paths and lower transition negative log-likelihood under the reference MSM than unbiased MD simulations of any length up to 100 μs, with Jensen-Shannon divergence lower than MD up to 50 μs. On unseen tetrapeptide sequences, OM optimization with a flow matching model produces paths competitive with 50–100 ns MD simulations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against MCMC two-way shooting (Bolhuis & Swenson, 2021), OM optimization requires roughly 100,000× fewer force-field/score evaluations (10K vs. ≥1B) and ~120× less wall-clock time (50 min vs. ≥100 hours) per transition path on alanine dipeptide, while also not requiring predefined collective variables. Against metadynamics (Laio & Gervasio, 2008), OM optimization is ~100× more efficient in evaluations (10K vs. 1M) and ~12× faster (50 min vs. 10 hours), and additionally avoids the need to manually select CVs; the free energy barrier estimated via umbrella sampling along OM-optimized paths (~6 kcal/mol) is consistent with metadynamics results. Against reference unbiased MD simulations (Lindorff-Larsen et al., 2011) on fast-folding proteins, OM-optimized paths outperform MD of any length up to 100 μs in fraction of valid paths and transition negative log-likelihood under the reference MSM, and match or exceed MD up to 50 μs in Jensen-Shannon divergence of visited state distributions. Against MD-based trajectory generation (Jing et al., 2024b) on held-out tetrapeptide sequences, OM optimization with a flow matching model achieves comparable MSM metrics to 50–100 ns MD simulations, demonstrating zero-shot generalization to unseen sequences without retraining.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate lines of work: (1) Onsager-Machlup (OM) action minimization for transition path sampling, which was historically limited to low-dimensional systems due to the absence of automatic differentiation for the divergence term, and (2) score-based generative models (denoising diffusion and flow matching) for molecular conformational sampling. The key conceptual departure from prior TPS methods (metadynamics, shooting, umbrella sampling, and ML-based biasing potentials) is that no task-specific training is required: the pre-trained generative model's score function directly parameterizes the drift of an overdamped Langevin SDE, making the OM action a differentiable objective over paths. This builds on Arts et al. (2023), who showed diffusion model scores approximate atomistic force fields, but extends the connection from single-point force evaluation to full trajectory optimization, and further generalizes to flow matching models via a score-extraction formula (Eq. 18).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The zero-shot, training-free nature of the approach means it automatically benefits from the rapid scaling of atomistic generative models and large-scale structural datasets, a direction already standard in language and vision. However, the method does not provably sample the full posterior over paths (unlike shooting methods), and all validation is on small systems (2D potentials, 22-atom dipeptides, coarse-grained proteins, tetrapeptides), leaving open whether the approach scales to full-atom proteins or complex materials. A key open question is whether pre-training on large structural databases (e.g., PDB) enables meaningful TPS on entirely unseen systems, and whether the OM framework can serve as a general-purpose interpolation mechanism beyond molecular dynamics.
