## Problem

GNNs applied to directed flow networks (river, traffic) are insensitive to edge directions because their message-passing mechanism acts as a low-pass filter, suppressing the high-frequency components (rapid flux changes, sharp transitions) that encode directional dynamics. This causes standard GNNs to perform nearly identically on forward, reversed, or randomly perturbed topologies, and to fail at capturing local, rapid changes that propagate only in specific physical directions. The paper further shows that reversing edge directions turns the prediction task into an ill-posed inverse problem that amplifies high-frequency noise, a regime where low-pass-filtering GNNs are fundamentally ill-suited.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a directed graph with nodal features (e.g., discharge, pressure, precipitation, temperature, soil moisture for river; flow, occupancy, speed for traffic) observed over a W=24-hour window, concatenated along the feature dimension, plus edge features (length, slope, distance for river; distance for traffic). All physical variables are normalized element-wise via standard score. The output is a single predicted flux volume per node at a configurable lead time n (default n=6 hours), framed as supervised node regression with MSE loss averaged over all nodes.

## Method

PhyNFP replaces the standard adjacency matrix in GNN message passing with discretized difference matrices derived from an upwind finite-difference scheme, which act as high-pass filters (confirmed via DTFT analysis) to preserve directional gradients and local variations. Two enhanced matrices are used: D₁ encodes spatial-distance-based gradients and D₂ encodes elevation-driven (gravitational) effects, both scaled by learnable MLP mappings of edge features. The message-passing update is reformulated as h^{l+1} = h^l − Δt(h^l ⊙ (D₁h^l W₁) + ĝ·(D₂h^l W₂)) for river networks (from the Saint-Venant momentum equation) and h^{l+1} = h^l − Δt(h^l ⊙ (D₁v^l W₁) + v^l ⊙ (D₁h^l W₂)) for traffic (from the Aw-Rascle mass-conservation equation), where Δt, ĝ, W₁, W₂ are learnable scalars/matrices. This design embeds physical conservation laws directly into the GNN's update rule, ensuring predictions remain consistent with governing PDEs while the upwind difference matrices maintain numerical stability and directional sensitivity even at up to 19 layers.

## Results

On the river network (LamaH-CE2, 358 nodes), PhyNFP achieves a Forward MSE of 0.0801 at prediction horizon n=6, reducing error by 31.6% over the average of all baselines (0.1170). On the traffic network (PEMS-04, 307 nodes), PhyNFP achieves a Forward MSE of 0.0696, a 4.9% reduction over the baseline average (0.0732). In terms of direction sensitivity (DS = Reverse MSE − Forward MSE), PhyNFP scores +0.0105 on river and +0.0028 on traffic, representing a 26× and 3.6× improvement in relative direction sensitivity (RDS) over the respective baseline averages. The ablation PhyNFP_DM (difference matrices only, no PDE regularization) degrades RDS by 40.0% (river) and 39.3% (traffic), confirming the contribution of physics-guided training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PhyNFP outperforms all seven baselines on both datasets in Forward MSE. The largest gains are on the river network: PhyNFP (0.0801) beats GCN (0.1365) by 41%, GAT (0.1233) by 35%, GWN (0.1101) by 27%, and MP-PDE Solver (0.1126) by 29%. On the traffic network, margins are tighter but consistent: PhyNFP (0.0696) edges out MP-PDE Solver (0.0700) by 0.6%, GWN (0.0709) by 1.8%, and GCN (0.0769) by 9.5%. In terms of direction sensitivity, PhyNFP is the only method with a strongly positive DS on both datasets; several baselines (GCN, GraphSAGE, MP-PDE Solver on river) actually show negative DS, meaning they perform *better* on reversed edges, confirming their directional insensitivity. Among physics-aware baselines, MP-PDE Solver and GNO improve over purely data-driven GNNs (GCN, GAT, GraphSAGE) but still trail PhyNFP by 26.5% (river) and 4.1% (traffic) in Forward MSE, underscoring the benefit of explicitly encoding PDE-specific difference matrices rather than relying on generic message-passing PDE solvers.

## Relation to Prior Work

PhyNFP repurposes discretized difference matrices and upwind schemes from numerical PDE solvers (LeVeque, 2002; Bermudez & Vazquez, 1994) as explicit adjacency replacements in GNN message passing, a connection not previously drawn in the graph learning literature. It extends the physics-informed GNN line (GNO, MP-PDE Solver, PDE-GCN) by embedding specific governing PDEs (Saint-Venant, Aw-Rascle) directly into the training objective as regularizers, rather than using them only as architectural inspiration. The paper also builds on the over-smoothing / high-frequency preservation literature (Bo et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2022; Rusch et al., 2022) by providing a concrete mechanistic explanation—low-pass filtering of message passing—for why GNNs are directionally insensitive, and by framing the reverse-topology setting as an ill-posed inverse problem to formalize this limitation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a principled, generalizable recipe (difference matrices + PDE-specific regularization) for making GNNs sensitive to edge direction and high-frequency flow dynamics, which is a persistent weakness in graph-based forecasting. However, validation is limited to two small real-world networks (358 and 307 nodes) and two specific hyperbolic PDE families; whether the approach scales to larger, more complex topologies or to parabolic/elliptic PDEs remains open. The authors themselves note that boundary and initial conditions of the governing PDEs are not yet incorporated, which limits physical fidelity. A key open question is whether the learned difference-matrix parameters transfer across different flow regimes or require retraining per network.
