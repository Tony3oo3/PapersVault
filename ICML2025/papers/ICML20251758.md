## Problem

No existing model can accept an arbitrary Signal Temporal Logic (STL) specification as input and directly produce a satisfiable trajectory without retraining. Prior learning-based approaches handle only fixed or parametrized STLs, while classical solvers (gradient-based, sampling-based) struggle with high-dimensional systems and complex logical structures. The field also lacked a large-scale dataset of diverse STL specifications with paired demonstrations and a principled analysis of how to encode STL syntax for downstream neural networks.

## Input / Output

The method takes an STL specification parsed into a directed syntax graph (nodes for each operator and atomic proposition with 8-dimensional features encoding operator type, time intervals, object coordinates, and radius; edges directed from child to parent) and an initial state x₀ ∈ Rⁿ. It outputs a full state-action trajectory τ ∈ R^{T×(m+n)} (T=64 for Linear/Dubins/Franka Panda, T=512 for PointMaze/AntMaze) generated by integrating a learned velocity field via Euler's method over N_s ODE steps. At inference, 1024 candidate trajectories are sampled and the one with the highest STL robustness score is selected.

## Method

TeLoGraF combines a GCN-based graph encoder (4 layers, 256 units, ReLU) that processes the STL syntax graph into a 256-dimensional embedding, with a conditional flow-matching model (rectified flow) using a UNet backbone to generate trajectories. The GNN's suitability is justified theoretically: since the 1-WL test is complete for trees, GNNs can distinguish any two distinct STL syntax trees. Training uses linear interpolation between Gaussian noise and demonstration trajectories, with the network predicting the velocity field ΔX = X₁ − X₀ conditioned on the STL embedding and initial state. The paper introduces four STL templates (single-goal, multi-goal, sequential, partial-order) and procedurally generates 200K specifications with paired demonstrations across five robot environments. Ablations show graph-based encoders (GNN, TreeLSTM) outperform sequence-based ones (GRU, Transformer) on generalization, particularly for sequential and partial-order constraints, due to inherited permutation-invariance and superior propagation of temporal dependencies.

## Results

TeLoGraD achieves the highest STL satisfaction rate in 4 out of 5 benchmarks (Linear, Dubins, PointMaze, AntMaze), while TeLoGraF (Fast) achieves the best quality–efficiency trade-off across all environments. On the high-dimensional Franka Panda environment, TeLoGraF (Fast) is 123.6× faster than the gradient-based Grad baseline and 60.7× faster than CEM, while maintaining a higher satisfaction rate than both. The Fast variant (10 ODE steps) matches TeLoGraF's (100 ODE steps) satisfaction rate at only 1/10 the inference runtime. Maze environments (PointMaze, AntMaze) yield the highest absolute satisfaction rates across all methods, while complex STL types (sequential, partial) remain challenging, with lower satisfaction on Linear, Dubins, and Franka Panda.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against classical planners, TeLoGraF (Fast) dominates on the high-dimensional Franka Panda environment, being 123.6× faster than Grad (Dawson & Fan, 2022) and 60.7× faster than CEM (Kapoor et al., 2020) while achieving a higher STL satisfaction rate than both; the advantage grows with dimensionality since gradient-based and sampling-based methods struggle in high-dimensional spaces. Against learning-based guidance methods CTG (Zhong et al., 2023) and LTLDoG (Feng et al., 2024), TeLoGraF avoids the expensive per-step guidance computation at inference, yielding better efficiency; on the maze benchmarks (PointMaze, AntMaze) all learning-based methods perform closely and achieve the highest absolute satisfaction rates, but TeLoGraD still edges ahead on Linear and Dubins. Among encoder ablations, GNN and TreeLSTM (Tai et al., 2015) outperform sequence-based encoders GRU (Chung et al., 2014) and Transformer (Vaswani, 2017) on validation-set STL satisfaction, with the gap most pronounced on sequential and partial-order specifications; the authors attribute this to the graph encoders' inherent permutation-invariance and superior propagation of temporal ordering. TeLoGraD (diffusion backbone) achieves the highest satisfaction in 4/5 environments, while TeLoGraF (flow backbone) shows a modest drop on Dubins and Franka Panda (likely due to nonlinear dynamics), but TeLoGraF (Fast) recovers most of the quality at 1/10 the runtime, making it the recommended deployment variant.

## Relation to Prior Work

TeLoGraF combines two relatively recent generative-modeling ingredients—rectified flow matching (Liu et al., 2022) for trajectory synthesis and a UNet backbone adapted from diffusion-based planning (Janner et al., 2022)—with a GNN encoder that treats STL syntax trees as graph data. It departs from prior learning-based STL methods (CTG, LTLDoG) that inject temporal-logic guidance only at inference time, instead baking STL structure into the training objective via the graph encoder. The closest prior work, Hashimoto et al. (2022), also explores STL encoders but targets shorter, obstacle-free specifications in a model-based setting; TeLoGraF generalizes to arbitrary STL templates, high-dimensional dynamics, and obstacle-rich environments, supported by a 200K-specification dataset that did not previously exist.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 200K paired STL–trajectory dataset and the four-template taxonomy fill a concrete gap that has limited progress in STL-conditioned learning, and the 10–100× inference speedup over gradient-based and CEM solvers makes learned STL planning viable for real-time or high-dimensional settings (e.g., 7-DoF robot arms). The theoretical grounding via the Weisfeiler–Leman completeness result for trees gives a clean justification for why GNNs should outperform sequence encoders, and the ablation confirms this empirically. Open questions remain: satisfaction rates drop noticeably for sequential and partial-order STLs, the model offers no soundness or completeness guarantees, and predicted state-action trajectories are not yet rolled out with dynamic-consistency controllers—addressing these will be necessary before deployment in safety-critical systems.
