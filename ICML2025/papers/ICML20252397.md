## Problem

In fixed-confidence pure exploration, batched algorithms can only adapt at batch boundaries, raising the question of how many batches are fundamentally required to achieve near-optimal sample complexity. Prior work provided batch complexity bounds only for specific tasks (BAI, Top-k) and often stated worst-case or existence-of-hard-instance results rather than instance-dependent guarantees. This paper establishes the first instance-dependent lower bounds on batch complexity for general pure exploration problems and constructs an algorithm whose batch complexity nearly matches those bounds.

## Input / Output

The algorithm receives a K-armed bandit with σ²-sub-Gaussian arm distributions, a confidence level δ, and a starting complexity parameter T₀ ≥ 1 that serves as the initial guess for the instance's sample complexity. It outputs the correct answer to the exploration query (best arm, top-k set, or thresholding set) while guaranteeing error probability ≤ δ. The batch structure means the agent commits to a full set of arm pulls at the start of each batch and observes all results only at the batch's end, so the number of batches R_δ (not just total samples τ_δ) is a first-class performance metric.

## Method

The Phased Explore then Track (PET) algorithm operates in phases r = 0, 1, 2, … with phase complexity T_r = 2^r T₀. In each phase, a first batch samples all arms uniformly until each has at least 2^r l_{1,r} pulls, building a confidence ball B_r around the empirical mean; if the worst-case complexity T̄⋆(B_r) exceeds T_r, the phase is skipped. Otherwise, a second batch samples each arm i proportionally to γ_r · w̄_i⋆(B_r) · T̄⋆(B_r), where w̄⋆ and T̄⋆ are the optimal sampling proportions and complexity over the entire confidence set. The stopping rule is a Gaussian Generalized Likelihood Ratio test with a threshold β(t,δ) derived via the method of mixtures and the Lambert W function, giving β ≈ ln(1/δ) + 2K ln ln t. The lower bound is constructed by building a sequence of instances with geometrically increasing complexity (T⋆(μⁿ) = T⋆(μ⁰)ζ⁻ⁿ) under Assumption 2.2 (quadratic scaling of T⋆ under linear interpolation toward a constant vector), then applying a generalized Tao et al. (2019) argument to show that any δ-correct algorithm must use Ω(ln(T⋆(μ)/T_min) / ln(ln(T⋆(μ)/T_min) + ln(γ ln(1/δ) · condition_number))) batches.

## Results

The paper establishes instance-dependent lower bounds on batch complexity for any δ-correct pure exploration algorithm, showing that for Top-k and thresholding bandits the batch complexity is Ω{ln(T*(μ)/T_min) / [ln(ln(T*(μ)/T_min)) + ln(γ ln(δ⁻¹)K max_i Δ_i²/min_i Δ_i²)]}. The proposed Phased Explore then Track (PET) algorithm achieves a batch complexity of log₂(T*(μ)/T₀) + 5 (under Assumption 3.9), nearly matching the lower bound, with expected sample complexity O(T*(μ) ln(1/δ) + KT*(μ) ln T*(μ)). In experiments with δ=0.05 on 10-arm Gaussian BAI instances, PET uses fewer samples than Opt-BBAI while using a comparable number of batches, and on a hard 2-arm thresholding instance PET avoids the high average sample complexity exhibited by batched Track-and-Stop.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares PET against three baselines: Round Robin (uniform sampling), a batched modification of Track-and-Stop (TaS), and Opt-BBAI (Jin et al., 2023). On 10-arm BAI instances (best arm mean 1, others uniform on [0.6, 0.9], δ=0.05), PET and Opt-BBAI use similarly few batches, but PET achieves lower sample complexity because Opt-BBAI's batch sizes are too large for moderate δ values despite being asymptotically optimal as δ→0. On a hard 2-arm thresholding instance (threshold 0.6, arm means 0.5 and 0.6), batched TaS exhibits high average sample complexity due to its only-asymptotic guarantees, while PET maintains low sample complexity. Round Robin serves as a simple baseline that checks the stopping rule only at geometrically spaced timesteps (900×2^r), providing a reference for both batch and sample efficiency. Overall, PET wins on sample complexity in both BAI and thresholding settings while matching or approaching the theoretical lower bound on batch complexity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Track-and-Stop framework (Garivier & Kaufmann, 2016) from fully sequential to batched pure exploration, and generalizes the lower-bound construction technique of Tao et al. (2019) from specific two-armed collaborative instances to arbitrary K-armed pure exploration problems. Prior batched bandit work (Hillel et al. 2013, Agarwal et al. 2017, Jin et al. 2019, Jin et al. 2023) addressed only BAI or Top-k with problem-specific elimination strategies; here the authors provide a single algorithm (PET) and instance-dependent lower bounds valid for any pure exploration task satisfying a mild scaling assumption. The key departure is making the batch-complexity lower bound a function of the instance's own complexity T*(μ) rather than merely asserting the existence of some hard instance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes the first instance-dependent lower bound on batch complexity for general pure exploration, showing that any δ-correct algorithm must use Ω(log(T*/T_min)/log(log(T*/T_min))) batches, and provides an algorithm (PET) whose batch complexity nearly matches this bound. The main open question is how to replace uniform exploration (which costs K·T* samples) with adaptive exploration for general problems, closing the gap to the optimal T* sample complexity; elimination strategies work for BAI/Top-k but rely on problem-specific gap structure. For practitioners in settings with large observation delays (clinical trials, A/B testing with slow feedback), the result quantifies the fundamental cost of limited adaptivity.
