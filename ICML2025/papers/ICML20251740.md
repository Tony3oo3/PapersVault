## Problem

Developing foundation models for time series forecasting requires choosing an effective discrete vocabulary for real-valued sequential inputs, yet this tokenization problem remains understudied. Existing approaches—temporal patches or uniform bin quantization—preserve the natural temporal ordering but struggle to simultaneously capture both local (e.g., sparse spikes) and global (e.g., evolving trends) patterns in non-stationary signals. The paper asks: what is the most eloquent 'language' for time series that is both compact and expressive enough to represent the high variety of real-world non-stationary data?

## Input / Output

The method takes a real-valued univariate time series context window of length 512, applies z-score normalization, and produces a sequence of discrete wavelet coefficient tokens (vocabulary size 1024) structured as [approximation coefficients, detail coefficients from coarsest to finest level]. At inference, the model autoregressively samples tokens for a 64-step horizon, which are de-quantized to bin centers, passed through the inverse DWT, and un-scaled to yield 20 sample paths from which probabilistic quantile forecasts (9 levels) and a median point forecast are extracted. The token sequence length equals the original signal length (512 for context, 64 for horizon) since the maximally decimated DWT preserves total coefficient count.

## Method

WaveToken is a tokenization pipeline that converts a time series into a compact sequence of wavelet coefficient tokens: (1) z-score scaling, (2) single-level Discrete Wavelet Transform using the Biorthogonal-2.2 family, (3) optional thresholding of detail coefficients (no-thresholding selected in the final 8-GPU configuration), and (4) quantization into 1024 bins sized by the Freedman-Diaconis rule on the empirical coefficient distribution. These tokens are concatenated coarse-to-fine and fed into a T5 encoder-decoder trained via next-token prediction (cross-entropy) on a large multi-domain corpus augmented with TSMixup and Gaussian-process synthetic series. The key insight is that wavelets provide inherent time-frequency localization and sparsity, so the model learns to forecast in a multi-scale hierarchy—attending to approximation coefficients for coarse structure and detail coefficients for fine features—rather than in raw time-domain values. This yields a vocabulary one-quarter the size of Chronos (1024 vs. 4096) while achieving superior generalization, as the sparse wavelet representation naturally compresses complex non-stationary patterns into a small number of significant coefficients.

## Results

On the in-domain benchmark (15 datasets), WaveToken-Large (199M params, 1024-token vocabulary) achieves the best average rank across all three metrics: WQL (rank 4.73), MASE (rank 4.47), and VRSE (rank 7.07), with an aggregated relative WQL score of 0.569 vs. Chronos-Large's 0.564. On the zero-shot benchmark (27 unseen datasets), WaveToken-Large achieves the best average rank on WQL (5.63) and MASE (4.47), outperforming all foundation models with an 83% per-dataset win rate against same-size Chronos models. Notably, WaveToken uses only 1024 tokens—four times smaller than Chronos's 4096—while matching or exceeding its forecasting accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Chronos (the closest competitor, sharing the same T5 encoder-decoder architecture), WaveToken-Large achieves a marginally better aggregated WQL on zero-shot (0.644 vs. 0.645) and a better average rank (5.63 vs. 6.63), while using only 1024 tokens versus Chronos's 4096. On in-domain WQL, Chronos-Large edges out WaveToken-Large slightly (0.564 vs. 0.569), but WaveToken wins on MASE (0.698 vs. 0.695) and achieves the best average rank on all three metrics. Against TimesFM, WaveToken-Large is consistently better on zero-shot WQL (0.644 vs. 0.692) and MASE (0.698 vs. 0.745), with a 5–10% relative improvement. Against Moirai-Large and Lag-Llama, WaveToken dominates by a wide margin (e.g., zero-shot WQL 0.644 vs. 0.707 and 1.097, respectively). Against task-specific models trained per-dataset, WaveToken is competitive: TFT achieves a slightly lower zero-shot WQL (0.639 vs. 0.644) but requires separate training on each dataset, while PatchTST and DeepAR are clearly outperformed (0.684 and 0.733 zero-shot WQL). On the VRSE metric (frequency-content fidelity), WaveToken substantially outperforms all foundation models on zero-shot, reflecting its ability to capture complex temporal patterns like trends, spikes, and evolving frequencies that other models miss.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Chronos framework (Ansari et al., 2024), which pairs a T5 encoder-decoder with a quantized vocabulary of raw time series values, by replacing the tokenization step with a discrete wavelet transform pipeline. It departs from other recent LLM-based forecasters (TimesFM, Moirai, Lag-Llama) that use temporal patches or direct value quantization, instead operating in the space of time-localized frequency coefficients. The method draws on classical signal processing tools—maximally decimated DWT, Biorthogonal-2.2 wavelets, and Freedman-Diaconis binning—repurposing them as a fixed, data-driven vocabulary construction for autoregressive language modeling of time series.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a 1024-token wavelet vocabulary matches or beats a 4096-token raw-value vocabulary (Chronos) while improving zero-shot generalization suggests that the choice of tokenization space is a first-order design decision for time series foundation models, not a minor implementation detail. However, the autoregressive decoding over wavelet coefficients is slower than patch-based alternatives, and the optimal single-level decomposition (despite wavelet theory's multi-scale motivation) limits the practical multi-resolution benefit. Open questions include whether wavelet tokenization transfers to patch-based or diffusion architectures, and whether longer contexts (>512) can be handled without degrading the coefficient structure.
