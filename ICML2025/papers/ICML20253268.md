## Problem

Despite the empirical success of overparametrized DNNs across high-dimensional domains, a unifying theoretical principle explaining why they avoid the curse of dimensionality in approximation, optimization, and generalization has remained elusive. This position paper argues that compositional sparsity—the property that target functions decompose into a small set of constituent functions each depending on only a few input variables—is the missing ingredient, and proves that this property is shared by all efficiently Turing-computable functions (class FP), making it nearly universal across practical learning problems.

## Input / Output

The theoretical framework takes as input any function f: X^d → Y that is efficiently Turing-computable (solvable by a deterministic Turing machine in polynomial time) along with i.i.d. training pairs (x_i, y_i) drawn from an unknown measure μ. The output is a polynomial-complexity deep sparse network (O(d·ε⁻²) parameters) that approximates f to accuracy ε, represented as a DAG whose internal nodes are constituent functions of bounded in-degree c ≪ d. The framework also characterizes learnability conditions (e.g., staircase structure, sparse Fourier spectra) and generalization bounds via Rademacher complexity of the sparse hypothesis class.

## Method

The core technical contribution is a proof of the conjecture from Poggio & Fraser (2024) that every efficiently Turing-computable function is compositionally sparse: any polynomial-time Turing computation can be translated into a Boolean circuit of polynomial size, and gates with unbounded fan-in can be decomposed into binary-fan-in trees at only polynomial overhead, yielding a DAG with bounded in-degree. Combined with the existing approximation result that deep networks mimicking such a DAG require only O(d·ε⁻²) parameters (versus O(ε⁻ᵈ) for shallow networks), this establishes DNNs as universal approximators for all practically computable functions with polynomial complexity. The paper then extends the framework to optimization—citing Goldreich et al.'s cryptographic hardness for worst-case learnability while identifying tractable subclasses (staircase functions, sparse polynomials, Fourier-sparse functions)—and to generalization, showing that architectural sparsity (CNN local patches, transformer sparse attention) reduces symmetry groups and tightens Rademacher bounds. Finally, the paper formalizes Chain-of-Thought as iterative application of a token predictor over a partition of the input space, where each subproblem is a sparse constituent function, thereby explaining how autoregressive training and CoT prompting circumvent the hardness of one-shot learning of dense compositionally sparse targets.

## Results

This is a theoretical position paper with no experimental results or benchmark evaluations. The key quantitative contributions are theoretical: Theorem 3.3 shows shallow networks require O(ε^{-d}) parameters to approximate a compositionally sparse function to accuracy ε, while deep networks mimicking the function's DAG require only O(dε^{-2}) parameters. Corollary 3.4 establishes that any efficiently Turing-computable function (i.e., any function in FP) can be approximated by a deep sparse network of polynomial complexity in both d and ε^{-1}. Theorem 3.2 (the paper's main proof) establishes that all functions in FP are compositionally sparse, closing a conjecture from Poggio & Fraser (2024).

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not run experiments or compare methods on benchmarks. The theoretical contrasts are: (1) Deep vs. shallow networks — Theorem 3.3 shows an exponential gap in approximation complexity (O(ε^{-d}) vs. O(dε^{-2})), establishing that depth is essential for avoiding the curse of dimensionality under compositional sparsity. (2) Compositional sparsity vs. manifold learning — the paper argues manifold learning requires the (often unverifiable) assumption that data lies on a smooth low-dimensional manifold, whereas compositional sparsity is guaranteed for all FP functions without distributional assumptions. (3) Compositional sparsity vs. the multi-index model — the multi-index model captures only a single linear projection followed by a link function, while compositional sparsity allows arbitrary hierarchical DAGs of sparse constituent functions, making it strictly more general. The paper also contrasts CNNs (fixed local sparsity) with Transformers (learned, input-dependent sparsity via attention) as two architectural strategies for exploiting compositional structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper directly proves a conjecture from Poggio & Fraser (2024) that all efficiently Turing-computable functions (class FP) are compositionally sparse, building on the deep-vs-shallow approximation complexity results of Poggio et al. (2017) and the universal-learner result for autoregressive predictors by Malach (2023). It positions compositional sparsity as a unifying alternative to manifold learning and the multi-index model for explaining DNN success in high dimensions, while acknowledging that these frameworks may be complementary. The paper also draws on hardness results from Goldreich et al. (1986) and tractable-subclass results (staircase functions, sparse polynomials) to delineate the boundary between representability and learnability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By proving that the entire class of polynomial-time computable functions is compositionally sparse, the paper provides a single structural principle that simultaneously explains DNN approximation power, motivates architectural choices (CNN locality, transformer attention sparsity), and frames chain-of-thought as an explicit decomposition of a sparse DAG into learnable subproblems. However, the paper is explicitly a position statement: the optimization and generalization theory remains underdeveloped, worst-case learnability is computationally hard, and critical open questions—such as whether SGD alone can discover compositional structure, how much intermediate supervision is needed, and how networks select among multiple valid decompositions—remain unresolved. The framework's practical impact will depend on whether these open questions can be addressed with concrete algorithms and guarantees.
