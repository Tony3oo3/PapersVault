## Problem

Existing LLM mathematical benchmarks rely on a single coarse metric—overall answer accuracy—which conflates genuine reasoning with pattern matching and data contamination. This paper asks whether LLMs truly master mathematical problems in the way humans do, and if not, at which specific cognitive stage and dimension their understanding breaks down.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251543/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a mathematical problem P (from GSM8K, MATH, or the newly constructed MExam dataset) along with its original answer, and produces a 9-dimensional pass/fail vector plus stage-level pass rates. Notably, the pass criterion is dimension-dependent: for counterfactual dimensions (Sentence Disruption, Missing Condition), passing means the LLM correctly identifies the problem as unsolvable rather than reproducing the original answer, while for the remaining seven dimensions, passing requires a correct answer to the generated inquiry. An LLM is deemed to have 'truly mastered' a problem only if it passes all nine dimensions simultaneously.

## Method

CogMath formalizes human mathematical reasoning into three cognitive stages (problem comprehension, problem solving, solution summarization) and decomposes them into nine evaluation dimensions, from fine-grained (sentence paraphrasing, numerical transformation) to coarse-grained (analogical reasoning, backward reasoning). For each dimension, an 'Inquiry-Judge-Reference' multi-agent system—implemented with GPT-4—generates a dimension-specific inquiry q_i, validates its quality through iterative Judge-agent review (up to δ=10 iterations), and produces a reference answer a_i against which the target LLM's response is scored. The key design insight is that dimensions 2 and 3 use counterfactual perturbations (word-order disruption, condition removal) to detect whether the model is genuinely solving the problem or merely recalling memorized patterns, while dimension 7 (Knowledge Redefinition) tests flexible knowledge application by altering mathematical definitions. The overall mastery score is the conjunction of all nine dimension passes, making it a strict, multi-faceted criterion that reveals a 30–40% overestimation in current LLMs' apparent mathematical ability.

## Results

Using Pass Rate (PR) as the metric, CogMath reveals a 30%–40% overestimation of LLM mathematical ability across all seven evaluated models. GPT-4 drops from 75.8% (Vanilla) to 39.3% (CogMath) on MATH (Δ = −36.5%) and from 95.4% to 67.1% on GSM8K (Δ = −28.3%). The overestimation persists on the newly constructed MExam dataset (GPT-4: 80.7% → 36.4%, Δ = −44.0%), confirming it is not solely due to data contamination. Weaker models (e.g., Llama2-13B) show the largest relative drops on GSM8K (44.6% → 6.4%), while stronger models (GPT-4, DeepSeek-V2.5) struggle most in Stage 2 (Problem Solving), particularly in knowledge redefinition (Dimension 7).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare CogMath against other evaluation frameworks; instead, it contrasts each model's standard answer-accuracy ("Vanilla") with its CogMath cognitive-mastery score. All seven models show a consistent 30%–40% drop, with the largest absolute declines for the strongest models (GPT-4: −36.5% on MATH; DeepSeek-V2.5: −37.9% on MATH), indicating overestimation is not confined to weaker models. On the contamination-free MExam dataset, the drops remain large (e.g., DeepSeek-V2.5: −51.3%), ruling out data leakage as the sole explanation. At the stage level, weaker models (Llama2-13B, Llama3-8B) fail most in Stage 1 (Problem Comprehension), while stronger models (GPT-4, DeepSeek-V2.5) fail most in Stage 2 (Problem Solving), specifically in Dimension 7 (Knowledge Redefinition). Prompting techniques (CoT, ICL) do not close the gap: on MATH they actually decrease CogMath scores by 0.7%–4.9%, while on GSM8K they yield only marginal gains of 0.5%–8.5%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm of evaluating LLMs on mathematical benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH, E-GSM, GSM-Plus, MPA) that rely solely on coarse answer-accuracy metrics. It repurposes psychological theories of human mathematical cognition (Schoenfeld; Lesh & Doerr; Dehaene) to structure evaluation into three stages and nine fine-grained dimensions, going beyond the perturbation-based robustness tests of GSM-Plus and MPA. The 'Inquiry-Judge-Reference' multi-agent system is a methodological contribution that automates the generation of dimension-specific probes, distinguishing it from manually annotated variants in prior work. Conceptually, it extends the counterfactual evaluation idea (unsolvable problems, missing conditions) from a single robustness check into a comprehensive cognitive diagnostic framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that all seven evaluated LLMs are overestimated by 30–40% under cognitive-stage evaluation, and that stronger models show *larger* drops on harder problems, challenges the assumption that benchmark accuracy reflects genuine mathematical understanding. The 'over-correction' behavior—where GPT-4 and DeepSeek-V2.5 perform worse on unsolvable problems than weaker models—suggests a fundamental limitation in how current LLMs handle out-of-distribution inputs, pointing toward the need for critical-thinking mechanisms rather than pattern matching. Limitations include the reliance on GPT-4 as the agent backbone (introducing circularity), computational cost of multi-agent evaluation, and the exclusion of techniques like PoT and RAG. Open questions include whether the 9-dimension framework generalizes to non-numeric reasoning (theorem proving, visual math) and how to train models to genuinely flex knowledge rather than rigidly apply memorized formulas.
