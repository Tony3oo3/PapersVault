## Problem

High-dimensional, finite-horizon stochastic optimal control problems are difficult to solve with existing methods: value-based approaches (e.g., deep BSDE) require explicit transition kernel modeling and struggle with nonconvexity, while gradient-based policy optimization methods (PPO, TRPO, SAC) suffer from high-variance Monte Carlo gradient estimates and are typically restricted to infinite-horizon settings. This paper addresses the more general finite-horizon, model-free regime where neither model knowledge nor reliable gradient estimates are available.

## Input / Output

The method takes a continuous state vector x ∈ R^d, time t, running cost f(t,x,α), terminal cost g(x_T), and the SDE dynamics (which are used only for simulating trajectories, not for explicit kernel modeling). The policy α(t,x;θ) is a fully connected neural network parameterized by θ ∈ R^D, and the value function u(t,x) is estimated as the Monte Carlo expectation over controlled dynamics. The method is mesh-free (no state/action discretization) and gradient-free, operating purely on scalar cost evaluations of the policy.

## Method

The paper introduces M-CBO and Adam-CBO, which optimize a population of N neural-network policy parameters via a stochastic differential equation where each agent is pulled toward a Boltzmann-weighted consensus policy M_β(Θ) = Σ Θ^i w_β(Θ^i) / Σ w_β(Θ^j), with w_β(θ) = exp(−βJ(θ)). A momentum variable Ω^i is coupled to each policy to enhance exploration and escape local minima, and adjustable Gaussian noise σ(t) (exponentially decaying in the convergence proof) regulates exploration strength. Adam-CBO further replaces the constant momentum coefficient m with an adaptive inverse second-moment term (analogous to the Adam optimizer), yielding faster convergence in practice. The entire procedure is gradient-free (only scalar cost J(θ) is evaluated via Monte Carlo), model-free (no explicit transition kernel or HJB equation is needed), and mesh-free. The paper proves that M-CBO converges to the optimal policy under regularity assumptions on the cost function, establishing exponential decay of the W₂ distance to the optimal Dirac measure.

## Results

On the 16-dimensional LQG problem with a nonconvex double-well terminal cost, Adam-CBO achieves value function estimates significantly closer to Monte Carlo reference values than the BSDE method (Han et al., 2018), with the gap widening as dimensionality increases. In the systemic risk mean-field control problem, Adam-CBO value function estimates for n=400 agents stay within 0.001–0.005 of the exact solution across all time steps (e.g., u(0,μ)=0.619 vs. exact 0.616 for Gaussian initial conditions). On Pendulum-v1 and CartPole-v1, Adam-CBO converges to the optimal policy faster in terms of learning steps than DDPG, PPO, SAC, TD3, TQC, and CrossQ, though at a higher wall-clock cost (1124.88 s vs. 145–709 s for baselines over 100,000 steps).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the BSDE method (Han et al., 2018), Adam-CBO outperforms in both convex and nonconvex LQG settings, with the advantage growing in higher dimensions (d=8, 16) and being most pronounced for nonconvex terminal costs where CBO's population-based exploration avoids local minima. In the RL benchmark (Pendulum-v1, CartPole-v1), Adam-CBO converges to the optimal policy in fewer learning steps than DDPG (Lillicrap, 2015), PPO (Schulman et al., 2017), SAC (Haarnoja et al., 2018), TD3, TQC, and CrossQ, though its wall-clock runtime is substantially higher (1124.88 s on Pendulum-v1 vs. 145.19 s for PPO and 288.83 s for DDPG). The authors note this runtime comparison is not strictly apples-to-apples, since PPO and SAC jointly optimize multiple networks (policy + value/Q-functions) while Adam-CBO optimizes only the policy. On CartPole-v1, Adam-CBO (3444 s) is slower than both PPO (150.58 s) and DQN (Mnih et al., 2015; 186.13 s) but still reaches the optimal policy faster in terms of training steps. The key differentiator is that Adam-CBO is gradient-free and model-free, making it applicable to finite-horizon problems where policy gradients are unavailable or high-variance, a setting where the gradient-based baselines (PPO, SAC, DDPG) are not directly applicable.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper applies the Consensus-Based Optimization (CBO) framework—originally developed for global optimization (Fornasier et al., 2024) and extended with adaptive momentum (Chen et al., 2022)—to the problem of high-dimensional, finite-horizon stochastic optimal control. It departs from two dominant paradigms: model-based methods (deep BSDE, HJB solvers) that require explicit transition kernels and suffer from the curse of dimensionality, and model-free gradient-based RL methods (PPO, SAC, DDPG) that rely on high-variance policy gradient estimates and are typically restricted to infinite-horizon settings. The key conceptual move is replacing gradient estimation with a population-based consensus mechanism where agents are pulled toward a Boltzmann-weighted average of their peers, with momentum and adaptive scaling providing exploration without any gradient computation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper fills a genuine gap: finite-horizon, model-free, high-dimensional SOC has been poorly served by existing methods, and this work provides both a practical algorithm (Adam-CBO) and the first convergence guarantee (for M-CBO) in this regime. The demonstrated scalability to 16-dimensional LQG, 32-dimensional Ginzburg-Landau, and 800-agent mean-field control problems, along with generalization across initial distributions without retraining, suggests broad applicability. However, the significantly higher computational cost (roughly 3–10× slower than gradient-based baselines in Table 4) and the fact that convergence theory covers only the simpler M-CBO variant (not Adam-CBO) are notable limitations. Open questions include extension to mean-field games, constrained and partially-observed control, and whether the batch-size sensitivity observed in training can be mitigated.
