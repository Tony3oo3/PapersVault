## Problem

Existing audio codec tokenizers (e.g., Encodec, SoundStream) encode individual audio frames independently without modeling cross-frame context, and they lack the semantic richness that autoregressive language models need for effective audio understanding and generation. The paper identifies through controlled experiments that both low bitrate (for training/inference efficiency) and semantic information (for LM modelability) are critical for audio language modeling, yet no prior codec achieves both simultaneously at very low frame rates (12.5 Hz).

## Input / Output

The model takes a single-channel 24 kHz audio waveform as input and produces two outputs: (1) a sequence of discrete tokens at 12.5 Hz frame rate with 3 RVQ layers and a 2048-entry codebook (37.5 tokens/sec, 0.41 kbps), and (2) a reconstructed waveform via the decoder. The patch size is 320 samples (75 frames per second before query compression), and the window size w (dynamically sampled from [2,10] during training) controls the compression ratio, enabling dynamic bitrate adjustment at inference. The discrete tokens are designed to be directly consumed by an autoregressive LLM (LLAMA 3.2 1B with LoRA) for tasks like TTS, ASR, and audio captioning.

## Method

ALMTokenizer introduces a query-based compression strategy where learnable [CLS] tokens are interleaved with audio frames at window-size w intervals, processed by a 24-layer causal transformer encoder, and then retrieved as compressed representations—explicitly modeling cross-frame context to capture holistic semantics at very low frame rates. To enrich semantic content, the VQ codebook is initialized with k-means clusters from wav2vec2 (speech) and BEATs (sound) encoder outputs and kept frozen, while a masked autoencoder loss (20–30% mask rate) encourages global information capture during training. An autoregressive prediction loss trains a lightweight continuous AR transformer to predict later VQ-layer quantized features from earlier layers, making the latent space more amenable to AR language modeling. Training follows a two-stage paradigm: Stage 1 trains a convolutional autoencoder with MAE loss (no quantization) to embed semantic structure in the Patchify module; Stage 2 initializes from Stage 1, freezes the Patchify, and trains the full model with reconstruction, adversarial (6-discriminator GAN), MAE, and AR losses.

## Results

On VCTK speech reconstruction at 0.41 kbps (12.5 Hz, 3 RVQ layers), ALMTokenizer achieves UTMOS 3.76, PESQ 2.0, STOI 0.81, and VISQOL 3.78, outperforming MimiCodec at the same bitrate (UTMOS 3.01, PESQ 1.5) and WavTokenizer (UTMOS 3.67, PESQ 1.9). For semantic information, ALMTokenizer attains ASR WER 18.3 and emotion recognition accuracy 29.0%, surpassing all codec baselines (next best: MimiCodec at 25.1% ASR WER) and approaching SSL models like WavLM (29.0% ER). In LM-based TTS on LibriSpeech test-clean, ALMTokenizer achieves WER 11.7 and UT-MOS 3.88, outperforming MimiCodec (WER 16.0, UT-MOS 2.93) and WavTokenizer (WER 18.5, UT-MOS 3.58), though trailing the single-layer semantic GLM4-Voice tokenizer (WER 9.9, UT-MOS 3.79).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against MimiCodec at the identical 0.41 kbps setting, ALMTokenizer improves UTMOS from 3.01 to 3.76, PESQ from 1.5 to 2.0, and ASR WER from 25.1 to 18.3, while matching emotion recognition (29.0% vs 28.0%). Versus MimiCodec at 1.1 kbps (8 RVQ layers), ALMTokenizer still wins on UTMOS (3.76 vs 3.65), DNS-MOS (3.64 vs 3.38), and ASR (18.3 vs 23.8) at roughly one-third the bitrate. Against WavTokenizer (0.48 kbps, 40 Hz), ALMTokenizer achieves comparable reconstruction (UTMOS 3.76 vs 3.67) but dramatically better semantics (ASR 18.3 vs 44.6, ER 29.0% vs 19.8%). StableCodec (0.4 kbps, 25 Hz) leads on UTMOS (4.22 vs 3.76) due to its denoising capability, but ALMTokenizer dominates on semantic metrics (ASR 18.3 vs 98.3, ER 29.0% vs 15.8%). In LM-based TTS (Table 4), ALMTokenizer (WER 11.7, UT-MOS 3.88) outperforms all codec baselines—MimiCodec (WER 16.0), WavTokenizer (WER 18.5), StableCodec (WER 22.7), DAC (WER 24.5), and Encodec (WER 22.9)—but trails the single-layer semantic GLM4-Voice tokenizer (WER 9.9, ASR 16.3) on robustness, indicating that pure semantic tokens still hold an edge for LM-based generation. On sound and music (Tables 2–3), ALMTokenizer surpasses Encodec, DAC, and WavTokenizer on both reconstruction and classification accuracy (sound: 44% vs 12–20%; music: 59% vs 45–54%), approaching SSL baselines BEATs (54%) and Wav2Vec2 (65%) on music classification.

## Relation to Prior Work

ALMTokenizer extends the encoder-quantizer-decoder codec framework established by Encodec and SoundStream, but departs from the frame-by-frame quantization paradigm by introducing a query-based compression strategy (inspired by BLIP2 and TiTok) that uses learnable query tokens to capture holistic cross-frame context via transformer layers. It also adapts the MAE training objective from vision (He et al., 2022) to audio codec training and incorporates semantic priors from wav2vec2 and BEATs to initialize VQ codebooks. The closest prior work is MimiCodec, which achieves low bitrate via WavLM distillation into the first VQ layer only; ALMTokenizer differentiates by encoding semantic information across all VQ layers, supporting both speech and general sound/music, and operating at a lower bitrate (0.41 vs. 1.1 kbps).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides empirical evidence that for audio language modeling, semantic richness in the tokenizer matters more than raw reconstruction quality—a finding that reframes how audio tokenizers should be evaluated and designed. The query-based compression strategy offers a practical mechanism for dynamic bitrate control at inference time, which is useful for deployment. However, limitations remain: ALMTokenizer still lags behind dedicated SSL models (HuBERT, WavLM) on ASR, reconstruction for sound and music at 0.41 kbps is challenging, and the two-stage training with discarded components (MAE encoder/decoder, AR transformer) adds complexity without clear reuse. Open questions include whether scaling the model (beyond 174M parameters) further closes the gap with SSL models, and whether the AR loss can be repurposed to initialize the depth transformer in downstream LM training.
