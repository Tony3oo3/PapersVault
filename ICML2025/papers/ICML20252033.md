## Problem

Cautious prediction systems that abstain when model confidence is low can be exploited by dishonest institutions to covertly discriminate against targeted groups by artificially suppressing confidence in specific input regions, while maintaining overall accuracy to evade standard auditing. No prior work had identified this specific abuse vector or provided a mechanism to cryptographically verify that reported confidence scores reflect genuine model uncertainty rather than malicious manipulation.

## Input / Output

The protocol takes a committed model M (IT-MAC authenticated), a public reference dataset D_ref that must cover the suspected uncertainty region, a number of confidence bins B, and a tolerated ECE threshold α. It produces a zero-knowledge proof that the model's bin-wise calibration error is below α, yielding a pass/fail verdict to the auditor while revealing nothing about model parameters or training data. The reference dataset can alternatively be hidden and provided by the service provider, trading off adversarial strength for lower data-gathering cost.

## Method

The attack (Mirage) fine-tunes a calibrated model with a hybrid loss: standard cross-entropy outside a designated uncertainty region X_unc, and KL divergence to a label-smoothed target distribution t_ε inside it, where t_ε biases probability mass toward the correct label by ε ∈ [0,1] to preserve accuracy while reducing confidence. The defense (Confidential Guardian) is a three-step ZKP protocol: (1) prove correct inference on all reference points using a ZKP of verified inference subroutine (Mystique/Wolverine), (2) assign each point to a confidence bin and accumulate per-bin count, confidence sum, and accuracy sum using zero-knowledge arrays, and (3) prove that |acc(B_m) − conf(B_m)|/|B_m| < α for every bin m. The key insight is that Mirage's underconfidence manifests as a systematic calibration deviation in the targeted confidence bin, making ECE a natural and ZKP-friendly (linear) detection statistic, while the cryptographic layer prevents the prover from fabricating confidence values and protects the model's intellectual property.

## Results

Mirage successfully reduces confidence in targeted regions while preserving accuracy: e.g., on CIFAR-100, accuracy drops only from 83.98% to 83.92% while ECE nearly triples from 0.0662 to 0.1821, and the calibration error in the targeted ε-bin reaches 0.5845. On tabular datasets (Credit, Adult), ECE increases by ~3× (0.0094→0.0292 and 0.0109→0.0234) with calibration errors of 0.1135 and 0.0916 in the target bin. Confidential Guardian's ZKP protocol runs at 0.033 sec/point (440.8 KB) on the Gaussian mixture and 0.42–0.73 sec/point (2.79–4.84 MB) on tabular datasets, making auditing practical for smaller models, though image models require ~333 sec/point and ~1.27 GB per point.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external baseline methods; instead, the central comparison is between a calibrated baseline model and the same model after Mirage fine-tuning. Across all five datasets, Mirage preserves accuracy to within 0.1–0.5% (e.g., CIFAR-100: 83.98%→83.92%; Credit: 91.71%→91.78%) while inflating ECE by 2.8–3.1×, confirming that the attack evades accuracy-based auditing. Confidential Guardian detects the induced miscalibration in every setting: the calibration error in the targeted ε-bin ranges from 0.0916 (Adult) to 0.5845 (CIFAR-100), well above the auditor's threshold α. Ablations (Table 2) show that lower ε values (0.10–0.15) produce the strongest attack (largest calibration gap, smallest confidence overlap ∩_ε ≈ 0.02–0.19) and are therefore easiest to detect, while higher ε (0.80) weakens both the attack and detectability (∩_ε up to 0.91 on UTKFace). At ε=0, label flips occur (e.g., CIFAR-100 Acc_unc drops to 6.11%), making the attack trivially detectable via accuracy checks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of selective classification, adversarial ML, and zero-knowledge cryptography. It departs from prior poisoning and backdoor attacks (which flip predictions or degrade performance) by preserving accuracy while selectively suppressing confidence, and from concurrent availability-attack work (Lorenz et al., 2023) by modeling an internal institutional threat rather than an external adversary. The Mirage loss repurposes the KL-divergence-to-label-smoothed-target idea from label smoothing (Szegedy et al., 2016) as an adversarial tool, while Confidential Guardian repurposes ZKP-of-verified-inference primitives (Weng et al., 2021b; Sun et al., 2024) from model-identity verification to calibration auditing. The theoretical feasibility result (Lemma 4.1) establishes that no ZKP of inference alone can prevent such manipulation, motivating the calibration-based detection strategy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals that abstention—a mechanism widely deployed in safety-critical domains (healthcare, finance, criminal justice)—can be weaponized for covert discrimination while evading accuracy-based audits, fundamentally challenging the assumption that cautious prediction is inherently protective. The ZKP-based Confidential Guardian offers a novel path to verifiable model auditing that protects institutional IP, but its practical adoption is constrained by computational cost for large models (333 s/point for ResNet-50) and the requirement that the reference dataset cover the targeted uncertainty region. Open questions include extending detection to non-neural model classes, handling cases where miscalibration has multiple causes, and developing policy frameworks that mandate such cryptographic audits in regulated settings.
