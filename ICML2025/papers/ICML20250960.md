## Problem

Existing PSRO variants for solving Nash equilibria in large-scale normal-form games lack an efficient, principled metric for evaluating strategy improvement. In zero-sum games, diversity-based exploration improves coverage but cannot deterministically guide strategies toward equilibrium; in general-sum games, multiple equilibria yield different rewards, yet prior methods do not consistently select higher-reward equilibria.

## Input / Output

The method takes a finite payoff matrix U (defining the normal-form game) and initial strategy populations P_i for each player as input. It iteratively produces mixed strategies (probability distributions over the pure action space) as the learned equilibrium, reporting exploitability (distance to Nash equilibrium) in zero-sum games and joint reward in general-sum games. The advantage function is computed entirely within the pure strategy space via matrix multiplication, making its cost comparable to a single best-response evaluation.

## Method

The core contribution is the Advantage function V_i(π_i) = U_i(π_i, BR(π_i) ∩ A_j), which measures a strategy's quality by its reward against its own best response. In symmetric zero-sum games, -V_i is convex with a unique global maximum of 0 at the Nash equilibrium, enabling deterministic convergence. The A-PSRO algorithm introduces a LookAhead module that generates new strategies as convex combinations (1-d)·θ_i + d·Δπ_i, where θ_i is the meta-equilibrium and Δπ_i is the pure strategy maximizing the advantage in that direction. For general-sum games, the advantage function is non-convex but its local maxima correspond to Nash equilibria; A-PSRO samples multiple initial points in the population's convex hull, runs fictitious play from each, and selects the meta-equilibrium with highest advantage to bias exploration toward higher-reward equilibria. The LookAhead module can be combined with existing diversity metrics (e.g., Expected Cardinality) to navigate cyclic dimensions, and the full framework extends to multi-player games via an optimistic equilibrium oracle.

## Results

In symmetric zero-sum games (AlphaStar, Transitive, Elo, Simplified Go), A-PSRO achieves a notable reduction in exploitability across all environments, sometimes by several orders of magnitude compared to baselines. A-PSRO requires fewer than 50 iterations to reach exploitability 10⁻² with total runtime under 1 minute, and fictitious play on A-PSRO populations needs only ~10³ iterations to reach exploitability 10⁻⁴ (vs. ~10⁴ for vanilla fictitious play). In two-player general-sum games (Advanced-Staghunt, Advanced-RSP, Randomly Generated Games), A-PSRO consistently converges to the highest-reward Nash equilibrium, while baselines often stagnate in suboptimal equilibria. The LookAhead module (advantage-based exploration) is the primary driver of improvement; removing it causes a significant performance decline across all games.

## Baselines & Comparisons

A-PSRO is compared against five PSRO variants: classic PSRO (Lanctot et al., 2017), Pipeline-PSRO (McAleer et al., 2020), DPP-PSRO (Perez-Nieves et al., 2021), UDF-PSRO (Liu et al., 2021), and PSD-PSRO (Yao et al., 2023). In zero-sum games, A-PSRO outperforms all diversity-based baselines (DPP-PSRO, UDF-PSRO, PSD-PSRO) in final exploitability, particularly in games with strong cyclic dimensions where diversity-based methods stagnate; however, A-PSRO shows slightly higher exploitability in early iterations due to incomplete strategy space coverage. In the Transitive game specifically, A-PSRO without the diversity module outperforms the full A-PSRO, indicating the diversity module is less beneficial in strongly transitive structures. In general-sum games, A-PSRO consistently achieves the highest joint reward, while all baselines converge to different (often suboptimal) equilibria. Computationally, A-PSRO's strategy exploration has O(n³) complexity (independent of population size) versus O(pn³+p²n²+p³n) for diversity-based methods, though A-PSRO's meta-game solving time is longer due to larger population sizes. Overall, A-PSRO wins on final exploitability in zero-sum games and on equilibrium reward in general-sum games, at the cost of slightly higher early-stage exploitability and longer meta-game solving time.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the PSRO (Policy Space Response Oracle) framework by introducing the Advantage function as a new evaluative metric for strategy improvement, complementing or replacing diversity-based objectives (Expected Cardinality, Behavioral Diversity, Policy Space Diversity) used in DPP-PSRO, UDF-PSRO, and PSD-PSRO. It adopts the pipeline improvement mechanism from Pipeline-PSRO for population management. The key conceptual departure is establishing a mathematical equivalence between advantage maximization and Nash equilibrium in zero-sum games, and showing that advantage local maxima correspond to Pareto-ranked equilibria in general-sum games, thereby unifying both settings under a single learning objective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

A-PSRO provides a theoretically grounded, unified framework that simultaneously reduces exploitability in zero-sum games and selects higher-reward equilibria in general-sum games—two problems previously addressed by separate algorithmic families. The convexity of the negative advantage function in zero-sum games yields deterministic convergence guarantees, while the Lipschitz continuity and non-convexity analysis in general-sum games clarify why gradient-based methods get trapped in suboptimal equilibria. Open questions include the computational overhead of multiple oracle calls in general-sum settings, the lack of convergence guarantees beyond zero-sum games, and the extension to large-scale extensive-form games (e.g., StarCraft, Go) where neural network approximations of the advantage function are needed.
