## Problem

Existing multi-bit LLM watermarking schemes face a fundamental trade-off: distribution-modifying methods (e.g., MPAC, Qu et al.) achieve high detectability but distort text quality and are vulnerable to watermark spoofing attacks, while distortion-free methods (e.g., Dipmark, ZMH) preserve stealthiness but lack robustness against text modifications. Moreover, zero-bit watermarks only flag AI-generated content without enabling provenance tracking (userID, timestamp, modelID), which is essential for accountability in real-world deployments.

## Input / Output

The encoder takes a text prompt, a 1024-bit secret watermark key (sk), and a multi-bit message structured as a MsgSeq of H chunks (each m bits, default m=1), and produces watermarked LLM-generated text of length L tokens. The texture key is derived from the last h=3 context n-grams at each generation step. At detection time, given the candidate text and sk, the decoder outputs a binary detection decision (via a z-score threshold) and the extracted MsgSeq, with extraction completing in ~0.01 s for 200-token texts.

## Method

StealthInk embeds multi-bit messages via a novel token-probability reweighting strategy over pseudorandom vocabulary permutations: for each generation step, a PRF (SHA-256) seeded with sk and the texture key produces a permutation θ, and the message bit M selects a red-list interval [γ_M|V|, (γ_M+γ)|V|] whose tokens are zeroed out while a complementary interval is doubled, preserving the expected distribution over random permutations (Theorem 4.4). Position allocation (borrowed from MPAC) pseudorandomly assigns each token to one of H message-chunk positions, enabling multi-bit embedding across the text. A per-query history log ensures permutation independence: when a texture key repeats, the original distribution is used, maintaining K-shot stealthiness without requiring a global shared history. Detection counts red-list tokens per position and per candidate M, selects the M minimizing the count at each position, and computes a z-score over the aggregated count to decide watermarked vs. non-watermarked.

## Results

On the text completion task (200 tokens, 24-bit message), StealthInk achieves an AUC of 0.978, TPR@1%FPR of 0.848, and bit accuracy of 0.875, with a median PPL of 7.14 (vs. 8.02 non-watermarked) and extraction time of 0.01 s. In contrast, MPAC reaches AUC 0.996 and bit accuracy 0.944 but inflates PPL to 10.19, while Qu et al. achieves near-perfect detection (AUC 1.0, bit accuracy 0.999) at the cost of PPL 42.63. Fernandez et al. is the weakest on detection (AUC 0.943, bit accuracy 0.61) and requires 113 s to extract a 24-bit message. Against spoofing attacks, StealthInk's forged texts are detected at only 29% TPR@10%FPR (Dolly-CW, H=1), compared to ~70% for MPAC and ~98% for Qu et al., confirming its stealthiness advantage.

## Baselines & Comparisons

StealthInk trades a modest amount of detection sensitivity for a large gain in text quality and security. Against MPAC (Yoo et al., 2024), StealthInk achieves lower AUC (0.978 vs. 0.996) and bit accuracy (0.875 vs. 0.944) at 24 bits, but its PPL is 7.14 versus MPAC's 10.19, and it is the only method that is provably stealthy—forged texts from StealthInk are detected at only 29% TPR@10%FPR versus ~70% for MPAC. Against Qu et al. (2024), the detection gap is larger (AUC 0.978 vs. 1.0, bit accuracy 0.875 vs. 0.999), but Qu et al.'s PPL of 42.63 is roughly 6× higher than StealthInk's, indicating severe text degradation; moreover, Qu et al.'s scheme is highly vulnerable to spoofing (98% of forgeries detected). Against Fernandez et al. (2023), StealthInk dominates on every axis: higher AUC (0.978 vs. 0.943), higher bit accuracy (0.875 vs. 0.61), comparable PPL (7.14 vs. 9.46), and 11,300× faster extraction (0.01 s vs. 113 s). On robustness to copy-paste attacks (ε=0.3), StealthInk retains 83.2% bit accuracy, competitive with MPAC (90.8%) and far above Fernandez et al. (51.9%), though Qu et al. remains nearly unaffected (99.9%). Under paraphrasing attacks all methods degrade sharply, with StealthInk at 61.2% (Dipper, ε=0.1) versus MPAC at 61.1% and Qu et al. at 63.4%.

## Relation to Prior Work

StealthInk sits at the intersection of two prior lines of work: (1) stealthy/unbiased zero-bit watermarking (Dipmark, ZMH) that preserves the original token distribution but cannot embed multi-bit messages, and (2) multi-bit watermarking schemes (MPAC, Qu et al., Fernandez et al.) that embed messages via logit bias or vocabulary shifts but distort the output distribution and are vulnerable to spoofing. The paper's core departure is a novel reweighting strategy—doubling probabilities in one permutation interval while zeroing another—that provably preserves the expected distribution (stealthiness) while enabling deterministic per-bit decoding, combined with MPAC's position-allocation mechanism for capacity. It also addresses the robustness gap left by distortion-free multi-bit schemes (Kordi Boroujeny et al., Zamir) by inheriting the statistical-test-based detection of reweighting methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As major LLM providers commit to watermarking, the ability to embed provenance metadata (userID, timestamp, modelID) while remaining statistically indistinguishable from unwatermarked text is a practical necessity for deployment; StealthInk is among the first schemes to jointly satisfy stealthiness, multi-bit capacity, and spoofing resistance with theoretical guarantees. The derived lower bound on tokens required for detection at a fixed equal error rate gives practitioners concrete capacity-planning guidance. Key limitations remain: paraphrasing attacks still cause severe bit-accuracy drops (0.92 → ~0.6), detectability at short sequence lengths lags behind non-stealthy baselines like MPAC, and the observed lower PPL of watermarked text (though explained by red-list filtering) warrants further scrutiny at scale. Open questions include whether the per-query history-log design scales to high-throughput API serving and whether the scheme can be made robust to semantic-level rewrites.
