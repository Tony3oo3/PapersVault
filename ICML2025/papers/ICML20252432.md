## Problem

Modern language models are distributions over token strings, not character strings, causing unintuitive behaviors when users condition on character-level prompts (e.g., a trailing space can swap the most likely completion). The paper identifies this as the 'prompt boundary problem' and asks: how can one correctly convert a token-level language model into a well-behaved character-level one that preserves relative probabilities regardless of where the prompt boundary falls?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252432/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained token-level language model p_Δ (any transformer LM with a strict-prefix monotone decoder κ, such as BPE or WordPiece) and an arbitrary character string prompt σ as input. It produces a full character-level interface: the conditional distribution over the next character (or EOS), the character-level prefix probability p̄_Σ(σ), and correctly conditioned token string generation. The key constraint is that κ must be strict-prefix monotone (each appended token adds at least one character), which holds for standard BPE and WordPiece tokenizers but not for stochastic ones like UnigramLM.

## Method

The core technical contribution is the notion of a *covering*: the finite set of minimal token strings whose decoded form has the prompt as a prefix. Under strict-prefix monotonicity, the infinite sum defining the character-level prefix probability reduces to a finite sum over this covering (Proposition 1). The paper provides an exact recursive enumeration algorithm (enum_cover) and a practical beam-based pruning heuristic (prune_top_K_buckets) that keeps at most K token strings modulo their last token, yielding O(N·K·|Δ|) running time. A bundled beam-summing implementation uses probability tries to filter next tokens character-by-character in bulk, improving constant factors. Correctness of the resulting conditional generation procedure is established in Proposition 2, and experiments across four LLMs (Llama-3.2-1B, Llama-3.1-8B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B, phi-4) show that even small beam sizes (K=8) achieve near-reference accuracy while significantly improving compression rate over the canonical tokenization baseline.

## Results

Across four models (Llama-3.2-1B, Meta-Llama-3.1-8B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B, phi-4) evaluated on the first 4000 bytes of wikitext-103-v1, the beam-summing method achieves a Jensen-Shannon distance of 0.00004–0.00005 JSD/byte at K=8 (vs. a K=128 reference), while running at 21–65 bytes/sec. The method also significantly improves compression: average surprisal drops from 0.66–0.93 bits/byte (canonical tokenization baseline) to 0.53–0.76 bits/byte, with the largest gain on Meta-Llama-3.1-8B (0.66 → 0.53 bits/byte). Error flattens after K≥8, indicating diminishing returns from larger beam sizes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: the canonically tokenized baseline (which assigns probability only to the single BPE tokenization of a string) and the token healing heuristic of Lundberg & Ribeiro (2023). On surprisal, the proposed method dramatically outperforms the canonical baseline across all four models—e.g., 0.532 vs. 0.66 bits/byte on Meta-Llama-3.1-8B and 0.761 vs. 0.93 on DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B—because it marginalizes over noncanonical tokenizations that the baseline discards. Against token healing, the proposed method wins by a wide margin on both surprisal (e.g., 0.533 vs. 2.116 bits/byte on Meta-Llama-3.1-8B) and approximation error (0.00016 vs. 0.1840 JSD/byte), while matching or exceeding its speed. The token healing baseline fails because backing up only one token is insufficient in the general case (e.g., it cannot recover 'd' after 'Hello,worl'), whereas the covering-based approach correctly identifies the highest-probability token string. Increasing K beyond 8 yields negligible gains in both JSD and surprisal, suggesting the language models concentrate probability mass on a small set of tokenizations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the token healing heuristic (Lundberg & Ribeiro, 2023), which the authors show is insufficient for the general case (e.g., the 'Hello,worl' example), and from the standard practice of conditioning token-level models via the canonical tokenization τ(σ), which they demonstrate is mathematically incorrect. The covering concept—a finite set of minimally covering token strings—is entirely new and provides the theoretical backbone for exact and approximate character-level probability computation. The work also connects to the broader literature on tokenization bias (Phan et al., 2024), marginalization over tokenizations (Cao & Rimell, 2021; Chirkova et al., 2023), and constrained decoding (Scholak et al., 2021; Willard & Louf, 2023; Koo et al., 2024), but formalizes the character-level interface problem in a way that prior work has not addressed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The prompt boundary problem affects every user and developer working with tokenized LLMs, and this paper provides the first principled, efficient solution with a clean theoretical foundation (the covering). The practical impact is broad: it enables correct character-level constraints, more accurate psycholinguistic surprisal computation for arbitrary substrings, and measurably better compression rates (e.g., ~16% surprisal reduction over canonical tokenization for Llama-3.2-1B). A key limitation is that the method requires large beam sizes for models trained with stochastic tokenizers (UnigramLM, BPE-Dropout), where probability mass is spread across many tokenizations, making the approach potentially expensive in that setting. Open questions include whether sampling-based alternatives can close this gap and whether the covering framework extends naturally to non-BPE tokenization schemes.
