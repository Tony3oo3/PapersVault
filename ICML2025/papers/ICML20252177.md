## Problem

MLPs used for implicit neural representations suffer from spectral bias, converging slowly on high-frequency components due to the uneven eigenvalue spectrum of the Neural Tangent Kernel. While recent training-dynamics adjustments (Fourier reparameterization, batch normalization) empirically alleviate this bias, the underlying mechanism linking kernel spectrum to spectral bias remains unexplained, and the NTK matrix itself is intractable for practical INR tasks with millions of data points. The paper asks: how can one purposefully and controllably adjust training dynamics to overcome spectral bias in a scalable way?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252177/cropped_page3_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a mini-batch of coordinate–label pairs where coordinates are 1D angles, 2D pixel positions (768×512 images), 3D voxel positions (512³ grids), or 5D ray points (3D location + 2D view direction). A key operational detail is the grouping strategy: data is partitioned into non-overlapping spatial patches (e.g., 32×32 for 2D, p adjacent coordinates for 1D, one ray for NeRF), and a single point per group is sampled to form a small eNTK matrix of size n×n where n ≪ N. The output is the INR's prediction at each coordinate: a scalar for 1D signals, an RGB triplet for images, a signed distance for 3D shapes, or color plus volume density for NeRF.

## Method

The paper derives from the linear dynamics model that the NTK eigenvalue spectrum directly controls per-frequency convergence rates, so balancing the spectrum should mitigate spectral bias. Since the NTK is intractable, Theorem 3.1 proves the empirical NTK (eNTK) is an asymptotically equivalent surrogate as network width grows. To handle the eNTK's quadratic size, the authors propose Inductive Gradient Adjustment (IGA): partition data into groups, compute the eNTK on one sampled point per group, construct a transformation matrix S̃_e that rescales eigenvalues to a common target (Eq. 5), and apply S̃_e to the residuals of all points in each group before backpropagation. Theorem 3.2 bounds the estimation error of this inductive generalization, showing it vanishes with increasing width and intra-group consistency. The number of balanced eigenvalues (the 'end' parameter) acts as a dial controlling the strength of spectral-bias correction, enabling tailored improvements across architectures (ReLU, PE, SIREN) and tasks.

## Results

On the Kodak 24 2D image approximation task, PE+IGA achieves the best average PSNR of 32.46 dB, outperforming PE+FR (31.65) and PE+BN (28.78), while also leading on frequency-aware metrics (SSIM 0.8822, MS-SSIM 0.9752, LPIPS 0.0938). For ReLU, IGA reaches 23.00 PSNR vs. 22.14 (FR) and 22.55 (BN); for SIREN, 33.48 vs. 32.61 (FR). On the Blender NeRF task, NeRF+IGA achieves 31.47 PSNR, nearly doubling the improvement of FR (31.35) and BN (31.37) over the vanilla baseline (31.23). On 3D shape representation, IGA yields the highest IOU (9.733e-1 for ReLU, 9.970e-1 for PE) and lowest Chamfer distance across all architectures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares IGA against two training-dynamics baselines: Fourier reparameterized training (FR, Shi et al. 2024a) and batch normalization (BN, Cai et al. 2024). IGA consistently outperforms both across all three INR architectures (ReLU, PE, SIREN) and all four vision tasks. On the most challenging PE architecture, IGA gains +0.81 dB PSNR over FR (32.46 vs. 31.65) and a substantial +3.68 dB over BN (32.46 vs. 28.78), which barely improves over vanilla (28.64). For ReLU, IGA leads FR by +0.86 dB and BN by +0.45 dB in PSNR, while also achieving the largest LPIPS reduction (0.5549 vs. 0.6315 for FR and 0.6182 for BN). On NeRF, IGA's 0.24 dB improvement over vanilla is nearly twice that of FR (0.12 dB) and BN (0.14 dB). The paper notes that FR and BN produce less stable gains across individual scenes, occasionally yielding suboptimal or negative results, whereas IGA delivers consistent improvements across almost all per-scene evaluations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the NTK linear dynamics framework (Arora et al. 2019; Lee et al. 2019; Du et al. 2018) to the INR setting, building directly on Geifman et al. (2023), who first proposed modifying the NTK spectrum to control convergence but were limited to toy two-layer networks on synthetic data. It departs from prior training-dynamics adjustments for INRs—Fourier reparameterized training (Shi et al. 2024a) and batch normalization (Cai et al. 2024)—which the authors characterize as 'serendipitous' in that they improve spectral bias without clear guidance on hyperparameter choice. The key conceptual departure is the inductive generalization step: rather than computing the full eNTK matrix (infeasible at INR scale), the method samples one point per local group, constructs a small eNTK-based transformation matrix, and generalizes it to the full population, making NTK-spectrum control practical for millions of data points.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

IGA provides the first principled, tunable mechanism for controlling spectral bias in INRs via gradient adjustment, replacing the ad-hoc nature of FR and BN with a knob (number of balanced eigenvalues) that directly governs the spectral range affected. The theoretical justification (eNTK approximates NTK in the wide limit; inductive generalization error vanishes with width) gives practitioners confidence in the approach, though the proofs are restricted to two-layer networks while experiments use four-layer MLPs. Open questions include whether the consistency bound ε (requiring intra-group similarity) degrades for highly non-uniform signals, whether the approach extends theoretically to deeper architectures, and whether more sophisticated sampling (clustering, segmentation) could further reduce the estimate error. The ~1.56× training-time overhead is modest but non-negligible for large-scale NeRF training.
