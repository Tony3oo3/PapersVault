## Problem

Static LLM benchmarks are saturating and susceptible to contamination, while expert-curated alternatives like GPQA cost over $120,000 for 500 questions. Crowdsourced platforms like Chatbot Arena generate real-world, open-ended queries but with highly variable difficulty that cannot be directly converted into challenging benchmarks. The paper addresses the need for an automated, scalable pipeline that continuously curates high-quality, human-aligned benchmarks from large crowdsourced datasets without manual annotation.

## Input / Output

The pipeline ingests 200,000 raw user prompts from Chatbot Arena (or 150,000 from WildChat-1M), filters out duplicates, multi-turn conversations, and non-English content, then produces a 500-prompt benchmark (Arena-Hard-Auto) by sampling 2 prompts from each of 250 high-quality topic clusters. For evaluation, model responses to these 500 prompts are judged pairwise against a fixed baseline (GPT-4-0314) by an LLM judge, yielding 1,000 judgments per model that are aggregated via the Bradley-Terry model into win rates with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals. The full curation cost is approximately $50 with GPT-4-Turbo or $45 with Llama-3-70B-Instruct, and per-model evaluation costs $20.

## Method

BenchBuilder operates in three stages: (1) topic modeling via BERTopic, where prompts are embedded with text-embedding-3-small, reduced with UMAP, and clustered with HDBSCAN (minimum cluster size 8) into ~4,000 topics; (2) LLM-based quality scoring, where GPT-4-Turbo (or Llama-3-70B-Instruct) assigns each prompt a 0–7 score based on seven predefined qualities (specificity, domain knowledge, complexity, technical accuracy, creativity, problem-solving, real-world application); and (3) cluster-level filtering and uniform sampling, discarding prompts scoring below 6 and clusters with mean score below 5, then sampling evenly across remaining clusters to ensure topical diversity. For evaluation, the paper uses LLM-as-a-Judge with chain-of-thought prompting, a two-game position-swap protocol, and a 5-point Likert scale, aggregated through an extended Bradley-Terry model that optionally incorporates style features (token length, markdown density) to control for stylistic confounders. The paper also introduces three new benchmark-quality metrics—Separability with Confidence, Agreement with Confidence, and Pair Rank Brier Score—that quantify both model discrimination and alignment with human preferences beyond what Spearman correlation captures.

## Results

Arena-Hard-Auto achieves 87.4% separability and 90.9% confidence agreement with Chatbot Arena rankings, substantially outperforming MT-Bench (22.6% separability, 26.6% agreement) and AlpacaEval 2.0 LC (83.2% separability, 82.5% agreement). When evaluated against the more in-distribution Chatbot Arena Hard Prompt leaderboard, Arena-Hard-Auto reaches 98.6% confidence agreement and 96.7% Spearman correlation with human preferences. The benchmark uses only 500 prompts at a cost of $20 per model evaluation, achieving 3× higher model separation than MT-Bench while producing tighter confidence intervals.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against MT-Bench, Arena-Hard-Auto achieves a dramatic improvement in separability (87.4% vs. 22.6%) and confidence agreement (90.9% vs. 26.6%), while Spearman correlation is comparable (93.2% vs. 89.9%)—the paper highlights this gap as evidence that Spearman fails to capture ranking variance among top models. Against AlpacaEval 2.0 LC, Arena-Hard-Auto wins on every metric: higher confidence agreement (90.9% vs. 82.5%), separability (87.4% vs. 83.2%), Spearman (93.2% vs. 91.9%), Kendall Tau (80.0% vs. 77.9%), and lower Brier Score (0.069 vs. 0.11), while using fewer prompts (500 vs. 800) at only 2× the cost. Against Chatbot Arena itself, Arena-Hard-Auto matches or exceeds its separability (87.4% vs. 85.8%) at a fraction of the cost ($20 vs. 'Very High'), and when compared to the in-distribution Hard Prompt subset, achieves 98.6% confidence agreement. The paper also demonstrates robustness: applying BenchBuilder to WildChat-1M produces Wild-Hard-Auto with 86.7% separability and 88.6% agreement, far exceeding a random 250-prompt baseline (75.6% and 36.4% respectively), confirming the pipeline generalizes across datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM-as-a-Judge evaluation paradigm established by MT-Bench (Zheng et al., 2023) and AlpacaEval (Dubois et al., 2024) by introducing an automated prompt curation pipeline (BenchBuilder) that replaces expensive manual benchmark construction with LLM-annotated quality scoring over crowdsourced data. It builds on the Bradley-Terry model for pairwise scoring and extends it with style-control regression features inspired by AlpacaEval's length control and Chatbot Arena's style-controlled leaderboard. The topic-modeling component (BERTopic + UMAP + HDBSCAN) repurposes standard NLP clustering techniques for the novel purpose of ensuring prompt diversity across a curated benchmark, while the proposed evaluation metrics (Separability with Confidence, Agreement with Confidence, Pair Rank Brier Score) go beyond the Spearman/Kendall correlations used in prior benchmark comparisons.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical bottleneck in LLM evaluation: the cost and time of manual benchmark curation (GPQA cost >$120K for 500 questions) versus the infeasibility of live human evaluation for frequent developer iterations. Arena-Hard-Auto's 98.6% correlation with human preferences at $20 per evaluation makes it a highly practical tool likely to be widely adopted, and the BenchBuilder pipeline generalizes to other datasets (demonstrated on WildChat-1M). Open questions remain around multi-turn and non-English evaluation, whether the seven quality dimensions fully capture prompt difficulty, and whether the pipeline's bias toward technical domains limits its applicability. The finding that standard Spearman correlation can mask severe ranking instability (MT-Bench: 89.9% Spearman vs. 22.6% confidence agreement) is a useful methodological caution for the field.
