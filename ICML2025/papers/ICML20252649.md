## Problem

While switchback experiments are widely used in ridesharing and other sequential A/B testing settings, the mechanisms by which carryover effects and reward autocorrelations jointly determine the optimal switching frequency remain unexplored. Prior analyses of switchback designs relied on simple importance sampling estimators within bandit frameworks, leaving open how state-of-the-art RL estimators (model-based, LSTD, DRL) behave under different designs and whether design recommendations are estimator-agnostic.

## Input / Output

The method consumes n days of sequential MDP trajectories (state S_{i,t} ∈ R^d, binary action A_{i,t}, scalar reward R_{i,t}) collected under a specified m-switchback design, where m controls the duration of each policy block and m=T recovers the alternating-day design. Beyond the ATE point estimate, the framework outputs a practitioner workflow that recommends the optimal m based on two diagnostic quantities: the magnitude of the carryover effect (measured by δ, the total variation distance between state transition functions under the two policies) and the sign/magnitude of reward residual autocorrelations. Confidence intervals are constructed via nonparametric bootstrap or cross-fitting, and the design recommendation is structured as a decision tree rather than a single closed-form optimum.

## Method

The paper models experimental data as a finite MDP with autocorrelated reward errors and derives a lower bound (Theorem 1) on the MSE difference between the alternating-day design and any m-switchback design, showing it decomposes into an autocorrelation term (sum of covariances σ_e over specific lag pairs) minus a carryover term proportional to δ·R_max². This bound is established uniformly across three estimator families—linear model-based OLS, LSTD with linear sieves, and DRL with cross-fitting—making the design recommendation estimator-agnostic. Under stationary autoregressive, moving-average, or exchangeable covariance structures (Corollaries 1–3), the autocorrelation term is shown to be monotonically decreasing in m when errors are positively correlated, formally proving that more frequent switching improves efficiency. The key insight is that the SB design's alternating-sign structure cancels positively correlated errors in the ATE estimator, an advantage absent in the AD design where all residuals enter with the same sign. Numerical experiments on synthetic linear/nonlinear DGPs and wild-bootstrap simulations from two ridesharing cities confirm the theory and demonstrate that RL-based estimators (DRL, LSTD) substantially outperform sequential IS, burn-in difference-in-mean, and simple IS baselines.

## Results

Theorem 1 establishes that the MSE difference between alternating-day (AD) and m-switchback (SB) designs is governed by two terms: an autocorrelation term (proportional to the sum of reward-error covariances at odd lags) and a carryover term (proportional to δ·R²_max/n). In synthetic experiments (T=48, d=3, n=16–52), with weak carryover (δ=0) and positive autocorrelation (ρ=0.3–0.9), the RMSE of OLS, LSTD, and DRL estimators decreases monotonically as m shrinks from 48 (AD) to 1 (most frequent switching), with the AD-vs-SB gap widening as ρ increases. When carryover grows (δ up to 0.5), AD regains efficiency. In real-data-based simulations (40 days, ridesharing), coverage probabilities exceed 92% at the nominal level, and average CI width is minimized by m=1 for small effect sizes (λ≤2%) but by AD (m=T) when λ reaches 10–15%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks three RL-based estimators (OLS model-based, LSTD, DRL) against three non-RL baselines in Figure 5: (i) the sequential multi-step IS estimator of Bojinov et al. (2023), (ii) the burn-in difference-in-mean estimator of Hu & Wager (2022), and (iii) the simple IS estimator of Xiong et al. (2024). All three RL-based estimators consistently achieve lower log-MSE than both Bojinov et al. and Hu & Wager across all combinations of n, m, and ρ in both linear and nonlinear DGPs. DRL and LSTD significantly outperform the simple IS estimator of Xiong et al. in most settings, with the gap being largest at small m (frequent switching) where the simple IS estimator suffers from severe bias due to unmodeled carryover. The paper notes that IS-type estimators are 'prone to large MSEs' (Appendix C), confirming that the RL framework's ability to model state transitions is critical for accurate ATE estimation under temporal dependence.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the switchback design literature (Bojinov et al. 2023, Xiong et al. 2024, Hu & Wager 2022) by moving from simple importance-sampling estimators in bandit frameworks to a full MDP setting with state-of-the-art RL estimators (model-based OLS, LSTD, and DRL). It builds directly on the MDP formulation of Puterman (2014), the LSTD methodology of Bradtke & Barto (1996), and the doubly-robust DRL estimator of Kallus & Uehara (2020), unifying them under a single design-comparison framework. The key departure from prior work is the demonstration that the optimal switchback design is estimator-agnostic and governed by the interplay between carryover effect magnitude and reward autocorrelation structure, rather than by the specific estimator used.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic theoretical and empirical characterization of when off-policy switchback designs outperform on-policy alternating-day designs, yielding a practical three-step workflow (check Markov assumption → assess carryover magnitude → examine error correlations) for practitioners at ridesharing and similar platforms. The counterintuitive finding that more frequent policy switching improves estimation efficiency under positive autocorrelation challenges the standard RL heuristic that off-policy estimation is inherently less efficient. Open questions include extending the analysis to POMDP settings (where the MDP assumption is severely violated), accommodating spatial-temporal interference simultaneously, and developing adaptive designs that learn the optimal m online rather than requiring prior knowledge of the autocorrelation structure.
