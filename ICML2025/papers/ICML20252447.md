## Problem

Message-passing GNNs lose critical local structural and feature information through iterative aggregation, a limitation that is especially severe in heterophilous graphs where neighboring nodes often have different labels. While the Euler Characteristic Transform (ECT) is a powerful, efficiently-computable topological invariant known to be invertible, all prior work has applied it only globally to entire shapes or graphs, leaving local neighborhood structure unexploited for graph learning tasks.

## Input / Output

The method takes a featured graph (nodes with feature vectors in R^n connected by edges) and a neighborhood radius k, embedding the graph into R^n via its node features. For each node, it produces an m·l-dimensional vector (with m=64 sampled directions on S^{n-1} and l=64 uniform filtration steps) encoding the Euler characteristic of sublevel sets in the k-hop neighborhood. These per-node vectors, concatenated with the original feature vector, are fed into a downstream classifier (XGBoost) to yield node class labels; for the spatial-alignment task, the method outputs a rotation matrix in SO(n) that re-orientates one embedded complex to match another.

## Method

The core contribution is the Local Euler Characteristic Transform (ℓ-ECT): for each node x in a featured graph G embedded in R^n, the ECT is computed on the full subcomplex spanned by x's k-hop neighbors, scanning m uniformly-sampled directions and recording the Euler characteristic at l filtration thresholds to produce a lossless m·l-dimensional fingerprint of the local neighborhood. Theoretically, the ECT's invertibility guarantees that the 1-hop ℓ-ECT encodes all information needed for one message-passing step (Theorem 1), and that global ECTs distinguish non-isomorphic featured graphs (Theorem 2), enabling subgraph counting that message-passing GNNs provably cannot perform. The approach is entirely model-agnostic—no GNN architecture is required—so the resulting vectors can be consumed by interpretable classifiers like XGBoost, which also expose feature-importance values linked back to specific ECT directions. Additionally, the authors construct a rotation-invariant metric d_ECT (Theorem 3) by taking the infimum over SO(n) of the L∞ distance between ECT vectors, enabling efficient spatial alignment of geometric graphs without computing all pairwise point distances.

## Results

On heterophilous node-classification benchmarks, the combined ℓ-ECT₁+ℓ-ECT₂ model achieves 81.1% accuracy on Roman Empire and 49.8% on Amazon Ratings, outperforming the best message-passing baselines (GraphSAGE at 77.4% and GAT at 44.6%, respectively) by 3.7 and 5.2 percentage points. On the WebKB collection, ℓ-ECT₁ reaches 81.2% on Wisconsin, surpassing the strongest baseline (GraphSAGE at 72.9%) by over 8 points. On homophilous Planetoid datasets, ℓ-ECT methods are competitive (e.g., 90.2% on PubMed vs. GIN's 87.0%) while remaining model-agnostic, using only an XGBoost classifier without task-specific GNN tuning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares ℓ-ECT against five standard GNN baselines: GCN, GAT, GIN, GraphSAGE, and the heterophily-specific H2GCN. ℓ-ECT₁+ℓ-ECT₂ decisively outperforms all five on the two primary heterophilous benchmarks (Roman Empire: 81.1% vs. best baseline 77.4%; Amazon Ratings: 49.8% vs. 44.6%), with particularly large margins over H2GCN (64.2% on Roman Empire) and GIN (56.8%). On WebKB datasets, ℓ-ECT₁ beats GraphSAGE by 8.3 points on Wisconsin (81.2% vs. 72.9%) and H2GCN by 11 points, while remaining competitive with GraphSAGE on Cornell and Texas. On homophilous Planetoid graphs, ℓ-ECT methods trail GCN/GAT by 0.3–0.5 points on Cora and CiteSeer but surpass all baselines on PubMed (90.2% vs. 87.0% for GIN). On the Amazon co-purchase graphs, ℓ-ECT₁+ℓ-ECT₂ matches or slightly exceeds GAT on Photo (94.9% vs. 94.8%) but falls short on Computers (92.2% vs. 92.4%). Overall, the critical difference diagram confirms that even the weakest ℓ-ECT variant (ℓ-ECT₂) outperforms the best non-ℓ-ECT method (GAT) in average rank across all 13 datasets, and no baseline achieves a statistically significant advantage over ℓ-ECT-based methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Euler Characteristic Transform (ECT) from Turner et al. (2014) and its invertibility guarantees (Curry et al., 2022; Ghrist et al., 2018) from a global shape descriptor to a local neighborhood fingerprint for graph learning. Prior ECT-based deep learning work (Roell & Rieck, 2024) applied only global ECTs to shape classification; here, the authors construct per-node local ECTs and show they subsume the information needed for a single message-passing step (Theorem 1) while additionally enabling subgraph counting (Theorem 2), a capability proven impossible for standard MPNNs (Chen et al., 2020). The method is positioned as a model-agnostic alternative to message-passing GNNs, deliberately avoiding the aggregation-induced information loss that plagues GCN/GAT/GIN architectures, particularly under heterophily.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical implication is that a non-learnable topological descriptor paired with a simple classifier (XGBoost) can match or exceed specialized GNNs across homophilous and heterophilous benchmarks, offering an interpretable, architecture-agnostic pipeline for node classification. However, the computational cost of computing per-node ECTs scales with neighborhood size and graph density, limiting applicability to very large graphs, and the experiments are confined to small-to-medium datasets (WebKB, Planetoid, Amazon co-purchase). Open questions include efficient computation at scale, integration of local ECTs into hybrid message-passing architectures, and extension to higher-order simplicial complexes where the method's topological grounding is most natural.
