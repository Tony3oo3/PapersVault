## Problem

The Learn-then-Test (LTT) framework provides finite-sample statistical guarantees for hyperparameter selection but is restricted to non-adaptive multiple hypothesis testing, preventing data-dependent sequential exploration and early termination. This limitation makes LTT inefficient in settings where each testing round is costly or carries safety risk, such as online policy validation or LLM prompt evaluation, because all rounds must be completed before any decision is made.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252965/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a discrete candidate hyperparameter set Λ of size N, a per-instance risk function R(λ,Z)∈[0,1] evaluated via held-out data or real-world interaction, a user-specified reliability threshold α, an error tolerance δ, and optional prior knowledge D⁰. It outputs a subset Λ̂^rel ⊆ Λ that is guaranteed to be (α,δ)-FWER- or (α,δ)-FDR-controlling, along with an adaptive stopping time T that can terminate calibration early once at least d reliable hyperparameters are identified. Unlike LTT, which returns an empty set until the fixed horizon T is reached, aLTT produces a non-empty prediction set at every round, enabling intermediate decisions.

## Method

aLTT replaces LTT's p-value-based MHT with sequential e-process-based MHT. For each null hypothesis H_i: R(λ_i)>α, a valid e-value E_i = 1+μ_i(α−R(λ_i,Z)) is computed per round, and the e-process E_i^t is the cumulative product of e-values across all rounds in which λ_i was tested. Anytime-valid p-values are derived as P_i^t = 1/max_{τ≤t} E_i^τ, enabling FWER control via standard procedures (Bonferroni, fixed-sequence) or FDR control via the e-Benjamini-Hochberg rule applied directly to e-values. An adaptive ε-greedy acquisition policy selects which hyperparameters to test next based on current e-process values, and the calibration horizon is adaptive, stopping as soon as the prediction set reaches a minimum cardinality d. The betting parameter μ_i^t can follow adaptive strategies (aGRAPA, online Newton step) or fixed values, with aGRAPA showing the best empirical performance.

## Results

On the Half Cheetah offline RL task (T=5000 rounds, δ=0.1), aLTT with ε-greedy acquisition (ε=0.25) achieves a true positive rate (TPR) of 0.85 under both FWER and FDR control, compared to 0.32 (FWER) and 0.4 (FDR) for the non-adaptive LTT baseline. On the Instruction Induction prompt engineering benchmark, aLTT (ε=0.25) identifies 50% of reliable instructions within the first 1000 testing rounds, while the non-adaptive procedure identifies fewer than 10% in the same budget; LTT's final TPR is less than half that of aLTT. Under quantile risk control (q=0.1, α=0.57), aLTT identifies up to 75% of reliable policies, whereas LTT fails to identify any.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is LTT (Angelopoulos et al., 2021), the original non-adaptive learn-then-test procedure using p-value-based MHT with a fixed calibration horizon. aLTT dominates LTT across every application and metric reported: on Half Cheetah RL, aLTT (ε=0.25) reaches TPR 0.85 versus LTT's 0.32–0.40 at T=5000, a 2–2.6× improvement; on prompt engineering, aLTT achieves roughly 2× the final TPR of LTT and identifies 50% of reliable prompts by round 1000 where LTT has not yet produced any output; under quantile risk control, aLTT finds up to 75% of reliable policies while LTT finds none. The gap narrows as ε increases toward 1 (fully random acquisition), where aLTT converges to LTT's performance at T, confirming that the adaptive acquisition policy is the source of the efficiency gain. In the wireless resource allocation experiments (Appendix B.2), aLTT also consistently returns policies with lower energy-delay product and lower average delay than LTT under both FWER and FDR control.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Learn-then-Test (LTT) framework of Angelopoulos et al. (2021), which casts hyperparameter selection as a non-adaptive multiple hypothesis testing problem using p-values and FWER/FDR-controlling procedures. The key methodological departure is replacing p-value-based MHT with e-process-based sequential MHT (building on Vovk & Wang, 2021; Waudby-Smith & Ramdas, 2024; Xu et al., 2021), which supports data-dependent acquisition policies and anytime-valid early termination. The e-value construction for bounded risk variables follows Waudby-Smith & Ramdas (2024), and the FDR-controlling e-Benjamini-Hochberg procedure follows Wang & Ramdas (2022). The paper positions itself as a data-efficiency improvement over LTT rather than a new calibration paradigm, and it is complementary to conformal prediction methods that calibrate a single threshold hyperparameter.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant in settings where each testing round is expensive or carries safety risk (e.g., deploying a control policy in a physical environment, or executing an LLM call): aLTT achieves comparable true positive rates to LTT while requiring only a fraction of the testing rounds, and can terminate early once a sufficient number of reliable hyperparameters are identified. The paper demonstrates this in two concrete applications—offline RL policy selection (Half Cheetah) and automated prompt engineering (Llama3)—where LTT often returns empty or uninformative sets while aLTT identifies reliable candidates well before the calibration horizon. Open questions flagged by the authors include extending the framework to distribution shift, data reuse across rounds, and simulation-aided calibration, all of which would broaden applicability beyond the i.i.d. testing assumption.
