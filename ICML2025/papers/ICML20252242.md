## Problem

Fine-tuning CLIP for downstream tasks in out-of-distribution scenarios leads to inconsistent predictions between training and test domains, and can even degrade CLIP's zero-shot ability for unseen classes. The paper identifies the root cause from a causal perspective: CLIP's prediction mechanism relies on both environment-invariant and environment-variant latent factors, and the variant factors shift across environments, breaking the causal mechanism that the predictor learned.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252242/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes frozen CLIP image embeddings (from an RGB image) and text embeddings (from class prompts like 'a photo of a [CLASS]') as input, and produces a class label as output. Crucially, it does not retrain the CLIP backbone; instead, it learns a linear projection matrix A_inv that maps both image and text embeddings into a shared invariant subspace where prediction is performed. Interventional data—generated via data augmentation (color jitter, grayscale, Gaussian blur, etc.) on images or via GPT-4o-modified text captions—serves as the training signal for estimating A_inv.

## Method

CLIP-ICM models CLIP's prediction process with a Structural Causal Model decomposing latent factors into invariant (Z_inv) and variant (Z_var) components, then proves that CLIP embeddings are an invertible linear transformation of the true latent space (Proposition 5.3). This guarantees the existence of a linear projection A_inv that maps CLIP embeddings to the invariant subspace, where the causal mechanism P(y|do(z_inv)) remains unchanged across environments (Proposition 5.1). The projection is estimated by solving a constrained optimization (Equation 9) that simultaneously enforces invariance on interventional data pairs (differences vanish under A_inv) and preserves maximum information from the original embedding. Interventional data is collected either by applying data augmentations that preserve Z_inv while altering Z_var, or by using GPT-4o to generate modified text descriptions that change variant features while keeping class-relevant content fixed. At inference, both image and text embeddings are projected through A_inv before computing cosine-similarity-based classification, yielding an invariant predictor with provably lower worst-case OOD risk (Theorem 5.6).

## Results

On the DomainBed benchmark (domain shift setting), CLIP-ICM achieves an average accuracy of 77.1% across PACS, VLCS, OfficeHome, Terra Incognita, and DomainNet, outperforming all prior CLIP fine-tuning methods. On Terra Incognita, CLIP-ICM Linear Probe surpasses the previous best method CLIPOOD by 6 percentage points. In the combined domain-shift + open-class setting (Table 3), CLIP-ICM's average accuracy on new classes exceeds CoOp by 5.5%, demonstrating that the method preserves CLIP's zero-shot capability even after fine-tuning. Ablations show that image-based interventional data outperforms text-based data alone, and combining both yields the best results.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CLIP-ICM outperforms all compared CLIP fine-tuning baselines on the DomainBed average. On Terra Incognita, CLIP-ICM Linear Probe (52.5%) surpasses CLIPOOD (Shu et al., 2023) by 6 percentage points, the largest single-dataset gain. In the open-class setting (Table 3), CLIP-ICM's average accuracy on new classes exceeds CoOp (Zhou et al., 2022a) by 5.5%, while CoOp and CoCoOp (Zhou et al., 2022b) degrade significantly on unseen classes after fine-tuning. CLIP-Adapter (Gao et al., 2023) and Domain Prompt (Zhang et al., 2023) achieve competitive results on PACS and VLCS but fall behind CLIP-ICM on the harder Terra Incognita and DomainNet splits. The mutual-information regularization baseline (Cha et al., 2022) is outperformed across all five DomainBed datasets. Among CLIP-ICM variants, the full method (combining image and text interventional data) consistently achieves the highest average, with image-based data alone (CLIP-ICM*) outperforming text-based data alone (CLIP-ICM†) by approximately 1.4 points on average.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of causal representation learning and vision-language model adaptation. It builds directly on CLIP's contrastive pretraining framework (Radford et al., 2021) and adapts the interventional causal representation learning technique of Ahuja et al. (2023) to the specific setting of CLIP's dual-encoder architecture. The key conceptual departure from prior OOD generalization work (IRL, domain-adversarial training, prompt learning) is the provision of a theoretical identifiability result: under a mild condition on CLIP's training objective, the embedding space is a linear transformation of the true latent factors, making a linear projection to the invariant subspace both exist and estimable from interventional data. This contrasts with prior causal representation learning results that typically require nonlinear identifiability assumptions or purely observational settings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical appeal is that CLIP-ICM requires no backbone retraining—only a linear projection matrix estimated from augmented or LLM-modified text data—making it a lightweight drop-in improvement for CLIP-based pipelines facing domain shift. However, several assumptions limit generality: the claim that data augmentation preserves Z_inv while altering Z_var is strong and dataset-dependent, the mutual information condition I(Z_inv; Z) > c in Theorem 5.6 is difficult to verify empirically, and the method's reliance on GPT-4o for text-based interventional data introduces cost and potential bias. Open questions include whether the linear identifiability result extends to non-CLIP vision-language models, whether the invariant subspace dimension D_inv can be learned automatically, and whether the approach generalizes to tasks beyond classification (e.g., detection, segmentation).
