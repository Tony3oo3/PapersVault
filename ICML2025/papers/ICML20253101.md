## Problem

Existing unsupervised skill discovery methods (DIAYN, DADS, LSD, METRA) assume skills are uniformly distributed and explore the skill space indiscriminately, ignoring whether a given skill has already been mastered by the policy. This leads to inefficient exploration, especially in high-dimensional and skill-asymmetric environments, where the agent wastes timesteps re-sampling converged skills instead of focusing on under-converged ones. The paper asks: can skill discovery be made regret-aware, so that exploration is adaptively directed toward skills where the policy still has room to improve?

## Input / Output

The agent policy takes a continuous environment state s ∈ R^n (e.g., 29-dim for Ant/Antmaze, 4-dim for Maze2d) and a non-unit skill vector z ∈ R^d (d=2 in main experiments) as input, and outputs a continuous action a ∈ R^m. Unlike prior work that restricts z to the unit sphere, RSD samples z from an unbounded latent space and normalizes it relative to the current state representation (z_updated = (z − φ(s_t))/||z − φ(s_t)||) to preserve magnitude information while keeping the inner-product scale stable. The skill generator outputs a Gaussian distribution over z, and the full skill population P_z is maintained as a Gaussian Mixture Model with up to 15 components, each with an adaptive sampling probability.

## Method

RSD frames skill discovery as a min-max adversarial game: the agent policy π_θ1 (trained with SAC) minimizes regret, estimated as the value-function difference V_k(s_0|z) − V_{k−1}(s_0|z) between consecutive training stages, while a skill generator policy π_θ2 (trained with policy gradient) maximizes this regret by proposing challenging skills. The representation function φ(·) is constrained to a bounded space via tanh (||φ||∞ ≤ 1) with an additional per-step Lipschitz constraint enforced through a dual variable, and the intrinsic reward is the decrease in distance to the skill target: r = ||z − φ(s_t)|| − ||z − φ(s_{t+1})||. To prevent degeneration, RSD maintains an updatable population of skill generators (a GMM) and regularizes π_θ2 with two terms: a KL-divergence penalty against the existing population for diversity, and a proximity regularizer that keeps new skill distributions close to observed state representations to avoid sampling skills far beyond the agent's capability frontier. A greedy adaptive sampling scheme further rebalances component probabilities based on recent trajectory progress.

## Results

On zero-shot goal-conditioned navigation, RSD achieves the best success rate (AR) and lowest final distance (FD) in both Maze2d-large (AR=0.811, FD=0.535) and Antmaze-large (AR=0.507, FD=6.42). The improvement is most pronounced in the higher-dimensional Antmaze-large environment, where RSD outperforms the strongest baseline METRA-f by 12.7% in AR (0.507 vs. 0.450) and by 35% in FD (6.42 vs. 9.90). In the lower-dimensional Maze2d-large, RSD still leads METRA-d by 3.7% in AR and reduces FD by 18.7% (0.535 vs. 0.658).

## Baselines & Comparisons

RSD outperforms all four baselines on both metrics (FD and AR) in both Maze2d-large and Antmaze-large. Against the closest competitor METRA-d (dynamic z), RSD gains 3.7% AR in Maze2d-large (0.811 vs. 0.782) and a more substantial 16.5% AR in Antmaze-large (0.507 vs. 0.435), confirming the paper's claim of ~15% zero-shot improvement in high-dimensional settings. Against METRA-f (fixed z), the gap widens further in Antmaze-large: RSD achieves 0.507 AR versus 0.450, a 12.7% relative gain. DIAYN and DADS, which lack temporal representation learning, struggle severely in high-dimensional Antmaze-large (AR≈0.043, FD≈23.7), while LSD degrades from competitive performance in Maze2d-large (AR=0.304) to a clear loss in Antmaze-large (AR=0.362 vs. RSD's 0.507). The paper also reports that RSD achieves higher state coverage (CoverCoords) in skill-asymmetric environments (Maze2d-large, Antmaze-medium, Antmaze-large) compared to all baselines, though in the skill-symmetric Ant environment its coverage is slightly lower than METRA due to sampling from a broader non-unit-vector skill space.

## Relation to Prior Work

RSD builds directly on METRA and LSD for temporal representation learning in unsupervised skill discovery, but departs from their core assumption that skills should be sampled uniformly from the latent space. Instead, it reframes skill discovery as a min-max adversarial game where a regret-aware skill generator (RSG) actively proposes skills that maximize the agent's learning regret, drawing conceptual inspiration from automatic curriculum learning (PLR, regret-based environment design). The bounded representation space with non-unit skill vectors extends METRA's Lipschitz-constrained formulation to preserve magnitude information, and the population of skill generators (modeled as a GMM) addresses the degeneration and forgetting problems that plague single-generator approaches in prior work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The regret-aware framing provides a principled mechanism for adaptive skill sampling that improves both efficiency and diversity without requiring additional intrinsic exploration rewards (e.g., RND), which is particularly valuable in high-dimensional environments where such rewards become unstable. However, the evaluation is limited to relatively simple 2D maze environments and the Ant robot (state dim 29), leaving open whether the approach scales to truly high-dimensional continuous control or vision-based settings. The paper also acknowledges that skill selection for downstream tasks in changing environments remains underexplored, and future integration with hierarchical RL architectures is identified as a natural next step.
