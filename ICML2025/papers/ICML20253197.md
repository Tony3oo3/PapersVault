## Problem

LLM-judges have become popular for automatic evaluation in chat and alignment settings, but their effectiveness as evaluators in test-time scaling procedures—where they actively guide generation via reranking, beam search, or critique-based refinement—remains largely unexplored. Existing benchmarks like RewardBench evaluate judges on fixed pairwise comparisons from different generators, which does not capture the harder task of distinguishing responses from the same generator under realistic test-time compute budgets.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20253197/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes 10 pre-computed responses per query (1 greedy + 9 sampled at temperature 1.0, top-p 0.95) from 8 generator models (6.7B–72B) across 8 datasets in math, code, and instruction following, along with a judge model (7B–70B). For reranking and beam search, the output is a normalized helpfulness score h = (p_judge − p_greedy)/(p_oracle − p_greedy), where negative values indicate the judge is worse than greedy decoding. For refinement, the output is an effective improvement ratio δ^(Eff) = min(δ^(RR), δ^(G)), requiring the refined response to beat both reranking and greedy to be considered useful. A key design constraint is that all responses being compared come from the same generator, eliminating stylistic shortcuts available in cross-generator benchmarks.

## Method

JETTS defines three test-time scaling tasks to evaluate LLM-judges: (1) response reranking via pairwise round-robin (with order-swapping for consistency) or single-instance rating (Likert or additive rubric), (2) (10,2)-step-level beam search where the judge reranks partial line-by-line responses at each step with a special prompt note about incomplete generation, and (3) (1,9)-critique-based refinement where the judge's natural language critique is fed back to the generator for iterative revision. The benchmark evaluates 10 judge models (SFR-Judge, Skywork-Critic, OffsetBias, Prometheus, Self-Taught-Evaluator, Themis) against 8 generators (Llama-3.1, Qwen-2.5, DeepSeek-Math/Coder) and includes outcome reward models (ORMs) and a process reward model (PRM) as baselines. A key finding is that pairwise reranking performance correlates strongly with beam search performance, suggesting a shared underlying 'fundamental judging capability,' while single-rating protocols suffer from over-leniency producing many tied top responses. The benchmark reveals that judges are competitive with ORMs in reranking but consistently lag PRMs in beam search, and that natural language critiques currently fail to improve generator performance across all task categories.

## Results

On the JETTS leaderboard (normalized helpfulness), the best LLM-judge for response reranking is Skywork-Critic-70B at 0.177, outperforming the best outcome reward model (BestRM) at 0.113. For step-level beam search, the best judge (SFR-Judge-70B, 0.138) lags significantly behind the 7B process reward model (BestPRM, 0.195). For critique-based refinement, no judge achieves an effective improvement ratio above 1.0 (the greedy baseline), with the best being Themis-8B at 0.996, indicating that natural language critiques currently fail to improve generator performance. Across all tasks, code generation is the hardest domain, with most judges showing negative helpfulness at all size ratios.

## Baselines & Comparisons

In response reranking, 70B judges (Skywork-Critic-70B at 0.177, SFR-Judge-70B at 0.171) substantially outperform the best outcome reward model (BestRM at 0.113), while 8B judges (SFR-Judge-8B at 0.035, Skywork-Critic-8B at 0.039) are competitive with or slightly below BestRM. In beam search, all LLM-judges are consistently outperformed by the 7B Qwen2.5-Math-PRM (0.195), with the best judge (SFR-Judge-70B) achieving only 0.138 — a 29% relative gap — confirming that process-level judgment remains out-of-distribution for generative judges. For refinement, all three capable judges (SFR-Judge, Prometheus, Themis) fail to exceed the greedy baseline (ratio < 1.0), with instruction following suffering the largest degradation (up to 10%). Compared to majority-vote (self-consistency) on math, no judge reliably outperforms this simple heuristic except SFR-Judge-70B on small-to-medium generators. The pairwise round-robin protocol consistently outperforms single-instance rating (Likert or additive rubric) across nearly all datasets, though at O(N²) inference cost versus O(N).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two active research threads: test-time scaling (Snell et al., 2024; Jaech et al., 2024), which typically relies on scalar reward models for reranking and beam search, and LLM-as-judge evaluation (Zheng et al., 2023; Dubois et al., 2024b), which has been benchmarked primarily through fixed pairwise preference sets like RewardBench and PPE. JETTS departs from these judge benchmarks by measuring the actual improvement a judge brings to a generator model across three test-time scaling tasks (reranking, step-level beam search, and critique-based refinement) rather than judgment accuracy on curated pairs. It also extends the self-refinement literature (Reflexion, SCORE, CriticEval) by systematically testing whether judge critiques actually improve generator outputs, and by comparing judges directly against outcome and process reward models under identical compute budgets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that the touted advantage of LLM-judges—natural language critiques—is currently ineffective: no evaluated judge achieves a beneficial improvement ratio on refinement, and critiques tend to over-focus on stylistic features rather than substantive errors. This finding, combined with the observation that judges are consistently outperformed by small process reward models in beam search, suggests that practitioners should not assume judges can replace RMs in test-time scaling pipelines. Open questions include whether improving single-rating calibration (to avoid the O(N²) cost of pairwise protocols), training judges with better chain-of-thought reasoning for math/code, and developing more actionable critique formats could close the gap. The benchmark also highlights a 'weak-to-strong' limitation: small judges cannot reliably guide large generators in reasoning-intensive domains, a gap invisible on RewardBench.
