## Problem

Pretraining loss scaling laws are well-established and highly predictable, yet predicting specific downstream capabilities such as accuracy on multiple-choice benchmarks with scale has remained elusive. This paper asks what specific mechanism breaks down between the predictability of pretraining log likelihoods and the unpredictability of downstream evaluation metrics, and identifies that mechanism as the dependence of downstream scores on probability mass over incorrect answer choices.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252118/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis consumes per-sample scores from five model families (Pythia, Cerebras-GPT, OLMo, INCITE, LLM360) evaluated on 12 multiple-choice benchmarks via the LM Evaluation Harness, with pretraining compute approximated as C ≈ 6ND. For each benchmark sample, the paper tracks the score through a chain of transformations—vocabulary-level negative log likelihood, vocabulary probability mass, choices-renormalized probability mass, and finally Accuracy or Brier Score—computing a per-sample correlation with compute at each stage. The output is a distribution of score-compute correlation values (over all samples) at each transformation step, summarized via complementary CDFs and four distribution statistics, plus scatter evidence of how probability mass on correct and incorrect choices covaries with compute.

## Method

The core contribution is showing that downstream multiple-choice metrics are derived from scaling-predictable negative log likelihoods through a sequence of transformations (exponentiation, masking/renormalization over available choices, argmax or squared-error aggregation) that progressively degrade the statistical relationship between scores and pretraining compute. The critical mechanism is that metrics like Accuracy and Brier Score depend not only on how probability mass concentrates on the correct choice with scale, but also on how mass fluctuates on the specific incorrect alternatives, introducing an additional unpredictable degree of freedom absent from pretraining loss. The authors quantify this degradation by computing per-sample score-compute correlations (Pearson, Spearman, Kendall) at each transformation step and demonstrating via complementary CDFs that the fraction of samples with high correlations drops sharply (e.g., from ~90% to ~40% for Spearman > 0.75) when moving from p^Vocab to p^Choices. They further show that Brier Score, despite being continuous, is insufficient to recover the lost predictability, and provide preliminary evidence that probability mass on correct and incorrect choices positively covaries with compute, suggesting that per-sample scaling laws for incorrect choices might be achievable.

## Results

Across 5 model families (Pythia, Cerebras-GPT, OLMo, INCITE, LLM360) and 12 multiple-choice benchmarks, the paper shows that ~90% of ARC Challenge samples have score-compute Spearman correlations > 0.75 under log p_Vocab (Correct Choice), but this drops to only ~40% after the transformation to p_Choices (Correct Choice). The full ordering log p_Vocab ≥ p_Vocab > p_Choices ≥ Brier Score > Accuracy held at least 82.4% of the time for Pearson, 85.6% for Spearman, and 90.4% for Kendall across all (benchmark, model family, statistic) tuples. The key mechanism identified is that probability mass on incorrect choices fluctuates by many orders of magnitude for a given value of p_Vocab (Correct Choice), breaking the clean mapping between compute and downstream scores.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare competing methods but rather compares five successive metrics along the computation pipeline from pretraining loss to downstream accuracy. The 'baseline' is the pretraining negative log likelihood (log p_Vocab), which is highly correlated with compute (~90% of samples above 0.75 Spearman correlation). Each subsequent transformation degrades this correlation: the vocabulary-to-choices renormalization (p_Vocab → p_Choices) causes the largest single drop (90% → 40%), while the choices-to-Accuracy step causes a further but smaller decline. Brier Score, often proposed as a continuous alternative to Accuracy, does not recover the lost predictability because it still depends on probability mass distributed across incorrect choices. The paper concludes that no single continuous metric can restore predictability without explicitly modeling how probability mass on each incorrect choice co-varies with compute, which it shows is positively correlated but with spread varying by several orders of magnitude.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a mechanistic diagnosis of a long-standing gap in the scaling laws literature: while pretraining loss scaling is well-characterized (Kaplan et al., Hoffmann et al.), downstream benchmark predictability has resisted similar treatment. It builds on Schaeffer et al. (2023), who showed that apparent emergent abilities can be artifacts of discontinuous metrics and insufficient resolution, and on Du et al. (2024) and Gadre et al. (2024), who proposed conditions under which downstream performance becomes predictable (loss thresholds, aggregation across tasks). Rather than proposing new predictive models, this paper traces the exact transformation chain from scaling-predictable log-likelihoods to scaling-unpredictable metrics, identifying the normalization-over-available-choices step as the critical point of degradation. It is concurrent with Ruan et al. (2024) on Observational Scaling Laws but takes a complementary, mechanism-focused approach.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clear, well-evidenced explanation for why multiple-choice benchmark scores resist scaling-law fitting, with implications for AI safety governance, model development planning, and evaluation design. A key open question is whether per-sample, per-incorrect-choice probability mass scaling trends can actually be fit and extrapolated—the paper shows positive covariation but with orders-of-magnitude spread, leaving feasibility uncertain. The finding that Brier Score does not recover predictability challenges the common recommendation to use continuous metrics as a fix for emergent-ability artifacts. The analysis is restricted to multiple-choice benchmarks, and generalization to generative evaluations or more complex capabilities remains an important open direction.
