## Problem

While LLM-based multi-agent systems excel at decomposing complex tasks, the impact of faulty or malicious agents on overall system performance remains underexplored. Prior safety work focused on inducing toxicity or misinformation but overlooked how different system structures (linear, flat, hierarchical) vary in their ability to recover from errors during general task collaboration, and how error characteristics (rate, type) modulate this resilience.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251030/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

AUTOTRANSFORM takes an agent's profile and task description as input and outputs a rewritten profile that retains original functionality while embedding stealthy error-generation instructions. AUTOINJECT takes inter-agent messages, task specifications, and two error-rate parameters—Pm (probability a message is corrupted) and Pe (fraction of lines/sentences within a corrupted message that are altered)—plus an error type label (semantic or syntactic), and outputs deterministically corrupted messages. The defense mechanisms (Challenger, Inspector) take the same inter-agent traffic and produce corrected or challenged outputs, with the Inspector recovering up to 96.4% of performance loss.

## Method

The paper proposes two complementary error-introduction methods: AUTOTRANSFORM, an LLM-based pipeline that analyzes an agent's profile, enumerates stealthy error strategies for the specific task, and rewrites the profile to embed those strategies while preserving the agent's nominal role; and AUTOINJECT, which intercepts messages between agents and uses an LLM to replace a controlled fraction (Pe) of lines or sentences with task-appropriate errors at a controlled message-level rate (Pm), enabling precise factorial experiments over error rate and type. For defense, the Challenger augments each agent's profile with instructions to challenge and question received outputs (mirroring AUTOTRANSFORM's profile-rewriting mechanism), while the Inspector is an additional agent that intercepts all inter-agent messages, detects errors, and corrects them before forwarding (mirroring AUTOINJECT's message-interception mechanism). The evaluation spans six multi-agent systems representing three organizational structures (MetaGPT and Self-collab for linear; Camel and SPP for flat; MAD and AgentVerse for hierarchical) across four downstream tasks (code generation, math, translation, text evaluation) using GPT-3.5 and GPT-4o backbones, with GPT-3.5 consistently used for error generation to ensure fair comparison.

## Results

Across six multi-agent systems and four downstream tasks, the hierarchical structure (MAD, AgentVerse) exhibits the smallest average performance drop of 5.51% under faulty agents, compared to 10.54% for flat (Camel, SPP) and 23.72% for linear (MetaGPT, Self-collab). Code generation is the most vulnerable task with a 22.56% average drop, while translation is the most resilient at 4.70%. The proposed Challenger + Inspector defense recovers up to 96.4% of lost performance on the weakest system (Self-collab), restoring scores from 40.9 to 73.8 under AUTOINJECT (vs. 76.2 without errors).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a single new method to beat baselines; instead it benchmarks six existing multi-agent systems to compare structural resilience. Hierarchical systems (MAD, AgentVerse) consistently outperform both flat (Camel, SPP) and linear (MetaGPT, Self-collab) systems under faulty agents: under GPT-3.5 + AUTOINJECT, MAD/AgentVerse retain 48.12 vs. 40.25 (flat) and 38.27 (linear); under GPT-4o + AUTO TRANSFORM the gap widens to 60.83 vs. 56.92 and 30.08. Among individual systems, Self-collab (linear) is the most fragile, dropping from 76.2 to 40.9 on code generation under AUTOINJECT, while MAD (hierarchical) is the most robust, retaining 53.70 under the same condition. The Challenger + Inspector defense narrows the gap substantially: Self-collab recovers to 73.8 (96.4% recovery) and Camel to 48.6, though neither fully matches the vanilla score. Notably, on GPT-4o, AUTO TRANSFORM causes a larger drop than AUTOINJECT (−18.22 vs. −11.28 overall), reversing the GPT-3.5 trend, because stronger models follow the stealthy faulty-profile instructions more faithfully.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of multi-agent system safety and organizational design. Prior work on multi-agent robustness (PsySafe, Evil Geniuses, Agent Smith, NetSafe) focused on safety benchmarks—inducing toxicity or misinformation spread—rather than measuring task-performance degradation under structural variation. By repurposing management science's taxonomy of linear, flat, and hierarchical organizations as a lens for LLM multi-agent topologies, and by introducing controlled error-injection methods (AUTOTRANSFORM, AUTOINJECT) that go beyond the prompt-injection attacks studied previously, the paper shifts the question from 'can an agent be made toxic?' to 'how much does a system's architecture buffer against a single faulty participant?'

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic, cross-structural quantification of multi-agent resilience, yielding actionable design guidance: hierarchical topologies with a reviewing agent at the top lose only ~5.5% performance versus ~24% for linear chains, and the proposed Challenger/Inspector mechanisms recover up to 96.4% of lost performance. The counterintuitive finding that deliberate error injection can *improve* performance in debate-based systems (via forced re-checking or distributional perturbation) opens a new line of inquiry into whether controlled noise is a useful regularization for LLM collaboration. Key limitations include reliance on GPT-3.5/GPT-4o backbones, text-only tasks, single-faulty-agent settings, and the entanglement of structural effects with per-system prompt engineering, leaving open whether the hierarchy advantage persists in multimodal, long-horizon, or multi-fault regimes.
