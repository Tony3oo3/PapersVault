## Problem

In repeated zero-sum games, minimax play guarantees no expected loss but cannot exploit suboptimal opponents, while no-regret algorithms can exploit such opponents but risk losing O(√T) units during learning. The paper asks whether an algorithm can simultaneously guarantee O(1) regret against a specific comparator (the minimax equilibrium) and Õ(√T) regret against any fixed strategy under bandit feedback—a question previously answered only in the full-information setting, where Lattimore (2015) showed a fundamental separation for bandit feedback when the comparator is arbitrary.

## Input / Output

The algorithm requires a game description (a cost matrix for NFGs or a tree structure with infosets for EFGs), a comparator strategy μ^c that is δ-bounded away from the boundary of the strategy polytope (i.e., every action or infoset-action pair receives probability ≥ δ), and bandit feedback consisting only of the realized cost along the single sampled trajectory. It outputs a mixed strategy per round (NFG) or a policy over infosets per round (EFG), achieving O(1) regret versus the comparator and Õ(δ⁻¹√T) regret versus any fixed strategy. The δ-dependence is shown to be unavoidable via matching lower bounds.

## Method

The core algorithm, Phased Aggression with Importance-Weighting, plays a convex combination μ^t = α·μ̂^t + (1−α)·μ^c between a parallel OMD iterate and the comparator, doubling α and restarting OMD whenever the comparator's estimated performance degrades beyond a threshold. The key technical insight is that during phases with α < 1, the comparator's δ-bounded support upper-bounds the importance-weighted cost estimates at 2/δ, which is what makes OMD regret bounds possible under bandit feedback—something that fails for arbitrary comparators. For EFGs, the algorithm operates over the treeplex using an unbalanced dilated KL divergence (Kozuno et al., 2021) during normal phases to avoid an extra √A factor, and switches to a balanced dilated KL divergence (Bai et al., 2022) in the final α=1 phase to avoid an extra √X factor, with both admitting efficient closed-form updates without treeplex projections.

## Results

The main theoretical contribution is Theorem 3.1 (NFGs) and Theorem 4.1 (EFGs): under bandit feedback, Algorithm 1/2 achieves O(1) regret against a full-support comparator while maintaining Õ(δ⁻¹√T) (NFGs) or Õ(δ⁻¹√(XH³T)) (EFGs) regret against any fixed strategy. The matching lower bound (Theorems 3.2, 4.2) shows Ω(√(δ⁻¹T) − δ⁻³/⁴T¹/⁴) regret is unavoidable, confirming near-optimality. In Kuhn Poker experiments (T=1000), Algorithm 2 never incurs losses (matching Min-Max) while exploiting weak opponents (BluffJ, RaiseKQ) at a rate comparable to OMD, whereas Min-Max fails to exploit BluffJ entirely and exploits RaiseKQ sub-optimally.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three strategies in Kuhn Poker: Min-Max equilibrium, OMD with dilated KL (Kozuno et al., 2021), and the proposed Algorithm 2. In the All-vs-All setting, both Min-Max and Algorithm 2 never incur losses, while OMD loses O(√T) units to both — confirming the theoretical risk of no-regret learning. Against exploitable opponents, Algorithm 2 dominates: it initially plays conservatively (gaining similar to Min-Max) before 'taking off' to exploit near-optimally (matching OMD's slope), whereas Min-Max does not exploit BluffJ at all and exploits RaiseKQ sub-optimally. OMD, by contrast, first loses a significant amount during exploration before matching Algorithm 2's exploitation rate. Thus Algorithm 2 achieves the 'best of both worlds': the safety of minimax play combined with the exploitation power of no-regret learning, without the initial loss that OMD suffers.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the Phased Aggression template of Even-Dar et al. (2008) from the full-information setting to bandit feedback by combining it with importance-weighted cost estimators, and further extends it from normal-form games to extensive-form games via the treeplex representation. For the EFG extension, it leverages the unbalanced dilated KL divergence of Kozuno et al. (2021) during exploration phases and the balanced dilated KL divergence of Bai et al. (2022) in the final exploitation phase. The key departure from prior work is circumventing Lattimore's (2015) lower bound—which showed O(1) regret to a single comparator implies Ω(AT) worst-case regret in bandits—by requiring only that the comparator strategy assigns probability at least δ to every action, a minimal and natural assumption for full-support equilibria.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result provides the first provable 'best of both worlds' guarantee under bandit feedback: Alice risks losing at most O(1) units (matching minimax safety) while still exploiting suboptimal opponents at the optimal Õ(√T) rate, resolving a natural question at the intersection of game theory and online learning. The matching lower bound confirms the δ^{-1} dependence is unavoidable, and the Kuhn Poker experiments demonstrate practical relevance even when the equilibrium is not full-support. Open questions include extending to adaptive opponents, convex-concave games, feedback graphs, and establishing no-swap-regret guarantees, all of which the authors identify as promising future directions.
