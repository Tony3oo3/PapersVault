## Problem

Existing local interpretability methods like LIME are designed for single-label tasks and cannot directly explain label distribution learning (LDL) models, where each sample is described by a full distribution over labels with inter-label dependencies. Applying LIME in parallel per label ignores label correlations, incurs high computational cost, and can produce invalid predictions (e.g., negative description degrees or sums exceeding 1) that violate the fundamental constraints of label distributions.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single instance (an RGB image segmented into superpixels via Quickshift, or a tabular row with features binned by entropy-based discretization) and a trained black-box LDL model as input. It produces a feature attribution matrix A ∈ R^{r×f} where each feature carries a full distribution over all r labels (rather than a single scalar weight), along with a local linear surrogate model G(Z) = ZA^T + b that outputs valid label distributions. The attribution matrix is the primary interpretable artifact: each column shows how a feature's presence or absence shifts the entire label distribution, capturing inter-label dependencies in a single explanation.

## Method

LIMEFLDL replaces LIME's scalar feature weights with a feature attribution matrix A ∈ R^{r×f}, so the local surrogate G(Z) = ZA^T + b outputs a full r-dimensional label distribution per sample rather than a single score. The optimization objective minimizes the weighted Frobenius distance between the surrogate and the black-box model's outputs over a single locally sampled region, subject to linear constraints (row sums equal 1, non-negativity) enforced via ADMM with L-BFGS inner iterations. A KL-divergence-based scoring function ranks features by their contribution to matching the black-box distribution, enabling sparse explanations. The paper further derives an analytical closed-form solution under the sum-to-1 constraint, proves convergence via the Banach fixed-point theorem with an explicit bound on the penalty parameter ρ, and establishes stability guarantees showing that small perturbations in the black-box model produce bounded changes in the attribution matrix.

## Results

On the R²-score metric (aligned with LIME's evaluation), LIMEFLDL achieves 0.99 on 5 of 8 datasets under the RBF-LDL-LRR black-box model, substantially outperforming LIME (0.16–0.93), DLIME (0.15–0.47), and GLIME (0.16–0.96). Under the simpler LDL-SCL model, LIMEFLDL still leads on 6 of 8 datasets (e.g., 0.67 vs. 0.16 on Yeast_alpha, 0.99 vs. 0.94 on iJAFFE). In the primary fidelity/consistency comparison against PULIME (parallel LIME), LIMEFLDL achieves higher Jaccard consistency indices on 6 of 8 datasets under RBF-LDL-LRR (e.g., 0.708 vs. 0.579 on JAFFE) and avoids the anomalous negative KL divergences that PULIME exhibits on 4 of 8 datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LIME (Ribeiro et al., 2016), LIMEFLDL achieves substantially higher R²-scores on 7 of 8 datasets under RBF-LDL-LRR (e.g., 0.99 vs. 0.17 on Yeast_alpha, 1.00 vs. 0.26 on Emotion6) and on 6 of 8 under LDL-SCL, with the largest gaps on the tabular datasets where LIME's single-label decomposition fails to capture inter-label dependencies. Against DLIME (Zafar & Khan, 2019), LIMEFLDL wins on 7 of 8 datasets under RBF-LDL-LRR and 5 of 8 under LDL-SCL; DLIME is not applicable to the two image datasets (marked '–'). Against GLIME (Tan et al., 2023), LIMEFLDL leads on 7 of 8 datasets under RBF-LDL-LRR and 6 of 8 under LDL-SCL, with the most notable margin on Natural Scene (0.99 vs. 0.47) and iEmo (0.99 vs. 0.94). In the primary PULIME comparison (Tables 2–3), LIMEFLDL outperforms on Jaccard consistency for 6 of 8 datasets under RBF-LDL-LRR (e.g., 0.708 vs. 0.579 on JAFFE) and avoids the negative KL divergence anomalies that PULIME produces on 4 datasets, confirming that the label-distribution-aware formulation yields more faithful and stable explanations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends the LIME framework (Ribeiro et al., 2016) from single-label classification to label distribution learning (LDL), where model outputs are probability distributions over labels rather than a single class. The key conceptual departure from the naive parallel-LIME approach (PULIME) is the introduction of a feature attribution matrix that captures inter-label dependencies within a single local surrogate, rather than fitting independent per-label models. The optimization is formulated with explicit label-distribution constraints (sum-to-one, non-negativity) solved via ADMM, and the paper provides convergence and stability guarantees via Banach fixed-point and matrix Hoeffding inequalities that go beyond the empirical validation typical of LIME extensions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to provide a principled interpretability framework specifically for LDL models, filling a gap the authors identify as largely unexplored. The theoretical guarantees (convergence rate, stability under model perturbation) add rigor that most LIME variants lack, though the sample complexity bounds are exponential in feature dimension, limiting practical tightness. Open questions include scalability to high-dimensional or deep-learning LDL models, whether the feature attribution matrix generalizes to other distributional output settings (e.g., multi-target regression), and whether the ADMM-based solver remains efficient as the number of labels grows. The small-scale human studies (10 participants, 20 cases) leave the practical trustworthiness claims under-validated.
