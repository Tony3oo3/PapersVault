## Problem

Existing error-monitoring methods for AI models primarily detect whether input data falls outside the training domain, but this misaligns with the practical goal of predicting whether a model will actually make a mistake—since models can err on familiar in-domain data while correctly handling out-of-scope samples. The paper asks whether a separate, independently trained 'mentor' network can learn to predict a 'mentee' model's errors across in-domain, out-of-domain, and adversarial attack scenarios without joint training or access to the mentee's training data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250858/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The mentor receives the same 224×224 RGB image as the frozen mentee and produces a binary correctness label (1 = mentee is correct, 0 = mentee is wrong) via a dedicated MLP stream. A second parallel stream outputs class-level logits that are aligned to the mentee's logits through a KL-divergence distillation loss, effectively forcing the mentor to internalize the mentee's full decision boundary rather than just its final prediction. The mentor is evaluated by averaging its binary prediction accuracy across nine error sources (1 ID, 4 OOD corruptions, 4 adversarial attacks) at multiple severity levels.

## Method

The mentor architecture consists of a pre-trained ImageNet-1K backbone (ResNet50 or ViT) feeding two non-shared MLP streams: one distills the mentee's output logits via a temperature-scaled KL divergence (L_d), and the other performs binary correctness prediction supervised by logistic regression (L_r), with a dynamic weighting α=(n/N)^q that shifts emphasis from distillation to classification over training epochs. The key empirical finding is that training exclusively on adversarial attack errors with minimal perturbations (PIFGSM, ε=1/255) yields the highest error-prediction accuracy, because these samples lie closest to the mentee's decision boundary and expose its most informative failure modes. The resulting SuperMentor combines a ViT backbone with PIFGSM training data, outperforming seven baselines (MCP, CPE, DTC, ConfidNet, TrustScore, SSL, SER) by 3.7–20.3% average accuracy on ImageNet-1K. The mentor generalizes across mentee architectures (ResNet50↔ViT) and to unseen OOD domains, and its vulnerabilities do not overlap with the mentee's, as confirmed by EigenCAM visualizations showing divergent attention patterns.

## Results

On ImageNet-1K with a ResNet50 mentee, SuperMentor (ViT backbone trained on PIFGSM adversarial images at ε=1/255) achieves 70.4% average error-prediction accuracy across all nine error sources, outperforming the best baseline SSL (66.7%) by 3.7 points. On CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100, SuperMentor reaches 64.9% and 63.6% average accuracy respectively. In the medical domain (NCTCRCHE100K, colorectal cancer histology), SuperMentor achieves 81.8% average accuracy, surpassing the best baseline (ConfidNet, 76.5%) by 5.3 points. The advantage is most pronounced on adversarial error sources, where SuperMentor reaches 73.1% on PGD and 75.4% on PIFGSM versus 64.7% and 67.9% for the strongest baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SuperMentor outperforms all seven baselines on average accuracy across ImageNet-1K (70.4% vs. 66.7% for the best baseline, SSL). The margin is largest on adversarial error sources: SuperMentor achieves 73.1% on PGD and 75.4% on PIFGSM, exceeding SSL by 8.4 and 7.5 points respectively, while threshold-based methods (MCP, CPE, DTC) drop to near-chance (45–53%) on these sources. On in-domain (ID) errors, MCP (γ=0.7) actually edges out SuperMentor (77.5% vs. 73.3%), reflecting that confidence thresholds are effective when the mentee's errors are mild. On the medical NCTCRCHE100K dataset, SuperMentor leads all baselines by a wide margin (81.8% vs. 76.5% for ConfidNet), with particularly strong gains on adversarial sources (88.2–88.7% vs. 77.6–82.9% for SSL/ConfidNet). The paper also shows that SuperMentor generalizes across mentee architectures: a mentor trained on ResNet50 mentee errors transfers to a ViT mentee with minimal accuracy loss, a property none of the baselines possess.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out a distinct niche by framing error prediction as a separate 'mentor' model trained independently from the 'mentee,' departing from selective prediction and rejection learning that jointly train the rejection function with the classifier. It repurposes knowledge distillation (Hinton et al., 2015) not for compressing models but as a mechanism for the mentor to internalize the mentee's decision boundaries. Unlike OOD detection methods that rely on hand-crafted metrics (confidence, entropy, distance to centroid), the mentor learns error patterns end-to-end from a dedicated deep network. The paper also distinguishes itself from adversarial training, which uses adversarial samples to improve the mentee, by instead using them to teach a separate predictor about the mentee's vulnerabilities.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that adversarial errors with minimal perturbations (ε=1/255) are the most informative training signal for error prediction, and that such a mentor generalizes across both in-domain and out-of-domain errors as well as across different mentee architectures, provides a practical and data-efficient recipe for deploying error monitors in high-stakes settings like medical imaging. Limitations include the restriction to image classification with only two mentee architectures, the white-box assumption for adversarial attack generation, and moderate absolute accuracy (~70%) that may be insufficient for safety-critical deployment without additional safeguards. Open questions include extending the framework to other modalities and tasks, enabling mutual learning where mentors feed corrections back to mentees, and predicting specific error types rather than binary correctness.
