## Problem

When language models are given k independent attempts per problem, the negative log of the aggregate success rate empirically follows a power law in k, yet a straightforward calculation shows that for any single problem the failure rate must decay exponentially with k. No prior work had explained how per-problem exponential scaling gives rise to aggregate polynomial scaling, nor provided a principled way to predict the power law exponent from limited data.

## Input / Output

The method consumes per-problem single-attempt success rates (pass_i@1 or ASR_i@1) from a fixed benchmark set, along with the total number of samples drawn per problem. A key practical constraint is that problems whose true single-attempt success probability falls below 1/(number of samples) cannot be individually resolved; the estimator handles this by matching the fitted distribution's probability mass in that left-tail interval to the empirical fraction of problems in that bucket. The output is the power law exponent b and intercept a, plus a parametric model that predicts aggregate success rate at any k without additional sampling.

## Method

The paper proves a pair of theorems establishing that -log(pass_D@k) scales as k^(-b) if and only if the distribution of single-attempt success rates over problems has a power-law left tail p_D(p) ~ C·p^(b-1) near zero, with the aggregate exponent b directly determined by the tail exponent. Empirically, the authors show that real success-rate distributions (on MATH and HarmBench) are well fit by a 3-parameter scaled Kumaraswamy distribution, and that the absence of such a heavy left tail (as in Llama 3 8B IT) explains why that model deviates from power law scaling. The practical distributional estimator fits this distribution via maximum likelihood under a discretization matching the sampling resolution, then evaluates the integral 1 − ∫(1−p)^k p̂_D(p) dp to simulate pass_D@k at arbitrary k for log-log regression. Backtesting on synthetic data shows this estimator recovers the true exponent with roughly an order of magnitude lower relative error than standard least-squares fitting, equivalent to 2–4 orders of magnitude less inference compute.

## Results

The paper establishes that per-problem success rates scale exponentially with the number of attempts k (confirmed empirically on Pythia models solving MATH problems and frontier AI models on HarmBench jailbreaking prompts), while the aggregate negative log average success rate scales as a power law $-\log(\text{pass}_\mathcal{D}@k) \approx a k^{-b}$. The key quantitative contribution is a distributional estimator for the power law exponent $b$ that achieves approximately one order of magnitude lower relative error $|\hat{b}-b|/b$ compared to the standard least-squares estimator in log-log space, equivalent to roughly 2–4 orders of magnitude less inference compute for the same estimation accuracy. The framework also explains why Llama 3 8B IT fails to exhibit aggregate power law scaling: its single-attempt success rate distribution lacks the heavy left tail (power-law-like density near zero) required by Theorems 3.1–3.2.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary empirical comparison is between two estimators of the power law exponent $b$: the standard least-squares regression in log-log space (used in prior scaling-law work such as Hoffmann et al. 2022 and Besiroglu et al. 2024) and the proposed distributional estimator that fits a scaled Kumaraswamy distribution to per-problem single-attempt success rates and then simulates aggregate pass@k. In backtesting experiments (Figure 7) with synthetic data of known ground-truth exponent, the distributional estimator recovers $b$ with roughly 10× lower relative error than least squares across a range of subsampled problem counts and samples-per-problem, and it remains robust under distributional mismatch. On real data (Figure 6), both estimators yield close agreement when full sampling budgets are available, confirming consistency. The distributional framework also correctly predicts the absence of power law scaling for Llama 3 8B IT on HarmBench, where the least-squares approach would simply fail to fit a meaningful power law, whereas the distributional perspective identifies the missing left-tail structure as the root cause.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper provides the theoretical explanation for the power law scaling empirically discovered by Brown et al. (2024) in pass@k and by Hughes et al. (2024) in ASR@k, both of which observed polynomial decay of negative log average success rate with the number of independent attempts but did not explain why. The authors build on the pass@k estimator from Chen et al. (2021) and the compound binomial structure of repeated sampling, but their core contribution is a distributional characterization: proving that a power-law left tail in the distribution of single-attempt success rates is both necessary and sufficient for aggregate power law scaling. This positions the work as a theoretical bridge between the empirical scaling-law literature (Kaplan et al., Hoffmann et al.) and the specific inference-time compute scaling setting, while also drawing on classical results about power laws arising from weighted sums of exponentials.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper resolves a genuine puzzle in the inference-time compute scaling literature: why aggregate scaling is polynomial when per-problem scaling is provably exponential. The necessity theorem (Theorem 3.2) is particularly valuable because it means any model/benchmark pair exhibiting power law scaling must have a specific distributional structure, providing a diagnostic tool for understanding deviations (as demonstrated with Llama 3 8B IT). The distributional estimator's ~10× sample efficiency for recovering power law exponents has direct practical value for practitioners forecasting compute requirements. Open questions remain about why such heavy-tailed distributions arise in benchmarks (design choice vs. selection bias) and whether the 'dark matter' analogy to pretraining scaling laws holds beyond the asymptotic regime.
