## Problem

Hypergradient descent (HDM), a 25-year-old heuristic for adaptive stepsize selection in gradient methods, has shown promising empirical performance but suffered from a complete lack of rigorous convergence theory. In practice, HDM is notoriously unstable—objective values can spike to 10^30 in early iterations—leading users to abandon the algorithm before it converges. This paper provides the first rigorous convergence analysis of HDM, explains the source of its instability, and develops practical variants with strong theoretical and empirical guarantees.

## Input / Output

The method takes a smooth convex function f: R^n → R (accessed via gradient oracle), an initial point x^1, and an initial preconditioner P_1 in a bounded closed convex set P (which need not be positive semidefinite). It produces an approximate minimizer x^{K+1} satisfying ||∇f||_∞ ≤ 10^{-4} and an adapted preconditioner P_{K+1} that converges to [∇²f(x*)]^{-1} on strongly convex problems. The practical variant HDM-Best uses a diagonal preconditioner, scalar heavy-ball momentum, and AdaGrad for online learning, requiring 7 vectors of size n in memory—comparable to L-BFGS with memory size 1.

## Method

The key insight is recasting HDM's preconditioner update as online gradient descent on a surrogate loss called hypergradient feedback, h_x(P) = [f(x − P∇f(x)) − f(x)] / ||∇f(x)||², which measures how well a preconditioner P reduces the objective in one gradient step. Sublinear regret bounds from online learning translate directly into convergence guarantees for the optimization problem, revealing that HDM automatically adapts to the local optimization landscape (both statically and dynamically) and achieves local superlinear convergence on strongly convex objectives by learning the inverse Hessian. The paper explains the observed instability as a warm-up phase where accumulated regret exceeds progress, and addresses it with null steps (skipping updates that increase the objective) and AdaGrad for the online learning component. The best practical variant, HDM-Best, combines a diagonal preconditioner, heavy-ball momentum, and AdaGrad-based joint learning of (P_k, β_k), achieving performance comparable to L-BFGS-M5/M10 while using less memory and cheaper per-iteration computation.

## Results

On 33 SVM and 33 logistic regression instances from LIBSVM (stopping at ‖∇f‖∞ ≤ 10⁻⁴ within 1000 gradient oracles), HDM-Best solves 32/33 SVM and 21/33 logistic regression problems, matching L-BFGS-M10 (31/33, 18/33) and tying BFGS on SVM (32/33) while using only 7 vectors of memory (comparable to L-BFGS-M1). HDM-Best dramatically outperforms all adaptive first-order baselines: Adam (26/33, 11/33), AdaGrad (9/33, 8/33), and vanilla GD (5/33, 2/33). The paper also demonstrates that HDM achieves local superlinear convergence on strongly convex objectives, with a rate of O(e^{-½K log K}), matching recent quasi-Newton methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HDM-Best decisively outperforms all first-order adaptive baselines: it solves 32/33 SVM problems versus Adam's 26/33 and AdaGrad's 9/33, and 21/33 logistic regression problems versus Adam's 11/33 and AdaGrad's 8/33. Against the L-BFGS family, HDM-Best (32/33 SVM, 21/33 LR) matches or exceeds L-BFGS-M10 (31/33, 18/33) and substantially outperforms L-BFGS-M1 (13/33, 11/33) and L-BFGS-M5 (26/33, 16/33), while using only 7 vectors of memory—comparable to L-BFGS-M1's footprint. On logistic regression, BFGS (26/33) still edges out HDM-Best (21/33), though the two tie on SVM (32/33). The paper emphasizes that HDM-Best's convergence curves (Figures 4–5) show it often matches L-BFGS-M5/M10 in wall-clock convergence speed while requiring cheaper per-iteration computation (no Hessian-vector products or line searches). Overall, HDM-Best positions itself as a practical first-order alternative to quasi-Newton methods for deterministic convex optimization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper provides the first rigorous convergence analysis of hypergradient descent (HDM), a 25-year-old heuristic originally proposed by Almeida et al. (1999) and rediscovered by Baydin et al. (2018). It builds directly on the online learning framework of Gao et al. (2024), which first identified HDM's P-update as online gradient descent on a surrogate loss, but goes substantially further by establishing global and local convergence guarantees, explaining the well-known instability as a warm-up phase, and proving local superlinear convergence. The paper also draws a conceptual parallel to quasi-Newton methods (BFGS, L-BFGS), showing that HDM learns the inverse Hessian through hypergradient feedback rather than the secant equation, positioning HDM as a new family of first-order methods with quasi-Newton-like convergence properties.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that a first-order method can achieve local superlinear convergence at rate O(e^{-K log K})—matching the Broyden family of quasi-Newton methods—while using only gradient information and O(n) memory is a significant theoretical advance for adaptive optimization. The practical variant HDM-Best matching L-BFGS-M5/M10 on convex problems with less memory suggests a viable alternative for large-scale optimization where quasi-Newton memory is prohibitive. However, all experiments are on deterministic convex problems (SVM, logistic regression) with 33 instances each, and the superlinear convergence requires strong convexity plus Lipschitz Hessian; extending the theory to stochastic and nonconvex settings, which is where most modern ML training occurs, remains the critical open question the authors identify.
