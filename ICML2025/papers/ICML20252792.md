## Problem

Despite LLMs' strong performance on mathematical reasoning benchmarks, physics reasoning—which requires domain-specific knowledge, multiple applications of physical laws, and practical interpretation—has received far less systematic evaluation. Existing physics benchmarks are predominantly middle/high-school level, multiple-choice, or too small in scope to capture the breadth of undergraduate physics, leaving a critical gap in assessing whether LLMs can genuinely reason about physics beyond abstract math.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252792/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark consists of 5,520 text-only physics problems (11,040 with bilingual EN/ZH versions) sourced from USTC undergraduate exercise books, each structured as Problem–Solution–Answer with one of seven answer types (numerical value, expression, equation, interval, true/false, multiple choice, or compound). Evaluation uses zero-shot prompts tailored per answer type with greedy decoding at temperature 0 and a 4,096-token output limit (8,192 for o1-like models). The MARJ pipeline outputs a binary correctness judgment per problem, aggregated into accuracy scores across 3 domains, 13 subjects, 59 topics, 4 reasoning skills, and 2 languages.

## Method

UGPhysics is constructed through a three-phase pipeline: (1) PDF-to-LaTeX conversion via Mathpix with manual correction, embedding-based deduplication, and splitting of progressive multi-part problems into independent ones; (2) filtering to retain only problems with definitive answers (excluding estimation, proof, and explanation types); and (3) annotation of answer types and four physics reasoning skills (Knowledge Recall, Laws Application, Math Derivation, Practical Application) using GPT-4o. The key evaluation contribution is the MARJ (Model-Assistant Rule-based Judgment) two-stage pipeline: a first stage applies type-specific rule-based matching (scientific-notation comparison with 1e-2 relative tolerance for numerical values, physical-constant normalization for expressions/equations, endpoint comparison for intervals, format normalization for TF/MC), and a second stage invokes GPT-4o with a few-shot prompt to re-judge cases flagged as incorrect, specifically attending to equivalent quantities and omitted constants. Data leakage is detected via 5-gram prediction accuracy across a subset of LLMs, confirming minimal contamination (<1% for most models).

## Results

On UGPhysics (5,520 bilingual undergraduate physics problems), the highest overall accuracy is 49.78% achieved by OpenAI-o1-mini, followed by DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-70B at 40.17% and Qwen2.5-Math-72B-Instruct at 39.52%. Notably, 15 out of 31 evaluated LLMs score below 20% overall accuracy, and only two models surpass 40%. For context, GPT-4o achieves 38.67% on UGPhysics versus 53.60% on GPQA and 76.60% on MATH, confirming UGPhysics poses substantially greater difficulty than existing physics and math benchmarks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

OpenAI-o1-mini (49.78%) leads all 31 evaluated models, outperforming the second-best DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-70B (40.17%) by ~10 percentage points and GPT-4o (38.66%) by ~11 points. Math-specialized LLMs yield only marginal gains over their general-purpose counterparts: Qwen2.5-Math-7B (22.69%) improves over Qwen2.5-7B (22.55%) by just 0.14%, and Qwen2.5-Math-72B (39.52%) over Qwen2.5-72B (36.22%) by 3.3%, far below the >10% gaps typical in math benchmarks. O1-like LLMs show the most promising trajectory: DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-70B (40.17%) surpasses GPT-4o and all non-o1 open-source models, while QwQ-32B (37.34%) approaches Qwen2.5-Math-72B despite being ~45B parameters smaller. Cross-benchmark comparison (Table 7) confirms UGPhysics is harder than GPQA (53.60%), OlympicArena (55.92%), MMLU college physics (68.60%), and MATH (76.60%) for GPT-4o, which scores only 38.67% on UGPhysics. Among open-source general-purpose models, Mistral-Large-Instruct-2407 (39.28%) is the strongest, but still trails o1-mini by ~10.5 points; smaller models like LLaMA-3.1-8B (12.45%) and DeepSeek-MOE-16B (4.68%) struggle significantly, with 15 of 31 models scoring below 20%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the physics-reasoning evaluation line initiated by OlympiadBench (He et al., 2024) and OlympicArena (Huang et al., 2024b), adopting their zero-shot prompting protocol and multi-type answer structure, while shifting the focus from competition-level or mixed-domain problems to a dedicated undergraduate-level physics curriculum spanning 13 subjects. The MARJ evaluation pipeline synthesizes the rule-based judgment approach from OlympiadBench with the model-based judging prompts from Omni-Math (Gao et al., 2024), and the n-gram data-leakage detection follows Xu et al. (2024a). The 7-answer-type taxonomy and bilingual design are also shared with the authors' own UGMathBench (Xu et al., 2025), but applied here to physics-specific challenges such as physical constants and equivalent quantities.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 49.8% best accuracy (o1-mini) versus 90%+ on MATH exposes a substantial and previously under-quantified gap between math and physics reasoning, while the near-zero benefit of math-specialized fine-tuning (0.14–3.3%) challenges the assumption that strong mathematical training transfers to physics problem-solving. The MARJ framework (98% agreement with human judgment) addresses a concrete evaluation bottleneck—prior benchmarks like OlympiadBench had a 12% judgment error rate on physics—and the n-gram leakage audit confirms the benchmark's integrity. Open questions include whether physics-specific pretraining corpora can close the gap, how multi-modal (image-containing) problems change the difficulty landscape, and whether the high clip ratios of o1-like models (up to 52%) reflect a fundamental token-efficiency problem in long-chain physics reasoning.
