## Problem

Benchmark contamination has become a critical concern in LLM evaluation, and existing Agents-as-an-Evaluator methods (e.g., MPA) that use LLMs to rephrase questions to mitigate contamination have themselves been shown to introduce unexplored biases. This paper provides the first theoretical decomposition of evaluation bias and empirically identifies two specific biases—data bias (accuracy imbalance across domains during generation) and model bias (inherent unfairness favoring the generating model)—that undermine the reliability of these agent-based evaluation protocols.

## Input / Output

The method takes existing benchmark questions (multiple-choice from ARC-C and MMLU, open-ended math from GSM8K) paired with their ground truth labels and applies Bags of Atomic Interventions (BOAT) to generate modified question variants. Interventions are applied under a constraint matrix (e.g., Answer Removal requires Distractor Hint; Binary Transformation is mutually exclusive with all others) with per-intervention probabilities of 0.5 (0.1 for Binary Transformation), and the same intervention combinations are propagated to 5-shot few-shot examples. Output is a per-model accuracy score averaged over 5 runs at temperature 0, plus a per-intervention performance breakdown that reveals which specific perturbations degrade model performance, enabling interpretable diagnosis of contamination and capability gaps.

## Method

The Unbiased Evaluator formulates LLM evaluation as a causal directed acyclic graph where input variables (instruction, question stem, options, labels) are the causes and the ground-truth answer is the effect, then applies atomic interventions to these variables to test whether the model's correct answer is robust to causal manipulation. The key mechanism is BOAT: six atomic interventions for MCQs (Distractor Hint, Distractor Question, Answer Removal, Option Shuffling, Label Replacement, Binary Transformation) and two for math (Question Jitter, Answer Jitter) that are organically combined under a predefined constraint matrix to produce diverse question configurations. This design is guided by a theoretical decomposition (Proposition 3.1) showing that the squared bias of a new evaluation protocol equals the sum of an original term, a related term (covariance between new and existing biases), and an independent term; interventions like Answer Removal and Binary Transformation are specifically chosen to counteract the related term by targeting known ambiguities in public benchmarks. A probing task with a minimal rephrasing-based Agents-as-an-Evaluator setup empirically validates the presence of data and model bias, while the Unbiased Evaluator's stability in Over-Confidence and Under-Confidence rates across varying intervention strength confirms its reduced bias.

## Results

On the Unbiased Evaluator, all seven evaluated LLMs experience significant performance degradation compared to vanilla benchmarks, with average drops ranging from ~8.3 points (GPT-4-Turbo) to ~21.8 points (Yi1.5-34B-Chat) across ARC-C, MMLU, and GSM8K. GPT-4-Turbo retains the highest Unbiased Evaluator scores (89.74 on ARC-C, 71.81 on MMLU, 90.18 on GSM8K), while open-source models like Yi1.5-34B-Chat (71.79, 56.70, 69.60) and Llama3.1-70B-Instruct (81.23, 61.93, 82.97) show the largest gaps. The contamination ablation (Table 3) confirms that even a model fine-tuned directly on the MMLU test set (96.6% vanilla) drops to only 37.1% under the Unbiased Evaluator, versus 33.7% for the untrained baseline, demonstrating the protocol's sensitivity to genuine capability rather than memorization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Agents-as-an-Evaluator baseline (Zhu et al., ICML 2024), the Unbiased Evaluator demonstrates substantially lower data bias: Kendall correlation coefficients between domain accuracy and R_CE drop from −0.17 to −0.39 (p < 0.006) under Agents-as-an-Evaluator to 0.02 to 0.14 (p > 0.05) under the Unbiased Evaluator across four LLMs (Table 1). On model bias, the Unbiased Evaluator keeps both R_OC and R_UC relatively stable as intervention strength increases, whereas Agents-as-an-Evaluator shows a significant rise in R_OC (over-confidence) with growing strength, indicating that rephrasing agents inadvertently favor their own implicit strengths (Figure 2). Against LiveBench as a reference standard, the Unbiased Evaluator achieves a perfect Kendall rank correlation of 1.000 and Pearson correlation of 0.949 with LiveBench global scores, compared to 0.600 and 0.918 for vanilla benchmarks (Table 4), confirming that the causal-intervention protocol better reflects true model capability rankings without requiring the continuous question updates that LiveBench demands.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the Agents-as-an-Evaluator paradigm (MPA, CogMath) by identifying and formalizing biases that those methods introduce, rather than building directly on them. It repurposes causal inference concepts—specifically interventions and directed acyclic graphs—from the broader causal ML literature into the LLM evaluation setting, framing question-answering as a causal process where atomic input variables can be systematically perturbed. The theoretical decomposition of evaluation bias (Proposition 3.1) is novel in the evaluation context, though the algebraic structure is standard; its practical contribution is the BOAT mechanism, which operationalizes causal interventions as concrete, rule-based transformations of benchmark items.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that Agents-as-an-Evaluator—the leading proposed solution to benchmark contamination—introduces its own model bias (over-confidence that grows with rephrasing strength) challenges the assumption that rephrasing is a neutral operation, and the 5–22 point performance drops across models suggest current benchmark scores are substantially inflated. The causal framing also yields interpretability (per-intervention breakdowns, confusion matrices) that static benchmarks cannot provide. Limitations include scope restricted to MCQ and math tasks, a somewhat ad-hoc intervention design, and the theoretical framework, while correct, is relatively simple. Open questions include extension to open-ended generation, whether the causal-DAG abstraction generalizes beyond rule-based tasks, and whether the BOAT constraints can be learned rather than hand-specified.
