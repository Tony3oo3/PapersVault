## Problem

Existing dynamic graph representation methods, including SNN-based approaches, update node representations using only current-time information, ignoring the inherent latency in real-world information propagation (delay representation). Additionally, as graphs evolve, earlier historical information is progressively overshadowed by new updates (information forgetting), causing loss of valuable temporal context. These two issues limit the accuracy of node representations in evolving graphs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252578/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sequence of T dynamic graph snapshots, each containing a node set, edge set, and node feature matrix X^t ∈ R^{N×d}. At each snapshot, second-order neighbors are sampled and aggregated via graph convolution before being encoded into sparse binary spike sequences by LIF neurons. The output is a fixed-dimensional node embedding (d ≪ input feature dimension) produced by weighted temporal pooling over all T time steps, which feeds a fully connected classifier for node classification.

## Method

Delay-DSGN introduces a learnable Gaussian delay kernel into the neighborhood aggregation of a spiking graph neural network. The kernel k_ij[n] = w_ij · exp(−(n − (K_s − d_ij − 1))² / 2σ²) is convolved with zero-padded spike sequences, where the learnable delay parameter d_ij shifts the kernel center to adaptively control how much historical spike information influences the current time step. Delayed features are then processed by LIF neurons with soft reset and adaptive thresholding to generate temporally rich node representations. A multi-timescale fusion layer performs weighted pooling across all T time steps to capture long-range topological evolution, and a temporal regularization term penalizes abrupt representation changes between consecutive steps. The paper further derives a theoretical lower bound on the Gaussian kernel's standard deviation σ in terms of kernel size K_s and weight magnitudes, guaranteeing bounded gradient propagation and preventing explosion or vanishing during training.

## Results

On the three large-scale dynamic graph datasets, Delay-DSGN achieves the best F1 scores across all training ratios (40%, 60%, 80%). At the 80% training ratio, it reaches Mi-F1 of 76.87% on DBLP, 67.88% on Tmall, and Ma-F1 of 84.20% on Patent, outperforming the strongest SNN baseline Dy-SIGN by 1.91, 2.06, and 0.29 points respectively. On Tmall, the gap over the best non-SNN method TGAT is particularly large (4.56 Mi-F1 points at 80%), while HTNE and M²DNE fail to complete on Patent due to prohibitive computational cost.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Delay-DSGN outperforms all eight baselines on every dataset, metric, and training ratio. The closest competitor is Dy-SIGN (the other SNN-based method), which Delay-DSGN beats by 1.4–2.2 Mi-F1 points on DBLP, 1.5–2.1 on Tmall, and 0.15–0.5 on Patent. Against the strongest non-SNN method TGAT, the advantage is most pronounced on Tmall (up to 4.56 Mi-F1 at 80%), while on DBLP and Patent the gap narrows to ~1.5–3.7 points. The point-process methods HTNE and M²DNE are competitive on DBLP and Tmall but fail entirely on the 2.7M-node Patent dataset due to excessive training cost. Static random-walk baselines (DeepWalk, Node2Vec) are consistently the weakest on dynamic tasks, trailing the best methods by 5–10+ points, confirming that temporal modeling is essential. Among SNN methods, SpikeNet is slightly behind Dy-SIGN on DBLP but competitive on Patent, and both are clearly surpassed by Delay-DSGN's learnable delay mechanism.

## Relation to Prior Work

Delay-DSGN extends the SNN-based dynamic graph representation learning pipeline established by SpikeNet (Li et al., 2023) and Dy-SIGN (Yin et al., 2024b), which use LIF neurons and graph convolution to encode dynamic graph snapshots as spike sequences. The key departure is the introduction of a learnable Gaussian delay kernel inspired by biological synaptic plasticity, which models the latency in information propagation between nodes—a phenomenon absent from prior SNN-GNN hybrids. The paper also contributes a theoretical gradient-stability bound (Theorem 5.1) constraining the kernel's standard deviation and size, which has no direct analogue in the existing SNN or dynamic GNN literature. Compared to RNN-based (EvolveGCN, TGAT) and point-process (HTNE, M2DNE) approaches, Delay-DSGN retains the computational efficiency of sparse spike representations while adding temporal depth through the delay mechanism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses two concrete failure modes in dynamic graph learning—delayed information propagation and historical information forgetting—with a biologically motivated mechanism that is both interpretable (learned delays reflect domain-specific temporal characteristics, e.g., slower in academic collaboration, faster in e-commerce) and theoretically grounded. However, the evaluation is limited to node classification on three datasets, and the theoretical analysis, while useful for parameter selection, relies on relatively loose triangle-inequality bounds. Open questions include whether the delay mechanism generalizes to other tasks (link prediction, graph-level classification), whether it captures multi-hop temporal dependencies beyond the kernel window, and how it interacts with more complex SNN architectures (e.g., multi-layer spiking networks). The consistent but moderate improvements (1–4.5% over the best baseline) suggest the delay mechanism is a useful but not transformative addition to the SNN-GNN toolkit.
