## Problem

Existing tree search methods for LLM reasoning (MCTS, DFS, BFS) rely on predefined heuristics and exhaustive exploration, making them computationally expensive and requiring expert-designed reward signals. This paper asks whether a learned policy can replace these hand-crafted search strategies to dynamically and efficiently navigate reasoning trees, reducing token costs while maintaining or improving accuracy.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251406/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt (the reasoning problem) and consumes LLM hidden states from the final layer (corresponding to the last generated token) as node features in the reasoning tree, along with edge features encoding immediate step rewards and a binary constraint vector indicating valid actions. The output is a complete reasoning chain (sequence of intermediate steps, each defined as one sentence) and a final answer, produced by the target LLM under the guidance of the external policy's expand/branch/backtrack/terminate decisions. The LLM itself is treated as the environment rather than the policy, with the lightweight GPS-based policy operating at the tree-node level rather than the token level.

## Method

PGTS formulates reasoning as a Tree Search MDP (TS-MDP) where the state is the revealed portion of the reasoning tree and the action space comprises four structured operations: expand (generate next step), branch (switch to a sibling node), backtrack (revert K steps), and terminate (conclude search). The policy is a Graph Transformer (GPS) that processes node features (LLM hidden states) and edge features (immediate rewards) through local message passing and global attention layers, augmented with random-walking structure embeddings, to produce a categorical distribution over valid actions. Training uses PPO with log-likelihood-based intermediate rewards and action-specific costs (higher for backtracking, zero for terminate), requiring only 1,000 examples per dataset. The key architectural insight is treating the LLM as an environment and delegating exploration decisions to a lightweight external policy, which avoids the exhaustive search overhead of MCTS while enabling adaptive, task-specific navigation of the reasoning tree.

## Results

On LLaMA3.1-8B, PGTS achieves 41.00% accuracy on MATH (vs. 34.40% for CoT) and 85.29% on GSM8K (vs. 83.47% for CoT), while using only 5.28× the tokens of CoT on MATH compared to MCTS's 16.25×. With self-consistency (SC8), PGTS reaches 52.20% on MATH and 89.99% on GSM8K, surpassing both CoT-SC8 (48.20%, 89.84%) and MCTS-Best (43.80%, 86.13%). On the planning benchmark Blocksworld(4), PGTS-SC8 achieves 36.84% versus CoT-SC8's 26.32% and MCTS-Best's 28.95%. The main exception is GPQA, where PGTS (18.69%) underperforms MCTS (34.34%) due to the task's extreme difficulty and limited training data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CoT, PGTS wins on every dataset: MATH improves from 34.40% to 41.00%, GSM8K from 83.47% to 85.29%, and AQUA from 51.57% to 60.63% (LLaMA3.1-8B). With self-consistency, PGTS-SC8 further extends these gains, reaching 52.20% on MATH (vs. CoT-SC8's 48.20%) and 66.54% on AQUA (vs. 55.12%), showing that PGTS generates more diverse, high-quality chains suitable for aggregation. Against MCTS, PGTS is competitive without SC (matching MCTS-Best on GSM8K at 85.29% vs. 86.13% and on AQUA at 60.63%) and surpasses it with SC on most tasks (e.g., MATH 52.20% vs. 43.80%, StrategyQA 85.70% vs. 79.00%), but clearly loses on GPQA (18.69% vs. 34.34%) due to the benchmark's extreme difficulty and limited training examples. The critical practical advantage is computational efficiency: PGTS uses only 5.28× the tokens of CoT on MATH and 1.29× on GSM8K, whereas MCTS requires 16.25× and 13.33× respectively, making PGTS substantially more deployable in cost-sensitive settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

PGTS sits at the intersection of tree search for LLM reasoning (ToT, MCTS/RAP, A* search) and learned policy optimization, departing from these methods by replacing fixed heuristics (UCB, BFS/DFS schedules) with a learned policy that dynamically selects among expand, branch, backtrack, and terminate actions. The policy is implemented as a Graph Transformer (GPS) operating over the partially revealed reasoning tree, drawing on the graph-transformer literature (Rampašek et al., 2022) to capture both local step relationships and global tree structure. A notable conceptual departure is treating the LLM as an environment rather than a policy, with an external lightweight GNN making exploration decisions — a framing that contrasts with prior work where the LLM itself drives the search or self-evaluates intermediate steps.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in the substantial token-efficiency gains (e.g., 5.28× CoT tokens vs. MCTS's 16.25× on MATH) while maintaining competitive accuracy, making structured search more deployable in cost-sensitive settings. The framework also directly addresses the 'overthinking' phenomenon in o1-like models via its terminate action. Key limitations include reliance on simple log-likelihood rewards (no PRM or self-evaluation), underperformance on highly diverse benchmarks like GPQA, and no guarantee of reasoning-chain faithfulness. Open questions include whether richer reward signals (LLM self-evaluation, task-specific verifiers) would close the gap to MCTS(Oracle), and whether the approach scales to longer-horizon planning or multi-agent settings.
