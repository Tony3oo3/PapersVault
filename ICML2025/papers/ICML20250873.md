## Problem

While recent works have connected GFlowNets to maximum-entropy (MaxEnt) RL through entropy-regularized objectives, the relationship between GFlowNets and standard (non-MaxEnt) RL remains largely unexplored despite both frameworks being rooted in sequential decision-making and temporal-difference learning. This paper asks whether a fundamental RL building block—policy evaluation of a fixed random policy—can reproduce GFlowNets' reward-matching behavior without any entropy regularization or policy learning.

## Input / Output

The method takes a DAG/MDP structure (states, actions, deterministic transitions), a terminal reward function R(x), and a fixed uniform random policy as input. The g-function (branching ratio, computed as the product of outgoing-to-incoming action counts along each path) is derived directly from the problem's action-space specification rather than learned. The output is a single learned flow function F_θ(s) (an MLP with 2 hidden layers of 2048 units), from which the sampling policy P_F(s'|s) = F_θ(s')·P_B(s|s')/F_θ(s) is derived using a uniform backward policy, producing generated objects such as molecules, DNA, or RNA sequences.

## Method

The core insight is that GFlowNets' flow iteration (propagating flows from children to parents via the backward policy) is structurally equivalent to standard policy evaluation (propagating values from successors to current states via the forward policy) when the policy is uniform and terminal rewards are scaled by the path-dependent branching ratio g(τ,s). Theorem 4.1 establishes this equivalence unconditionally for tree-structured DAGs (V(s_t) = F(s_t)·∏A(s_i)), and Theorem 4.2 extends it to non-tree DAGs under a path-invariance condition (V(s_t) = F(s_t)·g(τ,s_t)). Building on this, the Rectified Random Policy Evaluation (RPE) algorithm evaluates a fixed uniform policy with transformed terminal rewards R'(x) = R(x)·g(τ,x), learning only the flow function F_θ via MSE regression rather than jointly training forward and backward policies. This eliminates the non-stationarity of evolving policies in standard GFlowNets training and removes the need for entropy regularization, while preserving the same reward-matching capability (π(x)∝R(x)).

## Results

On the RNA sequence generation benchmark (4 tasks, L14RNA1–4), RPE achieves near-perfect accuracy of 99.81%, 100.0%, 100.0%, and 97.68%, substantially outperforming the best GFlowNet baseline (TB-GFN: 86.81–87.52%) and the best MaxEnt RL baseline (M-DQN: 89.55–89.99%). RPE also discovers the most modes across all four RNA tasks (42, 46, 52, 90) compared to the next-best baseline TB-GFN (34, 31, 31, 79). On TFBind (tree and DAG variants) and QM9 molecule generation, RPE achieves competitive accuracy and mode discovery comparable to GFlowNets and MaxEnt RL baselines, while discovering modes faster due to its fixed-policy evaluation framework.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RPE consistently outperforms all six baselines on the RNA generation benchmark, achieving 10–23 percentage points higher accuracy than the strongest GFlowNet baseline (TB-GFN) and 10–28 points higher than the strongest MaxEnt RL baseline (M-DQN). In terms of mode discovery, RPE finds 8–11 more modes than TB-GFN and 10–30 more than M-DQN across the four RNA tasks. On TFBind generation (both tree-structured and DAG-structured variants), RPE achieves comparable accuracy and mode discovery to GFlowNets (FM, DB, TB, SubTB) and MaxEnt RL (Soft DQN, M-DQN), with a small margin of improvement in accuracy. On QM9 molecule generation, RPE maintains comparable accuracy to GFlowNet and MaxEnt RL baselines while discovering modes faster, attributed to its simpler fixed-policy evaluation framework that avoids the non-stationarity challenges of learning evolving policies. The paper also notes that GFlowNet baselines with fixed uniform P_B (rather than learned P_B) converge faster but yield inferior performance, further highlighting RPE's advantage from its simplified parameterization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the GFlowNets framework (Bengio et al., 2021; 2023) and its various training objectives (Flow Matching, Detailed Balance, Trajectory Balance, SubTB), but departs from the recent line of work connecting GFlowNets to maximum entropy RL (Tiapkin et al., 2024; Deleu et al., 2024; Mohammadpour et al., 2024), which required entropy-regularized objectives and intermediate reward corrections. Instead, it establishes a connection to standard (non-MaxEnt) RL through the most basic RL building block—iterative policy evaluation of a fixed uniform random policy—showing that GFlowNets' flow functions are equivalent to scaled value functions under a transformed reward structure. The dynamic programming perspective via flow iteration (Algorithm 2) serves as the bridge, reformulating GFlowNets training in a way that directly parallels the Bellman backup in policy evaluation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that GFlowNets' reward-matching capability can be replicated by simply evaluating a fixed uniform policy with a transformed reward offers a conceptually simpler and potentially more stable training procedure (avoiding non-stationarity from evolving policies), though its practical applicability is constrained by the path-invariance condition for non-tree DAGs. This condition fails in environments like HyperGrid where boundary effects create trajectory-dependent branching ratios, limiting universal applicability. Open questions include whether the connection extends to non-uniform policies, whether adaptive methods can automatically satisfy structural requirements, and whether the simplified parameterization (learning only F_θ rather than separate forward/backward policies) generalizes to more complex or stochastic environments.
