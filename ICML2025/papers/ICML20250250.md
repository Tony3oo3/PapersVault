## Problem

Existing safe learning-based control methods rely on model uncertainty being small enough for conservative safety filters to remain feasible; when uncertainty is too large (e.g., with a zero-mean prior and no prior measurements), no control input satisfies the safety constraints and the method breaks down. Most prior works circumvent this by assuming a safe backup controller, but this paper is the first to address infeasible safety filters by collecting additional data on the fly, achieving provable safety without any backup controller.

## Input / Output

The method operates on continuous-state systems affine in the control input (ẋ = f(x) + g(x)u), taking as input the current state x, a locally Lipschitz nominal controller π_nom, a known CBF h defining the safe set, and a GP prior (which may be zero-mean with no prior data). It outputs a continuous control signal u at a fixed sampling frequency Δt, switching between a safe input (from a CBF-based second-order cone program) and an exploratory input (from maximizing the UCB of the CBF time derivative). The GP model uses composite kernels over (x, u) pairs, so each measurement is a noisy observation of ẋ at a specific state-input pair.

## Method

The algorithm alternates between two modes: when the robust safety filter (a CBF-SOCP constraining the LCB of ḣ to exceed −α(h(x)) + ε/2) is strictly feasible, it applies the safe controller; when infeasibility is detected, it switches to bandit exploration by applying the control input that maximizes the UCB of ḣ, collecting a measurement, and updating the GP. The key insight is that the CBF itself guides exploration: maximizing the UCB of the CBF time derivative ensures each collected data point is informative for recovering safety-filter feasibility, rather than being random. The GP models f and g jointly via composite kernels k_i(z,z') = k_{f_i}(x,x') + Σ_j u_j k_{g_{i,j}}(x,x') u'_j, and the RKHS norm bound yields a uniform model error bound that feeds into the LCB/UCB construction. Theorem 3.2 proves that if the sampling frequency Δt exceeds a threshold depending on the CBF violation margin ε, Lipschitz constants, and the GP information gain, the system is safe with probability at least 1−δ and stops exploring after at most ΔN_max observations.

## Results

In the cruise control simulation (Δt = 10⁻⁵ s), the CBF value h(x) remains strictly positive throughout the 100-second run, confirming safety; the robust safety filter (12) is infeasible for the first ~6 seconds, after which feasibility is permanently recovered and no further exploration is needed. In the quadrotor simulation (Δt = 10⁻⁵ s), the CBF stays positive over 50 seconds, with infeasibility lasting only ~2 seconds before the safety filter becomes feasible and the quadrotor hovers in place. In the comparison with random exploration (Figure 5), the proposed UCB-based method achieves a strictly lower failure rate than random inputs at every tested sampling frequency for both the cruise control and quadrotor settings, and higher sampling frequencies reduce the total number of collected data points due to tighter model updates.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The only explicit baseline comparison is against random exploratory control inputs (uniformly sampled from the input set U) during infeasibility periods. Across both the cruise control and quadrotor settings, the proposed UCB-guided exploration consistently achieves a lower failure rate than random exploration at all tested sampling frequencies (Figure 5). The advantage is most pronounced at low sampling frequencies, where random inputs are less likely to reduce model uncertainty in the critical region near the safe-set boundary. The paper does not compare against other safe-learning baselines (e.g., GP-MPC with safety filters, ensemble-based methods, or backup-controller approaches), as the stated contribution is the first method to handle infeasible safety filters without a backup controller and with a zero-mean prior. The authors note that the random-input comparison is informative but not a full state-of-the-art benchmark, since most existing methods assume the safety filter is always feasible and thus cannot be directly applied in the infeasibility regime this paper targets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of control barrier function (CBF) safety filters and Gaussian process (GP) bandit exploration. Prior safe learning-based control methods (Castañeda et al. 2021, 2022; Capone et al. 2022) formulate robust CBF constraints using GP uncertainty bounds but assume the resulting safety filter is always feasible, typically relying on a safe backup controller when infeasibility arises. The GP bandit framework (Srinivas et al. 2012; Capone & Hirche 2019) provides the information-theoretic tools for regret-bounded exploration, while safe exploration literature (Sui et al. 2015; Turchetta et al. 2016) uses UCB/LCB for expanding safe sets. This paper's key departure is repurposing the bandit UCB not to maximize reward or expand a safe set, but to collect data that restores feasibility of the CBF safety filter itself, eliminating the need for a backup controller.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first method to provably guarantee safety with a zero-mean prior (no prior dynamics model) and no backup controller, addressing a practical gap where learned model uncertainty is too high for conservative safety filters to be feasible. The main limitation is the sampling frequency requirement (Δt must be small enough per Theorem 3.2), which may be impractical for high-dimensional or slow-sensing systems, and the method does not address closed-loop performance beyond safety. Open questions include whether preemptive exploration (before infeasibility) can reduce the required sampling rate, how the approach scales to high-dimensional systems, and whether the affine-in-control assumption can be relaxed in practice without degrading the SOCP solvability.
