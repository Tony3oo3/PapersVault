## Problem

Kernel mean embeddings represent distributions as mean functions in an RKHS, and the resulting MMD is a widely used probability metric. However, it remains unclear whether the mean is the only meaningful RKHS representation, and MMD requires the kernel to be mean-characteristic—a condition that is difficult to verify outside bounded translation-invariant kernels on Euclidean spaces. This paper asks whether nonlinear RKHS representations, specifically quantiles, can yield probability metrics under strictly weaker kernel conditions while maintaining comparable computational and statistical guarantees.

## Input / Output

The method consumes two i.i.d. sample sets x_{1:n} ~ P and y_{1:n} ~ Q, a positive-definite kernel k on the data space, and m reference samples z_{1:m} ~ ξ drawn from a user-specified reference measure (default: the empirical mixture ½P_n + ½Q_n). It outputs a scalar discrepancy value (e-KQD or sup-KQD) that serves as a test statistic; a permutation-based threshold (95th percentile over 300 label shuffles) converts this into a binary hypothesis test decision at significance level 0.05. The near-linear Gaussian e-KQD estimator runs in O(n log² n) time, with l = log n projection directions and m = log n Gaussian reference samples per direction.

## Method

The paper defines kernel quantile embeddings (KQEs) as directional quantiles of the RKHS feature map: for direction u on the unit sphere S_H, the α-quantile is ρ_P^{α,u}(x) = ρ_{u#P}^{α} u(x), a two-step embedding that first maps data into the RKHS and then computes 1D quantiles along u. A Cramér-Wold-type theorem (Theorem 1) shows that under mild topological and separating-kernel assumptions, the full set of KQEs injectively represents any distribution, and Theorem 2 proves that quantile-characteristic is strictly weaker than mean-characteristic. The e-KQD aggregates squared RKHS distances between paired KQEs over quantile levels α and directions u ~ γ, while sup-KQD takes the supremum over u; both are proven to be probability metrics under weaker conditions than MMD. For efficient estimation, the paper takes γ to be the pushforward of a centered Gaussian measure on H with integral-operator covariance, reducing sampling from γ to drawing standard Gaussians λ_{1:m} ~ N(0,1) and forming f_i = m^{-1/2} Σ λ_j k(·, z_j) (Proposition 1). The resulting estimator sorts the projected samples along each of l directions and computes order-statistic quantile differences, achieving O(n log² n) total complexity and O(n^{-1/2} + l^{-1/2}) convergence rate.

## Results

The paper evaluates KQD-based test statistics on four two-sample hypothesis testing experiments (two synthetic, two real-world image datasets). In the high-dimensional power-decay experiment (d up to 512), e-KQD exhibits the slowest decline in rejection rate among all methods, maintaining significantly better performance than its O(n log²n) complexity benchmark MMD-Multi. In the Laplace-vs-Gaussian experiment, KQD methods achieve increasing rejection rates with sample size while all MMD-based methods fail entirely (rejection rate ≈ 0) because the degree-3 polynomial kernel cannot distinguish distributions with matching first and second moments. On CIFAR-10 vs. CIFAR-10.1 (d=3072), near-linear KQD estimators (e-KQD, sup-KQD) significantly outperform MMD-Multi of the same complexity, while quadratic-complexity methods (e-KQD-centered, MMD) show nearly identical performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares KQD methods against two families of MMD-based baselines: the standard MMD (U-statistic, O(n²) complexity, Gretton et al. 2012) and MMD-Multi (incomplete U-statistic, O(n log²n) complexity, Schrab et al. 2022). Against MMD (quadratic complexity), e-KQD-centered performs nearly identically on Galaxy MNIST and CIFAR-10 vs. CIFAR-10.1, suggesting the MMD term dominates in the centered variant; however, in the Laplace-vs-Gaussian experiment, MMD fails completely while KQD methods succeed, demonstrating the strict advantage of quantile-characteristic over mean-characteristic kernels. Against MMD-Multi (matching near-linear complexity), e-KQD and sup-KQD consistently outperform on all four experiments, with the gap being most pronounced in the high-dimensional power-decay setting and on CIFAR-10 vs. CIFAR-10.1. The key practical takeaway is that at the same O(n log²n) computational budget, quantile-based discrepancies deliver higher test power than the best available fast MMD approximations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the kernel mean embedding (KME) framework by replacing the mean function with directional quantiles in the RKHS, drawing on the multivariate quantile literature (Kong and Mizera, 2012) and the sliced Wasserstein distance (Bonneel et al., 2015). The key conceptual departure is showing that quantile-characteristic kernels form a strictly larger class than mean-characteristic kernels (Theorem 2), so the resulting discrepancies are probability metrics under weaker conditions than MMD. The efficient estimator leverages Gaussian measures on Hilbert spaces (Kukush, 2020) to sample RKHS directions, reducing the problem to standard Gaussian sampling on R^m. The paper positions KQDs as unifying sliced Wasserstein, max-sliced Wasserstein, and Sinkhorn divergences as special cases, while offering greater kernel flexibility.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The strict inclusion of mean-characteristic in quantile-characteristic kernels means KQDs can in principle distinguish distributions that MMD provably cannot, a gap demonstrated empirically with the Laplace-vs-Gaussian experiment where MMD fails entirely. The near-linear O(n log²n) estimator with O(n^{-1/2}) convergence rate makes KQDs practical for large-scale two-sample testing, and the power-decay experiment suggests greater robustness to the curse of dimensionality than MMD variants. Open questions include optimal kernel selection for KQD-based tests, connections to conditional mean embeddings, and whether the modest empirical gains over matched-complexity MMD approximations (MMD-Multi) grow with problem difficulty. The framework also opens the door to other non-mean RKHS representations (medians, cumulants) with similar quantile-based estimation strategies.
