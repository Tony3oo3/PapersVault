## Problem

Existing methods for selecting valid instrumental variable sets from observational data are largely restricted to additive linear, constant effects (ALICE) models, severely limiting their applicability when the relationship between treatment and instruments is nonlinear. This paper addresses the more general and challenging additive nonlinear, constant effects (ANICE) model, where prior IV-set selection methods either fail or require strong linearity assumptions that do not hold in practice.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250823/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The algorithm takes as input an observed tabular dataset containing the treatment X, outcome Y, optional covariates W, and a set of candidate IVs Z, along with a user-specified parameter K indicating the expected number of valid IVs. It outputs the selected valid IV set (a subset of Z of size K) and a point estimate of the causal effect β̂. When covariates are present, all variables are first residualized by regressing on W before the CAT condition is applied, and the causal effect is estimated via a point estimator (linear case) or generalized method of moments (nonlinear case).

## Method

The core contribution is the Cross Auxiliary-based independent Test (CAT) condition: for a candidate pair {Zi, Zj}, auxiliary variables A_{X→Y||Zi} = Y − β̂_i·X and A_{X→Y||Zj} = Y − β̂_j·X are constructed, and the condition requires the cross-independence A_{X→Y||Zi} ⊥ Zj and A_{X→Y||Zj} ⊥ Zi. The paper proves this condition is necessary (Theorem 1) and, under a mild algebraic equation condition on the joint density (Assumption 1), also sufficient (Proposition 1), yielding a necessary-and-sufficient criterion for valid IV-set identification in the ANICE model. Practically, the algorithm enumerates all subsets of size K from the candidate IV set, computes pairwise distance correlations between auxiliary variables and the cross IV for each subset, and selects the subset minimizing the total distance correlation. The selected IV set is then used to estimate the causal effect via a point estimator or GMM, with correctness guaranteed in the infinite-sample limit under Assumption 1.

## Results

In the ANICE model (true β=1), the CAT algorithm consistently produces causal effect estimates closest to the ground truth with minimal variance across all four violation scenarios (C2-only, C3-only, C2+C3, and Assumption-2-violating) and all three sample sizes (1k, 3k, 5k). On the Colonial Origins dataset, the CAT condition yields p-values of 0.21 and 0.25 for the two cross-independence tests, confirming {Mortality, Euro1990} as a valid IV set for Institutions → Economic Development. On the Children and Mothers' Labor Supply dataset (254,652 observations), the minimum distance correlation is 0.022 for the pair {boys2, girls2}, confirming their validity as IVs for morekids → weeksm1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The CAT algorithm outperforms all five baselines (NAIVE, MR-Egger, TSHT, CIIV, sisVIVE, IV-tetrad) in the ANICE model across all four cases and sample sizes, with the gap widening as nonlinearity increases. NAIVE (OLS regression) fails in every scenario due to unmeasured confounding. MR-Egger produces biased estimates because it additionally requires the InSIDE assumption (uncorrelated pleiotropic effects), which is violated in the test cases. TSHT, CIIV, sisVIVE, and IV-tetrad all assume additive linear models and therefore degrade substantially under the nonlinear ANICE data-generating process, while CAT handles the nonlinearities natively. In the linear model setting (Appendix Figure 7), CAT performs comparably to TSHT, CIIV, sisVIVE, and IV-tetrad, confirming that the advantage is specifically in the nonlinear regime. In the ANICE model with covariates (Appendix Figure 8), CAT again outperforms all baselines, which cannot properly account for both nonlinearity and covariate adjustment simultaneously.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the AIT (Auxiliary-based Independence Test) condition from Guo et al. (2024), which tested validity of a single IV, to the IV set setting via a cross-independence formulation (the CAT condition). It departs from the broader IV set selection literature (Kang et al. 2016, Silva & Shimizu 2017, Windmeijer et al. 2021, Lin et al. 2024) that is confined to additive linear models, by establishing necessary and sufficient identifiability conditions in the additive nonlinear (ANICE) model. The theoretical foundation draws on the Lin (1997) separability theorem, which characterizes independence via the Hessian of the log-joint density, adapted here to the auxiliary-variable construction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The CAT condition fills a practical gap: most existing IV selection methods assume linearity, which is often violated in real-world settings (e.g., Mendelian randomization with pleiotropic effects, continuous treatments). The paper's main limitation is that Assumption 1 (the algebraic equation condition) is difficult to verify a priori, and the algorithm's combinatorial search over subsets of size K limits scalability to large candidate IV pools. The constant-effects assumption is also restrictive; extending to non-constant (heterogeneous) effects via kernel or GMM-based estimators is identified as future work. For practitioners in economics, epidemiology, and social sciences, this provides the first principled, testable criterion for IV set validity in nonlinear settings without requiring majority/plurality rules.
