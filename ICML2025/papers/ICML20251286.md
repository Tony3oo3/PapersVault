## Problem

Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) are the dominant method for decomposing LLM activations into interpretable features, but their training cost (hundreds of millions to billions of tokens) limits open-source SAEs to models of at most 27B parameters, and their learned parameters are model-specific, making cross-model comparison difficult. This paper asks whether a lightweight, inference-time approach can match SAE reconstruction quality at a fraction of the cost while also enabling direct comparison of representation spaces across different LLMs.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single LLM residual stream activation vector (at a chosen layer and token position) and a pre-constructed dictionary of reference activations from the same model point. The dictionary size is not fixed a priori but is determined by a reconstruction-loss threshold τ during construction; sparsity level L0 is fixed per query. Outputs include the sparse coefficient vector from Matching Pursuit, the reconstructed activation, and the dictionary itself whose atoms carry interpretable labels (the source prompt and token index). For cross-model comparison, the method outputs a Jaccard similarity score computed over the prompt-token pairs constituting two dictionaries.

## Method

ITDA replaces the learned linear encoder of an SAE with an inference-time Matching Pursuit solver that greedily selects dictionary atoms to reconstruct a target activation under an ℓ0 sparsity constraint. The dictionary is built iteratively: starting from frequent activations, each new training sample is reconstructed against the current dictionary, and if the MSE exceeds threshold τ, the normalized activation is appended as a new atom. Because each atom is a real activation tied to a specific prompt and token (rather than a learned parameter), dictionaries from different models can be compared directly via the Jaccard index of their prompt-token sets, yielding a relative representation similarity measure that requires no learned alignment map. This design reduces training to a single forward pass over ~1M tokens (versus hundreds of millions for SAEs), enabling deployment on 70B and 405B LLMs and achieving state-of-the-art accuracy on the Kornblith et al. layer-matching benchmark.

## Results

On the layer-matching benchmark (Kornblith et al., 2019), ITDA Jaccard similarity achieves 0.88 accuracy on GPT-2 small and 0.89 on GPT-2 medium, outperforming all prior methods including the closest competitor, the Relative representation measure (0.87/0.78). ITDAs are trained in ~8 minutes per layer on GPT-2 small (vs. 2–8 hours for SAEs) using only 1.2M tokens, and were successfully trained on Llama-3.1 70B and 405B — an order of magnitude larger than any model with open-source SAEs. For reconstruction, ITDAs match or slightly exceed ReLU SAEs on Pythia models but underperform TopK SAEs, and perform substantially worse than pretrained SAEs on Gemma-2 2B.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ITDA Jaccard similarity outperforms all five baselines on the layer-matching task. Against SVCCA (Raghu et al., 2017), ITDA improves accuracy from 0.50→0.88 (GPT-2 small) and 0.44→0.89 (GPT-2 medium). Against Linear CKA (Kornblith et al., 2019), the gains are 0.69→0.88 and 0.61→0.89. Against the Relative representation method (Moschella et al., 2022), ITDA edges ahead on GPT-2 small (0.88 vs. 0.87) and more clearly on GPT-2 medium (0.89 vs. 0.78). For reconstruction quality (CE loss score), ITDAs trained on 1.2M tokens outperform ReLU SAEs (Bricken et al., 2023) on Pythia models but underperform TopK SAEs (Gao et al., 2025) trained on hundreds of millions of tokens; on Gemma-2 2B, ITDAs are substantially worse than pretrained SAEs. The key trade-off is that ITDAs achieve competitive reconstruction at 100× lower training cost, enabling deployment on 70B/405B models where SAEs are infeasible.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes classical Matching Pursuit (Mallat & Zhang, 1993) as an inference-time sparse coding solver, replacing the learned encoder of Sparse Autoencoders (Bricken et al., 2023; Cunningham et al., 2023) with a fixed dictionary of actual model activations. It extends the relative representation framework of Moschella et al. (2022), which uses random anchor points and cosine similarity, by constructing the dictionary greedily based on reconstruction error and decomposing activations via iterative matching pursuit rather than nearest-neighbor lookup. For representation similarity, it departs from absolute metrics like CKA (Kornblith et al., 2019) and SVCCA (Raghu et al., 2017) by using the Jaccard index over dictionary labels (prompt-token pairs), which avoids learning any cross-model alignment map.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 100× training speedup and 1% token requirement make sparse activation decomposition feasible on 70B and 405B LLMs (Llama 3.1), an order of magnitude beyond the largest models with open-source SAEs, opening a practical path to interpretability at frontier scale. The Jaccard-based dictionary comparison provides a simple, training-free cross-model similarity metric that achieves SOTA on layer matching, suggesting ITDA dictionaries could serve as lightweight 'model fingerprints' for diffing base vs. fine-tuned models. Key limitations include worse reconstruction than TopK SAEs (especially on Gemma-2), the open question of whether data ordering during greedy dictionary construction matters for autoregressive models, and the unexplored potential for behavioral diffing (e.g., detecting sycophancy or scheming changes after chat fine-tuning).
