## Problem

While sequential neural activity patterns are known to support short-term memory, the specific dynamical mechanisms underlying these sequences and the factors determining which mechanism a network learns remain unclear. In particular, it is unknown whether networks rely on slow-point manifolds (transient, non-repeating trajectories) or limit cycles (periodic orbits that locally approximate sequences), and how the choice between these mechanisms depends on task parameters like delay duration and optimization parameters like learning rate.

## Input / Output

The RNNs receive a binary rectangular pulse (one of two input channels active) during a cue period of duration T_in, followed by a delay period with zero input; for the simpler delayed activation task, the network is instead initialized to a random state with no external input. The output is a two-channel projection of firing rates (via W_out) that must match the cue class during the reaction period and zero otherwise. Beyond the task response, the key analytical outputs are a mechanism classification (slow-point manifold, limit cycle, or failure) computed via a Mechanism Discrimination Index based on the power spectral density of PCA correlation matrices, and critical learning rate thresholds as a function of delay that define phase boundaries in a (learning rate, delay) parameter space.

## Method

The paper identifies two latent dynamical mechanisms—slow-point manifolds and limit cycles—that can generate the neural sequences required for delayed responses, and derives their selection criteria through both analytical toy models and large-scale RNN experiments. For the slow-point mechanism, they generalize the 1D saddle-node bifurcation (ghost) model to asymmetric delay/response times, showing the critical learning rate scales as α_SP ~ T_delay^(−β_SP) with β_SP ∈ [4,5]. For the limit cycle mechanism, they analyze a 2D polar-coordinate oscillator with a learnable frequency parameter, deriving α_LC ~ T_delay^(−β_LC) with β_LC ∈ [2,3], revealing that limit cycles have a fundamentally better scaling and thus dominate for longer delays or higher learning rates. These theoretical predictions are validated by training approximately 80,000 full-rank leaky firing-rate RNNs (N=100 neurons, tanh nonlinearity, SGD with momentum) on delayed cue-discrimination tasks across a grid of learning rates, delay durations, and random seeds, with a post-reaction period variant shown to eliminate slow-point solutions entirely. A Mechanism Discrimination Index (MDI), computed from the low-frequency fraction of the PSD of PCA correlation matrices over an extended inference window, automatically classifies each trained network into one of the three dynamical phases.

## Results

The paper derives theoretical power-law scaling for critical learning rates beyond which RNNs cannot learn delayed-response tasks: α_SP ~ O(T_delay^{-β_SP}) with β_SP ∈ [4,5] for slow-point manifolds, and α_LC ~ O(T_delay^{-β_LC}) with β_LC ∈ [2,3] for limit cycles. Empirically, across ~80,000 trained full-rank RNNs on delayed cue-discrimination tasks, the measured exponents are β_LC = 2.72 ± 0.07 and β_SP = 4.05 ± 0.10, in close agreement with theory. A control experiment removing the memory-maintenance requirement (no delayed output) dramatically weakens the scaling to β = 0.38 ± 0.02, confirming that the power-law constraint is specific to memory tasks. Adding even a brief post-reaction window (T_post = 30–50 ms) eliminates slow-point solutions entirely, biasing all learned mechanisms toward limit cycles.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external baseline methods; instead, it validates its own theoretical predictions against large-scale empirical results and contrasts two internal mechanisms (slow-point manifolds vs. limit cycles). The theoretical scaling exponents (β_LC ∈ [2,3], β_SP ∈ [4,5]) are confirmed by empirical fits (β_LC = 2.72 ± 0.07, β_SP = 4.05 ± 0.10) within error bars across ~80,000 RNNs. The limit cycle mechanism is shown to have a strictly better (slower-decaying) scaling than the slow-point mechanism, explaining why limit cycles dominate for longer delays or higher learning rates. A key internal comparison shows that adding a post-reaction period (T_post = 30–50 ms) eliminates all slow-point solutions (confirmed via Fisher's exact test with Bonferroni corrections, p < 0.001), leaving only limit-cycle dynamics. The control experiment without a delayed output requirement (β = 0.38 ± 0.02) demonstrates that the steep power-law constraint is intrinsic to the memory-maintenance demand rather than a generic property of RNN training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the ghost-mechanism analysis of Dinc et al. (2025b), which derived a critical learning-rate scaling law (β_SP ∈ [4,5]) for slow-point manifolds in a 1D saddle-node bifurcation toy model, by introducing a parallel limit-cycle toy model and deriving a competing scaling law (β_LC ∈ [2,3]). It leverages the low-rank RNN latent dynamical system framework of Dubreuil et al. (2022) and Valente et al. (2022) to identify and classify the two mechanisms in trained networks, and builds on the latent computing formalism of Dinc et al. (2025a). The key conceptual departure from prior work is showing that limit cycles—previously associated with explicitly periodic tasks—can emerge as a memory mechanism in aperiodic delayed-response tasks, and that the choice between the two mechanisms is governed by a phase diagram in (delay, learning rate) space.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical scaling laws and phase diagram explaining when RNNs will learn slow-point versus limit-cycle solutions for short-term memory, offering a principled explanation for the historical difficulty of learning long-term dependencies in vanilla RNNs. The finding that a trivial task modification (adding a 30 ms post-reaction window) can qualitatively shift the learned mechanism is a cautionary result for systems neuroscience, where task design choices are often treated as arbitrary. Open questions include how to distinguish these mechanisms in biological recordings where synaptic plasticity continuously reshapes connectivity, whether higher-dimensional latent dynamics enable additional mechanisms beyond these two, and whether gated architectures (LSTMs, GRUs) can bypass the power-law constraint by learning adaptive timescales. The public release of ~80,000 trained RNNs provides a valuable resource for the community.
