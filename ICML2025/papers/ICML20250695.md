## Problem

Stump forests, a natural building block for generalized additive models, have only been learned via boosting or bagging—techniques that do not optimize a well-defined objective over a fixed-size parametric model, leaving them without optimality guarantees and difficult to analyze theoretically. No prior work has successfully learned a fixed-size stump forest through direct optimization while controlling the severe overfitting that naive optimization produces.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a tabular dataset of N instances with D features (both numerical and categorical, one-hot encoded) and a target variable, producing an additive prediction F(x) = μ + Σ_d f_d(x_d) where each f_d is a piecewise-constant shape function. The model has a fixed number T of stumps, and the per-feature shape functions are directly visualizable, providing inherent interpretability. For classification, a logistic link is applied to the additive output to yield class probabilities.

## Method

The paper learns a fixed-size stump forest via alternating optimization: each stump's (feature, threshold, left-leaf, right-leaf) parameters are optimized exactly by enumeration over all (feature, threshold) pairs while others are held fixed, and all leaf values are jointly optimized by solving a convex problem (via CVXPY/MOSEK) given the splits. To combat overfitting, two regularization terms are introduced: an ℓ₁-roughness penalty that sums absolute differences between consecutive constant pieces of each per-feature shape function (analogous to fused lasso), and a deviation-from-bias penalty on leaf values. The roughness penalty is incorporated into the stump enumeration step via a closed-form solution that shrinks leaf values toward each other, while both penalties enter the joint leaf-fitting convex problem. This is the first method to learn stump forests without any ensembling (no boosting, no bagging), and the model can be re-initialized from an existing forest for incremental retraining.

## Results

On the CPU Activity regression benchmark, ORSF achieves a test error of 2.37 with only 642 parameters, outperforming Gradient Boosting (2.43, 3.4k params) and EBM (2.50, 16.6k params). On California Housing, ORSF reaches 54.80 test error versus 56.15 (GB) and 55.23 (EBM) with 1.4k parameters. On classification, ORSF achieves 27.33% error on FICO (vs. 27.62% GB, 27.43% EBM) and 4.95% on IJCNN (vs. 5.10% GB, 5.00% EBM), consistently using 2–10× fewer parameters than tree-based baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ORSF outperforms Gradient Boosting (scikit-learn) on 5 of 6 regression datasets (e.g., Cpuact 2.37 vs 2.43, Housing 54.80 vs 56.15) while using roughly 2–4× fewer parameters, and similarly on 5 of 6 classification datasets (e.g., IJCNN 4.95% vs 5.10%, FICO 27.33% vs 27.62%). Against EBM, ORSF wins on most benchmarks with dramatically fewer parameters (e.g., 642 vs 16.6k on Cpuact, 550 vs 3.7k on FICO), though EBM edges out on Year prediction (9.82 vs 9.30 test error). Traditional cubic splines (PyGAM) are the closest competitor, often matching or slightly beating ORSF on classification (e.g., IJCNN 4.92% vs 4.95%, Covtype 22.72% vs 22.71%) while using fewer parameters, but ORSF is consistently better on regression. Neural Additive Models (NAM) are substantially outperformed across all datasets (e.g., 3.41 vs 2.37 on Cpuact, 22.53% vs 16.40% on Letter). FLAM is competitive on some tasks (Year: 9.29 vs 9.30) but generally trails ORSF on both regression and classification. FastSparse is the closest tree-based competitor, with ORSF winning on Cpuact (2.37 vs 2.91) and Housing (54.80 vs 56.29) but tying or slightly losing on Year (9.30 vs 9.29) and FPS (55.41 vs 55.42).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a particular case of Forest Alternating Optimization (FAO, Carreira-Perpiñan et al., CVPR 2023), but simplifies the problem to depth-1 stumps so that each tree can be optimized directly by enumeration rather than requiring the full TAO algorithm. It adopts the joint leaf-fitting step from Friedman & Popescu (2008) and extends it with new regularization terms (ℓ₁-roughness on shape functions and deviation-from-bias penalties) that are specific to the piecewise-constant GAM structure. The key departure from prior work is rejecting the boosting/bagging paradigm entirely: instead of greedily adding stumps (as in EBM or gradient boosting) or averaging over bootstrap samples, the paper defines a fixed-size parametric model and optimizes it end-to-end via alternating optimization, aligning stump forests with the standard ML learning paradigm used for SVMs and neural networks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first demonstration that stump forests can be learned effectively without any ensembling mechanism, challenging the near-universal assumption that boosting or bagging is essential for tree-based models to generalize. The practical impact is a smaller, retrainable, and fully interpretable model that matches or exceeds EBM and GB accuracy with an order-of-magnitude fewer parameters, which is valuable in domains like healthcare where model transparency is critical. Limitations include the restriction to univariate shape functions (no pairwise interactions), the piecewise-constant nature of the shapes (less smooth than splines), and the reliance on a generic convex solver (CVXPY/MOSEK) for the joint leaf step, which may limit scalability. Open questions include extending the regularization framework to bivariate interactions and to general (non-stump) decision forests.
