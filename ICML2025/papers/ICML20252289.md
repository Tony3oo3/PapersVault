## Problem

Average Certified Radius (ACR) has become the de facto metric for evaluating Randomized Smoothing training strategies, yet no prior work has questioned its validity. This paper proves that ACR is fundamentally flawed: a trivial classifier can achieve arbitrarily large ACR given sufficient certification budget, and improvements on easy samples contribute over 1000× more to ACR than equivalent improvements on hard samples. Consequently, the field has been optimizing a biased objective that systematically degrades robustness on hard inputs.

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard RGB images (CIFAR-10 at 32×32, ImageNet at 224×224) and produces class labels along with certified radii under the Cohen et al. (2019) certification protocol (N=10⁵, α=0.001). A key unusual aspect is that the training procedure dynamically discards a portion of the training set (up to 42% at σ=1.0) based on estimated p_A values, and the noise samples used for training are adversarially modified via PGD while preserving their L2 norm, rather than being drawn i.i.d. from the Gaussian.

## Method

The paper proposes three modifications to standard Gaussian training that collectively replicate and surpass SOTA ACR by explicitly focusing on easy samples. First, after a warm-up epoch E_t, all training samples with p_A below a threshold p_t are permanently discarded. Second, the remaining dataset is reweighted so that each sample's sampling frequency is proportional to its approximate certified radius (clipped at a minimum of 1), making ACR approximately linear in p_A during training. Third, an adaptive PGD attack is applied to each Gaussian noise sample to find the nearest perturbation on the same L2-norm sphere that causes misclassification, amplifying the gradient signal for easy samples without affecting hard ones. Theoretically, the paper proves (Theorem 1) that a trivial classifier predicting a single class can exceed any ACR bound with sufficient budget, and (Theorem 2) that all points on a sphere have identical Gaussian density, justifying the unconstrained spherical search. Empirically, these simple modifications achieve SOTA ACR on CIFAR-10 (0.844 at σ=1.0) and recover 85–97% of the SOTA advance on ImageNet, while the paper advocates replacing ACR with the empirical distribution of p_A as the evaluation metric.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, the proposed method (Gaussian training + discard hard data + data reweighting + adaptive attack) achieves ACR of 0.564 (σ=0.25), 0.760 (σ=0.5), and 0.844 (σ=1.0), setting a new SOTA that surpasses CAT-RS (0.562, 0.757, 0.815) and all other baselines. On ImageNet, the method recovers 85–97% of the ACR gap between Gaussian training and SOTA (e.g., 0.842 vs. SmoothMix's 0.846 at σ=0.5), though it does not always match the absolute best. Crucially, the method's certified accuracy at small radii (e.g., 0.00) is consistently *worse* than Gaussian training (e.g., 59.3% vs. 68.7% at σ=0.5), confirming that ACR gains come at the cost of robustness on hard samples.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all six baselines on ACR across all three σ values on CIFAR-10, with the largest margin over Gaussian training (e.g., +0.208 ACR at σ=0.5) and a narrow margin over the strongest baseline CAT-RS (+0.003 to +0.029). On ImageNet, the method is competitive but does not always win: at σ=0.5 it trails SmoothMix by 0.004 ACR (0.842 vs. 0.846), and at σ=1.0 it trails CAT-RS by 0.029 (1.042 vs. 1.071). However, the paper's central finding is that all ACR gains—including those of the SOTA baselines—come at the cost of certified accuracy at small radii: Gaussian training consistently achieves the highest certified accuracy at r=0.00 (e.g., 68.7% at σ=0.5 vs. 59.3% for the proposed method), revealing that every RS training strategy evaluated with ACR systematically degrades performance on hard samples. The paper further shows via gradient norm analysis (Table 2) that SOTA methods like CAT-RS allocate 4.28× more gradient weight to easy samples than hard ones, whereas Gaussian training allocates only 0.45×, confirming the selection bias introduced by optimizing for ACR.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Randomized Smoothing framework of Cohen et al. (2019) and the subsequent line of RS training strategies (SmoothAdv, MACER, Consistency, SmoothMix, CAT-RS) that have all been evaluated using Average Certified Radius (ACR). Rather than proposing yet another training algorithm, it takes a meta-level critique: it proves theoretically that ACR is fundamentally flawed (trivial classifiers can achieve arbitrarily large ACR) and empirically demonstrates that all existing SOTA strategies are biased toward easy samples as a consequence. The paper then shows that three simple modifications to vanilla Gaussian training—discarding hard samples, reweighting by certified radius, and adaptive PGD on the noise sphere—can replicate or exceed SOTA ACR without targeting general robustness, effectively exposing the metric as the driver of apparent progress.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

If the field accepts that ACR is a poor metric, then years of RS training research optimized for ACR may have systematically degraded performance on hard samples while inflating a single aggregate number. The proposed alternative—reporting the empirical distribution of p_A—provides a budget-independent, more informative comparison that reveals trade-offs hidden by ACR. However, the paper's own method explicitly sacrifices clean accuracy and small-radius certified accuracy to boost large-radius performance, raising the open question of whether the community will adopt a metric that rewards uniform robustness rather than peak performance. The broader implication is that evaluation metrics in certified robustness (and potentially other areas) deserve more scrutiny than they have received.
