## Problem

Existing propagation-based feature imputation methods for graphs (e.g., FP, PCFI) produce low-variance channels where imputed values are nearly identical across all nodes, rendering those channels useless for downstream tasks that require distinct node representations. The paper provides both empirical evidence and a theoretical proof that when observed features within a channel share the same value, the diffusion process fills all missing entries with that same value, causing significant performance degradation at high missing rates.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252401/cropped_page1_idx4.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an undirected graph adjacency matrix and a partially observed node feature matrix (with missing entries at rates up to 99.98%, under structural or uniform missing patterns) and produces a fully imputed feature matrix with no learnable parameters. The imputed matrix is then fed to an off-the-shelf GNN (GCN for node classification, GAE for link prediction) to produce task-specific outputs. The imputation itself is parameter-free and runs in O(|E| + γFN²) time, with the dominant cost being Dijkstra's algorithm for shortest-path distance computation per channel.

## Method

FISF operates in two diffusion stages. First, a pre-diffusion stage (using PCFI's pseudo-confidence-weighted channel-wise diffusion) produces a pre-imputed matrix, from which low-variance channels are identified by selecting the bottom r% of channels by variance. In each identified channel, one synthetic feature (a random value from U[0,1]) is injected into a randomly chosen missing node. Second, a diffusion-with-synthetic-features (DSF) stage re-diffuses only the low-variance channels using a novel weighted adjacency matrix that multiplies two pseudo-confidence ratios: one based on distance from all known nodes (preserving standard propagation) and one based on distance from only the synthetic node (amplifying the synthetic feature's influence). This distance-encoding design ensures the single synthetic value spreads widely across the graph, increasing channel variance and restoring discriminative power to the imputed features without any learnable parameters.

## Results

On semi-supervised node classification at 99.5% missing rate (structural missing), FISF achieves 79.29% on Cora, 69.68% on CiteSeer, 76.90% on PubMed, 88.22% on Amazon-Photo, 79.40% on Amazon-Computers, and 69.92% on OGBN-Arxiv, approaching full-feature performance (e.g., 81.87% on Cora). On link prediction (ROC AUC) at the same missing rate, FISF reaches 87.26% on Cora and 84.12% on CiteSeer (structural), outperforming PCFI by up to 4.74 points. FISF also demonstrates robustness at extreme missing rates: with only 0.1% of features observed (r_m=0.999), it matches or exceeds full-feature accuracy on Cora, CiteSeer, and PubMed.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FISF outperforms the closest propagation-based baseline PCFI by 4.67 points on Cora (79.29 vs. 74.62), 3.62 on CiteSeer (69.68 vs. 66.06), and 2.43 on PubMed (76.90 vs. 74.47) under structural missing at r_m=0.995. Against FP, the gap widens to 7.43 points on Cora and 4.94 on PubMed, confirming that the low-variance channel problem is more severe in simpler propagation schemes. GNN-architecture baselines (GCNMF, GRAFENNE) and the reconstruction-based MEGAE are substantially outperformed (by 20–60 points) and MEGAE suffers OOM errors on PubMed and OGBN-Arxiv. On link prediction (ROC AUC), FISF beats PCFI by 4.74 points on CiteSeer (84.12 vs. 79.38) and 0.74 on Cora (87.26 vs. 86.75), while PCFI retains a slight edge on Photo (96.65 vs. 95.86). On medical tabular datasets (Micro-F1), FISF surpasses both graph baselines (PCFI, FP) and tabular-specific methods (GAIN, MIWAE, GRAPE, IGRM) on Echocardiogram (86.67), Duke Breast Cancer (76.58), and Diabetes (53.75), though MIWAE/GRAPE/IGRM hit OOM on the larger datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the propagation-based imputation line initiated by FP (Rossi et al., 2022) and PCFI (Um et al., 2023), which impute missing graph features by diffusing observed values along edges without learnable parameters. The key departure is the identification of a fundamental failure mode in all such methods: when observed features within a channel are nearly identical, diffusion produces a zero-variance channel that contributes nothing to downstream tasks. FISF addresses this by injecting a single synthetic feature per low-variance channel and using a novel distance-encoding scheme to spread it widely, making it the first imputation method to employ synthetic features. The approach sits within the broader taxonomy of graph learning with missing features (GNN architectures, reconstruction, propagation) but specifically targets the propagation family's blind spot.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The low-variance channel diagnosis provides a useful lens for understanding why propagation-based methods plateau at high missing rates, and the parameter-free fix is immediately practical for real-world graphs where missing rates of 97–99.9% are common. Scalability remains a concern: the distance-encoding step is O(N²F), and while ScalableFISF and FastFISF mitigate this, they trade some accuracy. Open questions include whether the single-synthetic-feature-per-channel design generalizes to heterogeneous or temporal graphs, and whether the uniform [0,1] sampling for synthetic values is optimal when feature scales vary widely across channels.
