## Problem

The rapid proliferation of LLM bias probes has outpaced the field's ability to (1) select probes that match the intended construct, (2) reconcile conflicting results across probes, and (3) reason about when probe findings generalize to real user behavior. Existing taxonomies (e.g., intrinsic/extrinsic, embedding/probability/generated-text) organize probes but offer no principled guidance for probe selection or interpretation, leaving researchers to make consequential subjective decisions without a structured framework.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes a research question specifying a target construct (e.g., gender-occupation bias) and an associated task (e.g., word-level associations vs. disparate impact assessment), along with a pool of candidate probes. It produces a narrowed set of recommended probes at the appropriate abstraction level, an interpretation of what observed (or absent) bias means at that level, identified boundary conditions explaining when bias does or does not emerge, and testable predictions about cross-model generalization. Unlike a single probe that outputs a bias score, EcoLevels is a meta-level decision procedure that structures the entire probe-selection and interpretation workflow.

## Method

EcoLevels classifies bias probes along two axes: ecological validity (the degree to which a probe approximates the intended real-world task) and abstraction level, which spans three tiers—associations (task-independent semantic linkages, e.g., WEAT, WinoBias), task-dependent decisions (controlled decision contexts, e.g., BBQ, BiasInBios), and naturalistic output (probes mimicking real user interactions, e.g., resume scoring, interview generation). The framework operationalizes social science concepts—correspondence principle (matching probe specificity to construct specificity), boundary conditions (using conflicting probe results to identify when bias emerges or is suppressed), and ecological validity (probe-task alignment)—to guide researchers from a broad construct through a specific task to a defensible probe subset. It further generates four testable hypotheses about bias generalization, including the prediction that association-level probes will most closely reflect ground-truth workforce distributions (being least sensitive to RLHF) while naturalistic-output probes will show the greatest cross-model variability due to developer-specific alignment interventions.

## Results

This is a position paper proposing the EcoLevels framework rather than reporting quantitative benchmarks. Its key empirical observation is a systematic discrepancy: association-level probes (e.g., LLM IB, WinoBias, WinoGender) consistently reveal strong gender-occupation bias in LLMs, while naturalistic output probes (e.g., Resume Classification, LLM BTA, Resume Assessment) show minimal or no significant bias for the same models. The paper demonstrates that probe selection alone—shifting from association-level to naturalistic output—can flip the conclusion from 'strong bias present' to 'no significant bias,' underscoring the practical stakes of the three challenges it identifies (probe selection, reconciling conflicts, and generalizability).

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, EcoLevels does not benchmark against competing methods with quantitative metrics. Instead, it positions itself against three existing taxonomy approaches: (1) Gallegos et al.'s data-structure taxonomy (embedding/probability/generated-text), which the authors argue is difficult to relate across levels and does not guide probe selection; (2) Goldfarb-Tarrant et al.'s intrinsic/extrinsic distinction, which EcoLevels adapts to prompt-to-output space but finds too coarse to differentiate artificial from naturalistic downstream tasks; and (3) the explicit/implicit distinction borrowed from psychology, which the authors argue breaks down for LLMs because (a) LLMs aggregate data from billions of individuals (collapsing the individual-vs.-societal distinction) and (b) it is unclear whether automaticity/controllability gradients transfer to LLMs. EcoLevels claims to address all three limitations by adding ecological validity as a selection criterion and introducing three discrete abstraction levels (associations, task-dependent decisions, naturalistic output) that enable systematic probe selection and boundary-condition reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper bridges social science measurement theory (IAT, self-report, ecological validity, correspondence principle) with the rapidly expanding landscape of LLM bias probes. It builds directly on the intrinsic/extrinsic distinction from Goldfarb-Tarrant et al. (2021) but reinterprets it in prompt-to-output space, and critiques existing taxonomies (Gallegos et al., 2024; Goldfarb-Tarrant et al., 2021) for lacking actionable guidance on probe selection. The EcoLevels framework is novel in combining ecological validity as a selection criterion with a three-level abstraction hierarchy (associations, task-dependent decisions, naturalistic output), drawing on social science concepts like boundary conditions to reframe conflicting probe results as opportunities for mechanistic understanding rather than contradictions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As the number of LLM bias probes proliferates, researchers face practical challenges in probe selection, result reconciliation, and generalizability that this paper addresses with a structured framework. The key open question is whether EcoLevels' predictions (e.g., that naturalistic output probes will show the most model-to-model variability due to RLHF) hold empirically across domains and model families. The paper also raises a fundamental conceptual question about whether the implicit/explicit bias distinction, central to decades of social science, meaningfully translates to LLMs given their reliance on aggregated training data and the unclear role of automaticity/controllability. As a position paper, its impact hinges on community adoption and subsequent empirical validation.
