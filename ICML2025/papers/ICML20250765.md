## Problem

While scaling laws for machine learning task performance are well established, it remains unclear how scaling model parameters, dataset size, and compute affects functional alignment with the primate visual ventral stream. Prior work relied on heterogeneously trained off-the-shelf checkpoints or focused on single brain regions, making it impossible to disentangle the relative contributions of architecture, data volume, and optimization to brain modeling. This paper asks whether continued scaling of current architectures and datasets can yield improved models of the brain's visual cortex, or whether a fundamental saturation limit exists.

## Input / Output

Models are trained on RGB images from ImageNet (1,000 classes) or EcoSet (565 classes), with controlled subsets of 1–300 images per class to vary dataset size. For evaluation, model activations at committed layers are compared against primate single-unit recordings (102 V1, 103 V2 neurons) and Utah array recordings (88 V4, 168 IT electrodes) using the same stimulus images, with alignment measured as Pearson correlation from a 90/10 linear regression split repeated ten times. Behavioral alignment is assessed via a logistic classifier on 2,160 labeled images, comparing confusion patterns to primate behavioral data on 240 test images. All scores are normalized to their maximum possible values and averaged into a single alignment score S (with misalignment L = 1 − S).

## Method

The paper systematically trains over 600 models from scratch across six architecture families (ResNet, EfficientNet, ConvNeXt, ViT, CORnet-S, AlexNet) at multiple widths and depths, on controlled subsets of ImageNet and EcoSet, using a standardized SGD training recipe (100 epochs, batch 512, cosine LR schedule). For each model, alignment is measured on Brain-Score benchmarks spanning V1, V2, V4, IT, and behavior. The core analytical contribution is fitting parametric power-law curves of the form L = E + A·X^(−α) (and a modified version with an offset parameter λ for architectures with slow initial alignment) to the misalignment scores as a function of parameters N, samples D, and FLOPs C. By jointly fitting L(N,D) = E + A/N^α + B/D^β and modeling compute as C = m(ND)^n, the authors derive optimal allocation exponents (D ≈ C^0.7, N ≈ C^0.3) and quantify per-region alignment gains, revealing that behavioral alignment follows an unsaturated power law while neural alignment plateaus at ~0.48, with higher cortical areas (IT, behavior) benefiting disproportionately from scaling.

## Results

Across 600+ models trained from scratch, behavioral alignment follows the power law S = 1 − 1.4·C̃^(−0.06), converging to perfect alignment (S→1) at large compute, while neural alignment saturates at S = 0.48 − 0.55·C̃^(−0.16), plateauing at 0.48. Model-size scaling alone saturates at 0.44, whereas dataset scaling reaches 0.46 (ImageNet) and 0.48 (EcoSet), yielding an optimal compute allocation of D ≈ C^0.7 and N ≈ C^0.3. Alignment gains are ordered by cortical hierarchy: Behavior > IT > V4 > V2 > V1, with higher regions benefiting most from additional scale.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among architecture families, ResNet and EfficientNet (strong convolutional inductive bias) achieve substantially higher neural alignment at initialization and in the low-data regime compared to ConvNeXt and ViT, which require significantly more compute before alignment begins to rise; however, all families converge to similar saturation levels at scale. CORnet-S (recurrent) outperforms both convolutional and transformer models under limited supervision but loses this advantage as data increases. Data scaling outperforms model scaling: the fitted exponents show 70% of compute should go to dataset size (D ≈ C^0.7) versus only 30% to parameters (N ≈ C^0.3). EcoSet yields a slightly higher neural alignment ceiling (0.48) than ImageNet (0.46), while specialized datasets like Places365 and infiMNIST fall well below the generalist scaling curves. Self-supervised training (SimCLR, DINO) and adversarial fine-tuning (FGSM) reproduce the same neural-alignment saturation observed under supervised learning, confirming the plateau is not an artifact of the training objective. Pretrained models including CLIP and DINOv2 (trained on LAION-scale data) also exhibit the same neural alignment saturation, reinforcing that the bottleneck is architectural rather than data volume.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the compute-optimal scaling law framework from Hoffmann et al. (2022) and Kaplan et al. (2020) to the domain of brain alignment, replacing task loss with misalignment scores from Brain-Score benchmarks. It departs from prior brain-modeling studies (e.g., Linsley et al. 2023, Conwell et al. 2024) that relied on heterogeneously trained off-the-shelf checkpoints or focused on single cortical areas, instead conducting a controlled from-scratch training of over 600 models across multiple architecture families and dataset sizes. The work unifies scattered observations about the role of model size and data volume in brain alignment (Antonello et al. 2023, Muttenthaler et al. 2023) into quantitative power-law scaling curves with explicit compute-allocation prescriptions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that neural alignment saturates while behavioral alignment continues to improve with scale—challenges the prevailing assumption that simply scaling current architectures and datasets will yield better brain models, and points toward the need for fundamentally novel strategies (biologically inspired architectures, co-training with neural data, alternative objectives). The concrete compute-allocation prescription (D≈C^0.7, N≈C^0.3) and the demonstration that inductive biases (convolution, recurrence) confer sample efficiency provide actionable guidance for the brain-modeling community. Open questions include whether the saturation is an artifact of the specific benchmarks, whether alternative training paradigms (e.g., co-training with neural recordings) can break the plateau, and whether the ordered cortical effect (higher regions benefit more from scale) reflects a fundamental representational bottleneck.
