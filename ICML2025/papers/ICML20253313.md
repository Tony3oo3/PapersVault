## Problem

The widespread adoption of LLMs is destabilizing the human data sources that powered prior AI breakthroughs—contributors use AI to shortcut annotation tasks while the internet floods with synthetic content. The paper argues this crisis reveals a deeper design flaw: data collection systems prioritize speed, scale, and financial incentives over contributors' intrinsic motivation, creating a vicious cycle of declining engagement and quality that content filtering alone cannot fix.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20253313/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

As a position paper, the work takes as input a comparative analysis of existing data collection systems—crowdsourcing platforms (MTurk, Prolific), freelance platforms (UpWork), and organic communities (Wikipedia, Reddit)—framed along a quality-quantity Pareto frontier. The output is a design framework centered on a 'resolution of control' spectrum, proposing that sustainable data collection requires shifting from task-level, incentive-driven control toward environment-level, motivation-preserving structures, with games as the exemplar of the optimal middle zone.

## Method

The paper draws on foundational social science theories—Self-Determination Theory, Self-Perception Theory, the Overjustification Effect, and Goodhart's Law—to explain why extrinsic financial incentives and task fragmentation systematically erode the intrinsic motivation that sustains high-quality human contributions. It introduces 'resolution of control' as a central design variable, mapping existing systems from environment-level (Wikipedia, Reddit) to task-level (MTurk) control and identifying a middle zone where structured data collection can coexist with voluntary, self-directed engagement. Games are proposed as the natural instantiation of this middle zone, combining the structure needed for reliable data with the autonomy and psychological needs (competence, relatedness) that sustain long-term participation. The paper further argues that trust must be treated as a third axis alongside quality and quantity, and that compensation schemes must be redesigned to acknowledge contributor value without crowding out intrinsic drive.

## Results

This is a position paper without quantitative experimental results. Its central argument is qualitative: over-reliance on extrinsic financial incentives and task fragmentation in data collection systems (e.g., MTurk) erodes intrinsic motivation over time, leading to declining data quality and contributor engagement. The paper frames the quantity-quality trade-off as a Pareto frontier that can be expanded by shifting from task-level control to environment-level design, and identifies games as a promising middle-ground paradigm that sustains voluntary, high-quality participation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new method to benchmark against baselines; instead, it contrasts existing data collection paradigms qualitatively. Rapid crowdwork platforms (MTurk, Prolific) are characterized as high-throughput but low-quality systems that optimize for speed via micro-task fragmentation, while freelance platforms (UpWork) favor quality at the cost of scale. Community platforms (Wikipedia, Reddit) are presented as operating outside the quantity-quality trade-off entirely, sustaining high-quality contributions through intrinsic motivation without financial compensation. The paper positions game-based data collection (e.g., ESP Game, QuickDraw, Zooniverse, FoldIt) as a promising middle ground that combines structured data utility with sustained voluntary engagement, though it acknowledges no game-based ML data collection system has yet demonstrated sustained large-scale success. The conceptual 'baseline' the paper argues against is the prevailing incentive-centric view that financial compensation alone can reliably source high-quality human data at scale.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper does not extend a specific ML method lineage; instead, it repurposes decades of social-science findings (Self-Determination Theory, Self-Perception Theory, the Overjustification Effect, Goodhart's Law) to diagnose a structural flaw in how ML data-collection platforms are designed. It positions itself against the prevailing crowdsourcing paradigm (MTurk, Prolific, UpWork) by contrasting it with organic community platforms (Wikipedia, Reddit) and the Games-with-a-Purpose tradition (Von Ahn's ESP Game, QuickDraw, Zooniverse, FoldIt), arguing that the latter's intrinsic-motivation-driven engagement is the more sustainable model. The LLM-driven disruption of human data quality (Veselovsky et al., Brooks et al.) and the model-collapse risk of synthetic data (Taori & Hashimoto, Shumailov et al.) serve as the motivating context, but the paper's core argument is conceptual rather than empirical.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a timely and underappreciated design failure: that the very incentive structures meant to sustain human data supply are eroding the intrinsic motivation that makes that data high-quality, a problem now amplified by LLMs enabling low-effort annotation. Its central open question is practical—how to build game-like, mid-resolution-control environments that simultaneously yield structured, high-quality data at scale while preserving contributor trust and autonomy—and it offers no empirical validation of the proposed design principles. For the field, the paper reframes data collection from a throughput engineering problem to a socio-technical design problem, but the gap between the argument and a deployable system remains large, and the trust/compensation challenges it raises (e.g., attribution in multiplayer games, avoiding manipulative incentive loops) are far from resolved.
