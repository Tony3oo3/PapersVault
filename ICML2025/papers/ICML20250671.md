## Problem

Frequency-domain methods for time series imputation rely on the FFT, which assumes uniform sampling and requires interpolation or zero-filling of missing values before spectral computation—distorting the power spectrum, especially at high missingness. The Lomb-Scargle periodogram avoids this by estimating spectra directly from irregularly sampled data, yet it has remained largely unexplored in machine learning pipelines. This paper bridges that gap by integrating a differentiable Lomb-Scargle layer into a score-based diffusion model for imputation.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a partially observed multivariate time series X ∈ R^{K×L} with an observation mask M ∈ {0,1}^{K×L} and timestamps S, operating on a fixed grid (not truly irregular sampling). The Lomb-Scargle periodogram is computed only from the observed (conditional) entries, filtered by False Alarm Probability, log-transformed, and normalized before being encoded as a conditioning vector. The output is a complete time series reconstruction where missing entries are imputed, with the spectral structure of the observed portion preserved in the imputed signal.

## Method

LSCD builds on the CSDI conditional diffusion framework, adding two spectral components. First, a differentiable Lomb-Scargle layer computes the periodogram of the observed data over a frequency grid without any interpolation, and an attention-based spectrum encoder (two multi-head self-attention blocks along frequency and feature axes) produces a spectral embedding z_S that is concatenated with the observed context at every denoising step of a U-shaped transformer. Second, after standard score-matching training, a spectral consistency loss L_SCons = ‖LS(x₀^co) − LS(x̂₀^co)‖² is minimized as a fine-tuning stage, penalizing discrepancies between the periodogram of the observed data and that of the fully reconstructed signal. The FAP-based weighting (w(ω_k) = 1/(P_FA(ω_k)+ε)) ensures that statistically significant frequency components dominate the spectral supervision, while the sinusoidal fitting inherent to Lomb-Scargle provides a robust spectral estimate even under 90% missingness.

## Results

On PhysioNet, LSCD achieves the best MAE across all missingness levels: 0.211 (10%), 0.303 (50%), and 0.479 (90%), with corresponding RMSE of 0.494, 0.664, and 0.832. On PM2.5 (10% missing), LSCD reaches MAE 9.069 and RMSE 17.914, substantially outperforming the next-best CSDI (MAE 9.670, RMSE 19.093). LSCD also achieves the lowest S-MAE (spectral fidelity) in nearly all settings, e.g., 0.012 on PhysioNet 10% and 0.022 on PM2.5, confirming superior frequency-domain reconstruction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LSCD outperforms all eight baselines on MAE and RMSE across every real-dataset setting. The closest competitor is CSDI (the underlying diffusion framework without spectral conditioning): on PhysioNet 10%, LSCD improves MAE from 0.219 to 0.211 and RMSE from 0.545 to 0.494; on PM2.5, the gap widens to MAE 9.069 vs. 9.670 and RMSE 17.914 vs. 19.093. SAITS is competitive on time-domain MAE for some low-missingness cases (e.g., 0.232 on PhysioNet 10%) but consistently lags on S-MAE (0.014 vs. 0.012), indicating it fails to preserve spectral structure. TimesNet shows a systematic spectral bias (S-MAE 0.022 on PhysioNet 10% vs. 0.012 for LSCD) and degrades sharply at higher missingness. BRITS, GPVAE, US-GAN, and ModernTCN are uniformly weaker, with ModernTCN (a foundation model) achieving MAE 0.351 on PhysioNet 10%—nearly 66% worse than LSCD. On the synthetic Sines dataset, LSCD's S-MAE advantage is most dramatic: 0.003 at 10% MCAR missingness versus 0.008 for CSDI and 0.043 for SAITS, confirming that Lomb-Scargle conditioning is the primary driver of spectral fidelity gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the conditional score-based diffusion framework of CSDI (Tashiro et al., 2021) by introducing a differentiable Lomb-Scargle periodogram as a conditioning signal, replacing the FFT-based spectral representations used in prior frequency-aware imputation methods (e.g., TimesNet, Fourier Flows) that require uniform sampling or pre-interpolation. The key departure is that Lomb-Scargle computes power spectra directly from irregularly or partially observed data without any imputation step, sidestepping the spectral distortion introduced by zero-filling or interpolation. The spectral consistency loss and the attention-based spectrum encoder are novel architectural additions not present in prior diffusion-based imputation work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The differentiable Lomb-Scargle layer provides a practical, drop-in component for any learning pipeline dealing with irregular or missing time series, broadening the applicability of spectral guidance beyond astronomy and signal processing. A key limitation is that the current architecture operates on grid-based data, so the full advantage of Lomb-Scargle for truly irregular sampling is not yet realized; extending LSCD to continuous-time or non-grid settings remains an open question. The sinusoidal assumption underlying the periodogram may introduce bias for strongly non-sinusoidal signals, and the modest impact of the spectral consistency loss in ablations (MAE 0.211→0.213) suggests its practical value may be dataset-dependent.
