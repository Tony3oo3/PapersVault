## Problem

Neural network inference in real-time rendering pipelines (frame extrapolation, supersampling, image composition) consumes significant latency, and while temporal coherence between consecutive frames offers redundancy to exploit, existing intermediate feature caching techniques like DeepCache were designed for diffusion models with fixed, predictable iteration patterns. Real-time rendering presents a fundamentally different challenge: every single inference must produce a high-quality frame, and scene changes are unpredictable due to camera motion and dynamic content, making fixed refresh schedules suboptimal.

## Input / Output

ReFrame is applied to existing encoder-decoder networks (U-Net, U-Net++, or feature-fusion architectures) without any retraining. For frame extrapolation, the network receives historical frames (I_{t-2}, I_{t-1}, I_t) plus G-buffers (motion vectors, normals, depth, base color) and outputs an intermediate frame; for supersampling, a low-resolution ray-traced image (270p or 360p) is upscaled to 1080p; for image composition, a real photograph and virtual render are combined using estimated depth. The method requires storing only one or a few intermediate tensors plus one copy of the input image for delta computation, with peak memory overhead under 60 MB for the tested networks.

## Method

ReFrame caches the output of the last encoder block (or higher-level blocks in U-Net++) during a full inference frame, then in subsequent frames reuses the cached features concatenated with freshly computed skip connections from the first block, skipping all intermediate layers. The key contribution over DeepCache is an adaptive cache refresh policy: instead of a fixed every-N schedule, ReFrame computes the symmetric mean absolute percentage error (SMAPE) between the current input and the cached frame, refreshing the cache only when the change exceeds a tunable threshold (Delta_H or Delta_L). A motion-vector threshold variant is also explored, leveraging G-buffer data already available in rendering pipelines. The approach is architecture-agnostic within encoder-decoder networks with concatenations, and the saved inference time can be reallocated to rendering a higher-quality input (e.g., more ray-tracing samples or higher input resolution) to further offset any quality loss.

## Results

ReFrame achieves an average 1.4× inference speedup with negligible perceptual quality loss (FLIP scores of 0.0006–0.049) across three real-time rendering tasks (frame extrapolation, supersampling, and image composition) on an NVIDIA RTX 2080 Ti. With the low-sensitivity Delta_L policy, 72% of frames use cached features, yielding a 40% reduction in encoder-decoder FLOPs and 1.6× speedup; the high-sensitivity Delta_H policy still reduces 19% of FLOPs with even lower quality degradation. All FLIP scores remain well below the 0.05–0.28 range reported as acceptable in other neural rendering systems, confirming that the quality trade-off is imperceptible to end users.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary external baseline is DeltaCNN (Parger et al., CVPR 2022), which exploits inter-frame similarity by processing only pixel-level deltas. On the ExtraNet frame extrapolation task (Asian Village scene), DeltaCNN reduces theoretical FLOPs from 49.95 to 41.79 (frame 2), but actual FLOPs remain at 46.45 due to framework and tiling overheads, and it produces a FLIP score of 0.184 versus ReFrame's 0.024–0.046. ReFrame is more effective at reducing both FLOPs and actual inference latency because it eliminates entire network blocks rather than scattered pixel-level operations that commodity GPUs handle poorly. The authors also test a combined DeltaCNN + ReFrame approach and find only marginal additional gains over ReFrame alone, confirming that the scattered sparsity of DeltaCNN is the bottleneck. Within the paper's own ablation, the adaptive Delta_L cache policy outperforms fixed every-N policies (N-2, N-5) and the non-linear DeepCache policy on FLIP (0.035 vs. 0.040) and MSE (6.07 vs. 7.26) while requiring fewer cache refreshes (3 vs. 2 for N-5), demonstrating that adaptive refresh is critical for real-time rendering where scene changes are unpredictable.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends the layer-caching mechanism introduced by DeepCache (Ma et al., 2024) for diffusion models to encoder-decoder networks in real-time rendering pipelines. The key conceptual departure is that diffusion models have a fixed, predictable sequence of inference steps, whereas rendering workloads exhibit unpredictable camera motion and scene changes, necessitating adaptive cache-refresh policies (frame deltas, motion vectors) rather than fixed schedules. The paper also positions itself against delta-based methods like DeltaCNN, arguing that caching avoids the scattered-computation problem that prevents those techniques from delivering realized speedups on commodity hardware.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is meaningful for lower-end and mobile devices where every millisecond of inference latency matters, and the training-free nature makes it immediately applicable to existing rendering networks. However, the technique is limited to U-Net and encoder-decoder architectures with skip connections, excluding transformer-based models like DLSS 4.0, and it only reduces average latency rather than guaranteeing a consistently higher frame rate due to periodic cache refreshes. Open questions include whether dynamic sensitivity tuning, combination with quantization or pruning, and extension to transformer architectures could broaden applicability.
