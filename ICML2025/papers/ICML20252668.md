## Problem

Representation-based steering methods (SAEs, linear probes, steering vectors, representation finetuning) have been proposed as interpretable alternatives to prompting and finetuning for controlling LLM outputs, but no benchmark exists to compare them directly at scale. Existing evaluations cover only a handful of methods on toy tasks, leaving it unclear whether steering is a viable alternative to incumbent model-control techniques in realistic, open-vocabulary settings.

## Input / Output

AXBENCH takes natural-language concept descriptions (sampled from GemmaScope SAE concept lists) and synthesizes 144 labelled training examples per concept via GPT-4o-mini, split into positive (concept-present) and negative (concept-absent) responses, plus hard negatives for evaluation. Methods consume token-level hidden representations from specific residual-stream layers (L10/L20 for Gemma-2-2B, L20/L31 for Gemma-2-9B) and produce either a rank-1 projection vector for detection or an in-place activation modification for steering. Steering outputs are scored by an LLM judge on a 0–2 harmonic mean of concept presence, instruction-following, and fluency over 10 Alpaca-Eval instructions per concept.

## Method

The paper introduces AXBENCH, a large-scale benchmark that evaluates 12+ steering and detection methods along two axes (concept detection C and model steering S) on 500 concepts (CONCEPT500) and 16K concepts (CONCEPT16K) across four layer sites. The key novel method, ReFT-r1, jointly learns a single rank-1 vector w by combining a linear-probing detection objective (ReLU(h·w)) with a supervised steering objective: during training, each token's representation is modified by adding a scaled copy of w, where the scale is the L1 norm of the top-k detection latents, and the loss is the LM cross-entropy under this intervention plus L1 regularization on non-top-k latents. This makes ReFT-r1 a weakly-supervised dictionary-learning method that requires only 144 synthetic examples per concept yet achieves competitive steering performance. The benchmark's central finding is that prompting outperforms all steering methods, finetuning follows, and SAEs are not competitive on either axis, while ReFT-r1 is the only steering method that approaches finetuning-level performance.

## Results

On model steering (harmonic mean of concept, instruction, and fluency scores, 0–2 scale), prompting achieves the highest average score of 0.894, followed by LoReFT (0.741), SFT (0.676), LoRA (0.615), and the proposed ReFT-r1 (0.543). Among representation-based methods, DiffMean (0.239) substantially outperforms SAEs (0.165), while SSV (0.026) and Probe (0.098) are near-useless for steering. On concept detection (AUROC), DiffMean (0.942), Probe (0.940), and ReFT-r1 (0.938) are statistically indistinguishable and all significantly outperform SAEs (0.695) and SAE-A (0.917).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ReFT-r1 (the proposed method) is the strongest representation-based steering method at 0.543 average, but still trails prompting (0.894) by 0.35 and LoReFT (0.741) by 0.20; it does, however, trace a Pareto-optimal path between concept and instruction scores. DiffMean (Marks & Tegmark) is the best simple baseline for concept detection (0.942 AUROC, statistically tied with ReFT-r1's 0.938) and also the second-best steering representation method (0.239), roughly 1.4× SAE's 0.165. SAEs (Lieberum et al., GemmaScope) are the weakest competitive method on both axes: 0.695 AUROC for detection (vs. 0.942 for DiffMean) and 0.165 steering (vs. 0.543 for ReFT-r1); even with AUROC-based feature selection (SAE-A), performance does not improve (0.157 steering). LoRA (Hu et al.) achieves 0.615 steering, outperforming ReFT-r1 by 0.07, while LoReFT (Wu et al.) reaches 0.741, second only to prompting. SSV and LAT (Zou et al.) underperform SAEs on steering (0.026 and 0.127 respectively) despite SSV achieving 0.912 AUROC on detection, confirming that better detection does not translate to better steering. Integrated Gradients (Sundararajan et al.) and I×G are near-random on detection (0.426 and 0.246 AUROC) and inapplicable to steering.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the representation-steering literature (activation addition via difference-in-means, supervised steering vectors, linear artificial tomography) and the sparse autoencoder feature-discovery line (GemmaScope, Templeton et al. 2024), but departs from prior work by introducing the first large-scale, open-vocabulary benchmark (AXBENCH) that directly compares these representation-based methods against prompting and finetuning baselines. The novel ReFT-r1 method extends ReFT (Wu et al. 2024a) by jointly optimizing a detection objective (linear probe with ReLU) and a steering objective (language-modeling loss under intervention) with L1 sparsity regularization, unifying two previously separate training signals. Prior steering evaluations (Pres et al. 2024; Braun et al. 2024) were limited to a handful of methods on narrow, fixed tasks; AXBENCH generalizes to 500+ concepts across multiple layers and model sizes with synthetic data generation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that prompting outperforms all representation-steering methods, and that SAEs are not competitive even at scale—provides sobering evidence that the interpretability community's investment in SAE-based steering has not yet yielded practical advantages over simple baselines. ReFT-r1 demonstrates that joint learning of detection and steering can partially close the gap, suggesting a concrete path forward, while the released CONCEPT500/CONCEPT16K datasets and SAE-scale dictionaries establish a reusable evaluation infrastructure. Open questions include whether better SAE feature labeling (beyond current auto-interpretability pipelines) narrows the gap, whether steering can be made competitive on larger models, and how to resolve the persistent tradeoff between concept incorporation and capability preservation.
