## Problem

Even well-aligned LLMs lack true comprehension of their own reasoning processes, relying on probabilistic pattern matching that produces hallucinations and errors they cannot detect or correct, especially in long chains of thought. Existing step-level supervision methods (PRMs, step-wise preference data) require human or superior-model annotations, struggle to define fine-grained reasoning steps, and are difficult to scale. This paper asks whether self-play in a language game can enhance the rationality of the reasoning process without any external supervision.

## Input / Output

The game takes a math question and a critique of the prover's solution as input; the critique is either helpful (identifying a real error without giving the answer) or misleading (fabricating a false error in a correct solution). The prover outputs either a revised solution in \boxed{} format or the explicit marker \boxed{This critic is not critical.} to reject a misleading critique. All three roles (prover, helpful critic, misleading critic) are instantiated from the same base model, and the critics freely choose the granularity of steps they critique in natural language.

## Method

The Critic-Discernment Game (CDG) pits a prover against two types of critics: a helpful critic that identifies real errors in incorrect solutions, and a misleading critic that fabricates false errors in correct solutions. The prover's reward (Eq. 1) is higher for getting the answer right initially and resisting the misleading critic than for correcting an initial error with the helpful critic, creating an asymmetric incentive structure. Training uses ReST (Reinforced Self-Training) in an offline multi-round scheme: episodes are collected via self-play, filtered by role-specific reward thresholds (τ_π=0.5, τ_ρ=0.75, τ_μ=0.5), and used for supervised fine-tuning on the accumulated dataset. The key mechanism is that the adversarial co-evolution between the prover and the increasingly adaptive misleading critic forces the prover to develop a genuine understanding of its reasoning steps rather than merely pattern-matching, as confirmed by ablations showing that removing either critic degrades self-correction performance.

## Results

On the four-task evaluation (Math Reasoning, Error Detection, Self-Correction, Long-chain Reasoning), CDG achieves an average score of 45.5, outperforming Expert Iteration (43.4) and Step-DPO (41.0). The most substantial gains appear in Error Detection (76.9/71.4 F1 on GSM8K/MATH500 vs. 75.4/67.4 for Expert Iteration) and Long-chain Reasoning (29.7 vs. 22.8 for Expert Iteration and 27.6 for Step-DPO). On pure math reasoning (GSM8K P@1), CDG reaches 86.8, slightly below Expert Iteration's 87.2 but above the vanilla model's 85.3. CDG also flips Self-Correction from negative to positive (0.7/1.4 vs. -1.5/1.0 for the vanilla model), indicating the model no longer degrades correct answers during self-review.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CDG is compared against two RL baselines: Expert Iteration (outcome-reward fine-tuning on correct answers) and Step-DPO (step-level DPO with GPT-4o-generated preferences). CDG decisively outperforms both on Error Detection (76.9/71.4 F1 vs. 75.4/67.4 for Expert Iteration and 69.8/58.2 for Step-DPO), Self-Correction (0.7/1.4 vs. -0.6/0.8 and -2.2/-2.1), and Long-chain Reasoning (29.7 vs. 22.8 and 27.6). On Math Reasoning, CDG is competitive: it slightly trails Expert Iteration on GSM8K (86.8 vs. 87.2) but matches or exceeds it on MATH500 (51.7 vs. 50.8), while Step-DPO is weaker on GSM8K (84.6). The key differentiator is that Expert Iteration, relying solely on outcome rewards, actually degrades Long-chain Reasoning (22.8 vs. 25.3 vanilla), whereas Step-DPO's step-level supervision helps Long-chain but harms Error Detection and Self-Correction. CDG is the only method that improves across all four tasks simultaneously, achieving the highest average score of 45.5.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the self-play paradigm for LLMs (inspired by AlphaGo Zero and SPAG's adversarial taboo game) by introducing a three-role Critic-Discernment Game where a prover must both accept constructive feedback and resist adversarial misdirection. It departs from process reward models and step-wise preference methods (Step-DPO, PRMs) by deriving step-level supervision entirely from game rules rather than human or superior-model annotations, and from SPAG by targeting already-aligned instruction-tuned models rather than activating latent abilities in pre-trained models. The training algorithm builds directly on ReST's threshold-based sample selection, replacing the need for a reference model with offline multi-round self-play.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a well-recognized limitation—LLMs' inability to genuinely comprehend their own reasoning steps—through a scalable, supervision-free mechanism. The ablation showing that both the helpful and misleading critics are necessary (single-critic variants degrade self-correction) suggests that the key learning signal is the prover's ability to *discern* feedback quality, not merely follow or ignore it. However, improvements are modest (1–3 points on MATH500), evaluation is limited to mathematics and small models (≤8B), and only two rounds of self-play are explored, leaving open whether the approach scales to larger models, more reasoning domains, or additional training iterations.
