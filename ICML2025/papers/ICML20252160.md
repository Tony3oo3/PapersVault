## Problem

Existing in-training backdoor defenses either depend on impractical additional clean subsets/seeds (which are costly to acquire and raise privacy concerns) or rely on complex feature extraction that yields compromised performance. The paper identifies that prior methods fail to fully exploit two readily available signals within the poisoned dataset itself: the semantic structure of target labels and the loss statistics of clean hard samples, leading to incomplete separation of poisoned from benign data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252160/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes only the poisoned training set (RGB images with their associated labels) and requires no external clean samples, clean seeds, or auxiliary datasets. The target label y_t is approximated internally via the most frequent second-likely prediction of a warmed-up model rather than being provided. The output is a single clean model (e.g., ResNet-18 or MobileNet-v2) that achieves high clean accuracy while suppressing attack success rate, trained via a three-stage semi-supervised procedure (MixMatch) over dynamically updated clean and poison pools.

## Method

BSD initializes clean/poison pools through two complementary perspectives: (1) OSS reframes target-class poison detection as an open set recognition problem by warming up the main model on non-target classes only, then scoring target-class samples by minimum L2 distance to known-class feature cluster centers (poison samples behave as unknown-known classes with small distances; clean target samples form a new cluster with large distances); (2) ALS trains an independent altruistic model on the full poisoned set and identifies clean hard samples as those with high symmetric cross-entropy loss (poison samples overfit and exhibit low loss). The clean pool is the intersection of both splits. Subsequent training uses a loss-discrepancy indicator (altruistic loss minus main-model loss) to dynamically reassign samples, augmented by a class-completion strategy that rebalances underrepresented classes and a selective-dropping step that removes samples jointly predicted as the target label by both models, all within a MixMatch semi-supervised framework.

## Results

BSD achieves the best average Defense Effectiveness Rating (DER) across all three benchmark datasets: 99.3% on CIFAR-10, 99.8% on GTSRB, and 96.2% on ImageNet, surpassing the strongest baseline (ASD) by 0.7%, 0.2%, and 27.2% respectively. Against four additional threatening attacks (LC, SIG, ReFool, Narcissus) on CIFAR-10, BSD achieves 98.4% average DER versus 76.6% for ASD and 77.7% for ABL. BSD also achieves the lowest training cost (5.06 hours average) among all compared methods, even below ASD (6.48 hours) and far below DBD (25.09 hours).

## Baselines & Comparisons

BSD outperforms all seven baselines in average DER across the three main datasets. Against the closest competitor ASD (Gao et al., 2023), BSD leads by 0.7% on CIFAR-10 (99.3% vs 98.6%), 0.2% on GTSRB (99.8% vs 99.6%), and a decisive 27.2% on ImageNet (96.2% vs 69.0%), where ASD's reliance on clean seeds causes catastrophic failure (ASR=100% on BadNets). DBD (Huang et al., 2022) trails BSD by 1.0% on CIFAR-10 and 19.7% on ImageNet, while also suffering severe degradation on GTSRB Blended (DER 46.9%). ABL (Li et al., 2021a), the only other clean-seed-free method, is competitive on GTSRB (99.6% vs 99.8%) but falls behind on CIFAR-10 (96.8% vs 99.3%) and ImageNet (74.5% vs 96.2%). Post-training methods FP (Liu et al., 2018) and NAD (Li et al., 2021b) are substantially weaker, with average DERs of 69.2–81.2% and 80.8–91.0% respectively, and both require thousands of clean seed samples. Against clean-label attacks (LC, SIG, Narcissus) in Table 2, BSD achieves 98.4% average DER versus 76.6% for ASD and 77.7% for ABL, while DBD collapses to 57.5%. BSD also trains faster than ASD (5.06h vs 6.48h average) and far faster than DBD (25.09h).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the in-training data-splitting paradigm established by DBD (Huang et al., ICLR 2022) and ASD (Gao et al., CVPR 2023), which both rely on semi-supervised learning (MixMatch) to break trigger-label associations. The key departure is the clean-seed-free constraint: while DBD and ASD require external clean subsets for initialization, BSD replaces this with two complementary internal mechanisms — an open set recognition-based split (OSS, inspired by OpenMAX's distance-to-cluster scoring) and an altruistic model-based split (ALS) that exploits loss statistics from a deliberately backdoored auxiliary model. The intersection of these two independent perspectives (semantic and loss-based) is the core methodological contribution, replacing the single-perspective loss-guided splitting used by prior work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The clean-seed-free setting addresses a genuine practical gap: in real deployments (e.g., facial recognition with millions of individuals), acquiring additional clean data is prohibitively expensive and raises privacy concerns. BSD's bi-perspective design provides graceful degradation — if an adaptive attack evades the semantic (OSS) mechanism, the loss-based (ALS) mechanism still functions, and vice versa. However, the defense still assumes the defender controls the training process, the y_t approximation can fail in edge cases (e.g., confusingly similar target classes), and all2all attacks remain only partially addressed. Open questions include whether the bi-perspective intersection generalizes to non-visual modalities and whether the altruistic model's role can be further reduced to lower memory overhead.
