## Problem

Classic policy gradient methods work with arbitrary stochastic policies but do not exploit the gradient of the action-value function with respect to the action, while deterministic policy gradients (DPG) do exploit this gradient but are restricted to deterministic policies. Stochastic extensions of DPG such as SVG(0) and SAC rely on the reparameterization trick, which limits the class of usable policy distributions. This paper asks whether one can derive a policy update that uses action-value gradients while remaining valid for arbitrary stochastic policies without reparameterization.

## Input / Output

The method takes a continuous state vector s ∈ R^m from the environment and outputs an action a ∈ R^n sampled from a learned Gaussian policy π_θ(a|s) = N(μ_θ(s), Σ_θ(s)) with diagonal covariance. The algorithm is off-policy for states (drawing state sequences from a replay buffer) but on-policy for actions (resampling actions from the current policy at update time), making it effectively a hybrid. The action dimension can scale arbitrarily high; experiments demonstrate learning in combined tasks with up to 105 action dimensions.

## Method

WPO derives its update by treating policy optimization as a Wasserstein gradient flow over the space of probability densities, then projecting the resulting PDE onto a finite-dimensional parametric family (neural network) by minimizing local KL divergence. This yields the closed-form update θ_{t+1} = θ_t + F_θθ^{-1} E_π[∇_θ ∇_a log π(a|s) ∇_a Q^π(s,a)], which uses the action-value gradient (like DPG) but operates on samples from a stochastic policy (like classic policy gradient) without requiring reparameterization. In practice, the full Fisher information matrix is replaced by a diagonal approximation that rescales mean gradients by σ_i² and standard-deviation gradients by σ_i²/2, preventing numerical blowup as the policy becomes deterministic. A KL divergence penalty between the current and target policy (following MPO's formulation) is added for stability, and the critic is trained with standard n-step TD updates using a target network. The paper shows that for Gaussian policies the expected WPO update coincides with both DPG (with reparameterization) and the standard policy gradient, but the sampled updates have lower variance, and for non-Gaussian policies (e.g., mixtures) WPO exhibits qualitatively different and faster convergence.

## Results

On the DeepMind Control Suite (49 tasks), WPO robustly takes off and performs comparably to the best method (MPO) across nearly all tasks, while DDPG and SAC frequently fail to converge or reach lower asymptotic rewards. On the Humanoid CMU domain (21-dim actions), neither SAC nor DDPG takes off at all, whereas WPO makes progress on all tasks. On combined Humanoid Stand tasks scaling to 105 action dimensions (5 replicas), WPO takes off earlier than MPO, which takes off earlier than DDPG, with SAC being slowest. On the TCV tokamak fusion task (19-dim actions, 93 observations, 10k steps), WPO achieves a slightly higher reward than MPO and converges toward a deterministic policy, while MPO maintains approximately constant policy variance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against MPO (the strongest baseline), WPO is broadly competitive on the DeepMind Control Suite, with WPO sometimes learning faster (e.g., Humanoid Walk) and MPO sometimes converging faster (e.g., Dog). On the combined high-dimensional tasks, WPO consistently takes off earlier than MPO, and the gap widens as action dimensionality increases from 21 to 105. On the fusion task, WPO achieves a slightly higher final reward than MPO and adapts its policy variance more aggressively toward determinism. Against DDPG, WPO is clearly superior on high-dimensional and sparse-reward tasks where DDPG fails to take off (e.g., Humanoid CMU, Cartpole, Point Mass), though DDPG occasionally matches or slightly exceeds WPO on simpler tasks. Against SAC, WPO is more robust: SAC is particularly sensitive to its entropy weighting hyperparameter and struggles on multiple domains (Acrobot, Humanoid, and all combined tasks), while WPO maintains stable learning across the suite with a single set of hyperparameters.

## Relation to Prior Work

WPO is derived by projecting the Wasserstein gradient flow PDE (Ambrosio et al., 2008) onto a parametric policy space, yielding a closed-form update that uses the Fisher information matrix (Kakade, 2001) as a preconditioner. Conceptually, it unifies deterministic policy gradients (which use ∇_a Q but require deterministic policies) and classic stochastic policy gradients (which work with arbitrary distributions but don't exploit action-value gradients), without relying on the reparameterization trick that limits SVG(0) and SAC. Prior work on Wasserstein in RL (Abdullah et al., 2019; Moskovitz et al., 2020; Pacchiano et al., 2020) used the Wasserstein metric as a constraint, preconditioner, or regularizer rather than as the source of the learning dynamics, while Richemond & Maginnis (2018) and Zhang et al. (2018) formulated policy optimization as a Wasserstein flow but either stopped short of a parametric update or still required reparameterization. The KL regularization follows MPO (Abdolmaleki et al., 2018), and the variance-rescaling heuristic echoes an idea from the original REINFORCE paper (Williams, 1992).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

WPO provides a principled, reparameterization-free way to exploit action-value gradients with arbitrary stochastic policies, which is particularly relevant as action spaces grow beyond the few dozen dimensions typical of robotics benchmarks. The paper's demonstration that WPO's advantage over baselines widens with action dimensionality (on combined Humanoid tasks) suggests a path toward scalable continuous control, though this is validated only on synthetic multi-replica tasks rather than genuinely high-dimensional physical systems. Key limitations include the restriction to Gaussian policies in practice, sensitivity to network nonlinearity and squashing choices, and the use of KL rather than Wasserstein regularization (acknowledged as more mathematically consistent). Open questions include extending to non-Gaussian policies, distributional critics, and building real benchmarks with hundreds of action dimensions to test the scaling hypothesis.
