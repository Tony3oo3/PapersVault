## Problem

While neural scaling laws for training (model size, data, compute) are well-characterized, the scaling of inference performance with repeated attempts (pass@k) lacks a theoretical explanation. The paper asks whether the empirically observed pass@k curves for LLMs on reasoning tasks can be derived from a simple statistical model, and whether the resulting scaling behavior is universal across architectures.

## Input / Output

The analytical model takes as input a Beta(α,β) distribution over per-sample failure probabilities and a number of inference attempts k, and outputs a closed-form pass@k expression and an inference loss that decays as k^{-β}. For the empirical validation, the LLM experiments consume text prompts (MATH word problems, coding tasks) and produce binary correct/incorrect verdicts over k sampled completions, while the VAE experiment takes 28×28 Fashion-MNIST images and produces reconstructions whose per-sample error (normalized L2 distance) is thresholded to define success. The key output structure is a power-law scaling curve relating coverage or loss to either the number of trials k or the total inference FLOPS cost.

## Method

The paper proposes a memorization ansatz in which a model perfectly stores n_c samples but retrieves each with a sample-specific failure probability p_i drawn from Beta(α,β), where α controls the density of easy problems and β controls the density of hard ones. Averaging p_i^k over this distribution yields a closed-form pass@k involving Gamma functions (Eq. 7), whose large-k limit gives a power-law decay of the inference loss with exponent β. A second, complementary approach replaces the i.i.d. assumption with correlated trials by defining an effective number of independent trials k_eff = H_k(κ) (a generalized harmonic number), where κ governs the power-law decay of correlation-matrix eigenvalues, producing an alternative closed-form pass@k (Eq. 13). The framework is validated by fitting both forms to reported pass@k data for Gemma2B, Llama3-8B, and Pythia-2.8B on MATH, and by training a VAE on 400 Fashion-MNIST samples and confirming that its reconstruction-error correlation matrix exhibits the predicted power-law eigenvalue decay, with the effective-k formula matching the empirical coverage curves.

## Results

The paper derives that pass@k follows the analytical form A × (1 − Γ(β)k^(−β)/B(α,β)) at large k, where A = n_c/n is the model's memorization capacity fraction. Fitting to empirical LLM data (Gemma2B, Llama3-8B, Pythia-2.8B on the MATH dataset from Brown et al., 2024), the 'hard problem' parameter β is consistently around 0.35, while the 'easy problem' tail parameter α ranges from 2 to 20, indicating most problems are perceived as difficult. The corresponding inference loss L_inference(k) decays as a power law ∝ k^(−β), and the effective-k approach (with correlated trials, κ governing correlation decay) reproduces the same curves. VAE reconstruction experiments on Fashion-MNIST confirm the same power-law pass@k behavior and empirically verify that trial correlations follow a power-law eigenvalue decay, validating the effective-k model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical/analytical paper rather than a method-comparison study, so there are no traditional baselines to beat or lose to. The 'comparison' is between the paper's two analytical predictions (the Beta-distribution model in Eq. 7 and the effective-k correlated-trials model in Eq. 13) and empirical pass@k curves reported in Brown et al. (2024) for three LLMs on MATH and coding tasks. Both analytical forms capture the empirical curves well by adjusting α, β or p, κ, with the Beta model fitting the full range and the effective-k model matching the large-k asymptotics. The VAE reconstruction task on Fashion-MNIST serves as an independent controlled validation, where the effective-k prediction (Eq. 13) with empirically measured κ from the correlation matrix approximates the observed pass@k curves. The paper positions its contribution as a universal phenomenological framework rather than a competitive method, noting that many different modeling choices can yield the same functional form.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Hutter (2021) memorization model for learning curves by replacing the finite-training-set error mechanism with an inference-time retrieval failure, then characterizing the resulting pass@k scaling via a Beta distribution over per-sample failure probabilities. It departs from the dominant neural scaling laws literature (Kaplan et al., Henighan et al.), which focuses on training-time scaling of test loss with parameters/data/compute, by isolating the inference-time axis and showing that a simple probabilistic ansatz reproduces the empirical pass@k curves reported by Brown et al. (2024) and Snell et al. (2024). A second, complementary construction models trial-to-trial correlations through a power-law decay in the correlation matrix eigenvalues, yielding an effective-k formulation that also fits the data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a minimal, interpretable theoretical account of why inference scaling follows a particular functional form, and connects the inference loss directly to total compute cost, enabling practitioners to reason about the optimal number of sampling attempts for a given budget. The claimed universality—observed in both LLMs on MATH and a VAE on Fashion-MNIST—suggests the scaling behavior is a generic property of stochastic generative retrieval rather than an artifact of transformer architecture. Key open questions include how the analysis changes under imperfect verification, how inference scaling interacts with training-time scaling laws to determine the optimal train/inference compute split, and whether the Beta-distribution assumption holds for more diverse or open-ended tasks.
