## Problem

Prior adversarial ensemble methods characterize diversity solely through gradient cos values, ignoring other structural factors. This paper shows that precisely computing adversarial diversity is NP-hard and that, under first-order approximation, diversity decomposes into prediction diversity, gradient diversity, and a previously overlooked cross diversity term—meaning gradient cos values alone are insufficient to explain ensemble performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250813/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The ensemble takes standard images (28×28 grayscale for MNIST/F-MNIST, 32×32 RGB for CIFAR-10) and outputs a class label via averaging predictions across m ResNet-20 base learners (m=3 for MNIST/F-MNIST, m=8 for CIFAR-10). During training, each base learner receives its own PGD-generated adversarial example, and the orthogonality regularization operates on the (K−1)-dimensional probability vectors (ground-truth class removed, l1-normalized) of these adversarial predictions.

## Method

The paper proves NP-hardness of exact adversarial diversity computation via reduction from 3-SAT, then derives the first diversity decomposition under first-order Taylor approximation: adversarial ensemble loss equals average individual adversarial losses minus prediction diversity, gradient diversity, and cross diversity (for squared loss) or minus gradient diversity and KL-based cross diversity (for cross-entropy loss). Motivated by this decomposition, the AdvE_OAP method trains m ResNet-20 networks with a regularization Γ_α that maximizes both the log-determinant of AᵀA (where A stacks the l1-normalized, ground-truth-removed adversarial probability vectors) and the entropy of their average, thereby simultaneously increasing gradient and cross diversity. Training alternates PGD-attack generation of per-learner adversarial examples with SGD updates on the combined adversarial loss and orthogonality regularization, yielding base learners whose adversarial predictions are mutually orthogonal except on the true class.

## Results

On MNIST, AdvE_OAP achieves 95.68% accuracy under PGD20 and 94.07% under AutoAttack, outperforming the closest baseline AdvADP (95.06% and 92.95%) by 0.6–1.1 points. On CIFAR-10, the gap widens substantially: AdvE_OAP reaches 53.00% (PGD20) and 47.88% (AutoAttack) versus AdvADP's 46.80% and 42.17%, a margin of 6.2 and 5.7 points respectively. On Fashion-MNIST, AdvE_OAP scores 81.75% (PGD20) and 80.51% (AutoAttack), beating AdvADP by 2.7 and 2.8 points. The method also outperforms all baselines under EOT and BPDA attacks (Table 2), with the largest gains on CIFAR-10 (e.g., 53.38% vs. 53.40% for AdvADP under BPDA4, but 49.53% vs. 44.69% under BPDA1).

## Baselines & Comparisons

AdvE_OAP significantly outperforms all six baselines across all three datasets and all seven attacks in Table 1. The closest competitor is AdvADP (Pang et al., 2019), which also uses adversarial training with prediction orthogonality but only on clean examples; AdvE_OAP beats it by 0.6–1.1 points on MNIST, 2.7–2.8 points on Fashion-MNIST, and 5.7–6.2 points on CIFAR-10 under strong attacks (AutoAttack, MORA). TRS (Yang et al., 2021) and iGAT_AD P (Deng & Mu, 2024) are the next strongest baselines but trail by 7–15 points on CIFAR-10 under PGD20. Non-adversarial methods GAL (Kariyappa & Qureshi, 2019), ADP (Pang et al., 2019), and PDD (Huang et al., 2021) collapse to near-zero accuracy under most attacks, confirming that adversarial training is essential. DVERGE (Yang et al., 2020), which exchanges adversarial examples between base learners, also degrades severely under stronger attacks (e.g., 5.36% on CIFAR-10 under PGD10), while AdvE_OAP maintains 53.08%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the classical error-ambiguity decomposition of Krogh & Vedelsby (1994) and Zhou (2012) to the adversarial ensemble setting, where the adversarial perturbation couples with model structure in a fundamentally different way. It departs from prior adversarial ensemble methods (Kariyappa & Qureshi 2019, Dabouei et al. 2020, Huang et al. 2021, Bogun et al. 2022) that characterize diversity solely through gradient cos values, showing via decomposition that prediction diversity and a novel 'cross diversity' term are equally important. The orthogonal adversarial predictions idea generalizes Pang et al. (2019) from clean-example orthogonality to adversarial-example orthogonality, and the NP-hardness result formalizes why adversarial diversity cannot be analyzed as simply as traditional output-prediction diversity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The diversity decomposition provides the first principled theoretical framework for understanding what drives adversarial ensemble performance, moving the field beyond heuristic gradient-alignment objectives. The practical implication is that methods optimizing only cos values (GAL, TRS) leave performance on the table, and the orthogonal adversarial prediction regularization offers a concrete way to close that gap. Limitations include reliance on first-order approximation (second-order leads to intractable trust-region subproblems), experiments restricted to small-scale datasets (MNIST, F-MNIST, CIFAR10) with ResNet-20, and the O(m³) cost of the determinant-based regularization. Open questions include whether the decomposition and method scale to ImageNet-level datasets, deeper architectures, and whether higher-order approximations yield actionable closed-form solutions.
