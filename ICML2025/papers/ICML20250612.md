## Problem

Existing safe RL methods either enforce a user-specified safety budget via CMDP (failing to learn the truly minimal unsafe probability) or apply a fixed, state-agnostic threshold for action masking (as in SafeQ), which cannot differentiate actions with varying unsafe probabilities and thus fails to achieve maximal safety. This paper asks: can we learn a policy that first minimizes the probability of ever reaching an unsafe state and then maximizes reward among all such safest policies, even when safety cost signals are sparse?

## Input / Output

The method consumes environment state vectors whose format varies by task: time-to-collision features for TwoWay and Merge, a list of nearby-vehicle kinematics (positions, velocities) for Roundabout and Intersection, relative distance and speeds for ACC, and 2D position/velocity for Circle. It outputs a discrete action selected by maximizing Q(s,a) + f_pol(1−ψ(s,a)) over the full action set, where the polarization term softly suppresses unsafe actions. A parallel ψ-network outputs a per-state-action unsafe probability in [0,1] via a sigmoid head, which drives both the action mask and the prioritization of replay samples.

## Method

MAXSAFE formulates safe RL as a chance-constrained bi-level objective: minimize the probability of ever entering an absorbing unsafe state, then maximize discounted reward among all policies achieving that minimum. The key mechanism is a State-Action Reachability Estimation Function (SA-REF) ψ(s,a) learned by backward induction (ψ(s,a) = max{𝕀[s∈S_u], min_a' ψ(s',a')}), which converges to the true unsafe probability under the optimal policy (Theorem 4.3). From ψ, a state-dependent threshold ζ(s) = min_a ψ(s,a) + κ defines the optimal action mask C_ζ(s), and a safe Q-learning update restricts the max-operator to actions in C_ζ(s'), yielding convergence to the MAXSAFE-optimal policy (Theorem 4.4). For deep RL, hard masking is replaced by safety polarization: the Q-value is augmented with f_pol(1−ψ(s,a)) (e.g., 10·log(x)), which drives the effective Q-value toward −∞ for unsafe pairs while preserving a smooth gradient. To handle sparse crash signals, safety-prioritized experience replay assigns replay priorities proportional to |ψ-TD error| and corrects sampling bias with importance-sampling weights, accelerating ψ-network convergence in long-horizon tasks.

## Results

SPOM_PER achieves the best overall SWU (Safety-Weighted Utility) score of 0.98 across six safe RL benchmarks, with per-task scores of 0.98 (TwoWay), 0.94 (Merge), 1.22 (Roundabout), 0.68 (Intersection), 1.07 (ACC), and 0.96 (Circle). SPOM (without prioritized replay) achieves an overall SWU of 0.74, leading in TwoWay (0.88) and Intersection (0.96) but underperforming SPOM_PER in long-horizon tasks like ACC (0.87 vs. 1.07) and Circle (0.37 vs. 0.96). The next-best baselines, SafeQ and Recovery, both achieve only 0.60 overall SWU, while unconstrained DQN scores 0.43.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SPOM_PER outperforms all six baselines on overall SWU, with the largest margins over SafeQ (0.98 vs. 0.60) and DQN (0.98 vs. 0.43). Against SafeQ (state-agnostic ε-masking), SPOM_PER wins on every task, most dramatically on Circle (0.96 vs. 0.15) and ACC (1.07 vs. 0.50), confirming that a fixed threshold cannot achieve maximal safety. Against Recovery (action correction), SPOM_PER dominates on Circle (0.96 vs. 0.25) and TwoWay (0.98 vs. 0.34), while Recovery only edges ahead on ACC (0.85 vs. 1.07 is actually SPOM_PER winning; Recovery's strength is in Roundabout at 0.81 vs. 0.40 for SPOM). Against RCDQN (CMDP Lagrangian), SPOM_PER leads on all tasks, with the gap widest on Circle (0.96 vs. 0.14). The PPO-based CMDP methods RESPO and PPOLag (reported in Appendix Table 4) fail catastrophically with overall SWU scores of only 0.07 and 0.08 respectively, as the sparse crash cost signal prevents effective Lagrangian optimization. SPOM (without prioritized replay) is competitive with SPOM_PER on shorter-horizon highway tasks but lags significantly on long-horizon ACC and Circle, where the safety-prioritized experience replay mechanism provides its largest benefit.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the reachability estimation function (REF) from Ganai et al. (2023) from the state level to the state-action level (SA-REF), repurposing it to construct learned action masks rather than merely partitioning the state space into feasible/infeasible regions. It departs from the CMDP framework (which constrains expected cumulative cost below a user-specified budget) by formulating a chance-constrained bi-level objective that first minimizes the probability of ever entering an unsafe state, then maximizes reward among the safest policies. The action-masking mechanism generalizes SafeQ (Srinivasan et al., 2020) by replacing its fixed, state-agnostic threshold with a learned state-dependent threshold, and the safety-prioritized experience replay adapts PER (Schulman et al., 2017) from the Q-network to the safety network to address sparse cost signals.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The state-dependent threshold is a practically important correction: the paper provides a clean counterexample (Appendix A) showing that SafeQ's fixed threshold provably fails to achieve minimal unsafe probability even when set to the optimal value, and the state-dependent version resolves this. Safety polarization offers a soft, differentiable generalization of hard action masking that is more amenable to deep RL training, and the prioritized replay for the ψ-network is a targeted fix for the sparse-cost-signal problem that plagues CMDP-based methods (RESPO, PPOLag fail catastrophically on these tasks). Limitations include restriction to discrete action spaces, tabular convergence guarantees (deep RL convergence is heuristic), the absorbing-unsafe-state assumption, and the requirement that zero-unsafe-probability policies exist. Open questions include extension to continuous action spaces, non-absorbing unsafe states, and whether the bi-level structure generalizes to multi-agent or partially observable settings.
