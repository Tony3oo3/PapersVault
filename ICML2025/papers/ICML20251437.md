## Problem

Existing kernel-based independence and conditional independence tests fail to detect 'rare dependence'—dependence patterns that are significant only within a small region of the data distribution. Because the dependence signal is diluted across the full sample, conventional tests like HSIC cannot reject the null hypothesis even when a strong local dependence exists, and collecting more data does not help. This is particularly damaging in causal discovery, where undetected rare dependencies cause the PC algorithm to delete true edges and propagate errors throughout the graph.

## Input / Output

The method takes i.i.d. samples (x_i, y_i) along with an optional conditioning set Z and a reference variable C (which may be X, Y, or a third variable providing additional context). The dataset is randomly split 50/50 into a training portion (to learn the reweighting function) and a testing portion (on which the actual hypothesis test is performed). For independence/CI testing, the output is a p-value and test statistic; for the RD-PC causal discovery extension, the output is a directed acyclic graph recovered up to its Markov equivalence class.

## Method

The core mechanism is an adaptive importance reweighting function β(·) parameterized in an RKHS, learned by maximizing a reweighted kernel dependence measure (RHSIC or RKCIT) subject to non-negativity, unit-mean constraints, and two regularization terms (an RKHS smoothness penalty and a variance penalty to prevent trivial solutions that concentrate weight on a few points). The reweighted statistic replaces the standard centering matrix H with H_β = D_β(I − (1/n)1ᵀD_β), effectively amplifying the contribution of dependent sub-samples while preserving the factorization property under the null (so no spurious dependence is introduced). To avoid overfitting, β is learned on a training split and the test statistic is evaluated on a held-out testing split, with the null distribution approximated via permutation tests (standard permutation for UI, local nearest-neighbor permutation for CI). For causal discovery, the RD-PC algorithm integrates RKCIT into the PC skeleton-discovery phase and adds a correction rule (Rule 2) that shuffles the reference variable C to eliminate extraneous edges that arise when C is a common effect of X and Y.

## Results

On the Sachs protein-signaling dataset, RHSIC detects the (PKA, PJINK) dependence with p-value 0.004, while standard HSIC fails with p-value 0.601. On the FRED financial dataset (251 monthly samples), RHSIC achieves p-value 0.0005 versus HSIC's 0.2174, with learned weights highlighting the 2001 Dotcom recession and 2008 financial crisis. In synthetic CI tests (n=1000, |Z|=10, τ=0.03), RKCIT achieves approximately 50% higher test power than KCIT while maintaining Type I error at the nominal 0.05 level. Across all synthetic independence and CI experiments, the proposed method consistently controls Type I error near 0.05 and outperforms all baselines in power, with the advantage growing as the rare-dependence ratio τ decreases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

In independence tests (DG I and DG II), RHSIC consistently outperforms HSIC, RDC, FHSIC, FisherScan, and LFHSIC in test power while maintaining Type I error at 0.05; HSIC and LFHSIC show relatively unstable Type I error rates, and RDC performs well in DG I but degrades in DG II. In CI tests, RKCIT achieves roughly 50% higher power than KCIT (the closest baseline) and outperforms RCIT, CCIT, GCIT, FCIT, GCM, and NNLSCIT across all conditioning-set dimensions; KCIT's Type I error is slightly inflated due to its gamma-approximation of the null distribution. On real-world data, RHSIC detects dependence that HSIC completely misses (Sachs: p=0.004 vs 0.601; FRED: p=0.0005 vs 0.2174), demonstrating that kernel bandwidth optimization alone (LFHSIC) is insufficient for rare dependence. The advantage of the proposed method becomes most pronounced when the significantly dependent sub-population is small (τ→0), confirming that sample-level importance reweighting is necessary beyond existing adaptive-kernel or random-projection approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the kernel-based independence testing framework (HSIC, KCIT) by introducing an adaptive importance reweighting mechanism that amplifies dependence signals concentrated in small regions of the data distribution. It departs from local dependence measures (Abberger, 2002; Tjøstheim et al., 2022) and Context-Specific Independence (Pensar et al., 2016; Hwang et al., 2023), which either lack formal hypothesis testing procedures or approach the problem from the independence side with hard thresholds. The reweighting idea draws on importance sampling and the 'rare events' literature (Wang, 2020; Wang et al., 2021), but applies it to the testing problem rather than parameter estimation. The causal discovery component (RD-PC) extends the PC algorithm (Spirtes et al., 2000) by incorporating the reweighted conditional independence test and a correction rule for V-structure-induced false positives.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap where standard independence tests systematically fail on dependence patterns that are significant only in small sub-regions, with direct consequences for causal discovery where false negatives propagate through the PC algorithm. The theoretical contribution (asymptotic null/alternative distributions, uniform convergence bound) provides statistical guarantees that prior local-dependence heuristics lacked. Limitations include the requirement of a known reference variable C, the 50% data split for training the reweighting function, and the assumption of no latent confounders in the causal discovery setting. Open questions include whether the reweighting function can be learned end-to-end without the train/test split, whether the approach extends to high-dimensional or non-i.i.d. settings, and how to automatically select the reference variable C.
