## Problem

While the geometry of neuromanifolds (function spaces parameterized by neural networks) is known to govern sample complexity, expressivity, and training dynamics, no systematic framework has connected the rich invariants of algebraic geometry—dimension, degree, singularities, discriminants—to these machine learning questions. This position paper identifies that algebraic models (polynomial or ReLU networks) yield semialgebraic neuromanifolds amenable to algebraic-geometric analysis, and proposes 'neuroalgebraic geometry' as a unifying research program.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a parametric model specification (architecture class, activation function, parameter space) and produces a catalog of algebro-geometric invariants of the resulting neuromanifold: dimension and degree (via symbolic computation), singularity structure, generic fiber dimension, Euclidean distance degree, and data discriminants. The key constraint is that the model must be (piece-wise) polynomial in both parameters and input, ensuring the neuromanifold is a semialgebraic variety; for non-algebraic models, results extend via Weierstrass approximation or tropical geometry.

## Method

The paper establishes a dictionary mapping algebro-geometric invariants to ML concepts: dimension/degree bound covering numbers and hence sample complexity and expressivity; singularities correspond to subnetworks and induce implicit bias via enlarged Voronoi cells; fibers of the parameterization encode identifiability and invariance symmetries; and critical points of the loss are governed by the smooth locus geometry, Euclidean distance degree, and data discriminants from metric algebraic geometry. It unifies prior results on polynomial MLPs (Waring rank varieties), linear networks (determinantal varieties), CNNs (Segre-Veronese varieties), and linear attention (cubical layers) under this single framework. The paper further shows how polynomial approximation (Weierstrass) and tropical geometry (for ReLU/piecewise-linear networks) extend the algebraic toolkit to general architectures, and outlines open directions including skip connections, GNNs, and SSMs.

## Results

This position paper establishes a dictionary (Table 1) mapping fundamental machine learning concepts to algebro-geometric invariants of neuromanifolds: dimension and degree control sample complexity and expressivity via covering number bounds (log N_ε(M) = O(m log(d/ε) + C)); singularities correspond to subnetworks and implicit bias; fibers of the parameterization encode identifiability and invariance; and critical points/discriminants govern optimization dynamics. For the toy two-layer linear MLP, the paper explicitly computes the neuromanifold dimension as N₁(N₀+N₂−N₁), the degree via a product formula, and the Euclidean distance degree as C(min{N₀,N₂}, N₁), connecting these to the Eckart-Young-Schmidt theorem for low-rank matrix approximation. The sample complexity bound (Equation 14) shows n = Ω((D²/ε²) log(N_{ε/4C}(M))/δ) suffices for generalization, linking data requirements directly to the dimension and degree of the algebraic variety.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not present empirical baselines or quantitative comparisons against competing methods. Instead, it positions neuroalgebraic geometry relative to several existing mathematical frameworks for analyzing deep learning. Compared to information geometry (Amari, 2016), neuroalgebraic geometry offers richer invariants (degree, singularities) unavailable in the Riemannian setting and naturally handles singular neuromanifolds. Compared to the neural tangent kernel / infinite-width approach (Jacot et al., 2018), it studies finite-dimensional nonlinear algebraic varieties rather than infinite-dimensional linearized limits, making the two approaches complementary. Compared to tropical geometry (Maclagan & Sturmfels, 2021), which handles ReLU networks via piecewise-linear algebra, neuroalgebraic geometry covers polynomial and rational activations with full algebraic-geometric machinery. The paper argues that algebraic geometry uniquely captures aspects (degree, discriminants, Euclidean distance degree) that are undefined or intractable in purely differential-geometric or combinatorial formalisms.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper unifies and extends several scattered threads: singular learning theory (Watanabe, 2009) which connected algebraic geometry to statistical model selection, the geometry of polynomial and linear neural networks (Kileel et al., 2019; Trager et al., 2020; Kohn et al., 2022) which studied specific architectures' neuromanifolds, and metric algebraic geometry (Breiding et al., 2024) which provides tools for distance-based problems over algebraic varieties. The key conceptual departure from information geometry is privileging algebraic invariants (degree, singularities, discriminants) over Riemannian ones, arguing that the singular, non-smooth nature of neuromanifolds demands algebraic rather than differential-geometric tools. The paper positions neuroalgebraic geometry as the ML counterpart of algebraic statistics, explicitly distinguishing itself from kernel methods (NTK), geometric deep learning, and topological approaches by focusing on finite-dimensional nonlinear algebraic varieties rather than infinite-dimensional linearized limits or symmetry groups.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete dictionary (Table 1) mapping algebro-geometric invariants to ML quantities, giving theorists a shared vocabulary and a roadmap for connecting results across currently siloed subfields. Its main limitation is that it remains largely programmatic: most connections are outlined or cited from prior work rather than proven here, and the focus on algebraic (polynomial/ReLU) models, while justified by approximation arguments, leaves the most popular architectures (e.g., Transformers with softmax attention) only partially addressed. Open questions include extending the framework to skip connections, GNNs, and SSMs; understanding how data structure is reflected in neuromanifold geometry; and whether algebro-geometric invariants can prescriptively guide the design of new architectures with controlled theoretical properties.
