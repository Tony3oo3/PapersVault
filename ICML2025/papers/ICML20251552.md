## Problem

The traditional ELO rating system used in arena-based LLM evaluation suffers from two critical instabilities: (1) its iterative update mechanism makes scores highly sensitive to the order in which comparison records are processed, producing inconsistent rankings when the same data is shuffled, and (2) it treats all human annotators as equally reliable, ignoring individual variability in judgment quality. These issues undermine the credibility of model rankings used for high-stakes deployment decisions.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a static dataset of pairwise comparison records, where each entry specifies two competing models, the annotator ID, and the outcome (win/loss/tie encoded as 1/0/0.5). It jointly outputs model ELO scores R = (R_1, …, R_N) and annotator ability parameters Θ = (θ_1, …, θ_M), with the latter normalized to sum to 1. Annotators with θ_k < 0 are flagged as anomalous and can be filtered out, while the Stable Arena Framework (Algorithm 3) iteratively incorporates new annotations, re-estimates parameters, and prunes unreliable annotators below a threshold ε.

## Method

The framework has two components. First, m-ELO replaces the traditional per-sample iterative ELO update with a full Maximum Likelihood Estimation over the entire dataset, revealing that the original ELO update is equivalent to gradient descent on the log-likelihood with learning rate K/C but applied sample-by-sample (which rarely converges). By solving the MLE via gradient descent or Newton's method on the concave log-likelihood (Theorem 4.1 proves concavity with one score anchored), the resulting scores are order-independent and stable. Second, am-ELO modifies the ELO probability function P(R_i, R_j) = 1/(1+e^{−C(R_i−R_j)}) by replacing the fixed constant C with an annotator-specific discrimination parameter θ_k, inspired by the 2PL Item Response Theory model. This enables simultaneous estimation of model scores and annotator abilities via joint gradient descent (Equation 6), where Theorem 4.2 establishes that θ_k > 0 corresponds to annotators whose labels are consistent with the overall ranking, while θ_k < 0 identifies anomalous annotators who systematically invert or randomize their judgments. A normalization constraint (Σθ_k = 1) resolves the inherent scale ambiguity of the ELO scores and ensures the ranking direction is preserved.

## Results

On the Chatbot Arena dataset (42 annotators, 20 models, 4,321 response logs), am-ELO achieves the best prediction performance with MSE 0.1208±0.0034 and AUC 0.7581±0.0067, outperforming both traditional ELO (MSE 0.1238, AUC 0.7492) and m-ELO (MSE 0.1234, AUC 0.7503). In simulation experiments with perturbed annotators, am-ELO reduces the ELO score inconsistency rate to 30% compared to m-ELO or traditional ELO. For anomalous annotator detection, am-ELO achieves an F1-score of 90% at threshold ε=0 and up to 95% at ε=0.005 across four perturbation strategies.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three methods: traditional iterative ELO, m-ELO (MLE-based without annotator modeling), and am-ELO (MLE-based with annotator ability modeling). am-ELO outperforms traditional ELO on both prediction metrics (MSE reduced from 0.1238 to 0.1208; AUC improved from 0.7492 to 0.7581) and on stability, reducing the ELO score inconsistency rate to 30% under perturbation. m-ELO, which shares the same probability function as traditional ELO, achieves nearly identical loss at convergence, confirming the theoretical equivalence between the iterative and MLE formulations. However, am-ELO's improved probability function (incorporating annotator-specific θ_k) yields a significantly lower log-likelihood loss and better generalization, as evidenced by its superior AUC. In the case study, am-ELO and m-ELO correctly rank vicuna-7b above koala-13b based on head-to-head records against stronger opponents, while traditional ELO incorrectly ranks koala-13b higher due to disproportionate weighting of victories over weaker models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reformulates the classical ELO rating system (Elo, 1967) — itself grounded in the Bradley-Terry model (Hunter, 2004) — by replacing its order-sensitive iterative updates with a Maximum Likelihood Estimation approach (m-ELO), showing the two are structurally equivalent (ELO is gradient descent on the MLE objective). It then extends the probability function by borrowing the discrimination parameter concept from Item Response Theory (Embretson & Reise, 2013), replacing the fixed constant C with an annotator-specific ability parameter θ_k (am-ELO). The key departure from prior arena-evaluation work is the explicit joint estimation of model scores and annotator reliability within a single MLE framework, rather than treating all annotators as interchangeable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Arena-based evaluation underpins high-stakes LLM deployment decisions (e.g., Chatbot Arena), so the demonstrated instability of traditional ELO — where shuffling data changes rankings — is a practical credibility problem this paper addresses with theoretical guarantees (concavity/uniqueness of the MLE optimum). The ability to flag anomalous annotators (90–95% F1 in simulation) offers a concrete quality-control mechanism for crowdsourced evaluation platforms. However, the annotator model captures only a single discrimination dimension, the experiments rely on one dataset (Chatbot Arena, 42 annotators, 20 models), and the normalization assumption (majority of annotators are reliable) may not hold in adversarial or highly heterogeneous settings. Open questions include multi-dimensional annotator modeling, applicability to LLM-as-judge scenarios, and validation on larger, more diverse arenas.
