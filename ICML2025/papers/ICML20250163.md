## Problem

The aleatoric-epistemic view of uncertainty, despite its widespread use in machine learning, is insufficiently expressive to capture the many distinct quantities researchers actually need: predictive uncertainty, its reducibility with more data, predictive performance, and data dispersion. This conceptual overloading has led to incoherent discussions where different mathematical quantities are conflated under the same labels, tenuous assumptions are made about uncertainty decomposition on unseen data, and methods are designed and evaluated on shaky foundations.

## Input / Output

The framework takes as input a decision of interest (action space A and loss function ℓ(a,z)), training data y_{1:n}, a generic machine-learning method mapping data to a predictive distribution p_n(z), and a data-acquisition policy π. It produces a loss-grounded predictive uncertainty measure h[p_n(z)] (which reduces to variance or entropy depending on the loss), an expected uncertainty reduction EUR for finite m that explicitly accounts for the data-generating process, a rigorous irreducible/reducible decomposition, and a data-acquisition score. Unlike the standard aleatoric-epistemic decomposition, the irreducible/reducible split is well-defined for any updating method (not just exact Bayesian inference) and depends explicitly on the data-acquisition policy.

## Method

The paper constructs a decision-theoretic framework starting from a final decision with an associated loss function, showing that predictive uncertainty is the subjective expected loss of acting Bayes-optimally under p_n(z), which generalizes variance (quadratic loss) and Shannon entropy (negative-log-likelihood loss). By explicitly modeling the data-generating process and taking the limit of infinite new data, they derive a well-defined expected uncertainty reduction and an associated irreducible/reducible decomposition that holds for any data-to-prediction mapping. They then clarify that model-based uncertainty, predictive performance (expected score), and data dispersion are distinct quantities connected through proper scoring rules and discrepancy functions, generalizing classic bias-variance and KL-divergence decompositions. Finally, they reinterpret BALD (EIG_θ) as a Bayes estimator of both the infinite-step predictive information gain and a true one-step parameter information gain, demonstrating empirically that the latter approximation is tighter and better explains BALD's practical utility as a short-horizon data-acquisition objective.

## Results

The paper's key empirical finding (Figure 4) is that BALD (EIG_θ) is a highly inaccurate estimator of infinite-step predictive information gain IG_z(y_{1:∞}^+) for small n, with estimation error ε_z remaining large even in simple conjugate models where exact inference is possible. In contrast, BALD is a much more accurate estimator of the true one-step expected information gain in model parameters EIG_θ^true (error ε_θ is substantially smaller). In active learning experiments (Figure 5), BALD outperforms predictive entropy as a data-acquisition objective despite being a worse estimator of long-run predictive information gain, supporting the interpretation that BALD's utility stems from tracking short-run parameter uncertainty rather than long-run predictive uncertainty.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper without traditional method-vs-baseline comparisons. The key empirical contrast is between BALD and predictive entropy as data-acquisition objectives in active learning (Figure 5): BALD consistently achieves better predictive performance than simply maximizing predictive entropy, even in setups where predictive entropy is actually a more accurate estimator of the long-run information gain. The paper also contrasts BALD's accuracy as an estimator of two different target quantities (Figure 4): the infinite-step predictive information gain (where BALD performs poorly for small n) versus the true one-step parameter information gain (where BALD performs well). These findings reconcile BALD's practical utility in active learning with its theoretical inaccuracy as a measure of long-run reducible uncertainty, suggesting that short-horizon parameter information gain is the more relevant target for data acquisition.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant aleatoric-epistemic framework (Gal 2016; Gal et al. 2017; Kendall & Gal 2017) by arguing it is conceptually incoherent, and instead grounds uncertainty in decision theory following Dawid (1998) and DeGroot (1962). The proposed expected uncertainty reduction (EUR) generalizes the decision-theoretic entropies of Neiswanger et al. (2022) and the BALD/EIG objective of Houlsby et al. (2011) and Gal et al. (2017), unifying them under a single loss-grounded formalism. The paper also connects to the martingale posterior literature (Fong et al. 2023) and classic statistical decompositions (bias-variance, cross-entropy/KL/entropy), showing how these fit naturally within the decision-theoretic view.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies that the field's most widely used uncertainty decomposition conflates at least five distinct mathematical quantities under two labels, which has led to methods being designed and evaluated on shaky foundations. The reinterpretation of BALD as a short-run parameter-uncertainty estimator rather than a long-run predictive-uncertainty measure has direct practical implications for active learning practitioners. Open questions include whether the decision-theoretic framework scales to complex, misspecified deep models where the data-generating process is unknown, and how to best handle the approximation errors inherent in estimating EUR with finite data. The paper's empirical validation is limited to simple conjugate models, so broader applicability of the BALD reinterpretation remains to be established.
