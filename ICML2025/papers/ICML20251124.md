## Problem

Prior work established that small nonzero ℓ₂ weight decay combined with large-scale initialization induces grokking, and that the ℓ₂ norm of parameters serves as a progress measure for generalization. This paper asks whether grokking is fundamentally tied to ℓ₂ regularization or whether other inductive biases (sparsity, low-rankness, domain-specific structure) can equally drive delayed generalization, and whether the ℓ₂ norm remains a reliable proxy when the model is regularized toward a different property.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a training set of input-output pairs, a learning rate α, a regularization strength β, and a choice of regularizer h (ℓ₁, ℓ₂, nuclear norm ℓ_*, or Sobolev penalty) applied to a model (MLP, shallow or deep linear network). The output is the full training trajectory exhibiting a two-phase dynamic: a memorization phase where the loss g drops to ε = Ω(β^C), followed by a generalization phase where the regularizer h converges to its minimum over the loss-minimizing set within Θ(1/αβ) additional steps. The paper validates this on synthetic problems (sparse vector recovery, low-rank matrix completion, modular addition) and real datasets (MNIST), with the key quantitative output being the grokking delay scaling as 1/(αβ).

## Method

The core result (Theorem 2.1) proves that under the Chatterjee-Lojasiewicz (CL) inequality at initialization, subgradient descent on f = g + βh decomposes into two phases: the gradient G(x) dominates early, driving g to near-zero while iterates stay in a ball around initialization, then the subgradient H(x) dominates, driving h toward its minimum over the loss-minimizing set within Θ(1/αβ) steps. The paper specializes this to ℓ₁ regularization for sparse recovery (Theorem 3.1) and nuclear norm for matrix factorization (Theorem 3.4), showing that ℓ₂ alone produces only 'grokking without understanding'—an abrupt norm decrease that does not yield true generalization when N < n. For deep linear networks (depth L ≥ 2), the implicit bias of gradient descent substitutes for explicit ℓ₁/ℓ_* regularization, enabling grokking or ungrokking purely through architecture. Finally, the paper shows that data selection via local coherence (leverage scores) can exponentially reduce the number of samples needed and shorten the grokking delay, with no other hyperparameter changed.

## Results

The paper's central theoretical result (Theorem 2.1) establishes that grokking time scales as Θ(1/αβ) for an arbitrary regularizer h, where α is the learning rate and β is the regularization strength. Empirically, on modular addition (p=97, 40% training split), all three regularizers (ℓ₁, ℓ₂, ℓ*) exhibit the same two-phase dynamics: smaller αβ values produce longer memorization-to-generalization delays. In sparse recovery (n=100, s=5, N=30, α=10⁻¹, β=10⁻⁵), training error drops to 10⁻⁶ before recovery error ‖a⁽ᵗ⁾−a*‖₂ decreases, with generalization step t₂ ∝ ‖â−a*‖∞/(αβ). In matrix completion (n₁=n₂=10, r=2, N=70, α=10⁻¹, β=10⁻⁴), a multiscale singular value decay phenomenon drives generalization over Θ(1/αβ) steps. Notably, the ℓ₂ norm of parameters *increases* during generalization when ℓ₁ or ℓ* regularization is used, directly contradicting the prior belief that ℓ₂ norm decrease is a reliable grokking indicator.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark a single proposed method against baselines; instead, it compares the grokking dynamics induced by different regularizers and architectures. ℓ₁, ℓ₂, and ℓ* regularization all produce the same Θ(1/αβ) grokking delay on modular addition and MNIST, confirming that the phenomenon is not specific to weight decay. However, in sparse recovery and low-rank matrix factorization (N < n), ℓ₂ regularization alone produces a sharp drop in generalization error that does *not* correspond to true generalization—termed 'grokking without understanding'—because the least-squares solution cannot recover the sparse/low-rank structure. In contrast, ℓ₁ (for sparsity) or ℓ* (for low-rankness) regularization is necessary for genuine generalization in the shallow case. Adding depth (L ≥ 2) to a linear network provides an implicit sparsity bias, enabling grokking with only ℓ₂ regularization and reducing the generalization delay compared to the shallow case. Finally, coherence-based data selection (selecting entries with highest leverage scores) reduces the number of samples needed for generalization exponentially as the selection ratio τ → 1, amplifying grokking without changing any other hyperparameter.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends Lyu et al. (2023), which proved that ℓ₂ weight decay with large-scale initialization induces grokking in homogeneous models, by showing that the same two-phase memorization-to-generalization dynamic arises under any regularizer enforcing a generalization-aligned inductive bias (ℓ₁ for sparsity, nuclear norm for low-rankness, Sobolev penalties for smoothness). The theoretical backbone builds on Chatterjee's (2022) Chatterjee-Lojasiewicz inequality, requiring it only at initialization rather than globally, which is a weaker assumption than the Polyak-Lojasiewicz conditions used in prior convergence analyses. The sparse recovery and matrix factorization sections draw on compressed sensing theory (Foucart & Rauhut, 2013; Candes & Recht, 2012) to connect grokking to classical recovery guarantees via the robust null space property and robust rank null space property.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper corrects a widely-used heuristic in the grokking literature: the ℓ₂ norm of parameters is not a reliable indicator of generalization when the model is regularized toward a different property (sparsity, low-rankness), as it can grow monotonically while the model still generalizes. The 'grokking without understanding' finding—that ℓ₂ regularization alone can produce an apparent grokking transition that does not correspond to true generalization in underdetermined settings—challenges prior theoretical predictions and has practical implications for practitioners using weight decay on sparse or low-rank models (LoRA, pruning, mechanistic interpretability). Open questions include why grokking is naturally observed in modular arithmetic without explicit regularization, whether the CL-based framework extends to non-convex loss landscapes beyond the neighborhood of initialization, and how data selection strategies interact with implicit biases in deep (nonlinear) networks.
