## Problem

Prior work showed that fine-tuning on entirely benign datasets can degrade LLM safety alignment, and He et al. (2024) proposed selecting samples most similar to harmful anchors and dissimilar from safety anchors. However, that approach depends on external curated anchor datasets and relies on an impractical setup of fine-tuning on only 100 samples. This paper asks whether one can identify the specific benign samples that most severely break safety alignment without any external anchors, while keeping the attack practical and stealthy.

## Input / Output

The method takes a benign instruction-response dataset (e.g., Dolly with ~14.6K samples or Alpaca with ~50K samples, preprocessed to remove explicit harmful content) and an aligned LLM whose gradients are computed for each sample. It outputs a top-k subset (typically k=100) of benign samples ranked by the Self-Inf-N score. The selected samples are semantically harmless and pass all standard toxicity detection APIs (Perspective, OpenAI Moderation, LlamaGuard, etc.), yet fine-tuning on them produces a safety-compromised LLM that generates detailed harmful content across HEx-PHI categories.

## Method

The core contribution is Self-Inf-N, an anchor-free outlier detection score that identifies benign samples most likely to push an aligned LLM into harmful parameter regions during fine-tuning. The method computes the vanilla Self-Inf score (the squared norm of each sample's gradient, following Pruthi et al. 2020) and discovers it suffers from a severe length bias that selects predominantly short-token samples, which degrade safety but produce only terse outputs. To fix this, Self-Inf-N normalizes by adding log(len(a)+1) to log(Self-Inf(z)+1), balancing the influence magnitude with answer token length so that selected samples are both high-influence outliers and long enough to generate detailed harmful responses. The approach requires no external harmful or safety anchor datasets, and the selected samples transfer effectively across architectures (Llama-2, Llama-3, Qwen-2, Gemma-2, Mistral) and from smaller to larger models via LoRA, remaining effective in practical scenarios like continuous learning and low-ratio data poisoning.

## Results

On the HEx-PHI benchmark (330 harmful prompts, GPT-4 judge scoring 1–5), fine-tuning Llama-2-7B-Chat on 100 Self-Inf-N-selected samples from Dolly achieves a Harmfulness Score of 3.71±0.10, and from Alpaca achieves 3.47±0.26 — both approaching the Pure Bad upper bound of 3.55±0.13. The corresponding Utility Scores on MT-Bench are 3.48±0.27 (Dolly) and 3.85±0.16 (Alpaca), substantially higher than the vanilla Self-Inf scores (1.58/1.60) and close to full-benign fine-tuning levels. The attack transfers across architectures (Qwen-2, Gemma-2, Mistral, Llama-3) and from 7B to 70B models via LoRA, with harmfulness persisting through subsequent fine-tuning on 2,000 additional samples.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is Bidirectional Anchor (BA) from He et al. (2024), which selects samples most similar to a curated harmful anchor set and most dissimilar to a curated safety anchor set in gradient space. On Dolly, Self-Inf-N (HS 3.71) clearly outperforms BA (HS 3.40) by 0.31 points, while on Alpaca the two are statistically comparable (3.47 vs. 3.52, within overlapping standard deviations). Against the Random Selection baseline, Self-Inf-N achieves roughly 3× higher harmfulness (3.71 vs. 1.21 on Dolly; 3.47 vs. 1.13 on Alpaca), confirming that the outlier-detection signal is meaningful rather than a dataset artifact. A critical practical advantage is that Self-Inf-N is entirely anchor-free — it requires no external curated harmful or safety datasets — whereas BA's effectiveness depends heavily on the quality and choice of those anchors, limiting its generalizability. In the continuous-learning scenario (Appendix E.6), Self-Inf-N's induced harmfulness also decays more slowly than BA's across different second-stage learning rates (e.g., 3.39 vs. 2.13 at lr=5E-6), indicating greater persistence of the safety degradation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on Pruthi et al.'s (2020) self-influence (Self-Inf) scores for gradient-based outlier detection, repurposing them to identify benign samples that disproportionately shift LLM parameters toward harmful behavior during fine-tuning. It extends the line of work on benign fine-tuning compromising safety (Qi et al., 2023; He et al., 2024) by eliminating the dependency on external harmful/safety anchor datasets that the Bidirectional Anchor method requires, and by diagnosing and correcting a length bias in vanilla Self-Inf that limits practical harmfulness. The key conceptual departure is framing the selection of safety-breaking benign samples as an outlier detection problem within the safety distribution, rather than a similarity-to-anchors problem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that 100 carefully selected benign samples achieve harmfulness comparable to explicit harmful fine-tuning (HS ≈ 3.5–3.7 vs. 3.55 for pure harmful) while passing all standard toxicity detection APIs reveals a critical blind spot in current fine-tuning service security. The persistence of harmfulness through continuous learning and low-ratio data poisoning scenarios, combined with the failure of most existing mitigation strategies (Lisa, data augmentation, moderation APIs), underscores that the field lacks robust defenses for the fine-tuning stage specifically. Open questions include whether the attack generalizes to domain-specific datasets, whether the shallow-alignment mechanism (Qi et al., 2024) fully explains the effect, and what principled fine-tuning-stage safeguards could detect gradient-level outlier influence without requiring external anchors.
