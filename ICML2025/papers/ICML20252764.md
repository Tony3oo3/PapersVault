## Problem

Standard homotopy methods for global optimization rely on a costly double-loop mechanism (iteratively decreasing the smoothing parameter while solving an inner optimization), while existing single-loop alternatives like SLGH only guarantee convergence to a local optimum. This paper seeks a single-loop method that provably converges to a neighborhood of the global optimum with better iteration complexity than the standard zeroth-order homotopy approach.

## Input / Output

The method requires only function evaluations of a continuous (possibly non-concave, non-differentiable) objective f on a compact domain S⊂ℝ^d, plus two hyperparameters: a power N>0 controlling the weight amplification and a smoothing scale σ>0. At each iteration, K samples are drawn from N(μ_t, σ²I_d) to construct a zeroth-order gradient estimate, so no gradient or Hessian of f is ever needed. The output is a sequence {μ_t}_{t=1}^T from which the best candidate μ* is selected (e.g., by maximizing f(μ) over the trajectory), approximating the unique global maximizer x*.

## Method

GS-PowerOpt applies a two-stage transformation before optimization: first, a power transform f_N(x) = f^N(x) (PGS) or e^{Nf(x)} (EPGS) is applied to f, which amplifies the gap between f(x*) and all other function values as N grows; second, the transformed objective is Gaussian-smoothed to yield F_{N,σ}(μ) = E_{x~N(μ,σ²I_d)}[f_N(x)]. The key theoretical result (Theorem 2.1) shows that for any δ>0, there exists a threshold N_{δ,σ,M} such that whenever N exceeds it, every stationary point of F_{N,σ} lies within a δ-neighborhood of x*, reducing the problem to finding a stationary point of a smooth surrogate. Optimization proceeds via a single-loop stochastic gradient ascent with a sample-based zeroth-order gradient estimator (the average of (x_k − μ_t)f_N(x_k) over K Gaussian samples), requiring no differentiability of f. The iteration complexity is O(d⁴ε⁻²) in general and improves to O(d²ε⁻²) under Lipschitz conditions on f and ∇f, with the dependence on N removable under a bounded-objective assumption.

## Results

On the Ackley benchmark, EPGS (N=1) achieves a fitness of 22.683, second only to CMA-ES (22.718) and significantly outperforming STD-Homotopy (17.58). On the Rosenbrock benchmark, EPGS (N=3) reaches f(μ*)=−0.017, nearly matching CMA-ES (0.0) while all other smoothing-based methods fall far behind (worst: ZOSLGHd at −137.016). On the MNIST targeted adversarial attack, EPGS achieves a 100% success rate with an average R² of 87% (top-2) and the second-fastest convergence at 397 iterations. On CIFAR-10, EPGS attains 98% success rate and 98% R², matching the best smoothing-based method (ZOSLGHd) while producing a much smaller perturbation norm (3.05 vs. 10.06 for CMA-ES).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CMA-ES, EPGS is slightly behind on Ackley (22.683 vs. 22.718), Rosenbrock (−0.017 vs. 0.0), and MNIST R² (87% vs. 89%), but decisively wins on CIFAR-10 where CMA-ES's R² drops to 75% and perturbation norm balloons to 10.06, while EPGS maintains 98% R² with a norm of 3.05. Against the single-loop Gaussian homotopy variants (ZOSLGHd, ZOSLGHr), EPGS dominates on Rosenbrock (−0.017 vs. −137.016 and −88.477) and MNIST R² (87% vs. 74% and 65%), though ZOSLGHd edges out EPGS on CIFAR-10 R² (99% vs. 98%). Against ZOAdaMM and ZOSGD, EPGS wins on Rosenbrock by large margins and on MNIST R² (87% vs. 29% and 85%), while ZOAdaMM converges faster in iteration count (45 vs. 397) but produces much larger perturbations. Against STD-Homotopy, EPGS wins on every task: Ackley (22.683 vs. 17.58), Rosenbrock (−0.017 vs. −2.401), MNIST (100% vs. 97% SR, 87% vs. −4% R²), and CIFAR-10 (98% vs. 52% SR). Overall, EPGS ranks first or second among all smoothing-based methods across all four experimental settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Gaussian smoothing / homotopy framework for global optimization (Nesterov & Spokoiny, 2017; Mobahi & Fisher, 2015; Hazan et al., 2016) by inserting a power or exponential-power transformation of the objective before smoothing, and by adopting a single-loop stochastic gradient ascent scheme inspired by Iwakiri et al. (2022). The key conceptual departure from prior smoothing-based methods is that the power transform explicitly amplifies the gap between the global maximum value and all other function values, which the authors prove (Theorem 2.1) forces all stationary points of the smoothed surrogate into an arbitrarily small neighborhood of the true global optimum. The paper carefully distinguishes itself from related exponential-surrogate approaches (Dvijotham et al., 2014; Roulet et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2024), noting that those either require N in [0,1], impose positive-semidefinite constraints incompatible with pure Gaussian smoothing, or provide only stationary-point guarantees rather than global-optimality guarantees.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The main practical impact is a zeroth-order global optimization method with a provable convergence rate to a δ-neighborhood of the global optimum at O(d²ε⁻²) iterations under Lipschitz conditions—substantially faster than the O(d²/ε⁴) rate of standard zeroth-order homotopy—and without requiring the 'σ-nice' assumption. The convergence analysis also applies to non-differentiable objectives, broadening the method's scope. Open questions include: (1) the theory requires a unique global maximum, though experiments suggest the method works with multiple optima; (2) the iteration complexity still depends on N (unless Assumption 4.1 holds), creating a tension between accuracy (small δ needs large N) and efficiency; (3) whether the power transform is the optimal weight-shifting mechanism, which the authors explicitly leave for future work. The adversarial-attack experiments on MNIST and CIFAR-10 demonstrate practical relevance but show EPGS is competitive rather than dominant compared to CMA-ES.
