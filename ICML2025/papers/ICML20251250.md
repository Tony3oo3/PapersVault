## Problem

Early-exit models attach internal classifiers to intermediate layers, but existing methods adopt either joint training (backbone and exits trained simultaneously) or disjoint training (backbone trained first, then exits separately) without adequate justification for the choice. The paper demonstrates that both conventional regimes yield suboptimal performance: disjoint training impairs low-budget accuracy by freezing the backbone, while joint training biases the network and degrades high-budget performance due to conflicting gradient signals from multiple classifiers.

## Input / Output

The framework is evaluated on RGB images (CIFAR-100 at 32×32, Tiny-ImageNet at 64×64, ImageNet-1k at 224×224) and text sequences (20-Newsgroups at 512 tokens, STS-B at 128 tokens), producing class labels or regression scores. Performance is reported at four computational budgets (25%, 50%, 75%, 100% of backbone FLOPs), where each budget corresponds to a maximum exit depth: samples are routed to the earliest internal classifier whose confidence exceeds a threshold, so lower budgets force earlier exits and higher budgets allow deeper processing.

## Method

The paper proposes a 'mixed' training regime that first trains the backbone to convergence (Phase 1), then jointly trains the entire multi-exit network including all internal classifiers (Phase 2), ensuring the backbone is well-optimized before being adapted to accommodate the exits. To explain why this works, the authors introduce a four-metric analysis framework: gradient dominance (cosine similarity between individual IC gradients and the total gradient), mode connectivity (linear interpolation in weight space after permutation alignment), numerical rank of intermediate activations, and mutual information between input and internal representations. These metrics reveal that the mixed regime produces gradient dynamics dominated by the final IC (mimicking standard backbone training), places solutions in the same loss basin as disjoint training but with lower loss, increases activation rank across layers, and yields a more uniform information flow profile. Extensive experiments across ResNet, ViT, MSDNet, and BERT backbones, multiple datasets, and several early-exit methods (SDN, GPF, PBEE, entropy-based) confirm consistent improvements at medium and high computational budgets, and show that the mixed regime obviates the need for gradient or loss scaling techniques.

## Results

The proposed Mixed training regime (backbone pre-training followed by joint training of the full multi-exit network) consistently outperforms both Joint and Disjoint regimes across architectures and datasets. On CIFAR-100 with ViT-T, Mixed achieves 70.25% top-1 accuracy at 100% computational budget vs. 66.49% (Joint) and 63.99% (Disjoint). On ImageNet-1k with ViT-T, Mixed reaches 71.20% at 100% budget vs. 68.39% (Joint). The Disjoint regime suffers the largest gap at low budgets (e.g., 28.33% vs. 41.65% for ViT-T on CIFAR-100 at 25% budget), while Joint shows a notable performance drop at higher budgets relative to Mixed.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three training regimes—Joint (train all parameters simultaneously), Disjoint (train backbone then freeze it and train ICs separately), and the proposed Mixed (train backbone to convergence, then train the full multi-exit network jointly)—as the primary baselines. The Mixed regime dominates at medium and high computational budgets: on ViT-T CIFAR-100 it gains +3.76% over Joint and +6.26% over Disjoint at 100% budget, and on ResNet-50 TinyImageNet it gains +2.23% over Joint at 100%. The Disjoint regime is consistently weakest at low budgets (e.g., 28.33% vs. 41.65% for ViT-T CIFAR-100 at 25%), because freezing the backbone prevents learning effective early-layer representations. The Joint regime, while competitive at low budgets, shows a clear performance gap at higher budgets (e.g., 68.39% vs. 71.20% for ViT-T ImageNet-1k at 100%), which the authors attribute to gradient dominance by intermediate classifiers biasing the backbone away from optimal representations. Results generalize to NLP (BERT on 20-Newsgroups and STS-B), alternative early-exit methods (GPF, PBEE, entropy-based), and pre-trained fine-tuning setups, with Mixed consistently matching or exceeding the other regimes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of the early-exit/multi-exit training literature (BranchyNet, SDN, GPF, PBEE) and the broader analysis of neural network training dynamics. While prior work has established joint training as the default and disjoint training as an alternative, no prior study systematically compared these regimes or proposed a principled analysis framework for understanding their differences. The paper repurposes existing diagnostic tools—mode connectivity (Garipov et al.), numerical rank (Masarczyk et al.), and mutual information (Shwartz-Ziv & Tishby)—to characterize how training strategy shapes the internal representations and loss landscape of multi-exit models, and introduces gradient dominance as a new metric specific to this setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway—that a simple two-phase 'mixed' regime (pre-train backbone, then jointly train all parameters) consistently outperforms both the dominant joint approach and the disjoint approach across architectures, datasets, and modalities—gives practitioners a clear, low-cost recipe for improving multi-exit models. The finding that gradient/loss scaling techniques become largely unnecessary under mixed training simplifies the training pipeline. Open questions include whether the mixed-gradual variant (which further outperforms mixed) scales to very deep or very wide models, whether the analysis framework generalizes to non-classification objectives beyond the single regression task tested, and how these findings interact with modern techniques like LoRA-based early exits or speculative decoding.
