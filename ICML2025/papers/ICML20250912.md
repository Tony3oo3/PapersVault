## Problem

Existing LLM alignment methods (RLHF, DPO, KTO) all assume a specific parametric preference model—Bradley-Terry for DPO/RLHF, prospect theory for KTO—meaning that even with infinite data, the learned policy may not converge to the true human-preferred distribution if the real preference structure deviates from the assumed model. This statistical inconsistency is a fundamental reliability gap: more data does not guarantee better alignment.

## Input / Output

DDRO consumes unpaired preference data—individual (prompt, response) pairs each labeled as preferred or unpreferred—rather than the paired comparisons required by DPO. The data is assumed to originate from a reference LLM whose output distribution is a convex combination of the true preferred and unpreferred distributions with a fixed mixing proportion t ∈ (0,1). The output is a fine-tuned LLM policy p_θ(y|x) that, in the population limit, exactly recovers the true preferred response distribution p⁺(y|x), along with an implicit estimator of the unpreferred distribution p⁻(y|x).

## Method

DDRO reframes preference optimization as a direct density ratio estimation problem: it estimates g*(y|x) = p⁻(y|x)/p⁺(y|x) by minimizing a Bregman divergence between the model-implied ratio g_θ and the true ratio g*, where g_θ is defined from the reference model and the current policy via g_θ = (1/(1−t))(p_ref/p_θ) − t/(1−t). The Bregman divergence is parameterized by a strictly convex function f; the paper's implementation uses f(x) = x log x − (1+x) log(1+x), which reduces the loss to a logistic (cross-entropy) form. A KL-divergence regularization term γ·KL(p_θ‖p_ref) is added to prevent catastrophic forgetting, and a log-sigmoid smoothing function S is applied to each per-sample loss term to suppress gradient spikes during training. The key theoretical guarantee is that the Bregman divergence loss upper-bounds the L² distance between p_θ and p⁺, so minimizing it drives the policy to the true preferred distribution as data grows—without any assumption on the underlying preference model.

## Results

On the UF-G unpaired dataset, DDRO matches or exceeds KTO and BCO on 4 out of 5 benchmarks across Pythia 1.4B/2.8B/6.9B and LLaMA 7B. For example, on Pythia 6.9B, DDRO achieves 0.2375 TruthfulQA (vs. KTO 0.1946, BCO 0.2032) and 0.0296 GSM8K (vs. KTO 0.0212, BCO 0.0212), while KTO retains an edge on AlpacaEval (0.7214 vs. DDRO 0.6405). On the UF-B paired dataset converted to unpaired form, DDRO matches or slightly outperforms DPO on all benchmarks, e.g., on LLaMA 7B DDRO scores 0.3350 BBH vs. DPO 0.3281 and 0.5973 AlpacaEval vs. DPO 0.5564.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against KTO (the strongest unpaired baseline), DDRO wins on GSM8K, MMLU, and TruthfulQA across all model sizes (e.g., Pythia 6.9B: 0.2375 vs. 0.1946 on TruthfulQA, a ~4.3-point gain), but KTO retains a clear advantage on AlpacaEval LC Winrate (0.7214 vs. 0.6405 on Pythia 6.9B) and a slight edge on BBH. Against BCO, DDRO is consistently competitive or superior on most benchmarks, with the largest gaps on TruthfulQA (up to ~3.4 points on Pythia 6.9B). In the paired-to-unpaired comparison against DPO, DDRO matches or slightly exceeds DPO on all five benchmarks despite discarding explicit pairwise preference links; on LLaMA 7B, DDRO leads on BBH (0.3350 vs. 0.3281) and AlpacaEval (0.5973 vs. 0.5564) while trailing slightly on MMLU (0.3220 vs. 0.3332) and GSM8K (0.1001 vs. 0.1008). Overall, DDRO is the only method that simultaneously matches paired DPO performance while operating on unpaired data, at the cost of a consistent AlpacaEval gap versus KTO.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Bregman divergence framework for direct density ratio estimation (Sugiyama et al., 2012b) from the statistical learning literature into the LLM alignment setting, reframing preference optimization as a density ratio matching problem between preferred and unpreferred output distributions. It departs from DPO, KTO, and BCO by eliminating the need for a parametric preference model (Bradley-Terry or prospect theory), instead directly estimating the ratio p⁻/p⁺ from unpaired data. The paper positions itself as a theoretical fix for a gap it identifies in all existing methods: the lack of statistical consistency when the assumed preference model is misspecified, formalized in Proposition 2.1.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The statistical consistency guarantee (Theorem 4.1) addresses a legitimate concern that more data may not improve alignment if the BT/prospect-theory assumption is violated, which is plausible given the complexity of real human preferences. However, the method still relies on Assumption 3.2 (reference distribution as a fixed convex combination with constant t) and a finite response set, and the practical smoothing function S(x) is introduced ad hoc to fix training instability without a consistency proof. Open questions include how the choice of convex function f affects performance at scale, whether the constant-t assumption holds in practice, and whether the theoretical advantage translates to meaningful gains on larger models or more diverse preference data.
