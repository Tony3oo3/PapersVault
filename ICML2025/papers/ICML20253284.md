## Problem

Current AI safety research treats robustness as a static, one-shot property validated on fixed benchmarks before deployment, overlooking that environments evolve, world models contain blind spots for rare catastrophic events, and unchallenged systems maladapt over time. No existing framework formally captures the need for AI safety to be a time-evolving property where systems improve from stressors rather than merely resisting them, leaving the community without a rigorous criterion to distinguish genuinely adaptive safety practices from brittle patch-and-pray cycles.

## Input / Output

The framework operates on a two-timescale process: the fast loop generates a sequence of policies π_{t,i} (i=1,…,N) through internal optimization within a fixed perceived MDP M†(t), while the slow loop tracks the community's model updates across T discrete steps. The primary output is a dynamic regret R(T) that aggregates average within-model suboptimality across all fast-loop iterations at each slow-loop step, enabling a binary classification—antifragile if R(T) decreases over time, fragile if it increases. The robustness gap Δ(t) additionally quantifies the value difference between the perceived-optimal policy and the true-optimal policy in the real environment, providing a per-step diagnostic of how far the community's model has drifted from reality.

## Method

The paper introduces a formal MDP framework where the agent's perceived environment M† is related to the true MDP M through probability distortion w(·) and reward distortion u(·) functions, capturing systematic underestimation of rare catastrophic states. They prove that in any environment with |S|≥3 and T≥2, black swan events (large robustness gaps) are inevitable due to fundamental distortions in human and community perception of transitions and rewards. The key technical contribution is a two-timescale dynamic regret framework: the fast loop measures within-model suboptimality of policy iterations against a comparator MDP, while the slow loop captures how the community's model updates in response to newly discovered threats, with a volatility bound V(T) governing how rapidly the comparator can shift. Antifragility is formally defined as decreasing dynamic regret over time, achieved by systematically re-weighting the distortion functions w and u toward identity so that the perceived MDP converges to the true MDP, thereby shrinking the robustness gap rather than merely patching individual vulnerabilities after each discovery.

## Results

This position paper presents theoretical results rather than empirical benchmarks. The key contributions are: (1) Theorem 3.3 proves that in any MDP with |S| ≥ 3 and T ≥ 2, one can construct distorted transition/reward functions P†, R† such that the perceived-optimal policy is catastrophically suboptimal in the true environment, establishing that black swan events occur with non-zero probability; (2) Corollary 3.4 provides a lower bound on the robustness gap Δ(t), showing it is strictly positive whenever the perceived policy's environmental sensitivity c† exceeds δ† + ε*; and (3) Definition 4.1 formally defines antifragility as decreasing dynamic regret R(T; {M̃_t}) over time, contrasting with fragility (increasing regret). No quantitative experimental results are reported.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not conduct empirical comparisons against baselines. Instead, it positions the antifragile framework conceptually against several existing paradigms: (1) static robustness (e.g., adversarial training, certified bounds) is framed as necessary but insufficient, providing a safety net but not improving over time; (2) resilience (bounce-back) is distinguished from antifragility (bounce-forward), with the latter requiring the system to expand its safe operating regime; (3) online learning with nonstationary rewards is acknowledged as related but noted to treat disruptions as negatives to mitigate rather than opportunities to strengthen; (4) meta-learning and continual learning are seen as partial mechanisms that adapt smoothly but do not actively seek out extreme stressors to expand safety boundaries. The paper argues these approaches complement rather than replace antifragility, with the dynamic regret framework (building on Jin, 2024) providing the unifying formalism.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper repurposes Taleb's (2010, 2012) concept of antifragility—originally from finance and biology—and formalizes it for AI safety via a dynamic regret framework built on Jin (2024) and the MDP-based black swan formalism of Lee et al. (2025). It deliberately departs from the dominant 'robustness as a one-shot certification' paradigm (adversarial training, certified bounds, static benchmarks) by arguing that time-invariant testing inadvertently promotes brittleness. The paper positions antifragility as complementary to, not a replacement for, existing robustness and resilience approaches, drawing connections to online learning, meta-learning, continual learning, and iterative red-teaming practices (ANLI, Dynabench) while distinguishing its scope as a system-level, community-wide adaptation process rather than a single-model training technique.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first formal dynamic-regret-based definition of antifragility for AI safety and proves (via Theorems 3.2–3.3 and Corollary 3.4) that catastrophic robustness gaps are mathematically inevitable in any multi-step MDP with imperfect community models, regardless of testing intensity. This reframing shifts the AI safety conversation from 'pass the benchmark' to 'does the gap shrink over time,' offering a measurable criterion for long-term reliability. Key open questions remain: how to implement antifragile adaptation loops at scale under resource constraints, how to handle the ethical tensions of intentional stress-testing in deployed systems, and whether the dynamic regret framework can be made computationally tractable for real-world LLM or cyber-physical deployments where feedback is sparse and adversarial.
