## Problem

Trained neural networks consistently exhibit heavy-tailed (power-law) spectral densities in their activation, NTK, Hessian, and weight matrices, yet existing explanations—iid heavy-tailed elements, the Kesten mechanism, and population covariance—each fail to match empirical observations (e.g., elements are not heavy-tailed, and different architectures on the same data show different exponents). The paper asks: what unified random-matrix mechanism produces heavy-tailed spectra in strongly correlated trained feature matrices without requiring heavy-tailed matrix entries?

## Input / Output

The framework consumes a trained feature matrix (activation, NTK, or Hessian) together with the label covariance matrix Σ and a structure parameter κ that encodes eigenvector entropy (ranging from κ=0 for fully diagonal matrices to κ=mn for arbitrary symmetric matrices). It produces a limiting spectral density given by the free multiplicative convolution of the label covariance spectrum with the reciprocal HTMP distribution, yielding an explicit power-law exponent at infinity (κ/(2γ)−1+κ/2) and an inverse-Gamma law at zero, from which neural scaling law exponents and optimizer gradient-norm tail exponents are derived.

## Method

The paper introduces a Master Model Ansatz: trained feature matrices follow an inverse-Wishart-type density q(M) ∝ (det M)^{−α} exp(−β tr(ΣM^{−1})) π(M), derived via an entropic-regularization (stochastic minimization with KL divergence) framework that separates coefficient learning (temperature τ) from feature learning (temperature η). To extract a spectral density, the authors model the initialization density π through a high-temperature beta-ensemble with joint eigenvalue density proportional to ∏e^{−V(λ_i)} ∏|λ_i−λ_j|^{κ/N}, where κ/N is the 'high-temperature' scaling. The key insight is that κ counts the number of eigenvalue repulsions and thus measures eigenvector entropy: more rigid matrix structures (diagonal, block-diagonal, Kronecker) yield smaller κ and heavier tails. The resulting HTMP distribution generalizes Marchenko-Pastur, interpolating between MP (κ→∞) and maximally heavy-tailed spectra (κ→0⁺), and the full spectral density is obtained as the free multiplicative convolution with the label covariance spectrum. This single framework simultaneously explains neural scaling laws, lower power laws in optimizer gradient norms, and the five-plus-one phases of training observed in weight matrices.

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 4.3) establishes that the limiting spectral density of trained feature matrices exhibits a power law at infinity with exponent ρ(x) ~ c₊ x^{-(κ/(2γ) - 1 + κ/2)} and an inverse Gamma law at zero with ρ(x) ~ c₋ x^{-(κ/(2γ) - 1 - κ/2)} exp(-β₋/x), where κ controls the degree of matrix structure (κ→∞ recovers Marchenko-Pastur; κ→0 gives the heaviest tails). The derived neural scaling law (Proposition 5.1) yields generalization error L ≍ n^{-ℓ(2 + κγ/2 - κ/2)}, linking the tail exponent directly to the structure parameter. Empirically, NTK spectra of VGG11, ResNet9, and ResNet18 trained on CIFAR-10 (1000 samples) exhibit clear inverse Gamma behavior near zero both at initialization and post-training, and the 5+1 phases of MiniAlexNet weight matrices are well-fitted by HTMP with κ = ∞, 5.5, 1.9, and 10⁻³ across decreasing batch sizes (1000 to 5).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against competing methods in the standard empirical sense; instead, it positions HTMP against three previously proposed mechanisms for heavy-tailed spectra (Table 1). The iid heavy-tailed elements mechanism and the Kesten phenomenon both predict heavy-tailed matrix elements, which contradicts empirical observations that trained weight matrix elements are not heavy-tailed while their spectra are. The population covariance mechanism (PIPO principle) predicts that all architectures trained on the same data should exhibit identical tail exponents, which is also empirically false. HTMP is the only mechanism in Table 1 that simultaneously produces power-law spectra, an inverse Gamma law near zero, and does so without requiring heavy-tailed elements. For the 5+1 phases of training (Martin & Mahoney, 2021b), the standard Marchenko-Pastur law (κ=∞) captures only the Random-Like phase, while HTMP with finite κ (5.5, 1.9, 10⁻³) progressively matches the Bulk-Decay and Heavy-Tailed phases, providing a unified single-parameter interpolation across all five phases.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the heavy-tailed mechanistic universality (HT-MU) program initiated by Martin & Mahoney (2020, 2021b), which identified power-law spectra in trained weight matrices but left the generative mechanism open. It builds on the variational stochastic-minimization framework of Mandt et al. (2016) to derive an inverse-Wishart-type 'Master Model Ansatz' for trained feature matrices, and then leverages the high-temperature beta-Laguerre ensemble results of Dung & Duy (2021) to introduce the HTMP distribution as a new RMT tool. The key departure from prior RMT-for-ML work (Pennington & Worah, 2017; Liao et al., 2020; Mei & Montanari, 2022) is that heavy-tailed spectra arise from reduced eigenvector entropy (matrix structure) rather than from heavy-tailed matrix elements or data covariance alone, with the structure parameter κ interpolating between Marchenko-Pastur and fully heavy-tailed limits.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The HTMP ensemble provides the first RMT model that simultaneously captures the inverse-Gamma law near zero and the power-law tail at infinity observed in NTK and weight-matrix spectra, unifying neural scaling laws, lower-tail optimizer behavior, and the 5+1 phases of training under a single parameter (κ). A key open question is the precise quantitative link between κ (and the Master Model parameters α, β) and generalization performance; the authors explicitly note this connection remains to be established. Empirical validation is limited to CIFAR-10 with small CNNs (VGG11, ResNet9/18, MiniAlexNet) and 1000 data points, so whether the framework scales to large language models or more complex architectures remains untested. The free-probability separation of data-covariance effects from model-structure effects (Proposition 4.2) is a useful diagnostic tool, but the PIPO prediction it implies has not been tested across diverse architectures on identical datasets.
