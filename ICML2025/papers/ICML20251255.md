## Problem

While sparse autoencoders and dictionary learning can extract interpretable features from neural network layers, it remains unclear how many relative similarity comparisons (triplet feedbacks) an agent must provide to recover these features, and how this complexity scales with sparsity and rank. This paper establishes tight feedback complexity bounds for learning a feature matrix (PSD covariance) from triplet comparisons, covering both constructive (agent-designed) and distributional (sampled) activation settings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251255/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes relative triplet comparisons (α, β, ζ) with a sign label ℓ ∈ {+1, 0, −1} indicating whether ‖D(α−β)‖ ≤ ‖D(α−ζ)‖, where activations are either general vectors in ℝ^p or s-sparse (at most s nonzero entries). In the constructive setting the agent freely designs activations; in the distributional setting it selects from i.i.d. samples drawn from a Lebesgue or sparse distribution. The output is a symmetric PSD feature matrix Φ ∈ Sym_+(ℝ^{p×p}) equivalent to the target Φ* up to positive scaling (feature equivalence), which corresponds to recovering the dictionary D up to its normal transformation DD^T.

## Method

The core reduction (Lemma 2) converts triplet inequality constraints into pairwise equality constraints y^TΦy = z^TΦz, reformulating learning as finding a PSD matrix orthogonal to a set of rank-1 difference matrices {yy^T − zz^T} in the Frobenius inner product. For constructive feedback, the agent exploits the eigendecomposition Φ* = Σλᵢvᵢvᵢ^T to separately teach the kernel (via Lemma 3's linearly independent rank-1 construction, requiring r(r+1)/2 − 1 pairs) and the null space (via p − r pairs of (vᵢ, 0)), yielding a tight bound of Θ(r(r+1)/2 + p − r − 1) for general activations and O(p(p+1)/2) for 2-sparse activations using the standard-basis-plus-pairs construction. For sampled feedback, Sard's theorem guarantees almost-sure linear independence of rank-1 matrices from Lebesgue-sampled points, giving a tight Θ(p(p+1)/2) bound; for sparse sampling, the bound becomes O(p²(2/p_s² · log(2/δ))^{1/p²}) via Hoeffding's inequality on the probability p_s of drawing a useful sparse pattern. Experiments validate these bounds on Recursive Feature Machine kernels and SAE dictionaries from Pythia-70M and Board Game models (ChessGPT, OthelloGPT) at dimensions up to 32768×512.

## Results

The paper establishes tight feedback complexity bounds for learning feature matrices via triplet comparisons: Θ(r(r+1)/2 + p−r+1) for standard constructive feedback (where r is the rank of the feature matrix), O(p(p+1)/2) for sparse constructive feedback, and Θ(p(p+1)/2) for standard sampled feedback. Empirically, on a 4096×512 SAE dictionary from ChessGPT, sparse constructive feedback achieves a Pearson Correlation Coefficient (PCC) of 0.9773 with ~8.4M queries, while eigendecomposition reaches PCC 0.9427 with only 135K feedbacks. On Pythia-70M (32768×512), eigendecomposition achieves 93% PCC, and sparse sampling improves from PCC 0.024 (200K) to 0.77 (20M) feedbacks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper positions its feedback complexity bounds against four families of prior feature-retrieval methods: SAE-based approaches (Sharkey et al., 2025), CRAFT (Fel et al., 2023), probing/CCS methods (Marks & Tegmark, 2024; Burns et al., 2023), and LDA. The key advantage is that the constructive setting with general activations achieves a tight bound of Θ(r(r+1)/2 + p−r+1), which is substantially smaller than the Tnpd or npk learning complexities of prior SAE/CRAFT methods when the feature matrix is low-rank (r ≪ p). For sparse constructive feedback, the O(p(p+1)/2) bound matches the degrees of freedom of a symmetric matrix, improving upon the Tnp scaling of probing methods. In the sampling regime, the paper's Θ(p(p+1)/2) bound for general activations is tight and matches the information-theoretic minimum, while the sparse sampling bound O(p²(2/p_s² · log(2/δ))^{1/p²}) provides the first characterization of how sparsity degrades recovery. Empirically, sparse constructive feedback (PCC 0.9773) outperforms eigendecomposition (PCC 0.9427) on the ChessGPT dictionary, while eigendecomposition is far more sample-efficient (135K vs. 8.4M feedbacks), illustrating the trade-off between feedback quality and quantity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends dictionary learning and metric learning to a fundamentally weaker information channel: the learner receives only relative triplet comparisons (sign of distance differences) rather than direct samples or noisy measurements. It builds on the constructive sparse activation basis from Kumar & Dasgupta (2024) and the triplet-comparison framework of Schultz & Joachims (2003), but introduces an active/constructive element where an agent selectively provides informative feedback, aligning with the machine teaching paradigm. The recovery guarantee is up to normal transformation (DD^T), which generalizes the permutation-and-sign-change recovery of prior noiseless dictionary learning (Gribonval & Schnass, 2010) and connects to covariance estimation and Mahalanobis distance learning. The practical motivation draws directly from sparse autoencoder-based interpretability work on LLMs (Bricken et al., 2023; Marks et al., 2024b).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that recovering a feature dictionary from relative comparisons requires at least quadratic feedback in the ambient dimension (p(p+1)/2 for full-rank), but this can be sharply reduced under low-rank structure to r(r+1)/2 + (p-r), providing a concrete expressiveness-versus-recoverability trade-off relevant to model distillation and interpretability. A key open question is full recovery of the dictionary (beyond normal transformation), which remains unresolved except under strong incoherence assumptions. The gap between theoretical bounds and practical sample requirements in high-dimensional SAE settings (e.g., 32k×512 dictionaries for Pythia-70M) suggests that exploiting additional structure or dimensionality reduction is essential for scalable feature transfer.
