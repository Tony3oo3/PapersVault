## Problem

Major AI conferences now receive over 10,000 submissions per venue, yet the traditional one-way peer review system provides no mechanism for authors to evaluate review quality and no systematic reward structure for reviewers, creating a power imbalance that degrades review quality. The rise of LLM-generated reviews further compounds this problem, as authors lack reliable tools to identify and challenge superficial automated evaluations.

## Input / Output

The proposed system takes as input standard paper submissions, reviewer reviews (split into a positive/neutral section and a critical section), and an LLM-generated reference review produced by the system. Between the two review-release stages, authors produce structured feedback scores on reviewer comprehension and question constructiveness, plus optional LLM flags. The system outputs aggregated author feedback scores per reviewer, digital badge eligibility determinations (top 10%/30%), a reviewer impact score tracking downstream citation impact of reviewed papers, and enriched meta-review context combining per-paper feedback with cross-submission reviewer averages.

## Method

The core mechanism is a two-stage sequential review release that minimally modifies the existing double-blind workflow: Stage 1 discloses only the paper summary, strengths, and clarifying questions, after which authors score reviewer comprehension and constructiveness and may flag suspected LLM-generated reviews by comparing against a system-provided LLM reference review; Stage 2 then reveals weaknesses and ratings, after which author feedback is locked. The LLM reference review serves a dual purpose as a psychological deterrent against lazy LLM-only reviewing and as a soft baseline for author flagging. In the meta-review stage, area chairs receive both the per-paper author feedback and the reviewer's cross-submission average, enabling quantitative accountability without exposing individual author scores. The reward layer adds standardized digital badges (top 10% and 30%) displayable on OpenReview and Google Scholar profiles, a reviewer impact score analogous to the h-index that credits reviewers for the downstream citation impact of papers they reviewed, and optional in-kind compensation, all designed to convert peer review from invisible voluntary service into a visible, career-relevant academic credential.

## Results

The paper reports that major AI conferences exceeded 10,000 submissions per venue by 2025, with ICLR alone showing a 59.8% year-over-year increase. A temporal analysis of ICLR peer reviews (2017–2024) using Gemini-1.5-flash for typo detection shows the error rate dropped from 7.79 to 5.54 per 1,000 words (a 28.8% reduction), and the proportion of completely error-free reviews rose from 31.4% (2023) to 37.8% (2024), which the authors interpret as soft evidence of increasing LLM-assisted review writing. Additionally, NeurIPS consistency experiments found that 16–23% of papers could have been accepted or rejected depending on the assigned reviewer group, highlighting high randomness in current review quality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not propose a new method to benchmark against baselines. Instead, it performs comparative analyses across dimensions: (1) a cross-venue survey (Table 2, Appendix C) shows that only 10 of 14 major AI conferences offer any Reviewer Hall of Fame program, and only 4 provide in-kind compensation, illustrating the current gap in reviewer incentives; (2) a temporal comparison of ICLR review typo rates (2017 vs. 2024) and keyword turnover (~17% of top-20 keywords change annually) quantifies the pace of change that outstrips reviewer expertise; and (3) the authors contrast their proposed two-stage review with the existing simultaneous-release protocol, arguing the sequential design mitigates the author-outcome bias (documented by Goldberg et al., 2025) where authors rate reviews more favorably when the recommendation is acceptance. No head-to-head method comparison is conducted.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes several distinct threads—NeurIPS's review consistency experiments (Cortes & Lawrence, 2021; Beygelzimer et al., 2023), the 2022 peer-review-of-peer-reviews study (Goldberg et al., 2025), digital badge systems from education (Gibson et al., 2015; Facey-Shaw et al., 2017), and market/auction mechanisms for academic review (Frijters & Torgler, 2019; Ugarov, 2023)—into a unified, minimal-intervention framework. While prior work has studied author rating of reviews in isolation or proposed monetary compensation for reviewers, no prior position paper combines a two-stage sequential review release (to prevent retaliatory scoring) with a concrete, system-backed reward architecture (digital badges + reviewer impact scores) as a single accountability loop. The paper also repurposes the LLM-as-reviewer debate not as an end goal but as a design constraint: LLM-generated reference reviews serve as a psychological deterrent and a soft baseline for authors to flag suspicious human reviews.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a structural sustainability crisis—over 10,000 submissions per venue with no accountability mechanism for reviewers—that threatens the credibility of AI conferences as the field's primary dissemination channel. Its open questions are significant: whether the two-stage release truly neutralizes the author-outcome bias identified by Goldberg et al. (2025), whether digital badges will be perceived as meaningful credentials or as trivial gamification, and how the system should evolve if LLMs eventually produce reviews of comparable or superior technical depth to human reviewers. The practical barrier of requiring multi-venue coordination and OpenReview platform changes means the proposals remain aspirational without institutional buy-in, and the paper itself acknowledges that pilot implementation in smaller tracks is a prerequisite.
