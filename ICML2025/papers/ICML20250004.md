## Problem

Prior learning-augmented approximation work (Cohen-Addad et al., 2024) introduced ε-correlated vertex predictions only for MaxCut, leaving open whether predictions can break approximation barriers for NP-hard graph problems with edge constraints such as Vertex Cover, Set Cover, and Maximum Independent Set. The paper asks whether an edge-based prediction model—where each edge provides a bit for each of its two endpoints—is strictly more powerful than vertex predictions, and whether a unified framework can yield improved approximation ratios across multiple problems simultaneously.

## Input / Output

The algorithm receives an undirected graph G=(V,E) together with ε-correlated edge predictions: for every edge e=(u,v), two independent bits b_u(e) and b_v(e) are provided, each equal to the true membership of the corresponding endpoint in a fixed optimal solution with probability 1/2+ε. The output is a valid solution (vertex cover, independent set, set cover, or cut partition) whose expected approximation ratio improves over the classical worst-case bound as a function of ε, with the improvement growing faster than any polynomial in ε for Vertex Cover (e.g., 2−Ω(log log 1/ε / log 1/ε)).

## Method

The core framework separates vertices into high-degree (deg ≥ Δ, where Δ = Θ(log(1/ε)/ε²)) and low-degree groups. For high-degree vertices, majority voting over all incident edge prediction bits yields an accurate classification via Hoeffding concentration (error ≤ exp(−2·deg(v)·ε²)), and the algorithm either includes the vertex or its entire neighborhood in the solution. For the remaining low-degree subgraph, classical bounded-degree approximation algorithms (Halperin for VC, Halldórsson for MIS, Lovász for Set Cover) are applied. A technical charging argument bounds the expected cost of false-positive and false-negative vertices by relating them to the optimal solution size, and a boundary-handling step (a 2-approximation pass) resolves conflicts between the two regimes. For MaxCut, the method instead uses a (Δ,η)-wide/narrow graph decomposition combined with a convex optimization and rounding procedure adapted from Ghoshal et al. (2025), achieving α_GW + Õ(ε²) versus the prior α_GW + Õ(ε⁴).

## Results

The paper's headline theoretical results (Table 1) show that ε-correlated edge predictions break classical approximation barriers: Vertex Cover improves from 2 to 2 − Ω(log log 1/ε / log 1/ε), Set Cover from O(log n) to O(log 1/ε), Maximum Independent Set from Ω(log n / (n log log n)) to Ω(ε² / log log 1/ε), and MaxCut from α_GW ≈ 0.8786 to α_GW + Õ(ε²). Empirically, on the facebook, congress, and twitch social-network graphs, Algorithm 2 (learned MIS) outperforms both the degree-agnostic prediction-only heuristic and the standard Halldórsson greedy approximation across all tested ε values (0.10–0.35), approaching approximation ratio 1 at the upper end of the ε range.

## Baselines & Comparisons

In the empirical evaluation (Section 6, Figure 2), Algorithm 2 is compared against three baselines on facebook, congress, and twitch graphs: the optimal ILP solution, the standard Halldórsson greedy MIS approximation, and a degree-agnostic 'prediction-only' heuristic. The learned algorithm consistently outperforms the prediction-only baseline across all ε values, validating the theoretical insight that predictions are highly accurate for high-degree vertices but noisy for low-degree ones. Compared to the Halldórsson greedy baseline, the learning-augmented algorithm eventually surpasses it as ε increases, achieving the best approximation ratio at moderate-to-high prediction quality (ε ≈ 0.25–0.35). Theoretically, the edge-prediction model also dominates prior vertex-prediction approaches: for MaxCut it improves the advantage over α_GW from Õ(ε⁴) (Cohen-Addad et al.) to Õ(ε²); for MIS it achieves a constant factor for fixed ε versus Õ(√Δ/ε) (Braverman et al.); and for Vertex Cover it breaks the factor-of-2 barrier without requiring bounded false-positive/negative assumptions (Antoniadis et al.).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the ε-prediction framework of Cohen-Addad et al. (NeurIPS 2024) from vertex-level predictions to edge-level predictions, where each edge provides two independent bits (one per endpoint) that are ε-correlated with the true optimal solution. It also builds on classical bounded-degree approximation algorithms (Halperin 2002 for VC, Halldórsson 1998 for MIS, Lovász 1975 for Set Cover) as the 'low-degree solver' component, and adapts the MaxCut algorithm from Ghoshal et al. (SODA 2025) to the edge-prediction setting. The key conceptual departure from concurrent work (Antoniadis et al. 2024 for VC, Braverman et al. 2024 for MIS) is that edge predictions require no assumptions on the number of false positives/negatives, whereas vertex-prediction results need such bounds to go beyond classical approximation barriers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that even very weak predictions (each bit only 1/2+ε accurate) suffice to break known hardness-of-approximation barriers: the 2-approximation for Vertex Cover (tight under UGC), the O(log n) barrier for Set Cover, and the n^{1-δ} hardness for MIS. The unifying high-degree/low-degree separation framework suggests a general template for incorporating noisy predictions into approximation algorithms. Open questions include whether the edge prediction model is the natural one (vs. other information structures), whether the specific quantitative gaps (e.g., ε² vs ε⁴ for MaxCut) are tight, and how to obtain ε-correlated predictions in practice without access to an optimal solution. The empirical validation is limited to three small social-network graphs, leaving scalability and real-world prediction quality as open practical concerns.
