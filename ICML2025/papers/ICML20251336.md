## Problem

Standard LLM pretraining practice filters toxic data from the corpus to reduce harmful generation, but this reduces data diversity and impairs the model's ability to recognize and handle toxicity. No prior work had systematically studied whether *including* toxic data during pretraining could improve the model's alignability — i.e., how effectively its toxicity can be removed in post-training — treating pre- and post-training as a unified co-design problem rather than independent stages.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251336/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline takes a pretraining corpus with a controlled ratio of clean (C4) and toxic (4chan) text, trains an Olmo-1B transformer, and at inference time accepts a text prompt and produces a generated completion. The key unusual aspect is that the 'input' to the system deliberately includes toxic data (up to 25% of the corpus), and the output is evaluated on two axes: generational toxicity (Perspective API scores on Toxigen and Real Toxicity Prompts) and general capability preservation (cross-entropy loss on Open Web Text, MMLU). The detoxification is applied at inference time via activation steering or prompting rather than through a separate finetuning stage.

## Method

The paper first establishes a theoretical link between data frequency and feature entanglement using the superposition hypothesis: features underrepresented in training data occupy more entangled directions in the hidden space, making them harder to detect and edit. It then trains a spectrum of Olmo-1B models on C4 mixed with 0–25% 4chan data (in 5% increments, ~20–26B tokens total) and uses linear probing on attention head activations to show that toxic data produces a less entangled, more separable linear representation of toxicity (higher probe accuracy, fatter right tail in the accuracy distribution). At inference time, inference-time intervention (ITI) steers the top 30 attention heads along the learned toxicity direction at three strengths (weak/mid/strong), and the model trained with 10% toxic data achieves the lowest generational toxicity (2.63 on Toxigen, 7.11 on Real Toxicity Prompts under strong steering) while maintaining the lowest cross-entropy loss among all baselines including SFT, DPO, MEDA, and INST. The approach also improves robustness against GCG jailbreak attacks, reducing attack success rate from 82% to 38.5% with strong steering.

## Results

On Toxigen, the model pretrained with 10% toxic data and strong inference-time intervention (ITI) achieves a toxicity score of 2.63, compared to 19.82 for the clean-data model with the same intervention strength. On Real Toxicity Prompts, the same configuration reaches 7.11 versus 13.33 for the clean-data baseline. Even at weak steering strength, the toxic-pretrained model achieves 16.25 (Toxigen) and 20.09 (Real Toxicity Prompts) with a cross-entropy loss of 2.65, outperforming all baselines on both toxicity and capability preservation simultaneously.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against MEDA (Prabhumoye et al., 2023), the paper's method with weak steering achieves 16.25 on Toxigen and 20.09 on Real Toxicity Prompts with CE loss 2.65, beating MEDA's 22.02/28.32/2.71 on all three metrics. Against INST (same paper), the weak-steering configuration (16.25/20.09/2.65) also dominates INST (18.99/30.09/2.73), particularly on Real Toxicity Prompts where the gap is ~10 points. Against DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023), the advantage is dramatic: weak steering yields 16.25 on Toxigen versus DPO's 38.86, a 22.6-point improvement, while also maintaining lower CE loss (2.65 vs. 2.71). The paper further shows in Table 2 that SFT and DPO themselves benefit from toxic pretraining data (smile-shaped curves), suggesting the pretraining-data intervention is complementary to, rather than in competition with, standard post-training alignment methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the superposition framework of Elhage et al. (2022) to formalize a new 'entanglement' measure for feature representations, and on inference-time intervention (Li et al., 2023) as the primary post-training detoxification mechanism. It extends the observation from Longpre et al. (2023) that toxicity filtering harms model capability by reframing the problem as a pre-/post-training co-design question: rather than asking whether toxic data should be removed, it asks how toxic data composition affects downstream alignability. Methodologically, it is closest to Prabhumoye et al. (2023), who also modify pretraining data to improve toxicity control, but departs by adding raw toxic content rather than artificial annotations, and by coupling this with activation steering rather than prompt-based elicitation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges the near-universal practice of filtering toxic content from pretraining corpora, providing both a theoretical account (reduced feature entanglement via superposition) and empirical evidence that moderate toxic data (~10%) improves the model's internal representation of toxicity and makes it more amenable to post-training detoxification. Key open questions include whether the 10% sweet spot generalizes to larger models and different toxic data distributions, whether the finding extends beyond toxicity to other alignment-relevant features (e.g., dishonesty, role-play boundaries), and how to derive a principled relationship between feature frequency and steerability rather than relying on empirical tuning. The 1B-parameter scale and single toxic-data source (4chan) limit immediate generalizability, but the conceptual reframing of data curation as an end-to-end design choice has broad implications for pretraining pipelines.
