## Problem

Prior quantization-based backdoor attacks on LLMs only apply to zero-shot quantization methods (e.g., FP4, LLM.int8()) where exact quantization-preserving intervals can be analytically computed. The most widely deployed quantization method in practice—GGUF k-quants used by llama.cpp and ollama (over 100M downloads, 70K+ shared models)—relies on iterative optimization that makes exact interval computation infeasible, leaving it unknown whether these optimization-based schemes are also vulnerable to adversarial quantization attacks.

## Input / Output

The adversary takes a full-precision LLM (Qwen2.5-1.5B/3B, Llama3.1-8B), a target set of GGUF k-quant data types (Q2_K through Q6_K, including S/M/L variants), and a task-specific training dataset. The output is a full-precision model that scores comparably or better than the base model on standard benchmarks, but which—when independently quantized by a third party to any targeted k-quant type—exhibits the implanted malicious behavior (vulnerable code, content injection, over-refusal, or jailbreak susceptibility). Critically, the adversary does not perform the quantization step; they only constrain the full-precision weights so that the quantization error naturally preserves the malicious behavior.

## Method

The core contribution is error-based interval estimation: rather than computing intractable exact quantization-preserving bounds, the adversary uses the observed difference between each full-precision weight and its dequantized value as a one-sided constraint interval, allowing removal training only in the direction that reduces quantization error. To ensure stability, the adversary freezes the subblock parameters governing double-quantization scales (d_scales, d_mins) and the max/min of each subblock, which empirically preserves the quantized codes for ~80% of weights. For simultaneous attacks across multiple k-quant types, a heuristic expansion formula (parameterized by λ) widens narrow intervals so that intersections across types remain non-empty, trading a small over-approximation for feasible multi-target optimization. The full pipeline is: (1) fine-tune a malicious model in full precision, (2) compute error-based intervals from the target quantization, (3) perform constrained removal fine-tuning (via PGD) so the full-precision model loses the malicious behavior while the quantized version retains it, and (4) publish the benign-appearing full-precision model for community quantization.

## Results

On Llama3.1-8b across nine GGUF k-quant data types, the attack achieves a security contrast of Δ=88.7% for vulnerable code generation (code security drops from ~100% full-precision to 11.3–44.4% quantized), Δ=85.0% for content injection (keyword occurrence rises from ~0.7% to 23.9–85.9%), and Δ=30.1% for over-refusal (informative refusal increases from ~1.5% to 21.1–31.7%). In the all-at-once setting targeting all nine data types simultaneously, the smallest security contrast is 53.2% (code generation) while content injection still reaches up to 85.9%. Full-precision utility (MMLU, TruthfulQA, HumanEval) remains close to or above the base model, confirming stealth.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is against the prior quantization-attack framework of Egashira et al. (2024) and Ma et al. (2023), which rely on exact quantization-preserving intervals computable only for zero-shot methods. When that existing attack is naively applied to GGUF k-quants (Table 7), it produces no contrast at all: keyword occurrence remains 0.1% in both full-precision and quantized models, whereas the proposed error-based interval method achieves 0.2% full-precision vs. 50.1% quantized (Δ=50.0%). On zero-shot quantizations where exact intervals are available (Table 4), the error-based intervals are 3–4× smaller than exact intervals (e.g., 2.1e-4 vs. 6.8e-4 for LLM.int8() on Qwen2.5-3b), yielding slightly lower full-precision code security (73.5% vs. 87.9%), but still exceeding the original model's security (69.3%), confirming the intervals remain sufficient for a successful attack. The paper also extends the attack beyond GGUF to HQQ and GPTQ (Table 5), showing moderate transferability: vulnerable code generation achieves Δ=53.4% on GPTQ and near-parity with GGUF on HQQ, while content injection transfer is marginal (Δ≈0.6–1.2%). Overall, the proposed method is the first to demonstrate a working quantization-triggered backdoor on optimization-based k-quants, a setting where all prior baselines fail entirely.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the quantization-triggered backdoor attack introduced by Egashira et al. (2024) from zero-shot quantization methods (FP4, LLM.int8(), NF4) to optimization-based GGUF k-quants, which are the dominant quantization format in practice (llama.cpp, ollama). The key technical departure is replacing the exact-interval constraint derivation—feasible only for zero-shot methods—with 'error-based intervals' that use the observed quantization error as a tractable proxy for optimization constraints during removal training. The attack pipeline (inject → derive constraints → removal finetune → publish) follows the framework established by Ma et al. (2023) for image classifiers and Egashira et al. (2024) for LLMs, with the novelty lying entirely in making the constraint derivation work for the iterative, interdependent optimization of k-quants.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: GGUF k-quants underpin over 100M model downloads and 70K+ shared models, meaning the most widely deployed quantization format in the LLM ecosystem is vulnerable to stealthy adversarial injection. The finding that quantization complexity alone does not provide defense challenges a natural assumption among practitioners who may view optimization-based quantization as inherently safer than simple rounding. Open questions include: per-model calibration of Gaussian noise defense (no a priori recipe exists), whether the attack transfers to larger frontier models, and whether the partial extension to GPTQ/HQQ can be strengthened. The paper also raises the broader supply-chain concern that a benign third party performing quantization can unknowingly activate a planted backdoor.
