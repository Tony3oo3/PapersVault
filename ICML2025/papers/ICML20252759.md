## Problem

Diplomacy presents an exponentially large action space (up to 10^64 joint actions per turn) that makes direct LLM fine-tuning intractable, while traditional equilibrium-search methods like Cicero require massive computational resources (448 GPUs) and large-scale game data. The paper asks whether a fine-tuned LLM can learn equilibrium policies for Diplomacy with a fraction of the data and compute that domain-specific models demand.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252759/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference, the LLM is called sequentially once per unit (up to 34 times per turn). Each call receives a textual board state describing all seven powers' unit positions, supply centers, and last moves, concatenated with a task prompt listing the actions already assigned to previous units and the candidate orders for the current unit. The model outputs a single unit-level action string (e.g., 'moves to Greece'), and the post-processing module assembles all unit actions into the final joint order list (e.g., 'F MAO-POR') sent to the game environment. During training, each sample additionally carries a scalar Q-value Q_i^d used as a weighting term in the loss.

## Method

DipLLM introduces an LLM-based autoregressive factorization framework that decomposes the joint action policy π_i(a_i^{1:D}|s) into a product of unit-level conditional policies π_i^d(a_i^d|s, a_i^{1:d-1}), reducing the combinatorial action space to manageable per-unit sub-spaces. The learning objective is derived from piKL-Hedge: a KL-regularized Q-value Q_i^d is defined for each unit via a log-sum-exp over remaining actions, and the target policy is a softmax over these Q-values. The paper proves (Theorem 1) that the factorized product policy is exactly equivalent to the original joint equilibrium policy, and (Theorem 2) that in two-player zero-sum games the average policies converge to a (max β_i δ_i)-approximate Nash equilibrium. Fine-tuning uses Llama 3 8B with LoRA, minimizing a weighted cross-entropy loss where the log-probability of the ground-truth unit action is multiplied by exp(Q_i^d), effectively performing Q-weighted supervised fine-tuning. Training data is collected by running DipNet against the environment, applying piKL-Hedge to compute joint Q-values, and decomposing them into unit-level samples (s, a_i^{1:d-1}, a_i^d, Q_i^d) using a tight log-sum-exp lower bound to avoid exhaustive enumeration.

## Results

In a 1v6 no-press Diplomacy population evaluation, DipLLM achieves a SoS score of 23.0%±0.1% and a win rate of 22.3%±0.2%, outperforming the SOTA Cicero (20.8% SoS, 20.5% win rate) by 2.2% and 1.8% respectively. DipLLM also reduces the defeat rate from 29.4% (Cicero) to 27.4% while maintaining a comparable survival rate (50.3% vs 50.1%). Notably, DipLLM achieves this using only 1.5% of the training data required by Cicero, and requires only 10–20% of Cicero's inference time per turn.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DipLLM outperforms Cicero (Bakhtin et al., 2022a) by 2.2% in SoS and 1.8% in win rate in the population evaluation, and maintains a win rate above the 14.3% break-even line even when Cicero is augmented with up to 10 equilibrium search rollouts (Figure 5). Against the earlier domain-specific models DNVI and DORA (Bakhtin et al., 2021), DipLLM leads by over 16% in SoS, reflecting the gap between equilibrium-search-trained agents and the prior generation of RL-based Diplomacy agents. Compared to DipNet (Anthony et al., 2020), DipLLM surpasses it with only 100 games of fine-tuning data and extends the lead to 6.7% SoS at 500 games (Figure 6), demonstrating strong data efficiency. The prompt-based LLM agent Richelieu (Guan et al., 2024) and a one-step SFT LLM agent both underperform below the 14.3% break-even line and are excluded from the main population table. In training-method ablations (Table 7), the proposed Q-weighted SFT loss outperforms PPO (29.3% vs 23.4% SoS) and vastly outperforms DPO (29.3% vs 1.2% SoS) when fine-tuning against six DipNet opponents, confirming that structured game-reward weighting is critical over preference-based or generic policy-gradient approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two research threads: (1) equilibrium-search-based Diplomacy agents (piKL-Hedge, Cicero, DORA) that require massive compute to generate training data, and (2) autoregressive factorization techniques (Q-Transformer, Deep Switching ARF) that decompose joint action spaces into sequential sub-decisions. DipLLM departs from both by transplanting the piKL-Hedge KL-regularized equilibrium objective into an LLM's autoregressive token-prediction framework, so that the LLM is fine-tuned to approximate the Nash equilibrium policy unit-by-unit rather than over the full combinatorial action space. Unlike prior LLM-based Diplomacy agents (Richelieu) that rely on prompting or plain SFT, DipLLM introduces a theoretically grounded loss function (weighted cross-entropy with unit-level Q-values) that explicitly targets equilibrium alignment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that a fine-tuned 8B LLM surpasses Cicero (2.7B domain-specific model) using only 1.5% of its training data suggests that pre-trained language models encode genuinely useful strategic priors for complex multi-agent games, potentially reducing the compute barrier for equilibrium-based agents. However, the method still depends on externally generated equilibrium data (DipNet + piKL-Hedge), so full self-sufficiency via self-play remains an open question. The negative result that DPO fails catastrophically (1.2% SoS) in multi-agent strategic settings highlights the difficulty of preference-based learning when joint actions and long-horizon credit assignment make preference labels ambiguous. The autoregressive factorization + equilibrium objective framework is generalizable to other games with large combinatorial action spaces, making it a potentially reusable recipe.
