## Problem

Prior work on AI influence on human beliefs has focused on unidirectional effects in laboratory settings, leaving the mutual human-AI feedback loop uncharacterized mechanistically and untested on real-world usage data. This paper asks whether the iterative cycle of LLMs learning from human preferences and broadcasting reinforced beliefs back to users inevitably drives populations toward confident false beliefs (lock-in), and what the precise conditions for this phase transition are.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252017/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The formal model takes a trust matrix W ∈ R^{N×N} encoding pairwise trust levels and i.i.d. Gaussian observations, producing posterior belief trajectories and a closed-form lock-in threshold (N−1)λ₁λ₂ > 1. The empirical pipeline consumes WildChat-1M conversation logs (837,989 conversations from 167,062 users over 12 months) and a 5.4M-node concept hierarchy built via HDBSCAN clustering of 256-dim voyage-3-large embeddings, producing lineage diversity scores in [0,1] and regression kink design p-values at each GPT version switch date. The simulation component takes natural-language belief statements from r/ChangeMyView posts and maps them to [0,1] via GPT-4.1 evaluation at each of 200 rounds.

## Method

The paper combines three complementary approaches. First, a Bayesian multi-agent model shows that when the spectral radius of the trust matrix exceeds 1, agents double-count their own beliefs through the feedback loop, causing exponential convergence to false estimates—a phase transition validated numerically. Second, agent-based simulations with 100 GPT-4.1-Nano agents consulting a centralized LLM authority (which aggregates and broadcasts group beliefs) demonstrate belief convergence to extreme stances and entropy collapse over 200 rounds across four real r/ChangeMyView topics. Third, causal inference on WildChat-1M uses regression kink design to detect discontinuous drops in a novel lineage diversity metric (based on expected LCA subtree size in the concept hierarchy) at GPT version switch dates, with per-user mixed-effects regressions controlling for user identity, language, engagement progress, and temporal confounders to isolate the sustained effect of iterative training on diversity loss.

## Results

Theoretically, the paper proves that collective lock-in to a false belief is inevitable when the spectral radius of the trust matrix exceeds 1; for the human-LLM setting, this reduces to the threshold (N−1)λ₁λ₂ > 1, meaning that with N=101 agents, mutual trust as low as 0.1 (i.e., discounting each other by a factor of 10) suffices for lock-in. In agent-based simulations with 100 GPT-4.1-Nano agents over 200 rounds, beliefs converge to extreme viewpoints (≈0.9 or 0.1 on a [0,1] spectrum) across all four r/ChangeMyView topics and all 8 runs. On WildChat-1M (837,989 conversations, 167,062 users), regression kink design detects statistically significant discontinuous drops in lineage diversity of value-laden concepts at GPT-4-0125-preview (p<.05) and GPT-3.5-turbo-0613 (p<.05) deployment dates, while GPT-4 conversations show a sustained downward diversity trend over time (p<.05).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing methods in the traditional sense; instead, it cross-validates its findings across multiple diversity metrics, model families, and filtering policies. The primary comparison is between GPT-4 and GPT-3.5-turbo: GPT-4 consistently shows downward diversity trends (Hypothesis 1 supported, p<.05 for lineage diversity on value-laden concepts), while GPT-3.5-turbo shows an ambiguous or even upward trend, possibly reflecting a division-of-labor where cognitively demanding workloads shift to GPT-4. For Hypothesis 2 (discontinuous drops at version updates), results are strongest for GPT-4-0125-preview and GPT-3.5-turbo-0613 (p<.05 across most metrics), but weaker for GPT-3.5-turbo-0125 (ambiguous across metrics). The per-user mixed regression (controlling for user identity, language, engagement, and other confounders) shows negative coefficients for num_kinks_before across all six metric/filtering combinations, though only two reach p<.05 (Topic Entropy p=.035, Jaccard p=.030), providing moderate rather than definitive support. The lineage diversity metric (the authors' proposed metric) is the most consistently significant across settings, outperforming the secondary metrics (depth diversity, topic entropy, Jaccard distance) in statistical power.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper draws on three distinct research threads—echo chambers in recommender systems, LLM influence on human users, and model collapse/feedback loops in language models—but explicitly departs from each by focusing on the *bidirectional* human-LLM feedback loop rather than unidirectional influence. The formal model is inspired by iterated learning and information cascades, but introduces a trust-matrix framework with a spectral-radius threshold that has no direct analogue in those literatures. The empirical analysis repurposes regression kink design (from econometrics) to detect causal effects of LLM version updates on human conceptual diversity, a methodological transplant that is novel in the AI-safety context.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first formal characterization of when human-LLM feedback loops inevitably produce collective lock-in to false beliefs, with a surprisingly weak threshold condition (for N=101 agents, mutual trust factors above 0.1 suffice). The WildChat analysis offers the first real-world (if preliminary) evidence that conceptual diversity in user messages drops discontinuously at GPT version switches, supporting the feedback-loop mechanism. Key limitations include the observational nature of the data (confounders cannot be fully excluded), the simplified agent-based simulations, and the ambiguous GPT-3.5-turbo results. Open questions include how exploration-oriented forces interact with lock-in, whether RCTs can confirm the causal claims, and what algorithmic or policy interventions might mitigate the effect.
