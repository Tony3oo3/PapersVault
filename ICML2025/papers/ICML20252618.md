## Problem

Existing graph structures and GNN methods cannot natively model multi-level hierarchical systems where each node is itself a graph (e.g., proteins in a protein-protein interaction network, or papers in a citation network). Prior approaches either process each subgraph independently, ignoring inter-graph dependencies, or flatten all subgraphs into a single large graph, destroying internal structure. This paper introduces the Graph-of-Net (GON) formalism and a dedicated learning architecture for it.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252618/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The input is a Graph-of-Net: a set of N node-graphs (each with its own vertex set, edge set, and feature matrix) connected by inter-graph edges, plus one-hot class labels per node-graph. For standard network benchmarks, the GON is constructed by sampling k-hop neighborhoods around each node as its subgraph; for biomedical data, molecules and proteins are naturally represented as atom/amino-acid graphs. The model outputs low-dimensional node representation vectors that are used either for node classification (predicting the one-hot label) or for binary interaction prediction between pairs of node-graphs (e.g., drug-target affinity).

## Method

N2GON employs a dual-encoder message-passing architecture: Encoder I applies L₁ layers of GNN message passing within each node-graph to produce a per-graph embedding, and Encoder II applies L₂ layers of message passing over the inter-graph network to refine these embeddings with global relational context. A two-stage constraint network is then constructed: Grapher I computes a binary label-identity matrix C = Y·Yᵀ, and Grapher II refines it via Personalized PageRank (PPR) to produce a weighted importance matrix Π that captures topological proximity between node-graphs. The key training signal is a contrastive constraint loss (Eq. 7) that uses the Hadamard product (Π ∘ C) to modulate pairwise cosine similarities, pulling same-label nodes closer in proportion to their PPR-derived importance while pushing different-label nodes apart, combined with a standard NLL classification loss.

## Results

On the 9 network benchmark datasets, N2GON achieves the highest node classification accuracy on 8 out of 9 datasets, with notable margins such as +3.94% over GCNII on CiteSeer (81.27% vs 77.33%) and +5.03% over GGCN on Actor (42.57% vs 37.54%). The sole exception is Squirrel, where HopGNN leads (64.23% vs 60.98%). On biomedical interaction prediction tasks, N2GON achieves the best ROC-AUC across all 7 datasets, e.g., 95.27% on KIBA (vs 92.61% for PubChem+CNN) and 85.53% on TWOSIDES DDI (vs 83.03% for GoGNN).

## Baselines & Comparisons

N2GON outperforms all 20 baselines on 8 of 9 network datasets, with the largest gaps on heterophilic data: +5.03% over GGCN on Actor, +6.97% over ACM-GCN on Cornell, and +3.94% over GCNII on CiteSeer. The one dataset where N2GON trails is Squirrel, where HopGNN's hop-interaction mechanism yields 64.23% vs N2GON's 60.98%. On homophilic datasets (CiteSeer, PubMed, Cora), N2GON leads the strongest baselines (GCNII, GAT, GGCN) by 1–4 points. On biomedical interaction prediction, N2GON dominates all encoder-combination baselines from DeepPurpose (e.g., 95.27% ROC-AUC on KIBA vs 92.61% for PubChem+CNN; 91.53% on DAVIS vs 88.23% for PubChem+CNN) and also surpasses the purpose-built GoGNN on TWOSIDES DDI (85.53% vs 83.03% ROC-AUC). The ablation confirms both Grapher I (label-based constraint) and Grapher II (PPR-based positional refinement) contribute, with Grapher I having the larger individual impact (removing it drops Squirrel from 60.98% to 58.34%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper generalizes the graph-of-graphs concept from GoGNN (Wang et al., 2021), which was restricted to drug-drug interaction prediction, into a universal Graph-of-Net (GON) structure applicable across domains. The dual-encoder message-passing architecture draws on standard GNN frameworks (GCN, GAT, GraphSAGE), while the position-aware constraint network is a novel contribution inspired by Personalized PageRank, combining label-based similarity (Grapher I) with topological proximity (Grapher II). For heterogeneous biomedical networks, the paper adapts the homogeneous GNN backbone using the RGCN conversion from Schlichtkrull et al. (2018). The approach departs from prior GON-adjacent work (e.g., network-of-networks in physics, graph coarsening) by treating the GON as a first-class learning structure rather than a domain-specific heuristic.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The GON formulation offers a principled way to model hierarchical, multi-scale systems (proteins in interaction networks, papers in citation graphs, molecules in affinity networks) that standard flat graphs cannot naturally capture. However, a significant methodological concern is that for the 9 standard network benchmarks, GONs were artificially constructed by sampling k-hop neighborhoods, meaning the gains over flat-graph baselines may partly reflect the additional local structural information rather than the GON paradigm itself. The biomedical results, where GON is a natural representation, are more convincing. Open questions include noise robustness across encoding layers, sensitivity to graph construction strategies, and whether the PPR-based constraint network scales efficiently to very large GONs.
