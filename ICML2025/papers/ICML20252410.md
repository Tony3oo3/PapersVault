## Problem

Despite extensive evidence that neural networks can learn computational algorithms and generalize infinitely from finite training data, the mechanism by which gradient descent produces such algorithms remains unclear. This paper asks specifically how recurrent neural networks transition from memorizing individual training sequences to constructing a finite automaton that solves the task for all sequence lengths, and what inductive biases in gradient descent drive this transition.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252410/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The RNN receives binary sequences of 0s and 1s of varying length (trained on all sequences up to length 10, validated on sequences up to length 10000) and outputs a single binary prediction at each timestep indicating whether the running count of 1s is even or odd. Beyond the prediction, the method extracts a deterministic finite automaton at each training epoch by treating hidden activation vectors as states, the recurrent map as the transition function, and the output map as the state labeling, with a distance threshold (0.01× representational standard deviation) determining when two states are considered merged.

## Method

The paper develops an effective theory of representational dynamics in RNNs centered on a local interaction model for pairs of nearby hidden representations. By linearizing the output map around the representational mean and optimizing with respect to the effective linear parameters, the authors derive a self-contained 3-scalar dynamical system whose fixed points determine whether two representations merge. The key result is an implicit state merger condition: two states merge if and only if they agree on all possible future outputs, the corresponding sequences are sufficiently long, and the training set is large enough with small enough weight initialization. This explains the observed two-phase learning dynamics: an initial tree-fitting phase where agreeing pairs diverge (due to differing effective learning rates scaling with sequence length), followed by a merging phase that collapses the automaton to a finite set of states, producing a sharp phase transition to perfect infinite generalization. The theory is architecture-independent, relying only on smoothness and expressivity of the recurrent and output maps, and is validated on the streaming parity task, random regular tasks, and a transformer on modular subtraction.

## Results

On the streaming parity task, an RNN (100 hidden units, ReLU) trained on all sequences up to length 10 achieves perfect generalization to sequences up to length 10,000 (1000× longer than training), with validation accuracy jumping sharply from chance (0.5) to 1.0 at a phase transition determined by training set size and initial weight scale. The learned automaton initially expands into a complete tree of all training sequences, then undergoes state mergers until it becomes finite, at which point infinite generalization occurs instantaneously. The final automaton contains redundant states (far more than the minimal 2 required) but is provably equivalent to the minimal parity automaton after reduction via Hopcroft's algorithm. For transformers on modular subtraction, a similar two-phase pattern (divergence then merger) is observed in the attention matrix but not in the hidden layer, and transformers fail to fully generalize to arbitrary sequence lengths on parity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper is primarily a theoretical and analytical study rather than a method-comparison benchmark, so no direct baseline comparisons are made. The closest architectural comparison is between RNNs and transformers: RNNs achieve a clean phase transition to perfect infinite generalization on the streaming parity task, while transformers (tested on modular subtraction/addition) show state-merging dynamics only in the attention matrix and fail to fully generalize to arbitrary sequence lengths on parity (consistent with Anil et al., 2022). The interaction model is shown to be architecture-independent: qualitatively similar two-phase dynamics (initial divergence followed by merger) are reproduced with both ReLU and tanh activations, and with a step discontinuity in the output map (albeit noisier). The theoretical merger condition (Eq. 9) is validated empirically: all 103,460 merged representation pairs agreed on all future outputs, and mergers only begin once the minimum sequence length m exceeds a threshold dependent on the weight initialization scale G.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the local two-point interaction model from van Rossem & Saxe (2024) — originally developed for feed-forward networks — to recurrent architectures, and frames the resulting representational dynamics as a system of locally interacting particles following the grokking effective theory of Liu et al. (2022). It sits at the intersection of the automaton-extraction literature (Giles et al., 1992; Weiss et al., 2020; Merrill & Tsilivis, 2022), the implicit-bias literature (Neyshabur et al., 2015; Chizat & Bach, 2020), and the length-generalization literature for RNNs and transformers (Anil et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2024). The key departure from prior work is showing that continuity of the output map alone, via gradient descent's implicit bias, is sufficient to drive the state mergers that collapse a memorizing tree into a finite automaton, without any explicit regularization or architectural inductive bias.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a mechanistic, architecture-independent explanation for how RNNs develop computational algorithms and achieve infinite generalization from finite training data, identifying a sharp phase transition from tree-fitting to finite-automaton behavior. The finding that redundant states are a natural consequence of the learning dynamics (not an artifact) has direct implications for neuroscience, suggesting different animals may learn functionally equivalent but structurally different algorithms. Open questions include whether the local interaction model captures sufficient dynamics for more complex or continuous tasks, how the analogous attention-pattern merging in transformers leads to (or fails to lead to) algorithm development, and whether the phase transition persists under regularization, noise, or richer architectures.
