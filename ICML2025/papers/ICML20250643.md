## Problem

Standard pairwise comparison queries in RLHF suffer from two coupled limitations: humans struggle to label trajectories with subtle differences (causing high cognitive load and noisy feedback), and the resulting ordinal preferences cannot directly convey preference strength, which is essential for the cardinal utilities that RL requires for expected utility maximization. No prior method elicits cardinal utility information through a query structure that is simultaneously easier for the labeler to answer.

## Input / Output

The method consumes trajectory segments (sequences of state-action pairs) sampled from the agent's replay buffer and presents them to a human labeler as distinguishability queries—each query bundles two pairwise comparison queries. The labeler's feedback is a two-part response: a binary choice of which pair is more distinguishable (the meta-label d), followed by a standard pairwise preference label on the selected pair. The system outputs an ensemble of N neural-network reward functions and a trained SAC policy; the reward model is updated via a combined loss over both the distinguishability and preference feedback, while the policy is updated via standard off-policy RL on the learned reward.

## Method

DistQ introduces the distinguishability query (DQ), which presents two pairwise comparison queries simultaneously and asks the labeler to first select the more distinguishable pair before providing a preference label on it, thereby encoding cardinal utility (preference strength) through the meta-choice. Query selection operates in two stages: candidate PCQs are first filtered by ensemble prediction variance (epistemic uncertainty/informativeness), retaining the top n_I; then within that pool, the top and bottom n_E queries by negative prediction entropy (easiness) are selected and paired so that the i-th easiest is matched with the (i+n_E)-th easiest, ensuring each DQ contrasts an easy with a harder comparison. The training objective is a weighted sum of two cross-entropy losses: one on the distinguishability choice, modeled via a Bradley-Terry distribution over the learned distinguishability measure M (the absolute difference in predicted returns), and one on the standard pairwise preference of the selected pair. The entire pipeline is built on the PEBBLE framework with SAC for agent learning, and the ablation studies confirm that both the two-stage selection order (informativeness before easiness) and the structured pairing contribute meaningfully to final performance.

## Results

On DMControl locomotion tasks, DistQ (full budget) achieves episode rewards of 909.93 on Walker walk, 828.67 on Quadruped walk, and 420.76 on Humanoid stand, outperforming all baselines except QPA on Humanoid stand (456.17). On Meta-World manipulation tasks, DistQ reaches near-perfect success rates (0.98–1.00) on Window open, Door unlock, Door open, and Sweep into, substantially exceeding PEBBLE (0.46–0.70) and SURF (0.58–0.92). Even with half the query budget, DistQ remains competitive, e.g., 891.64 on Walker walk vs. MRN's 877.63 at full budget.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DistQ outperforms PEBBLE (its backbone) on every task by a wide margin, e.g., 909.93 vs. 795.28 on Walker walk and 828.67 vs. 398.45 on Quadruped walk. Against MRN, the strongest baseline on locomotion, DistQ leads by 32.3 points on Walker walk and 213.6 points on Quadruped walk. On manipulation tasks, DistQ (0.98–1.00 success rate) surpasses SURF (0.58–0.92), RUNE (0.80–1.00), and QPA (0.52–0.80) on most tasks, though RUNE ties DistQ on Door open (both 1.00). The sole exception is Humanoid stand, where QPA (456.17) edges out DistQ (420.76), likely due to QPA's policy-aligned query selection being particularly effective on this high-budget task. Notably, DistQ with only half the query budget still matches or exceeds most full-budget baselines, demonstrating the information efficiency of distinguishability queries.

## Relation to Prior Work

DistQ builds directly on the PEBBLE framework (Lee et al., 2021b), extending the standard pairwise comparison query (PCQ) used in preference-based RLHF with a second-order 'distinguishability query' that asks labelers to first select which of two comparisons is easier to judge before providing a preference. Conceptually, it draws on cardinal utility and difference-relation theory from decision science (Suppes & Winet, 1955; Krantz et al., 2006) to formalize preference strength, and on active-learning query selection (Biyik et al., 2019) to balance informativeness with labeler ease. Unlike prior approaches that infer cardinal strength indirectly from noisy pairwise comparisons (Chan et al., 2021) or from response times via drift-diffusion models (Shvartsman et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024), DistQ elicits strength explicitly through a relative, second-order query that avoids strong cognitive-modeling assumptions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The work addresses two well-recognized pain points in RLHF: the cognitive burden of comparing near-identical trajectories and the loss of cardinal preference strength inherent in ordinal pairwise labels. The consistent performance gains across locomotion and manipulation benchmarks, combined with the striking user-study result (100% vs. 0% task success), suggest the query design is both more informative and more human-friendly. However, evaluation is limited to simulated control tasks with a synthetic oracle, and the method has not yet been tested in language-model fine-tuning or with diverse real human labelers at scale—key settings where the ordinal-to-cardinal gap is most consequential. Open questions include whether the simple variance-plus-entropy selection scheme generalizes, how the approach scales to high-dimensional trajectory spaces, and whether the Bradley-Terry assumption on distinguishability choices holds for real humans.
