## Problem

External controls from historical studies or real-world data can augment small control arms in randomized clinical trials for survival outcomes, but direct integration introduces biases from both covariate shifts and outcome drift (differences in conditional survival distributions across studies). Existing integrative methods for survival data either assume full population homogeneity, rely on restrictive Cox model parametric assumptions, or fail to account for the time-varying nature of outcome drift, leaving a gap for a flexible, assumption-light framework with valid statistical inference.

## Input / Output

The method consumes two censored survival datasets—trial data with treatment assignment (Y, Δ, A, X, R=1) and external controls without treatment (Y, Δ, A=0, X, R=0)—along with a penalty function (e.g., adaptive lasso), a tuning parameter λ_N, and a restricted time horizon τ. It outputs a point estimate of the RMST difference θ_τ with a bootstrap-based confidence interval, the identified comparable external subset Ã, and the asymptotic variance. A key structural detail is that the estimator is two-stage: nuisance functions (survival curves, censoring probabilities, propensity scores) are first estimated via machine learning, then a penalized regression on DR-Learner pseudo-outcomes determines which external subjects to retain before the final EIF-based estimation is computed.

## Method

The core contribution is a doubly protected integrative estimator for the RMST difference built on the semiparametric efficient influence function (EIF). First, the paper derives the observed-data EIF for treatment-specific survival curves S_a(t|R=1) under combined trial and external data, incorporating the density ratio q_R(X) to correct covariate shifts and variance-optimal weights D(t,X) to optimally combine information from both sources. Second, to handle potential outcome drift (violation of the comparability assumption), the method recasts the EIF into a selection-based framework: it constructs subject-level pseudo-outcomes ξ_i via the DR-Learner that estimate the conditional RMST difference between trial and external populations, then applies a penalized regression (adaptive lasso) to identify the zero-bias subset Ã of comparable external controls. The final estimator θ̂_τ^adapt integrates only the comparable subset, achieving rate double robustness (consistency if either the survival model or the propensity/density-ratio model is correctly specified) and semiparametric efficiency. The 'doubly protected' label reflects protection against covariate shift (via density-ratio weighting in the EIF) and against outcome drift (via selective borrowing), with the asymptotic variance strictly smaller than the trial-only estimator unless the external data is entirely non-comparable.

## Results

In simulation studies (N_e=500, τ=2, N_0 ranging 50–400), under Setting 1 (no outcome drift), all integrative estimators improve Root-MSE over the trial-only AIPW benchmark, though the proposed θ̂_τ^adapt is slightly less efficient due to the cost of bias detection. Under Settings 2–3 (unmeasured confounding, lack of concurrency), the naive full-borrowing, PS-integrated, and transfer-learning Cox estimators become substantially biased, while θ̂_τ^adapt maintains bias comparable to the trial-only estimator with improved standard errors and controlled type-I error. In the real-data application (EVOLVE-1 + REGAIN, Galcanezumab 120mg vs. placebo), θ̂_τ^adapt achieves PrSS ≥ 0.6 for detecting θ_τ < −0.1 at τ=6 months with only 100 placebo patients, versus 150 required by the trial-only estimator, demonstrating a ~33% reduction in needed concurrent controls.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the trial-only AIPW estimator (Tsiatis, 2006), the proposed θ̂_τ^adapt wins on statistical efficiency: it achieves lower standard errors and higher power for detecting treatment effects while maintaining comparable bias, and in the real-data application it requires only 100 placebo patients (vs. 150) to reach PrSS ≥ 0.6 at τ=6. Against the propensity score-integrated estimator (Chen et al., 2020) and the transfer learning Cox regression (Li et al., 2023b), the proposed method clearly wins on bias control: under Settings 2 (unmeasured confounding) and 3 (lack of concurrency), both baselines exhibit substantial bias and inflated type-I error, whereas θ̂_τ^adapt selectively borrows only the comparable external subset and maintains nominal coverage. Against the naive full-borrowing estimator θ̂_τ^acw, the proposed method trades a small efficiency loss under Setting 1 (no drift) for dramatic bias reduction under Settings 2–5, where full borrowing introduces significant outcome-drift bias. Overall, the proposed doubly protected adaptive estimator dominates on robustness (bias, type-I error, coverage) across all five simulation settings while retaining efficiency gains over the trial-only benchmark.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the semiparametric efficient influence function (EIF) framework of Tsiatis (2006) and the data-adaptive borrowing approach of Gao et al. (2024a) from non-survival to censored survival outcomes, incorporating inverse probability censoring weighting and martingale-based augmentation terms. For the selective borrowing step, it repurposes the DR-Learner (Kennedy, 2020) from heterogeneous treatment effect estimation to detect subject-level outcome drift between trial and external populations. The method departs from prior survival integration approaches (Chen et al., 2020; Li et al., 2023b; Bellot & van der Schaar, 2019) by avoiding the Cox model assumption, handling time-varying baseline hazard differences, and providing valid semiparametric inference with confidence intervals rather than point estimates alone.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses a practically critical gap in rare-disease and small-control-arm clinical trials where external controls are the only feasible way to augment the control group, yet comparability assumptions are routinely violated. The real-data application demonstrates a concrete enrollment reduction (150→100 placebo patients for equivalent study power), which could accelerate drug development timelines. Key limitations acknowledged by the authors include degraded type I error control under unmeasured confounding in small samples, and the subject-level selection criterion that may discard partially comparable external data. Future work on joint estimation of treatment effects and bias functions, and on selecting comparable information rather than entire subjects, are identified as promising directions.
