## Problem

Estimating the conditional average treatment effect (CATE) from observational data with unobserved confounding typically requires strong, untestable parametric assumptions (linearity, additivity, homogeneity) for point identification via instrumental variables. Existing partial identification methods that avoid such assumptions are limited to discrete or binary instruments, or rely on unstable adversarial training that degrades with high-dimensional instruments. No reliable method existed for partial identification of the CATE using complex (continuous, potentially high-dimensional) instruments such as gene expressions in Mendelian randomization.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input an observational dataset of complex instruments Z ∈ R^d (continuous and potentially high-dimensional, e.g., polygenic risk scores or hundreds of SNPs), observed confounders X, binary treatment A ∈ {0,1}, and bounded outcome Y ∈ [s₁, s₂]. It produces valid lower and upper bounds b⁻(x) ≤ τ(x) ≤ b⁺(x) on the CATE for each covariate value x, guaranteed under only the standard IV assumptions (consistency, exclusion, independence) without additional parametric restrictions. The number of discrete partitions k is a tunable hyperparameter controlling the tightness-variance trade-off.

## Method

The core contribution is a two-stage neural procedure that maps complex instruments to a learned discrete representation φ(Z) → {0,…,k} via Gumbel-softmax, then applies closed-form discrete-IV bounds (Theorem 1) to obtain valid CATE bounds. The key theoretical insight is that for any discretization φ, the required nuisance quantities μ_φ^a(x,ℓ) and π_φ(x,ℓ) can be computed directly from first-stage estimates via simple averaging (Eqs. 12–13), eliminating the need for alternating or adversarial training. The second-stage network is trained with a compositional loss: bound-width minimization, a regularization term enforcing minimum partition probability p̂_{ℓ,φ} > ε (derived from the asymptotic variance analysis in Theorem 2 to control estimation variance), and an auxiliary cross-entropy loss for partition heterogeneity. This design yields a tightness-bias-variance trade-off (Lemma 2) where the regularization parameter λ explicitly balances population-level bound tightness against finite-sample estimation stability.

## Results

On Datasets 1 and 2 (1-D continuous instruments), the proposed method achieves 100% coverage of the true CATE while reducing bound width by 13.9% (1.05 vs. 1.22) and 13.0% (1.14 vs. 1.31) over the Naïve k-means baseline, with mean squared deviation (MSD) across k values reduced by 89.3% and 33.3% respectively. On the high-dimensional Dataset 3 (20-dim binary SNPs), the method achieves 97% coverage of oracle bounds (vs. 84% for Naïve), tighter valid bounds (Width* 1.82 vs. 1.88), and 70.3% lower MSD. On the real-world ADJUVANT study (22 genetic variants), the method reduces MSD by 76.9% (2.18 vs. 9.43) while achieving comparable bound width.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against three baselines: (1) DeepIV with bootstrapped confidence intervals (Hartford et al., 2017), (2) a Discretized IV baseline using fixed grouping rules, and (3) a Naïve k-means clustering baseline. DeepIV fails catastrophically, achieving only 52% coverage of the true CATE and 0% coverage of oracle bounds on Dataset 3, confirming that point-identification methods with untestable assumptions produce unreliable intervals. Against the Naïve k-means baseline, the proposed method wins on all informative metrics: 13–14% tighter bounds on Datasets 1–2, 15.5% higher oracle-bound coverage on Dataset 3 (97% vs. 84%), and up to 89.3% lower MSD, indicating far greater robustness to the choice of k. Against the Discretized IV baseline on Dataset 3, the method achieves 3.2% lower Width*, 16.6% lower MSE*, and 70.3% lower MSD. The performance gap widens with instrument complexity, suggesting the learned neural partitioning is most beneficial for high-dimensional or non-linear settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the discrete-instrument partial identification framework of Swanson et al. (2018) and Schweisthal et al. (2024) to complex (continuous, high-dimensional) instruments by learning a neural partitioning φ(Z) that maps instruments into a discrete representation space, after which the known closed-form discrete IV bounds apply directly. It departs from prior continuous-instrument partial identification methods (Kilbertus et al., 2020; Padh et al., 2023) that rely on unstable adversarial or alternating training, and from point-identification IV methods (Hartford et al., 2017; Singh et al., 2019) that impose untestable parametric assumptions like linearity or additivity. The key theoretical contribution (Theorem 1) shows that for *any* discretization φ, valid CATE bounds follow, and that the resulting nuisance quantities can be computed from pre-trained first-stage models without retraining, enabling a clean two-stage procedure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first method that handles complex instruments (e.g., hundreds of SNPs in Mendelian randomization) for partial identification of the CATE without strong parametric assumptions or adversarial training, filling a concrete gap for biostatisticians who need reliable bounds when unconfoundedness is violated. The practical relevance is high in settings like genetic epidemiology where instruments are inherently high-dimensional and the standard IV assumptions hold by design. Open questions include: how to select the number of partitions k without ground-truth CATEs in real-world applications (the paper offers heuristic guidelines but no formal guarantee), whether the approach extends to continuous treatments, and how performance degrades when the first-stage nuisance models are misspecified (the ablation with linear models shows coverage drops below 100%).
