## Problem

Despite widespread use of transfer learning in NLP and computer vision to address data scarcity, the transferability of MIL models in computational pathology remains poorly understood, with random weight initialization still being the standard in MIL model development. This paper asks: how well do pretrained MIL models transfer across organs, task types, and architectures, and can supervised pancancer pretraining rival or surpass self-supervised slide foundation models?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252151/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

WSIs are tokenized into 256×256 non-overlapping tissue patches at 20× magnification (0.5 μm/pixel), with patch features pre-extracted using a frozen pretrained encoder (default: UNI, a DINOv2-pretrained ViT-L/16 producing 1024-dim embeddings). The MIL aggregator pools variable-sized bags of ~1,000–10,000 patch embeddings into a single slide-level vector, which is then classified via a linear head (finetuning) or a KNN classifier (frozen evaluation). Evaluation spans 19 tasks across four organs with metrics including AUROC (binary), weighted Cohen's κ (grading), and balanced accuracy (multiclass).

## Method

The paper conducts a systematic empirical evaluation of 11 MIL architectures (ABMIL, CLAM, DSMIL, DFTD, TransMIL, Transformer, ILRA, RRT, WIKG, meanMIL, maxMIL) pretrained on 21 tasks (19 single-organ + 2 pancancer: PC-108 with 108 OncoTree classes, PC-43 with 43 cancer types) and transferred to 19 downstream tasks via KNN on frozen embeddings and end-to-end finetuning. Key mechanistic analyses include SVCCA to measure layer-level feature stability during finetuning, progressive layer-resetting ablations to isolate which components drive transfer gains, and attention heatmap visualization. The central finding is that the learned attention/aggregation layer is the most critical transferable component (resetting it causes the largest single performance drop), and that PC-108 pretrained ABMIL outperforms slide foundation models CHIEF and GigaPath on KNN and finetuning metrics while using only 6.5% and 2.3% of their pretraining data, respectively.

## Results

Across 19 CPath tasks and 11 MIL architectures, PC-108 supervised pretraining yields an average +3.3% improvement over random initialization, with ABMIL achieving the highest absolute performance (52.5% avg) and Transformer-based models (TransMIL, Transformer) showing the largest relative gains (+5.82% and +5.83% respectively). In frozen-feature (KNN) evaluation, PC-108 pretrained ABMIL outperforms the CHIEF slide foundation model in 12/15 tasks (+5.9% avg) and GigaPath in 13/15 tasks (+9.7% avg), while using only 6.5% and 2.3% of their respective pretraining data. In finetuning, PC-108 beats CHIEF in 11/15 tasks (+2.2%) and GigaPath in 10/15 tasks (+0.8%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among the 11 MIL architectures, ABMIL is the top performer under both random init and PC-108 pretraining, while Transformer and TransMIL benefit most from pretraining (+5.8% each) due to their higher parameterization and susceptibility to overfitting from random init. Non-parametric baselines (meanMIL, maxMIL) show minimal transfer gains (−8.0% and −0.5% on BRACS), confirming that learned aggregation is the key transferable component. Against slide foundation models, PC-108 pretrained ABMIL dominates: it beats CHIEF (ABMIL-based, 60K slides, supervised contrastive + CLIP) by +5.9% KNN and +2.2% finetuning on average, and beats GigaPath (LongNet-based, 171K slides, masked autoencoder) by +9.7% KNN and +0.8% finetuning. Notably, PC-108's pretraining set (3,944 WSIs) has zero sample overlap with the 15 evaluation tasks, whereas CHIEF was pretrained on TCGA/PANDA covering 11 of those 15 tasks, yet PC-108 still generalizes better. The paper concludes that supervised pancancer pretraining on a diverse 108-class hierarchical task is more effective than self-supervised scaling for slide-level representation learning, requiring <10% of the pretraining data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the well-established transfer learning paradigm from NLP and conventional computer vision to the MIL aggregation stage in computational pathology, a component that has been largely treated as a from-scratch training problem. It builds on the MIL architecture lineage (ABMIL, CLAM, TransMIL, DFTD, and others) and the recent wave of slide-level foundation models (CHIEF, GigaPath) that rely on self-supervised objectives, but departs from them by demonstrating that supervised pretraining on a diverse pancancer classification task is a simpler, more data-efficient alternative. The work also connects to the broader transfer learning literature on what features are transferred (SVCCA analysis, layer ablation) and how model scale interacts with initialization quality, applying these analytical tools to the two-stage patch-encoder-plus-aggregator structure unique to CPath.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical, well-evidenced roadmap showing that CPath practitioners should initialize MIL models with pretrained weights rather than random initialization, and that a single supervised pancancer task (PC-108, ~4K slides) can produce slide representations that outperform self-supervised foundation models trained on 60K–171K slides. This has significant implications for resource-constrained institutions that cannot afford large-scale self-supervised pretraining. Key limitations include reliance on a single institution's data (Brigham and Women's Hospital), absence of survival prediction and state-space MIL models, and acknowledged bias risks from non-diverse pretraining cohorts. Open questions include whether multi-institution or augmented pretraining further improves generalizability, and whether the data-efficiency advantage persists as downstream tasks become more heterogeneous.
