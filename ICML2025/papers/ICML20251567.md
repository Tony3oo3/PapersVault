## Problem

The canonical RLHF reward modeling pipeline collects fine-grained human feedback (e.g., Llama's 4-level scale) but trains with binary labels, discarding tied samples and collapsing distinct preference levels into a single bit. This paper asks how to properly model and exploit ordinal feedback of arbitrary granularity for reward learning, and what theoretical guarantees justify the benefit of finer-grained labels.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251567/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each training sample is a tuple (x, y₁, y₂, Z) where x is a prompt, y₁ and y₂ are two candidate responses, and Z is an ordinal feedback label drawn from a discrete set {z₁,…,z_m} ⊂ [0,1] (e.g., {0, 0.5, 1} for 3-level or {0, 0.2, 0.5, 0.8, 1} for 5-level) or a continuous oracle value. The trained reward model r_θ(x,y) outputs a real-valued score per response; the model is trained so that σ(r_θ(x,y₁) − r_θ(x,y₂)) approximates the population preference probability P(y₁ ≻ y₂ | x), with the ordinal label Z directly weighting the two terms of the cross-entropy loss rather than acting as a hard 0/1 indicator.

## Method

The paper introduces a marginal unbiasedness assumption ('wisdom of the crowd') requiring E[Z | (x,y₁,y₂)] = z_oracle(x,y₁,y₂), which generalizes the standard Bernoulli assumption for binary feedback and is validated via Galton's ox-weighting experiment. Under this assumption, Theorem 3.2 constructs explicit two-point probability distributions over the ordinal set that interpolate the oracle value between adjacent levels, ensuring the assumption is well-defined for any feedback granularity. The training objective is a generalized cross-entropy loss where the binary label is replaced by the ordinal value Z_i, preserving the Bradley-Terry sigmoid structure while allowing soft targets. The key theoretical result (Theorem 4.6) proves via a 'hierarchical expectation' coupling argument that any finer-grained feedback system reduces the Rademacher complexity relative to a coarser one, provided the loss satisfies an affinity condition (verified for cross-entropy and generalized hinge losses). This yields a clean ordering Rad_oracle ≤ Rad_ordinal ≤ Rad_binary and, as a corollary, a novel bias-variance tradeoff interpretation for knowledge distillation and soft labeling.

## Results

Across three base models (Llama-3.2-1B, Gemma-2-2B, Qwen2.5-1.5B), finer-grained ordinal feedback consistently improves out-of-distribution (OOD) accuracy on RewardBench over the canonical binary feedback: Llama improves from 76.67% (Binary) to 81.93% (Oracle), Gemma from 82.37% to 86.97%, and Qwen from 82.32% to 85.43%. In-distribution (ID) accuracy on the Skywork holdout set also improves monotonically, e.g., Llama from 93.29% (Binary) to 93.82% (Oracle). The 5-level feedback system ({0, 0.2, 0.5, 0.8, 1}) closely approximates the oracle in both ID and OOD settings, making it a practical choice. A secondary experiment shows that mixing 25–75% tied samples into 3-level training data further boosts OOD accuracy (e.g., Gemma reaches 84.87% at 25% tied vs. 83.19% at 0% tied), while 100% tied data causes reward collapse.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between four feedback granularities (Oracle, 5-level, 3-level, Binary) rather than against external methods; Binary feedback is the canonical baseline used in standard RLHF pipelines (e.g., InstructGPT, Llama 3). The theoretical prediction Oracle ≥ 5-level ≥ 3-level ≥ Binary holds consistently across all three base models on both ID and OOD metrics. The largest OOD gains come from moving from Binary to 3-level or 5-level: Llama gains +3.5 to +4.3 points, Gemma +3.4 to +4.6 points, and Qwen +1.2 to +1.9 points. The 5-level system ({0, 0.2, 0.5, 0.8, 1}) captures most of the oracle benefit (within ~1 point OOD) while remaining practical for human annotation. In the tied-data experiment (Table 2), mixing 25% tied samples into 3-level training outperforms pure binary (0% tied) on OOD accuracy for all three models (e.g., Gemma: 84.87% vs. 83.19%), whereas 100% tied data causes catastrophic reward collapse (ID accuracy drops to ~34–48%). The paper also discusses the Bradley-Terry with Ties (BTT) model as an alternative for handling ties but argues it introduces unnecessary hyperparameters and does not generalize to finer-grained feedback.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper generalizes the canonical Bradley-Terry reward modeling framework (Bradley & Terry, 1952; Ouyang et al., 2022) from binary feedback to arbitrary ordinal feedback, introducing a marginal unbiasedness assumption ('wisdom of the crowd') that subsumes the standard Bernoulli assumption. It departs from recent tied-preference models (Rao-Kupper, BTT) by avoiding extra hyperparameters and instead using a simple cross-entropy loss with soft targets, while providing a Rademacher complexity proof of the benefit. The hierarchical expectation framework also yields a novel bias-variance tradeoff perspective on knowledge distillation and soft labeling, connecting the RM literature to the distillation literature (Hinton, 2015; Zhou et al., 2021).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is immediate: LLM companies (e.g., Llama 3) already collect 4-level feedback but discard it during training; this paper provides a principled, drop-in loss function to exploit that data without architectural changes. The theoretical contribution (Rademacher complexity reduction under hierarchical expectation) is clean and potentially transferable to other soft-labeling settings. Open questions include whether the 'wisdom of the crowd' marginal unbiasedness assumption holds with real (non-ideal) annotators, how to calibrate the quantitative annotation guidance in practice, and whether the benefits persist at larger model scales or with more complex multi-dimensional preference structures.
