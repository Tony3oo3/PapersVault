## Problem

Estimating heterogeneous treatment effects (HTE) in right-censored survival data is challenging because existing methods like Causal Survival Forest (CSF) rely on modeling the censoring distribution, which becomes unreliable under heavy censoring or when censoring depends on unobserved covariates. Additionally, no prior nonparametric approach handles HTE estimation in survival data with unobserved confounders corrected via instrumental variables.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251755/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes tabular observations consisting of p baseline covariates, a binary treatment indicator, a right-censored survival time, and an event indicator; in the IV variant, a binary instrumental variable is added. The output is a point estimate of the conditional average treatment effect τ(x) = E[g(T⁰) − g(T¹) | X = x] for a user-specified transformation g (e.g., restricted mean survival time or survival probability at horizon h), along with a variance estimate combining within-imputation and between-imputation components via Rubin's rule. The estimand is defined on a finite horizon h, so observations reaching h contribute no missing information regardless of censoring.

## Method

MISTR avoids modeling the censoring mechanism entirely by using Recursively Imputed Survival Trees (RIST) to impute event times for censored observations. Specifically, M extremely randomized survival trees are trained to estimate the conditional survival distribution Pr(T̃ > t | X, W, T̃ > C) for each censored observation; this is refined over Q recursive imputation rounds. From the final RIST_Q model, A independent event times are sampled per censored observation, producing A complete (uncensored) datasets. A causal forest is then trained on each imputed dataset using the doubly-robust estimating equation, and the final HTE estimate is the average across all A forests, with variance aggregated via Rubin's rule. For the IV extension (MISTR-IV), the estimating equation is modified to use the instrument Z in place of the treatment W, and the same imputation procedure is applied unchanged, yielding the first nonparametric HTE estimator for survival data with unobserved confounding.

## Results

In the IV simulation settings (Table 3), MISTR-IV achieves MAE of 11.40–21.12 across Settings 200–204, substantially outperforming IPCW-IV (MAE 26.28–54.69) and the unconfounded methods CSF/MISTR (MAE 13.37–29.17) which are biased under unobserved confounding. On the MIMIC semi-simulated benchmark (Table 2), MISTR reduces MSE from 8.741 to 6.262 at 89.5% censoring (MIMIC 1) versus CSF, while matching CSF at lower censoring (5.494 vs 5.474 at 69.9%). On the HIV ACTG 175 dataset with added censoring (Table 4), MISTR achieves MSE 0.777–1.209 versus CSF's 1.320–1.875 across the three treatment comparisons.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CSF (Cui et al., 2023), MISTR matches performance in low-censoring settings (1–3, MIMIC 5) but clearly wins under high censoring: in Settings 8–9 (~92% censoring) MISTR achieves MSE 0.606–0.851 vs CSF's 0.924–1.289 on the Random test set, and on MIMIC 1 (89.5% censoring) MISTR's MSE is 6.262 vs CSF's 8.741. Against IPCW (Robins et al., 1994), the paper notes IPCW becomes inapplicable when censoring probability reaches very low values (Setting 6), where MISTR remains valid. Against IPCW-IV, MISTR-IV dominates across all nine IV settings, with the gap widening under heavier censoring: in Setting 204 (88% censoring), MISTR-IV achieves MAE 11.40 versus IPCW-IV's 32.10, and in the weakest-instrument variant 204-b, the gap is 15.18 vs 54.69. In the unconfounded IV settings, CSF and MISTR produce biased estimates (since they ignore the unobserved confounder U), confirming that MISTR-IV is necessary for valid inference under unobserved confounding.

## Relation to Prior Work

MISTR builds on two key prior threads: the Causal Survival Forest (CSF) of Cui et al. (2023), which combines causal forests with doubly-robust IPCW estimation for HTE in censored data, and the Recursively Imputed Survival Trees (RIST) of Zhu & Kosorok (2012), which handle censoring via iterative imputation without explicitly modeling the censoring distribution. The paper's conceptual departure is to decouple censoring handling from the causal estimation step: rather than up-weighting uncensored observations (as IPCW/CSF do), MISTR imputes event times via RIST and then applies standard causal forest machinery to the completed datasets, repeated A times and combined via Rubin's rule. This modularity also enables a straightforward extension to the instrumental-variable setting (MISTR-IV), which CSF and IPCW-based methods cannot accommodate.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MISTR is, to the authors' knowledge, the first nonparametric method for HTE estimation in survival data with unobserved confounders handled via instrumental variables, filling a gap where prior IV-survival methods were parametric (Cox-based, additive hazard) and did not target heterogeneous effects. The practical impact is demonstrated on the ACTG-175 HIV trial (75.6% censoring) and the Illinois unemployment experiments, where MISTR-IV identifies distinct subpopulations benefiting from treatment that confounded methods miss. Open questions include the computational cost of A=200 imputed causal forests (each with 2000 trees), the finite-horizon assumption (A.1) that limits applicability to unbounded survival times, and whether the monotonicity assumption (B.5) in the IV setting is too restrictive for real-world compliance patterns.
