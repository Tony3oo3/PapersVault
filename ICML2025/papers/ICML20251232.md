## Problem

Existing Decision Transformer-based multi-task RL methods degrade sharply as the number of tasks grows beyond a few dozen, and naively scaling model width fails to offset this degradation because gradient conflicts among tasks saturate shared parameters. No prior approach demonstrates effective task scalability to 160+ heterogeneous control tasks while simultaneously unlocking parameter scalability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251232/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The agent consumes a short K-step trajectory prompt (state, action, return-to-go tuples) concatenated with a K-step context window, where states and actions are zero-padded to 39 and 8 dimensions respectively to unify 160 heterogeneous tasks spanning Meta-World, DMControl, Ant, and Cheetah. At inference, the router dynamically weights expert outputs based on hidden states without requiring explicit task IDs, and the prediction head outputs a continuous control action vector with invalid padded dimensions masked out.

## Method

M3DT augments a Prompt-DT backbone with Mixture-of-Experts modules (experts identical to the FFN, plus an MLP router) in every transformer block, preserving the original FFN to retain shared knowledge while experts specialize in task-specific subgroups. The critical innovation is a three-stage training mechanism: (1) train the backbone on all tasks but stop early—just before gradient conflicts peak—to capture shared knowledge without overfitting to dominant tasks; (2) partition tasks into small subsets via random or gradient-based grouping (using agreement vectors + K-means), then train each expert independently on its subset with the backbone frozen; (3) train only the router on all tasks with backbone and experts frozen, enabling dynamic expert weighting. The paper shows that the MoE architecture alone is insufficient—end-to-end training still suffers severe gradient conflicts—and that the explicit task-to-expert assignment combined with sequential module training is what unlocks both parameter scalability (more experts → more parameters with less task load each) and task scalability (160 tasks with only 12.3% performance drop vs. ~20% for baselines).

## Results

On 160 continuous control tasks spanning Meta-World, DMControl, and Mujoco Locomotion, M3DT-Gradient achieves a normalized score of 78.21±0.47 with 174.12M parameters, outperforming the best baseline (HarmoDT-Large, 72.80±2.89) by 7.5%. At 80 tasks, M3DT-Gradient scores 80.66±0.97 (98.37M params), a 6.6% improvement over the best baseline (PromptDT-Large, 75.70±1.62). Notably, M3DT's performance degrades only 12.3% when scaling from 10 to 160 tasks, compared to ~20% for all baselines, and M3DT on 160 tasks outperforms every baseline on 80 tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

M3DT-Gradient wins across all three task scales against every baseline. At 10 tasks, it edges out MTDT-Large (88.92) by 0.31 points while using only 47.87M vs. 173.30M parameters. At 80 tasks, M3DT-Gradient (80.66) surpasses PromptDT-Large (75.70) by 4.96 points and HarmoDT-Large (75.56) by 5.10 points, with roughly half the parameters. At 160 tasks, the gap widens further: M3DT-Gradient (78.21) beats HarmoDT-Large (72.80) by 5.41 points and PromptDT-Large (71.65) by 6.56 points. The advantage grows with task scale, confirming that M3DT's MoE-based parameter separation and three-stage training specifically address the gradient conflict bottleneck that limits shared-parameter baselines. M3DT-Random (random task grouping) already outperforms all baselines at every scale, indicating that the core benefit comes from parameter separation and reduced per-expert task load rather than the specific grouping strategy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Prompt-DT (Xu et al., 2022) as its backbone architecture and borrows the agreement vector concept from HarmoDT (Hu et al., 2024) for gradient-based task grouping. It departs from the broader MTRL literature (Multi-Game DT, Gato, HarmoDT) by demonstrating that the standard approach of naively scaling Transformer width to handle more tasks rapidly hits a performance ceiling, and that simply adding MoE layers without a structured training protocol actually worsens gradient conflicts. The key conceptual departure is reframing the problem: rather than scaling shared parameters to absorb more tasks, M3DT separates parameters into task-specialized experts and reduces the effective task load per parameter subset, unlocking a different axis of scalability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that 160-task offline MTRL is achievable with superior performance, extending the practical frontier well beyond the typical 10–50 task range in prior work. The three-stage training mechanism (backbone → grouped experts → router) is a practical recipe for training MoE in RL that avoids the instability of end-to-end MoE training, and the finding that random grouping nearly matches gradient-based grouping at small scales simplifies deployment. Open questions remain around held-out task generalization, continual learning, and the inference cost of activating all experts; the authors note that Top-K sparsity fails to scale, leaving practical deployment at very large expert counts unresolved.
