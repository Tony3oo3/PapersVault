## Problem

Meta-parameters such as step sizes in machine learning are typically found via expensive search procedures (grid search, trial-and-error) that cost far more than training itself, and existing automatic adaptation methods (e.g., hypergradient descent) only minimize immediate loss while ignoring the long-term impact of step-size choices on future losses. This paper addresses both gaps by formulating step-size optimization as minimizing a discounted sum of future losses (a regret objective) and providing a causal, on-the-fly algorithm to do so.

## Input / Output

At each iteration the method consumes the gradient of the current loss, the current weight vector (plus internal optimizer state like momentum), and the current meta-parameter vector β (which maps to step sizes α via an exponential function σ(β)=exp(β) to guarantee positivity). It outputs updated weights, updated step sizes, and an eligibility-trace matrix H that encodes the cumulative discounted influence of all past β values on the current weights. The framework supports scalar, blockwise (per-layer), or per-weight step sizes, and the H matrix is maintained incrementally via a compact matrix product G_t rather than recomputed from scratch.

## Method

MetaOptimize converts the intractable forward-view gradient of the discounted future-loss regret into a causal backward-view update by borrowing the eligibility-trace mechanism from reinforcement learning, yielding the surrogate gradient H_t^T ∇f_t(w_t) where H_t is a discounted sum of Jacobians ∂w_t/∂β_τ. The H matrix is updated incrementally each step via a block matrix G_t whose entries are the partial derivatives of the base and meta update rules, making the per-step cost comparable to a few extra vector operations under Hessian-free approximations. Two key approximations reduce complexity: the 2×2 approximation drops the Q_t block (derivatives of H w.r.t. past β), and the L-approximation further zeros a cross-term in Y_t, recovering IDBD as a special case. The framework is fully modular—any first-order base optimizer (SGD, AdamW, Lion) can be paired with any first-order meta optimizer (SGD, Adam, Lion)—and Hessian-free variants (e.g., using Lion's sign function) eliminate second-order computations entirely while matching or exceeding state-of-the-art step-size adaptation methods on CIFAR-10/100, ImageNet, and TinyStories.

## Results

The paper primarily reports learning curves rather than single final-accuracy numbers. On CIFAR-10 (ResNet-18, batch 100), MetaOptimize outperforms its corresponding fixed-step-size baseline in every tested (base, meta) combination, including blockwise variants. On non-stationary CIFAR-100 (10 sequential tasks, batch 1), MetaOptimize with blockwise step-sizes consistently achieves higher cumulative top-1 accuracy than AdamW with the best fixed step-size. On ImageNet (ResNet-18, batch 256) and TinyStories (15M-param LM, batch 128), MetaOptimize outperforms four state-of-the-art step-size adaptation methods (DoG, gdtuo, Prodigy, mechanic) and fixed-step-size AdamW/Lion, with the only exception being a well-tuned cosine-decay scheduler on TinyStories within 30k iterations. The computational overhead of MetaOptimize (AdamW, Lion) is +44% wall-clock time and 33% GPU memory on ImageNet, and +13% time with 0% extra memory on TinyStories.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MetaOptimize outperforms all four state-of-the-art step-size adaptation methods—DoG, gdtuo, Prodigy, and mechanic—on both ImageNet and TinyStories learning curves, while incurring comparable or lower computational overhead (e.g., DoG adds +268% time on TinyStories vs. MetaOptimize's +13%; gdtuo adds +85% time and 64% memory on ImageNet vs. MetaOptimize's +44%/33%). Against fixed-step-size AdamW, MetaOptimize wins on every dataset tested (CIFAR-10, non-stationary CIFAR-100, ImageNet, TinyStories), with the notable exception that a well-tuned cosine-decay AdamW scheduler matches or slightly beats MetaOptimize on TinyStories within 30k iterations. Against fixed-step-size Lion, MetaOptimize (Lion, Lion) outperforms the baseline on CIFAR-10 and TinyStories. The paper also demonstrates that MetaOptimize is remarkably robust to initial step-size choices, maintaining strong performance even when initialized one to two orders of magnitude below the optimal fixed step-size, a property the fixed-step-size baselines do not share.

## Relation to Prior Work

MetaOptimize unifies and generalizes two previously separate lines of step-size optimization: the IDBD family (Sutton, 1992; Xu et al., 2018), which the paper shows is recovered under the L-approximation with SGD base/meta updates, and hypergradient descent (Baydin et al., 2017), which emerges as the γ=0 special case. The key conceptual departure is the adoption of an eligibility-trace-style backward view from reinforcement learning (Sutton, 1988) to make a forward-view gradient over a discounted sum of future losses computable causally, and the explicit modeling of meta-parameter dynamics (how changes in β_t propagate to future β values via the Y_t term). Unlike prior methods that minimize immediate loss or rely on implicit assumptions about step-size dynamics, MetaOptimize provides a general framework that wraps around any first-order base optimizer (SGD, Adam, Lion, etc.) with any first-order meta optimizer, with practical Hessian-free approximations that keep overhead low.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses a persistent practical pain point—expensive and often suboptimal hyperparameter search—by adapting step sizes on the fly with minimal overhead (13–44% wall-clock time, 0–33% memory over AdamW), and is particularly well-suited to continual learning where static schedules fail. Open questions remain: Hessian-based variants could offer superior performance but face noise and non-differentiability challenges; more accurate backward approximations (analogous to Dutch traces in RL) are needed for larger meta-step-sizes; and the framework has not yet been extended to discrete meta-parameters (batch size, architecture choices) or to per-weight step sizes at scale. The finding that a single default meta-step-size η=10^{-3} works universally across tasks without tuning is practically significant, reducing the meta-meta-parameter burden that plagues competing methods.
