## Problem

Existing clinical AI benchmarks and models are confined to a narrow set of modalities (primarily 2D images and text), preventing the development of holistic multimodal foundation models that can integrate the full spectrum of clinical data—imaging, temporal signals, graphs, and language—that clinicians routinely combine for patient assessment. No public benchmark unifies these modalities at sufficient scale and diversity to support large-scale multimodal pretraining and evaluation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250460/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

CLIMB accepts heterogeneous clinical inputs spanning 2D images (X-rays, dermoscopy, fundus, pathology), 3D volumes and video (CT, MRI, ultrasound, endoscopy), 1D time series (ECG, EEG, EHR, gait, genomics), graphs (brain networks, molecules), and multimodal combinations, totaling 4.51M samples across 19.01 TB. All tasks are unified into a single multi-label classification framework over a merged diagnostic vocabulary (e.g., 'Lung Opacity' from CheXpert and 'Infiltration' from VinDr-CXR are consolidated), or alternatively posed as closed-choice multiple-choice QA (CLIMB-QA) for LVLM evaluation. Outputs are disease/diagnostic labels from this unified vocabulary, patient outcome predictions (LOS, mortality), or QA answers.

## Method

CLIMB introduces a data collection and preprocessing pipeline that standardizes 44 public datasets from 33 medical institutions into a unified format while preserving natural missing-data patterns, then evaluates three research questions through controlled experiments. For multitask pretraining (RQ1), a single encoder per modality is trained jointly on all tasks within that modality using a shared classification head over the aggregated label vocabulary, comparing clinical-specific encoders (MedViT, PMC-CLIP, RAD-DINO, ECG-JEPA, BIOT) against general-domain architectures (ConvNeXTv2, Swin Transformer, EVA-2, InternViT, UniTS). For few-shot transfer (RQ2), pretrained models are fine-tuned on 1/8/32 samples from out-of-distribution datasets to measure generalization. For multimodal fusion (RQ3), unimodal encoders pretrained on CLIMB are combined on MIMIC-IV using three strategies of increasing cross-modal interaction—late fusion, MLP concatenation, and cross-attention—evaluated on LOS regression and 48-hour mortality prediction. Key findings: general-domain architectures outperform clinical-specific ones (ConvNeXTv2 achieves 35.9% improvement over MedViT), multitask pretraining yields the largest gains on understudied modalities (up to 32.54% AUC on COVID ultrasound), and fusion strategy should match task complexity (cross-attention for regression, MLP for classification).

## Results

Across 6 imaging modalities (Chest X-ray, Mammography, Dermoscopy, CT, Fundus, Ultrasound), ConvNeXTv2 achieves the best overall balanced AUC of 0.787 (Sen 0.537, Spe 0.806), outperforming the best clinical-specific encoder RAD-DINO (0.681) by 15.4% and MedViT (0.579) by 35.9%. Multitask pretraining on CLIMB yields up to 32.54% AUC gain on COVID ultrasound and 23 percentage-point AUC improvement on ECG (0.474→0.704) over single-task training. In few-shot transfer, CLIMB-pretrained models achieve up to 29% AUC improvement in ultrasound and 23% in ECG versus standard pretraining. For multimodal fusion on MIMIC-IV, CLIMB-pretrained encoders with cross-attention achieve 2.61 MAE on length-of-stay prediction (vs. 2.77 for SoTA-pretrained) and 0.672 AUC in 8-shot mortality prediction (vs. 0.556).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ConvNeXTv2 (general-domain) decisively outperforms all three clinical-specific encoders: it beats MedViT by 35.9% overall AUC (0.787 vs 0.579), PMC-CLIP by 24.1% (0.787 vs 0.635), and RAD-DINO by 15.4% (0.787 vs 0.681), with the largest gaps on Dermoscopy (0.901 vs 0.717) and Fundus (0.873 vs 0.606). Among general-domain encoders, ConvNeXTv2 edges out InternViT (0.787 vs 0.772) and Swin Transformer (0.787 vs 0.765), while EVA-2 underperforms despite strong CXR AUC (0.863) due to weak Fundus (0.780) and Ultrasound (0.462) scores. In the EEG domain, BIOT-pretrain variants (e.g., BIOT-pretrain-SHHS+PREST, overall AUC 0.870) surpass all from-scratch baselines (SPARCNet 0.838, CNNTransformer 0.854, FFCL 0.839), but training on more pretraining datasets does not guarantee gains. For ECG, the specialized ECG-JEPA outperforms the general-purpose UniTS by 36.8%, reversing the vision-domain pattern where general models win. In multimodal fusion on MIMIC-IV, CLIMB-pretrained encoders with MLP fusion achieve 0.961 AUC on 48-hour in-hospital mortality (full fine-tuning) versus 0.957 for SoTA-pretrained, and with cross-attention achieve 2.61 MAE on LOS prediction versus 2.77. Fine-tuned LLaVA-Med (55.5% average accuracy on CLIMB-QA) still trails CLIMB-ConvNeXTv2 by 32.2 percentage points, indicating that current clinical VLMs remain substantially behind dedicated vision encoders on structured diagnostic tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

CLIMB extends the line of unified clinical benchmarks (BenchMD, CARES, GMAI-MMBench, PMC-VQA) by incorporating time series (ECG, EEG, genomics, gait) and graph data (brain networks, molecules) alongside traditional 2D/3D imaging and text, achieving the widest modality coverage to date. Rather than proposing a new architecture, the paper repurposes existing general-domain encoders (ConvNeXTv2, Swin Transformer, EVA-2, InternViT), clinical-specific encoders (MedViT, PMC-CLIP, RAD-DINO), and domain-specialized models (ECG-JEPA, BIOT, SPARCNet) under a unified multitask pretraining protocol. The multimodal fusion experiments build on MIMIC-IV and standard fusion strategies (late, MLP, cross-attention), while the LVLM comparison positions CLIMB-QA as a controlled evaluation setting for models like LLaVA-Med.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As the largest public multimodal clinical benchmark (4.51M samples, 19.01 TB, 15 modalities, 13 domains), CLIMB provides the data foundation needed to train and evaluate holistic clinical foundation models that go beyond the image-and-text limitation of prior work. The practical finding that general-domain architectures outperform clinical-specific ones in multitask settings, and that LVLMs still lag behind dedicated encoders by large margins, offers concrete guidance for architecture selection. Open questions remain: whether the general>clinical gap persists with larger clinical pretraining corpora, how to design fusion mechanisms that adapt to task complexity, and whether the benchmark's public-data-only constraint limits representativeness for real-world deployment.
