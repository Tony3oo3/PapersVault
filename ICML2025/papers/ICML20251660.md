## Problem

Graph Transformers are widely regarded as superior to message-passing GNNs for graph-level tasks, with the quadratic attention mechanism believed to be essential for capturing long-range dependencies and mitigating over-smoothing. However, prior work showing that classic GNNs can match GTs has been limited to node-level tasks, and the only graph-level study (Tönshoff et al., 2023) found that even well-tuned GNNs still lag behind GTs. This paper asks whether the true potential of classic GNNs for graph-level tasks has been systematically underestimated.

## Input / Output

The method consumes graphs with node feature matrices (X ∈ R^{|V|×d_V}) and edge feature matrices (E ∈ R^{|E|×d_E}), augmented with Random Walk Structural Encoding (RWSE) concatenated to node features before the first layer. A graph readout function (mean, sum, or max pooling) aggregates final-layer node representations into a single graph embedding, which is passed through a prediction head to yield a classification label, regression value, or per-node label depending on the task. The framework is evaluated across heterogeneous graph types—molecular graphs, image superpixel graphs, protein-protein association networks, code abstract syntax trees, and synthetic SBM graphs—spanning 5,000 to 452,741 graphs per dataset.

## Method

The GNN⁺ framework systematically integrates six established techniques—edge feature integration, batch normalization, dropout, residual connections, a two-layer feed-forward network, and RWSE positional encoding—into the message-passing layers of three classic GNNs (GCN, GIN, GatedGCN). Each technique is treated as a tunable hyperparameter (on/off or rate), and residual connections enable stable training of deeper networks (3–20 layers) that would otherwise suffer from over-smoothing. The per-layer computation follows a fixed pipeline: neighborhood aggregation with edge features → batch normalization → activation → dropout → residual addition → FFN, with RWSE injected at the input. Evaluated on 14 datasets from GNN Benchmark, LRGB, and OGB with matched hyperparameter search spaces, the enhanced GNNs consistently rank in the top three across all datasets (first in eight) while running several times faster than GraphGPS, demonstrating that the architectural complexity of GTs is not necessary for competitive graph-level performance.

## Results

Across 14 graph-level datasets spanning GNN Benchmark, LRGB, and OGB, the enhanced classic GNNs (GCN+, GIN+, GatedGCN+) consistently achieve top-three rankings, securing first place in 8 of 14 datasets. On the GNN Benchmark, GatedGCN+ attains 98.71% accuracy on MNIST and 77.22% on CIFAR-10, surpassing all Graph Transformer baselines. On LRGB, GCN+ achieves the best Peptides-func Avg. Precision (0.7261) and Peptides-struct MAE (0.2421), while GatedGCN+ leads on MalNet-Tiny (94.60% accuracy). On OGB, GatedGCN+ ranks first on ogbg-ppa (82.58% accuracy) and ogbg-molpcba (0.2981 Avg. Precision), and is competitive on ogbg-molhiv (0.8040 AUROC) and ogbg-code2 (0.1896 F1). Training time is substantially lower: e.g., GCN+ takes 7s/epoch on ZINC vs. 21s for GraphGPS, and 476s vs. 1919s on ogbg-code2.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GatedGCN+ outperforms all Graph Transformer baselines on MNIST (98.71% vs. GEAET 98.51%, GraphViT 98.46%) and CIFAR-10 (77.22% vs. GRED 76.85%, GEAET 76.63%), and on MalNet-Tiny (94.60% vs. GSSC 94.06%, Exphormer 94.02%). GCN+ achieves the best results on Peptides-func (0.7261 Avg. Precision vs. GRED 0.7133, DRew 0.7150) and Peptides-struct (0.2421 MAE vs. GraphViT 0.2449, GPNN 0.2454). On OGB, GatedGCN+ leads on ogbg-ppa (82.58% vs. GIN+ 81.07%, GCN+ 80.77%) and ogbg-molpcba (0.2981 vs. Specformer 0.2972, EGT-pretrained 0.2961), and matches GSSC on ogbg-molhiv (0.8040 vs. 0.8035). However, GatedGCN+ trails the best GTs on ZINC (0.077 vs. TIGT 0.057, GRIT 0.059), PATTERN (87.03% vs. GRIT 87.20%), CLUSTER (79.13% vs. EGT 79.23%), PascalVOC-SP (0.4263 vs. GSSC 0.4561), COCO-SP (0.3802 vs. Graph-Mamba 0.3960), and ogbg-code2 (0.1896 vs. GECO 0.1915). Overall, the enhanced GNNs match or exceed SOTA GTs on 8 of 14 datasets while running 2–4× faster per epoch.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the node-level benchmarking results of Luo et al. (2024b) to graph-level tasks, where Tönshoff et al. (2023) had previously shown that tuned classic GNNs still lag behind Graph Transformers on LRGB. It builds directly on three classic message-passing architectures (GCN, GIN, GatedGCN) and wraps them in a unified GNN+ framework that integrates six standard deep-learning techniques (edge features, batch normalization, dropout, residual connections, FFN, and RWSE positional encoding). The work positions itself against the prevailing narrative that global attention mechanisms in GTs are necessary for superior graph-level performance, arguing instead that the gap is largely an artifact of under-tuned GNN baselines rather than a fundamental architectural limitation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that simple GNNs with standard training techniques match or exceed SOTA GTs across 14 diverse datasets while running several times faster challenges the field's default assumption that graph-level tasks require Transformer architectures, potentially redirecting research effort toward understanding *why* these techniques close the gap rather than designing ever-more-complex attention mechanisms. The paper acknowledges two key limitations: the results are purely empirical with no theoretical explanation for the observed advantage, and current benchmarks may not capture the complexity of real-world graph problems, so the relative advantage could shift on more challenging datasets. Open questions include a formal expressiveness analysis of GNN+ and whether the findings generalize to truly long-range or heterogeneous graph settings beyond current benchmarks.
