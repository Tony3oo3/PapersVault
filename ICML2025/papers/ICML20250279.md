## Problem

Existing ML-for-mathematics resources focus on proof writing, toy problems, or single-problem applications, leaving a gap for accessible datasets that capture the open-ended conjecturing process professional mathematicians face when working through raw combinatorial data. No prior collection provides the broader ML community with research-level open problems structured for supervised learning, with the difficulty and open-endedness characteristic of active pure mathematics research.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250279/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each of the nine datasets pairs a discrete combinatorial input (permutations in one-line notation, integer partitions, Young tableaux as nested lists, lattice paths as 0-1 step sequences, quiver adjacency matrices, or n×(n−1) integer matrices) with a target that is either a regression value (character values, permutation descent sets) or a classification label (mHeight, cluster variable indicator, KL coefficient, structure constant, partial order type, mutation class, weaving pattern validity). Datasets are stored as plain-text files with one instance per line, scale from ~10k to ~10M examples, and are often heavily class-imbalanced (e.g., mHeight is dominated by value 0; Schubert structure constants are balanced by explicit subsampling of zero-valued entries).

## Method

The core contribution is the ACD Repo itself: nine datasets each pairing an open-ended research-level question in algebraic combinatorics with a well-defined ML task, generated using Sage and custom scripts. For empirical baselines, the authors train vanilla MLPs (ReLU, grid-searched depth/dimension/learning rate), decoder-only transformers, and logistic/linear regression under a unified 60-epoch protocol on a single A100, reporting accuracy or MSE with 95% confidence intervals over three seeds. They additionally evaluate LLMs (GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, o1-mini, Claude 3.5 Sonnet) via in-context learning (0–100 shots, optional chain-of-thought) and program synthesis (100 generated Python programs, best selected on test set). Two case studies illustrate the conjecture-generation workflow: (i) a message-passing GNN trained on quiver mutation classes, analyzed with latent-space clustering and PGExplainer to rediscover known characterization theorems, and (ii) LLM program synthesis on Schubert structure constants, where models inadvertently reverse-engineered the zero-sampling procedure, revealing a spurious parity correlation with permutation length.

## Results

Across the nine ACD Repo classification datasets, MLPs achieve the most consistent performance, reaching 99.8% on mHeight (n=10), 99.3% on Grassmannian cluster algebras, and 99.8% on Schubert polynomials (n=6), while transformers match or exceed MLPs on several tasks (e.g., 99.9% on weaving patterns n=7, 99.7% on mHeight n=10). On the two regression tasks, performance is dramatically worse: symmetric group character prediction yields MSE of ~2.7×10⁹ (MLP, n=18) versus a trivial mean-guessing baseline of 1.6×10¹⁰, and RSK permutation prediction achieves MSE of only 0.43 (MLP, n=8) versus 0.21 for the mean baseline, indicating the model barely outperforms guessing. LLM program synthesis achieves 100% accuracy on Schubert polynomial structure constants for n=4 (GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini) and 95.5% on mHeight (n=11), though some 100% results are artifacts of reverse-engineering the data sampling procedure.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external methods but instead benchmarks three off-the-shelf architectures (logistic regression, MLP, transformer) against trivial baselines (guessing the largest class or the training mean) across all nine datasets. MLPs consistently outperform logistic regression by 10–50 percentage points on most tasks and match or slightly exceed transformers, with the notable exception of lattice paths where transformers degrade sharply (65.3% at n=10 vs. 90.6% for MLP) and cluster algebra quivers where transformers lead (92.9% vs. 86.5% for MLP). On the two regression tasks (symmetric group characters, RSK), all narrow models perform poorly: MLP MSE for S₁₈ characters is 2.7×10⁹, only modestly better than the 1.6×10¹⁰ mean-guessing baseline, and RSK MLP MSE (0.43) is actually worse than the mean baseline (0.21) at n=8. LLMs via program synthesis (GPT-4o, o1-mini, Claude 3.5 Sonnet) achieve 94–100% on Schubert polynomials and 94–97% on mHeight, substantially outperforming in-context learning (50–97%), though some 100% results are attributed to the models reverse-engineering the zero-structure-constant sampling procedure rather than learning genuine mathematics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by providing the first structured suite of research-level algebraic combinatorics datasets designed for the broader ML community, filling a gap the authors identify between toy problems used in interpretability research and the single-problem focus of prior ML-for-mathematics work (Davies et al., 2021; Wagner, 2021; Coates et al., 2024). While related to neural algorithmic reasoning benchmarks (Veličković et al., 2022) in that both generate synthetic data from combinatorial rules, the ACD Repo explicitly targets open mathematical problems and conjecture generation rather than algorithmic reasoning. The case studies build directly on He et al. (2024) for GNN-based quiver mutation analysis and on the program synthesis paradigm of Romera-Paredes et al. (2024) and Novikov et al. (2025), but reframe these as prototypes for a general workflow rather than as contributions to a single problem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ACD Repo provides a much-needed bridge between the ML community and pure mathematics by packaging research-level open problems (e.g., Schubert polynomial structure constants, quiver mutation equivalence) into ML-friendly tasks with large-scale data, enabling non-experts to explore conjecture generation. A key open question the paper highlights is whether high predictive accuracy translates into extractable mathematical insight — the authors explicitly caution that standard metrics may be misleading and that interpretability analysis or program synthesis is needed to convert model performance into conjectures. Limitations include the narrow scope to algebraic combinatorics, the fact that several foundational tasks (RSK, symmetric group characters) remain unsolved by small models, and the acknowledged risk that models may exploit sampling artifacts rather than learn genuine mathematical structure.
