## Problem

Equivariant diffusion models (e.g., EDM, GeoLDM) have become the dominant approach for 3D molecule generation by enforcing SO(3)-equivariance in the denoising network, but this constraint limits architectural flexibility, scalability, and efficiency compared to non-equivariant backbones like transformers. The paper asks whether equivariance is truly necessary, noting that non-equivariant baselines (GDM, GraphLDM) have performed significantly worse, and seeks to close this gap without imposing equivariance constraints.

## Input / Output

The model takes a molecule size N (sampled from the training distribution) and random Gaussian noise in a latent space of dimension N×3 (coordinates) plus N×d' (features), and produces 3D atomic coordinates and atom types/charges for a complete molecule with explicit hydrogens. In conditional mode, target molecular properties are appended to each atom's latent representation. The latent diffusion output is decoded back to the original coordinate space via the autoencoder decoder, and translations are handled by restricting all representations to the zero-center-of-gravity subspace.

## Method

RADM constructs a rotationally aligned latent space in two stages. First, a vanilla GNN rotation network outputs an arbitrary 3×3 matrix per molecule, projected to SO(3) via SVD (R = U·diag(1,1,det(UVᵀ))·Vᵀ), and a non-equivariant autoencoder (EGNN encoder + non-equivariant GNN decoder) is trained jointly with the rotation network to minimize reconstruction error of the rotated molecule, learning sample-dependent orientations that make the latent distribution more learnable. Second, a fully non-equivariant latent diffusion model—either a non-equivariant GNN or a DiT transformer with adaptive layer normalization for time conditioning and attention masks for variable atom counts—is trained with standard noise-prediction loss over the aligned latent representations. The key insight is that aligning molecules into consistent orientations (analogous to how 3D vision benefits from ShapeNet's aligned data) removes the rotational ambiguity that makes non-equivariant learning difficult, enabling transformer-based backbones that are far more scalable and efficient than equivariant EGNNs.

## Results

On QM9, RADM_DiT-B achieves 98.5% atom stability, 87.3% molecule stability, 94.1% validity, and 91.7% validity×uniqueness, matching or exceeding the best equivariant baseline GeoLDM (98.9/89.4/93.8/92.7) on validity while trailing slightly on molecule stability. On the larger GEOM-Drugs dataset, RADM_DiT-B reaches 85.0% atom stability and 99.3% validity, outperforming GeoLDM (84.4%/99.3%) on atom stability while matching its validity. The smaller RADM_DiT-S variant already surpasses EDM on molecule stability (83.4% vs. 82.0%) and validity (92.5% vs. 91.9%) on QM9, and both RADM variants dramatically outperform all previously reported non-equivariant baselines (e.g., GraphLDM-aug at 78.7% molecule stability).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the strongest equivariant baseline GeoLDM, RADM_DiT-B trades a small loss in QM9 molecule stability (87.3% vs. 89.4%) for a gain in validity (94.1% vs. 93.8%), and on GEOM-Drugs it surpasses GeoLDM on atom stability (85.0% vs. 84.4%) while matching its validity (99.3%). Against EDM, RADM_DiT-S already wins on molecule stability (83.4% vs. 82.0%) and validity (92.5% vs. 91.9%), and RADM_DiT-B extends this lead to 87.3%/94.1%. Against EDM-bridge, RADM_DiT-B leads on every QM9 metric (87.3% vs. 84.6% molecule stability, 94.1% vs. 92.0% validity). Against the non-equivariant baselines GDM, GDM-aug, GraphLDM, and GraphLDM-aug, RADM achieves large margins—e.g., RADM_DiT-B reaches 87.3% molecule stability versus GraphLDM-aug's 78.7% and GDM-aug's 71.6%—demonstrating that learned rotational alignment is the key factor closing the gap between non-equivariant and equivariant models. Against ENF and G-SchNet, RADM is overwhelmingly superior on all reported metrics (e.g., 94.1% vs. 40.2% validity vs. ENF on QM9).

## Relation to Prior Work

RADM builds directly on the latent diffusion framework of GeoLDM (Xu et al., 2023), reusing its autoencoder architecture and noise schedule, and on EDM (Hoogeboom et al., 2022) for the diffusion training setup. The key conceptual departure is replacing the equivariant EGNN denoising network with a fully non-equivariant architecture (GNN or DiT) by first learning sample-dependent SO(3) rotations via an unsupervised autoencoder, thereby constructing an aligned latent space where equivariance is no longer required. This positions the work at the intersection of learned canonicalization (Kaba et al., 2023; Dym et al., 2024) and the transformer-for-diffusion trend (Peebles & Xie, 2023), but unlike concurrent equivariant-DiT approaches (Zhang et al., 2024; Joshi et al., 2025) that rely on data augmentation for stochastic equivariance, RADM achieves competitive performance without any augmentation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges the prevailing assumption that SE(3)-equivariant architectures are necessary for high-quality 3D molecule generation, demonstrating that a non-equivariant model with learned rotational alignment can match or exceed equivariant SOTA while being substantially more efficient (7× faster sampling with DiT-S). This opens the door to leveraging the broader transformer ecosystem (FlashAttention, DiT scaling laws) for molecular generation, which equivariant architectures have been largely excluded from. Open questions include whether the learned alignment generalizes to larger or more diverse molecular datasets beyond GEOM-Drugs, whether the two-stage training (autoencoder then diffusion) can be unified, and whether the modest remaining gap to GeoLDM on molecule stability can be closed with further tuning or scaling.
