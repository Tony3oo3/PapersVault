## Problem

Process Reward Models (PRMs) provide dense step-level feedback that outperforms sparse Outcome Reward Models (ORMs) in both best-of-N inference and RL training, but training a PRM requires annotating every intermediate step, typically via expensive MCTS-based look-ahead sampling that costs ~38.8× more FLOPs than ORM training. This paper asks whether a strong PRM can be obtained without any step-level labels at all, by simply training an ORM on cheaper response-level data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252943/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference, the model receives an instruction concatenated with the first t−1 steps of a response and outputs a scalar process reward for step t, computed as β·log(π_φ(y_t|y_<t)/π_ref(y_t|y_<t)). The cumulative sum of these per-step rewards up to step t yields the Q value q_φ^t, which is shown to equal β·log E_{π_ref(y|y_≤t)}[e^{r_φ(y)/β}]. The same model that was trained as an ORM on complete responses automatically functions as a PRM on partial responses with no architectural change or additional training pass.

## Method

The core contribution is a theoretical proposition (Prop. 3.1) showing that when the outcome reward is parameterized as the log-likelihood ratio r_φ(y) = β·log(π_φ(y)/π_ref(y)), the cumulative per-token log-likelihood ratio up to step t is exactly the exponential average of the outcome reward under the reference model, i.e., the Q value. This makes the process reward r_φ^t = q_φ^t − q_φ^{t−1} = β·log(π_φ(y_t|y_<t)/π_ref(y_t|y_<t)) available for free during standard ORM training. Crucially, the result is agnostic to the training objective: it holds for DPO, KTO, NCA, and cross-entropy loss alike, as long as the reward takes the log-likelihood-ratio form. Empirically, the authors train Implicit PRMs on 33K math instructions with 8 rollouts each and show that all four objectives outperform MCTS-based baselines (Math-Shepherd, AutoPSV) with less than 1/38th the training data, with CE loss being the most data-efficient and the reference model removable at inference when built from a strong pretrained model.

## Results

On MATH-500 best-of-N sampling, the Implicit PRM trained with DPO achieves the highest average accuracy of 50.4 across three generation models (Mistral-7B, Llama-3.1-8B, Llama-3.1-70B), followed by NCA (49.4) and CE (48.4). All four Implicit PRM variants outperform the reimplemented Math-Shepherd baseline (47.8) and AutoPSV (45.7). The CE variant is notably data-efficient, achieving competitive performance even with only one response per instruction, and requires 38.8× fewer FLOPs than Math-Shepherd for data collection and training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The Implicit PRM (DPO) achieves the strongest average accuracy of 50.4, outperforming the reimplemented Math-Shepherd (47.8) by 2.6 points and AutoPSV (45.7) by 4.7 points on MATH best-of-N. The CE variant (48.4) also beats both reimplemented baselines (by 0.6 and 2.7 respectively) while being trained on unpaired, imbalanced data without any step-level annotations. Among off-the-shelf models, the Implicit PRM (DPO) surpasses EurusRM-7B (46.9), SkyworkRM-Llama3.1-8B (46.8), ArmoRM-Llama3-8B (46.6), and the official Math-Shepherd-7B (45.6), though it is slightly below RLHFlow-8B variants (49.1) which were trained on different data. The key practical advantage is that the Implicit PRM requires 38.8× fewer FLOPs than Math-Shepherd for data collection and training, making it substantially more accessible while delivering stronger performance under the same compute budget.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the DPO reward parameterization (Rafailov et al., 2023) and the observation from Rafailov et al. (2024) that DPO implicitly learns a Q function, but generalizes both: the Q-function property is shown to hold for any training objective that uses the log-likelihood ratio form (KTO, NCA, CE), not just DPO. It departs from the dominant PRM training paradigm (Math-Shepherd, AutoPSV) that relies on expensive MCTS-based step annotation, arguing instead that step labels are entirely unnecessary when the reward is parameterized as a log-likelihood ratio. The work also connects to the broader preference-learning literature by showing that the implicit reward representation, previously used only for policy alignment, can be repurposed as a free process reward model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 38.8× FLOPs reduction over MCTS-based PRM training makes process reward models dramatically more accessible, potentially enabling PRM-based inference-time scaling and RL in settings where step-level annotation was previously infeasible. The finding that the reference model can be dropped at inference without accuracy loss removes the main practical objection to the log-likelihood-ratio parameterization, making the approach drop-in compatible with existing PRM serving pipelines. Open questions include whether the result generalizes beyond math reasoning to other domains, whether better step-label annotation methods could eventually complement (rather than replace) the implicit approach, and why PRM ability does not translate into improved policy performance (an observed trade-off that remains unexplained).
