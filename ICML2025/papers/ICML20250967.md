## Problem

Concept Bottleneck Models (CBMs) provide interpretable predictions through a human-readable concept layer, but existing work assumes clean, static data and concepts. In practice, trained CBMs frequently require edits—correcting mislabeled concept annotations, removing spurious concepts, or deleting poisoned/erroneous training samples—yet the only available remedy is prohibitively expensive full retraining, especially for large-scale models. No prior work has addressed efficient, retraining-free editing of CBMs.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a fully trained CBM (a ResNet-18-based concept predictor g mapping inputs to k binary concept scores, plus a single linear label predictor f mapping concepts to class logits), the original training dataset, and an edit specification identifying which concept labels to correct, which concepts to remove, or which data samples to delete. It outputs closed-form parameter updates for both g and f that approximate the retrained model, with no gradient descent or forward-backward passes over the full dataset required at edit time. The three edit levels differ in granularity: concept-label-level modifies individual (sample, concept) pairs, concept-level removes entire concept dimensions (changing the output dimensionality of g and input dimensionality of f), and data-level removes complete training samples.

## Method

ECBMs derive closed-form parameter updates via influence functions, exploiting the CBM's two-stage structure through a sequential editing procedure: first approximate the updated concept predictor g_e using a Newton step with the Hessian of the concept loss, then propagate the change to the label predictor f_e using the updated g_e as input. The key technical challenge at the concept level is that removing concepts changes parameter dimensions; the authors resolve this by embedding the reduced-dimension predictor into the original space with zero rows (mapping P), applying the influence function under a constrained subspace, then stripping the zero rows. For the data level, they introduce an intermediate label predictor to decouple the effect of removing samples from the effect of the changed concept predictor input, yielding a two-term correction (A_G + B_G). To handle the non-convex loss and avoid explicit Hessian inversion, they replace H_θ with a damped Fisher approximation (G_θ + δI) computed via EK-FAC, reducing per-layer cost to eigen-decompositions of Kronecker-factored matrices.

## Results

ECBM achieves F1 scores within 0.002–0.005 of full retraining (ground truth) across all three edit levels and datasets, while reducing runtime by approximately 100×. For example, on OAI at the concept-label level, ECBM reaches F1 0.8808 vs. 0.8825 for retraining, with runtime dropping from 297.77 min to 2.36 min. On CUB, the runtime reduction is even more dramatic: 85.56 min (retrain) → 0.65 min (ECBM) at the concept-label level. The EK-FAC acceleration (ECBM vs. CBM-IF) consistently improves F1 by 0.01–0.03 over the raw influence-function version while maintaining comparable speed.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares ECBM against two baselines: (1) Retrain, which serves as the ground truth by retraining the CBM from scratch after edits, and (2) CBM-IF, a direct influence-function implementation without EK-FAC acceleration. ECBM matches Retrain's F1 score within 0.002–0.005 across all nine edit-level/dataset combinations (e.g., 0.8808 vs. 0.8825 on OAI concept-label; 0.7963 vs. 0.7971 on CUB concept-label), while reducing runtime by roughly 100× (e.g., 2.36 min vs. 297.77 min on OAI). Compared to CBM-IF, ECBM consistently achieves higher F1 scores (by 0.01–0.03) at similar or slightly lower runtime, confirming that the EK-FAC Hessian approximation stabilizes the influence-function computation for non-convex losses. The gap between ECBM and Retrain remains under 0.005 F1 even when editing up to 10% of samples or removing up to 20 concepts, validating the first-order Taylor approximation under moderate edit ratios.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of Concept Bottleneck Models (Koh et al., 2020) and influence function theory (Koh & Liang, 2017), applying the latter to the former's composite two-stage predictor structure—a combination the authors note has been largely unexplored. While prior CBM research focused on annotation efficiency and the accuracy gap from the bottleneck layer, and while influence functions have been widely used for data debugging and unlearning in standard architectures, no prior work addressed how to edit a CBM's parameters in response to concept-label corrections, concept removal, or data deletion without full retraining. The EK-FAC acceleration (George et al., 2018) is repurposed here to handle the ill-conditioned Hessians that arise from the non-convex deep-network loss, bridging the optimization literature with the interpretability setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for domains where CBMs are deployed (medical imaging, finance) and where data corrections, privacy-driven deletions, or concept updates occur frequently—retraining a ResNet-18-based CBM takes hours, while ECBM completes edits in minutes with near-identical F1 scores. The RMIA experiments providing direct evidence of data-influence erasure strengthen the privacy argument beyond mere parameter perturbation. Key limitations include the first-order (Newton) approximation, which may degrade under large or sequential edits, and the reliance on a linear label predictor for the concept-level editing proof. Open questions include scalability to very large models, handling non-linear label predictors, and whether the theoretical error bounds (which require convexity assumptions) hold in practice for deeper architectures.
