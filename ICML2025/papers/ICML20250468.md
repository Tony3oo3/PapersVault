## Problem

Existing adversarial attacks on Temporal Graph Neural Networks (TGNNs) assume the attacker has complete knowledge of the graph, including future changes, which is unrealistic in live environments where attackers can only observe the graph up to the attack time. Under this limited-future-knowledge constraint, prior attacks fail because their perturbations decay as the graph evolves and cannot influence unseen future nodes. The paper asks: can adversarial effects be made to persist and spread through a dynamic graph without knowing what will happen after the attack?

## Input / Output

The attacker receives the dynamic graph events up to the attack timestamp, the TGNN model parameters (white-box), and a budget specifying the fraction of nodes to target (e.g., 5%). The output is a set of temporary fake events (one fake node connected by one fake edge per victim node) injected at the attack time and removed immediately afterward. The lasting effect is not the structural change but the induced 'frozen state' in victim nodes' memory vectors, which persists through subsequent graph updates and propagates to one-hop and two-hop neighbors over time.

## Method

MemFreezing exploits the memory-update mechanism of TGNNs (typically GRU-based) to push node memories into stable, unresponsive 'frozen states' that resist future updates. The key insight, supported by theoretical analysis of GRU update gates, is that a single node cannot self-freeze (the update gate cannot reach 1), but a connected group of nodes with similar memories can mutually lock each other via a cross-freezing loss that minimizes MSE between each node's updated memory and its ideal frozen state while also aligning root and support neighbors' memories. To affect unseen future nodes, the method simulates future neighbors using homophily (sampling existing neighbors with Gaussian noise plus zero-initialized new nodes) and applies a propagating loss that encourages the frozen node's memory to pull simulated neighbors' memories toward itself. The two-stage attack first greedily selects high-degree root nodes and their high-degree neighbors as support nodes, then solves for adversarial messages by jointly minimizing the freeze and propagation losses, producing fake events whose injected messages drive each victim's memory to the computed frozen state.

## Results

On the edge prediction task (accumulated accuracy; lower = more effective attack), MemFreezing's key advantage is persistence: while all baselines recover to near-vanilla levels by t₂₅–t₅₀, MemFreezing's impact grows over time. For example, on WIKI with TGN, vanilla accuracy is 0.93; at t₀, TDGIA drops it most (0.77) while MemFreezing is moderate (0.89), but by t₅₀, TDGIA/FN/Meta-h all recover to 0.93–0.94 while MemFreezing drops further to 0.75. Under defenses (Adv_train, Lip_reg), baseline attacks cause only ~1.1% accumulated accuracy drop by t₅₀, whereas MemFreezing causes over 10% drop. On the large-scale Wiki-Talk-Temporal dataset (1.14M nodes, 1% budget), MemFreezing maintains a 6–13 point accuracy gap at t₅₀ versus FakeNode's near-zero gap.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MemFreezing is compared against three static GNN attacks: FakeNode (Wang et al., 2018), TDGIA (Zou et al., 2021), and Meta-Attack Heuristic (Li et al., 2022). At the immediate attack timestamp t₀, TDGIA typically causes the largest accuracy drops (e.g., WIKI/TGN: 0.77 vs. 0.93 vanilla; REDDIT/TGN: 0.74 vs. 0.97), while MemFreezing is moderate (WIKI/TGN: 0.89; REDDIT/TGN: 0.75). However, by t₂₅ and t₅₀, all three baselines recover to within 0–2 points of vanilla accuracy, whereas MemFreezing's impact persists and often deepens (e.g., WIKI/TGN drops from 0.89 at t₀ to 0.75 at t₅₀; REDDIT/TGN from 0.75 to 0.80). This persistence advantage holds across all four TGNN models (TGN, JODIE, Dyrep, Roland) and all four datasets, though JODIE is somewhat more resilient due to its memory decay mechanism. Under defenses (Adv_train, Lip_reg, GNNGuard), the gap widens further: baselines recover to ~1.1% drop by t₅₀ while MemFreezing sustains >10% drop.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior TGNN adversarial attacks (Lee et al. 2024, Sharma et al. 2022/2023, Chen et al. 2021) that assume the attacker has full knowledge of the graph's future evolution, instead introducing a 'limited future knowledge' threat model where the attacker only observes the graph up to the attack timestamp. Conceptually, it repurposes the node memory update mechanism (GRU-based) in memory-based TGNNs (TGN, JODIE, DyRep, Roland) as the attack surface, shifting the objective from maximizing prediction loss to driving node memories into stable 'frozen states.' The cross-freezing mechanism—where groups of nodes mutually reinforce each other's frozen memories—is a novel design that has no direct analogue in static GNN attack literature, though it draws on the homophily principle for simulating future neighbors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work exposes a practically relevant vulnerability: in real-world dynamic graph systems (social media moderation, recommendation, fraud detection), attackers cannot wait to observe the full graph evolution before acting, yet MemFreezing shows that a single injection at the attack time can produce adversarial effects that *worsen* over subsequent timestamps rather than decaying. The finding that standard defenses (adversarial training, Lipschitz regularization, GNNGuard) provide only partial protection against memory-freezing attacks highlights a gap in the TGNN robustness literature. Open questions include whether models with explicit memory decay (like JODIE) can be generalized as a defense, whether the homophily-based future-neighbor simulation holds in highly heterogeneous or adversarially structured graphs, and whether the attack transfers to TGNNs with fundamentally different memory architectures (e.g., transformer-based temporal encoders).
