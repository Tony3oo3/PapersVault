## Problem

AI conferences increasingly enforce per-author submission limits, but the standard order-based desk-rejection mechanism can unfairly penalize early-career researchers whose papers are rejected solely because a prolific co-author exceeded the limit. This paper formally proves that an ideal system—rejecting only the non-compliant author's excess papers without harming innocent co-authors—is mathematically impossible when more than three authors are involved, and asks how to design a desk-rejection policy that minimizes this unavoidable unfairness.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input a set of n authors, m papers with their authorship relationships (encoded as a binary author-paper incidence matrix W ∈ {0,1}^{n×m}), and a per-author submission limit x. It produces a binary rejection vector r ∈ {0,1}^m (equivalently, a subset S of papers to keep) such that every author retains at most x papers. The optimization is performed over the structured matrix W with a diagonal scaling D = Diag(|P_1|, …, |P_n|), making the problem amenable to standard LP solvers at the scale of ~10^4 submissions typical of major conferences.

## Method

The paper defines an author-wise cost function c(a_i, S) = fraction of a_i's papers rejected, then introduces two fairness metrics: worst-case fairness ζ_worst = max_i c(a_i, S) and average fairness ζ_avg = (1/n)Σ_i c(a_i, S). It proves that minimizing ζ_worst is NP-hard via a reduction from Set Cover, while minimizing ζ_avg reduces to a linear program: max 1_n^T D^{-1} Wr subject to Wr/x ≤ 1_n and r ∈ [0,1]^m. A key theoretical result (Theorem 5.16) shows the LP relaxation has an optimal solution that is integral, so the relaxed LP yields the exact optimum of the original integer program. The resulting Algorithm 1 builds W and D from the submission data, calls any off-the-shelf LP solver (e.g., PuLP), and returns the kept-paper set, achieving a 1/3 to 1/2 reduction in average fairness cost compared to the conventional order-based rejection used by CVPR 2025 on ICLR'21–'23 data.

## Results

The proposed fairness-aware desk-rejection algorithm (Algorithm 1) consistently outperforms the conventional order-based desk-rejection (Algorithm 2, used by CVPR 2025) on average fairness ζ_avg across all three ICLR datasets (2021–2023) and all submission limits x ∈ {4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14}. The reduction in ζ_avg ranges from approximately 34% (x=4) to over 70% (x=14 on ICLR'22), with the paper reporting a typical improvement of roughly 1/3 to 1/2. For example, on ICLR'23 at x=10, the conventional method yields ζ_avg = 0.022 while the proposed method achieves 0.011, halving the unfairness cost. The paper also proves that optimizing worst-case fairness is NP-hard (via reduction from Set Cover), while average fairness optimization reduces to a linear program solvable in O*(m^2.37 log(m/δ)) time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's sole formal baseline is the conventional order-based desk-rejection algorithm (Algorithm 2) currently deployed by major conferences such as CVPR 2025, ICCV 2025, and KDD 2025, which rejects all papers submitted after an author's x-th registration. The proposed LP-based fairness-aware method (Algorithm 1) wins on every dataset and every submission limit tested, with no ties or losses. The advantage grows with tighter limits: at x=4 the improvement is ~34–36%, while at x=14 it reaches 56–71%. The paper also informally compares against a 'roulette' heuristic (randomly rejecting papers from non-compliant authors), showing that its expected fairness metrics are strictly worse than the LP solution (e.g., E[ζ_worst] ≤ 1/26 vs. the LP's exact 1/26 in the toy example, but with nonzero probability of the worst outcome). No other published fairness-aware desk-rejection baselines exist, as the authors note this is the first formal study of the problem.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by formally defining the paper submission limit problem in desk-rejection systems, a task the authors note has received essentially no prior formal treatment. Conceptually, it draws on the fairness-in-algorithms literature (Aziz et al. 2023 on group fairness in peer review; Mehrabi et al. 2021 on bias/fairness surveys) to motivate two fairness metrics, and on the Matthew effect literature (Bol et al. 2018; Wang et al. 2019) to frame the social equity concern. Methodologically, it repurposes standard optimization tools—integer programming, LP relaxation, and a Set Cover reduction for NP-hardness—rather than introducing new algorithmic machinery, positioning the contribution as a problem formulation and complexity analysis rather than a methodological advance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical relevance is direct: every major AI conference (CVPR, ICCV, AAAI, KDD, IJCAI, WSDM) now enforces per-author submission limits, and the paper demonstrates that the conventional order-based rejection used by CVPR 2025 can catastrophically penalize early-career co-authors. The impossibility result (no ideal desk-rejection for n≥3) and the NP-hardness of worst-case fairness optimization set fundamental limits on what any policy can achieve, while the LP-based average-fairness algorithm offers a drop-in replacement solvable in near-linear time. Open questions include how strategic author behavior (collaborator selection, submission timing) reshapes fairness at equilibrium, and how to balance worst-case and average fairness, which the paper shows can diverge (Example C.2).
