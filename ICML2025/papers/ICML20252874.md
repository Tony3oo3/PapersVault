## Problem

Prior work empirically observed that smaller parameter initialization scales bias Transformer-based LLMs toward learning compositional reasoning rules rather than memorizing input-output mappings, but the underlying mechanism—how the optimization process interacts with the Transformer architecture to produce this reasoning preference—remained unexplained. This paper addresses that gap by deriving a theoretical framework from training dynamics that identifies the specific role of token label distributions in shaping embedding-space structure during early training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252874/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on token sequences of fixed length (L=9 for synthetic tasks, variable-length text for real tasks) drawn from either synthetic composition tasks built with anchor functions (where sequences contain designated key tokens from Z={21,...,120} and anchor tokens from A_mem={1,...,10} or A_rsn={11,...,20}) or real datasets (PrOntoQA for reasoning, TinyStories for memorization). The model produces next-token predictions via cross-entropy loss, but the primary analytical output is the evolving embedding-space geometry—specifically cosine similarity structures among anchor embeddings and the principal-component organization of the full embedding matrix—revealed during early training epochs.

## Method

The core contribution is a gradient-flow analysis showing that under small initialization (γ>0.5), each token's embedding update is governed by the probability distribution of labels over all sequences containing that token (Propositions 1–3). For memory anchors, all tokens share an identical uniform label distribution over Z, so their embeddings remain highly similar and undifferentiated; for reasoning anchors, the additive structure of the label (z + Σa_i) causes the label distribution to shift with the anchor value, producing distinct embedding directions that differentiate rapidly. In the full Transformer, Lemma 1 shows the first attention matrix converges to a causal average operator under small initialization, and Theorem 2 demonstrates that W^V preferentially aligns with reasoning anchor embeddings, propagating their information to all subsequent positions. Theorem 1 provides a closed-form Gaussian approximation of reasoning anchor embeddings whose predicted cosine-similarity structure matches experimental observations, confirming that label-distribution heterogeneity is the fundamental driver of the reasoning bias.

## Results

The paper demonstrates that increasing the initialization rate γ (i.e., decreasing initialization scale) progressively shifts model behavior from memorization to reasoning. On the synthetic composition task, at γ=0.8 the losses on D_rsn,train and D_rsn,test decrease significantly faster than D_mem, while at γ=0.3 all three losses decrease at similar rates with D_rsn,test remaining nearly unchanged. On real language tasks (GPT-2 trained on PrOntoQA + TinyStories), the reasoning bias metric ΔL = (L_TinyStories − L_PrOntoQA)/L_PrOntoQA shows a clear upward trend as γ increases from 0.3 to 0.8. At step 5000 with γ=0.8, the average cosine similarity among PrOntoQA token embeddings is 0.123 versus 0.531 among TinyStories tokens, confirming that reasoning tokens become more differentiated in embedding space early in training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an analysis paper rather than a method-comparison study, so there are no external baselines to beat. The central 'comparison' is across initialization scales (γ = 0.3, 0.5, 0.8) applied to the same Transformer and GPT-2 architectures. At the standard LeCun/He scale (γ = 0.5), the model exhibits mixed behavior with no strong preference. At the small-init scale (γ = 0.8), the model clearly favors reasoning: on the synthetic task, D_rsn,test loss drops substantially while D_mem loss lags, and on real data, PrOntoQA loss decreases much faster than TinyStories loss. The paper further shows that this reasoning bias is mechanistically driven by label-distribution-dependent embedding differentiation: reasoning anchors acquire distinct, hierarchically structured embeddings early in training (cosine similarity decreases with |s_i − s_j|), while memory anchors remain highly aligned (cosine similarity stays high). A controlled experiment (Figure 8) confirms that even among purely memory tasks, those with anchor-dependent label distributions (F_mem,2) learn significantly faster than those with uniform random labels (F_mem,1), isolating label distribution as the causal factor.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper provides the theoretical mechanism underlying an empirical observation from Zhang et al. (NeurIPS 2024), who showed that small initialization biases Transformers toward reasoning while large initialization favors memorization on compositional tasks. It leverages the anchor function framework (Zhang et al., 2024b) to construct controlled synthetic tasks and connects to the neuron condensation literature (Luo et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2022) for the broader context of how initialization scale shapes training dynamics. The key departure from prior work is the identification of label distribution structure as the driving force: reasoning anchors have structurally shifted label distributions that cause their embeddings to differentiate rapidly, while memory anchors share identical uniform label distributions that prevent early differentiation. This reframes the reasoning bias from a condensation/complexity argument to an embedding-space dynamics argument.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a clean theoretical explanation for a previously empirical phenomenon, giving practitioners a mechanistic understanding of why initialization scale acts as a reasoning/memorization dial. The practical takeaway—that small initialization (γ > 0.5) should be preferred when reasoning capability is the goal—is actionable and simple. However, the theoretical analysis relies on the small-initialization limit (γ→∞), linear approximations, and specific synthetic task structures, leaving open whether the mechanism generalizes to deeper architectures, more complex reasoning tasks, or realistic pretraining regimes. The connection to real language tasks (PrOntoQA vs. TinyStories) remains qualitative, and the role of the second attention layer and positional embeddings in propagating key information is described but not fully formalized.
