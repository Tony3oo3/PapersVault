## Problem

Existing LLM safety approaches either require expensive model retraining (safe RLHF) or rely on brittle prompt-based defenses that are easily circumvented by adversarial attacks, and none provide an explicit, interpretable model of what makes a response unsafe. This paper asks whether safety can be captured as an explicit geometric structure in the model's representation space, enabling both detection and correction of unsafe outputs without modifying model weights.

## Input / Output

The method consumes text prompts (including adversarial jailbreak strings) and extracts intermediate layer activations (layer 20) from a frozen pre-trained LLM at the last token of each sentence. These activations are projected through a learned Concept Encoder into a 16,384-dimensional space where the polytope operates. At inference, the system produces safe text responses by steering activations back into the polytope during token generation, and can also output binary safe/unsafe classifications per facet, with each facet corresponding to a distinct semantic safety concept (e.g., violence, sexual content, bullying).

## Method

SaP frames LLM safety as a geometric constraint learning problem motivated by constrained Markov decision processes, representing the safe region as a convex polytope in the LLM's representation space. A Concept Encoder (linear layer + ReLU) with L1 sparsity regularization disentangles polysemantic activations into interpretable safety features, and the Convex Polytope Machine (CPM) loss learns K hyperplane facets with learnable thresholds from binary safe/unsafe labels via gradient descent. At inference, a representation steering algorithm (Algorithm 1) checks whether each token's activation satisfies all facet constraints; when violated, it solves a Lagrangian-relaxation optimization to project the activation back into the polytope while minimizing L1 perturbation, then completes the forward pass to decode the next token. This post-hoc mechanism preserves model capabilities (MMLU accuracy unchanged) while reducing adversarial attack success rates by orders of magnitude, and the learned facets naturally specialize to distinct semantic safety categories without explicit category supervision.

## Results

On HarmBench (9 attack methods), SaP reduces average Attack Success Rate (ASR) from 12.92% to 0.26% on Llama2-7B, from 55.77% to 3.25% on Ministral-8B, and from 27.57% to 11.81% on Qwen2-1.5B, while preserving MMLU accuracy (45.8%→45.7%, 63.4%→63.3%, 52.7%→52.7% respectively). On JailBreakBench's Adaptive Attack, SaP achieves 2.00% ASR on Llama2 (vs. 60% original) and 14.00% on Ministral (vs. 100% original). On BeaverTails safety classification, SaP with 50 facets achieves 83.96–91.24% test accuracy across 14 categories, comparable to an MLP with equivalent parameter count.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SaP substantially outperforms all prompt-based baselines (Self Reminder, Response Check, SmoothLLM) on defense: on Ministral-8B, SaP achieves 3.25% average ASR versus 18.59% (Self Reminder), 16.92% (Response Check), and 34.61% (SmoothLLM), while also preserving MMLU accuracy (63.3% vs. 22.9% for Self Reminder). Rejection Sampling achieves comparable or slightly better ASR on Llama2 (0.02% vs. 0.26%) but catastrophically degrades Ministral-8B's MMLU from 63.4% to 28.5%, indicating over-rejection of benign requests. The MLP and MLP+5 baselines, which use the same parameter count and steering procedure but are trained with binary cross-entropy rather than the polytope loss, achieve similar classification accuracy (Table 1: 97.28% vs. 91.24% on Llama2) yet produce dramatically worse defense (e.g., 27.10% ASR on GCG for MLP vs. 0.40% for SaP on Llama2), demonstrating that the geometric polytope structure—not just classification accuracy—is essential for effective steering. On Qwen2-1.5B, SaP and Rejection Sampling perform similarly (11.81% vs. 11.53% ASR), both limited by the Adaptive Attack which achieves 100% ASR across all methods on this model.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes three distinct research threads into a unified geometric framework for LLM safety: (1) constrained Markov decision processes (Altman, 1999; Lindner et al., 2024), which provide the theoretical justification that safety constraints can be learned from binary safe/unsafe demonstrations as linear mappings of feature expectations; (2) the Convex Polytope Machine (Kantchelian et al., 2014), which supplies the scalable gradient-based algorithm for learning polytope facets in high dimensions; and (3) constrained reinforcement learning (Dalal et al., 2018), which motivates the Lagrangian-relaxation-based representation steering at inference time. The key departure from prior LLM safety work is the explicit geometric formulation: rather than treating safety as a reward to optimize (RLHF) or a prompt-level pattern to filter, SaP models the safe region as a polytope in the model's internal representation space, enabling post-hoc enforcement without weight modification and providing interpretable facet-level diagnostics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The emergent semantic specialization of polytope facets—where individual facets learn to detect distinct safety concepts (kidnapping, sexual content, bullying) without explicit category supervision—offers a new lens for mechanistic interpretability of safety in LLMs, complementing sparse autoencoder approaches. The practical advantage of post-hoc deployment (no retraining, no prompt engineering) makes this approach attractive for production settings where models are already deployed. However, significant limitations remain: SaP induces semantically incoherent outputs under certain attacks (notably for Ministral-8B), the Adaptive Attack defense is weak for smaller models, many facets remain inactive during training suggesting inefficiency in the CPM loss, and the theoretical sample complexity bounds rely on strong assumptions (perfect separability, restricted encoder) that are hard to verify in practice. Open questions include whether non-linear geometric representations could capture more complex safety structure, and whether the heuristic facet assignment can be replaced with principled alternatives from mechanistic interpretability.
