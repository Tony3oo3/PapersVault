## Problem

Standard policy gradient theorems in reinforcement learning assume either deterministic finite or infinite time horizons, yet many practical problems (robot navigation, game termination, molecular dynamics hitting times) involve random stopping times that depend on the policy itself. Because the stopping time is policy-dependent, naively applying existing gradient formulas yields incorrect expressions, and no rigorous derivation for random time horizons in continuous state spaces had been provided. This paper closes that gap by deriving correct policy gradient formulas for both stochastic and deterministic policies under random, trajectory-dependent stopping times.

## Input / Output

The method operates on continuous state and action spaces (S ⊂ R^{d_s}, A ⊂ R^{d_a}) with a time-homogeneous transition density p, a reward function r, and a target set T that defines the random stopping time N as the first hitting time of T. The policy (parametrized by a neural network) maps states to actions, and the gradient computation additionally requires the expected runtime E_π[N+1] as a scaling factor. In the model-based deterministic case, the transition density must be known analytically (e.g., Gaussian from an Euler-Maruyama discretization), and the gradient uses the log-derivative of p with respect to the action rather than a learned Q-function.

## Method

The paper derives policy gradient theorems from two complementary perspectives: a trajectory perspective (summing log-policy gradients weighted by Q-values over the random-length trajectory) and a state-space perspective (expectations under the on-policy state-space density ρ^π, scaled by E_π[N+1]). For stochastic policies, the key result is that the gradient equals E_π[Σ_{n=0}^N ∇_θ log π_θ(S_n,A_n) Q^π(S_n,A_n)], and the state-space version introduces the expected runtime as a multiplicative factor that varies with the policy. For deterministic policies, a novel model-based gradient is derived that decomposes ∇_a Q^μ into ∇_a r + V^μ ∇_a log p, eliminating the need for a learned Q-function. The critical practical insight is that omitting the E_π[N+1] factor (as standard formulas do) creates an uncontrolled, policy-dependent effective learning rate that can be orders of magnitude off during optimization, leading to significantly slower convergence or instability. The paper further shows that the discounted infinite-horizon case is a special case of random time horizons with geometrically distributed stopping times, unifying the two settings.

## Results

Across all three experimental benchmarks (modified mountain car, two-joint reacher, and molecular dynamics importance sampling), the policy gradient formulas that correctly incorporate the expected stopping time E_π[N+1] as a scaling factor consistently outperform the biased state-space gradient that omits this factor. In the molecular dynamics experiment (d=20, multi-well potential), the correct trajectory and state-space DPG formulas converge roughly twice as fast as the biased state-space DPG. The biased method's effective learning rate increases significantly during optimization (e.g., from N≈10⁴ to N≈10² in the mountain car problem), causing slower convergence and, in some cases, memory failures due to uncontrolled trajectory length growth.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three gradient estimators: (1) trajectory-based PG (Eq. 15/22), (2) state-space PG with the correct E_π[N+1] scaling (Eq. 16), and (3) state-space PG without the scaling factor (Eq. 21, termed 'biased'). The trajectory PG and correct state-space PG yield nearly identical performance across all three benchmarks, differing only by statistical noise. The biased state-space PG consistently underperforms: in the mountain car problem it converges more slowly and eventually fails due to memory overflow from increasingly long trajectories; in the reacher environment it shows slower convergence to optimal performance; and in the molecular dynamics experiment it converges approximately half as fast. The key mechanism is that omitting E_π[N+1] introduces a policy-dependent effective learning rate that is too small early in optimization (when trajectories are long) and too large later (when trajectories are short), making the optimization schedule suboptimal and problem-specific in a way that cannot be corrected by a fixed learning rate.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the classical policy gradient framework (Sutton et al., 1999; Silver et al., 2014) from deterministic or infinite time horizons to trajectory-dependent random stopping times in continuous state-action spaces. It generalizes the discrete-space state-space analysis of Bojun (2020) to continuous settings and adds deterministic policy formulas, while also recovering and extending the continuous-time stochastic optimal control gradient of Ribera Borrell et al. (2024) to discrete time with non-Gaussian transitions. The key conceptual departure is recognizing that the policy-dependence of the stopping time N invalidates standard gradient derivations, requiring a careful treatment of the expected runtime as a policy-dependent scaling factor.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper corrects a gap in the standard RL framework: for any problem with random episode lengths (common in robotics, games, and molecular dynamics), the conventional policy gradient is technically incorrect and implicitly introduces a policy-dependent effective learning rate that can significantly slow convergence. The practical impact is demonstrated across three environments with roughly 2× speedup, and the connection to stochastic optimal control theory opens pathways for integrating these corrections into advanced algorithms like TRPO/PPO. Open questions include whether the corrections remain critical at scale, how they interact with off-policy methods and replay buffers, and whether continuous-time formulations yield further simplifications.
