## Problem

Model regularization requires extensive manual tuning through cross-validation to balance complexity against overfitting, and existing adaptive methods (variational dropout, Concrete Dropout) optimize proxy objectives on training data rather than directly targeting generalization performance. This paper addresses the gap by showing that regularization parameters can be directly optimized through validation gradients during a single training run, eliminating discrete hyperparameter search while provably recovering cross-validation optima.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a standard train-validation split where the validation set is actively used during training to provide gradient signals for regularization parameters (not merely for evaluation). For neural networks, noise is injected as additive Gaussian perturbations after LayerNorm with per-layer learnable scales (O(L) parameters total), and validation loss is computed via Monte Carlo averaging over K noise samples. The output is a single trained model with learned per-layer noise scales, standard predictions at test time (deterministic, ε=0), and calibrated uncertainty estimates obtained by averaging over multiple noise samples at inference.

## Method

Cross-regularization splits optimization into two parallel tracks: model parameters θ are updated via gradient descent on training loss, while regularization parameters ρ (weight norms, noise scales, augmentation magnitudes) are updated via gradient descent on validation loss, using alternating updates. For neural networks, the key implementation is additive Gaussian noise after LayerNorm with per-layer scales σ_l = exp(ρ_l), where training uses single noise samples for stochastic regularization and validation uses Monte Carlo averaging to measure expected generalization. The critical insight is that by directly optimizing the complexity control quantity (e.g., noise scale) rather than an indirect hyperparameter λ, validation gradients provide continuous feedback about generalization during training. Theoretical analysis establishes linear convergence of the alternating scheme, equivalence to optimal cross-validation, and a statistical rate of O(√(k/m)) that depends only on the number of regularization parameters k rather than total model dimension d.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with VGG-16, cross-regularization achieves 83.7% test accuracy in a single training run, matching Population-Based Training (83.7%) while substantially outperforming the unregularized baseline (76.0%). For uncertainty calibration, the method achieves an Expected Calibration Error (ECE) of 0.038 at 79.5% accuracy, competitive with a 5-member Deep Ensemble (ECE 0.030, 81.3%) and significantly better than Temperature Scaling (ECE 0.057) or fixed regularization (ECE 0.175). On SVHN, adaptive data augmentation improves test accuracy from 82.8% to 86.3% while reducing the generalization gap from 16.2% to 7.3%. Learned noise scales reach up to 13σ post-normalization (equivalent to ~99.4% sparsity), far exceeding PBT's conservative maximum of 2.8σ.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Population-Based Training (PBT), cross-regularization matches the 83.7% CIFAR-10 accuracy in a single training run versus PBT's population of 20 parallel models, requiring only O(T(1+K/r)) forward passes versus PBT's O(PT). PBT discovers more conservative noise levels (max 2.8σ) with more volatile adaptation dynamics, while cross-regularization's gradient-based optimization reveals higher functional noise (up to 13σ) with smooth, stable trajectories. For uncertainty calibration, cross-regularization (ECE 0.038, 79.5% acc.) substantially outperforms Temperature Scaling (ECE 0.057, 69.6% acc.) and fixed regularization (ECE 0.175, 74.7% acc.), achieving near-ensemble-level calibration without requiring multiple training runs. The 5-member Deep Ensemble achieves slightly better ECE (0.030) and accuracy (81.3%) but at 5× the training cost, making cross-regularization the most efficient calibrated method. The paper also notes that variational dropout (Molchanov et al., 2017) was attempted as a comparison but proved unsuitable for layer-wise noise adaptation, collapsing to zero noise or becoming unstable despite extensive hyperparameter tuning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the validation-gradient framework (Bengio, 2000; Luketina et al., 2016) by shifting the optimization target from indirect hyperparameters (e.g., λ) to the quantities they control (weight norms, noise scales), eliminating the need for inverse Hessians or parameter-history tracking. It departs from variational approaches (Molchanov et al., 2017) that learn regularization through training-data proxy objectives, instead using validation gradients directly to target generalization. The method also subsumes the single-run efficiency of gradient-based noise optimization (Noh et al., 2017) while adding the validation-guided signal that Noh et al. lacked, and contrasts with Population-Based Training (Jaderberg et al., 2017) by achieving comparable adaptation with O(T) rather than O(PT) forward passes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is a drop-in replacement for manual regularization tuning that maintains single-run efficiency while providing continuous generalization feedback, with demonstrated extensions to data augmentation, uncertainty calibration, and growing datasets. The discovery of architecture-specific noise patterns (e.g., ResNet concentrating extreme noise in layers bypassed by skip connections) offers a new lens on how network topology shapes information flow and regularization needs. Open questions include whether the extreme-noise regime (σ≈13, ~99.4% effective sparsity) generalizes beyond CIFAR-10-scale networks, whether the method scales to ImageNet-level training, and whether the automatic calibration property holds under distribution shift. The theoretical guarantees, while clean, rely on strong convexity in ρ that may not hold in practice for very deep networks.
