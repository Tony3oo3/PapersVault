## Problem

While the Risk Difference is well-studied in observational causal inference, the Risk Ratio—a complementary relative measure that can highlight effects the RD downplays—lacks robust estimators and theoretical guarantees in observational settings. No prior work proposed or analyzed (non)parametric estimators of the ATE measured as a Risk Ratio in observational data, nor derived their asymptotic properties, leaving a clear methodological gap for treatment effect assessment.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250192/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The estimators consume an i.i.d. observational dataset of (X, T, Y) triples along with nuisance function estimates: a propensity score e(X) = P(T=1|X) and outcome regression surfaces μ(t)(X) = E[Y(t)|X=x] for t∈{0,1}. They produce a point estimate of τ_RR = E[Y(1)]/E[Y(0)] and an asymptotic (1−α) confidence interval derived from the established asymptotic normality. All theoretical results hold for both binary and continuous outcomes, and the confidence intervals are constructed on the original scale (not log-scale), with variance estimated via plug-in or influence-function-based formulas.

## Method

The paper derives four RR estimators for observational studies—RR-IPW, RR-G, RR-OS (one-step), and RR-AIPW—using influence function theory and M-estimation, establishing asymptotic normality, efficiency, and confidence intervals for each. The key technical insight is that the non-linearity of the RR (a ratio of expectations) causes the two doubly-robust constructions to diverge: RR-OS arises from one-step bias correction of the influence function, while RR-AIPW is simply the ratio of two standard AIPW estimators applied to the numerator E[Y(1)] and denominator E[Y(0)]. Both are semiparametrically efficient (minimal asymptotic variance), but RR-AIPW achieves this under weaker regularity conditions—it requires only consistency of nuisance estimates (o(1) rates) rather than the product-rate conditions needed by RR-OS. Cross-fitting (K-fold partitioning) is used to avoid overfitting in the nuisance estimation, and the authors recommend RR-AIPW for its weaker assumptions, simpler implementation as a ratio of two AIPW estimators, and competitive empirical performance across linear, non-linear, and real-world settings.

## Results

Across simulated Linear/Logistic and Non-Linear/Logistic DGPs (3000 repetitions), the doubly-robust estimators RR-AIPW and RR-OS (with linear nuisance) are unbiased even at small sample sizes and converge fastest to the true RR, while RR-IPW exhibits excessively large variance and RR-N shows near-zero CI coverage in observational settings. For 95% asymptotic confidence intervals at n=1000 (300 repetitions), only Linear RR-AIPW achieves coverage above 95% in both DGP settings; OLS RR-G has shorter average CI length but slightly lower coverage. On the real-world Traumabase dataset (8,270 TBI patients, 17 confounders, 3000 subsampling repetitions), most estimators yield RR > 1 suggesting a deleterious effect of tranexamic acid on 28-day mortality, consistent with prior findings; Forest RR-IPW is the sole estimator indicating a beneficial effect, and RR-N produces the highest (most extreme) RR value.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary baseline is the Risk Ratio Neyman estimator (RR-N) from Cornfield (1956), a simple ratio of group means valid only under a constant propensity score (RCT). In observational settings, RR-N is fundamentally invalid: its 95% CI coverage drops to nearly zero in both simulated DGPs, and on the Traumabase it produces the most extreme RR estimate among all methods. The proposed RR-AIPW (ratio of two crossfitted AIPW estimators) dominates RR-N on every metric—bias, variance, and CI coverage—and matches RR-OS in point-estimate accuracy while requiring strictly weaker regularity assumptions (weak double robustness: consistency if either the outcome model or propensity score is correct, versus RR-OS which needs joint consistency of both or of (e, μ₀)). RR-IPW, despite being a natural extension of the well-studied IPW framework for RD, performs poorly due to inflated variance from extreme propensity scores, and its CIs are excessively wide. RR-G (G-formula) is competitive when the outcome model is correctly specified (e.g., linear DGP with linear regression) but can suffer irreducible asymptotic bias under model misspecification (e.g., linear G-formula on a non-linear DGP). Overall, RR-AIPW is recommended as the best estimator, combining the lowest asymptotic variance (semiparametric efficiency), the weakest assumptions, and the simplest implementation as a ratio of two standard AIPW estimators.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the well-developed semiparametric estimation toolkit for the Risk Difference (RD) — IPW (Hirano et al., 2003), G-formula (Robins, 1986), and AIPW (Robins et al., 1992) — to the Risk Ratio (RR) in observational studies, a setting the authors identify as a clear gap in the literature. The key technical departure is that RR is nonlinear, so the standard delta-method and influence-function machinery that yields a single efficient AIPW estimator for RD now produces two distinct doubly-robust estimators (RR-OS via one-step bias correction and RR-AIPW via estimating equations), both proven efficient under cross-fitting (Chernozhukov et al., 2017) and influence function theory (Kennedy, 2022). The RCT analysis builds on the Neyman and Horvitz-Thompson estimators, with the RR-N estimator tracing back to Cornfield (1956) and Katz et al. (1978).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Risk Ratio is a clinically mandated reporting measure (CONSORT guidelines) yet lacked rigorous semiparametric estimators with established asymptotic properties in observational settings — a gap that limited the statistical foundation for relative-effect inference in real-world data. The paper's recommendation of RR-AIPW (a simple ratio of two standard AIPW estimators) as the preferred method is practically appealing due to its ease of implementation and weaker regularity conditions. Open questions remain: the paper acknowledges that choosing between parametric and non-parametric nuisance estimators lacks clear guidelines, and extending these methods to generalize RR from RCT populations to broader target populations is identified as future work. The unconfoundedness assumption, while standard, remains unverifiable in practice, limiting the applicability of all estimators discussed.
