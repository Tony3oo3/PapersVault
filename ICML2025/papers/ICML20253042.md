## Problem

Existing automated multi-agent system design methods (e.g., AFlow, GPTSwarm, ADAS) search for a single, static, one-size-fits-all agentic workflow, which cannot dynamically allocate inference resources based on query difficulty or domain. This paradigm forces practitioners to either over-provision resources for simple queries or under-provision for complex ones, and fails on multi-domain benchmarks where no single architecture is optimal across all task types.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20253042/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference, MaAS takes a single text query as input and produces a query-dependent multi-agent architecture (a DAG of agentic operators such as CoT, ReAct, Debate, Self-Refine) that is then executed to yield a final answer. The architecture depth is variable (up to L=4 layers) due to an early-exit operator, so simple queries may resolve in one or two layers while hard queries traverse the full depth. During training, oracle answers from the benchmark are additionally consumed to compute the cost-aware importance weights that drive supernet updates.

## Method

MaAS models the search space as an agentic supernet: a probabilistic, continuous distribution π over multi-layer operator configurations, analogous to DARTS/SNAS in neural architecture search. A Mixture-of-Experts-style controller network computes activation scores for each operator at every layer via an FFN over concatenated query and prior-layer operator embeddings, then sequentially activates operators until a cumulative score threshold is exceeded, yielding a query-dependent number of operators per layer. Training jointly optimizes two components: (1) the distribution π is updated via Monte Carlo sampling with cost-aware importance weights that favor high-accuracy, low-token-cost architectures, and (2) the operators themselves (prompts, temperatures, node structures) are updated via agent-generated textual gradients since their internal logic is non-differentiable. An early-exit operator and a cost penalty term λ·C(·) in the objective ensure the supernet learns to allocate minimal resources to easy queries while deploying deeper, more complex pipelines for hard ones.

## Results

On the five-benchmark average (GSM8K, MATH, MultiArith, HumanEval, MBPP), MaAS achieves 83.59%, surpassing the SOTA automated method AFlow (82.25%) by 1.34% and the best hand-crafted system DyLAN (80.69%) by 2.90%. On the GAIA tool-use benchmark, MaAS reaches 20.69% average accuracy, outperforming the next-best baseline TapeAgent (16.61%) by 4.08%. In terms of cost, MaAS requires only $3.38 in training tokens (vs. $22.50 for AFlow, a 6.8× reduction) and $0.42 in inference cost (vs. $1.66 for AFlow), while also achieving the shortest wall-clock training time of 53 minutes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MaAS outperforms all 14 baselines across the five-benchmark average. Against the strongest automated baseline AFlow, MaAS gains +1.14% on GSM8K, +0.54% on MATH, +2.58% on MultiArith, +1.92% on HumanEval, and +0.50% on MBPP. Against hand-crafted multi-agent systems, the margins are larger: MaAS beats DyLAN (best hand-crafted) by +2.32% on GSM8K and +4.74% on MBPP, and LLM-Debate by +2.83% on GSM8K and +11.88% on MBPP. On the GAIA benchmark, MaAS (20.69% avg) surpasses TapeAgent (16.61%), GPTSwarm (16.33%), and AgentSquare (16.34%) by 4.08–4.36%, with particularly strong gains on Level 1 (+2.25% over TapeAgent) and Level 2 (+7.54% over TapeAgent) tasks. In cost comparisons on MATH, MaAS uses 6.8× less training cost than AFlow ($3.38 vs. $22.50) and 4× less inference cost ($0.42 vs. $1.66), while also achieving the highest accuracy (51.82% vs. 51.28%). The only baseline that approaches MaAS's inference cost is AgentSquare ($0.39), but it sacrifices 4% accuracy due to its single-agent search limitation.

## Relation to Prior Work

MaAS draws a direct analogy to the supernet training paradigm in Neural Architecture Search (DARTS, SNAS), transplanting the idea of optimizing a continuous distribution over architectures into the agentic system design space. It departs from the dominant paradigm in automated agentic workflows (ADAS, AFlow, AgentSquare, GPTSwarm), which all search for a single optimal multi-agent system via heuristic search, MCTS, or evolution, by instead optimizing a query-conditional distribution over architectures. The textual gradient mechanism for operator updates is adapted from ADAS and AgentSquare, while the MoE-style controller for query-dependent operator selection is a novel application of routing to agentic architecture sampling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The shift from single-system search to distribution-based, query-conditional architecture sampling addresses a fundamental limitation of prior automated agentic design: the inability to allocate inference resources proportionally to query difficulty. The large efficiency gap (6–45% of baseline inference costs while outperforming) and strong cross-model/cross-dataset transferability suggest this paradigm could become the default for deploying multi-agent systems in production settings with heterogeneous workloads. Open questions include scalability to much larger operator spaces, whether oracle-answer supervision during training is truly necessary, and how the approach performs on genuinely open-ended tasks without well-defined ground-truth answers.
