## Problem

Existing screening programs are designed in isolation for individual diseases, ignoring interactions between conditions and the competing risks that alter patient outcomes. No prior method simultaneously addresses resource constraints (budget, diagnostic error limits) and competing risks across multiple diseases, leaving a gap in how to optimally allocate screening resources when a patient faces risks for several conditions at once.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251834/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a patient's risk vector x ∈ [0,1]^N (prior probabilities of each disease) and a set of pre-existing per-disease screening/diagnostic policies as input, and produces a referral decision ρ(x) ∈ {0,1}^N specifying which diseases to screen. The key structural output is a set of piecewise decision boundaries ζ_n(x_{-n}) that are functions of the *other* diseases' risks rather than static per-disease thresholds, yielding a partition of the risk space into regions corresponding to different screening combinations (none, disease 1 only, disease 2 only, both).

## Method

The paper casts unified multi-disease screening as a referral problem: given fixed per-disease screening policies, it optimizes which subset to activate per patient by solving a linear program over the action-selection distribution q(a|x) subject to a budget constraint and per-disease false-positive rate limits. Using Lagrange duality, the optimal policy is shown to be a pointwise argmax of (reward − λ*·cost) across the 2^N possible screening combinations, reducing the problem to comparing pairwise gaps κ_i(x) − κ_j(x). For N=2, the authors prove that the optimal activation threshold for one disease is a piecewise function of the other disease's risk, composed of three boundary segments (b_⊥, b_≡, b_⊤) with distinct monotonicity properties, producing curvy decision boundaries rather than the flat thresholds of independent screening. Monte Carlo simulations with N=2 diseases validate the theoretical characterizations and demonstrate that the unified model improves mean survival time (37.70 vs. 37.47 years) over independent screening, particularly for patients with high relative risk in one disease.

## Results

The unified screening model achieves a mean survival time of 37.70 years (excess of 50), compared to 37.47 years for the independent screening model, representing an overall improvement of approximately 0.23 years. The unified model also schedules more joint screenings (2363 vs. 1847 instances of action a=4), indicating more efficient resource allocation. The survival advantage is concentrated at risk points where one disease's risk substantially outweighs the other's, while the independent model performs slightly better in the central region where both risks are moderate and similar.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the independent screening model, which applies a fixed activation threshold per disease (ρ̄ₙ(xₙ) = 1{xₙ > ζ̄ₙ}) without considering the risk of the other disease. The unified model outperforms the independent model in overall mean survival (37.70 vs. 37.47 years) and in the number of joint screenings performed (2363 vs. 1847). The unified model's advantage is most pronounced at the boundaries of the independent model's activation thresholds, where it correctly identifies patients with high relative risk for one disease and activates screening that the independent model would miss. However, in the central region of the risk space where both disease risks are moderate and close in magnitude, the independent model achieves slightly higher survival, as the unified model's emphasis on prioritizing higher relative risks leads to reduced screening for these balanced-risk patients. The theoretical characterization (Proposition 4.5) confirms that the unified model's activation threshold ζ₁(x₂) is a non-constant, piecewise function of the other disease's risk, with ζ₁(0) ≤ ζ̄₁ ≤ max ζ₁(x₂), explaining the systematic differences in screening decisions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of single-disease screening optimization (POMDPs, Markov models à la Cevik et al. 2018, Yala et al. 2022), competing-risks survival analysis (Wolbers et al. 2014, Pintilie 2011), and sequential hypothesis testing (Chernoff 1992, Alaa & Van Der Schaar 2016). As shown in their Table 1, no prior work simultaneously addresses joint screening, multiple diseases, resource constraints, and competing risks; the authors fill this gap by recasting unified screening as a referral problem over pre-existing per-disease policies and applying Lagrange duality to a linear program. The methodological toolkit (convex optimization, Bayesian likelihood-ratio diagnosis, Monte Carlo simulation) is standard, but the specific formulation and the resulting characterization of risk-dependent, curved decision boundaries are novel.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central practical insight—that optimal screening activation thresholds for one disease are not fixed constants but functions of the patient's risk for other diseases—has direct implications for how multi-disease screening programs should be structured in resource-constrained healthcare systems. However, the work is limited to N=2 diseases, assumes a uniform (or Beta) risk distribution, validates only in silico, and assumes screening is always beneficial (ignoring overdiagnosis harms). Open questions include extending the characterization to N>2, incorporating correlated disease risks, accounting for detrimental screening effects, and developing a concrete clinical implementation roadmap.
