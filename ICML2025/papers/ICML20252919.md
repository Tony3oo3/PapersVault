## Problem

Existing LVLMs are optimized for general image understanding and lack the fine-grained sensitivity needed to detect subtle deepfake artifacts, while conventional deepfake detectors rely on data augmentation or feature engineering that cannot capture the rich human knowledge embedded in forgery descriptions. This misalignment between LVLM knowledge and forensics patterns limits both generalization to unseen manipulation types and the explainability of detection decisions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252919/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes 224×224 facial crops (extracted via Dlib/RetinaFace) and a text query prompt as input. It produces a binary real/fake score from the KFD, a pixel-level forgery segmentation map, and a natural-language explanation generated by the LLM that identifies specific manipulated regions (e.g., 'the artifact is at the left eye, right jaw'). For video evaluation, 32 frames are uniformly sampled per video and frame-level 'yes/no' responses are aggregated into a video-level probability score via rule-based keyword parsing.

## Method

The framework operates in two stages. First, a Knowledge-guided Forgery Detector (KFD) uses ImageBind-Huge to extract multi-layer visual features (layers 16, 24, 32) and correlates them with GPT-4-generated text description embeddings of pristine and deepfake images, producing consistency maps that are fed into a three-branch Forgery Locator (segmentation) and a three-branch Forgery Classifier (binary score), with a reference-based cosine-similarity optimization applied only during training. Second, a Forgery Prompt Learner converts the segmentation map, forgery score, and consistency maps into forgery prompt embeddings via two CNNs and a fully connected layer, which are concatenated with visual and question prompt embeddings and fed into a Vicuna-7B LLM (within the PandaGPT architecture) fine-tuned with LoRA (rank 32) on simulated forgery image-text pairs constructed via Poisson blending of affine-transformed facial regions. An alternating training schedule between deepfake and general VQA data preserves multi-turn dialogue capability.

## Results

On the FF++ intra-dataset evaluation, the KFD component achieves 99.53% AUC and 99.54% AP. In cross-dataset generalization (trained only on FF++ real data), the method attains 94.71% AUC on CDF2, 99.64% on DFD, 97.62% on CDF1, 91.81% on DFDCP, and 79.12% on DFDC, outperforming all prior methods on every reported dataset. The full LVLM pipeline (Ours-LVLM) achieves an average AUC of 92.39% across five cross-dataset benchmarks, substantially exceeding PandaGPT (58.32%), Qwen2-VL (47.99%), and FAK-Owl (67.39%). On the DF40 cross-manipulation benchmark, the method achieves state-of-the-art AUC across all three manipulation categories (face swap, face reenactment, and full-face synthesis), including 99.99% on LIA and 100.00% on StyleGAN2 and PixArt-α.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the strongest conventional detector LAANet (CVPR'24), the proposed KFD achieves higher AUC on CDF2 (94.71 vs. 95.40 — LAANet slightly leads), DFD (99.64 vs. 99.51), CDF1 (97.62 vs. not reported), and DFDCP (91.81 vs. 86.94), while LAANet reports a marginally higher FF++ AUC (99.96 vs. 99.53). Compared to SBI (CVPR'22), the method surpasses it on CDF2 (94.71 vs. 93.82), DFD (99.64 vs. 96.32), DFDC (79.12 vs. 74.47), CDF1 (97.62 vs. 93.44), and DFDCP (91.81 vs. 90.95). Against CADDM (CVPR'23), the method wins on CDF2 (94.71 vs. 93.88), DFD (99.64 vs. 99.03), DFDC (79.12 vs. 73.85), CDF1 (97.62 vs. 85.68), and DFDCP (91.81 vs. 74.19). In the LVLM comparison (Table 3), Ours-LVLM (avg 92.39%) dramatically outperforms PandaGPT (58.32%), Qwen2-VL (47.99%), and FAK-Owl (67.39%), with the largest gap on DFDCP (86.70 vs. 46.06/51.71/70.72). On the DF40 cross-manipulation benchmark, the method achieves the best AUC in 11 out of 15 manipulation subcategories, with particularly large margins on LIA (99.99 vs. 89.22 for SBI) and DiT-XL/2 (94.18 vs. 79.04 for SBI).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the PandaGPT LVLM architecture (Su et al., 2023) and the self-blended image data simulation technique from SBI (Shiohara & Yamasaki, 2022), but departs from both traditional deepfake detectors and prior LVLM-based forgery methods (FakeShield, FKA-Owl, FFAA) by introducing a dedicated Knowledge-guided Forgery Detector that computes visual-text consistency maps via ImageBind cross-modal alignment. Rather than relying on the LVLM's general image understanding to identify artifacts, the framework explicitly aligns image features with human-authored textual descriptions of pristine and deepfake characteristics, then converts the resulting forgery signals into prompt embeddings for the LLM. This positions the work at the intersection of knowledge-guided anomaly detection (inspired by WinCLIP) and instruction-tuned vision-language models, bridging a gap the authors identify between data/feature-augmentation-based detectors and the human knowledge embedded in textual descriptions of manipulation artifacts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine limitation in deepfake detection: existing methods struggle to generalize to unseen manipulation types because they cannot capture the human knowledge about what constitutes a 'fake' appearance. The multi-turn dialogue capability is a practical differentiator for real-world deployment, enabling interactive verification rather than a single binary output. However, the authors acknowledge that the alternating training strategy for dialogue introduces domain gaps that degrade detection performance (KFD achieves 96.66% avg AUC vs. 92.39% for the full LVLM), and the LLM occasionally produces incorrect textual judgments even when localization is correct. Open questions include whether the knowledge-guided approach extends beyond facial deepfakes to other manipulation types, whether the ~46× inference time overhead (1.211s vs. 0.026s per frame) is acceptable for real-time applications, and whether the simulated forgery data (Poisson blending of affine-transformed regions) adequately represents the diversity of real-world deepfake generation.
