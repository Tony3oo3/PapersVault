## Problem

In real-world RL, ground-truth rewards are often unavailable or sparse, forcing practitioners to handcraft reward functions from multiple feedback signals via linear combinations or piece-wise rules. This reward engineering is fragile: signals have different scales and intricate interdependencies that make simultaneous weight tuning difficult, and piece-wise designs in sparse-reward settings require extensive trial-and-error to determine region boundaries and values. The paper asks whether the natural hierarchical importance among feedback signals can be exploited to simplify and improve reward design.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a set of n environment feedback signals per timestep, a human-provided importance ranking over those signals, and trajectory data generated by an initial policy (obtained via behavior cloning, pre-training with a handcrafted reward, or random initialization). It produces a preference-based reward model R_φ parameterized by a neural network (e.g., a 3-layer MLP or a small CodeT5/Phi-2 variant), a trained policy π_θ, and binary trajectory-pair preference labels (μ ∈ {0,1,2}). A key design choice is the per-level margin δ_l, set to the standard deviation of z_l over collected data, which controls how much difference in a signal is needed before a preference is assigned at that hierarchy level.

## Method

HERON constructs a decision tree over trajectory pairs where each level l compares two trajectories on the l-th most important feedback signal z_l with a margin δ_l; if the difference exceeds δ_l a preference is assigned, otherwise the comparison descends to the next level, and pairs that are indistinguishable at all levels are discarded. The elicited preferences are used to train a reward model via the Bradley-Terry loss (following Ouyang et al., 2022), which is then used to train a policy with standard RL algorithms (QCOMBO, PPO, or DPO). The core insight is that this hierarchical comparison is scale-invariant—it depends on relative differences rather than absolute signal magnitudes—making the learned reward more robust to environment changes than linear-combination reward engineering. The framework supports multi-stage training (iterating preference elicitation, reward learning, and policy learning) to correct sampling bias from a weak initial policy, and an optional post-training reward rescaling step α^{F(τ)} that explicitly amplifies reward separation between trajectory categories according to their importance rank.

## Results

On the APPS code generation benchmark, HERON achieves Pass@50 of 10.19, outperforming CodeRL (9.81) and PPOCoder (7.62), with gains at larger K values passing a t-test at p<0.05. On the MBPP zero-shot generalization set, HERON reaches Pass@1 of 7.40 versus 6.58 for both CodeRL and PPOCoder. For language model alignment on HelpSteer, HERON-DPO achieves an average win-rate of 64.57% across four LLM judges, substantially exceeding reward engineering baselines (56.08% equal-weight, 57.06% decaying-weight). In traffic light control, HERON's policy significantly outperforms both the reward engineering baseline and the ground-truth reward from Zhang et al. (2019) with p<0.005.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HERON is compared against three main baselines. Against CodeRL (Le et al., 2022), which uses a hand-crafted piece-wise reward based on compilation/runtime/test-pass status, HERON wins on all Pass@K metrics on APPS (e.g., Pass@50: 10.19 vs. 9.81) and on MBPP (Pass@1: 7.40 vs. 6.58), with the advantage growing at larger K values. Against PPOCoder (Shojaee et al., 2023), which adds AST similarity to the CodeRL reward, HERON shows a much larger margin (Pass@50: 10.19 vs. 7.62; Pass@1: 1.72 vs. 1.23), suggesting that the hierarchical preference structure captures more nuanced program quality than a linear combination of signals. Against the ground-truth reward from Zhang et al. (2019) in multi-agent traffic light control, HERON's policy achieves significantly higher evaluation reward (p<0.005), indicating that the hierarchical decision tree leverages individual feedback signals more effectively than a fixed weighted sum. In robotic control environments where no clear hierarchy exists, HERON still matches or exceeds the ground-truth reward (e.g., Ant: 1.0 vs. 0.99) while consistently beating the reward engineering baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

HERON sits at the intersection of preference-based reward learning (Bradley-Terry model, Ouyang et al. 2022) and direct preference optimization (Rafailov et al. 2024), but departs from both by replacing human-annotated trajectory comparisons with an automated hierarchical decision tree over domain feedback signals. It explicitly contrasts with reward engineering (linear weighted combinations of signals), reward shaping, and inverse RL, arguing that ranking signals by importance is more natural and less brittle than specifying numerical weights. The framework also extends RLHF by showing that structured domain knowledge (a hierarchy) can substitute for expensive human preference labels in many practical settings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Reward design remains a major bottleneck in deploying RL to real-world problems where ground-truth rewards are unavailable or sparse; HERON offers a principled, low-tuning-cost alternative that is scale-invariant and flexible to practitioner preferences. The paper's limitation is that it requires a defensible importance ranking over feedback signals, which may not exist in genuinely multi-objective settings with conflicting objectives. Open questions include encoding preference strength (how much more important is level 1 vs. level 2?), handling near-ties in the hierarchy, and whether the approach generalizes to settings where feedback signals are learned rather than hand-specified.
