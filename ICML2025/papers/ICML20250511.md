## Problem

Classic ensemble theory predicts that aggregating underparameterized models improves generalization, but recent empirical studies show that overparameterized ensembles (e.g., deep ensembles of neural networks) offer little to no advantage over single larger models and that their predictive variance may not capture meaningful uncertainty. This paper provides the first theoretical characterization of overparameterized ensembles under minimal distributional assumptions, clarifying why intuitions from the underparameterized regime fail to transfer.

## Input / Output

The analysis takes a fixed training set of N points (x_i, y_i) and D > N i.i.d. random features drawn from a distribution π(·), operating in the overparameterized regime with ridgeless (λ=0) or small-ridge (λ>0) training. The key structural object is the whitened feature matrix W = R^{-T}Φ where R^T R = K is the Cholesky factor of the kernel matrix, which decouples the analysis from specific feature distributions. Outputs are scalar predictions from individual RF regressors, ensemble averages, and the predictive variance across ensemble members, with the critical finding that this variance equals the expected squared deviation from the infinite-width kernel regressor rather than a GP posterior variance.

## Method

The paper proves that an infinite ensemble of overparameterized RF regressors is pointwise almost-surely equivalent to a single infinite-width RF regressor (Theorem 3.2) by showing the cross-term E[w_⊥^T W^T (WW^T)^{-1}] vanishes under a subexponentiality assumption (Lemma 3.1), using the Woodbury matrix identity and concentration inequalities that do not require Gaussianity or independence of feature entries. For finite parameter budgets, a non-asymptotic bound (Theorem 3.3) shows the L2 difference between an ensemble of M models with D features and a single model with MD features is O(√log(1/δ)) + O(1/D). In the small-ridge regime, the difference between ensemble and single-model predictions is shown to be Lipschitz-continuous in λ (Theorem 3.5), with the bound growing at most linearly. For uncertainty, the paper demonstrates that ensemble variance decomposes as the expected squared difference between finite-width and infinite-width predictions, which coincides with (scaled) GP posterior variance only under i.i.d. Gaussian features but deviates substantially for realistic activations like ReLU.

## Results

The paper's central theoretical result (Theorem 3.2) proves that an infinite ensemble of overparameterized RF regressors is pointwise almost-surely equivalent to a single infinite-width RF regressor, under only a subexponentiality assumption on the random features. For finite parameter budgets, Theorem 3.3 shows the L₂ difference between an ensemble of M models (each with D features) and a single model with MD features is bounded by O(√log(1/δ)) + O(1/D) with probability 1−δ. In the small-ridge regime, Theorem 3.5 establishes that the prediction difference between the infinite ensemble and the single infinite-width model is Lipschitz-continuous in the ridge parameter λ, bounded by C·λ for compact input spaces. Empirically, ensemble variance for ReLU features deviates substantially from the Gaussian process posterior variance (Fig. 4), confirming that ensemble variance quantifies sensitivity to model capacity rather than a conventional Bayesian or frequentist uncertainty.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against competing methods in the traditional sense; instead, it compares overparameterized ensembles against three reference points. First, against single large models with the same total parameter budget: ensembles and single models exhibit nearly identical generalization error scaling (Fig. 3), with the L₂ prediction difference vanishing as O(1/D). Second, against underparameterized ensembles: the 'hockey stick' pattern in Fig. 2 shows that when D < N, ensembles behave like ridge regression with an implicit regularization, but this distinction disappears once D > N. Third, against Gaussian process posterior variance as an uncertainty baseline: for non-Gaussian features (ReLU, erf, softplus), ensemble variance diverges significantly from the GP posterior variance r⊥² (Fig. 4, Fig. 14), whereas under i.i.d. Gaussian features the two coincide (Eq. 5). The paper also contrasts its minimal-assumption analysis with prior Gaussian-based work (Jacot et al., 2020), showing that the ensemble/single-model equivalence is a fundamental property of overparameterization rather than an artifact of Gaussian universality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the analysis of random feature (RF) ensembles from the underparameterized regime (where ensembles induce ridge-like regularization, per Kaban 2014, Thanei et al. 2017) to the overparameterized regime, building on the RF model framework of Rahimi & Recht (2007) and the overparameterized RF literature (Adlam & Pennington 2020, Mei & Montanari 2022). The key departure from prior work—most notably Jacot et al. (2020), who analyzed ridge-regularized RF ensembles under Gaussian process feature assumptions—is the use of a much weaker subexponentiality assumption (Assumption 2.1) that permits dependent, non-Gaussian feature entries, which is more faithful to actual neural network features. This weaker assumption is what enables the paper to distinguish ensemble variance from Gaussian process posterior variance, a distinction invisible under the Gaussian universality assumptions common in high-dimensional asymptotic analyses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a theoretical foundation for the empirical observation (Abe et al. 2022b, Theisen et al. 2024) that deep ensembles offer little generalization or robustness advantage over larger single models, and that ensemble variance measures sensitivity to model capacity rather than epistemic uncertainty. This has direct implications for safety-critical applications and active learning that rely on ensemble variance as an uncertainty signal. Limitations include the restriction to RF models (linear last-layer training), which may not capture the full dynamics of end-to-end trained deep networks, and the fact that the equivalence results hold in the infinite-width/infinite-ensemble limit with only approximate finite-budget guarantees. Open questions include whether the non-Gaussianity-driven breakdown of the Bayesian interpretation persists in fully trained deep networks and whether there exist architectural or training regimes where ensembles provide genuine uncertainty benefits beyond capacity scaling.
