## Problem

Standard reward models for LLM alignment are trained exclusively on labeled human preference data, making them expensive to build and tightly coupled to specific tasks. This paper asks whether a single foundation reward model can be trained by leveraging large-scale unlabeled data, so that it generalizes broadly across rewarding tasks with little or no further fine-tuning.

## Input / Output

For preference prediction, the model receives a text prompt describing the task, an input x, and a pair of responses (y_a, y_b), and outputs a single token 'A' or 'B' indicating the preferred response. For scoring a single response y' given input x', the model generates a reference response y_ref and computes the reward as the average of the log-probabilities of y' being preferred under two positional orderings (y' before y_ref and y_ref before y'), yielding a score in [0, 1] that mitigates positional bias.

## Method

GRAM uses a generative LLM (LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct or LLaMA-3.2-3B-Instruct) trained in two stages: first, an unsupervised pre-training stage where the model learns to generate both responses in a pair given only the input (no preference labels needed), building general knowledge of input-response correspondence; second, a supervised fine-tuning stage on preference data where the model predicts the 'A'/'B' token. A key theoretical contribution shows that applying label smoothing (ε=0.1) to the generative training objective is algebraically equivalent to optimizing a regularized Bradley-Terry loss, where the regularization term ε·(Z_a − Z_b) penalizes overconfident score differences and improves OOD generalization. This unification reveals that generative and discriminative reward models share the same underlying pairwise-ranking objective, and the noisier, more diverse training signal of the generative formulation (modeling two responses plus a prompt in one sequence) naturally encourages better generalization than the discriminative pairwise ranking loss.

## Results

On the LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct model, GRAM achieves 85.1% average accuracy on RewardBench (OOD) and 83.7% on HHH-Alignment (OOD), surpassing the vanilla discriminative RM (74.1% / 76.0%) by 11.0 and 7.7 points, and the vanilla generative RM (80.0% / 80.2%) by 5.1 and 3.5 points, respectively. On the in-distribution Unified-Feedback test set, GRAM scores 70.4%, also exceeding both baselines. On the smaller LLaMA-3.2-3B-Instruct model, GRAM achieves 80.2% on RewardBench and 81.9% on HHH-Alignment, again outperforming both discriminative (72.8% / 70.5%) and generative (71.1% / 77.2%) baselines. In PPO fine-tuning, GRAM yields a 12.63% win rate on AlpacaEval, compared to 8.82% for the best generative baseline (G-Label Smoothing).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the vanilla discriminative RM trained on the same 400k Unified-Feedback data, GRAM wins decisively on all OOD benchmarks: +11.0 points on RewardBench (85.1 vs 74.1) and +7.7 on HHH-Alignment (83.7 vs 76.0), while also edging ahead on the ID set (70.4 vs 69.3). Against the vanilla generative RM, GRAM gains +5.1 on RewardBench and +3.5 on HHH-Alignment, confirming that the two-stage pre-training plus label smoothing provides substantial improvement over single-stage generative training. Compared to LLM-as-a-Judge (GPT-4o), GRAM is competitive on RewardBench (85.1 vs 84.6) but trails on HHH-Alignment (83.7 vs 90.6), suggesting that prompt-based judging still excels on safety-oriented preferences. Against open-source reward models trained on large-scale labeled data (e.g., pair-preference-model-LLaMA3-8B at 90.4 on RewardBench), GRAM trained on only 400k samples is slightly behind, but the paper notes that scaling unlabeled pre-training data to 400k+ closes this gap (reaching 91.6). In PPO fine-tuning, GRAM achieves a 12.63% AlpacaEval win rate versus 8.82% for the best generative baseline (G-Label Smoothing) and 6.75% for the generative baseline, demonstrating that its superior generalization translates into more stable and effective reinforcement learning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the generative reward model paradigm (Zhang et al., 2024; Mahan et al., 2024) by introducing a two-stage training procedure that borrows the foundation model recipe—unsupervised pre-training followed by supervised fine-tuning—from the LLM literature (Devlin et al., 2019; Brown et al., 2020). It departs from prior reward modeling work, which trains exclusively on labeled preference data, by showing that unlabeled input-response pairs can be leveraged via a response-generation pre-training objective. The theoretical contribution of showing that label smoothing in the generative setting is equivalent to optimizing a regularized Bradley-Terry loss provides a unifying view that connects the discriminative and generative reward model training objectives previously treated as separate paradigms.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By demonstrating that a single pre-trained reward model can be adapted to diverse tasks with as few as 1k–3k labeled examples (vs. 92k for task-specific training), GRAM offers a practical path toward reducing the annotation bottleneck in RLHF pipelines. The finding that label smoothing—which recent work (Ruan et al., 2024) shows degrades LLM performance—actually benefits generative reward models is a useful counterpoint for practitioners. Open questions include whether the pre-training objective scales effectively to much larger unlabeled corpora, how the approach transfers to multimodal or non-text reward settings, and whether the foundation reward model paradigm can replace the current practice of training task-specific reward models from scratch.
