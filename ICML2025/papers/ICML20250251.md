## Problem

Eliciting informative prior distributions for Bayesian linear models traditionally requires trained domain experts, is time-consuming, and is often infeasible in low-resource clinical settings where labelled data is scarce. While LLMs encode broad domain knowledge, their direct use via in-context learning is computationally expensive, opaque, and does not reliably perform Bayesian inference on numerical predictive tasks. The paper asks whether LLMs can be prompted to produce structured prior distributions over linear model parameters that are both informative and compatible with standard Bayesian inference.

## Input / Output

The method consumes natural-language task descriptions (a system role specifying the LLM's role as a prior elicitor and a user role describing the dataset and features), numerical feature values from tabular datasets, and optionally expert knowledge expressed as plain text. The LLM is prompted 100 times (the product of 10 rephrased system roles and 10 rephrased user roles) to return a JSON object with a Gaussian mean and standard deviation per feature. The output is a 100-component mixture of Gaussians over each linear model parameter, which is then used as the prior in a logistic or linear regression model; NUTS sampling (5 chains × 5000 samples) yields posterior predictive distributions for classification or regression predictions with calibrated uncertainty estimates.

## Method

AutoElicit constructs a mixture-of-Gaussians prior by prompting an LLM with K=100 task descriptions, each yielding a per-feature (μ_k, σ_k) pair, combined as p_{M,T}(θ) = Σ_k π_k N(θ|μ_k, σ_k²) with π_k ~ Dir(1). This prior is placed on the weights of a linear or logistic regression model, and standard Bayesian inference (NUTS sampler) produces the posterior, making the approach fully compatible with existing probabilistic programming frameworks. To compare against in-context learning, the paper extracts the LLM's implicit in-context prior and posterior via maximum likelihood estimation on the LLM's predictions over randomly generated inputs, then uses the energy statistic to quantify distributional differences and tests whether ICL approximates Bayesian inference by comparing the extracted posterior to a Monte Carlo posterior sampled from the extracted prior. Model selection between AutoElicit and ICL is performed via the Bayes factor (marginal likelihood ratio), and expert knowledge can be injected as natural-language statements in the task description to further refine the elicited priors without requiring formal elicitation training.

## Results

AutoElicit priors (mixture of 100 Gaussians elicited from GPT-3.5-turbo, GPT-4-turbo, or DeepSeek-R1-32B-Q4) substantially outperform an uninformative prior (θ~N(0,1)) in posterior accuracy and MSE across all six datasets, with the largest gains at small training sizes. On the synthetic task (y=2x₁−x₂+x₃), LLM-elicited priors achieve an order-of-magnitude lower test MSE after only 5 examples and match the uninformative prior by ~20 examples. On the private UTI dataset, all three LLMs yield greater accuracy than the uninformative prior at every training size, saving over 6 months (~220 days) of labelling effort to reach peak accuracy. The Bayes factor favours AutoElicit over both the uninformative prior and in-context learning on all five clinical datasets tested.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the uninformative prior (θ~N(0,1)), AutoElicit wins on every dataset and every training size tested, with the most dramatic gains at n≤20 labels (e.g., ~10× lower MSE on the synthetic task after 5 examples; consistently higher accuracy on UTI). Against in-context learning (ICL), AutoElicit is preferred by the Bayes factor on all five clinical datasets; ICL's implicit priors are less informative (centred near 0, as confirmed by energy statistics of 0.15–0.39 in Table 1), ICL only reliably imitated a linear model on 2 of 5 datasets, and it approximated Bayesian inference in only 1 of those 2 cases (Diabetes). Against Gouk & Gao (2024), which uses LLM-generated synthetic data to pre-train a logistic regression prior, AutoElicit achieves improved posterior accuracy and MSE on both private and public tasks (Appendix A.9.5, Figure 13), and avoids the data-memorisation risk that affects the Gouk & Gao approach on public datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

AutoElicit repurposes LLMs as expert proxies for the well-established task of Bayesian prior elicitation (Garthwaite et al., 2005; O'Hagan et al., 2006), replacing trained human experts with prompted language models that output Gaussian mixture priors over linear model parameters. It departs from the closest prior work—Gouk & Gao (2024), who generate synthetic data to define priors, and Selby et al. (2025), who elicit target-level distributions—by directly eliciting parameter-level priors that plug into a full Bayesian inference pipeline with a trusted linear model. The paper also extends the in-context learning literature (Brown et al., 2020; Xie et al., 2021) by proposing a concrete MLE-based procedure to extract the implicit prior and posterior that an LLM uses during ICL, enabling an empirical test of whether ICL approximates Bayesian inference.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is concrete: in a dementia-care study, LLM-elicited priors achieve comparable accuracy to uninformative priors roughly six months earlier in label collection, reducing a costly and ethically sensitive labelling burden. The finding that LLMs do not consistently apply the same prior knowledge in-context as they provide when directly asked to elicit one, and that they do not reliably perform Bayesian inference in-context, is an important caveat for anyone considering ICL as a drop-in predictive tool. Open questions include extending the framework beyond linear models (to random forests, Gaussian processes, or neural networks), addressing potential bias inherited from LLM training data, and studying whether fine-tuning LLMs on domain literature further improves elicited priors.
