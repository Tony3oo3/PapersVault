## Problem

Prior learning-augmented BSTs (Lin et al., 2022) achieve static optimality only under the restrictive Zipfian distribution assumption, and no general framework existed for learning-augmented search trees that handles arbitrary input distributions, supports dynamic tree modifications during access, or extends to B-Trees in the external memory model. This paper asks whether a single priority-based mechanism can simultaneously achieve static optimality for arbitrary distributions, the working-set bound in dynamic settings, and graceful degradation under prediction errors.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a weight vector w ∈ (0,1)^n (e.g., relative frequencies f_x/m or working-set scores 1/(1+work(i,x))²) and an access sequence X of length m over n items, producing a Treap or B-Treap whose expected depth of item x is O(log(1/w_x)) or O(log_B(1/w_x)) respectively. In the dynamic setting, the weight vector is locally updated at each time step—only the accessed item's score changes—so the tree is reorganized incrementally via O(|log(w'/w)|) rotations per access. The total access cost is bounded by O(Σ f_x log(m/f_x)) in the static case and O(n log_B n + Σ log_B(1+work(i,x))) in the dynamic case.

## Method

The core mechanism is a composite priority function priority_x = -⌊log₂ log_B(1/w_x)⌋ + δ_x where δ_x ~ U(0,1), which layers a frequency-dependent tier (the double-log term) with a random offset to break ties within each tier. The double-log structure is critical: it caps the number of items per tier at 2^{O(2^t)}, ensuring each item accumulates only O(2^t) ancestors at tier t, yielding total depth O(log(1/w_x)) for arbitrary weight vectors—whereas direct frequency-based priorities (Lin et al., 2022) can produce Ω(n) depth on adversarial distributions. For the dynamic setting, the working-set score ω(i,x) = 1/(1+work(i,x))² is shown to be locally changed (only the accessed item's score updates per step) and to have O(1) l₁-norm, enabling the same B-Treap machinery to achieve the working-set bound. Robustness follows algebraically: the total cost with predicted weights q equals m·Ent(p,q), so the overhead over the optimal is exactly m·D_KL(p,q), and in the dynamic case the overhead is linear in the mean absolute error of log-scores.

## Results

The paper evaluates its learning-augmented Treap (learn-bst) against six baselines using total number of comparisons as the access cost metric, with n=1000 items and sequence lengths m ∈ {2000, 6000, 10000, 16000, 20000}. Under the adversarial distribution (p_i = 2(n−i+1)/(n(n+1))), the proposed method maintains O(log n) expected depth while Lin et al. (2022) degrades to Ω(n), a gap of roughly an order of magnitude. Under prediction errors, the additional cost grows linearly with KL divergence between true and predicted frequencies (confirmed in Figure 6), and the method exhibits graceful degradation outperforming all alternatives even at KL divergence up to 0.7.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed learning-augmented Treap outperforms all seven baselines (Lin et al. 2022, learning-augmented skip-list, Red-Black Trees, AVL Trees, Splay Trees, B-Trees of order 3, and randomized Treaps) on the adversarial and uniform distributions, where it achieves the lowest total comparison count across all sequence lengths. On the Zipfian distribution, Lin et al. (2022) performs comparably since it is explicitly designed for that distribution, but the proposed method still matches or slightly exceeds it. The most dramatic separation occurs on the adversarial distribution, where Lin et al. (2022) suffers Ω(n) expected depth (proven in Theorem 2.14) while the proposed composite priority maintains O(log n), a gap that widens with n. Under inaccurate predictions (Section 5.2), the proposed method degrades gracefully with increasing KL divergence, consistently outperforming Splay Trees and randomized Treaps even when 75% of the data comes from an unknown distribution (Appendix E). The learning-augmented skip-list (Fu et al., 2025) is outperformed across all tested distributions, confirming that the composite priority design in the Treap framework is more effective than the skip-list variant.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Treap data structure (Aragon & Seidel, 1989) and the learning-augmented BST of Lin et al. (2022), which assigned priorities proportional to item frequencies but was restricted to Zipfian distributions. The key departure is a composite priority function — the log-log of the predicted frequency plus a uniform random offset — that preserves the balance guarantees of randomized Treaps while encoding frequency information, thereby extending static optimality to arbitrary input distributions. The B-Treap framework of Golovin (2009) is similarly repurposed to carry these composite priorities into the external memory model, and a new working-set priority is introduced for the dynamic setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By removing the Zipfian assumption, this work makes learning-augmented search trees applicable to a much broader class of real-world access patterns, and the KL-divergence-based robustness guarantee gives practitioners a clean, interpretable handle on how prediction quality translates to performance degradation. The dynamic working-set result is notable because it shows that a simple time-varying score (reciprocal of squared working-set size) can beat the best static tree on sequences with strong temporal locality, suggesting that prediction-aware reorganization is strictly more powerful than frequency-based static optimization. Open questions include whether the composite-priority idea extends to spatial-locality bounds (e.g., dynamic finger or the unified bound) and whether the working-set score can be predicted causally without oracle access to future accesses.
