## Problem

Subgraph GNNs overcome the 1-WL expressiveness limitations of standard message-passing GNNs by processing bags of node-marked subgraphs, but their O(N²·d_max) computational complexity makes them impractical for graphs beyond a few tens of nodes. Existing scaling strategies—random sampling, learnable policies via RL, or graph coarsening—are either suboptimal in quality, difficult to train, or rely on structural assumptions that may not hold. The paper asks: can a simple, non-learnable, and efficient subgraph selection strategy recover most of the full-bag performance?

## Input / Output

The method takes a graph as an adjacency matrix and node feature matrix, and produces a graph-level prediction (classification or regression). A key preprocessing step computes the Subgraph Centrality power series up to order K for every node via repeated matrix-vector products with the adjacency matrix; the top-T nodes by centrality are selected for marking, and the K intermediate series terms are concatenated to the original node features as Centrality-based Structural Encodings (CSEs). No subgraph connectivity is materialized—only marking indicators are stored in the feature tensor, reducing memory overhead relative to generic Subgraph GNN implementations.

## Method

HyMN (Hybrid Marking Network) combines two complementary mechanisms derived from walk-based Subgraph Centrality: (1) it subsamples the bag of node-marked subgraphs by selecting only the top-T nodes ranked by Subgraph Centrality, reducing bag size from N to a small constant T (typically 1–2); and (2) it augments node features with the K-order CSE vector [1, (A¹)ᵥᵥ/1!, (A²)ᵥᵥ/2!, …, (Aᴷ)ᵥᵥ/K!], which encodes discounted closed-walk counts. The theoretical justification comes from a perturbation analysis (via Tree Mover's Distance bounds) showing that the cumulative number of walks from a node upper-bounds the representation change induced by marking it, and that high-centrality marking empirically induces the largest perturbations correlated with substructure counts. Expressiveness theorems prove that subsampled Subgraph GNNs and CSE-enhanced MPNNs are not mutually subsuming, so their combination strictly improves discriminative power. The selected subgraphs are processed by a standard equivariant Subgraph GNN backbone (e.g., GIN or GCN) with a custom message-passing function that handles the marking information without materializing separate subgraph topologies.

## Results

On the OGB molecular benchmarks, HyMN(GIN, T=2) with CSEs achieves 81.01±1.17 ROC-AUC on MOLHIV and 81.16±1.21 on MOLBACE, outperforming the full-bag Subgraph GNN (76.54, 78.41) and the learnable POLICY-LEARN (79.13, 78.40) while processing only 2 subgraphs instead of N. On the Peptides-Func dataset (avg. 151 nodes), HyMN(GCN, T=2) reaches 0.6948 AP, matching Graph-ViT (0.6942) and outperforming GPS (0.6535), while training 42% faster than GCN per epoch. On MalNet-Tiny (up to 5,000 nodes), HyMN(T=1) without CSEs achieves 92.84% accuracy, outperforming all GPS variants (90.85–92.14%) at roughly 6× lower training and inference time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HyMN with T=2 subgraphs and CSEs outperforms the full-bag Subgraph GNN on MOLHIV (81.01 vs. 76.54) and MOLBACE (81.16 vs. 78.41) while processing only 2 subgraphs instead of all N nodes. Against the learnable POLICY-LEARN (T=2), HyMN wins on MOLHIV (81.01 vs. 79.13) and MOLBACE (81.16 vs. 78.40) without requiring RL-based training or 1,000–4,000 epochs. Random sampling (T=2) is consistently weaker, e.g., 77.55 vs. 81.01 on MOLHIV. On Peptides-Func, HyMN(GCN, T=2) matches Graph-ViT (0.6948 vs. 0.6942 AP) and G-MLP-Mixer (0.6948 vs. 0.6921) while outperforming GPS (0.6535) by ~4 AP points, with training time only 42% above a plain GCN. On MalNet-Tiny, HyMN(T=1) no-CSE (92.84%) beats GPS(Perf.) (92.14%), GPS(BigBird) (91.02%), and GPS(Transf.) (90.85%) at ~6× lower runtime. On RDT-B, HyMN(T=2) achieves 93.2% accuracy, marginally exceeding POLICY-LEARN(T=2) (93.0%) and clearly outperforming SIN (92.2%) and CIN (92.4%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of Subgraph GNNs (Bevilacqua et al., 2021; Papp & Wattenhofer, 2022) and network science centrality measures (Estrada & Rodriguez-Velazquez, 2005), repurposing Subgraph Centrality as both a subgraph sampling criterion and a source of Structural Encodings. It departs from prior scaling strategies for Subgraph GNNs—random sampling, RL-based policies (MAG-GNN), and gradient-based learnable policies (Policy-Learn)—by proposing a non-learnable, precomputable centrality-based selection that requires no additional training. The theoretical grounding draws on GNN perturbation/stability analysis (Chuang & Jegelka, 2022) to justify why high-centrality nodes are effective marking candidates, and the expressiveness analysis (Theorems 4.1–4.2) positions the method relative to both CSE-enhanced MPNNs and full-bag Subgraph GNNs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: by reducing the bag to T=1 or T=2 subgraphs, HyMN makes Subgraph GNNs applicable to graphs 10–100× larger than full-bag methods can handle (Peptides ~151 nodes, MalNet up to 5,000 nodes, Reddit ~429 nodes), with a ~6× speedup over GPS on MalNet. The conceptual contribution—that a simple, non-learnable, precomputable centrality measure matches or beats RL-trained sampling policies—suggests that the field may have over-engineered the subgraph selection problem. Open questions include whether multi-node marking or pairwise centrality features could further improve selection, whether the strategy remains optimal as T grows, and how to account for redundancy among already-sampled subgraphs.
