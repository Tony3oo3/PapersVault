## Problem

Zero-shot inference over text-attributed graphs (TAGs) with LLMs faces two fundamental obstacles: LLMs' limited context windows prevent processing large node neighborhoods, and aligning graph-aggregated embeddings with LLM token spaces is severely undertrained due to the scarcity of graph-domain data. Existing methods that rely on embedding-to-token-space projectors (e.g., LLaGA, GraphGPT) actually underperform simple SBERT encoders without any graph structure, indicating that the alignment step is the bottleneck rather than LLM reasoning.

## Input / Output

The method consumes raw node text attributes, the graph edge set, a natural-language task description, and class label names. Node texts are encoded via a frozen LLM2Vec encoder (Mistral-7B-based) using a structured prompt that injects both the task description and class information before the text. Graph structure is consumed indirectly: a small sample of connected node pairs (40–100 edges) is fed to GPT-4o-mini to estimate the homophily ratio r, which parameterizes the edge potentials. The output is a per-node class label (argmax over the final BP posterior) for node classification, or a cosine-similarity score between aggregated node embeddings for link prediction.

## Method

LLM-BP is a fully training-free framework built on two principles. First, it generates task-adaptive node embeddings by prompting a frozen LLM2Vec encoder with a template that conditions the embedding on the task description and class labels, exploiting the decoder-induced encoder's contextual learning to produce class-discriminative representations without any fine-tuning. Second, it models the TAG as a Markov Random Field and performs loopy belief propagation (or a single-step linear approximation) to aggregate information across the graph, where the edge potential ψ_ij(y_i, y_j) is set to r if y_i = y_j and 1−r otherwise, with r estimated by an LLM agent that judges whether sampled connected node pairs share the same class. The initial node belief is a softmax over cosine similarities between the task-adaptive embedding and class embeddings (obtained by clustering LLM-predicted labels on a small sample in zero-shot, or averaging labeled nodes in few-shot). This design requires no gradient updates, no projector training, and adapts to both homophilic and heterophilic structures through the sign of the log-likelihood aggregation controlled by r.

## Results

On 11 real-world text-attributed graph benchmarks (7 homophilic, 4 heterophilic), LLM-BP achieves the best average rank of 2.27 (Accuracy) and 2.55 (F1) in the zero-shot setting, outperforming all 17 baselines. The task-adaptive encoder (Principle I) improves accuracy by 8.10% on average over the best vanilla LM encoder, while the BP-inspired aggregation (Principle II) adds a further 1.71% gain. On heterophilic graphs (Cornell, Texas, Wisconsin, Washington), LLM-BP achieves 83.28%, 81.66%, 77.75%, and 73.14% accuracy respectively, substantially outperforming the next-best method (Text-Embedding-3-Large at 81.50%, 75.42%, 73.14%, 66.35%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

LLM-BP achieves the best average rank (2.27) across all 11 datasets, with particularly dominant performance on heterophilic graphs where it ranks 1.25 (e.g., Cornell: 83.28% vs. Text-Embedding-3-Large's 81.50% and SBert's 63.66%). Among vanilla encoders, Text-Embedding-3-Large is the strongest baseline (rank 5.36), but LLM-BP's task-adaptive prompting over LLM2Vec yields an 8.10% average accuracy improvement over the best LM-based encoder. A striking finding is that LLMs with graph adapters (LLaGA at 15.36 avg. rank, GraphGPT at 14.55) perform far worse than even vanilla SBert without any graph structure (7.27), indicating that the embedding-to-token-space alignment is severely undertrained. Among trained methods, GOFA (rank 8.00) is the strongest multi-task foundation model but still trails LLM-BP by a wide margin, especially on heterophilic graphs (e.g., Texas: 38.37% vs. 81.66%). GPT-4o as a direct text classifier is competitive on Pubmed (89.85% vs. 75.55%) and Children (30.76% vs. 24.81%) but loses overall due to its inability to leverage graph structure. The linear approximation LLM-BP(appr.) achieves nearly identical performance (rank 2.45) at a fraction of the computational cost, making it practical for large-scale deployment.

## Relation to Prior Work

LLM-BP departs from the dominant 'LLM with graph adapter' paradigm (LLaGA, GraphGPT, TEA-GLM) that aligns graph-aggregated embeddings to LLM token space via projectors, instead using LLMs in two separate, training-free roles: as task-adaptive text encoders (via LLM2Vec with class-conditional prompting) and as agents to estimate graph homophily for parameterizing a classical loopy belief propagation algorithm. The graph aggregation mechanism draws directly on MRF/BP theory (Murphy et al., 2013) and the linear approximation insight from Wei et al. (2022), while the embedding component builds on LLM2Vec (BehnamGhader et al., 2024) and the unidirectional-attention variant from Li et al. (2024a). The paper explicitly positions itself against both smaller-LM encoder tuning (ZeroG, UniGLM) and multi-task graph foundation models (OFA, GOFA), arguing that first-principles statistical inference offers better cross-domain generalization than learned alignment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The most impactful finding is the negative result: LLMs-with-graph-adapters (LLaGA, GraphGPT) dramatically underperform even vanilla SBERT without any graph structure, suggesting that the field's investment in embedding-to-token-space alignment is misdirected given the scarcity of TAG-domain training data. This reframes the question of how LLMs should interact with graph data — as encoders and property estimators rather than as processors of compressed graph representations. Limitations include the simplified binary homophily parameterization (no per-class edge potentials), preliminary link prediction results, and reliance on GPT-4o-mini for homophily estimation, which introduces API cost and potential bias. Open questions include whether richer MRF potentials can be estimated by LLMs, whether the approach scales to graphs with millions of nodes, and whether task-adaptive prompting generalizes beyond node classification to other graph tasks.
