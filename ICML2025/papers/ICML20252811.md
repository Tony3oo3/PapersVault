## Problem

Existing methods for reducing confounding bias in causal inference split training samples into subpopulations by treatment value before aligning their distributions, which reduces the number of samples available for distribution estimation and alignment. This data reduction is particularly harmful when sample sizes are limited, since precise distribution estimation and alignment rely on sufficient training data. The paper asks whether confounding bias can be characterized and reduced without splitting, by instead modeling different probability measures over the full sample set.

## Input / Output

The method takes tabular covariates x ∈ ℝ^d and a treatment value t (binary or continuous) as input, and outputs an estimated potential outcome μ̂(x,t) via a prediction head ψ(φ(x), t). A key design choice is that all n training samples participate in both the marginal distribution q̂ (uniform weights 1/n) and the conditional distribution q̂_t (weights proportional to generalized propensity scores θ(φ(x_i))), so no sample is discarded. For continuous treatments, a discrete set T̂ of sampled treatment values is used to approximate the integral over the treatment space, with all samples weighted for each t ∈ T̂.

## Method

ORIC characterizes confounding bias as the Wasserstein distance between the marginal covariate distribution q(x)=p(x) and the conditional distribution q_t(x)=p(x|t), both defined over all training samples with different probability weights. The theoretical contribution establishes that both the balancing error and the gap between factual and target outcome estimation errors are upper-bounded by these Wasserstein distances (Theorems 4.1–4.3). The training objective jointly minimizes the factual prediction loss (MSE on observed outcomes) and the sum of entropically-regularized Wasserstein distances W(c_φ, q̂_t, q̂) over sampled treatment values, where the cost c_φ is the L2 distance in the learned representation space. The representation network φ and prediction head ψ are trained end-to-end, with the Sinkhorn algorithm solving the inner optimal transport problem at each iteration. A generalized propensity score model θ(φ(x)) assigns per-sample weights for the conditional distribution, avoiding the need to split data by treatment value.

## Results

On the continuous treatment setting (Table 1), ORIC achieves the lowest √AMSE across all datasets: 0.1098 on Synthetic (β=0.25), 0.3595 on IHDP, and 0.1507 on News, outperforming the strongest competitor VCNet+TR (0.1155, 0.3712, 0.1675 respectively). On the binary treatment setting (Table 2), ORIC attains the best √PEHE on IHDP (1.1129) and News (1.7183), and the best MAE on News (0.1624), though DKLite achieves a lower √AMSE on News (1.9610 vs. 2.3972). The method demonstrates robustness across confounding bias levels (β = 0.25 to 1.0) in the synthetic experiments.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ORIC consistently outperforms all 14 baselines on the continuous treatment √AMSE metric, with the closest competitor being VCNet+TR (Nie et al., 2021), which ORIC beats by 5–10% relative improvement across all settings (e.g., 0.1098 vs. 0.1155 on Synthetic β=0.25; 0.3595 vs. 0.3712 on IHDP). Data-splitting methods (DRNet, ADMIT) suffer from reduced sample sizes per group, yielding √AMSE values 30–50% higher than ORIC on IHDP. Traditional statistical baselines (KNN, BART, GPS) are substantially outperformed, with KNN achieving 0.8364 on IHDP versus ORIC's 0.3595. In the binary treatment setting, ORIC wins on √PEHE for both IHDP (1.1129 vs. CFRNet's 1.2809) and News (1.7183 vs. DragonNet's 1.7916), and on MAE for News (0.1624 vs. DKLite's 0.2328). However, DKLite achieves a lower √AMSE on News in the binary setting (1.9610 vs. ORIC's 2.3972), and CFRNet achieves a lower MAE on IHDP (0.1582 vs. ORIC's 0.2134), indicating ORIC's advantage is most pronounced on the continuous treatment and √PEHE metrics.

## Relation to Prior Work

ORIC departs from the dominant data-splitting paradigm in causal inference (CFRNet, ADMIT, DRNet) that aligns distributions between subpopulations receiving different treatment values. Instead, it reframes the problem by modeling the conditional distribution p(x|t) as a probability measure over all training samples weighted by generalized propensity scores, then aligning it with the marginal distribution p(x) via Wasserstein distance. This connects to the optimal transport literature in causal inference (CausalOT, ESCFR) but applies OT to a fundamentally different pair of distributions—two measures on the same sample set rather than two disjoint subsets. The theoretical analysis builds on RKHS-based generalization bounds (Shalit et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2022) but extends them to the continuous treatment setting with a unified formulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The no-splitting formulation is practically important because it preserves the full sample budget for distribution estimation, which is critical in settings with limited data. The unified treatment of binary and continuous treatments under a single theoretical framework (Theorems 4.1–4.3) provides a clean generalization bound that prior methods lacked for continuous treatments. However, the method's O(n²d) computational cost limits scalability, and the ignorability assumption remains a fundamental constraint—unobserved confounders are explicitly acknowledged as an open challenge. Future work on complex treatments (bundles, graphs) and proxy-variable approaches for unobserved confounding would extend the practical reach of this framework.
