## Problem

Genetic algorithms require hand-crafted, problem-specific crossover and mutation operators to respect domain constraints, making them difficult to generalize across tasks. Meanwhile, deep generative models produce solutions in a single sequential pass without the iterative, population-based refinement that GAs provide. This paper asks whether the evolutionary search mechanism of GAs can be integrated directly into the generation process of pretrained sequential models in a problem-agnostic manner, rather than being chained after generation or relying on domain-specific rules.

## Input / Output

The method requires a pretrained sequential generative policy (e.g., a GNN producing edge heatmaps for routing, an LSTM for SMILES generation, or a language model for prompt generation), a problem instance, and a reward function. It produces a population of discrete sequences (edge sequences, text prompts, or SMILES strings) that have been iteratively refined over N_iter evolutionary generations. A key implementation detail is that crossover is realized as a token-level mask applied during autoregressive decoding, so the policy's architecture and training remain unchanged; the only modification is restricting the sampling vocabulary at each step to tokens observed in the selected parents.

## Method

NGS defines crossover as parent-conditioned generation: given two parent sequences, the offspring is sampled from the pretrained policy with its vocabulary restricted to the union of tokens appearing in both parents (Eq. 2), implemented simply by masking out-of-vocabulary tokens during sequential decoding. Mutation removes this restriction, occurring either forcibly when all parent tokens are infeasible (constraint-enforcing) or stochastically with probability μ. Selection and replacement use rank-based prioritized sampling (a stochastic elitism scheme) to balance exploitation and exploration across the population. The algorithm iterates for N_iter generations, each producing N_off offspring from N_pop parents, progressively steering the population toward higher-reward solutions. Because the genetic operators are defined purely in terms of token vocabulary masking, NGS is problem-agnostic and can be plugged into any sequential generative model without redesigning operators for each domain.

## Results

On TSP with 500 nodes, NGS achieves a 0.322% optimality gap, outperforming Sampling (1.827%), Beam Search (20.637%), MCTS (1.324%), and ACO (1.733%) by large margins. On CVRP with 500 nodes, NGS reaches 1.840% gap versus 1.982% (Sampling) and 1.975% (ACO). In molecular design (PMO benchmark, 10K evaluations), NGS achieves the best average Top-10 score of 0.835, surpassing Graph GA (0.768), STONED (0.748), SynNet (0.703), and SMILES GA (0.686), winning 8 out of 10 tasks. In red-teaming, NGS shows the strongest transfer robustness, e.g., achieving 0.59 toxicity on Qwen2.5-7B versus 0.40 for the best sampling baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On routing problems (TSP, CVRP, PCTSP, OP), NGS consistently outperforms all baselines—Sampling, Beam Search, MCTS, and ACO—using the same pretrained GNN policy, with the largest gains on TSP-500 where NGS (0.322%) beats MCTS (1.324%) by over 4× and ACO (1.733%) by 5.4×. Even with a 10× larger search budget, NGS(long) (0.110% on TSP-500) surpasses ACO(long) (1.162%) and MCTS(long) (1.268%), demonstrating superior search efficiency. Against LEHD+RRC (Luo et al., 2023), NGS achieves a lower gap (0.152% vs. 0.172% on TSP-500) while being problem-agnostic rather than routing-specific. In molecular design, NGS (avg. 0.835) beats all four GA baselines—Graph GA (0.768), STONED (0.748), SynNet (0.703), and SMILES GA (0.686)—winning 8 of 10 PMO tasks, with the largest margin on jnk3 (0.891 vs. 0.830 for Graph GA). In red-teaming, NGS significantly outperforms all sampling-based decoders (top-k, top-p, temperature) in the transfer setting across five unseen victim models, e.g., 0.59 vs. 0.40 on Qwen2.5-7B and 0.23 vs. 0.08 on phi-4, while maintaining competitive diversity; beam search achieves higher source toxicity but collapses in diversity (0.21–0.24 vs. NGS's 0.79) and transfers poorly.

## Relation to Prior Work

NGS builds on the classical genetic algorithm framework (Holland, 1992) and rank-based prioritized sampling (Tripp et al., 2020) for selection, but departs fundamentally from prior work that chains GAs *after* deep generative models or relies on hand-crafted, domain-specific crossover/mutation operators (e.g., Graph GA for molecules, edge-assembly crossover for TSP). The key conceptual shift is integrating GA operators *into* the sequential generation process itself: crossover becomes token-restriction (masking the vocabulary to tokens present in parents) and mutation becomes un-masking, both governed by the pretrained policy's conditional distribution. This positions NGS between SynNet's embedding-conditioned offspring generation and LLM-as-black-box GA approaches, offering a simpler, fully problem-agnostic mechanism that requires no prompt engineering or domain rules.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

NGS provides a drop-in test-time search upgrade for any sequential generative model, eliminating the need to design task-specific GA operators—a significant practical simplification demonstrated across routing, adversarial prompt generation, and molecular design. The population-based iterative generation also confers robustness to distribution shifts (evidenced by strong transfer performance in red-teaming), suggesting it could serve as a general-purpose decoding strategy. Key limitations include dependence on the pretrained policy's coverage of high-quality solutions, the introduction of GA hyperparameters, and the absence of theoretical convergence guarantees. The authors identify integration with model-based optimization frameworks as a promising direction, particularly for settings with conservative proxy models.
