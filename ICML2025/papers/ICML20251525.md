## Problem

In-context learning with language models is highly volatile: predictions depend sensitively on prompt templates, demonstration order, and the number of examples, and many-shot ICL is further constrained by context-length limits and quadratic inference-time growth. Prior activation-vector methods (Function Vectors, Task Vectors) were designed for synthetic tasks and show significant performance degradation when applied to real-world ICL benchmarks, leaving a gap in practical, training-free techniques that can reliably steer ICL performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251525/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference time, the method takes a single-shot (or few-shot) text query and a set of pre-extracted Iterative Vectors — one vector per class per attention layer, plus a query vector. The vectors are added to the model's attention activations specifically at the input-output separator token positions (the newline tokens that trigger label generation), scaled by an inference strength α₂. The output is a standard class-label prediction obtained by applying softmax to the label-token logits, identical in format to clean ICL but with the activation edit applied.

## Method

IVs extract 'meta-gradients' by subtracting zero-shot query activations from complete-episode activations at input-output separator positions across all attention layers, then averaging per class to obtain preliminary vectors. The key innovation is an iterative refinement loop that emulates batched gradient descent without backpropagation: the training set is split into sequential batches, and for each subsequent batch, the model's activations during extraction are edited by the cumulative average of all previously computed batch vectors (scaled by extraction strength α₁), so that each batch's vectors are refined by the task signal accumulated so far. The final Iterative Vectors are the average across all batches. This mechanism is grounded in the dual-form interpretation of ICL as meta-optimization, where demonstrations act as gradient-like updates applied through attention; IVs make these implicit updates explicit, stable, and reusable across future queries without recomputation.

## Results

Across four models (GPT-J-6B, Llama-2-7B, Llama-3.1-8B, Llama-2-13B) and 13 classification tasks, Iterative Vectors (IV) achieve the highest average macro-F1 in every model setting: 41.76 (GPT-J-6B), 46.59 (Llama-2-7B), 48.79 (Llama-3.1-8B), and 52.89 (Llama-2-13B), representing an average gain of +3.2% over standard one-shot ICL. The improvement grows with model size, reaching +4.69 points over the Clean baseline on Llama-2-13B. On the emoji dataset (20-class), IV matches 2-shot ICL performance (12.90 macro-F1) while running 41% faster than 2-shot and 57% faster than 3-shot inference, and reduces extraction time by 95% compared to Function Vectors.

## Baselines & Comparisons

IV outperforms both Function Vectors (FV) and Task Vectors (TV) on average macro-F1 across all four models: by +2.12 to +3.33 over FV and +2.38 to +4.44 over TV. Critically, FV and TV exhibit performance degradation below the Clean (standard ICL) baseline in 37% of model-task combinations (3 out of 8), whereas IV never falls below Clean on average. The gains are most pronounced on larger models: on Llama-2-13B, IV achieves 52.89 vs. 49.56 (FV) and 48.45 (TV), a +4.69 improvement over Clean. In terms of practicality, FV extraction is approximately 20× slower than IV (438.3 min vs. 23.75 min on the emoji dataset) due to its exhaustive search over all attention heads, while TV, though fast, shows catastrophic failure in 50% of model averages. On the micro-F1 metric, IV also leads in 15 of 16 model-task settings, with the sole exception being GPT-J-6B where FV edges ahead (48.51 vs. 47.81), which the authors attribute to FV's bias toward majority classes inflating micro-F1 while degrading macro-F1.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper operationalizes the theoretical insight from Dai et al. (2023) that pretrained LMs act as meta-optimizers performing implicit gradient descent during ICL, grounded in the dual form of neural networks (Irie et al., 2022), by extracting and iteratively refining the resulting 'meta-gradients' as activation vectors. It departs from prior activation vector methods—Function Vectors (Todd et al., 2023) and Task Vectors (Hendel et al., 2023)—which were validated only on synthetic tasks, by adapting them to real-world ICL benchmarks and showing their direct applicability is limited. Unlike prompt tuning or PEFT methods that require backpropagation, IVs operate entirely in activation space during inference, and unlike generative steering methods (Subramani et al., 2022; Turner et al., 2023) that target transfer or truthfulness, IVs are specifically designed for the ICL setting with class-conditional vectors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

IVs offer a practical, backpropagation-free mechanism to boost ICL accuracy while reducing prompt length and inference time, which is valuable for deployment scenarios where context windows are constrained or many examples must be processed. The finding that prior activation vector methods (FV, TV) degrade performance in 37% of model-task combinations on real ICL tasks is a useful caution against naively transferring synthetic-task techniques. Limitations include evaluation restricted to single-token classification tasks, reliance on a fixed verbalizer template, and testing only up to 70B parameters; open questions include whether the meta-gradient extraction generalizes to generative or multi-turn ICL, whether the iterative refinement converges to a meaningful fixed point, and how IVs interact with other inference-time interventions.
