## Problem

MoE-based LLMs on memory-constrained edge devices suffer 15–30× latency overheads over full-memory inference, with 50–85% of that latency caused by frequent expert swapping. The root cause is that standard token-level routing is dominated by token-identity information, producing highly sparse and temporally inconsistent expert activations across consecutive tokens. No prior work has addressed this from an architectural perspective; existing solutions rely on engineering heuristics like quantization, pruning, or frequency-based expert retention.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt (batch size 1, mimicking single-user edge-device scenarios) and autoregressively generates text tokens under a fixed GPU memory budget (e.g., 8 GiB on NVIDIA Jetson Xavier NX), where only a subset of experts can reside in memory at any time. During the decode phase, the KV cache of previously generated tokens is retained to compute attention scores for semantic group membership without additional memory overhead. The output is a sequence of generated tokens with significantly reduced expert-swapping events compared to token-level MoE baselines.

## Method

Oracle-MoE replaces the standard linear-gate token-level routing with a two-stage oracle-space routing mechanism. First, attention scores are used to partition tokens into semantic groups (maximally connected components in a thresholded attention graph), and each group's embedding is computed as the mean of its member token embeddings, forming a compact 'oracle space' that preserves high-level semantics while suppressing token-identity noise. Second, K-means clustering is performed in this oracle space (after SVD dimensionality reduction) to assign experts, and each token is routed to the expert corresponding to its semantic group's cluster. A theoretical result (Theorem 1) proves that oracle-space routing yields strictly lower Consecutive Semantic Difference than token-level routing with high probability. Training proceeds in two phases: a warm-up stage with standard token-level MoE, followed by a one-time clustering step and oracle-space routing for the remainder of pretraining; at inference, the decode phase leverages the KV cache to incrementally update semantic group embeddings as new tokens arrive.

## Results

On downstream tasks (TriviaQA, GLUE, MAG, Sci-Cite, XSum), Oracle-MoE matches or slightly exceeds Switch Transformer across all four model sizes (195M–2.06B), with average scores of 30.75 vs 30.73 (195M), 33.28 vs 32.68 (295M), 36.35 vs 35.86 (729M), and 44.09 vs 43.73 (2.06B). On the 729M model under 25% memory budget, Oracle-MoE adds only ~3s latency over full-memory inference, while Switch Transformer incurs up to 2000% additional latency. First-token latency drops from 12.77–23.43s (Switch with various swapping strategies) to 4.91s for Oracle-MoE, and expert swapping accounts for only 50% of total latency (vs >99% for Switch Transformer).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Switch Transformer (the primary token-level MoE baseline), Oracle-MoE achieves comparable or marginally better downstream task performance (average scores within 0.02–0.66 points across four model sizes) while dramatically reducing inference latency: at 25% memory on the 729M model, Oracle-MoE adds only ~3s over full-memory inference versus up to 2000% latency increase for Switch Transformer. First-token latency is reduced from 12.77s (Switch+SwapMoE) or 22.4–23.4s (Switch+FIFO/LRU) to 4.91s. Expert swapping contributes only 50% of total latency for Oracle-MoE versus >99% for Switch Transformer. Against SwapMoE specifically, Oracle-MoE wins on first-token latency (4.91s vs 12.77s) and overall memory-latency curves, as SwapMoE still requires expert swapping during prefilling while Oracle-MoE loads only 1–2 experts. The paper also reports that on highly diverse data (randomly mixed sentences), Oracle-MoE changes experts only 12.20 times per 100 tokens versus 90.54 for Switch Transformer.

## Relation to Prior Work

Oracle-MoE builds directly on the Switch Transformer architecture (Fedus et al., 2022), replacing its token-level linear gate routing with a semantic-group-level routing mechanism in a compact 'oracle space' derived from attention scores. It departs conceptually from prior memory-constrained MoE inference work (SwapMoE, EdgeMoE, SE-MoE), which addressed expert swapping latency through engineering heuristics like quantization, expert pruning, and frequency-based retention, by instead attacking the root cause: the temporal inconsistency of token-level routing. The semantic group construction draws on attention analysis literature (Kovaleva et al., 2019; Clark et al., 2019) and meta-embedding techniques (Poerner et al., 2019), while the theoretical grounding leverages representation space analysis (Kamath et al., 2019) and high-dimensional probability arguments.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides a principled, architecture-level solution to the dominant latency bottleneck in edge MoE inference (expert swapping), showing that routing in a semantically compact space can reduce swapping by over an order of magnitude without sacrificing downstream task performance. However, the evaluation is limited to GPT-2-scale models (200M–2B) on a single hardware platform (Jetson Xavier NX), leaving open whether the approach scales to truly large MoE models (70B+) where expert counts and memory pressures are far greater. The assumption of semantic locality across consecutive tokens, while well-motivated for chat and generation scenarios, may degrade in highly diverse or adversarial input settings, and the warm-up phase plus SVD dimensionality reduction add practical complexity that has not been stress-tested at production scale.
