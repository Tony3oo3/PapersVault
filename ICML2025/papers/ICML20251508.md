## Problem

Token-level discrepancies in mathematical reasoning trajectories have not been systematically explored, and standard DPO-based preference optimization struggles with math tasks because lexical overlap between positive and negative examples causes the model to reduce likelihood of correct tokens. This paper identifies 'critical tokens'—specific positions in incorrect trajectories whose presence deterministically leads to wrong answers—and shows they diverge from human-annotated error tokens in 65–87% of cases, revealing a previously unexplored failure mechanism.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251508/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes pairwise preference data (problem x, positive trajectory y^p, negative trajectory y^n) along with token-level contrastive scores s_t computed for each token in the negative trajectory. The contrastive scores are derived from two auxiliary models: a positive model SFT-trained on a single correct trajectory per problem and a negative model trained on the most frequent incorrect trajectories (top-50% by answer frequency). The final output is a refined policy model that generates reasoning trajectories ending in a numerical answer, evaluated at temperature 0 on GSM8K and MATH500.

## Method

The core mechanism is contrastive estimation: two lightweight models are trained via SFT (one on correct trajectories, one on frequent incorrect trajectories), and the token-level score s_t is computed as (1+β)log P^p(y_t|x,y_<t) − β log P^n(y_t|x,y_<t) − log Z, where low s_t flags critical tokens. This replaces prohibitively expensive rollout sampling (64 samples per token) with a single forward pass per model, reducing cost to ~0.002% of rollout sampling on GSM8K. The cDPO loss then modifies only the negative-example reward in DPO, weighting each token's log-likelihood ratio by (1−s_t) so that critical tokens (low s_t, high 1−s_t) receive stronger penalty while non-critical tokens are barely affected. Theoretically, the CE distribution is shown to remain Gaussian with mean shifted away from the negative distribution by β(μ_p−μ_n), and empirically achieves AUC 0.77–0.84 against rollout-based ground truth.

## Results

On GSM8K, cDPO achieves an average accuracy of 77.2% across Llama-3-8B (67.9%), Llama-3-70B (90.8%), and DeepSeek-math-7B (72.9%), outperforming the strongest baseline RPO (75.4% avg) by 1.8 points. On MATH500, cDPO reaches 33.3% average accuracy (Llama-3-70B: 45.0%, DeepSeek-math-7B: 35.2%), surpassing the baseline (32.1%) and all preference-optimization methods. All cDPO results are statistically significant (p < 0.005). The method also generalizes to Qwen-2.5 models, improving GSM8K from 85.5% to 87.5% (7B) and MATH500 from 49.2% to 54.0% (7B).

## Baselines & Comparisons

cDPO consistently outperforms all four baselines across both datasets and all three model architectures. On GSM8K, cDPO (77.2% avg) beats RPO (75.4%) by 1.8 points, TokenDPO (71.7%) by 5.5 points, and DPO-from-base (71.3%) by 5.9 points; the largest single-model gain is on DeepSeek-math-7B where cDPO reaches 72.9% vs. RPO's 68.9%. On MATH500, cDPO (33.3% avg) edges out TokenDPO (32.5%) and RPO (32.3%) by roughly 1 point, while DPO variants actually underperform the baseline (31.0% and 30.7% vs. 32.1%), confirming the known issue of DPO reducing positive-example likelihood in reasoning tasks. Notably, DPO-from-SFT-start degrades MATH500 performance below the baseline on Llama-3-8B (14.6% vs. 18.6%), whereas cDPO improves it to 19.6%. The learning-curve analysis (Figure 4) further shows that cDPO uniquely increases the log-probability of chosen sequences while simultaneously decreasing that of rejected sequences, whereas DPO reduces both and RPO only partially suppresses rejected sequences.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends DPO (Rafailov et al., 2024) from example-level to token-level preference optimization by introducing token-specific reward weights derived from contrastive estimation (Gutmann & Hyvärinen, 2010). It departs from prior token-level DPO variants like TokenDPO (Zeng et al., 2024) and RPO (Liu et al., 2024; Pang et al., 2024), which apply uniform or KL-based adjustments across all tokens, by selectively penalizing only 'critical tokens' identified via a positive/negative model contrast. The contrastive estimation mechanism is repurposed from its traditional role in inference-time decoding (contrastive decoding, Li et al., 2023; O'Brien & Lewis, 2023) to a training-time annotation tool, avoiding the need for external LLMs or human step-level annotations used by TLCR (Yoon et al., 2024) and Step-DPO (Lai et al., 2024).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that critical tokens diverge from human-annotated error tokens in 65–87% of cases challenges the assumption that the 'wrong' token is the one that causally derails a trajectory, suggesting that reasoning failures often originate earlier than the visible error. The 0.002% computational cost relative to rollout sampling makes token-level process supervision practically scalable, which is a significant step toward fine-grained reasoning alignment without expensive external models. However, the method is validated only on mathematical reasoning with LoRA fine-tuning, and the modest gains over RPO (~1–2 points) raise questions about whether the critical-token signal captures something fundamentally different from what existing baselines already exploit. Open questions include generalization to non-mathematical reasoning, interaction with larger-scale RLHF pipelines, and whether the Gaussian distributional assumption in the theoretical analysis holds for real LLM token probabilities.
