## Problem

Few-shot in-context learning (ICL) for mathematical reasoning is unreliable: demonstrations can degrade performance, with 1.83%–34.96% of problems correct in zero-shot becoming incorrect in one-shot. Existing analyses identify empirical factors (similarity, diversity, perplexity) but provide no theoretical quantification of when demonstrations actually reduce prediction loss, nor a principled mechanism for deciding when to reject demonstrations entirely.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a test problem, a pool of candidate demonstrations (each comprising a problem context and its labeled solution), and the inference LLM's attention projection matrices (W_K, W_V) to compute per-sample scores. It outputs either a set of k selected demonstrations to prepend to the test prompt, or a rejection decision that triggers a zero-shot fallback. The scoring is computed by encoding each demonstration and the test problem individually into the LLM (O(M+N) API calls rather than O(MN) pairwise), making it practical for large pools.

## Method

The paper proves (Theorems 1 and 2) that the empirical prediction loss under ICL is bounded by two quantities: an LLM-oriented semantic similarity Sim(X) = ‖h_test − W_K^T W_Q h‖ measuring distance in the inference LLM's own encoding space, and an inference stability Stab(X) = ‖W_V h / √d‖ measuring the gradient norm on the demonstration. LMS3 selects demonstrations by minimizing the product Score(X) = Sim(X)·Stab(X), which avoids the hyperparameter tuning required by additive scoring. A novel rejection mechanism then checks whether the top-k scored demonstrations also rank in the top-λ smallest by Sim(X); if not, all demonstrations are rejected and the model falls back to zero-shot, preventing the negative effects observed empirically. The theoretical framework interprets the Transformer attention in ICL as a linear gradient step (following Dai et al., 2023) and applies influence-function analysis to derive sufficient conditions for loss reduction, extending naturally from one-shot to k-shot via an additive decomposition.

## Results

In the one-shot setting, LMS3 achieves answer accuracy of 0.854/0.447/0.124 on MAWPS/GSM8K/MATH with Llama2-13B and 0.966/0.837/0.353 with Llama3-8B, outperforming all 10 baselines across every dataset–LLM combination. LMS3 is the only method that consistently improves over the zero-shot baseline on all three datasets for both LLMs (e.g., 0.124 vs. 0.096 zero-shot on MATH with Llama2-13B; 0.353 vs. 0.324 on MATH with Llama3-8B). On the harder MATH benchmark, LMS3 gains up to 2.2 percentage points over the strongest baseline (Influence: 0.102) with Llama2-13B and 0.7 points over OpenAI (0.346) with Llama3-8B.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LMS3 wins against all 10 baselines (Random, Best-validate, TF-IDF, BM25, T5, BGEM3, OpenAI, SPELL, Influence, IDS) on every one of the six dataset–LLM combinations in the one-shot setting, with statistically significant improvements (p ≤ 0.05). The largest margins appear on MATH with Llama2-13B, where LMS3 (0.124) surpasses the next-best Influence (0.102) by 2.2 points and zero-shot (0.096) by 2.8 points. On GSM8K with Llama2-13B, LMS3 (0.447) leads T5 (0.426) and TF-IDF (0.424) by roughly 2 points. A critical distinction is that LMS3 is the only method that consistently beats zero-shot across all datasets; for example, on MAWPS with Llama2-13B, most baselines (Random 0.816, SPELL 0.797, OpenAI 0.805) actually fall below the zero-shot score of 0.835, while LMS3 reaches 0.854. The paper also shows that adding LMS3's rejection mechanism to other selection methods (Table 3) yields consistent gains, confirming the mechanism's general applicability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the meta-gradient interpretation of in-context learning from Dai et al. (2023), which approximates Transformer attention as linear attention and frames ICL as implicit gradient descent. However, prior theoretical work (Dai et al., 2023; Han et al., 2023; Yan et al., 2024) provided only qualitative mechanistic explanations without quantifying when demonstrations actually help or hurt reasoning performance. The key departure is providing the first formal bound decomposing demonstration impact into LLM-oriented semantic similarity and a newly introduced inference stability term, and extending this to k-shot settings. On the methodological side, LMS3 unifies and generalizes existing Similar-ICL approaches (TF-IDF, BM25, T5, BGE-M3, OpenAI embeddings) by showing they correspond to different choices of the W_K^T·W_Q projection, while adding the stability criterion and a rejection mechanism absent from all prior selection methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that one-shot ICL causes LLMs to fail on 1.83%–34.96% of problems they solved correctly in zero-shot challenges the widespread assumption that providing examples monotonically improves reasoning, and the rejection mechanism offers the first principled way to decide when to withhold demonstrations. The theoretical framework gives practitioners actionable conditions (similarity threshold, stability bound) for when ICL is beneficial, though it relies on a linear attention approximation and a positive-definite Hessian assumption that may not hold in all layers or architectures. Open questions include whether the additive independence assumption in Theorem 2 (k-shot) holds when demonstrations interact, how the theory extends to multimodal or code-based reasoning, and whether the LLM-parameter-dependent selection criterion can be further decoupled from specific model access for API-only settings.
