## Problem

Transductive node embedding methods like node2vec (N2V) can only produce embeddings for nodes present during training, making them inapplicable to new nodes that appear in real-world graphs over time. This restricts their use to the transductive setting and leaves a gap for inductive node representation learning where test nodes are entirely unseen during embedding training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251327/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a graph's edge structure and N2V embeddings trained only on the training subgraph, then iteratively propagates embeddings to unseen validation and test nodes. A lookup vector tracks which nodes have received embeddings, and the procedure runs for enough iterations (plus a configurable delay) to cover all nodes with a path to at least one training node. Optionally, the resulting iN2V embeddings are concatenated with original node features before being fed to a downstream GNN (MLP, GraphSAGE, GAT, or GIN) for node classification.

## Method

iN2V consists of two components: a post-hoc propagation algorithm and modifications to N2V training. The post-hoc algorithm iteratively assigns each unembedded node the mean embedding of its already-embedded neighbors, while simultaneously updating existing training-node embeddings via a convex combination (controlled by λ) of their own embedding and their mean neighbor embedding—unlike Feature Propagation, which freezes training embeddings. This dual update prevents all embeddings from collapsing to a graph average and efficiently propagates information through long chains of test nodes. The N2V training is modified in two ways: a sampling-based approach that replaces some node embeddings with their mean neighborhood embedding during training (simulating the post-hoc step), and a loss-based approach that adds a 'close' loss pulling each node toward its mean neighbor embedding and a 'div' loss promoting diversity among a node's neighbors, both weighted by hyperparameters α and β.

## Results

Using GraphSAGE as the downstream model, iN2V achieves an average accuracy of 63.95% (with loss-based modifications) across all 9 datasets and 5 training splits, outperforming Feature Propagation (63.20%) and plain inductive N2V (59.45%). On homophilic datasets (Cora, CiteSeer, PubMed, Computers, Photo, WikiCS), iN2V gains ~1 point over Feature Propagation; on heterophilic datasets (Actor, Amazon-ratings, Roman-empire), the gain is ~0.7 points. In the most data-scarce setting (10% training), iN2V outperforms Feature Propagation by 1.2 points, and on Cora, Computers, and Amazon-ratings it even surpasses the original graph features. When combined with original features, iN2V embeddings add ~10 points over N2V-only input, with MLP benefiting more than GraphSAGE.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against plain inductive N2V (where test nodes simply have no embeddings), iN2V wins by a large margin on all datasets: e.g., on Cora at 10% training, iN2V reaches 78.88% vs. 42.18% for inductive N2V, and on PubMed 79.93% vs. 66.02%. Against Feature Propagation (the closest comparable baseline that also extends embeddings to test nodes), iN2V achieves a consistent but modest advantage: +1.0 point average on homophilic datasets and +0.7 on heterophilic datasets with GraphSAGE, or +1.3 points with MLP. The advantage is largest in data-scarce settings (10–20% training: +1.2 points) and shrinks at 60–80% training (+0.8 points), where both methods have more training nodes to propagate from. The notable exceptions are Actor and Roman-empire, where all N2V-based methods (including iN2V) perform near the largest-class baseline (~25% and ~14% respectively), because the extreme heterophily and low average degree make random-walk embeddings uninformative. When original graph features are concatenated with the iN2V embeddings, the combined input outperforms original features alone by ~3 points with MLP, confirming that the structural information in N2V embeddings is complementary to the dataset-provided features.

## Relation to Prior Work

iN2V extends the node2vec (Grover & Leskovec, 2016) embedding framework from its native transductive setting to inductive graphs by introducing a post-hoc iterative propagation procedure. It departs from Feature Propagation (Rossi et al., 2022), which keeps training-node embeddings fixed during propagation, by allowing them to adapt via a convex combination with their neighborhood mean (λ < 1). The paper also modifies N2V training with a sampling-based augmentation and a pair of auxiliary losses (closeness to neighborhood mean + neighbor diversity) to better prepare embeddings for inductive extension, though these modifications contribute only marginally compared to the post-hoc step itself.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

iN2V provides a simple, plug-in mechanism to make any shallow random-walk embedding usable in inductive node classification, which is practically important for dynamic graphs where new nodes appear without features. However, the approach inherits N2V's fundamental limitation on heterophilic graphs (Actor, Roman-empire), where performance collapses to majority-class prediction, and it requires connectivity between training and test nodes. The finding that the N2V training modifications contribute very little (~0.2 points) compared to the post-hoc extension suggests the core value lies in the propagation design rather than in embedding quality, opening the question of whether other embedding methods (e.g., struc2vec for heterophilic structures) would benefit more from this framework.
