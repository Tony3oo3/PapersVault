## Problem

Supervised fine-tuning of large language models causes catastrophic forgetting of pretrained knowledge, and while weight-averaging ensembles of pretrained and fine-tuned models empirically mitigate this, no theoretical explanation existed for why ensembling works in over-parameterized settings. More strikingly, the paper identifies an 'overadaptation' phenomenon where the ensemble outperforms the fine-tuned model even on the fine-tuning task itself, a behavior that prior theory (focused on under-parameterized or independently trained ensembles) could not account for.

## Input / Output

The method consumes two sets of model weights—the pretrained foundation model θ₁ and the fine-tuned model θ₂ (or ridge-regularized θ_λ)—and produces a single ensemble via weighted averaging (1−τ)θ₁ + τθ with a tunable coefficient τ ∈ [0,1]. In the theoretical formulation, inputs are min-norm and ridge regression estimators trained on two related linear tasks sharing a common parameter component θ_c, and the output is the excess mean-squared error on both tasks as a function of τ and the regularization parameter λ.

## Method

The paper develops a formal analysis in an over-parameterized linear regression setting where pretraining is modeled as a 'ridgeless' (min-norm) estimator on Task 1 and fine-tuning as either ridgeless regression (capturing overadaptation without early stopping) or ridge regression (capturing early stopping) on Task 2. The key insight is a bias-variance decomposition: the pretrained estimator has high bias on Task 2 (lacking task-specific information), while the ridgeless fine-tuned estimator has high variance from overfitting noisy fine-tuning data. The paper proves that for appropriate τ, the ensemble estimator simultaneously reduces the combined error L_pre + L_ft (mitigating forgetting) and reduces L_ft alone (improving fine-tuning performance), outperforming both individual models and regularization alone. The theoretical conditions include a high-dimensional eigenvalue structure with a few large and many small eigenvalues (matching NTK spectra), a sparse structure for Task 2 reflecting knowledge specialization, and a shared parameter component θ_c between tasks. Empirical validation on Llama-3-8B, Qwen2-7B, and Gemma-2-9B fine-tuned on Dolly confirms that ensembling with Norm-Penalty or DiffNorm-Penalty regularization consistently improves MT-Bench scores while preserving MMLU and Commonsense-QA performance.

## Results

On MT-Bench (instruction-following), ensembling the pre-trained model with the fine-tuned model (Avg-Norm-Penalty) achieves the best scores across all three base models: 5.96 on Llama-3-8B, 7.10 on Qwen2-7B, and 6.83 on Gemma-2-9B, consistently outperforming vanilla fine-tuning (5.68, 6.57, 6.52) by 0.28–0.53 points. The paper further demonstrates that ensembling improves the trade-off between downstream performance (MT-Bench) and upstream retention (Commonsense-QA, MMLU), with ensemble models maintaining higher general-knowledge scores at comparable or better instruction-following quality. In the LoRA comparison (Appendix Table 2), DiffNorm-Penalty+Ensemble scores 5.85 on MT-Bench and 74.49 on Commonsense-QA, while LoRA alone scores 5.83 and 73.71 respectively, though LoRA retains a slight edge on MMLU (65.29 vs. 63.97).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between five fine-tuning variants (vanilla, two regularizers, and their ensembled counterparts) on MT-Bench across Llama-3-8B, Qwen2-7B, and Gemma-2-9B. Ensembling consistently wins: Avg-Norm-Penalty outperforms its non-ensembling counterpart Norm-Penalty by 0.12–0.29 points across all three models, and outperforms vanilla fine-tuning by 0.28–0.53 points. Norm-Penalty also beats DiffNorm-Penalty in nearly all settings, suggesting the choice of regularizer matters. In the LoRA comparison (Appendix Table 2), DiffNorm-Penalty+Ensemble edges out LoRA on MT-Bench (5.85 vs. 5.83) and Commonsense-QA (74.49 vs. 73.71), but trails on MMLU (63.97 vs. 65.29). Notably, adding ensembling on top of LoRA (LoRA+Ensemble) further improves MT-Bench to 5.99 and MMLU to 65.31, confirming that ensembling provides complementary gains regardless of the fine-tuning method used.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the empirical observation from Wortsman et al. (2022b) on weight-averaging ensembling in vision models and Lin et al. (2024) in RLHF to the supervised fine-tuning of LLMs, and crucially provides the first theoretical analysis of why this simple technique works in the over-parameterized regime. It departs from prior ensemble theory (Brown et al., 2005; Allen-Zhu & Li, 2020; Hao et al., 2024), which either focused on under-parameterized settings or independently trained models, by analyzing the specific case of ensembling a pretrained model with its own fine-tuned counterpart. The theoretical framework draws on the benign overfitting literature (Bartlett et al., 2019) and ridge regression theory (Lin & Rosasco, 2017) to decompose excess risk into bias and variance terms in an over-parameterized linear setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean bias-variance decomposition that explains why ensembling pretrained and fine-tuned weights simultaneously improves downstream performance and mitigates forgetting—a phenomenon previously only supported by empirical evidence. This gives practitioners theoretical grounding for the simple practice of weight averaging and suggests that the optimal ensemble weight τ can be understood through the lens of balancing task-specific bias against noise-driven variance. Key limitations include the restriction to a linear setting with specific eigenvalue structures (Condition 1), and the NTK-based connection to nonlinear networks remains informal. Open questions include whether the bias-variance framework generalizes to deep nonlinear architectures, how to select τ without access to a validation set, and whether the theory extends to multi-step fine-tuning or continual learning scenarios.
