## Problem

Existing conformal selection (CS) methods are restricted to univariate responses with selection criteria of the form y > c, yet many practical applications—drug discovery, LLM alignment, precision medicine—require selecting candidates that simultaneously satisfy multiple interdependent criteria, naturally represented as multivariate responses falling within an arbitrary closed target region R ⊆ R^d. No principled method with finite-sample FDR guarantees existed for this multivariate selection problem.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a labeled calibration set {(x_i, y_i)}_{i=1}^n, unlabeled test features {x_{n+j}}_{j=1}^m, a user-specified closed target region R ⊆ R^d (which may be convex, nonconvex, rectangular, spherical, or otherwise irregular), an FDR level q, and a pre-trained multivariate predictor μ̂. It outputs a selection set S ⊆ {1,…,m} of test indices whose responses are deemed to lie in R, along with per-sample conformal p-values. A key practical detail is that for each test sample one must choose a reference point r_{n+j} ∈ R (optimally on ∂R) to compute the test nonconformity score, and for mCS-learn the calibration data is further partitioned into training, validation, and final-calibration subsets (default 8:1:1).

## Method

mCS introduces regional monotonicity—a generalization of univariate monotonicity requiring V(x, y') ≤ V(x, y) for all y' ∈ R^c and y ∈ R—as the key property ensuring conservative conformal p-values and hence finite-sample FDR control via the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. Two score families are proposed: mCS-dist uses a distance-based score V(x,y) = D_1(y, R^c) − D_2(μ̂(x), R^c) where the clipped variant (D_1 = M·1{y ∈ R\∂R}) is shown asymptotically superior for power; and mCS-learn parameterizes the score as V^θ(x,y) = M·1{y ∈ R\∂R} − f_θ(x,y;R) and trains f_θ (e.g., a two-layer MLP) by minimizing a differentiable loss that either approximates the smoothed BH selection size via soft-sorting/soft-ranking (Blondel et al., 2020) or directly penalizes smooth conformal p-values with a balancing coefficient γ. The mCS-learn variant is particularly effective for high-dimensional or nonconvex target regions where closed-form distances are intractable, and Proposition 4.2 guarantees that the score family is expressive enough to contain the optimal FDR-controlled score for any selection task.

## Results

On simulated Task 1 (shifted first orthant, d=30, q=0.3), mCS-dist achieves the highest power among FDR-valid methods, reaching 0.555 in Setting 1 and 0.324 in Setting 4, while CS_int violates FDR (0.72–0.81) and CS_ib is overly conservative (power = 0.000). On Task 2 (spherical region), mCS-dist achieves power 0.760 in Setting 1, outperforming bi (0.636) and mCS-learn (0.534). On the real ADMET dataset (d=15, 22,805 compounds), mCS-dist reaches power 0.759 on Task 2 at q=0.5 while bi selects no compounds (power = 0.000), and mCS-learn achieves 0.589 on the nonconvex Task 3 at q=0.5.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares mCS-dist and mCS-learn against four baselines adapted from univariate conformal selection (Jin & Candès, 2023): CS_int (per-dimension CS with intersection), CS_ib (Bonferroni-corrected CS_int), CS_is (adaptive-level CS_int), and bi (binary indicator + univariate CS). CS_int substantially violates FDR (0.72–0.81 vs. nominal 0.3) by selecting nearly all samples (power ≈ 1.0), while CS_ib is so conservative it selects nothing (FDR = 0.000, power = 0.000). CS_is provides only approximate FDR control and can be compromised (e.g., FDR = 0.374 in Setting 6). Among the methods with valid FDR guarantees (bi, mCS-dist, mCS-learn), mCS-dist and mCS-learn consistently achieve the best and second-best power across all six simulation settings and all three real-data tasks. On the ADMET dataset, the gap is most dramatic: bi selects zero compounds (power = 0.000) due to a weak underlying classifier (F1 = 0.31), while mCS-dist achieves power 0.759 on Task 2 at q=0.5. mCS-learn shows particular advantage for nonconvex target regions (Task 3), where it outperforms mCS-dist (0.589 vs. 0.449 power at q=0.5).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Conformal Selection (CS; Jin & Candès, 2023) from univariate responses to multivariate settings by introducing regional monotonicity as a generalization of the univariate monotonicity condition on nonconformity scores. The conformal p-value machinery and BH-based FDR control are inherited directly from CS and the broader conformal prediction framework (Vovk et al., 2005), while the mCS-learn variant borrows differentiable soft-sorting and soft-ranking operations from Blondel et al. (2020) to make the inherently discrete selection procedure amenable to gradient-based optimization. The paper positions itself against naive multivariate adaptations of CS (per-dimension intersection, Bonferroni correction, binary reduction), showing these either violate FDR or are overly conservative.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method addresses a practical gap in drug discovery, LLM alignment, and precision medicine where selection must satisfy multiple interdependent criteria simultaneously while maintaining rigorous finite-sample FDR guarantees. The mCS-learn variant is particularly valuable for nonconvex or irregular target regions where closed-form distance functions are intractable, and its advantage grows with response dimension. Open questions include extension to hierarchical or structured responses, scalability to very large candidate pools, and whether the learned score family can be made more computationally efficient for high-dimensional settings. The reliance on a separate calibration set and the need to tune the constant M and balancing coefficient γ remain practical considerations.
