## Problem

Obtaining provably sufficient and minimal explanations for neural network predictions requires solving multiple NP-complete verification queries, making the process computationally intractable for large networks and high-dimensional inputs. While verification-based methods guarantee explanation correctness, their scalability remains a major bottleneck, and heuristic alternatives (e.g., Anchors, SIS) lack formal sufficiency guarantees. This paper addresses the gap by making the verification-based explanation process substantially more efficient without sacrificing formal guarantees.

## Input / Output

The method takes a trained neural network classifier, a specific input image (28×28 grayscale for MNIST, 32×32 RGB for CIFAR-10 and GTSRB), and a perturbation radius ε_p (ε_∞ = 0.01 for MNIST/GTSRB, 0.001 for CIFAR-10). It produces a minimal sufficient explanation as a subset of input features (pixels), where for RGB images all three color channels of a pixel are included or excluded together, capping the maximum explanation size at 32×32 rather than 32×32×3. Additionally, the algorithm yields intermediate explanations at each abstraction level, enabling early stopping when a sufficient-but-not-minimal explanation suffices.

## Method

The core contribution is a neuron-merging-based abstraction-refinement loop (Alg. 2) that replaces the original network with a progressively larger sequence of reduced networks. In the abstraction step, neurons with similar behavior over the bounded input set are merged using the Ladner & Althoff construction with Minkowski-sum error bounding, producing an abstract network f' whose sufficient explanations are provably sufficient for the original network f (Prop. 1). In the refinement step, when a subset is found insufficient on f', a counterexample is extracted and checked against the original network; if the counterexample is spurious (an artifact of the abstraction), some merged neurons are split to produce a slightly larger refined network f''. The algorithm starts at ρ=10% of original neurons and increases ρ by 10% per refinement until ρ=100%, with features processed in ascending sensitivity order. CORA serves as the verification backend, and the final output is provably minimal and sufficient for the original network (Prop. 5), while intermediate results provide a fine-grained, multi-level interpretation of the prediction.

## Results

On MNIST, CIFAR-10, and GTSRB, the abstraction-refinement method produces mean explanation sizes of 204.4, 308.2, and 230.6 pixels respectively (under 100s/1000s/10000s timeouts), roughly halving the explanation size compared to the standard verification-based approach (408.7, 448.7, 502.4). Computation time is reduced by 41% (MNIST), 36% (CIFAR-10), and 56% (GTSRB) relative to the standard greedy method. Against heuristic baselines (Anchors, SIS), the proposed method achieves 100% provable sufficiency, whereas Anchors reaches only 25%/3%/17% and SIS only 22%/0%/6% sufficiency across the three datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the standard verification-based greedy method (Bassan & Katz, 2023; Wu et al., 2023), the proposed abstraction-refinement approach wins decisively on both metrics: it produces explanations roughly 50% smaller (e.g., 204.4 vs. 408.7 on MNIST) while also being 36–56% faster in wall-clock time. Against the heuristic Anchors method (Ribeiro et al., 2018), the proposed method achieves 100% provable sufficiency versus Anchors' 25% (MNIST), 3% (CIFAR-10), and 17% (GTSRB), though Anchors is faster (0.56s vs. 121.95s on MNIST). Against SIS (Carter et al., 2019), the gap in sufficiency is even wider (22%/0%/6% for SIS vs. 100% for the proposed method), and SIS is substantially slower (322.72s on MNIST) while still lacking formal guarantees. The key trade-off is that heuristic methods are orders of magnitude faster but provide no formal certification, whereas the proposed method is the only approach that simultaneously guarantees both sufficiency and minimality of the explanation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper transplants the counterexample-guided abstraction-refinement (CEGAR) paradigm from symbolic model checking into the formal explainability setting, specifically applying neuron-merging abstractions (Ladner & Althoff, 2023) that were previously used only for adversarial robustness verification. It builds directly on the greedy minimal sufficient explanation search of Bassan & Katz (2023) and Wu et al. (2023), which invoke a linear number of neural network verification queries on the full network, and replaces this with an iterative process over progressively larger abstract networks. The key conceptual departure is that prior abstraction work in neural network verification targeted robustness certification, whereas here the abstraction is designed so that sufficiency of an explanation on the abstract network implies sufficiency on the original network (Prop. 1), a property that does not automatically carry over from the robustness setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for the formal XAI community: by 41–56% speedup and substantially smaller explanations under timeout, provable explanations become feasible for networks that were previously intractable, and the intermediate explanations at each abstraction level offer a new form of fine-grained interpretability that practitioners can exploit for early stopping. The main limitation is inherited from neural network verification itself—scalability to state-of-the-art architectures remains open—and the method is currently validated only on small-to-medium networks (≤200 hidden neurons) across three image benchmarks. Open questions include whether the abstraction-refinement loop can be adapted to other explanation types (e.g., necessary explanations, probabilistic explanations) and whether the neuron-merging criterion can be made adaptive per-layer rather than using a uniform reduction rate.
