## Problem

Algorithmic comment ranking systems (civility classifiers, diverse approval, bridging algorithms) optimize for conversational quality but can inadvertently suppress legitimate viewpoints from minority groups, undermining the representational ideal of deliberative democracy. This paper asks: what is the price of enforcing a formal representation guarantee—justified representation (JR) from social choice theory—on top of existing quality-oriented scoring functions, and can both objectives be satisfied simultaneously?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252060/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes as input a set of m comments, an approval matrix specifying which of n users approve which comments (e.g., from upvotes or explicit agreement votes), a scoring function f (engagement count, maximin diverse approval across pre-defined groups, or a content-based classifier like Perspective API), and a selection size k. It outputs a set of k comments that maximizes f while satisfying JR (every cohesive group of ≥ n/k users has at least one approved comment in the selected set), along with the 'price of JR'—the ratio of the unconstrained optimal score to the JR-constrained score. The approval matrix is the critical structural input that distinguishes this from pure content-based ranking; the score function may or may not depend on it.

## Method

The paper formulates comment ranking as a constrained optimization: select k comments maximizing a score function f subject to the JR constraint, then analyzes the resulting price of JR across scoring function classes. Theoretically, it proves the price is unbounded for general (approval-independent) scoring functions, at most k for approval-dependent functions like maximin diverse approval, and at most k/(k−γ) when users partition into γ cohesive groups—showing the cost vanishes in the clustered settings where bridging interventions are most relevant. For a more realistic Mallows mixture model of preferences, it derives high-probability bounds showing the price approaches k/(k−γ) as within-group dispersion φ→0. Practically, it implements the GreedyCC approximation algorithm (two-stage: greedily find a small n/k-justifying set, then fill remaining slots with highest-scoring comments) and validates on Remesh data about campus protests, finding prices of 1.05–1.18 across engagement, diverse approval, and Perspective API scoring while reducing unrepresented users from 18%/15%/4% to 5%/4%/2%.

## Results

On the Remesh dataset (10 questions, ~300 participants each, k=8 comments selected), enforcing JR via GreedyCC reduces the fraction of unrepresented users from 18% (engagement), 15% (diverse approval), and 4% (Perspective API) to 5%, 4%, and 2% respectively. The average price of GreedyCC is very low: 1.05 for engagement, 1.06 for maximin diverse approval, and 1.18 for the Perspective API bridging classifier — far below the worst-case bounds of Θ(√k), k, and unbounded respectively. The n/k-justifying set found by GreedyCC has at most 2 comments for all 10 questions, and 47 of 48 resulting JR feeds also satisfy the stronger EJR+ axiom.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external baseline methods but instead contrasts the unconstrained optimal set S* (top-k by score) against the JR-constrained set S_JR^Greedy across three scoring functions. The unconstrained S* satisfies JR only 30% of the time for engagement, 50% for diverse approval, and 100% for the Perspective API — meaning that for engagement and diverse approval, the standard ranking approach frequently leaves cohesive groups unrepresented. Enforcing JR via GreedyCC improves representation for all three political groups (left, center, right) 100%, 100%, and 70% of the time respectively, without relying on any explicit ideology labels. The price of JR is consistently low (1.05–1.18), well below the theoretical worst-case bounds, which the authors attribute to the small n/k-justifying sets (≤2 comments) that arise when user opinions cluster into a few groups. The Perspective API classifier, despite not explicitly targeting JR, already satisfies it in all 10 questions, likely because its bridging attributes appeal to a broader range of groups than the three pre-defined political groups used in diverse approval.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper transplants the justified representation (JR) axiom from multi-winner approval voting (Aziz et al., 2017) into the comment-ranking setting, where prior work has focused exclusively on conversational quality objectives (civility classifiers, diverse approval, bridging scores) without any formal representation guarantee. It extends Elkind et al.'s (2022) price-of-JR analysis—which was limited to the utilitarian/engagement scoring rule—to arbitrary and approval-dependent scoring functions, and introduces a clustered-preference (Mallows mixture) setting that yields tight, near-optimal bounds. Unlike prior JR-in-comment-generation work (Halpern et al., 2023; Fish et al., 2024), the paper treats the score function as exogenous and studies the constrained optimization trade-off, and unlike algorithmic-fairness approaches it requires no demographic labels, relying instead on the users' own approval patterns to identify cohesive groups.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first principled framework showing that proportional-representation guarantees in comment ranking are compatible with quality and engagement objectives when user preferences cluster into a few groups—a condition that holds in the politically polarized settings where bridging interventions are most relevant. Key open questions remain: the approval matrix must be inferred from engagement signals (upvotes, likes) that may diverge from genuine approval, the experiments are offline re-rankings of historical Remesh data without A/B testing, and the analysis is limited to the base JR axiom rather than stronger variants (EJR, EJR+, BJR) or direct ranking extensions. For platform designers, the finding that a small n/k-justifying set (≤2 comments) suffices in practice suggests the JR constraint is cheap to enforce, but deployment on live platforms with heterogeneous content and incomplete voting remains untested.
