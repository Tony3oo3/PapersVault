## Problem

ML model licensing lacks standardization: publishers freely mix OSS licenses (Apache, GPL), free-content licenses (CC), and model-specific licenses (OpenRAIL, Llama2/3) without clear guidance, creating three compounding risks—license mismatch (terms undefined for model weights), license proliferation (copyleft violations in derivatives), and license conflict (incompatible terms coexisting in merged models). The implicit dependency structure of ML (fine-tuning, adapters, quantization, merging) makes it nearly impossible to trace the full supply chain, so traditional OSS compliance tools fail, leaving the community in a state where widespread, undetected legal noncompliance is likely.

## Input / Output

The analysis consumes self-reported HF model card metadata (license labels, base-model declarations) and crawled dependency trees from the top 8K downloaded models, yielding a graph of 151,302 models and 146,818 typed edges (finetune, adapter, quantization, merge). Outputs include a 15-point license clarity score per license across four dimensions (copyright, patent, ownership, comprehensiveness), per-model noncompliance flags across six conflict categories with counts of affected models and their reuse-method breakdowns, and a complete set of reusable ModelGo Licenses (MGLs) accompanied by structured Model Sheets that tabulate every grant, restriction, and distribution requirement in a machine-readable checklist format.

## Method

The paper builds a quantitative compliance framework by first crawling HF model trees to construct a typed dependency graph, then classifying each edge into four reuse methods (finetune, adapter, quantization, merge) and mapping them to legal concepts (derivative vs. collection vs. transformed copy). It scores 25+ licenses on a 15-item clarity rubric tailored to model publishing (e.g., remote-access governance, derivative ownership, patent grants) to quantify how poorly OSS and CC licenses fit the ML context. Noncompliance is then detected along three axes: (1) mismatch—flagging models licensed under non-model-specific licenses; (2) proliferation—checking whether derivatives of copyleft or copyleft-style-licensed models adopt incompatible or null licenses; and (3) conflict—encoding four known incompatible license pairs (ND, FSF-freedom, CC-freedom, Llama2/3-exclusive) as graph constraints and flagging violations. To address the identified gaps, the authors draft the ModelGo Licenses (MGLs), a reusable, element-based license family (BY, NC, ND, RAI, OS) with a companion Model Sheet that distills all grants and restrictions into a standardized checklist, submitted to OSI for review.

## Results

Across 151,302 models and 146,818 dependencies on Hugging Face, the paper finds that 81.8% of models suffer from license mismatch (using OSS or free-content licenses not designed for ML). Copyleft-style terms (e.g., in RAILs, Llama2/3) affect 63.5% of qualified models, while direct copyleft violations occur in 38.5% of qualified cases. Overall, 2.2% of models exhibit at least one detected license conflict, with the most severe cases being Llama3-exclusive merge conflicts (56.6% noncompliance among qualified models) and Llama2-exclusive merge conflicts (55.0%). The proposed ModelGo Licenses (MGLs) achieve a perfect clarity score of 15.0/15, compared to 11.5 for AGPL-3.0 and 4.5 for MIT.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not benchmark a new method against baselines in the traditional sense; instead, it compares existing license families (OSS, free-content, model-specific) on clarity and conflict-proneness. In the clarity assessment (Table 1), model-specific licenses like MGLs (15.0) and AI2-ImpACT (14.0) substantially outperform OSS licenses (AGPL-3.0 at 11.5, MIT at 4.5) and free-content licenses (CC-BY at 9.5, CC0 at 8.0) on ML-specific dimensions such as derivative ownership, output rights, and remote access governance. Among model-specific licenses, MGLs are the only ones that are both highly clear and reusable (not bound to a proprietary model), whereas Llama2/3, Gemma, and OpenRAIL-M contain proprietary terms or structural biases favoring the licensor. The paper shows that 95% of FSF-freedom conflicts are caused by copyleft-style behavioral terms in model-specific licenses (RAILs, Llama2/3), a risk largely absent in traditional OSS projects, motivating the MGLs proposal which omits such restrictive clauses while still offering optional RAI elements.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the well-established OSS license compliance literature (license mismatch, proliferation, conflict detection) to the ML model domain, where it identifies three unique challenges absent from traditional software: implicit dependencies (learning representations without copying), model file formats that defeat SPDX-based scanning, and model-specific licenses (Llama, RAIL) with behavioral restrictions that create novel conflict types. It builds on prior empirical studies of HF model reuse (Jiang et al., 2023) and the authors' own ModelGo tool (Duan et al., 2024a), but departs from them by providing the first large-scale quantitative noncompliance analysis across 151K models and 147K dependencies, and by proposing MGLs as a reusable, OSI-submitted model-specific license set. The paper also engages with the ongoing debate over behavioral use clauses (Contractor et al., 2022; McDuff et al., 2024) and data licensing (Longpre et al., 2024), positioning model license compliance as an unresolved piece distinct from data provenance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that 81.8% of models exhibit license mismatch and that 63.5% of qualified models under copyleft-style terms are noncompliant reveals a systemic legal risk that grows with the increasing frequency of model reuse (finetuning, merging, quantization). The paper's key open question is whether behavioral restrictions in model licenses should exist at all, given that they are the primary source of FSF-freedom conflicts (95% of such conflicts) and create barriers in the AI supply chain. Limitations include reliance on self-reported HF metadata (only 4 dependency types tracked, proprietary model dependencies undisclosed), and the unresolved legal question of whether model weights are even copyrightable. The MGLs proposal and Model Sheet concept offer a concrete path forward, but adoption will depend on community consensus and whether platforms like HF integrate AI BOM standards.
