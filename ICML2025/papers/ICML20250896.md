## Problem

Existing mechanistic interpretability methods discover circuits on a task-by-task basis, yielding no cross-task generalization and requiring costly human or LLM inspection to interpret every computational node. This paper asks whether a reusable, task-agnostic vocabulary of modular circuits (MCs) can be discovered from an LLM, enabling global interpretability and amortized interpretation costs across diverse language tasks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250896/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained LLM, a set of domain-specific tasks (each with input text and a single-token target output, unified into multi-choice QA format), and the LLM's full computational graph of attention heads and FFN neurons. It produces a modular circuit vocabulary where each entry is a small connected subgraph (a partition of a reusable subgraph spanning multiple task circuits) paired with a natural-language functional interpretation generated by GPT-4 from token-level activation heatmaps. The vocabulary is designed so that new task circuits can be matched against existing MCs to reuse cached interpretations without re-deriving them.

## Method

ModCirc operates in three stages. First, it collects significant computational subgraphs (SCSs) for each task using edge attribute patching with integrated gradients, a first-order Taylor approximation that reduces circuit discovery from two forward passes per node to two forward passes plus one backward pass for all nodes. Second, it identifies reusable subgraphs via pairwise intersection of SCSs (preserving subgraph integrity over frequent-subgraph mining), then partitions each reusable subgraph into modular SCSs using a GNN trained with policy gradients (adapted from NeuroCut) over a repartition MDP; the reward function encodes locality (semantic + physical proximity), composability (mutual information between adjacent MCs via KDE), and globality (bounded partition count and size). An initial partitioning algorithm ensures internal connectivity, balanced sizes, and cross-subgraph compatibility before RL refinement. Third, it generates functional interpretations by feeding token-wise l2-norm activation heatmaps of each modular SCS to GPT-4, producing a task-agnostic description of the subgraph's role.

## Results

On four medical-domain evaluation datasets (Coreference, MediAttr, MediStatus, PubSumm.), ModCirc achieves reusability scores of 0.32–0.37, substantially outperforming all baselines whose reusability scores remain below 0.21. Composability scores range from 0.70 to 1.00, with ModCirc matching or slightly trailing baselines on some datasets (e.g., 0.70 vs. 0.75 on Coreference and PubSumm.). Consistency scores are competitive at 0.26–0.40, with ModCirc achieving the highest value (0.40) on Coreference. The standout advantage is reusability, where ModCirc's scores are roughly 1.5–2× those of the strongest baseline (Frequent Random at 0.17–0.21).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ModCirc is compared against four baselines: Activation (grouping nodes by co-activation patterns), KMeans clustering (Clust.), Frequent Random (Freq., random partitioning within reusable subgraphs), and Random (ego-graph sampling). ModCirc decisively wins on reusability across all four datasets, scoring 0.32–0.37 versus 0.17–0.21 for the best baseline (Frequent Random) and 0.05–0.06 for Activation. On composability, ModCirc ties or slightly trails baselines on Coreference (0.70 vs. 0.75–0.80) and PubSumm. (0.70 vs. 0.75), but matches them perfectly on MediAttr (0.95 vs. 1.00) and MediStatus (1.00). On consistency, ModCirc achieves the best score on Coreference (0.40) and PubSumm. (0.34) but is marginally behind on MediAttr (0.33 vs. 0.35) and MediStatus (0.26, tied). The paper attributes ModCirc's slightly lower composability to the connected-partition constraint, and notes that the reusability gap is the primary differentiator validating the modular circuit approach.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends mechanistic interpretability (MI) research, which has traditionally identified task-specific computational circuits via activation patching (Wang et al., 2022; Conmy et al., 2023; Nanda et al., 2023), by proposing a task-agnostic modular circuit (MC) vocabulary that is shared across tasks. The conceptual motivation draws on Nanda (2023)'s hypothesis of circuit modularity, while the technical core adapts the RL-based graph partitioning framework of NeuroCut (Shah et al., 2024) to the discrete partitioning problem over reusable subgraphs extracted from causal tracing. The key departure from prior MI work is shifting from per-task circuit discovery to a global vocabulary problem, where each modular subgraph carries a reusable functional interpretation that can be cached and transferred to new tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The MC vocabulary framework addresses two practical bottlenecks in MI: the lack of cross-task generalization and the high cost of generating functional interpretations for every node in every circuit. If the vocabulary concept scales, it could make interpretability of large models substantially more tractable by amortizing FI generation across tasks. However, the current evidence is limited to a single medical-domain LLM (MedLLaMA 8B) and a small GPT-2 model with only a handful of tasks, and the authors acknowledge that the vocabulary's coverage is bounded by the diversity of training tasks. Open questions include whether the vocabulary generalizes across domains, whether it scales to frontier-scale models, and whether the five evaluation criteria adequately capture the true modularity structure of LLMs.
