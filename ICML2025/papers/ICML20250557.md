## Problem

Existing methods for peer effect estimation in observational network data either treat peer effects as a monolithic quantity without distinguishing direct from indirect pathways, or they are restricted to one-to-one (two-person) transmission settings. This paper addresses the gap of separately identifying and estimating peer direct effects (PDE), peer indirect effects (PIE), and self-treatment effects (STE) in general many-to-one network structures, where an individual's outcome is influenced by the aggregated treatments and outcomes of multiple neighbors.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250557/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes observational network data consisting of high-dimensional node features (reduced to 10 dimensions via LDA), binary treatment assignments, continuous outcomes, and an adjacency matrix defining the network topology. Neighbor-level aggregated variables (W_t_i, W_y_i, W_x_i) are computed as attention-weighted sums over each node's neighbors, where weights are derived from cosine similarity of feature vectors. The output consists of group-level (rather than individual-level) estimates of PDE, PIE, and STE, with the total peer effect recoverable as PDE + PIE via the decomposition proposition.

## Method

The gDIS framework rests on a theoretical identifiability result showing that the aggregated neighbor feature set W_x_i satisfies the sequential ignorability criterion, enabling PDE, PIE, and STE to be expressed as do-free probability formulas via Pearl's back-door adjustment and do-calculus. Algorithmically, gDIS is a three-stage supervised pipeline: (1) a 2-layer GNN with graph attention and a single fully connected layer estimates the conditional P(W_y_i | W_t_i, W_x_i) via MSE; (2) a 2-layer GNN with attention and a 3-layer FC network estimates P(Y_i | W_t_i, W_y_i, W_x_i) using MSE plus an HSIC regularization term that penalizes dependence between node features and learned embeddings in an RKHS; (3) kernel density estimation recovers the marginal densities P(X_i) and P(W_x_i). The multi-layer GNN captures high-order neighbor interference, the attention mechanism assigns heterogeneous influence weights to different neighbors, and the final PDE, PIE, and STE values are computed by plugging the learned conditional and marginal estimates into the closed-form identification equations.

## Results

On the BlogCatalog (BC) within-sample setting, gDIS achieves PEHE of 0.2093 for peer effect, 0.1265 for peer direct effect, 0.1358 for peer indirect effect, and 0.0588 for self-treatment effect, substantially outperforming the best baseline (TNet at 0.6591 for peer, 1-GNN at 0.3600 for self-treatment). On Flickr within-sample, gDIS reaches PEHE of 0.1262 (peer), 0.1118 (PDE), 0.0901 (PIE), and 0.1578 (STE), again dominating all baselines. Out-of-sample results confirm consistent gains, with gDIS achieving 0.2034 (peer) and 0.0545 (STE) on BC, and 0.1206 (peer) and 0.1502 (STE) on Flickr. Notably, only gDIS can separately estimate PDE and PIE; all other baselines are inapplicable to these effect types.

## Baselines & Comparisons

gDIS outperforms all six baselines (CFR+(N), ND+(N), TARNet+(N), 1-GNN, NetEst, TNet) across every dataset and effect type where comparison is possible. On BC within-sample peer effect, gDIS achieves PEHE 0.2093 versus the best baseline TNet at 0.6591 — a 3.2× improvement. For self-treatment on BC, gDIS (0.0588) beats the next-best 1-GNN (0.3600) by over 6×. On Flickr, the gap is even more dramatic: gDIS reaches 0.1262 for peer effect while the best baseline NetEst sits at 0.9650, an approximately 7.6× reduction in error. Critically, none of the six baselines can separately estimate PDE or PIE (marked '/' in Table 2); only gDIS provides these decompositions, which is the paper's central contribution. The HSIC ablation (gDIS(-HSIC)) consistently trails the full model by a small margin (e.g., 0.2478 vs. 0.2093 on BC peer), confirming that the HSIC regularization contributes meaningfully to robustness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the causal mediation framework of Pearl (2014) and the PDE/PIE definitions of VanderWeele et al. (2012) from one-to-one (two-person) transmission units to many-to-one group-level network settings. Prior methods that separate PDE from PIE (VanderWeele 2012, Chiba 2013, Shpitser 2017, Cai 2021) are restricted to binary outcomes and pairwise interactions, while methods handling many-to-one networks (Jiang & Sun 2022, Cai 2023, Chen 2024) estimate only aggregate peer effects without disentangling direct from indirect components. gDIS is the first to provide identifiability conditions and a practical GNN-based estimator for PDE, PIE, and STE simultaneously in general network data, combining attention mechanisms, multi-layer GNNs, and HSIC regularization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The PDE/PIE distinction has direct practical consequences: in vaccination, a zero-PDE/non-zero-PIE scenario implies prioritizing high-infection-risk individuals for herd immunity rather than maximizing coverage; in marketing, it determines whether to expand ad reach or optimize early-adopter satisfaction. The main limitation is reliance on the network unconfoundedness assumption, which is often violated in practice; the authors identify instrumental variables, network representation learning, and causal discovery as promising directions for relaxation. The framework also opens questions about higher-order neighbor effects, time-varying networks, and scalability to very large graphs where the KDE and attention computations become costly.
