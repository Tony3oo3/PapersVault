## Problem

Simulating a single policy trajectory in supply chain optimization (SCO) requires serially evaluating a deep neural network policy over hundreds of millions of time-steps, taking hours per sample path and severely limiting policy optimization methods that need many rollouts per update. Existing parallel discrete event simulation approaches like Time Warp fail for SCO due to the absence of local causality, causing cascading rollbacks that reduce parallelism to sequential execution while adding message-passing overhead.

## Input / Output

Given an initial state s₁, horizon T, a fixed disturbance sequence {ω_t}, a DNN policy π, and a cheap transition function f, the algorithm produces the exact same action sequence {a_t^seq} as sequential simulation. The critical asymmetry is that π evaluation (a DNN forward pass) dominates runtime while f evaluation (simple inventory/capacity bookkeeping) is negligible, making batched GPU evaluation of π the key optimization target. The algorithm is structured for GPU execution where M processes each evaluate π on their assigned subset of time-steps in a single batched forward pass per iteration, with no inter-process communication.

## Method

Picard Iteration partitions the T time-steps across M virtual processes (e.g., by product in Fulfillment Optimization), initializes a cache of actions, and iteratively refines it: each process evaluates π only on its assigned time-steps while using cached actions elsewhere, then updates the cache at the end of the iteration. For FO problems, the paper proves convergence in at most |Q_T|+1 iterations (bounded by the number of nodes J), yielding an effective speedup of ~M/J, by showing that 'wrong' cached actions can only route to nodes that would exhaust capacity in the sequential scenario and these errors are eliminated one node at a time per iteration. The proof requires the policy to satisfy Inventory Independence, Consistency, and Monotonicity conditions met by standard bid-price policies. Practical optimizations including chunked execution and early termination achieve 400x+ speedup on a single A100 GPU for large-scale FO instances, and the approach extends to inventory control with replenishment (converging in 2R iterations where R is the number of central-warehouse replenishments) and general RL environments (converging in far fewer than T iterations on MuJoCo benchmarks).

## Results

On a large-scale Fulfillment Optimization problem (3M orders, 1M products, 30 nodes), the Picard iteration achieves a 441× speedup over sequential policy evaluation with M=10,000 parallel processes on a single A100 GPU. In an end-to-end policy gradient pipeline, the same problem reduces from ~10 hours (sequential) to 1 minute 39 seconds (Picard), a 363× speedup. The speedup scales approximately linearly with batch size M and remains above 350× even under heavy-tailed demand distributions (β down to −1.0). On OpenAI Gym MuJoCo environments (T=200), the Picard iteration converges to ≤0.1% relative RMSE in under 15 iterations for all 8 environments, with 5 of 8 converging within 5 iterations, suggesting a potential 13–40× speedup.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is sequential (serial) policy simulation, which the Picard iteration outperforms by 350–450× on policy evaluation and 363× on end-to-end RL for the largest FO instance (3M orders). The only explicit algorithmic baseline is a GPU-adapted Time Warp implementation (Liu & Andelfinger, 2017), which the authors carefully tailor to exploit FO problem structure (exploiting the observation that processes can run in parallel between t₀ and t₀ + min_j c_{t₀,j} without rollback). Even with this tailored adaptation, Time Warp achieves only a 5× speedup over sequential, making Picard 88× faster than Time Warp on the same 3M-order instance. The authors attribute this gap to Time Warp's inability to reuse policy evaluations computed on incorrect states during rollbacks, whereas Picard's iterative cache naturally reuses and refines evaluations. On MuJoCo environments, no direct parallel-simulation baseline is provided, but the convergence within 5–15 iterations (vs. T=200) implies a 13–40× potential speedup if dynamics evaluation is sufficiently cheaper than policy evaluation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the Time Warp paradigm for parallel discrete event simulation, arguing that the local causality assumption required by Time Warp is violated in MDP policy evaluation (every processor must communicate with every other, causing cascading rollbacks). It repurposes the Picard fixed-point iteration concept from numerical analysis to the novel domain of parallel policy simulation, where an initial 'guess' of the full action trajectory is iteratively refined via cheap state transitions. The approach is orthogonal to parallel RL methods (A3C, RLlib, Envpool) that parallelize across independent trajectories or agents; here the parallelism is within a single trajectory. The theoretical analysis draws on the structural properties of Fulfillment Optimization (product partitioning, capacity monotonicity) to establish convergence bounds that are independent of the horizon T.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 400x+ speedup for policy simulation (10 hours → 2 minutes for end-to-end RL) removes a critical bottleneck that has limited the application of RL to large-scale supply chain problems, where horizons reach hundreds of millions of time-steps. The provable convergence bound of J+1 iterations (independent of T) is a rare theoretical guarantee in parallel computing and provides a clear diagnostic: the number of iterations captures how 'coupled' the problem is. Open questions include whether the convergence behavior observed in MuJoCo environments (<15 iterations out of T=200) can be theoretically characterized, and how the speedup degrades when the dynamics f(·) are not negligible relative to policy evaluation (as in physics-based environments where η ≈ 1/5). The worst-case construction showing T iterations are needed highlights that the guarantee is problem-structure-dependent.
