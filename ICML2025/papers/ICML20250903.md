## Problem

Frank-Wolfe algorithms require a step-size strategy that is simultaneously effective (makes good progress), efficient (low per-iteration cost), and adaptive (exploits local smoothness), but existing options force tradeoffs: backtracking and adaptive line searches are computationally expensive, while open-loop schedules ignore local geometry. This paper introduces a step-size strategy that achieves all three properties by solving the line search subproblem to near-optimality at very low cost.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a smooth convex function f, a compact convex set X, the current iterate x_t, and a descent direction d_t (typically x_t − v_t from the linear minimization oracle), requiring only first-order gradient evaluations. It returns the step size γ* minimizing f(x_t − γd_t) over [0, γ_max] (typically [0,1] due to the convex-combination structure of FW iterates), with a user-specified tolerance ε controlling the stopping criterion |φ(γ)| < ε. A warm-start from the previous iteration's γ* further reduces the number of inner secant iterations.

## Method

The core idea is to reformulate the FW line search as a root-finding problem for the directional derivative φ(γ) = ⟨∇f(x_t − γd_t), d_t⟩ and apply the classical secant method to locate the root. Convergence is guaranteed (Theorem 3.1) by exploiting two structural properties of FW: the step is confined to a bounded interval [0, γ_max], and strict convexity makes φ' strictly increasing, so the secant iterates remain on the correct side of the root and converge monotonically (Lemma 2.1). Each secant iteration requires only one new gradient evaluation (after the initial two points), and the method typically converges in 6–7 iterations, or in a single iteration for quadratic objectives. The approach is adaptive because it implicitly captures local smoothness along the descent direction without requiring knowledge of the global Lipschitz constant L, and it generalizes to any FW variant (Away-Step, Pairwise, Blended, etc.) that maintains iterates as convex combinations of extreme points.

## Results

Across 8 problem classes (Birkhoff, ill-conditioned quadratics, nuclear norm ball, A/D-optimal experiment design, portfolio, standard quadratics, spectraplex), SLS achieves the lowest FW iteration count on 6 of 8 classes and the lowest wall-clock time on 6 of 8 classes. The advantage is most dramatic on the nuclear norm ball (204.6 s, 1071 iterations) and A-optimal design (475.7 s, 2194 iterations), where both Adaptive and Backtracking time out at 3600 s. The average number of secant iterations per line search call is approximately 1.5 for quadratic objectives (converging in one step per Remark 3.4) and around 6–7 for non-quadratic self-concordant objectives, keeping the per-iteration overhead minimal.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Adaptive step size (Pedregosa et al., 2020; Pokutta, 2024), SLS reduces FW iterations by 30–60% on most problems (e.g., 10,624 vs 17,506 on Birkhoff; 1,071 vs 2,547 on Nuclear) and is the only method that solves the A-optimal design instances within the time limit. Against the Agnostic open-loop step size 2/(t+2), SLS is overwhelmingly superior: on Birkhoff it needs 10,624 iterations versus 731,017 for Agnostic, and Agnostic times out entirely on Nuclear and OA. Against Backtracking line search, the comparison is closer: Backtracking wins on Birkhoff (248.5 s vs 427.6 s) and OD (158.8 s vs 199.2 s), but SLS wins on Nuclear (solved in 204.6 s vs timeout), OA (solved in 475.7 s vs timeout), Portfolio (0.5 s vs 166.8 s), QuadProb (340.7 s vs 426.4 s), and Spectraplex (34.5 s vs 168.7 s). Overall, SLS is the best or near-best strategy on every problem class, with its decisive advantage on non-smooth self-concordant objectives (OA, OD, Portfolio) where open-loop and backtracking strategies struggle most.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the classical secant method (a 3000-year-old root-finding technique) as a step-size strategy for Frank-Wolfe algorithms, exploiting the fact that FW's bounded step interval [0, γ_max] and descent direction structure (x_t − v_t) naturally satisfy the conditions for secant convergence. It departs from the current gold-standard adaptive backtracking line search of Pedregosa et al. (2020) and the Barzilai-Borwein-style two-point step sizes of Malitsky & Mishchenko (2020), which the authors identify as 'effectively a modified (higher-dimensional) secant step' but which cannot be transplanted to FW due to their reliance on iterate convergence to the optimum. The key conceptual contribution is showing that the secant method's usual lack of global convergence is not a problem in the FW setting, where the root is confined to a bounded interval and the function along the search direction is strictly convex.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SLS provides a drop-in replacement for existing step-size strategies in any FW variant that is both theoretically grounded (convergence guaranteed under strict convexity and smoothness) and computationally cheap (typically 6–7 secant iterations, one gradient evaluation per iteration, and exactly one iteration for quadratics). The superlinear convergence expected for self-concordant functions (Remark 3.2) makes it particularly attractive for problems like optimal experiment design and portfolio optimization where the objective is not globally smooth. Open questions include whether the approach extends beyond FW to other first-order methods (the authors note it 'might be more broadly applicable' but may require stronger assumptions), and whether the practical advantage over Newton's method (shown in Table 2) persists in higher-dimensional or more complex settings.
