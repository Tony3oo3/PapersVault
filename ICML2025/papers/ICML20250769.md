## Problem

Transformers trained on sequences of length L frequently fail to generalize to longer test sequences, yet the structural properties of data that enable or inhibit length generalization remain poorly understood. While position coupling has empirically improved length generalization on arithmetic tasks, no principled theoretical framework explains why it works or when it should apply. This paper asks: what distributional assumptions on the data guarantee length generalization, and can they justify architectural modifications to positional embeddings?

## Input / Output

The theoretical model takes a sequence of tokens X_1,...,X_ℓ from a vocabulary V and predicts the embedding Y ∈ Y ⊂ R^d of the next token, with the constraint that Y = g*(X_{S*}) for a fixed set S* of k 'planted' positions drawn from a distribution Q^pos_ℓ. For Predictive Position Coupling (PPC), the transformer additionally outputs a coupled position ID at each step alongside the token ID; at generation time, both predicted values are fed back as the position and token for the next position. The model is trained on sequences of length ≤ L and evaluated on sequences of length L̄ > L without any fine-tuning on longer sequences.

## Method

The paper introduces a theoretical framework built on two key abstractions: (1) k-sparse planted correlations (Definition 3.2), where each label depends on only k tokens drawn from a correlated distribution while the rest are i.i.d. background noise, and (2) sparse functional attention (Definition 3.3), a hypothesis class that applies softmax-weighted aggregation over all size-k subsets of positions, generalizing single attention heads. The main theorem (Theorem 4.3) proves length generalization when k is fixed, the attention class is local (bounded span L_local) and relative, and the position distribution has bounded coverage. Proposition 4.4 shows that position coupling—rewriting position IDs so that coupled tokens fall within a local window—removes the locality requirement. Building on this theory, the authors propose Predictive Position Coupling (PPC), which adds an auxiliary output head so the transformer learns to predict input-dependent coupled position IDs at generation time, extending position coupling to tasks like variable assignment where the coupling structure varies per instance. Experiments on sparse parity, parity with scratchpad, variable assignment, and C4 natural language confirm that sparsity (small fixed k) is the dominant factor governing length generalization.

## Results

On the sparse parity task, transformers trained with sparsity K_train ≤ 8 achieve near-perfect length generalization up to 500 tokens (10× training length) for all test sparsities k_test ≤ K_train, while performance degrades rapidly for k_test > K_train. On variable assignment with scratchpad, Predictive Position Coupling (PPC) achieves good length generalization up to 3× the training sequence length (trained on [20,60], tested up to 200) for depths d ∈ {2,3,4,5}. On natural language (C4), a model trained at context length 64 with PoSE achieves lower perplexity at context length 128 than at 64, and unmasking just k=5 'influential' tokens from the first 64 positions nearly recovers the full perplexity improvement from unmasking all 64 tokens, confirming sparse dependence on distant context.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary empirical comparison is between Predictive Position Coupling (PPC) and RoPE with PoSE (Zhu et al., 2023) on the variable assignment task with scratchpad. The paper reports that 'using RoPE with PoSE (with or without scratchpad) performs significantly worse than PPC,' with PPC achieving good length generalization up to 3× training length while RoPE+PoSE fails to extrapolate effectively. Ablation-style comparisons (Figs. 5a–5c) further show that removing either position coupling or the scratchpad individually significantly harms length generalization, confirming both components are necessary. In the sparse parity experiments, the key comparison is across sparsity levels: smaller training sparsity (K_train ≤ 8) yields near-perfect generalization to 500 tokens, while larger sparsity (K_train = 10, 12) shows degraded out-of-distribution performance despite equivalent in-distribution accuracy. On natural language, the model beats the trivial baseline (ignoring all tokens beyond L=64) at context length 128, and the scatter plot (Figure 2) shows that the model's dependence on the first 64 tokens is sparse — 5 well-chosen tokens recover most of the perplexity gain from all 64.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the position coupling technique (Cho et al. 2024a,b; McLeish et al. 2024) by introducing Predictive Position Coupling (PPC), which extends it to settings where coupled position IDs are input-dependent. Conceptually, it departs from prior theoretical analyses of length generalization (Zhou et al. 2023; Huang et al. 2024; Sabbaghi et al. 2024; Ahuja & Mansouri 2024) by providing a non-asymptotic framework that does not require exact recovery of the ground-truth hypothesis, instead relying on distributional assumptions (sparse planted correlations) and a locality condition on the hypothesis class. The sparse functional attention class (Definition 3.3) is positioned as a principled abstraction that generalizes single attention heads while enabling clean theoretical analysis, and the paper explicitly contrasts its approach with prior works that proceed by showing the learned model equals the ground truth on the entire domain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first clean theoretical separation of two distinct requirements for length generalization—sparsity of token dependencies and locality of the hypothesis class—and shows that position coupling specifically addresses the latter. This gives practitioners a diagnostic principle: if a task's next-token prediction depends on only a fixed small number of prior tokens, length generalization should be achievable, and PPC broadens the range of tasks where position coupling can be applied. Key limitations include the simplified distributional assumptions (i.i.d. background tokens, fixed position distributions), the strong locality requirement in the base theorem, and experiments on relatively small models; open questions include extending the theory to Markov/HMM backgrounds, understanding inference-time position modifications like position interpolation within this framework, and validating the sparsity principle at larger scales.
