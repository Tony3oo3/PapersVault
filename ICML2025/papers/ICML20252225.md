## Problem

A common belief in the representational comparison literature is that if two models can be functionally aligned (i.e., stitched together), they must capture similar information. This paper demonstrates that this assumption is false: models can achieve full stitching compatibility while relying on entirely different input patterns, even when one model's representations contain no information relevant to the other's task. The gap is the lack of controlled evidence showing that functional alignment measures like model stitching are blind to the semantic content of representations.

## Input / Output

The method takes two trained networks with identical architectures (e.g., ResNet-18, ResNet-50, VGG19) and a stitch training dataset, then inserts a 1×1 convolutional layer with batch normalization at a matching cut point (before a residual block or before the linear classifier). The sender and receiver are frozen; only the stitch is optimized. The output is the stitched model's test accuracy compared against the receiver's own baseline accuracy, plus a numerical rank analysis of the feature maps at the receiver's first convolutional layer. Notably, the stitch training data can come from a completely different task or modality than the receiver's training data, and the 'sender' can even be synthetic clustered random noise rather than an actual network.

## Method

The paper constructs a series of controlled experiments to show that model stitching (Bansal et al., 2021) achieves high compatibility between models known to use different input cues. Starting with Colour MNIST variants that bias models toward colour or shape, they show all variants stitch into a shape-based receiver at every layer. They then remove all shape information from the sender's input entirely (Colour-Only into Greyscale MNIST) and still achieve 100% stitching accuracy, ruling out information leakage. They further demonstrate that clustered random noise—simulating a sender's representation with no learned structure—stitches successfully into both MNIST and ImageNet models. Extending to real-world settings, they stitch ImageNet into Stylized ImageNet (texture vs. shape bias), a 10-class ImageNet model into a birdsong recognition model (cross-modality), and autoencoders trained on Fashion-MNIST or CIFAR-10 into an MNIST decoder. The key insight is that stitching measures whether a linear map can make the receiver functional, not whether the sender and receiver encode the same input-level patterns; the numerical rank analysis confirms that even stitch-compatible representations are processed with different linear dependence by the receiver.

## Results

In the controlled Colour-MNIST experiments, all models (Colour, Correlated, Colour-Only) achieve stitching compatibility with the Digit receiver at every layer, with the Colour-Only model (containing no digit information) reaching 100% accuracy when stitched into a Greyscale-MNIST receiver (baseline 99.0%). In the real-world setting (Table 1), Clustered-Noise achieves 100% top-1 accuracy when stitched into a pretrained ImageNet ResNet-50 at Block 4 and beyond (baseline 76.13%), and a 10-class ImageNet model achieves 88.40% top-1 when stitched into a birdsong-recognition model, exceeding the 77.98% receiver baseline. The ImageNet-to-Stylized direction also shows stitch compatibility at multiple layers (74.34% top-1 at Linear vs. 60.18% baseline), while the reverse (Stylized-to-ImageNet) fails, likely due to the Stylized model's weaker performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new method to compare against existing baselines; rather, it empirically examines whether model stitching (Bansal et al., 2021; Lenc & Vedaldi, 2015) can reliably distinguish between models that capture different input-level information. The key finding is that stitching compatibility is achieved in cases where it should not be: Clustered-Noise (purely synthetic, unstructured representations) reaches 100% top-1 when stitched into ImageNet, and a model trained solely on background colour (with no digit present) achieves 100% accuracy when stitched into a Greyscale-MNIST model. In the cross-modality setting, a 10-class ImageNet model exceeds the birdsong receiver's own accuracy (88.40% vs. 77.98% top-1), despite the two models processing entirely different input modalities. The one direction where stitching fails (Stylized→ImageNet) is attributed to the receiver's weak performance on the sender's task rather than genuine representational incompatibility. The authors conclude that stitching compatibility reflects cluster separability in representation space rather than informational similarity, and that the method cannot serve as a reliable indicator of whether two models have learned the same input patterns.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper critically examines model stitching, building directly on Lenc & Vedaldi (2015) and Bansal et al. (2021), who established the functional-alignment framework where a 1×1 convolutional stitch between two networks' intermediate layers is trained to test representational compatibility. It extends the analysis to the generative (autoencoder) setting following Moschella et al. (2022) and Fumero et al. (2024), and situates its findings within the shortcut-learning literature (Geirhos et al., 2020) to argue that functional compatibility does not imply informational similarity. The paper departs from the prevailing interpretation in the stitching literature (Bansal et al., 2021; Hernandez et al., 2022) that successful stitching indicates models 'encode similar information,' and also engages with the Platonic Representation Hypothesis (Huh et al., 2024) as an alternative explanation for cross-task alignment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a widely-held assumption in the representational comparison community—that functional alignment (stitching compatibility) is a valid proxy for informational similarity—by showing that clustered random noise, models with opposite biases, and even cross-modality representations can be successfully stitched. This has broad implications for how stitching results (and potentially other functional alignment measures) should be interpreted in architecture comparison, continual learning, and interpretability research. A key open question the paper raises is how to construct comparison tools that capture informational (semantic) similarity rather than mere functional compatibility, and it advocates for controlled shortcut-learning benchmarks as a starting point. Limitations include the reliance on toy problems for the strongest claims, the absence of a proposed replacement metric, and the fact that real-world experiments are framed as supporting evidence rather than primary results.
