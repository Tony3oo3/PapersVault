## Problem

Classical universal approximation theorems assume real-valued parameters and exact arithmetic, but practical neural networks operate under floating-point constraints where parameters are drawn from a finite set and operations incur round-off errors and overflows. Prior work on floating-point expressivity was limited to ReLU and Step activations, leaving the expressivity of networks using widely adopted activations (GELU, SeLU, Swish, Mish, sin, Sigmoid, tanh) under floating-point arithmetic completely uncharacterized.

## Input / Output

The networks consume floating-point vectors from a format-specific domain (e.g., all finite floats for Sigmoid and tanh; all finite floats with magnitude below 1/8 of the largest float for ReLU, ELU, SeLU, GELU, Swish, Mish, and sin) and produce floating-point values or vectors that may include ±∞. All weights, biases, and intermediate computations are confined to a given IEEE-754 format (float8, bfloat16, float16, float32, or float64), and the representation is exact on the domain rather than approximate.

## Method

The paper identifies 'distinguishability'—the ability of the first layer to produce different activated outputs for every pair of distinct inputs—as both necessary and sufficient for universal representation under floating-point arithmetic. Sufficiency is established via a four-layer construction that builds indicator functions for each input point using sequential addition, which exploits the non-associativity of floating-point addition and round-off errors to synthesize binary step functions without overflow. The key technical tool is the 'separating point' concept: if an activation function has separating points at both small and moderate-to-large magnitudes, the domain is distinguishable. Easily verifiable conditions on real-valued activation functions (bounded between L₁x and L₂x with derivative lower-bounded by L₁) guarantee the existence of separating points, yielding universal representation for Sigmoid, tanh, ReLU, ELU, SeLU, GELU, Swish, Mish, and sin across all standard floating-point formats.

## Results

The paper proves that 4-layer floating-point neural networks can represent *all* functions from a domain to F∪{-∞,∞} under mild conditions on the activation function (Condition 1 + distinguishability). For Sigmoid and tanh, the domain is all finite floats F^d; for ReLU, ELU, SeLU, GELU, Swish, Mish, and sin, the domain is (-2^{e_max-2}, 2^{e_max-2})_F^d. The constructed network has width O(n·d_in·2^{(p+q)(d_in+1)}), and the paper shows this exponential-in-d_in width is necessary for constant-depth networks. A negative result shows that the correctly rounded cos activation cannot represent all functions even over [-2^{⌊(p+7/2)⌋}, 2^{⌊(p+7/2)⌋}]_F.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines. The key comparisons are against prior theoretical results: Park et al. (2024) proved universal representation only for ReLU and Step activations over a unit cube, relying on piecewise linearity; this paper extends to GELU, SeLU, Swish, Mish, sin, and others over a much wider domain (up to 2^{e_max-2} in magnitude). Hwang et al. (2024) studied fixed-point (not floating-point) arithmetic. A concurrent work by Hwang et al. (2025) also studies floating-point universal approximation but requires a strictly stronger condition on the activation function and constructs deeper networks; the current paper's Theorem 3.4 subsumes their pointwise result with fewer layers. The paper also provides a *necessary* condition (distinguishability, Lemma 3.2) that prior works did not establish, and uses it to show cos is fundamentally insufficient.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the floating-point expressivity line initiated by Park et al. (2024), who proved universal representation for ReLU and Step activations under floating-point arithmetic, and by Hwang et al. (2024), who studied fixed-point arithmetic. The key departure is handling general practical activations (GELU, SeLU, Swish, Mish, sin, Sigmoid, tanh) under true IEEE 754 floating-point operations, where the proof technique of Park et al. (2024) — relying on piecewise linearity — does not apply. The paper introduces 'distinguishability' as a unifying necessary and (under mild conditions) sufficient condition, and a 'sequential addition' mechanism that exploits the non-associativity of floating-point addition to construct indicator functions, which is a new technical tool absent from prior floating-point or fixed-point analyses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work closes a notable gap between the classical universal approximation theorem (real-valued parameters, exact arithmetic) and the reality of neural networks running on hardware with finite-precision floating-point operations. The clean necessary-and-sufficient characterization via distinguishability gives practitioners a verifiable criterion for whether a given activation function supports universal representation in a target precision format. Open questions remain on minimum depth/width and depth-width trade-offs in the floating-point setting, and the exponential width in input dimension (though expected) limits immediate practical applicability. The negative result for cos — a smooth, bounded activation that fails due to periodicity creating indistinguishable inputs — is a useful cautionary example for activation design in low-precision regimes.
