## Problem

Sparse fine-tuning has lagged behind low-rank PEFT methods in the LLM era because there is no principled criterion for identifying which parameters are truly critical for reasoning. Full fine-tuning is computationally expensive and prone to overfitting and catastrophic forgetting on limited data, while the most naive sparse baseline—magnitude-based selection—performs poorly on LLMs. The paper asks: can a simple weight-selection rule become effective if applied after filtering out 'noisy' higher-order components via low-rank approximation?

## Input / Output

LIFT takes a pre-trained LLM's weight matrices and a supervised fine-tuning text dataset as input. It produces a sparse binary mask (selecting ~5% of parameters by magnitude in the rank-r SVD approximation of each weight matrix) that is applied to the original weights during training, yielding fine-tuned weights with only a small subset updated. The mask is dynamically refreshed at a fixed interval, and optimizer states (momentum, variance) are stored only for masked positions, reducing optimizer memory from 27 GB to 1.3 GB on LLaMA-2-7B—comparable to LoRA.

## Method

LIFT performs a rank-r SVD approximation on each trainable weight matrix W to obtain W', which filters out higher-order components interpreted as noise. It then selects the top-K entries of W' by absolute magnitude to form a binary mask, and applies this mask to the original W so that only those positions receive gradient updates and maintain optimizer states. The mask is periodically re-computed at a chosen update interval to track the shifting Principal Weights as training progresses. Unlike LoRA or DoRA, LIFT does not add adapter matrices; it directly updates a sparse subset of the original weights, giving it a substantially larger update rank (close to Full FT) while retaining the memory efficiency of PEFT. The key insight is that the largest-magnitude entries in the low-rank approximation—'Principal Weights'—are disproportionately critical: perturbing them causes dramatic performance drops on both pre-training knowledge and downstream reasoning tasks, far exceeding the effect of perturbing weights selected by raw magnitude, gradient, or movement score.

## Results

LIFT consistently outperforms Full FT across all evaluated domains: on commonsense reasoning it achieves 87.88% average (LLaMA-3-8B) vs. 86.64% for Full FT, on arithmetic reasoning 81.78% vs. 80.18%, on GLUE 89.24% vs. 88.36%, and on GPQA Diamond 34.85% vs. 33.33% (Qwen2.5-3B). Compared to LoRA, LIFT gains up to 4.42% on commonsense reasoning and 2.34% on arithmetic reasoning (LLaMA-3-8B). In terms of memory, LIFT reduces optimizer state from 27 GB (Full FT) to 1.3 GB (<5%) on LLaMA-2-7B, matching LoRA's footprint while updating only the top 5% Principal Weights.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LIFT beats Full FT on every main benchmark: +1.24% on commonsense (LLaMA-2-7B), +1.13% on commonsense (LLaMA-3-8B), +1.60% on arithmetic (LLaMA-3-8B), +0.88% on GLUE, and +2.02% on GPQA Diamond (Qwen2.5-3B). Against LoRA, LIFT gains 3.41% on commonsense (LLaMA-3-8B: 87.88 vs. 83.46) and 2.34% on arithmetic (LLaMA-3-8B: 81.78 vs. 79.44), while using a comparable number of trainable parameters. LIFT also outperforms DoRA, PiSSA, and S2FT across all model sizes and task domains, with the largest margins on the hardest tasks (GSM8K, SVAMP, AQuA). On sparse fine-tuning baselines, LIFT surpasses SpIEL by 2.70% on GSM8K (LLaMA-3.2-3B) and SIFT by 1.47% on GLUE average (RoBERTa-large) and 2.44% on Humaneval pass@1. Notably, LIFT also retains up to 20% more source-domain (commonsense) knowledge than LoRA after arithmetic fine-tuning, indicating substantially less catastrophic forgetting.

## Relation to Prior Work

LIFT sits at the intersection of two research threads: (1) the observation that low-rank approximation of LLM weight matrices filters out 'noisy' higher-order components and improves reasoning (Sharma et al. 2024; Chen et al. 2025), and (2) sparse fine-tuning, which trains only a subset of parameters but has struggled in the LLM era due to the lack of a principled weight-selection criterion. The paper's key conceptual move is repurposing the low-rank structure insight—previously used for inference-time denoising—as a *training-time* weight selection mechanism: the top-magnitude entries of the rank-r approximation define the 'Principal Weights' to update. This departs from PEFT methods (LoRA, DoRA, PiSSA) that constrain updates to a low-rank subspace, and from prior sparse FT methods (SIFT, SpIEL, S2FT) that select weights by magnitude, gradient, or structured patterns without the rank-reduction step.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LIFT offers a practically attractive trade-off: Full-FT-level (or slightly better) reasoning performance with optimizer memory reduced to ~5% of Full FT, comparable to LoRA. The finding that a small set of Principal Weights is sufficient for both retaining pre-trained knowledge and acquiring new task-specific capabilities has broader implications for understanding where reasoning capacity is encoded in LLMs. Open questions the authors highlight include: combining LIFT with RL-based reasoning training (e.g., GRPO), designing adaptive per-layer ranks instead of a global rank, GPU-accelerated implementation, and connecting the observed eigenspace rotation to learning dynamics theory. A limitation is that the dynamic mask update interval and global LRA rank are hyperparameters that require tuning, and the method adds SVD computation overhead not present in LoRA.
