## Problem

For smooth convex-concave bilinearly-coupled saddle-point problems, existing first-order algorithms cannot simultaneously achieve optimal gradient evaluation and matrix-vector multiplication complexities because they perform the same number of each operation per iteration, while the respective lower bounds can differ substantially. No lower complexity bounds existed for the general setting where f(x) and g(y) are merely smooth and convex (not necessarily strongly convex), and no algorithm achieved the 'complexity separation' needed to match both bounds simultaneously.

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes two smooth convex functions f(x) and g(y) with known smoothness/strong-convexity parameters (L_x, μ_x, L_y, μ_y), a coupling matrix B with spectral bounds (L_xy, μ_xy, μ_yx), an initial point z_in, and a target precision ε. It produces an ε-approximate saddle-point (x_out, y_out) measured by a weighted squared distance R²_{δx,δy}(x,y) to the solution set. Crucially, the algorithm operates under a continuous execution-time model with separate costs τ_f, τ_g for gradient evaluations and τ_B for matrix-vector multiplications, enabling it to skip one type of operation while performing the other — the mechanism behind complexity separation.

## Method

The core algorithm (Algorithm 1) is a recursive sliding procedure for monotone variational inequalities, built on a new perspective of Nesterov acceleration: a single accelerated step is reinterpreted as one gradient-descent step with fixed stepsize 1/L applied to a rescaled and shifted function h_t(z) = α_t^{-2} h(α_t z + (1−α_t) z̄^t). The algorithm applies a nested sequence of such transformations to n component functions p_i(z) and operators Q_i(z), with the recursion depth and inner iteration counts T_i chosen based on the relative Lipschitz constants L_i and M_i. For the saddle-point problem, the method reformulates it as a three-component variational inequality (f, g, and a quadratic regularizer p_3 that enforces linear convergence even when μ_x or μ_y is zero), then applies the recursive sliding with a restarting scheme to achieve the linear rate Õ(√κ_x) gradient evaluations of ∇f, Õ(√κ_y) of ∇g, and Õ(√κ_xy) matrix-vector multiplications — matching the newly established lower bounds exactly.

## Results

The paper establishes that any first-order method for smooth convex-concave bilinearly-coupled saddle-point problems requires at least Õ(√κ_x) evaluations of ∇f(x), Õ(√κ_y) evaluations of ∇g(y), and Õ(√κ_xy) matrix-vector multiplications with B and B^T to reach ε-precision (Theorem 3.3). The proposed Algorithm 1 with restarting matches all three bounds simultaneously (Corollary 4.8), achieving the first optimal complexity separation in the literature. In the strongly-convex-strongly-concave special case, this yields Õ(√(L_x/μ_x)) gradient calls for ∇f, Õ(√(L_y/μ_y)) for ∇g, and Õ(L_xy/(√μ_x·μ_y)) matrix-vector products, each with a log(1/ε) factor.

## Baselines & Comparisons

In the strongly-convex-strongly-concave (SCC) setting, the proposed algorithm is the first to simultaneously achieve the separate optimal bounds √(L_x/μ_x) for ∇f, √(L_y/μ_y) for ∇g, and L_xy/(√μ_x·μ_y) for B-multiplications. All prior methods (Kovalev 22b, Li 23, Jin 22, Du 22, Thekumparampil 22) use the same combined complexity √(L_x/μ_x)+√(L_y/μ_y)+L_xy/(√μ_x·μ_y) for every operation type, failing to exploit the separation. Borodich 23 partially separates but retains the full sum for B-multiplications; Alkousa 20 separates but with suboptimal exponents (e.g., √(L_xy³) instead of L_xy for B). In the strongly-convex-concave (SC-C) setting, the improvement is dramatic: the optimal √(L_x/μ_x) for ∇f replaces Kovalev's (L_xy/μ_yx)√(L_x/μ_x)+√(L_xL_y)/μ_yx+L_xy²/μ_yx², and the B-complexity drops from (L_xy/μ_yx)√(L_x/μ_x)+L_xy²/μ_yx² to just (L_xy/μ_yx)√(L_x/μ_x). In the convex-concave (C-C) setting, the gradient complexity improves from L_xy√(L_xL_y)/μ_xy²+L_xy²/μ_xy² (Kovalev 22b) to √(L_xL_y/μ_xy), while the B-complexity L_xy√(L_xL_y)/μ_xy² matches Kovalev's. The paper also shows its variational inequality subroutine (Algorithm 1) outperforms Lan & Ouyang (2021) when √(L_1R²)/ε ≫ M_2R²/ε, a regime essential for achieving the optimal saddle-point complexities.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the gradient sliding framework of Lan & Ouyang (2021) and Kovalev et al. (2022a), extending it with a recursive multi-level transformation inspired by Nesterov's accelerated gradient method to handle multiple component functions simultaneously. It departs from prior saddle-point optimization work (Kovalev et al. 2022b, Li et al. 2023, Jin et al. 2022, Du et al. 2022) by introducing complexity separation—skipping gradient evaluations and matrix-vector multiplications at different rates—which no existing algorithm could achieve while maintaining linear convergence. The lower bounds unify and generalize previously separate results for strongly-convex-strongly-concave (Nesterov 2013; Zhang et al. 2022b), bilinear (Ibrahim et al. 2020), and affinely constrained (Salim et al. 2022) settings under a single framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This resolves a long-standing open question in first-order optimization theory: whether optimal linearly converging algorithms exist for the general smooth convex-concave bilinearly-coupled saddle-point problem, and if so, what their exact complexity is. The clean characterization via Assumption 2.6 (min{δ_x, δ_y} > 0) as both necessary and sufficient for linear convergence provides a complete map of when fast convergence is possible. However, the results are purely theoretical with no experimental validation, and the algorithm's practical competitiveness (constant factors, implementation overhead of the recursive sliding) remains untested. Open questions include whether the complexity separation idea extends to stochastic or non-smooth settings, and whether the quadratic regularizer p_3(z) can be replaced by simpler mechanisms.
