## Problem

Burns et al. (2024) empirically demonstrated that a strong student (GPT-4) can outperform a weak teacher (GPT-2) when trained only on teacher-generated labels, but the mechanism and fundamental limits of this weak-to-strong generalization remained unexplained. This paper asks whether the phenomenon can be proven to arise purely from a size advantage in simple, non-pretrained models, and what quantitative bounds govern how much a student can improve over its teacher.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251751/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The teacher (M_TE random-feature units) is trained on ground-truth labels from distribution D to population optimum, while the student (M_ST ≫ M_TE units) is trained via gradient flow exclusively on labels f_teacher(x) — it never accesses true labels. The student's bottom-layer weights are drawn i.i.d. from the same distribution as the teacher's, and only the top layer is trained from zero initialization. The analysis operates in the infinite-width limit for the student (M_ST → ∞), and the key outputs are closed-form error bounds relating the student's test loss L_ST to the teacher's loss L_TE, along with the Performance Gap Recovered (PGR) metric.

## Method

The paper analyzes two-layer random feature networks (ReLU with spherical inputs, or linear with anisotropic Gaussian inputs) where the student is trained by early-stopped gradient flow on teacher-generated labels. The core mechanism is that gradient flow in function space decomposes along the kernel's eigenbasis, and early stopping selectively learns high-eigenvalue directions (where the teacher's signal lives) while zeroing out low-eigenvalue directions (where the teacher's approximation error/noise resides). This yields L_ST = Õ(L_TE^1.49) for ReLU networks (under the Gaussian Universality Ansatz) and L_ST = Õ(L_TE^2) for linear networks with anisotropic inputs. Complementing these upper bounds, the paper proves a universal lower bound L_ST ≥ (3/4)L_TE^2 via a shrinkage-optimality argument that applies to any teacher-student pair trained with gradient flow from zero initialization or any convex regularizer, establishing that quadratic improvement is the maximum possible and that a near-chance teacher cannot be boosted to a near-perfect student.

## Results

The paper establishes that in random feature networks, a strong student (M_ST ≫ M_TE units) trained only on teacher labels can provably outperform the weak teacher. For ReLU networks (Theorem 3.1), the student error satisfies L_ST = Õ(L_TE^{1.49}) under the Gaussian Universality Ansatz, with PGR → 1 as M_TE grows. For linear networks with anisotropic inputs (Theorem 3.2), the improvement is quadratic: L_ST ≤ Õ(k · L_TE²). Crucially, Theorem 4.3 proves a matching lower bound L_ST ≥ (3/4)·L_TE² for any random feature model (and more broadly, any shrinkage-optimal learning rule), establishing that quadratic improvement is the fundamental limit of weak-to-strong generalization in this setting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper without empirical baselines, so comparisons are against prior theoretical frameworks. Relative to Burns et al. (2024), who demonstrated W2S empirically with GPT-2→GPT-4 and introduced the PGR metric, this paper provides the first provable W2S result in a minimal model (random feature networks) and shows PGR→1 is achievable. Relative to Charikar et al. (2024), who studied W2S in linear regression with capacity constraints, this paper handles the case where the student is strictly larger (overparameterized) and shows the quadratic gap is both achievable and optimal. The key new contribution over all prior work is the universal lower bound (Theorem 4.3) via shrinkage optimality, which applies to any feature space, any convex regularizer, and any early-stopped gradient flow—showing that no student can reduce error below (3/4)·L_TE² regardless of how specialized or pre-trained its features are. The paper also clarifies that the limitation does not apply if the student fine-tunes from a non-origin initialization (e.g., pre-trained weights), distinguishing generic from specialized inductive bias.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the empirical weak-to-strong generalization (W2S) phenomenon identified by Burns et al. (2024) — where a GPT-4 student outperforms a GPT-2 teacher trained only on teacher-generated labels — into a provable theoretical setting using the simplest possible models: two-layer random feature networks with no pre-training. It departs from prior theoretical W2S work (Charikar et al., 2024; Wu & Sahai, 2025; Lang et al., 2024) by showing the phenomenon arises purely from a size advantage (more random features) combined with early stopping, without requiring specialized inductive bias, pre-training, or robustness to mislabeling. The analysis builds on the random feature/kernel method literature (Rahimi & Recht, 2007; Mei et al., 2022) and gradient flow dynamics, introducing the concept of 'shrinkage optimality' as the unifying property that yields a universal quadratic lower bound on student error.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that W2S is not an artifact of large pretrained models but a fundamental property of overparameterized learners with early stopping, while simultaneously proving a hard limit: student error is always Ω(teacher error²), meaning one cannot 'boost' a barely-better-than-chance teacher to near-perfect performance. This quadratic bound is tight (achieved in linear networks) and applies broadly to any feature space, any convex regularizer, and any early-stopped gradient flow — not just random features. Key open questions include whether the bound can be circumvented by fine-tuning from a pretrained initialization (which breaks shrink optimality), whether it extends to deeper networks or non-convex training dynamics, and whether the polynomial exponents (1.49 for ReLU, 2 for linear) are truly optimal or artifacts of the analysis.
