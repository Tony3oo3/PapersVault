## Problem

The classical Black-Litterman portfolio model requires human experts to heuristically specify investor views (q, Ω) and their uncertainty, and prior data-driven approaches estimate these via external models, causing incoherent parameter learning and error propagation across separate estimation stages. No existing framework treats (q, Ω) as latent variables to be learned jointly with asset returns from feature data within a single, unified Bayesian network.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251505/cropped_page5_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes historical daily asset returns (20–30 years of adjusted closing prices for 11 sector ETFs or 41 Dow Jones stocks), nine asset-specific time-series indicators organized into a block-diagonal feature matrix F ∈ R^{m×dm}, and a homoscedastic feature error matrix Ω^F estimated via kernel density estimation. It produces a predictive distribution over unobserved asset returns r̃ (Gaussian for SLP-BL, Student-t for FIV-BL) and, via mean-variance optimization, a portfolio weight vector w that maximizes the Sharpe ratio at each monthly rebalance. Notably, no human-specified views or confidence levels are required at any stage.

## Method

The paper recasts the Black-Litterman model as a Bayesian network in which (q, Ω) are latent variables rather than externally supplied parameters, and identifies two distinct causal effects of features: Effect 1 (features extracted from the return parameter θ) and Effect 2 (features influencing view formation). For latent views, two configurations are proposed: SLP-BL, where features enter through a θ↔F linear model yielding a closed-form Gaussian posterior with precision G^F = Σ_0^{-1} + (Ω^F)^{-1}; and FIV-BL, where features enter through a q↔F linear model, requiring marginalization over latent (q, Ω) that produces an infinite Gaussian mixture, approximated via a Normal-Inverse-Wishart conjugate prior to yield a tractable Student-t predictive distribution. The differentiation of the two effects (Remark 3.4) prevents bias when features are macroeconomic rather than asset-specific. Both models recover classical Black-Litterman and Markowitz portfolios as special cases, and the SLP-BL variant achieves ~50% Sharpe ratio improvement and ~55% turnover reduction over Markowitz and index baselines on 20- and 30-year datasets.

## Results

On the 30-year Dow Jones dataset, the SLP-BL model achieves Sharpe ratios of 0.78–0.87 across rolling windows (50d–150d), compared to 0.45–0.62 for the Markowitz baseline, representing a ~49.8% mean improvement. On the 20-year SPDR Sector ETFs dataset, SLP-BL reaches Sharpe ratios of 0.66–0.70 versus 0.35–0.57 for Markowitz. The model also reduces maximum drawdown (e.g., 39.81% vs. 46.73% at 120d on Dow Jones) and cuts turnover by 55.1% relative to Markowitz, while matching or exceeding the CAGR of the DJIA (9.33% vs. 5.49%) and S&P 500 (7.04% vs. 6.69%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The SLP-BL model is compared against three baselines: the traditional Markowitz mean-variance optimizer (at five rolling-window lengths), the equal-weighted portfolio, and the relevant market index (DJIA or S&P 500). Against Markowitz, SLP-BL wins decisively on every metric and every window: Sharpe ratios are 0.78–0.87 vs. 0.45–0.62 on Dow Jones and 0.66–0.70 vs. 0.35–0.57 on ETFs, while maximum drawdown is reduced by roughly 6–20 percentage points and annualized volatility drops from ~20% to ~16–17%. Against the market indices, SLP-BL outperforms the DJIA (Sharpe 0.78–0.87 vs. 0.52) and the S&P 500 (Sharpe 0.66–0.70 vs. 0.59) on both Sharpe ratio and CAGR. Against the equal-weighted portfolio, SLP-BL matches or slightly exceeds EQW on Sharpe (0.78–0.87 vs. 0.75 on Dow Jones; 0.66–0.70 vs. 0.61 on ETFs) while achieving substantially lower drawdown (39.81–42.42% vs. 58.90% on Dow Jones). The paper also reports a 55.1% reduction in portfolio turnover relative to Markowitz, indicating the Bayesian shrinkage produces more stable weights.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Black-Litterman-Bayes (BLB) reformulation of Kolm & Ritter (2017), which already cast the classical Black-Litterman model as a Bayesian network with market equilibrium as prior and investor views as likelihood. The key departure is treating the view vector q and uncertainty matrix Ω as latent variables rather than externally estimated inputs, thereby unifying feature integration and parameter inference within a single coherent Bayesian network. This contrasts with the large body of data-driven BL literature (Beach & Orlov 2007; Kara et al. 2019; Kolm & Ritter 2021) that generates (q, Ω) via separate external models (GARCH, SVM, LSTM, etc.), which the authors argue introduces incoherent parameter learning and error propagation across disjoint estimators.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework eliminates the subjective human bottleneck in Black-Litterman portfolio construction and provides closed-form posterior estimation (SLP-BL) or a tractable Normal-Inverse-Wishart approximation (FIV-BL), making it computationally practical for monthly rebalancing. However, empirical validation is limited to the SLP-BL configuration on two datasets with simple technical-indicator features, and the FIV-BL model—which handles non-asset-specific features like macroeconomic indicators—remains untested. Open questions include whether the approach scales to high-dimensional feature spaces, multi-period settings, and whether the gains persist against stronger data-driven BL baselines (e.g., GARCH-derived views or factor-based approaches) rather than the relatively weak Markowitz and index benchmarks used here.
