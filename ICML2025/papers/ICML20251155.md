## Problem

The model collapse literature produces conflicting predictions because different papers study different training workflows (replace vs. accumulate) and different task settings without cross-validation. Gerstgrasser et al. (2024) claimed that accumulating data avoids collapse, but this was never formally tested in the three key settings (multivariate Gaussian modeling, kernel density estimation, and language-model fine-tuning) from the influential Shumailov et al. (2024) Nature paper. Furthermore, no prior work examined a more realistic 'fixed compute' scenario where data accumulates indefinitely but each model trains on only a constant-size subsample.

## Input / Output

Experiments begin with real data drawn from a source distribution (Gaussian with known parameters, sklearn datasets like Blobs/Circles/Moons/Swiss Roll, HelpSteer2 instruction pairs, or TinyStories kindergarten text) and iteratively generate n synthetic samples per iteration from the most recently fitted model. Each iteration produces a trained model (Gaussian mean/covariance, KDE density, or fine-tuned Gemma 2B 2B weights) and a test loss evaluated on held-out real data. The key output is the trajectory of test loss across 100 iterations under three data-management workflows, revealing whether loss diverges (collapse) or plateaus (stability).

## Method

The paper systematically compares three training workflows—replace (delete all prior data, train only on new synthetic), accumulate (train on all real plus all synthetic data, growing linearly), and accumulate-subsample (accumulate all data but subsample to a fixed size each iteration, reflecting fixed compute budgets)—across five generative modeling settings. For multivariate Gaussian modeling, they prove (Theorem 1) that under accumulate the covariance converges to σ₀²·sin(π/√n)/(π/√n) rather than collapsing to zero, and the mean-squared error remains bounded, in contrast to the replace workflow where both diverge. For KDE, they prove NLL divergence under fixed bandwidth for both replace and accumulate (Theorems 5–6), but show that scaling bandwidth as c(tn)^(−1/5) yields finite asymptotic variance (Theorem 7). For language-model fine-tuning on HelpSteer2, they discover a regime-dependent interaction: when real data is scarce (≤1024 samples), adding ~1024 synthetic samples improves test loss, but when real data is abundant, any synthetic data degrades performance, suggesting data quality filtering may matter more than data volume.

## Results

Across five generative modeling task settings (multivariate Gaussian modeling, kernel density estimation, supervised fine-tuning, linear regression, and language model pretraining on TinyStories), the replace workflow causes test loss to diverge, while the accumulate workflow keeps test loss bounded. The new accumulate-subsample workflow (fixed compute budget) yields test loss that is strictly between replace and accumulate, typically plateauing at a higher level than accumulate but avoiding the unbounded divergence of replace. For the univariate Gaussian accumulate case, Theorem 1 gives the exact limit: E[σ_t²] → σ₀²·sin(π/√n)/(π/√n) and E[(μ_t−μ₀)²] → σ₀²·(1−sin(π/√n)/(π/√n)), confirming neither the mean nor variance diverges. In the SFT experiment (Gemma 2 2B on HelpSteer2), both real-data cardinality (R²=0.59 for 1/√n transform) and real-data proportion (R²=0.34 for log-ratio) significantly predict test loss (p < 10⁻²⁴), and adding ~1024 synthetic samples improves test loss when real data ≤ 1024 but degrades it when real data > 1024.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three data training workflows rather than different model architectures. The replace workflow (Shumailov et al., 2023), which discards all prior data and trains only on the latest synthetic generation, causes model collapse (diverging test loss) in all five task settings tested. The accumulate workflow (Gerstgrasser et al., 2024), which retains all real and synthetic data across iterations, avoids collapse entirely in all five settings, with test loss remaining bounded and in some cases (KDE, TinyStories pretraining) actually decreasing below the real-data-only baseline. The paper's new accumulate-subsample workflow, which accumulates data but subsamples to a fixed training set size each iteration, consistently produces test loss strictly between the other two: it degrades more than accumulate but far less than replace, and typically converges to a finite plateau rather than diverging. This confirms that the key factor preventing collapse is retaining access to real data (even in vanishing proportion), not the total dataset size. In the SFT ablation (Sec. 4), the paper further shows that when real data are scarce (≤1024 samples), a small amount of synthetic data (~1024 points) improves test loss, but when real data are abundant (>1024), any synthetic data degrades performance—suggesting the value of synthetic data is context-dependent on real-data availability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the model collapse literature initiated by Shumailov et al. (2023/2024), who demonstrated collapse under a 'replace' training workflow in multivariate Gaussian modeling, kernel density estimation, and language model fine-tuning, and by Gerstgrasser et al. (2024), who hypothesized that an 'accumulate' workflow (retaining all prior real and synthetic data) avoids collapse. The key contribution is formally testing Gerstgrasser's two claims in the exact task settings from Shumailov's influential Nature paper, which had not been previously validated, and introducing a third 'accumulate-subsample' workflow that models fixed-compute constraints. The paper also draws on the broader synthetic-data-value literature (Dohmatob et al., Jain et al., Ferbach et al.) to frame its final analysis of when synthetic data helps versus hurts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The results provide actionable guidance for model providers: as synthetic content floods the internet, the critical design choice is whether to discard or retain prior data, and the paper shows that even under realistic fixed-compute constraints (accumulate-subsample), test loss plateaus rather than diverges, making catastrophic collapse unlikely. The finding that a small amount of synthetic data can improve test loss when real data is scarce (≤1024 samples) but degrades performance when real data is abundant offers a concrete, if preliminary, decision rule for practitioners. Open questions include how data-quality filtering interacts with these dynamics, whether results hold in multi-model ecosystems where different models generate different synthetic data, and whether the fixed-compute plateau eventually degrades over very long horizons.
