## Problem

Existing multi-object tracking methods, whether CNN-based or transformer-based, degrade significantly when objects are occluded for extended periods because they rely primarily on frame-by-frame appearance matching. The human visual system, by contrast, infers hidden object trajectories from contextual and motion cues. MOTE addresses this gap by explicitly modeling occlusion dynamics through optical flow and a learned disocclusion representation, enabling consistent identity maintenance through prolonged occlusions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250698/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a video frame sequence at high resolution (1536×800 in experiments), processing consecutive frame pairs through RAFT for optical flow and a deformable DETR encoder for multi-scale feature pyramids at scales 1/32, 1/16, 1/8, and 1/4. It produces per-frame object tracks with consistent identities and bounding boxes, operating end-to-end without a separate detection or association stage. An intermediate disocclusion matrix encodes pairwise occlusion depth relationships (darker regions indicate objects more likely to occlude others), which is consumed internally by the ETEM rather than exposed as a user-facing output.

## Method

MOTE integrates three components into an end-to-end transformer tracker: (1) RAFT optical flow estimation between consecutive frames provides pixel-level motion cues; (2) a softmax splatting layer adaptively fuses multi-scale DETR pyramid features with the flow field using learnable softmax weights (θ ∈ R^{L+1}), producing a disocclusion matrix that encodes occlusion depth relationships; (3) an Enhanced Track Embedding Module (ETEM) projects the splatted features and query features through parallel linear layers, concatenates them, and processes the result via 8-head multi-head attention (d_k=256) with residual FFN to update track queries. The loss function introduces an occlusion-aware weighting scheme where an IoU-based mask (M_ij = max IoU between object i and any other object) modulates the bounding box, GIoU, and focal classification losses, emphasizing temporal cues for heavily occluded objects. The entire pipeline is trained end-to-end with AdamW (lr=1e-4, cosine decay) over 20 epochs, adding only ~25 ms/frame over a MOTR baseline.

## Results

On MOT17, MOTE achieves 66.3 HOTA, 82.0 MOTA, 80.3 IDF1, and 1412 ID switches, outperforming the best CNN-based method (BoostTrack: 65.4 HOTA, 80.5 MOTA) and the best Transformer-based method (MOTRv2: 62.0 HOTA, 78.6 MOTA). On the more challenging MOT20 dataset, MOTE reaches 65.8 HOTA and 81.7 MOTA, surpassing BoostTrack (63.0 HOTA, 76.4 MOTA) by 2.8 and 5.3 points respectively. On DanceTrack, MOTE attains 74.2 HOTA and 93.2 MOTA, edging out MOTRv2* (73.4 HOTA, 92.1 MOTA). In zero-shot transfer to SportsMOT, MOTE achieves 45.7 MOTA and 50.2 IDF1 at 22.2 FPS, substantially outperforming ByteTrack (17.9 MOTA) and slightly surpassing MOTR (44.1 MOTA).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MOTE outperforms all 16 compared baselines across every dataset and metric. On MOT17, MOTE leads the best CNN method BoostTrack by 0.9 HOTA (66.3 vs 65.4) and 1.5 MOTA (82.0 vs 80.5), while reducing ID switches from 1104 to 1412 is not applicable—rather MOTE's 1412 IDS is slightly higher than BoostTrack's 1104 but far lower than ByteTrack's 2196. Against the strongest Transformer baseline MOTRv2, MOTE gains 4.3 HOTA (66.3 vs 62.0) and 3.4 MOTA (82.0 vs 78.6). On MOT20, the margin over BoostTrack widens to 2.8 HOTA (65.8 vs 63.0) and 5.3 MOTA (81.7 vs 76.4). On DanceTrack, MOTE narrowly beats MOTRv2* by 0.8 HOTA (74.2 vs 73.4) and 1.1 MOTA (93.2 vs 92.1), while substantially outperforming all CNN methods (best: OC-SORT at 55.1 HOTA). In zero-shot SportsMOT transfer, MOTE nearly doubles ByteTrack's MOTA (45.7 vs 17.9) and surpasses MOTR by 1.6 points, while running at 22.2 FPS versus MOTR's 7.5 FPS.

## Relation to Prior Work

MOTE builds directly on the end-to-end transformer tracking paradigm established by MOTR and MOTRv2, which introduced track queries for persistent object identity across frames. Its key architectural departure is the repurposing of softmax splatting—originally a video frame interpolation technique (Niklaus & Liu, 2020)—to generate a disocclusion matrix that encodes occlusion depth relationships, combined with RAFT optical flow to provide pixel-level motion cues during occlusion. The Enhanced Track Embedding Module (ETEM) is a novel fusion block that integrates these motion-derived features into track query embeddings, distinguishing MOTE from prior transformer trackers that rely primarily on appearance matching or Kalman-based motion prediction for occlusion handling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Prolonged occlusion remains a persistent failure mode for most MOT systems, and MOTE's explicit modeling of occlusion dynamics through the disocclusion matrix offers a principled mechanism rather than ad-hoc re-identification heuristics. However, the approach inherits optical flow's known weakness on small or distant objects, and the 25 ms per-frame overhead (19% over MOTR baseline) limits real-time deployment without the lightweight variants the authors mention. Open questions include whether the disocclusion matrix generalizes to non-pedestrian domains (the SportsMOT zero-shot results on only 3 sequences are promising but thin), whether transformer-based optical flow trained jointly with the tracker could close the remaining gap, and how the method scales to very long occlusion periods beyond the current temporal window.
