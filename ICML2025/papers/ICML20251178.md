## Problem

Diversity in training data, architecture, and providers is widely assumed to mitigate homogeneity across LLMs, yet there is no large-scale empirical evidence on whether different models actually differ meaningfully in their errors. The paper asks: how correlated are LLM errors, what model features predict high correlation, and what are the downstream consequences of that correlation in high-stakes settings like LLM-as-judge evaluation and hiring?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251178/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis consumes three datasets: 349 LLMs' answers on 14,402 MMLU multiple-choice questions (HuggingFace), 71 LLMs on 12,032 MMLU questions (HELM), and 20 LLMs scoring 1,800 resume-job description pairs against 450 human hand-labels. The key output metric is the pairwise error agreement rate—conditional on both models being wrong—rather than overall agreement, which controls for the confound that two accurate models trivially agree on correct answers. Downstream outputs include accuracy inflation curves for LLM-as-judge, systemic exclusion rates, and match probabilities in stable-matching labor market simulations.

## Method

The paper measures error correlation via agreement rate when both models err (for multiple-choice) or correlation in residuals from human labels (for resumes), then regresses this pairwise metric on model characteristics (same provider, same base architecture, parameter size, individual accuracy, and their interaction) across all model pairs. A striking finding is that the accuracy interaction term is positive and significant: more accurate models have more correlated errors even after conditioning on shared architecture and provider. For downstream effects, the paper (1) uses the most accurate model per provider/architecture as a judge and shows it systematically inflates the accuracy of less-accurate models, especially same-family ones, and (2) simulates stable-matching labor markets under five firm preference methods spanning full monoculture (same LLM) to full polyculture (uniformly random), testing theoretical predictions from Peng & Garg (2024a) on systemic exclusion, applicant welfare, and differential application access.

## Results

On the HELM dataset (71 models, 12,032 MMLU questions), the mean pairwise error agreement rate is 0.60 when both models are wrong, compared to a random baseline of 1/3; on HuggingFace (349 models, 14,042 questions), it is 0.423 versus a 0.127 baseline. Regression analysis (R² = 0.34–0.62) shows that same-provider (+0.022 to +0.066), same-architecture (+0.076), and higher individual accuracy (interaction term +0.023 to +0.043) all significantly increase error correlation. In the hiring simulation, even 20 firms using independently random LLMs produce ~20% systemic exclusion (vs. ~0% under uniformly random preferences), and full monoculture (one shared LLM) yields the worst firm welfare but the best applicant welfare in stable matching.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new method to benchmark against prior approaches; instead it empirically compares five firm-preference settings spanning the monoculture-to-polyculture spectrum. Against the uniformly random baseline (no LLMs), all LLM-based settings produce substantially higher systemic exclusion: even 'Random LLMs' (maximally diverse) yields ~20% exclusion with 20 firms, versus near-zero for random preferences. 'Same LLM' (full monoculture) produces the highest exclusion and the worst firm welfare (lowest match probability for top-rated applicants), while 'Latest LLMs' (most individually accurate but most correlated) paradoxically maximizes firm welfare, suggesting accuracy can outweigh diversity. On applicant welfare, the trend reverses: 'Same LLM' gives the best average applicant rank, and 'Uniformly Random' the worst, consistent with the theoretical prediction that monoculture benefits those who receive offers. For differential application access, monoculture is most robust: an applicant submitting 30 applications is only ~2× more likely to match than with 1 application under 'Same LLM', versus ~7× under 'Uniformly Random'.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper provides the first large-scale empirical test of the component sharing hypothesis (Bommasani et al., 2022; Toups et al., 2023) and the theoretical predictions of algorithmic monoculture (Kleinberg & Raghavan, 2021; Peng & Garg, 2024a), using 350+ LLMs across three datasets. It extends the LLM-as-judge literature (Zheng et al., 2023; Panickssery et al., 2024) by showing that self-preferencing generalizes to cross-model bias within the same provider or architecture family. Concurrent work by Goel et al. (2025) and Wenger & Kenett (2025) also study model correlation, but this paper uniquely quantifies correlates of similarity (provider, architecture, size, accuracy) via regression and connects them to downstream market outcomes in hiring.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that more accurate models exhibit *more* correlated errors directly challenges the assumption that ecosystem diversity (different providers, architectures) meaningfully reduces homogeneity, with concrete implications for LLM-as-judge calibration and hiring algorithm deployment. The persistence of ~20% systemic exclusion even with 20 distinct LLMs suggests that simply using different models is insufficient to avoid algorithmic monoculture harms. Key limitations include reliance on multiple-choice and single-score evaluations (not open-ended generation), and the open question of whether correlation will decrease as models diverge in training data or objectives. The paper calls for leaderboard developers to continuously track model correlation as a standard ecosystem metric.
