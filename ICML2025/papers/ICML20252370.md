## Problem

The Platonic Representation Hypothesis posits that independently trained unimodal models become increasingly aligned as they scale, and that this alignment predicts downstream multimodal performance. However, it remains unclear when and why alignment emerges implicitly, and whether alignment is a universally reliable indicator of performance. This paper investigates these questions by examining how data characteristics—specifically the balance of redundant versus unique information (interactions) and the degree of similarity between modalities (heterogeneity)—govern both the emergence of alignment and its relationship to task performance.

## Input / Output

The study takes multimodal data pairs (image-text, vision-audio, vision-text, audio-text) with associated task labels, along with independently trained unimodal encoders (single-layer MLPs for synthetic experiments; DINOv2, CLIP, MAE, and ImageNet classifiers for vision; LLaMA, BLOOM, and OpenLLaMA for language). For synthetic data, inputs are binary vectors decomposed into redundant and unique components with controlled nonlinearity depth; for real data, GPT-4 generates captions at 10%–100% uniqueness levels on the Wikipedia caption dataset. Outputs include alignment scores (unbiased CKA, mutual KNN, SVCCA), per-model task performance, and Spearman correlations between alignment and performance across model depths and dataset configurations.

## Method

The paper constructs a controlled synthetic data generation framework where task labels are computed as nonlinear functions of a fixed-size feature subset drawn from redundant (x_r) and unique (x_{u1}, x_{u2}) components, with heterogeneity introduced via a depth-parameterized MLP transformation φ(·) applied to the second modality. Unimodal encoders E_1 and E_{2,D_Enc} are trained independently on each modality, and alignment is measured between their learned representations using CKA and mutual KNN. The key empirical finding is that maximum achievable alignment is fundamentally bounded by the degree of uniqueness and heterogeneity: as uniqueness increases, the correlation between alignment and performance vanishes or becomes negative, while model depth continues to improve performance independently of alignment. This is validated on large-scale vision-language models (DINOv2 + LLaMA/BLOOM/OpenLLaMA) and MultiBench affective computing datasets, where alignment-performance correlations are strongly positive only for redundant tasks (e.g., AVMNIST digit classification) and weak or negative for tasks requiring unique information (e.g., sentiment, humor, sarcasm detection). The paper further demonstrates a practical algorithmic use case: the alignment-performance correlation computed from unimodal models predicts how much explicit alignment (via CLIP loss weighting) should be applied during finetuning to maximize downstream performance on MM-IMDb.

## Results

The paper's central finding is that the maximum achievable alignment between independently trained unimodal encoders decreases monotonically as informational uniqueness U increases (Spearman ρ between max alignment and U is strongly negative across all heterogeneity levels, Figures 4–5). In highly redundant settings (U=0), alignment is strongly correlated with downstream performance, but for U>3 the alignment–performance correlation drops toward zero and becomes negative in a significant fraction of tasks (Figure 7). On the real-world MultiBench datasets, AVMNIST (highly redundant digit images + spoken digits) shows strong positive alignment–performance correlations (0.887 for vision, 0.723 for audio), whereas affective-computing datasets (MOSEI, MOSI, URFUNNY, MUStARD) exhibit weak or negative correlations (ranging from −0.641 to +0.438 depending on modality pair). On MM-IMDb, the per-class alignment–performance linear-fit slope reliably predicts whether increasing the CLIP alignment loss weight w improves or degrades downstream classification accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical study rather than a method paper, so there are no traditional method-vs-baseline comparisons. The key comparison is against the Platonic Representation Hypothesis (Huh et al., 2024), which posits that better cross-modal alignment predicts better performance as models scale. The paper confirms this hypothesis holds in the redundant, low-heterogeneity regime (e.g., AVMNIST with ρ ≈ 0.89) but demonstrates it breaks down when modalities carry significant unique information: on four affective-computing benchmarks the alignment–performance correlation is weak or negative, and on synthetic data the correlation vanishes or inverts for U > 3. The paper further contrasts its findings with the implicit assumption in explicit-alignment literature (contrastive learning, CLIP-style objectives) that more alignment is always beneficial; the MM-IMDb experiment (Figure 10) shows that for classes with low alignment–performance slopes, increasing the CLIP loss weight w actually degrades performance, whereas for classes with high slopes it helps. Across all alignment metrics tested (unbiased CKA with linear/RBF kernels, SVCCA, mutual k-NN) and batch sizes (256, 512, 1024), the qualitative conclusions remain consistent, ruling out metric-specific artifacts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a systematic empirical follow-up to the Platonic Representation Hypothesis (Huh et al., 2024), which demonstrated that independently trained vision and language models become increasingly aligned with scale. While prior work on representation alignment (CKA, SVCCA, mutual KNN) and explicit alignment methods (contrastive learning, CLIP) treated alignment as a universally desirable property, this paper repurposes the existing alignment measurement toolkit to ask a different question: under what data conditions does alignment actually emerge and predict performance? It draws on the multimodal data taxonomy of interactions and heterogeneity (Baltrušaitis et al., 2018; Liang et al., 2024) to systematically vary these dimensions, extending the analysis beyond the redundant, similar-modality setting where the Platonic hypothesis was originally validated.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that alignment can be negatively correlated with performance when modalities contain significant unique information challenges the default assumption in multimodal learning that more alignment is always better, and provides practitioners with a concrete diagnostic (the alignment-performance slope) for deciding whether to apply explicit alignment objectives. The MM-IMDb experiment demonstrates a practical algorithmic use case: using unimodal alignment-performance correlations to tune the weight of a CLIP-style alignment loss during finetuning. Open questions remain about the theoretical mechanisms governing when and why alignment emerges, whether deliberately 'unaligned' representations could be beneficial for tasks requiring unique information, and whether these findings generalize beyond the two-modality setting studied here.
