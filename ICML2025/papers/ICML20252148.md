## Problem

No single anomaly detection algorithm performs well across all datasets, and existing methods are highly sensitive to hyperparameters. All prior AD model selection algorithms are either supervised (requiring anomaly labels) or offline (requiring the full test set upfront), making them infeasible for streaming settings where data arrives sequentially and labels are unavailable. This paper asks whether one can select the best anomaly detector online, in a fully unsupervised manner, as data streams in.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a stream of data points (tabular, time-series, or mixed-type) and, at each timestep, queries N base anomaly detectors (13 configurations spanning IForestASD, xStream, RRCF, and SPC) to obtain raw anomaly scores. These raw scores are normalized to [0,1] via streaming empirical quantiles (t-digest) to ensure cross-detector comparability, then aggregated into a single anomaly score S_t ∈ ℝ₊ per data point. The entire per-point computation is O(1) in stream length, and no anomaly labels are ever accessed during inference or weight updates.

## Method

SEAD's key insight is that anomalies are rare by definition, so a good detector consistently outputs small scores on a given stream; this allows anomaly scores themselves to serve as an unsupervised proxy loss for model selection. Concretely, SEAD treats each base detector as an expert in a Follow-the-Regularized-Leader (FTRL) framework, where the normalized anomaly score is both the expert's prediction and its incurred loss. Weights are updated via a single gradient step on a loss combining the weighted anomaly score with a KL-divergence regularizer toward a prior distribution π (uniform by default, reducing to classical multiplicative weights update). A theoretical regret bound of O(√(T ln N)) guarantees SEAD's cumulative score is competitive with the best single detector in hindsight. SEAD++ further reduces runtime by ~2× using Thompson sampling to query only K of N detectors per timestep, trading a small AP drop for halved inference cost.

## Results

Across 15 diverse datasets (ranging from 214 to 567,000 points, 1 to 71 features), SEAD achieves the best average rank of 5.07 with the lowest variance (6.64) among all 14 methods (13 base detectors + SEAD), outperforming ~60% of base models. Its worst-case rank is 9th, while every individual base model has at least one dataset where it ranks 10th or worse. SEAD++ (K=6 sampled detectors) achieves a ~2× runtime reduction (e.g., 1746s → 1002s on Ozone) with only a modest AP rank degradation (avg 5.8 vs 5.07). On the Ozone dataset, SEAD achieves APS 0.059 in 1746s, compared to MetaOD's 0.052 in 173,684s (35× slower) and Goswami et al.'s 0.070 in 61,543s.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the 13 individual base detectors (SPC, 4× RRCF, 4× xStream, 4× IForestASD), SEAD achieves the best average rank (5.07 vs. 5.40 for the best single variant xStream_2) and the lowest variance (6.64 vs. 6.89 for IForestASD_0), with a worst-case rank of 9th versus ≥10th for every base model. Against simple ensemble baselines (mean, max, min aggregation), SEAD wins on both average rank (5.07 vs. 5.73/5.80/6.60) and variance (6.64 vs. 12.35/6.74/20.69), with the largest gains on Letter (rank 4 vs. 9–11) and Optdigits (rank 5 vs. 6–13). Against offline model-selection methods on the Ozone dataset, SEAD slightly trails Goswami et al. (2023) on APS (0.059 vs. 0.070) but is 35× faster than MetaOD (1,746s vs. 173,684s) and 35× faster than Goswami et al. (1,746s vs. 61,543s). SEAD++ (K=6) trades a small AP degradation (avg rank 5.8 vs. 5.07) for roughly 2× runtime reduction across all datasets. In a robustness test adding 13 random detectors, SEAD's average performance drops only 0.88% versus 9.75% (mean), 22.31% (max), and 22.04% (min) for the ensemble baselines, confirming that SEAD's adaptive reweighting effectively neutralizes uninformative detectors.

## Relation to Prior Work

SEAD repurposes the classical multiplicative weights update (MWU) / FTRL framework from online learning (Cesa-Bianchi et al., 1997) as an unsupervised model-selection mechanism for streaming anomaly detection, treating each base detector as an 'expert' whose anomaly score serves as both prediction and loss. It departs from prior AD model selection work (MetaOD, ELECT, Goswami et al.) which is either supervised or requires all test data upfront with O(n²) operations, and from outlier ensembles (Rayana & Akoglu, 2014; 2016) that use expensive rank aggregation. The SEAD++ variant further borrows Thompson sampling (Russo et al., 2018) to subsample base detectors, reducing per-step computation from N to K without significant accuracy loss.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The O(1) per-point complexity makes SEAD practically viable for latency-sensitive streaming applications (e.g., ozone monitoring requiring <1 min detection) where offline methods like MetaOD take ~2 days on a 2,534-point stream. A key limitation is that SEAD is only as good as its best base detector in the pool, requiring at least one model whose assumptions match the data. Open questions include deriving regret guarantees under non-stationarity (the current O(√(T ln N)) bound assumes stationarity) and leveraging offline labeled datasets to inform the prior distribution π for improved online selection under drift.
