## Problem

Convergence of learned optimization algorithms is a long-standing open problem because traditional convergence proofs rely on limits and induction, which are inapplicable when the problem instances are functions that cannot be observed globally. Existing work circumvents this by imposing geometric safeguards (e.g., restricting updates to descent directions), which severely limits the algorithm's performance and design freedom. The paper asks: can one prove convergence of a fully unconstrained learned algorithm with high probability, without restricting its update rule?

## Input / Output

The learned algorithm A takes as input the current iterate z^(t), the previous iterate z^(t-1), the problem parameter p (e.g., matrix A and vector b for quadratic problems, or a dataset for neural network training), and internal randomness r^(t+1), and outputs the next iterate z^(t+1) = z^(t) + β^(t) d^(t), where both the direction d^(t) and step-size β^(t) are predicted by separate neural network blocks from features including the normalized gradient, normalized momentum, their coordinate-wise product, and logarithmically transformed norms and losses. Beyond the iterate, the framework produces a PAC-Bayesian lower bound on the probability that the full trajectory converges to a critical point of ℓ(·,p), computed from empirical estimates of the sufficient-descent, relative-error, and boundedness conditions over the training set.

## Method

The core contribution is a proof strategy that replaces direct convergence analysis with a generalization argument: the authors show that the three observable conditions from the Attouch–Bolte–Svaiter convergence theorem (sufficient-descent, relative-error, boundedness) define measurable sets in the trajectory space, and that their intersection is contained in the set of trajectories converging to a critical point (via the Kurdyka-Lojasiewicz property). By applying the PAC-Bayesian generalization theorem of Sucker & Ochs (2024) to the complement of this intersection, they obtain a high-probability lower bound on the convergence probability for unseen problem instances, without any geometric constraint on the update rule. The neural network architecture is deliberately unconstrained—it predicts a direction and step-size freely—and training uses a progressive probabilistic constraining procedure that iteratively increases the empirical probability of satisfying the sufficient-descent condition while preserving performance. The result is a generalization theorem (Theorem 7.6) that, for any parametric class of possibly non-smooth non-convex KL loss functions, certifies convergence to a critical point with high probability based solely on training-time observations.

## Results

In the quadratic problems experiment (d=200, 250 test sets of size N=250), the learned algorithm converges substantially faster than the worst-case optimal Heavy-Ball with Friction baseline, and the PAC-Bayesian bound guarantees convergence to a critical point in approximately 75% of test problems. In the non-smooth, non-convex neural network training experiment (d=351, 250 iterations), the learned algorithm minimizes the loss faster than Adam (tuned step-size κ=0.008) and the PAC-bound guarantees convergence in approximately 92% of problems. In both experiments, the PAC-bound is described as 'quite tight' for the observable event A (sufficient-descent + relative-error + boundedness), though a substantial gap remains between P{A} and P{A_conv} in the quadratic case.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the learned algorithm against two baselines. Against Heavy-Ball with Friction (HBF) on strongly convex quadratic problems, the learned algorithm clearly outperforms the worst-case optimal baseline in convergence speed (distance to minimizer over iterations), as shown in Figure 1. Against Adam on the non-smooth, non-convex task of training a two-layer ReLU network, the learned algorithm also minimizes the loss faster and appears to converge to a critical point, as shown in Figure 2. Crucially, the learned algorithm provides a probabilistic convergence guarantee (via the PAC-Bayesian bound) that neither baseline offers: ~75% for the quadratic case and ~92% for the neural network training case. The baselines are standard, well-tuned algorithms (HBF with optimal worst-case parameters from Nesterov 2018; Adam with grid-searched step-size), making the learned algorithm's advantage in both speed and guaranteed convergence the central empirical claim.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper combines two previously independent theoretical tools: the PAC-Bayesian trajectory generalization framework of Sucker & Ochs (2024), which bounds the probability of observing trajectories with certain properties on unseen problems, and the Kurdyka-Lojasiewicz-based convergence theorem of Attouch et al. (2013), which guarantees convergence to critical points under sufficient-descent, relative-error, and boundedness conditions. Prior work on convergence guarantees for learned optimizers has relied on either safeguarding the update step (constraining it to descent directions, as in Möller et al. 2019, Prémont-Schwarz et al. 2022) or unrolling with a fixed number of iterations (Gregor & LeCun 2010, Xin et al. 2016). This paper departs from both by showing that no design restriction is needed: one simply observes whether the KL conditions hold during training and uses generalization to bound the probability of convergence on test problems.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This resolves a long-standing gap in learning-to-optimize by providing convergence guarantees without the performance-degrading design constraints that prior safeguarded methods require, and the framework extends beyond optimization to any sequential prediction model with Markovian structure. However, practical limitations are significant: the full trajectory cannot be observed (only a finite prefix), training the algorithm to actually satisfy the KL conditions on a majority of problems is described as 'quite difficult and time-consuming,' and the sufficient-descent condition makes the approach inapplicable to stochastic optimization. The substantial gap between the PAC-bound and the true convergence probability (visible in both experiments) suggests the bound is conservative, and tightening it or extending to the stochastic setting are natural next steps.
