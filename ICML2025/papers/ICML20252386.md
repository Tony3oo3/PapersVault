## Problem

Deep attention networks are central to modern sequence modeling, yet their theoretical understanding lags far behind that of shallow multi-index models. A key obstacle is that multi-index models lack both the hierarchical structure of multiple attention layers and the sequential structure of token-level covariates, making it unclear whether existing analytical tools transfer. This paper bridges that gap by showing deep attention architectures are a special case of sequence multi-index (SMI) models, enabling a sharp high-dimensional characterization of their statistical and computational learning limits.

## Input / Output

The model consumes N i.i.d. Gaussian input sequences x^μ ∈ R^{D×M} (length M sequences of D-dimensional tokens) and produces K-dimensional outputs via a link function g applied to the low-rank projection Wx/√D. The analysis operates in the joint limit D, N → ∞ with α = N/D fixed, while sequence length M, weight rank P, and output dimension K remain finite. The SMI-to-multi-index mapping flattens each sequence into R^{DM} and replaces W with a block-diagonal matrix W_e ∈ R^{PM×DM} (Kronecker product W⊗I_M), converting the problem into a standard multi-index model with a non-separable prior on the shared weight blocks.

## Method

The core insight is that post-activations at every layer of a deep attention network depend on the input only through the indices Z_l = w_l x/√D, so the entire network reduces to a single SMI function g(W⋆x/√D) where W⋆ vertically concatenates all layer weights. For the Bayes-optimal error, the authors adapt the adaptive interpolation framework (Aubin et al., 2020; Barbier et al., 2019) to the block-structured multi-index formulation, yielding a finite-dimensional sup-inf variational problem (Theorem 2.1). For computational limits, they derive a Generalized AMP (GAMP) algorithm whose state evolution (Lemma 2.2) tracks the overlap matrix Q ∈ S_P^+ through a finite-dimensional recursion, and is provably optimal among first-order methods. Weak recovery thresholds are extracted via linear stability analysis of the Q=0 fixed point (Theorem 2.3), revealing a 'grand-staircase' mechanism where different layers become learnable at distinct sample complexities. For a two-layer attention model, this predicts that the second layer is learned first (at α₁ ≈ 0.14), followed by the first layer (at α₂ ≈ 0.79), a sequential learning pattern confirmed both in GAMP iterations and in SGD training dynamics.

## Results

For a two-layer tied-weights attention model (P₁=P₂=1, c=1, M=2), the paper identifies two sharp weak-recovery thresholds: α₁ ≈ 0.14 (above which the second layer weights become learnable) and α₂ ≈ 0.79 (above which the first layer weights become learnable), demonstrating that layers are learned sequentially in reverse order of depth. GAMP predictions from the state-evolution equations (Eq. 14) match numerical implementations in dimension D=1000 to high accuracy, with convergence in at most 50 iterations. SGD on the same two-layer architecture (D=500, α=15) reproduces the same sequential learning pattern, with the second layer learned after one epoch and the first layer only after the second is nearly perfectly reconstructed. On the TREC classification task with BERT embeddings, a practical two-layer transformer exhibits the same sequential learning phenomenon but with the layer order reversed (shallower layers converge first), consistent with prior observations in the literature.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a primarily theoretical paper and does not include a traditional benchmark comparison table against competing methods. The main empirical validation is internal: GAMP algorithm predictions (from the state-evolution equations) are compared against direct numerical implementations of GAMP in D=1000, showing excellent agreement for both the overlap matrix Q and the prediction error across the full range of sample complexities α. A second comparison is between GAMP dynamics and SGD dynamics on the same two-layer attention model, where both algorithms exhibit the same sequential layer-learning behavior (second layer first, then first layer), with SGD requiring approximately 10 iterations for the first layer to begin learning after the second layer is nearly converged. The TREC experiment serves as a qualitative sanity check rather than a quantitative comparison, showing that the sequential learning phenomenon persists in a realistic transformer trained with AdamW on real text data, though the layer order is reversed relative to the theoretical model.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate research threads: the high-dimensional statistical physics analysis of multi-index models (Gardner & Derrida, 1988; Aubin et al., 2018; Troiani et al., 2025) and the theoretical study of attention architectures (Cui et al., 2024a; Cui, 2025). The key conceptual move is showing that deep self-attention networks with tied, low-rank weights are a special case of sequence multi-index (SMI) models, a generalization of multi-index models to sequential covariates introduced by Cui et al. but not previously connected to attention. This mapping allows the entire GAMP/state-evolution and free-energy/adaptive-interpolation toolkit developed for multi-index models to be transferred to attention architectures, extending prior results that were limited to M=1 (single token) or P=1 (single index) to the general M>1, P>1 regime.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first rigorous characterization of both statistical and computational limits for learning multi-layer attention networks, revealing a sequential layer-wise learning phenomenon with sharp sample-complexity thresholds (a 'grand-staircase' mechanism) that is observed in GAMP, SGD, and even a real TREC classification task. The finding that the last layer is learned first, with earlier layers requiring progressively more data, provides a theoretical explanation for empirically observed training dynamics in transformers. Key limitations include the restriction to tied weights, Gaussian i.i.d. inputs, and the absence of fully connected layers between attention blocks; the phenomenology of untied, multi-head architectures and the precise factors determining layer-learning order in realistic settings remain open.
