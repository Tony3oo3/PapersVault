## Problem

Prior work established that in-context learning (ICL) in transformers is transient—emerging and then disappearing after long training—but left unexplained why ICL emerges at all if it is not asymptotically preferred. This paper seeks a mechanistic account of the cooperative and competitive dynamics between ICL and the strategies that eventually replace it, going beyond the purely competitive framing of earlier work.

## Input / Output

The model consumes sequences of two exemplar-label pairs (context) followed by a query image exemplar, where images are Omniglot handwritten characters embedded via a frozen ResNet-18 encoder pretrained on ImageNet. The transformer (2-layer, attention-only, d_model=64, 8 heads) outputs a one-hot class label prediction over 12,800 classes. Evaluation uses four OOD probes—ICL, IWL, CIWL, and Flip—that selectively invalidate or preserve different information channels to isolate which strategy the network is using at each training checkpoint.

## Method

The paper trains 2-layer attention-only transformers on 'bursty' few-shot sequences and uses behavioral evaluators plus causal ablations to identify the asymptotic strategy as context-constrained in-weights learning (CIWL), implemented by Layer 2 skip-trigram-copier heads with K- and V-composition from Layer 1. The key mechanistic finding is that ICL and CIWL share Layer 2 induction-head sub-circuits (cooperation) while competing in Layer 1, where heads switch from previous-token attention (ICL) to self-attention (CIWL). A minimal mathematical model with multiplicative loss terms for two mechanisms sharing sub-circuit vectors, plus a competition term, reproduces the transience and a non-monotonic 'divot' in CIWL formation. Guided by the model, the authors show that matching context and query exemplars removes the asymptotic bias toward CIWL, making ICL persistent even in larger-scale setups.

## Results

In a 2-layer attention-only transformer (d_model=64, 8 heads) trained on bursty Omniglot few-shot data (12,800 classes, 20 exemplars each), ICL emerges around 3×10⁶ sequences seen and then disappears, with the asymptotic strategy being context-constrained in-weights learning (CIWL) reaching 98.7% in-context accuracy. Pure in-weights learning (IWL) remains near chance (0.08% vs. ~0.008% chance). Ablations reveal substantial K- and V-composition between layers: preserving all but values drops CIWL accuracy to 59.3%, all but keys to 57.5%, while all but queries only drops to 95.4%. Training on data where the context exemplar exactly matches the query exemplar (removing the asymptotic bias toward CIWL) leads to persistent ICL, with the Flip evaluator converging to accuracy 1 in both small and large models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a mechanistic interpretability study rather than a method-comparison paper, so there are no traditional baselines. The paper instead compares three competing strategies—ICL, CIWL, and IWL—through four OOD evaluators (ICL, IWL, CIWL, and Flip) across training checkpoints. ICL dominates early (Flip ≈ 1.0), CIWL dominates asymptotically (Flip ≈ 0.0, CIWL ≈ 1.0), and IWL is negligible throughout. The key experimental comparisons show that (1) ICL cannot emerge on ICL-only data from scratch (loss plateau) but emerges rapidly when Layer 2 weights from a CIWL-trained network are clamped in; (2) CIWL-only checkpoints between ~10M–32M sequences enable ICL emergence, while later checkpoints do not; and (3) once CIWL is fully formed, switching to bursty data does not re-trigger ICL. These results establish that ICL emergence is cooperatively enabled by the shared Layer 2 sub-circuit with CIWL, while competition in Layer 1 drives its eventual transience.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the mechanistic interpretability line of work on induction heads (Olsson et al., 2022) and ICL transience (Singh et al., 2023) by identifying the actual asymptotic strategy as a hybrid 'context-constrained in-weights learning' (CIWL) rather than pure in-weights learning, and by revealing that ICL and CIWL share critical Layer 2 sub-circuits. It builds directly on the causal-intervention-on-dynamics framework and phase-change toy model of Singh et al. (2024), and on the data-properties framework of Chan et al. (2022), to introduce the concept of 'strategy coopetition'—where two strategies simultaneously cooperate (enabling each other's emergence) and compete (one eventually displacing the other). The minimal mathematical model of strategy racing with shared sub-circuits generalizes the single-mechanism loss landscape of Singh et al. (2024) to a multi-mechanism setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that ICL emergence is *enabled* by the very CIWL strategy that eventually replaces it is a counterintuitive reframing of strategy dynamics: the 'faster' strategy can only appear because the 'slower' strategy is partially formed, creating a backwards-hysteresis effect in learning. The toy model provides a general, testable framework for predicting when transient strategies will emerge, and the practical implication—that matching context and query exemplars removes the asymptotic bias and makes ICL persistent—offers a concrete data-design lever. Limitations include the 2-layer attention-only setting and synthetic task, though the authors show the matched-exemplar result extends to the larger setup of Singh et al. (2023). Open questions include whether coopetition dynamics generalize to deeper architectures, other strategy pairs, and real-world language tasks, and whether the superposition hypothesis for Layer 2 heads holds at scale.
