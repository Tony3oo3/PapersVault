## Problem

Existing causal abstraction definitions require the Abstract Invariance Condition (AIC), which forbids mapping two low-level values to the same high-level value if they have different downstream effects. This condition is routinely violated in practical settings such as dimensionality reduction and representation learning, leaving lossy abstractions formally undefined and preventing consistent causal inference across abstraction levels.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252720/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a low-level SCM (or observational data from it), a constructive abstraction function τ that may violate the AIC, and a projected C-DAG encoding the high-level causal structure. It produces a high-level SCM consistent with the low-level model for all Pearl Causal Hierarchy queries, and enables identification of high-level interventional and counterfactual quantities from low-level data. In experiments, inputs are colored MNIST images (28×28 RGB) paired with digit labels, and outputs include causal effect estimates (e.g., P(y_x|z)) and generated images at L1, L2, and L3 query levels.

## Method

The paper introduces projected abstractions, defined as partial SCM projections of τ-abstractions, where each low-level variable is split into an observed portion (retained at the high level) and an unobserved portion (pushed into the exogenous space to capture lost information). Algorithm 1 constructs the high-level model by assigning the unobserved portions a conditional distribution (Eq. 11) that encodes a soft intervention mapping high-level interventions to distributions over low-level interventions. For inference without the full model, the paper defines a projected C-DAG (Def. 8) that augments the standard C-DAG with additional edges around AIC-violation variables to capture new dependencies introduced by partial projection. A key result (Thm. 3) reduces abstract identification to classical identification on the high-level projected C-DAG, enabling standard ID algorithms to determine which high-level queries are estimable from low-level data. Experiments use GAN-NCMs to validate both the necessity of the projected C-DAG for correct estimation and the ability to generate high-quality images from binary representations via projected sampling.

## Results

In the MNIST estimation experiment (Fig. 6), the projected C-DAG model achieves decreasing mean absolute error (MAE) with more samples, while the standard C-DAG model fails to converge to the correct query. The projected C-DAG model also outperforms the abstractionless baseline in MAE, benefiting from operating in a lower-dimensional space. In the colored MNIST image generation experiment (Fig. 4), the projected sampling approach successfully reproduces L1, L2, and L3 causal queries using only a binary (1-bit) representation, whereas the RNCM baseline requires at least a 16-dimensional representation to function and fails entirely when forced to binary.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the standard C-DAG framework (Anand et al., 2023), the projected C-DAG clearly wins: in the MNIST estimation task, the C-DAG-constrained model is unable to learn the correct interventional query at all (its error does not decrease with more samples), while the projected C-DAG model converges to the correct answer. Against the RNCM baseline (Xia & Bareinboim, 2024), the projected sampling approach wins on representation efficiency: RNCM with a 16-dimensional representation produces reasonable L1/L2/L3 image samples but cannot be compressed further due to AIC limitations, and when forced to a binary representation it fails to generate coherent images. In contrast, the projected sampling method reproduces all three causal query types (L1 conditional, L2 interventional, L3 counterfactual) faithfully even with a single binary digit of representation, demonstrating that the soft-intervention mechanism in Eq. 11 preserves the information lost by the lossy abstraction in the exogenous space.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the causal abstraction framework of Beckers & Halpern (2019) and the constructive abstraction functions of Xia & Bareinboim (2024) by removing the Abstract Invariance Condition (AIC), which prior work required to hold for abstractions to be well-defined. The key technical departure is the introduction of partial SCM projections, where information lost in a lossy abstraction is captured as new exogenous variables, and the corresponding extension of the C-DAG (Anand et al., 2023) to a 'projected C-DAG' that accounts for new dependencies introduced by AIC violations. The experimental implementation builds on Neural Causal Models (Xia et al., 2021) via GAN-NCMs, and the identification theory connects to classical causal identification through a duality theorem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The AIC is frequently violated in practical settings involving dimensionality reduction or representation learning, yet prior causal abstraction theory was undefined in such cases; this paper provides the first complete formal framework (construction algorithm, graphical identification criteria, and duality theorem) for lossy abstractions. The dual abstract ID result (Thm. 3) elegantly reduces the abstract identification problem to standard identification on the high-level space, making existing ID algorithms directly applicable. Limitations include the requirement to specify the abstraction function τ and the soft-intervention distribution (Eq. 11), which is one choice among many, and experimental validation is confined to MNIST-scale settings. Open questions include how to learn τ under AIC violations, whether the projected C-DAG criteria remain tractable in large-scale models, and how the framework extends to continuous or high-dimensional exogenous spaces.
