## Problem

Mechanistic interpretability aims to reverse-engineer a neural network's internal computation into a human-readable circuit, but the notion of a valid mechanistic interpretation has been defined only ad-hoc, with no principled criteria for judging whether a proposed interpretation faithfully captures the model's behavior. Existing evaluations (e.g., Nanda et al., 2023a) check individual components in isolation but ignore the compositional structure of the network, leaving open the possibility that per-component errors compound into a globally invalid interpretation.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes a neural network model expressed in a purely functional language λ_T, a candidate mechanistic interpretation in a human-interpretable language λ_H, a layer- or block-wise decomposition of the model, task-specific abstraction (α) and concretization (γ) functions that map between real-valued activations and discrete symbolic features, and an unlabeled test dataset drawn from the input distribution. It produces a per-axiom error rate ε (the fraction of test inputs violating each axiom's equality condition) with Clopper-Pearson confidence intervals, yielding a quantitative validity verdict for the interpretation at both the component and prefix (compositional) levels.

## Method

The paper formalizes mechanistic interpretation via four axioms inspired by abstract interpretation from program analysis: ε-Prefix Equivalence and ε-Component Equivalence require the abstract and concrete intermediate states to agree with probability ≥ 1−ε, while ε-Prefix Replaceability and ε-Component Replaceability require that substituting abstract components into the concrete network leaves the final output unchanged with probability ≥ 1−ε. The key technical insight is that the prefix-level axioms (1 and 3) are not implied by the component-level axioms (2 and 4) because per-component errors compound, so both levels must be checked independently. The α and γ functions, analogous to abstraction and concretization in Cousot & Cousot's framework, bridge the type gap between continuous activations and discrete symbolic features, and must be instantiated per interpretation. The authors validate the framework on two case studies: a new two-layer ReLU Transformer trained on 2-SAT (where the first block is interpreted as a clause parser via decomposed QK attention analysis and the second as an exhaustive evaluator via decision-tree learning over 34 sparse hidden neurons), and the modular addition model of Nanda et al. (2023a), confirming that the latter's interpretation satisfies all four axioms with ε ≈ 0.000335.

## Results

The 2-SAT Transformer model achieves 99.76% test accuracy. Axiomatic validation of the first block (parser) yields near-perfect prefix equivalence (ε ≈ 0.0000374 with order-independent clause matching) and modest prefix replaceability (ε ≈ 0.0418). For the second block's hidden layer, decision-tree neuron interpretations achieve ε ≈ 0.182 for component equivalence versus ε ≈ 0.309 for simpler disjunction-only interpretations, while both achieve strong replaceability (ε ≈ 0.0128 and 0.00290 respectively). The modular addition model from Nanda et al. (2023a) is validated with ε = 0.000335 under a three-decimal discretization, confirming their interpretation satisfies all four axioms.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against competing interpretability methods in the traditional sense; instead, it evaluates two neuron-interpretation strategies (decision trees vs. disjunction-only) under the same axiomatic framework. Decision trees achieve substantially better component-level fidelity (ε ≈ 0.182 vs. 0.309) at the cost of reduced human interpretability, while disjunction-only interpretations paradoxically yield better replaceability scores (ε ≈ 0.00290 vs. 0.0128), illustrating a non-trivial trade-off between internal-state accuracy and end-to-end behavioral equivalence. The paper also contrasts its four-axiom framework with the evaluation provided in Nanda et al. (2023a), noting that the latter supplies evidence only for Axioms 2 and 4 (component-level) but omits the compositional Axioms 1 and 3, which are necessary to rule out error compounding across components. Compared to causal abstraction (Geiger et al., 2021; 2025) and causal scrubbing (Chan et al., 2022), the proposed axioms directly evaluate equivalence of intermediate representations (Axioms 1–2) rather than relying solely on intervention-based output comparisons, and require both abstraction (α) and concretization (γ) functions, whereas causal abstraction lacks a concretization operator and causal scrubbing lacks an abstraction operator.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the abstraction/concretization framework from abstract interpretation in program analysis (Cousot & Cousot, 1977) to formalize what it means for a mechanistic interpretation to be valid. It positions itself as complementary to causal abstraction (Geiger et al., 2021, 2025) and causal scrubbing (Chan et al., 2022), noting that the former lacks a concretization operator and the latter lacks an abstraction operator, while both omit direct evaluation of intermediate-state equivalence. The paper explicitly critiques the evaluation methodology in Nanda et al. (2023a) for checking only component-level and end-to-end behavior while ignoring compositional (prefix-level) error accumulation, which the new Axioms 1 and 3 address.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The axiomatic framework provides the first quantitative, automated criterion for judging mechanistic interpretation validity that explicitly accounts for compositional error compounding—a gap the authors demonstrate empirically (Appendix F). However, the axioms are necessary but potentially not sufficient, the derivability axioms (5–6) remain unimplemented, and the framework currently requires discrete outputs, limiting applicability to continuous-valued tasks. Open questions include whether the axioms scale to larger models, how to handle the trade-off between interpretability and fidelity quantitatively (as illustrated by the decision-tree vs. disjunction-only comparison), and whether the framework can be extended to parallel computational graphs without the efficiency costs noted in Appendix B.1.
