## Problem

Diagonal groups of isometries, which describe invariance under simultaneous movement of multiple objects, arise naturally in many-body quantum problems but lack natural invariant maps except in special cases. It is unclear whether standard ML symmetrization techniques (data augmentation, group averaging, canonicalization) improve or degrade neural network solvers trained via variational Monte Carlo, where the infinite-data regime creates a unique computational-statistical tradeoff not captured by existing analyses.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251025/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes n-electron position configurations in R^{3n} (restricted to a finite supercell), fixed atom positions under the Born-Oppenheimer approximation, and a space group G (e.g., P6mm, Fm3m, Im3m) as input. It produces an anti-symmetric ground state wavefunction ψ: R^{3n} → C, with performance measured by energy in Hartree and local energy variance in Hartree². The 'training data' is generated on-the-fly via MCMC sampling from the current wavefunction's probability distribution, meaning sample size is limited only by computational budget rather than a fixed dataset.

## Method

The paper systematically compares three symmetrization strategies—data augmentation (DA), group averaging (GA), and smoothed canonicalization (SC)—applied both during training and post-hoc to the DeepSolid architecture, with all hyperparameters held fixed. The key theoretical insight is that VMC's infinite-data regime means every symmetry operation during training forgoes one new MCMC sample, inflating gradient variance (Proposition 4.1, Lemma 4.2) and destabilizing optimization. Post hoc averaging (PA) circumvents this tradeoff because inference incurs only sampling cost, not gradient cost, allowing moderate averaging over group elements without reducing effective sample size. The paper also develops a smoothed canonicalization for diagonal invariance under space groups (Theorem E.1) that respects anti-symmetry but requires n×|G_ε| evaluations, making it computationally prohibitive for training. Empirically, PA outperforms all in-training symmetrization methods in energy, variance, and symmetry metrics, with one case approaching the performance of 10× more training compute.

## Results

On Graphene 1×1 (12 electrons, P6mm), post hoc averaging (PA) achieves the best energy of −76.050(3) Ha and lowest variance of 0.33(1) Ha², outperforming the unsymmetrized DeepSolid baseline (OG: −76.039(6) Ha, 2.02(3) Ha²) by ~0.011 Ha in energy and ~6× in variance reduction. On LiH 2×2×2 (32 electrons, Fm3m), PA over the full Fm3m group reaches −8.1507(8) Ha with variance 0.0118(7) Ha², approaching the energy reported by Li et al. (−8.15096(1) Ha) which required 10× more training steps. On bcc-Li 2×2×2 (48 electrons, Im3m), PA over Im3m achieves −15.022(3) Ha and 0.031(3) Ha², improving over OG (−15.011(1) Ha, 0.059(2) Ha²). In-training symmetrization (DA, GA) is consistently worse than PA at matched computational budgets, and post hoc canonicalization (PC) is catastrophically unstable (−70.1(9) Ha on graphene).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the original unsymmetrized DeepSolid (OG) from Li et al. (2022), used as the reference architecture with identical hyperparameters across all symmetrization variants. PA consistently wins over OG on both energy and variance across all three systems: on graphene, PA improves energy by 0.011 Ha and reduces variance by 6×; on LiH, PA over Fm3m improves energy by 0.013 Ha and variance by 5×; on bcc-Li, PA over Im3m improves energy by 0.011 Ha and variance by ~2×. In-training symmetrization methods (DA, GA, GAs) are all inferior to PA at matched GPU-hour budgets: DA actually worsens variance (2.48 vs 2.02 Ha² on graphene), GA with N=1000 and k=12 requires 351 GPU hours for a worse energy (−76.034) than PA's 281 hours (−76.050), and GAs, while competitive, still falls short of PA. The paper further notes that PA with 40k training steps matches the performance of GA with 10k steps, meaning PA achieves equivalent results with 1/4 the training budget. Post hoc canonicalization (PC) is a clear failure, producing an energy of −70.1 Ha on graphene (vs. −76.05 for PA), attributed to derivative blowup in the smoothing function.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on DeepSolid (Li et al., 2022), a FermiNet-based neural network solver for periodic solids, and adapts the smoothed canonicalization framework of Dym et al. (2024) to the diagonal invariance setting of space groups. It departs from the standard ML symmetrization literature (Chen et al. 2020; Huang et al. 2022; Lyle et al. 2020), which analyzes data augmentation and group averaging in finite-dataset regimes, by identifying a fundamentally different computational-statistical tradeoff in VMC's 'infinite-data' setting where every symmetry operation displaces a new MCMC sample. Prior VMC symmetry work has focused on translations, simple molecular point groups, or continuous groups (SO(2), SU(2), E(3)); this paper targets the harder case of restricted diagonal symmetries induced by crystallographic space groups, where no natural smooth invariant map is known to exist.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that in-training symmetrization destabilizes VMC optimization and can worsen performance challenges the prevailing assumption that incorporating physical symmetries always helps, and provides practitioners with a clear, low-cost alternative (post hoc averaging) that improves energy, variance, and symmetry without any training modification. Open questions include whether more efficient canonicalizations exist for space groups, whether the negative in-training findings generalize to other architectures and optimizers beyond DeepSolid/KFAC, and whether an optimal subgroup selection strategy exists that balances symmetry coverage against computational cost. The paper also notes that for simpler symmetries (translations, E(3)), more efficient symmetrizations exist that may avoid the observed tradeoffs, limiting the scope of the negative result to restricted diagonal groups.
