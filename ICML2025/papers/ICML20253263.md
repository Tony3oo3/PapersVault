## Problem

The widespread framing of AGI as a unifying 'north-star goal' in AI research creates an illusion of consensus while simultaneously aggravating existing problems with scientific rigor, value-laden assumptions, and community exclusion. The paper addresses the gap that no prior work has systematically identified how AGI discourse specifically obstructs productive goal-setting across scientific, engineering, and societal dimensions, despite growing critiques of individual aspects like SOTA-chasing or underspecification.

## Input / Output

As a position paper, the analytical input is the current landscape of AGI discourse spanning research papers, corporate definitions (e.g., OpenAI's economic-value framing), popular media claims, and benchmark initiatives like ARC-AGI. The output is a structured diagnosis of six interrelated goal-setting 'traps' paired with three concrete recommendations (goal specificity, pluralism, greater inclusion), plus a rebuttal against the counterargument that refined AGI definitions would resolve the identified problems.

## Method

The paper identifies six interrelated traps—Illusion of Consensus, Supercharging Bad Science, Presuming Value-Neutrality, Goal Lottery, Generality Debt, and Normalized Exclusion—and argues each is structurally aggravated by AGI narratives rather than merely correlated with them. It builds on prior work about exploratory vs. confirmatory research ambiguity (Herrmann et al., 2024), SOTA-chasing (Raji et al., 2021), homogenization (Kleinberg & Raghavan, 2021), and values embedded in ML (Birhane et al., 2022b), synthesizing these into a unified goal-setting framework. The authors then propose three recommendations: prioritizing specificity in goal language, centering pluralism about multiple worthwhile approaches, and fostering innovation through greater inclusion of disciplines and communities. They preemptively rebut the strongest counterargument (that improved AGI definitions, e.g., Morris et al.'s levels framework, would suffice) on three grounds: conflict with pluralism, AGI's cultural hype potential, and the argument that 'benefiting humans' is a more legitimate unifying goal.

## Results

This is a position paper with no quantitative experiments or results tables. The authors identify six 'traps' in AI research goal-setting that are aggravated by AGI discourse: Illusion of Consensus, Supercharging Bad Science, Presuming Value-Neutrality, Goal Lottery, Generality Debt, and Normalized Exclusion. They propose three recommendations to avoid these traps: (1) prioritize specificity in scientific, engineering, and societal goals, (2) center pluralism about multiple worthwhile approaches, and (3) foster innovation through greater inclusion of disciplines and communities. The paper concludes by rebutting the counterargument that improved AGI definitions would suffice, arguing instead that 'supporting and benefiting human beings' is a more appropriate overarching goal.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not compare methods against baselines in the empirical sense. The closest analogue is the rebuttal in §4, where the authors engage with the alternative view that AGI can remain a north-star goal if accompanied by improved definitions (e.g., Morris et al., 2024's 'Levels of AGI' framework). The authors acknowledge that such proposals may mitigate individual traps (e.g., Illusion of Consensus, Presuming Value-Neutrality) but argue they fall short on three grounds: (1) a single unifying AGI goal conflicts with their pluralism recommendation, (2) the cultural weight of 'intelligence' and 'generality' perpetuates hype regardless of definitional precision, and (3) a human-benefit framing better supports collective legitimacy and democratic accountability. No empirical head-to-head comparison is provided.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes a broad and previously fragmented set of critiques—on bad science in ML (Herrmann et al., 2024; D'Amour et al., 2022), SOTA-chasing (Raji et al., 2021; Church & Kordoni, 2022), values embedded in research (Birhane et al., 2022b; Blili-Hamelin & Hancox-Li, 2023), exclusion (Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018), and contested AGI definitions (Mitchell, 2024; Mueller, 2024; Altmeyer et al., 2024)—into a unified framework of six 'traps' that AGI discourse aggravates. Rather than proposing a new AGI definition (as Morris et al., 2024 or Chollet, 2019 do), it departs from the definitional debate entirely, arguing the concept itself is counterproductive as a north-star goal. The 'Generality Debt' framing, borrowed from software engineering's technical debt (Sculley et al., 2014), is a novel conceptual repurposing that links vague generality claims to deferred engineering and societal decisions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central claim—that AGI discourse systematically undermines scientific rigor, inclusion, and goal-setting rather than unifying the field—challenges a dominant narrative that has shaped funding, hiring, and research agendas since the LLM era. Its three recommendations (specificity, pluralism, inclusion) are actionable but face the practical difficulty that AGI framing is deeply embedded in corporate strategy (e.g., OpenAI's $100B profit threshold) and media discourse, making a community-wide shift unlikely without structural incentives changing. Open questions include whether 'supporting and benefiting human beings' is sufficiently specific to function as an alternative organizing principle, and whether the six-trap framework is too broad to generate concrete methodological changes in how individual labs set and evaluate research goals.
