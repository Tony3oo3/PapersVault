## Problem

Classical change-point detection methods (CUSUM, MOSUM) are inapplicable to dynamic networks that grow cumulatively, because adjacency matrices change size over time, edges persist irreversibly, and post-change networks are generated by mixtures of multiple graphon functions. This paper introduces the Graphon Attachment Network Model (GAN-M) to formalize such evolving networks and develops a new detection statistic tailored to the resulting piecewise polynomial subgraph count signals.

## Input / Output

The method operates on a single final network G_T (not a time series of networks), from which subgraph counts N_H(G_t) are computed retrospectively for each prefix t. The user specifies a subgraph structure H (e.g., triangle, line segment) and a data-driven threshold τ; the algorithm outputs the estimated number of change-points K̂ and their locations η̂₁,…,η̂_K̂. The threshold is set via a short-window CUSUM-like statistic over intervals of length h = ⌊k log T⌋, with k chosen based on suspected degree heterogeneity.

## Method

The key insight (Lemma 2.6) is that expected subgraph counts in GAN-M evolve as piecewise polynomial functions of degree |V(H)|, with the leading coefficient determined by the homomorphism density of the current graphon. To detect shifts in these polynomial trends under strong temporal dependence, the authors propose WESUM (Weighted SUM), a multi-component statistic where each component uses weights constructed via orthogonal projection against polynomial basis vectors up to order |V(H)|, ensuring polynomial trends are removed while the change-point signal is preserved. The full statistic is the Euclidean norm across all |V(H)|+1 components, reducing to classical CUSUM when |V(H)|=0. The detection algorithm combines seeded intervals (Kovács et al., 2023) with random interval distillation (Fan & Wu, 2024) for O(T log T) complexity, and localizes each change-point via argmax of the WESUM statistic within refined intervals. Theoretical guarantees establish consistency and a localization rate of O((T^{|V(H)|−1/2}√log T / min κ_k)^{1/(|V(H)|+1/2)}).

## Results

Across four simulation scenarios (degree heterogeneity, triangle detection, SBM, RDPG), WESUM achieves near-perfect change-point count recovery (90–100% correct K) and high ARI values (0.87–1.00) when using small subgraphs like edges and triangles. The best performance occurs in the SBM scenario with (C₁,C₂)=(0.12,0.04) using the edge subgraph: 100/100 correct K, normalized Hausdorff distance H(η̂,η)/T = 0.26×10⁻², and ARI = 1.00. On the real email-Eu-core network (986 nodes, 803 days), the method detects 2–3 change points depending on subgraph choice, with triangles and rectangles both identifying shifts at nodes ~432–434 and ~795. Smaller subgraphs (edges, triangles) consistently outperform larger ones (rectangles, 4-cycles), with H/T values roughly 3–10× lower.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not include a head-to-head comparison table against other change-point detection methods. The authors explicitly argue that classical CUSUM and MOSUM statistics are inapplicable to this setting due to three fundamental obstacles: varying network sizes over time, cumulative (irreversible) edge growth, and the mixture-of-graphons structure after change points. They note that 'it is difficult to find suitable benchmark methods in simulations,' which is why the evaluation focuses on the method's own performance across diverse scenarios (dense, sparse, degree-heterogeneous) and subgraph choices rather than direct method-vs-method comparison. The closest implicit baseline is the classical CUSUM statistic, which the paper shows theoretically fails (Section 2.2) because its population version is no longer maximized at the true change point when the signal is piecewise polynomial rather than piecewise constant. The sensitivity analysis (Table 3) serves as an internal comparison across five subgraph types, showing that edges and triangles (2–3 nodes) consistently outperform rectangles and 4-cycles (4 nodes) by a factor of 3–10× in normalized Hausdorff distance, confirming the theoretical prediction that smaller subgraphs yield tighter detection bounds.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of graphon theory (Lovász, 2012), preferential attachment models (Barabási & Albert, 1999), and classical change-point detection (CUSUM/MOSUM). It builds directly on the random interval distillation procedure of Fan & Wu (2024) and the seeded intervals method of Kovács et al. (2023) for the algorithmic pipeline, while drawing on Yu et al. (2022) for localization bounds in piecewise polynomial signals. The key departure from prior work is the identification of three structural obstacles—varying network sizes, cumulative irreversible growth, and post-change mixture of graphon functions—that render standard CUSUM/MOSUM inapplicable, motivating the WESUM statistic with polynomial-orthogonal weights.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first framework to unify graphon-based attachment probabilities with a temporal growth process, providing a principled setting for change-point detection in networks that grow cumulatively. The theoretical guarantees (consistency of K̂ and localization rate ε/Δ → 0) establish that the problem is tractable under a signal-to-noise condition, which is valuable for the network science community. Open questions include extending WESUM to abrupt (non-polynomial) graphon changes, incorporating positive feedback mechanisms (as sketched in Appendix A.6), and determining optimal subgraph choices for detection. The method's reliance on subgraph counts limits its applicability to settings where such counts are computationally feasible, and the real-data validation is limited to a single email network.
