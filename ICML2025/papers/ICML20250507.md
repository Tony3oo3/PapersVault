## Problem

Influence functions for data attribution have been restricted to decomposable losses (M-estimators) where the total loss is a sum of per-sample terms, because the standard derivation perturbs individual loss weights. This excludes widely used objectives like Cox partial likelihood, contrastive losses, and listwise ranking losses, where a single loss term couples multiple data points and cannot be decomposed. The paper asks whether IF-based attribution can be extended to these non-decomposable losses without case-specific mathematical derivations.

## Input / Output

Given a trained model at its full-dataset optimum θ̂(1), the training dataset, and a non-decomposable loss L(θ, b) parameterized by a binary presence vector b, VIF computes for each training object i the gradient difference ∇θ[L(θ̂(1), 1) − L(θ̂(1), 1_{−i})] and the Hessian ∇²θL(θ̂(1), 1)/n, both evaluated at the original parameters without any retraining. The resulting influence vector can be projected onto any target function f(z, θ) via the chain rule to yield a scalar influence score per training point. No model retraining or per-point optimization is required; all quantities are obtained through standard auto-differentiation on the existing model.

## Method

VIF is derived by revisiting the general statistical influence function, which defines the IF as a functional derivative of the estimator θ̃(P) with respect to the data distribution P in a perturbation direction Q. The key move is to replace the infinitesimal limit in this general formula with a finite difference: instead of evaluating the gradient change under an ε-perturbation of P, VIF computes the exact gradient difference between the loss on the full dataset and the loss with one object removed, both at the original parameters. This finite-difference form (Eq. 10) is shown to recover the classical IF exactly for M-estimators (Theorem 3.6) and to approximate the exact Cox regression IF with O_p(1/n) error (Theorem 3.10). Because the formula only involves the Hessian and a gradient difference of the full loss, it can be computed entirely through auto-differentiation without any loss-specific derivation, and it scales to non-convex models via the same heuristic tricks (Conjugate Gradient, LiSSA) used in prior IF work.

## Results

VIF achieves Pearson correlation coefficients of 0.997 (METABRIC), 0.943 (SUPPORT), 0.407 (Karate), 0.823 (Mediamill), and 0.906 (Delicious) against brute-force leave-one-out retraining, closely matching the upper-bound correlation between two independent brute-force runs (0.997, 0.955, 0.419, 0.999, 0.999 respectively). In terms of runtime, VIF achieves speed-ups of up to 1097× over brute-force LOO retraining (e.g., 2.43 sec vs. 24 min on METABRIC, 12.3 sec vs. 225 min on SUPPORT). The lower correlation on the Karate node embedding task (0.407) is attributed to inherent randomness in the DeepWalk random-walk procedure, and VIF still matches the brute-force upper bound (0.419).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare VIF against other influence-function or data-attribution methods; instead, it validates VIF against brute-force leave-one-out (LOO) retraining as the ground-truth reference. VIF matches the brute-force upper-bound correlation almost exactly on Cox Regression (0.997 vs. 0.997 on METABRIC) and closely on SUPPORT (0.943 vs. 0.955). On the Karate node embedding task, VIF (0.407) is within 0.012 of the brute-force upper bound (0.419), with the moderate absolute correlation explained by randomness in the DeepWalk random-walk construction. On Listwise LTR, VIF achieves 0.823 (Mediamill) and 0.906 (Delicious) versus the near-perfect brute-force upper bound of 0.999, indicating some approximation error but still strong agreement. The key practical advantage is computational: VIF reduces runtime by 593×–1097× on Cox regression, 20×–236× on LTR, and 1.87× on the small Karate graph, making it feasible for large-scale data attribution where brute-force retraining is intractable.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the influence-function-based data attribution framework of Koh & Liang (2017), which was restricted to decomposable (M-estimator) losses, by revisiting the general functional-derivative formulation of influence functions from robust statistics (Huber & Ronchetti, 2009). The key conceptual move is replacing the intractable limit in the general IF with a finite-difference approximation that is computable via standard auto-differentiation, eliminating the need for case-specific derivations. This departs from prior graph-specific IF adaptations (Chen et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2023) by providing a unified formulation applicable to any non-decomposable loss, and from the statistics literature (e.g., Reid & Crepeau, 1985 for Cox regression) by avoiding closed-form derivations in favor of a generic computational recipe. The non-convexity handling follows the same heuristic tricks used in Grosse et al. (2023) for large-scale IF computation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

VIF opens IF-based data attribution to a substantially broader class of learning objectives—contrastive losses, ranking losses, survival analysis—that were previously inaccessible to the standard IF recipe, with speedups of up to 10³× over brute-force LOO retraining. However, the formal derivation assumes convexity, and the paper acknowledges that adapting VIF to large-scale non-convex neural networks requires additional tricks (CG, LiSSA) whose effectiveness on truly large models remains untested. A key open question is whether the finite-difference approximation maintains accuracy for highly non-convex, high-dimensional losses where the Hessian is ill-conditioned, and whether more advanced inverse-Hessian approximations (EK-FAC, ensembles, gradient projection) can be seamlessly integrated. The empirical validation, while thorough, is limited to small-to-medium datasets and mostly linear or shallow models, leaving scalability to deep architectures as an important future direction.
