## Problem

Existing convergence guarantees for policy gradient methods to deterministic policies require a fixed stochasticity level set to O(ε) from the start of learning, which is impractical. In practice, practitioners dynamically adjust stochasticity during training, but no theoretical guarantees existed for this common practice. This paper asks whether PG methods can provably converge to the optimal deterministic policy when stochasticity is varied over the course of learning.

## Input / Output

The method takes an MDP (state/action spaces, transition model, reward, discount factor), a parametric deterministic policy class μ_θ, and for PES a deterministic decreasing stochasticity schedule σ_p = σ_max(p+1)^(-y) along with per-phase iteration counts K_p and learning rates ζ_p. For SL-PG, it additionally takes a stochasticity mapping f: R → [σ_min, σ_max] and jointly optimizes the scalar parameterization ξ. The output is a deterministic policy parameter θ_P (PES) or a joint parameterization v_K = (θ_K, ξ_K) (SL-PG), accompanied by last-iterate convergence bounds to the optimal deterministic or stochastic policy respectively.

## Method

PES (Phased Exploration Schedule) runs a PG subroutine (GPOMDP for action-based or PGPE for parameter-based exploration) with fixed stochasticity σ_p in each phase p, reducing σ according to a deterministic power-law schedule across P = (ε/σ_max)^(-1/y) phases. The key theoretical ingredient is proving that the expected return J_†(θ, σ) is Lipschitz-continuous in σ under a scale-invariant noise assumption (Assumption 5.3), which bounds the performance loss when switching from phase p-1 to phase p. Under weak gradient domination on the deterministic objective J_D, PES achieves Õ(ε^{-5}) sample complexity for last-iterate convergence to the optimal deterministic policy. The paper also analyzes SL-PG, which jointly learns policy parameters and stochasticity via gradient ascent on a joint objective, achieving Õ(ε^{-3}) convergence but only to the optimal stochastic (hyper)policy and requiring a direct WGD assumption on the joint objective rather than inheriting it from J_D.

## Results

Theoretically, PES achieves last-iterate global convergence to the optimal deterministic policy with a sample complexity of Õ(ε⁻⁵), while SL-PG achieves Õ(ε⁻³) convergence to the optimal stochastic (hyper)policy. Empirically on Swimmer-v5 (MuJoCo, T=200), deterministic deployment scores (Table 1) show PES-A achieving 61.14±1.1 (PB) and 28.93±9.89 (AB), SL-PG achieving 61.43±2.73 (PB) and 49.83±13.38 (AB), and PES-B achieving 60.40±2.31 (PB) and 29.24±8.74 (AB). The noise-removal loss Δ† = J_D(θ_K) − J_†(θ_K) is smaller for PES (−0.12 to −0.27) than for SL-PG (−0.21 to −1.19), confirming the theoretical advantage of controlled stochasticity decay.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| PES-A (P=25, K_p=200, y=1) | 61.14 ± 1.1 | −0.12 | 28.93 ± 9.89 | +0.44 |
| SL-PG (K=5000, σ=e^ξ) | 61.43 ± 2.73 | −0.21 | **49.83 ± 13.38** | −1.19 |

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares PES (two schedule variants) and SL-PG against their static-stochasticity counterparts: GPOMDP (Baxter & Bartlett, 2001) for action-based exploration and PGPE (Sehnke et al., 2010) for parameter-based exploration, both run for K=5000 iterations with fixed σ ∈ {1, 0.5, 0.04, 0.014}. In the parameter-based setting, PES-B outperforms the static PGPE baselines in convergence speed to optimal performance (Figure 1a), while PES-A converges more slowly due to its longer phase length (K_p=200). In the action-based setting, SL-PG substantially outperforms both PES variants in deterministic deployment (49.83 vs. ~29), because PES can get stuck in local optima when K_p is insufficient, whereas SL-PG adapts σ to escape them. However, PES incurs a smaller noise-removal penalty (Δ_A ≈ +0.4) compared to SL-PG (Δ_A = −1.19), meaning the deterministic policy deployed from PES is closer to its stochastic training performance. Overall, the empirical results validate the theoretical trade-off: PES provides guaranteed deterministic convergence with controlled noise removal, while SL-PG offers better exploration adaptability at the cost of uncontrolled final stochasticity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends Montenegro et al. (2024, ICML), which established last-iterate convergence guarantees for PG methods with *static* stochasticity (fixed σ = O(ε) throughout learning). The key conceptual departure is replacing this impractical fixed-σ requirement with either a deterministic phased schedule (PES) or a jointly-learned stochasticity parameter (SL-PG), thereby bridging a theory-practice gap where practitioners have long used dynamic exploration (Schulman et al. 2015; Wierstra et al. 2014) without convergence guarantees. The analysis reuses the weak gradient domination framework from the broader PG convergence literature (Yuan et al. 2022; Bhandari & Russo 2024; Fatkhullin et al. 2023) but introduces new regularity conditions (scale-invariant noise, Lipschitz continuity of J_D w.r.t. σ) needed to control performance loss when σ changes between phases.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work validates a ubiquitous practical choice (dynamic stochasticity in PG) with formal guarantees, giving practitioners confidence that phased or learned exploration schedules do not fundamentally degrade convergence. The PES vs. SL-PG tradeoff is practically important: PES guarantees deterministic-policy convergence at O~(ε^{-5}) but can get stuck in local optima if phase lengths are insufficient, while SL-PG adapts σ to escape local optima at O~(ε^{-3}) but only converges to the optimal *stochastic* policy under stronger assumptions. Open questions include whether SL-PG can achieve deterministic convergence at its faster rate, whether single-iteration phases (K_p = 1) suffice, and how to characterize the learned σ dynamics in SL-PG without additional assumptions.
