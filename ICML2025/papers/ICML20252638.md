## Problem

Many real-world bandit applications (online advertising, mobile health, freemium games) produce sparse rewards that are zero with high probability, yet existing bandit algorithms either impose parametric assumptions that misspecify the distribution or use non-parametric bounds that ignore the structural sparsity. This leads to loose concentration bounds, inefficient exploration, and higher regret. The paper initiates the first formal study of zero-inflated bandits, providing finite-sample concentration analysis and regret guarantees for this specific reward structure.

## Input / Output

At each round the agent observes a context vector x_t and a feasible action set A_t, selects an action, and receives a zero-inflated reward R_t = X_t × Y_t where Y_t is a Bernoulli indicator and X_t is the non-zero magnitude (observable only when Y_t = 1). The algorithm maintains two separate running estimators—one for the binary indicator (all observations) and one for the non-zero magnitude (only non-zero observations)—and outputs an action selected by maximizing the product of two component-wise confidence scores. The key structural constraint is that the number of X_t observations per arm is itself a random variable governed by Y_t, which the analysis must account for.

## Method

The core contribution is a 'product method' that decomposes the reward R_t = X_t × Y_t and constructs separate concentration bounds for the non-zero mean μ (via sub-Weibull or trimmed-mean inequalities on the observed X_t values) and the non-zero probability p (via Bernoulli concentration on Y_t), then multiplies the two UCBs to obtain a valid bound on r = μp. This is tighter than applying a single concentration inequality to R_t directly because the variance of R_t scales with μ, inflating standard bounds unnecessarily. For Thompson Sampling, the algorithm samples from a clipped Gaussian posterior for μ and a clipped Beta posterior for p, then multiplies the samples; the clipping is shown to be critical for controlling overestimation of suboptimal arms in the product. The analysis handles the complication that the number of non-zero observations is random by establishing a deterministic-rate concentration bound (Lemma 2.2) that depends on np_k rather than the random count, enabling clean regret proofs for both MAB and GLM contextual bandit settings.

## Results

Theoretically, the proposed TS algorithm for sub-Gaussian ZI MAB achieves the minimax-optimal regret rate R(T) ≲ √(KT) + K, improving over the UCB rate by a factor of √(log T). For ZI GLM contextual bandits, both UCB and TS achieve Õ((d∨q)√T), matching the minimax lower bound up to a logarithmic factor and independent of the number of arms K. Empirically, across simulated MAB (K=10, T=75000) and contextual bandit (K=100, d=10, T=20000) settings, the proposed methods consistently achieve sub-linear regret while most baselines exhibit linear or significantly higher regret. On the real U.S. auto loan dataset (5000 rounds), both the proposed UCB and TS algorithms demonstrate significantly lower cumulative regret compared to the integrated UCB and TS baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the MOTS Thompson Sampling baseline (Jin et al., 2021), the proposed product TS algorithm outperforms in both Gaussian and Gaussian-mixture MAB settings, achieving lower cumulative regret; for exponential rewards, fair comparison is complicated by the need for CMS transformation. Against the integrated UCB/TS baselines built on Li et al. (2017) generalized linear contextual bandit algorithms, the proposed product UCB and TS consistently achieve lower sub-linear regrets across all tested distributions in both simulated and real-data experiments. Notably, the proposed UCB even outperforms the 'strong baseline' (which has access to the true size parameter for the reward) in Gaussian and Gaussian-mixture MAB cases, demonstrating that ignoring the ZI structure leads to fundamentally looser concentration bounds. On the real auto loan dataset, the misspecified UCB/TS baselines (which ignore the ZI structure entirely) sometimes appear competitive but can exhibit linear regret in other settings, while the proposed methods consistently achieve the lowest overall regret. The integrated TS baseline also achieves sub-linear regret in contextual bandit simulations, but the proposed product TS demonstrates superior performance across all tested sparsity levels and link functions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out a new problem formulation—zero-inflated bandits (ZIB)—by decomposing the reward into a Bernoulli indicator and a non-zero component, then applying concentration inequalities to each separately via a 'product method.' It builds on the clipped-distribution Thompson sampling framework of Jin et al. (2021) and the generalized linear contextual bandit machinery of Li et al. (2017), while adapting the trimmed-mean technique of Bubeck et al. (2013) for heavy-tailed non-zero components. The authors explicitly position this as distinct from semiparametric bandits (which address regression misspecification), hierarchical bandits (which model structure across instances), and sparse-reward bandits (which focus on zero-mean arms), and claim it is the first finite-sample concentration analysis of general ZI models even outside the bandit literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical relevance is clear: sparse rewards (ad clicks, loan acceptances, health outcomes) are ubiquitous, and the paper demonstrates that even with oracle knowledge of distribution parameters, ignoring the ZI structure yields meaningfully looser confidence bounds and higher regret. The main open question is asymptotic order-optimality: the authors show their algorithms satisfy a necessary condition but leave the design of an algorithm that attains the full lower bound (requiring adaptive probability allocation between the two concentration components) as future work. Broader implications extend to general hierarchical reward structures beyond binary zero/non-zero splits, which the authors flag as a natural next direction.
