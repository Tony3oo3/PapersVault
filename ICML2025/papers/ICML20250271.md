## Problem

RLHF-based ethical alignment suffers from reward hacking, social bias amplification, and a 'Whack-A-Mole' limitation where correcting one behavior does not prevent others, often causing catastrophic forgetting in unrelated capabilities. The paper addresses the deeper question of whether regulating emotional states can shape linguistic behaviors holistically, rather than patching individual outputs reactively, and how to do so while preserving the integrity of the underlying knowledge model.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250271/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The system consumes raw text documents (e.g., love letters) along with administrator-defined guardrail ranges G on a 7-level behavior spectrum Ψ and cultural context specifications for Eris. GPT-4 first rewrites each document into L behavioral variants, then extracts top-M emotion frequencies per variant to build behavior vectors Γ. Outputs include a classified behavior level with its emotion vector, a binary ethical compliance decision against G, a structured adversarial debate transcript (Dike's arguments Θ⁺ vs. Eris's counterarguments Θ⁻), and an optionally rectified text D′k that preserves the intended emotional tone while satisfying guardrails.

## Method

The framework separates three neurally independent components: the knowledge LLM (executive), Dike (legislative, mapping emotions to behaviors via a self-supervised pipeline), and Eris (judicial, adversarial cultural challenge). Dike's pipeline rewrites N documents into L behavioral variants using GPT-4, extracts emotion distributions per variant, and constructs behavior vectors Γ that classify new texts by similarity to these vectors—leveraging the insight that emotion-mediated classification outperforms direct zero-shot behavior labeling by 11.3 percentage points. The Beam model quantifies emotions along seven antonym-paired spectra with four intensity levels (±0.3, ±0.6), enabling fine-grained modulation. Eris challenges Dike's decisions through a structured debate algorithm where contentiousness decays geometrically (Δ←Δ/δ) from 90% to below 10%, forcing both agents to justify claims against dissent before reaching consensus or escalating to human moderators (~5% of cases). Only 54 rewritten letters suffice as training data, explained by the UCCT theory: few-shot examples serve as semantic anchors that activate pre-existing latent patterns in the LLM rather than teaching new knowledge.

## Results

Dike's emotion-mediated behavior classification outperforms GPT-4's zero-shot direct classification by 11.3 percentage points on the 24-letter test set (with ~5% error bars reflecting annotation variability). Dike also achieves higher prediction entropy (2.13 bits) compared to GPT-4 (1.80 bits), indicating a more nuanced distribution across the seven behavioral categories rather than polarized predictions. The adversarial Dike-Eris debate requires human escalation in only ~5% of cases, and the entire pipeline operates with just 54 few-shot training examples drawn from the Love Letters Collection.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's sole quantitative baseline is GPT-4's zero-shot behavior classification (directly prompting GPT-4 to assign a linguistic behavior label). Dike's self-supervised, emotion-mediated approach wins on both accuracy (by 11.3 percentage points) and distributional quality (entropy 2.13 vs. 1.80), with GPT-4's predictions clustering at the two poles of the spectrum while Dike produces a more even spread. The inter-annotator entropy among five human raters (2.56 bits) exceeds both systems, underscoring the inherent subjectivity of the task and motivating the adversarial dual-LLM design. No comparison with proprietary RLHF pipelines is possible due to access restrictions, and no other published emotion-behavior classification baselines are benchmarked.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as an architectural alternative to RLHF and its variants (DPO, KTO, RLAIF, Constitutional AI), arguing that those methods conflate knowledge and behavior in a single model, leading to catastrophic forgetting and reward hacking. It draws on Plutchik's and Scherer's emotion wheels to build a quantitative emotion model (Beam), and on emotion-regulation psychology (James-Lange, Schachter-Singer, affect-as-information) to justify using emotion as the mediating variable between linguistic behavior and ethical judgment. The multi-agent adversarial debate mechanism extends prior work on LLM dialectic (Chang, 2023; 2024a) into a structured checks-and-balances architecture with a governmental metaphor, while the few-shot efficiency is grounded in the authors' own Unified Cognitive Consciousness Theory (UCCT) rather than established prompting literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The core architectural insight—decoupling ethical oversight from knowledge generation to avoid RLHF's backpropagation-induced forgetting—is practically relevant as alignment methods scale, and the emotion-mediated behavioral modulation offers a more interpretable alternative to opaque reward signals. However, the empirical validation is limited to a single domain (love letters, 54 training / 24 test examples), with no direct comparison against RLHF baselines, no ablation isolating the contribution of each component, and no demonstration on the hate-speech datasets the framework is ultimately designed for. Open questions include whether the Beam emotion spectra generalize beyond affective text, how the Dike-Eris debate scales to high-throughput moderation, and whether the UCCT's threshold-crossing formalism makes testable predictions beyond post-hoc rationalization of few-shot success.
