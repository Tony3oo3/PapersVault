## Problem

Current AI safety research concentrates on existential risks, adversarial robustness, and content filtering, while largely neglecting the medium- and long-term disruption generative AI causes to labor markets, economic justice, and human agency. The paper argues that this narrow framing overlooks structural risks—job displacement, income inequality, erosion of creative labor, and the concentration of economic power in a few AI firms—that collectively threaten shared prosperity and democratic participation.

## Input / Output

As a position paper, the analytical 'input' consists of established economic theories (intertemporal consumption theory, rent-seeking theory, Coasean transaction-cost analysis, collective action problems) combined with empirical evidence on AI's labor-market effects (e.g., the 21% drop in writing/coding job posts post-ChatGPT, freelancer displacement data). The 'output' is a structured set of six policy recommendations (R1–R6) spanning worker support, open-source AI development, AI-content watermarking mandates, royalty-based copyright compensation via Shapley Royalty Share, and multi-stakeholder governance to prevent regulatory capture.

## Method

The paper's core analytical contribution is a multi-theory economic lens applied to AI safety discourse. It models AI-driven job insecurity through the Life-Cycle Hypothesis and Permanent Income Hypothesis, showing how disrupted career trajectories break traditional consumption-smoothing patterns and increase precautionary savings among automation-prone workers. It frames closed-source AI development and training-data monopolization as rent-seeking behavior, and uses Coasean transaction-cost analysis to explain why individual copyright licensing is infeasible at scale, motivating collective licensing mechanisms. A Prisoner's Dilemma / collective-action framework explains why individual firms rationally avoid watermarking despite its social benefit. Finally, it draws on cooperative game theory (Shapley value) to propose a fair royalty-sharing mechanism for compensating creators whose data trains AI models, tying all threads into a unified pro-worker governance argument.

## Results

This is a position paper without empirical experiments or a main results table. The authors support their arguments with cited quantitative evidence: a 21% decrease in writing/coding job posts and a 17% decrease in image-creation jobs following the introduction of ChatGPT and image-generating AI (Demirci et al., 2024); programmers becoming 55.8% faster with AI assistance (Peng et al., 2023); and approximately 80% of the U.S. workforce having at least 10% of tasks affected by LLMs, with ~19% facing 50%+ task impact (Eloundou et al., 2023). The paper's core contribution is a set of six identified risks (P1–P6) and six policy recommendations (R1–R6) grounded in economic theories such as the Life-Cycle Hypothesis, Permanent Income Hypothesis, rent-seeking theory, and Coasean analysis.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not benchmark a method against baselines. Instead, it positions its argument against the prevailing AI safety paradigm, which the authors characterize as narrowly focused on adversarial robustness, alignment faking, and existential (x-risk) scenarios. The authors contrast their 'future of work' framing with three alternative views they explicitly address: (1) the market-forces-will-adapt view (citing Smith, Autor, Aghion), which they counter by arguing that the speed and breadth of cognitive-task automation differ qualitatively from prior technological transitions; (2) the x-risk-only view (citing Hendrycks et al., 2023), which they challenge by invoking Kasirzadeh's (2025) distinction between decisive and accumulative existential risks; and (3) the technical-solutions-over-policy view, which they rebut by arguing that human frailty in assessing long-term risks necessitates complementary policy interventions. The paper does not claim to outperform any prior AI safety framework on a quantitative metric but rather argues for a broader scope of AI safety research.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper departs from the dominant AI safety framing (existential risks, alignment, jailbreaking) by explicitly arguing that labor market disruption constitutes a core AI safety concern rather than a separate policy question. It repurposes economic theories—intertemporal consumption (LCH, PIH), rent-seeking (Tullock, Krueger), Coasean transaction-cost analysis, and inclusive vs. extractive institutions (Acemoglu & Robinson)—to structure the argument, while drawing on empirical labor-market studies (Demirci et al. 2024, Eloundou et al. 2023, Hui et al. 2024) for grounding. The Shapley Royalty Share framework (Wang et al. 2024b) is cited as a concrete mechanism for fair compensation, and the paper positions itself against both the 'market forces will adapt' view (Smith, Autor, Aghion) and the 'focus exclusively on x-risks' view (Hendrycks et al. 2023), carving out a middle ground that treats accumulative economic erosion as a safety-relevant risk.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central contribution is reframing: if accepted, it would expand the scope of AI safety research to include workforce transition, copyright compensation, and governance design, potentially redirecting funding and research attention. However, as a position paper it lacks empirical validation of its specific policy proposals (e.g., whether collective licensing would actually reduce rent-seeking, or whether mandated watermarking would survive the Prisoner's Dilemma dynamics the authors identify). Open questions include how to balance innovation incentives against worker protection at scale, whether the economic analogies (intertemporal consumption, Coasean bargaining) hold under AI's unique disruption speed, and how to implement the proposed international governance framework without triggering the regulatory capture the paper warns against.
