## Problem

Neural networks lack a built-in mechanism to quantify the uncertainty of their predictions under stochastic (noisy) inputs, which limits their deployment in safety-critical applications. While deterministic robustness verification is well-studied, computing guaranteed (not merely estimated) upper and lower bounds for the output CDF under random inputs remains open: existing approaches like Monte Carlo simulation or the PLT method of Krapf et al. (2024) produce point estimates that can fall outside the true distribution, and no prior work provides exact, convergent bounds over the full output support for general activation functions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251222/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a trained feedforward (or CNN) network with continuous monotonic piecewise C² activations and a random input vector whose pdf is piecewise continuous and bounded on a compact hyperrectangle. It produces guaranteed upper and lower CDF bounds evaluated on a user-specified grid over a superset of the output range (computed via Interval Bound Propagation). For ReLU networks the exact CDF is obtained analytically; for general networks, the bound tightness is controlled by the number of piecewise-linear segments per activation (resolution parameter) and a hard cap on total polytope vertices (set to 50,000 in experiments), with the gap between bounds shrinking as resolution increases.

## Method

The approach has two pillars. First, for ReLU networks, the input space is decomposed into activation-pattern polytopes (extracted via a GPU-accelerated edge-subdivision algorithm), and the CDF at any output point y is computed as a sum of exact polynomial integrals over reduced polytopes (intersections of activation polytopes, pdf simplices, and output-constraint halfspaces), using Delaunay triangulation and Lasserre's simplex integration formula. Second, for general networks, each activation function is bounded from above and below by piecewise-linear approximations constructed adaptively: linear interpolation on convex segments yields upper bounds while piecewise tangent approximation yields lower bounds (reversed for concave segments), and these are converted into ReLU-equivalent subnetworks. The sequences of upper/lower ReLU approximations are proven to converge uniformly and monotonically to the target network (via Dini's theorem and uniform convergence of compositions), and the input pdf is locally bounded by piecewise constants on the resulting simplices, reducing the problem to the exact ReLU CDF computation. This yields a Universal Distribution Approximation Theorem guaranteeing convergence of the CDF bounds to the true distribution for any continuous function on a compact domain.

## Results

The proposed U/L-Dist method produces guaranteed upper and lower bounds on the output CDF whose mean width ranges from 0.0000 (1D Iris/Wine) to 0.2019 (3D Iris50), reflecting the curse of dimensionality under a 50,000-vertex cap. In 1D settings where bounds are extremely tight (mean width ≤ 0.0001), Monte Carlo (10⁸ samples) and PLT (Krapf et al., 2024) produce estimates outside the guaranteed bounds at 103–896 and 77–999 out of 1,000 grid points respectively (Banana), and at 1,510–2,594 and 1,864–3,026 out of 8,000 grid points (Iris25, Wine225). In 2D and 3D, out-of-bounds counts drop substantially for both baselines as bounds widen, but PLT still exceeds MC in median violations (e.g., 207 vs. 1 for Diabetes 2D). Runtime is competitive: the proposed method takes 1–100 s versus 0.23–504 s for PLT and ~20 s for MC.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: Monte Carlo simulation (10⁸ samples) and the Piecewise Linear Transformation (PLT) method of Krapf et al. (2024). The proposed U/L-Dist method is the only approach that provides *guaranteed* bounds by construction; MC and PLT are pointwise estimators that can (and do) fall outside these bounds. In 1D settings where the guaranteed bounds are extremely tight (mean width ≈ 0.0000–0.0001), both baselines violate the bounds frequently: PLT shows median out-of-bounds counts of 565–2,311 out of 1,000–8,000 grid points, while MC shows 103–491. In 2D, PLT still produces median violations of 4–207 points versus MC's 1–70, indicating PLT's coarser grid causes more inaccuracies where the pdf fluctuates. In 3D, the bounds widen substantially (mean width 0.18–0.20) due to the vertex cap, and both baselines show fewer violations (MC: 18–449 median; PLT: 31–63 median), with PLT actually outperforming MC on Wine225 3D (63 vs. 449 median OOB). Runtime is comparable: the proposed method (1–100 s) is generally faster than PLT (0.23–504 s) and similar to MC (~20 s), though PLT can be faster in some 2D/3D cases.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the polytope decomposition characterization of ReLU networks (Raghu et al., 2017; Sudjianto et al., 2020) and the GPU-accelerated extraction algorithm of Berzins (2023), combined with exact polynomial integration over simplices (Lasserre, 2021) and Interval Bound Propagation (Gowal et al., 2019). It departs most directly from Krapf et al. (2024), whose PLT method propagates input densities through piecewise-linear networks but only produces estimates on a grid; here, the authors show that exact integration over polytopes is in fact feasible (contradicting the infeasibility assumption in Krapf et al.'s Lemma 3) and extend the framework to non-ReLU activations (tanh, softmax) via constructive ReLU upper/lower bounding. The Universal Distribution Approximation Theorem (UDAT) generalizes the classical UAT from function approximation to distribution approximation, providing a theoretical anchor for the entire pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The guaranteed (not merely estimated) CDF bounds with proven uniform convergence offer a principled alternative to Monte Carlo or histogram-based uncertainty propagation, particularly relevant for safety-critical deployment where knowing the worst-case output probability is essential. However, the curse of dimensionality is a severe practical limitation: the 50,000-vertex cap on triangulation causes bound widths to balloon from ~0.0001 in 1D to ~0.18–0.20 in 3D, restricting current applicability to low-dimensional inputs and small networks. Open questions include scaling to higher-dimensional inputs, handling arbitrary (non-piecewise-polynomial) input distributions, and extending to unions of bounded polytopes rather than a single hyperrectangle.
