## Problem

Simulating how cells change morphology under chemical or genetic perturbations in silico requires learning a conditional distribution from unpaired control and perturbed images, but prior methods either ignore control cells (making predictions vulnerable to batch effects), rely on outdated generative architectures (GANs, normalizing flows), or use suboptimal two-step diffusion pipelines that cannot directly model distribution-to-distribution transformation. CellFlux addresses this by reframing the task as a many-to-many distribution mapping problem and solving it with flow matching, which natively transforms one distribution into another via a learned velocity field.

## Input / Output

The model takes a single control cell image (96×96 multi-channel fluorescence microscopy, 3–6 channels depending on dataset) and a perturbation condition (a chemical compound identified by Morgan fingerprint or a genetic perturbation encoded via Gene2Vec/HyenaDNA embeddings) and produces a corresponding perturbed cell image in the same pixel space. Because cell painting is destructive, training uses unpaired sets of control and perturbed images from the same experimental batch rather than paired (x₀, x₁) samples. A secondary output is a continuous interpolation trajectory between the control and perturbed states, enabled by the reversible ODE solution.

## Method

CellFlux learns a conditional velocity field v_θ(x_t, t, c) parameterized by a four-stage U-Net that transforms the control image distribution p₀ into the perturbed distribution p₁(·|c) by solving an ODE. Training uses the rectified flow formulation with a straight-line probability path x_t = (1−t)x₀ + t·x₁, minimizing an L2 loss between the network's predicted velocity and the ground-truth velocity (x₁ − x₀). Three key adaptations distinguish it from vanilla flow matching: (1) perturbation conditioning via learnable embeddings injected into U-Net blocks, (2) classifier-free guidance that interpolates between conditional and unconditional velocity predictions at inference, and (3) stochastic Gaussian noise augmentation on x₀ to smooth the learned field over the empirical distribution. A theoretical proposition (Proposition 1) proves that conditioning on same-batch control images yields a tighter KL approximation to the true conditional p(x₁|x₀,c) than conditioning on the perturbation alone, formalizing why the distribution-to-distribution formulation outperforms prior approaches that ignore control cells.

## Results

CellFlux achieves state-of-the-art FID scores of 18.7 (BBBC021), 33.0 (RxRx1), and 9.0 (JUMP), improving overall FID by 21–45% over the best baseline. On mode-of-action (MoA) classification, CellFlux reaches 71.1% accuracy, closely matching ground-truth perturbed images (72.4%) and surpassing the best baseline IMPA (63.7%) by 7.4 percentage points. On out-of-distribution perturbations never seen during training, CellFlux achieves 43.2% MoA accuracy versus 16.0% for IMPA and 9.6% for PhenDiff, a 170% improvement over the best baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CellFlux outperforms both baselines—PhenDiff (diffusion-based, MICCAI 2024) and IMPA (GAN-based, Nature Communications 2025)—on FID and KID across all three datasets. Against PhenDiff, CellFlux reduces overall FID by 62% on BBBC021 (18.7 vs. 49.5), 50% on RxRx1 (33.0 vs. 65.9), and 82% on JUMP (9.0 vs. 49.3). Against IMPA, the gains are more modest on RxRx1 (33.0 vs. 41.6 FIDo) but substantial on BBBC021 (18.7 vs. 33.7) and JUMP (9.0 vs. 14.6). On MoA classification, CellFlux (71.1%) approaches ground-truth performance (72.4%) while IMPA reaches only 63.7% and PhenDiff 52.6%. A third comparison against MorphoDiff (ICLR 2025, which does not use control images) shows CellFlux achieving 18.7 FIDo versus 65.8 and 71.2% MoA accuracy versus 38.3%, confirming the importance of the distribution-to-distribution formulation with control conditioning.

## Relation to Prior Work

CellFlux builds directly on flow matching (Lipman et al., ICLR 2023) and its rectified-flow variant (Liu et al., ICLR 2023), adapting the velocity-field ODE framework to the cellular morphology domain with conditional perturbation encoding, classifier-free guidance, and noise augmentation. It departs from prior cell morphology prediction methods—PhenDiff (diffusion-based, MICCAI 2024) and IMPA (GAN-based, Nature Communications 2025)—by reframing the problem as a direct distribution-to-distribution transformation between control and perturbed cell images within the same batch, rather than mapping through an intermediate noise distribution or using adversarial style transfer. The key conceptual departure is that flow matching's native many-to-many distribution transport eliminates the need for the two-step noise intermediary that diffusion-based approaches require, enabling batch-aware conditioning and reversible interpolation that prior architectures cannot support.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CellFlux advances the long-standing goal of virtual cell modeling by providing a principled, distribution-level framework for simulating perturbation responses, with the added practical benefit of disentangling true biological effects from batch artifacts—a persistent confound in high-content screening data. The interpolation and reverse-trajectory capabilities open a path toward studying dynamic morphological transitions that destructive imaging cannot capture, though the biological validity of these intermediate states remains unverified. Key limitations include reliance on a small number of cancer cell lines (MCF-7, HUVEC, U2OS, A549), unvalidated interpolation trajectories, and the open question of whether the approach scales to the petabyte-scale, multi-cell-type datasets that a true virtual cell would require.
