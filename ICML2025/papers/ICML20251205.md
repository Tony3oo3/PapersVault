## Problem

In linear bandits, the agent typically observes only a subset of the reward-generating features, yet standard algorithms (OFUL, LinTS) that ignore unobserved features can suffer linear regret because the latent component of the reward is unaccounted for. Prior work on partially observable features relies on structural assumptions (Gaussian distributions, linear dynamical systems) on the latent features, leaving the general case—where no such structure is known—unresolved.

## Input / Output

The algorithm takes as input the observed feature vectors {x_a ∈ R^d : a ∈ [K]} for all arms, a coupling probability p ∈ (1/2,1), and a confidence parameter δ > 0. Crucially, it requires no knowledge of the unobserved feature dimension d_u, the number of latent features, or even whether partial observability exists at all. At each round it outputs an arm selection a_t and maintains a K-dimensional estimated reward parameter μ̂_t^L (or μ̂_t^R for the Ridge variant), with the regret bound depending on d + d_h rather than K, where d_h is the number of nonzero coefficients needed to represent the latent reward projected onto R(X)⊥.

## Method

The RoLF algorithm combines two key mechanisms. First, feature augmentation: the reward vector for all K arms is decomposed into its projection onto the row space R(X) of observed features and onto the orthogonal complement R(X)⊥; observed features are then augmented with an orthonormal basis {b_1^⊤, …, b_{K-d}^⊤} of R(X)⊥ to form K-dimensional vectors x̃_a, reformulating the problem as a standard linear bandit with no misspecification. Second, a doubly robust Lasso estimator constructs unbiased pseudo-rewards for all arms by imputing missing rewards via a Lasso fit and calibrating with inverse-probability weights, while a resampling-and-coupling strategy (repeatedly drawing the actual action a_t and a pseudo-action ã_t until they match) keeps all inverse-probability weights bounded by O(K), stabilizing variance. The Lasso penalty exploits the sparsity of the latent-reward coefficients (at most d_h nonzero entries in μ*_u), and because the augmented basis vectors are orthogonal, the Gram matrix over the full action space has a bounded minimum eigenvalue without requiring extensive exploration—reducing the exploration phase from O(K^4 log KT) to O(K^3 log KT) and yielding a regret bound of Õ(√((d+d_h)T)).

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 3) establishes a cumulative regret bound of $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{(d+d_h)T})$ for the RoLF algorithm, where $d$ is the observed feature dimension and $d_h$ captures the intrinsic difficulty of the unobserved component. In numerical experiments with $K=30$ arms, $d_z=35$ true feature dimensions, and $T=1200$ rounds, RoLF-Lasso and RoLF-Ridge consistently achieve lower cumulative regret than all baselines across both partial-observability and full-observability scenarios. The regret bound reduces to $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{dT})$ when the latent feature space is contained within the observed space ($d_h=0$), and to $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{KT})$ in the worst case ($d_h=K-d$), matching the MAB baseline only in the most adversarial setting.

| Feature space relationship | Regret bound |
|---|---|
| span(observed) $\supseteq$ span(latent) | $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{dT})$ |
| span(observed) $\subseteq$ span(latent) | $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{KT})$ |
| otherwise (general case) | $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{(d+d_h)T})$ |

## Baselines & Comparisons

RoLF (both Lasso and Ridge variants) outperforms all four baselines in cumulative regret across every experimental scenario. Against LinUCB and LinTS (OLB algorithms relying solely on observed features), RoLF achieves substantially lower regret because these baselines cannot capture the reward component associated with unobserved features, leading them to select suboptimal arms with probability $\Theta(1)$ (Theorem 1 shows they can suffer linear regret). Against DRLasso (Kim & Paik, 2019), RoLF improves by incorporating feature augmentation with orthogonal projection and a resampling-coupling strategy that stabilizes the DR estimator's variance, whereas DRLasso's fixed averaged-context approach limits its expressiveness under the fixed-feature setting. Against UCB($\delta$) (the MAB baseline that ignores features entirely), RoLF achieves a tighter bound of $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{(d+d_h)T})$ versus $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{KT})$, exploiting feature structure to reduce regret when $d+d_h < K$. In Scenario 2 (no latent features, $d=60 > K=30$), RoLF remains competitive while OLB algorithms degrade due to the curse of dimensionality in high-dimensional feature spaces.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the doubly robust (DR) estimation framework for linear bandits (Kim & Paik, 2019; Kim et al., 2021; 2023c) and adapts it to a new problem: linear bandits where the reward depends on features the agent cannot observe. It departs sharply from prior partially-observable bandit work (Park & Faradonbeh, 2022/2024; Kim et al., 2023a; Zeng et al., 2025), which all impose structural assumptions (Gaussian features, known linear decoders, Kalman-filter dynamics) to recover latent features. The key conceptual departure is that RoLF never attempts to recover or model the unobserved features; instead it projects the inaccessible reward component onto the orthogonal complement of the observed-feature row space and uses DR Lasso estimation on the augmented vectors, requiring no prior knowledge of the latent dimension or distribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first sublinear regret guarantee for linear bandits with partially observable features under no structural assumptions on the latent features, closing a gap the authors explicitly identify as unsolved. The regret bound Õ(√((d+d_h)T)) interpolates between the OLB rate Õ(√(dT)) (when latent features are spanned by observed ones) and the MAB rate Õ(√(KT)) (when they are not), and is strictly better than both baselines in the intermediate regime. Open questions include extending the approach to generalized linear reward models, relaxing the linearity assumption on the latent reward component, and handling the (shown non-learnable) case where latent features vary over time. Practical relevance is clear in settings like online advertising or clinical trials where unquantifiable latent factors affect outcomes.
