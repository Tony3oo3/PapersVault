## Problem

A widely held assumption is that learning disentangled (factorized) latent representations naturally enables compositional generalization to out-of-distribution combinations. This paper investigates why this assumption fails mechanistically: even when inputs are explicitly and fully disentangled by construction, standard CNN/MLP decoders re-entangle factors in subsequent layers and resort to memorizing and superposing training data rather than composing independent factors, leading to systematic OOD generation failures.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251480/cropped_page2_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a 2D (x, y) coordinate pair encoded in a fully disentangled format—either population-based (each coordinate as a 1D Gaussian bump in a separate vector of length N) or ramp-based (each coordinate as a single analog scalar)—and must generate an N×N grayscale image containing a single 2D Gaussian bump centered at that (x, y) location. Training data covers a square-donut region of the (x, y) plane with a large OOD hole in the center (or a corner), so all individual x and y values are seen but many combinations are held out. The architecture is a decoder-only CNN (4 ConvTranspose2d layers, 64 channels, ~315K params) or MLP (3 linear layers, 256 units, ~272K params) with ReLU activations, trained with AdamW at lr=1e-3.

## Method

The paper first diagnoses the failure using two complementary tools: a Jacobian-based metric tensor g = JᵀJ tracked across layers to quantify layer-by-layer erosion of factorization (via a cross-term ratio metric and log-volume distortion), and a kernel analysis showing the model learns a 'binary factorized kernel' where OOD outputs are superpositions of memorized ID samples aligned independently on x and y. To fix this, the paper proposes two strategies that force disentangled latents to render into the output pixel space: (1) an architectural modification where each 1-hot input token is mapped to a dedicated N×N 2D embedding matrix, regularized via SVD decomposition with an entropy penalty on singular values (enforcing low rank) and a variance penalty on row/column basis vectors (discouraging index memorization); and (2) a data curriculum that replaces or supplements 2D bump images with 1D Gaussian stripes (isolated x or y factors), teaching the network additive composition in pixel space. Both approaches achieve data-efficient OOD compositional generalization, with the stripe curriculum reducing data scaling from ~N³ (bumps only) to ~N (stripes + few bumps), demonstrating that factorization must be preserved in the output representational space, not merely in an abstract latent bottleneck.

## Results

On the 2D Gaussian bump generation task, standard CNN and MLP decoders fail to compositionally generalize to OOD (x,y) combinations regardless of input encoding format (bump, ramp, 1-hot, or positional), instead superposing memorized ID data. The key quantitative finding is a dramatic data-efficiency gap: models trained on bumps-only data require a dataset scaling as ~N³ to reach 90% coordinate accuracy, whereas models trained on 1D stripes plus a small number of bumps scale only linearly as ~N, breaking the curse of dimensionality through zero-shot compositional generalization. In the regularization ablation (Fig. 3e), the Entropy+Var configuration achieves the best OOD MSE while maintaining low ID MSE, outperforming No Reg (lowest ID but highest OOD), Entropy Only (marginal OOD improvement), and Variance Only (strong OOD but higher ID loss).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external baseline methods; instead, it conducts a mechanistic study with internal ablations. All disentangled input encodings (population-based bump, ramp, 1-hot, positional) fail to support OOD compositional generalization, confirming that factorized latents alone are insufficient. Between the two proposed solutions, low-rank regularization on 2D embedding matrices (Entropy+Var) and dataset augmentation with 1D Gaussian stripes both enable OOD generalization, with the stripe-based data curriculum showing the strongest data-efficiency gains (linear vs. cubic scaling). The regularization approach also generalizes to corner OOD regions (extrapolative composition), and the stripe approach achieves zero-shot OOD generation with no 2D bump examples in training. CNN and MLP decoders exhibit qualitatively similar failure and recovery patterns, indicating the phenomenon is architecture-agnostic.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself against the widely held assumption in disentangled representation learning (β-VAE, FactorVAE, and related work) that factorized latents automatically yield compositional generalization. It builds on prior empirical observations of failure (Montero et al., Xu et al., Lippl & Stachenfeld) but goes further by providing a mechanistic explanation—manifold warping through dimension expansion and a binary factorized kernel 'superposition' strategy—using Jacobian-based metric tensors and Schmidt decomposition. The two proposed solutions (low-rank SVD regularization on embedding matrices and stripe-based data curation) draw on nonnegative matrix factorization (Cichocki et al.) and data curriculum ideas, but the key conceptual contribution is the 'forced rendering' principle: disentangled factors must be expressed in the output (pixel) space, not merely in an abstract latent bottleneck.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a foundational assumption in representation learning and offers a clear mechanistic account of why it fails, which should inform architectural and training design beyond the toy setting. The data-efficiency result (linear vs. cubic scaling with image size) is a concrete quantitative demonstration of compositional generalization's value. However, all evidence comes from a 2D Gaussian bump task with small CNNs/MLPs and a single MNIST rotation example, leaving open whether the 'forced rendering' principle transfers to high-dimensional, multi-factor, or real-world generative tasks. The authors themselves note that extending to diffusion models, transformers, and naturalistic data is the critical next step.
