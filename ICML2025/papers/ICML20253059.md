## Problem

Existing finite-sample estimators for average treatment effect (ATE) under active sampling either rely on asymptotic normality or achieve suboptimal sample complexity (e.g., O(d log d + d/ε) for leverage score sampling). The paper asks how to achieve the most optimal finite-sample error bounds for causal effect estimation within an active sampling framework, and whether these guarantees can extend to network interference settings where SUTVA is violated.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20253059/cropped_page5_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates in a design-based (finite population) setting where all randomness originates from the treatment assignment process rather than data variability. It consumes a covariate matrix X ∈ R^{n×d} (assumed column-orthogonalized), potential outcome vectors Y^(1), Y^(0) ∈ R^n, a binary treatment assignment Z, and sampling parameters ε, φ. It outputs a regression-adjusted ATE estimate τ̂_act together with a non-asymptotic error bound decomposed into three components (HT variance on S, GSW design error on S̄, and ITE approximation error). In the network extension, the input additionally includes an adjacency matrix H and graph-aggregated features X^G produced by a GNN.

## Method

RWAS (Re-Weighting Active Sampling) splits the population into two parts: S is used for an Iterative Re-weighting in Design-based Setting (IRD) algorithm that adaptively samples O(d/ε) points with importance weights w_i = α_i/P_{i,r(i)}, learning regression coefficients via weighted least squares; S̄ is used for a Gram-Schmidt Walk (GSW) design with regression adjustment using the coefficients from S. The IRD algorithm is a design-based adaptation of Chen & Price's active regression, where each iteration samples a point with probability proportional to its leverage under a dynamically updated spectral interval [l_i, u_i], ensuring eigenvalues of A^T A stay in [1−ε₁, 1+ε₁] with high probability. This yields a sample complexity of O(d/ε + m′), improving over leverage score sampling's O(d log d + d/ε), and a matching lower bound of 2.86d/ε (via Fano's inequality and the Shannon-Hartley theorem) establishes near-optimality. The framework extends to network interference via a Conflict-Graph Design (CGD) that handles arbitrary neighborhood exposure mappings, with graph neural network features replacing raw covariates in the regression adjustment.

## Results

On synthetic ATE estimation (d=50, n=1000/2000), RWAS achieves the lowest relative error among all methods in most settings: 0.71 (sd 0.47) at 50% sample proportion with n=1000, and 0.30 (sd 0.22) at 80% proportion with n=2000, outperforming the next-best GSW (0.80 and 0.30 respectively) and RAHT (0.80 and 0.32). On real-world datasets, RWAS achieves 0.05 (sd 0.04) on IHDP at 80% proportion and 1.04 (sd 0.70) on LaLonde at 80% proportion, consistently beating HT, Hajek, SC, and 4-Vectors baselines while remaining competitive with CRA (which lacks finite-sample guarantees). The sample complexity of RWAS is O(d/ε + m′), improving over the O(d log d/ε² + m′) of RAHT (Ghadiri et al., 2024) and O(d log d + d/ε) of leverage-score sampling (Addanki et al., 2022).

## Baselines & Comparisons

RWAS consistently outperforms the HT and Hajek estimators across all synthetic and real-world settings, with relative errors roughly 30–50% lower (e.g., 0.71 vs. 1.14 for HT at n=1000, 50% proportion). Against the finite-sample-guaranteed baselines RAHT (Ghadiri et al., 2024) and SC (Addanki et al., 2022), RWAS achieves lower error in nearly every configuration, e.g., 0.59 vs. 0.70 (RAHT) and 0.71 (SC) on synthetic n=2000 at 50% proportion. RWAS ties or slightly beats GSW (Harshaw et al., 2024) on synthetic data (0.30 vs. 0.30 at n=2000, 80% proportion) and clearly wins on real-world datasets like LaLonde (1.04 vs. 1.08 at 80% proportion). The 4-Vectors method (Mou et al., 2022) is consistently the weakest among finite-sample methods, with RWAS achieving 20–40% lower error. The main caveat is that CRA (Li & Ding, 2020), which lacks finite-sample guarantees, occasionally matches or slightly beats RWAS on real-world data (e.g., 0.02 vs. 0.05 on IHDP at 80% proportion; 0.54 vs. 0.74 on Boston at 80% proportion), but RWAS provides the theoretical non-asymptotic error bound that CRA does not.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes three distinct threads: the iterative re-weighting (beyond-leverage-score) sampling framework of Chen & Price (2019), the Gram-Schmidt Walk (GSW) covariate-balancing design of Harshaw et al. (2024), and the partitioned regression-adjusted estimator of Ghadiri et al. (2024). The key conceptual departure is replacing leverage-score sampling (which yields O(d log d / ε²) complexity) with the IRD algorithm to achieve O(d/ε) sample complexity while retaining finite-sample guarantees, and then extending the entire pipeline to network interference via the conflict-graph design of Kandiros et al. (2024). The paper positions itself as the first to combine active sampling, regression adjustment, and finite-sample bounds in a unified estimator, and the first to derive such bounds under network interference.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The near-optimality result (upper bound O(d/ε) matching a lower bound of 2.86d/ε up to constants) establishes a theoretical ceiling for sample-efficient causal estimation, which is practically important in settings where data collection is expensive or ethically constrained (clinical trials, policy evaluation). The extension to network interference is timely given the growing recognition that SUTVA is violated in social and epidemiological contexts, though the authors acknowledge a bias-variance trade-off that remains unresolved. Open questions include whether the accuracy-robustness trade-off inherent in GSW can be improved with alternative designs, and whether the network interference results can be made unbiased under additional structural assumptions.
