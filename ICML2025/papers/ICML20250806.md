## Problem

Existing active feature acquisition (AFA) methods treat sequential feature selection as an exploration problem, using reinforcement learning or conditional mutual information estimation to discover which features to acquire next. The authors identify that local explanation methods (SHAP, LIME) can directly provide instance-specific feature importance rankings, yet no prior work has formally leveraged these rankings to guide AFA or compared them against state-of-the-art AFA techniques. This paper closes that gap by reframing AFA as a feature *prediction* task grounded in explainability-driven rankings rather than feature *exploration*.

## Input / Output

The policy network receives a sequential context of length ℓ=4, where each timestep consists of three tokens: the masked input vector (only acquired features visible, rest zeroed), the action (index of the most recently acquired feature), and the reward (the predictor's class probability output on the current masked input). For image datasets, individual non-overlapping patches serve as the atomic features (e.g., 4×4 patches yielding 64 features for CIFAR-10, 16×16 patches yielding 196 for ImageNette); for tabular data, each column is one feature. The system outputs two things per step: the next feature index to acquire (argmax over the policy's softmax, with already-acquired indices suppressed via a large negative logit offset) and the final class probability distribution from the predictor once the budget k is exhausted. Initialization fixes the first three features to those with the highest average importance ranking across the training set.

## Method

The core contribution is a two-stage training procedure for a decision transformer (causal GPT-style) policy network that learns to predict the next most important unacquired feature from instance-specific importance rankings φ^i generated by local explanation methods (FastSHAP for images, TreeSHAP over CatBoost for tabular data). In stage 1, features are acquired in the ground-truth ranking order and the policy is trained with cross-entropy to predict φ^i(t+1) given the current masked input, action history, and predictor output; the predictor f_θ is trained simultaneously with its own cross-entropy loss on the same masked inputs. In stage 2, the policy's own (imperfect) predictions define the acquisition order, and both networks are retrained on these self-generated feature subsets so the predictor learns to handle the distribution shift that occurs at inference when the policy deviates from the true ranking. The two networks share a backbone (ResNet or MLP) but maintain independent loss functions with no gradient flow between them. At inference, the policy sequentially selects features under a hard budget constraint k, and the predictor produces the final classification from the acquired subset.

## Results

On the second-stage (final) model, the method achieves 78.44% accuracy on CIFAR-10, 46.99% on CIFAR-100, 83.87% on BloodMNIST, 78.96% on ImageNette, 69.83% on Metabric, 67.45% on CPS, 0.916 AUROC on CTGS, 0.836 AUROC on CKD, and 0.955 AUROC on Spambase (averaged over the first 20 features). On ImageNette, the method reaches 63.64% and 74.95% accuracy with only 2 and 5 patches out of 196, respectively. The second-stage training yields mean accuracy gains of 0.94% (CIFAR-100) to 7.35% (Metabric) over the first stage, confirming the benefit of training on imperfect feature subsets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all five baselines (DIME, GDFS, CAE, OPL, and center-cropping/random selection) on all nine datasets in terms of predictive accuracy at every feature budget, as shown in Figure 2. Against the nearest-neighbor baseline inspired by AACO (Valancius et al., 2024), the method achieves consistent gains on all five tabular datasets: e.g., 0.96 vs. 0.95 AUROC on Spambase, 69.8% vs. 68.1% on Metabric, and 0.84 vs. 0.83 AUROC on CKD. The empirical oracle (which follows precomputed explanation-based rankings perfectly) consistently outperforms the learned method, confirming that the explainability-driven ranking is a strong upper bound and that the decision transformer policy approximates it well. The method's advantage over DIME and GDFS is most pronounced at low feature budgets (fewer than 10 features), where instance-specific ordering matters most, while CAE and OPL remain competitive only at higher budgets. The second-stage training is critical: without it, performance drops by up to 7.35% (Metabric), and simply extending first-stage training to 250 or 300 epochs does not recover this gap.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reframes active feature acquisition (AFA) as a feature *prediction* task rather than the standard exploration (RL-based, e.g., OPL) or information-theoretic (CMI-based, e.g., GDFS, DIME) formulations. It builds on the Decision Transformer architecture (Chen et al., 2021) as the policy network, but the key conceptual departure is using local explanation methods (SHAP, LIME, FastSHAP) to precompute instance-specific feature importance rankings that serve as supervision signals, effectively turning AFA into an imitation-learning problem. The paper explicitly positions this as bridging two previously disconnected literatures: the local explanation community (which showed that explanation-derived rankings affect model performance when features are inserted/removed) and the AFA community (which had not formally compared explanation-based rankings against purpose-built acquisition policies).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is significant: in domains like medicine where explainability tools are already deployed for clinical trust, the same precomputed SHAP/LIME rankings can directly guide sequential feature acquisition without the computational overhead of RL training or CMI estimation at inference time. The two-stage training strategy (oracle rankings → policy's own imperfect predictions) is a clean solution to the distribution shift between training and inference. Key limitations include the assumption of uniform feature costs, dependence on ranking quality (a weaker pre-trained model degrades performance), and the computational cost of generating rankings during training. Open questions include extending to non-uniform acquisition costs, whether the approach generalizes to settings where no good explanation method exists, and whether the decision transformer's advantage over simpler policy architectures persists at larger scale.
