## Problem

Existing knowledge transfer paradigms—Knowledge Distillation and Multi-Task Learning—cannot simultaneously acquire new task capabilities and improve performance on existing tasks through inter-model interaction. KD causes blind imitation that degrades original task performance, while MTL sacrifices task-specific expertise for generalization. The paper introduces Socialized Coevolution (SC), a paradigm where specialized models mutually enhance each other's performance through dynamic, selective interaction.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252872/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on a pair of specialized CNN models (ResNet-50 backbone) that jointly process the same RGB image: one specialized for classification (CIFAR-100, 32×32) and one for detection (VOC07+12, standard resolution). During training, the auxiliary model's intermediate-layer features and final logits are injected into the main model, so each model's output is a function of both its own and the partner's representations. At inference, both models run independently and their predictions are combined via the DSC weighting scheme to produce the final class label or bounding boxes.

## Method

DISC (Dynamic Information Socialized Collaboration) implements SC through two complementary modules. The Dynamic Hierarchical Collaboration (DHC) module injects the auxiliary model's cumulative layer-wise features into the main model's input at each depth, gated by a sine-based progressive schedule that ramps auxiliary influence from zero to full over training epochs, mimicking curriculum learning. The Dynamic Selective Collaboration (DSC) module computes per-sample logits weights where each model's contribution is proportional to its own prediction confidence and inversely related to its loss, enabling sample-adaptive decision fusion. Training alternates between the two models (fixing one while updating the other) with a combined classification and regression loss. Theoretically, the paper proves that dynamic weighting yields a tighter generalization bound than static weighting via negative covariance between collaboration weights and losses, and formalizes the coevolution benefit through conditional mutual information (sociability information) that quantifies complementary knowledge between models.

## Results

On CIFAR-100 (classification) and VOC07+12 (detection) under a data-efficient 10% training setting, DISC achieves 72.28% CLS accuracy, 84.92% DET mAP, and 78.60% average, outperforming all baselines. The most striking gain is on the average metric, where DISC leads the next-best method (CrossKD(DET)+MTL at 68.49%) by 10.11 points, demonstrating simultaneous improvement on both tasks rather than trading one for the other. DISC also improves upon the pre-evolution expert models (70.68% CLS, 83.89% DET) by +1.60 and +1.03 points respectively, confirming the coevolution effect.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DISC outperforms all seven baseline families on every metric. Against the strongest KD+MTL baselines, DISC leads CrossKD(CLS)+MTL by 1.15 points on CLS (72.28 vs. 71.13) and CrossKD(DET)+MTL by 0.91 points on DET (84.92 vs. 84.01). The gap widens dramatically on the average metric: DISC scores 78.60% versus 68.49% for CrossKD(DET)+MTL, a 10.11-point margin, because KD+MTL methods sacrifice one task to preserve the other (e.g., LSKD(DET)+MTL drops CLS to 50.13%). Unsupervised baselines (SwAV, DeepClusterV2, MoCo v2) and CLIP trail by 8–14 points on average, reflecting their inability to leverage cross-task interaction. The key differentiator is that DISC is the only method that simultaneously improves both CLS and DET beyond their respective pre-evolution expert levels, whereas all baselines either degrade one task or fail to exceed the expert ceiling on both.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions Socialized Coevolution (SC) as a departure from both Knowledge Distillation (one-way, blind imitation) and Multi-Task Learning (shared parameters sacrificing specialization), drawing on the dynamic fusion framework of Zhang et al. (ICML 2023) for its generalization bound and the hierarchical augmentation/distillation analysis of Zuo et al. (TPAMI 2024) for its sociability information theory. Conceptually, it repurposes ideas from cognitive science (social learning, cultural evolution) to frame cross-model interaction as mutual, sample-adaptive collaboration rather than fixed teacher-student transfer or parameter sharing. The DISC architecture instantiates this through two modules—DHC for progressive hierarchical feature interaction and DSC for dynamic logit-level decision weighting—that operate on independently trained, task-specialized CNNs (ResNet-50) for classification and detection.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central claim—that models can simultaneously improve on their own tasks through cross-task interaction without sacrificing specialization—addresses a genuine gap left by KD and MTL, and the information-theoretic framing (sociability information as the key factor in coevolution) offers a useful lens for future multi-model systems. However, the evidence is narrow: only two tasks (classification, detection), two datasets (CIFAR-100, VOC07+12), a single backbone (ResNet-50), and a data-efficient 10% setting, making it unclear whether the mutual-improvement effect generalizes to more heterogeneous task pairs, larger models, or real-world deployment. Open questions include scalability to many tasks, integration with federated or continual learning, and whether the dynamic weighting mechanism remains effective under distribution shift.
