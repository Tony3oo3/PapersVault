## Problem

The two main topological data analysis (TDA) methodologies each have critical limitations: geometric complexes (Vietoris-Rips, Alpha, Witness) scale poorly with data size due to exponential simplex growth, while Mapper graphs are difficult to tune (requiring a filter function, cover of its range, and clustering parameters) and inherently capture only 1-dimensional topology. This paper identifies cover learning as a standalone unsupervised learning problem and formulates it from an optimization perspective to address both limitations simultaneously.

## Input / Output

The method takes a point cloud X ⊆ ℝ^N as input and produces a fuzzy cover g: X → Γ^{k-1} (a function mapping each point to a vector in [0,1]^k with max coordinate 1), which induces a filtered simplicial complex indexed by [0,1] via the nerve construction. Thresholding at λ ∈ [0,1] yields a single simplicial complex whose vertices correspond to groups of data points (not individual points), and whose higher-dimensional simplices encode non-empty intersections between cover elements. The user specifies only the number of cover elements (n_cov) and a neighborhood graph parameter (n_neigh), with all other parameters fixed to defaults.

## Method

The core contribution is formulating cover learning as optimization over fuzzy covers (continuous relaxations of discrete covers), with a loss combining three terms: measure (small cover elements via L1 norms), geometry (smooth boundaries via gradient L1 norms), and topology (homology-goodness via total persistence of suplevel sets, justified by the nerve theorem). The key theoretical result (Theorem 3.2) shows these loss terms reduce to computable quantities—L1 norms, gradient L1 norms, and barcode lengths—making optimization tractable. The practical algorithm (ShapeDiscover) builds a UMAP-style neighborhood graph, initializes with spectral clustering, parametrizes fuzzy covers via a per-point matrix composed with softmax and p-norm normalization, and optimizes using gradient descent with topological persistence optimization (big steps) for the 0-dimensional homology loss. This avoids the need for a filter function entirely and produces simplicial complexes with far fewer vertices than geometric complexes while retaining higher-dimensional topological information that 1D Mapper cannot capture.

## Results

On the topological inference benchmark (Table 1), ShapeDiscover achieves the target homology recovery quotient with dramatically fewer vertices and simplices than all baselines: e.g., on the 3-sphere at quotient 0.5, it needs only 19 vertices and 1.6×10⁴ simplices versus 184 vertices and 1.7×10⁹ simplices for Vietoris-Rips. On the neuroscience grid-cell dataset, ShapeDiscover recovers toroidal topology (H₁=2) with a 15-vertex simplicial complex, while Vietoris-Rips with 5,000 vertices yields a homology recovery quotient of 0. In the fixed-vertex comparison (Table 2), ShapeDiscover achieves the highest homology recovery quotient on all four datasets (0.74, 0.51, 0.70, 0.38), outperforming the next-best method (Witness v=2) by a wide margin.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ShapeDiscover outperforms all geometric-complex baselines (Vietoris-Rips, sparse Rips, Alpha complex, Witness complex) on every dataset in Table 1, requiring 3–10× fewer vertices and orders of magnitude fewer simplices; e.g., on the 3-sphere it needs 19 vertices vs. 184 for Rips (a 10× reduction) and 1.6×10⁴ vs. 1.7×10⁹ simplices. The closest competitor is Witness (v=1), which uses 6 vertices on the 2-sphere vs. ShapeDiscover's 5, but ShapeDiscover still wins on all other datasets and by larger margins. Discrete Morse theory sparsification (S+Rips+DMS) and Alpha complexes run out of memory on the 3-sphere and dynamical system datasets, while ShapeDiscover handles all four datasets within memory. In the fixed-vertex comparison (Table 2), ShapeDiscover achieves the best homology recovery quotient on all datasets (e.g., 0.74 vs. 0.30 for Alpha on the 2-sphere; 0.70 vs. 0 for Witness on the human dataset). In visualization experiments, 1D Mapper and Differentiable Mapper recover only 1-dimensional structure (graphs) and cannot capture higher-dimensional topology such as the hollow interior of the human shape, while Ball Mapper's single global ε parameter is difficult to tune and fails to recover correct topology; ShapeDiscover captures the full 2-dimensional topology with intuitive parameters (n_cov, λ). On the grid-cell neuroscience dataset, ShapeDiscover recovers toroidal topology with 15 vertices, a task that Vietoris-Rips fails to accomplish even with 5,000 vertices.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reframes the implicit cover-construction step in existing TDA pipelines (1D Mapper, Ball Mapper, Witness complex) as a first-class optimization problem, introducing 'cover learning' as a unified unsupervised learning task. Theoretically, it builds on the nerve theorem to derive a principled loss function whose terms (measure, geometry, topology) are shown to be computable via standard quantities (L1 norms, gradient norms, barcode length), and relies on topological persistence optimization with big steps (Nigmetov & Morozov, 2024) for the topological term. The key conceptual departure from Mapper-family methods is eliminating the need for a user-chosen filter function and its associated tuning burden, while departing from geometric complexes (Vietoris-Rips, Alpha) by producing covers whose nerve is inherently small rather than sparsifying an already-large complex.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses two persistent pain points in TDA: the prohibitive size of geometric complexes (Vietoris-Rips scales as Θ(|X|^{d+1})) and the difficulty of tuning Mapper's multiple hyperparameters while being limited to 1-dimensional output. The practical impact is demonstrated by recovering toroidal topology in neuroscience data with 15 vertices where Vietoris-Rips fails at 5000, and by achieving homology recovery quotients with 5–19 vertices where baselines need 48–283. Key open questions the authors identify include: lack of consistency guarantees (no topology recovery theorem for the optimization-based approach), the inability to minibatch during topological persistence optimization (requiring the full dataset each step), and the prevalence of spurious intersections in learned covers that complicate visualization and can corrupt topology recovery.
