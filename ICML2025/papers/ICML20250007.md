## Problem

While LLMs can solve tasks never seen in training, no quantitative framework existed to characterize how many training tasks suffice for generalization across an entire task family. Prior work demonstrated that Transformers fail at out-of-domain sparse parity learning with standard in-context learning, but the role of compositional structure in enabling or blocking task generalization remained unexplored. This paper asks: can a model trained on only Õ(D) tasks generalize to all D^T tasks in a compositional class?

## Input / Output

The model receives a single concatenated prompt of m in-context demonstration pairs (x_j, f(x_j)) followed by a query x_query, where x_j ∈ {0,1}^d are binary sequences. Without CoT the output is a single bit; with CoT the output is a sequence of k intermediate XOR values that progressively reveal the parity computation step by step. For the translation task, inputs are words in a source language (with a language label prepended, e.g. 'English: cat') and outputs are sequentially translated words across T languages.

## Method

The paper introduces the AutoRegressive Compositional (ARC) framework, where each task is a composition of T conditional distributions each drawn from a family of D subtasks, yielding D^T total tasks. The central insight is that Chain-of-Thought reasoning transforms the sparse parity problem from ARC(d^k, 1) — requiring O(d^k) training tasks — into ARC(d, k) — requiring only Õ(d) tasks, by decomposing the single-step XOR into k sequential subtask identifications. Theoretically, Theorem 3.3 proves that a learner using MLE to identify subtask families during training and total-variation-based distribution discrimination at inference can recover any unseen task from Õ(D) i.i.d. training tasks. Empirically, standard Transformers trained with CoT on i.i.d. sampled parity tasks achieve >95% accuracy on held-out tasks with ~3d·ln(d) training tasks, and the same Õ(D) scaling holds for arithmetic (ARC(2, d−1)) and multi-step language translation (ARC(D, T−1)).

## Results

The paper demonstrates exponential task generalization: training a Transformer on only Õ(d) tasks enables generalization to D^T unseen parity tasks. Specifically, training on 306 tasks (d=30, k=15) achieves 98.10% accuracy on ~155 million unseen tasks, and 122 tasks (d=15, k=7) achieves 99.12% on ~6,400 unseen tasks. Without Chain-of-Thought (CoT), standard ICL performs at near-chance level regardless of the number of training tasks, while with CoT, accuracy exceeds 95% with just 3d·ln(d) ≈ 122 training tasks. The empirical scaling closely matches the theoretical bound of D·ln(D) tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between standard ICL (without CoT) and ICL with CoT reasoning on sparse parity functions. Without CoT, the Transformer performs at near-chance level (consistent with Bhattamishra et al., 2024) regardless of the number of training tasks presented, confirming that the problem is ARC(\binom{d}{k}, 1) with exponential breadth. With CoT, the same architecture achieves >95% accuracy on unseen tasks with only ~d·ln(d) training tasks, transforming the problem into ARC(d, k) with linear breadth. In the adversarial task selection experiments (d=10, k=3), excluding all tasks where s₂=5 (a missing coordinate) drops generalization to 45.6% (near chance), while excluding tasks containing both 4 and 6 (a missing pair) retains 96.2% accuracy, showing that positional diversity is critical for generalization. For arithmetic tasks (ARC(2, d-1)), training on a few hundred tasks generalizes to 2^25 > 33 million tasks, and for multi-step language translation (ARC(D, T-1)), a few hundred tasks generalize to 4^10 ≈ 10^6 tasks, though T-scaling shows linear rather than logarithmic dependence due to error accumulation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the ICL framework of Garg et al. (2022) and Bhattamishra et al. (2024), who showed that Transformers fail to generalize to unseen parity functions in-context, by introducing Chain-of-Thought reasoning (building on Abbe et al., 2024; Wen et al., 2025) as the mechanism that enables task generalization. The key departure is a formal AutoRegressive Compositional (ARC) structure that quantifies how CoT reduces the effective task breadth from O(d^k) to d, converting an intractable generalization problem into one solvable with Õ(d) training tasks. Unlike prior qualitative studies of compositional generalization (Saparov et al., 2023; Dziri et al., 2023), this work provides a quantitative sample-complexity bound and identifies task selection (i.i.d. vs. adversarial) as a critical factor.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ARC framework offers the first quantitative answer to 'how many tasks must a model see to generalize to all D^T tasks?', with the clean result that Õ(D) suffices under compositional structure. The adversarial task-selection experiment—where excluding a single coordinate value (s₂=5) from training causes chance-level performance despite training on nearly all D^T tasks, while excluding a pair (4,6) preserves generalization—reveals that positional diversity, not just task count, governs generalization. Limitations include the asymptotic training-sample requirement (n_x → ∞), reliance on small 3-layer GPT-2 models on synthetic tasks, and the observed linear (rather than logarithmic) T-dependency in translation that the theory does not capture. Open questions include whether the ARC assumption holds for real-world compositional tasks and whether the Õ(D) scaling survives at larger model and data scales.
