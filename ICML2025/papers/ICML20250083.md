## Problem

VLM safety alignment is unevenly distributed across image encoder layers: using intermediate layer embeddings (early exit) instead of the default penultimate layer creates an out-of-distribution scenario where the language backbone interprets visual features differently, causing it to generate harmful responses even with safe images. No prior work had examined how layer-wise internal embeddings from the vision encoder affect overall VLM safety, leaving this vulnerability (termed ICET) unexplored despite the growing trend toward multi-layer feature usage for efficiency and reasoning.

## Input / Output

The method takes a benign RGB image and a harmful text prompt; the image is forward-propagated through the image encoder and activations are captured at a chosen intermediate layer l (rather than the default penultimate layer L), then projected through a frozen multi-modal MLP and concatenated with the text embedding to form the state for the language backbone. The output is an autoregressive text response. The critical detail is that the same (image, prompt) pair can yield a safe refusal or a coherent harmful answer depending solely on which encoder layer's embeddings are fed to the language model, since intermediate layers were never seen during pre-training or safety alignment.

## Method

The core contribution is Layer-wise Clip-PPO (L-PPO), a modification of standard Clip-PPO in which the RL policy (language backbone) is trained using intermediate layer embeddings e_l of the image encoder as input rather than the default penultimate layer embeddings. The policy ratio K is computed with P_β(e_l) in both numerator and denominator, so the policy learns to generate safe responses specifically conditioned on that layer's visual features, while the image encoder and projection network remain frozen and only the language backbone is updated via LoRA adapters (r=8, α=16). The theoretical foundation extends the monotone improvement theorem of PPO to L-PPO, showing the performance bound naturally applies to any intermediate layer and guarantees consistent reduction in layer-specific vulnerability. GPT-2-based reward models trained on HH-RLHF provide scalar preference scores, and the approach is validated across three VLMs (LLaVA-1.5, LLaVA-NeXT, Llama 3.2 Vision) using Llama Guard ASR, Perspective API toxicity, and reward-based metrics, achieving up to 48% ASR reduction and 33.64% toxicity reduction while preserving VQA utility.

## Results

L-PPO consistently reduces the ICET vulnerability across all three test sets. On the most challenging setting (RT train, MR test), average AASR drops from 50.58% to 10.50% (a ~79% relative reduction), ATS from 7.92 to 5.19, and ATR improves from −0.34 to +0.31. On the custom prompts (CP) test, average AASR falls from 64.62% to 37.96%, and ATS from 10.71 to 6.24. Utility is preserved: on VQA-v2, ATR-UT and AAS-UT remain nearly unchanged after alignment (e.g., AAS-UT average stays within 0.5 points), confirming that safety gains do not come at the cost of general question-answering ability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark L-PPO against other alignment methods in the main experiments; the primary comparison is between the unaligned (original) VLM and the L-PPO-aligned VLM under ICET conditions. L-PPO outperforms the unaligned baseline on every metric and every layer group across all three test sets, with the largest gains on the MR test (AASR: 50.58→10.50) and the CP test (AASR: 64.62→37.96). The authors note in Appendix L that L-PPO also outperforms SFT-based safety alignment on the same ICET setting, though specific SFT numbers are deferred to the appendix. Utility is preserved: on VQA-v2, AAS-UT remains within ~0.5 points of the original (e.g., 48.73→48.23 average), and over-refusal checks on XSTest (Appendix K) confirm the model does not simply refuse all queries. Human evaluation (Fleiss' Kappa = 0.8916) corroborates the automated metrics, with AASR dropping from 50.21 to 16.74 on CP and from 62.47 to 17.79 on AC.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Clip-PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) framework for multi-modal RLHF by introducing a layer-wise variant (L-PPO) that trains the language backbone using intermediate image encoder embeddings rather than only the default penultimate-layer embeddings. It departs from prior VLM safety alignment work, which focuses on adversarial input perturbations (malicious images, gradient-based noise) or LLM-layer skipping, by identifying an architectural vulnerability: simply switching which encoder layer feeds the language model creates an out-of-distribution scenario that breaks safety alignment without any adversarial token. The theoretical grounding draws on the monotone improvement guarantees of TRPO/PPO (Schulman et al., 2015; Kakade & Langford, 2002), extended to show that L-PPO ensures consistent reduction in layer-specific vulnerability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As multi-layer feature fusion (e.g., DenseConnector) and early-exit inference become increasingly popular for efficiency and reasoning gains, the finding that safety alignment does not generalize across encoder layers exposes a practical blind spot in current VLM deployment. The per-layer L-PPO fix is effective but limited: it must be applied separately for each vulnerable layer, shows weaker improvements on early layers where outputs become incoherent, and is validated only on LLaVA-1.5. Open questions include whether a unified cross-layer alignment strategy can preserve fine-grained safety guarantees, how to handle reward modeling for incoherent early-layer outputs, and whether the vulnerability persists in newer architectures with different encoder designs.
