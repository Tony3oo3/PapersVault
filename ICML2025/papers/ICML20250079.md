## Problem

Previous algorithmic contract design assumes the agent always plays an exact best response to the principal's payment scheme, but in practice agents may respond suboptimally due to imperfect information, limited computation, or misinterpretation. This paper initiates the study of hidden-action principal-agent problems under approximate best responses, where the agent may select any action up to δ-suboptimal, and asks whether optimal robust contracts can be computed efficiently and learned online—contrasting sharply with the NP-hardness of analogous problems in Stackelberg games and Bayesian persuasion.

## Input / Output

The computation algorithm takes as input the full instance tuple (A, Ω, F, r, c) with the robustness parameter δ ∈ (0,1), and outputs an optimal δ-robust contract p* ∈ R_+^m, i.e., a non-negative payment vector over outcomes that maximizes the principal's worst-case utility over all δ-best responses. In the online learning setting, the contract space is restricted to the hypercube [0,1]^m, the principal observes only the realized outcome each round (not the agent's action or type), and the algorithm outputs a sequence of contracts achieving Õ(T^{1−1/(2(m+1))}) pseudo-regret against the optimal robust contract.

## Method

The core algorithm (Algorithm 1) enumerates all O(n²) pairs (a*, a^δ) of the agent's exact best response and worst δ-best response, then for each pair solves a disjunctive optimization problem. The key structural insight is that the disjunctive robustness constraints partition the contract space into only O(n) subspaces (rather than exponentially many) because the separating hyperplanes are parallel—their coefficients on p are invariant across actions—so within each subspace the problem reduces to a linear program, yielding O(n³) total LPs. For the online learning component, the paper proves a continuity lemma (Lemma 3) showing that shifting a contract toward the reward vector via p' = (1−√ε)p + √ε·r degrades utility by at most 2√ε, enabling a simple uniform-grid discretization of [0,1]^m with UCB1 that does not require prior knowledge of the principal's rewards, unlike the state-of-the-art approach of Zhu et al. (2023).

## Results

The paper establishes that the value of an optimal δ-robust contract satisfies OPT(δ) ≥ OPT − 2√δ + δ (lower bound) and OPT(δ) ≤ max{0, SW − δ} (upper bound), where OPT is the non-robust optimum and SW is the social welfare. The main algorithmic result is a polynomial-time algorithm (Algorithm 1) that computes an optimal δ-robust contract by solving O(n³) linear programs, in contrast to the NP-hardness of analogous problems in Stackelberg games and Bayesian persuasion. For the online learning setting, Algorithm 2 achieves a regret bound of Õ(T^(1−1/(2(m+1)))) against an optimal robust contract, and Õ(T^(1−1/(2(m+1)))) + 2√δ·T against an optimal non-robust contract (sublinear when δ = 1/T^α for α > 0).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's comparisons are entirely theoretical rather than empirical. Against Gan et al. (2023) on robust Stackelberg equilibria, the key contrast is computational: while computing an optimal robust commitment in Stackelberg games is NP-hard (requiring a QPTAS), the paper shows that computing an optimal δ-robust contract in hidden-action principal-agent problems is polynomial-time solvable via O(n³) LPs. Additionally, unlike Stackelberg games where OPT(δ) depends on the inducibility gap Δ and may be discontinuous for δ > Δ, here OPT(δ) is always continuous and its bounds depend only on δ, OPT, and SW. Against Zhu et al. (2023) on non-robust online contract design, the paper achieves a comparable regret bound of Õ(T^(1−1/(2(m+1)))) (vs. their Õ(√m · T^(1−1/(2m+1)))) while using a simpler uniform grid discretization of the hypercube and not requiring prior knowledge of the principal's reward vector. The paper also notes that the lower bound Ω(T^(1−1/(m+2))) from Zhu et al. (2023) still applies in the robust setting when δ is arbitrarily small, confirming that exponential dependence on the number of outcomes m is unavoidable.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper initiates the study of hidden-action principal-agent problems under approximate best responses, drawing a direct parallel to the robust Stackelberg equilibrium work of Gan et al. (2023) and the Bayesian persuasion robustness results of Yang & Zhang (2024), both of which established NP-hardness for computing optimal robust commitments. The key departure is that the special structure of contract design (solution space R_+^m rather than the simplex Δ_m) allows the robustness constraints to be partitioned into O(n) linear programs, yielding a polynomial-time algorithm where intractability was expected by analogy. The online learning component builds on Zhu et al. (2023) for non-robust contract learning, while the continuity argument (Lemma 3) extends the payment-shifting technique of Dutting et al. (2021) to the robust setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The polynomial-time tractability result is significant because it identifies a structural property of contract design that distinguishes it from other robust mechanism design problems, potentially guiding future work on which robustness notions admit efficient computation. The price-of-robustness bounds (OPT(δ) ≥ OPT − 2√δ + δ and OPT(δ) ≤ max{0, SW − δ}) provide clean, instance-independent characterizations that practitioners can use to quantify the cost of robustness. Open questions include whether the O(n³) LP-based algorithm can be improved, whether the results extend to multi-agent or combinatorial contract settings, and whether the online learning regret bounds can be tightened beyond the exponential dependence on the number of outcomes m.
