## Problem

The paper argues that the default trajectory for language model agent (LMA) development will produce 'platform agents' controlled by incumbent platform companies, amplifying surveillance harms, market manipulation, attention control, and illegitimate governance over users. No serious framework or advocacy exists for the alternative — user-controlled 'agent advocates' that serve individual users as fiduciaries rather than platforms as self-interested go-betweens. The paper identifies this as a critical, time-sensitive fork requiring deliberate intervention before the platform-agent path locks in.

## Input / Output

The paper describes the operational model of agent advocates rather than a specific system: they consume user tasks, preferences, and constraints, then execute digital actions (browsing, transacting, communicating) on the user's behalf, producing personalized recommendations, research reports, and transaction outcomes. A key architectural distinction from platform agents is that agent advocates would run on local hardware or encrypted private clouds, giving users visibility into and control over data collection, and would be designed to provably forget content they have processed. The output is structured around fiduciary obligation to a single principal (the user), with no competing loyalty to a platform's commercial interests.

## Method

As a position paper, the core contribution is a theoretical framework distinguishing platform agents from agent advocates using intermediary theory (representative vs. go-between, self-interested vs. fiduciary, constitutive vs. neutral), followed by three coordinated interventions. The first calls for broad public access to compute and open-weights AI models independent of platform companies, including a public complement to private frontier AI research. The second proposes open interoperability protocols, agent credentialing infrastructure certifying architecture and fiduciary compliance, and digital clearinghouses to secure agent-to-agent microtransactions and prevent walled gardens. The third advocates market regulation preventing platforms from blocking agent access to their interfaces, extending data portability protections to LMAs, and constraining vertical integration. The paper addresses counterarguments on feasibility (alignment, institutional incentives, past decentralization failures) and situates agent advocates within a broader solution space including AI regulation, platform regulation, privacy law, and antitrust enforcement.

## Results

This is a position paper with no quantitative experiments or benchmark results. The authors argue qualitatively that platform agents (the default LMA trajectory) will intensify surveillance harms, market manipulation, attention control, and illegitimate governance, while agent advocates (user-controlled, fiduciary agents) would mitigate these harms. Their three proposed interventions—public access to open models and compute, open interoperability/safety standards, and antitrust-style market regulation—are framed as necessary to shift the default trajectory toward user-centric agents. No metrics, ablations, or empirical evaluations are reported.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not empirically compare methods against baselines; it contrasts two conceptual pathways for LMA development: platform agents (self-interested constitutive go-betweens controlled by incumbent platforms) versus agent advocates (fiduciary representatives acting solely in users' interests). The authors position agent advocates as a Pareto improvement over the default platform-agent trajectory, arguing they reduce surveillance harms, lower switching costs, enable more competitive markets, and restore user autonomy. They acknowledge counterarguments—that platform agents may be more convenient, that agent advocates face unresolved alignment/loyalty challenges, and that prior decentralization efforts have often failed—but respond that LMAs present more favorable conditions for user-centric design than prior internet infrastructure. No head-to-head empirical comparison is conducted.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes platform economics (van Dijck, Susskind, Lazar), the fiduciary/loyal AI literature (Aguirre et al., 2020; Benthall & Shekman, 2023), and the AI alignment tradition (Russell, 2019; Christian, 2020) to carve out a new design-and-policy space for language model agents. Rather than proposing a new architecture or benchmark, it repurposes the intermediary taxonomy (representative vs. go-between, constitutive vs. neutral) from legal and economic theory to frame the LMA development trajectory as a binary: platform agents that amplify incumbent power, or agent advocates that serve users as fiduciaries. It also builds directly on the authors' prior work on open foundation models (Kapoor et al., 2024) and agent infrastructure (Chan et al., 2024, 2025), extending those threads into a concrete policy proposal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central contribution is framing the LMA ecosystem's default trajectory as a policy choice rather than a technical inevitability, and specifying three coordinated interventions (public compute and open models, interoperability/credentialing standards, and antitrust-style market regulation) to shift that trajectory. Key open questions it surfaces include whether open-weight models can sustainably match frontier closed models, whether agent loyalty/fiduciary alignment is technically solvable at the required reliability level, and whether past failures of decentralization (Narayanan et al., 2012) will repeat. For the field, it provides a vocabulary and analytical scaffold that could shape both research agendas (e.g., provable forgetting, agent credentials, defensive agents) and regulatory discussions as LMAs move from narrow 'Deep Research' tools to universal digital concierges.
