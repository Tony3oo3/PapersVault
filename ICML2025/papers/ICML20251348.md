## Problem

Despite excelling at high-level perception and reasoning, state-of-the-art MLLMs consistently underperform humans on simple, intuitive tasks like counting, spatial reasoning, and perspective-taking (Moravec's Paradox). The paper hypothesizes these deficiencies stem from the absence of core knowledge—rudimentary cognitive abilities innate to humans from early childhood—and asks whether MLLMs genuinely possess such foundational understanding or merely approximate it through shortcut learning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251348/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each benchmark item pairs an image or video with a multiple-choice text question probing one of 12 core knowledge concepts, with 95+ samples per concept. Evaluation employs 11 prompting techniques per model, cyclically rotates answer options to eliminate position bias, and scores via a two-stage pipeline (template matching followed by LLM-as-a-Judge for free-form outputs). The Concept Hacking protocol additionally presents each model with 45 controlled samples and their manipulated counterparts where task-relevant features are inverted to flip ground-truth labels, yielding a 2×2 outcome matrix per model.

## Method

The paper introduces CoreCognition, a 1,503-sample benchmark organized around 12 core knowledge concepts mapped to Piaget's developmental stages (Sensorimotor, Concrete Operational, Formal Operational), curated by 12 cognitive-science-trained annotators with criteria for discriminativeness, minimal confounding, and minimal text shortcuts. The key analytical contribution is Concept Hacking: a controlled evaluation that systematically manipulates task-relevant visual features while preserving task-irrelevant conditions to completely invert ground-truth labels, distinguishing four response types (core knowledge, shortcut, core deficit, and blind) to separate genuine understanding from spurious correlation. Across 230 models (1B–110B parameters, proprietary and open-source) and 11 prompts (2,530 data points), the authors analyze performance by developmental stage, inter-ability correlations, scaling slopes via linear regression, and reasoning-model comparisons, revealing that low-level abilities show reduced or absent scalability, weak cross-stage dependencies, and persistent shortcut reliance that intensifies rather than resolves with scale.

## Results

On the CoreCognition benchmark (1,503 samples across 12 core knowledge concepts), the best-performing model GPT-o1 achieves a mean accuracy of 74.91%, trailing human performance (86.98%) by 15.91 percentage points. GPT-4o (69.25%) and Qwen2.5-VL-72B (68.29%) trail humans by 21.57% and 22.53% respectively. The deficit is most pronounced on lower-level sensorimotor abilities (e.g., Permanence: GPT-o1 at 57.03% vs. human 88.10%) and Perspective Taking (GPT-o1 at 55.21% vs. human 91.99%), while higher-level abilities like Tool Use approach or exceed human levels (GPT-o1: 98.16% vs. human 91.87%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-o1 leads all 230 evaluated models with a 74.91% mean accuracy, but still trails humans (86.98%) by 15.91 points; GPT-4o (69.25%) and Qwen2.5-VL-72B (68.29%) trail by 21.57 and 22.53 points respectively. Proprietary models do not consistently dominate open-source ones: GPT-4o outperforms Qwen2.5-VL-72B by only ~1% on average, while Gemini-1.5-Pro and Claude-3.5-Sonnet underperform Qwen2.5-VL-72B, QVQ-72B-Preview, and InternVL3-78B by 2–3%. On Perspective Taking, the gap is most severe—GPT-o1 scores 55.21% versus 91.99% for humans, and InternVL3-78B is the only model above 70% (70.79%). Reasoning-augmented models (e.g., Kimi-VL-A3B-Thinking, QVQ-72B-Preview, R1-OneVision-7B) show no statistically significant improvement over their instruction-tuned counterparts, with the sole exception being a slight degradation on Perspective Taking (P=0.0037). The Concept Hacking experiment further reveals that larger models (including GPT-4o) cluster in the 'shortcut' quadrant (high control accuracy, below-chance manipulation accuracy), indicating that scaling amplifies shortcut reliance rather than genuine core knowledge acquisition.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of developmental cognitive science and MLLM evaluation. While prior MLLM benchmarks (SEED-Bench, MathVerse, M3GIA, Marvel) target high-level reasoning or general intelligence, and DevBench probes language learning trajectories, none systematically evaluates the foundational 'core knowledge' abilities (object permanence, spatiality, continuity, etc.) that developmental psychology identifies as prerequisites for higher cognition. The paper repurposes Piaget's stage taxonomy and Spelke's core knowledge framework to define 12 evaluable concepts, and introduces Concept Hacking—a controlled perturbation method borrowed from shortcut-learning literature—as a diagnostic tool to distinguish genuine understanding from spurious correlation. It does not build on a specific prior method but synthesizes ideas from cognitive science, shortcut learning, and MLLM benchmarking into a new evaluation paradigm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The findings challenge a central assumption in the field: that scaling model size will eventually close the gap on all cognitive tasks. The evidence that low-level core abilities show little or no scaling benefit, and that larger models exhibit *more* shortcut-taking rather than progressing toward genuine understanding, suggests that current training paradigms are structurally incapable of instilling these foundational capacities. The Concept Hacking methodology offers a reusable, controlled diagnostic that could be applied to future models. Open questions include whether pretraining strategies that explicitly target core knowledge (e.g., distilling from developmental data or synthetic physics environments) could close the gap, and whether non-human pathways to AGI would necessarily develop these abilities or could bypass them entirely. Limitations include the relatively small sample size (1,503 items), multiple-choice format, and the fact that 12 concepts may not exhaust the full scope of core knowledge.
