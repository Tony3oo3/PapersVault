## Problem

The K-means algorithm has been widely assumed since the 1980s to converge to a locally optimal solution, a claim often attributed to Selim & Ismail (1984) whose proof contains a critical error. This paper provides the first rigorous counterexample demonstrating that standard K-means can converge to a solution that is neither C-local (continuous) nor D-local (discrete), and then develops minimal modifications that provably guarantee local optimality without increasing computational complexity.

## Input / Output

The method accepts a set of N unique points in R^d with associated positive weights, a number of clusters K, and a Bregman divergence D (squared Euclidean, KL, Itakura-Saito, or squared Mahalanobis). It outputs a binary cluster assignment matrix P ∈ {0,1}^{K×N} and cluster centers C ∈ R^{d×K}, where P can be stored as a compact label array of size N for O((N+K)d) space. The key guarantee is that the output is certified as C-local or D-local, and the algorithm only performs additional work after standard K-means convergence, making it a drop-in post-processing step.

## Method

The paper first establishes necessary and sufficient conditions for C-local and D-local optimality: a converged K-means solution is C-local if and only if the set of optimal assignments A(C*) is a singleton (Theorems 4.2–4.3). Based on this, two lightweight functions are invoked only when K-means converges: C-LO checks for ties in nearest-center assignment and resolves them by reassigning a tied point to the other cluster, while D-LO (and its Min-D-LO variant) evaluates the exact clustering loss change Δ₁ for all N(K−1) adjacent reassignments and performs the first (or best) move that strictly decreases the loss. Both functions use a precomputed weight-sum array s_k(P) to update centers in O(d) per reassignment via a closed-form incremental formula, preserving the O(NKΓ_φ(d)) per-iteration complexity of standard K-means. The Min-D-LO variant, which selects the adjacent point minimizing Δ₁ rather than the first improving one, achieves comparable accuracy to D-LO with significantly fewer iterations, making it the recommended practical choice.

## Results

On the News20-1 dataset (N=2,000, d=1,089), Min-D-LO reduces the mean clustering loss from 919,058 (K-means) to 642,980 at K=10 (a ~30% improvement), and from 790,822 to 485,809 at K=25 (~39% improvement). On Iris (N=150, d=4), improvements are more modest: mean loss drops from 31.55 to 30.55 at K=10. C-LO yields no clustering loss improvement on real-world datasets (where A(C) is almost always a singleton), but its verification overhead is negligible. Min-D-LO requires fewer iterations than D-LO (e.g., 317 vs. 870 at K=10 on News20-1), achieving similar accuracy with substantially reduced runtime.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against standard K-means (Lloyd, 1982), the proposed D-LO and Min-D-LO variants consistently achieve lower clustering loss across all real-world datasets and cluster sizes, with the largest gains on high-dimensional data (e.g., ~30–39% reduction on News20-1). The C-LO variant matches K-means exactly on real-world datasets because the set A(C) of optimal assignments is almost always a singleton when features are continuous, though it does improve on synthetic integer-valued data. Against the D-local algorithm of Peng & Xia (2005), all D-local methods (D-LO, Min-D-LO, D-LO-P&X) reach similar clustering loss values that are significantly lower than K-means, but D-LO-P&X is substantially slower than both proposed methods. Min-D-LO is highlighted as the best accuracy–speed trade-off: it matches D-LO's solution quality while requiring far fewer iterations (e.g., 317 vs. 870 at K=10 on News20-1), and is much faster than D-LO-P&X.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Lloyd's (1982) K-means algorithm and the Bregman divergence clustering framework of Banerjee et al. (2005), extending the local optimality analysis of Peng & Xia (2005) from squared Euclidean distance to general Bregman divergences. Its key conceptual departure is from the long-standing assumption—cited in Selim & Ismail (1984), scikit-learn documentation, and Balcan et al. (2018)—that K-means converges to a locally optimal solution. The authors show this assumption is false via a simple counterexample, then propose minimal modifications (LO-K-means) that guarantee convergence to either a C-local or D-local solution without increasing per-iteration complexity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Correcting the misconception that K-means always converges to a locally optimal solution is important for the theoretical foundations of clustering, as it invalidates the justification used in several prior works (K-means--, Bregman K-means) that cite monotone loss decrease as evidence of local optimality. The practical impact is somewhat limited: on real-world datasets, C-LO rarely improves over K-means (suggesting the failure mode is rare in continuous settings), while D-LO and Min-D-LO provide consistent but modest clustering loss reductions at the cost of more iterations. Open questions include whether the gap between C-local and D-local solutions matters for downstream tasks, and whether the approach extends naturally to other clustering formulations (e.g., k-medians, spherical k-means).
