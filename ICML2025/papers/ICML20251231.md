## Problem

Existing Pareto Set Identification (PSI) algorithms for multi-armed bandits cannot incorporate feasibility constraints, yet practical applications such as clinical trials require identifying the Pareto Set only among arms satisfying minimal efficacy or safety thresholds. This paper introduces constrained PSI (cPSI) and the more demanding explainable variant (e-cPSI), where the learner must additionally justify each rejected arm as either infeasible or dominated by a feasible arm, and shows that the natural two-stage approach (identify feasible arms, then their Pareto Set) can be arbitrarily suboptimal.

## Input / Output

The method takes a K-armed bandit with d-dimensional mean vectors, a known polyhedron P = {x : Ax ≤ b} defining m linear feasibility constraints, and a target error probability δ. The output is a partition (O, S, I) of [K] where O is the Pareto Set of feasible arms, S contains arms dominated by a feasible arm, and I contains arms deemed infeasible; arms that are both infeasible and dominated may be assigned to either S or I, so multiple valid partitions exist. The algorithm operates in the fixed-confidence regime, stopping adaptively with probability at least 1−δ of returning a valid partition.

## Method

The paper proposes two algorithms: Game-cPSI, a saddle-point/online-learning method for cPSI that matches the information-theoretic lower bound asymptotically, and e-cAPE, a confidence-bound algorithm for e-cPSI that is the primary contribution. e-cAPE uses a top-two (leader–challenger) sampling rule: the leader b_t is the arm minimizing the current stopping statistic (either Z_1 confirming Pareto feasibility of O_t or Z_2 confirming all other arms are classified), and the challenger c_t is the arm in the empirical feasible set closest to dominating b_t. Stopping occurs when both Z_1(t) ≥ 0 (arms in O_t are confidently feasible and mutually non-dominated) and Z_2(t) ≥ 0 (every arm outside O_t is confidently infeasible or dominated). The key insight is that e-cAPE's sample complexity scales with C*_M(ν), the minimum over all valid partitions of the sum of per-arm costs, which for arms in SubOpt ∩ F^c pays only the cheaper of infeasibility or dominance verification—making it provably and empirically superior to the two-stage baseline that always pays both costs.

## Results

On both the Secukinumab dose-finding trial (5 arms, 2 objectives) and the CovBoost 19 vaccine trial (20 arms, 3 objectives), e-cAPE achieves the lowest sample complexity among all compared methods, with results averaged over 500 runs at δ=0.1. The advantage is most pronounced in the Secukinumab instance, where the 75mg arm lies near the feasibility boundary and is dominated by the 150mg arm: e-cAPE classifies it as suboptimal without paying the full feasibility-verification cost, while the two-stage baseline (A-A) incurs a large additional overhead. On CovBoost, the Uniform baseline is excluded from the comparison due to its excessively high sample complexity, and e-cAPE again dominates the remaining methods (A-A and R-CP).

## Baselines & Comparisons

e-cAPE outperforms all three baselines on both datasets in terms of sample complexity. Against the two-stage MD-APT+APE (A-A) baseline (Katz-Samuels & Scott 2018 for feasibility + Kone et al. 2023 for PSI), e-cAPE wins most decisively on the Secukinumab trial, where a suboptimal arm near the feasibility boundary forces A-A to pay a high feasibility-verification cost that e-cAPE avoids by classifying the arm as dominated instead. Against the Racing algorithm (R-CP), an adaptation of the elimination-based approach of Auer et al. (2016), e-cAPE also achieves lower sample complexity, with theoretical and empirical evidence of R-CP's sub-optimality provided in Appendices F and G.3. Against Uniform sampling (U), e-cAPE is substantially more efficient; on the CovBoost dataset, U's sample complexity is so high that it is excluded from the reported comparison entirely. The paper also notes that e-cAPE's leading complexity term is always smaller than that of the two-stage approach, as it only pays the PSI cost for arms in SubOpt∩F and the minimum of infeasibility or dominance cost for arms in SubOpt∩Fᶜ.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate lines of bandit pure-exploration research: Pareto Set Identification (PSI) from Kone et al. (2023), Auer et al. (2016), and Crepon et al. (2024), and feasibility detection from Katz-Samuels & Scott (2018). The key departure is that no prior work combines these two tasks; the authors explicitly note that existing PSI algorithms cannot handle constraints on the Pareto Set. Methodologically, e-cAPE adapts the top-two/LUCB confidence-bound framework (Kalyanakrishnan et al., 2012; Kone et al., 2023) to jointly track feasibility and dominance, while Game-cPSI extends the saddle-point/gradient-ascent approach of Degenne et al. (2020) and Crepon et al. (2024) to the constrained setting. The multiple-correct-answers framework of Degenne & Koolen (2019) provides the theoretical scaffolding for the explainability variant (e-cPSI).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that the intuitive two-stage approach (identify feasible arms, then their Pareto Set) can be arbitrarily worse than a unified algorithm, which is a practically important finding for applications like clinical trials where sample budgets are limited. The near-optimality guarantees (matching lower bounds up to constants for specific instance classes) provide a clear benchmark for future work. Open questions include whether the moderate-confidence performance of the asymptotically optimal Game-cPSI algorithm is competitive in practice, and the authors explicitly flag adaptive strategies allowing controlled constraint violations during exploration as a critical direction for clinical applications.
