## Problem

The traditional aleatoric/epistemic uncertainty dichotomy, designed for fixed-input, fixed-output tasks like classification, is fundamentally inapplicable to LLM agents operating in open, multi-turn, interactive settings. The paper demonstrates that popular definitions of aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty directly contradict each other even in toy examples, and that in dynamic conversations the boundary between reducible and irreducible uncertainty shifts over time, rendering the binary classification of uncertainty sources meaningless.

## Input / Output

The paper discusses LLM agents that consume a user query and the evolving conversation context (a growing string x_t) as input, and should produce not just a scalar uncertainty score but a rich output comprising clarifying follow-up questions, a natural-language explanation of which competing answers are plausible and why, and optionally verbalized or phonetic uncertainty cues. The key distinction from prior work is that the output space is the full string/speech space rather than a single probability or a pair of (aleatoric, epistemic) scalars, and the input is dynamic across turns rather than a single fixed vector.

## Method

As a position paper, the core contribution is a three-part argumentative structure. First, it constructs a toy binary-classification example showing that epistemic uncertainty is simultaneously maximal (under disagreement/mutual-information definitions) and minimal (under axiomatic model-counting definitions), and that aleatoric uncertainty is simultaneously reducible and irreducible depending on whether one fixes the model class. Second, it cites empirical evidence (Mucsanyi et al., 2024) showing aleatoric and epistemic estimators are nearly perfectly correlated (rank correlations 0.8–0.999), and theoretical results (Gruber et al., 2023) showing additive decomposition fails even for linear models. Third, it proposes three new research directions specific to interactive LLM agents: underspecification uncertainties (task- and context-level ambiguity at conversation start), interactive learning (choosing follow-up questions to reduce underspecification, analogous to active learning but with a human in the loop), and output uncertainties (communicating leftover uncertainty via coherent natural-language summaries of competing possibilities rather than scalar scores, extending conformal prediction ideas to the string space).

## Results

This is a position paper without a proposed method or experimental evaluation, so there are no headline metrics or main results tables. The key quantitative findings it cites from the literature include: (1) Mucsanyi et al. (2024) report rank correlations between 0.8 and 0.999 between aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty estimators across deep ensembles, Gaussian processes, and evidential deep learning on ImageNet-1k, demonstrating near-perfect entanglement; (2) GPT-3.5-Turbo-16k detects ambiguous questions with only 57% accuracy (vs. 50% random), and human annotators rate only 53% of generated follow-up questions as helpful; (3) Min et al. (2020) show that 56% of Natural Questions test items contain ambiguities. These numbers are used to motivate the paper's three proposed research directions rather than to benchmark a new method.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new method and therefore does not compare against baselines in the traditional sense. Instead, it surveys and contrasts multiple schools of thought on aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty (summarized in Table 1), showing that they produce contradictory conclusions even on a simple binary-classification toy example (e.g., whether a two-point posterior represents maximal or minimal epistemic uncertainty). The closest thing to a 'comparison' is the empirical evidence from Mucsanyi et al. (2024) and de Jong et al. (2024) showing that aleatoric and epistemic estimators are nearly perfectly correlated (rank correlations 0.8–0.999), undermining the practical utility of the dichotomy. The paper's contribution is thus a critical literature synthesis and a proposal of three new research avenues (underspecification uncertainties, interactive learning, and output uncertainties) rather than a methodological comparison.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper critically surveys the aleatoric/epistemic uncertainty decomposition literature (Hüllermeier & Waegeman 2021; Wimmer et al. 2023; Depeweg et al. 2018; Kendall & Gal 2017) and argues that the dichotomy is internally contradictory—different equally-grounded definitions yield opposite conclusions for the same scenario. It departs from this tradition by repurposing ideas from active learning (reframed as 'interactive learning'), conformal prediction (extended to 'output uncertainties'), and missing-variable problems (generalized to 'underspecification uncertainties') into a unified framework tailored to multi-turn LLM agent interactions. The paper also draws on recent LLM hallucination impossibility results (Kalai & Vempala 2024; Banerjee et al. 2024) and NLP-specific uncertainty frameworks (Baan et al. 2023) to motivate why the traditional scalar-based approach is insufficient for open-ended chat settings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By demonstrating that the aleatoric/epistemic split is both definitionally incoherent and empirically entangled (rank correlations of 0.8–0.999 between estimators), the paper challenges a foundational organizing principle in uncertainty quantification and redirects attention toward interaction-aware uncertainty handling. The three proposed research directions—underspecification detection, interactive learning strategies, and rich output uncertainty communication—remain largely open, with current systems (e.g., GPT-3.5-Turbo) detecting ambiguous questions at only 57% accuracy. A key limitation is that the paper is purely positional: it offers no new methods, benchmarks, or empirical validation of the proposed directions, leaving the community to develop them. Open questions include how to define metrics for output uncertainties beyond scalar scores, how to balance the number of clarifying questions against user patience, and whether the proposed framework generalizes beyond text to multimodal or speech-based agents.
