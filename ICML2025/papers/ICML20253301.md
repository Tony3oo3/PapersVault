## Problem

Current AI governance frameworks—ranging from the EU AI Act to OECD guidelines—operate within top-down or expert-led paradigms that grant communities only peripheral or tokenistic roles in shaping AI systems. As AI proliferates into healthcare, education, finance, and urban planning, this concentration of design authority risks entrenching algorithmic bias, eroding epistemic autonomy, and reducing affected populations to passive recipients of infrastructure they did not help build. The paper identifies the absence of a principled, rights-based framework that elevates community participation from consultation to genuine co-governance of AI.

## Input / Output

The paper draws on three analytical inputs: (1) theoretical frameworks from urban theory (Lefebvre's Right to the City, Arnstein's Ladder of Citizen Participation, Jacobs' grassroots urbanism), (2) nine empirical case studies of participatory AI spanning healthcare, urban planning, NLP, humanitarian logistics, and AI alignment, and (3) existing policy instruments from the EU, OECD, and national governments. It produces a four-tier governance model (Consumer-Based → Private Organization-Led → Government-Controlled → Citizen-Controlled), a set of eight concrete implementation recommendations, and a reconceptualization of AI as societal infrastructure subject to collective stewardship rather than proprietary control.

## Method

The core contribution is a theoretical reframing: AI is reconceptualized from a product of expert design to societal infrastructure analogous to utilities or educational systems, thereby inheriting the governance obligations of public goods. Building on this, the authors adapt Arnstein's eight-rung ladder into a four-tier model distinguished by stakeholder agency, transparency, and inclusivity, with explicit transition mechanisms between tiers (e.g., structured feedback channels, statutory councils, cooperative data trusts with legally enforceable ownership). The argument is grounded in four overlapping justifications—democratic legitimacy, social justice/pluralism, epistemic autonomy, and collective data production—and stress-tested against nine participatory AI case studies that reveal success factors (early engagement, transparent goals, resource equity) and failure modes (participationwashing, cooptation, resource asymmetry). The paper positions its participatory approach as complementary to, rather than replacing, market-led and state-centric governance, and distinguishes the Right to AI as a 'power right' (per Wenar) rather than a narrower right to explanation or contestation.

## Results

This is a position paper with no quantitative benchmarks. Its main empirical contribution is a qualitative analysis of nine participatory AI case studies (Table 1), spanning domains from healthcare to urban planning to machine translation. The paper's central theoretical output is a four-tier 'Ladder of Right to AI' model (Consumer-Based → Private Organization-Led → Government-Controlled → Citizen-Controlled) adapted from Arnstein's Ladder of Citizen Participation, which it uses to situate current AI governance practices and articulate an aspirational endpoint.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not run quantitative comparisons against baselines. Instead, it contrasts its participatory 'Right to AI' framework against two dominant alternative governance paradigms: (1) market-led governance, which relies on competition and consumer choice to drive responsible AI, and (2) state-centric regulation, which imposes top-down standards through government agencies. The authors argue that market-led models systematically overlook communities lacking purchasing power or market influence, while state-centric models risk overlooking localized knowledge and community-specific concerns. The proposed participatory approach is positioned as complementary to both—offering a 'missing dimension' of inclusive community-driven frameworks that can coexist with market incentives and centralized regulations. The nine case studies in Table 1 serve as qualitative evidence that participatory methods can yield more equitable and context-sensitive outcomes (e.g., WeBuildAI's algorithm produced more equitable donation allocations than traditional human-led methods), though the paper acknowledges structural barriers such as resource inequalities, cooptation risks, and participation fatigue that limit current implementations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes three distinct intellectual traditions—Lefebvre's Right to the City (urban theory), Arnstein's Ladder of Citizen Participation (political science), and Ostrom's commons governance (institutional economics)—into a unified normative framework for AI governance. It extends the participatory AI literature (Sloane et al., 2022; Birhane et al., 2022; Sieber et al., 2024) and pluralistic alignment work (Sorensen et al., 2024) by reframing stakeholder engagement not as a design technique but as a collective *power right*, deliberately departing from both top-down regulatory paradigms (EU AI Act, OECD guidelines) and market-led self-regulation. The four-tier model (Consumer-Based → Private Organization-Led → Government-Controlled → Citizen-Controlled) is a novel structural contribution that maps existing AI governance practices onto a graduated spectrum of stakeholder agency.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete diagnostic tool (the four-tier ladder) for assessing where current AI governance initiatives actually sit on the participation spectrum, and a normative target for where they should aim. Its nine case studies reveal recurring structural barriers—resource asymmetries, cooptation, participation fatigue—that any implementation must address, but the paper stops short of resolving how to scale participatory mechanisms to national or global AI systems or how to fund them sustainably. Key open questions include: how to balance community sovereignty with domain expertise in high-stakes settings (healthcare, aviation), how to prevent localized biases from fragmenting broader protections, and whether the 'power right' framing can gain legal or institutional traction beyond academic discourse.
