## Problem

Transformers exhibit systematic position biases—lost-in-the-middle, attention sinks, order sensitivity in in-context learning—yet a comprehensive theoretical understanding of how attention masks and positional encodings jointly shape these biases remains elusive. Prior work has documented these phenomena empirically and proposed task-specific mitigations, but lacks a unified framework that quantifies how masking structure and PE mechanisms interact across multiple attention layers to produce position-dependent behavior independent of semantic content.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a token sequence X ∈ R^{N×d}, an attention mask formalized as a directed graph G (causal, sliding-window, or prefix), and a positional encoding configuration (none, decay mask, or RoPE), and produces closed-form bounds on the cumulative attention rollout probability P^(t)(z_i=j|X^(0)) for each token pair (i,j) at depth t. The key structural object is the directed graph whose paths encode multi-layer information flow: a directed edge (j,i) means token i attends to token j, and reachability captures indirect contextual influence. Empirically, the method outputs retrieval accuracy gaps between position-paired test sequences and attention sink metrics computed over 10,000 sequences.

## Method

The paper develops a graph-theoretic framework in which attention masks define directed graphs and the cumulative attention probability P^(t)(z_i=j|X^(0))—equivalent to attention rollout—is analyzed as aggregate path probability in that graph. For causal masks, the key result (Theorem 4.1) shows that P^(t)(z_i=1) → 1 exponentially because softmax cannot disconnect graph edges, so center nodes (the first token) accumulate dominant influence through iterative contextualization. For relative PEs, the analysis reveals a non-monotonic trade-off: within a single layer, the decay mask or RoPE imposes exponential (or quadratic) distance-based decay, but across t layers the combinatorial path count C(t+i-j, i-j) counteracts this decay, shifting the peak attention distance x* = t/(e^m − 1) (decay mask) or an implicit function of t and θ₁ (RoPE) toward earlier positions as depth increases. The framework is validated through controlled experiments on a synthetic information-retrieval task, reproducing lost-in-the-middle, attention sinks, and the differential effects of causal masking versus explicit PEs on positional bias.

## Results

The paper's central theoretical result (Theorem 4.1) shows that under a causal mask, the cumulative attention probability P^(t)(z_i=j|X^(0)) decays exponentially as C(1-(j-1)ε)^t, meaning attention converges to the first token regardless of semantic content. For relative PEs, Theorem 4.5 establishes that the decay mask produces a non-monotonic profile P_decay^(t)(z_i=j) = Θ(C(t+i-j, i-j)·e^(-(i-j)m)), with a critical point x* = t/(e^m−1) that shifts toward earlier positions as depth t increases. Theorem 4.7 extends this to RoPE with a quadratic decay e^(-c(i-j)²θ₁²), yielding a similar non-monotonic trade-off. Experiments on a synthetic retrieval task (n=8, d=64, 10,000 test sequences per condition) confirm that deeper attention layers (2→6) consistently amplify bias toward earlier positions, that the decay mask induces substantially stronger distance-based decay than RoPE, and that the 'lost-in-the-middle' U-shape emerges only when training data is biased toward both sequence ends.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper with controlled empirical validation rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so no direct performance table exists. Instead, the paper compares architectural design choices: (1) causal mask vs. no mask — the causal mask without any PE introduces a measurable position bias toward the first position (Figure 3), while no mask does not; (2) decay mask vs. RoPE vs. no PE — the decay mask produces a substantially stronger distance-based decay (exponential in distance) than RoPE (quadratic in distance with a very small θ₁ ≈ 1/10000), as confirmed both theoretically (Lemmas 4.4 vs. 4.6) and empirically (Figure 2); (3) causal mask alone vs. causal mask + PE — the causal mask without PE captures positional bias only at the first position, whereas sin PE and RoPE capture biases at both ends of the sequence, refuting the hypothesis (Kazemnejad et al., 2023) that the causal mask implicitly simulates a full PE. The framework also explains attention sink emergence: under the causal mask, sinks appear only on the absolute first token; under the prefix mask, they appear on all K prefix tokens; under the sliding-window mask, they appear on the absolute first token but not on window-local first tokens — all matching observations in Gu et al. (2025) and Xiao et al. (2024).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies the graph-theoretic analysis of attention masks (Wu et al., 2023; 2024; Barbero et al., 2024a) with the attention rollout quantity from Abnar & Zuidema (2020), repurposing it from an empirical visualization tool into a rigorous theoretical object for bounding position bias across layers. It builds on the synthetic experimental framework of Reddy (2024) for controlled validation, and positions itself against a body of empirical observations on position bias (lost-in-the-middle, attention sinks, in-context learning order sensitivity) that lacked a unified theoretical explanation. The key departure is treating the cumulative attention probability P^(t)(z_i=j|X^(0)) as the central analytic quantity, enabling precise characterization of how causal masks and relative PEs interact across depth.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first principled theoretical framework connecting multiple previously separate empirical phenomena—attention sinks, lost-in-the-middle, and depth-dependent position bias—to the graph structure of attention masks and the decay properties of positional encodings. The non-monotonic trade-off (per-layer decay toward recent tokens vs. cross-layer accumulation toward early tokens) has direct implications for architectural design, suggesting that simply adding stronger PEs cannot eliminate position bias in deep networks. Open questions include how residual connections, value projections, and MLPs modulate these effects, whether the framework extends to multi-head and multi-query attention, and whether natural language's own positional structure (primacy-recency effects) shapes the lost-in-the-middle phenomenon in the way the simplified experiments suggest.
