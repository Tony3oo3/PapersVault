## Problem

Social predictions are performative: they actively shape the data distribution they are meant to forecast, raising the question of whether one can efficiently find predictors that influence outcomes while still producing valid, calibrated forecasts. While existence of such predictors was established in the 1950s (Simon, Grunberg & Modigliani), the algorithmic question of how to find them efficiently—both computationally and statistically—remained open. This paper resolves that gap and further shows that even perfectly calibrated performative predictors can be arbitrarily poor in a risk-minimization sense.

## Input / Output

The learner receives n rounds of interaction with an unknown outcome-performative distribution map D(·), where at each round a feature x is drawn from a fixed distribution D_x, a prediction p is sampled from the current predictor f(x), and an outcome y is drawn from D_y(x,p)—a potentially discontinuous function of p. The output is a randomized predictor f_A: X → Δ([0,1]) constructed by uniformly mixing over the T deterministic predictors from the online algorithm's transcript, requiring no smoothness or Lipschitz assumptions on D_y(x,p) beyond bounded outcomes.

## Method

The core technique is an online-to-batch reduction: any online algorithm A that guarantees multicalibration (bounded cumulative weighted error) against adversarial sequences is converted into a batch predictor f_A that is performatively multicalibrated, with error O(Regret(n)/n + √(log|C|/n)). The conversion works by uniformly randomizing over all past online predictors and applying a martingale (Azuma-Hoeffding) argument to bound the generalization gap, crucially without requiring the distribution map to be continuous in the prediction. The paper instantiates this reduction using the K29 kernel-based algorithm to obtain concrete guarantees for finite function collections, linear functions, and low-degree polynomials, achieving the optimal O(n^{-1/2}) statistical rate. A second result shows that performatively multicalibrated predictors are automatically performatively stable (no alternative model has lower loss on the induced distribution), yet a construction demonstrates they can simultaneously maximize performative risk, revealing a fundamental tension between calibration and accuracy in performative settings.

## Results

The main algorithmic result (Theorem 3.4) establishes that performatively multicalibrated predictors can be found with error O(n^{-1/2}) via an online-to-batch reduction, requiring no smoothness assumptions on the distribution map beyond bounded outcomes. For specific function classes (Corollary 3.5), the K29 kernel algorithm yields: (a) finite collections with error √(|C|/n) and runtime O(n²|C|); (b) linear functions with error √(2/n) and runtime Õ(n·d); (c) degree-s Boolean functions with error 10√(d^s/n) and runtime O(ds·n²). The structural result (Theorem 4.2) shows performatively multicalibrated predictors are also performatively stable, while Theorem 5.1 demonstrates that perfectly calibrated predictors can simultaneously maximize performative risk.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or experimental comparisons. The key theoretical advance over prior work is that the algorithmic results require no Lipschitzness or continuity assumptions on the distribution map D(·), whereas all previous performative prediction convergence results (Perdomo et al., 2020; Mendler-Dünner et al., 2020; Drusvyatskiy & Xiao, 2023; Khorsandi et al., 2024; Taori & Hashimoto, 2023) imposed such regularity conditions. The paper also contrasts its notion of performative stability (the natural limit of online calibration) with performative optimality (studied by Kim & Perdomo, 2023), showing that stability is what online algorithms naturally achieve. The negative result (Theorem 5.1) contrasts with supervised learning intuition, where calibration implies optimal risk minimization, by showing the inequality flips in performative settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of performative prediction (Perdomo et al., 2020), multicalibration/outcome indistinguishability (Hébert-Johnson et al., 2018; Dwork et al., 2021), and online calibration algorithms (Vovk, 2007; Foster & Kakade, 2006). It departs from prior performative prediction work by eliminating all smoothness/continuity assumptions on the distribution map, replacing optimization-based convergence arguments with a clean online-to-batch reduction using the K29 kernel algorithm. Structurally, it connects performatively multicalibrated predictors to performative stability (not optimality) via loss-outcome-indistinguishability tools from Gopalan et al. (2023), and it provides a modern algorithmic answer to a question posed by Morgenstern (1928) and Simon (1954) that had only existential (fixed-point) solutions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper resolves a century-old methodological question by showing performatively calibrated predictors are always efficiently findable, but its most impactful contribution is the negative result: a predictor can be multicalibrated with respect to all bounded functions while simultaneously maximizing performative risk, inverting the supervised-learning intuition that calibration implies good prediction. This fundamentally challenges the use of calibration as a validity criterion in performative settings and calls into question the interpretation of public social forecasts that stake their credibility on calibration. Open questions include whether results extend to stateful (history-dependent) settings, feature performativity, and what the correct loss function should be when predictions actively shape outcomes.
