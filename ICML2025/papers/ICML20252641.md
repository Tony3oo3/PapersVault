## Problem

Self-consuming generative models iteratively trained on user-curated synthetic data provably converge toward user preferences under benign curation (Ferbach et al., 2024), but in competitive settings, rival platforms can recruit malicious users to adversarially curate data and gradually misalign a target model from genuine user preferences. This paper addresses two open questions: (1) under what conditions does the self-consuming loop remain robust to adversarial curation, and (2) how can an attacker with a limited budget most effectively disrupt model alignment over successive retraining rounds?

## Input / Output

The attack takes as input a pairwise preference dataset D = {(x_i, z_i, o_i)} collected from the adversarial platform's own users (where o_i ∈ {0, 0.5, 1} encodes which of two samples is preferred) and a budget parameter κ ∈ (0,1) bounding the fraction of labels that can be flipped. It produces a discrete perturbation vector δ with δ_i ∈ {-1, 0, 1} (selecting the top κ·n indices by magnitude) and a perturbed reward model R̃_θ̃ trained on the modified dataset. The attack is re-solved at each retraining round as the target model p_t evolves, making it a dynamic, iterative process rather than a one-shot perturbation.

## Method

The core theoretical result (Lemma 3.3) shows that the per-round change in expected reward E_{p_{t+1}}[e^{r(x)}] is governed by the covariance Cov_{p_t}[e^{r(x)}, e^{r̃_t(x)}] between the true and adversarial reward functions: positive covariance guarantees continued alignment, while negative covariance causes the reward to deviate from its maximum. Building on this, the attack is formulated as a bi-level optimization that minimizes this covariance plus a distance penalty dist(R_θ, R̃_θ̃) (to avoid detection), subject to R̃_θ̃ being the MLE reward model trained on the label-flipped dataset. Two solution strategies are proposed: a gradient-based method that relaxes δ_i to [-1,1], computes gradients through the inner reward-model training via the implicit function theorem (Hessian of the MLE loss), and applies projected gradient descent; and heuristic alternatives including a reward-based method (flipping labels of pairs with the largest |R_θ(x_i) − R_θ(z_i)| or max{|R_θ(x_i)|, |R_θ(z_i)|}) and a multi-objective Pareto search using NSGA-II. Experiments use a DDPM generative model with VGG11/ResNet56-based reward models on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100, demonstrating that the gradient-based attack drives the model to generate predominantly from least-preferred classes while maintaining image quality.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, the paper evaluates one-round attack effectiveness by measuring the expected reward E_{p_1}[r(x)] at t=1, where lower values indicate greater disruption of model alignment. Benign curation yields an average reward of 6.3606, while the gradient-based attack (GRADIENT #2, identical reward models) reduces it to 5.5959 and the multi-objective Pareto heuristic achieves the lowest reward of 5.4740. On CIFAR-100, iterative retraining under gradient-based adversarial curation causes a continuous decline in expected reward over iterations, whereas benign curation produces a steady increase, confirming the theoretical predictions of Lemma 3.3.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external baselines but instead evaluates multiple attack strategies against benign curation as the reference. The gradient-based attack (Algorithm 1) consistently outperforms random attacks, which show high variability across runs and sometimes even increase the reward in later iterations. Among the proposed methods, the multi-objective Pareto heuristic (NSGA-II) achieves the lowest average reward (5.4740), slightly outperforming both gradient-based variants (5.5959–5.6460) and the reward-based heuristics (5.5612–5.5982). However, the Pareto method comes at significantly higher computational cost. The gradient-based attack is highlighted as the most practical choice, balancing effectiveness with computational feasibility, and it produces the most dramatic qualitative shift in class proportions (e.g., shifting generation from preferred classes 0–1 to least-preferred classes 7–9 on CIFAR-10).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the self-consuming generative model framework of Ferbach et al. (2024), which showed that benign user curation drives models to optimize reward functions, by introducing adversarial curation into the iterative retraining loop. It also builds on the preference poisoning attack of Wu et al. (2025), which targets static reward model learning, by adapting the label-flipping attack to the dynamic, multi-round self-consuming setting where the attacker must repeatedly re-solve a bi-level optimization as the target model evolves. The theoretical analysis draws on the Bradley-Terry discrete choice model and the covariance-based bounds from Ferbach et al., while the gradient-based attack leverages the implicit function theorem as in Wu et al. (2025).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that the standard defense against model collapse—injecting real data into the training mix—does not protect against adversarial curation, which is a practically important negative result for platform operators. The competitive-platform threat model (one platform recruiting malicious users to disrupt a rival's model alignment) is realistic and raises urgent questions about defense mechanisms that remain open. Limitations include the assumption of global-optimum convergence at each retraining step, a known attack success rate κ, and experiments restricted to CIFAR-scale datasets with simple reward functions, leaving open whether findings generalize to large-scale LLM or video generation settings.
