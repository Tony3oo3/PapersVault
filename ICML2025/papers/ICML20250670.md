## Problem

Current SR benchmarking (e.g., SRBench) evaluates algorithms only by relative Pareto-dominance against a selected set of competitors, providing no indication of absolute efficiency or attainable performance limits. This means conclusions can shift depending on which algorithms are included, and researchers lack a fixed reference for how close their methods are to the best achievable R² at a given expression length.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250670/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes tabular datasets (n×d matrices with mean-0, std-1 normalized features) from SRBench, a fixed primitive function set {Add, Sub, Mul, Div, Pow}, and a head-length hyperparameter (3 or 4) that bounds expression complexity. It outputs, per dataset, an APO front: a sequence of expressions in strictly increasing length where each expression achieves the highest R² among all expressions of that length and must outperform all shorter expressions. The full exhaustive search data (every expression with its R² across all 8 optimizers and 10 random seeds) is also released.

## Method

The core technique is exhaustive enumeration of all K-expressions of a fixed head length using GEP's genome-phenome encoding, where the head is built from all permutations of primitive symbols and a tail of terminal symbols guarantees valid decoding without a validity check. For each decoded expression, the Ephemeral Random Constants (ERCs) are optimized using 8 different numerical methods (Powell, CG, trust-constr, L-BFGS-B, BFGS, TNC, SLSQP, Nelder-Mead) with random initial guesses from 10 seeds. The APO front is then extracted by selecting the single best-R² expression at each unique length, enforcing the constraint that longer expressions must strictly outperform shorter ones. The key design choice is using K-expressions rather than tree-based representations, which avoids the need for validity checks and enables systematic coverage of a larger class of expression structures than prior exhaustive-search SR methods.

## Results

Across 34 SRBench datasets, the exhaustive-search APO front reveals that no SR algorithm is close to the absolute performance limit: 85.3% of datasets have fewer than 1% of expressions in the top R² bin, and 35.2% have fewer than 0.01%. Only SBP-GP exceeds the APO front R² (at substantially higher expression length), while DSR is consistently closest to the APO front. For numerical optimization, the choice among 8 methods (Powell, CG, trust-constr, L-BFGS-B, BFGS, TNC, SLSQP, Nelder-Mead) has negligible impact: the mean standard deviation of R² across methods is 0.00043, and only 1.47% of expressions show a standard deviation exceeding 0.01. On average, only 23.7% of expressions two-mutations away from the APO front can greedily mutate back, indicating a rugged structural loss landscape.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks 14 SR algorithms from SRBench against the newly computed APO front. DSR stands out as the most consistently close to the APO front (100% of datasets on the relative Pareto front, 62% R² closeness), while SBP-GP is the only method whose R² exceeds the APO front, but only at much larger expression lengths. EPLEX, FEAT, and GP-GOMEA achieve 65–68% R² closeness, indicating they capture near-optimal short expressions on most datasets. In contrast, AIFeynman (12%) and BSR (15%) are far from the APO front, suggesting they under-explore the space of short expressions. The numerical optimization comparison shows Powell (32.1%), CG (25.0%), and trust-constr (20.0%) produce APO front expressions most frequently, while the commonly used BFGS (9.41%) and Nelder-Mead (0.00%) offer no clear advantage, indicating the choice of local optimizer is not a bottleneck for SR performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on Gene Expression Programming (Ferreira, 2002) for its K-expression representation, which enables exhaustive enumeration of valid expressions without validity checks, and on the SRBench benchmark (La Cava et al., 2021) as the source of datasets and comparison algorithms. It departs from SRBench's relative Pareto analysis (which ranks algorithms against each other) by proposing absolute Pareto-optimal (APO) fronts obtained via exhaustive search, providing a fixed universal baseline independent of which algorithms are included in a given comparison. The approach is distinct from other exhaustive-search SR methods (Kammerer et al., 2020; Bartlett et al., 2023) in that the goal is not to develop a competitive SR algorithm but to extract reference fronts with minimal structural assumptions, and it extends the K-expression framework used in DistilSR (Fong & Motani, 2023) by using raw variables rather than weighted linear combinations to cover a broader class of expression structures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The APO fronts provide the SR community with a fixed, dataset-specific performance ceiling that does not shift when new algorithms are introduced, addressing a fundamental limitation of relative Pareto benchmarking. The finding that 85.3% of datasets have fewer than 1% of expressions in the top R² bin, combined with the observation that for Type IV datasets all 14 SRBench algorithms fail to approach the APO front for short expressions, suggests that current SR methods substantially under-explore the short-expression search space. Key limitations include the restricted primitive function set {Add, Sub, Mul, Div, Pow}, reliance on local (not global) numerical optimizers, and head lengths capped at 3–4 due to exponential search-space growth. Open questions include whether the APO front shifts materially with expanded function sets or true global optimization, and how the proposed conventions (actual-value axes, per-dataset supplementary analysis) will be adopted in future SRBench iterations.
