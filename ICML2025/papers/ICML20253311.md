## Problem

Existing theory of mind benchmarks for LLMs measure only 'literal theory of mind'—the ability to predict a partner's actions—rather than 'functional theory of mind'—the ability to adapt one's own behavior rationally based on that prediction. This distinction is critical because LLMs lack the process consistency assumed in human ToM evaluation: they can correctly predict what a partner will do yet fail to act on that prediction, making standard benchmarks misleading about an agent's true interactive competence.

## Input / Output

The LLM receives a text-encoded interaction history (observations, actions, rewards over T=100 steps), game rules with explicit payoff tables, and a prompting instruction, all formatted as token sequences. It produces a single game action per step (e.g., R/P/S for RPS, J/F for IBS, Cooperate/Defect for IPD). Evaluation yields two decoupled metrics: literal ToM accuracy (ToM%, the fraction of correctly predicted partner actions) and functional ToM regret (Δ_Functional/T, the per-step reward gap versus the optimal response to the partner's policy), plus a third metric Δ_ToM/T measuring the regret of a rational policy that faithfully follows the literal ToM predictions.

## Method

The paper formalizes two distinct definitions within a partially observable stochastic game (POSG) framework: Definition 2.1 (literal ToM loss) measures the distance between predicted and actual partner action sequences, while Definition 2.2 (functional ToM regret) measures the cumulative reward gap between the agent's policy and the optimal response to the partner. Empirically, they evaluate seven LLMs (LLAMA-2 7B/13B/70B base and Chat, Falcon 40B, Mixtral 8x7B, LLAMA-3 70B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-32B) across three canonical games (RPS, Iterated Battle of Sexes, Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma) against simple single-action and tit-for-tat partners, testing seven prompting strategies (LM, QA, CoT, Plans+Insights, Reflexion, Social LM/QA, and Oracle variants). A tabular RMax baseline provides a near-optimal reference. The central finding is a persistent and large gap between literal and functional ToM performance across all models and strategies—even when the partner's actual action is provided as oracle input—demonstrating that strong prediction does not imply effective adaptation.

## Results

On Rock, Paper, Scissors against single-action partners over 100 rounds, the tabular RMax baseline achieves a functional regret of 0.083±0.004 per step with 97.4% literal ToM accuracy. The best LLM, Mixtral 8x7B Instruct v1, achieves 0.542±0.070 functional regret with 96.7% literal ToM accuracy, while LLAMA-2 70B reaches 0.971±0.067 functional regret with 96.8% literal ToM. Critically, the rational-response regret (Δ_ToM/T) for all LLMs is near-optimal (0.048–0.119), revealing a massive gap between what the LLM *predicts* the partner will do and what it *actually does* in response. Across IBS and IPD (Table 2), the functional regret gap persists: Mixtral's LM prompting yields 2.055 and 0.949 regret per step on IBS and IPD respectively, versus 0.211 and 0.086 for the tabular baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The tabular RMax baseline dominates all LLMs on functional theory of mind by a wide margin: 0.083 regret/step on RPS versus 0.542 (Mixtral, best LLM) to 1.015 (LLAMA-2 13B). Among LLMs, Mixtral 8x7B Instruct v1 outperforms the entire LLAMA-2 family and Falcon 40B on functional regret (0.542 vs. 0.857–1.015), while all models cluster tightly on literal ToM accuracy (92–97%). Within the LLAMA-2 family, larger models show slightly better functional performance, and instruction-tuned 'Chat' variants trade ~3–5% literal ToM accuracy for ~0.05–0.10 lower functional regret. The most striking comparison is between Δ_ToM/T and Δ_Functional/T: even LLAMA-2 70B achieves a near-optimal rational-response regret of 0.048 (close to RMax's 0.039) yet its actual functional regret is 0.971, a 20× gap. DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B (Table 4) narrows this gap substantially on single-action partners (0.074 on RPS, 0.126 on IBS, 0.121 on IPD), approaching or matching RMax, but degrades sharply against tit-for-tat partners (0.906 on RPS, 4.789 on IPD vs. 0.224 and 0.248 for RMax).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Akata et al. (2023), who first evaluated LLMs in repeated matrix games (Prisoner's Dilemma, Battle of Sexes), but departs fundamentally by arguing that the entire theory-of-mind evaluation paradigm for LLMs is misframed. Prior work in both human psychology (Sally-Anne false-belief tests) and multi-agent RL (Lowe et al., 2017; Rabinowitz et al., 2018) conflates predicting a partner's behavior with actually adapting one's own policy to that prediction. The paper formalizes this conflation as the gap between 'literal ToM' (prediction accuracy) and 'functional ToM' (regret relative to the optimal response), drawing on the predict-then-optimize literature (Elmachtoub & Grigas, 2022) to argue that prediction quality and decision quality are decoupled. It also situates its findings within the broader in-context learning and continual learning literature, noting that LLMs must adapt to partners without weight updates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central finding—that LLMs achieve 96%+ partner-action prediction accuracy while still playing near Nash equilibrium (effectively random) in actual gameplay—challenges a core assumption in the field that good prediction implies good adaptation. This has direct practical implications for deploying LLMs as interactive agents: a model that 'understands' its user's behavior may still fail to act optimally in response. Open questions include whether RL fine-tuning or reasoning-model training (e.g., DeepSeek-R1, which shows competitive but inconsistent performance) can close the functional ToM gap, whether the difficulty is fundamentally a long-context reasoning problem, and how these findings generalize to richer, non-matrix-game interactive settings. The paper calls for new benchmarks that directly measure functional ToM rather than relying on passive question-answering formats.
