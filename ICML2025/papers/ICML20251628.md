## Problem

Most semantic segmentation loss functions are pixel-wise, failing to capture structural and regional dependencies critical for preserving thin boundaries and small instances. Existing region-based or topology-aware losses (e.g., RMI, clDice, Hausdorff Distance) either restrict analysis to very small regions to limit computation or incur prohibitive computational costs, leaving a gap for a loss that models multi-scale structural dependencies efficiently.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251628/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The CWMI loss operates on paired binary segmentation masks (prediction and ground truth) of arbitrary spatial size, decomposing them via a complex steerable pyramid with N=4 levels and K=4 orientations before computing mutual information per subband. The base segmentation networks consume RGB or grayscale images (ranging from 584×565 in DRIVE to 1500×1500 in MASS ROAD, cropped to 512×512 or 448×576 during training) and output pixel-wise class probability maps that are thresholded to binary masks for loss computation.

## Method

CWMI loss decomposes both the prediction and ground truth masks into complex subbands using a complex steerable pyramid, where each subband encodes both magnitude (feature strength) and phase (structural details like edges and corners) across multiple scales and orientations. Mutual information is then estimated between corresponding subband pairs at each decomposition level using a covariance-based lower bound (from Zhao et al., 2019) extended to the complex domain via Hermitian transposes. The total loss is the sum of negative MI across all N levels, combined with a small cross-entropy term (λ=0.1) to provide directional learning signal, since MI is inherently symmetric. The key advantage over L1/L2/SSIM in the wavelet domain is that MI captures joint statistical dependencies across the K-dimensional orientation features at each pixel, making it robust to noise and well-suited to high-dimensional directional representations. The overall computational complexity is O(HW log(HW)) with K=4, comparable to standard pixel-wise losses and far cheaper than topology-based alternatives.

## Results

On SNEMI3D with VMUNet, CWMI achieves mIoU 0.783, mDice 0.882, VI 0.872, ARI 0.660, and HD 0.629, outperforming the strongest baseline (RMI: mIoU 0.762, HD 1.071) by 2.1% in mIoU and 41% in Hausdorff distance. Across all four datasets (SNEMI3D, GlaS, DRIVE, MASS ROAD) and both U-Net and Attention U-Net architectures, CWMI achieves statistically significant improvements (p<0.05 to p<0.001) on all metrics for SNEMI3D and DRIVE, and on clustering metrics (VI, ARI) for GlaS and MASS ROAD. The full complex CWMI consistently outperforms its real-valued variant (CWMI-Real), confirming the importance of phase information. Training overhead is minimal: CWMI adds only 0.23 s/epoch over cross-entropy, comparable to RMI (0.25 s) and well below Skea-topo (0.69 s) or clDice (0.34 s).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CWMI outperforms all 11 baselines on every metric for SNEMI3D (VMUNet), with the closest competitor being RMI (Region Mutual Information, Zhao et al. 2019), which CWMI beats by 2.1% mIoU (0.783 vs 0.762), 0.55 VI (0.872 vs 1.419), 7.2% ARI (0.660 vs 0.588), and 0.44 HD (0.629 vs 1.071). Among pixel-wise losses (CE, BCE, Focal, Dice, Jaccard, Tversky), CWMI leads by 4.4–10.5% in mIoU and 0.6–1.1 in HD. Topology-focused baselines such as clDice (HD 1.990) and WCE (HD 1.432) are substantially outperformed on boundary accuracy, while ABW and Skea-topo, which use precomputed weight maps, show the weakest mIoU (0.678 and below) and thicker boundaries in qualitative results. The Sensitive loss (Tang et al. 2025) and Focal loss perform mid-pack on pixel metrics but lag CWMI by >5% mIoU. Notably, CWMI's advantage is most pronounced on topological and clustering metrics (VI, ARI, HD), confirming its design goal of preserving structural coherence beyond pixel-level accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the complex steerable pyramid — originally developed for parametric texture modeling (Portilla & Simoncelli, 2000) and built on the shiftable multiscale transform framework (Simoncelli et al., 1992) — as a feature extraction backbone for a segmentation loss function. It extends the mutual information estimation strategy from RMI loss (Zhao et al., 2019), which was constrained to 3×3 spatial regions due to computational cost, into the frequency domain where the steerable pyramid's multi-scale, multi-orientation subbands naturally provide larger effective regions without explicit spatial windowing. Conceptually, the paper departs from the dominant spatial-domain loss paradigm (pixel-wise, region-overlap, boundary/skeleton-based) by arguing that structural similarity is better captured through joint statistics of wavelet subband coefficients than through pixel or region overlap in the spatial domain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CWMI offers a practical drop-in loss that addresses instance imbalance and boundary preservation with only ~0.23 s/epoch overhead over cross-entropy, making it attractive for high-resolution segmentation where topology-aware losses like clDice or Hausdorff distance become prohibitive. The paper's experiments are limited to binary segmentation on four datasets; extension to multi-class and 3D segmentation is acknowledged but left unvalidated, which is a significant open question given that most real-world segmentation tasks (e.g., organ segmentation, autonomous driving) are multi-class. The finding that wavelet-domain SSIM performs catastrophically (mIoU ≈ 0.47 vs. 0.78 for CWMI) raises the broader question of which similarity metrics are actually compatible with high-dimensional directional feature spaces, a question the paper does not fully explore.
