## Problem

Existing conformal inference methods for survival analysis (Candès et al., 2023; Gui et al., 2024) require type-I censoring where all censoring times are observed, a restrictive assumption not met in most practical survival studies. Under the more common right-censoring regime, censoring times are unobserved for individuals who experienced the event, rendering these methods inapplicable. This paper extends conformalized survival analysis to right-censored data while preserving rigorous coverage guarantees.

## Input / Output

The method requires a split-conformal setup with an independent training set (for fitting survival and censoring models) and a calibration set of right-censored observations (X_i, T̃_i, E_i), where T̃_i = min(T_i, C_i) and E_i indicates whether the event occurred before censoring. Given test covariates X_{n+1} and a significance level α, it outputs a scalar lower prediction bound L̂(X_{n+1}) — a time value representing the minimum survival duration guaranteed at the specified confidence level — satisfying P[T_{n+1} ≥ L̂(X_{n+1})] ≥ 1−α asymptotically.

## Method

DR-COSARC operates in two steps. First, it imputes unobserved censoring times for event individuals by sampling from the truncated conditional distribution C | X, C > T̃_i using the density estimated by a pre-trained censoring model, converting right-censored data into a synthetic type-I censored dataset. Second, it applies weighted conformal calibration (fixed-cutoff à la Candès et al. or adaptive-cutoff à la Gui et al.) on the imputed data to calibrate a candidate LPB of the form q̂_α(X) − a, with weights correcting for covariate shift induced by the censoring filter. A final double-robustness adjustment takes the minimum of the calibrated LPB and the uncalibrated survival model quantile, ensuring asymptotic validity if either the censoring model or the survival model is consistently estimated, even if the other is misspecified.

## Results

On the hardest synthetic setting (Setting 1, p=100, complex survival and censoring distributions), DR-COSARC (adaptive) achieves ~90% empirical coverage while the Uncalibrated method undercovers and KM Decensoring (Qi et al., 2024) also fails to reach the target. On seven real datasets (VALCT, PBC, GBSG, METABRIC, COLON, HEART, RETINOPATHY) with the grf survival model, DR-COSARC consistently achieves estimated coverage closest to the 90% nominal level (e.g., 0.92 on PBC, 0.90 on METABRIC, 0.91 on GBSG), while KM Decensoring undercovers on PBC (0.85) and HEART (0.84). LPB values are competitive: on PBC, DR-COSARC yields an average LPB of 852.27 with valid coverage, versus 1035.49 for KM Decensoring which sacrifices 7 percentage points of coverage.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary external baseline is KM Decensoring (Qi et al., 2024), which imputes latent survival times via the Kaplan-Meier estimator before applying conformal calibration. DR-COSARC clearly outperforms KM Decensoring on coverage in the hardest synthetic setting (Setting 1), where KM Decensoring undercovers because the marginal KM curve cannot approximate the conditional survival distribution T|T>C,X. On real data, DR-COSARC achieves coverage closer to the 90% target in most datasets: on PBC, DR-COSARC reaches 0.92 versus 0.85 for KM Decensoring (a 7-point gap), and on HEART, 0.88 versus 0.84. KM Decensoring sometimes produces higher LPB values (e.g., 1035 vs. 852 on PBC) but at the cost of invalid coverage. Against the internal baselines, Naive CQR is consistently overly conservative (coverage 0.93–0.97 with very low LPBs, e.g., 3.58 on HEART), while the Uncalibrated raw model output is competitive on easier datasets but undercovers on HEART (0.84) and PBC with the Cox model (0.85). The double robustness property means DR-COSARC maintains valid coverage even when the survival model is misspecified, as long as the censoring model is adequate—a property KM Decensoring lacks since it relies entirely on the KM approximation of the conditional survival distribution.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the conformalized survival analysis framework of Candès et al. (2023) and Gui et al. (2024), which were designed for the more restrictive type-I censoring setting where all censoring times are observed, to the far more common right-censoring scenario where censoring times are unobserved for individuals who experienced the event. The key methodological departure is a two-step procedure: first imputing latent censoring times via a trained censoring model (Algorithm 1), then applying the existing type-I conformal calibration to the resulting semi-synthetic dataset. This contrasts with the concurrent work of Qi et al. (2024), which also targets right-censoring but imputes latent survival times using the Kaplan-Meier estimator rather than imputing censoring times, and lacks the double robustness guarantee established here.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Right-censoring is the dominant censoring mechanism in practical survival studies (clinical trials, engineering reliability), so extending conformal survival analysis to this setting has direct practical relevance for obtaining calibrated uncertainty estimates from arbitrary ML survival models. The asymptotic double robustness property—valid coverage if either the censoring or survival model is correctly specified—provides a valuable theoretical safety net that the competing KM-decensoring approach lacks. Open questions include extending the method to two-sided prediction intervals (upper bounds), handling data errors or mislabeled events, and reducing the algorithmic variability introduced by stochastic imputation of latent censoring times. The method's reliance on a reasonably accurate censoring model for the imputation step remains a practical vulnerability, particularly in high-dimensional settings with limited training data.
