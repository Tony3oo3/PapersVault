## Problem

Despite a decade of adversarial robustness research, it remains unclear how scaling model size, attack compute, and defense compute each affect the robustness of language models, and whether the offense-defense balance shifts in favor of either side as both scale up. Prior work has studied attacks and defenses at fixed model sizes and compute budgets, making it impossible to determine whether larger models are inherently more robust or whether defenders can eventually outpace attackers.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250944/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The paper evaluates transformer classifiers (Pythia 14M–12B, Qwen2.5 0.5B–14B) on six binary classification tasks and one generative refusal task. For classification, the unembedding matrix is replaced with a classification head and the model outputs a single label; for generation (StrongREJECT), Qwen2.5-Instruct models produce free-form text judged by GPT-4o. Attacks append an adversarial suffix of N tokens (10 for GCG/RandomToken, 25 for BEAST) to the prompt, and attack success is measured as the fraction of correctly-classified (or correctly-refused) examples that flip after the suffix is applied.

## Method

The paper conducts the first large-scale empirical study of robustness scaling by systematically varying model size (three orders of magnitude), attack compute (iterations and FLOPs), and defense compute (adversarial training rounds and FLOPs) across two model families, seven tasks, and three attacks (RandomToken, GCG, BEAST). Adversarial training iteratively attacks a subset of training data, adds successful attacks to a growing pool, and trains on a 1000-example mixture of clean and adversarial examples, with attack strength ramped linearly across rounds. To enable fair cross-size comparison, all compute is normalized as a fraction of pretraining FLOPs. The key analytical contribution is combining attack and defense scaling curves to quantify the offense-defense balance: for each model size, they measure the slope relating attack compute to defense compute needed to maintain a fixed attack success rate, and track how this slope and the absolute compute frontier shift with model size.

## Results

On clean data, the smallest Pythia model (7.6M) already achieves 98.0% on Spam and 99.5% on PasswordMatch, while the largest (11.6B) reaches 99.0% and 99.5% respectively; the gap is much wider on the generative StrongREJECT task, where Qwen2.5-0.5B scores 55.6% versus 98.1% for Qwen2.5-14B. Without safety training, larger models are not consistently more robust: on IMDB, GCG attack success drops from ~100% (7.6M) to <20% (11.6B), but on Spam, increasing size from 123.7M to 6.7B actually *increases* attack success rate. After adversarial training, attack success drops from >90% to <20% within 5 rounds, and the offense-defense balance slope is <1 for all model sizes (attackers can double compute at a slower rate than defenders and still maintain the same ASR), though the slope improves with model size, suggesting defense could eventually gain the advantage.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper uses three attacks as its experimental tools: GCG (Zou et al., 2023), BEAST (Sadasivan et al., 2024), and a RandomToken baseline. GCG is the strongest attack, achieving near-saturation success rates on easy tasks (Spam, IMDB, PasswordMatch) with only 10 iterations, while BEAST (25 iterations, beam size 7) is competitive but generally weaker, and RandomToken (1280 iterations) is the weakest. In terms of robustness transfer, adversarial training against GCG transfers well to the weaker BEAST attack across all model sizes, but training against RandomToken transfers poorly to GCG—small models improve from >95% to <75% ASR while large models gain little, suggesting larger models need more sophisticated attack signals. For threat-model transfer, larger models generalize better to a 90%-infix attack (maintaining most robustness) while smaller models plateau, but no model size transfers effectively to a prefix attack. Across all settings, attack compute scaling outpaces defense compute scaling (slope < 1), though the gap narrows with model size.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the adversarial robustness evaluation literature by applying a scaling-law lens—previously used for capability scaling (Kaplan et al., 2020) and computer vision robustness (Carmon et al., 2022; Xie & Yuille, 2019)—to language model robustness across three orders of magnitude. It builds directly on GCG (Zou et al., 2023) and BEAST (Sadasivan et al., 2024) as attack primitives, adapting them to classification settings, and on the offense-defense balance framework from Garfinkel & Dafoe (2021). Unlike Ganguli et al. (2022), who observed a size-robustness correlation across only three model sizes, this work systematically varies model size, attack compute, and defense compute independently, and is the first to explicitly quantify the offense-defense scaling tradeoff for LMs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a reusable analytical paradigm—plotting attack and defense compute on log-log scales to extract scaling slopes—that can be applied to future attacks and defenses as they emerge, addressing a gap where prior evaluations fix compute and model size, making long-term projections impossible. Key open questions include whether the offense-defense balance shifts with latent-space adversarial training, input-output safeguard models, or other industry-standard defenses not studied here, and whether the classification-task findings transfer to open-ended generative jailbreaking at frontier scale. The finding that scale alone does not reliably improve robustness, combined with the observation that attack scaling currently outpaces defense scaling, has direct implications for how safety teams should allocate compute and interpret robustness benchmarks.
