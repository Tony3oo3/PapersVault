## Problem

It remains unclear whether fixed measurement strategies ("measure-first" protocols) that convert quantum data to classical representations before learning can universally replace fully-quantum protocols that process quantum data coherently. Prior lower bounds on measure-first protocols were restricted to worst-case scenarios and physically unrealizable states, leaving open whether the separation persists in the average-case, physically realizable regime that is relevant to actual experiments.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250840/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The learner receives polynomially many copies (ℓ = poly(n)) of n-qubit phase states |ψ_f⟩ paired with classical labels (x, y, b), where x ∈ {0,1}^n identifies the target POVM and (y, b) is sampled from the Hidden Matching relation R_f(x) = {(y,b) : f(y) ⊕ f(y⊕x) = b}. The output is a classical description of a polynomial-time quantum algorithm that, on a new input state (or its classical shadow ψ̂_f in the measure-first case), generates a sample from the correct outcome distribution π_x(f). A critical constraint is that the measurement strategy M in measure-first protocols must be fixed across all target concepts x (depending only on the concept class C), whereas fully-quantum protocols may adapt measurements based on the training data.

## Method

The paper constructs a learning separation around the Hidden Matching relation: the concept class maps phase states |ψ_f⟩ to distributions over (x, y, b) satisfying f(y) ⊕ f(y⊕x) = b. The fully-quantum protocol simply reads x from training data, constructs the poly(n)-size circuit U_x from Aaronson et al. (2024), and measures U_x|ψ_f⟩ in the computational basis to sample from the correct distribution. The lower bound for measure-first protocols reduces to the classical one-way communication complexity hardness of Hidden Matching (Bar-Yossef et al., 2004): any successful measure-first protocol would yield an efficient classical communication protocol for HM, contradicting the Ω(√N)-bit lower bound, with Yao's principle bridging the gap between the learning (average-case) setting and the communication complexity (worst-case) bound. To extend the separation to efficiently preparable states, the paper invokes quantum-secure pseudorandom functions (Brakerski & Shmueli, 2019): if a measure-first protocol succeeded on pseudorandom phase states, it could be repurposed as a quantum distinguisher between pseudorandom and truly random functions, violating the pseudorandomness assumption.

## Results

The paper proves an exponential sample-complexity separation: for a concept class defined by Hidden Matching relations over n-qubit phase states, no measure-first protocol can learn the target measurement with (1−ε)·(1−δ) > 7/8 (Theorem 3.3), while a fully-quantum protocol achieves (0,0,1)-learnability with polynomial data (Proposition 3.2). The separation extends to efficiently preparable pseudorandom phase states under the assumption of quantum-secure pseudorandom functions, where the threshold becomes (1−ε)·(1−δ)·p_succ > c for any constant c > 7/8 (Theorem 3.6). The 7/8 threshold is tight, matching the classical one-way communication complexity lower bound for the Hidden Matching problem (Bar-Yossef et al., 2004).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The central comparison is between measure-first protocols (any fixed randomized measurement strategy M followed by a learning algorithm A) and fully-quantum protocols (adaptive coherent measurements on training data). The paper shows that *no* measure-first protocol—regardless of how powerful the measurement strategy M is, including coherent measurements on polynomially many copies—can solve the learning task with polynomial data, while a specific fully-quantum protocol succeeds with O(poly(n)) samples and O(poly(n)) time. Compared to prior lower bounds (Grier et al., 2024; Aaronson et al., 2024), this work improves in two key dimensions: (1) the separation holds in the average case over efficiently preparable states rather than only in worst-case over arbitrary (potentially unphysical) states, and (2) the proof leverages quantum pseudorandom functions to ensure the input states are physically realizable in experiments. The paper also notes that the separation persists even with preparation and measurement noise (Appendix A.1), and that it holds even for superpolynomial-time algorithms or exponential training sets, since the underlying communication complexity bound is resource-independent.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of randomized measurement protocols (classical shadows, shadow tomography) and quantum machine learning, drawing on the Hidden Matching problem from one-way communication complexity (Bar-Yossef et al., 2004) and quantum-secure pseudorandom functions (Brakerski & Shmueli, 2019) as its two key technical pillars. It departs from prior lower-bound results (Grier et al., 2024; Aaronson et al., 2024) in two important ways: moving from worst-case to average-case analysis, and extending the separation from unphysical states to efficiently preparable ones via QPRFs. The POVM construction that outputs Hidden Matching relations is borrowed from Aaronson et al. (2024), while the PAC-style learnability framework generalizes Valiant's classical formulation to quantum randomized functions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first concrete, physically meaningful learning task where fully-quantum processing is not merely more efficient but strictly necessary, with the separation holding for efficiently preparable states and remaining robust to preparation and measurement noise. This resolves a natural question raised by the success of classical shadows and shadow tomography: whether sufficiently powerful fixed measurement strategies can universally replace adaptive quantum processing. Open questions include whether the construction generalizes beyond pseudorandom phase states to ground states of local Hamiltonians (suggested by Aaronson & Drucker, 2010) or to other computationally indistinguishable state families, and whether the separation can be tightened or made unconditional.
