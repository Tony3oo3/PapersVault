## Problem

Estimating the causal effect of simultaneously intervening on multiple variables (joint interventional effect) is critical in domains like multi-channel marketing and multi-drug treatment, but running experiments for every combination is prohibitively expensive. Nonparametric identifiability results show this is impossible in general from single-variable interventions and observational data alone, and prior parametric approaches required restrictive Gaussian additive noise assumptions. This paper asks what structural assumptions on the outcome mechanism enable identifiability while remaining broadly applicable to both discrete and continuous variables with arbitrary noise distributions.

## Input / Output

The method consumes one observational dataset and K single-intervention datasets (one per action variable), where each dataset contains pairs of the outcome Y and the full action vector A. A key constraint is that the action variables must have identical support across all interventional regimes (Assumption 1), which in practice means intervention distributions must be chosen to match the observational marginal. The output is the joint interventional effect E[Y | do(a₁,...,a_K)] for arbitrary action values, computed by evaluating a decomposed estimator with all intervention indicators set to 1. The framework also extends to mixed regimes where only a subset of actions are intervened on.

## Method

Under the assumption that the outcome is generated by an additive mechanism Y = Σ_k f_k(A_k, C_k) + U with pairwise unobserved confounders C_k, the paper proves that the joint interventional effect is identifiable from single-variable interventions and observational data. The practical estimator defines K functions f̂_k(a₁,...,a_K, R_k) where the binary indicator R_k selects between an observational mode (R_k=0, capturing confounded contribution) and an interventional mode (R_k=1, capturing the unconfounded contribution). Training fits the sum of these K functions to match the observational conditional expectation and each single-intervention conditional expectation; the joint effect is then recovered by evaluating all R_k=1, which algebraically cancels the confounding through the difference between interventional and observational modes. In experiments, the estimators are third-order polynomials regularized with ridge regression, and the method achieves RMSE close to a topline model trained directly on joint interventional data, though at roughly an order of magnitude higher sample cost.

## Results

On synthetic SCMs with 5 actions and confounders, the Intervention Generalization method achieves RMSE close to the topline model trained directly on joint interventional data, significantly outperforming both the pooled-data and observational-only baselines. The optimal ratio of single-interventional to observational data (N_sint/N_obs) falls between 0.1 and 0.9, indicating that more observational data is favorable. However, the method requires over an order of magnitude more total data points than direct joint interventional training to reach comparable accuracy, highlighting a sample-efficiency trade-off.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four estimation approaches on the joint interventional effect E[Y | do(a1,...,a5)], evaluated by RMSE on a held-out joint interventional test set. (i) The topline model, trained directly on joint interventional data, sets the lower bound on achievable error. (ii) The proposed Intervention Generalization method, trained on observational and single-intervention data, achieves RMSE close to this topline. (iii) A naive estimator trained on the pooled dataset of all observational and single-interventional data performs substantially worse than both (i) and (ii). (iv) An observational-only estimator (fitting E[Y|a1,...,a5] and using it to predict the interventional effect) performs worst of all. The key finding is that the structured decomposition into confounded and unconfounded contributions per action (the core of the proposed method) is what enables the large gap over the naive pooled-data baseline, while the gap to the topline reflects the inherent sample-efficiency cost of not having joint interventional data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper addresses the Intervention Generalization Problem (Bravo-Hermsdorff et al., 2023) by showing that joint interventional effects are identifiable from single-variable interventions and observational data under an additive outcome mechanism assumption. It departs from the most closely related work of Saengkyongam & Silva (2020), which achieves identifiability by restricting the exogenous noise to be Gaussian and additive, whereas here the restriction is placed on the causal mechanism itself (additivity of the outcome function), allowing arbitrary noise distributions and both discrete and continuous variables. The paper also extends beyond prior nonparametric identification results (g-identification, Jung et al. 2023) by demonstrating that those approaches are insufficient in the presence of unobserved confounding, motivating the parametric assumption. The additive structure connects to a long lineage in statistics (GAMs, Neural Additive Models) and to Lachapelle et al. (2023), who showed additive decoders enable Cartesian-Product Extrapolation in representation learning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result provides a principled, constructive method for estimating joint interventional effects without the prohibitively expensive joint experiments, which is directly relevant to domains like multi-channel marketing, drug combination design, and public health policy where the number of possible intervention combinations grows exponentially. However, the additivity assumption may fail in domains with synergistic interactions (e.g., ecological stressors), and the empirical results reveal a significant sample-efficiency cost—over an order of magnitude more data is needed compared to direct joint-intervention training. Open questions include identifying the minimal set of assumptions for identifiability, extending to more general function classes (e.g., post-nonlinear models), handling complex confounding structures, and establishing finite-sample guarantees.
