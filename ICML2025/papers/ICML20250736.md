## Problem

In multiplayer symmetric games (more than two players), Nash equilibria are non-unique, have distinct values, and are exploitable against non-equilibrium opponents, so standard equilibrium concepts provide no meaningful guarantee of securing a fair share. State-of-the-art AI systems for games like Mahjong, Poker, and Diplomacy rely on self-play meta-algorithms with limited theoretical support, and it remains unclear whether these approaches generalize. This paper asks: under what structural conditions can a learning agent provably secure an equal share (expected payoff C/n), and what principled algorithms achieve this?

## Input / Output

The learner knows the full game rule (the symmetric payoff function U_1) and observes the opponents' sampled actions each round; in the theoretical framework, opponents are assumed to share a common meta-strategy y^t with bounded total variation V_T across T rounds. In experiments, the meta-strategy is directly provided (idealized imitation learning), and the learner outputs a mixed strategy over its action space. The success criterion is achieving average payoff at least C/n (equal share), with error bounds that vanish as T grows.

## Method

The paper identifies a new solution concept—min_x max_{x_1} U_1(x_1, x^{⊗(n-1)}) = 0—as the unique minimax formulation that guarantees equal share, requiring opponents to play an identical strategy and the learner to adapt to them (not an equilibrium in general). For fixed opponents, the Hedge no-regret algorithm achieves equal share up to Õ(√(log A)/√T) error. For slowly adapting opponents with variation budget V_T, the Strongly Adaptive Online Learner (SAOL^H) achieves Õ(V_T^{1/3} T^{-1/3}) dynamic regret against the per-round best action, while a simple behavior-cloning strategy (mimicking the opponents' previous action) achieves O(V_T/T) error in the intermediate regime where the meta-strategy changes too fast for no-dynamic-regret to help. Matching lower bounds via hard symmetric game instances confirm these rates are near-optimal, and experiments on majority-vote and switch-dominance games show that prior self-play meta-algorithms (SP_scratch, SP_BC, SP_BC_reg) fail to secure equal share in the worst case while the proposed Hedge-based approach consistently succeeds.

## Results

On the 3-player Majority Vote (MV) game, the proposed Hedge algorithm achieves a utility of +1.03±0.10 (×10⁻²) against the fixed meta-strategy, while all self-play variants (SP_scratch, SP_BC, SP_BC_reg) achieve a negative utility of −1.00±0.09 (×10⁻²), failing to secure an equal share. On the 30-player Switch Dominance Game (SDG), Hedge achieves a utility of +1.00±0.00, whereas self-play variants achieve −12.67±0.01. On exploitability, all methods tie: both Hedge and self-play variants are equally exploitable (−1.00 in MV, −29.00 in SDG), indicating that the advantage of the proposed method lies in securing equal share rather than in robustness to adversarial opponents.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its Hedge-based algorithm against three self-play meta-algorithms from prior state-of-the-art multiplayer AI systems: SP_scratch (Brown & Sandholm, 2019, self-play from scratch), SP_BC (Li et al., 2020, self-play initialized from behavior cloning), and SP_BC_reg (Jacob et al., 2022, self-play with KL-regularization toward the meta-strategy at multiple regularization coefficients). On the MV game, Hedge consistently converges to the favorable equilibrium [0,1] (100% of runs), while all self-play variants have roughly 50% probability of converging to the unfavorable equilibrium [1,0], resulting in a negative worst-case utility of −1.00×10⁻² versus Hedge's +1.03×10⁻². On the SDG game, the gap is even more dramatic: self-play variants converge to the dominated action [0,0,1] yielding utility −12.67, while Hedge converges to [0,1,0] with utility +1.00. On exploitability, all methods perform identically (−1.00 in MV, −29.00 in SDG), meaning the self-play algorithms are no more or less exploitable than Hedge; the key differentiator is the ability to secure a non-negative payoff (equal share) against the fixed meta-strategy, where self-play fails in the worst case.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes no-regret learning tools (Hedge, SAOL) that were previously developed for finding equilibria in two-player zero-sum games, redirecting them toward the novel objective of securing an equal share in multiplayer symmetric games. It builds on the self-play meta-algorithms adopted by prior state-of-the-art multiplayer AI systems (Pluribus for Poker, Suphx for Mahjong, Diplomacy agents) but demonstrates that these frameworks are fundamentally insufficient for guaranteeing even a basic fairness baseline. The paper also draws on classical game theory (Nash, correlated, and coarse correlated equilibria) to show that none of these solution concepts provide the needed guarantees in multiplayer settings, and introduces a new minimax solution concept (min_x max_{x_1} U_1(x_1, x^{⊗(n-1)})) as the appropriate target.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This paper provides the first principled theoretical framework for multiplayer symmetric games, identifying exactly when and how a learning agent can guarantee a fair share, and showing that the self-play approach underpinning all major multiplayer AI systems is not a general-purpose solution. The practical implication is significant: current systems for Mahjong, Poker, and Diplomacy lack the theoretical guarantees this paper establishes, and their meta-algorithms can fail in worst-case scenarios. Key limitations include the restriction to normal-form games, the requirement that all opponents deploy identical strategies, and the need for the learner to model opponents. Open questions include extending the framework to extensive-form and Markov games, handling asymmetric settings, and developing algorithms that work when opponents play genuinely different strategies.
