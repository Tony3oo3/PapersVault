## Problem

Existing neural sketches (meta-sketches) rely on fixed-size MANN configurations with MLP-based embeddings, requiring full retraining whenever the data domain shifts or the space budget changes. Additionally, their accuracy advantage over handcrafted sketches diminishes at larger space budgets, limiting practical deployment across diverse streaming applications.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250638/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The Lego sketch consumes individual data stream items (from arbitrary domains such as web clicks, text edits, or document identifiers) in a single-pass streaming model, performing a constant-time Store or Query operation per item. It outputs a frequency estimate for the queried item, combining a neural prediction with a rule-based min(m_i/v_i) estimate. The memory is organized as K independent 100KB bricks (d₁=5, d₂=5120), and scaling the total budget simply increases K without any retraining.

## Method

The Lego sketch replaces the conventional MANN with a modular, Lego-brick architecture comprising five modules: a normalized multi-hash embedding (domain-agnostic via hash-based random projections with L₁ normalization), a hash-addressing scheme, a scalable memory of K independently hash-distributed bricks, a DeepSets-based memory scanning module that reconstructs global stream characteristics (distinct item count n and skewness α) from compressed storage, and an ensemble decoder that fuses neural and rule-based estimates. Training uses self-supervised meta-learning over 4 million synthetic Zipf-distributed meta-tasks, with a novel self-guided weighting loss that dynamically reweights error metrics across meta-tasks by normalizing each by the rule-based estimate's error, addressing the diminishing-advantage problem at large budgets. Theoretical analysis provides the first error bound for a neural sketch (Theorem 4.3) and proves domain-agnostic embedding distribution (Theorem 4.1) and sub-skewness preservation across memory bricks (Theorem 4.2).

## Results

On the Lkml dataset, the Lego sketch achieves an ARE of 0.93 at the smallest budget (0.6 MB) and 0.074 at the largest budget (3.6 MB), representing only 85% and 21% of the Meta-sketch error, respectively. In terms of AAE at 3.6 MB on Lkml, the Lego sketch's error drops to just 13% of the Meta-sketch. The elastic derivative variant (D-Lego) further reduces AAE to 0.03 at 3.6 MB, outperforming D-CMS by a factor of 3–5×. Throughput on CPU reaches millions of operations per second for Store and tens of millions for Query, exceeding the Meta-sketch by 6.9× (Store) and 12.47× (Query).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The Lego sketch consistently outperforms all six baselines across five real datasets (Aol, Lkml, Kosarak, Wiki, Webdocs) and six synthetic Zipf streams. Against the Meta-sketch (MS), the Lego sketch reduces ARE from 15% (at 0.6 MB) to 79% (at 3.6 MB), directly addressing the diminishing advantage of prior neural sketches at larger budgets. Compared to the handcrafted CM-sketch (CMS) and C-sketch (CS), the Lego sketch achieves ARE that is only 21–46% of CMS and 16–26% of CS across the budget range, with the gap widening at larger budgets. The learned augmented C-sketch (LCS) is outperformed across all budgets, particularly at larger spaces where LCS's reliance on prior knowledge of n becomes a liability. The elastic derivative D-Lego outperforms D-CMS by a consistent 3–5× margin in AAE, demonstrating the Lego sketch's effectiveness as a core structure for derivative frameworks. On synthetic streams with extreme skewness (α=1.5), the Lego sketch maintains robustness comparable to CMS while MS and LCS degrade significantly, with their absolute errors exceeding 8.

## Relation to Prior Work

The Lego sketch directly extends the meta-sketch (Cao et al., 2023; 2024), the first neural sketch built on a memory-augmented neural network (MANN) framework (Graves et al., 2016), by replacing its MLP-based embedding with a normalized multi-hash embedding, its fixed-size memory with a scalable multi-brick design, and its standard loss with a self-guided weighting loss. It also introduces a memory scanning module (DeepSets-based) absent from prior neural sketches, enabling reconstruction of global stream characteristics. Conceptually, the paper positions itself as resolving two specific limitations of existing neural sketches—domain/space-budget scalability requiring retraining, and diminishing accuracy advantage at larger budgets—while retaining the MANN-based core structure that distinguishes neural sketches from handcrafted ones like CM-sketch and C-sketch.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Lego sketch provides the first error bound for any neural sketch (Theorem 4.3), a theoretical gap that has persisted since the introduction of meta-sketches, though the authors acknowledge the bound is wider than handcrafted sketch guarantees due to normalization-induced dependencies. The modular, retraining-free scalability (across domains and up to 1400× memory expansion) makes neural sketches practically deployable in production settings where data distributions and space budgets shift. Open questions include tightening the error bound, extending the scalable MANN architecture to related tasks like graph stream summarization and vector compression, and whether the self-guided weighting loss generalizes beyond the Zipf-distribution meta-task training regime.
