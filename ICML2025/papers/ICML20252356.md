## Problem

Learning to rank N items from limited K-way human comparison feedback (K << N) is central to RLHF and web search, but existing optimal design methods for selecting informative K-subsets are computationally infeasible because they must optimize over O(C(N,K)) exponentially many subsets. Prior work (Mukherjee et al., 2024) only handled the special case K=N, and standard Frank-Wolfe solvers run out of memory even for N=100, K=10 (requiring 68 TB of RAM for the gradient alone).

## Input / Output

The method takes N items represented by d-dimensional feature vectors (e.g., 100-dim BERT or 1024-dim BGE-M3 embeddings), a subset size K, and a feedback budget T. It produces a sparse probability distribution over the C(N,K) possible K-subsets (stored via combinadics indexing, with at most T+1 non-zero entries) that dictates which subsets to query for human ranking feedback. After collecting T rankings under the Plackett-Luce model, a maximum likelihood estimate yields a full ranking of all N items, evaluated by normalized Kendall-tau ranking loss and NDCG.

## Method

DopeWolfe is a randomized Frank-Wolfe algorithm for solving the D-optimal design that minimizes worst-case ranking loss under the estimated Plackett-Luce model. It replaces the intractable full LMO (maximizing over all C(N,K) subsets) with a randomized LMO that samples only R subsets uniformly, reducing per-iteration cost from O(C(N,K)) to O(N²d² + R·K²). Three key efficiency mechanisms enable this: (1) memoization of all O(N²) pair gradients D_{j,k} = z_{j,k}^T V^{-1} z_{j,k} so that each subset's partial derivative is a simple sum of K²/2 cached terms; (2) low-rank Woodbury updates for the matrix inverse and log-determinant, exploiting that each update adds only a rank-K²/2 matrix, reducing cost from O(d³) to O(r³ + rd²); and (3) sparse iterate storage using the combinadics number system. The paper also provides a convergence guarantee by generalizing randomized FW analysis beyond Lipschitz smoothness to logarithmically homogeneous self-concordant barrier (LHSCB) objectives, yielding O(max(1, C(N,K)/R)·ε^{-1}) iterations.

## Results

On the Nectar dataset (30,000 lists of 7 items, K=2), DopeWolfe at T=100 achieves a lower ranking loss than all baselines (Uniform, DBSCAN, Medoids) at T=1000, representing an order-of-magnitude sample efficiency gain. On the Anthropic and Nectar datasets (Section 6.1), DopeWolfe matches Dope's ranking loss while running 3× faster (6.18s vs. 20.15s on Anthropic) and 2× faster (0.41s vs. 0.96s on Nectar). In the real-feedback experiment (N=100, K up to 10, 17 trillion subsets), DopeWolfe consistently achieves ~20% lower ranking loss than Uniform sampling across all K values. The per-iteration complexity is reduced from O(C(N,K)) to O(N²+K²), making the problem tractable at scales where the standard Frank-Wolfe method requires 68 TB of RAM.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Dope (Mukherjee et al., 2024), DopeWolfe achieves comparable ranking loss on the Anthropic and Nectar datasets but is 2–3× faster in wall-clock time, confirming that the randomized LMO and low-rank updates do not sacrifice solution quality. Against DBSCAN clustering, DopeWolfe substantially outperforms on all three synthetic datasets (BEIR-COVID, TREC-DL, Nectar) for both K=2 and K=3; DBSCAN often fails to even beat the simple Uniform baseline, and on Nectar it requires 2–8 TB of RAM. Against Beat-the-Pivot (Saha & Gopalan, 2019a), DopeWolfe achieves significantly lower ranking loss (e.g., ~0.21 NDCG for Beat-the-Pivot vs. much higher NDCG for DopeWolfe on BEIR-COVID K=2), because Beat-the-Pivot is non-contextual and cannot share information across the 30,000 lists in Nectar. Against Uniform sampling, DopeWolfe consistently wins by roughly 20% in ranking loss on real feedback (Figure 3) and by larger margins on synthetic feedback, with the gap widening at smaller sample budgets T.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the D-optimal design framework for human preference elicitation from Mukherjee et al. (2024), which assumed K=N (ranking all items from a single K-way comparison), to the more general and practically important setting of ranking N items from K-way feedback where K≪N. Algorithmically, it builds on the randomized Frank-Wolfe method of Kerdreux et al. (2018) and the LHSCB convergence analysis of Zhao & Freund (2023), combining them with memoization, low-rank Woodbury updates, and sparse iterate maintenance to eliminate the exponential O(C(N,K)) per-iteration cost that makes standard FW infeasible. The theoretical contribution generalizes the randomized FW convergence guarantee beyond Lipschitz smoothness to the broader class of logarithmically homogeneous self-concordant barrier objectives.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine computational bottleneck in RLHF reward modeling: when ranking 100 LLM responses from 10-way comparisons, the number of candidate subsets is 17 trillion, making all prior optimal design methods infeasible (requiring 68 TB of RAM). DopeWolfe reduces per-iteration complexity to O(N²+K²) and demonstrates order-of-magnitude sample efficiency gains over uniform and clustering baselines. Key limitations include the restriction to linear models over frozen embeddings (not fine-tuned LLMs), the offline setting, and the O(d²) dependence in the prediction error bound that could be tightened to O(d). Open questions include extending to online feedback collection, absolute feedback models, and validation on high-dimensional LLM embeddings used in production RLHF pipelines.
