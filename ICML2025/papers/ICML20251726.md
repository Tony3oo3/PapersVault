## Problem

Standard LLM benchmarks (AP exams, MMLU, etc.) are designed for humans, where the space of possible misunderstandings is small and structured, making a few well-chosen 'keystone' questions sufficient to certify understanding. The paper asks: what justifies transferring this logic to LLMs, whose space of possible misinterpretations may be fundamentally different from humans'? Without a formal criterion, high benchmark scores may reflect 'potemkin understanding'—the illusion of comprehension produced by answers that no human with a coherent concept representation could give.

## Input / Output

The benchmark procedure takes a concept name and task type (definition, classification, constrained generation, or editing) as a text prompt and produces a binary correct/incorrect label per response, aggregated into a potemkin rate conditioned on the model having answered the keystone (definition) question correctly. The automated procedure takes MMLU questions, and for each correctly answered question, generates 5 related follow-up questions plus a deliberately flawed variant; the same model then acts as judge on its own answers, producing a lower-bound potemkin rate. The incoherence test pairs a generation prompt with a subsequent classification prompt on the model's own output, yielding a rescaled disagreement score where 0 is perfect consistency and 1 is chance-level.

## Method

The paper formalizes conceptual understanding via interpretation functions f: X → {0,1} and defines a keystone set S as a minimal subset where correct answers on all elements in S rule out every human misconception. An LLM exhibits potemkin understanding when it answers all keystone questions correctly yet its interpretation f ≠ f*, meaning its errors on non-keystone items are irreconcilable with any human misunderstanding pattern. To measure this, the authors build a 3,159-point benchmark across 32 concepts in literary techniques, game theory, and psychological biases, using four task types (definition as keystone, classification, constrained generation, editing) with domain-expert or automated annotation. A second, fully automated procedure uses the LLM as its own judge: after answering MMLU questions correctly, the model generates related questions, answers them, and then grades its own responses (with a sycophancy control via deliberately incorrect variants), so any judge–answer disagreement constitutes a documented potemkin. An incoherence probe—generate an instance, then re-prompt the model to classify its own output—further distinguishes internally consistent but slightly misaligned understanding from genuinely incoherent concept representations.

## Results

Across 7 LLMs and 32 concepts in three domains, models define concepts correctly 94.2% of the time, yet exhibit high potemkin rates when asked to apply those concepts: 0.44 (classify), 0.40 (generate), and 0.40 (edit) overall (Table 1). The automated lower-bound procedure on MMLU yields an overall potemkin rate of 0.62 and incoherence score of 0.22 (Table 2), confirming that conceptual incoherence is pervasive. No model achieves a potemkin rate below 0.36 on any task, and the worst performers (Qwen2-VL, Llama-3.3) reach rates of 0.62–0.66 on generation and classification.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new method to compare against baselines; instead, it measures the prevalence of potemkin understanding across 7–9 LLMs. No model is immune: the lowest overall potemkin rate is Claude-3.5 (0.23 on generation, 0.29 on edit), while the highest is Qwen2-VL (0.66 on classify, 0.62 on generate). On the automated MMLU-based lower-bound procedure (Table 2), GPT-o3-mini achieves the lowest incoherence (0.02) but still shows a 0.66 lower-bound potemkin rate, while Gemini-2.0 and Qwen2-VL reach 0.82–0.86. The key finding is that even the best-performing models on conventional benchmarks exhibit substantial conceptual incoherence, with incoherence scores ranging from 0.02 to 0.64 across models and domains, indicating that no current LLM consistently applies concepts in ways reconcilable with human understanding.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new conceptual ground by formalizing a question that prior work addressed only informally: when do human-designed benchmarks validly measure LLM understanding? It repurposes the well-known observation that human exams work because human misunderstandings are structured and sparse, and introduces the keystone-set framework (F_h vs. F_l) to make the validity condition explicit. The authors position prior consistency-checking work (reversal curse, generator-validator alignment, consistency benchmarks) as 'implicit tests for potemkins' and subsume them under a unified formalism. The empirical procedures draw on LLM-as-judge prompting and expert-annotated benchmarks, but the core contribution is the theoretical framing rather than a new prompting technique.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that potemkins are ubiquitous across all seven major LLMs tested—despite 94% definition accuracy—challenges the field's default interpretation of benchmark scores as evidence of understanding and provides two concrete, reproducible tools for detecting this failure mode. Limitations include the benchmark's scope (32 concepts, 3 domains) and the fact that the automated procedure yields only a lower bound, leaving open how to systematically reduce potemkin rates during training. A key open question is whether the incoherence the paper documents (models failing to recognize their own outputs) reflects a fundamental architectural limitation or a prompt-sensitivity artifact that could be mitigated.
