## Problem

Cross-conformal prediction improves on split conformal prediction by leveraging cross-validation, but its coverage guarantee relies on the conservative 'twice the average' p-value combination rule valid under arbitrary dependence. This paper asks whether the prediction sets can be made strictly smaller while preserving the same 1−2α marginal coverage guarantee, by exploiting the exchangeability structure of the conformal p-values across folds and a simple randomization trick.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250730/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes n iid training pairs (X_i, Y_i), test covariates X_{n+1}, a miscoverage rate α, and a number of folds K (typically 5 or 10), and produces a prediction set for Y_{n+1} by combining K rank-based conformal p-values—one per fold—using either an asymmetric sequential rule or a randomized scaling factor. In regression, the resulting set may be a union of intervals; the convex hull can be taken to recover a single interval. The procedure requires training the prediction algorithm only K times, matching the cost of standard cross-conformal prediction.

## Method

The key insight is that the K conformal p-values from different folds are exchangeable (Proposition 4.1), enabling more powerful combination rules than the 'twice the average' rule valid under arbitrary dependence. Three nested variants are proposed: (1) e-mod-cross uses the exchangeable rule min_{ℓ∈[K]} (1/ℓ)Σ_{k=1}^ℓ P_k(y) > α, which is a valid p-value up to a factor of 2 under exchangeability (Gasparin et al., 2025); (2) u-mod-cross divides the average p-value by (2−U) where U~Uniform(0,1), a randomization trick that shrinks the set without requiring exchangeability; (3) eu-mod-cross combines both by taking the minimum of the randomized first p-value (1/(2−U))P_1(y) and the exchangeable sequential average. All three produce sets nested within the modified cross-conformal set while maintaining the 1−2α coverage guarantee, and the same techniques extend to the original cross-conformal prediction by adjusting the threshold to α′ = α + (1−α)(K−1)/(K+n).

## Results

On the Online News Popularity dataset (n=39,797, p=55, K=10, α=0.1), eu-mod-cross achieves mean empirical coverage of 0.852 (linear regression), 0.845 (lasso), and 0.847 (random forest), lying between the guaranteed 1−2α=0.8 and the nominal 1−α=0.9, while producing significantly smaller prediction sets than cross-conformal prediction (coverage ≈0.90) and mod-cross (coverage ≈0.90). In the simulation study (n=100, K=5, α=0.1), eu-mod-cross consistently yields the smallest prediction sets across all values of p, with coverage stable between 0.8 and 0.9. On the Electricity Consumption dataset (n=35,411, K=10), eu-mod-cross achieves a mean set size of 32.27 versus 50.71 for cross-conformal and 59.59 for split conformal, while the Bonferroni-based combination produces sets of size 196.68, confirming that mean-based combination is far more powerful under dependence.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to cross-conformal prediction (Vovk, 2015), the proposed eu-mod-cross produces strictly smaller prediction sets (Theorem 4.9) while maintaining the same worst-case coverage guarantee of ≥1−2α; empirically, cross-conformal overcovers at ≈1−α (0.90) while eu-mod-cross sits closer to the guaranteed floor (0.85), reflecting its greater statistical efficiency. Against split conformal prediction (Lei et al., 2018) trained at level α, eu-mod-cross is smaller in most settings; in the simulation, for p∈[25,60] where the algorithm is unstable, eu-mod-cross even beats split conformal trained at the more conservative level 2α. Versus jackknife+ (Barber et al., 2021), both methods guarantee ≥1−2α coverage, but eu-mod-cross requires only K model trainings (e.g., K=5 or 10) compared to n trainings for jackknife+, making it substantially cheaper for large n; empirically their set sizes are comparable, with eu-mod-cross sometimes smaller. Full conformal prediction (Vovk et al., 2005) provides a stronger ≥1−α guarantee but at a cost of n_grid model trainings, making it infeasible for large datasets; the proposed methods offer a practical middle ground with K trainings and a valid (if weaker) finite-sample guarantee. The Bonferroni combination rule, tested on the Electricity dataset, produces sets roughly 4× wider than eu-mod-cross (196.68 vs. 32.27), confirming that mean-based p-value combination is far more appropriate for the highly dependent conformal p-values across folds.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Vovk's cross-conformal prediction (2015) and its modified variant (Vovk et al., 2018; Barber et al., 2021) by replacing the conservative 'twice the average' p-value combination rule (valid under arbitrary dependence) with sharper rules that exploit exchangeability of fold-level p-values and/or a simple randomization trick. The theoretical foundation draws directly on Gasparin et al. (2025), which derived more powerful combination rules for exchangeable and randomized p-values using extensions of Markov's inequality. The paper positions itself as a purely statistical-efficiency improvement: the same K-fold training cost, the same worst-case coverage guarantee of 1−2α, but smaller prediction sets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is that practitioners can obtain meaningfully narrower prediction intervals (often 10–30% smaller in the experiments) at no additional computational cost, which matters in settings where full conformal or jackknife+ are infeasible. An open question the paper highlights is the trade-off between reproducibility and efficiency: the randomized variants (u-mod-cross, eu-mod-cross) are more efficient but introduce extra randomness, and the authors note this is particularly relevant in industrial pipelines making thousands of predictions daily. The paper also leaves open the question of which variant to choose in practice, offering a heuristic (use α/2 if worst-case guarantees are critical, use α if conformal prediction is 'weak guidance'), but a principled selection rule remains unresolved.
