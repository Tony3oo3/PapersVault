## Problem

Adaptive ATE estimation has been studied primarily through asymptotic lenses that obscure finite-sample exploration-exploitation tradeoffs, or through nonasymptotic analyses that rely on the suboptimal IPW estimator. Existing clipping-based algorithms (ClipSDT, ClipSMT, ClipOGD) under-exploit due to the inherent asymmetry of the Neyman loss, and their regret guarantees are defined against a weaker baseline (minimum-variance IPW) rather than the true semiparametric efficiency bound. This paper asks: can we design an adaptive algorithm that achieves logarithmic Neyman regret against the stronger, estimator-and-allocation-agnostic baseline while also adapting to problem difficulty?

## Input / Output

At each round t, the algorithm consumes the full history of past (allocation, treatment, outcome) triples to produce two outputs: a treatment allocation probability π_t ∈ [0,1] (used to sample the next unit's treatment) and an ATE estimate h_t computed via the A2IPW formula with the current sample-mean reward estimates. The allocation is not a point estimate but is derived by projecting a time-uniform confidence sequence for the Neyman allocation onto the point closest to 1/2, making the output inherently sequential and data-dependent. The final ATE estimate is the average of the per-round A2IPW values over T rounds.

## Method

OPTrack (Optimistic Policy Tracking) implements the principle of optimism in the face of uncertainty, borrowed from multi-armed bandit theory, in the context of adaptive ATE estimation. Rather than clipping an empirical Neyman allocation estimate (as prior work does), the algorithm constructs time-uniform confidence sequences for each arm's standard deviation via martingale-based concentration inequalities, combines them into a confidence sequence for the Neyman allocation π*, and then plays the allocation in that confidence set that is closest to 1/2. This 'optimistic' choice is principled: when π* is near 1/2, exploration and exploitation objectives align, so acting as if the problem is the easiest feasible instance accelerates convergence. The A2IPW estimator with sample-mean reward estimates provides the per-round ATE estimate. The key theoretical result is a logarithmic Neyman regret bound Õ(Δ_σ^{-2} + (1/π̲*)² log T) against the stronger baseline, whereas prior clipping-based methods incur linear regret under this definition, and the exploration phase length adapts to the problem gap Δ_σ.

## Results

On the macro-insurance dataset, OPTrack achieves a normalized MSE of 0.103 at T=100, compared to 0.117 for ClipSDT (a ~12% improvement), and 0.092 for the full oracle with knowledge of both π* and r*. At T=1500, OPTrack reaches 0.092, matching the oracle's 0.091. On six synthetic Bernoulli problem instances (500,000 simulations each), OPTrack consistently outperforms ClipSDT by 10–15% in normalized MSE (T·MSE) for small-to-moderate T, with the gap closing as T grows. Theoretically, OPTrack achieves logarithmic Neyman regret (Theorem 5.3), whereas prior nonasymptotic methods (ClipSMT, ClipOGD) incur linear Neyman regret under the paper's stronger regret definition.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary empirical baseline is ClipSDT (Cook et al., 2024), a clipping-based adaptive allocation method. OPTrack outperforms ClipSDT on every problem instance and every sample size tested: on the macro-insurance dataset, the MSE gap is 0.014 at T=100 (0.103 vs. 0.117) and narrows to 0.003 by T=1500. On the six synthetic Bernoulli instances, OPTrack delivers a consistent 10–15% reduction in normalized MSE over ClipSDT for T ≤ 500, with the advantage diminishing as T grows since both algorithms converge to the Neyman allocation. OPTrack is also competitive with the reward-estimation oracle (which knows π* but estimates r*) and even surpasses it on some synthetic instances due to its more exploratory early-phase allocation. The paper notes that ClipSMT and ClipOGD are excluded from experiments because their variances fail to converge to the oracle variance (linear Neyman regret), making direct comparison uninformative.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the A2IPW estimator from Kato et al. (2020) and the nonasymptotic Neyman regret framework from Neopane et al. (2025), but departs from both in critical ways. Prior nonasymptotic work (ClipOGD, ClipSMT) relies on the suboptimal IPW estimator and defines regret against the minimum-variance IPW baseline, whereas this paper uses the doubly-robust A2IPW estimator and defines a strictly stronger regret against the minimum attainable variance over all estimator-allocation pairs. The algorithmic design principle of optimism in the face of uncertainty, borrowed from multi-armed bandit literature, is repurposed here to resolve the exploration-exploitation tradeoff in adaptive allocation, replacing the clipping heuristics used by all prior approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first nonasymptotic guarantee with logarithmic Neyman regret under the stronger doubly-robust baseline, and reveals that previously published 'logarithmic regret' algorithms actually incur linear regret under this more meaningful definition. The optimism-based design principle offers a general algorithmic template that could extend to covariate-adjusted settings, multi-arm experiments, and reinforcement learning, though the authors note the multi-arm extension introduces an unfavorable K-factor. For practitioners running randomized clinical trials with limited sample sizes, the 10-15% MSE improvement over ClipSDT in the small-sample regime is practically relevant, and the algorithm's ability to match or beat an infeasible oracle baseline on some instances suggests the exploration strategy is near-optimal.
