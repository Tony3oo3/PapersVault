## Problem

Formal logic-based reasoning in NLP can produce conclusions that factually contradict the premises when applied rigidly to natural language, yet humans typically reject such conclusions using common sense and factual knowledge. These 'rulebreaker' scenarios have been studied in cognitive science but have received no systematic evaluation in NLP. The paper asks whether LLMs can recognize and respond to rulebreakers in a knowledge-informed, human-like manner rather than over-rigidly applying logical rules.

## Input / Output

Each prompt presents two natural language premises and a proposed conclusion, followed by a yes/no or true/false question (e.g., 'Do the Premises entail the Conclusion?'). Every instance is evaluated across 10 phrasing variations (5 question templates × 2 answer-option pairs) to control for prompt sensitivity. Beyond the single-token answer, the full output probability distribution is extracted to compute model confidence in positive answers, enabling fine-grained analysis of latent discrimination ability even when the argmax token is incorrect.

## Method

The paper constructs RULEBREAKERS, a 25,600-instance dataset of minimally differing rulebreaker/non-rulebreaker pairs generated from four templates based on modus tollens and disjunctive syllogism, populated with geographical (country–city from WikiData) and categorical (type–instance from ConceptNet) entity pairs. Non-rulebreakers are created by swapping a single entity so that the conclusion becomes factually consistent with the premises. Evaluation uses paired accuracy (both members of a pair must be answered correctly), rulebreaker- and non-rulebreaker-specific accuracy, and a confidence metric comparing mean token probability of positive answers across the two sets. Analysis employs feature attribution (input × gradient and attention scores) to measure relative importance assigned to the factual second premise versus the logical first premise, and probes entity familiarity via completion probability to test whether knowledge confidence predicts rulebreaker recognition.

## Results

On RULEBREAKERS (25,600 instances), paired accuracy (τ) ranges from 7.14% (Gemma-2-27b-it) to 60.92% (Meta-Llama-3-8B-Instruct), with only the latter exceeding the 0.6 threshold. All models achieve near-perfect non-rulebreaker accuracy (86.5–100%), but rulebreaker accuracy is substantially lower (7.1–73.6%), indicating a systematic tendency to over-rigidly apply logical rules. GPT-4o, despite being the largest model, underperforms several smaller open-source models (e.g., scoring only 15.06% paired accuracy vs. 60.92% for Meta-Llama-3-8B-Instruct) and achieves a near-zero 0.22% on the MT categorical-entity subset. Model confidence analysis (Table 2) shows all models are significantly more confident in positive answers to non-rulebreakers than rulebreakers (p<0.0001), revealing a latent but underutilized ability to distinguish the two.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares seven LLMs (six open-source + GPT-4o) on the same RULEBREAKERS benchmark. Meta-Llama-3-8B-Instruct is the clear winner on paired accuracy (60.92%), outperforming Meta-Llama-3-70B-Instruct (49.71%) by over 11 points despite being ~9× smaller, and GPT-4o (15.06%) by ~46 points. GPT-4o and Gemma-2-27b-it achieve perfect non-rulebreaker accuracy (100%) but the worst rulebreaker accuracy (15.06% and 7.14% respectively), demonstrating the most extreme over-rigid application of logical rules. Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 (47.64%) and Meta-Llama-3-70B-Instruct (49.71%) occupy the middle tier, while the Phi-3 models (20.75% and 29.19%) lag behind. The key finding is that no model achieves human-like behavior: all models accept factually contradictory conclusions in rulebreaker cases far more often than typical human reasoners would, with the gap between non-rulebreaker and rulebreaker accuracy (13–93 points) being the central diagnostic of this failure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper draws on cognitive science literature on mental models (Johnson-Laird, 1983; Quelhas et al., 2010; Quelhas & Johnson-Laird, 2017) that identified 'rulebreaker' scenarios where humans reject logically-valid but factually-inconsistent conclusions, and repurposes this concept for systematic LLM evaluation. It departs fundamentally from prior NLP logical reasoning benchmarks (LogicNLI, ProntoQA, LogicBench, FOLIO) that test whether models can apply rules regardless of semantic content; RULEBREAKERS instead tests whether models can recognize when rigid rule application should be overridden by world knowledge. The paper also positions itself as a counterbalance to a growing body of work that uses formal logic to enhance LLMs (Wang et al., 2022; Olausson et al., 2023; Pan et al., 2023; Weir et al., 2024), warning that such approaches may increase divergence from human-like reasoning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that models excelling at non-rulebreakers (rigid rule application) simultaneously fail worst on rulebreakers reveals a fundamental tension in current LLMs: robustness to semantic content and human-like knowledge-informed reasoning appear to be in conflict. The confidence analysis showing all models have higher token-level confidence on correct non-rulebreaker answers than incorrect rulebreaker answers suggests the latent ability exists but is not expressed in final outputs, pointing to a decoding or decision-boundary problem rather than a knowledge deficit. Limitations include coverage of only two propositional logic rules (MT, DS) and two entity-pair types, and the attention/familiarity analyses yield only partial, non-universal patterns. Open questions include whether logic-enhanced models (fine-tuned on logic data, integrated with symbolic solvers) fare any better, and whether the over-rigid rule application observed here generalizes beyond these specific inference patterns.
