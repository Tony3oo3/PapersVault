## Problem

Benchmarking quantum reinforcement learning (QRL) against classical RL lacks a statistically rigorous methodology: prior studies typically report learning curves from only 5 seeds with inconsistent statistical ranges, making claims of quantum superiority potentially misleading. No consensus exists on proper statistical validation metrics for RL in general, and the additional randomness sources in QRL (shot noise, hardware imperfections) further complicate fair comparison. This paper asks: how do we meaningfully assess whether a QRL agent outperforms its classical counterpart, and what does 'outperform' mean in the context of quantum advantage?

## Input / Output

The RL agent receives a 3-dimensional observation (previous antenna index, previous codebook element, and received beam intensity, all normalized to [0,1]) and outputs an antenna selection from a discrete action set. For the benchmarking estimator, the input is the full validation trajectory (evaluated expected return at each of 100 epochs) from N=100 independently seeded training runs, parameterized by a relative error threshold ε∈[0,1] and a probability threshold δ∈(0,1]. The output is an empirical sample complexity estimate Ŝ (number of environment interactions to exceed threshold 1−ε with probability δ) with 5th/95th percentile error bars from cluster resampling, plus a binary outperformance verdict based on non-overlapping extended interdecile ranges.

## Method

The paper introduces a statistical estimator for sample complexity (Definition 4.1) that counts time steps where the empirical probability P̂_t of exceeding a performance threshold falls below a confidence level δ, computed from N=100 i.i.d. training runs; they prove consistency and asymptotic unbiasedness of this estimator. They define 'significant outperformance' (Definition 4.2) as non-overlapping 5th–95th percentile ranges (extended interdecile ranges) estimated via cluster resampling to capture temporal correlations in learning curves. The experimental platform is a novel BeamManagement6G environment inspired by 6G wireless beam management, where an agent selects the optimal base station given only previous-step observations, with flexible complexity controlled by antenna count and trajectory support points. They compare DDQN and PPO with classical DNNs against hybrid classical-quantum models (a variational quantum circuit with 10–14 qubits sandwiched between two small fully-connected layers), performing 100 training runs per configuration across multiple model sizes and ε-δ settings, requiring ~40,000 core hours of compute.

## Results

On the BeamManagement6G environment (3 antennas, 200k interactions), the hybrid quantum DDQN model (14 qubits, 4 layers, ~538 trainable parameters) significantly outperforms the similar-sized classical DNN (width 16, depth 2, 387 parameters) in empirical sample complexity across a wide range of ε-δ threshold configurations, while remaining competitive with the 10× larger classical DNN (width 64, depth 2, 4,611 parameters). All comparisons are based on 100 training runs per configuration with cluster-resampled 5th/95th percentile error bars. On CartPole-v1, the best quantum setup (4 qubits, single layer) performs competitively with the best classical model across nearly all ε-δ configurations, though the gap is not always statistically significant due to overlapping confidence bounds.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares hybrid quantum-classical models (VQC between two small classical FC layers) against purely classical DNNs of varying sizes, using DDQN and PPO as the RL algorithms. The 14-qubit hybrid quantum model (~538 params) significantly outperforms the similar-sized 387-parameter classical DNN in sample complexity, with non-overlapping 5th/95th percentile ranges confirming statistical significance per the paper's Definition 4.2. The quantum model is competitive with the 4,611-parameter large DNN (10× more parameters), though the large DNN retains a slight edge in some ε-δ configurations. The medium DNN (1,283 params) performs slightly worse than the large quantum model for most but not all threshold configurations. On CartPole-v1, the 4-qubit quantum model matches the best classical model across nearly all ε-δ settings, but the paper cautions that the performance gap is not always statistically significant. The authors explicitly state these results do not constitute proof of quantum advantage, since the classical baseline may not be optimally tuned and classical intractability at scale is not established.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the sample complexity framework from Kearns & Singh (1999) and Kakade (2003), extending it from algorithms with convergence guarantees to heuristic RL methods via a statistical estimator. It departs from prior QRL benchmarking (Chen et al., 2020; Skolik et al., 2022; Jerbi et al., 2021), which typically relied on 5-seed visual comparisons of learning curves, by introducing a rigorous estimator with cluster-resampling-based significance testing and a formal definition of outperformance. The hybrid classical-quantum architecture follows the VQC-as-function-approximator paradigm (Bharti et al., 2022), while the statistical methodology draws on the reproducibility standards advocated by Henderson et al. (2018) and Agarwal et al. (2021) for classical RL.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper casts doubt on several prior claims of QRL superiority by showing that with statistically robust evaluation (100 runs vs. the field-typical 5), the quantum advantage is more nuanced: hybrid quantum models outperform similar-sized classical networks but are competitive with, not superior to, 3–10× larger classical models. The BeamManagement6G environment is specifically designed to scale beyond classical simulatability, addressing a key limitation of existing benchmarks. Open questions include whether the observed quantum trends persist at larger qubit counts (limited by trainability/barren plateau concerns) and whether the methodology generalizes to tasks where the optimal value is unknown. The paper explicitly cautions that no definitive statement on quantum advantage can be made without proving classical intractability at scale.
