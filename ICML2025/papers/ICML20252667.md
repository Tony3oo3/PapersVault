## Problem

Softmax probabilities from trained DNNs are overconfident on misclassified samples, and popular post-hoc calibration methods have been shown to be detrimental for failure prediction. While pointwise information-theoretic measures (PMI, PVI, PSI) have been applied individually in related contexts, no systematic comparison of their theoretical properties and empirical performance as confidence estimators exists. This paper fills that gap by deriving invariance, convergence-rate, and geometric properties for all three measures and evaluating them on benchmark vision tasks.

## Input / Output

For PMI and PSI, the method consumes penultimate-layer feature vectors from a trained network along with predicted class labels; for PVI, it uses the original input images (and all-zero null inputs of the same shape) processed by a same-architecture network. All three measures produce per-sample scalar values that are normalized to [0,1] via a softmax function with temperature scaling, yielding confidence scores directly comparable to standard calibration and failure-prediction metrics. The PVI estimator additionally requires training a second network on null inputs to estimate the baseline V-entropy, while PMI uses a shallow 2-layer variational JS-bound critic and PSI uses a Gaussian projection model with 500 random directions.

## Method

The paper proposes using three pointwise information measures—PMI (log joint-to-marginal density ratio), PVI (difference in V-entropy with and without input), and PSI (average PMI over random 1D projections)—as post-hoc confidence scores, each estimated with a lightweight auxiliary model without modifying the original network's architecture or training. A key theoretical contribution is a comparative analysis showing PMI is invariant to homeomorphisms, PVI to invertible linear maps, and PSI to rotations and scales, with convergence rates that favor PSI over PMI in dimension but make PVI's rate depend on the Rademacher complexity of the predictive family. The central insight is that PVI strikes the best balance for confidence estimation: it is invariant to the linear transformations neural networks naturally induce across random initializations, avoids PMI's overly broad homeomorphic invariance (which can be counterproductive since non-linearity between features and labels signals lower confidence), and achieves superior failure prediction (AUROC, AUPR, FPR95, E-AURC) while matching temperature-scaled softmax on calibration metrics.

## Results

PVI consistently outperforms PMI, PSI, and all six post-hoc baselines (MSP, SM, ML, LM, NE, NG) in failure prediction across all seven datasets. The gains are most pronounced on larger models and complex datasets: on ResNet50/CIFAR-10, PVI achieves AUROC_f of 95.14 and E-AURC of 2.72×10⁻³, compared to MSP's 93.59 and 3.52×10⁻³; on ResNet101/CIFAR-100, PVI reaches AUPR_{f,error} of 71.54×10⁻² versus MSP's 60.83×10⁻². For confidence calibration, PVI matches temperature-scaled softmax (MSP) across SCE, CC-SCE, Ada-SCE, and RMS-SCE, confirming that PVI achieves superior failure prediction without compromising calibration quality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PVI dominates all six post-hoc baselines (MSP, SM, ML, LM, NE, NG) on failure prediction across every dataset, with the largest margins on complex, high-class-count benchmarks. On ResNet101/CIFAR-100, PVI improves AUPR_{f,error} by ~10.7 points over the best baseline (LM at 59.40) and reduces FPR95_f by ~18 points (47.17 vs. 65.23). On DenseNet121/TinyImageNet, PVI's E-AURC of 35.59×10⁻³ is ~8×10⁻³ lower than the best baseline (SM at 43.50×10⁻³). Among the three PI measures, PVI outperforms both PMI and PSI on failure prediction; PSI is consistently the weakest of the three, while PMI is competitive only on simpler datasets like MNIST (AUROC_f 97.13 vs. PVI's 96.94). For confidence calibration, PVI matches MSP (temperature-scaled softmax) on all four metrics (SCE, CC-SCE, Ada-SCE, RMS-SCE), confirming that its failure-prediction advantage comes without a calibration trade-off. PMI is omitted for CIFAR-100, Stanford Dogs, and TinyImageNet due to prohibitive computational cost, further limiting its practical applicability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies three pointwise information-theoretic measures—PMI (from Tsai et al., 2020), PSI (from Wongso et al., 2023b), and PVI (from Ethayarajh et al., 2022, originally for dataset difficulty; Xu et al., 2020 for the aggregate V-information framework)—under a common confidence-estimation lens. It departs from prior work by (1) extending PVI from its original dataset-difficulty application to post-hoc confidence scoring, (2) providing the first systematic theoretical comparison of invariance, convergence rates, and margin sensitivity across all three pointwise measures, and (3) evaluating them under identical experimental protocols against standard post-hoc baselines (MSP, SM, ML, LM, NE, NG) across seven benchmark vision tasks. The paper positions itself as a natural extension of Wongso et al. (2023b), which introduced PSI for confidence and explainability but lacked the comparative and theoretical depth provided here.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that PVI offers a post-hoc confidence score that improves failure prediction (especially on larger models and harder datasets like CIFAR-100 and TinyImageNet) without sacrificing calibration quality relative to temperature-scaled softmax—a combination that prior calibration methods have been shown to fail at achieving simultaneously (Zhu et al., 2022). The conceptual finding that margin sensitivity (strongest in PSI) does not translate to better failure prediction challenges the intuitive assumption that tracking decision-boundary proximity is what makes a good confidence signal. Open questions include whether PVI can be integrated into ensemble or dropout-based methods, whether it extends to other modalities (audio, tabular), and whether more efficient estimators can reduce the overhead of training auxiliary networks. The computational cost of PMI for high-class-count datasets and the need for additional model training for all three PI measures remain practical limitations.
