## Problem

Traditional ML evaluation paradigms built on IID test-train splits and static benchmarks are fundamentally insufficient for GenAI, whose models have unbounded input/output spaces, lack well-defined ground truth, and are subject to severe data leakage and contamination. The paper argues the field needs novelty-centric generalization—performing well on tasks truly never seen before—rather than IID generalization, making leakage the dominant and most difficult evaluation risk to address.

## Input / Output

As a position paper proposing an evaluation framework rather than a model, the 'input' is GenAI models or systems submitted by independent teams to isolated, secure backends without network access, and the 'output' is comparative rankings on time-bound novel tasks. The evaluation structure guarantees that each participating model encounters the task for the first time at evaluation, with test data either not yet existing (prospective ground truth), freshly generated by domain experts, or collected only after the submission deadline.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is arguing that AI Competitions (Kaggle-style) provide the structural solution to GenAI evaluation through three mechanisms: (1) a MapReduce-like parallelized structure where thousands of independent teams evaluate simultaneously on the same novel task, ensuring novelty for all models at test time and enabling direct apples-to-apples comparison; (2) leak-proof competition designs including prospective ground truth (CAFA 5 protein function prediction), novel task generation by domain experts (AI Mathematical Olympiad), and post-deadline data collection (Konwinski Prize, WSDM Cup); and (3) a field-wide rule that any evaluation is considered 'leaked' the moment test data is shared online or sent over the wire, explicitly prioritizing robustness over reproducibility. The paper recommends replacing static benchmarks with evergreen repeatable processes, treating the steady stream of AI Competitions as a renewable pipeline of novel evaluation tasks, and conducting meta-analyses across competition results analogous to those valued in medicine.

## Results

This is a position paper without a standard quantitative results table. The key empirical observations cited in support of the argument include: (1) Roelofs et al. (2019a) showing that Kaggle public leaderboard rankings were a remarkably good indicator of private holdout rankings despite thousands of teams, suggesting overfitting was not the main issue in traditional ML; (2) the median number of forum messages per Kaggle featured competition was 1,400, illustrating the transparency and knowledge-sharing culture; and (3) in the OpenVaccine Challenge, competitors improved state-of-the-art mRNA vaccine degradation rate prediction by 25% within 4 weeks, with hosts validating generalization to longer RNA sequences.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare methods in the traditional benchmark sense but instead contrasts five evaluation paradigms for GenAI: static benchmarks, unreleased holdout sets (e.g., SEAL, ARC-AGI, FrontierMath), dynamic benchmarks (e.g., LiveBench, LiveCodeBench, SWE-Bench), community benchmarks (e.g., LM Arena), and AI Competitions. The authors argue that static benchmarks are effectively invalidated once published due to contamination risk. Unreleased holdout sets mitigate leakage but sacrifice reproducibility and require trust in API providers. Dynamic benchmarks improve reproducibility by releasing older data but still use publicly available internet data, failing the paper's 'leaked the moment shared online' rule of thumb. Community benchmarks provide a constant stream of novel inputs but scale poorly for specialized tasks and are subject to voter sampling bias. AI Competitions are positioned as superior because they combine parallelized evaluation (thousands of teams tested simultaneously on the same novel task), structural leak-proofing (prospective ground truth, post-deadline data collection, isolated code execution), and built-in transparency (open code sharing, forum discussion), making them the 'gold standard' for empirical rigor.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper departs from the foundational IID-based evaluation paradigm (Mitchell, 1997; Vapnik, 1999) that has underpinned ML benchmarking for decades, arguing it is 'broken beyond repair' for GenAI. It builds on the novelty-based generalization concept from Chollet (2019) and Dennett (1991), and on the growing contamination literature (Magar & Schwartz, 2022; Oren et al., 2023), but its key conceptual move is repurposing the AI Competition structure—originally designed to prevent cheating among thousands of parallel teams—as a general-purpose evaluation methodology. It positions itself against existing leak-mitigation benchmarks (LiveBench, SWE-Bench, ARC-AGI, LM Arena) by arguing none fully solve the reproducibility-robustness tension that the paper frames as fundamental.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central claim—that a published static benchmark is effectively invalidated the moment it is shared, and that reproducibility and robustness are fundamentally in tension for GenAI—challenges the field's deep institutional investment in static benchmarks and could reshape how evaluations are designed and valued. It calls for meta-analyses of competition results (common in medicine, rare in ML) and for field-wide standards on anti-contamination practices for API-based models, both of which remain open. Limitations include the inherent difficulty of applying competition structures to domains lacking natural task streams, the trust assumptions required for proprietary API models, and the fact that the paper is a position argument without new empirical evidence beyond illustrative case studies.
