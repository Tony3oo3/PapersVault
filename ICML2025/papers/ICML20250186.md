## Problem

No existing theoretical framework simultaneously captures the joint effects of network width, depth, dataset size, and feature-learning richness on the training dynamics of randomly initialized deep networks. In particular, no prior theory can characterize both the monotonic 'wider is better' improvement and the approximately stable optimal learning rate across model scales that are necessary for successful hyperparameter transfer, while also distinguishing between NTK and mean-field/μP parameterizations.

## Input / Output

The theory takes as input a network architecture specification (depth L, width N, parameterization choice via γ₀ scaling, and for residual nets a branch multiplier β), data statistics (isotropic or power-law covariance with exponents α, β), and training hyperparameters (learning rate η, dataset size P = αD, or SGD batch size B = α_B D). It outputs a closed set of scalar stochastic integro-differential equations whose solutions yield per-layer correlation and response functions from which train loss, test loss, power-law scaling exponents, and optimal learning rate curves are directly read off. Crucially, the number of order parameters scales only with the number of training steps T and layers L, not with D, N, or P, making the theory tractable in the joint proportional limit where all three dimensions grow together.

## Method

The paper develops a dynamical mean field theory (DMFT) tailored to deep linear networks by exploiting the fact that sums over training samples and neuron indices can be reordered in the linear case, enabling a joint proportional limit (P, D, N → ∞ with fixed ratios α = P/D, ν = N/D) rather than the standard N → ∞ at fixed P, D. The derivation proceeds via a Martin-Siggia-Rose path integral over the full training trajectory, followed by Gaussian disorder averages over the data matrix, label noise, and random initial weights at every layer, and a saddle-point evaluation in the D → ∞ limit. This yields single-site scalar stochastic processes for the forward (h^ℓ) and backward (g^ℓ) variables at each layer, driven by colored Gaussian noise and causal memory kernels built from response functions that are computed as functional derivatives of the single-site variables with respect to their noise sources. For residual networks, the residual stream introduces statistical dependence across layers, requiring a larger set of cross-layer correlation and response functions; scaling the branch multiplier as β = β₀/√L then admits a well-defined L → ∞ limit where the residual stream is described by a stochastic process over continuous layer time τ = ℓ/L driven by Brownian motion. The theory is further extended to structured (power-law) covariates via a dimension-free mean field approximation that captures accelerated convergence exponents for hard tasks.

## Results

The paper's headline quantitative result is the power law scaling exponent for deep linear networks on structured data: in the infinite-width feature learning regime, the test loss scales as L(t) ~ t^(-2β/(1+β)) for hard tasks (β < 1) and L(t) ~ t^(-β) for easy tasks (β ≥ 1), matching the one-hidden-layer result of Bordelon et al. (2024b) and extending it to arbitrary depth L. The DMFT also provides a closed-form decomposition of test loss into bias, label noise, limited-width variance (scaling as 1/(νγ₀²)), and limited-data variance (scaling as 1/α), where ν = N/D and α = P/D. For learning rate transfer, the theory predicts that the optimal learning rate is approximately constant across width in μP parameterization (γ₀ = Θ(1)) but increases as O(ν^(1-1/L)) in NTK parameterization (γ₀ = Θ(ν^(-1/2))) for very rich dynamics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper without traditional method-vs-method benchmark comparisons; instead, it validates its DMFT predictions against numerical simulations of finite-width networks and contrasts different parameterization choices. The key comparison is between NTK and μP parameterizations: in NTK scaling, the effective feature learning rate decreases as 1/√ν with width, causing the optimal learning rate to shift rightward (failing hyperparameter transfer), while in μP scaling the optimal LR is preserved across widths. For residual networks, keeping the branch multiplier β = Θ(1) constant causes the optimal LR to shift by several orders of magnitude as depth L increases, whereas the depth-μP scaling β = β₀/√L maintains a well-defined limit and preserves LR transfer across depths. The theory also recovers the accelerated power law exponent from Bordelon et al. (2024b) for the L=1 case and shows it is universal across depths L ≥ 1 for hard tasks. The DMFT predictions are validated against simulations for L=4 networks across various widths, depths, batch sizes, and data quantities, with the dimension-free approximation becoming accurate around N, B ~ 16 for power law eigenspectra.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the dynamical mean field theory (DMFT) framework of Bordelon & Pehlevan (2022) to deep linear networks by exploiting the reordering of sums over neurons and data points to take a joint proportional limit (P, D, N → ∞ with fixed ratios), which prior DMFT approaches could not handle at large dataset sizes. It builds on the μP parameterization of Yang et al. (2021) and the depth-μP scaling of Bordelon et al. (2024c) to provide a first-principles derivation of why hyperparameter transfer succeeds or fails across widths and depths. The structured data analysis recovers and extends the accelerated power law exponents of Bordelon et al. (2024b) from one-hidden-layer to arbitrary-depth linear networks, suggesting architectural universality in the hard-task regime.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first theory to simultaneously characterize the effects of width, depth, dataset size, and richness on training dynamics from random initialization, providing a minimal model that explains both the 'wider is better' effect and learning rate transfer in μP while their failure in NTK parameterization. The practical impact is significant for hyperparameter search: the theory predicts optimal learning rates across model scales without empirical tuning. Key limitations include restriction to linear networks (no nonlinear function approximation), computational cost scaling as L² for residual networks, and the open question of whether edge-of-stability effects in discrete time spoil the predicted power law scaling at long training times. Extending the framework to nonlinearities, normalization layers, and learning rate schedules are natural next steps.
