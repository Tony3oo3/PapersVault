## Problem

While transformers demonstrably exploit long-range correlations in natural language, the specific computational strategy they implement via attention remains largely opaque, especially for structured data with hierarchical dependencies. Prior work on context-free grammars suggested transformers approximate dynamic programming algorithms but failed to identify a fully plausible implementation within the standard architecture. This paper asks: how do transformers sequentially discover and embed hierarchical correlations during training, and can the exact Belief Propagation inference algorithm be concretely realized within a vanilla encoder-only transformer?

## Input / Output

The input is a fixed-length sequence of 2^ℓ discrete symbols (e.g., 16 tokens from a vocabulary of q=4) generated by a binary tree-based probabilistic process with a tunable filtering parameter k that truncates correlations at depth k. For root classification, the entire sequence is fed to the encoder and a linear readout over the concatenated final-layer tokens produces a q-dimensional probability distribution over the root symbol. For MLM, a single randomly chosen position is masked, and the corresponding final-layer token is passed to a linear readout producing a q-dimensional distribution over the masked symbol's value. The filtering parameter k∈{0,…,ℓ} controls the effective correlation range: k=0 gives full hierarchical structure, while k=ℓ yields conditionally independent leaves given the root.

## Method

The paper introduces a hierarchical generative model on fixed binary trees where a filtering parameter k truncates correlations at a chosen depth, enabling controlled comparison against Belief Propagation oracles (BP_k) at each level. Vanilla encoder-only transformers with n_L=ℓ attention layers are trained via SGD on root classification (supervised) and single-symbol MLM (self-supervised), and their predictions are compared to exact BP marginals via KL divergence, accuracy, and scatter plots. The key empirical finding is that transformers sequentially resolve correlations from short-range to long-range during training, with attention maps developing block structures of size ~2^(ℓ−k) that mirror the generative tree hierarchy. Probing experiments on frozen encoder layers confirm that ancestor information at level m becomes available at transformer layer m, consistent with a bottom-up BP implementation. Finally, the paper provides an existence proof that BP can be embedded within exactly ℓ transformer layers by storing O(q²) auxiliary message slots per token, allowing the downward pass to be computed in parallel with the upward climb rather than requiring 2ℓ layers.

## Results

On the fully hierarchical model (ℓ=4, q=4, k=0), encoder-only transformers achieve 100% in-sample root classification accuracy with P ≥ 2^17 labeled examples, exactly matching the Belief Propagation (BP) oracle. For masked language modeling with P=2^18 sequences, transformers likewise reach the BP_0 optimal accuracy, with the Kullback-Leibler divergence between transformer output probabilities and exact BP marginals approaching zero. Out-of-sample, transformers trained on filtered data (k_train > 0) match the mismatched BP_{k_train} prediction on fully hierarchical test data, confirming they implement the inference algorithm matched to their training distribution rather than the data-generating process.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the exact Belief Propagation (BP) algorithm, which serves as the gold-standard inference oracle for the tree-based generative model. Transformers match BP in both test accuracy and full probability calibration (measured via KL divergence) across all filtering levels k=0,1,2,3,4, for both root classification and MLM tasks. In the deterministic k=0 root classification case, both achieve 100% accuracy; in the ambiguous k>0 cases, transformers match the sub-optimal BP_k accuracy exactly, including in mismatched settings where the training and test filtering levels differ. The comparison extends to out-of-sample evaluation on uniformly random sequences, where transformers still show high correlation with BP marginals despite the atypical inputs. No other learned baselines (e.g., CNNs, RNNs) are included; the paper's contribution is interpretability rather than benchmarking against competing architectures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work connecting transformers to hierarchical generative models, building most directly on the Random Hierarchy Model (Cagnetta et al., 2024) but replacing its uniform transition rates with log-normal distributions and a single transition tensor across all tree levels. It also builds on prior evidence that transformers trained on CFGs implement dynamic programming (Zhao et al., 2023; Allen-Zhu & Li, 2023), which found behavioral evidence but no plausible architectural implementation. The key departure is the introduction of a 'filtering' parameter k that truncates the hierarchy at a controlled depth, providing a new interpretive tool absent from both the RHM and prior CFG-based transformer studies, and enabling the first concrete existence proof of a BP implementation within a standard encoder-only transformer with ℓ layers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first mechanistic account of how a vanilla transformer embeds exact inference (Belief Propagation) on structured data, with attention maps that mirror the tree topology and a probing-confirmed sequential reconstruction of ancestor information across layers. The filtering tool is a reusable interpretive methodology that could be applied to other structured-sequence problems (proteins, immunology, variable-length CFGs). Key limitations include the fixed tree topology, small vocabulary (q=4), and short sequences (16 tokens), and the main open question is whether these findings generalize to the variable-length, unknown-topology setting of natural language where the parsing tree is not given a priori.
