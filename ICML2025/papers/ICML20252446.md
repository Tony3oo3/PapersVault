## Problem

Feature importance metrics computed on datasets with missing values lack rigor: analysts do not specify whether the reported metric reflects importance under the full (unobserved) data or under the observed data with its measurement policy, identification assumptions (MCAR/MAR/MNAR, positivity) are rarely stated, and common estimation methods (conditional mean imputation, MI excluding the label) introduce bias. Moreover, neither full-data nor observed-data importance directly answers the actionable question of which features should be measured more frequently to improve prediction.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes observed features X (with '?' placeholders for missing entries), binary missingness indicators R, a trained classifier f_cl that maps (X, R) to class probabilities, and the measurement policy π governing how features are sampled. It produces two LOCO scores per feature (full-data and observed-data variants) plus the FMIG, a scalar gradient quantifying the expected loss reduction per unit increase in that feature's measurement probability. A key constraint is that the classifier must be trained under conditions matching the target estimand: full-data LOCO requires a classifier trained on complete data, while observed-data LOCO requires one trained with missingness present.

## Method

The paper formalizes the distinction between full-data LOCO (expectation over ground-truth features X_(1) with R=1) and observed-data LOCO (expectation over observed X, R under the current measurement policy), then shows that the former requires SUTVA plus a missingness assumption and positivity for identification, while the latter is directly identified. It categorizes estimation methods (IPW, multiple imputation, conditional mean imputation, complete-case analysis) by target estimand and bias, proving that conditional mean imputation is biased for both estimands. The central novelty is the Feature Measurement Importance Gradient (FMIG), which adapts Kennedy's incremental propensity score intervention to the measurement process: a perturbation δ_j scales the odds of measuring feature j via π_{j,δ_j} = δ_j·π_j / (δ_j·π_j + 1 − π_j), and FMIG is the gradient of expected negative loss with respect to δ_j at δ_j=1. The g-formula yields a closed-form identification that requires no positivity assumption because the odds-ratio perturbation preserves support, and estimation reduces to an empirical mean weighted by a learned measurement-policy model, optionally with cross-fitting for efficiency.

## Results

Across four synthetic time-series experiments (d=3 features, T=3 time-points, 100k samples), the paper demonstrates that full data LOCO and observed data LOCO can yield qualitatively different rankings: in Experiment 1, full data LOCO increases monotonically from X1 to X3 (reflecting weights W1=1/6, W2=2/6, W3=3/6), while observed data LOCO remains flat as higher missingness rates (0.75, 0.5, 0.3) offset the growing predictive value. Under MNAR missingness (Experiment 2), the observed data LOCO of X3 drops further as its information leaks into correlated missingness indicators. Experiment 3 shows that complete case analysis, unbiased under valid positivity, becomes strongly biased when missingness rates drop to 0.4/0.2/0.1, violating positivity. Experiment 4 confirms FMIG correctly identifies X2 as the feature to measure more frequently (positive FMIG), while assigning near-zero or slightly negative FMIG to the already-fully-observed X1 and the constant X3.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against competing feature importance methods in the traditional sense; instead, it compares different estimands (full data LOCO vs. observed data LOCO vs. FMIG) and different estimation strategies (complete case analysis, IPW, multiple imputation, conditional mean imputation, mean/zero imputation) under controlled missingness mechanisms. The key finding is that these estimands answer fundamentally different questions: a costly medical test with high full data LOCO may have low observed data LOCO due to rare measurement, and only FMIG captures the actionable question of which feature to measure more frequently. Among estimation methods, the paper shows that conditional mean imputation is biased for both full data and observed data LOCO, while multiple imputation (with the label included) is unbiased for full data LOCO but requires strong identification assumptions. For observed data LOCO, simple mean/zero imputation is unbiased, whereas for full data LOCO, IPW or MI are required. The FMIG estimator, requiring only a learned measurement policy model, avoids the positivity assumption entirely, making it more robust in high-missingness settings where full data LOCO estimation breaks down.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LOCO feature importance metric (Lei et al., 2018) by formally distinguishing between full data and observed data estimands under missingness, a distinction the authors argue has been implicitly conflated in prior clinical ML work. The novel FMIG metric adapts Kennedy's (2019) incremental propensity score intervention framework from causal inference to the feature measurement setting, repurposing the g-formula and SUTVA assumptions to identify a gradient of prediction performance with respect to measurement probability. The paper also systematically re-categorizes existing estimation methods (multiple imputation, IPW, conditional mean imputation, complete case analysis) by which estimand they target, revealing that widely used practices like excluding the label from the imputation model are biased for both full data and observed data metrics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a rigorous causal-inference framework that clarifies why many published feature importance results in medical and clinical ML may be answering the wrong question or using biased estimators, with direct practical implications for how practitioners should report and interpret feature importance under missing data. The FMIG offers a genuinely actionable metric for optimizing data collection strategies (e.g., which costly tests to measure more frequently), complementing active feature acquisition methods. Key limitations include evaluation on synthetic data only, reliance on a sequential MAR measurement process assumption for FMIG identification, and the fact that FMIG provides local (gradient) rather than global guidance on measurement policy changes. Open questions include real-world validation, extension to non-sequential measurement processes, and integration with cost-aware active feature acquisition.
