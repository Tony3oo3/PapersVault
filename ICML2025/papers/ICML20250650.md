## Problem

Pretrained LMs can generalize to implications of facts they are finetuned on (e.g., learning 'John Doe lives in Tokyo' enables answering 'What language do people in John Doe's city speak?'), but the mechanisms enabling this out-of-context reasoning (OCR) and how they are acquired during pretraining remain largely unknown. This paper asks: what specific LM components coordinate to produce OCR, and what pretraining dynamics give rise to that coordination?

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pair of weight vectors (pretrained W and finetuned W') for a transformer LM, along with implication prompts whose continuations are scored by mean rank over a fixed option set. It produces three per-component scores — informative, upstream, and downstream — computed for every MLP and attention head at every layer and token position, effectively yielding a 3D map (layer × token position × component type) of which components store, query, and decode the finetuned fact. The linearized scores are computed via a small number of forward/backward passes on both W and W', with the upstream score using a surrogate that replaces the expensive per-component late-layer recomputation with a single backward pass on W'.

## Method

The paper formalizes OCR as a communication problem where finetuning encodes facts as weight changes and inference decodes them, then decomposes the decoding into three roles: informative components (whose weight updates store the fact), upstream extractive components (which parse the prompt to query informative components), and downstream extractive components (which post-process extracted information into the correct continuation). Each role is defined by a causal intervention on the LM's computational graph (e.g., swapping only W_C to W'_C for informative; feeding z'_C into the original component for downstream; a difference-in-differences ablation for upstream), and all three are linearized into first-order perturbation terms of a single importance quantity, making them comparable in magnitude and computable with a handful of forward/backward passes. To explain how these structures arise, the authors hypothesize that extractive structures are learned during pretraining when the model encounters implications of facts it already knows, and validate this with two controlled experiments: a data-ordering effect (OCR fails when all facts appear after their implications in continued pretraining) and a weight-grafting effect (the weight delta from the implication-training phase can be transplanted onto a model trained on counterfactual facts, transferring the ability to predict counterfactual implications). Experiments are conducted on OLMo-7b, Llama-3-8b, Gemma-2-9b, and Qwen-2-7b using synthetic two-hop datasets (FIRST-HOP and SECOND-HOP) where the novel fact occupies either the first or second hop of a known reasoning chain.

## Results

The weight grafting experiment (Table 3) is the headline quantitative result: grafting the extractive-structure weight change onto a model trained on counterfactual facts reduces the mean rank on counterfactual implications from 9.13 (base) to 1.13, versus 8.38 for a control graft that only transfers memorized implications. The layer-freezing ablation (Table 2) shows that freezing early-middle layers (first 24) degrades FIRST-HOP implication rank from 0.00 to 6.50 while leaving SECOND-HOP nearly unchanged (1.80→0.50), and vice versa for late layers (last 8), confirming that different layers enable different forms of generalization. In the data-ordering experiment, the 'Impl-first' ordering produces significantly worse OCR generalization than 'Facts-first' or 'Joint' orderings, even though all three successfully learn the training facts and implications.

| Weights | Impl F′ (counterfactual) | Impl F (original) | F′ (counterfactual facts) |
|---|---|---|---|
| W_F′ (facts only) | 9.13 | 8.55 | 0.00 |
| W_graft (grafted) | **1.13** | 3.30 | 0.10 |
| W_control (control graft) | 8.38 | **0.43** | 0.32 |

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against competing OCR or knowledge-editing methods; instead it validates its extractive-structures hypothesis through internal ablations and controls. The weight-grafting result is the strongest: W_graft achieves a mean rank of 1.13 on counterfactual implications, dramatically outperforming both the ungrafted model W_F′ (9.13) and the control graft W_control (8.38), confirming that the weight change carries transferable extractive structures rather than merely memorized implications. The control graft, which transfers only the implication-training weight delta, succeeds on original implications (0.43) but fails on counterfactual ones (8.38), cleanly separating memorization from structural learning. In the data-ordering comparison, 'Facts-first' and 'Joint' orderings both enable strong OCR generalization, while 'Impl-first' is significantly worse, supporting the hypothesis that extractive structures form only when the model encounters implications of facts it already knows. The layer-freezing experiments further show that no single layer range suffices for all generalization forms, challenging prior knowledge-editing approaches that target a single localized component.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the circuit-based interpretability tradition (Wang et al., 2022; Elhage et al., 2021) by applying causal intervention metrics not to activations but to weight changes, formalizing three component roles (informative, upstream, downstream) that coordinate to enable out-of-context reasoning. It builds on prior mechanistic analyses of multi-hop recall in pretrained LMs (Yang et al., 2024; Biran et al., 2024) and on the OCR/ripple-effects literature (Berglund et al., 2023a; Cohen et al., 2024), but departs by proposing a concrete learning mechanism—encountering implications of already-known facts during pretraining—that explains why extractive structures form, and by showing that layer localization matters for generalization properties, partially contradicting Hase et al. (2023). The weight-grafting experiment is a novel use of weight-space arithmetic to isolate and transfer the learned extractive structures, going beyond prior influence-function or ablation-based approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a mechanistic account of why OCR succeeds for some implication types (two-hop) but fails for others (reversal curse), and shows that knowledge editing must target all layers to preserve generalization, not just a single localized site. The data ordering effect reframes optimization as a dynamical process where the model's internal state (whether it already knows a fact) determines what it learns from subsequent data, challenging the standard model-selection view. Open questions include the unidentified alternative mechanism that gives 'Impl-first' models partial OCR, the strong learning-rate sensitivity that limits practical applicability, and whether these findings on small synthetic datasets (20 entities) transfer to natural-language knowledge. A broader theory of generalization grounded in extractive structures remains the stated long-term goal.
