## Problem

This position paper argues that the entire premise of using supervised classifiers trained on in-distribution data for OOD detection is fundamentally misspecified: feature-based methods conflate feature-space distance with distributional difference, and uncertainty-based methods conflate label uncertainty with OOD uncertainty. The authors contend this is not a fixable flaw but a conceptual misalignment that produces irreducible errors, since a classifier trained only on in-distribution classes has no reason to learn the distinctions needed to identify out-of-distribution points.

## Input / Output

The methods under examination take a test RGB image and a pre-trained supervised classifier (ResNet, ViT, ConvNeXt, etc.) and extract either penultimate-layer features or classification logits to compute a scalar OOD score, which is thresholded into a binary OOD/ID decision. Performance is evaluated via AUROC and FPR@95 on standard benchmarks pairing ImageNet-1K as ID with ImageNet-OOD, Describable Textures, or iNaturalist as OOD. The paper's diagnostic experiments additionally train Oracle binary linear classifiers on both ID and OOD features to establish the upper bound on achievable feature-based performance, and use PCA on joint ID+OOD features to identify the optimal feature subset for Mahalanobis distance.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a systematic diagnosis of why supervised OOD detection methods answer the wrong question, supported by extensive empirical evidence across 14+ model architectures. For feature-based methods, two irreducible failure modes are identified: (1) OOD features can be indistinguishable from ID features (shown via Oracle classifiers achieving <1.0 AUROC even with ground-truth OOD labels), and (2) irrelevant feature dimensions degrade Mahalanobis distance by over 10 percentage points on average, and the optimal feature subset is OOD-dataset-specific and non-transferable. For logit-based methods, the paper demonstrates that label uncertainty and OOD uncertainty are fundamentally different: multi-label ImageNet images (clearly ID) exhibit high uncertainty, while OOD classes like Textures 'Striped' are confidently misclassified with FPR@95 exceeding 60% across all 14 benchmarked models. The paper then shows that popular interventions—hybrid feature-logit methods (ViM, Hybrid-Add), outlier exposure, epistemic uncertainty via Laplace approximation or deep ensembles, adding an unseen class, generative models (RealNVP, DiT diffusion), and scaling to ViT-G/14 DINOv2—none resolve the fundamental objective misalignment, with epistemic uncertainty paradoxically worsening as more ID data is observed.

## Results

Across 14 models (ResNets, ViTs, ConvNeXt V2) and three OOD datasets (ImageNet-OOD, Textures, iNaturalist), the average FPR@95 for logit-based methods (MSP, Max Logit, Energy, Entropy) is 66.5%, meaning well over half of OOD examples are confidently misclassified as ID. For feature-based methods, an oracle binary classifier trained on both ID and OOD features reveals irreducible error: even with ground-truth OOD labels, features are indistinguishable on challenging datasets, and using only the most relevant PCA components improves Mahalanobis AUROC by over 10 percentage points on average. In a controlled CIFAR-10 experiment (airplane/cat/truck ID, dog OOD), Mahalanobis distance achieves only 0.537 AUROC—barely above random chance. Even the largest model tested (ViT-G/14 DINOv2, pretrained on 142M images) retains over 5% irreducible error from indistinguishable features on ImageNet-OOD.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not propose a new method but systematically demonstrates that all major OOD detection families fail due to a fundamental objective misalignment. Logit-based methods (MSP [Hendrycks 2016], Max Logit [Jung 2021], Energy Score [Liu 2020], Entropy [Ren 2019]) all exhibit FPR@95 exceeding 60% on average across 14 models, with the largest model (ViT-G/14 DINOv2) still misclassifying 45–56% of OOD as ID on IN-OOD. Feature-based methods (Mahalanobis [Lee 2018], Relative Mahalanobis [Ren 2021]) suffer from two irreducible error sources: indistinguishable ID/OOD features and irrelevant feature dimensions, with oracle feature selection improving Maha by >10 points but being infeasible without OOD access. The hybrid method ViM [Wang 2022] does not consistently outperform standalone Maha or MSP, and the simple Hybrid-Add baseline (sum of normalized Maha and MSP scores) shows no clear advantage on iNaturalist or IN-OOD. Outlier exposure [Hendrycks 2018] improves semantic-shift detection but degrades covariate-shift generalization by over 10% in accuracy. Scaling to ViT-G/14 DINOv2 (142M images, DINOv2 pretraining) reduces but does not eliminate the irreducible error, and the gap between the best method and the oracle feature-selected Maha persists, confirming that no amount of scale resolves the misspecification.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper departs from the entire lineage of OOD detection methods that leverage supervised classifier outputs (MSP, Mahalanobis distance, Energy Score, ViM, etc.) by arguing they are fundamentally misspecified rather than merely suboptimal. Unlike prior critiques that target specific scoring rules (max-logit vs. MSP), benchmark design (semantic vs. covariate shift conflation), or architectural limitations (normalizing flow coupling layers), this paper challenges the conceptual premise itself: that a model trained only on in-distribution classes can be expected to identify out-of-distribution points. It also critically re-examines popular interventions—hybrid methods, outlier exposure, epistemic uncertainty via ensembles/Bayesian methods, generative models, and scaling—showing each fails to resolve the core objective misalignment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

If the paper's central claim holds, it reframes a large body of OOD detection research as solving the wrong problem, with practical implications for model safety and deployment. The finding that epistemic uncertainty degrades with more in-distribution data (posterior collapse) and that better generative models can yield worse OOD detection are counterintuitive results that challenge common intuitions in the field. Open questions include: what principled methods directly estimate p(OOD|x) rather than proxy signals, how to handle the tension between OOD detection and graceful generalization under covariate shift, and whether the 'unseen class' approach can be made robust to the diversity of real-world OOD inputs. The paper's prescriptions—distinguishing detection from generalization goals, carefully selecting OOD training examples, and developing methods that directly target distribution membership—point toward a potential paradigm shift but leave the concrete solution space largely unexplored.
