## Problem

When an AI system is deployed in a novel environment, its behaviour is governed by an internal world model that is not directly observable. The paper asks: given only the AI's observed decisions and outcomes in training environments, how tightly can we bound its future actions, perceived fairness, and perceived harm in unseen settings? Prior work in inverse reinforcement learning and causal inference identified under-determination of agent preferences, but no prior result provided closed-form, architecture-agnostic bounds on an AI's out-of-distribution decision-making under the assumption of a causal world model.

## Input / Output

The method consumes samples of the joint distribution P_d(V) (decisions, covariates, and utility) for all decisions d in one or more grounded environments, and optionally covariate distributions P_{σ,d}(c) from the shifted environment plus qualitative causal structure assumptions. All outputs are closed-form inequalities expressed solely in terms of these observed quantities: tight lower/upper bounds on the preference gap Δ between any pair of decisions, necessary-and-sufficient conditions for weak or strong predictability, a width-1 bound on the counterfactual fairness gap Υ (showing it is completely unconstrained), and tight bounds on the counterfactual harm gap Ω. No access to the AI's internal parameters, architecture, or true world model is required.

## Method

The paper models the AI's internal world model as a Structural Causal Model (SCM) and assumes the AI is grounded (its model's interventional distributions match the true environment) and approximately maximizes expected utility. Using the σ-calculus for stochastic interventions and the axioms of counterfactuals, the authors derive the tightest possible bounds on the AI's preference gap Δ under various shift types: atomic interventions (Thm. 1), multi-environment grounding (Thm. 2), completely unknown shifts (Thm. 3, showing no predictability), and shifts with known covariate shifts (Thm. 4). The key mechanism is that grounding constrains the AI's model to a set of compatible SCMs, and the bounds are obtained by optimizing the preference gap over this set, yielding expressions involving only observed probabilities like P_d(c,z) and E_{P_d}[Y|c,z]. The paper then extends these results to practical relaxations—approximate grounding via a discrepancy measure ψ, bounded rationality via a discrimination threshold λ, approximate inner alignment via a correlation constraint α between observed and proxy utility, and partial unconfoundedness via causal structure—showing how each additional assumption tightens the bounds.

## Results

The paper derives tight analytical bounds on an AI's preference gap Δ (the difference in expected utility between two decisions) under distribution shift, expressed solely in terms of observable quantities P_d(V). Under an atomic intervention do(z), the minimum preference gap is bounded below by a closed-form expression (Thm. 1); in the Medical AI example, min Δ_{d1>d0} = −0.4 and min Δ_{d0>d1} = −0.8, meaning neither decision can be ruled out. Under completely arbitrary shifts (Thm. 3), the preference gap is entirely unconstrained (min Δ = −1 for all pairs), establishing a fundamental limit. For counterfactual fairness (Thm. 5), the fairness gap Υ is bounded with width exactly 1, meaning an AI's perceived fairness is completely unidentifiable from behaviour alone.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or comparison experiments. The contribution is a set of novel theorems extending causal identification bounds (from Pearl 1999, Tian & Pearl 2000, and the σ-calculus of Correa & Bareinboim 2020) to the specific problem of predicting an AI agent's out-of-distribution decisions. The paper positions itself relative to inverse reinforcement learning (which studies partial identifiability of reward functions) by instead bounding the implications of world-model uncertainty on decision-making. The key theoretical advance over prior causal bounds is the application to an agent whose internal model is unknown but assumed to be a valid SCM consistent with observed behaviour, yielding the first formal characterization of when an AI's future actions are provably predictable versus fundamentally under-determined.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the causal inference literature on bounding treatment effects from non-interventional data (Robins, 1989; Manski, 1990; Pearl, 1999) and the Causal Hierarchy Theorem (Bareinboim et al., 2022) to the specific problem of predicting AI agent decisions in novel environments. It builds on the σ-calculus for reasoning about distribution shifts (Correa & Bareinboim, 2020a) and the representation of rational agents via SCMs (Halpern & Piermont, 2024; Richens & Everitt, 2024), but departs from inverse reinforcement learning by focusing on the partial identifiability of the agent's world model rather than its reward function. The paper also connects to counterfactual fairness (Kusner et al., 2017) and counterfactual harm (Richens et al., 2022) by showing how behavioral data constrains (or fails to constrain) an AI's subjective notions of these quantities.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central negative result—that an AI's counterfactual fairness gap is completely unconstrained by its external behavior (Theorem 5, with bound width exactly 1)—challenges the assumption that observing an AI's decisions reveals anything about its intentions regarding fairness. More broadly, the paper establishes that without additional assumptions (causal structure, approximate grounding, known shift characteristics), behavioral data provides no information about an AI's out-of-distribution decisions. Key limitations include the infinite-sample assumption, the requirement of acyclic SCMs (excluding feedback loops), and the reliance on exact expected utility maximization; the paper sketches relaxations but leaves finite-sample guarantees and empirical validation as open questions. For AI safety, the results argue that world model inference and design are necessary complements to behavioral monitoring.
