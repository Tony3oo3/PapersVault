## Problem

Existing active causal discovery methods select interventions by maximizing information-theoretic objectives (e.g., mutual information), which do not directly optimize for the probability of reaching a correct and decisive causal direction conclusion. This misalignment means that information-gain-optimal interventions may be suboptimal for the actual decision task of distinguishing cause from effect under a fixed experimental budget, particularly when causal effects are weak.

## Input / Output

The method takes n i.i.d. observational pairs (x_i, y_i) from the joint distribution P(X,Y) and a total intervention budget B as input. It produces a sequence of intervention values {x̄_1, ..., x̄_B} to apply to variable X, the observed responses {ȳ_1, ..., ȳ_B}, and the posterior probabilities P(H_j | D_int) for j ∈ {0,1} indicating which causal direction is supported. For discrete variables, the intervention space is finite and the method uses dynamic programming; for continuous variables, it employs gradient-based optimization with Monte Carlo estimation and a smooth approximation of the indicator function. The Bayes factor thresholds k_0 and k_1 (e.g., 10 and 1/10) define the evidence level required for a 'decisive' conclusion.

## Method

The paper formulates bivariate causal direction identification as a hypothesis testing problem (H_0: X←Y vs. H_1: X→Y) and defines an objective P_DC that measures the probability of obtaining decisive and correct evidence, computed via the Bayes factor BF_01 under each hypothesis. For single-step intervention, it maximizes P_DC over the intervention value x̄ by integrating over all possible outcomes ȳ under each hypothesis's interventional distribution. For multi-step optimization, it introduces a discounted cumulative objective J = Σ γ^k P_DC(x_{1:k}) that balances immediate and future evidential gains. Two key theoretical properties—symmetry of P_DC in its arguments (Prop. 4.1) and dependence only on count vectors (Prop. 4.2)—enable a dynamic programming algorithm that reduces complexity from O(|X|^K) brute-force to O(|X|·K^|X|) polynomial time. The framework extends to tree-structured graphs by summing P_DC over all neighbor directions for each intervention target, and to continuous variables via Laplace-approximated marginal likelihoods and ReLU-based smooth indicator approximations for gradient-based optimization.

## Results

On the bivariate causal discovery task with weak causation (|p₀−p₁| ∈ [0.1, 0.2]), the proposed method achieves higher recall than information-gain baselines while maintaining competitive Type I error rates, with the advantage driven by more efficient collection of decisive evidence (higher P_DC). On tree-structured causal graphs (30 vertices), the method requires approximately 140 interventions at the hardest setting (δ=0.05), roughly 10% fewer than information-theoretic baselines. On the embodied AI switch-light task, the method requires 32.11±13.2 interventions at the weakest causal strength (|p₀−p₁| ∈ (0.2,0.3)), compared to 33.51±13.9 for InfoGain and 46.15±14.5 for random intervention.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the InfoGain baselines (Tigas et al., 2022; Toth et al., 2022), the proposed method wins on recall in the weak-causation bivariate setting (Setting-1) while matching Type I error rates, and requires ~10% fewer interventions on tree-structured graphs (140 vs. ~155 at δ=0.05). In the strong-causation setting (Setting-2), all methods perform comparably since the causal signal is easily identifiable without refined intervention strategies. Against the Multi-step InfoGain baseline, the proposed method achieves slightly lower intervention counts in the embodied AI task (e.g., 32.11 vs. 32.52 at |p₀−p₁| ∈ (0.2,0.3)) and lower variance, indicating more consistent performance. Against Greenewald et al. (2019) on tree-structured graphs, the method consistently requires fewer interventions across all δ settings, with the gap widening as the causal effect weakens. The ablation study confirms that the multi-step dynamic programming optimization provides a significant boost over the single-step greedy version, particularly in challenging weak-causation scenarios.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant information-theoretic (mutual information) objective used in active causal discovery (Tong & Koller, 2001; Toth et al., 2022; Tigas et al., 2022) by reframing causal direction identification as a Bayesian hypothesis testing problem guided by Bayes factors. For the multi-step sequential optimization, it builds on the look-ahead frameworks of GLASSES (González et al., 2016) and Binoculars (Jiang et al., 2020), but applies them to a decision-focused objective rather than information gain. It extends Greenewald et al. (2019) on tree-structured causal graph learning by optimizing intervention *values* (not just targets) via a Bayes factor criterion, and generalizes Castelletti & Consonni (2024) from passive sample-size calculation to active environment interaction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, decision-theoretic alternative to information gain for active causal discovery, with a clean dynamic programming algorithm that reduces multi-step intervention optimization from exponential to polynomial complexity. However, the method's applicability to general multivariate graphs is limited by the requirement that p{y|do(x)} be identifiable, which the authors acknowledge as a key open problem. The modest empirical gains (~10% fewer interventions on trees, recall improvements in weak-causation bivariate settings) suggest the framework is most valuable when causal signals are subtle and budget-constrained, but its broader impact awaits extension beyond tree-structured and bivariate settings.
