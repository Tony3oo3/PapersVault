## Problem

LLM-based reward design methods like EUREKA rely solely on text descriptions, which cannot capture users' latent preferences, cannot handle the mathematical trade-offs of feature weighting, and generalize poorly to out-of-distribution robotic tasks. Meanwhile, standard IRL suffers from reward ambiguity—infinitely many reward functions can explain the same demonstrations—without a mechanism to constrain the feature space. The paper addresses how to combine the semantic reasoning of vision-language models with the demonstration-matching rigor of IRL to produce reward functions that are both interpretable and well-aligned with human intent.

## Input / Output

The method takes three inputs: the MDP environment code (defining the state space), a natural-language task description, and a visual demonstration whose format is task-dependent—superimposed motion sequences for locomotion tasks or four expert-selected keyframes for manipulation tasks to avoid cluttered poses. The VLM outputs a TorchScript-compatible Python feature function (a dictionary of named scalar features computed from the observation buffer), which is then used by IRL to produce a linear reward function R(s)=θᵀφ(s) and a PPO-trained policy. The self-reflection loop iteratively revises the feature function code based on feature-count discrepancies between policy rollouts and demonstrations.

## Method

ELEMENTAL operates in three phases. Phase 1 prompts a VLM (GPT-4o) with the environment code, task text, and visual demonstration to draft an executable feature function φ(s)→ℝⁿ, re-prompting up to three times with traceback if the code is not runnable. Phase 2 runs Approximate MaxEnt-IRL: it alternates PPO policy optimization on R_θ(s)=θᵀφ(s) with gradient ascent on θ, where the gradient is the difference between feature expectations under the demonstration and under the current policy (avoiding the intractable partition function), with L1 gradient and weight normalization for stability. Phase 3 computes feature-count vectors for the learned policy and the demonstration, feeds the discrepancy back to the VLM, which revises the feature function (adding, removing, rescaling, or rewriting features), and the loop repeats. The key architectural insight is the separation of concerns: the VLM handles semantic feature identification (its strength), while IRL handles the mathematical weight assignment (its strength), and the self-reflection loop closes the gap between the two iteratively.

## Results

On nine IsaacGym robotic tasks, ELEMENTAL achieves an average 42.3% higher task success rate than EUREKA (the prior SOTA), outperforming it on 8 out of 9 tasks. Notable gains include Allegro Hand (22.97 vs. 11.12), Shadow Hand (2.71 vs. 0.001), and Ant (8.49 vs. 6.88). On out-of-distribution Ant variants (reversed observations, lighter gravity, running backward), ELEMENTAL achieves 41.3% better generalization than EUREKA. In a real-world user study with 12 participants teaching a Kinova JACO arm, ELEMENTAL significantly outperformed EUREKA on both task performance (20.58 ± 4.93 vs. 12.42 ± 4.72, p < .001) and strategy alignment (19.83 ± 6.13 vs. 10.50 ± 4.32, p < .001).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against EUREKA (Ma et al., 2023), the closest and strongest baseline, ELEMENTAL wins on 8 of 9 IsaacGym tasks with a 42.3% average improvement, with the largest gaps on Allegro Hand (22.97 vs. 11.12, ~107% gain) and Shadow Hand (2.71 vs. 0.001, where EUREKA failed to produce any executable reward). ELEMENTAL also surpasses EUREKA on all four out-of-distribution Ant variants, achieving 41.3% better generalization, suggesting EUREKA's GPT-4o may have memorized IsaacGym reward structures. Against standard IRL (Ziebart et al., 2008), ELEMENTAL dominates across nearly all tasks, as vanilla IRL without VLM-derived features struggles in IsaacGym's high-dimensional state spaces (e.g., 0.88 vs. 8.49 on Ant). Against Behavior Cloning (Ross et al., 2011), ELEMENTAL wins on all complex tasks where BC suffers from covariate shift (e.g., 0.36 vs. 0.01 on Franka Cabinet, 8.49 vs. -0.05 on Ant), though BC remains competitive on simpler tasks like Cartpole (149.85) and Ball Balance (344.55). A supplementary VLM+BC baseline (using VLM features with BC) performs poorly across all tasks, confirming that the IRL-based reward matching and self-reflection loop are essential beyond simply extracting features.

## Relation to Prior Work

ELEMENTAL builds on the MaxEnt-IRL formulation (Ziebart et al., 2008) and the interleaved policy-reward optimization paradigm from AIRL (Fu et al., 2018), upgrading them into an Approximate MaxEnt-IRL algorithm suitable for high-dimensional robotic state spaces. It departs from EUREKA (Ma et al., 2023b), which uses LLMs to generate complete reward functions from text alone, by delegating reward weighting to IRL and restricting the VLM's role to feature function drafting from multimodal (text + visual demonstration) input. It also extends the LLM-assisted feature design approach of Peng et al. (2024b) by replacing text-only state-vector demonstrations with visual inputs and by introducing an iterative self-reflection loop that feeds feature-count discrepancies back to the VLM for refinement.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ELEMENTAL is the first successful application of IRL to IsaacGym's high-dimensional environments, demonstrating that VLM-guided feature extraction combined with demonstration-matching IRL can outperform text-only LLM reward design by 42.3% on task success and 41.3% on out-of-distribution generalization. The real-world user study with a Kinova JACO arm validates the approach beyond simulation, showing interactive teaching is feasible within 4-minute rounds. Key limitations include the 2.5× runtime overhead from environment rollouts, dependence on reasonably high-quality demonstrations, and reliance on VLM coding reliability; open questions include whether continuous video demonstrations, real-time human-in-the-loop feedback during self-reflection, and integration with suboptimal-demonstration learning could further improve robustness and reduce the barrier for non-expert users.
