## Problem

Given a fixed budget of total model parameters, should one train a single large model or an ensemble of smaller models? While ensembling is known to reduce predictor variance, it is unclear whether this benefit can compensate for the reduced expressivity of each ensemble member, and prior work has not provided a principled characterization of when ensembles approach single-model performance. This paper addresses the trade-off for random-feature ridge regression (RFRR) ensembles, where deterministic equivalent risk estimates make the analysis tractable.

## Input / Output

The analysis takes as input the limiting kernel eigenspectrum {η_t} and target weights {w_t} (which fully characterize the learning problem under the Gaussian universality assumption), along with ensemble size K, features per member N, training set size P, and ridge parameter λ. The output is a deterministic equivalent test risk E_g^K that concentrates around the true stochastic error in the large P, N limit, decomposed into bias and variance components. The scaling-law analysis further produces power-law exponents s as explicit functions of the growth exponent ℓ, capacity α, and source r, enabling prediction of error scaling without simulation.

## Method

The core contribution is a 'no free lunch' theorem proving that with fixed total features M = KN, the ridge-optimized test risk increases monotonically with ensemble size K, so a single large model always achieves optimal performance. The proof exploits a bias-variance decomposition where ensembling reduces variance by 1/K but leaves bias unchanged, combined with an invariance-based argument showing that at fixed M, increasing per-model size decreases variance faster than adding ensemble members. In the overparameterized regime (N ≫ P), a power-series expansion in 1/N and λ reveals that error depends on K and N only through their product M at leading order, explaining why overparameterized ensembles consistently achieve near-optimal performance. For the underparameterized regime, the paper introduces a 'growth exponent' ℓ ∈ [0,1] controlling how K and N scale jointly with M, and derives scaling laws E_g^K ~ M^{-s} with s = min(2αℓ·min(r,1), 1−ℓ+2αℓ·min(r,1/2)), showing that while ℓ=1 (fixed K, grow N) is always optimal, near-optimal scaling is achievable for ℓ > ℓ* on easier tasks (r > 1/2). Results are validated empirically on binarized CIFAR-10 and MNIST using ReLU random features with the NNGP kernel.

## Results

The paper proves two main theorems: (1) 'More is Better' (Thm 4.1) — ridge-optimized test risk decreases monotonically with ensemble size K, model size N, and sample size P; and (2) 'No Free Lunch' (Thm 5.1) — with fixed total features M = KN, the optimal error is always achieved by a single large model (K=1), and error increases monotonically with K. In the overparameterized regime (N ≫ P), the error depends on K and N only through their product M = KN to leading order (eq. 24), so ensembles achieve near-optimal performance. In the underparameterized regime, the scaling exponent is s = min(2αℓ·min(r,1), 1−ℓ+2αℓ·min(r,1/2)) (eq. 26), where ℓ ∈ [0,1] controls the relative growth of K vs. N. Empirically, CIFAR-10 (α≈1.33, r≈0.038) and MNIST (α≈1.46, r≈0.14) both fall in the 'difficult' regime (r < 1/2), where the scaling exponent increases linearly with ℓ and near-optimal scaling requires ℓ close to 1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper, so 'baselines' are alternative parameter allocations rather than competing methods. The central comparison is between a single large model (K=1, N=M) and ensembles of smaller models (K>1, N=M/K) at fixed total parameter count M. Theorem 5.1 proves the single model always wins at optimal ridge, and Figure 2 confirms this empirically on binarized CIFAR-10: ridge-optimized error increases monotonically with K for all tested P values. However, the gap is small in the overparameterized regime (N ≫ P), where eq. 24 shows error depends on K and N only through M=KN. In the underparameterized regime, the growth exponent ℓ=1 (scaling N with fixed K) always gives the best scaling law, but for easy tasks (r > 1/2), any ℓ > ℓ* achieves near-optimal scaling — e.g., with α=1.5 and r=0.8, ℓ*≈0.4, so even ℓ=0.5 (joint scaling) achieves s=1.2 vs. the optimal s=1.5. The paper also notes that ensembling provides a wider robustness region in λ (Fig. 2C), meaning near-optimal performance is maintained over a broader range of ridge values, which is practically useful when fine-tuning is infeasible.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the 'more is better' theorem of Simon et al. (2023) from single RFRR models to ensembles, and builds on the Gaussian universality framework and deterministic equivalent error formulas from Atanasov et al. (2024, 2022) and Defilippis et al. (2024). The scaling-law analysis generalizes the width-bottlenecked scaling results of Bahri et al. (2024), Maloney et al. (2022), and Bordelon et al. (2024a) to the ensembled setting by introducing a novel 'growth exponent' ℓ that parameterizes joint scaling of ensemble size K and per-model width N. The 'no free lunch' theorem is positioned as a stronger statement than known equivalences between infinite ensembles and single infinite-width predictors (Patil & LeJeune, 2024; LeJeune et al., 2020), since it applies at finite total parameter count M and finite K.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a rigorous theoretical resolution to the practical question of whether to allocate a fixed parameter budget to one large model or an ensemble of smaller ones, confirming the industry trend toward single large models while identifying precise conditions (overparameterized regime, or easy tasks with r > 1/2 and sufficient ℓ) under which ensembles achieve near-optimal performance. A key limitation is the assumption of statistically homogeneous ensembles (all members trained on the same data for the same task), leaving open whether functionally specialized ensembles like mixture-of-experts could escape the 'no free lunch' constraint. The authors also note that feature-learning effects in deep networks could dramatically alter the predicted scaling laws, making extension to the rich regime an important open direction.
