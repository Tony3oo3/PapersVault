## Problem

Tuning the Markov kernels in sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) samplers with unadjusted kernels (e.g., Langevin Monte Carlo) is a significant practical challenge: standard adaptive MCMC objectives like acceptance rate control or expected-squared jump distance do not apply, and the typical alternative—end-to-end stochastic gradient descent—requires thousands of full SMC runs per tuning iteration, making it prohibitively expensive for kernels with only a few scalar parameters like step size.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251210/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes an unnormalized target density γ(x), a reference distribution q (typically a standard Gaussian), the number of SMC steps T, and the number of particles N. It produces a full schedule of tuned scalar step sizes h_1, …, h_T (or (h_t, ρ_t) pairs for KLMC) obtained online during a single SMC run using only B ≪ N subsampled particles per adaptation step, followed by an unbiased normalizing constant estimate and particle samples from a subsequent vanilla SMC run with the tuned schedule. The adaptation cost is a small fraction of one SMC run—roughly 10 objective evaluations per step after warm-starting.

## Method

The paper introduces a path divergence D_path between the target and proposal path measures that decomposes into T incremental exclusive KL terms, each of which is easy to estimate from weighted particles (unlike the forward KL, which requires an intractable expectation over the target conditional). A greedy scheme then minimizes each incremental term sequentially, fixing previously tuned parameters. For scalar step sizes, the optimization is solved with a gradient-free golden section search in log-space, preceded by an exponential bracketing phase and a feasibility check for cold starts; for KLMC's two parameters, coordinate descent alternates between GSS on the step size and a coarse grid search on the momentum refreshment rate. Three practical ingredients—particle subsampling (B ≪ N), sample average approximation (fixing the kernel randomness during optimization), and a regularization term penalizing large step-size changes from the previous iteration—make the per-step optimization cheap and stable. Under a unimodality assumption on the objective, the algorithm achieves O(log(Δ/ε)) objective evaluations, where Δ measures the log-scale distance between the initial guess and the optimum.

## Results

The adaptive SMC sampler (SMC-LMC and SMC-KLMC) achieves estimates of log Z that are closer to or comparable to the best fixed step size found via grid search on 20 out of 22 benchmark problems, with the sole exception being the Rats problem. Against end-to-end differentiable AIS optimization, the adaptive method produces more accurate log Z estimates on Sonar and Brownian, and comparable estimates on Pines, while requiring only a few vanilla SMC runs rather than thousands of SGD iterations. In the dimensional scaling experiment (Gaussian target with d up to 256), SMC-LMC with adaptive tuning outperforms SMC-MALA (with both ESJD and ARC adaptation) for d ≥ 128, as the unadjusted kernel's larger step sizes avoid the O(d^{-1/3}) step-size shrinkage required by MALA.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against fixed step sizes (grid search over h and ρ), the adaptive method matches or beats the best fixed configuration on 20 of 22 problems (Figs. 2–3), with the Rats problem being the notable failure case where the adaptive schedule underperforms. Against end-to-end differentiable AIS (Geffner & Domke 2021/2023; Zhang et al. 2021), the adaptive SMC achieves lower-variance log Z estimates on Sonar and Brownian and comparable estimates on Pines (Fig. 6), while the end-to-end method requires ~5,000 SGD iterations with 32 particles per iteration versus the adaptive method's single SMC pass with ~10 objective evaluations per step. Against SMC-MALA with ESJD maximization (Pasarica & Gelman 2010) and SMC-MALA with acceptance rate control (Roberts & Tweedie 1996), the unadjusted SMC-LMC with adaptive tuning produces more accurate estimates in higher dimensions (d ≥ 128) because its step sizes remain larger, avoiding the dimension-dependent shrinkage that MALA requires (Fig. 5). Overall, the adaptive method's key advantage is achieving competitive or superior accuracy at a fraction of the computational cost of gradient-based tuning, with no manual hyperparameter tuning required.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the incremental KL minimization framework from Annealed Flow Transport (Arbel et al., 2021; Matthews et al., 2022) from training normalizing flows to tuning off-the-shelf MCMC kernels (LMC, KLMC) within SMC samplers. It builds on the SMC/Feynman-Kac framework (Del Moral et al., 2006) and the pretuning principle of Buchholz et al. (2021), but departs from both the expensive SGD-based end-to-end optimization used for unadjusted kernels (Geffner & Domke, 2021; Zhang et al., 2021) and the acceptance-rate/ESJD strategies that only apply to Metropolis-adjusted kernels. A key theoretical contribution is the introduction of a 'path divergence' that properly decomposes into incremental terms, providing a principled objective that goes beyond the approximation used in AFT.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical, gradient-free, and tuning-free alternative to costly SGD-based tuning for SMC samplers with unadjusted kernels, achieving comparable or better normalizing constant estimates at a fraction of the computational cost. The dimensional scaling experiments showing SMC-LMC outperforming SMC-MALA in high dimensions are practically useful for practitioners choosing kernels. Open questions include the consistency of the adaptive scheme (acknowledged by the authors as future work), whether the approach extends to multi-parameter or matrix-valued kernel parameters beyond the scalar step size and 2D (h, ρ) cases handled here, and whether the 'time-correct forward kernel' backward kernel choice generalizes beyond Langevin-type kernels.
