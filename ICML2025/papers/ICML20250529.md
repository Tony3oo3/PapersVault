## Problem

Existing fMRI-based GNNs for mental disorder classification lack interpretability regarding demographic factors (e.g., age), overlook the brain's hierarchical clustering of network activity, and cannot effectively predict functional connectivity alterations across different age stages. The paper addresses this by proposing a framework that decodes fMRI network features into interpretable tree-structured pathways while incorporating age as a first-class modeling variable.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250529/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes ROI-level BOLD fMRI time series (90 ROIs via Stanford atlas for Cannabis, 118 ROIs via Harvard-Oxford for COBRE) segmented into dynamic windows, along with each subject's chronological age as a scalar parameter. Beyond the binary disease label, it produces a hierarchical brain tree with multiple trunk levels mapped to Yeo's seven-network parcellation, per-ROI importance scores, and a predicted brain age for regression analysis of age-related FC deterioration.

## Method

NEUROTREE integrates three key components: (1) a k-hop ODE-based AGE-GCN where a neural ODE models dynamic FC evolution with age θ as an explicit parameter (dX/dt = ηA^d(t)X(t) + ρ·θX(t)), and a generalized k-hop convolution operator combines static and dynamic adjacency matrices with a learnable directional balance λ to capture multi-hop brain region interactions; (2) a Contrastive Masked Functional Connectivity (CMFC) loss that simultaneously pulls similar FC-strength nodes together and pushes dissimilar ones apart in latent space; (3) a hierarchical brain tree construction pipeline that prunes the FC graph via MST, identifies optimal weighted paths balancing node scores and high-order FC contributions, and aggregates connectivity across multiple tree depths to produce anatomically meaningful trunk-and-branch structures for interpreting disease-specific subnetwork alterations.

## Results

NEUROTREE achieves state-of-the-art graph classification performance on both datasets: on the Cannabis dataset it reaches AUC 0.80±0.05 and Accuracy 0.73±0.04, and on the COBRE (schizophrenia) dataset it reaches AUC 0.71±0.10 and Accuracy 0.65±0.08. Compared to the strongest baseline (MixHop), NEUROTREE improves AUC by 8.11% on Cannabis and 2.89% on COBRE, with consistent 3–6.5% gains in Accuracy, Precision, and Recall across both datasets. Ablation results confirm that both the CMFC contrastive loss and the age parameter θ contribute meaningfully, with the full model outperforming the no-CMFC variant by ~2–3% on COBRE.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NEUROTREE outperforms all seven baselines on both datasets across every metric. Against the strongest competitor MixHop, it gains 8.11% AUC on Cannabis (0.80 vs 0.73) and 2.89% AUC on COBRE (0.71 vs 0.69), with 6.3–6.5% improvements in Accuracy, Precision, and Recall on COBRE. Against brain-specific GNNs, the gains are substantial: BrainGNN is surpassed by 23.72% Accuracy on Cannabis and 30% on COBRE; BrainUSL by 12.30% (Cannabis) and 20.37% (COBRE); BrainGSL by 12.30% and 27.45% respectively. Among path-based models, NEUROTREE achieves a 14% AUC improvement over PathNN on Cannabis (0.80 vs 0.70), while PathNN actually underperforms MixHop on COBRE (AUC 0.49 vs 0.69). GAT, the best attention-based baseline, trails NEUROTREE by 8% AUC on Cannabis and 4% on COBRE. The ablation row 'Ours (w/o θ)' shows that removing the age parameter causes a dramatic collapse (AUC drops to 0.49–0.50), confirming that demographic integration is critical to the method's effectiveness.

## Relation to Prior Work

NEUROTREE extends three distinct lines of work: (1) ODE-based brain network modeling (STE-ODE, Tang et al. 2024) by incorporating a scalar age parameter θ into the continuous-time dynamics, yielding the AGE-GCN; (2) k-hop graph convolution (MixHop, Abu-El-Haija et al. 2019) by replacing static adjacency powers with a dynamic, directionally-weighted k-hop operator that blends static and time-varying FC matrices; and (3) path-based GNNs (PathNN, Michel et al. 2023) by replacing arbitrary node-to-node paths with anatomically constrained hierarchical tree paths derived from a minimum spanning tree. The key conceptual departure is the post-hoc decoding of learned graph embeddings into a multi-level brain tree structure, which prior brain GNNs (BrainGNN, BrainUSL, BrainGSL) do not attempt, and the introduction of a contrastive masked FC loss (CMFC) that explicitly separates similar from dissimilar connectivity pairs during training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The hierarchical brain tree provides a visually interpretable decomposition of which functional networks (DMN, FPN, VN, SUB) are disrupted at different levels of connectivity strength, offering neuroscientists a structured view of disease propagation that flat graph embeddings cannot provide. The age-modulated ODE formulation enables brain age gap analysis, revealing that addiction accelerates apparent brain aging more than schizophrenia, a finding with potential clinical relevance for tracking disease progression. However, the evaluation is limited to two relatively small datasets (n≈323 and n≈142), the tree construction is a post-hoc heuristic rather than an end-to-end learned component, and the CMFC loss yields only modest gains on the Cannabis dataset. Open questions include whether the tree structure generalizes to larger multi-site cohorts, whether the k-hop convergence properties hold for other imaging modalities (EEG, PET), and whether the age parameter can be extended to continuous longitudinal tracking rather than cross-sectional snapshots.
