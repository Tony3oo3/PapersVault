## Problem

Existing LLM alignment methods (e.g., RLHF) optimize machine-scored objectives without provable guarantees on human-perceived tail risks, and prior conformal risk control frameworks only bound expected loss, which is ill-suited for rare but catastrophic events like toxic outputs. This paper asks: can we control arbitrary distortion risk measures (CVaR, VaR, weighted quantile averages) of human-rated disutility with finite-sample guarantees, without retraining the LLM and without assuming any alignment between human and machine scores?

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained LLM p(y|x), a pre-trained machine disutility scorer r_m(·), and a calibration set of n prompts with human-annotated disutility scores as input, and produces a single scalar threshold λ̂ as output. At deployment, for a new prompt the LLM samples responses sequentially until one achieves a machine score below λ̂ (a geometric number of draws, adaptive to the prompt), then returns that response or abstains if none qualify. The threshold is the sole tunable parameter—no model weights are modified—making the calibration cost O(n) human annotations rather than a full retraining pass.

## Method

The core reduction is to replace high-dimensional model retraining with a univariate search over a threshold λ on machine scores: for each prompt, candidate responses are partitioned into nested sets C_λ(x) = {y : r_m(y) < λ}, and the induced human score r_λ(x) = max_{y∈C_λ(x)} r(y) is monotone in λ. The distortion risk R_ψ(λ) is estimated via L-statistics (weighted averages of order statistics), which are shown to be asymptotically normal with a consistent variance estimator (Theorem 3.1), yielding a pointwise upper confidence bound R̂_ψ⁺(λ). The threshold λ̂ is then obtained by inverting this UCB: the largest λ such that R̂_ψ⁺(λ') ≤ α for all λ' ≤ λ. The key advantage over prior DKW or Berk-Jones envelope approaches is that L-statistics target the specific weight function ψ, producing tighter bounds that are less conservative and hence lower-cost, while requiring no assumptions on the LLM or the machine scorer's alignment with human ratings.

## Results

On the REALTOXICITYPROMPTS dataset with LLaMA2-7B (|D|=6000, ρ=0.57), CDRC-L achieves realized CVaR_β closest to the target α across all β∈{0.5, 0.75, 0.9} while incurring the lowest average sampling cost among all three conformal methods. In the best-of-N comparison (β=0.5, n=1000), Best-of-3 yields CVaR_β = 0.2427±0.0068 and Best-of-5 yields 0.2015±0.007, whereas CDRC-L matches these levels (α≈0.25 and α≈0.20 respectively) using fewer than 3 and 3.5 average LLM responses. The gap between target and realized CVaR_β remains nearly constant for CDRC-L across different β values, indicating adaptivity to the choice of distortion weight ψ.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares CDRC-L against two alternative conformal distortion risk control methods (CDRC-DKW using the Dvoretzky-Kiefer-Wolfowitz inequality and CDRC-BJ using Berk-Jones statistics) and against the best-of-N inference-time heuristic. CDRC-L is consistently the least conservative across all settings of β, ρ, and calibration set size, meaning its realized CVaR_β or VaR_β sits closest to the target α line, while CDRC-DKW and CDRC-BJ are increasingly conservative as β grows. In terms of deployment cost, CDRC-L is the most cost-efficient in every configuration, with the advantage being most pronounced at small calibration set sizes (|D|∈{50,100,200}) and high β. Against best-of-N, CDRC-L achieves comparable or better CVaR_β control at lower average inference cost: Best-of-3 requires exactly 3 samples per prompt to reach α≈0.25, while CDRC-L needs fewer than 3 on average; Best-of-5 requires 5 samples for α≈0.20, while CDRC-L needs fewer than 3.5. The method also demonstrates that better human-machine alignment (higher Spearman ρ) monotonically reduces the sampling cost, confirming that the framework gracefully handles misalignment without requiring model refitting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the conformal risk control framework of Bates et al. (2021) from expected-loss risk measures to the broader class of distortion risk measures (weighted averages of quantiles), leveraging the asymptotic theory of L-statistics from van der Vaart (1998) to construct tight, problem-specific upper confidence bounds. It departs from the DKW and Berk-Jones confidence envelope approaches of Snell et al. (2022) by directly estimating the distortion risk via L-statistics, yielding less conservative bounds tailored to the chosen weighting function ψ. In the LLM alignment context, the method differs from RLHF by requiring no model refitting and from best-of-N by using an adaptive, risk-calibrated number of samples rather than a fixed generation budget.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides assumption-free, finite-sample guarantees for controlling tail risks (CVaR, VaR, or any distortion risk) of LLM outputs, which is critical for safety-sensitive deployments where rare toxic or harmful events carry disproportionate harm. The practical advantage of adaptive sampling cost over best-of-N makes risk-controlled deployment more economically viable. Open questions include extending guarantees to distribution-shifted prompt populations (via reweighting), adapting to preference data with U-statistic structure, and whether the asymptotic L-statistics bounds remain tight enough in very small calibration regimes where human annotation cost is the bottleneck.
