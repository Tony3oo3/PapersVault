## Problem

Existing time encoding methods—whether hand-crafted or functional (e.g., Time2Vec, FTR)—rely on fixed, pre-defined nonlinear transformations (primarily trigonometric functions) that impose a strong periodicity inductive bias. This limits their ability to capture non-periodic trends, irregular patterns, and the mixed temporal dynamics commonly observed in real-world data such as financial transactions or dynamic social interactions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250344/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

LeTE accepts a scalar timestamp (absolute) or a relative time difference between events as input and produces a d-dimensional time embedding vector. In time series forecasting, the embedding is added element-wise to feature embeddings (requiring matching dimensions); in dynamic graph models, it is concatenated with node and edge features, allowing independent dimensionality. The method is invariant to time rescaling, so the same encoding works regardless of whether time is measured in seconds, hours, or days.

## Method

LeTE replaces the fixed sine activation in Functional Time Encodings with learnable nonlinear transformation functions φ_i, parameterized either by Fourier series expansion (capturing periodic structure) or B-spline basis functions (capturing arbitrary non-periodic shapes), inspired by KAN's learnable activations. The Combined LeTE assigns Fourier-based transformations to a fraction p of dimensions and Spline-based to the rest, with a Layer Normalization and learnable scaling to harmonize output magnitudes. All parameters—linear coefficients ω_i, φ_i and the nonlinear function parameters—are jointly optimized under downstream task supervision, enabling the encoding to adapt to whatever temporal patterns the data exhibits. The paper proves that previous FTEs (Time2Vec, FTR) are special cases of LeTE (Proposition 3.1) and that LeTE is invariant to time rescaling (Proposition 3.2), while experiments show it outperforms FTE across time series forecasting, dynamic graph link prediction, and a real-world financial risk control application, often with fewer embedding dimensions.

## Results

On time series forecasting (5 models × 4 datasets × 4 prediction lengths), LeTE achieves a 98% win rate on MAE and 95% on MSE against hand-crafted time encoding (HCTE), with the Transformer baseline showing a 25.1% average MAE reduction and 46.5% MSE reduction when switching from HCTE to LeTE. On dynamic graph link prediction (4 models × 4 datasets, both transductive and inductive), LeTE achieves 100% win rate over FTE on both AP and AUC-ROC, e.g., improving TGN on MOOC from 89.15 to 91.41 AP (transductive) and TCL on LastFM from 67.27 to 76.08 AP (transductive). On the sequential MNIST task where time is the sole input, LeTE substantially outperforms FTE in classification accuracy. In the real-world Tencent financial risk control application, LeTE yields the best AUC-ROC, TP10, and Recall@10, outperforming both FTE and the no-time-encoding baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares LeTE against two categories of prior time encodings: Functional Time Encoding (FTE, encompassing both Time2Vec and Functional Time Representation) and Hand-Crafted Time Encoding (HCTE). Against FTE on dynamic graph link prediction, LeTE wins on every single combination of model, dataset, and setting (transductive/inductive), with the largest gains on TCL (e.g., +8.81 AP on LastFM transductive) and TGN on MOOC (+2.26 AP transductive). Against HCTE on time series forecasting, LeTE achieves a 98% win rate on MAE across all 5 backbone models, 4 datasets, and 4 prediction lengths; FTE is inconsistent, sometimes outperforming HCTE but also failing in many cases, whereas LeTE is consistently superior. Notably, LeTE with only 2–16 dimensions outperforms the 100-dimensional FTE on dynamic graph tasks, demonstrating that the learnable transformation captures complexity that would otherwise require higher-dimensional embeddings. The paper also proves (Proposition 3.1) that both FTE variants are strict special cases of LeTE, so the empirical dominance is consistent with the theoretical generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper generalizes Functional Time Encoding (FTE) methods—specifically Time2Vec and Functional Time Representation—by replacing their fixed sine-based non-linear transformation with a learnable one, parameterized via Fourier series expansion or B-splines. The B-spline parameterization directly draws on KAN (Liu et al., 2025), which made activation functions in neural networks trainable, repurposing that idea for the specific sub-problem of time encoding. The authors prove that FTE is a strict special case of LeTE (Proposition 3.1), positioning their method as a principled superset rather than an alternative, and demonstrate invariance to time rescaling analogous to prior FTE guarantees.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Time encoding is a ubiquitous, low-level component in time series forecasting and dynamic graph models, so a drop-in replacement that consistently improves performance across five time series backbones and four dynamic graph architectures has broad practical utility. The dimensional efficiency finding—that 2- to 16-dimensional LeTE outperforms 100-dimensional FTE—suggests practitioners can reduce embedding dimensions while gaining accuracy, which matters for memory-constrained deployments. Open questions include how LeTE behaves at very long forecasting horizons, whether the learnable transformations remain stable under distribution shift, and whether the Fourier/Spline split in Combined LeTE is optimal or whether other function families (e.g., neural ODEs) might further generalize the approach.
