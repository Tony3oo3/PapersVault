## Problem

Standard LLM evaluation practices rely on the Central Limit Theorem (CLT) to compute confidence intervals, but this asymptotic approximation breaks down for the small, specialized benchmarks (tens to hundreds of examples) that are increasingly used to evaluate frontier model capabilities. The paper demonstrates that CLT-based intervals systematically underestimate uncertainty, can extend beyond the valid [0,1] range, or collapse to zero width, rendering them unreliable for model selection and deployment decisions.

## Input / Output

The methods consume binary per-question eval outcomes (length-N vectors), confusion matrix counts (TP/FP/FN/TN), or paired/clustered eval structures, with N ranging from 3 to 100 in the experimental suite. Outputs are credible or confidence intervals at user-specified confidence levels for accuracy, performance differences, odds ratios, and F1 scores, plus direct posterior probability statements such as P(θ_A > θ_B | data) that have no frequentist analogue. All methods are packaged in a lightweight Python library (bayes_evals) with computational costs under 200 ms, negligible relative to running the evals themselves.

## Method

The paper conducts a large-scale simulation study (80,000 datasets across N = 3, 10, 30, 100) measuring empirical coverage of six interval methods across five settings: IID Bernoulli questions, clustered questions (Beta-Binomial hierarchical model), independent two-model comparison, paired two-model comparison (bivariate Gaussian latent model), and non-average metrics (F1 via Dirichlet-Categorical). For Bayesian inference, the paper uses conjugate Beta-Bernoulli posteriors for simple settings and importance sampling with the prior as proposal for hierarchical and paired models, requiring only a bivariate Gaussian CDF evaluation for the paired case. The key finding is that Bayesian credible intervals with uniform Beta(1,1) priors achieve nominal coverage across all settings and sample sizes, while CLT-based and bootstrap intervals systematically under-cover; for the simple IID setting, the Wilson score interval provides a comparable frequentist alternative. Ready-to-use Python snippets are provided for each setting, and the paper ablates prior sensitivity, interval type (QBI vs. HDI), and computational cost.

## Results

Across 80,000 simulated LLM eval datasets (N = 3, 10, 30, 100; 100 parameter values × 200 datasets × 4 sizes), CLT-based 95% confidence intervals achieve only 92.5% empirical coverage at N = 100 in the IID Bernoulli setting, and coverage degrades further at smaller N. In the clustered-questions setting, neither simple CLT nor clustered-CLT intervals achieve nominal coverage at any sample size tested, while the Bayesian Beta-Binomial model with importance sampling does. For non-linear metrics like F1-score, the delta-method CLT interval and bootstrap both systematically under-cover, whereas the Dirichlet-Multinomial Bayesian credible interval tracks the nominal 1−α line across all N. Real-world examples (LangChain N=20, AIME N=15) confirm CLT intervals collapsing to zero width or extending beyond [0,1].

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Wilson score interval (Wilson, 1927), the Bayesian Beta-Bernoulli credible interval performs comparably in the IID single-model setting—both achieve correct coverage and reasonable width—though Wilson is slightly easier to implement via SciPy. The Clopper-Pearson exact interval (Clopper & Pearson, 1934) is shown to be overly conservative (too wide) and is mathematically equivalent to the Bayesian credible interval with the uniform prior removed, so the Bayesian approach effectively provides a shrinkage correction. For the odds ratio in independent model comparison, Fisher's exact test (Fisher, 1922) guarantees at least 1−α coverage but is excessively conservative at small N (equivalent to Bayesian analysis with extreme improper priors Beta(1,0) and Beta(0,1)), while the Bayesian interval achieves near-nominal coverage for both the difference and the odds ratio. The delta method (Oehlert, 1992) applied to the F1-score fails to achieve nominal coverage, and the bootstrap also systematically under-covers, whereas the Dirichlet-Multinomial Bayesian interval tracks the target. In the clustered-questions setting, the clustered standard error adjustment (Abadie et al., 2023) still produces invalid intervals, and no readily available frequentist method matches the Bayesian Beta-Binomial importance-sampling approach, which is the only method achieving correct coverage across all sample sizes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper directly challenges the CLT-based uncertainty quantification approach popularized by Miller (2024) and adopted in recent LLM eval reports (Madaan et al., 2024; Dubey et al., 2024), arguing it is systematically invalid for the small, specialized benchmarks that are becoming standard in frontier-model evaluation. Rather than proposing a new statistical method, the paper repurposes well-established tools—the Wilson score interval, Clopper-Pearson exact interval, and conjugate Bayesian posteriors (Beta-Bernoulli, Beta-Binomial, Dirichlet-Categorical)—and demonstrates via large-scale simulation that they outperform CLT and bootstrap intervals across five distinct eval settings (IID, clustered, independent comparison, paired comparison, and non-average metrics). The paper also connects to the broader NLP statistics literature (Dror et al., 2018) on the need for statistical rigour in evaluation, and to the econometrics literature on clustered standard errors (Abadie et al., 2023) that Miller (2024) borrowed for the clustered-question case.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As LLM evaluation shifts toward smaller, higher-quality, domain-specific benchmarks (AIME with 15 problems, FrontierMath with ~300, MLE-Bench with 75 competitions), the CLT-based error bars currently reported in the literature are likely invalid, potentially leading to overconfident model comparisons and deployment decisions. The paper's practical contribution—concrete Python snippets and a small library (bayes_evals)—lowers the barrier to adopting correct methods, and the finding that bootstrap intervals also fail (a less obvious result) closes off a common 'fix.' Open questions include: whether the 'few hundred' threshold in the title can be made more precise, how to handle hierarchical models spanning both questions and models (e.g., pass@K), and whether the mild prior-sensitivity of Bayesian intervals (shown in ablations) becomes problematic in adversarial or high-stakes evaluation contexts.
