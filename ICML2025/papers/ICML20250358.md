## Problem

The tensor rank condition, a key tool for identifying discrete latent variable causal structures from observed data, is inapplicable when observed variables are continuous or of mixed type, since the full-rank assumption it relies on is typically defined only for discrete contingency tables. This limits causal structure learning for latent confounders in realistic settings where measurements of the same latent variable are collected at varying levels (e.g., continuous blood oxygen alongside discrete heart rate). The paper asks whether the tensor rank condition can be extended to nonlinearly dependent continuous observations and, if so, how to make it practically computable.

## Input / Output

The method takes a dataset of n observed variables that may be a mix of continuous and discrete variables, all nonlinearly generated from discrete latent confounders with a common support size r. Continuous variables must first be discretized using a 'ranks' stop increasing' criterion (iteratively increasing bin count until the joint contingency table rank stabilizes) before any tensor rank computation. The output is a partial DAG over the latent variables (identified up to a Markov equivalence class via Meek rules) together with the causal clusters that map each latent variable to its set of observed children, including the number of latent parents per cluster in multi-factor structures.

## Method

The core theoretical contribution is showing that under the completeness condition (e.g., satisfied by exponential families and convolution densities), there exists a discretization of continuous observed variables such that the full-rank condition holds, thereby making the tensor rank condition applicable to mixed-type data (Theorem 3.4). Practically, the paper introduces a 'ranks' stop increasing' discretization procedure (Theorem 4.5) that iteratively refines bin boundaries until the rank of the joint contingency table between a continuous and a discrete variable stops increasing, guaranteeing the full-rank condition without access to the unobserved latent variable. The identification algorithm is two-stage: Step I identifies causal clusters among observed variables by distinguishing one-factor from multi-factor structures via tensor rank invariance tests, then merges clusters sharing common latent parents; Step II applies an extended PC-TENSOR-RANK algorithm that tests d-separation among latent variables by checking whether the rank of a probability tensor over their sufficient measured variables equals r^{|L_p|}. The two-sufficient measurement condition (each latent variable has at least two measured variables that are either continuous or discrete with support larger than the latent parent) is the key structural assumption enabling identifiability.

## Results

The paper evaluates its method on simulated data across three latent structure cases (Case I: L1→L2, Case II: L1→L2→L3, Case III: a more complex 4-latent structure) with sample sizes of 5k, 10k, and 50k, using F1 score for latent structure recovery and latent omission for causal cluster identification. The proposed method consistently achieves the best F1 scores and lowest latent omission across most scenarios. The TS-PC baseline performs poorly due to random discretization violating the full-rank assumption, while Mixed-LT shows suboptimal structure learning due to its restriction to tree structures and binary latent variables. Exact numerical values are reported in Figure 3 (bar charts) rather than a tabular format.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: TS-PC (PC-TENSOR-RANK, Chen et al. 2024) and Mixed-LT (Zhou et al. 2020). The proposed method outperforms both across most experimental settings. TS-PC performs poorly because it requires discrete data, and the paper's authors had to apply random discretization to continuous variables before running it, which frequently violates the full-rank condition needed for correct tensor rank computation, leading to incorrect causal structure identification. Mixed-LT shows suboptimal performance in latent structure learning because it is restricted to specific structural models (e.g., tree structures) and assumes latent variables are binary, making it unable to handle the general multi-factor DAG structures or multi-level discrete latents considered in this paper. The proposed method's advantage stems from its principled discretization procedure (ranks' stop-increasing criteria, Theorem 4.5) that ensures the full-rank condition holds, combined with the two-stage identification algorithm that handles both one-factor and multi-factor causal clusters.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the tensor rank condition framework of Chen et al. (2024, PC-TENSOR-RANK), which was restricted to purely discrete observed variables, to the mixed-type setting where observed variables can be continuous or discrete. The key theoretical bridge is the completeness condition (borrowed from the proximal causal inference literature, e.g., Miao et al. 2018), which ensures that the full-rank assumption required by the tensor rank condition can be satisfied after appropriate discretization of continuous variables. The paper also departs from mixture-model-based approaches (Kivva et al. 2021) that require unbiased parameter estimation, instead offering a statistically testable rank-based criterion. Structurally, it generalizes the pure measurement model to allow multi-factor configurations where multiple latent parents cause the same set of observed variables.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result is practically important because real-world latent variable models (e.g., disease status measured by blood oxygen levels and heart rate) naturally involve mixed-type indicators, and prior tensor-rank-based methods were inapplicable to such settings. The completeness condition is satisfied by many common parametric families (exponential families, location-scale families), making the assumption broadly applicable. However, the identification still requires the purity assumption, three-pure-children condition, and identical latent support across all latent variables—relaxations are sketched in the appendix but not fully developed. Open questions include handling impure observed structures, heterogeneous latent supports, and more general nonlinear measurement functions beyond the completeness condition.
