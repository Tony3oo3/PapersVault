## Problem

Most existing membership inference attack (MIA) studies in federated learning either ignore local differential privacy (LDP) protections or rely solely on empirical validation without theoretical guarantees on attack success rates. This paper asks whether a dishonest FL server can still perform active membership inference with provably high success rates when client data is protected by LDP, and characterizes the fundamental privacy-utility tradeoff that results.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251852/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The adversarial server takes a target sample T (an image embedding or text pattern) and crafts model parameters θ based on T; the client then computes gradients on its LDP-perturbed data D′ = M^ε(D), and the server inspects specific gradient entries—the bias b₂[1] for FC attacks or W^O weights for attention attacks—to produce a binary membership decision. The theoretical output consists of a lower bound on the attack advantage (Theorems 1 and 3) and an upper bound (Theorem 2), expressed as functions of the privacy budget ε, dataset size n, alphabet cardinality |𝒳|, and data separation Δ^ε.

## Method

The paper constructs two low-polynomial-time AMI adversaries that exploit trainable layers in federated vision models. The FC-based adversary (O(d_X²)) configures two fully connected layers to compute z₀ = max{τ^𝒟 − ‖M^ε(X) − T‖_{L₁}, 0}, so that a non-zero gradient of b₂[1] reveals T's presence; Theorem 1 lower-bounds the advantage by 1 − (n+|𝒳|−1)/(|𝒳|−1)·P_M^ε, while Theorem 2 upper-bounds it by (e^ε−1)/(e^ε+1). The attention-based adversary (O(d_X³)) exploits the Hopfield-network equivalence of self-attention by configuring head 1 to filter out the target pattern v (outputting the batch average) while head 2 does not, making the inter-head output difference a membership indicator; Theorem 3 lower-bounds the advantage in terms of projection and box probabilities over the LDP-protected distribution. The attention attack is extended from the discrete token domain of LLMs to the continuous embedding domain of Vision Transformers, where LDP noise is applied to patch embeddings before the attention layers, and the analysis accounts for how noise affects pattern separation Δ^ε and the false-negative rate via P_box.

## Results

The FC-based AMI adversary achieves near 100% membership inference success rate when the privacy budget ε approaches 6 on both CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100, with the theoretical lower bound corroborating the empirical ≈100% success at ε=8. For BitRand-protected CIFAR-10, reducing the attack success rate below 80% requires the model to suffer at least 20% accuracy loss, illustrating a severe privacy-utility tradeoff. The attention-based AMI adversary on ViT-B-32-224 (CIFAR-10) and ViT-B-32-384 (ImageNet) reaches ~100% inference success at ε=3 or higher, at which point model performance degrades significantly. The theoretical upper bound on any adversary's advantage is (e^ε−1)/(e^ε+1), which approaches 1 as ε grows, confirming that no LDP mechanism can fully eliminate the attack risk.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against competing attack methods in a head-to-head table; instead, it evaluates the same FC-based and attention-based AMI adversaries across four LDP mechanisms (BitRand, GRR, RAPPOR, dBitFlipPM) and multiple privacy budgets. Among the LDP mechanisms, GRR, RAPPOR, and dBitFlipPM preserve higher model accuracy at lower ε values but expose the model to greater privacy risk, with both empirical and theoretical attack success rates approaching 100% at ε=5–6. BitRand requires a larger privacy budget to achieve comparable model utility, but at the cost of higher noise that more effectively (though insufficiently) degrades attack success. The theoretical lower bound from Theorem 1 closely tracks the empirical success rates across all mechanisms, validating the analysis. For the attention-based attack, the authors show that increasing the hyperparameter β (which strengthens memorization) paradoxically reduces the lower bound on attack advantage under LDP, as it increases the false-negative probability P_box, and that β=0.01 yields consistently good results across realistic ε values.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the low-polynomial-time AMI attacks on FC and self-attention layers introduced by Vu et al. (AISTATS 2024), which provided theoretical success-rate guarantees only for unprotected data, to the LDP-protected setting. It also generalizes the attention-based attack from discrete LLM token embeddings (Vu et al., 2024) to continuous image patch embeddings in Vision Transformers. The security game formulation follows Nguyen et al. (AISTATS 2023), while the memorization analysis of self-attention builds directly on the Hopfield-network equivalence established by Ramsauer et al. (ICLR 2021). The key departure from prior work is providing rigorous lower and upper bounds on attack advantage under LDP, filling a gap where most existing MIA studies either ignored LDP or offered only empirical validation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The results demonstrate that LDP, widely regarded as a gold-standard privacy mechanism, does not eliminate membership inference risk in FL under an active (dishonest-server) adversary, and the noise levels required to meaningfully reduce attack success (below ~80%) cause at least 20% model accuracy degradation. This sharpens the privacy-utility tradeoff discussion for FL practitioners and suggests that LDP alone is insufficient as a defense against a malicious coordinator. Open questions include whether the theoretical bounds extend to discrete NLP embeddings (the paper acknowledges this gap), whether passive-attack variants achieve similar success under LDP, and whether alternative defenses (SMPC, homomorphic encryption) can close the gap without the same utility cost.
