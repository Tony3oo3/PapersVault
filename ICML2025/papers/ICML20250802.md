## Problem

Small language models (SLMs) cannot match the math reasoning of frontier models like OpenAI o1 without distillation from superior LLMs, which hits diminishing returns and cannot exceed the teacher's capability. Two fundamental obstacles prevent self-improvement: (1) synthesizing high-quality step-by-step reasoning trajectories is hard because correct final answers do not guarantee correct intermediate steps, and (2) training reliable process reward models requires precise step-level annotations that are impractical to obtain at scale.

## Input / Output

The method takes a math word problem as plain text input. The policy SLM generates a multi-step reasoning trajectory where each step consists of a natural-language chain-of-thought embedded as a Python comment alongside executable Python code; only steps whose concatenated code executes successfully are retained as valid nodes. The final output is a complete verified trajectory ending in a boxed numerical answer, with each intermediate step carrying a Q-value reflecting its contribution to reaching the correct solution.

## Method

rStar-Math introduces three coupled innovations. First, a code-augmented CoT synthesis method performs extensive MCTS rollouts where each step generates NL CoT plus Python code, filtering out hallucinated steps via code execution and assigning step-level Q-values through back-propagation over many rollouts (steps that consistently lead to correct answers receive higher Q-values). Second, a Process Preference Model (PPM) is trained by constructing positive-negative preference pairs from the MCTS tree—selecting top-Q-value steps that lead to correct answers versus bottom-Q-value steps leading to incorrect ones—and optimizing with a Bradley-Terry pairwise ranking loss, thereby avoiding the noise of using raw Q-values as regression targets. Third, a four-round self-evolution recipe progressively builds both the policy SLM and PPM from scratch: Round 1 bootstraps SFT data using DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Instruct (236B), Round 2 trains the first reliable PPM, Round 3 uses PPM-augmented MCTS to generate substantially higher-quality trajectories, and Round 4 increases rollouts to 64–128 to cover Olympiad-level problems, ultimately achieving 90.25% coverage of 747k math problems and boosting Qwen2.5-Math-7B from 58.8% to 90.0% on MATH.

## Results

On the MATH benchmark, rStar-Math (Qwen2.5-Math-7B) with 64 MCTS trajectories achieves 90.0% pass@1 accuracy, surpassing o1-preview (85.5%) by +4.5% and matching o1-mini (90.0%). On AIME 2024, rStar-Math solves 53.3% (8/15) of problems, exceeding o1-preview (44.6%) by +8.7% and all open-source LLMs. On Olympiad Bench, rStar-Math reaches 65.6%, outperforming o1-mini (65.3%) and DeepSeek-V3 (55.4%). The 7B model also improves Qwen2.5-Math-7B from 58.8% to 90.0% on MATH and from 0.0% to 53.3% on AIME, while the 1.5B variant improves from 51.2% to 88.6% on MATH.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GPT-4o (MATH 76.6, AIME 9.3), rStar-Math wins decisively on every benchmark, with the 7B model scoring 90.0 on MATH and 53.3 on AIME. Against o1-preview, rStar-Math surpasses it on MATH (+4.5%), AIME (+8.7%), and College Math (+2.7%), though o1-preview retains an edge on AMC 2023 (90.0 vs. 87.5). Against o1-mini, rStar-Math matches on MATH (90.0 vs. 90.0) and Olympiad Bench (65.6 vs. 65.3) but trails on AIME (53.3 vs. 56.7) and AMC (87.5 vs. 95.0). Against the Qwen2.5-Math-72B-Instruct+72B ORM System 2 baseline, rStar-Math's 7B model outperforms it on MATH (89.4 vs. 85.8), AIME (50.0 vs. 36.7), Olympiad Bench (65.3 vs. 54.5), and College Math (59.0 vs. 50.6) despite using a 10× smaller reward model. Against DeepSeek-V3 (MATH 90.2, AIME 39.2), rStar-Math wins on AIME (+14.1%), Olympiad Bench (+10.2%), and College Math (+1.6%) but is marginally behind on MATH (90.0 vs. 90.2). Against GPT-4-distilled datasets (NuminaMath-CoT: MATH 69.6; MetaMath: MATH 55.2), rStar-Math's step-by-step verified trajectories yield substantially higher SFT accuracy (78.4 on MATH, 26.7 on AIME), demonstrating that self-generated data surpasses frontier-model distillation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the MCTS-based test-time compute scaling paradigm (rStar, AlphaGo) and the process reward model framework (Lightman et al., 'Let's Verify Step by Step') into a self-evolving training loop for small language models. It departs from prior work in three key ways: (1) replacing GPT-4 distillation with code-augmented CoT synthesis where Python execution verifies each intermediate step, (2) introducing the Process Preference Model (PPM) that uses Bradley-Terry pairwise ranking loss on Q-value-selected preference pairs rather than noisy per-step Q-value regression, and (3) a four-round self-evolution recipe where both the policy SLM and PPM are built from scratch and iteratively improved without any superior teacher model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The demonstration that a 7B model can match or surpass o1-preview on MATH (90.0% vs 85.5%) entirely through self-generated data challenges the prevailing assumption that frontier reasoning requires distillation from frontier models. The PPM finding—that pairwise preference learning outperforms direct Q-value regression despite the latter having 'more information'—offers a practical recipe for training reward models without expensive human step-level annotation. Limitations include the inability to handle geometry/visual problems (8 of 7 unsolved AIME problems), reliance on a 236B bootstrap model in round 1, and the fact that only 90.25% of the 747k training problems were solvable after 4 rounds. Open questions include whether the self-evolution recipe generalizes beyond math (e.g., to theorem proving, where the PPM currently lacks step-level proof training) and whether further rounds yield diminishing returns.
