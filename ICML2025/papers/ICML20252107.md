## Problem

Prior work (Mutti et al., 2023) showed that finite-horizon GUMDPs implicitly assume an infinite number of evaluation episodes, but suggested this distinction fades in the infinite-horizon setting. This paper demonstrates that the number of trials (sampled trajectories) still plays a key role in infinite-horizon GUMDPs: for non-linear objective functions, the expected performance of a policy depends on how many trajectories are drawn to evaluate it, and the magnitude of this mismatch depends on the structure of the underlying Markov chain.

## Input / Output

The analysis takes a GUMDP tuple (state space S, action space A, transition function p, initial distribution p₀, objective f: Δ(S×A)→ℝ), a fixed stationary policy π, and the number of independently sampled trajectories K; for the discounted setting it additionally requires a discount factor γ and a truncation length H for the empirical estimator. The outputs are analytical: a lower bound on f_K(π) − f_∞(π) expressed as a weighted sum of variances (of discounted returns in the discounted case, or of Bernoulli absorption indicators in the multichain average case), a high-probability upper bound on |f(d̂_{T_K,H}) − f_∞(π)| scaling as O(1/√K + γ^H), and a complete equivalence/non-equivalence characterization depending on whether f is linear or non-linear and whether the GUMDP is unichain or multichain.

## Method

The core mechanism exploits the gap between f(E[d̂]) and E[f(d̂)] via Jensen's inequality for convex f, then quantifies this gap using strong convexity and Lipschitz continuity assumptions on f. For the discounted setting, Theorem 4.2 derives a 1/K lower bound by expanding the strong-convexity inequality and identifying the variance term with the variance of discounted returns under indicator reward functions; Theorem 4.3 then provides a high-probability upper bound combining a union-bound concentration argument over H timesteps with an exponential truncation bias term 2γ^H. For the average setting, the key structural distinction is unichain vs. multichain: under unichain GUMDPs, the ergodic theorem guarantees each trajectory's empirical occupancy converges almost surely to the unique stationary distribution, so bounded convergence yields f_K(π) = f_∞(π) (Theorem 4.4); under multichain GUMDPs, trajectories are absorbed into different recurrent classes with class-dependent probabilities, and Theorem 4.6 lower-bounds the gap by a weighted sum of Bernoulli variances of these absorption events, again with 1/K scaling. Empirical validation on three illustrative GUMDPs (entropy maximization, KL-imitation, quadratic minimization) confirms the theoretical predictions across varying K, H, and γ.

## Results

The paper establishes that for non-linear (convex) objective functions, the finite-trials objective f_K(π) differs from the infinite-trials objective f_∞(π) in general. Theorem 4.2 provides a lower bound f_K(π) − f_∞(π) ≥ (c/2K) Σ_{s,a} Var[ĝ_T(s,a)], showing the gap scales as 1/K. Theorem 4.3 gives a high-probability upper bound |f_∞(π) − f(d̂_{T_K,H})| ≤ L(√(2|S||A|log(2H/δ)/K) + 2γ^H), revealing a 1/√K dependence on the number of trajectories and an exponential bias term 2γ^H in trajectory length. Under the average setting, the gap vanishes for unichain GUMDPs (Theorem 4.4) but persists for multichain GUMDPs (Theorem 4.5), with a lower bound involving the variances of absorption probabilities into recurrent classes (Theorem 4.6).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper without traditional method-vs-method baselines; the primary comparison is between the finite-trials and infinite-trials formulations of GUMDPs across different structural settings. The key contrast is with Mutti et al. (2023), who showed a finite/infinite trials mismatch for finite-horizon GUMDPs and suggested the gap fades in the infinite-horizon setting. This paper directly refutes that suggestion, proving the mismatch persists in infinite-horizon discounted GUMDPs for any non-linear f (Theorem 4.1), and further characterizing when it vanishes (unichain average) or persists (multichain average). Empirically, under M_{f,3} (a multichain GUMDP with quadratic objective), the gap between f(d̂_{T_K,∞}) and f(d_π) remains significant even as γ→1 for small K, while under M_{f,1} and M_{f,2} (which induce unichain chains under the chosen policies) the gap fades. Adding noise to transitions (making all GUMDPs unichain) eliminates the gap in the average setting, confirming Theorem 4.4. The paper also provides the first lower bounds on the finite/infinite trials mismatch, whereas Mutti et al. (2023) only proved upper bounds in the context of policy optimization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the finite trials formulation introduced by Mutti et al. (2023) from finite-horizon to infinite-horizon GUMDPs, where the objective function depends on empirical rather than expected occupancies. While Mutti et al. suggested that the gap between finite and infinite trials formulations fades away in the infinite-horizon setting, this paper demonstrates the opposite: the number of sampled trajectories K plays a key role in both discounted and average GUMDPs. The technical analysis draws on tools from Markov chain theory (ergodic theorem, absorption probabilities), convex optimization (Jensen's inequality, strong convexity), and concentration inequalities, but the specific combination for characterizing the finite/infinite trials mismatch in infinite-horizon settings is new.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper corrects a misconception in the GUMDP literature by showing that the infinite trials assumption is not automatically satisfied in infinite-horizon settings, which has practical implications for any application where the agent is evaluated over a finite number of episodes (e.g., imitation learning, constrained RL, risk-averse RL). The clean characterization—equivalence for unichain average GUMDPs, non-equivalence for multichain and discounted cases, with explicit 1/K dependence—gives practitioners a principled understanding of when finite trials suffice. A key open question, acknowledged by the authors, is how this mismatch propagates through policy optimization (e.g., generalized policy iteration), which remains unaddressed.
