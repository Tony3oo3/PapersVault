## Problem

Existing graph rewiring methods for mitigating over-squashing in GNNs either substantially alter the original graph topology (discarding spectral properties critical for downstream tasks) or add many edges that increase computational cost and over-smoothing risk. No prior method simultaneously enhances connectivity, maintains the original graph's sparsity, and explicitly preserves the Laplacian spectrum.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251467/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an undirected weighted graph with node feature vectors as input and produces a rewired weighted graph with the same number of edges (controlled by parameter β) but improved connectivity (higher spectral gap) and a Laplacian spectrum within (1±ε)² of the original. The output graph retains weighted edges from the spectral sparsification process, and the total edge weight is preserved across both the densification and sparsification phases. The method operates as a preprocessing step before standard GNN training (GCN, GIN, GCNII).

## Method

GOKU implements a Densification-Sparsification Rewiring (DSR) paradigm in two phases. In densification (USS), the input graph is treated as a spectral sparsifier of an unknown latent graph, and an MLE problem is solved to recover 'missing edges' that are crucial for connectivity; candidate edges are identified between nodes with large absolute Fiedler vector values and small degrees, then sampled probabilistically. In sparsification (ISS), the densified latent graph is pruned using effective-resistance-based spectral sparsification where each edge is sampled with probability proportional to (1 + cosine_similarity) × effective_resistance, retaining connectivity-critical edges while filtering feature-dissimilar (noisy) edges. The key theoretical guarantee is that spectral similarity is preserved through the transitivity of the (1±ε) approximation: since both the input and output are spectral sparsifiers of the same latent graph, the output spectrum stays within (1±ε)² of the input spectrum. The number of added edges is derived from a theoretical bound (Theorem 4.1) rather than heuristically chosen, and the overall time complexity is near-linear in the number of edges.

## Results

Using GCN as the backbone, GOKU achieves the best average rank (AR) of 1.90 across 10 datasets, with particularly large gains on heterophilic node classification: Texas (72.4% vs. 67.8% for the next-best DR), Cornell (69.4% vs. 57.8%), and Wisconsin (68.8% vs. 62.8%). On homophilic datasets, GOKU is competitive (Cora 86.8%, Citeseer 73.6%) though slightly behind BORF by <1%. With GIN as the backbone, GOKU again achieves the best AR (1.80), leading on Texas (60.2%), Wisconsin (57.6%), and Chameleon (62.1%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GOKU's most dramatic wins are on heterophilic node classification: it outperforms the next-best method (DR) by 4.6 pp on Texas (72.4 vs. 67.8), 11.6 pp on Cornell (69.4 vs. 57.8), and 6.0 pp on Wisconsin (68.8 vs. 62.8), and beats BORF by 1.7 pp on Chameleon (63.2 vs. 61.5). On homophilic datasets (Cora, Citeseer), GOKU trails BORF by 0.7 pp and 0.2 pp respectively, indicating that curvature-based rewiring still has a slight edge where bottlenecks are less severe. Among graph classification baselines, GOKU leads on Mutag (81.0 vs. 80.1 for DR) and Enzymes (27.6, tied with LASER), but is marginally behind LASER on Imdb (49.8 vs. 50.3) and GTR on Proteins (71.9 vs. 72.4). DR (Delaunay Rewiring) consistently underperforms on homophilic graphs (e.g., 78.4 on Cora vs. 86.8 for GOKU) because it discards original topology entirely. Overall, GOKU achieves the best average rank (1.90 for GCN, 1.80 for GIN) across all 10 datasets, confirming that spectrum-preserving sparsification provides a more balanced improvement than methods that aggressively add edges or ignore spectral structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the spectral sparsification framework of Spielman & Srivastava (2008) and Spielman & Teng (2011), repurposing it in an 'inverse' direction: rather than sparsifying a dense graph, it treats the input graph as a sparsifier of an unknown latent graph and reconstructs that latent graph via an MLE formulation. The Densification-Sparsification Rewiring (DSR) paradigm departs from prior rewiring methods (FoSR, SDRF, BORF, GTR, Delaunay Rewiring, LASER) by being the first to explicitly preserve the Laplacian spectrum while simultaneously improving connectivity and maintaining sparsity. The ISS component adapts the ER-based sparsification of Spielman & Srivastava by incorporating node feature cosine similarity to handle heterophily, bridging graph rewiring with topological denoising ideas.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Over-squashing remains a fundamental bottleneck for GNNs on long-range dependency tasks, and the practical constraint of preserving spectral properties (critical for clustering, semi-supervised learning, and graph classification) has been largely ignored by prior rewiring methods. GOKU's near-linear time complexity makes it scalable, and the consistent spectral fidelity demonstrated across datasets offers a principled alternative to ad-hoc edge-adding heuristics. Key limitations include the reliance on weighted graphs for spectral sparsification (adding implementation complexity for standard GNNs) and the fact that the MLE solution involves approximations (edge independence, uniform weights) whose tightness is not fully characterized. Open questions include how the method interacts with very deep architectures, dynamic graphs, and whether the spectral preservation guarantee translates to downstream task guarantees beyond classification.
