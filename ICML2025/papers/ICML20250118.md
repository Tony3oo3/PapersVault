## Problem

Bandit learning in two-sided matching markets with uncertainty on both the user and arm sides has been largely unaddressed, with prior work either restricting to single-sided uncertainty or relying on the standard Gale-Shapley algorithm, which over partial rankings can only guarantee weakly stable matchings that may not be truly stable. Moreover, standard GS-based approaches require resolving the top N ranks for all agents, failing to capture the intrinsic complexity of the stable matching problem.

## Input / Output

Each round, users and arms observe noisy 1-subgaussian rewards from their matched pair (or zero if unmatched/collided), and maintain UCB-LCB-based partial rankings represented as directed acyclic graphs over the opposite side. In the decentralized variant, coordination is achieved through only two shared binary flags (restart and success), making the communication budget extremely restrictive compared to full (user, arm) broadcast. The output is a per-round matching between N users and K arms (N ≤ K) with a logarithmic pessimal stable regret bound O(K log(T)/Δ_A²) that depends on an instance-dependent admissible gap Δ_A rather than the global minimum gap Δ_min.

## Method

The core algorithm replaces the standard Gale-Shapley procedure with the Extended Gale-Shapley algorithm from Irving (1994), which either recovers a super-stable matching under the current UCB-LCB partial rankings or certifies that none exists. The key insight, building on Spieker (1995), is that a super-stable matching under a partial rank compatible with the true full rank is guaranteed to be a true stable matching, so exploitation is regret-free whenever the partial rank lies in the admissible set. When no super-stable matching is found, the algorithm triggers round-robin exploration, and the number of exploration rounds needed is governed by the admissible gap Δ_A (the maximum minimum gap over admissible partial ranks), which can be substantially larger than Δ_min. For the decentralized setting, the centralized algorithm is emulated in phases of at most N² steps using two shared binary flags to synchronize phase transitions and signal success/failure, incurring only an O(N²) additive regret overhead. The paper further establishes the first instance-dependent lower bound for binary stable regret via a KL-divergence optimization over a novel 'bad parameter' set defined through the admissible partial rank structure.

## Results

The paper achieves a logarithmic pessimal stable regret bound of O(K log(T)/Δ_A²) in the centralized setting (Theorem 3.5) and O(K log(T)/Δ_A²) + O(N²) in the decentralized setting (Theorem 4.1), where Δ_A is the instance-dependent admissible gap. This improves upon prior two-sided results that depend on the smaller minimum gap Δ_min (with Δ_A ≥ Δ_min, and the ratio Δ_A/Δ_min can be unbounded). The paper also establishes the first instance-dependent lower bound for binary stable regret of Ω(K_eff log(T)/Δ_eff²) in the centralized setting, confirming that the admissible gap is an intrinsic hardness parameter. Experiments with N=K=8, Δ_min=0.2, Δ_max=0.5 show the Extended-GS algorithm consistently outperforms Centralized ETGS across noise levels and gap configurations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary experimental baseline is Centralized ETGS (Kong & Li, 2023), which explores until the top-N ranks are fully resolved for all users and arms before committing to the Gale-Shapley solution. The proposed Extended-GS algorithm consistently achieves lower cumulative pessimal stable regret across all tested configurations (N=K=5 and N=K=8, varying σ and gap parameters), because it identifies an admissible partial rank much earlier than the full top-N resolution required by ETGS. Theoretically, the paper's O(K log(T)/Δ_A²) bound strictly improves upon all prior two-sided results (ETGS+BB, CA-ETC) that depend on Δ_min, since Δ_A ≥ Δ_min with the gap ratio Δ_A/Δ_min being unbounded in general instances. The paper also provides the first instance-dependent lower bound for two-sided matching markets, whereas prior lower bounds (Sankararaman et al., 2021) apply only to one-sided uncertainty in specialized serial dictatorship settings. The decentralized variant incurs only an O(N²) additive regret over the centralized bound, using just 2 shared binary flags per round.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant line of bandit-learning-in-matching-markets work (Liu et al. 2020, Sankararaman et al. 2021, Kong & Li 2023) that pairs UCB-style exploration with the standard Gale-Shapley algorithm, which requires resolving the full top-N ranking before committing. Instead, it repurposes Irving's (1994) Extended Gale-Shapley algorithm and the super-stable matching concept, together with Spieker's (1995) lattice structure, to exploit partial rankings directly: a super-stable match under a compatible partial rank is guaranteed stable under the true full rank, eliminating the need for complete top-N resolution. For the lower bound, the paper adapts the instance-dependent optimization framework of Combes et al. (2017) to the multi-solution, two-sided setting, introducing a 'bad parameter' set defined via the admissible partial rank set rather than a single optimal arm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The introduction of the admissible gap Δ_A as the governing hardness parameter is the paper's most consequential conceptual contribution: it can be arbitrarily larger than the standard minimum gap Δ_min (with unbounded ratio in a constructed example), meaning prior GS-based algorithms overestimate the intrinsic difficulty of many instances. The 2-bit decentralized protocol is a practical contribution for settings without a central coordinator, though the O(N²) regret overhead and the tension between sparse communication and incentive compatibility remain open. Tight regret bounds are still unresolved—the current upper bound uses sub-optimal round-robin exploration, and the lower bound's connection to set covers of the admissible set boundary suggests the true complexity may be even more structured. Extending from pessimal to user-optimal or social-optimal stable regret, and incorporating contextual rewards, are natural next steps.
