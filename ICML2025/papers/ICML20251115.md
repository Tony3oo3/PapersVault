## Problem

Unsupervised pre-training and transfer learning are standard practices for initializing neural networks, yet their benefits in terms of sample complexity for high-dimensional supervised learning with non-convex losses lack rigorous theoretical characterization. This paper asks: under what conditions do pre-training and transfer learning provably reduce the number of labeled samples needed to recover the true parameter vector, and by how much, compared to random initialization?

## Input / Output

The supervised task takes labeled pairs (y_i, a_i) where features a_i are Gaussian with spiked covariance N(0, I_d + λvv^T) and the spike direction v is correlated with the unknown parameter v_0 (v·v_0 = η_1). Pre-training additionally consumes unlabeled feature samples a'_i (same distribution as a_i) to run PCA and estimate the spike direction; transfer learning consumes a sequence of correlated vectors v^(d) from a related task. The output is the recovered parameter vector v_0 via spherical SGD, along with high-dimensional sample complexity bounds N = f(d) characterizing when recovery succeeds or fails.

## Method

The paper analyzes a single-layer network with known activation f trained by spherical SGD (step size δ/d) on the squared loss, where the population loss depends on a 2D correlation vector (m_1, m_2) tracking alignment with v_0 and the residual spike direction. Pre-training is modeled as PCA on unlabeled data to estimate the spike direction v, then initializing SGD with X_0 = v̂; transfer learning initializes with correlated vectors from a related task. The key technical insight is that the spiked covariance introduces a local minimum at m_1 = 0 whose attractor region is dimension-independent, trapping random initializations regardless of data volume. The main results establish that pre-training achieves recovery with N = αd (α = ω(1)) samples under Assumption 3.2 on the population loss landscape, while random initialization requires N = Ω(d²) (Theorem 3.4), and in the perfectly aligned case (η_1 = 1) random initialization fails entirely while pre-training succeeds in linear time (Theorem 3.5). For transfer learning with isotropic features, the sample complexity improves polynomially from d^{k-2} to d^{2ζ(k-2)} where ζ parameterizes the correlation decay rate and k is the information exponent.

## Results

The paper establishes three main theoretical results on sample complexity for single-layer networks with spiked Gaussian features. (1) Pre-training via PCA yields effective initialization requiring only N = αd steps with α = ω(1) (Theorem 3.3), i.e., just beyond linear in dimension. (2) Random initialization requires at least N = Ω(d²) samples under moment conditions on the activation (Theorem 3.4), giving a polynomial separation of d^ζ for all ζ < 1. (3) In the extreme case where the spike is perfectly aligned with the parameter vector (η₁ = 1), random initialization cannot recover the solution even with exponential data, while pre-training solves the problem in linear time (Theorem 3.5). For transfer learning with isotropic features, the sample complexity improves from α ≫ d^{k-2}(log d)² to α ≫ d^{2ζ(k-2)}(log d)², where ζ parameterizes the correlation decay (Theorem 4.2).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three initialization strategies: random (uniform on the sphere), pre-training via PCA on unlabeled features, and transfer learning from a correlated upstream task. Pre-training dominates random initialization by a polynomial factor in dimension: where random requires Ω(d²) samples, pre-training needs only O(d·ω(1)), yielding a separation of d^ζ for any ζ < 1. In the most striking case (Theorem 3.5, η₁ = 1), the gap becomes infinite — random initialization is fundamentally incapable of recovery regardless of sample size, while pre-training achieves linear-time recovery. Transfer learning with isotropic features provides a more moderate but still polynomial improvement: for information exponent k and correlation decay rate ζ ∈ (0, 1/2), the required samples drop from d^{k-2}(log d)² to d^{2ζ(k-2)}(log d)². When the correlation is dimension-independent (ζ = 0), transfer learning reduces the requirement to just α = ω(1), matching the pre-training result. The simulations in Section 5.2 (d = 1000, f(x) = x³ - 3x) empirically confirm these theoretical predictions, showing pre-training converges to the global optimum while random initialization makes negligible progress.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the information-exponent framework of Ben Arous et al. (2021) for single-index models from isotropic Gaussian features to spiked covariance, introducing a 2-dimensional population dynamics (correlations with both the parameter vector and the spike residual) in place of the 1-dimensional dynamics of the isotropic case. The proof technique draws directly on the bounding-flows methodology from Ben Arous et al. (2020a, 2020b). Conceptually, it departs from Damian et al. (2022) on transfer learning for index models by fixing the link function and varying the representation (spike direction), rather than the reverse, and from Mousavi-Hosseini et al. (2023) by using the spiked-covariance structure to model pre-training information rather than as a data-generating nuisance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first rigorous sample-complexity separation between pre-trained and random initialization for non-convex high-dimensional learning, showing polynomial (d^ζ) and even exponential gaps. Theorem 3.5 reveals a fundamental limitation: under spiked covariance with perfect alignment, no amount of labeled data suffices from random initialization, yet pre-training solves the problem in near-linear time—challenging the intuition that data volume alone overcomes non-convexity. Limitations include restriction to single-layer networks, PCA as the sole pre-training method, Gaussian features, and specific activation functions (Hermite polynomials of degree ≥3). Open questions include whether these separations persist for multi-layer networks, more expressive pre-training (autoencoders, contrastive methods), and non-Gaussian or structured features.
