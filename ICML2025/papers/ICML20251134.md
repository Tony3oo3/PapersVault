## Problem

Existing interaction attribution methods (Faith-Shapley, Faith-Banzhaf, Shapley-Taylor) enumerate all O(n^d) feature interactions, making them infeasible beyond ~20 input features, while marginal methods like SHAP and LIME scale to large n but completely miss interactions. This paper asks whether interaction attribution can be performed at LLM scale (n ≈ 1000) with reasonable computational complexity, exploiting the natural sparsity of interactions observed in real-world data.

## Input / Output

SPEX takes a trained model (any architecture: decoder LLMs, encoder-only models, or vision-language models) and a single input with n features (words, sentences, or image patches). It queries the model under structured masking patterns—replacing features with [MASK] tokens for text or applying Gaussian blur to image patches—and scalarizes outputs into a real-valued function (positive-class logit for classification, negative log-perplexity for generation). The output is a sparse set of Fourier interaction coefficients F(k) over a small index set K, from which a surrogate function f̂ is constructed; standard attribution scores (Shapley values, Banzhaf interaction indices, Möbius coefficients) are then derived in closed form from these coefficients.

## Method

SPEX exploits the observation that LLM value functions are sparse and low-degree in the Boolean Fourier (Hadamard) domain, and replaces exhaustive O(n^d) interaction enumeration with a sparse Fourier transform. The key mechanism is embedding a BCH channel code into the masking pattern design: random linear codes M_c create controlled aliasing (coefficient collapse) that produces singletons, while rows of a BCH parity matrix serve as shift vectors whose sign patterns encode the identity of each dominant interaction as a codeword. After collecting ≈ C·2^b·t·log(n) model queries and applying the Boolean Fourier transform, an iterative message-passing algorithm decodes the dominant interaction indices via Berlekamp-Massey BCH decoding (with Chase soft decoding for robustness), estimating each Fourier coefficient and subtracting its contribution before decoding the next. The orthonormality of the Fourier basis—unlike the non-orthogonal Möbius basis used in prior work—prevents noise amplification, and the overall complexity is Õ(sdn) rather than Ω(n^d), enabling scaling to n ≈ 1000.

## Results

SPEX achieves up to 20% higher faithfulness (R²) than the best marginal attribution methods across all three datasets. On Sentiment at n=1024–2047, SPEX reaches R²=0.71 versus 0.59 for both LIME and Banzhaf; on DROP at n=32–63, SPEX achieves R²=0.58 versus 0.29 for LIME/Banzhaf; on HotpotQA at n=8–15, SPEX reaches R²=0.87 versus 0.38 for LIME. Where interaction indices are computable (n≤64), SPEX matches or exceeds the best Faith-Banzhaf (4th-order) values (e.g., 0.93 vs 0.90 on Sentiment n=32–63), while maintaining constant runtime as n grows. The learned surrogate functions are extremely sparse, with only ~10⁻¹⁰⁰ percent of Fourier coefficients non-zero.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SPEX consistently outperforms all three marginal attribution baselines (LIME, SHAP, Banzhaf) across every dataset and input length. The gap is most dramatic on DROP and HotpotQA, where SPEX achieves roughly 2× the faithfulness of LIME/Banzhaf (e.g., 0.58 vs 0.29 on DROP n=32–63; 0.87 vs 0.38 on HotpotQA n=8–15). Against interaction indices, SPEX matches or slightly exceeds the best Faith-Banzhaf (4th-order) where both are computable (e.g., 1.00 vs 1.00 on Sentiment n=8–15; 0.93 vs 0.90 on Sentiment n=32–63), but the critical advantage is scalability: Faith-Banzhaf, Faith-Shap, and Shapley-Taylor become infeasible beyond n≈64, while SPEX maintains competitive faithfulness up to n≈2000 with no runtime increase. SHAP and Shapley-Taylor frequently produce negative R² values (e.g., SHAP=−17.31 on Sentiment n=1024–2047), indicating they fail to reconstruct model outputs at scale. On the HotpotQA recovery-rate metric, SPEX achieves the highest recovery of human-labeled interactions across all sample sizes, reaching near-maximum performance with far fewer training masks than Faith-Banzhaf or Faith-Shap.

## Relation to Prior Work

SPEX builds on the sparse Fourier (Hadamard) transform framework from Li et al. (2014) and the q-ary sparse transform codebase of Erginbas et al. (2023), but introduces a novel application of BCH channel codes and Berlekamp-Massey decoding to the problem of identifying sparse feature interactions in LLMs. It departs from Kang et al. (2024), which attempted sparse interaction attribution via the Möbius transform but failed in practice due to noise amplification from the non-orthogonal basis; SPEX replaces this with the orthonormal Fourier transform to ensure robustness. The key conceptual departure from all prior interaction attribution methods (Faith-Shap, Faith-Banzhaf, Shapley-Taylor) is the reframing of interaction discovery as a channel decoding problem, avoiding the O(n^d) exhaustive enumeration that limits existing approaches to n ≈ 20.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SPEX closes a significant practical gap by being the first model-agnostic interaction attribution method that scales to ~1000 input features, enabling trustworthy explanations for LLMs in high-stakes domains like medical diagnosis and drug discovery where interactions among hundreds of features are critical. The extreme sparsity observed (non-zero coefficients at ratios as low as 10^-100) provides empirical evidence that LLM outputs are driven by very few interactions, a structural property that future methods can exploit. Key limitations include the central sparsity assumption (SPEX fails without it), non-trivial sample complexity (~C·2^b·t·log(n) model inferences), and the open question of how to make high-order interactions interpretable to humans. Future directions include adaptive masking for sample efficiency, combining with internal model structure, and connections to sparse attention mechanisms in transformers.
