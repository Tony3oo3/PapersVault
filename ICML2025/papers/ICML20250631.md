## Problem

Affinity bias in hiring creates a self-reinforcing feedback loop: today's hires shape tomorrow's committee, amplifying bias toward already-represented groups. Standard bandit algorithms (UCB, EXP3) suffer linear regret in this setting because the observed rewards are multiplicatively reweighted by the fraction of times each arm has been pulled, while the true rewards remain fixed and unobserved. The paper formalizes this as a new 'affinity bandits' problem and asks: what is the fundamental cost of this feedback loop, and can one achieve sublinear regret without knowing the bias model?

## Input / Output

The algorithm receives a time horizon n and interacts with a K-armed environment where each arm i has a fixed but unknown mean μ_i ∈ [0,1]. At each step, the policy selects an arm and observes a scalar feedback Y_t whose conditional mean is μ_{A_t}·W_{A_t}(t), where W_i(t) = f(T_i^bias(t-1)/(t^bias-1)) is an unknown non-decreasing L-Lipschitz function of the cumulative pull fraction. Regret is measured against the unobserved true rewards X_{i,t}, not the biased feedback, and the algorithm does not know f, the initial biases T_i^0, or the true means. The output is a deterministic round-robin elimination schedule with an instance-dependent logarithmic regret bound.

## Method

The paper proposes a phased elimination algorithm (a variant of Auer & Ortner, 2010) that plays each active arm in round-robin order for m_r ≈ log(n)/Δ̃_r² pulls per round, then eliminates arms whose empirical feedback falls more than Δ̃_r = 2^{-r} below the best. The key analytical insight is that the bias term in the expected gap decomposition—arising from differing reweightings W_i(r) across arms—scales as Δ̃_r²·L·(1+(T_max^0−T_min^0)/K)·log(log n)/log n, which is negligible relative to the Δ̃_r elimination threshold for large n, ensuring only truly suboptimal arms are removed. For the lower bound, the authors develop a novel KL-divergence decomposition for biased environments where the divergence depends on a 'biased proxy' E[Σ W_i(t)²·1{A_t=i}] rather than E[T_i(n)] directly, combined with a pigeonholing argument over a random set of arms to derandomize the stopping-time analysis. The resulting lower bound shows any consistent policy must incur regret at least f(O(log K/K))^{-2}·Σ log(n)/Δ_i, matching the upper bound up to polylog(K) factors.

## Results

The paper's main quantitative contribution is a pair of matching (up to polylog factors) instance-dependent regret bounds for the affinity bandit setting. The elimination algorithm (Algorithm 1) achieves regret $R_{\nu,\pi}(n) \lesssim f(1/15K)^{-2} \sum_{i:\Delta_i>0} \log(n)/\Delta_i$, while the lower bound shows any consistent policy must incur regret $\gtrsim f(\mathcal{O}(\log K / K))^{-2} \sum_{i:\Delta_i>0} \log(n)/\Delta_i$. Compared to the standard Lai–Robbins lower bound of $\sum 2\log(n)/\Delta_i$, the affinity setting is inherently harder by a multiplicative factor of roughly $f(\mathcal{O}(\log K/K))^{-2}$, which can be as large as $\Omega(K^2)$ for polynomial bias functions $f(x)=x^\alpha$. Empirically, UCB, EXP3, and EXP3-IX all pull the suboptimal arm more than 50% of the time with constant probability under moderate initial bias, confirming linear regret in practice.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its elimination algorithm against four standard bandit baselines in the affinity bandit setting. UCB (Auer et al., 2002), EXP3 (Auer et al., 1995), and EXP3-IX (Kocák et al., 2014) all suffer linear regret: Figure 3 shows that for high initial bias on the suboptimal arm, all three algorithms pull the suboptimal arm more than 50% of the time horizon with probability bounded away from zero, and even with high initial bias on the optimal arm the failure probability remains non-negligible. UCB-V (Audibert et al., 2007), which attempts to 'debias' the feedback by multiplying by $W_{A_t}(t)^{-1}$, also fails in practice (Figure 4): the inverse reweighting causes the variance to explode for infrequently-played arms, leading to regret scaling that is unlikely to be logarithmic. The proposed phased-elimination algorithm is the only method that achieves logarithmic instance-dependent regret, and its guarantee nearly matches the proven lower bound up to an $\mathcal{O}(\log(K)^{2L'})$ factor (Corollary 5.4), making it essentially optimal in this setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper introduces 'affinity bandits,' a new non-stationary bandit variant where observed feedback is multiplicatively reweighted by the fraction of times each arm has been pulled, while the true reward remains fixed. It departs from prior non-stationary bandit work (Gittins, Whittle, Kleinberg & Immorlica) where the environment itself changes, and from biased-feedback bandits (Tang & Ho, Gaucher, Schumann) where bias depends on pull counts or linear functions of the current action rather than the global fraction of pulls with mutual exclusion across arms. The lower bound proof extends the divergence decomposition framework of Garivier et al. and Kaufmann et al. to handle time-varying, policy-dependent feedback, requiring new pigeonholing and stopping-time arguments. The algorithm builds on the phased elimination approach of Auer & Ortner (2010), adapted to handle unknown bias models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a rigorous mathematical characterization of how affinity bias creates a self-reinforcing feedback loop in hiring, proving that standard algorithms (UCB, EXP3) incur linear regret and that the problem is fundamentally harder than standard bandits by a multiplicative factor depending on the number of groups. The key practical insight is that a simple round-robin elimination strategy—giving every group a chance before eliminating—is nearly optimal, suggesting systemic 'give everyone a fair shot' policies are theoretically justified. Limitations include assuming fixed arm means (groups don't improve or degrade), an ever-growing committee (full history), and groups defined by skill-set rather than sensitive attributes; generalizing to time-varying rewards, bounded committee sizes, or contextual bandits remains open.
