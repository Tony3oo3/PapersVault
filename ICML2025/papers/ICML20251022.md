## Problem

Prior work on correlation detection between unlabeled random graphs (e.g., Wu et al., 2023) assumed full observation of all edges, which is impractical when only partial graph data is available due to API limitations, testing costs, or visualization constraints. This paper asks: when sampling induced subgraphs of size s from each of two n-vertex Gaussian Wigner graphs, what is the optimal rate of s required to detect edge correlation through a latent vertex permutation?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251022/cropped_page8_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes two weighted random graphs G1, G2 with n vertices each (edges drawn from independent standard normals under H0, or correlated normals with coefficient ρ under H1) and samples induced subgraphs of s vertices from each without replacement. The output is a binary hypothesis test decision (H0: independent vs. H1: correlated) based on a test statistic that maximizes a similarity score over injective mappings between vertex subsets of size m = ⌊(1−ε)s²/n⌋. The key constraint is s ≤ n, and the sampling introduces a random overlap of vertices whose size follows a hypergeometric distribution HG(n,s,s).

## Method

The theoretical analysis uses two complementary estimators for the possibility results: a maximal overlap estimator f(x,y)=xy for ρ=1−Ω(1) and a minimal mean-squared error estimator f(x,y)=−½(x−y)² for ρ=1−o(1), each controlled via Chernoff bounds and concentration inequalities on the hypergeometric overlap. For impossibility, the paper employs a conditional second moment method where the likelihood ratio is decomposed using a correlated functional digraph (from Huang et al., 2024) into independent paths and cycles; the key insight is that the second moment reduces to the 'core set' I* (vertices participating in cycles), whose size is bounded via hypergeometric concentration, yielding the tight threshold s² ≍ (n log n / log(1/(1−ρ²)) ∨ n). An efficient polynomial-time algorithm approximates the optimal estimator by first matching small cliques of size K₁ to find candidate mappings, merging K₂ compatible candidates into a seed, and then iteratively extending the mapping one vertex pair at a time until reaching size m.

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 1.4) establishes that the optimal sample complexity for correlation detection is s ≍ (n log n / log(1/(1−ρ²)) ∨ n)^(1/2): above this rate, TV(P,Q) ≥ 0.9 (strong detection when s² = ω(n)), and below it, TV(P,Q) ≤ 0.1 (vanishing when s² = o(n)). Empirically, the proposed clique-based algorithm (Algorithm 1) achieves AUC = 1.0 for ρ = 1−10⁻⁶ and ρ = 1−10⁻⁷ at n=50, s=30, while the AUC increases from 0.52 to 1.0 as sample size s grows from 10 to 50 (fixed ρ=0.98), and from 0.55 to 1.0 as ρ increases from 0.95 to 0.99 (fixed s=40).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its clique-based detection algorithm against the classical Graph Edit Distance (GED) (Sanfeliu & Fu, 1983), a widely used graph similarity measure. At n=50, s=30, ε=0.01, the proposed method substantially outperforms GED across all correlation levels tested: at ρ=0.98, the proposed method achieves AUC 0.92 versus GED's 0.53 (a 39-point gap); at ρ=1−10⁻⁶, the proposed method reaches AUC 1.00 versus GED's 0.73; and at ρ=1−10⁻⁷, the proposed method again achieves AUC 1.00 versus GED's 0.88. The advantage is most pronounced at moderate correlation (ρ=0.98), where GED is barely above chance (0.53) while the proposed method is near-perfect (0.92). The paper also notes that a trivial pairwise-difference baseline (equivalent to K₁=2, K₂=1 in Algorithm 1) is outperformed by the full clique-based approach (K₁=4, K₂=3), confirming that exploiting higher-order structure is essential for effective detection.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the correlation detection framework for the Gaussian Wigner model from the fully observed setting (Wu et al., 2023) to the practically motivated scenario where only induced subgraphs of size s are sampled. The impossibility proof for weak correlation directly reduces to the detection threshold of Wu et al. (2023) via a data processing argument, while the strong correlation regime requires a new conditional second moment analysis built on the correlated functional digraph introduced in Huang et al. (2024) to decompose edge dependencies into independent paths and cycles. The proposed clique-based iterative algorithm draws on the broader graph matching literature (seeded matching, message passing, tree counting) but adapts these ideas to the partial-alignment setting where only a fraction of vertices are shared between the two sampled subgraphs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes the first tight sample complexity bound for correlation detection under partial graph observation, with direct implications for privacy (impossibility results guarantee that no test can de-anonymize a network below the threshold) and for cost-efficient biological network analysis (possibility results indicate when sampled subgraphs suffice). Key open questions include: theoretical guarantees for the proposed polynomial-time algorithm, extension to the Erdős-Rényi model where the edge probability parameter p introduces additional regimes, and whether the low-degree likelihood ratio conjecture yields matching computational hardness thresholds as a function of sample size. The gap between the information-theoretic threshold and the best known polynomial-time algorithm remains unresolved.
