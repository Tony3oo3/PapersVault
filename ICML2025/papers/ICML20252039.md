## Problem

Natural language communication between LLM agents incurs high inference costs that scale with the number of agents and messages, and the decoding process discards rich internal information by collapsing the model's full belief distribution into a single token. This paper asks whether LLMs can communicate more effectively and efficiently by exchanging internal activations rather than natural language, and whether such activation-based communication can outperform state-of-the-art natural language debate protocols.

## Input / Output

The method takes two text prompts: one given to model A (encoding the information to be shared) and one given to model B (encoding the task to be solved). Model A runs only a partial forward pass up to layer k, and model B runs a partial forward pass up to layer j; the last-token activations at these layers are combined via a function f, and B then completes its forward pass to produce a standard decoded text response. No additional tokens are exchanged between models, and the output format is identical to a single-model completion, making the method drop-in compatible with existing evaluation pipelines.

## Method

The core mechanism pauses Transformer LM B's computation at an intermediate layer j in the residual stream, replaces B's last-token activation at that layer with a function f of A's last-token activation at layer k (and B's own activation), then resumes B's forward pass through the remaining layers to completion. Three non-learned functions are explored—sum, mean, and replace—with replace (directly substituting A's activation for B's) performing best because it guarantees the output vector remains within B's activation space. When A and B have different dimensions or misaligned activation spaces, a task-agnostic linear mapping W ∈ R^{d_B × d_A} can be learned once per model pair on general text (e.g., C4) to project A's activations into B's space, introducing zero task-specific parameters or data. The key insight is that intermediate-layer activations (around the halfway point of computation) encode a strict superset of next-token prediction and vocabulary-level belief, so a single activation grafting step communicates all of A's knowledge about its prompt to B in one operation, eliminating the iterative multi-round structure required by natural language debate.

## Results

On seven reasoning benchmarks (Biographies, GSM8k, and five MMLU subsets), activation communication (AC) with f=replace consistently outperforms both single-model baselines (LLaMA-3.2-3B and LLaMA-3.1-8B) and Natural Language Debate (NLD), achieving up to 27.0% improvement over NLD across six of the seven datasets while using less than 1/4 the compute. On the coordination games, AC(replace) with LLaMA-3.1-8B reaches 83.0% on Countries and 95.7% on Tip Sheets, nearly closing the gap to the single-agent skyline (86.0% and 100.0%) while outperforming natural language communication (77.0% and 85.7%). Across six cross-family model pairs (Qwen-2.5, Gemma-2, LLaMA-3), AC beats NLD on every pair without requiring a learned projection layer.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is Natural Language Debate (NLD) from Du et al. (2023), where two LLMs iteratively refine responses over multiple rounds. AC outperforms NLD on 6 of 7 reasoning benchmarks (e.g., 85.0% vs. 83.0% on HS Psychology, 74.0% vs. 63.0% on Public Relations, 47.0% vs. 37.0% on Formal Logic) and on 48 of 57 full MMLU datasets, while using less than 1/4 the compute. AC also consistently beats both individual single-model baselines across all seven benchmarks, whereas NLD does not always improve over the larger single model. On the coordination games, AC(replace) substantially outperforms natural language communication (83.0% vs. 77.0% on Countries; 95.7% vs. 85.7% on Tip Sheets for LLaMA-3.1-8B). In cross-family experiments (Table 4), AC beats NLD on all six model pairs tested, including pairs from entirely different architectures (e.g., Qwen-2.5-1.5B + Gemma-2-2B: 73.0/55.0 vs. 49.3/50.0 on Biographies/GSM8k), demonstrating robustness without a learned projection layer.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm of natural language inter-agent communication (multi-agent debate, Du et al. 2023; CIPHER, Pham et al. 2024) by proposing activation-level grafting as the communication medium. Conceptually, it unifies and simplifies activation-level model grafting (CALM, Bansal et al. 2024, which learns an attention layer per model pair) by showing that zero-parameter functions—sum, mean, or simple replacement—suffice, and it repurposes activation engineering techniques (Li et al. 2024; Turner et al. 2023) from single-model steering to inter-model information transfer. The paper also positions itself as 'outsourcing chain-of-thought to another model' in the spirit of latent reasoning work (Geiping et al. 2025; Hao et al. 2024), but across distinct models rather than within a single model's recurrent depth.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: activation communication saves over 3/4 of the inference compute relative to natural language debate while simultaneously improving accuracy by up to 27%, making multi-agent LLM systems substantially more accessible. The cross-family results (Qwen, Gemma, LLaMA) without any learned projection layer suggest that intermediate-layer activations share enough geometric structure across architectures to be directly transferable, which is a useful empirical finding for the interpretability community. Key limitations include the requirement for white-box model access (no API-only use), the assumption of roughly aligned embedding spaces when not using the learned mapping W, and the fundamental interpretability tradeoff of communicating in high-dimensional activation space rather than human-readable text. Open directions include applying AC to more complex coordination games (Diplomacy, negotiation), using small tunable models as 'knowledge bases' during large-model decoding, and developing interpretability tools for activation-based messages.
