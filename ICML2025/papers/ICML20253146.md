## Problem

Electromagnetic structure (EMS) design faces a dual challenge: the design space is astronomically large (e.g., 2^288 for a 12×24 binary grid), and each evaluation requires expensive full-wave electromagnetic simulation (660–42,780 seconds per design). Existing predictor-based and generative methods either suffer from the curse of dimensionality when operating at pixel level or demand tens of thousands of labeled samples to train high-fidelity surrogates, making them impractical under the tight budgets and deadlines of real-world industrial EMS design.

## Input / Output

The method takes a binary grid layout (e.g., 12×12×2 for DualFSS or 15×20 for HGA, where each cell is metal or empty), multi-objective performance criteria defined over specific frequency bands (e.g., S-parameter suppression in 31.5–34.5 GHz and 10.5–15.5 GHz, or Realized Gain in 2.45–2.55 GHz and 5–6 GHz), a small initial dataset of 300 simulated samples, and a total simulation budget of 1000 evaluations. It outputs an optimized binary layout whose performance is measured by the minimum across all objective criteria (worst-case aggregation), with individual per-band metrics also reported. The quadtree representation compresses the effective search space from 2^(m×n) to 2^|L|, where |L| ≤ N_max (set to 32), so the predictor operates on a much lower-dimensional variable set.

## Method

PQS integrates two complementary modules. First, the Quadtree-based Search Strategy (QSS) represents the layout as a hierarchical quadtree where homogeneous regions remain as single-bit leaf nodes while intricate regions are recursively split into four children; a progressive tree search starts from the root and iteratively resamples or splits randomly chosen leaf nodes, reconstructing the full matrix and evaluating it with a ResNet50 predictor, maintaining a Top-K list until the leaf count reaches N_max. Second, the Consistency-based Sample Selection (CSS) mechanism computes Kendall's tau between the predictor's ranking at consecutive iterations to gauge prediction reliability, then allocates a fraction τ of the evaluation budget to predictor-ranked candidates (exploitation) and the remaining (1−τ) to random samples (exploration), adaptively balancing the two as model confidence evolves. The predictor is retrained online after each batch of true simulations, and N_max grows across outer-loop iterations to permit progressively more complex designs, all within a fixed 1000-simulation budget.

## Results

On the Dual-layer Frequency Selective Surface (DualFSS) task, PQS achieves an aggregate objective of 15.20 dB with only 1000 simulations, surpassing the best baseline (cVAE at 8.93 dB) by 70% while using 7× fewer simulations. On the High-gain Antenna (HGA) task, PQS reaches 3.66 dB aggregate objective versus the best baseline InvGrad at 3.18 dB (4000 simulations), a 15% improvement with 4× fewer evaluations. In robustness tests (10 independent runs on HGA), PQS achieves 4.34 ± 0.34 dB aggregate objective with the lowest variance among all methods, while baselines show substantially higher standard deviations (e.g., cVAE: −4.04 ± 1.09 dB).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PQS outperforms all nine baselines on both tasks. Against predictor-based methods (Surrogate-RS, Surrogate-GA, Surrogate-GW, TS-DDEO, SAHSO), PQS achieves substantially higher aggregate objectives on DualFSS (15.20 vs. 3.19–5.81 dB) and HGA (3.66 vs. −1.66 to 3.09 dB), demonstrating that surrogate-guided search without hierarchical representation struggles under tight budgets. Against generative methods (cGAN, cVAE, IDN, GenCO), PQS wins decisively on DualFSS (15.20 vs. 1.18–8.93 dB) and HGA (3.66 vs. −11.96 to −0.53 dB), despite using 7× fewer simulations (1000 vs. 7000) or 4× fewer (1000 vs. 4000). The closest competitor is InvGrad on HGA (3.18 dB), but it requires 4000 simulations and still trails PQS by 0.48 dB. Notably, several predictor-based baselines (Surrogate-GW, TS-DDEO, SAHSO) produce negative aggregate objectives on HGA, indicating they fail to find viable designs under the 1000-simulation budget, whereas PQS consistently delivers positive performance across all 10 independent runs (4.34 ± 0.34 dB).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from both dominant paradigms in EMS design—surrogate-assisted optimization (Koziel et al., Zhu et al., Li et al.) and conditional generative models (An et al., Lin et al., Ma et al.)—by rejecting the assumption that every pixel must be a free optimization variable and that high-fidelity predictors trained on tens of thousands of samples are necessary. Conceptually, PQS repurposes the quadtree data structure (well-known in computer graphics and spatial indexing) as a progressive design-space representation, and borrows Kendall's tau from rank-correlation statistics as an adaptive exploration/exploitation signal. The authors explicitly contrast EMS design with NAS and molecule design (Table 1), arguing that the absence of public datasets, pretrained models, and data augmentation in EMS makes those domains' infrastructure inapplicable, thereby carving out a distinct problem setting that demands budget-aware search rather than data-hungry modeling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for industry: achieving competitive designs with 1,000 simulations versus 7,000–4,000 for generative baselines translates to 20–39 days of saved design cycle, which is substantial for antenna and FSS product development. However, the method is validated only on two 2D binary (metal/empty) layouts, and the robustness study substitutes a surrogate for true full-wave simulations, leaving open whether the quadtree strategy generalizes to 3D structures, continuous material properties, or more complex multi-physics objectives. A key open question is whether the progressive refinement idea can be combined with differentiable or physics-informed predictors to further reduce the initial 300-sample requirement, and whether the consistency-based selection mechanism extends naturally to multi-fidelity simulation hierarchies.
