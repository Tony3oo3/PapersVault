## Problem

While weight averaging techniques (SWA, LAWA, EMA) have been shown in isolated settings to improve generalization and accelerate convergence, no large-scale systematic evaluation has characterized their practical benefits across diverse modern architectures and tasks. It remains unclear whether averaging reliably reduces training time, whether it meaningfully improves generalization beyond the baseline optimizer, and whether it can fully replace learning rate annealing schedules as suggested by recent theoretical work.

## Input / Output

The method consumes model weight checkpoints collected every ν optimization steps along a training trajectory produced by a baseline optimizer (NadamW, AdamW, or Distributed Shampoo). For LAWA, a circular queue of length L stores the most recent checkpoints; for EMA, a single parameter vector is updated via θ_EMA ← βθ_EMA + (1−β)θ_t. The output is a set of averaged weights used exclusively for evaluation on held-out data—the averaged parameters do not feed back into the optimization updates, distinguishing this from schedule-free methods that incorporate averaging directly into the gradient step.

## Method

The paper conducts the largest empirical evaluation of weight averaging to date on the AlgoPerf benchmark, spanning seven architectures (ViT, U-Net, Conformer, DeepSpeech, DLRM, GNN, Transformer) and their corresponding datasets. Two averaging schemes—LAWA (rolling-window average of the latest L checkpoints) and EMA (exponential moving average with discount factor β)—are layered on top of strong baselines without modifying their hyperparameters. The central finding is that averaging acts as a proxy for a shorter learning rate decay: the averaged model closely tracks what a run with faster LR annealing would produce, explaining the ~12% GPU-hour reduction in reaching validation targets. However, averaging cannot fully replace LR schedules; combining both yields the best final validation scores across all workloads. The authors further characterize the optimal averaging horizon (~1% of total training budget) and the interaction between update frequency ν and memory length (L or β), showing that moderate averaging windows are robust across architectures and that gains persist even under suboptimal baseline hyperparameters.

## Results

Across seven AlgoPerf workloads, LAWA and EMA reduce the number of steps needed to reach the validation target to 82% of NadamW's, corresponding to an estimated 12% reduction in GPU-hours for the full suite. In terms of generalization, both averaging schemes consistently match or slightly surpass the baseline, with the most notable gains on WMT (BLEU 31.16 → 31.43/31.46) and OGBG (MAP 0.301 → 0.313/0.309). On a 124M decoder-only language model trained on 5B FineWebEdu tokens, averaging shortens step-to-target by roughly 30% (3200 → 2240/2200 steps) while final validation loss improves marginally (3.0425 → 3.0421/3.0413).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against NadamW, both LAWA and EMA consistently reduce steps-to-target to 82% of the baseline across all seven workloads, and achieve slightly better final validation scores (e.g., WMT BLEU 31.16 → 31.43–31.46; OGBG MAP 0.301 → 0.309–0.313), though gains on Criteo1TB and FastMRI are negligible. Against the more sophisticated Distributed Shampoo optimizer, averaging still yields consistent speed-ups, reaching validation targets sooner and occasionally reducing run-to-run variability, confirming that the benefit is not merely compensating for a weak baseline. In the language modeling setting, AdamW requires ~3200 steps to reach the target validation loss of 3.28, while LAWA and EMA cut this to 2240 and 2200 steps respectively (~30% speed-up), with final validation loss improving only marginally (3.0425 → 3.0421/3.0413). The paper also shows that averaging cannot fully replace learning-rate decay: when the cosine schedule is removed, averaged checkpoints generally underperform the annealed baseline, except on Criteo1TB and OGBG where the target is relatively easy to reach.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of the Polyak-Ruppert averaging lineage (Polyak 1990, Ruppert 1988), the SWA/LAWA/EMA family of practical averaging schemes (Izmailov et al. 2019, Kaddour 2022, Li et al. 2024), and the recent theoretical work connecting iterate averaging to learning rate annealing (Sandler et al. 2023, Defazio et al. 2024b). It departs from prior studies by performing the largest systematic evaluation to date—across seven diverse architectures on the AlgoPerf benchmark with heavily tuned baselines—rather than demonstrating averaging on a single task. A key conceptual departure is from Defazio et al. (2024b), who embed averaging directly into the update rule to replace LR schedules; here the averaged weights do not influence optimization, and the paper shows this 'passive' flavor cannot fully substitute for annealing.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 12% GPU-hour reduction across the full AlgoPerf suite is practically significant at industrial scale, and the finding that averaging accelerates even a sophisticated optimizer like Distributed Shampoo suggests the benefit is inherent to the trajectory rather than a compensation for a weak baseline. The clear delineation—that averaging acts as a proxy for shorter LR decay but cannot fully replace it (except on two easy workloads)—resolves an open question raised by Sandler et al. and Defazio et al. and guides practitioners toward combining both techniques. Limitations include reliance on NadamW/AdamW as primary baselines, exclusion of schedule-free methods, and the small-scale language modeling experiment; whether the conclusions hold for frontier-scale LLM pretraining or for averaging schemes that do influence updates remains open.
