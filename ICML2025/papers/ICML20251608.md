## Problem

Existing spatiotemporal learners train independent models per data source, so even correlated tasks require separate design and training, and no mechanism exists to share cross-domain knowledge or evolve the model as new domains arrive. Three critical gaps remain: no theoretical guarantee that collective intelligence improves cross-domain transfer, no principled way to filter which tasks genuinely share commonality (avoiding noise from unrelated tasks), and no elastic mechanism for the model to grow and adapt when data distributions shift.

## Input / Output

The method consumes multi-source spatiotemporal graph observations X ∈ R^{N×T×C} (e.g., traffic flow, speed, taxi pickups/drops, crowd counts) where N is the number of graph nodes, T is the temporal horizon, and C indexes distinct data sources. It produces future spatiotemporal predictions for a held-out source or temporal period, supporting both few-shot adaptation (a few training steps on the new domain) and zero-shot transfer (no training on the target domain). The model is trained iteratively: sample groups from different sources are fed sequentially in a curriculum order, and the architecture elastically grows as each new domain is absorbed.

## Method

SynEVO couples three neuro-inspired modules on a GraphWaveNet backbone. First, a curriculum-guided reordering computes per-domain gradient vectors after initial convergence and sorts sample groups by their L2 distance to the easiest domain, enforcing an easy-to-difficult learning sequence. Second, a complementary dual-learner pair disentangles commonality from individuality: an Elastic Common Container (analogous to neocortex) dynamically adjusts dropout probability and L2 weight decay via a neurotransmitter-release-inspired formula driven by successive gradient differences, allowing the parameter space to grow as cross-domain knowledge accumulates; a Task Independent Personality Extractor (analogous to hippocampus) uses contrastive learning to pull same-domain representations together and push cross-domain ones apart. Third, an Adaptive Dynamic Coupler computes the minimum distance between the new domain's personality embedding and all stored embeddings; if this distance falls below a threshold κ, the new domain is incorporated into the common container with adjusted regularization, otherwise the personality extractor is re-instantiated for quick standalone adaptation. A theoretical proposition (Prop. 3.1) proves via information-entropy arguments that iteratively absorbing domains with positive mutual information strictly increases the information boundary of the learned model.

## Results

On cross-source domain adaptation, SynEVO achieves the best MAE on NYC (6.494), CHI (1.486), and SIP (0.697), outperforming the strongest baseline CMuST by 1.2%, 0.8%, and 5.4% respectively. On cross-temporal adaptation (SD), SynEVO achieves MAE 10.984, improving over the backbone GWN by 41.8% and over CMuST by 0.4%. In terms of efficiency, SynEVO requires only 21.75% of the GPU memory of CMuST on NYC (2170 MB vs. 4034 MB) and 21.8% on SD (4252 MB vs. 19533 MB), a 4.59× reduction on the largest dataset.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SynEVO outperforms all seven baselines on the majority of metrics across four datasets. Against the closest competitor CMuST (a continuous multi-task spatiotemporal learner), SynEVO wins on MAE for NYC (6.494 vs. 6.576), CHI (1.486 vs. 1.498), and SIP (0.697 vs. 0.737), but narrowly loses on SD MAE (10.984 vs. 10.940) while still achieving lower RMSE (18.654 vs. 19.113) and MAPE (0.157 vs. 0.162). Compared to the backbone GWN, SynEVO improves MAE by approximately 36.7% on NYC (6.494 vs. 10.263) and 41.8% on SD (10.984 vs. 18.890), demonstrating the benefit of the neuro-inspired elastic architecture over a single-domain model. Among the remaining baselines, STGCN is the second-best on NYC MAE (6.774) but trails SynEVO by 11.3%, while STGODE and ASTGCN perform poorly on NYC (MAE > 9.5), suggesting they struggle with multi-source cross-domain settings. Notably, SynEVO achieves these gains with substantially lower GPU memory: 2170 MB vs. CMuST's 4034 MB on NYC and 4252 MB vs. 19533 MB on SD, a 4.59× reduction on the largest dataset.

## Relation to Prior Work

SynEVO extends the line of cross-domain spatiotemporal learning initiated by CMuST (Yi et al., 2024) and UniST (Yuan et al., 2024), which introduced multi-task and prompt-based approaches to share knowledge across urban data sources. It departs from these by replacing attention/prompt mechanisms with neuro-inspired components—curriculum-based task reordering, an elastic common container (dynamic dropout and weight decay inspired by synaptic neurotransmitter release), and a contrastive personality extractor—framed within a complementary learning system (neocortex/hippocampus) analogy. The backbone remains GraphWaveNet, and the theoretical grounding via information bottleneck (Tishby et al., 2000) provides a formal justification for why cross-domain information increases model capacity, a guarantee absent in prior spatiotemporal transfer work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in reducing repetitive model training costs for urban computing: SynEVO achieves competitive accuracy at roughly 22% of CMuST's GPU memory, and demonstrates zero-shot temporal transfer that prior independent models cannot. However, the neuro-inspired framing is largely metaphorical—the underlying mechanisms (dynamic dropout, contrastive loss, curriculum ordering) are standard ML techniques, and the theoretical proposition relies on a strong assumption of equal initial entropy across domains. Open questions include whether the elastic growth mechanism generalizes beyond traffic to other spatiotemporal modalities (e.g., air quality, energy), whether the gradient-based difficulty metric scales to hundreds of domains, and whether the information-theoretic guarantee holds when domain commonality is weak or adversarial.
