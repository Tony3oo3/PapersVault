## Problem

High-quality datasets for training LLMs to translate natural language descriptions of optimization problems into solver-ready mathematical formulations are scarce, limiting both the robustness of learning-based modeling methods and their generalization to complex, real-world problems. Existing benchmarks (NL4OPT, MAMO) are restricted primarily to linear programming and contain relatively short, simple problem descriptions, leaving a gap in coverage of MILP, NLP, SOCP, and long-context optimization tasks.

## Input / Output

The model takes a natural language description of an optimization problem (ranging from ~1,000 to 25,000 characters in OptMATH-Train) and produces a structured chain-of-thought output: a general mathematical formulation with standard notation, an instance-specific formulation with concrete parameters, and executable Python code using the Gurobi API. Evaluation is based on whether the optimal objective value obtained by running the generated solver code matches the ground truth within a relative tolerance of 1e-6, with rule-based substitutions applied for integer-vs-continuous variable ambiguities.

## Method

The framework operates as a bidirectional synthesis loop. In the reverse direction, 53+ expert-curated seed generators produce problem data (LP/MPS files) with controllable complexity (measured by a weighted scoring function over variable types, constraint types, Big-M frequency, and expression length), and an LLM backtranslation pipeline with a self-critique/self-refine loop converts the mathematical formulation plus concrete instance into a natural language description. In the forward direction, a fine-tuned AutoFormulator translates the generated NL back into a formulation and Gurobi solver code. Rejection sampling retains only triplets where the optimal value from the generated code exactly matches the original instance's optimal value, yielding a 99.6% verified equivalence rate. The resulting OptMATH-Train dataset (~200k validated triplets, including 50k augmented instances) is used for LoRA-based supervised fine-tuning of Qwen2.5 and Llama3.1 models (0.5B–32B parameters), establishing a self-improving data flywheel where stronger models produce higher acceptance rates in subsequent synthesis rounds.

## Results

The best model, OptMATH-Qwen2.5-32B (pass@1), achieves a macro-average of 62.0% across six benchmarks, surpassing GPT-4 (57.4%) and GPT-3.5-turbo (51.4%) while approaching Deepseek-V3 (62.8%). On the hardest benchmark, OptMATH-Bench, it reaches 34.7% versus 16.6% for GPT-4 and 1.6% for the Qwen2.5-7B baseline. With pass@8 sampling, OptMATH-Qwen2.5-32B further improves to 76.4% macro-average, indicating substantial untapped modeling capacity. The 7B variant (OptMATH-Qwen2.5-7B) achieves 55.8% macro-average, outperforming the comparable ORLM-Llama-3-8B (45.2% reproduced) by over 10 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

OptMATH-Qwen2.5-32B (62.0% macro) outperforms GPT-4 (57.4%) by 4.6 points and GPT-3.5-turbo (51.4%) by 10.6 points, while trailing Deepseek-V3 (62.8%) by a narrow 0.8 points. Against the same-size Qwen2.5-32B baseline (48.7%), fine-tuning on OptMATH-Train yields a 13.3-point macro-average gain, with the largest improvement on OptMATH-Bench (34.7% vs. 9.3%). The 7B model (55.8%) surpasses the reproduced ORLM-Llama-3-8B (45.2%) by 10.6 points and OptiMUS (49.4%) by 6.4 points, while remaining within 1.6 points of GPT-4. The Llama3.1-8B base model starts near 0% on all benchmarks but, after OptMATH fine-tuning, reaches 44.7% macro-average, demonstrating that the dataset can substantially rescue weak base models. On the hardest benchmark (OptMATH-Bench), OptMATH-Qwen2.5-32B leads all baselines except Deepseek-V3, achieving 34.7% versus 16.6% for GPT-4 and 20.2% for OptiMUS.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM-for-optimization-modeling line (NL4OPT, OptiMUS, ORLM, LLMOPT) by addressing the data scarcity bottleneck through a bidirectional synthesis loop. It adapts the self-critique/self-refine paradigm from Madaan et al. (2024) into a three-phase backtranslation pipeline (generate → criticize → refine) for producing natural language descriptions from mathematical formulations, and borrows the rejection-sampling validation strategy from Yuan et al. (2023) but applies it via optimal-value matching between original and regenerated solver instances. Unlike prior synthesis efforts (OR-Instruct, MILP-Evolve, Yang et al. 2025) that generate data in one direction, OptMATH closes the loop by requiring the forward AutoFormulator to reproduce the same optimal value, creating a self-validating data flywheel.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The OptMATH-Train dataset (200k+ validated triplets spanning LP, MILP, IP, NLP, SOCP) and OptMATH-Bench (long-context, multi-type hard instances) fill a genuine gap: prior benchmarks were limited to LP/MILP and had short problem descriptions. The optimal-value-matching validation is a practical and scalable quality gate, though the authors acknowledge it does not guarantee full formulation equivalence (99.6% accuracy via LLM-committee + manual check). Open questions include whether the self-improving data flywheel converges, whether the 0.4% false-acceptance rate matters for downstream training, and whether the framework extends to stochastic or multi-objective optimization. The finding that a 7B fine-tuned model rivals GPT-4 on several benchmarks suggests the bottleneck for optimization modeling is data quality rather than model scale.
