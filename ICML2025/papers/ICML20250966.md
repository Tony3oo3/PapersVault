## Problem

Existing deep learning methods for PDE-constrained control (diffusion models, decision transformers, MPC) do not enforce safety constraints, yet real-world applications like fluid dynamics and controlled nuclear fusion require guaranteed safety to prevent catastrophic failures. The core difficulty is twofold: offline training data is often suboptimal and contains unsafe samples, creating a distribution shift between observed data and the desired safe-optimal distribution, and there is an inherent tension between aggressive control (reducing error) and respecting safety thresholds. No prior method provides formal safety guarantees while maintaining competitive control performance.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input an initial state trajectory u₀, a target state or control objective (e.g., desired final state for Burgers', target smoke bucket for 2D fluid, target β_p and l_i for tokamak), and PDE system parameters, along with a held-out calibration set D_cal for uncertainty estimation. It outputs a full control sequence w(t,x) and corresponding state trajectory u(t,x) over the time horizon [0,T]. The diffusion model jointly models the [u,w] pair, and for the 2D fluid task, control is restricted to peripheral regions with up to 1,792 spatial control parameters, while the tokamak task operates on 0D plasma parameters at 100 ms resolution. Safety is enforced by requiring the safety score s(u) to remain below a task-specific bound s₀ at all times and spatial locations.

## Method

SafeDiffCon introduces an 'uncertainty quantile' derived from weighted conformal prediction to quantify the model's safety prediction error under distribution shift, yielding a conformal interval that covers the true safety score with probability ≥ 1−α (Theorem 4.1). The method operates in three phases: (1) pre-training a diffusion model on the joint [u,w] distribution, (2) post-training with a reweighted diffusion loss L_post-train that multiplies the standard denoising loss by e^{−W}, where W penalizes safety violations measured via the upper bound of the conformal interval plus the control objective, provably steering the learned distribution toward p*(u,w) ∝ p(u,w)·e^{−W} (Theorem 4.2), and (3) inference-time fine-tuning that iteratively samples with guidance G=W and updates model parameters via a fine-tune loss on sampled trajectories, incorporating the uncertainty quantile to prevent constraint violations from model uncertainty. The weighted conformal prediction (Tibshirani et al., 2019) handles the covariate shift between the calibration set and model-generated data, with weights ω ∝ e^{−W} justified by Theorem 4.3 when the KL divergence between the true and model distributions is small.

## Results

SafeDiffCon is the only method that achieves 0% unsafe trajectories across all three PDE control tasks (1D Burgers', 2D incompressible fluid, and KSTAR tokamak). On the 1D Burgers' equation, it attains a control objective J=0.0016 with zero safety violations, outperforming all other safe methods (TREBI: J=0.0074, MPC-Lag: J=0.0092, SL-Lag: J=0.0115). On the 2D incompressible fluid task, SafeDiffCon achieves J=−0.3548 with SVM=0 and 0% unsafe rate, while all baselines either violate safety (BC: 88% unsafe, CDT: 30% unsafe) or sacrifice control performance. On the tokamak fusion reactor, it reaches J=0.0121 with 0% unsafe rate, beating TREBI (J=0.0261) and all Lagrangian-based methods (J>0.86).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SafeDiffCon outperforms all six baselines on the joint criterion of safety compliance and control quality. Behavior Cloning (BC) and its safe-filtered variant (BC-Safe) both fail to meet safety constraints on every task (38%/14% unsafe on Burgers', 88%/8% on 2D fluid, 42%/4% on tokamak), confirming that simple imitation learning cannot handle safety constraints. CDT achieves the lowest J on Burgers' (0.0012) and tokamak (0.0071) but violates safety in 16% and 8% of trajectories respectively, and on 2D fluid it has 30% unsafe rate with SVM=0.2519. TREBI, the other diffusion-based safe method, satisfies safety constraints but sacrifices substantial control performance: J=0.0074 vs. 0.0016 on Burgers' (4.6× worse) and J=0.0261 vs. 0.0121 on tokamak (2.2× worse). The Lagrangian-based methods (SL-Lag, MPC-Lag) and PID are safe but achieve markedly worse control objectives (e.g., J=0.8812 for SL-Lag on tokamak vs. 0.0121 for SafeDiffCon, a 73× gap). The ablation study further confirms that removing any single component (post-training, fine-tuning, or the uncertainty quantile Q) causes safety violations, validating the necessity of the full conformal adaptation pipeline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two well-developed lines of work: diffusion-based PDE control (Wei et al. 2024; Hu et al. 2024) and conformal prediction under distribution shift (Tibshirani et al. 2019). It departs from prior diffusion-based PDE control by introducing explicit safety constraints via a reweighted diffusion loss and inference-time fine-tuning, both driven by a conformal uncertainty quantile. It also departs from safe offline RL methods (TREBI, CDT, CPQ) by operating in a PDE-constrained setting with formal coverage guarantees rather than soft penalty-based safety, and by being the first to apply conformal prediction to safety control problems. The method is not a new architecture but a new training/inference protocol layered on a standard diffusion model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Safe PDE control is critical for high-stakes applications (nuclear fusion, fluid dynamics) where constraint violations can be catastrophic, yet prior deep learning methods lacked formal safety guarantees. The conformal interval provides a distribution-free probabilistic guarantee on safety score coverage, which is a meaningful step toward certifiable ML-based control. Limitations include the offline setting (no online adaptation), the KL-divergence approximation in Theorem 4.3, and evaluation on only three tasks. Open questions include whether the approach extends to other generative models, scales to higher-dimensional PDEs, and whether the calibration set requirement is practical in deployment.
