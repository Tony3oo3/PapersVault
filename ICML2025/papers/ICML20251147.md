## Problem

LLM-based game agents consistently underperform rule-based heuristic bots due to weak planning capabilities, and prior Pokemon AI approaches require extensive task-specific training or self-play. Pokemon battles present a particularly intractable testbed: a state space of ~10^354 for the first turn, partial observability of the opponent's team, and battle lengths of 6–100+ turns that make exhaustive search impossible. The paper asks whether an LLM's generalist knowledge can serve as an informative prior to constrain minimax search to human-relevant strategies, achieving expert-level play with zero additional training.

## Input / Output

The agent consumes the current infoset (own team details, visible opponent Pokémon, last N turns of battle history), the set of legal actions, and a one-step lookahead heuristic computed by a damage calculator that estimates turns-to-KO for each move under various Terastallization and Dynamax scenarios. It outputs a single optimal action (move, switch, or generation-specific mechanic) selected via minimax over a truncated tree of depth k, where the LLM scores leaf nodes rather than expanding to terminal states. The entire pipeline must complete within 150 seconds total plus 15 seconds per turn, and the LLM operates as a black box with no fine-tuning or Pokemon-specific data.

## Method

PokéChamp replaces three modules of classical minimax tree search with LLM-based generation: (1) player action sampling, where the LLM proposes a small candidate set of viable actions to aggressively prune the exponentially growing search tree, supplemented by tool-generated top moves from the one-step lookahead and the Abyssal bot; (2) opponent modeling, where the LLM infers hidden opponent states and predicts likely actions under partial observability, augmented by historical Showdown data for stat estimation (attack/defense); and (3) value function estimation, where the LLM scores leaf nodes at depth k using criteria like remaining Pokémon, move effectiveness, and win probability, replacing terminal rewards. A world model approximates game transitions by mathematically computing damage via the official formula with LLM-predicted opponent stats, handling stochastic elements (move accuracy, critical hits) via expected values. The framework is general for two-player zero-sum games, requires no LLM training or fine-tuning, and is designed to work with any frontier model as a drop-in replacement.

## Results

In Gen 9 OU with Terastallization, PokéChamp powered by GPT-4o achieves an 84% win rate against the Abyssal bot (Elo 1268) and a 76% win rate against PokéLLMon (GPT-4o). Even with the open-source 8B-parameter Llama 3.1 model, PokéChamp outperforms PokéLLMon (GPT-4o) with a 64% win rate. On the online Showdown ladder, PokéChamp reaches a projected Elo of 1300–1500, placing it in the top 30%–10% of human players. On 1v1 battle puzzles, PokéChamp achieves 86% win rate versus PokéLLMon's 76%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PokéChamp (GPT-4o) decisively outperforms PokéLLMon (GPT-4o) in Gen 9 OU, winning 76% of head-to-head matches (implied by the 84% vs. Abyssal Elo gap of ~248 points and the 40% vs. Abyssal for PokéLLMon). Against the rule-based Abyssal bot, PokéChamp wins 84% of games versus PokéLLMon's 40%, a 44-percentage-point margin. Notably, PokéChamp with the much smaller Llama 3.1:8b model still beats PokéLLMon (GPT-4o) at 64%, demonstrating that the minimax framework compensates for weaker LLM capabilities. In mirror matchups without Terastallization (Table 3), the gap widens further: PokéChamp (GPT-4o) wins 90% vs. Abyssal and 90% vs. PokéLLMon (GPT-4o), while PokéLLMon only manages 60% vs. Abyssal. On 1v1 puzzles, PokéChamp achieves 86% win rate compared to PokéLLMon's 76%, a 10-point advantage attributed to effective one-step lookahead utilization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from two dominant paradigms in competitive game AI: RL self-play (AlphaGo, Libratus, OpenAI Five), which achieves superhuman performance but demands extensive task-specific training, and pure LLM prompting agents (PokéLLMon, NetHack agents), which leverage generalist knowledge but fail at multi-step planning. PokéChamp carves new ground by embedding LLMs as three specific black-box modules—action sampling, opponent modeling, and value estimation—within a classical minimax tree search, requiring no fine-tuning. The one-step lookahead world model, which mathematically computes damage and uses historical statistics to approximate hidden opponent states, is a novel bridge between symbolic game mechanics and LLM-based inference that neither prior RL nor prior prompting approaches employ.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework demonstrates that game-theoretic search structure, rather than raw LLM scale, is the critical factor for competitive play—evidenced by Llama 3.1:8b in the minimax loop outperforming GPT-4o in a pure prompting setup. The 3M+ game dataset and puzzle benchmarks provide a reusable resource for the Pokemon AI community. Key open questions include: how to handle the covariate shift between the LLM's fixed pre-training knowledge and the evolving metagame, how to improve opponent modeling to resist adversarial exploitation of its static nature, and whether increasing search depth/breadth within real-time constraints can close the gap to true superhuman play. The timeout losses (one-third of ladder games) remain a significant practical limitation.
