## Problem

Despite growing interest in applying sequence models (Transformers, SSMs) to graph data, there is no unified framework or mathematical characterization of what makes a good graph sequence model, nor a clear understanding of when different sequence backbones and tokenization strategies excel or fail. The paper addresses this gap by providing theoretical analyses of counting, connectivity, and sensitivity properties across model families, and by identifying that no single architecture or tokenization dominates across all graph tasks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250127/cropped_page5_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a graph G=(V,E) with node feature matrix X∈R^{n×d} and edge set, tokenizing it into sequences via HAC tree traversals (DFS paths or BFS levels) augmented with hierarchical positional encodings that encode shortest-path distances at multiple granularity levels. Outputs are task-dependent: node embeddings for classification, binary connectivity labels, per-color node counts, shortest-path distances, and graph-level classification scores. A key design constraint is that the HAC tokenization produces sequences of length |V| (or log₂|V| per BFS level), keeping the sequence length manageable for the hybrid encoder while preserving permutation-equivariant structure through the clustering hierarchy.

## Method

GSM++ combines three novel components: (1) HAC tokenization, which builds a Hierarchical Affinity Clustering tree and traverses it via DFS (subgraph-level sequences encoding each node's hierarchical neighborhood) or BFS (node-level sequences ordered so similar nodes are adjacent, achieving k-locality for bounded-node-locality graphs); (2) a hybrid global encoder consisting of two Mamba (selective SSM) layers followed by a Transformer block, motivated by theory showing SSMs provide distance-sensitive inductive bias while the Transformer's permutation equivariance prevents the representational collapse that deep SSMs suffer; and (3) Mixture of Tokenization (MoT), a learned discrete router that selects the top-2 tokenizations from a set per node and concatenates their encodings. Theoretical results establish that recurrent models with width ≥ C can count colors (unlike non-causal Transformers), that SSM sensitivity decays with token distance (giving a U-shape effect in deep models), and that hybrid architectures solve connectivity instances more efficiently than either pure recurrent or pure Transformer models.

## Results

GSM++(MoT) achieves the best results across all four GNN benchmark datasets in Table 3: 98.84% on MNIST, 77.81% on CIFAR10, 87.93% on PATTERN, and 94.37% on MalNet-Tiny. On heterophilic datasets (Table 5), GSM++(MoT) leads with 91.77% on Roman-empire, 53.90% on Amazon-ratings, and 91.49% ROC AUC on Minesweeper. On long-range datasets (Table 6), it achieves 38.01 F1 on COCO-SP, 41.93 F1 on PascalVOC-SP, and 70.92 AP on Peptides-Func. Overall, GSM++ outperforms all baselines in 8 out of 10 dataset-task comparisons.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GSM++(MoT) consistently outperforms the strongest Transformer-based baselines: on CIFAR10 it beats GRED (76.85%) and GRIT (76.46%) by roughly 1–1.4 points, and on PATTERN it surpasses DIFFormer (87.01%) and GRIT (87.19%) by about 0.7–0.9 points. On heterophilic graphs, GSM++(MoT) leads Exphormer by 2.7 points on Roman-empire (91.77% vs. 89.03%) and by 0.8 points on Amazon-ratings (53.90% vs. 53.51%), while on Minesweeper it edges out Exphormer (91.49% vs. 90.74% ROC AUC). On long-range tasks, GSM++(MoT) narrowly beats GRED on Peptides-Func (70.92 vs. 70.85 AP) and GMN on PascalVOC-SP (41.93 vs. 41.69 F1), and surpasses GPS on COCO-SP (38.01 vs. 37.74 F1). Traditional MPNN baselines (GCN, GIN, Gated-GCN) are substantially outperformed across all datasets, with gaps of 10–30+ points on CIFAR10 and PATTERN. The paper reports that GSM++ wins in 8 out of 10 dataset-task comparisons, with the two non-winning cases being very close margins (e.g., Peptides-Func where GRED is within 0.07 AP).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies the growing family of graph sequence models (Graph Mamba, S4G, GRED, NAGphormer, GraphGPS) under a single three-stage framework (Tokenization → Local Encoding → Global Encoding) and provides the first systematic theoretical comparison of which sequence model backbones (Transformers vs. SSMs/RNNs) suit which graph tasks. It departs from prior graph SSM work (Behrouz & Hashemi 2024; Song et al. 2024) by introducing the first hybrid Mamba+Transformer architecture for graphs, motivated by a sensitivity analysis showing SSMs suffer representational collapse in depth while non-causal Transformers avoid it. The HAC-based hierarchical tokenization builds on the affinity clustering algorithm of Bateni et al. (2017) but repurposes it as a graph-to-sequence tokenizer with provable locality guarantees, and the Mixture of Tokenization (MoT) mechanism draws on Mixture-of-Experts routing to let each node select its best tokenizer.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theoretical results (counting impossibility for non-causal Transformers, sensitivity decay in SSMs, parameter-efficiency of recurrent models under bounded node locality) give practitioners a principled basis for choosing tokenization and sequence-model backbones rather than relying on empirical trial-and-error. The 'no free lunch' conjecture for graph-to-sequence modeling—validated across 54 model configurations—suggests future work should focus on task-aware or adaptive architectures rather than seeking a single dominant design. Open questions include whether the HAC tokenization scales efficiently to graphs with millions of nodes, whether the hybrid Mamba+Transformer block generalizes beyond the two-layer configuration tested here, and whether the theoretical connectivity lower bounds for sub-quadratic models tighten as kernel/attention approximations improve.
