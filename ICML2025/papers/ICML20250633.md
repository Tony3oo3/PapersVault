## Problem

Existing Earth Observation foundation models produce coarse patch-sized embeddings that limit pixel-level prediction and prevent integration with high-resolution LiDAR data such as full-waveform GEDI signals. Moreover, these models rely solely on image-based modalities, excelling at horizontal structure tasks (land cover, species) but failing at vertical structure tasks (canopy height, waveform retrieval). DUNIA addresses both gaps by learning pixel-sized embeddings through cross-modal alignment between satellite imagery and sparse LiDAR waveforms.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250633/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a single 14-channel median composite image (stacked S-1 VV/VH and S-2 bands at 10 m GSD) as input, processed at 64×64 pixels during pre-training and 256×256 at inference. It produces two independent sets of 64-dimensional pixel-level embeddings: O^V for vertical structure and O^H for horizontal structure. Additionally, a conditional latent diffusion model can synthesize a full 256-bin GEDI waveform from a single pixel embedding, a capability no prior EO model supports. The pixel-level granularity (as opposed to patch-level) is what enables direct matching with GEDI's 25 m footprint waveforms.

## Method

DUNIA uses a 16-layer transformer encoder with two separate hierarchical convolutional decoders (inspired by DPT) that progressively upsample to full pixel resolution, one dedicated to vertical structure (O^V) and one to horizontal structure (O^H), each augmented with neighborhood attention at the finest scale. Training combines two non-negative-pair contrastive objectives: Zero-CL aligns sparse GEDI waveform embeddings (from a VQ-VAE with 8-stage RVQ) to O^V pixel embeddings, while VICReg aligns O^H to a multi-temporal ConvLSTM autoencoder's temporally pooled output, capturing phenological patterns without requiring time series at inference. A key architectural finding is that a single shared decoder fails to encode both horizontal and vertical structure simultaneously (cosine similarity of −0.42 for waveform retrieval), necessitating the dual-decoder design. For waveform generation, a 1D UNet-based latent diffusion model is trained on the RVQ-quantized waveform latents, conditioned on O^V pixel embeddings via cross-attention with classifier-free guidance (scale 3).

## Results

In the fine-tuning setting (Table 2), DUNIA achieves the best performance on all three vertical structure tasks: canopy height (RMSE 1.3 m, r=0.95), canopy cover (RMSE 9.8%, r=0.85), and plant area index (RMSE 0.62, r=0.71), outperforming the next-best model AnySat by 2.1×, 1.2×, and 1.5× in RMSE respectively. On horizontal structure tasks, DUNIA matches AnySat on land cover (wF1 90.3 vs. 90.1) and tree species (wF1 82.2 vs. 82.3), but trails by 4.1% on crop type (wF1 77.0 vs. 81.1). In the zero-shot setting (Table 1), DUNIA with KNN=50 retrieval outperforms supervised SOTA on all vertical structure products (e.g., canopy height RMSE 2.0 m vs. 5.2 m for Schwartz et al.) and achieves 80.1% wF1 on land cover with only 500 labeled images, while waveform retrieval reaches r=0.70 correlation with reference waveforms.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DUNIA decisively outperforms all five competing foundation models (AnySat, CROMA, DOFA, DeCUR, SatMAE) on the three vertical structure tasks, with RMSE improvements of 2.1× over AnySat on canopy height (1.3 vs. 2.8 m) and 3× on canopy cover (9.8 vs. 29.2 for DOFA/DeCUR/SatMAE). On horizontal structure tasks, DUNIA essentially ties AnySat on land cover (90.3 vs. 90.1 wF1) and tree species (82.2 vs. 82.3 wF1), but loses by 4.1% on crop type (77.0 vs. 81.1 wF1), which the authors attribute to the inability of a single median composite to capture crop phenological cycles. In the zero-shot setting, DUNIA surpasses supervised SOTA baselines (Schwartz et al., Liu et al., Tolan et al., Lang et al.) on all vertical structure products by 2–3× in RMSE, and slightly underperforms the PureForest supervised baseline by 0.8% at KNN=50 but exceeds it by 1.4% at KNN=5. DUNIA is the only model capable of the waveform generation task (r=0.78), a capability not supported by any competing method. The ablation studies confirm that the separate-decoder design is critical: a shared decoder yields a cosine similarity of −0.42 between pixel and waveform embeddings, versus 0.99 with separate decoders.

## Relation to Prior Work

DUNIA extends the multi-encoder contrastive learning paradigm (analogous to CLIP's image-text alignment) to the Earth Observation domain, but at pixel granularity rather than instance level, aligning satellite imagery with sparse GEDI LiDAR waveforms. It builds on VICReg for pixel-pixel alignment and Zero-CL for pixel-waveform alignment, combining them with a VQ-VAE-based waveform encoder and a latent diffusion model for waveform generation. The key departure from prior EO foundation models (SatMAE, DOFA, CROMA, AnySat) is the use of two separate decoders to disentangle horizontal and vertical structure understanding, and the explicit cross-modal alignment with LiDAR data that enables pixel-level vertical structure retrieval and generation—capabilities absent in existing patch-based, image-only approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DUNIA demonstrates that a single median composite image, aligned with sparse LiDAR waveforms via contrastive learning, can yield pixel-level embeddings that zero-shot outperform supervised SOTA on vertical structure tasks (canopy height RMSE 2.0m vs. 5.2m) and generate full GEDI waveforms from pixel inputs—a capability no prior method supports. However, the evaluation is confined to metropolitan France, the model relies on static single-date composites (limiting crop-type performance), and generalization to other regions or seasons would require re-pre-training. Open questions include whether the dual-decoder design and pixel-level alignment strategy transfer to other geographies, whether multi-temporal inputs can be natively incorporated without the ConvLSTM AE workaround, and whether the approach scales to global coverage with diverse LiDAR sources.
