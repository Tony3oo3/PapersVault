## Problem

Loss-to-loss scaling laws relate training or validation loss to downstream test loss, enabling prediction of generalization behavior, but prior work has only characterized these laws within a single architecture and training recipe. It remains unclear which design choices—pretraining data, architecture, tokenizer, model size, context length, or optimizer settings—actually determine the shape of these scaling curves, and whether different architectures encode meaningfully different inductive biases.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251742/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis consumes 200–800 intermediate training checkpoints per model configuration (spanning over 6,000 total checkpoints across Llama, Mamba, and GPT variants), recording each checkpoint's loss on multiple validation corpora and downstream test tasks. Losses are normalized to bits-per-byte (BPB) to enable fair comparison across tokenizers with different vocabulary sizes. The output is a fitted shifted power law per configuration (parameters K, κ, and irreducible errors E_x|p, E_y|p), plus a quantitative ranking of intervention effects measured as the area between fitted curves on the interval [0, 2].

## Method

The paper performs a systematic interventional (causal) analysis: it trains Llama-3 and Mamba models from scratch using the Lingua framework at multiple sizes (59M–421M parameters) on three pretraining corpora (FineWeb-Edu, C4, The Pile UC), with two tokenizers (tiktoken 128k, GPT-2 50k), and varying context lengths and optimizer settings, supplemented by Hugging Face pretrained GPT, Llama, and Mamba models. For each configuration, it first fits compute-to-loss scaling laws (Eq. 2–3) to estimate irreducible errors, then fits the shifted power law (Eq. 1) relating validation loss to test loss. By holding two factors constant and intervening on the third, it isolates each factor's causal effect on the scaling curve. The key finding is that pretraining data produces large, consistent shifts in the loss-to-loss curve across all architectures, while architecture (transformer vs. state-space), tokenizer, model size, context length, and optimizer settings produce negligible changes—implying that current LLM architectures converge to similar generalization behavior given the same data.

## Results

Across over 6,000 model checkpoints, loss-to-loss scaling consistently follows shifted power laws (Eq. 1) across all architectures and training settings. The quantitative intervention analysis (Table 4) shows that changing pretraining data produces the largest shift in scaling curves (area between fitted BPB curves = 0.22), while tokenizer (0.01), architecture (0.02), optimizer (0.02), model size (0.02), and context length (0.02) all have negligible impact. Point-wise comparisons (Table 1) confirm that Mamba and Llama models of similar size trained on the same data achieve nearly identical downstream losses (e.g., avg test loss 3.88 vs. 4.04 on FineWeb-Edu at ~420M params).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an interventional study rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so the 'baselines' are the different training configurations being ablated. Pretraining data is the dominant factor: switching from FineWeb-Edu to C4 or The Pile shifts the loss-to-loss curve by an area of 0.22 (vs. base), and even deduplicating The Pile (The Pile vs. The Pile Deduped) produces a measurable shift. In stark contrast, changing architecture from Llama (transformer) to Mamba (state-space model) yields only a 0.02 area difference, and switching tokenizers (tiktoken 128k vs. gpt2 50257) gives 0.01. Model size (59M–420M), context length (1024–3076), and optimizer/schedule variations (AdamW, cosine vs. WSD, LR 3e-4 vs. 3e-3, weight decay 0.1 vs. 3.3e-2) all produce areas ≤ 0.02, confirming they are negligible. The practical upshot, validated in Table 1, is that a 421M Mamba and a 417M Llama trained on the same data achieve within ~0.1–0.2 nll on every downstream task (ARC, HellaSwag, MMLU, PIQA), making architecture choice effectively irrelevant for downstream generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the loss-to-loss scaling framework introduced by Brandfonbrener et al. (2024) and Du et al. (2025), which analyzed train-to-test loss relationships within a single architecture and training recipe (OLMo or GPT). Drawing conceptual inspiration from the vision robustness literature (Taori et al., 2020; Fang et al., 2022), which showed that pretraining data dominates distributional robustness while most other interventions are negligible, this paper performs the first systematic causal intervention study on loss-to-loss scaling across diverse LLM architectures (Llama, Mamba, GPT), tokenizers, and training configurations. The key departure from prior work is the multi-factor interventional design: rather than fitting scaling laws within one setup, the authors retrain models from scratch under controlled changes to isolate each factor's causal effect.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that pretraining data is the dominant factor while architecture (even the transformer-vs-state-space distinction), model size, context length, and optimizer settings are negligible provides practitioners with a clear prioritization: invest in data curation, not architectural innovation, for downstream generalization. Open questions remain about whether this implies genuine representational convergence across architectures, whether novel training paradigms (e.g., structured growth, weight tying) could alter scaling trends, and whether the results hold at frontier scale or with longer contexts. The study's scope is limited to ~400M-parameter models, zero-shot evaluation, and modest context lengths (≤3076), so extrapolation to production-scale LLMs warrants caution.
