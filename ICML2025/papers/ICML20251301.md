## Problem

DropEdge, a widely proposed data augmentation technique for GNNs that randomly removes edges during training, consistently fails to improve performance in practice despite its theoretical motivation. The paper identifies the root cause: a fundamental architectural limitation in GNNs where the aggregation (AGG) operation prevents a constant discrepancy bound between outputs under different adjacency matrices, creating an inescapable bias-robustness tradeoff that prevents DropEdge from optimizing its robustness objective without degrading the bias term.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251301/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates in a two-stage pipeline: it first consumes a fully pre-trained GNN (with all backbone weights frozen) along with the graph adjacency matrix and node feature matrix, then trains only the lightweight AGG_B parameters using DropEdge-augmented adjacency matrices. The output is standard node class predictions, but the key distinction is that AGG_B is a post-processing module that can be attached to or detached from any existing GNN without retraining the backbone, making it applicable to already-deployed models.

## Method

Aggregation Buffer (AGG_B) is a degree-normalized linear transformation, (D+I)^{-1} H^{(0:l-1)} W^{(l)}, added to each GNN layer's aggregation output, where H^{(0:l-1)} concatenates all preceding layer representations. It is trained with a robustness-controlled loss L_RC that explicitly balances a bias term (KL divergence between the frozen pre-trained model and the augmented model on labeled training nodes) and a robustness term (KL divergence between model outputs on original vs. edge-dropped subgraphs across all nodes, including unlabeled ones), weighted by a hyperparameter λ. The two-step training procedure—pre-train the GNN without DropEdge to minimize bias, then train only AGG_B with DropEdge while freezing the backbone—ensures the robustness term can be optimized without the bias degradation observed in standard DropEdge training. Theoretically, the paper proves that GNNs cannot satisfy a constant discrepancy bound (unlike MLPs) due to the AGG operation, and shows AGG_B satisfies two essential conditions: edge-awareness (its output changes with the adjacency matrix) and stability (its output norm is smaller under the original graph than under a perturbed one).

## Results

On 12 node classification benchmarks, GCN_B (GCN + Aggregation Buffer) achieves the highest overall accuracy in 9 out of 12 datasets and the second-best in the remaining 3. The most dramatic gains appear on heterophilous nodes: GCN_B reaches 70.77% on PubMed (vs. 67.34% for GCN, +3.43 pp) and 82.75% on CS (vs. 76.81% for GCN, +5.94 pp). On homophilous nodes, GCN_B is competitive but occasionally trails the best random-dropping variant by <0.5 pp (e.g., 95.83% vs. 96.18% for DropMessage on PubMed). Across all GNN architectures tested (SAGE, GAT, SGC, GIN), AGG_B yields consistent improvements of 0.5–2.5 pp.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the base GCN, GCN_B delivers consistent improvements across all 12 datasets, with the largest gains on heterophilous nodes (e.g., +3.43 pp on PubMed, +5.94 pp on CS, +1.51 pp on Cora). The three random-dropping baselines (DropEdge, DropNode, DropMessage) fail to consistently outperform the untuned GCN, confirming the paper's theoretical claim that edge perturbation alone cannot overcome the architectural discrepancy bound. TUNEUP and GraphPatcher, designed specifically for degree bias, improve tail-node accuracy but show inconsistent or even negative effects on heterophilous nodes (e.g., TUNEUP drops to 74.16% on CS heterophilous vs. GCN's 76.81%), whereas GCN_B improves both degree and structural disparity simultaneously. On the two datasets where MLPs surpass all GNNs (CS and Actor), GCN_B still achieves the best GNN performance, narrowing the gap to the MLP baseline. Overall, GCN_B is the only method that consistently ranks first or second across both homophilous and heterophilous node groups without degrading the other.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly builds on DropEdge (Rong et al., 2020), providing a theoretical diagnosis of why it underperforms: the AGG operation in GNNs introduces an input-dependent discrepancy term that prevents the robustness objective from being optimized without inflating the bias term. It departs from prior random-dropping methods (DropNode, DropMessage) and degree-bias-specific approaches (TUNEUP, GraphPatcher) by reframing the problem as an architectural limitation rather than an algorithmic one, and by proposing a modular parameter block (AGG_B) trained in a separate second stage with a robustness-controlled loss. The theoretical tools—Lipschitz continuity of aggregation operators and discrepancy bounds—draw on standard analysis but are applied to GNNs in a way that isolates the AGG operation as the root cause.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clear theoretical explanation for a widely observed but previously unexplained phenomenon (DropEdge's limited effectiveness), which should inform how the community designs augmentation strategies for GNNs. The modular, two-step AGG_B approach is practically attractive since it can be attached to any pre-trained GNN without retraining, and it unifies solutions to degree bias and structural disparity under a single edge-robustness framework. Key limitations include the dependency on pre-trained knowledge (the two-step procedure), modest absolute gains (typically 1–2%), and the open question of whether end-to-end joint optimization of bias and robustness is possible without the architectural bottleneck identified here.
