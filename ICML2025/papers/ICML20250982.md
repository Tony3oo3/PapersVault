## Problem

Best-of-N (BoN) sampling is the most widely used inference-time alignment technique, but it provably suffers from reward overoptimization—performance degrades as the number of candidates N grows—because the reward model's errors at the tail of the response distribution cause the algorithm to select high-scored but low-quality responses. No prior theoretical framework existed to characterize the fundamental limits of inference-time alignment or to design algorithms whose performance scales monotonically with compute. This paper formalizes the problem as a statistical query-complexity task and asks: what is the best achievable reward given an imperfect reward model, and can deliberate algorithm design eliminate the overoptimization phenomenon entirely?

## Input / Output

The algorithm operates in a sample-and-evaluate framework: given a prompt x, it accesses the base policy π_ref only by drawing responses y ~ π_ref(·|x) and querying the reward model r̂(x,y) and log-probability π_ref(y|x), with no explicit parameter access to either model. Beyond the compute budget N (total number of such queries), the method introduces a regularization parameter β that controls the degree of pessimism, decoupling statistical performance from computational cost. The output is a single response ŷ whose per-instance regret against the optimal policy is bounded by √(C^{π*}(x) · ε²_RM(x)), depending on the base policy's coverage over high-quality responses and the reward model's mean-squared error.

## Method

InferenceTimePessimism samples from a χ²-regularized distribution π_β^χ(y|x) ∝ π_ref(y|x) · relu(β⁻¹(r̂(x,y) − λ(x))), which is the exact solution to a χ²-regularized reward maximization objective and implements pessimism in the face of reward model uncertainty. The key algorithmic innovation is a novel rejection sampling scheme that approximates this distribution purely at inference time: it first estimates the normalization constant λ̂(x) via an O(N log N) dynamic programming subroutine over sorted reward values, then draws candidates and accepts them with probability proportional to relu(β⁻¹(r̂(x,y) − λ̂(x))) / M where M ≈ R_max/β. Critically, this separates the compute budget N from the statistical regularization β, so that increasing N never degrades performance (scaling-monotonicity), unlike BoN where N simultaneously controls exploration and overfitting risk. The χ²-regularizer's heavy-tailed density ratio is bounded by R_max/β (rather than exponential as in KL-regularization), making rejection sampling computationally feasible, and the resulting regret bound matches the information-theoretic lower bound established in the paper's skyline analysis.

## Results

InferenceTimePessimism achieves the optimal regret bound of √(C^π*(x) · ε²_RM(x)), matching the information-theoretic lower bound, while BoN-Alignment is provably suboptimal with regret scaling as ε_RM^(2/3)(x). Empirically, InferenceTimePessimism is scaling-monotonic (accuracy does not degrade as N increases), whereas BoN-Alignment exhibits the characteristic overoptimization dip. On GSM8K with Phi-3-Mini and OASST reward model, InferenceTimePessimism achieves +0.87% lift over the base policy while BoN-Alignment degrades to −5.61%; on MATH with LLAMA-RM, InferenceTimePessimism reaches +41.47% vs. BoN-Alignment's +41.74% (statistically tied).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is BoN-Alignment (Best-of-N sampling with argmax reward selection). InferenceTimePessimism clearly outperforms BoN-Alignment when the reward model is weaker: on GSM8K with OASST (1.4B), InferenceTimePessimism achieves +0.87% lift while BoN-Alignment degrades to −5.61%; on MMLU with OASST, the gap is −0.71% vs. −5.61%. With stronger reward models (LLAMA-RM, ARMO-RM), the two methods are statistically comparable on most tasks, which the authors attribute to the binary reward setting making C^π* = C_∞^π*, a regime where Theorem 3.4 predicts BoN is near-optimal. The critical qualitative difference is scaling behavior: BoN-Alignment's accuracy degrades monotonically past a critical N (the overoptimization dip), while InferenceTimePessimism remains monotonic for any fixed β, making it robust to compute budget choices. On Mistral-7B with MATH and OASST, the gap widens dramatically: InferenceTimePessimism achieves +10.32% while BoN-Alignment drops to −20.13%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the χ²-regularized offline alignment framework of Huang et al. (2024b, χPO) from training time to inference time, implementing the same pessimism-in-the-face-of-uncertainty principle via a novel rejection sampling scheme that requires no retraining. It builds on the approximate rejection sampling analysis of Block & Polyanskiy (2023) to handle the heavy-tailed density ratios inherent to χ²-regularized policies. The key departure from prior inference-time alignment work (Gao et al. 2023; Beirami et al. 2024; Yang et al. 2024b) is that those analyses characterize BoN's reward-KL tradeoff but do not provide regret guarantees under reward model mismatch; this paper is the first to formalize inference-time alignment as a query-complexity problem and prove that BoN is fundamentally suboptimal under general (L₁-type) coverage.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that the widely-used Best-of-N heuristic is provably suboptimal under realistic coverage conditions (achieving only ε_RM^{2/3} regret versus the optimal ε_RM rate), and that this gap cannot be closed by any choice of N. InferenceTimePessimism closes this gap by separating the compute budget (N) from the statistical regularizer (β), yielding scaling-monotonic guarantees. However, the empirical validation is limited: with binary rewards (as in GSM8K, MMLU, MATH), the coverage coefficient C^π* equals C_∞^π*, placing the experiments in the regime where BoN is already near-optimal, so the practical advantage of InferenceTimePessimism is modest. Open questions include whether the gap widens under non-binary or process-level rewards, whether inference-aware training can further optimize for the pessimistic policy, and whether the worst-case reward model error assumption can be relaxed.
