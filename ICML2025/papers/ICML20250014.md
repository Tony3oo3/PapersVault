## Problem

Existing data pruning methods rely on the empirical mean to identify representative samples, but the mean has a breakdown point of zero—a single adversarial point can arbitrarily skew the selection. Under gross corruption (up to 50% of data arbitrarily perturbed), this causes pruning to select corrupted subsets, and methods that avoid outliers by keeping only 'easy' samples sacrifice diversity, creating a fundamental robustness-versus-diversity trade-off that has no principled resolution in the literature.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a dataset of RGB images (e.g., CIFAR-100 at 32×32, Tiny-ImageNet at 64×64) and a pretrained encoder (CLIP ViT-B/32 or ResNet-50) that maps each image into a fixed-dimensional embedding space. It outputs a pruned k-subset of the original images (not embeddings), selected via batched greedy iterations over the embedding space. A key practical detail is that the geometric median is computed over only a γ_GM-fraction subsample of the full dataset to reduce cost, and selection is performed in B batches of k/B samples each, enabling near-linear speedups at scale.

## Method

GM Matching replaces the empirical mean in kernel herding with the geometric median (GM), a robust estimator with an optimal 1/2 breakdown point. Concretely, it first computes an ε-approximate GM of the (potentially corrupted) embedding set via Weiszfeld's algorithm, then performs herding-style greedy selection: at each step it picks the sample whose embedding maximizes inner product with a direction vector θ, and updates θ by adding (GM − selected embedding), thereby steering future picks toward under-represented regions while penalizing distant outliers. The algorithm is batched into B groups of k/B selections for efficiency. Theoretically, GM Matching achieves O(1/k) convergence of the subset mean to a bounded neighborhood of the true uncorrupted mean—matching kernel herding's rate in clean settings while remaining valid under arbitrary corruption up to 50%—and the neighborhood radius depends only on the clean-data variance and the GM approximation error ε.

## Results

On CIFAR-100 (clean), GM Matching achieves a mean test accuracy of 66.01% across selection ratios (20%–100%), outperforming the strongest baseline Moderate-DS (64.37%) by ~1.6% and Random (62.67%) by ~3.3%. On Tiny-ImageNet (clean), GM Matching reaches 37.44% mean accuracy vs. 35.64% for Moderate-DS. Under 20% feature corruption on CIFAR-100, GM Matching achieves 63.21% mean accuracy, a ~3% improvement over the best baseline. Under 20% symmetric label noise, GM Matching yields 52.64% at 20% selection ratio on CIFAR-100, a ~12% absolute gain over Moderate-DS (40.25%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GM Matching outperforms all six baselines across every dataset and corruption setting. In the clean setting (Table 1), it beats Moderate-DS (the strongest spatial baseline) by ~1.6% on CIFAR-100 and ~1.8% on Tiny-ImageNet in mean accuracy, with the largest gap at low selection ratios (e.g., 55.93% vs. 51.83% at 20% on CIFAR-100). Under 20% feature corruption (Table 2), the advantage widens to ~3% over Moderate-DS (63.21% vs. 60.87% on CIFAR-100), while gradient-based methods like EL2N-score degrade severely (41.42% mean). Under 20% symmetric label noise (Table 3), GM Matching achieves 52.64% at 20% selection on CIFAR-100, surpassing Moderate-DS (40.25%) by over 12 points and Herding (42.29%) by ~10 points. Under PGD adversarial attacks, GM Matching yields 45.41% at 20% selection vs. 43.60% for Moderate-DS, a ~2% gain. Kernel Herding (Welling, 2009) and its parametric variant (Chen et al., 2010) are the closest architectural relatives; GM Matching matches or exceeds Herding in the clean setting but pulls significantly ahead under any corruption, confirming that the geometric median substitution is the key differentiator.

## Relation to Prior Work

GM Matching is a direct robustification of Kernel Herding (Welling, 2009; Chen et al., 2010), replacing the vulnerable empirical mean with the Geometric Median—a classical robust estimator with optimal 1/2 breakdown point. This places the paper at the intersection of robust statistics (Gross Corruption framework of Diakonikolas et al., 2019) and kernel-based coreset construction (Mirzasoleiman et al., 2020; Feldman, 2020). It departs from the broader data-pruning literature (importance scoring, Moderate-DS, gradient/uncertainty-based selection) by showing that all methods relying on the empirical mean inherit a zero breakdown point, and by framing the selection as a robust moment-matching objective in an RKHS rather than a per-sample scoring problem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first method to provide O(1/k) convergence guarantees for data pruning under arbitrary (up to 50%) corruption, closing a gap where prior robust pruning methods either handled only specific noise types or sacrificed diversity for robustness. The practical recipe—compute GM via Weiszfeld, then run herding-style greedy selection in a pretrained encoder space—is simple and drop-in replaceable for existing pipelines. Key limitations include dependence on embedding quality (a biased encoder undermines the guarantee) and the need for approximate GM computation, which can be ill-conditioned in degenerate cases. Open questions include whether adaptive or learned embeddings can further tighten the gap, and whether the approach extends naturally to multi-modal or sequential data.
