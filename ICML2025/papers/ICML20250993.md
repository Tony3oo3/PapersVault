## Problem

Free-form natural language hypotheses are difficult to validate directly because they are abstract and must be decomposed into measurable implications, and the surge of LLM-generated hypotheses makes manual validation impractical at scale. Existing LLM-driven approaches to hypothesis testing lack principled statistical error control, risking false verifications that misdirect research. The paper asks: how can we rigorously validate free-form hypotheses at scale with guaranteed Type-I error control?

## Input / Output

POPPER takes a natural language hypothesis H, a collection of hypothesis-free datasets (provided as schema/metadata to the design agent and as raw data to the execution agent), and a pre-specified significance level α. The design agent operates under metadata-only access (table names, column names, short descriptions) to satisfy the sequential information assumption and avoid training-data leakage, while the execution agent queries and analyzes the raw data in a Python sandbox. Output is a binary validation decision (reject/fail-to-reject H₀), the full sequence of designed falsification experiments with their p-values, and the cumulative e-value trajectory that determines the stopping decision.

## Method

POPPER is an agentic sequential falsification framework with two complementary LLM agents operating in an iterative loop. The Experiment Design Agent (with Self-Refine) proposes a falsification sub-hypothesis h_i⁰ that is a logical implication of the main null H₀, specifying null/alternative hypotheses and an experimental protocol; a Relevance Checker (LLM-as-a-judge) then verifies the implication strength to enforce Assumption 1. The Experiment Execution Agent (ReAct-based) implements the experiment in a Python environment, performing data retrieval, preprocessing, statistical testing, and error correction to produce a valid p-value. The key statistical contribution is a sequential testing framework that converts each p-value into an e-value via the p-to-e calibrator (e_i = κ·p_i^(κ−1)), aggregates them multiplicatively as E_i = ∏e_s, and applies Doob's optional stopping theorem to guarantee that the terminal e-value E_τ is a valid e-value (E[E_τ] ≤ 1 under H₀), yielding Type-I error control at level α when rejecting if E_τ ≥ 1/α. This super-martingale structure enables adaptive, any-time-valid stopping: the process can halt as soon as cumulative evidence crosses the threshold, without inflating the error rate.

## Results

POPPER is the only method that simultaneously controls Type-I error (α=0.1) across all three benchmarks while achieving the highest power among error-controlled methods: 63.8% on DiscoveryBench, 58.0% on TargetVal-IL2, and 59.1% on TargetVal-IFNG. On DiscoveryBench, POPPER delivers 66.5% greater power than ReAct (63.8% vs. 38.3%), and on TargetVal-IL2 it outperforms Self-Refine by a factor of 3.17 (58.0% vs. 18.3%). In a human expert study with nine PhD-level biostatisticians, POPPER matched human performance on both Type-I error and power while completing tasks 9.7× faster and generating 3.6× more lines of code.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among the three general-purpose baselines, ReAct is the only one that controls Type-I error across all three benchmarks, but its power is dramatically lower than POPPER's: 38.3% vs. 63.8% on DiscoveryBench (a 66.5% relative gap) and 1.0% vs. 58.0% on TargetVal-IL2. CodeGen fails Type-I error control on DiscoveryBench (0.145 vs. the 0.1 threshold) and achieves only 37.8% power there; its enhanced variant CodeGen(o1) is even worse on error (0.248) despite slightly higher power (41.9%). Self-Refine also exceeds the Type-I threshold on DiscoveryBench (0.117) and trails POPPER substantially on TargetVal-IL2 power (18.3% vs. 58.0%, a 3.17× gap). The Fisher Combined Test variant, while achieving the highest raw power (74.1–80.0%), completely fails Type-I error control on all three benchmarks (0.173–0.311), rendering its power estimates invalid. POPPER's advantage stems from its sequential e-value aggregation and the ReAct-based execution agent, which together enable iterative evidence accumulation without inflating false-positive rates.

## Relation to Prior Work

POPPER sits at the intersection of two largely separate literatures: LLM-based scientific reasoning (hypothesis generation, experiment coding) and rigorous sequential statistical testing. It builds directly on Vovk & Wang's p-to-e calibrator and Grünwald et al.'s safe testing framework (e-processes, optional stopping) to provide the statistical backbone, while drawing on ReAct and Self-Refine for the agentic execution loop. Conceptually, it departs from prior LLM-for-science work (DiscoveryBench, SciCode, BLADE, AI Scientist) by shifting the focus from hypothesis generation or code execution to the underexplored problem of statistically rigorous validation, and from Popperian falsification philosophy by operationalizing it as a sequential, adaptive testing procedure rather than a one-shot refutation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As LLMs generate hypotheses at scale, the validation bottleneck becomes critical; POPPER demonstrates that without principled error control, LLM agents systematically inflate Type-I errors (Fisher's combined test reaches 0.31 at α=0.1), making the statistical framework not optional but essential. The human study showing comparable accuracy to PhD-level biostatisticians at ~10× speedup is practically compelling, though the small sample (9 participants, 18 tasks) limits generalizability. Key open questions include extending per-hypothesis Type-I control to multiple-testing correction, addressing training-data leakage in static databases, and moving from data-analysis experiments to active wet-lab or real-time data collection where the sequential assumptions are more naturally satisfied.
