## Problem

Existing fMRI-based visual decoding methods are confined to single-subject paradigms or rely on implicit alignment that maps all subjects into a shared latent space, which suffers from suboptimal alignment due to substantial inter-subject brain variability and provides no functional interpretability. The paper asks whether an explicit, voxel-level brain alignment framework can enable both effective cross-subject decoding from limited fMRI data (a single ~1-hour session) and interpretable cross-subject functional analysis.

## Input / Output

The method takes a novel subject's fMRI signal (one scanning session, ~1 hour, representing 2.5% of the full NSD dataset) as input and produces a reconstructed RGB image via a pre-trained MindEye2 decoder. Additionally, it outputs a Brain Transfer Matrix (BTM) — a voxel-level linear transformation (e.g., 15,724×14,278 for subject pairs) — that encodes region-level inter-subject functional correspondences usable for neuroscience analysis. During inference, only the lightweight BTM (~139M parameters, 6% of the full model) is applied before feeding the aligned signal into the frozen decoder.

## Method

MindAligner learns a Brain Transfer Matrix (BTM) that explicitly projects a novel subject's fMRI into a known subject's voxel space, decomposed into two low-rank linear layers (A ∈ R^{n×h}, B ∈ R^{h×k}, h=4096) to create a shared latent space. To train the BTM without strictly paired fMRI under identical stimuli, a Brain Functional Alignment Module (BFA) employs a Cross-Stimulus Neural Mapper that uses CLIP embedding differences between visually similar stimuli as FiLM-style conditioning to transform fMRI embeddings across subjects. A multi-level alignment loss combines signal-level reconstruction (L_rec), distribution consistency (L_KL), and a semantic-guided latent alignment loss (L_latent) that enforces consistency between fMRI embedding dissimilarity matrices and CLIP image embedding dissimilarity matrices. The key design choice is explicit pairwise alignment (one novel subject mapped to one known subject at a time) rather than implicit multi-subject alignment to a shared space, which avoids semantic conflicts and preserves fine-grained functional correspondences. The linear structure is justified by established neuroscience findings that inter-subject representational differences are largely linear, and ablations confirm linear layers outperform Transformer alternatives in this data-limited regime.

## Results

On the NSD dataset with only 1 hour of fMRI data (2.5% of full sessions), MindAligner achieves 0.206 PixCorr, 85.6% Alex(2), 91.6% Alex(5), 83.0% Inception, and 81.2% CLIP similarity, outperforming MindEye2 (0.195, 84.2%, 90.6%, 81.2%, 79.2%) on nearly all metrics. The most striking gain is in brain retrieval, where MindAligner reaches 75.3% versus MindEye2's 57.4% (a 17.9% absolute improvement). MindAligner achieves this while training only 139.23M parameters (6.2% of the full 2.21G MindEye2 model), with inference time increasing by only 0.056s per image.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against MindEye2 (the primary baseline), MindAligner wins on 8 of 10 metrics in the 1-hour average setting, with the largest gains in brain retrieval (75.3% vs. 57.4%, +17.9 pp), CLIP similarity (81.2% vs. 79.2%), and Inception (83.0% vs. 81.2%). MindAligner slightly trails MindEye2 on SSIM (0.414 vs. 0.419) and ties on image retrieval (both 79.0%). Against MindBridge, MindAligner dominates across every reported metric, e.g., PixCorr 0.206 vs. 0.109 and Alex(2) 85.6% vs. 79.0%, reflecting MindBridge's reliance on pseudo-stimulus generation rather than explicit voxel-level alignment. Per-subject, MindAligner consistently surpasses MindEye2 on brain retrieval (e.g., subj7: 62.58% vs. 37.77%) and high-level semantic metrics, while MindEye2 retains a small edge on low-level SSIM for subj1 and subj2. The paper also reports that MindAligner trains only 139.23M parameters (6.2% of MindEye2's 2.21G), making it substantially more parameter-efficient for new-subject adaptation.

## Relation to Prior Work

MindAligner builds directly on MindEye2 (Scotti et al., 2024b), which established the cross-subject fMRI-to-image decoding paradigm using implicit alignment (ridge regression into a shared latent space) followed by a diffusion-prior decoding backbone. The key conceptual departure is replacing this implicit, many-to-one latent alignment with an explicit Brain Transfer Matrix that performs voxel-level, one-to-one mapping between a novel subject and a single known subject, drawing on the explicit functional alignment tradition (Bazeille et al., 2019; Thual et al., 2022) but removing the requirement for shared stimuli via a FiLM-based cross-stimulus neural mapper. The low-rank decomposition of the BTM and the multi-level alignment loss (reconstruction + KL + latent semantic consistency) are novel architectural choices that enable reliable learning from only one hour of fMRI data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By requiring only 6% of the decoding model's parameters to be trained per new subject, MindAligner makes cross-subject brain decoding practically feasible in clinical and BCI settings where fMRI acquisition is expensive and time-limited. The explicit BTM also yields interpretable, region-level cross-subject variability maps (e.g., conserved early visual cortex vs. highly variable ventral pathway), offering neuroscience insights that implicit latent-space alignment cannot provide. Open questions include whether the approach scales beyond the four NSD subjects tested, whether the CLIP-semantic guidance generalizes to non-visual or more diverse stimulus categories, and whether the BTM can be learned without any paired (even soft-paired) fMRI data at all.
