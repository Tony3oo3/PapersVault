## Problem

Reward models trained via standard BTL regression on observational preference data are prone to learning spurious correlations rather than true causal drivers of user preferences, leading to poor generalisation under distribution shift. The paper identifies and formalises a previously unaddressed source of bias—confounding due to user-specific objectives that influence both the prompts users write and the preferences they express—and shows that naive models fail catastrophically when this confounding is present. It asks what causal assumptions are necessary for reliable generalisation and how violations manifest in practice.

## Input / Output

The method consumes prompt-response pairs (x, y, y') as natural language text, processed through frozen Llama-3-8B embeddings, along with a binary preference label ℓ and, in the multi-objective setting, a user-specific objective label c ∈ {0,1}. It outputs a scalar reward score per response, with the preference prediction derived from the BTL sigmoid of the reward difference. The multi-objective variant routes the shared latent representation through objective-specific heads, so the same text can receive different reward scores depending on the evaluation objective (e.g., helpfulness vs. harmlessness).

## Method

The paper reframes preference learning within the potential outcomes framework, treating each (X, Y, Y') tuple as a treatment and the preference label L as the outcome, then identifies the assumptions (consistency, unconfoundedness, positivity) required for causal identifiability. Because observational positivity is infeasible in the high-dimensional space of natural language, the authors introduce a latent treatment model that compresses text into interpretable factors Z^X (prompt artifacts) and Z^T (response features in context), replacing observational positivity with the weaker latent positivity assumption. The key architectural contribution is an adversarial multi-objective reward model: a shared encoder g_θ maps (x,y) to a latent representation, two independent heads predict rewards for each objective, and an adversarial discriminator h_φ trained via gradient reversal forces the latent representation to be uninformative about the confounding variable (prompt type as a proxy for user objective). The training objective is min_{θ,w} max_φ L_BTL − λ·L_adv, balancing reward prediction against removal of spurious confounding information. Empirically, this architecture significantly reduces overfitting and improves generalisation to inconsistent (out-of-distribution) prompt-objective pairings compared to a base concatenated model.

## Results

On the UltraFeedback experiment, BTL model OOD accuracy drops from 64.5% (ρ^tr=0.0) to 57.8% (ρ^tr=0.9) as latent factor correlation increases, while ID accuracy remains stable (69.3%→67.6%), demonstrating that limited latent positivity is the key bottleneck for generalisation. On the HH-RLHF case study, the proposed Adversarial multi-objective reward model achieves 63.7% test accuracy on inconsistent (OOD) samples at ρ=0.5, outperforming the Base model (60.0%) and Multihead model (62.9%). Under full confounding (ρ=1.0), all models fail to generalise to inconsistent samples (~53–55%), confirming that latent overlap violations make the task fundamentally infeasible without additional data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three multi-objective BTL reward model architectures: (1) Base, which concatenates the objective label with the prompt-response embedding; (2) Multihead, which learns a shared latent representation and routes it to objective-specific prediction heads; and (3) Adversarial, which adds a gradient-reversal-based adversarial objective to the Multihead architecture to remove confounding information from the latent space. The Adversarial model consistently outperforms Base on inconsistent (OOD) test samples by 3.7–5.4 percentage points across all confounding levels (e.g., 63.7% vs. 60.0% at ρ=0.5; 58.9% vs. 55.9% at ρ=0.9). The Multihead architecture also beats Base on inconsistent samples by 2.3–2.9 points, confirming that separating latent representation learning from objective-conditioned reward prediction improves robustness. On consistent (ID) samples, all three models perform comparably (60–68%), with the gap between architectures being minimal. At ρ=1.0 (full confounding, no overlap), all models collapse to near-chance on inconsistent samples (~53–55%), demonstrating that architectural modifications alone cannot overcome fundamental identifiability failures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reframes preference learning for RLHF through the potential outcomes framework, extending the latent treatment model of Fong & Grimmer (2016, 2023) from single-outcome text analysis to the pairwise BTL preference setting. It departs from prior reward-hacking work (Singhal et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024) that focuses on response-specific artefacts like length or formatting by identifying user-specific objectives as a confounding variable that simultaneously shapes prompt generation and preference scoring—an issue the authors claim is unaddressed in the alignment literature. The proposed adversarial multi-objective architecture adapts domain-adversarial training (Ganin et al., 2016) and adversarial balancing for treatment effect estimation (Bica et al., 2020; Ozery-Flato et al., 2020) to the reward modelling context, while the identifiability analysis draws on standard causal inference assumptions (consistency, unconfoundedness, positivity) adapted to the high-dimensional text space.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of user-specific confounding as a systematic threat to reward model generalisation is a genuinely important conceptual contribution: if preference data is collected opportunistically with self-written prompts, the resulting reward models may encode spurious correlations between prompt type and user objective rather than true causal response features. However, the experimental evidence is limited to a single real dataset (HH-RLHF) and a synthetic UltraFeedback setup, and the paper assumes explicit access to user objectives—unobserved confounding remains an open challenge. The authors outline promising but unrealised directions (rationale elicitation, active interventional data collection, interpretable LLM control) that would be needed to move from this theoretical framework to practical deployment, and the multi-dataset/personalised reward modelling setting is explicitly deferred to future work.
