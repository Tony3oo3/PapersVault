## Problem

Training LLMs at FP4 (4-bit floating-point) precision remains infeasible because the E2M1 format offers only 16 representable values, causing severe quantization errors that lead to training divergence or large accuracy gaps. While FP8 training has been validated, no prior work has demonstrated a stable FP4 pretraining framework for LLMs that achieves accuracy comparable to BF16 or FP8 baselines.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252492/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes a standard LLaMA 2 transformer architecture and a text corpus (DCLM dataset) with 2048-token sequences and batch size 2048 (~4M tokens per step). During GeMM operations, both activation tensors (quantized token-wise) and weight tensors (quantized channel-wise) are cast to the E2M1 FP4 format via absmax scaling with a look-up table, while non-GeMM operations (LayerNorm, attention scores, softmax, GeLU) remain in BF16/FP16. The output is a fully pretrained LLM (1.3B, 7B, or 13B parameters) trained on up to 100B tokens whose downstream zero-shot accuracy and perplexity match BF16-trained counterparts within a small margin.

## Method

The paper introduces two key mechanisms to make FP4 training stable. For weights, a Differentiable Gradient Estimator (DGE) replaces the Straight-Through Estimator by approximating the hard quantization function with a smooth power-based surrogate f(x) = δ/2·(1 + sign(2x/δ−1)·|2x/δ−1|^(1/k)) (k=5), whose derivative provides a correction term applied element-wise to the weight gradient during backpropagation while the forward pass retains hard quantization for hardware efficiency. For activations, an Outlier Clamping and Compensation (OCC) strategy clamps values exceeding a 99th-percentile threshold to prevent underflow, then compensates the clamped residual ΔY via a sparse high-precision matrix multiplication (only ~2% non-zero elements). The full scheme uses vector-wise (not tensor-wise) quantization granularity, mixed-precision Adam with FP8 gradient communication, and is validated by emulating FP4 on H100 FP8 tensor cores, achieving training loss within ~0.09 of BF16 at 100B tokens across 1.3B–13B models.

## Results

After training on 100B tokens, FP4 models achieve training losses of 2.55 (1.3B), 2.17 (7B), and 1.97 (13B), compared to BF16 losses of 2.49, 2.07, and 1.88 respectively — a gap of only 0.06–0.09. On zero-shot downstream tasks, FP4-trained models match or slightly exceed BF16 baselines in average accuracy: 53.13 vs. 53.23 (1.3B), 54.42 vs. 53.87 (7B), and 54.95 vs. 54.44 (13B). Perplexity evaluations confirm comparable performance, with FP4 achieving average PPL of 36.86, 35.62, and 33.99 versus BF16's 37.38, 35.06, and 33.69 across the three model sizes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is BF16 mixed-precision training, against which FP4 achieves a training loss gap of only 0.06–0.09 after 100B tokens and matches or slightly exceeds BF16 average zero-shot accuracy at 7B (+0.55) and 13B (+0.51), while being marginally lower at 1.3B (−0.10). In the ablation study (Figure 6a), both FP8 baselines — MS-AMP FP8 (Peng et al., 2023) and NVIDIA Transformer Engine FP8 (Nvidia, 2022) — maintain pretraining accuracy comparable to BF16, and the proposed FP4 method (W4A4+DGE+OCC) achieves a similar loss trajectory to these FP8 methods. In contrast, directly casting to FP4 without the proposed DGE and OCC techniques results in a significantly higher training loss gap, confirming that the two key innovations are necessary to close the precision gap. The FP4 method thus demonstrates that 4-bit training can match 8-bit and 16-bit baselines in both training loss and downstream task performance, validating FP4 as a viable ultra-low-precision training format.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the low-precision training lineage from FP8 (Peng et al., 2023; NVIDIA Transformer Engine) down to FP4, the first time 4-bit floating-point has been used for LLM pretraining from scratch. It departs from the Straight-Through Estimator (Bengio et al., 2013) by introducing a differentiable gradient estimator (DGE) that corrects the vanishing-gradient problem inherent to hard quantization, and from prior 4-bit work (which targeted CNNs, fine-tuning, or inference-only PTQ/QAT) by addressing the unique activation outlier dynamics of LLM pretraining through a clamping-and-compensation strategy. The mixed-precision training scheme and vector-wise quantization granularity build directly on the FP8-LM framework, adapted to the tighter dynamic range of E2M1.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first empirical validation that FP4 pretraining is feasible for LLMs up to 13B parameters, establishing a foundation for next-generation hardware (NVIDIA B200) that natively supports FP4 tensor cores with 2× throughput over FP8. The practical impact is significant: if the ~3× theoretical speedup materializes on real hardware, training costs for large-scale LLMs could drop substantially. Key limitations include the absence of native FP4 hardware (all results are simulated via FP8 tensor cores, so actual speedup and energy savings remain unverified) and the lack of experiments at trillion-token or 100B+ parameter scales. Open questions include whether the DGE and OCC techniques generalize to other 4-bit formats (E1M2, E3M0) and whether the approach remains stable at much larger scales where quantization errors may compound.
