## Problem

Gradient clipping, Adam, Adagrad, and methods for (L₀,L₁)-smooth functions are widely used in practice but lack a unifying theoretical framework that explains their behavior and provides convergence guarantees. Standard Lipschitz smoothness is too restrictive for problems where the Hessian norm grows with the gradient norm, and existing generalized smoothness notions (Bregman, Hölder) do not naturally capture the coordinate-wise or norm-dependent stepsize adaptations these algorithms employ.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a C¹ objective f, a reference function φ generated by a 1-D kernel h (either isotropic φ=h∘‖·‖ or anisotropic φ=Σᵢh(xᵢ)), and two scalar stepsizes γ, λ. Unlike standard gradient descent, φ may have a non-full domain (e.g., h(x)=-|x|-ln(1-|x|) with dom h=(-1,1)), which constrains the iterates implicitly. The output is a sequence {x^k} with an O(1/K) bound on a φ-based stationarity measure in the nonconvex case, and an O(1/K) suboptimality gap rate in the convex case (the latter requiring either isotropic φ or 2-subhomogeneity of φ).

## Method

The core iteration is x^{k+1} = x^k − γ∇φ*(λ∇f(x^k)), where φ* is the convex conjugate of a strongly convex, even reference function φ. The key insight is that ∇φ* acts as a sigmoid-shaped nonlinear preconditioner on the gradient: in the isotropic case it scales the gradient by a scalar depending on ‖∇f‖, and in the anisotropic case it applies coordinate-wise scaling, thereby unifying gradient clipping, Adam (β₁=β₂=0), Adagrad (without memory), and (L₀,L₁)-smoothness algorithms under a single majorization-minimization framework. The paper introduces (L,L̄)-anisotropic smoothness—a descent inequality with two constants and a possibly non-full-domain φ—and proves it is strictly less restrictive than L-smoothness. Sufficient second-order conditions are derived via the Clarke generalized Jacobian of ∇φ*, yielding novel characterizations of (L₀,L₁)-smooth functions (e.g., monotonicity of the associated forward operator). Convergence in the nonconvex setting follows directly from the descent inequality with stepsizes up to 2L⁻¹, while the convex analysis leverages the envelope representation of anisotropically smooth functions and a nonlinear proximal-point interpretation to obtain O(1/K) suboptimality rates.

## Results

This is primarily a theoretical paper with illustrative experiments rather than a benchmark study with tabulated metrics. In the nonconvex phase retrieval experiment (n=100, m=3000), both the isotropic and anisotropic versions of the proposed method (using cosh−1 kernel) outperform vanilla gradient descent, gradient clipping, and Chen et al. (2023) Algorithm 1 in terms of convergence speed to the minimum cost value. Averaged over 100 random instances, the isotropic algorithm consistently outperforms gradient clipping. In the MNIST neural network training experiment (4-layer FC, m=600 subset), the proposed methods with cosh and −||x||−ln(1−||x||) kernels show competitive or superior convergence compared to gradient clipping, though the relative performance depends on the choice of stepsize parameters γ and λ.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two main baselines: gradient clipping (Zhang et al., 2020b) and the adaptive method from Chen et al. (2023, Algorithm 1). In the nonconvex phase retrieval problem, both the isotropic and anisotropic variants of the proposed nonlinearly preconditioned gradient method outperform all baselines, with the isotropic version showing a clear and consistent advantage over gradient clipping when averaged across 100 random instances (Figure 3). In the MNIST neural network training experiment, the comparison is more nuanced: the proposed methods with different kernel functions (cosh−1 and −||x||−ln(1−||x||)) exhibit different convergence behaviors depending on the tuning of γ and λ, and no single method uniformly dominates across all parameter settings (Figure 4). The paper also notes that the gradient clipping method itself can be recovered as a special case of the proposed framework (Example 1.7), providing a unifying theoretical perspective rather than a strict performance win in all settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the anisotropic smoothness framework of Laude & Patrinos (2025) and the dual-space preconditioning scheme of Maddison et al. (2021) by introducing a two-constant (L, L̄) generalization that permits reference functions with non-full domain, and by providing novel first- and second-order sufficient conditions via nonsmooth analysis rather than the restrictive non-negative cross curvature condition used by Léger & Aubin-Frankowski (2023). A key conceptual departure is the explicit connection to Φ-convexity and majorization-minimization, which reveals that gradient clipping, Adam, and Adagrad are all instances of the same nonlinearly preconditioned gradient iteration with different kernel functions. The paper also builds on the (L₀, L₁)-smoothness literature (Zhang et al., 2020b; Gorbunov et al., 2024) by showing these functions are a special case of anisotropic smoothness and deriving new monotonicity characterizations for the associated forward operator.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The unification of gradient clipping, Adam, and Adagrad under a single majorization-minimization framework provides a principled lens for understanding why these heuristics work and for designing new preconditioners with guaranteed convergence. The novel (L₀, L₁)-smoothness characterization via forward-operator monotonicity (Corollary 2.7) offers a new tool for analyzing the recently popular class of functions whose Hessian norm grows linearly with gradient norm. Open questions include extending the analysis to momentum-based variants, the stochastic setting, and the additive nonsmooth composite case, all of which the authors note are non-trivial extensions. The experimental validation, while illustrative, is limited to toy problems and a small MNIST network, leaving open whether the theoretical advantages translate to large-scale deep learning.
