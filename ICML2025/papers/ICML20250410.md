## Problem

Dataset contamination—where benchmark evaluation data overlaps with a model's pre-training corpus—inflates reported performance and undermines the reliability of LLM evaluations. While membership inference attacks can classify individual samples as seen or unseen, there is no reliable dataset-level scoring function that quantifies the *degree* of contamination and satisfies both monotonicity (score increases with contamination fraction) and consistency (stable across i.i.d. resamples at the same rate). Existing baselines such as perplexity, Min-K%, and SRCT exhibit inconsistent or even reversed correlations across different datasets.

## Input / Output

The method takes a benchmark dataset of n text samples and a pre-trained LLM as input, and produces a single scalar contamination score S(D, M) that is positively correlated with the unknown fraction λ of seen data. Unusually, the method requires an intermediate fine-tuning step: the model is LoRA-fine-tuned on the benchmark dataset itself for one epoch with SGD (batch size 4), and embeddings are extracted from the final layer both before and after this fine-tuning. The score is computed from the n×n kernel similarity matrices of these two embedding sets, so computational cost scales as O(N²) for the kernel computation (though in practice this is sub-millisecond for typical dataset sizes).

## Method

KDS exploits the observation that supervised fine-tuning on a benchmark dataset shifts the embeddings of unseen samples more than those of seen samples, which the model has already internalized during pre-training. Concretely, it computes the RBF kernel similarity matrix Φ(Z) = exp(−γ‖Zᵢ−Zⱼ‖²) over normalized final-layer embeddings before fine-tuning, and the analogous matrix Φ(Z′) after one epoch of LoRA fine-tuning on the benchmark. The Kernel Divergence is then the normalized sum of |Φ(Z)ᵢⱼ · log(Φ(Z)ᵢⱼ / Φ(Z′)ᵢⱼ)| over all pairs, which decomposes into a soft-gating term (original similarity) multiplied by the absolute change in pairwise distance induced by fine-tuning. The final contamination score is the negation of this divergence, so higher scores indicate greater contamination. Ablations show the fine-tuning component is critical (removing it drops Spearman from 0.999 to 0.683), while the method is robust to kernel choice (RBF, Euclidean, cosine, dot-product all perform comparably) and to bandwidth γ across a wide range.

## Results

On the Monotonicity evaluation (Table 1), KDS achieves an average Spearman correlation of 0.990 and Pearson of 0.982 across WikiMIA, BookMIA, and ArxivTection, with near-perfect per-dataset scores (e.g., 0.999 Spearman on WikiMIA, 0.997 on BookMIA). The best non-kernel baselines (Perplexity, Min-K%) reach only 0.964–0.966 average Spearman, and several baselines exhibit *negative* correlations on BookMIA (e.g., Zlib at −1.000, Min-K%+FSD at −0.526). On the PILE subsets (Table 5), KDS achieves an average Spearman of 0.944, outperforming the next-best baseline (Min-K%+FSD at 0.879).

## Baselines & Comparisons

KDS outperforms all six baseline families on average Spearman correlation (0.990 vs. best baseline 0.966 for Perplexity). The most striking gap is on BookMIA, where Zlib (−1.000), all FSD variants (−0.526 to −0.888), and Min-K%++ (OOM) fail catastrophically, while KDS maintains 0.997 Spearman. Perplexity and Min-K% are the strongest individual baselines, achieving near-perfect correlations on two of three datasets but still trailing KDS by ~3 points in average Spearman. SRCT (Oren et al., 2024), the only other dataset-level method, is evaluated only on WikiMIA and scores a negligible 0.080 Spearman. On the PILE subsets (Table 5), KDS leads with 0.944 average Spearman versus 0.879 for Min-K%+FSD and 0.868 for Zlib+FSD, confirming the advantage generalizes beyond the MIA benchmarks. The paper attributes KDS's consistency to its direct use of structural kernel information rather than per-sample likelihood scores, which are more susceptible to distributional shifts across datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of membership inference attacks (MIA) for LLMs and dataset-level contamination scoring. It draws on the fine-tuning-based detection idea from FSD (Zhang et al., 2025), which showed that score changes after fine-tuning can reveal pre-training data exposure, but repurposes this signal through a fundamentally different lens: rather than comparing per-sample likelihood scores before and after fine-tuning, KDS computes the divergence of the full RBF kernel similarity matrix over sample embeddings. This elevates the analysis from instance-level to a structural, pairwise-relationship level, which is the first use of kernel-based information for contamination scoring. The dataset-level scoring framing (a single scalar per benchmark) aligns with SRCT (Oren et al., 2024) and PACOST (Zhang et al., 2024a), but KDS is the first to exploit embedding geometry via kernels rather than likelihood statistics or rank comparisons.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Dataset contamination undermines the reliability of LLM benchmark evaluations, and a principled, monotonic scoring function is needed to rank benchmarks by contamination level. KDS provides this with near-perfect correlation in controlled settings and strong (though slightly degraded) performance on more realistic PILE subsets, while being robust to kernel choice, bandwidth, and embedding layer. Key limitations include the O(N²) kernel computation that becomes a bottleneck for very large datasets, the requirement for a fine-tuning pass (adding compute overhead), and evaluation on only three model families and relatively small subsets (≤4000 samples). Open questions include whether the approach scales to million-sample benchmarks, whether PU learning can handle partially known contamination, and whether kernel calibration is needed for cross-model comparability.
