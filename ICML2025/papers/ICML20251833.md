## Problem

Training LLMs in FP8 (8-bit floating point) precision is challenging due to reduced numerical range and resolution. Existing approaches require dynamic per-tensor scaling factors, additional hyperparameters beyond standard training, or restricting certain critical matmuls to BF16, making it difficult to achieve stable FP8 training across all hidden layers at large model scales while also enabling zero-shot hyperparameter transfer from small proxy models.

## Input / Output

The method trains decoder-only transformer LLMs on text tokens (sequence length 4096, global batch sizes 1024–2048) and produces models ranging from 1B to 13B parameters. All hidden linear layers perform computation in FP8 (E4M3 for weights and activations, E5M2 for gradients) with a static 1/√fan_in scaling factor, while the embedding table and LM head remain in BF16. Because training already occurs in FP8, the resulting model is immediately deployable for W8A8 FP8 inference without additional quantization steps.

## Method

unit Scaling (μS) combines μP and Unit Scaling into a simplified scheme requiring only three hyperparameters (learning rate η, weight decay λ, residual coefficient τ). The key architectural modifications are: (1) Square-Root Softmax attention, which takes the square root of attention scores to make self-attention variance-preserving (addressing the 1/k variance decay proven in Prop. 2.1); (2) Res-Post-LayerNorm, moving normalization to the end of each residual branch to ensure consistent token variance regardless of sequence position; and (3) a fixed residual modification x_{l+1} = √(1−τ)·x_l + √τ·f(x_l) for variance-preserving skip connections. All hidden linear layers use unit-variance initialization with a static 1/√fan_in output multiplier fused into the GEMM call, and hidden-layer learning rates scale as √(d_base/d_new) when transferring from a base width of 256 to widths up to 5120, enabling 20× zero-shot hyperparameter transfer without dynamic scaling factors.

## Results

Across 1B, 3B, 7B, and 13B parameter models, μS FP8 models achieve final training loss within 0.01–0.02 of their BF16 counterparts (e.g., 2.119 vs 2.108 at 13B) and match or exceed SP BF16 quality on most of 16 evaluation benchmarks. At the 13B scale, SP FP8 (TransformerEngine) diverges with catastrophic quality loss (e.g., 35.7% on ARC Easy vs 72.3% for SP BF16), while μS FP8 maintains 69.7%. FP8 training with μS achieves 25–33% higher throughput than BF16 and 1–6% higher throughput than TransformerEngine on 64 H100 GPUs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against TransformerEngine (SP FP8), μS FP8 matches or exceeds SP BF16 quality at all model sizes (1B–13B), while SP FP8 suffers frequent loss spikes and fails to converge at 13B (e.g., 35.7% vs 72.3% on ARC Easy, 1.5% vs 52.9% on SQuAD). In throughput benchmarks on 64 H100 GPUs, μS FP8 is 1–6% faster than TransformerEngine FP8 and 25–33% faster than BF16, owing to the elimination of dynamic scaling factor computation. Against Unit Scaling (US) at 7B scale with FP8, μS converges smoothly while US diverges early in training (Appendix A.5), motivating the paper's focus on SP and μS for final large-scale results. Overall, μS is the only method that simultaneously provides FP8 computation for all hidden linear layers, zero-shot hyperparameter transfer (20× width), no dynamic scaling, and matched training-inference precision.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes and simplifies two prior lines of work—Maximal Update Parametrization (μP, Yang et al., 2021) for zero-shot hyperparameter transfer and Unit Scaling (Blake et al., 2023) for variance-preserving low-precision training—into a single scheme (μS) that requires only 3 hyperparameters versus 6–7 for μP or u-μP. It departs from prior Unit Scaling work by extending FP8 computation to all hidden linear layers (rather than excluding ~42% of FLOPs as 'critical matmuls'), eliminating dynamic scaling factors used by libraries like TransformerEngine, and introducing Square-Root Softmax attention plus Res-Post-LayerNorm to fix the variance collapse in causal self-attention that prior analyses had not addressed. The hyperparameter transfer rules follow directly from the μP invariance theorem (Eq. 16) with a specific choice of scale factor θ that yields unit-variance initialization and 1/√fan_in output scaling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: μS enables 25–33% faster training than BF16 with no dynamic scaling overhead, stable FP8 training up to 13B parameters (where SP+TransformerEngine diverges), and immediate W8A8 inference readiness—properties that collectively lower the barrier to large-scale low-precision training. Open questions include whether the method scales beyond 13B parameters, the unresolved tension between GELU's better convergence and its up-to-30% FP8 underflow in FFN inputs (the authors chose GELU despite this), and whether Square-Root Softmax attention has subtle long-term effects on model quality that short training runs cannot reveal. The activation outlier analysis suggesting μS models are more easily quantizable hints at downstream inference benefits not yet fully explored.
