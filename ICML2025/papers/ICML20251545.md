## Problem

Standard MARL-based chute mapping policies in robotic sortation warehouses degrade significantly when deployment-time package induction distributions deviate from training-time patterns, a common occurrence due to seasonal and year-over-year volume shifts. Existing robust RL methods target adversarial perturbations in transition dynamics or reward functions but do not explicitly model distributional uncertainty in the induction process itself, leaving a gap in handling the specific reward-distribution shifts that drive recirculation in sortation systems.

## Input / Output

Each destination agent receives a local observation consisting of its cumulative recirculation count, the total number of available chutes (shared across all agents), and its currently assigned chute count. The environment additionally draws a daily induction pattern from a multinomial distribution over D×T destination-time pairs with a fixed total volume V, which determines the stochastic reward. The output is a per-destination action specifying the number of new chutes to acquire (0–10 in the large-scale setting), with the joint action constrained by a global budget on total chutes, resolved via an integer program (solved with OR-Tools or Xpress) at each step.

## Method

DRMARL formulates chute mapping as a group DRO problem where the ambiguity set is the convex hull of m historical induction distributions, and derives a distributionally robust Bellman operator that takes the infimum of expected reward across all groups at each state-action pair, proven to be a contraction mapping under the ℓ∞ norm. To avoid the O(m) cost of exhaustively evaluating every group at each step, the paper introduces a contextual bandit (implemented as an independent DQN) that takes (s,a) as context and the m groups as arms, learning to predict which group yields the worst-case reward in O(1) time. Training is two-phase: first, Q_CB is trained via Algorithm 1 using the existing MARL policy to guide state-action exploration; then the DRMARL policy is trained via Algorithm 2, where at each step Q_CB selects the worst-case group to compute the robust Bellman target. The joint Q-function uses a shared VDN decomposition with budget-constrained integer programming for feasible action generation, and the overall framework reduces average recirculation by ~80% over the MARL baseline across 21 OOD induction groups.

## Results

In the simplified warehouse (9 groups, 100 runs), DRMARL with Q_CB achieves a recirculation rate of 0.56%±0.18% and recirculation amount of 67.79, compared to MARL's 2.16%±2.35% and 259.02, while throughput improves from 11740.98 to 11932.21. In the large-scale warehouse (21 groups, 120 destinations), DRMARL reduces recirculation rate by 79.97% and increases throughput by 5.62% on average over the MARL baseline. Against robust RL baselines, DRMARL achieves 77.09% CVaR reduction, 76.61% worst-case recirculation reduction, and 79.97% recirculation rate reduction, outperforming all three robust RL methods on every metric.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DRMARL substantially outperforms the standard MARL baseline (Shen et al., 2023) across all metrics: in the large-scale warehouse, recirculation rate drops from 2.16% to 0.56% (79.97% reduction) and throughput increases by 5.62%, while the oracle group-specific MARL achieves only marginally better results (85.42% reduction), confirming DRMARL's near-optimal robustness. Against the three robust RL baselines in Table 3, DRMARL dominates on all three metrics: it achieves 77.09% CVaR reduction vs. 61.11% (Adversarial Agent, Mandlekar et al., 2017), 58.48% (Adversarial Environment, Pinto et al., 2017), and 58.97% (Robustified Reward, Wiesemann et al., 2013); 76.61% worst-case recirculation reduction vs. 53.57%, 23.68%, and 60.23% respectively; and 79.97% recirculation rate reduction vs. 66.19%, 77.09%, and 70.43%. The Adversarial Environment baseline is particularly weak on worst-case recirculation (23.68%), while the Robustified Reward baseline is closest to DRMARL on worst-case recirculation (60.23%) but still trails by over 16 percentage points. The Q_CB-based estimator matches the exhaustive-search DRMARL in final performance (0.56% vs. 0.55% recirculation rate) while reducing training time from over 2900 seconds to under 300 seconds in the simplified environment, and from ~924 hours to a fraction of that in the large-scale setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the authors' own prior MARL framework for dynamic chute mapping (Shen et al., 2023) by incorporating group distributionally robust optimization to handle distributional shifts in package induction patterns. It departs from existing distributionally robust RL work, which primarily addresses uncertainty in MDP transition probabilities, by instead formulating robustness over the reward distribution induced by varying induction patterns. The contextual bandit-based worst-case reward estimator is a novel contribution that bridges group DRO (originally developed for supervised learning, e.g., Sagawa et al., 2020) with multi-agent RL, addressing the computational intractability of exhaustive group evaluation. The robust RL baselines compared (adversarial agents, adversarial environments, robustified rewards) represent the standard robustness toolkit, but none explicitly model distributional shifts in the reward-generating process.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 80% average reduction in package recirculation and the O(m)→O(1) complexity reduction for worst-case group identification make this framework practically deployable in large-scale warehouse operations where exhaustive evaluation is infeasible. The demonstrated generalization to distributions outside the training ambiguity set (Wasserstein distance 818 vs. intra-group 543) suggests the approach can handle genuinely novel operational conditions, though the paper acknowledges that the conservative strategy can underperform when a large fraction of destinations become inactive. Open questions include whether the CB-based estimator remains accurate as the number of distribution groups grows substantially, how the method performs under truly adversarial (rather than historical) distribution shifts, and whether the approximation in the large-scale Bellman operator (Eq. 30) introduces compounding errors over long horizons.
