## Problem

Responsible AI (RAI) has gained significant traction but lacks a stable definition and fails to specify who is responsible, for what, to whom, while application-driven machine learning (ADML) advances methods without adequately integrating responsible innovation principles. The AI research community remains constrained by disciplinary boundaries, narrow success metrics, and opaque black-box processing, producing solutions that are misaligned with contextual needs, societal values, and public trust.

## Input / Output

As a position paper proposing a research framework rather than a computational method, RAD-AI has no traditional data input or output. The framework takes as input the full contextual landscape of an AI application—sector-specific ethical codes, legal and policy requirements, community values, domain-specific constraints, and public discourse—and produces a structured three-stage research process yielding contextually aligned AI systems with tailored success metrics, staged validation protocols, and a sustained community of practice.

## Method

RAD-AI proposes a three-staged pathway to break out of the 'boxes' of disciplinary boundaries, problem definition, narrow metrics, and opaque processing. Stage 1 (Laying the Foundation) builds transdisciplinary teams incorporating non-traditional knowledge holders and employs people-centred research methods—human-centred design or community-based participatory research—to ensure co-creation and two-way knowledge exchange. Stage 2 (Navigating Complexity) advances explainability of black-box methods through intermediate concept learning and differentiable models, co-identifies application-specific legal and ethical considerations, critically examines tacit assumptions and unmeasured factors, and establishes context-specific success metrics beyond standard benchmarks (e.g., via the Delphi method). Stage 3 (Testing and Sustaining Efficacy) uses staged digital testbeds for iterative validation against both technical and ethical standards while building a RAD-AI community of practice to sustain the approach across generations. The framework is explicitly positioned as a complement to existing resources such as the AAAS Decision Tree and ISO/IEC 42001, treating responsible AI engagement as a continuing, dynamic conversation rather than a one-time checklist.

## Results

This is a position paper proposing a three-staged framework (RAD-AI) for responsible, application-driven AI research. It presents no quantitative experiments, benchmarks, or empirical results. The contributions are conceptual: a critique of technological determinism and RAI's under-specification, a three-stage pathway (transdisciplinary teams → context-specific methods/ethics/metrics → staged testbeds and community of practice), and a rebuttal of three counter-positions (AGI sufficiency, RAI checklists, and open-source AI).

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not compare methods empirically against baselines. The closest analogues to 'comparisons' are the conceptual contrasts drawn in Section 4 (Alternative Views): (1) RAD-AI vs. AGI — the authors argue AGI's scale obscures failure points and homogenises defects across downstream models, whereas RAD-AI's staged approach dynamically addresses context-specific risks; (2) RAD-AI vs. a static RAI checklist — they argue checklists oversimplify context, while RAD-AI treats responsibility as a continuing, dynamic conversation; (3) RAD-AI vs. 'open' AI — they cite Widder et al. (2024) to argue open-source AI lacks precision and scrutiny of industry concentration, and cannot fully address the participatory, context-sensitive needs RAD-AI targets. No numerical performance comparisons are made.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes three existing but under-integrated streams—Responsible AI (RAI), Application-Driven Machine Learning (ADML, Rolnick et al. 2024), and Responsible Research and Innovation (RRI)—into a unified three-staged framework called RAD-AI. It departs from RAI by critiquing its diffuse, under-specified notion of responsibility (Powles, 2023) and from ADML by arguing that technical application alone is insufficient without embedded ethical, legal, and participatory structures. The framework repurposes RRI's four dimensions (anticipation, reflexivity, inclusion, responsiveness) and participatory research methods (human-centred design, community-based participatory research) for the AI context, positioning itself alongside but distinct from existing tools like the AAAS Decision Tree and ISO/IEC 42001.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete gap in the AI research community: the absence of a structured, context-sensitive pathway that simultaneously addresses technical efficacy, domain-specific ethics, legal compliance, and community trust. Its main limitation is that it remains a high-level framework without empirical validation or case-study demonstration of the three stages in practice, leaving open how the approach scales across very different domains (medicine vs. agriculture vs. broadcasting) and how trade-offs between model accuracy and ethical constraints are operationally resolved. The call for a RAD-AI community of practice and staged digital testbeds points toward future work that could make the framework actionable, but the paper does not yet specify concrete implementation protocols or success criteria for the framework itself.
