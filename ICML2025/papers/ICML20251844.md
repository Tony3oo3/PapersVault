## Problem

In PSRO-based game solving, when multiple equilibria exist, the algorithm may converge to lower-welfare solutions because discovering higher-welfare equilibria sometimes requires adding strategies that are not best responses to any current strategy profile. This equilibrium selection problem is particularly acute in social dilemmas and bargaining games where Pareto-dominant equilibria are inaccessible through strict best-response operations alone, regardless of the meta-strategy solver used.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a multi-agent game environment (MDP), initial random policies, and a meta-strategy solver (regularized replicator dynamics), and produces a strategy set of trained policies plus a joint mixed-strategy equilibrium profile. A key constraint is that the approach requires no additional simulation beyond what vanilla PSRO already generates; it reuses existing simulation trajectories. Because the regularization operates in policy space via KL divergence, the method requires policy-based RL (discrete SAC) rather than value-based methods, which cannot be regularized by a policy.

## Method

Ex²PSRO steers strategy exploration by (1) filtering all simulation trajectories accumulated during PSRO iterations using a maximin welfare criterion (minimizing the worst player's return), (2) training an exploration policy via behavior cloning on the top-N selected trajectories, and (3) augmenting the best-response training objective with a KL-divergence regularization term that pulls the new policy toward the exploration policy, weighted by a linearly annealed coefficient β. The trajectory buffer is shared across all players, so each player's response is regularized toward behaviors that coordinate well with others, addressing the coordination barrier that prevents discovery of high-welfare equilibria. The first iteration skips regularization since the buffer contains only random-rollout trajectories. This explicit, policy-level regularization is composable with other PSRO variants (e.g., Generalized Response Objectives) and requires no extra simulation cost.

## Results

Ex²PSRO achieves equilibrium welfare of 150.2±7.9 on Harvest-Dense, 129.0±7.5 on Harvest-Sparse, 12.86±0.26 on Bargaining γ=.99, and 14.02±0.22 on Bargaining γ=.95, outperforming vanilla PSRO (119.6±3.4, 108.6±8.9, 12.50±0.55, 13.76±0.34) with p<.05 on all four benchmarks. The welfare improvement over vanilla PSRO ranges from approximately 25% (Harvest-Dense) to about 3% (Bargaining γ=.99). When combined with Generalized Response Objectives (GRO), the GRO+Ex²PSRO variant achieves the highest welfare on 3 of 4 benchmarks (e.g., 151.0±6.2 on Harvest-Dense, 15.24±0.08 on Bargaining γ=.95), significantly outperforming GRO alone (p<.01).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against vanilla PSRO, Ex²PSRO achieves statistically significant welfare improvements on all four benchmarks (p<.05), with the largest gains in Harvest-Dense (+30.6 welfare, ~25% relative improvement) and Harvest-Sparse (+20.4, ~19%). Against GRO (Generalized Response Objectives), Ex²PSRO alone is competitive but not uniformly superior: GRO achieves higher welfare on Harvest-Dense (134.6 vs 150.2 for Ex²PSRO) and Bargaining γ=.95 (15.07 vs 14.02), while Ex²PSRO leads on Harvest-Sparse (129.0 vs 115.2) and is close on Bargaining γ=.99 (12.86 vs 12.99). The key composability result is that GRO+Ex²PSRO outperforms GRO alone on 3 of 4 benchmarks with p<.01 (Harvest-Dense: 151.0 vs 134.6; Bargaining γ=.99: 13.49 vs 12.99; Bargaining γ=.95: 15.24 vs 15.07), though the improvement on Harvest-Sparse is not significant (122.3 vs 115.2, p=.07). Among the ablation variants, Ex²PSRO's maximin-welfare criterion (Eq. 2) consistently outperforms alternatives like Uniform and PrevBR in Bargaining, while in Harvest the gap narrows because any regularization that reduces immediate apple collection naturally improves welfare.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the PSRO framework (Lanctot et al., 2017) for game-theoretic multiagent RL by introducing an explicit exploration policy constructed via behavior cloning on high-welfare simulation trajectories, then using KL-divergence regularization to steer best-response training toward that policy. It departs from Generalized Response Objectives (Wang & Wellman, 2024), which influence equilibrium selection by modifying the response *objective* (e.g., optimizing social welfare), by instead modifying the *process* of strategy generation through policy-level regularization. The approach also draws on policy regularization techniques from offline RL and self-imitation learning, repurposing them for the specific goal of equilibrium selection in iterative game-solving. The paper demonstrates composability with GRO, positioning Ex²PSRO as an orthogonal mechanism that can be layered on top of existing PSRO variants.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Ex²PSRO provides a simple, zero-extra-simulation-cost mechanism for steering PSRO toward higher-welfare equilibria, and its demonstrated composability with GRO suggests it can be integrated into the broader PSRO ecosystem as a modular component. The ablation results showing that the *choice* of exploration policy (maximin welfare vs. max welfare vs. uniform) significantly affects performance in some games but not others highlight an open question about how game structure determines which regularization targets are beneficial. Limitations include testing only on 2-player symmetric games with at most 30 PSRO iterations, conservative hyperparameter tuning, and the requirement for policy-based (SAC) rather than value-based RL methods. Future work could explore scaling to larger player counts, asymmetric games, and adaptive regularization schedules.
