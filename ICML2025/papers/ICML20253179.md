## Problem

Existing medical LLM benchmarks evaluate capabilities at isolated cognitive levels—most focus on knowledge recall via QA, while a few target scenario-based diagnosis—leaving a gap in understanding how LLM performance degrades as cognitive complexity increases. The paper asks: how far are current LLMs from being truly applicable in real-world medical scenarios that require progressive cognitive skills from memorization to multi-step clinical reasoning?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20253179/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes three input formats: (1) standard MCQs from MedQA (5-option) and MedMCQA (4-option) for Low-Level; (2) reformulated questions in three sub-types—true/false statements, two-step verify-and-correct prompts, and answer-existence judgments—generated via GPT-4o from the same MCQs for Mid-Level; and (3) full patient EHR records (history of present illness, physical exam, lab/microbiology/imaging results) from MIMIC-IV for High-Level, where the model interacts multi-turn to sequentially order examinations before outputting a diagnosis. Outputs are normalized accuracy scores (adjusted for task-specific random-guessing baselines) at each level, plus a composite full-path diagnosis accuracy that multiplies endpoint diagnosis correctness by examination recall, penalizing models that reach the right diagnosis without requesting all necessary tests.

## Method

The framework defines three cognitive levels aligned with Bloom's Taxonomy and the medical training progression (medical school → internship → residency), governed by three design principles: task relevance, knowledge consistency across levels, and metric alignment. For Mid-Level, original MCQs are reformulated into three task types that progressively remove answer-choice scaffolding and expand the decision space (statement validation removes option cues; multi-step rectification adds sequential reasoning; answer existence judgment simulates open-ended diagnosis). For High-Level, a recognize-and-retrieve procedure uses MedCAT NER to extract diseases from Low/Mid-Level tasks, then filters MIMIC-IV admission records by matching ICD-10-CM primary discharge diagnoses to ensure knowledge coverage alignment. The full-path diagnosis metric (Eq. 1) multiplies the indicator of correct final diagnosis by the recall of correctly ordered examination items, and all levels use a normalized accuracy formula (Eq. 4) that subtracts task-specific random-guessing baselines to enable fair cross-level comparison.

## Results

On the proposed MultiCogEval benchmark, the best-performing model DeepSeek-R1 achieves 89.8% normalized accuracy on Low-Level (MedQA), 77.3% on Mid-Level (MedQA), and 26.5% on High-Level (MIMIC-IV full-path diagnosis). GPT-4o reaches 78.3% (Low), 57.0% (Mid), and 19.3% (High), while DeepSeek-V3 scores 78.3%, 44.8%, and 19.4% respectively. All models exhibit a significant performance drop (~20–40 percentage points) from Low to Mid level, and a further decline to High level, where even the strongest model (DeepSeek-R1) achieves only 26.5% full-path diagnosis accuracy. Smaller models (~7–8B) close the gap on Low-Level tasks (e.g., Llama3-8B at 37.3% vs Llama3-70B at 63.7% on MedQA) but remain far behind on Mid- and High-Level tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Inference-time scaling yields the largest gains: DeepSeek-R1 improves over DeepSeek-V3 by +11.5 pp (Low), +20.5 pp (Mid), and +7.1 pp (High), while o3-mini improves over GPT-4o-mini by +25.0 pp (Low), +28.7 pp (Mid), but only +1.2 pp (High), indicating diminishing returns at the scenario-based level. Medical-domain finetuning (e.g., Meditron3-70B, Med42-v2-70B, MMed-Llama3-8B) consistently outperforms their Llama3 backbones on Low- and Mid-Level tasks (gains up to ~15 pp on MedQA) but fails to improve—and sometimes degrades—High-Level performance (e.g., Meditron-70B scores 0.32% vs Llama2-70B's 5.16% on MIMIC-IV). Model size matters more at higher levels: the 7B-to-70B gap widens from ~26 pp (Low, Llama3) to ~30 pp (Mid) but both 70B models still score below 20% on High-Level. Among open-weight models, Qwen2.5-72B (67.8% Low, 49.5% Mid, 16.1% High) and Llama3-70B (63.7% Low, 41.8% Mid, 17.8% High) are the strongest, yet both trail GPT-4o and DeepSeek-V3 by 10–15 pp on Low-Level and 5–15 pp on Mid-Level. The Answer Existence Judgment sub-task shows the steepest drop (over 40 pp below Low-Level for most models), highlighting that larger decision spaces remain a critical bottleneck.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on Hager et al. (2024) for the High-Level full-path clinical diagnosis task and draws on Bloom's Taxonomy (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001) as its organizing theoretical framework. It departs from prior medical benchmarks (MedQA, MedMCQA, MIMIC-IV-Ext, CLIMED-Bench) by arguing they each target only a single cognitive level—most focusing on QA-based knowledge recall—rather than providing a holistic, multi-level view. The key conceptual contribution is reformulating existing MCQs into three distinct Mid-Level task types (statement validation, multi-step rectification, answer existence judgment) to fill the gap between simple recall and full scenario-based diagnosis, creating a continuous cognitive spectrum.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that even SOTA models (GPT-4o, DeepSeek-V3) drop from >70% on Low-Level to ~19% on High-Level tasks, and that medical-domain finetuning actively hurts High-Level performance, provides a clear diagnostic of where current medical LLMs fail. The framework's normalized metrics and knowledge-consistency design make cross-level comparisons meaningful, offering a reusable template for multi-cognitive evaluation in other domains. Open questions include whether the 42-disease, English-only scope generalizes, whether inference-time scaling (DeepSeek-R1, o3-mini) can close the High-Level gap, and how to design training strategies that specifically target planning and information-seeking behaviors in clinical scenarios.
