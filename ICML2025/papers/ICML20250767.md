## Problem

Monitoring LLM outputs alone is insufficient for detecting strategic deception, since deceptive models purposefully craft benign-looking responses while their internal reasoning is misaligned. Prior probe-based work focused on factual falsehoods, evaluated off-policy human-written text, or did not consider multi-turn agent settings. This paper asks whether simple linear probes trained on minimal contrastive datasets can generalize to realistic scenarios where Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct exhibits strategic deception, such as concealing insider trading or sandbagging safety evaluations.

## Input / Output

The probe consumes per-token residual stream activations at layer 22 (of 80) from Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct, normalized to zero mean and unit variance using statistics from the training set. It produces a real-valued deception score at every token position via projection onto a single learned direction, which is then aggregated by mean across all tokens in the model's response to yield a single per-response deceptiveness score. The probe is evaluated on two classification tasks: separating deceptive from honest responses within a dataset (AUROC), and separating deceptive responses from a neutral control distribution (recall at 1% false positive rate on Alpaca chat data).

## Method

The core probe is a logistic regression classifier with L2 regularization (λ=10) fit on flattened, normalized activations from contrastive pairs where the only difference is an instruction to be honest versus deceptive (Instructed-Pairs from Zou et al., 2023). The classifier weights define a single probe direction in activation space; at inference time, each token's activation is projected onto this direction to yield a per-token score. The key empirical finding is that this probe, trained on a simple dataset where responses are not model-generated and contain no actual deception, generalizes to on-policy multi-turn agent scenarios (insider trading, sandbagging) with AUROCs of 0.96–0.999 and 95–99% recall at 1% FPR on control data. The authors compare against Difference of Means, LDA, Linear Artificial Tomography, SAE-activation probes, a follow-up-question probe, a descriptive-question probe (following MacDiarmid et al.), and a GPT-4o black-box transcript classifier, finding logistic regression on raw activations performs best at distinguishing deceptive from control responses. A notable limitation is that the probe partially tracks 'is the scenario deception-related' rather than purely 'is the response deceptive,' causing it to fire on honest responses in deception-adjacent contexts.

## Results

The Instructed-Pairs-trained linear probe (logistic regression on layer-22 residual stream activations of Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct) achieves AUROCs of 0.964 (Roleplaying), 0.999 (Insider Trading Report), 0.974 (Insider Trading Confirmation), and 0.983 (Sandbagging) at distinguishing deceptive from honest responses. At a decision threshold calibrated to 1% false positive rate on Alpaca control data, the probe catches >96% of deceptive responses across all evaluation datasets (recall at 1% FPR). The probe also activates on deceptive responses before explicitly deceptive text is generated, and distinguishes deceptive from control responses with AUROCs >0.998.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the Instructed-Pairs probe against five alternatives. Against the MacDiarmid et al. (2024) 'single descriptive question' probe (difference in activations for 'Yes' vs. 'No' to 'Are you being deceptive?'), the Instructed-Pairs probe performs significantly better on all datasets; notably, the MacDiarmid-style probe points in the reverse direction on these datasets (admitting deceptiveness correlates with honesty, likely because honest responses often involve confessing past misaligned actions). Against a GPT-4o black-box classifier that labels full transcripts, the white-box probe performs roughly equivalently on AUROC, though the black-box baseline's recall at 1% FPR is degraded by Llama's factually-incorrect (but not strategically deceptive) control responses. The Roleplaying-trained probe achieves perfect AUROC on its training distribution but generalizes significantly worse to Sandbagging and is much worse at separating deceptive from control responses. The SAE-activation probe is consistently worse than the raw-activation probe. The follow-up-question probe ('Was your last response deceptive? No.') matches the Roleplaying probe on honest/deceptive separation but is substantially worse on Sandbagging; however, it is the best method at distinguishing deceptive from control responses (recall >99% at 1% FPR on all datasets).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on linear probes for detecting falsehoods in LLMs (Burns et al., 2022; Marks & Tegmark, 2023; Zou et al., 2023) from factual truthfulness on TruthfulQA to strategic deception in multi-turn agent settings. It departs from prior probe work in three key ways: using on-policy model-generated responses rather than off-policy human-written text, focusing on goal-directed strategic deception rather than factual errors, and explicitly testing generalization from simple contrastive training pairs to realistic evaluation scenarios (insider trading, sandbagging). The Instructed-Pairs training dataset is directly derived from Zou et al.'s representation engineering framework, but the probe fitting method (logistic regression with L2 regularization) and the evaluation protocol (recall at 1% FPR on a control distribution) are novel contributions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

White-box deception monitoring is a critical component of AI Control approaches, and this paper provides the first systematic benchmark of linear probes for strategic deception detection, establishing that they achieve AUROC 0.96–0.999 and 95–99% recall at 1% FPR on current (relatively blatant) deception settings. However, the authors are candid that current performance is insufficient as a robust defence, since the tested models are not yet capable of the sophisticated, covert scheming that is the ultimate safety concern. Key open questions include whether probes can detect deception when black-box methods fail (e.g., alignment faking without chain-of-thought), whether the probes' sensitivity to 'deception-related scenarios' rather than pure deceptiveness is a feature or bug, and whether performance degrades on more subtle or novel deception types.
