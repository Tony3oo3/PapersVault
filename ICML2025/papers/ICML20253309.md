## Problem

AI systems are increasingly replacing human decision-makers in domains subject to constitutional due process (disability benefits, criminal sentencing, tax audits), yet their opacity and trade secret protections make it impossible for affected individuals to understand or contest decisions. The paper argues this is not merely a transparency gap but a fundamental incompatibility: even AI creators cannot explain why modern models produce specific outputs, rendering due process's core requirements of notice and hearing unsatisfiable in principle.

## Input / Output

The paper takes as analytical inputs two recent court decisions (KW v. Armstrong on disability benefits and State v. Loomis on criminal risk assessment) and a taxonomy of AI system types spanning logistic regression, neural networks, LLMs, CART ensembles, and statistical tools. It produces a legal-technical position arguing that current AI cannot guarantee due process, along with model-type-specific recommendations for what interpretability standards would be needed and where AI should be barred entirely from decision-making.

## Method

The paper's core analytical mechanism is a model-type-by-model-type examination of interpretability and due process compatibility, grounded in two detailed case studies. For each AI system family, it identifies what technical information would be needed to satisfy notice and hearing requirements and demonstrates why trade secret protections prevent even that limited disclosure. The key technical insight is that the combination of (1) the current inability to provide causal explanations for model decisions—especially for deep networks and LLMs—and (2) sweeping trade secret protections blocking even basic architecture disclosure creates a 'fatal anti-due-process combination' that no amount of additional transparency regulation can remedy. The paper further distinguishes between transparency failures (potentially fixable with regulation) and fundamental interpretability failures (not fixable), arguing the latter is the true barrier. It closes with five concrete recommendations for how AI researchers should engage with the legal process to restore due process protections.

## Results

This is a position paper with no quantitative experiments, metrics, or results tables. The paper's core argument is qualitative and legal: it contends that current AI systems are fundamentally incompatible with due process because (1) their decisions cannot be causally explained even by their creators, and (2) trade secret protections prevent independent technical scrutiny. The paper supports this position through two case studies — KW v. Armstrong (2016, disability benefits) and State v. Loomis (2016, criminal sentencing via COMPAS) — and a taxonomy of AI model types (regression, neural networks, LLMs, CART, statistical tools) ranked by interpretability and due-process compatibility.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not compare methods against baselines in the empirical sense. Instead, it contrasts different AI model families (logistic regression, shallow/deep neural networks, LLMs, decision trees, statistical tools) along the axis of interpretability and due-process compatibility, arguing that simpler models (e.g., logistic regression with disclosed coefficients, shallow decision trees) are more amenable to due process scrutiny while LLMs and deep networks are fundamentally unsuitable for high-stakes decisions requiring notice and hearing. It also contrasts the legal outcomes in KW v. Armstrong (where trade secret claims were largely overruled and the vulnerable population won) versus State v. Loomis (where sweeping trade secret protection for COMPAS was upheld and due process was effectively denied), using these as illustrative endpoints of the spectrum of judicial engagement with AI transparency.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the 'technological due process' line of legal scholarship initiated by Citron (2007) and Citron & Pasquale (2014), which flagged due process concerns for hard-coded scoring tools like credit-scoring algorithms. The key conceptual departure is the argument that modern AI systems present a fundamentally different problem: earlier work treated the issue as one of transparency (reveal the algorithm's logic), whereas this paper contends that even full transparency would not resolve the incompatibility, because the causal reasoning behind modern model decisions is unknown even to their creators. The technical analysis draws on interpretability research (Doshi-Velez & Kim, 2017; Rudin, 2019; Adebayo et al., 2018) and the xAI-for-courts literature (Deeks, 2019), but repurposes these findings to argue a legal position rather than to propose new interpretability methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central claim—that the combination of inherent unexplainability and trade secret protections creates a 'fatal anti-due-process combination' that cannot be remedied by regulation alone—raises urgent open questions about where to draw the legal line between statistical tools, classical ML, deep learning, and LLMs, and when AI should be categorically excluded from high-stakes decisions. A significant limitation is that the paper is a position argument rather than an empirical study; its legal analysis is US-specific, and the technical claims about interpretability limitations, while well-supported in the literature, are presented without new evidence. The call for AI researchers to actively participate in legal proceedings (providing amicus briefs, defining model categories, setting transparency standards) is a concrete but untested proposal whose feasibility in the current adversarial legal system remains unclear.
