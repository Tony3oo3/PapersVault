## Problem

Existing ML competition analyses assume a single homogeneous data distribution, yet real-world markets feature multiple providers competing across heterogeneous data sources with distinct distributions. This paper asks how data heterogeneity—captured by concept shift and covariate shift across sources—interacts with competitive forces to shape pure Nash equilibria, and whether providers converge on a single 'universal' model or specialize in distinct sources.

## Input / Output

The game is parameterized by K data sources, each specified by a ground-truth parameter θ_k ∈ ℝ^D, a positive-definite covariance matrix Σ_k, and a weight w_k (ordered w_1 > w_2 > … > w_K > 0, summing to 1), plus N model providers and a choice model (proximity or logit-probability with temperature t > 0). The loss between a provider's parameter and a source is the squared Mahalanobis distance (θ̂_n − θ_k)ᵀΣ_k(θ̂_n − θ_k), which reduces to MSE in linear model settings. The output is a set of necessary and sufficient (or sufficient) conditions on the weights, N, and t that determine whether a PNE exists and, if so, whether it is homogeneous (all providers choose the weighted-loss minimizer θ̂^M) or heterogeneous (providers specialize in ground-truth parameters θ_k), along with the exact allocation of providers to sources.

## Method

The Heterogeneous Data Game models N providers each selecting a parameter θ̂_n ∈ ℝ^D to maximize their weighted market share across K sources, where each source selects a provider via either a proximity rule (lowest loss, ties broken uniformly) or a logit probability rule with temperature t. The key structural insight is that any PNE strategy must lie in the set ϑ of weighted-loss minimizers θ̄(q) for some q ∈ Δ_K, reducing the infinite strategy space to a finite-dimensional simplex. Under the proximity model, the paper proves PNE must be heterogeneous (no two providers share a non-ground-truth strategy) and derives explicit existence conditions when a few sources dominate (w_{k_0} > 3∑_{j>k_0} w_j), showing providers allocate proportionally to source weights. Under the probability model, both homogeneous and heterogeneous PNE can coexist: a homogeneous PNE exists iff t ≥ t̲ (a threshold scaling with 2ℓ_max), while a heterogeneous PNE exists for t ≤ t̲' and approximates the proximity PNE within O(t²). The proofs leverage Pareto optimality, Gordan's theorem, convexity of the Mahalanobis loss, and the Brouwer fixed-point theorem to establish existence in the continuous strategy space.

## Results

The paper establishes that under the proximity choice model, a duopoly PNE exists if and only if the dominant source weight w₁ ≥ 0.5, and any such PNE is heterogeneous (both providers select θ₁). Under the probability choice model, any duopoly PNE must be homogeneous (both providers select θ^M), existing only when temperature t ≥ t̲, where synthetic experiments show t̲ ≈ 0.1–0.2 × (2ℓ_max). For N > 2 providers under the proximity model, PNEs are always heterogeneous with providers specializing in dominant sources, and the number of providers per source is approximately proportional to source weight. Under the probability model with N > 2, both homogeneous and heterogeneous PNEs can coexist at intermediate temperatures, though coexistence becomes unlikely as N grows.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against external methods; instead, it compares two internal choice-model formulations (proximity vs. probability) and different market sizes. The proximity model yields exclusively heterogeneous PNEs for N ≥ 2 (providers specialize in distinct sources), while the probability model admits both homogeneous and heterogeneous equilibria depending on temperature t. A key contrast is that under the proximity model, duopoly PNE existence requires a dominant source (w₁ ≥ 0.5), whereas under the probability model, PNE existence depends on t exceeding a threshold t̲ that scales with data heterogeneity (ℓ_max). Synthetic experiments confirm that as N increases, the maximal t for heterogeneous PNE drops below the threshold t̲ for homogeneous PNE, meaning large markets under the probability model converge to a single homogeneous solution. The paper also contrasts its framework with prior ML competition work (Jagadeesan et al., 2023a; Ben-Porat & Tennenholtz, 2017), which assumes a single homogeneous data distribution, showing that heterogeneity fundamentally alters equilibrium structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two previously separate literatures: competitive ML model provider analysis (Jagadeesan et al. 2023a; Ben-Porat & Tennenholtz 2017/2019) and data heterogeneity / distribution shift (Liu et al. 2021b; Arjovsky et al. 2019). Prior competitive ML work assumes a single homogeneous data distribution, while prior heterogeneity work assumes a single provider seeking robustness. The choice-model machinery (proximity/Hotelling and logit/probability) is borrowed directly from competitive location theory (Hotelling 1929; Drezner & Eiselt 2024; Train 2009), but the paper departs from that literature by working in high-dimensional parameter spaces with source-specific Mahalanobis metrics rather than low-dimensional Euclidean or network distances.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic characterization of when competitive ML markets over heterogeneous data reach stable equilibria, remain unstable, or converge to homogeneous vs. heterogeneous model portfolios. Its policy-relevant finding—that a few dominant data sources cause providers to specialize and neglect smaller ones, and that the temperature parameter can be tuned to encourage diversity—offers a quantitative basis for regulatory intervention. Limitations include the restriction to linear models with squared Mahalanobis loss, the need for regularity assumptions (affine independence, distinct losses), and the absence of dynamic or repeated-game analysis. Open questions include extending the framework to non-linear models, mixed strategies, and settings where providers can form coalitions or where data sources have heterogeneous choice behaviors.
