## Problem

The pretrain-then-adapt paradigm dominates modern machine learning, yet there is no principled theoretical framework for selecting which pretraining checkpoint will best adapt to downstream tasks. Existing heuristics (e.g., pretraining loss, flatness, neural collapse) lack a unified statistical justification, and it remains unclear how to quantify a checkpoint's adaptability without access to downstream data.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pretraining checkpoint w* = (v*, θ*) and the pretraining dataset D⁰ as input, operating on the backbone parameters θ within a γ-neighborhood B_γ(w*) while freezing the head v. It produces a scalar pretraining free energy score (estimated via localized WBIC using SGLD sampling) per checkpoint, which can be used to rank and select among multiple candidate checkpoints. The WBIC computation requires only the sample pretraining train loss evaluated in the local neighborhood, making it computable without any downstream data or task knowledge.

## Method

The paper introduces the downstream free energy, defined as the negative log of a local marginal likelihood over a neighborhood of the checkpoint, which quantifies the concentration of well-performing weights for the downstream task. Since downstream data is unavailable during pretraining, they prove (Proposition 5.1) that under a bounded likelihood-ratio condition between pretraining and downstream distributions, the downstream free energy is upper-bounded by the pretraining free energy, justifying the latter as a proxy. The pretraining free energy is estimated via a localized Widely Applicable Bayesian Information Criterion (WBIC) computed with SGLD sampling, whose asymptotic expansion decomposes into a fit term (pretraining test loss) and a complexity term (local learning coefficient × log n). Empirically, the paper verifies that mechanisms known to reduce pretraining free energy—larger learning rates, smaller batch sizes, and higher momentum—correlate with improved fine-tuning accuracy, and that the free energy metric outperforms geometric complexity and neural collapse as predictors of downstream performance.

## Results

On CIFAR-FS with ResNet-18, the proposed pretraining Free Energy metric achieves Pearson correlation coefficients of -0.820 (full fine-tune transfer accuracy) and -0.8901 (average 5-shot transfer accuracy) with downstream performance, substantially outperforming Geometric Complexity (-0.767, -0.443) and Neural Collapse (-0.632, -0.1875). The advantage is most pronounced in the few-shot setting, where Free Energy's correlation is roughly double that of Geometric Complexity and nearly five times that of Neural Collapse. Additional experiments on mini-ImageNet with VGG-16 (Figure 3) confirm the same trend: lower pretraining WBIC consistently yields better transfer accuracy across learning rate, batch size, and momentum sweeps.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its Free Energy criterion against two existing pretraining metrics: Geometric Complexity (Dherin et al., 2022; Munn et al., 2024) and Neural Collapse (Galanti et al., 2022). Free Energy wins decisively on both downstream evaluation settings. On full fine-tune transfer accuracy, Free Energy achieves a correlation of -0.820 versus -0.767 for Geometric Complexity and -0.632 for Neural Collapse. The gap widens dramatically in the 5-shot few-shot setting, where Free Energy reaches -0.8901 compared to only -0.443 for Geometric Complexity and -0.1875 for Neural Collapse, indicating that the latter two metrics become nearly uninformative in data-limited regimes. The paper also notes that pretraining train loss, a natural baseline, collapses to similar values across hyperparameter settings and is ineffective for distinguishing fine-tuning behavior, whereas Free Energy remains discriminative.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Bayesian free energy criterion (rooted in Watanabe's algebraic statistics framework and the WBIC for singular models) as a pretraining checkpoint selection metric, which the authors note is the first application of this criterion in transfer learning. It builds directly on Lau et al. (2025) for the local learning coefficient and SGLD-based WBIC estimation, and on Watanabe (2009, 2013) for the asymptotic expansion machinery. Conceptually, it sits alongside prior pretraining-characteristic work by Liu et al. (2023a) (Hessian flatness), Galanti et al. (2022) (neural collapse), and Munn et al. (2024) (geometric complexity), but departs by framing checkpoint selection as a local Bayesian model selection problem over the neighborhood of a checkpoint rather than a global geometric or collapse-based analysis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, distribution-free (with respect to downstream data) criterion for selecting pretraining checkpoints, with a theoretical guarantee (Proposition 5.1) linking pretraining free energy to downstream free energy under bounded distributional shift. However, practical adoption faces a significant barrier: computing the pretraining WBIC via SGLD sampling is computationally expensive and has not been demonstrated at the scale of modern foundation models (billions of parameters). Empirical validation is limited to small-scale settings (CIFAR-FS, mini-ImageNet with ResNet-18 and VGG-16), leaving open whether the free energy signal remains discriminative and computable for large pretrained models. The authors also acknowledge that the theoretical link to downstream predictive performance currently holds only under Bayesian prediction, not standard SGD fine-tuning.
