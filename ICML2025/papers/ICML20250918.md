## Problem

Stochastic Poisson Surface Reconstruction (SPSR) requires a two-stage pipeline: Gaussian process interpolation followed by a global volumetric finite-element Poisson solve, making it computationally expensive, grid-dependent, and unable to support local queries or smooth posterior samples. The paper asks whether the posterior of the implicit surface can be computed directly with only one linear solve, eliminating the separate Poisson solve entirely.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250918/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an oriented point cloud (surface points with normals) defined on a periodic torus domain and produces the full posterior distribution of the implicit surface function f — including means, covariances, and Monte Carlo samples — that can be evaluated at arbitrary spatial locations without any volumetric mesh or grid. Beyond the implicit surface, it supports downstream queries such as joint collision probabilities, ray transmittance for next-view planning, and uncertainty-aware hitbox generation, all computed via local evaluations rather than global solves.

## Method

The key insight is to reformulate both the vector field v and the implicit surface f as Gaussian processes on the torus using periodic Matérn kernels, so that the Poisson equation Δf = ∇·v becomes a simple multiplication by n_i/‖n‖² in Fourier space. This yields closed-form Fourier series expressions for the cross-covariance k_{f,v} and for joint samples of (f, v) via Karhunen-Loève expansions, effectively replacing the Poisson solve with a grid-independent matrix multiplication. The only remaining linear solve is the standard GP regression system (K_vv + Σ)^{-1}, which is handled at graphics scale using stochastic gradient descent (dual SGD variant) for large point clouds. The cross-covariance is further amortized by precomputing it on a small grid and interpolating, since it is smooth and problem-independent. This single-solve formulation makes the method output-sensitive: computational cost scales with the number of queried points rather than the volume of the enclosing bounding box.

## Results

The paper is primarily a methods contribution with qualitative and runtime-focused evaluations rather than a single numerical benchmark table. Key quantitative findings include: (1) the method's runtime is output-sensitive, scaling linearly with the number of queried test points (Figure 11), whereas SPSR requires a global volumetric FEM solve regardless of query size; (2) runtime is empirically constant with respect to the kernel length scale, while SPSR's runtime scales roughly as 2^d per halving of the length scale (e.g., 0.053 s → 0.54 s → 9.1 s for three successive length-scale halvings in SPSR); (3) the dual SGD solver converges to a solution visually indistinguishable from Cholesky factorization within ~1000 iterations (Figure 13). The method qualitatively matches SPSR's posterior mean and variance on standard meshes (Armadillo, Bunny, Dragon, etc.) while enabling capabilities SPSR cannot support (local ray-based queries, smooth posterior samples, length-scale-independent resolution).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is Stochastic Poisson Surface Reconstruction (SPSR; Sellán & Jacobson, ACM TOG 2022), the original two-stage pipeline that first performs GP interpolation then solves a volumetric Poisson equation via finite elements. On qualitative reconstruction quality (posterior mean, variance, probability maps), the proposed method matches SPSR (Figure 1, Figure 3). However, the proposed method wins decisively on three axes: (i) length-scale independence — SPSR couples the GP length scale to the FEM grid resolution, so halving the length scale requires a 2^d finer grid and roughly 2^d more runtime, while the proposed method's runtime remains constant (Figure 2, Figure 16); (ii) output sensitivity — SPSR must solve the Poisson equation over the entire bounding volume even for a single ray query, whereas the proposed method's cost scales only with the number of evaluated points (Figure 11); (iii) sample fidelity — SPSR approximates the inverse kernel matrix with a diagonal, discarding all inter-point correlations and producing irregular, over-smoothed samples (Figure 12), while the proposed method generates smooth posterior samples that preserve kernel correlations (Figure 6). The proposed method does not lose on any reported metric; the trade-off is that it requires periodic boundary conditions and a Fourier-series truncation (L ≈ 100³ basis functions) that introduces a small, controllable approximation error (Figure 3, Figure 4).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Stochastic Poisson Surface Reconstruction (Sellán & Jacobson, 2022), which introduced a Bayesian GP formulation for surface reconstruction but required a two-stage pipeline: GP interpolation followed by a global finite-element Poisson solve on a volumetric grid. The key departure is the use of geometric Gaussian processes on the torus (Borovitskiy et al., 2020) and inter-domain GP conditioning (Lázaro-Gredilla & Figueiras-Vidal, 2009) to analytically absorb the Poisson equation into the cross-covariance structure via Fourier series, collapsing both stages into a single kernel-matrix solve. Scalability is handled through the dual SGD approach of Lin et al. (2024) for large point clouds, and pathwise conditioning (Wilson et al., 2020) enables direct posterior sampling without storing full covariance matrices.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By eliminating the volumetric grid dependency, the method becomes output-sensitive (scaling with query points rather than grid volume) and decouples the GP length scale from discretization resolution—two practical limitations that constrained prior stochastic reconstruction in graphics pipelines. The preservation of full kernel correlations (vs. SPSR's diagonal approximation) enables principled joint-probability queries for collision detection, ray casting, and next-view planning, opening a path toward Bayesian-optimization-style active scanning. Open questions include whether the periodic boundary condition assumption holds in practice for non-convex or open surfaces, how the Fourier truncation error interacts with very small length scales, and whether the single dense kernel solve (even with SGD) remains the bottleneck at truly large N.
