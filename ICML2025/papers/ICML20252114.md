## Problem

ML models fail to learn modular arithmetic at scale—summing many elements modulo a large prime—which is the core computational bottleneck for ML-based attacks on LWE, the foundation of standardized post-quantum cryptosystems. Prior work could only sum N≤6 elements mod q≤1000, while practical LWE requires summing dozens or hundreds of elements. The paper identifies two root causes: training data that only exposes models to uniformly hard examples, and loss functions that ignore the circular geometry of the modular field, causing predictions to collapse toward the origin.

## Input / Output

For modular addition, the input is a vector of N integers in Z_q, each mapped via angular embedding to a point (cos(2πt/q), sin(2πt/q)) on the unit circle; the output is the sum mod q, also represented as a unit-circle point. For the LWE application, the input is a matrix A ∈ Z_q^(m×n) and the model predicts b = A·s (mod q), from which a binary distinguisher recovers the sparse binary secret s. Evaluation uses both MSE of angular predictions and τ-accuracy (fraction of predictions within τ·q of the true answer), with τ = 0.5% and 1% as primary thresholds.

## Method

The paper introduces two training modifications to an encoder-only transformer (4 layers, 256 hidden dim, 4 heads) with angular embeddings. First, a custom data distribution f_inv_sqrt(z) ∝ 1/√(N−z+1) controls the number of nonzero elements per training sample, injecting sparse (easier) vectors alongside dense (harder) ones; this is a static distribution rather than a curriculum, and the key is achieving a mid-range KL divergence between train and test sets. Second, a custom loss adds a regularization term α(x′²+y′²+1/(x′²+y′²)) to standard MSE, which drives the loss to infinity at the origin and encourages predictions onto the unit circle, preventing the model collapse observed in harder settings. Together these enable modular addition of up to N=128 elements mod q=974269 with near-perfect τ-accuracy, and when applied to LWE (ignoring error), recover binary secrets with Hamming weight up to 6—doubling the difficulty of prior ML attacks.

## Results

The proposed methods (f_inv_sqrt data distribution + custom loss regularization + angular embeddings) enable encoder-only transformers to sum up to N=128 elements mod q=974269 with near-zero MSE and 92–99.8% τ=0.5% accuracy, dramatically exceeding prior work which managed at most N=6 mod q=1000. On the base case (N=16, q=257), the method achieves 99.8% τ=0.5% accuracy versus 1.3% for the standard baseline (f_default + MSE loss). Applied to LWE cryptanalysis (ignoring error), the approach recovers binary secrets with Hamming weight up to 6, doubling the effective secret complexity recoverable by prior ML attacks (which were limited to Hamming weight ≤3).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the standard baseline (f_default data distribution + plain MSE loss, as used in Jelassi et al. 2023), the proposed method achieves 99.8% vs. 1.3% τ=0.5% accuracy on the base case (N=16, q=257) — the baseline essentially fails to learn the task. Compared to curriculum learning (CL) following Abbe et al. (2023), the fixed-distribution approach is consistently more stable: at N=128, q=974269, the method achieves 92.1%±0.4% while CL yields only 77.3%±7.5% (mean±std over 8 trials), and the gap widens with N (e.g., 98.5%±0.3% vs. 95.7%±2.8% at N=64, q=257). Relative to all prior modular-addition results (Table 1), the paper extends the maximum number of summed terms from N≤6 to N=128 and the maximum modulus from q≤1000 to q=974269, a roughly 20× increase in modulus and 20× increase in term count. On the LWE application, the custom loss (α=10⁻²) enables 10–20% secret recovery at Hamming weight 6, while plain MSE loss yields 0% recovery across all tested q values, confirming that the regularization term is essential for harder settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the angular embedding framework of Stevens et al. (2024) and the encoder-only transformer architecture for modular addition from Jelassi et al. (2023), while applying the binary distinguisher of Wenger et al. (2022) for LWE secret recovery. Its two core contributions—a sparse-vector sampling distribution (f_inv_sqrt) for training data and a regularization term preventing model collapse at the origin—depart from prior work that either used uniform random training data or curriculum learning (Abbe et al., 2023). The paper explicitly positions its fixed-distribution approach as simpler and more stable than curriculum learning, which requires careful phase scheduling and hyperparameter tuning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The techniques enable ML models to sum up to N=128 elements mod q=974269, a ~20× and ~1000× improvement over prior state-of-the-art, and double the hardness of LWE secrets recoverable by ML attacks—relevant to assessing the practical security of standardized post-quantum cryptosystems like CRYSTALS-KYBER. The generalization to parity, associative recall, and copy tasks suggests the data-distribution principle may be broadly applicable. Key open questions include scaling to real-world LWE parameters (N=512 with noise), understanding why a 'Goldilocks' KL divergence between train and test distributions is necessary, and whether pretraining on modular addition can transfer to full LWE with error terms.
