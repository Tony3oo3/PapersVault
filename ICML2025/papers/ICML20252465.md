## Problem

Existing provably accurate manifold denoisers require a linear scan of the entire dataset at test time, yielding worst-case complexity at least O(De^{cd}) that is exponential in intrinsic dimension d. Meanwhile, practical neural network denoisers lack theoretical guarantees on accuracy or efficiency. This paper bridges the gap by developing denoisers that are simultaneously test-time efficient, provably near-optimal, and learnable from noisy data alone.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252465/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a stream of noisy D-dimensional training samples x_i = x_{i,⊥} + z_i (with z_i ~ N(0, σ²I)) drawn from an unknown d-dimensional manifold M ⊂ R^D, processing them one at a time in an online fashion. At test time, a single noisy query x = x_⊥ + z is denoised by traversing the learned graph, producing a denoised point x̂ on M with guaranteed error ‖x̂ − x_⊥‖ ≲ κσ√d. The denoising radius R(i) = √(σ²D + σ²D/N_i + σ²d) adapts per landmark as more samples accumulate, controlling the tradeoff between graph sparsity and accuracy.

## Method

The core idea reframes denoising as Riemannian optimization: projecting the noisy point x onto M by minimizing ½‖q−x‖² over the manifold. Since M is unknown, the method learns a tangent bundle graph G online, where vertices are landmarks with estimated tangent spaces, first-order edges encode local gradient directions for efficient descent, and zero-order edges act as 'tunnels' that escape suboptimal local minimizers. The mixed-order traversal (Algorithm 1) alternates between cheap first-order gradient steps (cost O(Dd + d·deg¹)) and expensive zero-order jumps (cost O(D·deg⁰)) only when the gradient step fails to decrease the objective. The online learning algorithm (Algorithm 2) grows this graph incrementally: inliers update local models, suboptimal local minimizers trigger tunnel creation, and outliers spawn new landmarks. A novel geometric quantity, the vista number S_r^η(M), bounds the number of tunnels needed per landmark, yielding a test-time complexity that is polynomial in D and d for the gradient phases rather than exponential in d as in exhaustive search.

## Results

Theoretically, the mixed-order traversal achieves near-optimal denoising error d_M(q_*, x_∥) ≤ 2ε₂ with ε₂ > c₈ max{κ,1}σ√d, at a test-time cost of O((D + e^{c′log(a)d})(diam²(M)/(ε₁δ) + 1/(κδ))d + D × S_{c₆τ_M}^η(M)) arithmetic operations. Empirically, on 20,000 noisy 2048-dimensional gravitational wave signals (intrinsic d=2), the method's training MSE decreases with the number of training points (100,000 total) and approaches the theoretical lower bound σ²d. The key empirical result (Figure 9) shows that across 12 network configurations (varying R(i) and R_nbrs), the mixed-order method achieves a significantly better test-time complexity–accuracy tradeoff than nearest neighbor search over the same landmark set, with complexity measured in number of multiplications and accuracy in MSE.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the proposed mixed-order manifold traversal against nearest neighbor (NN) search, which underpins existing provable manifold denoising approaches (Hein & Maier, 2006; Gong et al., 2010). Both methods search over the identical set of landmarks Q_j for each of 12 network configurations, ensuring a fair comparison. The mixed-order method consistently achieves lower MSE at the same computational budget, or equivalently, reaches the same MSE with substantially fewer multiplications, demonstrating a superior complexity–accuracy tradeoff (Figure 9). The advantage is particularly pronounced at lower computational budgets, where NN search must scan a large fraction of all landmarks while the mixed-order method exploits first-order gradient steps (cost O(Dd + d·deg¹(u))) to navigate efficiently, resorting to the more expensive zero-order steps (cost O(D·deg⁰(u))) only when trapped at suboptimal local minimizers. The paper notes that decreasing R(i) as the number of samples N_i assigned to each landmark increases leads to better accuracy than maintaining a constant radius, further improving the tradeoff.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Riemannian optimization framework (Absil et al., 2008; Sato, 2021) and the manifold denoising literature (Hein & Maier, 2006; Genovese et al., 2012; Yao et al., 2023), but departs from prior provable denoisers that require exhaustive linear scans of the dataset. It draws on graph-based nearest neighbor search (Malkov & Yashunin, 2018, HNSW) for the tunneling mechanism that escapes local minimizers, and on manifold-free Riemannian optimization (Sober et al., 2020; Shustin et al., 2022) for learning optimizers from noisy data. The key conceptual departure is reframing 'learning-to-denoise' as 'learning-to-optimize' over a tangent bundle graph, with an online algorithm that grows the optimizer one sample at a time rather than requiring a pre-computed dense covering.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work bridges a significant gap between provable manifold denoisers (which have near-optimal error but exponential test-time cost) and practical neural denoisers (which are fast but lack guarantees), offering a framework with both theoretical accuracy bounds and polynomial test-time complexity. The vista number S(M) provides a new geometric complexity measure quantifying how many tunnels are needed, connecting the optimization landscape to manifold topology. Key limitations include the assumption of ideal landmarks in the theoretical analysis (the online learning component lacks its own guarantees), and empirical validation is restricted to d=2 manifolds (Swiss roll, Möbius strip, gravitational waves), leaving open whether the approach scales to higher intrinsic dimensions where the vista number can be exponential. Future directions include integrating this as a denoiser block within diffusion or compressed sensing pipelines and developing sparser networks via pruning.
