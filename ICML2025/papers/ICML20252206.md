## Problem

Existing geospatial encoding methods are designed for specific object types (points, polylines, or polygons) and cannot handle mixed geometry types in a unified framework, while common conversion approaches (rasterization to images, text encoding) discard crucial spatial information such as absolute position and relative alignment. This limits generalizability in GeoAI tasks that simultaneously involve multiple geometry types, such as land use classification combining POIs and building footprints.

## Input / Output

The method accepts a 2D coordinate array P ∈ R^{N×2} representing any of three geometry types (point, polyline, or polygon), with coordinates normalized to [-1,1]×[-1,1]. It produces a fixed-size d=32 real-valued embedding vector that is polymorphic—the same dimensionality regardless of input geometry type. The Fourier transform is computed analytically via closed-form expressions (not numerically), with 210 frequency components sampled geometrically between f_min=0.1 and f_max=1.0, making the encoding differentiable and lightweight.

## Method

Poly2Vec computes the 2D continuous Fourier transform analytically for each geometry type: points are modeled as Dirac delta functions, polylines are decomposed into line segments, and polygons are triangulated into non-overlapping triangles. Each component is affine-transformed to a canonical geometry (unit line segment or isosceles right triangle), its Fourier transform is evaluated in closed form, and the affine transformation property is applied to recover the transform in the original coordinate frame. The resulting complex-valued frequency vector is decomposed into magnitude (capturing shape and spatial extent) and phase (capturing position and orientation), which are then processed by two separate MLPs before being concatenated and passed through a final MLP to produce the real-valued embedding. A learned fusion strategy adaptively balances the contribution of magnitude versus phase based on geometry type and downstream task, which is critical because, for example, point magnitudes are always 1 and thus carry no shape information.

## Results

POLY2VEC consistently outperforms all object-specific baselines across five geometry-pair types on both Singapore and New York datasets. On topological relationship classification, POLY2VEC achieves up to 17% higher accuracy (e.g., 0.955 vs. 0.892 for point-polyline in Singapore; 0.980 vs. 0.909 for point-polygon in New York). On directional classification, gains reach 26% (e.g., 0.906 vs. 0.834 for polyline-polyline in Singapore). For distance estimation, POLY2VEC reduces MAE by up to 75% (e.g., 0.016 vs. 0.065 for point-point in Singapore). When integrated into the RegionDCL GeoAI pipeline, POLY2VEC reduces population prediction MAE by 14% (4957.58 vs. 5807.54 in Singapore) and improves land use L1-distance by 3% (0.484 vs. 0.498).

## Baselines & Comparisons

POLY2VEC outperforms all seven baseline categories across every geometry pair and both cities. Among point encoders, THEORY (Mai et al., 2020) is the strongest baseline, but POLY2VEC still leads by 5–11% on topological tasks (e.g., 0.955 vs. 0.892 for SG point-polyline) and by up to 18% on directional tasks (e.g., 0.906 vs. 0.834 for SG polyline-polyline). WRAP (Mac Aodha et al., 2019) and GRID (Mai et al., 2020) trail THEORY by 2–5%, while DIRECT (Chu et al., 2019) and TILE (Berg et al., 2014) are weakest, with TILE particularly poor on directional classification (~0.25–0.47 accuracy). For polyline-specific encoding, T2VEC (Li et al., 2018a) achieves 0.728/0.807 on polyline-polyline topological tasks but drops to 0.268/0.249 on directional tasks due to grid discretization artifacts, whereas POLY2VEC reaches 0.812/0.830 and 0.906/0.863 respectively. Polygon-specific encoders NUFTSPEC and RESNET1D (Mai et al., 2023) are competitive only on polygon-polygon pairs (0.602/0.585) but cannot handle mixed geometry types. In the end-to-end GeoAI pipeline, RegionDCL (Li et al., 2023) with its distance-biased transformer achieves MAE 5807.54 on Singapore population prediction; replacing its image-based encoding with POLY2VEC and removing the distance bias yields MAE 4957.58 (−14.6%), demonstrating that POLY2VEC inherently encodes the spatial information that RegionDCL needed the bias term to recover.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes and generalizes the line of object-specific geometry encoders—point encoders (THEORY, GRID, WRAP), trajectory encoders (T2VEC), and polygon encoders (NUFTSPEC, RESNET1D)—into a single polymorphic framework. Conceptually, it extends the 2D continuous Fourier transform approach from NUFTSPEC (which handled only polygons via spectral mapping) to points (via Dirac delta representation) and polylines (via affine-transformed line segments), leveraging the linearity and affine-transformation properties of the Fourier transform. The learned fusion module that adaptively balances Fourier magnitude and phase is a new architectural element not present in any prior geometry encoder, which either used one component or simply concatenated both.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

POLY2VEC addresses a practical bottleneck in GeoAI pipelines where mixed geometry types (POIs as points, roads as polylines, buildings as polygons) require separate encoders or lossy conversions to images/text. The demonstration that replacing RegionDCL's distance-biased transformer with POLY2VEC embeddings improves performance while simplifying the architecture suggests Fourier-based encodings can inherently capture spatial relationships that previously required explicit geometric mechanisms. Limitations include evaluation on only two cities and 2D geometries; the authors note future work on 3D extension and integration into Geo-Foundation models. An open question is whether the fixed geometric frequency sampling generalizes to geometries with vastly different scales or to higher-dimensional spaces.
