## Problem

Multi-agent optimal stopping (MAOS) becomes computationally intractable as the number of agents grows, and mean-field approximations—while standard for control problems—had never been formalized or computationally solved for stopping problems in discrete time and finite space. This paper closes that gap by establishing the first dynamic programming principle for discrete-time MFOS, proving an O(1/√N) approximation guarantee linking the mean-field solution back to the N-agent problem, and introducing the first scalable deep learning algorithms to solve MFOS.

## Input / Output

The neural network receives three inputs: the agent's state x (one-hot encoded in R^{|X|} and passed through a learnable embedding layer), the extended population distribution ν over S = X × {0,1} (a vector in R^{2|X|} encoding both stopped and non-stopped mass), and the time step n (sinusoidal embedding, used only in the Direct Approach). The output is a stopping probability p_n(x) ∈ [0,1] for each state, defining a randomized stopping rule. The total input dimension is 3|X|, reaching 300 in the 10×10 drone-fleet experiment.

## Method

The paper proposes two complementary deep learning algorithms. The Direct Approach (DA) minimizes the full mean-field social cost J(p) by backpropagating through the entire T-step trajectory, using a single time-dependent network ψ_θ(x, ν, n). The Dynamic Programming Approach (DP) exploits the newly proved DPP (Theorem 4.1) to train T separate networks backward in time, where each network at step n minimizes the one-step cost plus the value of the evolved distribution under the already-trained future networks, requiring only constant memory independent of T. Both methods use MLPs with residual blocks, GroupNorm, and SiLU activations, trained with AdamW. A key design choice is the extension of the state with an 'alive' indicator A_n ∈ {0,1} to restore Markovianity, and the use of randomized (Bernoulli) stopping times, which the paper shows are necessary for the mean-field limit to correctly approximate the N-agent optimum.

## Results

The paper establishes two main theoretical results: (1) a Dynamic Programming Principle (DPP) for discrete-time, finite-space MFOS (Theorem 4.1), and (2) an O(1/√N) approximation guarantee showing the MFOS solution is ε-optimal for the N-agent MAOS problem (Theorem 3.2). Empirically, both the Direct Approach (DA) and Dynamic Programming (DP) methods recover known optimal values in solvable cases (e.g., V* = 0.6 for Example 1 with T=4; V* = 1.6525 for Example 2) and scale to a 10×10 grid (spatial dimension 300) with T=50 in Example 6, matching target letter distributions under common noise. The N-agent validation (Fig. 3) confirms the predicted N^{-1/2} decay in both L² distribution distance and optimality gap. All experiments complete in under 3 minutes on a single RTX 4090 GPU.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper introduces the first computational methods for discrete-time, finite-space MFOS, so no external baselines exist for direct comparison. The internal comparisons focus on two axes: (1) DA vs. DP — DA learns the stopping decision faster when compute is unrestricted, but requires differentiating through the full trajectory (memory scales with T), while DP uses constant-order memory independent of the time horizon by training one network per time step via backward induction; (2) synchronous vs. asynchronous stopping — in Example 2, asynchronous stopping achieves V* = 1.6525 versus 3.25 for synchronous (a ~49% cost reduction), and in Example 3, asynchronous stopping with congestion produces visibly different (and lower-cost) distributional outcomes. The paper notes that single-agent optimal stopping methods (e.g., Becker et al. 2019, Herrera et al. 2023) cannot be adapted because they fail to capture inter-agent interdependence through the population distribution, and continuous-time MFOS results (Talbi et al. 2023, 2024) are purely theoretical with no computational component.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the classical single-agent optimal stopping framework to a multi-agent cooperative setting (MAOS) and then to the mean-field limit (MFOS), drawing on Mean Field Control (MFC) theory (Bensoussan et al., 2013; Carmona & Delarue, 2018) for the theoretical foundation. It departs from prior continuous-time MFOS work (Talbi et al., 2023; 2024), which characterized solutions via PDEs on infinite-dimensional measure spaces and offered no computational methods, by restricting to discrete time and finite state spaces where the distribution is finite-dimensional. The key conceptual departure is reframing MFOS as an MFC problem rather than a pure optimal stopping problem, which enables the use of dynamic programming principles and deep learning methods previously developed for MFC (Carmona & Laurière, 2021; Germain et al., 2022a). Single-agent deep learning OS methods (Becker et al., 2019; Herrera et al., 2023) are explicitly shown to be inapplicable because they cannot capture inter-agent dependence through the population distribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to formalize and computationally solve MFOS in discrete time and finite space, providing both a theoretical guarantee (O(1/√N) approximation of the N-agent problem) and two scalable deep learning algorithms (Direct Approach and Dynamic Programming Approach) validated up to 300-dimensional state spaces. The connection to MFC opens a pathway to importing the rich toolbox of mean-field control (PDE methods, BSMDE methods, RL) into the optimal stopping domain. Key limitations include the absence of convergence proofs for the neural network training algorithms, restriction to finite/discrete spaces (continuous-space MFOS remains open), and the lack of a detailed analysis of how different stopping time classes (synchronous vs. asynchronous) affect optimality. Extending to continuous spaces and real-world applications (e.g., actual drone fleet coordination) are identified as important future directions.
