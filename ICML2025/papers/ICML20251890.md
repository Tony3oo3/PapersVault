## Problem

While prior work has documented representational convergence across LLMs, these similarities have not been leveraged for practical behavioral intervention. This paper asks whether a behavior exhibited by model A (e.g., backdoor removal, refusal) can be transferred to model B by learning a mapping between their activation spaces, thereby enabling smaller models to efficiently align larger ones without explicitly analyzing the target's activations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251890/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes paired activations from a source model at a specific layer (identified via a steering sweep) and the corresponding target model activations, trained over 3 epochs on task-specific datasets. It produces a mapped steering vector applicable at the target model's corresponding layer, which is injected at trigger token positions (not the full sequence) with a tunable magnitude α. The autoencoder itself (0.32% of model parameters) can be deployed as a standalone 'safety switch' that toggles between base and fine-tuned model behaviors at inference time with minimal memory overhead.

## Method

The core mechanism is a two-layer autoencoder (linear encoder with ReLU activation followed by a linear decoder) trained to map source model activations at layer l to target model activations at layer l', using MSE reconstruction loss over paired token-position activations. Steering vectors are first computed on the source model via difference-in-means or prompt steering, then passed through the trained autoencoder to yield the target model's steering vector. The key finding is that this non-linear mapping outperforms affine (linear + bias) mappings across most transfer settings, and that tokenizer similarity is a critical factor for cross-architecture transfer (Qwen→Llama succeeds where Gemma→Llama fails). For the base-to-fine-tuned switching application, the autoencoder is trained to predict base model activations from fine-tuned model activations at the most steerable layers, enabling a single-layer intervention to replicate the effect of modifying ~50% of model weights.

## Results

The autoencoder-based activation mapping achieves a 100% win rate (MvA = 1.00) over mean-ablated baselines across all evaluated tasks and model pairs, with LLM-Judge scores of 2.6–4.5 and KL-divergence of 0.00–8.04 depending on the task. For cross-architecture transfer, Qwen→Llama (similar tokenizer) achieves LLM-Judge 3.0, KL-Div 6.97, and COH 3.70, while Gemma→Llama (different tokenizer) degrades to 1.2, 9.19, and 2.40 respectively. The mapped refusal vector from Llama-1B to Llama-3B yields perplexity of 16.50 (The Pile) and 8.32 (Alpaca) versus 15.78 and 7.26 for the native target vector, with comparable Llama-Guard safety scores. Single-layer activation patching between base and fine-tuned Qwen 0.5B reduces backdoor trigger rate by up to 60%, with the mapper comprising only 0.32% of the full model's parameters.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to the native refusal vector from Arditi et al. (2024) applied directly to Llama-3B, the mapped refusal vector transferred from Llama-1B achieves comparable Llama-Guard-3 safety classification scores across harm categories, though sub-string matching scores are lower because mapped completions begin with refusal phrases (e.g., 'I can't') but are followed by harmful content. Perplexity is slightly elevated (16.50 vs. 15.78 on The Pile; 8.32 vs. 7.26 on Alpaca), indicating minor coherence degradation. Against native steering via Turner et al.'s (2023) Activation Addition applied directly on the target model, the mapped steering vectors achieve comparable backdoor removal rates in most model pairs (Llama 1B→3B, Qwen 0.5B→1.5B for IHY), with the notable exception of Qwen 0.5B→1.5B Code Vulnerability, where the mapped vector is less effective at the default magnitude α=5 but improves when the magnitude is reduced. The autoencoder (non-linear) mapping consistently outperforms the affine (linear) baseline in reconstruction and language modeling losses across most transfer experiments, though the affine map is competitive on the Code Vulnerability task specifically.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the activation steering literature (Turner et al., 2023; Panickssery et al., 2023; Arditi et al., 2024) by asking whether steering vectors can be transferred across models rather than computed natively. It builds on cross-model representation patching work (Ghandeharioun et al., 2024; Stolfo et al., 2024) that used affine mappings, but departs by showing that a non-linear autoencoder mapping is both more effective and generalizes across model families. The paper also connects to the model-stitching and representational similarity literature (Lenc & Vedaldi, 2015; Klabunde et al., 2023) by converting those analytical insights into a practical intervention tool, and to sleeper-agent backdoor research (Hubinger et al., 2024) as a testbed for the transfer mechanism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication that a small model's learned steering vector can be mapped to align a larger model offers a compute-efficient path to safety intervention, and the 'lightweight safety switch' (a 0.32%-parameter autoencoder toggling between base and fine-tuned behaviors) is a concrete new tool. However, the evidence is confined to sub-3B models, the corrupted-capabilities task achieves only 6.34% success with mapped vectors, and MMLU scores drop substantially post-intervention, raising questions about whether the approach preserves general capabilities at scale. Open questions include whether multi-layer or circuit-level transfers are needed for complex behaviors, whether the tokenizer-similarity requirement for cross-architecture transfer can be relaxed, and how the method behaves on frontier-scale models.
