## Problem

Prior non-adaptive local differential privacy (LDP) protocols for quantile estimation required a polylog(B) factor more users than the information-theoretic optimum, and it was unknown whether this gap was inherent to non-adaptivity or merely a limitation of existing mechanisms. Additionally, the shuffle-DP trust model was fundamentally incompatible with highly adaptive protocols (which query one user per round), leaving no protocol that simultaneously harvested the privacy amplification from shuffling and the sample efficiency of adaptivity.

## Input / Output

Each of n users holds exactly one data point in the discrete ordered domain [B] = {1,…,B} and is queried at most once; the aggregator adaptively selects the next threshold based on all prior responses, consuming users in a random permutation. In the shuffle variant, users are partitioned into r = log₂(B) disjoint batches of size n/r, and each batch is randomly permuted by a trusted shuffler before the aggregator observes the responses. The output is a value m ∈ [B] such that the target quantile q lies in the interval [F_X(m)−α, F_X(m+1)+α] with probability at least 1−β, where F_X is the empirical CDF.

## Method

The core insight is that quantile estimation under LDP reduces to a private noisy binary search: each threshold query is answered by applying randomized response to the indicator [x ≤ m], making the observed head probability a known linear transform of the empirical CDF. The main technical contribution is proving that the Bayesian Screening Search (BayeSS) algorithm remains correct when coin probabilities drift by O(α) over time—exactly the perturbation that arises when sampling users without replacement in a sequentially adaptive protocol—via a potential-function/submartingale argument showing the Bayesian posterior still concentrates on good intervals. For shuffle-DP, the protocol runs a binary-search-with-repetitions across r = log₂(B) adaptive rounds, shuffling batches of n/r users per round to harvest privacy amplification from shuffling while retaining the sample efficiency of adaptivity. Matching lower bounds (Fano's inequality for adaptive protocols; a reduction to non-interactive CDF learning for non-adaptive protocols) prove the O(log B) vs. Ω(log² B) user separation is fundamental.

## Results

The paper establishes that a sequentially adaptive ε-LDP protocol (DpBayeSS) estimates the median within error α using only O(log B / (ε²α²)) users, which is optimal up to constant factors (Theorem 1.1, matching the lower bound in Theorem 1.2). In contrast, any non-adaptive ε-LDP protocol requires Ω(log²B / (α²ε²·log(1/α)⁴)) users (Theorem 1.3), demonstrating a fundamental separation between adaptive and non-adaptive settings. For shuffle-DP, a protocol with r = log₂B rounds achieves O((1/α² + 1/ε²)·log B·√(log(1/δ)·log(log B/β))) users (Theorem 1.4). Experimentally, with n = 2500 users and α_test = 0.04, DpBayeSS achieves substantially higher success rates than both baselines across all tested privacy budgets ε ∈ [0.1, 5] and domain sizes B up to 10⁶.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares DpBayeSS against two baselines: DpNaiveNBS (naive binary search with randomized response, from Karp & Kleinberg 2007) and the Hierarchical Mechanism (Cormode et al. 2019, the state-of-the-art non-adaptive LDP protocol). DpBayeSS achieves far higher success rates than both baselines on both the Pareto-like dataset (B = 4⁹, n = 2500, ε varying from 0.1 to 5) and the uniform dataset (n = 2500, ε = 1, B from 10³ to 10⁶), with confidence intervals far from overlapping, confirming statistical significance. The Hierarchical Mechanism performs particularly poorly: with n = 2500 users the number is insufficient to obtain a meaningful median estimate, consistent with its O(log³B / (ε²α²)) sample complexity being much larger than the O(log B / (ε²α²)) needed by the adaptive protocol. DpNaiveNBS, while better than the Hierarchical Mechanism, is still consistently outperformed by DpBayeSS in both success rate and absolute quantile error across all tested parameter regimes. The theoretical separation is also confirmed: the adaptive protocol's O(log B) dependence on the domain size is provably optimal, while non-adaptive protocols must incur at least an additional log B factor.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions quantile estimation under local differential privacy as a private instance of noisy binary search, repurposing the Bayesian Screening Search algorithm of Gretta & Price (2024) from a non-private setting and proving its robustness to adversarial probability shifts. It departs from the dominant non-adaptive approach (the hierarchical mechanism of Cormode et al., 2019, requiring O(log³B) users) by showing that sequential adaptivity reduces the sample complexity to O(log B), and introduces a novel framework combining shuffle-DP with bounded rounds of adaptivity to recover privacy amplification from shuffling while retaining adaptive efficiency. The lower-bound proofs build on the mutual-information framework of Duchi et al. (2013) for adaptive protocols and on CDF-learning lower bounds of Edmonds et al. (2020) for the non-adaptive separation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The proven fundamental separation—adaptive protocols need O(log B) users while non-adaptive protocols provably require Ω(log²B)—gives practitioners a clear theoretical justification for the engineering cost of adaptivity in federated settings. The shuffle-DP protocol with bounded adaptive rounds opens a template for combining trust-model benefits (shuffling) with sample-efficiency benefits (adaptivity) that may transfer to other distributed estimation problems. A key open question the authors highlight is whether a protocol can converge to the non-private O(1/α²) sample complexity as ε→∞, and whether the high logarithmic dependence on 1/α in the non-adaptive lower bound is an artifact of the proof.
