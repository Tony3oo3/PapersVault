## Problem

Existing data pruning algorithms retain only a fraction of training data but struggle to match full-dataset accuracy, particularly at high compression ratios. A key oversight is that the teacher model trained on the full dataset to compute pruning scores is typically discarded, wasting the distributional knowledge it encodes. This paper asks whether that discarded knowledge can be repurposed to improve training on the pruned subset.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250132/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pruned image subset (selected by any scoring-based or random pruning algorithm at fraction f of the original dataset) and a teacher model pre-trained on the full dataset, both operating on standard RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR/SVHN, 224×224 for ImageNet). The teacher's temperature-scaled soft predictions (τ=4) are computed on-the-fly during student training as an additional supervision signal alongside ground-truth labels. The output is a student classifier with the same architecture as the teacher (self-distillation setting), trained to minimize a weighted combination of cross-entropy and KL-divergence losses.

## Method

The core mechanism is self-distillation for data pruning: a teacher pre-trained on the full dataset provides soft predictions that guide a same-architecture student trained on the pruned subset, with the combined loss L = (1−α)·L_cls + α·L_KD. Three key practical findings emerge: (1) simple random pruning with KD matches or exceeds sophisticated score-based methods (forgetting, EL2N, GraNd, D2, DUAL) across all pruning regimes, eliminating the need for expensive score computation; (2) the optimal KD weight α should increase as the pruning fraction f decreases, since aggressive pruning concentrates noisy and ambiguous samples that the teacher's soft labels help mitigate; (3) at low f, smaller-capacity teachers outperform larger ones, revealing an amplified capacity-gap sensitivity in the pruned-data regime. Theoretically, the paper proves for regularized linear regression that distilling from a teacher trained on a larger data subset (f_t > f) strictly reduces the student's estimation bias compared to self-distillation on the same pruned data.

## Results

On CIFAR-100 with pruning factor f=0.1, incorporating KD with random pruning improves top-1 accuracy by 17 percentage points (from 39.8% to 56.8%). On ImageNet at f=0.1, top-5 accuracy increases by 5 points (82.37%→87.19%) with random pruning and by 20 points (62.47%→82.47%) with EL2N pruning. At higher retention (f=0.7 on ImageNet), a random 70% subset trained with KD surpasses the full-data baseline by +1.6% top-1 accuracy. Across all datasets and pruning factors, KD consistently yields the largest gains in high-compression regimes (low f).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among pruning methods, random pruning with KD consistently outperforms Forgetting, GraNd, EL2N, Memorization, DUAL, and Moderate-coreset at low pruning fractions (f≤0.4 on CIFAR-100, f≤0.08 on SVHN), both with and without KD; D2+KD achieves comparable accuracy to random+KD but requires tuning multiple hyperparameters per dataset and pruning fraction. At high pruning fractions (f≥0.5), KD renders the student robust to the pruning method choice, making all methods converge in accuracy. Among KD variants on CIFAR-100 (Table 1), VID (Ahn et al., 2019) achieves the best accuracy at every pruning level (e.g., 32.5% at 5%, 49.46% at 10%), outperforming vanilla Hinton KD (28.62%, 46.27%) by 3.9 and 3.2 points respectively. AB (Heo et al., 2018) is the second-best KD variant at 5% (30.5%), while RKD (Park et al., 2019) performs worst at extreme compression (21.69% at 5%). All KD methods substantially outperform the no-KD baseline (14.46% at 5%), confirming that the teacher's soft predictions are the primary source of gain rather than the specific KD formulation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of data pruning (coreset selection) and knowledge distillation, repurposing the teacher model that score-based pruning algorithms already train on the full dataset as a KD guide for the student trained on the pruned subset. The theoretical analysis directly extends Das & Sanghavi's (2023) self-distillation error decomposition to the asymmetric setting where the teacher is trained on a larger data fraction (f_t > f), showing that this reduces the student's estimation bias. Empirically, the paper builds on the observation by Sorscher et al. (2022) that random pruning outperforms sophisticated score-based methods in high-pruning regimes, demonstrating that this effect is amplified and made more robust when KD is incorporated.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway—that simple random pruning combined with KD matches or beats sophisticated pruning algorithms across all regimes—offers a significantly simpler pipeline for data-efficient training, particularly relevant when full dataset access is lost (e.g., privacy constraints). The counterintuitive finding that smaller teachers outperform larger ones under aggressive pruning challenges the standard KD assumption that teacher capacity should always exceed student capacity, opening questions about optimal teacher-student capacity matching in data-limited settings. Limitations include the theoretical analysis being restricted to regularized linear regression, and all experiments being confined to standard image classification with CNNs; whether these findings generalize to other modalities, transformer architectures, or non-self-distillation settings remains open.
