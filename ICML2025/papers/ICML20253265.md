## Problem

The field of explainable machine learning remains in a pre-paradigmatic state with no consensus on what constitutes an explanation or when a model is interpretable. A critical gap is that papers routinely introduce novel explanation algorithms without discussing their interpretation—i.e., how the mathematical formalism relates to intuitive human questions—leading to widespread misinterpretation of explanation outputs analogous to the well-documented misinterpretation of p-values and confidence intervals in applied statistics.

## Input / Output

The paper's formal framework defines an explanation algorithm as a functional F: F(R^d) × P(R^d) × R^d → R^k, taking a learned function f, a data distribution D, and a specific data point x, and producing a low-dimensional statistic vector. This single definition unifies a wide range of methods—SHAP values, LIME, Grad-CAM, layer-wise relevance propagation, counterfactual explanations, anchors, and even fairness and robustness metrics—under the same mathematical structure as traditional statistics of probability distributions and datasets. The unusual aspect is that the output is not a prediction or a loss but a summary statistic whose value is only meaningful if it admits a clear interpretation linking it to a specific intuitive question.

## Method

The core contribution is a conceptual reframing rather than a new algorithm: the paper formally defines 'statistic of a function' (Definition 2.3) and 'interpretation' (Definition 4.1), then argues that post-hoc explanation algorithms are fundamentally equivalent to traditional statistical quantities applied to high-dimensional functions. It distinguishes two research questions—(a) the mathematical/computational properties of the functional and (b) whether the functional formalizes an intuitive concept—and shows that a large portion of the literature addresses only (a) while neglecting (b). Using tools from the philosophy of science (convergence arguments and arguments by derivation), the paper demonstrates that popular feature attribution methods exhibit divergence rather than convergence, meaning they cannot all formalize the same intuitive concept. From this, it derives concrete research-practice recommendations: design explanations to answer specific questions, recognize that working with explanations requires statistical expertise, do not conflate explanations with fairness or robustness measures, and treat benchmark datasets as probes of empirical properties rather than sources of interpretation.

## Results

This is a position paper with no quantitative experiments or results tables. The central contribution is a formal framework (Definition 2.3) showing that post-hoc explanation algorithms are functionals F: F(R^d) × P(R^d) × R^d → R^k, i.e., statistics of functions, analogous to how p-values and confidence intervals are statistics of datasets. The paper argues that many popular explanation methods (SHAP, LIME, Grad-CAM, LRP) lack a clear interpretation in the philosophical sense (Definition 4.1), meaning they do not formalize a specific intuitive concept with a well-defined relationship, and that this is the primary source of misinterpretation errors in the field.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not compare methods empirically. Conceptually, it positions itself against the dominant framing in XAI that treats explanations as answers to 'why-questions' drawn from social science and psychology (Miller, 2019; Mittelstadt et al., 2019), arguing instead that the analogy to applied statistics is more productive. It contrasts methods with clear interpretations (counterfactual explanations, anchors, fairness metrics, model reliance) against those without (local feature attributions like SHAP, LIME, Grad-CAM), noting that the latter exhibit 'divergence' rather than 'convergence' across different formalizations of the same intuitive concept. The paper also distinguishes its view from the alternative perspective that algorithmic explanations should mirror human explanations (Section 9), arguing this analogy is misguided and that explanations are better understood as complex statistical tools requiring expertise.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper repurposes the conceptual apparatus of applied statistics—functionals, interpretation, convergence arguments from the philosophy of science—to reframe the entire XAI literature. It builds on the prior observation that explanation algorithms are functionals of learned predictors (Fisher et al., 2019; Marx et al., 2023; Scholbeck et al., 2024) but pushes this insight into a full methodological analogy: explanations are statistics of high-dimensional functions, just as p-values and confidence intervals are statistics of datasets. The paper explicitly positions itself against the dominant social-science/psychology framing of explanations as answers to 'why-questions' (Miller, 2019; Mittelstadt et al., 2019) and aligns with a small recent cluster of work taking a more statistical view (Bilodeau et al., 2024; Senetaire et al., 2023; Cherian & Candès, 2024), while claiming to be the first to make the full 'XAI as applied statistics' case.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central practical implication is that many widely-used explanation methods (SHAP, LIME, Grad-CAM) lack a clear interpretation in the philosophical sense, meaning their outputs are prone to the same misinterpretation errors that plague p-values and confidence intervals in applied science. This reframing sets more realistic expectations: explanations cannot formalize broad concepts like 'trust' or 'understanding,' and they cannot replace dedicated fairness or robustness statistics. Open questions include how to rigorously establish that a given explanation method does formalize an intuitive concept (the convergence/derivation arguments from philosophy of science), and how to communicate the expertise requirements of working with complex explanation statistics to non-specialist decision-makers.
