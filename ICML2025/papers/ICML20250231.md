## Problem

Robot-gated Interactive Imitation Learning methods rely on fixed uncertainty thresholds to decide when to request human help, but these thresholds fail to adapt as the agent improves, causing unnecessary expert interventions even after the agent has mastered a state. The paper asks how to design a robot-gated intervention criterion that mimics the human expert's own intervention logic and automatically reduces the intervention rate as the agent's policy converges toward the expert's.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250231/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a state observation (a 259-dimensional sensory vector in MetaDrive or a 7×7 semantic grid in MiniGrid) together with the agent's proposed action to produce an intervention decision. The proxy Q-function Q_θ^I(s, a_r) is trained on (state, action) pairs from both the human buffer and the agent's self-exploration buffer, and the switch-to-human threshold β is dynamically recomputed as the (1−δ)-th quantile of Q-values over agent-collected states, while the stop criterion ε is the running expected squared action difference. The final outputs are the agent policy π_r and the proxy Q-function, with the intervention decision being a binary request/stop signal that gates whether the human expert takes control.

## Method

AIM trains a proxy Q-function Q_θ^I(s, a_r) with a two-part loss: an AIM loss that labels Q as −1 for expert actions (agent aligned) and +1 for agent actions deviating from the expert by more than ε, and a Temporal Difference loss that propagates Q-values to agent-collected states for generalization beyond intervened regions. The switch-to-human threshold β is set as the (1−δ)-th quantile of Q-values over the agent's own state distribution, so as the agent's policy converges toward the expert, fewer states exceed β and the intervention rate shrinks automatically without a hand-tuned schedule. The continue-with-human criterion uses the expected action difference ε to stop expert intervention once the agent and expert actions align. This design eliminates the need for ensemble-based uncertainty estimation and task-specific hyperparameter tuning, while the TD component allows AIM to anticipate future mistakes and request help before they occur.

## Results

On MetaDrive (continuous control), AIM achieves a success rate of 0.82±0.06, episodic return of 328.4±20.4, and route completion of 0.91±0.03 under a 2K expert-involved-step budget, approaching the neural expert's 0.84/336.5/0.93. On MiniGrid Four Room (discrete control), AIM reaches a 0.63±0.05 success rate using only 0.4K expert data (9% intervention rate) and 4.4K total data, far exceeding all baselines. AIM also requires fewer total environment interactions (7.7K) than Thrifty-DAgger (9.5K) while using comparable expert data (1.9K vs. 2K).

## Baselines & Comparisons

AIM outperforms all four baselines on every reported metric in both MetaDrive and MiniGrid. Against the robot-gated baselines, AIM achieves a 0.82 success rate on MetaDrive versus 0.58 for Thrifty-DAgger (+24%) and 0.60 for Ensemble-DAgger (+22%), while also reducing total environment interactions from 9.5K to 7.7K. Against the human-gated baselines, AIM matches or exceeds PVP (0.82 vs. 0.62 success rate) and HG-DAgger (0.82 vs. 0.61) without requiring continuous expert monitoring—PVP's expert monitors every step, whereas AIM's expert is only invoked when the proxy Q-function exceeds a threshold. On MiniGrid, AIM's 0.63 success rate surpasses Thrifty-DAgger (0.42), Ensemble-DAgger (0.38), PVP (0.34), and HG-DAgger (0.20) by wide margins, using only 0.4K expert data (9% intervention rate) compared to 2K for the robot-gated baselines. The paper further shows that AIM's requested expert demonstrations are at least as informative as those collected under continuous human supervision (PVP), as an offline BC policy trained solely on AIM's human buffer nearly matches PVP's offline performance curve.

## Relation to Prior Work

AIM sits at the intersection of human-gated IIL (HG-DAgger, PVP) and robot-gated IIL (Ensemble-DAgger, Thrifty-DAgger), but departs from both by learning the intervention criterion rather than prescribing it. It inherits the imitation-learning training loop and policy loss from HG-DAgger, and borrows the temporal-difference propagation of the proxy value function from PVP, but replaces the fixed-threshold uncertainty heuristics of prior robot-gated methods with a learned proxy Q-function that adapts to the evolving agent policy. The key conceptual departure is treating the human intervention decision as a learnable signal (labeled +1/−1 based on agent–expert action divergence) rather than a hand-engineered confidence or novelty score.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By automatically shrinking the intervention rate as the agent converges, AIM removes the need for continuous human monitoring while still targeting safety-critical states, which is practically important for deploying IIL in settings where expert availability is limited. The finding that a policy trained purely offline on AIM's collected demonstrations matches the human-gated PVP baseline suggests the learned criterion captures the same information that continuous supervision would provide. However, the paper relies on neural experts rather than real humans, evaluates on only two simulated environments, and assumes a perfectly knowledgeable expert, leaving open whether the adaptive criterion generalizes to noisy, inconsistent, or multi-agent human feedback in real-world deployments.
