## Problem

Most prior LLM distillation methods apply a single loss function uniformly to both teacher-generated and student-generated responses, overlooking the synergy between loss formulations and data types. Naive extensions of DPO-style contrastive losses to distillation (e.g., DPKD) suffer from severe reward hacking because the teacher's probability on student responses is inherently small, causing the student to lose pre-trained knowledge rather than align with the teacher. This paper asks how to design a contrastive distillation objective that exploits the complementary behaviors of forward and reverse divergences on different response types without degenerating.

## Input / Output

The method takes a teacher LLM, a student LLM (initialized via SFT on ground-truth responses), and a set of training prompts. For each prompt, both the teacher and the current student generate responses, forming triplets (x, y_t, y_s) that are collected in a batched on-policy fashion at the start of each epoch (compatible with vLLM for efficient generation). The output is a distilled student LLM trained with LoRA (r=16) on all linear layers, with an effective batch size of 128 on 4×A100 80GB GPUs.

## Method

DISTILLM-2 introduces CALD, a contrastive loss that applies skew KL (SKL) to teacher-generated responses and skew reverse KL (SRKL) to student-generated responses, exploiting the fact that SKL naturally pulls up student likelihood where teacher probability is high (the 'head') while SRKL pushes down student likelihood where teacher probability is low (the 'tail'). This avoids the reward hacking of DPKD because the linear formulation (rather than log-sigmoid) allows token-level decomposition and the inherent linear dependency between the skewed distributions regularizes excessive likelihood reduction. Two curriculum mechanisms are added: (1) an adaptive α computed in closed form via a first-order Mercator series approximation, allocating smaller α to easy samples and larger α to hard samples, and (2) a linearly increasing coefficient β on the SRKL term that gradually shifts emphasis from teacher imitation to student-feedback-driven correction. The approach is validated across instruction-following, math reasoning, code generation, preference alignment, vision-language distillation, quantized model recovery, and speculative decoding acceleration.

## Results

On instruction-following (Table 2), DISTILLM-2 achieves average winning rates of 58.69% (Qwen2-1.5B), 56.45% (Danube2-1.8B), and 74.83% (Gemma-2-2B), outperforming the second-best method (DistiLLM) by +2.34%, +4.08%, and +4.53% respectively. On mathematical reasoning, DISTILLM-2 reaches 76.27% GSM8K / 35.58% MATH (Qwen2-Math-1.5B) and 81.20% / 42.94% (Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B), surpassing DistiLLM by ~1% on both benchmarks. On code generation, it achieves 59.92% HumanEval / 75.66% MBPP (DS-Coder-1.3B) and 42.24% / 62.70% (Qwen2.5-Coder-1.5B), leading GKD and DistiLLM by 3–6 points on pass@1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DISTILLM-2 consistently outperforms all eight baselines (KD, SeqKD, ImitKD, GKD, DistiLLM, Speculative KD, DPO, DPKD) across instruction-following, math, and code tasks. Against the closest competitor DistiLLM (v1), it gains +2.34% to +4.53% average winning rate on instruction-following, +0.86–0.97 points on GSM8K/MATH, and +3.3–5.9 points on HumanEval/MBPP. GKD is the strongest non-DistiLLM baseline but trails DISTILLM-2 by 2.5–5.8% on instruction-following and 1–4 points on code. Speculative KD (Xu et al., 2024b) is competitive on Danube2 (+1.95% gap) but lags more on Qwen2 and Gemma-2. DPKD (Li et al., 2024b), which naively applies DPO with the teacher as reference, suffers severe reward hacking (6.85 ROUGE-L vs. 26.37 for DISTILLM-2 in the on-policy GPT-2 setup) and is excluded from main baselines. On the capacity-gap study (Table 8), DISTILLM-2 shows monotonic improvement as teacher size grows from 1.8B to 14B (+4.67% over DistiLLM at 14B), where DistiLLM plateaus.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on DistiLLM (Ko et al., 2024), which introduced skew KL (SKL) and skew RKL (SRKL) loss functions along with an adaptive off-policy training strategy for LLM distillation. The key conceptual departure is the introduction of a contrastive framework—inspired by DPO—that assigns SKL to teacher-generated responses (exploiting the 'pulling-up' effect at the head of the teacher distribution) and SRKL to student-generated responses (exploiting the 'pushing-down' effect at the tail), rather than applying a single loss uniformly to all data. This addresses a specific failure mode of DPKD (Li et al., 2024b), which naively substituted the teacher for the reference model in DPO and suffered from severe reward hacking due to the inherently small teacher likelihood on student responses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides practitioners with a clean, well-motivated recipe for LLM distillation that consistently outperforms prior methods across instruction-following, math reasoning, code generation, and vision-language tasks, with the added benefit of a curriculum-based adaptive mechanism that removes the need for manual hyperparameter tuning of α. A notable open question is whether the contrastive framework's reliance on the specific SKL/SRKL pairing is truly optimal or whether other f-divergences with similar pulling-up/pushing-down properties could substitute. The finding that higher-quality responses from stronger LLMs do not improve the SKL term challenges the common assumption that 'better data' always helps in distillation, suggesting distributional alignment with the teacher may matter more than absolute response quality.
