## Problem

Prior theoretical work on offline alignment (RLHF and DPO) treats privacy and adversarial corruption as separate concerns, and typically analyzes only one of the two paradigms. This paper asks whether a unified analysis can capture the interplay between local differential privacy and strong adversarial corruption in both RLHF and DPO, and whether the order in which privacy and corruption are applied (CTL vs. LTC) fundamentally changes the difficulty of alignment.

## Input / Output

The method consumes an offline preference dataset of n samples, each containing a context s_i, two candidate actions a_i^0 and a_i^1, and a binary preference label y_i, along with a privacy budget ε and a corruption fraction α. The input dataset D_in may have labels that have undergone privacy randomization (randomized response) and/or adversarial flipping in either order (CTL, LTC, or CLC). The output is a learned policy π̂ (greedy or pessimistic for RLHF; log-linear for DPO) together with explicit suboptimality bounds expressed in terms of the relative condition number, ε, α, and sample size n.

## Method

The core technical contribution is a reduction that maps both RLHF (with a linear reward model) and DPO (with a log-linear policy class) to parameter estimation in logistic regression, where the feature is x_i = φ(s_i, a_i^1) − φ(s_i, a_i^0) and the true parameter is θ* (RLHF) or β(θ* − θ_sft) (DPO). For estimation under private and corrupted labels, the paper introduces a new loss function that applies a 'shifting and scaling' transformation to the received labels z_i — specifically (z_i + σ(ε) − 1)·c(ε) — making it an unbiased surrogate for the standard log-loss under randomized response. This single loss is adaptive: it does not require knowing in advance whether the setting is CTL, LTC, or CLC. The key separation result shows that under LTC the corruption bias term is amplified by a factor c(ε) = (e^ε+1)/(e^ε−1) relative to CTL, because the privacy mechanism contracts the total variation distance and thus amplifies the adversary's effective corruption, yielding a non-vanishing bias that grows with the privacy cost.

## Results

The paper's primary contribution is theoretical: it establishes the first suboptimality bounds for both RLHF and DPO under simultaneous local differential privacy (LDP) and adversarial label corruption, achieving an O(1/√n) rate for DPO under corruption (improving the prior O(1/n^{1/4}) of Chowdhury et al., 2024) and an O(√α) dependence on the corruption fraction (improving O(α^{1/4}) of Mandal et al., 2024). A key separation result shows that LDP-then-Corruption (LTC) incurs an additional multiplicative factor c(ε) = (e^ε+1)/(e^ε−1) in the bias term compared to Corruption-then-LDP (CTL). Empirically, on a synthetic finance preference dataset with GPT2-large, rDPO outperforms standard DPO under privacy (e.g., 65.8% vs. 60.4% win rate at ε=0.5), and the CTL-vs-LTC separation is confirmed: rDPO achieves 69.6% under CTL vs. 65.4% under LTC at (ε=1, α=0.1).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares rDPO (the robust DPO variant from Chowdhury et al., 2024, which corresponds to the paper's Algorithm 1 under a log-linear policy) against standard DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) under local differential privacy. rDPO consistently outperforms standard DPO: at ε=0.1, rDPO achieves 59.0% win rate vs. 55.4% for DPO; at ε=0.5, 65.8% vs. 60.4%. The gap widens as privacy tightens (ε decreases), consistent with the theoretical c(ε) cost. The paper also validates its key theoretical separation between CTL and LTC: rDPO performs better under CTL than LTC in both tested settings, with the gap increasing as ε decreases (4.2 points at ε=1 vs. 5.8 points at ε=0.5). Theoretically, the paper's O(1/√n) rate for DPO under corruption strictly improves upon the O(1/n^{1/4}) rate of Chowdhury et al. (2024) under the same (weaker) random-flipping corruption model, and the O(√α) dependence on corruption improves upon the O(α^{1/4}) of Mandal et al. (2024) for RLHF.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate lines of work—private offline alignment (Chowdhury et al., 2023) and robust offline alignment (Mandal et al., 2024; Chowdhury et al., 2024)—under a single reduction to logistic regression parameter estimation. The RLHF side builds directly on the pessimistic confidence-set framework of Zhu et al. (2023), while the DPO side extends the rDPO algorithm of Chowdhury et al. (2024) with a new 'shifting and scaling' loss that is adaptive to the privacy-corruption ordering. The corruption-robust offline RL results of Zhang et al. (2022) serve as the template for the strong-corruption analysis, which the authors extend from reward-based to preference-based settings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The clean separation between CTL and LTC (an extra c(ε) factor in the corruption bias when privacy precedes corruption) provides practitioners with a concrete, theoretically grounded reason to prefer corrupting-then-privatizing over the reverse, a distinction that had no prior formal treatment in the alignment literature. The O(1/√n) rate for DPO under strong corruption, matching the noiseless rate, is a meaningful improvement over the O(1/n^{1/4}) of prior work. Key limitations include the linear/log-linear modeling assumption, the open question of whether the c(ε) separation factor is tight (the authors conjecture √c(ε) may suffice without coverage), and the thin empirical validation on a single small synthetic dataset with GPT2-large.
