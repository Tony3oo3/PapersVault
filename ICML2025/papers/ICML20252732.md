## Problem

Existing AIGI detection benchmarks use randomly generated images without quality filtering, while real-world synthetic images tend to be high-quality and aligned with human preferences, raising the question of whether higher-quality AIGIs are harder for detectors to identify. This paper systematically investigates this question and reveals a counterintuitive finding: images with higher quality scores as assessed by human preference models are consistently easier to detect across multiple generators and preference models.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252732/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The evaluated detectors consume 224×224 center-cropped RGB patches (no resizing) and output a real/fake binary prediction. Quality scoring uses human preference models (ImageReward, HPS v2, MPS) that take an image–text prompt pair and produce a scalar preference score. The regression models introduced in the paper take six scalar low-level image characteristics (lightness, contrast, saturation, sharpness, texture richness, structural complexity) or six PCA-reduced DINOv2 high-level features and predict either a quality score or average detector accuracy, enabling patch-level ranking for input selection.

## Method

This is primarily an empirical study rather than a new architecture. The authors construct a diverse dataset of 4,000 prompts spanning 10–120+ words, generate images with six text-to-image models (SD 2.1, SDXL, SD 3, PixArt-α, FLUX.1, Infinity), and score them with multiple human preference models. They test six off-the-shelf detectors and demonstrate a positive correlation between quality scores and detection accuracy, then decompose the cause through three analyses: (1) text prompt complexity (shorter prompts yield both higher quality and easier detection due to lower structural complexity), (2) K-Means clustering on ImageReward feature space revealing that high saturation, high contrast, and rich texture co-occur with high quality and high accuracy, and (3) multiple linear regression quantifying how each low-level and high-level feature contributes to both dependent variables. As a practical application, they use the fitted regression models to rank 224×224 patches and select the most detectable one, which improves end-to-end detectors (e.g., SuSy gains ~2% AP) while having the opposite effect on fingerprint-based detectors (NPR, SSP), revealing that the 'easiest' patches for one detector family are the 'hardest' for another.

## Results

The paper's central empirical finding is counterintuitive: across 6 detectors and multiple generators (SD 2.1, SDXL, SD 3, PixArt-α, FLUX.1, Infinity), images with higher quality scores (ImageReward, HPS v2) consistently achieve higher average detector accuracy, with the trend most pronounced above the quality-score distribution peak. In the patch-selection application (Table 3), the 'Easiest' strategy (selecting the patch with the highest regression-predicted score) improves average precision for most end-to-end detectors: SuSy reaches 89.4% AP (vs. 87.6% Center), DRCT(Conv-B) reaches 87.6% AP (vs. 84.8% Center), and RINE reaches 91.9% AP (vs. 90.2% Center). Conversely, fingerprint-based detectors benefit from the opposite 'Hardest' strategy: NPR achieves 78.6% AP (vs. 75.9% Center) and SSP reaches 81.0% AP with its 'Simplest' patch selection.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 7 detectors (RINE, CoDE, SuSy, DRCT Conv-B, DRCT CLIP, NPR, SSP) under four patch-selection strategies (Center, Easiest, Random, Hardest) across GenImage, DRCT-2M, and Synthbuster. For end-to-end detectors, the Easiest strategy consistently outperforms Center: SuSy gains +1.8 AP (89.4 vs. 87.6), DRCT(Conv-B) gains +2.8 AP (87.6 vs. 84.8), and RINE gains +1.7 AP (91.9 vs. 90.2). DRCT(CLIP) is the notable exception where Hardest is best (88.6 AP vs. 88.4 Center), likely because it learns different discriminative features with lower real-image accuracy (68.9% vs. ~97–99% for others). For fingerprint-based detectors, the effect reverses: NPR's Hardest strategy yields 78.6% AP versus 75.9% for Center (+2.7), and SSP's Simplest strategy (selecting the least textured patch) achieves 81.0% AP and 56.4% ACC_fake, outperforming Center (79.7% AP, 51.7% ACC_fake). The Random strategy consistently underperforms both Center and Easiest for end-to-end models, confirming that center-cropping is not arbitrary but captures genuinely informative regions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of AIGI detection and image quality assessment, departing from prior work that observed poor detector generalization on real-world images (Cavia et al., 2024; Yan et al., 2025) without isolating quality as the explanatory variable. Rather than proposing a new detector architecture, it repurposes existing detectors (NPR, RINE, CoDE, DRCT, SuSy, SSP) and human preference models (ImageReward, HPS v2, MPS) as analytical instruments, applying standard clustering and regression tools to uncover the mechanistic link between image quality and detection difficulty. The patch selection application extends the SSP line of work (Chen et al., 2024b) by replacing its texture-diversity criterion with a quality-regression-based criterion, revealing an interesting duality where 'easiest' patches for end-to-end detectors are 'hardest' for fingerprint-based ones.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The counterintuitive finding that higher-quality AIGIs are easier to detect provides a concrete explanation for why detectors trained on randomly generated benchmark images underperform on real-world, human-curated content, and identifies specific low-level confounders (saturation, contrast, texture richness) that future detector designs should account for. However, the authors acknowledge that correlation does not imply causation—their intervention experiments show inconsistent causal effects across detectors—leaving open the question of whether the observed relationship will persist as generators improve or whether it reflects artifacts of current model architectures. The practical patch selection strategy offers a lightweight, training-free improvement but yields only modest gains (1–3% on average), and the regression models' generalization to entirely new generator families remains untested.
