## Problem

While the computational and engineering costs of training frontier LLMs are well-documented and growing, the human labor embedded in their training datasets is almost never quantified or compensated. This paper asks: what would it cost to pay workers to produce an equivalent volume of text from scratch, and how does that figure compare to the hardware, energy, and R&D costs of the training run itself?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20253285/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis consumes the approximate word count of each model's pre-training corpus (as disclosed in technical reports), along with training hardware specifications, energy consumption profiles, and institutional salary data. It produces two cost estimates per model—a training cost (hardware + energy + engineering labor) and a dataset labor cost—then reports their ratio. A key constraint is that only the total word count is used for dataset valuation; no attempt is made to account for text quality, domain distribution, or source-specific expertise, making the estimate deliberately a lower bound.

## Method

The paper applies a labor-theory-of-value framework to estimate dataset production cost: total words divided by an assumed writing speed of 30 words per minute, multiplied by a median global minimum wage of $3.85/hour (across 167 countries). Training costs are estimated using the methodology of Cottier et al. (2024), summing depreciated hardware value, energy consumption at historical prices, and R&D staff salaries. Both estimates are computed for 64 language models released between 2016 and 2024, and the resulting cost ratio (dataset labor / training cost) is shown to range from 10× to 1000×, with GPT-4 at ~300× and DeepSeek-V3 at ~6000×. The authors deliberately bias every parameter toward underestimation (low typing speed, minimum wage, no quality weighting) so that the conclusion—that data labor should be the dominant cost center—holds even under the most conservative assumptions.

## Results

Across 64 LLMs released between 2016 and 2024, estimated training data labor costs exceed training compute costs by 1–3 orders of magnitude (10×–1000×). Specifically, GPT-4's dataset is estimated at 300× its training cost, and DeepSeek-V3's at 6000×. For 8 of 10 major LLM companies studied, the estimated data creation cost exceeds 10% of annual revenue, and for 3 of 10 it surpasses total annual revenue, making compensation financially infeasible even at the paper's deliberately conservative $3.85/hour median minimum wage assumption.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not compare ML methods against baselines; instead, it contrasts two cost categories—training compute (hardware, energy, R&D salaries, following Cottier et al. 2024) versus training data labor cost (estimated at 30 words/min and $3.85/hr median minimum wage). The headline finding is that data costs dominate by 10–1000× across all 64 models studied, with no model showing training costs exceeding data costs. The paper further compares data costs against company revenues, finding that only the wealthiest organizations (e.g., those with multi-billion-dollar revenues) could theoretically absorb even these conservative data costs, while 3 of 10 companies would face data costs exceeding their entire annual revenue. The authors note that real-world costs are likely far higher (e.g., the actual Encyclopedia Britannica 15th edition cost ~$32M vs. their $85,560 estimate, a 400× gap), meaning the true disparity is even more extreme than reported.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on Cottier et al. (2024) for training cost estimation (hardware, energy, engineering salaries) but introduces a novel economic lens: applying the labor theory of value to quantify the human effort embedded in LLM training datasets. It departs from the data valuation literature (influence functions, Data Shapley, TRAK), which measures per-sample contribution to model quality, by instead asking what it would cost to reproduce the text from scratch at minimum wages. The scaling-law framework (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022) provides context for why both model and dataset sizes keep growing, but the paper's core contribution is the economic comparison between data production labor and compute costs, a question it frames as previously unaddressed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that training data labor costs exceed training compute costs by 1–3 orders of magnitude—even under deliberately conservative assumptions (30 wpm, $3.85/hr)—challenges the field's compute-centric framing of LLM development costs and raises the practical question of whether any organization could afford to compensate data creators. The paper identifies concrete open problems: establishing authorship provenance standards, scaling opt-in data collection, developing data-efficient training to reduce the volume of text needed, and designing sustainable compensation structures (e.g., royalties) that don't require prohibitive upfront payments. A key limitation is that the analysis uses only word count as a proxy for data value, ignoring quality, diversity, and curation effort, which would only widen the cost gap.
