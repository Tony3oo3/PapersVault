## Problem

Frontier AI safety policies identify automation of AI R&D by AI agents as a critical capability to monitor, yet no existing evaluation provides a highly realistic, direct comparison between AI agents and human experts on research engineering tasks. The paper addresses this gap by asking: can current frontier LLM agents, given equivalent resources and conditions, match or exceed the performance of skilled ML research engineers on realistic AI R&D tasks?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252661/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each agent (or human expert) receives a starting solution (simple but poorly performing), a scoring function callable at any time (producing timestamped score log entries), and a secure VM with 1–6 H100 GPUs, 20–48 vCPUs, and 200–400 GB RAM. The output is a normalized score on a 0–2 scale (0 = starting solution, 1 = reference solution, >1 = exceeding reference), reported as best-of-k (score@k) across varying allocations of total time budget between number of independent runs and per-run duration. The final score for most environments is the highest entry in the score log; for Scaling Law Experiment, only the final JSON submission is scored.

## Method

RE-Bench consists of seven hand-crafted ML research environments spanning kernel optimization, LLM finetuning, scaling-law prediction, restricted-architecture model design, embedding recovery, QA finetuning, and Rust code-contest scaffolding, each with a scoring function, a starting solution, and a hidden reference solution used for normalization. Human baselines come from 61 experts (71 eight-hour attempts) recruited from professional networks, hiring pipelines, and graduate programs, working under identical VM conditions and allowed to use the internet and LLMs. AI agents (o1-preview and Claude 3.5 Sonnet) are evaluated in two scaffolds—Modular (a basic general-purpose agent with Python/Bash tools) and AIDE (a data-science-automation scaffold)—using wall-clock time limits rather than token budgets to ensure equal GPU-hour allocation. The evaluation protocol uses best-of-k sampling with varying per-run durations (30 min, 2 h, 8 h) and total budgets up to 32 hours, with scores normalized via a linear transform mapping the starting solution to 0 and the reference solution to 1. Key findings: the best AI agents achieve 4× human scores at a 2-hour budget, but humans display steeper returns to additional time, narrowly exceeding top agent scores at 8 hours and achieving 2× the top agent score at 32 total hours.

## Results

Across 71 eight-hour attempts by 61 human experts, 82% achieved a non-zero score and 24% matched or exceeded the reference solution, with an average normalized score of 0.64. At a 2-hour budget, the best AI agents (o1-preview in AIDE, Claude 3.5 Sonnet in Modular) achieve scores approximately 4× higher than human experts. However, humans display steeper returns to additional time: at 8 hours humans narrowly exceed the top agent, and at 32 total hours (best-of-k) humans achieve roughly 2× the score of the best AI agent. Notably, both o1-preview and Claude 3.5 Sonnet find novel Triton kernel solutions in 'Optimize a Kernel' that beat all 9 human experts, while agents submit scored solutions ~10× more frequently (36.8/hr for AIDE, 25.3/hr for Modular vs. 3.4/hr for humans).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates two frontier models—o1-preview (OpenAI) and Claude 3.5 Sonnet (Anthropic, both June and October 2024 versions)—in two scaffolds (Modular and AIDE) against 61 human experts under identical hardware and time conditions. At 2 hours per environment, both AI agents substantially outperform humans (≈4× score), with o1-preview in AIDE and Claude 3.5 Sonnet in Modular being the strongest configurations. At 8 hours, humans narrowly surpass the best agents, and at 32 hours (best-of-4 for humans, best-of-k for agents) humans pull ahead by roughly 2×. Performance is heterogeneous across environments: o1-preview exceeds human performance on 'Optimize a Kernel' (beating all 9 human experts with a novel Triton solution), while Claude 3.5 Sonnet approaches human levels on 'Finetune GPT-2 for QA' and 'Scaling Law Experiment' where o1-preview underperforms. Both agents struggle with long-horizon reasoning, instruction-following (e.g., repeatedly proposing transformers in 'Restricted Architecture MLM' despite the no-division constraint), and recovering from environmental failures, which limits their scores relative to top human experts despite their much higher trial frequency.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on evaluating AI agents on ML tasks (MLE-bench, ML-Agent-Bench, SciCode) by specifically targeting the capability of automating frontier AI R&D through direct human-AI performance comparison under equivalent conditions. It builds on AIDE as an agent scaffold (selected for its strong MLE-bench performance) and adopts MLE-bench's wall-clock time-limit evaluation protocol, but departs from prior work by introducing hand-crafted, novel ML research environments with reference solutions, normalized scoring, and 8-hour human expert baselines collected under identical hardware and access conditions. Unlike SWE-bench or GPQA, which test narrower or non-ML-research skills, RE-Bench covers a range of frontier R&D subtasks (kernel optimization, scaling law prediction, architecture design under constraints, RL fine-tuning) that collectively approximate the workflow of an ML research engineer.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

RE-Bench provides the first systematic, apples-to-apples measurement of how close current frontier models are to automating expert ML research work, which is a key capability threshold in AI safety frameworks (Anthropic's RSP, Google DeepMind's FSF, OpenAI's Preparedness Framework). The finding that AI agents outperform humans at 2 hours but humans still lead at 8+ hours gives a concrete, quantifiable signal for tracking the trajectory toward AI R&D automation. Key limitations include only 7 environments, 8-hour human baselines, and tasks that are self-contained rather than involving multi-month coordination, meaning the real-world human-AI gap is likely larger. Open questions include how the gap evolves with longer time horizons, more complex multi-workstream tasks, and whether the agents' cost advantage (~$123 vs ~$1,855 per 8-hour run) changes the economic calculus of automation.
