## Problem

Reward models are black boxes critical to LLM alignment, yet it remains unclear which attributes they actually reward. Naive evaluation—comparing average rewards across labeled groups—conflates the target attribute's effect with spurious correlations in the evaluation data, producing misleading estimates even when the reward model is insensitive to the attribute. The paper formalizes RM explainability as a causal inference problem and develops an estimator that works despite the impossibility of generating perfect counterfactuals with LLM rewrites.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a labeled dataset of prompt-response pairs (each tagged with a binary attribute value w∈{0,1}), a reward model R, and an LLM rewriter (GPT-4o). It produces three causal effect estimates—ATT, ATU, and ATE—each representing the expected reward change when flipping the target attribute while holding all other response properties fixed. The procedure requires two sequential LLM rewrite calls per sample (a rewrite and a rewrite-of-rewrite), roughly doubling the API cost compared to a single-rewrite baseline.

## Method

RATE's core mechanism replaces (original, rewrite) comparisons with (rewrite, rewrite-of-rewrite) comparisons, so that systematic off-target changes introduced by the LLM rewriter (e.g., typo correction, formatting normalization) appear on both sides of the reward difference and cancel in expectation. The theoretical guarantee rests on a latent variable decomposition Y = Y(W, Z, ξ) where W is the target attribute, Z are immutable off-target attributes (topic, language), and ξ are mutable off-target attributes affected by rewriting (grammar, tone). Under two assumptions—that rewrite errors are direction-independent (Assumption 1) and that the reward decomposes additively into a (W,Z) component and a ξ component (Assumption 2)—the double-rewrite estimator is proven unbiased and √n-consistent for ATT, ATU, and ATE. The ATE is computed as a proportion-weighted average of the ATT and ATU estimators, and the entire procedure is summarized in Algorithm 1, requiring no explicit enumeration of confounding factors.

## Results

In semi-synthetic experiments with known ground truth, RATE accurately recovers the true (near-zero) treatment effect of 'starts with a vowel' on reward scores even as spurious typo correlations increase, while the naive and single-rewrite estimators show growing bias (Figure 3). In a second semi-synthetic test using a DistilBERT sentiment classifier as a proxy reward model, RATE remains invariant to distributional shift in the length–sentiment correlation, whereas the single-rewrite estimator reports an increasingly negative effect (Figure 4). On real-world reward models (FsfairX-LLaMA3-RM-v0.1, NCSOFT, ArmoRM) evaluated across IMDB, ELI5, and HelpSteer, RATE estimates differ substantially from naive estimates: the naive method yields much larger Cohen's d effect sizes for length, complexity, and helpfulness, and smaller ones for sentiment (Figure 5), suggesting that the widely reported 'length bias' in reward models may be largely an artifact of correlational evaluation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three estimators: the naive (correlational) estimator, the single-rewrite estimator, and RATE (double-rewrite). In the controlled typo/vowel experiment, RATE stays near the ground-truth zero effect across all typo percentages, while both the naive and single-rewrite estimators drift increasingly negative as the spurious correlation grows (Figure 3). In the length–sentiment distributional-shift experiment, RATE is invariant to the induced correlation, the single-rewrite estimator degrades (reporting an increasingly negative effect), and the naive estimator is most sensitive (Figure 4). On real-world reward models, RATE consistently produces smaller effect sizes for length, complexity, and helpfulness than the naive estimator, and larger effect sizes for sentiment (Figure 5), indicating that naive evaluations overstate the influence of structural attributes and understate the influence of content attributes. The authors further note that NCSOFT's claimed improvement over FsfairX-LLaMA3-RM-v0.1 on length bias is less pronounced under RATE than under the naive method, and that NCSOFT may have inadvertently penalized complexity. Overall, RATE outperforms both baselines in every controlled setting and reveals qualitatively different (and, per the authors, more accurate) causal attributions on real reward models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the causal inference for text literature (CausaLM, CEBAB) to the specific domain of reward model explainability, where prior work (RewardBench, Jiang et al.) used non-causal or example-wise contrastive approaches. The key methodological departure is the double-rewrite cancellation mechanism: while Gat et al. (2023) also use LLMs to generate counterfactuals, they do not correct for the systematic off-target modifications that LLM rewriters introduce. RATE's insight—that comparing rewrites to rewrites-of-rewrites cancels these errors in expectation—repurposes the rewrite process itself as a self-correcting instrument, requiring no explicit enumeration of confounders.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that 'length bias' in reward models may be substantially an artifact of naive evaluation methodology is practically significant for the alignment community, as it suggests some widely-reported reward model pathologies may be measurement errors rather than genuine model behavior. However, RATE is limited to binary attributes, depends on subjective judgment of rewrite quality, and does not address how causal sensitivity to attributes translates into downstream aligned-LLM behavior. Open questions include formal validation of rewrite quality beyond qualitative inspection and extension to continuous or multi-valued attributes.
