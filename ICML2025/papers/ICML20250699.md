## Problem

Causal representation learning (CRL) methods are evaluated almost exclusively on synthetic datasets generated to match their own theoretical assumptions, leaving their real-world applicability untested. This paper asks whether representative CRL methods can recover known causal factors from a simple, tightly controlled physical system where their core assumptions are satisfied and ground truth is available.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250699/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The physical light tunnel takes five control inputs (RGB LED brightness, two polarizer angles) and produces 64×64 RGB camera images plus numeric sensor readings (IR and visible light intensity at three positions, electrical current, and two angle measurements). The three evaluated methods consume these observations in different configurations—CCRL uses per-variable interventional environments, Multiview CRL groups outputs into four disjoint views, and CITRIS uses temporal sequences with per-step intervention labels—and output learned latent representations scored against ground truth via MCC, SHD, R², or Spearman correlation matrices.

## Method

The paper constructs a physical light tunnel benchmark where a controllable RGB light source, two rotating linear polarizers, a camera, and multiple sensors create a data-generating process satisfying CRL's core assumptions with known ground-truth causal factors. Three representative CRL methods are evaluated: Contrastive CRL (Buchholz et al., 2023) on interventional data, Multiview CRL (Yao et al., 2024c) on four disjoint observation views, and CITRIS (Lippe et al., 2022) on temporal intervened sequences. A deterministic simulator combining a mechanistic sensor model and an MLP image generator substitutes the real mixing function for synthetic ablation experiments, isolating the effect of physical noise. All three methods fail to recover the ground-truth factors on real data, and two of three also fail on the synthetic ablation, revealing a reproducibility problem beyond real-world noise. The CCRL method succeeds on synthetic data but fails on real data, pinpointing its sensitivity to the stochastic, non-additive noise inherent in the physical mixing process.

## Results

All three representative CRL methods fail to recover the ground-truth causal factors (R, G, B, θ₁, θ₂) on the real light tunnel data. CCRL (Buchholz et al., 2023) achieves MCC = 0.285 ± 0.054 and SHD = 7.6 ± 0.9 on real data, but recovers well on the deterministic synthetic ablation (MCC = 0.891 ± 0.005, SHD = 2.0 ± 0.0), implicating the stochastic mixing function as the failure cause. CITRIS (Lippe et al., 2022) fails on both real and synthetic data with R² diagonal = 0.092 ± 0.056 (real) and 0.120 ± 0.068 (synthetic), while off-diagonal values remain high (0.62–0.64). The multiview CRL method (Yao et al., 2024c) also fails on both settings, unable to disentangle the polarizer angle variables from the content block.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This benchmark paper evaluates three CRL methods against a shared ground truth rather than comparing them head-to-head, and the central finding is that all three fail. CCRL (Buchholz et al., 2023) is the only method that succeeds on the deterministic synthetic ablation (MCC 0.891, SHD 2.0), but its performance collapses on real data (MCC 0.285, SHD 7.6), which the authors attribute to the stochastic, non-additive noise in the real mixing function violating the method's deterministic-mixing assumption. The multiview CRL method (Yao et al., 2024c) fails on both real and synthetic data: while it recovers the RGB content variables reasonably well (R² ≈ 0.7–0.9), it cannot separate the polarizer angle style variables (θ₁, θ₂) from the content, with style R² scores remaining as high as 0.3–0.6. CITRIS (Lippe et al., 2022) fails catastrophically on both settings, with near-zero diagonal R² scores (0.09–0.12) and high off-diagonal values (0.62–0.64), indicating the model neither disentangles the factors nor learns the correct latent-to-factor mapping. The authors conclude that CCRL's failure is a misspecification issue (stochastic mixing), while the multiview and CITRIS failures likely involve both misspecification and optimization difficulties, underscoring a broader reproducibility and real-world applicability gap in the CRL literature.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing a real-world physical benchmark for causal representation learning, positioned as orthogonal to the existing synthetic benchmarks (CausalWorld, Causal Triplet, game-based environments) that dominate the field. It builds on the Causal Chambers hardware platform from Gamella et al. (2025) but repurposes it specifically as a CRL sanity check with known ground-truth causal factors. The paper evaluates three representative methods from distinct CRL families—contrastive CRL (Buchholz et al., 2023), multiview CRL (Yao et al., 2024c), and temporal CRL (CITRIS, Lippe et al., 2022)—each of which has been validated only on synthetic data matching their own assumptions. The key departure from prior work is the insistence on a real, noisy, physical mixing function rather than the deterministic or additively-noisy mixing functions assumed in theory.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that all three representative CRL methods fail to recover ground-truth causal factors on a simple, well-controlled physical system where their core assumptions are satisfied is a significant negative result that challenges the field's confidence in the practical applicability of current CRL theory. The synthetic ablation revealing that multiview CRL and CITRIS fail even with a deterministic mixing function points to optimization and implementation fragility beyond assumption misspecification, raising open questions about whether these methods can be made robust enough for real-world deployment. The paper also highlights a reproducibility problem: performance is highly sensitive to pre-processing, architecture choices, and hyperparameters, and public code is often unavailable or incomplete. Future work must bridge the gap between identifiability theorems (which assume infinite data and perfect optimization) and finite-sample, finite-architecture practice.
