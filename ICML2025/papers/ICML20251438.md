## Problem

While cross-entropy (CE) is known to maximize intra-class compactness and inter-class distinctiveness (neural collapse) at its minimum, it remains unproven whether binary cross-entropy (BCE) also leads to neural collapse, since BCE is a linear combination of CEs and the contrastive property is not linear. Furthermore, the practical mechanism by which BCE might outperform CE in feature learning—particularly the role of classifier biases—was unexplored.

## Input / Output

The method takes standard RGB images (MNIST 28×28, CIFAR-10/100 32×32, ImageNet-1K 224×224) and produces both a class label and a d-dimensional feature vector from the penultimate layer of a CNN. The key analytical output is the set of K decision scores {w_j^T h^(k) − b_j} per sample, whose geometric distribution (positive vs. negative separation, uniformity across samples) is measured via uniform accuracy, compactness, and distinctiveness metrics rather than standard classification accuracy alone.

## Method

The paper proves (Theorem 3.2) that BCE, when reaching its global minimum on a balanced dataset with d ≥ K−1, also satisfies all neural collapse properties (intra-class collapse, simplex ETF, self-duality), extending NC beyond losses satisfying the contrastive property. The key mechanistic insight is that CE measures relative decision scores via Softmax (requiring only that the positive score exceeds each negative score per sample), while BCE measures absolute scores via independent Sigmoids (requiring all positive scores to exceed a unified threshold and all negative scores to fall below it across all samples). This makes BCE explicitly constrain feature geometry across samples, and the classifier biases in BCE serve as a substantial, consistent separator between positive and negative decision scores—unlike CE where biases converge to zero or an arbitrary ridge. Empirically, BCE-trained ResNets and DenseNets on MNIST, CIFAR-10/100, and ImageNet-1K achieve faster NC convergence, higher uniform accuracy (up to +20% on CIFAR-100), and better feature compactness and distinctiveness than CE-trained counterparts.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, BCE-trained ResNet18 achieves 93.2% accuracy and 91.9% uniform accuracy (vs. 92.8%/85.2% for CE) with SGD+DA1, and 95.6%/95.2% (vs. 95.7%/94.3%) with AdamW+DA1+DA2. On CIFAR-100, the uniform accuracy gap widens dramatically: BCE-trained ResNet18 reaches 63.3% vs. 43.2% for CE (SGD+DA1), a gain of over 20 percentage points. On ImageNet-1k, BCE consistently outperforms CE in uniform accuracy by 25–32 points (e.g., ResNet50: 66.92% vs. 34.48%), while top-1 accuracy improves by 0.3–0.6 points. BCE also improves feature compactness (E_com) and distinctiveness (E_dis) in most configurations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is BCE loss vs. CE loss as the training objective for the same architectures (ResNet18/50/101, DenseNet121/161). BCE wins on uniform accuracy (A_Uni) in virtually every configuration: on CIFAR-10 the gain is at least 0.82% and up to ~7% (e.g., R18 SGD+DA1: 91.9% vs. 85.2%), while on CIFAR-100 the gain reaches 20+ points (e.g., R18 SGD+DA1: 63.3% vs. 43.2%). On ImageNet-1k, BCE's uniform accuracy advantage is even more dramatic (ResNet50: 66.92% vs. 34.48%; ResNet101: 70.46% vs. 38.85%; DenseNet161: 69.08% vs. 43.56%), though top-1 accuracy gains are modest (0.3–0.6 points). BCE also consistently improves feature compactness (E_com) and distinctiveness (E_dis) metrics on CIFAR-10/100. On the long-tailed CIFAR-100-LT dataset, BCE outperforms CE at all imbalance factors (IF=10: 71.54% vs. 70.91%; IF=50: 58.49% vs. 57.59%; IF=100: 52.88% vs. 51.48%). The paper notes that BCE's superiority is not merely due to alignment with Mixup/CutMix augmentations, as BCE still outperforms CE under DA1 alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the neural collapse (NC) theory established by Papyan et al. (2020) and generalized by Zhou et al. (2022b) to the binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss, proving for the first time that BCE also leads to NC at its minimum despite not satisfying the contrastive property that prior theory required. It builds on the empirical observations of Wightman et al. (2021) and Touvron et al. (2022) that BCE works well for multi-class classification, but provides the missing theoretical explanation by showing BCE explicitly constrains absolute decision scores (via classifier biases) rather than merely relative scores as CE does. The paper also departs from prior NC analyses by highlighting the critical role of classifier biases, which are essentially inert in CE but substantially shape feature geometry in BCE.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides a principled justification for adopting BCE over CE in multi-class training, with the practical implication that BCE-trained features exhibit significantly better intra-class compactness and inter-class distinctiveness—critical for downstream tasks like face recognition, re-identification, and tracking. The large uniform accuracy gap (up to 20+ points on CIFAR100) suggests CE-trained models may be underutilizing their learned features in retrieval-style applications. Open questions remain for the K > d regime (common in face recognition), imbalanced datasets, and Transformer architectures, though the authors provide preliminary evidence that BCE's advantages persist in these settings.
