## Problem

LLMs consistently fail to accurately repeat a single token when prompted to do so, instead diverging into unrelated text, yet the underlying cause of this failure mode remained unexplained. This vulnerability is exploitable by end users to extract memorized training data, but no prior work identified the specific neural circuit responsible. The paper asks: what mechanism causes repeated tokens to trigger attention-sink-like behavior, and can this be both explained and mitigated at the circuit level?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252904/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pretrained LLM (LLaMA-1/2/3, Mistral, Pythia) and a text sequence containing either exact token repetitions or tokens drawn from the same attention-head cluster. It produces: (1) identified sink neuron IDs and their layer location (e.g., neurons 7890 and 10411 in layer 1 of LLaMA-2), (2) a targeted code patch that modifies the MLP output at the sink layer during prefill and decode phases, and (3) a demonstration that the patched model passes standard benchmarks (MMLU, HellaSwag, TruthfulQA, WinoGrande, AI2-ARC) with negligible performance change while no longer diverging on repeated-token or cluster-token inputs.

## Method

The paper identifies a two-stage attention-sink circuit: (1) the first attention layer marks the initial token by mapping first vs. non-first tokens into linearly separable subspaces, implemented via near-orthogonal query/key vectors that act as 'other token detectors' combined with causal masking to force self-attention on the first token; (2) a sparse set of 'sink neurons' in an early MLP layer, triggered by this mark, inject high-magnitude values into the residual stream, creating the disproportionately high hidden-state norm that attracts attention in subsequent layers. The key theoretical result (Theorem 4.1) proves that as the number of repetitions grows, the representation of the last repeated token converges to the representation of a singleton sequence, making the first attention layer unable to distinguish a repeated sequence from a single token and thus falsely activating the sink circuit. This insight enables a 'cluster attack' where tokens from the same attention-head cluster (not necessarily identical) trigger the same mechanism, bypassing surface-level repetition filters. The mitigation is a targeted patch that stores the sink neuron's 'no-sink' activation from the second token during prefill and overwrites all subsequent tokens' activations with this value, preventing false sink induction without affecting general model performance.

## Results

The proposed targeted patch (zeroing the sink neuron's output for non-first tokens) resolves repeated-token divergence while preserving model performance: across LLaMa-1, LLaMa-2, and Mistral, benchmark scores shift by at most ±1.83 points (e.g., LLaMa-2 MMLU goes from 41.20 to 42.17, HellaSwag from 57.12 to 57.13). Ablation of the identified sink neurons (e.g., neurons 7890 and 10411 in LLaMa-2 layer 1) dramatically reduces the abnormally high hidden-state norms of repeated tokens, confirming their causal role. The convergence predicted by Theorem 4.1 is empirically visible with fewer than 500 repetitions in LLaMa-2, well below the maximum context window.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against competing mitigation or interpretability methods; the primary quantitative comparison is between the original and patched model on five standard NLP benchmarks (MMLU, HellaSwag, TruthfulQA, WinoGrande, AI2-ARC) across three model families. The patch introduces negligible performance changes—most deltas fall within ±0.5 points, with the largest single drop being −1.83 on LLaMa-1 TruthfulQA—demonstrating that the targeted intervention does not degrade general capabilities. The paper also contrasts its mechanistic, circuit-level explanation against prior work (Nasr et al., 2023; Barbero et al., 2025) that offered only intuitive or theoretical accounts without identifying the specific neurons responsible. The cluster attack is presented as a novel extension that bypasses surface-level repetition-detection defenses, but no quantitative success-rate comparison against the original repeated-token attack is provided due to the difficulty of measuring training-data leakage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the mechanistic interpretability program (Wang et al., 2022; Olsson et al., 2022) to explain a specific LLM failure mode by linking two previously studied but disconnected phenomena: repeated token divergence (Nasr et al., 2023) and attention sinks (Xiao et al., 2024). It builds on the theoretical framework of Barbero et al. (2025) and Veličković et al. (2024) to prove that long repeated-token sequences become indistinguishable from single-token sequences, and then identifies the specific neural circuit (first-layer attention marking + sparse MLP 'sink neurons') that turns this indistinguishability into a behavioral failure. Unlike prior attention-sink work that characterized the mechanism in isolation, this paper reveals its unintended security consequences and extends the vulnerability beyond exact repetition via the cluster attack.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first circuit-level explanation for repeated token divergence, showing that a mechanism essential for LLM fluency (attention sinks) is simultaneously the source of an exploitable vulnerability that can leak training data. The cluster attack demonstrates that surface-level defenses filtering repeated tokens are insufficient, pushing the field toward more fundamental architectural fixes. Open questions remain: why the divergence specifically triggers memorized training data rather than arbitrary text, whether the first-layer 'attend to others' strategy is unique to LLaMA-2 or generalizes, and whether the targeted patch fully addresses the underlying causal-masking vulnerability across all token types.
