## Problem

Conventional nonparametric density ratio estimation methods (e.g., KLIEP, uLSIF) suffer severe performance degradation as data dimension grows due to the curse of dimensionality, while existing dimension-reduction approaches (D³) impose a restrictive linear low-dimensional intrinsic space assumption. This paper seeks a nonparametric DRE method that avoids parametric misspecification bias, handles high-dimensional inputs, and provides formal theoretical guarantees.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252500/cropped_page5_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes two independent i.i.d. samples {x_i^p} from the target density p(x) and {x_i^q} from the reference density q(x), both in R^d for arbitrary d. It outputs a pointwise-evaluable estimated density ratio function r̂_K(x) = ∏_{k=1}^K f̂_k(â_k^T x), with negative values truncated to the minimum positive estimate at each iteration to enforce non-negativity. The number of projections K and basis size J_k are selected via 5-fold cross-validation on a validation loss.

## Method

The density ratio is approximated by a multiplicative projection pursuit decomposition r*(x) ≈ ∏_{k=1}^K f_k(a_k^T x), where each f_k is a univariate pursuit function and a_k is a unit projection direction. At iteration k, given the partial product r̂_{k-1}(x), the method estimates f_k from a linear sieve class of Gaussian basis functions and simultaneously optimizes a_k by minimizing a regularized empirical L² loss that couples the q-sample quadratic term, the p-sample linear term, and an ℓ₂ ridge penalty on the sieve coefficients. For fixed a, the sieve coefficients admit a closed-form ridge solution; the direction a_k is then updated via stochastic gradient descent (Adam). The key insight is that each iteration reduces the high-dimensional estimation to a univariate single-index problem, so the approximation error vanishes as K → ∞ without requiring the restrictive linear-subspace assumption of D³ methods. The paper establishes uniform consistency and explicit convergence rates for both the pursuit functions and projection directions under standard sieve regularity conditions.

## Results

On the IHDP-continuous dose response task (25 covariates), ppDRE achieves the lowest average squared error (ASE) in most settings, e.g. 0.091 for ADRF vs. 0.106–0.117 for all baselines, and 0.266 for QDRF at τ=0.9 vs. 0.267–0.408. In mutual information estimation, ppDRE shows dramatic gains at high dimension: at p=20, ρ=0.8 it achieves MAE 2.966 versus 8.6–10.2 for all other methods. On covariate shift adaptation across five real-world regression benchmarks, ppDRE attains the best NMSE in every dataset (e.g., 0.199 on Computer Activity vs. 0.273–0.668 for baselines; 0.133 on Diamond Prices vs. 0.183–0.360). In stabilized weights estimation, ppDRE's median RMSE at d_X=100 is 0.9, compared to 1.2–2.1 for all competitors.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ppDRE consistently outperforms all seven baselines across every application. Against the classical linear sieve methods (uLSIF, KLIEP), the advantage grows sharply with dimension: at d_X=100 in stabilized weights estimation, ppDRE's median RMSE is 0.9 versus 1.32 (uLSIF) and 1.39 (KLIEP), and in MI estimation at p=20, ρ=0.8, ppDRE achieves MAE 2.966 versus 10.24 (uLSIF) and 9.99 (KLIEP). The dimension-reduction method D³-LHSS, which assumes a linear low-dimensional subspace, is also surpassed by ppDRE in all settings, confirming that the multiplicative PP approximation captures non-linear structure that linear projections miss. The feature-based fDRE and kernel-regularized RRND methods, both designed for high-dimensional data, are outperformed by ppDRE on MI estimation (e.g., 2.966 vs. 8.71 and 10.24 at p=20, ρ=0.8) and covariate shift (e.g., 0.133 vs. 0.233 and 0.232 on Diamond Prices). The neural network baseline nnDRE, which uses the same loss but replaces PP with a feedforward network, is consistently worse and exhibits high variance (e.g., NMSE 0.806±0.829 on Abalone), while the LightGBM classification approach is competitive at low dimensions but degrades at high dimensions. Overall, ppDRE wins or ties on every reported metric across all four application domains, with the largest margins appearing in high-dimensional settings where the curse of dimensionality most severely affects competing methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper applies the classical projection pursuit framework (Friedman & Tukey, 1974) to density ratio estimation, approximating the density ratio as a product of univariate functions of linear projections estimated iteratively via univariate sieve bases. It departs from D³ methods (Sugiyama et al., 2010; 2011), which assume the density ratio is confined to a single fixed low-dimensional linear subspace, by instead using multiple adaptive projections that collectively approximate the full function. It also contrasts with regularized kernel methods (Que & Belkin, 2013; Nguyen et al., 2024) and neural network DRE (Nam & Sugiyama, 2015; Rhodes et al., 2020), offering a middle ground: more flexible than parametric or single-subspace approaches, yet with the theoretical guarantees (consistency, convergence rates) that neural network methods lack.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method provides a principled, theoretically grounded solution to the curse of dimensionality in DRE without requiring the restrictive single-subspace assumption of D³ or the large-sample demands of neural networks. Consistent empirical gains across causal inference (stabilized weights, dose-response functions), mutual information estimation, and covariate shift adaptation demonstrate broad practical utility. Open questions include extending ppDRE to independence testing (requiring limiting distribution theory under the null), developing adaptive stopping criteria to avoid cross-validation over K, and understanding computational scaling as the number of projections grows.
