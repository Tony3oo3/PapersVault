## Problem

Mean-field models describe collective behavior of indistinguishable particles via infinite-dimensional vector fields on probability measures, but learning these dynamics from finite particle trajectories has lacked both effective architectures and theoretical guarantees. Prior approaches (kernel methods, binning, SINDy) either restrict to density-valued measures or provide no convergence rates. This paper asks whether transformers, which are naturally permutation-equivariant, can approximate mean-field vector fields with quantifiable error bounds that improve as the number of observed particles grows.

## Input / Output

The transformer receives a variable-length sequence of n+1 particle states (one query particle plus n context particles), each a 2d-dimensional vector of position and velocity, and outputs the corresponding 2d-dimensional time derivative for the query particle. In practice the number of particles varies per time step (e.g., ~220 of 300 fish in the milling dataset), and the model is trained to predict centered-difference velocities from observed positions. The theoretical object, the expected transformer T_n(x, μ), takes a single particle state x ∈ Ω and a Borel probability measure μ ∈ P(Ω) and returns a vector in R^d, with the measure consumed via an expectation over the n-fold product measure μ^{⊗n}.

## Method

The core construction is the 'expected transformer': given a standard finite-dimensional transformer T: Ω^{n+1} → R^{(n+1)×d}, the continuum map T_n(x, μ) := E_{z~μ^{⊗n}}[(T([x; z]))_1] lifts T to a map on Ω × P(Ω) → R^d by averaging the first output over all n context particles drawn from μ. Training is standard supervised regression (MSE loss) on particle trajectories, predicting ẋ(t) from z(t). The key theoretical result (Theorem 4.7) bounds the uniform approximation error ||T_n − F||_* by the finite-dimensional error E plus a term L·diam(Ω)^p·(1/(n+1) + C·G(n,p,q)), where G(n,p,q) is the optimal Wasserstein convergence rate from Fournier–Guillin, showing the error vanishes as n grows. A second result (Theorem 4.14) then applies Gronwall's inequality to the flow maps to show the 1-Wasserstein distance between the true and approximate continuity-equation solutions is bounded by εt·exp(2Lt), linking vector-field accuracy to trajectory accuracy over finite time horizons.

## Results

The transformer achieves the lowest mean-squared error (MSE) in approximating the mean-field vector field across both datasets: (1.9±0.3)×10⁻⁶ on Cucker-Smale and (2.2±0.0)×10⁻² on Fish Milling, outperforming all baselines by at least one order of magnitude. In the dynamics simulation, the transformer-driven Cucker-Smale trajectories track the true dynamics with 2-norm error below 10⁻⁴, and the transformer successfully generalizes from 20 training particles to 30 test particles. For the two-layer neural network training dynamics, the maximum Frobenius norm difference between transformer-updated and gradient-descent-updated parameters is at most 5×10⁻⁵ after 500 iterations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The transformer outperforms all baselines on both datasets. Against the Cylindrical NN from Pham & Warin (2023), the transformer achieves roughly 3 orders of magnitude lower MSE on Cucker-Smale (1.9×10⁻⁶ vs. 1.1×10⁻³) and over 10× lower on Fish Milling (2.2×10⁻² vs. 2.7×10⁻¹). Against GraphConv (Morris et al., 2019), the transformer wins by ~2 orders of magnitude on Cucker-Smale (1.9×10⁻⁶ vs. 2.0×10⁻⁴ for m=3) and by ~5× on Fish Milling (2.2×10⁻² vs. 1.2×10⁻¹ for m=3); GraphConv m=20 fails entirely on Fish Milling (MSE > 1). Against TransformerConv (Shi et al., 2021), the transformer achieves lower MSE on both datasets, with the closest competitor being TransformerConv m=20 on Cucker-Smale (3.3×10⁻⁶ vs. 1.9×10⁻⁶) and TransformerConv m=3 on Fish Milling (6.5×10⁻² vs. 2.2×10⁻²). The FNN and Kernel baselines, which lack permutation equivariance, are only evaluated on Cucker-Smale where |ẑ(t)| is constant, and both underperform the transformer by roughly 3–4×.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of two established threads: (1) learning mean-field dynamics from particle trajectories (Pham & Warin 2023, Messenger & Bortz 2022, Feng et al. 2024) and (2) universal approximation of measure-valued maps by transformers (Furuya et al. 2025, Geshkovski et al. 2023). The key departure is the 'expected transformer' construction, which lifts a standard finite-sequence transformer to a map on measures via expectation over product measures, rather than defining attention directly on measures as in Furuya et al. (2025). This provides a practical bridge between off-the-shelf transformer architectures and the infinite-dimensional mean-field setting, and yields quantitative approximation rates (via Wasserstein convergence of empirical measures) that the concurrent continuous-attention approach does not explicitly provide.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first quantitative error rates connecting a finite-dimensional transformer's approximation quality to the infinite-dimensional mean-field vector field, with explicit dependence on the number of training particles n and the regularity parameter p. The exponential error growth bound (via Gronwall's lemma) is a critical caveat: even small vector-field errors amplify over time, limiting the practical simulation horizon. Open questions include whether the Lipschitz-in-Wasserstein assumption is too restrictive for realistic systems, how the rates behave in higher dimensions, and whether the expected-transformer construction can be extended to non-compact domains or time-dependent vector fields. The empirical validation on Cucker-Smale and two-layer NN training dynamics is encouraging but limited to low-dimensional, well-behaved systems.
