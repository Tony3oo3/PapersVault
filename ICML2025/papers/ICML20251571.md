## Problem

Despite POMC achieving the best empirical performance among evolutionary algorithms for subset selection with a linear cost constraint, its known approximation guarantee of (1/2)(1−1/e) ≈ 0.32 lagged well behind practical greedy methods such as 1-guess-Greedy+ (0.6174). This gap raised two questions: whether POMC's theoretical bound was tight or whether a finer analysis could reveal stronger guarantees, and whether a new EA could match the best practical greedy approximation ratio while retaining its empirical advantages.

## Input / Output

The method operates on Boolean vectors x ∈ {0,1}^n representing subsets of a ground set V, where the objective f is monotone submodular and the cost c is linear (a sum of per-item costs). POMC reformulates the constrained problem as a bi-objective maximization in which infeasible solutions (c(x) > B) are excluded by setting f₁ = −∞, and the algorithm maintains a Pareto front of mutually incomparable solutions. EPOL further decomposes the problem into K_B residual subproblems solved independently by POMC, with the final output being the best feasible solution among all candidate combinations {X_v ∪ v}.

## Method

The paper makes two contributions. First, it re-analyzes POMC using a fine-grained lower bound on the marginal gain of adding a specific item to a current solution (Lemma 3.3), combined with a special function z(r) (Lemma 3.4) that characterizes the achievable approximation as 1−z(r), improving POMC's guarantee from (1/2)(1−1/e) to 1/2. Second, it proposes EPOL, which for each item v creates a residual problem (V\v, f(·|v), c, B−c(v)) where f(·|v) = f(·∪v) − f(v), solves each residual problem via POMC, and returns the best feasible solution among {X_v ∪ v : v ∈ V}. The 0.6174 guarantee is proven by splitting on whether the highest-value item o_f in the optimal solution satisfies f(o_f) ≥ (1/3)f(X*): in the first case the 1/2-approximation of POMC on the residual problem suffices, while in the second the (1−z(r′))-approximation from Lemma 3.2 applied to the residual problem yields the 0.6174 bound. EPOL is parallelizable across the K_B residual problems, reducing expected runtime to O(nBP_max/δ) with n processors, matching POMC's complexity.

## Results

EPOL achieves an average rank of 1.0 across all three maximum coverage datasets (frb30-15-1, frb35-17-1, congress) and 1.0–1.6 across all three influence maximization datasets (graph100, graph200, insecta), outperforming all seven baselines. On frb35-17-1 with q=5 and B=500, EPOL covers 445.7 vertices versus 441.5 for POMC, 440.0 for 1-guess-Greedy+, and 424.0 for GGA. Theoretically, POMC's approximation ratio is improved from (1/2)(1−1/e)≈0.32 to 1/2, and EPOL achieves the best-known practical guarantee of 0.6174, matching 1-guess-Greedy+ while using evolutionary search.

## Baselines & Comparisons

EPOL consistently outperforms all seven baselines across both maximum coverage and influence maximization, with an average rank of 1.0 on every maximum coverage dataset and 1.0–1.6 on influence maximization. Against the strongest greedy baseline, 1-guess-Greedy+ (avg rank 2.2–2.6 on MC, 3.6–5.4 on IM), EPOL achieves a 1.3–4.4 vertex improvement on frb35-17-1 (q=5) and a 0.5–1.2 influence spread gain on graph100 (p=0.05). Against POMC (the prior best EA, avg rank 2.4–2.8 on MC, 2.8–3.2 on IM), EPOL improves by 1.5–4.2 vertices on maximum coverage and 0.3–0.7 on influence spread. The other EA baselines (EAMC, FPOMC, EVO-SMC) are clearly outperformed by EPOL on maximum coverage (ranks 4.0–8.0 vs. 1.0) but are more competitive on influence maximization, where EAMC and EVO-SMC occasionally rank 2nd–3rd. Notably, EPOL's advantage over POMC is statistically significant in nearly all settings per the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, and EPOL is never significantly outperformed by any baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on POMC (Qian et al., 2017), a multi-objective evolutionary algorithm that simultaneously maximizes a submodular objective and minimizes a linear cost. The key conceptual move is a fine-grained re-analysis of POMC's approximation guarantee, inspired by the analytical techniques used for Greedy+ and 1-guess-Greedy+ (Feldman et al., 2023), which tightens POMC's bound from (1/2)(1−1/e)≈0.32 to 1/2. The proposed EPOL then wraps POMC in a residual-problem decomposition (for each item v, solve a subproblem on V\{v} with budget B−c(v)), an idea borrowed from 1-guess-Greedy+, but executed through the EA's parallelizable search rather than deterministic enumeration. This positions EPOL as the first EA to match the best-known practical greedy approximation ratio of 0.6174 while retaining the empirical robustness that made POMC the top-performing EA in prior comparisons.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By closing the theoretical gap between EAs and greedy algorithms for budgeted submodular maximization, this work provides stronger justification for using EAs in practice where greedy methods can get trapped in local optima. EPOL's parallelizability (reducing runtime to O(nB·P_max/δ) with n processors) and its consistent empirical superiority across six real-world graph datasets make it a practical tool for maximum coverage and influence maximization. Open questions include whether the 1/2 bound for POMC is tight, whether EPOL's residual-problem decomposition can be extended to other constraint structures (matroids, knapsack with multiple constraints), and whether the gap to the optimal 1−1/e≈0.632 ratio can be further narrowed by EAs. A practical limitation is that EPOL in experiments only solves K_B residual problems (top-K_B items by f-value) rather than all n, trading some theoretical completeness for computational feasibility.
