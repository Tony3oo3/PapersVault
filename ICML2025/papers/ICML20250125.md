## Problem

The PIVOT algorithm for correlation clustering achieves a tight 3-approximation, and all known better approximations require solving large linear programs, making them impractical in dynamic, parallel, or streaming settings. This paper asks whether the 3-approximation barrier can be broken while preserving the same implementation efficiency as PIVOT, e.g., polylogarithmic update time in the fully dynamic model.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250125/cropped_page7_idx4.webp)

## Input / Output

The input is a complete graph on n vertices where each pair is labeled as an edge (similar) or non-edge (dissimilar); the output is a partition of all vertices into clusters minimizing the number of disagreements (edges cut plus non-edges kept together). The algorithm is randomized—pivots are selected uniformly at random—and the approximation guarantee of 2.997 holds in expectation. In the dynamic setting, the algorithm maintains the clustering under edge insertions/deletions in polylogarithmic time per update against an oblivious adversary.

## Method

MODIFIEDPIVOT locally modifies PIVOT's output by, at each pivot v, identifying two sets: D_v (neighbors of v whose neighborhoods differ substantially from v's, moved to singleton clusters) and A_v (non-neighbors of v whose neighborhoods closely match v's cluster, moved into v's cluster). To prevent any single cluster from changing size dramatically, only a δ|C_v|-sized subsample of each set is actually moved. The approximation analysis uses a charging scheme over bad triangles that differs from the original PIVOT analysis in two key ways: charges are assigned fractionally across multiple triangles per mistake, and triangles not involving the current pivot are also charged (non-local charging). This yields a 2.997-approximation, and the same local-move criteria allow the algorithm to be maintained in polylogarithmic time per update by extending the data structures of Behnezhad et al. (FOCS'19).

## Results

Theoretically, MODIFIEDPIVOT achieves a 2.997-approximation ratio for correlation clustering (Theorem 4.1), breaking the long-standing 3-approximation barrier of the PIVOT algorithm. In the fully dynamic setting, the algorithm maintains a 2.99-approximation in polylogarithmic time per update (Theorem 1.1). Empirically, across 8 real-world datasets and Stochastic Block Models, MODIFIEDPIVOT makes less than 77% of the mistakes (disagreements) made by PIVOT on average, with the ratio of MODIFIEDPIVOT-to-PIVOT disagreements consistently below 1 across all tested datasets and parameter settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the PIVOT algorithm of Ailon, Charikar, and Newman (STOC 2005), which achieves a tight 3-approximation. MODIFIEDPIVOT strictly outperforms PIVOT on every dataset tested: the ratio of disagreements (MODIFIEDPIVOT / PIVOT) is below 1 for all 8 real-world graphs (email, Erdos991, Netscience, SmaGri, celegans-neural, celegans-metabolic, Harvard500, polblogs) and for all Stochastic Block Model configurations, with an average ratio below 0.77. The improvement is most pronounced on sparse real-world graphs where PIVOT tends to output small clusters while ignoring vertices with similar neighborhoods, and on dense SBM instances where PIVOT fails to prune cross-block neighbors. Theoretically, MODIFIEDPIVOT improves the approximation guarantee from 3 to 2.997, and in the dynamic setting it is the first algorithm to achieve a better-than-3 approximation in polylogarithmic update time, resolving an open problem from Behnezhad et al. (FOCS 2019).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the PIVOT algorithm of Ailon, Charikar, and Newman (STOC'05), which provides a simple 3-approximation for correlation clustering, and on the dynamic maintenance framework of Behnezhad et al. (FOCS'19) that tracks PIVOT's output in polylogarithmic time per update. While substantially better approximations exist (down to 1.437 by Cao et al., STOC'24), all require solving large linear programs and are infeasible in dynamic, parallel, or streaming settings. MODIFIEDPIVOT departs from prior work by showing that a minimal local modification—moving a small subsample of dissimilar neighbors to singleton clusters and a small subsample of similar non-neighbors into the pivot's cluster—suffices to break the 3-approximation barrier while preserving PIVOT's implementation efficiency. The analysis also departs from the original Ailon et al. charging scheme by introducing fractional charges and non-local bad triangles that do not involve the pivot.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This paper resolves a 5+ year open problem (Open Problem 1 from Behnezhad et al., FOCS'19) by showing that a better-than-3 approximation can be maintained in polylogarithmic time per update in the fully dynamic setting, a result that was previously unknown even for linear update time. The practical significance lies in the fact that MODIFIEDPIVOT is as simple and efficient to implement as PIVOT itself, making it a drop-in replacement in dynamic, MPC, streaming, and distributed settings where LP-based methods are infeasible. The numerical improvement is modest (2.997 vs. 3), and the authors explicitly note they did not optimize the constant; a natural open question is whether the gap can be widened while retaining the same implementation simplicity. Empirically, the algorithm makes less than 77% of PIVOT's mistakes on average, suggesting the practical benefit may be larger than the worst-case guarantee indicates.
