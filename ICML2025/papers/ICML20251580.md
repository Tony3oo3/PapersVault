## Problem

While fast tree-based estimators exist for SHAP values (e.g., TreeSHAP), no comparably efficient algorithm has been proposed for estimating all Partial Dependence (PD) functions, which are needed to recover the full functional decomposition that cleanly separates main effects from higher-order interactions. Moreover, the widely used path-dependent TreeSHAP is shown to be inconsistent when features are correlated, producing SHAP estimates that do not converge to the population value even with infinite background data.

## Input / Output

FastPD takes a pre-trained tree-based model (e.g., an XGBoost ensemble), a background sample of n_b i.i.d. draws from P_X, a set of n_e evaluation points, and an arbitrary feature subset S ⊆ [d]. It outputs the empirical PD function v̂_S(x_S) for every subset S, from which SHAP values, PD plots, and the complete Möbius-inverse functional decomposition {m_S} are extracted at negligible additional cost. The key practical constraint is that the target model must be a decision tree or tree ensemble; for other model classes, a surrogate tree model must first be fitted.

## Method

FastPD exploits the fact that a decision tree is a weighted sum of leaf-indicator functions, decomposing the PD estimator into a leaf-membership indicator (depending only on the evaluation point) and an empirical probability of the complementary features falling within leaf bounds (depending only on background samples). The algorithm separates these into two phases: an augmentation step that recursively traverses the tree, maintaining for each leaf and each subset of split features a list of background observations that would reach that leaf if those splits were ignored, and an evaluation step that, for a query point and feature set, traverses only the relevant leaves and reads off precomputed sample counts. This separation breaks the O(n_b · n_e) product of naive estimation into O(n_b + n_e), yielding linear scaling in the number of observations for moderate tree depth. Because FastPD exactly computes the empirical PD function (8), it inherits its consistency (Proposition 3.2), in contrast to TreeSHAP-path which conditions on the evaluation point's path and is provably inconsistent under correlated features (Proposition 3.1).

## Results

FastPD achieves a complexity of O(2^(D+F)(n_e+n_b)) for estimating all PD functions, reducing the quadratic scaling of VanillaPD (O(R·2^d·n_e·n_b)) and TreeSHAP-int (O(D·2^D·n_e·n_b)) to linear in the number of samples. At n_b=n_e=8000 on a 20-tree XGBoost model (depth 5, d=7), FastPD completes in 0.0899s versus 13,722s for VanillaPD and 1.26s for TreeSHAP-int — a ~150,000× speedup over the naive approach. The paper proves (Proposition 3.1) that TreeSHAP-path is inconsistent under correlated features, and empirically shows its MSE does not shrink with more background samples while FastPD's does. Across 96 OpenML benchmark datasets, the path-dependent Friedman method exhibits relative importance errors exceeding ±30% in 33 datasets, versus only 9 for FastPD with 100 background samples.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against **Friedman (2001)** (path-dependent tree traversal), FastPD is strictly superior: Friedman's method is inconsistent under correlated features (same pathology as TreeSHAP-path), and on 96 benchmark datasets it produces >30% relative importance errors in 33 cases, while FastPD-100 reduces this to 9. On the Adult dataset, Friedman-path estimates a zero age×relationship interaction effect, whereas FastPD correctly identifies a positive effect for husbands at prime working age. Against **TreeSHAP-path** (Lundberg et al., 2020), FastPD wins on consistency: Proposition 3.1 proves TreeSHAP-path can assign opposite-sign SHAP values to two functionally identical trees under correlated features, and Figure 3 shows its MSE plateaus while FastPD's decreases with more background samples. Against **TreeSHAP-int**, FastPD matches or beats it on runtime (0.0899s vs 1.26s at n=8000) while additionally providing full PD functions from which the complete functional decomposition can be extracted at no extra cost. Against **Zern et al. (2023)**, FastPD has comparable runtime (0.0899s vs 1.26s at n=8000, though Zern is slightly faster at n=1000: 0.161s vs 0.0157s) but uniquely estimates all PD functions rather than only SHAP differences, enabling functional decomposition and higher-order interaction extraction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the functional decomposition framework of Hiabu et al. (2023), which unifies PD plots, SHAP values, and higher-order interaction effects under a single Möbius-inverse representation of PD functions. It departs from the widely-used path-dependent TreeSHAP (Lundberg et al., 2020) by proving its inconsistency under correlated features, and extends the interventional approach of Zern et al. (2023) by recovering full PD functions (not just SHAP differences) at comparable complexity. The key algorithmic insight—separating a one-time tree augmentation from per-point evaluation to break the n_b × n_e product into a sum—generalizes the tree-traversal trick of Friedman (2001) into a consistent estimator for arbitrary feature subsets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant because TreeSHAP-path is the default explanation method in XGBoost and LightGBM, and the paper demonstrates (both theoretically and empirically) that it can produce qualitatively wrong attributions—opposite signs for identical predictions—whenever features are correlated. FastPD enables the full functional decomposition (main effects, pairwise and higher-order interactions) at essentially the same cost as computing SHAP alone, giving practitioners a more complete and interpretable picture. Limitations include applicability only to tree-based models, space complexity growing with tree depth, and the need for sufficient background samples (~100+) for accurate estimates. Open questions include how to handle extrapolation outside the training support for ground-truth PD functions and whether the augmentation step can be made memory-efficient for very deep trees.
