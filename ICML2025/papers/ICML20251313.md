## Problem

Combinatorial reinforcement learning with preference feedback—where an agent selects a subset of items and receives MNL choice feedback—lacks any theoretical regret guarantees. Existing MNL bandit theory assumes known item values and single-step (myopic) decisions, while empirical combinatorial RL work (e.g., SlateQ) provides no statistical guarantees. The core challenges are: estimating unknown long-term item values, ensuring optimism in an exponentially large assortment space, and achieving regret tighter than naively summing H independent MNL bandit regrets.

## Input / Output

At each step h of an H-step episode, the agent observes a user state s (encoding demographics, interests, and interaction history) and selects an assortment A ⊆ I of at most M items (always including the outside option a₀). The user's choice a ~ P_h(·|s,A) follows a contextual MNL model with linear mean utilities φ(s,a)ᵀθ*, and the system then transitions to s' ~ P_h(·|s,a) with reward r_h(s,a,s'). The algorithm outputs the selected assortment per step and, theoretically, a regret upper bound of Õ(d√(HK) + √(dim(F)·KH·log|F|)) where d is the MNL feature dimension and dim(F) is the generalized Eluder dimension of the item-value function class.

## Method

MNL-VQL combines three key mechanisms. First, it estimates MNL parameters via online mirror descent with constant O(Md³) per-step cost, yielding a confidence interval C_h^k. Second, it estimates item-level Q-values using variance-weighted regression under general function approximation with a consistent bonus oracle (implemented via online sensitivity sub-sampling). The central novelty is the optimistic assortment selection: because item values are unknown, simply inflating MNL utilities can *decrease* expected value (e.g., if the outside option's estimated value is highest). MNL-VQL resolves this by switching between optimistic and pessimistic MNL utilities based on whether any item's estimated Q-value exceeds the outside option's, then solving the resulting assortment optimization as a linear program (via Charnes-Cooper transformation) in polynomial time. Finally, instead of summing H MNL bandit regrets, the analysis bounds MNL regret via the sum of value-function variances and the law of total variance, reducing the MNL component by a factor of √H and yielding a nearly minimax-optimal Õ(d√(HK) + d^lin√(HK)) bound for linear MDPs, matched by a novel lower bound.

## Results

Theoretically, MNL-VQL achieves a regret upper bound of Õ(d√(HK) + √(dim(F)KH log|F|)) under general function approximation (Theorem 5.1), and for linear MDPs, a nearly minimax-optimal regret of Õ(d√(HK) + d^lin√(HK)) (Theorem 5.2), matching a novel lower bound of Ω(d√(HK) + d^lin√(HK)) (Theorem 5.3). Empirically, on both a synthetic budget-based shopping environment and the MovieLens dataset (K=10,000 episodes, H=3, M=4, N∈{50,100,200}), MNL-VQL substantially outperforms baselines in episodic return, while the Myopic baseline converges to a suboptimal solution. The algorithm also runs significantly faster than LSVI-UCB, which becomes intractable as the number of items N grows (LSVI-UCB could not be run for N=20 or N=40 in the synthetic setting).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares MNL-VQL against two baselines: (1) Myopic, a variant of OFU-MNL+ (Lee & Oh, 2024) adapted for unknown rewards that selects assortments based only on immediate rewards, and (2) LSVI-UCB (Jin et al., 2020), which treats each assortment as a single atomic action and requires enumeration of all possible assortments. On both the synthetic environment and the MovieLens dataset, MNL-VQL substantially outperforms both baselines in cumulative episodic return. The Myopic baseline converges to a clearly suboptimal policy, demonstrating the importance of accounting for long-term state transitions rather than optimizing myopically. LSVI-UCB is computationally intractable at scale: due to its exponential enumeration over the assortment space (|A| = 1,333,500 for N=200, M=4), it could not be run for larger N values in the synthetic experiment and produced only partial results on MovieLens. MNL-VQL avoids this by reformulating assortment selection as a linear program (via the Charnes-Cooper transformation), making it polynomial in |I| and thus scalable to larger item sets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate research threads: MNL bandits (which handle combinatorial assortment selection with preference feedback but assume known item values and no state transitions) and RL with general function approximation (which handles multi-step decision-making but typically assumes single actions). The structural decomposition of the Q-function into a preference model and item-level values is inspired by SlateQ (Ie et al., 2019), while the variance-weighted regression and generalized Eluder dimension machinery follows Agarwal et al. (2023) and Zhao et al. (2023). The key departure from MNL bandits is the introduction of unknown long-term item values and state transitions, which fundamentally changes how optimism must be ensured in the combinatorial action space—simply inflating MNL utilities no longer guarantees an optimistic Q-value when the outside option's estimated value may exceed that of actual items.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to provide any theoretical regret guarantee for combinatorial RL with preference feedback, a setting that directly models recommender systems and online advertising where multiple items are presented simultaneously and user engagement evolves over time. The nearly minimax-optimal result for linear MDPs establishes a clear benchmark for future work in this space. Open questions include whether the completeness assumption (requiring the function class to contain both the Bellman operator and its second-moment version) can be weakened, whether the approach extends to other choice models (e.g., nested logit, mixed logit), and how the algorithm scales when the item set N is very large. The practical experiments, while confirming theoretical predictions, are conducted at relatively small scales (N ≤ 200, H = 3), leaving open the question of performance in industrial-scale settings.
