## Problem

Graph Neural Networks rely on message passing that propagates the influence of manipulated nodes or edges across neighborhoods, making them uniquely vulnerable to adversarial data poisoning. Existing graph unlearning methods consistently fail to recover model performance even when the complete set of manipulated entities is known, and the non-i.i.d. structure of graph data means that simply removing identified samples is insufficient to undo the propagated effects on neighboring nodes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251226/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a trained GNN model M, the manipulated graph G', and a known representative subset S_f of the manipulated entities (as small as 5% of the full manipulation set S_m), and produces an unlearned GNN M* that recovers accuracy on the affected distribution while preserving performance on the remaining data. The method handles both node-level manipulations (label flips) and edge-level manipulations (spurious edge additions), with the graph topology preserved during unlearning so that message passing continues to function over the full structure.

## Method

Cognac alternates between two complementary components that address the two distinct adverse effects of manipulation. CoGN (Contrastive unlearning on Graph Neighborhoods) identifies affected neighbors via a logit-change heuristic—flipping features of deletion-set nodes and selecting the top-k% neighbors with the largest output logit shifts—then optimizes a sigmoid-based contrastive loss that pushes affected node embeddings away from deletion-set nodes while anchoring them to unaffected neighbors. AC DC (Ascent Descent decoupled) performs gradient ascent on the task loss over the deletion set to unlearn incorrect label associations, while simultaneously performing gradient descent on the remaining data using separate optimizer instances with independently tuned learning rates. Theoretical analysis shows that interclass confusion attacks reduce the Wasserstein-2 distance between class embeddings (Theorem 3.1), and that optimizing the contrastive loss guarantees monotonic improvement of the positive-negative embedding separation (Theorem 3.3).

## Results

Cognac achieves state-of-the-art corrective unlearning across 10 benchmark datasets, recovering most of the Oracle's accuracy on the affected distribution (Acc_aff) even when only 5% of the manipulated set is identified for deletion. On the large-scale OGB-Arxiv dataset (170K nodes, 1.1M edges), Cognac outperforms retraining from scratch by more than 10% while all baseline methods fail to improve over the original poisoned model. The method is 8x more efficient than retraining and maintains Acc_rem within ~1-2% of the original model, whereas GNNDelete suffers drops of up to 10.9% on Cora. Ablations confirm both components are necessary: CoGN alone does not improve Acc_aff over the original model, while AC DC alone reaches only 67.2% vs. Cognac's 79.3% on CS (Oracle: 90.1%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

All five graph-specific and i.i.d. baselines fail to recover Acc_aff even when the full manipulation set is known (S_f/S_m = 1.0). GNNDelete causes the largest utility degradation (up to -10.9% Acc_rem on Cora edge unlearning) while barely improving affected-class accuracy. UtU (zero-cost edge unlinking) provides no unlearning benefit at all for either attack type. MEGU and GIF, despite being designed for adversarial edge removal, fail on targeted binary class confusion attacks, likely because their KL-divergence regularization inadvertently reinforces unidentified manipulated entities. Surprisingly, the non-graph-specific baselines AC ½DC and naive Finetune outperform all graph-specific methods, suggesting that existing graph unlearning methods are poorly suited to corrective (as opposed to privacy-oriented) unlearning. Cognac consistently beats Retrain across all datasets and fractions, and occasionally surpasses the Oracle (trained on fully clean data) on multiple settings, while maintaining Acc_rem within 1-2% of the original model.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the corrective unlearning framework of Goel et al. (2024), originally formulated for i.i.d. classification, to the graph-structured, non-i.i.d. setting of GNNs, where message passing propagates manipulation effects across neighborhoods. The AC DC component adapts the ascent/descent unlearning strategy from Kurmanji et al. (2023) (SCRUB) to graphs, while the CoGN component introduces a novel contrastive loss specifically designed to counteract the neighborhood-level influence propagation unique to GNNs. The paper departs from prior graph unlearning work (GNNDelete, GIF, MEGU, UtU) by showing these methods were designed for privacy-oriented or untargeted deletion and fundamentally fail at corrective unlearning of targeted manipulations, even with full knowledge of the manipulation set.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that all existing graph unlearning methods fail at corrective unlearning—even with complete knowledge of the manipulation set—challenges the assumption that these methods are broadly applicable and redirects the field toward methods that explicitly handle partial identification of corrupted data. Cognac's ability to recover most oracle performance with only 5% of the manipulation set identified is practically significant for real-world settings where full corruption detection is infeasible. Key limitations include reliance on the homophily assumption (evaluated only on homophilic datasets) and the restriction to node classification, leaving open the question of whether the approach generalizes to heterophilic graphs, link prediction, or other GNN tasks.
