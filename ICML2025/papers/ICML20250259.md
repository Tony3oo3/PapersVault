## Problem

Existing evaluations of human and AI workers conflate reasoning (decision-making) with execution (action) skills, use non-standardized metrics, and lack a unified mathematical framework to quantify when GenAI complements versus replaces human labor. This paper asks: under what precise conditions does combining a human's decision-level strength with an AI's action-level strength produce a sharp, superadditive gain in job success probability, and can this formally explain the empirically observed productivity compression effect?

## Input / Output

The framework consumes a bipartite task-skill dependency graph (e.g., 17 tasks × 18 skills for Computer Programmers from O*NET), per-skill difficulty scores s_j ∈ [0,1], a decision-level degree λ_j ∈ [0,1] per skill (estimated via GPT-4o), and worker ability profiles α_ℓ(s) parameterized by a mean-ability scalar (e.g., slope a for linear profiles) and a noise level σ. Outputs are the job success probability P = Pr[Err(ζ) ≤ τ], the phase-transition width γ₁ governing how sharply P jumps with ability, the productivity compression measure PC = |P₂−P₁| − |P₂′−P₁′|, and the merging gain Δ = P_merge − max{P₁, P₂}. A notable design choice is that each skill's single difficulty score s_j is split into two subskill difficulties s_{j1} = λ_j·s_j and s_{j2} = (1−λ_j)·s_j, and the skill-level error function h is set to the sum (ζ_{j1}+ζ_{j2}) so that the original skill-level distribution can be approximately reconstructed.

## Method

The core mechanism is a hierarchical probabilistic model: each skill j is decomposed into a decision-level subskill and an action-level subskill, each governed by an independent ability distribution α_ℓ(s_{jℓ}) (linear, polynomial, or constant mean plus bounded uniform or truncated-normal noise). Error rates ζ_{jℓ} = 1−X are aggregated upward through monotonic functions h (skill), g (task), and f (job) into a single job error Err(ζ), and success is defined as Err(ζ) ≤ τ. The key theoretical tool is a concentration inequality (a refinement of McDiarmid's inequality for subgaussian inputs) that bounds the probability that Err deviates from its expectation, yielding a sharp phase-transition theorem: P jumps from ≤θ to ≥1−θ within a window γ₁ = L·√(MaxDisp·ln(1/θ))/MinDer_{μ₁}, where L is the Lipschitz constant of Err and MinDer captures sensitivity to decision-level ability. A second theorem shows that merging two workers with complementary subskill strengths (one strong in decision, the other in action) yields a gain of at least 1−2θ when their ability gap exceeds the sum of their respective transition widths. The framework is instantiated empirically by extracting job structure from O*NET, fitting ability profiles to Big-bench Lite human and PaLM scores, and using GPT-4o to estimate decision-level degrees and task-skill mappings, then validating phase transitions and merging gains under both independent and dependent (correlated via a latent status variable β) noise settings.

## Results

The framework's headline quantitative findings are: (1) a mere 4.3% increase in decision-level ability (a₁ from 0.492 to 0.513) at noise level σ=0.1 raises job success probability P from 0.2 to 0.8, confirming the sharp phase transition predicted by Theorem 3.2; (2) merging two complementary workers (one strong in decision-level, one in action-level) yields P₁₂ = 1 versus P₁ = P₂ = 0.4, a superadditive gain of 0.6 (Theorem 3.3); (3) under dependent subskills (correlation parameter p=0.4), a 0.44 increase in ability is needed to move P from 0.2 to 0.8, compared to only 0.27 for independent subskills (p=0); (4) when workers have distinct ability profile families, P_merge = 1 while P₁, P₂ ≤ 0.6, confirming a gain of at least 0.4 even without identical profile structures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical framework paper without direct method-to-method baselines; the 'comparisons' are between different configurations of the same model. The key comparison is single-worker vs. merged-worker performance: merging complementary workers (P₁₂) consistently outperforms either worker alone (P₁ or P₂) by 0.4–0.6 in success probability, with the gap widening as noise σ decreases. A second comparison examines independent vs. dependent subskills: phase transitions persist under dependence but the transition window widens (0.27 → 0.44 in ability parameter), showing that reducing skill interdependencies sharpens the benefit of ability improvements. A third comparison tests identical vs. distinct ability profile families: the merging gain persists (≥0.4) even when workers have non-monotonically ordered abilities, though the bright region in the gain heatmap is narrower than in the identical-profile case. The framework's predictions are validated against empirical findings from Brynjolfsson et al. (2023) on productivity compression (AI assistance raising lower-skilled workers' RPH by up to 36%) and Noy & Zhang (2023), providing a theoretical explanation for the observed narrowing of the productivity gap.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing a mathematical framework for worker-job fit that decomposes skills into decision-level and action-level subskills, drawing on cognitive science distinctions (Licklider, 1960; Kahneman, 2011) and the O*NET task-skill dependency structure (Arora & Goyal, 2023; Okawa et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2024). It repurposes concentration inequalities (McDiarmid's inequality via Kontorovich, 2014) in a novel application domain to prove phase transitions in job success probability, a technique not previously applied to workforce modeling. The paper explicitly notes that Acemoglu's (2025) macroeconomic AI-labor model was developed independently but shares structural assumptions (task decomposition, differential AI performance), while this work focuses on job-level success rather than aggregate market outcomes. Empirically, it builds on the productivity compression observations of Brynjolfsson et al. (2023) and Noy & Zhang (2023) by providing the first formal theoretical explanation for those findings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is one of the first formal mathematical models explaining productivity compression under realistic assumptions, providing organizations with quantitative conditions for when human-AI merging yields superadditive gains versus when a single worker suffices. The phase transition result has practical implications: it shows that targeted upskilling in decision-level skills or reducing performance noise can produce disproportionately large jumps in job success, guiding investment in training and workflow design. Key limitations include the focus on job success probability alone (excluding efficiency, time, and cost dimensions), the reliance on static O*NET data that may not capture dynamic or emerging digital tasks, and the use of GPT-4o to estimate subskill divisions, which introduces modeling bias. Open questions include extending the framework to dependent noise settings with tighter bounds, incorporating behavioral and temporal dynamics, and validating against richer empirical benchmarks beyond Big-bench Lite.
