## Problem

Despite the widespread adoption of external slow-thinking methods (test-time scaling) for LLM reasoning, the mechanisms underlying their effectiveness remain poorly understood, hindering the design of more efficient strategies. This paper asks: how do cumulative reasoning errors (snowball errors) theoretically connect to the probability of correct reasoning, and how do different slow-thinking frameworks—ranging from Best-of-N to MCTS—relate to this probability?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250700/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a text question and produces a step-by-step reasoning response, modeled as a sequence of implicit thoughts t mapped to observed responses r. The key analytical quantity is the mutual information I(t;r) between implicit and observed sequences, estimated via HSIC using a 70B rewriter to generate golden-answer approximations. Evaluation spans GSM8k, PrOntoQA, MATH-500, and Game24 using Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen2.5-7B/14B/32B, and Skywork-o1-8B models.

## Method

The paper defines snowball errors as cumulative information loss H(t|r) across reasoning steps and uses Fano's inequality to derive a lower bound on the per-step reasoning error probability, showing it grows with path length. It then hypothesizes an exponential decay in per-step correctness, yielding an exponential decay in the probability of a fully correct L-step response. External slow-thinking methods are formalized as width-expansion strategies that factor the overall success probability into a generation component (scaled by sampling width k) and a selection component (governed by reward-model reliability ε_b). The paper derives closed-form upper bounds for BoN and MCTS (best and worst cases), proving that the minimal N for BoN to match MCTS is O(b) in the best case and O(b^{L/2}) in the worst case, with asymptotically comparable total reasoning costs. Empirical results on GSM8k and PrOntoQA confirm that BoN with N in the cost-matched range achieves accuracy on par with or exceeding MCTS, supporting the conclusion that total reasoning cost and reward-function quality matter more than the specific search framework.

## Results

On Game24, the baseline MCTS achieves 64.80% accuracy, while BoN with ORM-Max selection reaches 68.23% at N=20 and BoN with ORM-Vote reaches 67.68% at N=20, both exceeding MCTS within the comparable-cost range (Ñ_res=6.08 to Ñ_call=18.24). On GSM8k and PrOntoQA (Figure 3), BoN with ORM-guided selection matches or slightly surpasses MCTS accuracy when N falls between the aligned reasoning-step and LLM-call cost thresholds. The snowball error verification on GSM8k confirms that mutual information between responses and golden answers decays nearly exponentially with response length across Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen2.5-7B, and Skywork-o1-8B models, validating the theoretical prediction that information loss accumulates faster than linearly.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares two external slow-thinking strategies: Best-of-N (BoN, from Brown et al., 2024) and Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS, from Feng et al., 2023). The central finding is that when total reasoning cost is matched, BoN achieves comparable or slightly better accuracy than MCTS across all three evaluated tasks (GSM8k, PrOntoQA, Game24). Specifically, on Game24, BoN with ORM-Max at N=20 (68.23%) outperforms MCTS (64.80%) by 3.4 points, while on GSM8k and PrOntoQA the gap is smaller and direction depends on the exact N within the comparable-cost window. The paper's theoretical analysis (Corollaries 5.4–5.5) shows that the minimal N for BoN to match MCTS is O(b) in the best case and O(b^{L/2}) in the worst case, implying that the elaborate tree-search structure of MCTS does not provide a fundamental advantage over simple parallel sampling plus reward-model selection. The key differentiator is the reliability of the reward/value function (captured by ε_b), not the search framework itself.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the information-theoretic analysis of LLM reasoning initiated by Ton et al. (2024) and the token-level snowball error concept from Bachmann & Nagarajan (2024) to the sentence/reasoning-step level, formalizing how cumulative information loss across reasoning steps bounds the probability of reasoning errors via Fano's inequality. Rather than proposing a new slow-thinking method, it provides a unifying theoretical lens under which diverse external slow-thinking approaches (BoN, CoT-SC, ToT, MCTS) are all interpreted as width-expansion-plus-selection strategies. The key departure from prior work is the demonstration that the specific search framework (tree vs. flat sampling) is secondary to total reasoning cost and reward function reliability, reframing a field that has largely treated these as distinct methodological choices.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers the first systematic information-theoretic account of why external slow-thinking works, giving practitioners a principled basis for allocating inference compute rather than relying on ad-hoc hyperparameter tuning. Its central practical implication—that a simple Best-of-N can match MCTS at comparable cost—suggests significant computational savings for teams currently investing in complex tree-search infrastructure. However, the analysis relies on a simplified exponential decay model (Proposition 4.3), an idealized reward model assumption for the BoN-vs-MCTS comparison, and empirical validation on relatively small models (7B–8B) and straightforward reasoning tasks (GSM8k, PrOntoQA, Game24), leaving open whether the conclusions hold for frontier-scale models on harder, multi-domain reasoning. The paper also points toward where future gains are most likely: improving reward function reliability and base-model reasoning capacity, rather than designing more elaborate search topologies.
