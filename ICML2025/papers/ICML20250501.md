## Problem

Existing Generalized Category Discovery (GCD) methods are limited to non-graph data and lack theoretical foundations to answer when and how known classes help discover novel ones. The paper introduces the Graph GCD (GGCD) task and provides the first rigorous theoretical analysis of parametric GCD, revealing that popular graph contrastive learning methods inherently violate the embedding-space conditions required for effective GCD.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250501/cropped_page4_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates in a transductive setting on an attributed graph G=(A, X) where all edges and node features are accessible during training, but only labels of nodes from C_o known old classes are provided. At test time, the model must simultaneously classify old-class nodes to their known labels and cluster new-class nodes into distinct novel categories, with performance measured by Old RACC, New RACC, Reject ACC, and a combined HRScore.

## Method

The paper's core theoretical contribution quantifies the old-and-new class relationship via the Wasserstein distance W between joint (embedding, label) distributions, deriving the first provable GCD loss upper bound (Theorem 3.5) that identifies two necessary conditions: a sufficiently small W (aligning embedding distances with semantic relations) and limited old-new overlap. A PMRF analysis (Theorem 4.3) then proves that standard InfoNCE/SupCon GCL losses leave category embedding relations random and uncontrolled, violating these conditions. To address this, SWIRL (Semantic-aWare dIlation contRastive Learning) uses SS-KM to obtain K prototypes from augmented embeddings, then applies six differentiated repulsion force levels between node-prototype pairs based on whether each involves old or new class prototypes, explicitly controlling inter-category distances in the embedding space. The total training loss combines InfoNCE (for local structure), the SWIRL instance-to-prototype contrastive loss (for global category relations), Entropy Regularization (to prevent old-class bias), and Cross-Entropy on labeled nodes.

## Results

On the five real-world graph datasets, SWIRL achieves the highest HRScore (harmonic mean of Old RACC and New RACC) on four out of five: Cora (64.53%), Wiki (52.93%), A-Photo (77.62%), and A-Computers (61.46%), while trailing Vanilla GCD on Citeseer (55.62% vs. 65.35%). On the largest dataset A-Computers, SWIRL dominates all baselines across every metric, reaching 85.34% Old RACC, 48.32% New RACC, and 88.38% Reject ACC. On synthetic CSBM graphs, SWIRL-GCN achieves 96.24% HRScore on D₁ and 94.03% on D₃, substantially outperforming SimGCD (68.22% and 64.76% respectively).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SWIRL outperforms SimGCD (the strongest parametric GCD baseline) on HRScore across all five real-world datasets, with the largest margin on A-Computers (61.46% vs. 32.38%) and A-Photo (77.62% vs. 67.13%). Against the non-parametric Vanilla GCD, SWIRL wins on HRScore for Cora, Wiki, A-Photo, and A-Computers but trails on Citeseer (55.62% vs. 65.35%), where Vanilla GCD's SS-KM classifier excels at Old RACC (65.23% vs. 55.95%). Compared to UNO+, SWIRL achieves higher HRScore on all five datasets, with particularly large gains on A-Computers (61.46% vs. 49.68%) and Cora (64.53% vs. 30.47%). On the synthetic CSBM graphs, SWIRL-GCN achieves 96.24% HRScore on D₁ and 94.03% on D₃, beating SimGCD by 28 and 29 points respectively, while SimGCD-GPR (replacing GCN with GPR to reduce smoothing) narrows the gap to 10.5 and 5.3 points. The paper notes that SWIRL's advantage is most pronounced on larger, more complex graphs (A-Computers, A-Photo) where category embedding relations matter most.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Visual GCD line of work (Vaze et al., 2022; Wen et al., 2023) to graph data by introducing the GGCD task, adapting VGCD baselines (Vanilla GCD, SimGCD, UNO+) via the GRACE graph contrastive learning framework. Its most significant departure is theoretical: it provides the first provable GCD loss upper bound parameterized by the Wasserstein distance between old- and new-class joint distributions, and uses a Pairwise Markov Random Field analysis to show that standard GCL objectives (InfoNCE, SupCon) render global category embedding relations uncontrollable. The proposed SWIRL method builds directly on SimGCD's parametric framework (ER + CE + self-distillation) but replaces the GCL module with a prototype-based contrastive loss that applies differentiated repulsion forces to enforce the embedding conditions identified by the theory.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The PMRF result that GCL optimization makes the variance of global category-center positions infinite is a genuinely important conceptual finding: it explains *why* parametric GCD methods struggle on graphs beyond ad-hoc heuristics, and suggests that any future GCD method must explicitly constrain inter-class geometry. However, the empirical validation is limited to five relatively small citation/product graphs and two synthetic CSBM graphs, and SWIRL does not uniformly dominate all baselines (e.g., it trails Vanilla GCD on Citeseer HRScore). Open questions include whether the Wasserstein-based theory and SWIRL's differentiated repulsion strategy transfer to larger-scale or inductive graph settings, and whether the GCN smoothing issue identified here interacts with deeper or heterogeneous GNN architectures in ways not captured by the current analysis.
