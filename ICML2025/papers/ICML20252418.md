## Problem

Causal axioms and rules (e.g., transitivity, d-separation) are typically incorporated into ML systems as inductive biases or inferred from data, but it remains unclear whether a model can learn causal reasoning directly from symbolic demonstrations of these axioms. The key open question is whether a model trained on simple axiom instances over small causal graphs can generalize to substantially more complex structures (longer chains, branching, reversed edges) without ever seeing them during training.

## Input / Output

The method consumes structured natural-language triplets of the form <premise, hypothesis, conclusion>, where the premise lists directed causal edges (e.g., 'X causes Y. Z causes Y.'), the hypothesis poses a causal question (e.g., 'Does X cause Z?' for transitivity, or 'Are X and Y d-separated given {Z}?' for d-separation), and the conclusion is a binary Yes/No label. Node names are short alphanumeric strings (1–3 characters in training, up to 10 in evaluation), and a custom tokenizer treats node names at the character level while keeping causal keywords as whole tokens, yielding a vocabulary of only 69 tokens. For d-separation, the hypothesis additionally specifies a conditioning set of up to 5 nodes drawn from the graph.

## Method

The core contribution is axiomatic training: generating ~175k synthetic <premise, hypothesis, conclusion> triplets by sampling causal DAGs (chains of 3–6 nodes) and exhaustively enumerating valid axiom applications, then training a 67M-parameter GPT-2-based transformer from scratch using a label-conditional loss −log P(L|P,H) rather than next-token prediction. A critical design choice is structured perturbation of the training data along three axes—random alphanumeric node names, random edge-direction flipping (introducing forks and colliders), and varying chain lengths—which the paper shows is essential for generalization: models trained only on clean sequential chains fail on branching or reversed graphs, while those with edge-flipping perturbations generalize across all OOD settings. Positional encoding ablations reveal that RoPE and no positional encoding (NoPE) outperform sinusoidal and learnable encodings for causal generalization. The same axiomatic dataset is also used to LoRA fine-tune Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, enabling zero-shot evaluation on external benchmarks (Corr2Cause, CLEAR) where the finetuned model surpasses GPT-4 on several causal reasoning tasks.

## Results

On the Corr2Cause benchmark, axiomatic finetuning of Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct on transitivity data yields an F1 of 0.32, a ~21 percentage-point improvement over the base model (F1=0.11) and surpassing GPT-4 (F1=0.29). On the CLEAR d-separation task, finetuning improves Yes/No accuracy from 60% to 70% and Multi-Choice accuracy from 33% to 50%, both exceeding GPT-4 (63.33% and 36.67% respectively). On synthetic d-separation with branching graphs, the finetuned Llama model reaches 79.6% accuracy at 5 nodes versus 53% for GPT-4, while the 67M model trained from scratch with RoPE achieves 70% at 5 nodes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GPT-4, the axiomatically finetuned Llama-3.1-8B wins on Corr2Cause (F1 0.32 vs 0.29), CLEAR d-separation Yes/No (70% vs 63.33%), CLEAR d-separation Multi-Choice (50% vs 36.67%), and synthetic d-separation with branching (79.6% vs 53% at 5 nodes). However, GPT-4 retains a clear advantage on the transitivity axiom tasks, achieving 0.95–1.00 accuracy on MultiEval_SLR and branching graphs versus 0.76–0.88 for the best 67M model (TS2 w RoPE). Against Gemini Pro and Phi-3, the axiomatically trained 67M model consistently outperforms both across all transitivity evaluation settings (length, reversal, branching, node-name shift), often by 10–20 percentage points. Compared to the unfinetuned Llama-3-8B-Instruct base, axiomatic finetuning yields gains of 10 pp (CLEAR YN), 17 pp (CLEAR MC), and 21 pp (Corr2Cause F1), demonstrating that the synthetic axiomatic data transfers effectively to natural-language causal reasoning without any task-specific finetuning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the synthetic-data-for-reasoning paradigm (Gunasekar et al., 2023; Morishita et al., 2024) to causal inference, but departs from prior causal ML where axioms serve as inductive biases (regularization, architecture constraints) by instead treating them as training data in symbolic <premise, hypothesis, conclusion> form. It also builds on the positional-encoding literature for length generalization (Kazemnejad et al., 2023; Haviv et al., 2022), extending those findings to causal graph reasoning and showing RoPE and NoPE outperform sinusoidal/learnable encodings for causal generalization. Unlike prior LLM causal-reasoning evaluations (Jin et al., 2024a; Kiciman et al., 2023) that test pre-trained models zero-shot, this work introduces a training procedure that explicitly teaches axioms, and validates it both from scratch (67M GPT-2-based model) and via LoRA fine-tuning of Llama-3-8B.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The core practical implication is that causal reasoning capability can be injected into LLMs cheaply via synthetic axiomatic data without access to real causal datasets, yielding ~20pp F1 gains on Corr2Cause and substantial CLEAR improvements that surpass GPT-4. The finding that a 67M model trained on 3–6 node chains generalizes to 15-node branched graphs challenges the assumption that causal reasoning requires scale or real-world data. Open questions include whether the approach extends beyond the two axioms tested (transitivity, d-separation) to richer causal formalisms (do-calculus, counterfactuals), whether it handles unobserved confounders, and whether axiomatic pretraining (rather than fine-tuning) could provide a stronger inductive foundation for RL-based alignment.
