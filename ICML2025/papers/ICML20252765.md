## Problem

Strategic language games like Werewolf require both high-level strategic decision-making and free-form conversational ability, but existing LLM-based agents suffer from intrinsic bias in their action distributions (inherited from pretraining data) and limited exploration of the unbounded text action space. Classic game-theoretic solvers like CFR cannot be directly applied to free-form utterances, while prompt-based LLM agents remain constrained by the base model's capabilities and predictable biases that opponents can exploit.

## Input / Output

The agent receives a language observation — a structured list of natural language statements logging the full game history up to the current step, including private information (own role, secret night actions, Werewolf teammate) and public information (eliminations, discussions, voting results) — along with the current round/phase indicator. Outputs are three types of actions: discrete secret actions (kill/see/save a specific player), free-form natural language discussion statements (the primary strategic communication channel), and discrete voting decisions (vote for a player or abstain). The discussion actions are the only truly open-ended component and are the focus of the latent space abstraction.

## Method

LSPO is an iterative framework with three components that alternate to progressively strengthen the agent. First, latent space construction: the current LLM self-plays as each role across many trajectories, generating N strategically distinct discussion candidates per turn; these utterances are embedded via text-embedding-3-small and clustered with k-means into k discrete latent strategies, yielding a finite abstracted extensive-form game. Second, policy optimization: Deep CFR is applied to this abstracted game to learn near-optimal mixed strategies for each role, overcoming the intrinsic bias of the raw LLM output distribution. Third, latent space expansion: the LLM is fine-tuned via DPO using preference pairs derived from the CFR regret values (lower-regret discussion candidates are preferred), then the fine-tuned LLM regenerates and re-clusters to expand the latent space with one additional cluster per iteration. The key insight is that while the language space is combinatorially infinite, the underlying strategic intent space is relatively compact and can be iteratively uncovered, with convergence observed empirically within five iterations.

## Results

In the 7-player Werewolf game, the final-iteration LSPO agent (Llama-3-8B-Instruct) achieves a 0.73 win rate as the Werewolf side and 0.27 as the Village side, yielding an overall win rate of 0.50. Performance improves monotonically across iterations: the Werewolf-side win rate rises from 0.54 (Iter.1) to 0.63 (Iter.2) to 0.73 (Iter.3), while the Village-side win rate increases from 0.18 to 0.23 to 0.27. Prediction accuracy also improves steadily, with the overall accuracy for the Werewolf side rising from 0.74 to 0.83 and the Village side from 0.60 to 0.73 over three iterations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LSPO outperforms all four baselines on every metric. Against the prompt-based methods ReAct (0.58/0.16) and ReCon (0.60/0.16), LSPO gains +0.15 and +0.13 win rate as Werewolf respectively, with even larger margins on the Village side (+0.11 for both), reflecting that game-theoretic policy learning overcomes the intrinsic bias that limits pure prompting. The Cicero-like agent (0.66/0.21), which uses a fixed set of 13 atomic actions with an RL policy, is outperformed by LSPO by +0.07 (Werewolf) and +0.06 (Village), showing the advantage of an expanding latent strategy space over a predefined action set. The closest competitor is SLA (0.69/0.25), which combines LLM candidate generation with RL to mitigate bias; LSPO still leads by +0.04 (Werewolf) and +0.02 (Village), attributed to LSPO's iterative latent space expansion enabling broader strategy exploration beyond what a fixed LLM can propose.

## Relation to Prior Work

LSPO sits at the intersection of two research threads: game-theoretic solvers for imperfect-information games (CFR/Deep CFR, originally developed for poker and extended by DeepRole for Avalon) and LLM-based strategic agents (ReAct, ReCon, SLA, Cicero). It departs from SLA, which calibrates a fixed LLM's output distribution via RL but cannot escape the base model's exploration limits, and from Cicero, which relies on a hand-defined finite action space. The key conceptual departure is the iterative loop: clustering free-form utterances into a discrete latent strategy space, solving the abstracted extensive-form game with CFR, then using DPO to align the LLM with the learned policy and regenerate a broader latent space—making the action space itself an evolving object rather than a fixed input.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a principled recipe for tackling the two core obstacles in free-form language games—LLM intrinsic bias and unbounded action-space exploration—without requiring expensive human-annotated data or hand-engineered action sets. However, the evaluation is confined to a single seven-player Werewolf variant with Llama-3-8B-Instruct, and the modest win-rate gains (e.g., 0.73 vs. 0.69 for SLA as Werewolf) leave open whether the iterative latent-space expansion yields compounding benefits in larger or more diverse language games. Open questions include scalability to games with more players or richer role structures, sensitivity to the clustering granularity beyond the small k ablations, and whether the approach transfers to non-deduction free-form language tasks such as negotiation or debate.
