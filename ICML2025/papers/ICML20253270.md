## Problem

Mainstream theoretical RL evaluates algorithms by whether their regret upper bounds match lower bounds up to multiplicative constants, treating those constants as irrelevant. This paper argues that the convention is harmful: overly conservative exploration bonuses inflated by loose constants cause significant unnecessary exploration in practice, and the field's focus on matching asymptotic orders rather than constants creates a persistent gap between theoretical guarantees and empirical performance.

## Input / Output

The algorithm operates on finite-horizon tabular MDPs with S states, A actions, horizon H, and known deterministic rewards in [0,1]. At each stage the agent observes the current state and selects an action via the optimistic Q-value; the refined output is a tighter exploration bonus b_{k,h}(x,a) that directly controls the degree of over-exploration, alongside a regret upper bound whose multiplicative constants are strictly smaller than those in prior analyses. The comparison is made against both the original UCBVI bonuses and the state-of-the-art MVP algorithm under identical MDP parameters.

## Method

The paper re-derives the exploration bonuses for UCBVI (both Chernoff-Hoeffding and Bernstein-Freedman variants) to be as tight as possible, reducing the dominant-term constants by factors of 7/2 and √2 respectively, and corrects the minimum horizon requirement in the original analysis. The regret analysis is refined to yield upper bounds with smaller leading constants (reduced by factors of 2 and 5/4 for CH and BF) and lower-order terms reduced by factors of ~90 and ~4. Empirically, the tighter Bernstein-Freedman bonus (UCBVI-BF-I) reduces cumulative regret by a factor of 1.87 on average compared to the original UCBVI-BF, and substantially outperforms MVP, which suffers from sample inefficiency due to its doubling trick that discards previously collected data. The paper positions these results as evidence that constants and lower-order terms should be treated as first-class design objectives in algorithm development rather than dismissed as irrelevant to practical performance.

## Results

The improved UCBVI-BF-I algorithm achieves a 1.87±0.03× reduction in empirical regret compared to the original UCBVI-BF (Azar et al., 2017), with the exploration bonus reduced by a factor of √2 and the regret upper bound reduced by a factor of 5/4 in the dominant terms. In the RiverSwim environment (S=5, H=10), UCBVI-BF-I achieves approximately half the cumulative regret of UCBVI-BF, while MVP fails to exhibit a sublinear regret trend. In toy MDPs (S=3, A=3, H∈{5,10}), UCBVI-BF-I outperforms all baselines across all evaluated horizons (K∈{10⁵, 10⁶}).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the original UCBVI (Azar et al., 2017), the refined UCBVI-BF-I strictly dominates: the Bernstein-Freedman bonus is reduced by a factor of √2 (from √8 to √4 in the variance term, from 14 to 7 in the linear term, and from 100² to 84² in the truncation term), yielding a 1.87× lower empirical regret in RiverSwim and consistently lower regret in all toy MDP configurations. Against MVP (Zhang et al., 2024), UCBVI-BF-I wins decisively on empirical performance: MVP's doubling trick causes it to discard previously collected samples, resulting in over-exploration and a failure to achieve sublinear regret in RiverSwim (S=5, H=10) within the tested horizon, whereas both UCBVI variants show clear sublinear trends. In the toy environments, MVP only begins to show sublinear regret at K=10⁶, while UCBVI-BF-I outperforms it in every scenario including K=10⁵. The paper argues that MVP's theoretical advantage (removing the T ≥ O(H³S³A) requirement) comes at a significant practical cost that is invisible in the asymptotic regret order but dominates finite-horizon performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on UCBVI (Azar et al., 2017), the foundational optimistic value-iteration algorithm for finite-horizon tabular MDPs, by re-deriving its exploration bonuses (both Chernoff-Hoeffding and Bernstein-Freedman variants) with tighter multiplicative constants. It positions itself against the state-of-the-art MVP algorithm (Zhang et al., 2024), which achieves the same regret order without the large-T assumption but at the cost of a sample-inefficient doubling trick. The paper also draws a conceptual lineage from the MAB literature, where analogous constant-tightening efforts (UCB1 → Bubeck's peeling argument, KL-UCB) have long been recognized as empirically significant, arguing that the RL community has been slower to adopt this perspective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a deeply entrenched convention in theoretical RL—that matching lower bounds up to multiplicative constants constitutes a 'closed' problem—by showing that a ~1.87× empirical regret reduction is achievable purely through tighter constants in the exploration bonus. This raises the open question of whether the field's current evaluation criteria (order-matching) systematically produce algorithms that over-explore and waste samples in practice. The authors conjecture the effect will be amplified in deep RL settings but acknowledge the gap between tabular analysis and complex function approximation remains unresolved, leaving the practical transfer of these refinements as a significant open problem.
