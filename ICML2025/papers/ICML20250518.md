## Problem

In the mean-shift contamination model, consistent mean estimation is information-theoretically possible (unlike Huber contamination), but all known multivariate algorithms require runtime exponential in the dimension. This paper asks whether a sample- and computationally-efficient multivariate robust mean estimator exists for this model, and answers affirmatively with the first such algorithm.

## Input / Output

The algorithm receives n = Õ(d/ε^{2+o(1)} + 2^{O(1/ε²)}) i.i.d. samples from R^d, where each sample is drawn from N(μ, I) with probability 1−α or from N(z_i, I) with probability α (α ≤ 0.49, unknown to the algorithm), along with an accuracy parameter ε. It outputs a vector μ̂ ∈ R^d satisfying ||μ̂ − μ|| ≤ ε with probability at least 0.99, running in poly(n, d) time. Notably, the algorithm does not require knowledge of the contamination fraction α.

Two phases use different β values: β = √log k for high dimensions (requiring Matrix Bernstein concentration with truncation of the exponential weight) and β = ε for the final low-dimensional iterations. Once the dimension drops to O(1/ε²), a brute-force one-dimensional mean-shift estimator (from Kotekal & Gao, 2025) is applied on the remaining subspace to recover the final estimate to ε accuracy.

## Results

The main result (Theorem 1.2) establishes the first computationally efficient algorithm for high-dimensional robust mean estimation under mean-shift contamination. The algorithm achieves ℓ₂-error ε with probability ≥ 0.99 using n = Õ(d/ε^{2+o(1)} + 2^{O(1/ε²)}) samples and runs in poly(n, d) time, tolerating contamination rate α ≤ 0.49. The sample complexity is near-optimal: a 2^{Ω(1/ε²)} lower bound holds even in one dimension, and linear dependence on d is necessary even without outliers. Prior to this work, all known algorithms achieving arbitrary accuracy ε had runtime exponential in the dimension d.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or experimental comparisons. The key comparisons are against prior theoretical and algorithmic results. Against the Huber contamination model, the paper highlights a fundamental distinction: Huber's model has an information-theoretic lower bound of Ω(α) error (consistency is impossible), whereas the mean-shift model permits consistent estimation to any desired accuracy ε. Against prior algorithmic approaches for the mean-shift model (e.g., the brute-force reduction in Proposition 2.1), the new algorithm reduces runtime from 2^{O(d+1/ε²)} to poly(n,d) by introducing a reweighted second-moment-based dimension reduction procedure that iteratively halves the effective dimension. The sample complexity Õ(d/ε^{2+o(1)} + 2^{O(1/ε²)}) matches the information-theoretic lower bound up to logarithmic factors, making it minimax near-optimal. The algorithm also does not require knowledge of the contamination parameter α, and Appendix F extends the guarantee to α ∈ (0, 1/2 − c) for any constant c.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the one-dimensional mean-shift estimators of Kotekal & Gao (2025) and Li (2023), which established that consistent estimation is achievable in 1D but required exponential samples, and on the warm-start rough estimation from Diakonikolas & Kane (2023). The key departure from prior multivariate work is that all existing high-dimensional methods (e.g., brute-force sphere covers) run in time exponential in d, whereas this paper introduces a reweighted second moment matrix with exponential norm-based weighting to enable iterative dimension reduction. The authors explicitly contrast their approach with the centered second moment technique of Lai et al. (2016), noting that the non-centered reweighted matrix is essential because the inlier accuracy within the 'good' subspace can be as bad as Ω(α), which is insufficient for the ε → 0 goal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first polynomial-time algorithm for high-dimensional robust mean estimation under mean-shift contamination, resolving a natural open question that followed from the 1D information-theoretic results. The near-optimal sample complexity (within log factors of the 2^Ω(1/ε²) lower bound) and the fact that the algorithm does not require knowing α a priori make it practically relevant for settings where outliers are structured (e.g., Efron's two-groups model in high-dimensional inference). Open problems include extending to non-Gaussian base distributions, the unknown-covariance case, and understanding whether the reweighted second moment technique generalizes to other structured contamination models.
