## Problem

Prior analysis of assistance games assumed full observability by both the human and the AI assistant, leaving open whether a perfectly aligned assistant would ever have an incentive to obstruct the human's observations in the partially observable setting that arises in practice. This paper asks whether observation interference emerges as optimal behavior in a game-theoretic model where the assistant shares the human's reward function but only the human knows its parameterization.

## Input / Output

The theoretical analysis takes a POAG specification (state space, action spaces for both players, transition and observation kernels, reward parameterization θ known only to H, and discount factor γ) along with assumptions about human rationality (optimal, naive, or Boltzmann with parameter β) and produces characterizations of when optimal policy pairs require or avoid observation interference. The experimental component uses a product selection game with d=5 products, each having two attributes (H_i, R_i) drawn i.i.d. from Unif(0,1), where A observes k of the R_i values and chooses which products to suppress; Monte Carlo simulations with 30,000 trials compute expected payoff as a function of k and β.

## Method

The paper defines observation interference via Blackwell's informativeness ordering: an action interferes if it yields the human a less informative signal than an otherwise-equivalent action (same state/reward effects). It then distinguishes action-level interference (individual actions are less informative) from policy-level interference (the full strategy, accounting for H's knowledge of A's policy, is less informative). The key theoretical results show: (1) when A has no private information, a non-interfering optimal policy pair always exists (Theorem 4.2); (2) when A has private information, all optimal policy pairs may require action-level interference to communicate that information (Proposition 4.4); (3) at the policy level, a non-interfering optimal pair always exists (Theorem 4.7), resolving the apparent contradiction with the nonnegativity of the value of information by showing H can internally 'ungarble' observations when she knows A's policy. Additional results show that naive human behavior (acting on immediate reward without interpreting observations as communication) and Boltzmann rationality (where more information can make decisions harder) create further interference incentives. Experiments validate the tradeoff: more private information for A increases the incentive to interfere, while higher human rationality increases the cost of interference.

## Results

The paper's primary contributions are theoretical: it proves three distinct incentives for observation interference in POAGs (communicating private information, querying human preferences, and accommodating human irrationality). In the experimental product-selection game (d=5 products, 30,000 Monte Carlo trials), when A has 2 private observations, the optimal number of interferences k decreases monotonically as H's rationality β increases from 0.01 to 100: at β=0.01, A should interfere with all 4 remaining observations (effectively choosing for H), while at β=100, A should interfere with 0 observations. When A has 0 private observations, expected reward strictly decreases with each additional interference across all β values, confirming Theorem 4.2. In the Boltzmann-rationality example (Example 6.2), interference (tldr) yields expected utility ≈3.928 vs. non-interference (man) yielding ≈3.862 at β=1, demonstrating that less information can improve a bounded-rational human's decisions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper that does not compare against competing methods or baselines in the traditional sense. Instead, it establishes existence and impossibility results about optimal policy pairs in POAGs. The key 'comparisons' are between different structural settings: (1) with vs. without private information for A (Theorem 4.2 vs. Proposition 4.4), (2) with vs. without a communication channel (Theorem 4.5, Theorem 5.3), (3) action-level vs. policy-level interference (Proposition 4.4 vs. Theorem 4.7), and (4) optimal vs. Boltzmann-rational human behavior (Section 4 vs. Section 6). The experimental section validates the theory by showing that the incentive to interfere increases with A's private information and decreases with H's rationality, consistent with the theoretical predictions. No external baselines or competing approaches are benchmarked.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the assistance games framework (Shah et al., 2020; Hadfield-Menell et al., 2016) from the fully observable setting to partially observable assistance games (POAGs), a case that Shah et al. allowed in their definition but did not analyze. It formalizes 'observation interference' using Blackwell's informativeness ordering (Blackwell, 1953) and connects to the AI deception literature (Lang et al., 2024; Wen et al., 2024; Williams et al., 2024), but studies the aligned case where the assistant shares the human's exact reward. The Boltzmann rationality model (Luce, 1959; McFadden, 1973) is borrowed from (C)IRL to model human bounded rationality, and the value-of-information analysis extends the classic single-agent theorem into the cooperative multi-agent DecPOMDP setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper shows that even a perfectly value-aligned AI assistant can have three distinct incentives to interfere with a human's observations (communicating private information, querying preferences, simplifying decisions for irrational humans), which has direct implications for AI transparency and trust. The resolution via policy-level vs. action-level interference is reassuring but does not eliminate the practical concern: the non-interfering optimal policy may require implausible communication conventions from the human. Open questions include extending to multiple humans/assistants, other human irrationality models, empirical validation with human subjects, and whether the experimental tradeoff trends generalize beyond the toy product-selection game.
