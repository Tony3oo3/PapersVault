## Problem

GNNs and Transformers dominate brain network analysis through their message-passing mechanisms, yet brain networks are constructed from pairwise Pearson correlations between all ROI pairs, meaning the adjacency matrix already encodes holistic inter-region relationships. This paper shows that GNNs cannot fully exploit message passing because they lack distinct node attributes separate from topology, and Transformers cannot add value because the holistic relations they seek to learn are already present in the input. A simple linear classifier using the connectivity matrix as features consistently outperforms basic GNNs and Transformers, revealing that message passing is fundamentally mismatched to the brain network structure.

## Input / Output

The sole input is the brain functional connectivity matrix (N×N Pearson correlation matrix between ROIs, typically 200×200 for Craddock atlas or 90×90 for AAL atlas), which simultaneously serves as both the graph topology and the node feature matrix — an unusual duality that the paper identifies as the root cause of message-passing redundancy. The output is a disease class label (binary for ABIDE/ADNI/ADHD-200, three-class for PPMI) produced by a linear classification head applied to the final-layer representation. No separate node attributes, positional encodings, or external features are required.

## Method

BQN replaces the matrix-product-based message passing with a Hadamard (element-wise) product update: H^l = H^(l-1) ⊙ (A W_A^l) + (H^(l-1) ⊙ H^(l-1)) W_H^l, where the first term multiplies the previous layer's representation element-wise with a learned linear projection of the adjacency matrix, and the second term is a Hadamard residual for training stability. The key insight is that the quadratic/second-order function underlying the Hadamard product can implement XOR logic and possesses superior universal approximation properties compared to linear functions, making it a more expressive fundamental operator for brain connectivity data. Theoretically, the first term is proven equivalent to the multiplicative update rule of nonnegative matrix factorization (NMF) of the adjacency matrix, meaning BQN implicitly performs community detection while learning representations. Computationally, the element-wise product has O(n²) complexity versus O(n³) for the matrix multiplication in GNNs and Transformers, yielding the fastest training time among all compared methods.

## Results

On the ABIDE dataset, BQN achieves 79.85% AUC and 72.53% ACC, outperforming the strongest GNN baseline A-GCL (73.86% AUC, 71.04% ACC) and the strongest Transformer baseline ALTER (77.99% AUC, 70.10% ACC). On ADNI, BQN reaches 74.18% AUC and 68.62% ACC, again surpassing all 13 baselines. In terms of efficiency, BQN completes 100 training epochs in 11.31 s on ABIDE and 3.33 s on ADNI, making it the fastest method tested—roughly 86× faster than Graphormer (973.52 s) and 63× faster than SAN (712.01 s) on ABIDE.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BQN outperforms all 13 baselines (6 GNNs + 7 Graph Transformers) on both AUC and ACC for ABIDE and ADNI. On ABIDE, BQN's 79.85% AUC exceeds the closest competitor ALTER (77.99%) by 1.86 points and the best GNN A-GCL (73.86%) by 5.99 points; on ADNI, BQN's 74.18% AUC leads ALTER (71.86%) by 2.32 points and A-GCL (68.15%) by 6.03 points. On the supplementary ADHD-200 dataset, BQN achieves 83.34% AUC and 75.68% ACC, narrowly beating ALTER (83.16% AUC, 73.48% ACC) and clearly outperforming A-GCL (74.78% AUC) and BrainMGT (78.33% AUC). On PPMI, BQN (74.26% accuracy) is outperformed by AGT (77.69% accuracy), the only comparison where BQN does not rank first. In terms of efficiency, BQN is the fastest method on every dataset tested (11.31 s on ABIDE, 3.33 s on ADNI, 6.56 s on ADHD-200, 4.79 s on PPMI), with the gap reaching over 80× versus Graphormer and SAN on ABIDE, attributed to its O(n²) Hadamard-product core versus the O(n³) matrix multiplications in GNN/Transformer baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant message-passing paradigm (GNNs and Graph Transformers) in brain network analysis by identifying a structural redundancy: when brain networks are constructed from pairwise Pearson correlations, the adjacency matrix simultaneously serves as both graph topology and node features, making neighborhood aggregation or global attention largely redundant. It builds on the Quadratic Neural Network literature (Fan et al., 2018; 2020), which established that Hadamard/element-wise products implement XOR logic and possess superior universal approximation properties over linear functions, and repurposes this operator as the fundamental brain network update rule. The theoretical contribution connects the resulting BQN update to the multiplicative update rule of NMF-based community detection, bridging representation learning with a well-studied graph clustering paradigm. Unlike prior brain-specific GNNs (BrainGNN, BrainGB, FBNetGen) or Transformers (BrainNETTF, ALTER, BioBGT) that modify the message-passing mechanism, BQN eliminates it entirely in favor of element-wise quadratic interactions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a widely adopted assumption in neuroimaging ML—that message passing is the natural and necessary operator for brain functional connectivity graphs—and offers a simpler, more computationally efficient (O(n²) vs. O(n³)) alternative with a clean theoretical interpretation. However, the performance gains over the strongest baselines (ALTER, BrainNETTF) are modest (≈2 AUC points on ABIDE, ≈2.3 on ADNI), and the evaluation is limited to four fMRI-based datasets with Pearson-correlation construction, leaving open whether the structural argument generalizes to brain networks built from other connectivity measures or to non-brain graphs where topology and features are genuinely distinct. The NMF connection, while elegant, relies on a normalization step (Eq. 14→15) that is not fully rigorous, and the clustering experiments (Appendix D) use only three layers on a single dataset. Future work should test BQN on structurally different brain graphs (e.g., structural connectivity, effective connectivity) and on larger-scale or multi-modal settings to validate the generality of the 'no message passing needed' claim.
