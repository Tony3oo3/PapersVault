## Problem

LLMs lack the strategic information-gathering ability needed for sequential decision-making in interactive environments, and existing approaches either require known optimal algorithms to generate training trajectories (limiting scope to simple tasks like bandits) or are confined to task-specific fine-tuning. The paper asks whether a general in-context RL capability—where the model adapts its exploration strategy based on environment feedback without gradient updates—can be instilled through fine-tuning on self-generated data from diverse tasks, and whether the resulting behavior transfers zero-shot to entirely unseen task groups.

## Input / Output

The agent receives a text task description, a sequence of environment observations (which may be LLM-generated answers, hardcoded rule-based feedback, or grid states), and its own prior actions as context, and must produce a text action at each turn (a yes/no question, a word guess, a grid cell coordinate, an arm selection, etc.). Tasks are POMDPs with heterogeneous observation spaces, variable maximum turn limits (6–21), and either LLM-simulated or hardcoded environment feedback. The final output is a fine-tuned LLM checkpoint that, at inference time, performs multi-turn interaction with a new environment using only in-context adaptation—no additional gradient updates or task-specific training.

## Method

PAPRIKA fine-tunes an LLM on preference pairs of self-generated interaction trajectories from 10 diverse task groups (twenty questions, Wordle, Mastermind, Battleship, Minesweeper, cellular automata, customer service, murder mystery, guess-my-city, and bandit best-arm selection). Trajectories are sampled at high temperature with Min-p sampling to encourage diversity; for each task, the highest-scoring trajectory is paired with a randomly sampled lower-scoring one. The model is trained with RPO, which combines a multi-turn DPO objective (applied only to agent action tokens, excluding environment-generated tokens) with an SFT loss on the preferred trajectory to mitigate unintentional unalignment. To address the fact that data generation—not model updates—is the computational bottleneck, PAPRIKA introduces a UCB-based curriculum that treats each task group as an arm and uses the coefficient of variation (standard deviation of reward divided by mean reward) as a learning-potential signal, dynamically allocating sampling budget to groups where the current model can extract the most useful contrastive signal.

## Results

PAPRIKA increases Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct's average success rate by 47% of its original value across all 10 task groups using only ~22,500 training trajectories. On the bandit best arm selection task, success rate improves from 42.25% to 62.25% without any bandit-specific training. Leave-one-out experiments show PAPRIKA (LOO) improves success rate on 9 out of 10 unseen task groups, and PAPRIKA (full) outperforms single-task-group training in 7 out of 10 groups. On standard benchmarks (MT-Bench, AlpacaEval, GPQA, Math, MMLU-Pro, IFEval), PAPRIKA causes no noticeable degradation relative to the base model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares PAPRIKA-fine-tuned models against two base model baselines: Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Gemma-3-12B-IT. PAPRIKA improves both models across all 10 task groups (Figure 2), with the 47% relative gain on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct being the headline result. In leave-one-out experiments (Figure 3), PAPRIKA (LOO) trained on 9 groups matches or exceeds group-specific training on 9/10 held-out groups, demonstrating genuine cross-task generalization; however, transfer is uneven (e.g., minimal improvement on Mastermind, negative transfer on Wordle). PAPRIKA (full, all 10 groups) outperforms single-task-group training in 7/10 groups, indicating that exposure to diverse tasks improves in-group strategy. The curriculum learning variant outperforms uniform task sampling by 1.4% (average) and 3.3% (pass@4) after 3 rounds on Twenty Questions. On standard benchmarks, PAPRIKA maintains parity with the base model, confirming no capability degradation.

## Relation to Prior Work

PAPRIKA sits at the intersection of multi-turn preference optimization (building on Rafailov et al.'s multi-turn DPO and Pang et al.'s RPO loss), in-context reinforcement learning (Laskin et al., 2022), and meta-RL (Beck et al., 2023). It departs from prior LLM-RL work (Krishnamurthy et al., 2024; Nie et al., 2024) that required known optimal algorithms to generate synthetic trajectories, instead relying on diverse self-generated data with Min-p sampling and preference-based fine-tuning. The curriculum learning component repurposes UCB bandit algorithms (Auer, 2000) for task selection, a novel application in the LLM fine-tuning context, and the task environments build on LMRL-Gym (Abdulhai et al., 2023) while extending to more diverse and complex settings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical insight is that the bottleneck for teaching LLMs decision-making is data generation, not model updates, which reframes how compute should be allocated in agentic training pipelines. The zero-shot cross-task generalization finding (LOO models matching or exceeding group-specific training) suggests that a sufficiently diverse training distribution can substitute for task-specific fine-tuning, a promising direction for scalable agent training. However, limitations are significant: the approach requires a strong base model, uses offline preference tuning rather than online RL, the 10 task groups are relatively simple, and environment design required substantial human effort. Open questions include whether the approach scales to genuinely complex domains, whether online RL would yield substantially stronger results, and whether automated task generation can replace the manual environment design.
