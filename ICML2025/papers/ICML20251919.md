## Problem

Neural networks suffer from plasticity loss, losing their ability to learn new tasks or adapt to distribution shifts during continual or reinforcement learning. While Dropout is a standard regularization technique, recent work has shown it is ineffective against plasticity loss. This paper investigates why Dropout fails—showing it merely creates subnetworks that each independently lose plasticity—and proposes a targeted alternative.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251919/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

AID operates on preactivation value vectors (real-valued, any dimension) and produces postactivation vectors of the same dimension, serving as a direct drop-in replacement for ReLU in any architecture. During training, each element is stochastically masked with a probability that depends on its sign (positive vs. negative), while during testing the output is a deterministic modified leaky ReLU r_p(x) = ½x + (p−½)|x|, preserving nonlinearity at inference. The method shares the same computational complexity as standard Dropout and requires no architectural changes beyond swapping the activation function.

## Method

AID applies interval-specific Dropout probabilities to preactivation values, with the simplified version AID_p using rate (1−p) for positive values and p for negative values. The key theoretical contribution (Theorem 4.1) proves that the expected AID loss decomposes into a modified-leaky-ReLU loss plus a regularization term that penalizes deviation from linear network behavior, with the penalty strength scaling as 4p(1−p)/(n(2p−1)²)—strongest near p=0.5 where AID becomes purely linear. Since deep linear networks are known to be immune to plasticity loss, this linearity-inducing regularization is the mechanism by which AID preserves plasticity. The method is shown to be compatible with Kaiming He initialization (Property 2), generalizes ReLU, standard Dropout, and DropReLU as special cases (Property 3), and can be implemented with a single Bernoulli mask per element, making it a trivial swap for any existing ReLU layer.

## Results

On standard supervised learning (Table 1), AID achieves 74.8% on CIFAR-10 (CNN), 69.0% on CIFAR-100 (ResNet-18), and 51.9% on TinyImageNet (VGG-16), outperforming Dropout (73.3%, 65.1%, 46.6%) and L2 (74.2%, 57.4%, 41.7%) across all three benchmarks. In the warm-start generalizability experiment, AID reduces the accuracy gap between warm-start and cold-start training to 3.3 percentage points, compared to 10.9%p for vanilla and 10.1%p for Dropout. In reinforcement learning (17 Atari games, DQN with replay ratio 1), AID significantly improves IQM Human Normalized Score over vanilla DQN, while Dropout provides only marginal gains. In trainability experiments (Permuted MNIST, Random Label MNIST), AID consistently maintains high training accuracy across all tasks and optimizers, whereas vanilla, Dropout, and most baselines show progressive degradation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AID outperforms Dropout across all evaluated settings: in Table 1 it gains +1.5%p on CIFAR-10, +3.9%p on CIFAR-100, and +5.3%p on TinyImageNet; in the warm-start experiment it narrows the plasticity gap to 3.3%p versus Dropout's 10.1%p; and in trainability experiments Dropout consistently degrades while AID maintains accuracy. Against L2 regularization, AID wins on CIFAR-100 (+11.6%p) and TinyImageNet (+10.2%p) but is comparable on CIFAR-10 (+0.6%p). Among plasticity-specific methods, AID surpasses S&P, ReDo, CBP, CReLU, RReLU, and Fourier in most continual learning and trainability settings, with the gap widening over longer training horizons. DropReLU, the closest structural relative (AID generalizes DropReLU per Property 3), performs comparably to AID in trainability but AID extends its advantage in generalizability and RL. In the class-incremental setting, AID is the only method that consistently maintains an advantage over full reset throughout all 20 stages, whereas Dropout and DASH initially lead but lose their edge as training progresses. In RL (replay ratio 1), AID substantially improves sample efficiency over vanilla DQN, while Dropout at small rates (0.001–0.01) yields only marginal gains attributed to ensemble effects rather than plasticity preservation.

## Relation to Prior Work

AID is positioned as a principled generalization that unifies ReLU, standard Dropout, and DropReLU within a single interval-wise masking framework (Property 3), building directly on the Bernoulli masking mechanism of Dropout (Srivastava et al., 2014) and the probabilistic activation idea of DropReLU (Liang et al., 2021). Conceptually, it departs from the broader plasticity-loss literature (CReLU, ReDo, CBP, S&P, DASH, Fourier features) by reframing the solution as an activation-function modification rather than a weight-regularization or neuron-resetting strategy. The theoretical analysis is grounded in the recent finding that deep linear networks are immune to plasticity loss (Lewandowski et al., 2024; Dohare et al., 2024), and AID is shown to regularize toward that linear regime.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

AID offers a drop-in replacement for ReLU that simultaneously addresses trainability, generalizability, and reinforcement-learning plasticity loss without architectural changes or additional hyperparameter-heavy components, making it broadly applicable. The clean theoretical decomposition (Theorem 4.1) linking the AID loss to a linearity-encouraging penalty provides a mechanistic explanation for why it works, which is rare in the plasticity-loss literature. Key limitations include the fact that experiments focus on the simplified two-interval version, the relationship between the trainability and generalizability improvements remains unexplained, and scalability to very deep or large-scale architectures is not yet established.
