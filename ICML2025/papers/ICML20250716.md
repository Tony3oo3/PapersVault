## Problem

Existing neural solvers for combinatorial optimization exhibit complementary performance across instances—no single solver dominates all, and distribution shifts can reverse dominance relationships. Prior ensemble approaches (e.g., Poppy, distributionally robust ensembles) use solvers with identical architectures and run all solvers for every instance, limiting both diversity and inference efficiency. This paper proposes the first general framework to coordinate diverse, open-source neural solvers at the instance level by learning to select the most suitable solver(s) for each problem instance.

## Input / Output

The selection model takes a COP instance (TSP or CVRP) represented as a fully connected graph with node coordinates (and demands for CVRP) plus the instance scale N, and produces a score vector over the solver pool indicating compatibility. Based on the chosen strategy (greedy, top-k, rejection-based, or top-p), one or more solvers are selected and executed to produce the final tour or route. The selection model is trained on 10,000 synthetic instances (with 8-fold augmentation, N∈[50,500]) and generalizes zero-shot to out-of-distribution benchmarks up to N=1002 (and N=2000 in extended experiments).

## Method

The framework comprises three components: (1) a feature extraction module using either a 4-layer graph attention encoder or a proposed hierarchical graph encoder that successively downsamples the instance graph via learned graph pooling (α=0.8) and aggregates multi-level features through mean+max pooling readouts; (2) a selection model (MLP on top of the encoder) trained with either cross-entropy classification or a listwise ranking loss that leverages the full dominance ordering among solvers; and (3) adaptive selection strategies—greedy, top-k, rejection-based (using softmax confidence to switch between greedy and top-k), and top-p (selecting the smallest solver subset whose normalized scores sum to ≥p)—that balance optimality and inference cost. The key insight is that ranking loss outperforms classification because it captures sub-optimal solver relationships, and the hierarchical encoder's graph pooling captures multi-scale structural information critical for out-of-distribution generalization.

## Results

On synthetic TSP, the ranking-loss model with top-k(k=2) selection achieves a 1.51% optimality gap, reducing the gap by 0.82% over the best individual solver DIFUSCO (2.33%). On synthetic CVRP, the same configuration achieves 4.82% gap versus Omni's 6.82% (a 2.00% reduction). On out-of-distribution benchmarks, the framework reduces the optimality gap by 0.88% on TSPLIB (1.07% vs. T2T's 1.95%) and by 0.71% on CVRPLIB Set-X (5.39% vs. ELG's 6.10%), while consuming only ~2.5s average time compared to Oracle's ~9s. Greedy selection (single solver) already outperforms the best individual solver on all four datasets with comparable or lower inference time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The framework outperforms all individual neural solvers across every dataset. On TSP, it beats DIFUSCO (2.33%) by 0.82% (top-k) or 0.47% (greedy), and T2T (2.40%) by a similar margin; on CVRP, it beats Omni (6.82%) by 2.00% (top-k) or 1.51% (greedy). On out-of-distribution TSPLIB, the ranking+top-k configuration (1.07%) surpasses T2T (1.95%) by 0.88% and DIFUSCO (2.13%) by 1.06%; on CVRPLIB Set-X, it beats ELG (6.10%) by 0.71% and Omni (6.21%) by 0.82%. Against the traditional algorithm-selection baseline of Seiler et al. (2020) using the salesperson feature set with random-forest classification, the proposed hierarchical encoder + ranking model achieves 1.33% vs. 1.55% on TSPLIB and 1.86% vs. 2.12% on synthetic TSP (greedy), confirming that learned graph features and ranking loss outperform hand-crafted features with classification. The top-k selection also consistently outperforms a static solver portfolio of the same size k across all four datasets, demonstrating that instance-level dynamic selection is superior to a fixed portfolio.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper draws on the graph attention encoder from Kool et al. (2019) and the self-attention graph pooling mechanism from Lee et al. (2019) to build its instance feature extractor, and adopts the listwise ranking loss from Xia et al. (2008) for training the selection model. Conceptually, it departs from prior multi-solver approaches for COPs—ensemble learning (Jiang et al., 2023) and population-based training (Grinsztajn et al., 2023)—which require running all solvers at inference, by instead selecting a subset per instance. It also extends the classical algorithm selection literature (Kerschke et al., 2019; Seiler et al., 2020) from traditional heuristic solvers to the domain of diverse end-to-end neural solvers with heterogeneous architectures and inductive biases.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic framework for instance-level neural solver selection in combinatorial optimization, and it demonstrates that the complementary strengths of architecturally diverse solvers can be exploited with minimal overhead (greedy selection is nearly as fast as a single solver). Key open questions include: whether the preliminary solver-feature extraction can be refined for truly zero-shot generalization to unseen solvers, how to incorporate runtime into selection when the pool includes expensive learn-to-search methods, and whether the solver zoo itself can be enhanced through targeted fine-tuning or new-solver training. The framework is currently validated only on TSP and CVRP, leaving its applicability to other COP families (scheduling, assignment, etc.) as an important next step.
