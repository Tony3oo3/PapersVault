## Problem

Existing data selection methods for instruction tuning rely on next-token prediction (NTP) loss as a differentiable proxy for task performance, but real evaluation metrics for long-form generation tasks (code pass rate, LLM-judge, reward models) are non-differentiable and often poorly aligned with NTP loss. This mismatch means that selecting data to minimize validation loss does not necessarily improve the actual task metric, and loss-based methods also require ground-truth labels even when the evaluation metric does not.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a training pool of instruction-response pairs (e.g., Tulu's mixed-source datasets or task-specific sets like CodeAlpaca 20k), validation prompts, and a reward function that scores model-generated responses. Crucially, when the reward function is label-independent (reward models, code unit tests), NICE operates on validation prompts alone without ground-truth responses, unlike loss-based baselines that require full (prompt, label) pairs. The output is a ranked list of influence scores per training data point and a selected subset (typically top 5–20%) used for fine-tuning via LoRA with random-projected gradients (8192-dim).

## Method

NICE replaces the validation loss gradient in TracIn (or Influence Function) with a Monte Carlo policy gradient of the non-differentiable evaluation metric, treating the LLM as a policy and the metric as a reward. Concretely, for each validation prompt, multiple responses are sampled from the model, scored by the reward function, and the policy gradient is estimated as the reward-weighted gradient of the log-likelihood of those responses; this gradient is then inner-producted (via cosine similarity, following LESS's Adam-gradient adaptation) with each training data point's gradient across warmup epochs to yield an influence score. An optional Assisted Monte-Carlo (AMC) variant replaces the model's own samples with responses from a stronger LLM (GPT-4) to improve gradient quality when the base model is weak. The framework generalizes to the Influence Function by substituting the policy gradient into the Hessian-inverse formulation (NICEIF), and Remark 3.1 shows NICE reduces to standard TracIn for single-token accuracy tasks.

## Results

On the task-agnostic setting with Llama2-7B, NICE achieves 27.61 on AlpacaEval (vs. 22.59 full dataset, 26.94 LESS), 3.61 on TLDR (vs. 2.40 full, 3.37 LESS), 2.82 on RLHF (vs. 2.31 full, 1.44 LESS), and 48.59 on HumanEval pass@100 (vs. 47.44 full, 47.50 LESS). NICEAMC further boosts AlpacaEval to 30.45 and RLHF to 3.03 on Llama2-7B, and reaches 47.40 on AlpacaEval and 3.42 on RLHF on Mistral-7B. In the task-aware setting, NICE achieves 55.09 on HumanEval (vs. 51.27 full, 52.67 LESS) and 1.69 on RLHF (vs. 1.01 full, 1.62 LESS) for Llama2-7B, and 87.35 on HumanEval (vs. 84.27 full, 85.26 LESS) for Mistral-7B. Selected subsets from NICE/NICEAMC frequently outperform the full dataset, demonstrating that less but better-curated data yields superior instruction-tuned models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NICE consistently outperforms LESS (the strongest loss-based influence estimation baseline) across all four tasks and both model architectures: e.g., on RLHF with Mistral-7B (task-agnostic), NICE scores 3.10 vs. LESS's 1.70, and on HumanEval with Llama2-7B (task-aware), NICE reaches 55.09 vs. LESS's 52.67. Against the non-influence baselines, NICE dominates: on AlpacaEval (Llama2-7B, task-agnostic), NICE (27.61) beats BM25 (19.60), DSIR (20.27), RDS (14.70), TSDS (17.40), and Random (16.13) by 8+ points. NICEAMC further amplifies gains in the task-agnostic (large-pool) setting, reaching 47.40 on AlpacaEval (Mistral-7B) and 3.42 on RLHF (Mistral-7B), but underperforms NICE in the smaller task-aware pool where fewer data points align with the assisted gradient direction. The extension to Influence Functions (NICEIF vs. DataInf) confirms generality: NICEIF outperforms DataInf on all four tasks (e.g., 20.44 vs. 11.11 on AlpacaEval, 3.97 vs. 2.01 on TLDR). Notably, NICE/NICEAMC subsets frequently exceed the full-dataset performance (e.g., 30.45 vs. 22.59 on AlpacaEval, 87.35 vs. 84.27 on HumanEval for Mistral-7B), validating that targeted selection of a small fraction (5–20%) of training data is more effective than using all data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the influence-estimation framework for data selection (TracIn, Influence Function) by replacing the validation NTP-loss gradient with a Monte Carlo policy gradient of the non-differentiable evaluation metric, drawing on the log-derivative trick from reinforcement learning. It builds directly on LESS (Xia et al., 2024), which adapted TracIn for LLM instruction tuning, and on DataInf (Kwon et al., 2024) for efficient Hessian-inverse computation, but departs from both by targeting the actual evaluation metric (pass@k, reward model, LLM-judge) rather than the NTP loss proxy. The Assisted Monte-Carlo (AMC) sampling variant further distinguishes the work by leveraging a stronger external model (GPT-4) to generate high-quality responses for gradient estimation, a mechanism absent from prior influence-estimation methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap: when the evaluation metric is non-differentiable (code pass rate, reward model scores), loss-based influence estimation can select data that minimizes NTP loss while actually degrading task performance, as demonstrated by the checkpoint with minimum loss exhibiting the worst reward. NICE's ability to perform data selection without validation labels (when the reward function is label-independent) reduces annotation costs and broadens applicability. Open questions include the computational overhead of MC sampling (14.67h vs. 0.11h for validation gradients on average), whether NICEAMC's advantage over NICE holds as training pools grow, and whether more efficient policy-gradient estimators (PPO, DPO-style) can reduce the sampling cost while preserving the quality gains.
