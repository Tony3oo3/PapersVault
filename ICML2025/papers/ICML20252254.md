## Problem

Existing Stein discrepancy methods for assessing MCMC sample quality face a fundamental trade-off: the kernel Stein discrepancy (KSD) is quadratic in sample size and fails to detect moment non-convergence (the primary source of bias in SG-MCMC), while linear-time alternatives (FSSD, RFSD) suffer from curse-of-dimensionality, extensive tuning requirements, and reduced statistical power. No existing method simultaneously achieves linear-time computation, high power for detecting moment discrepancies, and minimal hyperparameter tuning.

## Input / Output

The method consumes n MCMC samples from Q, the score function ∇log p(x) of the unnormalized target, and a user-chosen polynomial order r (typically r=2 for biased samplers). It outputs a scalar PSD discrepancy score computed in O(n·binom(d+r,d)) time, a bootstrap-based goodness-of-fit test decision at a chosen significance level, and a continuous sample-quality metric suitable for step-size selection in SGLD. The U-statistic form of the squared PSD is a degenerate U-statistic, which is what enables the linear-time bootstrap procedure rather than requiring eigenvalue decomposition of a J×J covariance matrix.

## Method

PSD replaces the infinite-dimensional RKHS in KSD with the finite-dimensional span of all r-th order monomials {x^α : |α|≤r}, applying the second-order Langevin-Stein operator A = Δ + ∇log p · ∇ to each basis monomial. The discrepancy is the L2 norm of the vector of sample means of A·P_k(x) over all J = binom(d+r,d)−1 monomials, yielding a closed-form solution that avoids kernel matrix construction entirely. For Gaussian targets, the paper proves PSD=0 if and only if the first r moments of P and Q match (Proposition 3.2), via an inductive argument showing the resulting system of linear equations forces all moment differences to vanish using positive-definiteness of Σ⁻¹. The associated goodness-of-fit test uses a multinomial bootstrap on the degenerate U-statistic, which the authors show empirically outperforms the asymptotic chi-squared test in power and type-I error control while remaining linear-time.

## Results

In the RBM goodness-of-fit test (Table 1), PSD with r=2 achieves a null rejection rate of 1.00 at perturbation σ²=0.02, matching IMQ KSD (0.99) and Gauss KSD (0.95), while outperforming FSSD-opt (0.70) and RFSD (0.48). In the Gaussian moment-detection experiments (Figure 1), PSD with r=4 is the only method to consistently achieve power ≈1 for detecting kurtosis discrepancies (Student-t, Laplace), and PSD with r=2 achieves power of 1 for variance-perturbed Gaussians. At d=20, PSD achieves up to 2× the statistical power of IMQ KSD and 4× that of linear-time competitors (FSSD, RFSD). As a sample quality measure for SGLD step-size selection, PSD with r=2–4 agrees with IMQ KSD in selecting the optimal ε=0.005, while running ~70× faster than KSD and ~7× faster than RFSD.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PSD outperforms both linear-time competitors (FSSD-opt and RFSD) in statistical power across all moment-detection experiments, with the gap widening at higher dimensions (up to 4× at d=20). Against the quadratic-time IMQ KSD, PSD with r≥2 matches or exceeds its power for detecting first- and second-order moment discrepancies while being ~70× faster computationally; however, IMQ KSD retains an advantage for detecting discrepancies in moments higher than order r (e.g., kurtosis with r<4). In the RBM example, PSD r=2 achieves a perfect 1.00 rejection rate at σ²=0.02, surpassing FSSD-opt (0.70) and RFSD (0.48) while matching IMQ KSD (0.99). For SGLD hyperparameter tuning, PSD r=2–4 selects the same optimal step size as IMQ KSD (ε=0.005), whereas RFSD and FSSD are not directly compared in that setting but are noted to be slower. The bootstrap-based PSD test is recommended over the asymptotic test, which shows degraded power and poor type-I error control in the Student-t case (Appendix C.1).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Stein discrepancy framework of Gorham & Mackey (2015, 2017) by replacing the infinite-dimensional RKHS function class with a finite-dimensional space of r-th order polynomials, yielding a linear-time discrepancy (PSD) that directly tracks moment convergence for Gaussian targets. The bootstrap goodness-of-fit test follows Liu et al. (2016) and Arcones & Gine (1992) for degenerate U-statistics, while the asymptotic test inherits from Jitkrittum et al. (2017). The key departure from FSSD (Jitkrittum et al., 2017) and RFSD (Huggins & Mackey, 2018) is that PSD requires no optimization of test locations, feature maps, or importance sampling distributions—only the polynomial order r, which has a direct moment-matching interpretation. The polynomial function class is motivated by zero-variance control variates (Assaraf & Caffarel, 1999; Mira et al., 2013) used in MCMC variance reduction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PSD addresses a practical bottleneck: KSD's quadratic cost makes it infeasible for the large sample sizes typical of SG-MCMC, while existing linear-time alternatives (FSSD, RFSD) suffer from tuning sensitivity and power degradation in high dimensions. The guarantee that PSD = 0 iff the first r moments match (for Gaussian P) is directly relevant to biased MCMC where moment bias is the primary failure mode. Key limitations include: PSD is not convergence-determining (finite-dimensional), it cannot detect discrepancies in moments beyond order r, and it is not translation invariant—making optimal parameterization an open problem. The paper also honestly shows that for non-Gaussian targets far from the Bernstein-von Mises regime (e.g., Rosenbrock), PSD may not track moments at all, limiting its universality.
