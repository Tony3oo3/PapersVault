## Problem

Evaluating whether an LLM's output distribution meaningfully shifts under a perturbation (input change, model swap, prompt modification) is non-trivial: single-output comparisons conflate true effects with stochastic sampling noise, and full distributional comparison is computationally intractable due to the exponential output space. Existing text-analysis methods (bias metrics, counterfactual fairness, summarization scores) either require white-box access, ground-truth labels, or do not provide rigorous statistical inference. The paper addresses this gap by casting LLM perturbation analysis as a frequentist hypothesis testing problem with interpretable p-values and effect sizes.

## Input / Output

The method takes a base prompt x, a perturbed prompt x' (or an alternative model), a black-box LLM, and a deterministic embedding function (e.g., text-embedding-ada-002). It requires k Monte Carlo samples from each output distribution (k is a tunable hyperparameter trading off estimator variance and test power). The primary outputs are a permutation-test p-value and a scalar effect size computed as the Jensen-Shannon divergence between two empirical similarity distributions; when applied to a family of m perturbations, it additionally yields TPR/FPR curves parameterized by significance level α and a ROC-AUC summary score.

## Method

Distribution-based Perturbation Analysis (DBPA) proceeds in four steps: (1) draw k i.i.d. outputs from S(x) and k from S(x′) via Monte Carlo sampling; (2) compute all pairwise cosine similarities within each group (P₀) and across groups (P₁) using a semantic embedding, reducing the high-dimensional output space to scalar similarity scores; (3) measure the discrepancy between P₀ and P₁ via Jensen-Shannon divergence, which serves as the effect size; and (4) perform a permutation test on the pooled 2k outputs to obtain a valid p-value under the exchangeability assumption that holds when H₀ is true. The key insight is that projecting to scalar pairwise similarities simultaneously resolves computational intractability (avoiding enumeration of |V|^L sequences) and interpretability (capturing semantic rather than purely probabilistic differences). The permutation test reuses the same 2k generated outputs across B permutations, avoiding additional costly model calls, and the framework extends to multiple perturbations via Bonferroni FWER control.

## Results

In the persona perturbation experiment (Table 2), GPT-4 and GPT-3.5 show no statistically significant effect sizes (all p > 0.05) for medical personas (Doctor, Nurse, Medical Practitioner, Medical Supervisor), while non-medical personas (Comedian, NeurIPS Reviewer) yield significant shifts (e.g., GPT-4: 0.32*** for Comedian, 0.28** for NeurIPS Reviewer). Smaller models (GPT-2, Gemma-2-9B, SmolLM-135M) show significant effects even for medical personas, indicating lower robustness. In the TPR/FPR analysis, the best model depends on the allowed false-positive rate: Phi-3-mini is optimal at 1% FPR (TPR = 0.05) while Mistral-7B is optimal at 20% FPR (TPR = 0.34). For model alignment with GPT-4, only SmolLM-135M and LLaMA-3.1-8B show significantly different response distributions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark DBPA against competing perturbation-analysis methods in a head-to-head empirical comparison; instead, it positions DBPA against three families of related work (unintended-bias metrics, counterfactual fairness, and text-summarization metrics) via a qualitative criteria table (Table 8), showing DBPA is the only approach that simultaneously supports black-box models, arbitrary perturbations, statistical inference, effect-size computation, and assumption-free operation. The primary empirical 'comparison' is across LLMs themselves: GPT-4 and GPT-3.5 are the most robust (no significant shifts under medically irrelevant persona changes), while smaller open-source models (GPT-2, Gemma-2-9B, SmolLM-135M) exhibit significantly higher sensitivity to irrelevant perturbations, with effect sizes up to 0.45*** (Gemma-2-9B, Medical Practitioner at 600 tokens). In the TPR/FPR setting, no single model dominates across all FPR thresholds—Phi-3-mini wins at 1% FPR while Mistral-7B wins at 20% FPR—demonstrating that model selection is task- and threshold-dependent. The paper also shows that alternative distance measures (Euclidean, Wasserstein, Energy) yield consistent significance decisions with JSD, and that BLEU/ROUGE-based similarities are overly sensitive to surface-level changes compared to embedding-based cosine similarity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes classical frequentist tools—permutation testing, Jensen-Shannon divergence, and Monte Carlo sampling—for a new application domain: auditing LLM output distributions under perturbation. It explicitly departs from three adjacent literatures (unintended bias measurement, counterfactual fairness, and text summarization metrics) by being model-agnostic, supporting arbitrary perturbations, and providing formal statistical inference without requiring ground-truth labels or white-box access. The key conceptual move is shifting from single-output comparison to distribution-level comparison in a low-dimensional semantic similarity space, resolving both the computational intractability of the full output space and the interpretability gap between probabilistic and semantic differences.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DBPA provides a statistically principled, model-agnostic framework for LLM auditing that yields interpretable p-values, scalar effect sizes, and TPR/FPR trade-offs for model selection—capabilities that existing evaluation metrics lack. The TPR/FPR formulation is particularly practical for high-stakes deployment, enabling practitioners to select models based on a desired false-positive rate rather than a single aggregate score. However, the paper is explicitly conceptual in scope: empirical evidence is limited to a healthcare domain with five seeds, and key design choices (distance metric, embedding model, sample size) lack systematic selection guidelines. Open questions include whether the framework's conclusions generalize beyond the tested models and domains, and how to translate detected vulnerabilities into concrete robustness improvements.
