## Problem

RL from human feedback (RLHF) suffers from data inefficiency: it requires large amounts of preference data and extensive environment exploration to learn a reward function and optimize a policy. Existing meta-RL approaches for adaptation from human feedback (e.g., ANOLE) train the task encoder with reward signals but infer embeddings from preference data at test time, creating a data-type mismatch that prevents the encoder from capturing preference-relevant structure across tasks. This paper addresses the gap by designing a task embedding space that is explicitly aligned with human preference orders.

## Input / Output

During meta-training, the method consumes trajectories with reward signals (state, action, next-state, reward, optimal Q-value tuples) from multiple sampled tasks. During meta-test adaptation, it takes only a small number of trajectories without rewards (at most 20) and binary preference queries comparing trajectory pairs (at most 10 queries) from a human oracle on the new task. The output is a task-specific policy π_θπ(·|z_π^{T_new}) decoded from the inferred task embedding, requiring no reward signals or further RL optimization at test time.

## Method

POEM trains a VAE-based encoder that maps task context to a latent embedding, which is then split into a reward embedding z_r and a policy embedding z_π via two separate networks. The key architectural choice is an asymmetric similarity metric S(z^{T1}, z^{T2}) = ⟨z_r^{T1}, z_π^{T2}⟩ that measures the fitness of task T2's optimal policy to task T1, guaranteeing the existence of a preference-order-preserving embedding space (Theorem 1). Training combines four losses: KL regularization, reward reconstruction, policy optimization (SAC), and a Bradley-Terry preference loss that enforces Property 1 (embedding similarity order matches preference order). At adaptation time, the method samples candidate embeddings from N(0,I), iteratively eliminates candidates inconsistent with human preference queries using an active query strategy that maximizes candidate elimination, and selects the remaining embedding with the lowest preference loss to decode the task-specific policy.

## Results

POEM achieves 20%–50% improvement in average accumulated reward over the best baselines across all nine MuJoCo and MetaWorld scenarios, using only 10 preference queries and 20 reward-free trajectories during adaptation. The method reaches performance comparable to the meta-RL oracle (which has access to ground-truth reward signals during meta-test), confirming that the preference-order-preserving embedding enables effective few-shot policy derivation without explicit rewards. Under a noisy preference oracle (Boltzmann model), POEM maintains performance close to the noiseless setting, demonstrating robustness to human annotation errors.

## Baselines & Comparisons

POEM is compared against two families of baselines: (1) ANOLE (Ren et al., 2022) with its two adaptation strategies—Greedy Binary Search and Random Query—and (2) MAML-reward (Hejna, 2023), which trains a meta-reward model via MAML and then solves RL under the adapted reward. POEM outperforms all ANOLE variants by 20%–50% in accumulated reward across all nine scenarios, with the gap widening as the number of preference queries increases (1→10). MAML-reward is the weakest baseline, as it still requires solving a full RL problem after reward adaptation, making it data-inefficient in the few-shot setting. POEM also approaches the oracle upper bound (reward signals available at meta-test), whereas ANOLE and MAML-reward fall substantially short. Under noisy preference oracles, POEM's advantage over ANOLE is maintained, confirming that the preference-order-preserving structure provides inherent robustness that ANOLE's reward-decoder-based approach lacks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the context-based meta-RL paradigm (PEARL, VARIBAD) to the setting of adaptation from human feedback, where reward signals are unavailable at test time. It departs from ANOLE (Ren et al., 2022), which directly reuses a PEARL-style encoder trained on reward data and then infers embeddings from preference queries at test time—a data-type mismatch the authors identify as the key weakness. POEM instead trains the encoder with an explicit preference-order-preserving loss (using the Bradley-Terry model) alongside reward and policy reconstruction, and introduces an asymmetric cross-similarity metric S(z^T1, z^T2) = ⟨z_r^T1, z_π^T2⟩ that the authors prove is necessary (Proposition 1 shows the standard inner-product similarity is insufficient). The recursive sampling-and-training procedure and the VAE-based encoder architecture follow standard context-based meta-RL practice, while the theoretical convergence guarantee (Theorem 2) is the first provided for any meta-RL-with-human-feedback method.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By reducing the number of required human preference queries from the typical 1k–10k in RLHF to as few as 5–10, POEM addresses a critical practical bottleneck in deploying RL in settings where reward design is infeasible (personalized robotics, ambiguous goals). The preference-order-preserving property provides a clean geometric interpretation of the embedding space that could inform future work on embedding-space design for preference-based learning more broadly. Limitations include strong assumptions in the theory (well-trained encoder, normal posterior, optimal policy network), the complexity of the recursive training procedure, and evaluation restricted to continuous-control benchmarks; whether the approach transfers to discrete-action or high-dimensional perceptual tasks remains open.
