## Problem

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems are widely deployed to answer medical queries with citation-grounded responses, and are typically evaluated on factual accuracy and source attribution. This paper argues that even when individual facts are literally accurate and traceable to real sources, RAG systems are 'pragmatically misaligned': they decontextualize information, omit critical context, and reinforce patient biases in ways that can produce dangerous downstream interpretations for patients making health decisions.

## Input / Output

The study sends 178 procedurally generated medical queries (templated to probe specific pragmatic failure modes such as presupposing a disputed condition exists or embedding opposing safety valences) to two production RAG systems: Google AI Overview and Perplexity AI (llama-3.1-sonar-huge-128k-online). Responses are evaluated by dual GPT-4o LLM-as-a-judge against predetermined binary criteria (e.g., whether a disputed condition's controversy is mentioned, whether rarity statistics or procedure benefits are included), with manual adjudication of low-confidence disagreements.

## Method

The core contribution is a theoretically-motivated empirical analysis of pragmatic misalignment in production RAG systems, grounded in Gricean pragmatics and the Rational Speech Acts (RSA) framework. The authors design four query categories—disputed conditions (n=13), procedure safety with opposing valences (n=28), procedure complications (n=28), and miscellaneous—totaling 178 queries, and collect responses from Google AI Overview (over 3 days) and Perplexity AI. Quantitative results show, for example, that presupposing a disputed condition's existence drops the rate of controversy acknowledgment from 100% to 56% (Google) / 69% (Perplexity), and that only 4–5% of complication responses mention rarity statistics. A qualitative analysis then organizes observed failures into three pragmatic failure modes: (A) narrow literal interpretation of patient intent that ignores presuppositions and ambiguity, (B) decontextualization and misrepresentation of source intent, and (C) failure to reason about downstream interpretive and emotional consequences of generated text. The paper closes with concrete proposals for pragmatic benchmarks, RSA-inspired training objectives (e.g., simulated listener-speaker objectives for citation grounding), and HCI directions for empathetic, context-aware medical communication.

## Results

For disputed medical conditions, both Google AI Overview and Perplexity correctly flag the controversy in 100% of direct queries, but this drops sharply to 56% (Google) and 69% (Perplexity) when the query presupposes the condition's existence (e.g., 'Symptoms of <condition>'). For procedure-complication queries, responses mention rarity statistics in only 4% (Google) and 5% (Perplexity) of cases, and mention procedure benefits in only 6% (Google) and 10% (Perplexity). For safety queries with opposing valence ('why is X safe' vs. 'why is X dangerous'), the Jaccard similarity of cited sources is only 0.16 (Google) and 0.31 (Perplexity), indicating the systems retrieve and emphasize drastically different material depending on the query's framing.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position/analysis paper rather than a method paper, so it does not propose a new system to benchmark against baselines. Instead, it compares two production RAG search engines—Google AI Overview and Perplexity AI—across 178 procedurally generated medical queries. Both systems exhibit the same qualitative failure modes (decontextualization, confirmation-bias reinforcement, omission of source intent), with Perplexity performing slightly better on disputed-condition recognition (69% vs. 56%) and benefit mention (10% vs. 6%), but both remaining far below clinically appropriate levels. The paper also contrasts RAG-generated responses against what a vanilla search engine (which surfaces source documents for the user to interpret directly) would provide, arguing that the latter is currently the safer option. No head-to-head method comparison is made; the 'baseline' is effectively the ideal of a pragmatic, clinician-like communicator.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the formal pragmatic communication literature (Gricean maxims, Rational Speech Acts framework, Relevance Theory) as an analytical lens for evaluating retrieval-augmented generation systems in the medical domain. It departs from the standard RAG evaluation paradigm—which focuses on factuality, citation accuracy, and hallucination reduction (Shuster et al., 2021)—by arguing that even perfectly citation-grounded responses can be pragmatically misleading. The empirical analysis builds on prior observations of LLM sycophancy and compliance with presuppositional queries (Chen et al., 2024; Kaur et al., 2023) but extends them to production search engines and frames the issue through the lens of downstream patient decision-making rather than surface-level factual correctness.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a widely held assumption in the RAG community: that grounding responses in citable sources is sufficient to make them safe for high-stakes domains like medicine. The quantitative findings (e.g., only 4–5% of complication responses mention rarity statistics; a 44–34% drop in acknowledging disputed conditions when queries presuppose their existence) are concerning for production systems used by millions of patients. Key open questions include how to operationalize pragmatic reasoning in RAG training objectives, how to ethically study downstream user belief changes, and whether the proposed Rational Speech Acts–inspired approaches can scale to real-time search. The paper explicitly acknowledges it cannot directly measure patient harm, leaving the causal chain from misleading response to harmful decision as an important gap.
