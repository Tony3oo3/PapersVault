## Problem

Theoretical understanding of catastrophic forgetting in continual learning has been largely confined to linear models or lazy-training (NTK) assumptions that do not capture feature learning dynamics in practical networks. This paper addresses the gap by establishing a unified theoretical framework for a two-layer CNN with polynomial ReLU activation, revealing how the geometric angle between task signal vectors governs whether forgetting is benign or harmful.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252455/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The theoretical model takes sequential binary classification tasks where each data point decomposes into a signal patch (aligned with a task-specific vector μ_k) and a noise patch (Gaussian, orthogonal to the signal), with the signal-to-noise ratio controlling learnability. The two-layer ReLU^q CNN (q>2) is trained via full-batch gradient descent on each task in sequence, and the key output is a theoretical bound on the test error of the previous task as a function of cos θ_{1,2}. For empirical validation, inputs are grayscale MNIST digits (28×28) or RGB CIFAR-100 images (32×32) organized into binary classification pairs, with outputs being class predictions and measured forgetting metrics.

## Method

The paper applies feature learning theory via a signal-noise decomposition of each weight vector into a signal coefficient γ(μ_k) along the task signal direction and noise coefficients ρ(ξ_{k,i}) along noise directions, reducing the CNN training dynamics to tracking these scalar coefficients under gradient descent. The central insight is that the cosine similarity cos θ_{1,2} between the two task signal vectors determines forgetting: when cos θ_{1,2} ≥ −C₁ (acute or small obtuse angles), the neurons that learned task T₁ continue to support correct classification (benign forgetting), whereas when cos θ_{1,2} < −C₂ (large obtuse angles), the neurons learning μ₂ actively antagonize those learning μ₁, causing harmful forgetting. For replay-based CL, the authors show that including old-task examples in the training set expands the benign-forgetting angular range to cos θ_{1,2} ≥ −(1+C₂)/2 by stabilizing the signal coefficients of the previous task. These theoretical results inspire a mid-angle sampling strategy for the replay buffer, which selects examples with moderate cosine similarity to the class prototype rather than random or extreme-angle examples, and is validated within the iCaRL framework on CIFAR-100.

## Results

On CIFAR100-10 (10 tasks × 10 classes), mid-angle sampling achieves 48.02% accuracy on previously trained classes, outperforming random sampling (47.17%), herding (47.40%), small-angle (45.63%), and big-angle (45.34%) sampling. On CIFAR100-5 (5 tasks × 20 classes), mid-angle sampling reaches 56.51% accuracy versus 56.08% for random and 56.12% for herding. In terms of average forgetting (lower is better), mid-angle sampling achieves 14.84% on CIFAR100-10 and 10.15% on CIFAR100-5, the lowest among all methods, compared to 15.72% (random) and 15.51% (herding) on CIFAR100-10.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the proposed mid-angle sampling against four baselines within the iCaRL replay framework: random sampling, small-angle sampling, big-angle sampling, and herding (the original iCaRL strategy). Mid-angle sampling consistently outperforms all baselines on both accuracy and forgetting metrics across both CIFAR100-10 and CIFAR100-5 settings. On CIFAR100-10, mid-angle sampling gains +0.85% accuracy over random and +0.62% over herding, while reducing average forgetting by 0.88 and 0.67 percentage points respectively. The angle-based baselines (small-angle and big-angle) perform worst, confirming the theoretical prediction that extreme angles (too similar or too dissimilar to the prototype) lead to higher forgetting. The improvements, while statistically significant given the small standard deviations, are modest in absolute magnitude (roughly 0.5–1% accuracy gain), which the authors acknowledge as a limitation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the feature learning theory framework of Cao et al. (2022) on benign overfitting in two-layer ReLU^q CNNs to the continual learning setting, adapting the signal-noise decomposition and two-stage convergence analysis to sequential tasks. It departs from the dominant line of CL theory that relies on linear regression models (Evron et al., 2022; Lin et al., 2023; Ding et al., 2024) or neural tangent kernel / lazy training assumptions (Bennani et al., 2020; Doan et al., 2021), which the authors argue cannot capture feature learning dynamics. The mid-angle sampling strategy is a direct practical corollary of the theoretical finding that the cosine similarity between task signal vectors governs the benign/harmful forgetting boundary.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first theoretical framework to characterize forgetting in CL using feature learning theory with a practical (non-linearized) neural network, providing a clear geometric explanation—via the angle between task signal vectors—for when forgetting is benign versus harmful and how replay mechanistically expands the benign-forgetting angular range. However, the framework is limited to binary classification under a single-head setting, and the practical gains from mid-angle sampling are modest (e.g., ~0.85% accuracy improvement over random sampling on CIFAR100-10). Open questions include whether the angle-based characterization generalizes to multi-class, multi-head, or deeper architectures, and whether more principled replay strategies can be derived from the theoretical mechanism.
