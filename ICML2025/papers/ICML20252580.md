## Problem

Value Iteration Networks (VINs) fail to scale to long-term, large-scale planning: success rates drop below 40% on 100×100 mazes and to 0% on 35×35 mazes when planning horizons exceed 60 steps. The paper identifies two root causes — the limited representational capacity of the invariant latent transition kernel and the vanishing/exploding gradient problem that prevents training sufficiently deep planning modules.

## Input / Output

For maze navigation, the input is a binary M×M obstacle/road map image plus start and goal positions; for continuous control (Point/Ant Maze), it is a 4M×4M top-down view augmented with agent/goal coordinates and body-part velocities; for rover navigation, it is a terrain orthomosaic image cropped into 18×18 patches. The output is a probability distribution over discrete actions (4-directional movement) or continuous control torques, produced by a policy mapping module that reads the final latent value function. The observation is first mapped to a latent MDP (reward matrix and transition kernel) before any planning occurs, so the network never explicitly models the environment's dynamics.

## Method

DT-VIN introduces two architectural changes to the standard VIN. First, it replaces the invariant transition kernel T^inv ∈ R^{|A|×F×F} with a fully dynamic kernel T^dyn ∈ R^{M×M×|A|×F×F} that varies with both the latent state (i,j) and the observation x; the observation-dependence is implemented via a lightweight CNN transition mapping module that maps each local F'×F' patch to a |A|×F×F kernel, and a softmax is applied to kernel values to prevent gradient explosion. Second, it proposes an adaptive highway loss that adds the task loss directly at every hidden layer n ≥ l (where l is the known expert path length) and n mod J = 0, creating skip connections from intermediate layers to the final loss rather than between adjacent layers as in ResNets. This selective, depth-aware loss construction mitigates vanishing gradients without disrupting the value-iteration computation, enabling stable training of 5000-layer planning modules that solve 1800-step planning tasks.

## Results

On 100×100 maze navigation (the most challenging setting), DT-VIN with 5000 layers achieves ~100% success rate for shortest path lengths (SPLs) up to 600, 99.56% for SPLs in [600,1200], and 88.65% for SPLs in [1200,1800]. On 35×35 mazes, DT-VIN achieves near-perfect success rates (≥99.77%) across all SPL ranges up to 300. On continuous control (100×100 Ant Maze), DT-VIN reaches 93.26% success rate, outperforming the second-best baseline (GPPN at 61.78%) by over 31 percentage points. On rover navigation with 630×630 terrain images, DT-VIN achieves 77.4% success rate versus 71.71% for VIN and 76.31% for GPPN.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DT-VIN decisively outperforms all three baselines across every maze size and SPL range tested. VIN (Tamar et al., 2016) is the weakest baseline: on 100×100 mazes it achieves only 45.05% SR for SPLs in [1,600] and drops to 0% beyond SPL 100; on 35×35 mazes it also hits 0% for SPLs above 100. GPPN (Lee et al., 2018) performs better on short-term tasks (95.71% on 35×35, SPL [1,100]) but collapses to 0% for SPLs above 200 on 35×35 and above 600 on 100×100. Highway VIN (Wang et al., 2024a) is the strongest baseline, maintaining 54.40% on 35×35 at SPL [200,300] and 69.12% on 100×100 at SPL [1,600], but it still drops to 0% for SPLs above 600 on 100×100 mazes. DT-VIN is the only method that maintains non-zero success rates across the full 1800-step planning horizon on 100×100 mazes, achieving 88.65% even at the longest SPLs. On continuous control (100×100 Ant Maze), DT-VIN's 93.26% SR exceeds GPPN (61.78%) by 31.5 points and Highway VIN (52.91%) by 40.4 points.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Value Iteration Network (VIN) lineage by addressing two specific architectural deficiencies: the invariant latent transition kernel and the limited depth of the planning module. It builds most directly on Highway VIN (Wang et al., 2024a), which first established the depth–planning-horizon connection and introduced skip connections for VINs, but departs from it by replacing the gating-based highway mechanism with a simpler adaptive highway loss that connects intermediate layers directly to the final loss based on path length. The dynamic transition kernel draws on the broader idea of input-dependent convolution (dynamic convolution in CV) but applies it specifically to the latent MDP's transition structure, a component that prior VIN variants (GPPN, dVIN, AVIN) left invariant.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DT-VIN demonstrates that VIN-style differentiable planners can scale to 5000 layers and ~1800 planning steps, a regime where all prior VIN variants (VIN, GPPN, Highway VIN) fail completely, opening the door to long-horizon planning in large spatial environments. The adaptive highway loss is a simple, general-purpose training trick that could potentially transfer to other deep iterative architectures. However, the upper bound on scalability remains unknown, the computational cost is non-trivial (61 GB GPU memory for 5000 layers), and the evaluation is confined to grid-based planning and maze-like continuous control; whether the approach extends to truly high-dimensional or stochastic environments is an open question.
