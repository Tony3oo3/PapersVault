## Problem

Despite extensive study of transformer in-context learning, the training dynamics of multi-head softmax attention on linear regression tasks remain poorly understood under standard random initialization. Prior theoretical analyses relied on constrained regimes—NTK, mean-field approximations, or specialized initialization protocols—leaving open fundamental questions about how learned weight patterns emerge, what statistical properties the final model possesses, and whether multi-head softmax attention offers measurable advantages over single-head or linear attention architectures.

## Input / Output

The model takes L covariate-response pairs {(x_ℓ, y_ℓ)} and a query covariate x_q, embedded into a (d+1)×(L+1) matrix where the last column holds the query and the last row holds responses (with a zero at the query position). A one-layer multi-head softmax attention with H heads—deliberately omitting layer normalization and positional embeddings due to the permutation invariance of the regression task—processes this embedding and outputs a scalar prediction ŷ_q read from the (d+1, L+1)-th entry. Training minimizes the population mean-squared error over the joint distribution of the coefficient vector β, covariates, and Gaussian noise, with no explicit sequence-length parameter in the architecture.

## Method

The paper decomposes the trained attention into KQ and OV circuits and shows that random-initialization training converges to a universal structure: diagonal KQ matrices (ω^(h)·I_d) with last-entry-only OV vectors (μ^(h)), reducing the model to 2H scalar parameters. A two-stage gradient flow analysis on a reparameterized approximate loss explains the emergence: Stage I establishes sign-matching (KQ and OV share signs per head) and zero-sum OV (Σμ^(h)≈0), while Stage II drives homogeneous KQ scaling (|ω^(h)|≈γ) and groups heads into positive, negative, and dummy categories. Under this structure, the multi-head predictor becomes a sum of two kernel regressors that, for small γ, approximates a one-step debiased gradient descent on centered covariates. Theorem 4.2 proves the learned model is nearly Bayesian-optimal up to a constant factor, outperforms single-head attention (which learns a nonparametric Nadaraya-Watson kernel estimator), and generalizes in sequence length at test time—advantages over linear attention that requires a fixed 1/L normalization factor.

## Results

The trained multi-head softmax attention model (H≥2) learns a predictor that closely matches the debiased gradient descent estimator, with effective learning rate η_eff ≈ 0.89 and ICL loss tracking the debiased GD curve across all tested sequence lengths. The learned KQ scaling is |ω^(h)| ≈ 0.13 and OV magnitude |μ^(h)| ≈ 3.5 for non-dummy heads, compared to ω ≈ 0.52 and μ ≈ 1.42 for single-head attention. In the high-dimensional regime (d/L → ξ), the learned predictor's risk is bounded by a constant factor above the Bayes risk, specifically E[ŷ_q^gd(η*)]/BayesRisk ≤ 1 + σ⁻²·{(1+ξσ²)(1+σ²+ξ⁻¹)}⁻¹. All softmax attention models (single- and multi-head) generalize in length, with ICL loss decreasing as test-time sequence length increases beyond the training length L=40.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares multi-head softmax attention against four baselines: single-head softmax attention, linear attention (Von Oswald et al., 2023), vanilla gradient descent, and the Bayes-optimal estimator. Multi-head attention (H≥2) clearly outperforms single-head attention in ICL loss, as the multi-head model learns a parametric debiased GD predictor while the single-head model is limited to a nonparametric Nadaraya-Watson kernel regressor with bandwidth d^{1/4}. Against linear attention, softmax attention wins decisively on length generalization: when trained on L=40 and tested on longer sequences, softmax attention's prediction error continues to decrease, while linear attention's error increases once the test length exceeds 40 (Figure 7), because linear attention bakes the 1/L normalization factor into its weights. Against the canonical estimators, the trained multi-head softmax attention closely tracks both the vanilla GD and debiased GD predictors in loss (Figure 3b), confirming that the learned algorithm is essentially one-step preconditioned gradient descent with debiased covariates. Finally, the learned model is provably within a constant factor of the Bayes risk in the proportional regime, establishing near-optimality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work connecting transformer attention to gradient-based optimization (Von Oswald et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024) from linear attention to multi-head softmax attention, and builds on the single-head softmax training dynamics analysis of Chen et al. (2024a). It adopts the KQ/OV circuit decomposition from Elhage et al. (2021) as its mechanistic framework. The key departure is showing that multi-head softmax attention learns a *debiased* gradient descent predictor through positive-negative head coupling, rather than the vanilla or preconditioned GD found in linear attention, and that this emerges from standard random initialization without specialized protocols.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a mechanistic explanation for why multi-head attention outperforms single-head attention in ICL (parametric GD vs. nonparametric kernel regression) and why softmax attention generalizes in sequence length while linear attention does not—insights directly relevant to understanding LLM in-context learning. Extensions to anisotropic covariates and multi-task settings (including a superposition phenomenon when tasks exceed heads) suggest the framework generalizes. Open questions include whether these patterns persist in deeper architectures, with layer normalization, or on realistic non-synthetic data, and whether the 'river valley' loss landscape and edge-of-stability dynamics identified here explain broader training behaviors in large-scale transformers.
