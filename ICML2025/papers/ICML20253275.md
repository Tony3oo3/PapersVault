## Problem

Despite parallel advances in large-scale AI and iPSC-based bioengineering, there has been no practical experimental platform to empirically validate computational models of biological neural computation beyond simulation. This position paper argues that in vitro neural networks—simpler, causally intervenable, and increasingly accessible via cloud-based platforms—offer a timely and unique testbed for reverse-engineering neural computation and for benchmarking next-generation AI approaches such as spiking neural networks and neuromorphic computing.

## Input / Output

The proposed control framework takes as input high-dimensional, noisy, stochastic neural activity recorded from in vitro cultures (via multi-electrode arrays sampling hundreds of channels at tens of kilobytes per second per channel, or via optogenetic readout) and produces a stimulation sequence—electrical pulses through specific electrodes or laser-induced optogenetic activation—as its output. The in vitro system is treated as an unknown stochastic transition function IVN(s_{t+1}|s_t, a_t), so the policy must operate without assumptions about internal dynamics, and closed-loop inference must meet sub-millisecond latency constraints to keep pace with millisecond-scale neural dynamics.

## Method

The paper proposes framing interaction with in vitro neural networks as a reinforcement learning control problem: a policy π_θ: S→A is trained to steer observable neural dynamics toward task-relevant states, with the implicit goal of discovering the system's working principles through successful control. Training follows a pretrain-then-fine-tune paradigm, where a large-scale policy sequence model is first trained on high-fidelity synthetic neural simulations and then adapted to limited real in vitro data, leveraging evidence that pre-trained representations can bridge sim-to-real gaps. Optimization can employ model-free or model-based RL, with surrogate gradient techniques (or exact gradients for leaky-integrate-and-fire models) enabling backpropagation through non-differentiable spiking dynamics. A key design principle is that the in vitro system itself is a 'trainable' component: ideally, as training progresses, the artificial policy should approximate the identity function, meaning the biological network has been reconfigured to solve the task autonomously—analogous to a teacher-student or knowledge-distillation setting with a biological student.

## Results

This is a position paper and does not report quantitative experimental results or benchmark evaluations. The authors argue that in vitro neural networks (e.g., iPSC-derived organoids on multi-electrode arrays) present a timely opportunity to test and validate computational models of neural computation beyond simulation. They reference prior proof-of-concept work, including Kagan et al. (2022) who demonstrated closed-loop interaction where in vitro neurons played a basic video game, and Li et al. (2024) who used a Soft Actor-Critic policy trained offline on prerecorded C. elegans data to discover viable neural control policies. The paper's central claim is qualitative: that ML-driven closed-loop control of in vitro systems can serve as a practical method of arbitration for reverse-engineering biological neural computation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not propose a new method to be benchmarked against baselines, and no direct quantitative comparisons are presented. The authors situate their argument relative to existing approaches by contrasting (a) purely simulation-based SNN/neuromorphic research, which they note has largely failed to demonstrate advantages over standard ANNs in the broader ML literature (citing an ICLR reviewer's observation), with (b) the proposed in vitro closed-loop control paradigm, which they argue provides a unique empirical testbed. They also contrast their control-based framing with data-driven neuroscience approaches (citing Jonas & Kording, 2017), arguing that the latter cannot arbitrate between equally plausible models without a functional criterion. No head-to-head performance comparisons are made, as the paper's contribution is a research agenda rather than a methodological advance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper carves new ground by reframing the interaction with in vitro neural cultures as a formal control/optimization problem for machine learning, rather than a purely neuroscientific data-analysis exercise. It builds conceptually on the proof-of-concept closed-loop work of Kagan et al. (2022) (in vitro neurons playing a video game) and the RL-based neural policy discovery of Li et al. (2024) (Soft Actor-Critic on C. elegans), but generalizes these into a broader research agenda: treating the in vitro network as an unknown stochastic transition function to be learned and exploited. The paper departs from the dominant simulation-only paradigm in SNN and neuromorphic research by arguing that empirical, causal intervention on living tissue is now feasible and necessary to validate computational models beyond simulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a critical gap: SNN and neuromorphic research has grown substantially but rarely demonstrates advantages over standard ANNs in real-world settings, and in vitro platforms could provide the missing empirical testbed. Key open questions include what simulation fidelity is needed for effective pretraining, how to design graduated benchmarks appropriate to current experimental capabilities, and whether the 'mortality' of biological substrates (inability to checkpoint or copy weights) yields genuine energy-efficiency insights transferable to silicon. The position also raises important ethical and practical barriers—donor consent, potential consciousness, latency constraints for closed-loop control—that must be addressed before the field can scale.
