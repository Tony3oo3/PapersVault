## Problem

Despite extensive empirical work on backdoor attacks in large language models, a rigorous theoretical understanding of when and why these attacks succeed remains lacking. This paper addresses that gap by providing a theoretical framework for analyzing how adversaries exploit the token selection process within the attention mechanism of transformer architectures to corrupt model predictions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252464/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a sequence of T d-dimensional tokens X ∈ R^{T×d} augmented with a single learnable prompt token p ∈ R^d, and outputs a scalar prediction f(X) ∈ R whose sign yields a binary classification label. Tokens are partitioned into a relevant set R carrying class-conditional signal and an irrelevant set I containing only Gaussian noise; in poisoned samples, a subset P ⊂ I is replaced with fixed poisoned signal vectors of strength α. The key structural detail is that only the prompt token p is optimized via gradient descent (with a fixed orthogonal key-query matrix W and a fixed linear head ν initialized by one gradient step), making the attention weights the sole mechanism through which the model selects which tokens to aggregate.

## Method

The paper analyzes a single-head self-attention transformer under prompt-tuning, where the model prediction is f(X) = ν^T X^T S(XW^T p), and proves that gradient descent on the logistic loss interpolates all poisoned training data. The central technical tool is a notion of 'uncertainty in token selection' (G_r and G_p), which quantifies the variance of softmax mass over relevant versus poisoned token sets at each training step. The main theorem (Theorem 4.1) characterizes five conditions on the cumulative uncertainty trajectories—balanced across classes, dominance of relevant-token variance, clean-signal dominance over poisoned influence, comparability of relevant and poisoned contributions, and poisoned-token variance dominance—under which the model correctly classifies clean test inputs while reliably misclassifying triggered inputs. The key insight is that the adversary succeeds by making the correlation between poisoned tokens and the (flipped) label exceed that between relevant tokens and the true label, causing attention to shift from relevant to poisoned tokens only when the trigger is present. Experiments on synthetic data with orthogonal signal directions validate all five conditions and demonstrate the predicted regime: successful backdoor injection requires the trigger strength α and poisoning ratio β to be sufficiently large but not overly dominant.

## Results

On a synthetic binary classification task (n=20 training samples, T=8 tokens, d=4000, |R|=|P|=1), the paper validates its theoretical predictions across three attack-strength regimes. In the ideal backdoor setting (α=4.0, β=0.1), the model achieves 100% standard test accuracy and 0% poisoned test accuracy, confirming successful backdoor injection. When the trigger is too weak (α=1.0, β=0.1), both accuracies reach 100%, indicating the attack fails. When the trigger is too strong (α=4.0, β=0.4), standard accuracy drops to 69.1% while poisoned accuracy remains 0%, showing that overly dominant triggers degrade clean-data generalization. Theorem 4.1's five conditions are empirically verified across a grid of (α, β) configurations, with all ratio constants (C₁–C₅) remaining within reasonable bounds.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper that does not compare its method against competing backdoor attack or defense baselines. Instead, the empirical section serves to validate the paper's own theoretical predictions (Theorem 4.1) on a controlled synthetic setup. The 'comparisons' are between different attack parameter regimes: the ideal setting (α=4.0, β=0.1) achieves the desired dual behavior (100% clean accuracy, 0% poisoned accuracy), while the insufficient-trigger setting (α=1.0) and overpowered-trigger setting (α=4.0, β=0.4) demonstrate the boundaries predicted by Assumptions (A3) and (A4). The paper positions its contribution as the first theoretical framework for understanding when and why backdoor attacks succeed in attention mechanisms, rather than proposing a new attack or defense to benchmark against existing ones.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate research threads: empirical backdoor attack studies in LLMs (Wan et al. 2023, Shi et al. 2023, Hubinger et al. 2024) and theoretical analyses of transformer optimization dynamics (Tarzanagh et al. 2023b, Sakamoto & Sato 2024). It directly builds on Tarzanagh et al.'s equivalence between W and p dynamics under gradient descent, and adapts Sakamoto & Sato's uncertainty-based framework for token selection, extending their benign-overfitting conditions to the adversarial setting. The key departure is that prior theoretical work on transformers addressed expressiveness or generalization under standard training, while prior backdoor work was purely empirical; this paper provides the first formal characterization of when and why backdoor triggers succeed in attention mechanisms.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first theoretical framework explaining the mechanism by which backdoor attacks exploit token selection in attention, identifying five concrete conditions that must hold simultaneously for an attack to succeed while preserving clean-data generalization. The 'sweet spot' finding—that triggers must be strong enough to dominate but not so dominant as to degrade clean performance (α=Θ(T), β=Θ(1/T²))—gives practitioners a principled understanding of attack calibration. However, the analysis is limited to single-head, single-layer transformers with a fixed linear head and synthetic data; extending to multi-head/multi-layer architectures, joint optimization of all parameters, and practical fine-tuning strategies (e.g., LoRA) remains open and is where the most impactful future work lies.
