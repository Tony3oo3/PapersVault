## Problem

Traditional antibody and vaccine design is myopic: it optimises binding to the current viral strain while ignoring that therapy-induced selective pressures drive the virus to evolve resistant variants, rendering the therapy ineffective over time. No existing framework designs antibodies that both anticipate and actively steer viral evolution toward less harmful variants.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252378/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a fixed virus antigen structure from the PDB (discretised into ~1.5M binding poses by Absolut!, pruned to the top 100 most relevant), a starting CDRH3 antibody amino acid sequence of length N_a, and a viral escape horizon H. It produces a trajectory of N optimised antibody sequences (the final one being the 'shaper'), the corresponding simulated viral escape trajectory of H+1 virus sequences, and the escape-averaged antibody fitness F_v^H(a). The binding function B(·,·) is computed via a JAX-accelerated reimplementation of Absolut! using the Miyazawa-Jernigan energy potential, achieving a 10,000× speedup over the original CPU implementation.

## Method

ADIOS frames antibody design as a two-player zero-sum game where the antibody's payoff R_a(v,a) = B(v,a) − B(t_a⁻,a) − B(v,t_v⁺) balances virus binding against anti-target and host-receptor binding, preventing degenerate strategies. The inner loop (simulated viral escape) evolves the virus over H generations using a population of P=15 mutants selected by a Boltzmann distribution over virus payoffs with inverse temperature β. The outer loop (antibody optimisation) is a genetic algorithm over N=30 meta-steps with population P_a=40, where each candidate antibody's fitness is the escape-averaged payoff F_v^H(a) estimated via η=5 Monte Carlo rollouts of the inner loop. The key distinction from myopic design is that the outer-loop objective integrates the antibody's performance across the entire viral escape trajectory rather than only against the initial virus, enabling the optimised 'shaper' antibodies to actively constrain viral evolutionary trajectories toward more targetable variants.

## Results

ADIOS-optimised shapers (H=100) significantly outperform myopic antibodies on the escape-averaged fitness F_v^100(a), with none of the myopic antibodies matching the top 10% of shapers on this long-term objective. The JAX reimplementation of Absolut! achieves a 10,000× speedup (1.8 s → 2.1×10⁻⁴ s per binding query). Viruses induced by H=100 shapers are consistently more exploitable by antibodies across all optimisation horizons, confirming active shaping of viral evolution. Shaping effects are confirmed across four additional pathogens (West Nile, Influenza, MERS-CoV, C. difficile), with C. difficile showing the strongest shaping signal.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between ADIOS shapers (optimised with horizon H=100) and myopic antibodies (H=0, optimised only for immediate binding). Shapers clearly win on the long-horizon escape-averaged objective F_v^100(a), while myopic antibodies retain an advantage on the short-term myopic objective R_a(v,a), reflecting a deliberate trade-off. Among shaper horizons, H=100 consistently outperforms H=20, H=10, and H=5 on F_v^100(a), though H=20 nearly matches H=100 when normalised by computational cost (number of binding simulator queries). The cross-evaluation experiment (Figure 5) shows that viruses evolved under H=100 shapers are more vulnerable to antibodies from all other horizons, demonstrating that the benefit is not merely robustness but active shaping. Under external pressure from a second myopic antibody (Equation 3), the shaping effect is reduced but remains clearly visible, confirming transferability to multi-therapy scenarios.

## Relation to Prior Work

ADIOS repurposes the opponent-shaping paradigm from multi-agent reinforcement learning (LOLA, M-FOS) into the protein-design domain, framing antibody-virus interaction as a two-player zero-sum game with nested inner/outer optimisation loops. For the binding function, it builds directly on Absolut! (Robert et al., 2022) and the Miyazawa-Jernigan energy potential, reimplementing the core computation in JAX for GPU acceleration. Conceptually, it departs from prior antibody design work (energy-based, language-model, or structure-based methods) that optimises only against the current antigen, and from viral-escape prediction methods (EVEscape, Han et al.) that forecast future strains without closing the loop to influence which strains emerge.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first proof-of-concept that a therapy can be optimised not merely to neutralise the present pathogen but to steer its evolutionary trajectory toward more targetable variants, with the 'attack is the best defence' result (shapers make evolved viruses more vulnerable to *other* antibodies) suggesting practical value for cocktail therapies. However, the evidence rests on a simplified binding model (discretised poses, Miyazawa-Jernigan matrix), single-point mutations, and a fixed antigen structure, so direct therapeutic translation is far off. Key open questions include whether the shaping effect persists under more realistic structural co-evolution (e.g., with AlphaFold3), in multi-antigen settings, and with continuous rather than discrete mutation processes.
