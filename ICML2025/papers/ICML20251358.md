## Problem

While catastrophic forgetting in continual learning is well-studied, it remains unclear how the order in which tasks are presented affects final performance and how to systematically optimize that order. Previous empirical work found that longest Hamiltonian paths on task dissimilarity graphs outperform shortest paths, but lacked a theoretical explanation for why, and existing theory (Lin et al., 2023) captured only absolute task positions rather than relative ones. This paper seeks to derive general analytical principles that explain and predict optimal task ordering.

## Input / Output

The method takes a set of P image classification tasks (binary or multi-class) and a small pilot dataset—potentially as little as 1% of training data per task—to estimate pairwise task similarity. Similarity is computed via zero-shot transfer: train a network on task A, measure its error on task B, normalize by the chance-level (label-shuffled) error, take the square root, and average over both A→B and B→A directions. The output is a single task ordering permutation designed to minimize the expected average error across all tasks after sequential training to convergence.

## Method

The paper analyzes task-order dependence in a linear teacher-student model with a low-dimensional latent factor, where task similarity is encoded through correlated Gaussian mixing matrices. They derive a closed-form expression (Theorem 3.1) showing the average final error depends only on the upper-triangular components of the input correlation matrix, making it inherently order-dependent rather than permutation-invariant. A linear perturbation analysis then decomposes the order effect into two interpretable factors: an absolute-order term yielding the periphery-to-core rule (learn least representative tasks first, most typical last) and a relative-order term yielding the max-path rule (maximize Hamiltonian path length on the task dissimilarity graph). These principles are validated on Fashion-MNIST, CIFAR-10, and CIFAR-100 using both MLPs and CNNs, with task similarity estimated from as little as 1% of data, showing robust improvements over random ordering.

## Results

For chain-structured tasks with five tasks, the best task order achieves approximately a 7-fold lower final error than the worst order, underscoring the critical importance of task ordering. On Fashion-MNIST (MLP), CIFAR-10 (CNN), and CIFAR-100 (CNN), both the periphery-to-core rule and the max-path rule robustly outperform their counterparts (core-to-periphery and min-path) as well as random ordering, with the periphery-to-core advantage increasing as the number of tasks grows from 3 to 7. Notably, estimating task similarity from only ~1% of the training data was sufficient to achieve a performance gain comparable to using the full dataset, while at 0.1% (~10 samples per task) the advantage over random ordering became non-significant.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four task-ordering strategies—periphery-to-core, core-to-periphery, max-path, and min-path—against random ordering across three datasets and two architectures (MLP on Fashion-MNIST; CNN on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100). The periphery-to-core rule consistently outperforms core-to-periphery and random ordering in all settings, with the margin widening as the number of tasks increases (Fig. 5g). The max-path rule similarly beats both min-path and random ordering, confirming and providing theoretical justification for the empirical finding of Bell & Lawrence (2022). On average, the periphery-to-core rule slightly outperforms the max-path rule, though the difference is small. When task similarity is estimated from only 1% of the data, both rules retain a significant advantage over random ordering; however, at 0.1% data the gain becomes non-significant, particularly for CIFAR-100 in the negative-similarity regime.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the analytical framework of Lin et al. (2023) on task-order effects in linear continual learning by introducing a latent-factor teacher-student model that captures input similarity, revealing that tasks' *relative* positions in the sequence—not just their absolute positions—govern final performance. It also provides the first theoretical explanation for the empirical finding of Bell & Lawrence (2022) that the longest Hamiltonian path on a task dissimilarity graph outperforms the shortest, decomposing the order dependence into a periphery-to-core (absolute position) component and a max-path (relative distance) component via linear perturbation analysis. The work sits within the broader teacher-student statistical physics tradition (Gardner & Derrida, 1989; Bahri et al., 2020) applied to continual learning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The two derived principles (periphery-to-core and max-path) offer a simple, data-driven recipe for task ordering that requires only O(P) training runs for similarity estimation and as little as 1% of the data, making it practical for settings where full data access is unavailable (e.g., parallel data acquisition in autonomous driving or medical imaging). Key limitations include the linear-model derivation, the assumption that each task is learned once to convergence, and validation restricted to binary image classification; extending these rules to non-convergent training, multi-epoch settings, and domains like deep RL or language model corpus ordering remains open. The ~7-fold performance gap between best and worst orders for just five tasks underscores that task ordering is a first-order design choice, not a minor hyperparameter.
