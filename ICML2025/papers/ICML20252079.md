## Problem

Prior LLM privacy attacks (membership inference, extractable memorization) assume the adversary has access to a complete, ordered training sample or at least a long prefix. This paper asks whether fine-tuned LLMs remain vulnerable when the attacker possesses only a small set of unordered, publicly available fragments (e.g., a few medical terms from social media) and seeks to infer additional sensitive fragments about a specific individual.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252079/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The attack takes an unordered set of public fragments S (typically 4–30 short keywords like medical terms or legal phrases) and a candidate target fragment y*, then queries three black-box models—target (fine-tuned on data containing the individual), shadow (fine-tuned without the individual), and world (an ensemble of models fine-tuned on neither)—with a task-appropriate prompt embedding S, extracting the probability each model assigns to y*. The output is a scalar likelihood score ℓ that y* co-occurs with S in the individual's training sample, thresholded at τ to yield a binary membership prediction.

## Method

The paper formalizes the PIFI (Partial-Information Fragment Inference) threat model and proposes two data-blind INFER functions. LR-Attack computes the likelihood ratio p_D / p_D' (target-model probability of y* given prompt(S) divided by shadow-model probability), directly adapting the Neyman-Pearson shadow-model framework from membership inference to the fragment-level setting. PRISM (Posterior-Refined Inference for Subset Membership) extends this by incorporating a world-model probability p_world as a prior: it treats the LR-Attack statistic as evidence for sample membership M and applies a Bayesian update, using p_world to capture the population-level association between S and y* so that common fragments (e.g., 'parkinsons') do not trigger false positives. A data-aware LightGBM classifier trained on the 3D vector [p_D, p_D', p_world] serves as an upper-bound baseline. Experiments on medical and legal summarization tasks across Llama-3, Qwen-2, and Mistral-7B models show both data-blind attacks rival the classifier at low FPRs, with LR-Attack excelling on rare fragments and PRISM on common ones.

## Results

On the medical summarization task (MTS Dialog), all three PIFI attack methods substantially outperform random guessing across Llama-3-8B, Qwen-2-7B, and Mistral-7B. The data-blind LR-Attack achieves 9.5% TPR at 2% FPR on Qwen-2-7B (exceeding the data-aware Classifier's 8.2%), while PRISM reaches 11.5% TPR at 5% FPR on Llama-3-8B. ROC-AUC scores range from 0.59 (LR-Attack on Mistral-7B) to 0.73 (Classifier on Qwen-2-7B), confirming that meaningful fragment-level leakage persists even without access to labeled in-distribution data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary baseline is a data-aware Classifier (LightGBM) trained on labeled in-distribution examples, which serves as an upper-bound reference for the data-blind attacks. LR-Attack matches or exceeds the Classifier on Qwen-2-7B (9.5% vs. 8.2% TPR@2% FPR; 16.2% vs. 16.3% TPR@5% FPR) but underperforms on Llama-3-8B (5.3% vs. 6.3%) and Mistral-7B (2.9% vs. 5.5%). PRISM is more consistent across model families (4.2–4.4% TPR@2% FPR) and achieves the best AUC on Llama-3-8B (0.67 vs. 0.68 for Classifier), though it trails the Classifier on Qwen-2-7B AUC (0.66 vs. 0.73). When stratified by fragment frequency, LR-Attack dominates for rare fragments (17.5% TPR@2% FPR vs. 10.8% for Classifier), while PRISM nearly matches the Classifier for common fragments (AUC 0.73 vs. 0.74). All methods substantially outperform random guessing, with TPRs doubling or quadrupling chance at low FPRs, confirming that the PIFI threat model captures real, exploitable signal in fine-tuned LLMs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the membership inference (MI) and extractable memorization (EM) attack paradigms by relaxing their core assumption of access to a complete, ordered training sample. The LR-Attack directly adapts the shadow-model likelihood-ratio framework from Carlini et al. (2022a), while PRISM introduces a Bayesian posterior update using an external 'world model' prior—a mechanism not present in prior MI or EM work. The PIFI threat model is positioned as a unification of MI and EM under strictly weaker adversarial assumptions (unordered fragments rather than full samples or ordered prefixes), carving out a middle ground that prior literature had left unaddressed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As organizations increasingly fine-tune LLMs on sensitive medical and legal data and deploy them as public-facing chatbots, the PIFI threat model highlights a realistic and previously unquantified privacy risk: even a handful of publicly available fragments (e.g., a social media post mentioning a diagnosis) can enable extraction of additional sensitive information. The finding that DP fine-tuning suppresses LR-Attack but not PRISM or the Classifier suggests that no single defense is sufficient, and that the field needs fragment-level privacy guarantees beyond sample-level DP. Open questions include whether the attacks scale to larger production models, whether more sophisticated prompt engineering or multi-turn interactions amplify the threat, and what practical defenses (beyond DP) can mitigate fragment-level leakage without destroying model utility.
