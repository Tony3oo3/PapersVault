## Problem

Existing zeroth-order methods for performative prediction (optimization with unknown decision-dependent distributions) treat the known objective function f as a black box, discarding its readily available gradient information. This limits the efficiency of the random search direction and inflates the number of real-world samples needed to reach a stationary point, which is costly since each sample requires deploying a decision in practice.

## Input / Output

The method consumes function evaluations f(x, ξ) at perturbed points, the analytically computable partial gradient ∇_x f(x, ξ) (since f is known), and samples ξ drawn from the unknown distribution D(x). It outputs a stationary point x̂ satisfying E[‖∇F(x̂)‖²] ≤ ε². The critical cost metric is sample complexity—the total number of ξ ~ D(x) draws—because each draw requires a real-world deployment (e.g., selling products at price x to observe demand), making sample efficiency the primary practical constraint rather than wall-clock time.

## Method

The core mechanism is a two-point gradient estimator g = [f(x+μu, ξ¹) − f(x−μu, ξ²)]/(2μ) · u where the perturbation direction u is drawn from a structured Gaussian N(0, Σ) with Σ = (α/d)I_d + (1−α)hhᵀ, and h is the normalized partial gradient ∇_x f(x, ξ) estimated from samples. This biases the random search toward the descent direction of the known function while retaining isotropic exploration controlled by the balance parameter α, which is annealed from a small value (emphasizing guidance) toward 1 (unbiased estimation) over iterations. Two variants are provided: GZO-NS computes the partial gradient from fresh samples at each iteration, while GZO-HS reuses historical samples from a sliding window of past iterations to avoid extra sampling. The smoothing parameter μ is also geometrically decayed. Under standard smoothness and Lipschitz assumptions, the method achieves O(σ³ d⁴ ε⁻⁶) worst-case sample complexity, improving the d-dependence over the prior O(σ³ d^{9/2} ε⁻⁶) bound without requiring any correlation assumption between the partial gradient and the true gradient.

## Results

On the multiproduct pricing task (10 products, 40 buyers), GZO-NS and GZO-HS achieve objective values of −12.53 and −12.85 (week 2/21–2/27), substantially outperforming ZO-TG (−10.92), ZO-OG (5.10), and ZO-OGVR (−6.30). On strategic classification (12-dim classifier, 11 features), GZO-HS achieves the best test AUC of 0.69 at τ=8.0 and test accuracy of 0.63, versus 0.50/0.49 for ZO-TG, 0.51/0.52 for ZO-OG, and 0.46/0.48 for ZO-OGVR. Theoretically, the proposed methods achieve a worst-case sample complexity of O(σ³d⁴ε⁻⁶), improving upon O(σ³d^{9/2}ε⁻⁶) (Hikima & Takeda, 2025b) and O(G⁶d²ε⁻⁶) (Liu et al., 2024a).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against three existing zeroth-order baselines: ZO-TG (two-point gradient with isotropic Gaussian direction, analogous to Hikima & Takeda 2025b and Liu et al. 2024a), ZO-OG (one-point gradient, analogous to Ray et al. 2022), and ZO-OGVR (one-point gradient with variance reduction, Hikima & Takeda 2025b Algorithm 1). GZO-NS and GZO-HS consistently outperform all three baselines on both applications. On multiproduct pricing, the guided methods reduce the objective by approximately 1.5–5.4 units over ZO-TG and by 13–18 units over ZO-OG across all six weeks; the advantage over ZO-OGVR is 2.5–5.0 units. On strategic classification, GZO-HS achieves a test AUC of 0.62–0.69 versus 0.44–0.51 for all baselines, and a train loss of 0.78–0.88 versus 1.21–1.35 (ZO-TG), 2.75–4.39 (ZO-OG), and 1.51–2.24 (ZO-OGVR). The performance gap is most pronounced for ZO-OG, whose one-point estimator has high variance, and is least pronounced for ZO-TG, which already uses a two-point estimator but lacks the guided direction. All reported differences are statistically significant (two-sided t-test, p < 0.05) except one case (GZO-HS vs. ZO-TG on week 7/18–7/24).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of zeroth-order methods for performative prediction (Hikima & Takeda, 2025b; Liu et al., 2024a; Ray et al., 2022) by incorporating partial gradient information from the known function f into the sampling direction, inspired by guided evolutionary strategies (Maheswaranathan et al., 2019). While prior zeroth-order methods treat f as a black box and sample from an isotropic Gaussian, this work constructs a non-isotropic covariance Σ that blends the isotropic component with a rank-one component aligned to the estimated partial gradient. The theoretical contribution is a tighter convergence analysis yielding an improved worst-case sample complexity of O(σ³d⁴ε⁻⁶) compared to O(σ³d^{9/2}ε⁻⁶) for the closest prior method, achieved through careful tuning of mini-batch size and step size rather than a fundamentally new estimator.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The sample complexity improvement (d⁴ vs. d^{9/2}) is practically meaningful in performative prediction settings where each sample requires deploying a real-world decision (e.g., setting product prices), making sample efficiency a critical constraint. The method's robustness—requiring no assumptions about correlation between the partial gradient and the true gradient—makes it broadly applicable without distributional assumptions beyond standard smoothness and Lipschitz conditions. Open questions include adaptive tuning of the balance parameter α (which controls the trade-off between bias and directional information) and principled selection of the historical window size p in Algorithm 2, both of which are currently set heuristically. The strategic classification experiment notably violates the Wasserstein smoothness assumption (Remark 5.1), suggesting the method may be more robust than its theory guarantees, but this is not formally established.
