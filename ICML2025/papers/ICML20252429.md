## Problem

Existing GED approximation methods face a trilemma: neural approaches require NP-hard ground-truth training data, operate as uninterpretable black boxes, and fail to generalize across domains without expensive retraining; non-neural heuristics avoid these issues but produce significantly worse approximations. GRAIL addresses this by shifting the learning objective from predicting a GED value to generating an interpretable program that computes one, eliminating the need for ground-truth supervision entirely.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252429/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes pairs of node-labeled undirected graphs (provided as adjacency matrices with node label vectors); smaller graphs are padded with dummy nodes labeled ε to equalize sizes. At inference, the system executes a fixed set of b≈15 discovered programs, each producing a weight matrix for a bipartite graph, then returns the minimum GED upper bound across all b mappings along with the corresponding node alignment (edit path). The training output is executable code, so inference is CPU-bound and requires no GPU or model weights.

## Method

GRAIL reduces GED approximation to maximum weight bipartite matching, where edge weights are computed by LLM-generated programs rather than learned neural parameters. An LLM (Gemini-1.5 Pro) is prompted to iteratively improve a weight-matrix program, with prompt construction guided by a FunSearch-style evolutionary algorithm using an islands model that selects high-scoring, short programs for mutation. A critical design choice is that the predicted GED is always an upper bound on the true value (being the minimum over a subset of mappings), so minimizing this upper bound on unlabeled graph pairs is equivalent to minimizing approximation error without any ground-truth supervision. A budget-constrained submodular selection (greedy hill-climbing) picks b complementary programs from the pool to maximize collective coverage across diverse graph pairs, and the process terminates when the objective converges.

## Results

GRAIL achieves an average rank of 2 across six datasets (AIDS, Linux, IMDB, ogbg-molhiv, ogbg-code2, ogbg-molpcba) in RMSE, outperforming all 11 neural and non-neural baselines on average. On the IMDB dataset, GRAIL achieves RMSE 0.55, dramatically outperforming the best neural baseline GEDGNN (4.43) and ERIC (42.44). GRAIL-MIX, trained once on a mixture of all datasets, achieves an average rank of 2.17, demonstrating strong cross-domain generalization without dataset-specific training. GRAIL also achieves near-perfect Exact Match Ratio (≈1) on Linux and 0.99 on IMDB, while requiring no ground-truth GED labels during training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GRAIL outperforms all five neural baselines (GREED, GEDGNN, ERIC, H2MN, GRAPHEDX) on average, with the most dramatic wins on IMDB where GRAIL's RMSE of 0.55 is 8× lower than GEDGNN (4.43) and 77× lower than ERIC (42.44). Against GREED, the strongest neural baseline, GRAIL wins on 4 of 6 datasets (AIDS: 0.57 vs 0.61; Linux: 0.13 vs 0.41; IMDB: 0.55 vs 4.8; ogbg-code2: 4.22 vs 5.52) but loses on ogbg-molhiv (2.96 vs 1.75) and ogbg-molpcba (3.18 vs 2.48), where GREED's multi-depth jumping knowledge provides an advantage. Against all six non-neural baselines (ADJ-IP, NODE, LP-GED-F2, BRANCH-TIGHT, COMPACT-MIP, IPFP), GRAIL achieves lower RMSE on every dataset, with particularly large margins on IMDB (0.55 vs 7.36–69.45) and ogbg-code2 (4.22 vs 8.34–19.46). GRAIL also outperforms an ensemble of 15 non-neural heuristics while being 168× faster. The key differentiator is that GRAIL achieves these results without any NP-hard ground-truth GED labels, whereas all neural baselines require 10,000 graph pairs with true GED values (up to 15 days of computation).

## Relation to Prior Work

GRAIL builds directly on the FunSearch framework (Romera-Paredes et al., 2024) for LLM-guided evolutionary program search, adopting its islands model, score-based clustering, and selection mechanism. However, it departs fundamentally from the dominant GED approximation paradigm: rather than training a GNN to predict GED values (as in GREED, GEDGNN, ERIC, H2MN, GraphEdX) or solving optimization formulations (MIP, branch-and-bound), GRAIL reframes the problem as learning a program that computes bipartite-matching weights, with the LLM generating and evolving candidate programs. The submodular greedy selection of complementary programs (Problem 3) and the upper-bound guarantee from evaluating only a subset of mappings are novel design choices that eliminate the need for NP-hard ground-truth supervision entirely.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GRAIL addresses three structural limitations of neural GED approximators simultaneously: the NP-hard cost of generating training data, the black-box nature of GNN predictions, and the inability to generalize across domains without retraining. The demonstrated cross-domain transfer (a single program set handling molecules, code ASTs, movie co-occurrence graphs, and protein networks) is a capability no prior neural or non-neural method has achieved for GED. Open questions include whether the paradigm extends to other combinatorial optimization problems, whether the discovered programs can be further refined by domain experts, and how sensitive performance is to the choice of underlying LLM. The CPU-bound inference also makes the approach deployable in resource-constrained settings, unlike GPU-dependent neural baselines.
