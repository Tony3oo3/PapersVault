## Problem

Hippocampal place fields exhibit three distinct phenomena during navigation learning—high density at reward locations, elongation against the trajectory, and ongoing spatial drift during stable behavior—but disparate mechanisms have been proposed for each, and no single normative framework unifies them or clarifies their computational role in policy learning. This paper asks whether a single reward-maximization objective, with the TD error driving both policy and representational updates, can naturally produce all three phenomena and whether these representational changes confer a measurable advantage for navigation.

## Input / Output

The agent receives its true continuous position (scalar x_t in 1D, vector x_t in 2D) at every timestep, along with a fixed start location and a target location whose reward follows a Gaussian distribution (σ_r = 0.05). The output is a one-hot discrete action vector (2 directions in 1D, 4 in 2D) that is smoothed by a low-pass filter (α_env = 0.2, v_max = 0.1) to produce continuous trajectories. Critically, the place field parameters themselves—amplitude α_i, center λ_i, and width σ_i (or 2×2 covariance in 2D)—are also learned outputs updated online at every timestep, not fixed features.

## Method

The architecture is a two-layer network: N Gaussian radial basis function place fields transform continuous position into a representational substrate, which feeds into an actor (M units for movement directions, softmax policy) and a critic (linear value estimate). The key mechanism is a joint gradient-ascent objective where the TD error δ_t simultaneously updates the actor weights W^π, critic weights w^v, and all place field parameters θ = {α, λ, σ} via backpropagated gradients, with a timescale separation (η_θ = 0.0001 ≪ η = 0.01) between field and readout updates. A perturbative expansion under this separation yields closed-form predictions: field centers shift proportionally to w_v,i² (so high-value locations attract fields first), amplitudes grow as 2(η_α/η)w_v,i², and the sign of the center displacement term causes fields near the reward to converge while others drift toward the start. Adding Gaussian noise to field parameter updates (σ_noise ∈ [10⁻⁴, 10⁻³]) produces representational drift that preserves stable navigation behavior while enabling relearning of new targets, and the model is contrasted against Successor Representation and Metric Representation agents to show that all three objectives produce field elongation but with distinct firing-rate and correlation dynamics.

## Results

In the single-target navigation task (1D track, N=16, σ=0.1), agents with fixed place fields plateau at cumulative discounted reward G=33, while optimizing field widths (σ) yields the highest G and the largest reduction in trials to policy convergence (T_{G>45}), followed by amplitude (α) optimization; center (λ) optimization alone provides minimal benefit and actually degrades performance when combined with amplitude optimization. With N=8 fully optimized fields, policy convergence matches that of N=128 randomly initialized fields, suggesting representation flexibility compensates for limited neuron count. For multi-target relearning (5 sequential targets), agents without noisy field updates (σ_noise=0) fail to learn targets 3–5, while σ_noise=0.0005 achieves the highest average cumulative reward across all targets; noise magnitudes beyond 0.001 cause performance collapse.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the proposed Reward Maximization (RM) agent against two baselines: a Successor Representation (SR) agent (Stachenfeld et al., 2017) and a Metric Representation (MR) agent (Foster et al., 2000). All three agents recapitulate the experimentally observed field elongation against the trajectory (increased field size, backward COM shift), but with qualitatively different dynamics. The SR agent's mean firing rate closely tracks the agent's occupancy probability throughout learning (high positive correlation), while the RM agent shows a strong negative correlation between mean firing rate and occupancy in early learning that transitions to positive correlation in later phases. In a 2D arena with an obstacle, the RM agent exhibits significantly more pronounced field elongation spanning the entire corridor, whereas SR elongation is subtle and MR elongation is concentrated only at the start location. The SR and RM agents eventually converge to similar spatial representations (positive correlation in later learning), while MR remains distinct. For new target learning, the RM agent with noisy field updates (σ_noise=0.0005) outperforms both baselines, which lack the mechanism to reorganize fields toward new reward locations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the two-layer place-field-to-actor-critic navigation architecture established by Foster et al. (2000) and Kumar et al. (2022), where previously place fields were fixed and only downstream synapses were updated. The key departure is making place field parameters (amplitude, center, width) jointly optimizable with actor-critic weights under a single TD-error-driven reward maximization objective, adapted from the meta-RL formulation of Wang et al. (2018) and the basis function adaptation analysis of Menache et al. (2005). The paper positions itself against two competing explanations for field elongation—the successor representation (Stachenfeld et al., 2017) and metric representation (Foster et al., 2000)—by showing that a pure reward-maximization objective also produces elongation but with qualitatively different dynamics, offering a new normative account that can be experimentally distinguished from these alternatives.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The unification of three previously disparate phenomena (reward-location density, trajectory-against elongation, and representational drift) under a single normative objective provides a parsimonious framework with concrete, testable predictions for distinguishing competing mechanistic accounts in hippocampal neuroscience. The finding that noisy field parameter updates are functionally necessary for learning new targets (not merely a byproduct of instability) reframes representational drift as potentially adaptive rather than a bug. Key limitations include the reliance on backpropagating TD error through the actor-critic (not biologically plausible), single-peaked Gaussian fields, and simple 1D/2D environments; open questions include how dopaminergic modulation adaptively controls drift rates, how the framework scales to complex multi-goal environments, and whether biologically local learning rules can reproduce the same representational dynamics.
