## Problem

Prior constrained online convex optimization methods achieve O(√T) regret but only guarantee cumulative constraint satisfaction (Σ g(x_t) ≤ 0), meaning individual rounds may violate constraints. This is insufficient for safety-critical applications where every action must be feasible. The paper asks whether anytime constraint satisfaction g(x_t) ≤ 0 for all t can be achieved with O(√T) regret using only first-order constraint feedback at played actions, without expensive projections.

## Input / Output

Each round, the algorithm plays an action x_t ∈ R^d, receives the cost gradient ∇f_t(x_t), and makes exactly one constraint query at the played action to obtain g(x_t) and a subgradient s_t ∈ ∂g(x_t). It outputs the next action x_{t+1} via a gradient descent step followed by a Polyak feasibility step with projection onto R·B. No projection onto the feasible set X = {x : g(x) ≤ 0} is ever computed; instead, feasibility is enforced through the Polyak step. The guarantees require either a known strictly-feasible starting point (for all-rounds feasibility) or accept O(log T) rounds before feasibility is guaranteed.

## Method

The algorithm alternates between a standard OGD step on the cost function (y_t = x_t − η∇f_t(x_t)) and a Polyak feasibility step that applies subgradient descent on the constraint with step-size [g_t + s_t^T(y_t − x_t) + ρ]_+ / ‖s_t‖². Two key design choices make this work: (1) a first-order approximation g_t + s_t^T(y_t − x_t) replaces the unavailable constraint value at the intermediate iterate y_t, and (2) a tightening parameter ρ creates a buffer zone so that adversarial cost gradients cannot push actions outside the true feasible set. The Polyak step is equivalently a projection onto a halfspace containing the tightened feasible set, which preserves the standard OGD regret analysis. For feasibility, a fictitious iterate z_{t+1} (applying the exact Polyak step from x_t) bridges the gap to the first-order approximation, yielding geometric contraction of distance to the feasible set at rate √γ = √(1 − σ²/G_g²) per round.

## Results

The paper achieves O(√T) regret with anytime constraint satisfaction g(x_t) ≤ 0 for all t ∈ [T] (Corollary 1, requiring a known strictly-feasible point), matching the best-known regret rate while providing a strictly stronger constraint guarantee than prior work. Without a known strictly-feasible point, the algorithm guarantees constraint satisfaction after O(log T) rounds and cumulative constraint satisfaction for sufficiently large T (Corollary 2). In numerical experiments on 2D quadratic costs with linear constraints, PFS achieves zero instantaneous constraint violation across all rounds, while the DPP baseline (Yu et al., 2017) incurs measurable violation; tightening DPP (DPP-T) reduces violation at the cost of larger regret.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Mahdavi et al. (2012), Jenatton et al. (2016), and Yuan & Lamperski (2018), the proposed PFS algorithm strictly dominates on both regret (O(√T) vs. O(T^{3/4}) or O(T^{2/3})) and constraint guarantee (anytime per-round satisfaction vs. only cumulative). Against the closest competitor Yu et al. (2017) (DPP), PFS matches the O(√T) regret rate but upgrades the constraint guarantee from cumulative (Σ g(x_t) ≤ 0) to anytime per-round satisfaction (g(x_t) ≤ 0 ∀t), at the cost of requiring a known strictly-feasible point for the strongest guarantee. In the numerical experiments, DPP achieves smaller regret than PFS but incurs nonzero constraint violation in early rounds; the tightened variant DPP-T reduces violation substantially but at the price of larger regret, whereas PFS maintains zero violation throughout. The paper also notes that PFS uses only 1 constraint oracle call per round, compared to O(d log T) calls for membership-oracle methods and O(log T) or O(κ) calls for separation-oracle methods, giving a practical advantage when constraint evaluation is expensive.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the classical Polyak step-size from unconstrained convex optimization (Polyak, 1969) to the constrained OCO setting, departing fundamentally from the primal-dual framework used by Mahdavi et al. (2012), Jenatton et al. (2016), and Yu et al. (2017), which maintain coupled primal and dual iterate sequences. Instead, the algorithm uses a single iterate sequence that alternates between cost gradient descent and a Polyak feasibility step on the constraint function, with a first-order approximation of the constraint at the intermediate iterate to avoid requiring constraint information beyond the played action. The constraint tightening technique is shared with prior work, and the approach is conceptually related to projection-free OCO (Hazan & Kale, 2012) and constrained offline optimization (Nedić, 2011; Nedić & Necoara, 2019), though the adversarial online setting introduces the key technical challenge that the suboptimality gap is not guaranteed non-negative.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The anytime constraint satisfaction guarantee (g(x_t) ≤ 0 for all t) is strictly stronger than the cumulative constraint satisfaction (Σg(x_t) ≤ 0) achieved by prior work, making this approach directly relevant to safety-critical applications where per-round feasibility is non-negotiable. The algorithm requires only one constraint query per round at the played action, avoiding the computational cost of projections or solving sub-problems with the constraint function. Key limitations include the requirement of a known strictly-feasible point for the anytime guarantee (or O(log T) warm-up rounds otherwise), and a larger dependence on G_g in the regret bound compared to OGD with full projection. Open questions include whether the approach extends to time-varying constraints, whether the G_g dependence can be tightened, and whether the first-order approximation strategy generalizes to other step-size designs.
