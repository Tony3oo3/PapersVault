## Problem

Existing jailbreak evaluations measure only whether an attack successfully bypasses a model's safety guardrails (i.e., elicits any non-refusal response), but never assess whether the resulting output is actually correct or useful. This leaves a critical gap: a jailbreak that produces a fluent but wrong answer is functionally different from one that restores the model's true capability, yet both are scored identically as 'successful.' The paper introduces the 'jailbreak tax'—the degradation in model utility incurred when circumventing alignment—as a new metric to quantify this distinction.

## Input / Output

The framework takes verifiable questions (GSM8K/MATH word problems requiring a numeric answer in a prescribed format, or WMDP biology multiple-choice questions with A/B/C/D answers) and feeds them through a pseudo-aligned model under one of three refusal-inducing conditions. After a jailbreak attack is applied, the model's text response is parsed for the prescribed answer format and checked against ground truth. The two reported metrics are JailSucc (fraction of prompts yielding any non-refusal response) and JTax = (BaseUtil − JailUtil) / BaseUtil, where BaseUtil is the unaligned model's accuracy on the same questions. A key design constraint is that for EvilMath, only questions whose semantics survive a second rewording to benign-but-unusual contexts (UnicornMath) are retained, filtering out rewording artifacts.

## Method

The paper constructs five evaluation suites by taking benign, objectively verifiable tasks and making them appear harmful through three alignment techniques: (1) a system prompt instructing refusal on a topic, (2) supervised fine-tuning on (question, refusal) pairs, and (3) rewording math questions into harmful contexts (EvilMath) to trigger a model's existing safety alignment. Eight jailbreak attacks spanning prompt engineering, in-context learning (many-shot), adversarial optimization (GCG), genetic algorithms (AutoDAN), multilingual translation (MultiJail), and LLM-based iterative rephrasing (PAIR, TAP) are then applied to four models (LLaMA 3.1 8B/70B/405B, Claude 3.5 Haiku). The core insight is that by using tasks with known ground truth, the paper can rigorously measure whether a jailbreak restores the model's original capability or merely produces a superficially compliant but incorrect response. The EvilMath/UnicornMath pair serves as a control: UnicornMath (harmful terms replaced with benign but out-of-distribution terms like 'mystical creatures') isolates the effect of domain context from the effect of the harmful framing, ensuring that any observed utility drop is attributable to the jailbreak rather than to the rewording itself.

## Results

Across eight jailbreak attacks evaluated on LLaMA 3.1 70B with system prompt alignment, the jailbreak tax (relative utility loss) ranges from 0% (system prompt jailbreak, finetuning, many-shot) to up to 92% (PAIR on GSM8K). On the EvilMath benchmark with Claude 3.5 Haiku, PAIR and TAP achieve >99% jailbreak success but incur up to 26% accuracy drop. On the hardest MATH level 5 problems, PAIR eliminates >80% of the model's baseline accuracy, while MultiJail and TAP reduce it by >40%. Critically, no correlation exists between jailbreak success rate and jailbreak tax, and scaling to LLaMA 3.1 405B does not reduce the tax.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates eight jailbreak attacks and finds they are far from equivalent in preserving model utility. PAIR (Chao et al., 2023) and TAP (Mehrotra et al., 2023) incur the highest jailbreak tax—up to 92% on GSM8K and >80% on MATH level 5—despite achieving similar success rates to lower-tax attacks like GCG (Zou et al., 2023). Many-shot jailbreaking (Anil et al., 2024) and the simple system prompt jailbreak baseline preserve model utility almost perfectly (near-zero JTax), demonstrating that the tax is not inherent to bypassing alignment. MultiJail (Deng et al., 2023) shows moderate tax, reducing MATH level 5 accuracy by >40%, while AutoDAN (Liu et al., 2023) achieves low success rates (<0.5% on WMDP with SFT alignment) and is often omitted from results. The key finding is that success rate and jailbreak tax are uncorrelated: GCG and TAP can achieve similar JailSucc on GSM8K yet differ substantially in JTax, meaning a jailbreak that 'works' may still deliver significantly degraded outputs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by reframing jailbreak evaluation around a previously unasked question: does bypassing safety guardrails actually restore the model's original capabilities? Prior work (StrongREJECT, AgentHarm) evaluated whether jailbroken outputs are 'harmful' or 'convincing,' relying on LLM judges or human experts, but none systematically measured the *utility degradation* relative to an unaligned baseline. The paper repurposes existing verifiable benchmarks (GSM8K, WMDP, MATH) by making models treat benign tasks as harmful through pseudo-alignment, thereby converting an intractable evaluation problem (is this bomb design good?) into a tractable one (is this math answer correct?). Conceptually, it extends the 'alignment tax' literature (Christiano, 2020) by asking whether the tax is incurred at alignment time or at jailbreak time.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The jailbreak tax metric provides a critical missing dimension for evaluating both attacks and defenses: a jailbreak with 99% success rate that destroys 92% of model utility is far less dangerous than one that preserves it, with direct implications for risk assessment and defense prioritization. The finding that model scale does not mitigate the tax challenges the assumption that larger models are inherently more robust to adversarial prompt modifications. A key limitation is that all evaluations use 'pseudo-alignment' on benign topics rather than genuinely harmful tasks, leaving open whether the tax generalizes to real-world dangerous domains; the authors acknowledge this and call for research access to unaligned frontier models to close the gap.
