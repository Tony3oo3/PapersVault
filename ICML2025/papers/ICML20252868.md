## Problem

Existing time series causal discovery algorithms suffer from three compounding efficiency bottlenecks: high computational costs from binning continuous data for independence tests, reliance on brute-force traversal over all possible time lags without a theoretical mechanism to identify the optimal one, and exponential growth of candidate variable sets (2^{dT} tests for d variables and T lags). These issues make causal discovery impractical for large-scale multivariate time series, and no general data-level acceleration framework existed that could address all three simultaneously across different algorithm families.

## Input / Output

The method takes a multivariate time series X with d variables and T time points as input. Each value x_i^t is first transformed via time weaving (window w=1) into a 2-bit binary tuple encoding the trend relative to its immediate neighbors, producing a binary matrix X_tw. The output is a pruned set of candidate variable pairs with their identified optimal time lags, which is then fed into a downstream causal discovery algorithm (PCMCI, SURD, NGC, or VARLiNGAM) to produce the final summary causal graph as a d×d adjacency matrix. The binary encoding eliminates the need for arbitrary binning granularity and naturally handles irregular time series by filling missing values based on adjacent trends rather than mean imputation.

## Method

ARROW is a three-component data-level accelerator: (1) Time Weaving encodes each time point as a binary tuple (tr⟨t−w,t⟩, tr⟨t,t+w⟩) capturing local trend context, discretizing continuous values into 0/1 without binning uncertainty; (2) Time Lag Discovery applies XOR operations on time-weaving representations of variable pairs and derives Theorem 4.2, which proves that if v causally affects v' at lag k, then P(Xor(v,v',k)=(0,0)) or P(Xor(v,v',k)=(1,1)) exceeds 1/3−ε, enabling efficient bitwise identification of the optimal lag without brute-force search; (3) Candidates Pruning filters variable pairs by retaining only those whose maximum XOR-based score exceeds 0.33 and caps the candidate set at Thr·d² pairs, reducing the exponential search space. The accelerator is algorithm-agnostic and plugs into constraint-based, score-based, Granger-based, and information-theoretic methods alike, achieving up to 153× speedup (PCMCI) and 70× speedup (SURD) while improving Lag AUC and Graph AUC in most settings.

## Results

On synthetic datasets with linear causal relationships (10 variables, T=1000), ARROW achieves up to 153× speedup over PCMCI (245.99s → 6.67s at lag 5), 69× over SURD (424.90s → 6.17s), 20× over NGC (12.91s → 0.80s), and 1.57× over VARLiNGAM (9.72s → 6.20s). In terms of accuracy, ARROW improves Lag AUC from 0.50 to 0.98 for SURD (a 46% gain) and Graph AUC from 0.50 to 0.96, while also boosting PCMCI's Graph AUC from 0.92 to 0.99. On the real-world Dream3 dataset, ARROW achieves 7×–300× speedup across methods while retaining or improving summary graph accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ARROW consistently outperforms all four baselines on runtime, with the largest gains on SURD (69×) and PCMCI (153×), which rely on brute-force time lag traversal and expensive conditional independence tests. On accuracy, ARROW dramatically improves SURD (Lag AUC 0.50→0.98, Graph AUC 0.50→0.96) and NGC (Lag AUC 0.50→0.98, Graph AUC 0.75→1.00), which originally performed near random on lag discovery. PCMCI also benefits in accuracy (Graph AUC 0.92→0.99 at lag 5), while VARLiNGAM shows a mixed picture: ARROW improves its Graph AUC at lag 15 (0.50→0.93) but slightly degrades Lag AUC at lag 5 (0.98→0.98, essentially tied), attributed to time weaving altering the non-Gaussian noise distribution that VARLiNGAM exploits. Overall, ARROW wins on both efficiency and accuracy for 3 of 4 baselines across all settings, with VARLiNGAM being the only case where accuracy is comparable rather than strictly superior at small lags.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by proposing a data-level acceleration framework (ARROW) that is orthogonal to and complementary with existing hardware acceleration approaches (GPU-based cuPC/gpuPC, FPGA-based CSF, CPU-based ParallelPC). Rather than building on a specific causal discovery algorithm, it introduces the 'Time Weaving' encoding—a binary tuple representation of local trend changes—and an XOR-based theorem for optimal time lag identification, which together replace the brute-force lag search and exponential candidate enumeration that plague all four major families of time series causal discovery methods (constraint-based, score-based, Granger-based, information-theoretic). The approach repurposes the simple observation that causally related variables exhibit aligned or opposed local trends, formalized through a probabilistic theorem with a 1/3 threshold, into a practical pruning and lag-selection pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 153x speedup (up to 300x on Dream3) makes time series causal discovery tractable for settings where brute-force lag traversal and exponential candidate sets were previously prohibitive, and the method's generality across four algorithm families increases its practical adoption potential. However, limitations remain: VARLiNGAM+ARROW degrades at small lags due to noise distribution distortion from binary encoding, NGC still struggles with non-linear datasets, and experiments are limited to 10 variables. Open questions include whether the 2-bit time weaving encoding loses critical information for subtle or multi-path causal effects, how the method scales to hundreds of variables, and whether the 0.33 threshold generalizes beyond the theoretical 1/3 bound under heavy noise or non-stationary regimes.
