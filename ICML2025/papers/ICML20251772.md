## Problem

Prior work established strong impossibility results for computing voting rule winners from pairwise or t-wise comparison feedback, but left open whether an alternative feedback modality—improvement feedback, where voters iteratively refine a suggested candidate by returning a better one from a local neighborhood—can overcome these limitations. This paper provides a complete characterization of which positional scoring rules and Condorcet-consistent rules are computable under t-improvement feedback, revealing that while plurality becomes learnable (unlike under pairwise feedback), most other rules remain fundamentally uncomputable.

## Input / Output

The algorithm receives the exact probability Pr[q^t(b)=a] of observing candidate a as an improvement when querying candidate b, for all pairs (a,b), under a fixed t-improvement feedback distribution (e.g., uniform, linear decay, or exponential decay over the t-above neighborhood). This idealized full-distribution access is strictly stronger than finite-query settings, making the impossibility results fundamental. The output is the winner candidate under a specified voting rule (positional scoring or Condorcet-consistent), and the impossibility results show that no randomized algorithm can exceed 1/m success probability for unlearnable rules.

## Method

The core technique constructs t-indistinguishable preference profiles—pairs D and D^{a↔b} that yield identical feedback distributions but different winners—using a parametric family D_{i,j,ℓ} where two candidates are fixed at specific positions with probability p chosen to equalize all feedback probabilities. A permutation-based amplification (adapted from Halpern et al.) then produces m mutually indistinguishable profiles each with a distinct winner, yielding the 1/m lower bound via a pigeonhole argument. For positional scoring rules, the paper proves a complete characterization: a scoring vector s is learnable if and only if s ∈ span(s_plu, s_t*), where s_t* = (P_1^t, ..., P_m^t) encodes the cumulative improvement-reachability of each position. For Condorcet-consistent rules, the paper shows that under uniform t-improvement feedback no algorithm can identify the Condorcet winner for any t, and under general distributions the impossibility holds for t ≤ m/2−2 except one special case. Experiments under IC, Mallows, and Plackett-Luce models show that t-improvement feedback can empirically outperform pairwise comparisons for Borda and Copeland despite the worst-case impossibility.

## Results

The paper establishes a complete characterization of positional scoring rules computable under t-improvement feedback: for t ≤ m/2−2, only rules in span(s_t*, s_plu) are learnable, and no randomized algorithm can identify the winner for any other scoring vector with probability exceeding 1/m. For Condorcet-consistent rules, the impossibility is even stronger: under uniform t-improvement feedback, no algorithm can identify the Condorcet winner with probability > 1/m for all t ∈ [m−1] (Theorem 5.3), and this extends to general distributions for t ≤ m/2−2 (Theorem 5.5). Experimentally, with m=20 candidates and t=5, t-improvement feedback and pairwise comparison queries yield identical approximation ratios under the Impartial Culture model, but diverge under structured models: pairwise comparisons achieve higher Borda/Copeland approximation under the Plackett-Luce model, while t-improvement feedback outperforms pairwise comparisons under the Mallows model (φ=1/3).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between t-improvement feedback queries and pairwise comparison queries (the standard approach studied by Halpern et al., 2024). Theoretically, the two feedback types have sharply complementary capabilities: pairwise comparisons suffice to compute Borda, Copeland, Kemeny, and the Condorcet winner, while t-improvement feedback can compute plurality (via Pr[q^t(a)=a]) but provably cannot compute any Condorcet-consistent rule or most positional scoring rules in the worst case. Empirically, the comparison is model-dependent: under the Impartial Culture model, both feedback mechanisms perform identically (no exploitable structure); under the Plackett-Luce model, pairwise comparisons yield a higher approximation ratio for both Borda and Copeland because the high variance in relative candidate rankings benefits direct pairwise queries; under the Mallows model, t-improvement feedback outperforms pairwise comparisons because rankings concentrate near a central ordering, making the top candidate (which t-improvement feedback identifies efficiently) more likely to coincide with the Borda/Copeland winner. All three t-improvement distributions (uniform, linear decay, exponential decay) behave identically in experiments, and results are qualitatively stable across different values of t.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on computing voting rules from incomplete feedback, building directly on Halpern et al. (2024), who provided a complete characterization of positional scoring rules computable from t-wise comparison queries. The key conceptual departure is introducing 'improvement feedback'—where a user refines a queried candidate by selecting a better one from a local neighborhood—as a fundamentally different feedback modality from pairwise or t-wise comparisons. The methodological toolkit (t-indistinguishable preference profiles, information-theoretic impossibility via pigeonhole arguments) is inherited from Halpern et al., but the feedback model and the resulting characterizations are new. The paper also positions itself within the broader preference elicitation literature (Filmus & Oren's t-top queries, Bentert & Skowron's t-wise comparisons) and connects motivationally to RLHF and coactive learning frameworks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a complete characterization of which positional scoring rules are learnable under improvement feedback (only plurality and one distribution-specific rule, plus their linear combinations), and a striking negative result showing that no Condorcet-consistent rule is computable from improvement feedback—despite pairwise comparisons being sufficient for this. This has direct implications for RLHF and deliberation platforms where iterative refinement is the natural interaction paradigm, suggesting that such systems fundamentally cannot recover certain collective decision outcomes. Open questions include whether the negative results extend to all t under general distributions, how heterogeneous t values across agents affect the picture, and whether hybrid feedback mechanisms (combining improvement with pairwise queries) can overcome these limitations. The experimental finding that improvement feedback can outperform pairwise comparisons in practice for Borda/Copeland—despite theoretical impossibility—highlights a gap between worst-case guarantees and typical-case behavior that warrants further study.
