## Problem

Gradient-based OOD detection methods like GAIA rely on the non-zero gradient behavior of attribution maps to distinguish ID from OOD samples, but this signal lacks sufficient distinguishability when ID samples are perturbed in high-dimensional spaces, causing feature components that should be classified as ID to be misidentified as OOD. Additionally, prior methods assume uniform linear influence of all intermediate layers and use semantically empty baselines (e.g., black images), which fails to capture the heterogeneous sensitivity of early vs. late layers and degrades attribution accuracy.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20253058/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained CNN (ResNet34, WRN40, or BiT-S) and a single RGB image (32×32 for CIFAR-100, 224×224 for ImageNet) as input, operating entirely post-hoc without retraining. It produces a scalar OOD score τ computed as the L2 norm of per-layer, per-channel non-zero gradient expectations; a threshold ξ on τ yields the binary ID/OOD decision. For small label spaces the score uses direct non-zero gradient density, while for large label spaces it uses a ratio of gradient matrix norms restricted to the top-90% class outputs.

## Method

S&I introduces two key mechanisms: (1) layer splitting, where at each intermediate layer j the adversarial example is updated using only the parameters θ^(j+1~l) of subsequent layers, decoupling the heterogeneous sensitivity of early (texture/edge) and late (semantic) features; and (2) adversarial attribution gradient integration, where a PGD-generated adversarial example (constrained by L2 norm ε) replaces the black-image baseline, and the loss gradients ∂L(f(x_i))/∂x_i are accumulated along the attribution path from the adversarial example back to the true input via first-order Taylor expansion. The per-feature attribution A_rs^jk = Σ Δx_rs^jk ⊙ g(x_rs^jk) is computed for each layer-channel pair, and the non-zero density of these attributions across T iterations yields the per-layer expectation E[ε|x^jk]. The final OOD score τ = √(Σ_j Σ_k (E[ε|x^jk])²) aggregates evidence across all l layers and K channels, with a theoretical proof (Propositions 1–2) showing that ID samples retain predominantly zero gradients under the adversarial baseline while OOD samples exhibit higher non-zero gradient prevalence.

## Results

On the CIFAR100 benchmark, S&I achieves an average FPR95 of 29.05% (ResNet34) and 38.61% (WRN40), with a corresponding AUROC of 94.93% and 90.3%, respectively. On the larger ImageNet-1K benchmark with the BiT-S backbone, S&I achieves an average FPR95 of 37.31% and AUROC of 91.84%, outperforming the main gradient-based baseline GAIA by 2.66% in FPR95. The method also reduces FPR95 by 48.88% over the output-based ODIN baseline and by 31.79% over the gradient-based GradNorm baseline on ImageNet.

## Baselines & Comparisons

S&I outperforms all seven baselines on the ImageNet benchmark, achieving the lowest average FPR95 (37.31%) and highest AUROC (91.84%). Against the main competitive baseline GAIA, S&I improves FPR95 by 2.66% (37.31% vs. 38.33%) and AUROC by 0.17%, with consistent per-dataset wins on iNaturalist, Textures, SUN, and Places. Compared to output-based methods (MSP, ODIN, Energy), S&I reduces FPR95 by 40–48%, demonstrating the advantage of gradient-based detection. Against feature-based methods (RankFeat, ReAct), S&I achieves a 2.38% FPR95 reduction over RankFeat and a 20.35% reduction over ReAct, though RankFeat remains competitive on Textures (27.88% vs. 39.17%). On CIFAR100, the improvement over GAIA is marginal (29.05% vs. 29.1% FPR95 on ResNet34), which the authors attribute to the smaller label space limiting the effectiveness of adversarial perturbation in amplifying ID/OOD differences. Against the 2024 baseline SCALE, S&I achieves a substantial 46.45% FPR95 reduction on CIFAR100/ResNet34 (29.05% vs. 83.76%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends GAIA (Chen et al., 2023), the current state-of-the-art gradient-based OOD detection method, by replacing its zero-image attribution baseline with adversarial examples and introducing a layer-splitting mechanism that decomposes intermediate layers rather than assuming uniform linear cumulative influence. The attribution framework itself draws on Integrated Gradients (Sundararajan et al., 2017), while the adversarial perturbation strategy connects to the PGD attack literature (Kurakin et al., 2018). The key conceptual departure from GAIA is the argument that non-zero gradient behaviors of OOD samples lack sufficient distinguishability from randomly perturbed ID samples in high-dimensional spaces, motivating both the adversarial baseline and the per-layer gradient integration.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is limited by the very small empirical gains over GAIA—essentially tied on CIFAR100 (29.05% vs 29.1% FPR95) and only ~1% better on ImageNet (37.31% vs 38.33%)—while incurring higher computational cost (4.9 vs 6.36 FPS). The layer-splitting idea and the theoretical proof that adversarial baselines preserve zero-importance for ID samples are conceptually interesting and could inform future attribution-based detection methods, but the paper does not demonstrate that these ideas generalize beyond CNN backbones or that the adversarial iteration count can be reduced. An open question is whether the modest ImageNet advantage scales to even larger label spaces or non-vision modalities where the gradient-distinguishability argument might be more pronounced.
