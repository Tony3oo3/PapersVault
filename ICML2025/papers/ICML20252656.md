## Problem

Prior evaluations of single-cell foundation models (scFMs) for perturbation response prediction lacked a standardized protocol, ignored distribution shift between train and test splits, and focused exclusively on highly variable genes rather than biologically relevant differentially expressed genes. This made it difficult to determine whether scFM embeddings genuinely encode perturbation-specific information or merely capture average expression patterns. PertEval-scFM addresses these gaps by introducing a controlled, reproducible benchmark that explicitly measures generalization under increasing distribution shift.

## Input / Output

The framework takes raw single-cell RNA-seq expression counts from control cells (pseudo-bulked by averaging 500 randomly sampled cells to reduce sparsity) and a perturbation identity (target gene to knock out in silico by setting its expression to zero). For scFM-based probes, the input is the concatenation of control and perturbed cell embeddings (dimension 2e); for the gene-expression baseline, it is the concatenation of log-normalized basal expression and a Pearson co-expression vector encoding the perturbed gene's identity (dimension 2v). The output is a predicted log-fold-change perturbation effect vector over 2,000 HVGs or the top 20 DEGs per perturbation, scored via MSE, AUSPC (integrated MSE across sparsification levels 0.1–0.7), E-distance, and contextual alignment.

## Method

PertEval-scFM generates zero-shot embeddings from five frozen pre-trained scFMs (scBERT, Geneformer, scGPT, scFoundation, UCE) and trains a lightweight 1-hidden-layer MLP probe on the concatenated control–perturbed embedding pair, isolating the information content of the representations from any task-specific prediction head. Distribution shift is controlled via SPECTRA, a spectral graph partitioning method that stochastically removes edges from a sample-similarity graph with probability s, producing train-test splits of increasing dissimilarity; performance is summarized by the Area Under the SPECTRA Performance Curve (AUSPC), the trapezoidal integral of MSE over s. Three baselines provide reference points: an MLP on raw log-normalized expression plus a gene co-expression matrix, the graph-based GEARS model trained from scratch, and a mean-expression null model. The framework further introduces E-distance to stratify perturbations by effect magnitude and a contextual alignment metric (cosine similarity between pre-training and fine-tuning corpora) to quantify how dataset overlap influences zero-shot performance.

## Results

Across all four dataset configurations (Norman single-gene, Norman double-gene, Replogle K562, Replogle RPE1), GEARS achieves the lowest AUSPC by an order of magnitude (e.g., 0.00815 on Norman single-gene vs. ~0.045 for the best scFM). Among the five zero-shot scFMs, performance differences are minimal and often not statistically significant, with AUSPC values tightly clustered (e.g., 0.1251–0.1258 on Replogle RPE1). The MLP baseline using raw gene expression is competitive with or slightly better than most scFMs, and the mean baseline (predicting no perturbation effect) is nearly as good. On the more stringent top-20 DEG evaluation, GEARS again dominates (AUSPC 0.266 single-gene, 0.223 double-gene) while scFMs show only marginal gains over the mean baseline (best scFM UCE: 0.334 vs. mean 0.345 on Norman single-gene).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GEARS, the task-specific graph-based model, outperforms all zero-shot scFMs and simple baselines by roughly an order of magnitude in AUSPC across every dataset (e.g., 0.00815 vs. 0.0448 on Norman single-gene; 0.0082 vs. 0.1384 on Replogle K562), confirming that inductive biases tailored to perturbation prediction are critical. The MLP baseline using raw gene expression is competitive with or slightly better than most scFMs: on Norman single-gene it ranks 2nd (AUSPC 0.0448) ahead of all scFMs, and on Replogle RPE1 it ties with Geneformer (0.1252 vs. 0.1251). The mean baseline (predicting zero perturbation effect) is nearly indistinguishable from the MLP baseline on Norman double-gene (4.255 vs. 4.253), underscoring how little signal the models extract. Among the five scFMs, differences are minimal and often within error bars; the notable exception is scGPT, which degrades sharply on Norman double-gene (AUSPC 5.184, rank 8) due to a large MSE spike at high sparsification. On the stricter top-20 DEG evaluation, UCE is the best scFM (AUSPC 0.334 on Norman single-gene) but still trails the MLP baseline (0.342) and GEARS (0.266) substantially, with the best scFM outperforming the worst (Geneformer, 0.351) by only 4.8%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of perturbation prediction benchmarks (PerturBench, Ahlmann-Eltze et al.) by introducing a standardized zero-shot evaluation framework that explicitly accounts for distribution shift via the SPECTRA graph-partitioning method. It borrows the MLP probing paradigm from NLP (Alain & Bengio; Tenney et al.) to isolate the information content of scFM embeddings from task-specific prediction heads, and positions GEARS as a task-specific inductive-bias baseline against which zero-shot representations are measured. The key departure from prior benchmarks is the focus on biologically relevant DEGs rather than all HVGs, and the systematic quantification of robustness under controlled distribution shift via AUSPC.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that five current-generation scFMs (scBERT, Geneformer, scGPT, scFoundation, UCE) offer no consistent advantage over a simple MLP on raw expression or a mean baseline in the zero-shot setting—particularly under distribution shift and on biologically relevant DEGs—provides a rigorous negative result that tempers enthusiasm for scFM transfer to perturbation prediction. The contextual alignment analysis (scBERT's smaller but better-aligned pre-training corpus outperforming scGPT's 10× larger corpus) suggests data relevance matters more than scale, pointing toward curated pre-training as a path forward. Open questions include how to standardize in silico perturbation representations across architectures, how to integrate biological inductive biases (as in GEARS) into scFM pre-training, and how to capture the full range of perturbation-induced cellular states in training data.
