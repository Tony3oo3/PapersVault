## Problem

Existing hierarchical robotic manipulation policies impose rigid constraints on the interface between high-level and low-level agents (e.g., fixing the high-level output as the trajectory endpoint), limiting flexibility and forcing both levels to perform fine-grained reasoning in high-dimensional spaces. Additionally, prior hierarchical methods do not exploit the geometric symmetries inherent in robotic tasks, resulting in poor sample efficiency and generalization under spatial variations.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a multi-camera point cloud with per-point RGB features (n × 6D) concatenated with t history steps of gripper state (3D position, SO(3) orientation, aperture), which is voxelized into a 3D grid. The high-level agent outputs a single 3D translation (keypose) selected as the argmax of a learned probability heatmap, while the low-level agent outputs a sequence of m gripper states (position, orientation, aperture) via iterative diffusion denoising (18 steps in simulation). The Frame Transfer interface shifts all spatial coordinates into the keypose frame before low-level processing, making the low-level policy translation-invariant.

## Method

HEP decomposes the policy into a high-level agent (SO(2)-equivariant 3D U-Net predicting a keypose translation over a stacked voxel feature map) and a low-level agent (SO(2)-equivariant diffusion policy generating the full SE(3) trajectory), connected by a Frame Transfer interface that translates the observation and action coordinate frame to the predicted keypose rather than constraining the trajectory endpoint. The stacked voxel encoder replaces standard voxel downsampling with an equivariant PointNet that aggregates all points within each voxel, preserving fine-grained spatial detail while provably maintaining T(3)×SO(2) equivariance. The paper provides a theoretical proof that the full hierarchical system is T(3)×SO(2)-equivariant, decomposing the symmetry into global (high-level) and local (low-level) components. Training uses behavior cloning with cross-entropy loss on the high-level heatmap and DDPM denoising loss on the low-level trajectory, with 100 demonstrations per task.

## Results

On 30 RLBench tasks in open-loop control, HEP achieves a mean success rate of 88%, outperforming Chained Diffuser (78%) and 3D Diffuser Actor (56%) by an average of 10 percentage points. In closed-loop control on 10 selected tasks, HEP reaches 79% mean success rate versus EquiDiff's 57%, a 23-point improvement. In real-world experiments, HEP achieves 80–90% success on long-horizon manipulation tasks (Pot Cleaning, Blocks to Drawer, Blocks Stacking) compared to Chained Diffuser's 20–40%, and demonstrates strong one-shot generalization (80% vs. 5% for Chained Diffuser).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Chained Diffuser (Xian et al., 2023), the primary open-loop hierarchical baseline, HEP wins on 28 of 30 RLBench tasks with a 10-point mean improvement; the largest gains appear on tasks requiring fine control (Turn On Lamp: +55, Open Microwave: +26) and long-horizon reasoning (Push 3 Buttons: +12). Against 3D Diffuser Actor, an end-to-end diffusion policy with 3D scene representations, HEP achieves a 32-point mean advantage, with particularly dramatic improvements on non-prehensile tasks (Close Door: +83, Wipe Desk: +72, Open Oven: +84). In closed-loop control, HEP outperforms EquiDiff (Wang et al., 2024), an SO(2)-equivariant diffusion policy, by 23 points on average across 10 tasks, with the largest gap on Push 3 Buttons (+36) and Open Drawer (+41). In the real-world setting, HEP (open-loop) achieves 80–90% success versus Chained Diffuser's 20–40%, and in a one-shot generalization test HEP reaches 80% while Chained Diffuser drops to 5%. A comparison with Hierarchical Diffusion Policy (Ma et al., 2024) in Appendix H shows HEP gaining 20 points on average over 7 shared tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of hierarchical policy learning (Chained Diffuser, HDP) and equivariant robot learning (EquiDiff, Equivariant Transporter Networks). It builds directly on the equivariant diffusion policy of Wang et al. (2024) as its low-level trajectory generator and on Stacked Voxels (Zhou & Tuzel, 2018) for point-cloud encoding. The key departure from prior hierarchical methods is the Frame Transfer interface: instead of passing a full SE(3) keypose that rigidly constrains the low-level trajectory endpoint, HEP passes only a T(3) translation that re-anchors the low-level agent's coordinate frame, decoupling the two levels while preserving a strong inductive bias. The authors claim this is the first work to formally prove equivariance composition across a hierarchical policy structure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Frame Transfer interface offers a practical design pattern for hierarchical policies that prior rigid-constraint approaches (Chained Diffuser, HDP) lacked, and the theoretical proof that T(3)×SO(2) equivariance composes across levels even when the low-level agent is not itself T(3)-equivariant is a clean and reusable result. The 80% one-shot success rate (vs. 5% for Chained Diffuser) suggests the equivariant inductive bias substantially reduces data requirements. Limitations include restriction to tabletop manipulation, absence of memory mechanisms (causing the closed-loop Pot Cleaning failure), and the symmetry group being limited to T(3)×SO(2) rather than full SE(3). Extending Frame Transfer to incorporate rotational specification and integrating temporal memory are natural next steps.
