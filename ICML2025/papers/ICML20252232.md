## Problem

Standard strategic classification assumes feature manipulation costs are exogenous and fixed, yet in practice costs often emerge from market forces (e.g., SAT prep courses priced by demand). This paper challenges that assumption by modeling a setting where a deployed classifier creates demand for features, inducing a competitive market whose prices are endogenous and depend on the learned classifier through aggregate user demand.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252232/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes continuous non-negative feature vectors x ∈ R^d, an individualized monetary budget b ≥ 0 (observed at train time but private at test time), and binary labels y for training. It produces a linear classifier h_{w,τ}(x) = sign(w^T x + τ) along with equilibrium market prices p^h proportional to w. A key structural constraint is that users can only purchase features (δ ≥ 0) and are limited by their budget, making the cost function c_p(δ) = δ^T p with p determined by revenue-maximizing equilibrium rather than being fixed a priori.

## Method

The core insight (Proposition 1) is that equilibrium prices are always proportional to the classifier's weight vector w, reducing the multi-dimensional pricing problem to optimizing a single scalar ρ. Algorithm 1 computes exact empirical prices in O(m log m) by sorting units-per-budget and selecting the revenue-maximizing price. For learning, the paper introduces a market hinge loss (Eq. 15) that adapts the strategic hinge to individualized budgets via the scalar price ρ, avoiding explicit best-response computation. To enable end-to-end gradient optimization, Algorithm 2 replaces the hard sort and argmax in the pricing procedure with differentiable softsort and softmax operators, allowing gradients to flow 'through the market' and making the entire objective amenable to standard ERM with ADAM.

## Results

On the Adult dataset, MASC (the proposed market-aware strategic classifier) clearly outperforms the naive non-strategic baseline by a large margin across all budget inequity scales (2² to 2¹⁰). MASC also outperforms the strategic baseline (strat) in long-term accuracy for all scales above 2⁴, including the dataset's natural scale of ~2¹⁰, while showing similar performance at the lowest scales. A key behavioral finding is an abrupt drop in the ratio of negative points crossing the decision boundary at α ≈ 2⁴, coinciding with a sharp accuracy jump for MASC. On the Folktables dataset, MASC outperforms both baselines from scale 2² onward, though the non-strategic benchmark remains notably higher and harder to surpass.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares MASC against two baselines: (1) **naive**, a conventional non-strategic classifier, and (2) **strat**, a strategic classifier that anticipates user responses to fixed prices but does not account for how the market re-equilibrates. MASC decisively outperforms naive across all budget scales on both Adult and Folktables, with the gap widening as budget inequality increases. Against strat, MASC wins in long-term accuracy (after prices re-equilibrate) for all scales above 2⁴ on Adult and from 2² on Folktables; at the lowest scales the two are comparable. A critical weakness of strat is its large short-to-long performance gap: it is optimized for current prices but degrades significantly once the market adapts to the new classifier, a failure mode MASC avoids by jointly optimizing over the induced market. The non-strategic benchmark (naive evaluated on unmodified data) serves as an upper reference and remains higher than all strategic methods, particularly on Folktables where the positive-crosser ratio stays low even at large scales.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the strategic classification framework (Hardt et al., 2016; Brückner et al., 2012) by challenging its core assumption that feature-manipulation costs are exogenous and fixed. Instead, it proposes that costs emerge endogenously through market formation: the classifier creates demand for features, sellers set prices to maximize revenue, and users purchase features subject to individual budgets. The learning approach adapts the strategic hinge loss from Levanon & Rosenfeld (2022) into a 'market hinge' that incorporates budget-dependent margins, and introduces a differentiable pricing algorithm (via softsort and softmax) to enable end-to-end gradient-based optimization. This departs from prior work on unknown costs, personalized costs, and externalities by introducing cross-user dependencies through a market equilibrium mechanism rather than through utility functions or network structures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper raises important questions about socioeconomic equity in ML-driven decision systems: classifiers that maximize accuracy under market-induced costs tend to favor users with larger budgets, and the common heuristic of 'raising the bar' to combat strategic behavior can paradoxically increase the number of points that cross. However, the market model is highly stylized (unlimited supply, fixed exclusive sellers, no externalities, perfect information), and empirical validation is limited to two tabular datasets with simulated market behavior. Open questions include how these dynamics play out in richer market structures, how information asymmetry between the learning system and users/sellers affects outcomes, and what regulatory mechanisms might mitigate inequitable market effects.
