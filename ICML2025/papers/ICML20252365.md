## Problem

Existing speculative decoding (SD) methods require the drafter and target models to share the same vocabulary, which severely limits the pool of usable drafters and often forces practitioners to train a drafter from scratch. This constraint is particularly problematic when the target model is not part of a model family with a smaller in-family variant, or when even the smallest in-family model is too large and slow to serve as an effective drafter.

## Input / Output

The algorithms take a text prompt, a target model with vocabulary T, a drafter model with vocabulary D (where T and D may be entirely different), and a drafting lookahead value i. The output is a non-empty sequence of 1 to i+1 tokens drawn from the target vocabulary T, guaranteed to follow the target distribution (lossless). A key constraint is that for SLEM, the target vocabulary must be expressible in the draft vocabulary and vice versa (T → D* and D* → T*), which holds in practice for standard tokenizers that include all alphabet characters.

## Method

The paper introduces three lossless SD algorithms for heterogeneous vocabularies. SLEM (Algorithm 2) uses plain text as a shared intermediate representation: draft tokens are decoded to text, re-tokenized with the target vocabulary, and verified via exact matching, with a suffix/prefix alignment mechanism to handle non-injective tokenizers. TLI (Algorithm 4) projects the drafter's distribution onto the intersection T∩D by normalizing q(x) over tokens in T, then applies standard SD rejection sampling; Theorem 4.1 proves this dominates the naive 'union' strategy. SLRS (Algorithm 3) performs rejection sampling at the string level rather than the token level, summing over all draft-token sequences that produce a given target token, though its computational cost grows exponentially with token length (Lemma 3.1), making it practical only for drafters with short tokens. All three methods require no retraining and work with off-the-shelf models, achieving up to 2.8× speedup (SLEM) and 1.7× (TLI) over autoregressive decoding.

## Results

SLEM (Algorithm 2) achieves throughput speedups of up to 2.79× over autoregressive decoding (AR), with the best result on CodeLlama-13b-Instruct-hf (humaneval, 1×A6000) reaching 59.7 Tok/s vs. 21.4 Tok/s for AR. TLI (Algorithm 4) achieves up to 1.77× speedup over AR, with CodeLlama-13b-Instruct-hf (humaneval) reaching 39.1 Tok/s vs. 22.1 Tok/s. Across all evaluated model pairs (Mixtral-8x22B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen, phi-4, CodeLlama), SLEM consistently outperforms AR on summarization, programming, and long-context tasks, while TLI provides more modest but still significant gains. Notably, some configurations (e.g., DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B on CNN-DailyMail with a 1.5B drafter) show slowdowns (0.73×), confirming that drafter accuracy and speed remain critical.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is autoregressive decoding (AR), which the paper's methods consistently outperform when a sufficiently fast and accurate drafter is available: SLEM achieves 1.37×–2.79× speedup and TLI achieves 1.03×–1.77× across the evaluated configurations. The secondary baseline is standard speculative decoding (SD, Algorithm 5 from Leviathan et al. 2023 / Hugging Face's implementation by Gante 2023), which is only applicable when the drafter shares the target's vocabulary. Where both methods are applicable (e.g., gemma-2-9b-it with gemma-2-2b-it), SLEM with a heterogeneous drafter (vicuna-68m) achieves 40.2 Tok/s (1.49× speedup) versus SD with the homogeneous in-family drafter at 33.2 Tok/s (1.23×), demonstrating that heterogeneous drafters can outperform in-family ones. However, SD with a well-matched homogeneous drafter (e.g., DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B for the 70B target) still achieves higher absolute speedups (2.25×–2.3×) than any heterogeneous configuration tested. The paper's key contribution is enabling SD where no homogeneous drafter exists at all, rather than beating SD in the homogeneous setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the standard speculative decoding (SD) framework of Leviathan et al. (2023) and Chen et al. (2023), which requires the drafter and target to share an identical vocabulary, by removing that constraint entirely. The three proposed algorithms—SLEM, TLI, and SLRS—each take a different route: SLEM uses plain text as a shared intermediate representation with exact matching, TLI projects the drafter's distribution onto the vocabulary intersection and reuses standard SD verification, and SLRS generalizes rejection sampling from the token level to the string level. The work also addresses a practical gap in prior SD literature: the non-injective behavior of real-world tokenizers (BPE, SentencePiece, etc.), which prior methods implicitly assumed away by operating within a single vocabulary.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is already substantial: SLEM and TLI have been adopted as the default heterogeneous SD methods in Hugging Face Transformers, the most widely used LLM library, enabling any off-the-shelf model to serve as a drafter without retraining. Key limitations remain: SLRS suffers exponential computational cost in ψ(t) for longer tokens (Lemma 3.1), restricting it to drafters with compact vocabularies, and all methods still depend on drafter accuracy—insufficiently accurate drafters can cause slowdowns rather than speedups. Open questions include whether approximate or heuristic methods can make SLRS practical for larger vocabularies, and whether drafter adjustment strategies can further improve TLI's acceptance rates beyond the vocabulary intersection.
