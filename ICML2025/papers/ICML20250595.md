## Problem

Standard quantum optimal control methods (gradient-based, analytic) struggle with complex open quantum systems due to quadratic computational scaling, sensitivity to noise, and inability to produce experimentally feasible control signals with finite rise-times and bandwidth limits. Existing RL approaches for quantum control lack physical constraints that enforce adiabatic dynamics and realistic signal characteristics, and do not adequately address time-dependent experimental noise. This paper asks whether incorporating physics-motivated constraints directly into the RL optimization loop can simultaneously improve solution fidelity, robustness, and computational scalability.

## Input / Output

The method takes a quantum system Hamiltonian (drift + control terms), a target density matrix, and physical constraint parameters (maximum solver steps N_max, Gaussian convolution width t_σ for bandwidth, pulse area bounds) as input. The output is a single continuous action vector in [-1,1]^d representing the full time-discretized control signal (amplitudes Ω_i and frequencies Δ_i), which after Gaussian convolution yields a smooth, experimentally implementable waveform. In the multi-step variant, the action is split into four temporal segments and the agent additionally observes the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck noise bias μ_Ω at three intermediate time points, enabling feedback-corrected control under strong perturbations.

## Method

The core contribution is a physics-constrained PPO algorithm operating in a continuous bandit setting where the entire control signal is selected as a single action. The key constraint is a hard penalty: any action whose quantum simulation requires more than N_max adaptive Runge-Kutta solver steps receives a reward lower than any achievable environment reward, effectively removing non-adiabatic, computationally expensive signals from the policy's support. The reward function combines a log-infidelity term with ReLU-gated smoothness penalties (comparing signal roughness to a reference) and an optional pulse-area penalty, while a Gaussian convolution filter applied before simulation enforces finite bandwidth. The implementation uses JAX to JIT-compile the full quantum-solver-plus-RL loop, enabling up to 1024 parallel agents on a single GPU where the N_max constraint eliminates the slowest-simulation bottleneck that would otherwise govern the synchronized training loop. For strong noise regimes, a multi-step variant lets the agent observe the noise bias at three intermediate checkpoints, achieving a 2× infidelity reduction over the bandit setting.

## Results

On the four-level Λ system, the constrained PPO method achieves a mean population-transfer fidelity of 0.999 ± 0.0003 (averaged over 32 seeds), exceeding all prior methods. For the two-photon Rydberg C-Z gate, the method reaches F = 0.9996 at 0.25 µs duration with an order-of-magnitude lower peak Rabi frequency than prior work. For Transmon qubit reset under realistic bandwidth constraints, the RL-discovered waveform achieves 0.9997 fidelity, an order-of-magnitude improvement over the 0.983 experimental result of Egger et al. (2018). PPO also reaches >0.99 fidelity approximately 100× faster than DDPG or TD3.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The constrained PPO method outperforms all ten baselines across the three quantum systems. On the Λ system, it surpasses gradient-based optimal control (Giannelli et al., 0.890) and Krotov (0.99) by a clear margin, and dramatically exceeds the PINN approach (Norambuena et al., 0.83) and the analytic STIRAP solution (Vasilev et al., 0.901). Among RL baselines, constrained PPO (0.999) beats vanilla PPO (0.989), vanilla DDPG (0.985), and vanilla TD3 (0.625), with the constrained formulation providing a consistent ~0.01 fidelity gain over its vanilla counterpart. For the Rydberg gate, the method achieves F = 0.9996 versus 0.99 (analytic) and 0.997 (numerical) from Saffman et al., while requiring an order-of-magnitude lower peak Rabi frequency than Sun (2023). For Transmon reset, the RL waveform (0.9997) matches the ideal square-pulse fidelity and improves upon the calibrated square pulse from Egger et al. (0.983 experimental, 0.992 simulated) by roughly an order of magnitude. The Brown et al. (2021) RL solution, while competitive in fidelity (0.930), is noted as not experimentally feasible due to non-zero boundary amplitudes and instantaneous parameter changes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of RL-based quantum control (Bukov et al. 2018; Niu et al. 2019; An et al. 2021) by formulating the problem as a constrained bandit/multi-step RL task where physics-based constraints—maximum solver steps, Gaussian-smoothed amplitudes, and pulse-area penalties—restrict the action space. It departs from gradient-based optimal control (GRAPE, Krotov) which requires exact differentiability and struggles with noisy/open systems, and from prior RL quantum control that used discrete actions or lacked physical feasibility constraints. The key conceptual departure is the dual-purpose constraint on solver steps: it simultaneously enforces adiabatic dynamics (improving solution quality and robustness) and caps per-iteration compute (enabling GPU-parallel hyperparameter search via JAX), a coupling not present in prior work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Achieving >0.999 fidelity across three physically distinct quantum systems (Lambda, Rydberg, Transmon) meets the threshold often cited for fault-tolerant quantum computing with error correction, and the discovered HCGS reset waveform for Transmons is a concrete, hardware-implementable contribution that outperforms prior experimental results by an order of magnitude. The physics-constrained RL formulation is a general principle that could transfer to other constrained sequential decision problems. Key open questions include validation on real quantum hardware (the authors note they are 'actively working on' this), scaling to multi-qubit and higher-dimensional systems, and whether the adiabatic constraint inadvertently excludes useful non-adiabatic control strategies. The method also depends on accurate system models, limiting applicability to black-box or poorly characterised devices.
