## Problem

Tree-Sliced Wasserstein (TSW) methods replace one-dimensional projection lines with hierarchical tree systems to capture topological structure, but they remain restricted to linear projections onto hyperplanes. Meanwhile, nonlinear projection techniques (circular, spatial) have been developed for Sliced Wasserstein to improve expressiveness, yet no framework integrates them into the tree-sliced setting while preserving injectivity and the resulting metric structure.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252382/cropped_page5_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes two discrete probability measures with m and n support points in R^d (or S^d for the spherical variant), along with tree system parameters: L Monte Carlo tree samples, k lines per tree, and a fixed radius r ≥ 0 for CircularTSW. For SpatialTSW, an injective continuous map h: R^d → R^{d_θ} (e.g., coordinate-wise odd polynomials or a neural network concatenation) is required. The output is a scalar Wasserstein distance computed via closed-form 1D OT on tree-metric spaces, which can then drive gradient flows, DDGAN training, or SSL uniformity losses.

## Method

The paper defines two new Radon Transforms on Systems of Lines with nonlinear projection: the Circular Radon Transform on Systems of Lines (CRTSL), which integrates f over spheres {y: t = ||y − x_i − rθ_i||₂} with a splitting map α, and the Spatial Radon Transform on Systems of Lines (SRTSL), which first maps f through an injective h into R^{d_θ} before projecting onto tree lines. Injectivity is guaranteed by E(d)-invariance (or E(d_θ)-invariance) of the splitting map, proven by reducing to the known injectivity of the classical Circular Radon Transform or the linear RTSL. The resulting CircularTSW and SpatialTSW distances are shown to be valid metrics on P(R^d). A key efficiency insight is that CircularTSW with r=0 makes all k lines share identical projection coordinates, reducing the per-tree sorting cost from O(kn log n) to O(n log n), matching vanilla SW speed while retaining the tree structure's topological information through the distance-based splitting map.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 DDGAN, CircularTSW-DD achieves the best FID of 2.33, outperforming the previous state-of-the-art Db-TSW-DD⊥ (2.53) by 0.2 FID while also reducing training time by 10%. On the 25 Gaussians gradient flow task, SpatialTSW reaches a final W₂ distance of 1.17e-7 at iteration 2500, surpassing Db-TSW (1.30e-7) and vanilla SW (3.59e-2) by orders of magnitude. On spherical tasks, SpatialSTSW achieves the best log W₂ of -4.6859 (gradient flow) and 80.68% linear probe accuracy (SSL), consistently outperforming STSW and all S3W variants.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CircularTSW-DD achieves the best FID (2.33) among all 12 compared methods, beating the strongest baseline Db-TSW-DD⊥ (2.53) by 0.2 FID and vanilla SW-DD (2.90) by 0.57 FID. SpatialTSW-DD matches Db-TSW-DD⊥ (2.52 vs. 2.53) while CircularTSW_{r=0}-DD (2.48) also surpasses it, and is the fastest tree-sliced method at 211 s/epoch (19% faster than Db-TSW-DD⊥). In gradient flow on 25 Gaussians, SpatialTSW converges to 1.17e-7 W₂ distance, outperforming Db-TSW (1.30e-7), TSW-SL (3.16e-6), and vanilla SW (3.59e-2) by 1–4 orders of magnitude. On spherical gradient flow, SpatialSTSW (-4.6859 log W₂) beats STSW (-4.6486) and ARI-S3W(30) (-3.6661); in SSL, SpatialSTSW achieves 80.68% accuracy vs. STSW's 80.51% and SimCLR's 79.86%. CircularTSW and CircularTSW_{r=0} are approximately 5% and 16% faster than vanilla SW in gradient flow, respectively, though they underperform SpatialTSW on that task, consistent with prior findings that polynomial-based projections outperform circular ones for distribution matching.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate research threads in sliced optimal transport: (1) the Tree-Sliced Wasserstein (TSW) framework (Tran et al., 2025c,b,a), which replaces 1D projection lines with tree-structured metric spaces but was restricted to linear projections, and (2) nonlinear projection variants of the Sliced Wasserstein distance, including the Generalized Radon Transform (Kolouri et al., 2019), the Spatial Radon Transform (Chen et al., 2022), and the Circular Radon Transform (Kuchment, 2006). The key conceptual move is showing that the splitting mechanism and tree structure of TSW are compatible with nonlinear defining functions, yielding CircularTSW and SpatialTSW with full injectivity and metric guarantees under E(d)-invariance of the splitting map. The spherical extension (SpatialSTSW) similarly adapts the Spatial Radon Transform to the spherical tree setting from Tran et al. (2025a).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, theoretically grounded way to combine the topological expressiveness of tree-sliced projections with the flexibility of nonlinear projections, closing a gap where TSW was limited to hyperplane-based linear projections. The r=0 special case of CircularTSW is practically significant: it reduces sorting cost to a single line per tree, making tree-sliced methods competitive with vanilla SW in runtime while retaining tree-structure benefits. Open questions include whether the E(d)-invariance requirement on splitting maps is overly restrictive, how the choice of injective map h (polynomial vs. neural network) scales with dimension, and whether the framework extends to other curved domains beyond the sphere. The spherical variant (SpatialSTSW) opens the door to nonlinear tree-sliced metrics on manifolds more broadly.
