## Problem

Existing sensor scheduling methods for Kalman filtering either assume continuous measurements, discrete-time LTI systems, or treat sensor selection in isolation from the physical dynamics that govern sensor availability and quality. No prior work addresses the joint optimization of measurement scheduling and auxiliary state dynamics (e.g., robot trajectory, energy storage, sensor degradation) in a continuous-discrete setting where measurements arrive at irregular, sensor-specific times.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250028/cropped_page6_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a continuous-time linear-Gaussian SSM with S sensors (each with its own output matrix C_s and noise covariance R_s, both potentially dependent on the auxiliary state), auxiliary state dynamics split into a measurement-perturbed part ξ_p and an unperturbed part ξ_u, initial conditions, and a finite horizon with running/terminal costs and constraints. It produces time-varying per-sensor Poisson rates λ_s(t), an optimized auxiliary input trajectory u(t), and deterministic measurement times per sensor selected via closed-form Wasserstein-2 quantization of the optimized rate, along with a differentiable upper bound on the posterior covariance that tracks the true covariance closely in practice.

## Method

The core mechanism models each sensor's measurement arrivals as an independent inhomogeneous Poisson process with a time-varying rate λ_s(t), converting the stochastic covariance jump dynamics into a deterministic ODE where the Kalman update term is scaled by λ_s(t). Two key propositions provide the theoretical foundation: (1) a continuously differentiable upper bound on the mean posterior covariance, derived via Jensen's inequality exploiting the convexity of the Kalman update map (shown via a Schur-complement LMI argument), and (2) a bound on the mean perturbed auxiliary state under concavity/convexity assumptions, derived via ODE comparison theorems. These bounds feed into a finite-horizon optimal control problem that jointly optimizes λ(t) and u(t) subject to the bounded covariance and auxiliary-state dynamics, solved numerically via multiple shooting with IPOPT. Finally, deterministic measurement times are extracted from the optimized rates by minimizing the squared Wasserstein-2 distance between the normalized Poisson intensity measure and a discrete atomic measure, yielding closed-form conditional centroids that partition the horizon into equal-probability intervals.

## Results

In the radioactive-environment robot experiment (T=1), the proposed 'Optimized' method achieves a mean covariance trace of 1.902 (std 0.341, worst case 2.945), a mean residual energy of 21.54 (worst case 0.43), and a mean sensor degradation of 0.091 (worst case 0.186). The deterministic quantization-based scheduling ('Optimized') matches the performance of the expensive 'M-Optimized' baseline (which samples 10,000 Poisson realizations and selects the best) on covariance trace (1.902 vs. 1.890) while maintaining strictly positive energy, whereas M-Optimized's worst-case energy drops to −1.12. Both Greedy and Random baselines violate the energy constraint (mean energy −1.67 and −23.25 respectively) and exhibit substantially higher degradation (0.193 and 0.874).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against three baseline scheduling strategies (Random, Greedy, M-Optimized) that all use the same optimized auxiliary-state inputs but differ in how measurement times are selected. The proposed 'Optimized' method outperforms Greedy and Random on every metric: it reduces mean covariance trace by 27% vs. Greedy (1.902 vs. 2.608) and 16% vs. Random (1.902 vs. 2.279), while keeping energy strictly positive (mean 21.54) where both baselines go infeasible (−1.67 and −23.25). Sensor degradation is reduced by 53% vs. Greedy (0.091 vs. 0.193) and 90% vs. Random (0.091 vs. 0.874). Against M-Optimized (which samples 10,000 Poisson realizations and picks the best), the proposed method is nearly identical on covariance trace (1.902 vs. 1.890) and energy (21.54 vs. 21.22), but avoids the worst-case energy violation (0.43 vs. −1.12) and achieves lower degradation (0.091 vs. 0.098), all without the computational cost of sampling thousands of realizations. The authors note that no existing method jointly optimizes auxiliary-dynamics inputs and sensor scheduling in a continuous-discrete setting, so the baselines are simplified scheduling heuristics rather than full competing frameworks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies three previously separate research threads: (1) sensor scheduling in Kalman filtering (Ny et al. 2009; Orihuela et al. 2014; Marelli et al. 2019), which has been restricted to discrete-time or continuous-measurement settings; (2) active sensing and trajectory optimization (Yoon et al. 2018; Qin et al. 2024; Napolitano et al. 2024), which targets classification or GP regression without coupling to auxiliary physical dynamics; and (3) optimal quantization theory (Graf & Luschgy 2000; Villani 2009), repurposed here to deterministically select measurement times from optimized Poisson rates. The key theoretical departure is deriving a continuously differentiable upper bound on the CD-KF posterior covariance (via Jensen's inequality on the convex Kalman update, Lemma A.1) that is tractable for gradient-based optimal control, something prior scheduling works could not achieve in the continuous-discrete setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides the first principled way to jointly optimize measurement rates, sensor selection, and auxiliary-state inputs (energy, position, degradation) in a continuous-discrete filtering context, with a clean separation between the stochastic rate optimization and the deterministic time selection via Wasserstein quantization. Limitations include the concavity/convexity requirement on auxiliary dynamics (Assumption 5.1), the conservatism of the covariance bound in nonlinear regimes, and the computational cost of solving the OCP (multiple shooting + IPOPT), which may limit real-time deployment. Open questions include scalability to large sensor networks, handling truly nonlinear target dynamics beyond EKF linearization, and whether the receding-horizon extension (Appendix G) can provide closed-loop stability guarantees under model mismatch.
