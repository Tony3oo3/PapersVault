## Problem

RL policies trained under a fixed state feature space fail when sensors are maintained, replaced, or added during deployment, causing the state space to evolve unpredictably. Existing approaches either require prior knowledge of the new state space to learn sensor-invariant policies or enumerate many source policies for selection, both of which cannot guarantee performance under arbitrary, unforeseen evolutions without costly online exploration.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20253070/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes paired old/new state observations D_n (only 10 trajectories for MuJoCo, 15 for Atari) collected during a brief overlap period where both sensor sets are active, along with the previously deployed policy π_n. It produces an ensemble policy that outputs continuous actions (MuJoCo) or discrete actions (Atari) in the new state space, with the ensemble weight κ_{n+1} automatically adjusted at each evolution stage based on the old policy's retained performance and the divergence between the two candidate policies. A mild assumption is made that which sensors will be added or removed is known in advance, enabling the short paired-data collection window.

## Method

Lapse reuses the old policy in two complementary directions: (1) a conditional GAN (Pix2Pix) reconstructs old state features from new ones, and the old policy is composed with this reconstruction model, with robustness regularization (minimizing TV divergence of action distributions under ε-perturbations) added during initial training to bound performance degradation from reconstruction error; (2) an offline adaptive policy is trained via TD3+BC (MuJoCo) or CQL (Atari) on the limited behavioral data D_n, with a dynamically scheduled conservatism coefficient β_{n+1} that shifts from imitation toward optimistic updates as evolution uncertainty grows, plus a robust smoothing regularizer from RORL. The two policies are aggregated with an automatically adjusted ensemble weight κ_{n+1} that balances the reconstruction policy (closer to the well-performed old policy) against the offline policy (better at exploiting new sensors), computed from the old policy's performance ratio and KL divergences. Theoretically, the paper defines ε_R-ε_P consistency for the evolution mapping and proves that robust policy reuse bounds the performance gap by O(ε_R/(1-γ) + (γ ε_P + 4η)R_max/(2(1-γ)²)), where η captures the reconstruction model's approximation error.

## Results

Lapse achieves an overall normalized adaptation return of 91.09 across 8 tasks (4 MuJoCo + 4 Atari), nearly doubling the strongest baseline (Offline at 45.51). The improvement is most dramatic on HalfCheetah (82.56 vs. 28.31 for PAD) and Pong (100.0 vs. 33.56 for Offline), while on Ant the gap is smallest (84.02 vs. 82.63 for Offline). Results are averaged over 5 evolving stages, 10 episodes per stage, and 5 random seeds, with performance normalized against the initial-stage return.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Lapse outperforms all six baselines on 7 out of 8 tasks, with the largest margins on HalfCheetah (82.56 vs. 28.31 for PAD, a ~3× gap) and Pong (100.0 vs. 33.56 for Offline, a ~3× gap). RL-GAN (22.64 overall) and LUSR (17.24) struggle most, as their domain-adaptation approaches require substantial training data that the limited D_n (10–15 trajectories) cannot provide. PAD (31.56) learns sensor-invariant representations from M_0 alone but fails to cope with unpredictable evolutions, particularly on Pong (6.54) and RoadRunner (28.67). FPT (38.41) shows only marginal gains over RL-GAN, confirming that few-shot fine-tuning is insufficient for significant state-space changes. CUP (26.51) degrades sharply when source-target state-space divergence is large, scoring near zero on BankHeist and Pong. Offline (45.51) is the strongest baseline and is competitive on Ant (82.63 vs. 84.02) and Hopper (74.16 vs. 98.89), but its performance collapses on high-dimensional Atari tasks like RoadRunner (6.92) where complex evolution mappings make offline learning unreliable.

## Relation to Prior Work

Lapse sits at the intersection of domain adaptation in RL (RL-GAN, LUSR, PAD), policy reuse (CUP, hierarchical aggregation), and robust RL (Wocar-PPO, RADIAL-DQN), but deliberately departs from each. Unlike domain adaptation methods that assume access to target-domain data, Lapse operates with only a short window of paired old/new state observations. Unlike policy reuse methods that enumerate source policies for task transfer, Lapse reuses a single evolving policy through state reconstruction and offline behavioral cloning. The conceptual lineage from FESL (Hou et al., 2017) on evolvable feature streams is acknowledged, but the RL-specific formulation with MDP sequences connected by stochastic evolution functions and the no-costly-exploration constraint is new.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically important deployment gap: RL agents in robotics, autonomous driving, and other sensor-rich systems face inevitable state-space changes as sensors degrade or are upgraded, yet existing methods either require expensive retraining or assume prior knowledge of the new state space. The theoretical bound showing that robustness regularization (not reconstruction quality alone) is the key to limiting performance degradation is a useful conceptual insight for the broader robust RL community. Key limitations include the requirement for a short paired-data window during transition (which may not always be available) and reduced effectiveness under drastic state-space changes. Open directions include multi-agent settings with jointly evolving state spaces and relaxing the behavioral-experience requirement via LLMs or human instructions.
