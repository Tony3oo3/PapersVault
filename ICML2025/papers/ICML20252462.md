## Problem

Direct sparse linear solvers are too slow for interactive rates and neural architecture integration, while iterative solvers suffer from problem-dependent runtime, parameter sensitivity, and unreliable convergence. The paper identifies that direct solvers' speed has been significantly underestimated because they fail to exploit modern GPU parallelism for batched dense operations, leaving a gap for a solver that is simultaneously exact, fast, robust, and differentiable.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252462/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a sparse n×n matrix A with 9-point stencil sparsity (nonzeros only between adjacent pixels on an image grid) and a right-hand side vector b, producing the exact solution x = A^{-1}b. The global sparse matrix A is never explicitly stored; instead, the image is partitioned into overlapping 5×5 patches and the system is represented as a 4D tensor of batched dense Dirichlet-to-Neumann sub-matrices. The solver supports both float-32 and float-64, handles indefinite and nonsymmetric systems, and is fully differentiable via the adjoint method (requiring a transposed solve for ∂x/∂A).

## Method

The core contribution is 'Schwarz-Schur involution,' a recursive procedure that converts sparse solving into batched dense matrix operations on GPU. A Schwarz step partitions the image into overlapping 5×5 patches and eliminates interior 3×3 pixels via batched dense inversion, producing a wire-frame of Dirichlet-to-Neumann (DtN) matrices stored in a compact 4D tensor. Subsequent Schur steps recursively merge pairs of adjacent subdomains by applying the Schur complement formula (D = X − YW⁻¹Z), eliminating interface pixels and halving the number of subdomains each round. This is equivalent to parallel Gaussian elimination under nested dissection ordering, but all operations—slicing, inversion, multiplication—are batched dense BLAS calls that exploit GPU throughput. The backward pass recovers interior solutions from boundary values, and the entire pipeline is differentiable, enabling integration into learning-based PDE solvers and optimization layers.

## Results

On Laplacian systems (Dirichlet), the proposed Schwarz-Schur Involution solver achieves 10.9 ms for a 513×513 image and 220 ms for a 2561×2561 image, representing 40–168× speedups over CUDA (cuDSS) and 61–1151× speedups over SciPy (SuperLU). On a diverse set of 513² problems (anisotropic Laplacian, Helmholtz, random sparse), the solver maintains a consistent ~12.2 ms (float-32) or ~35.5 ms (float-64) runtime regardless of problem difficulty, while iterative solvers range from 58 ms to over 400,000 ms or fail entirely (NaN). On the Darcy flow benchmark, the method achieves 0.869% ± 0.0927% relative error in 5–7 ms without any training, outperforming the state-of-the-art PINO solver (1.56% error).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against direct solvers, the method dominates: it is 40–168× faster than CUDA cuDSS (LDU with METIS ordering) and 61–1151× faster than SciPy spsolve (SuperLU with COLAMD ordering), with the gap widening for larger images. Against iterative solvers (CG, MINRES, LSMR, LSQR, biCGstab, CGS, DIOM, FOM, QMR, BILQ, GMRES, DQGMRES, FGMRES), the method is consistently faster and, critically, problem-independent: on the Helmholtz equation (κ²=1.0/64) and random sparse systems, most iterative methods either diverge (NaN) or require 100–1000× more time, while the proposed solver maintains ~35.5 ms. AMG-preconditioned variants (PCG+AMG, GMRES+AMG, FGMRES+AMG) improve elliptic cases but still fail on Helmholtz and random problems, and PyAMG is 6× slower on anisotropic Laplacians. On the Darcy flow task, the zero-shot direct solver achieves 0.869% error (vs. 1.56% for PINO [Li et al., 2024]) at comparable inference speed (~5–7 ms) without any training data or parameters.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reinterprets classical nested dissection (George, 1973) and multifrontal elimination (Duff & Reid, 1983) through the lens of modern GPU batched dense BLAS, and frames the recursive merging of subdomains as an explicit Dirichlet-to-Neumann (DtN) composition following the Schwarz domain-decomposition tradition (Quarteroni & Valli, 1999). The key departure from existing direct solvers (SuperLU, UMFPACK, cuDSS) is the data-structure choice: rather than maintaining a global sparse matrix and performing sequential or limited-parallel factorization, the method 'condenses' the problem into a 4D tensor of small dense DtN matrices that are sliced and inverted in parallel. This repurposes decades-old algebraic ideas (Schur complement, nested dissection ordering) in a way that was infeasible before the GPU throughput and memory capacity enabled by deep learning hardware.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 60–1000× speedup over highly optimized direct solvers and the first demonstration of interactive-rate exact sparse solving on common image sizes (513² in ~11 ms) remove a long-standing bottleneck that has forced vision, graphics, and PDE communities to rely on unreliable iterative schemes or avoid exact solves entirely. As a zero-parameter, problem-independent, differentiable module, it provides a strong baseline for learned PDE solvers (beating the state-of-the-art learned Darcy-flow solver without training) and enables solver-in-the-loop architectures in robotics and inverse problems. Limitations include higher memory usage, restriction to 2D image grids with 9-point stencils (3D and larger stencils are future work), and focus on problems fitting a single GPU rather than billion-variable scientific computing. Open questions include whether the involution can be extended to arbitrary meshes/graphs, integrated with learnable discretizations, or converted to approximate solvers via DtN downsampling.
