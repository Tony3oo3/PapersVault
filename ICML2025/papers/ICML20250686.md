## Problem

In continual model-based RL, naively training a world model across sequential tasks causes catastrophic forgetting of previously learned dynamics, and real-world constraints (storage limits, privacy regulations) prohibit retaining raw interaction data from prior tasks. Existing anti-forgetting strategies either assume access to past data (replay-based methods) or rely on parameter regularization (e.g., EWC) that struggles in RL settings, leaving a gap for retaining and aggregating world-model knowledge without any stored past experience.

## Input / Output

The agent receives low-dimensional state vectors and a task-specific reward function at each step, with the constraint that only the current task's replay buffer is available—no past transitions are retained. It outputs actions selected via CEM-based planning over the learned world model, and maintains two incrementally updated artifacts: a shared dynamics predictor T_ψ and a VAE generative model G_θ over state-action pairs. The world model is initialized from the previous task's frozen copy at each task boundary, while reward, value, and policy networks are re-initialized per task.

## Method

DRAGO (built on TD-MPC) combines two complementary mechanisms to retain world-model knowledge without storing past data. First, Synthetic Experience Rehearsal uses a continually trained VAE to generate synthetic state-action pairs representative of all prior tasks, which are passed through a frozen old world model T_old to produce synthetic next states; the current dynamics model is then trained to match these predictions, anchoring previously learned transitions. Second, Regaining Memories Through Exploration introduces an intrinsic reward r_cont that rewards states where the old model predicts accurately but the current model does not, driving a separate 'reviewer' agent to physically explore and discover connections between task regions. The reviewer and learner share the same world model but maintain independent policies, decoupling task-solving from memory-reinforcement objectives. The VAE itself is continually trained on a mixture of current-task data and its own generated samples to avoid its own forgetting, and synthetic-data updates to the dynamics model occur at fixed intervals to mitigate noise from model inaccuracies.

## Results

On the few-shot transfer benchmark (20 episodes of interaction), DRAGO achieves the best average cumulative reward in 6 out of 8 test tasks across Cheetah and Walker domains. Headline numbers include 331.85±11.05 on Cheetah jump2back (vs. 73.98 for Continual TDMPC and 29.93 for EWC), 332.38±20.07 on Walker walk2run (vs. 229.14 for Continual TDMPC), and 265.50±8.40 on Walker stand2run (vs. 182.71 for Continual TDMPC). DRAGO does not fully eliminate plasticity loss: on Cheetah jump&runbackward, training from scratch (129.75) still slightly outperforms DRAGO (147.30), though DRAGO substantially improves over Continual TDMPC (140.82). Qualitative heatmaps (Figure 4) confirm that DRAGO retains prediction accuracy across previously learned state regions while naive continual TDMPC forgets nearly all prior knowledge.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DRAGO is compared against four baselines. Against Continual TDMPC (Hansen et al., 2022), DRAGO wins decisively in 7 of 8 tasks, with the largest margin on Cheetah jump2back (331.85 vs. 73.98, a 4.5× improvement) and Cheetah jump2run (106.78 vs. 93.96). Against EWC (Kirkpatrick et al., 2016), DRAGO wins 6 of 8 tasks but loses narrowly on Walker run2back (145.98 vs. 150.19) and Walker back2run (229.79 vs. 254.09), where EWC's regularization provides a slight edge. Against Pseudo-rehearsal MBRL (Ketz et al., 2019), DRAGO consistently achieves higher or faster-improving returns on all four MiniGrid transfer tasks (Figure 6), attributed to its continual VAE training and intrinsic reward mechanism. Against Continual PETS (Chua et al., 2018), DRAGO-PETS outperforms on 3 of 4 MiniGrid tasks (e.g., 233.21 vs. 150.84 on MiniGrid1to3after3), though it underperforms on MiniGrid1to2after2 (101.03 vs. 161.70). The paper also notes that Continual TDMPC suffers severe plasticity loss, often performing worse than training from scratch, a problem DRAGO partially but not fully resolves.

## Relation to Prior Work

DRAGO builds on TD-MPC as its base MBRL algorithm and extends the generative replay paradigm (Shin et al., 2017; Ketz et al., 2019) to the continual MBRL setting, where prior work had not applied generative models to world model training. It departs from replay-based continual learning methods by eliminating the need to store raw transitions, and from regularization approaches (EWC) by using a continually trained VAE plus a frozen old world model to synthesize rehearsal data. The intrinsic exploration reward complements the generative rehearsal by actively seeking real transitions that bridge previously learned state regions, a mechanism not present in prior pseudo-rehearsal or generative replay work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DRAGO addresses a practically important constraint—storage and privacy limitations that prevent agents from retaining past interaction data—by showing that a compact generative model plus a frozen world model can substitute for raw replay. However, the evaluation is limited to relatively simple domains (MiniGrid gridworlds, Cheetah/Walker locomotion) with only 3–4 sequential tasks, and the authors acknowledge that the single generative model may suffer mode collapse as task count grows. Open questions include whether the approach scales to high-dimensional visual environments, how to perform continual learning for the generative model itself, and whether the residual plasticity loss (where scratch still beats DRAGO on one task) can be further mitigated.
