## Problem

Existing prompt-injection defenses for LLMs demonstrate strong attack resistance, but it remains unclear whether they teach models to genuinely differentiate system from user roles or merely memorize adversarial patterns. This paper shows that fine-tuned models exploit two hidden shortcuts—task-type association and proximity to begin-of-text—rather than learning true role separation, leaving them vulnerable to novel attacks and simple prompt restructurings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252503/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard multi-role chat prompts (system instruction followed by user query in Llama-3 or Gemma-2 chat template format) and produces a single text response. The key unusual aspect is that during training, the position IDs assigned to tokens are modified: a fixed gap d (256 or 512) is inserted between the last system token and the first user token, while internal sequential ordering within each role is preserved. Evaluation uses adversarial attack datasets (Gandalf, TensorTrust) where the model must treat user input purely as data, plus ordinary datasets (password verification, Alpaca) to confirm no utility loss.

## Method

The paper first identifies two shortcuts through controlled experiments: (1) task-type association, where the model follows certain task types as privileged instructions regardless of role, and (2) proximity to begin-of-text, where the model treats only the earliest tokens as system instructions. While targeted data augmentation (swapping roles, inserting filler text) partially mitigates each shortcut, the authors argue this leads to endless iterative patching. Their proposed fix, Position-enhanced Fine-Tuning (PFT), modifies the position ID encoding by inserting a fixed numerical gap d between system and user token positions, creating a persistent token-wise signature that marks role boundaries throughout the input. This strengthens the invariant positional signal so the model can distinguish roles independent of task type or instruction placement. PFT is applied via standard LoRA fine-tuning on query and key projections, and empirically improves adversarial robustness across Llama-3-8B and Gemma-2-9B without degrading performance on ordinary data.

## Results

On the adversarial evaluation (Table 2), PFT achieves 94% on Gandalf Ignore and 62% on TensorTrust Extraction for Llama-3-8B, substantially outperforming vanilla SFT (86% and 33%) and delimiter-enhanced SFT (89% and 35%). For Gemma-2-9b-it, PFT reaches 92% on TensorTrust Extraction and 50% on TensorTrust Hijacking, compared to 70% and 37% for SFT. On utility metrics, PFT maintains 97% accuracy on the password dataset and log-likelihood of -13.97 (vs. -14.44 for SFT) on Alpaca, with minimal KL divergence from the baseline model, confirming that position ID modification improves robustness without degrading general performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares PFT against two baselines: vanilla SFT (standard LoRA fine-tuning) and SFT-Delim (which additionally fine-tunes the <|sh|> and <|eh|> delimiter embeddings). PFT wins on 3 out of 4 attacks for Llama (Gandalf Ignore: 94% vs. 90%/89%; TensorTrust Extraction: 62% vs. 33%/35%; TensorTrust Hijacking: 37% vs. 33%/32%) but loses on Gandalf Summarization (85% vs. 90%/93%). For Gemma, PFT ties on the two Gandalf attacks (99% and 100%) and wins decisively on both TensorTrust attacks (92% vs. 70%/75% for Extraction; 50% vs. 37%/37% for Hijacking). The delimiter-enhanced baseline provides only marginal gains over vanilla SFT (1-5% improvement), suggesting that strengthening delimiter tokens alone is insufficient. The largest PFT advantage appears on TensorTrust attacks, which involve more complex multi-sentence adversarial prompts where the proximity-to-begin-of-text shortcut is most damaging.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant prompt-injection-defense framing (Wallace et al. 2024, Chen et al. 2024) by isolating role-separation learning as a fundamental representational question rather than a security benchmark. It builds on the instruction-hierarchy training framework for data construction and on LoRA for efficient adaptation, but its core methodological contribution—Position-enhanced Fine-Tuning (PFT)—repurposes the positional-encoding modification techniques developed for long-context extension (Chen et al. 2023, Peng et al. 2023) to create explicit numerical boundaries between roles. Concurrent work by Wu et al. (2024) similarly targets token-wise role differentiation via learned embeddings, but PFT operates on the positional signal rather than adding new parameters, and the paper's controlled diagnostic framework (benign training, adversarial evaluation, role-swap tests) is distinct from the standard attack-benchmark evaluation used in prior defenses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that instruction-hierarchy finetuning produces near-identical behavior regardless of which role contains an override instruction (99.8% vs 99.2% refusal) challenges a core assumption in the prompt-injection-defense literature and suggests that current SOTA defenses may be brittle against novel, structurally different attacks. The PFT fix is appealing in its simplicity (a position-ID gap requires no architectural change and no extra parameters), but its evaluation is confined to two 8–9B models, a closed-domain setting, and a handful of attack templates; whether the approach generalizes to multi-role pipelines, open-domain settings, or larger models remains open. The broader 'find-and-fix' critique of data augmentation—each shortcut patched reveals the next—points to a deeper question about whether concatenated-prompt formats can ever provide sufficiently strong invariant signals for role learning without explicit architectural support.
