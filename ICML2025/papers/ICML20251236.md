## Problem

Existing drag-based image editing methods focus exclusively on matching user-defined handle points to target positions, neglecting the broader geometric context of the object. This causes geometric inconsistencies—artifacts, structural distortions, and unnatural deformations—particularly for rigid edits like rotation or pose changes where surrounding parts must move coherently. Additionally, prior drag-editing benchmarks provide no ground-truth edited images, making it impossible to objectively measure whether an edit faithfully reproduces the intended transformation.

## Input / Output

The method takes a 512×512 RGB image, 1–5 user-specified handle-to-target point pairs, and an editable region mask as input, and produces a single edited RGB image. Internally, the input image is first converted to a 3D mesh (via Marigold depth estimation or Hunyuan3D 2.0 diffusion), and the handle/target/mask are mapped onto mesh vertices to define movable and constraint regions. The edited image is generated by optimizing a Stable Diffusion 1.5 latent (DDIM-inverted to step 38 of 50) with LoRA-fine-tuned weights, so the output is a full-resolution image in the same space as the input.

## Method

FlowDrag is a three-stage pipeline that injects explicit 3D geometric structure into a motion-based diffusion drag-editing loop. (1) A 3D mesh is reconstructed from the input image using either a depth-based approach (Marigold depth → vertex/facet construction) or a diffusion-based image-to-3D model (Hunyuan3D 2.0). (2) The mesh is deformed via a progressive SR-ARAP energy minimization: the handle vertex is moved incrementally toward the target over K steps, and an inter-step smoothness term (β‖v̂⁽ᵏ⁺¹⁾−v̂⁽ᵏ⁾‖²) penalizes large per-iteration displacements to avoid local distortions; the resulting per-vertex 2D displacements form a continuous vector flow field Φ. (3) A subset of 5–30 vectors from Φ (selected via magnitude-based sampling on a 20×20 grid within the mask) replaces the single handle-point in the standard motion-supervision loss, and the 2D projection of the deformed mesh is DDIM-inverted and injected as attention features at an early denoising timestep (t′=30) to provide layout-level structural guidance. This continuous, geometry-aware displacement field—rather than a single point correspondence—preserves spatial coherence across the entire edited region.

## Results

On VFD-Bench (the paper's new ground-truth benchmark), FlowDrag achieves the best scores across all three metrics: PSNR 26.3, 1-LPIPS 0.85, and MD 24.51, outperforming the strongest baseline GoodDrag (PSNR 25.2, 1-LPIPS 0.82, MD 25.65). On DragBench, FlowDrag attains the lowest Mean Distance (MD 22.88), indicating the most accurate handle-to-target point alignment, though DiffEditor and DragDiffusion score higher on 1-LPIPS (0.89 vs. 0.82) because they induce minimal edits. A user study with 25 raters on 50 images confirms FlowDrag is preferred over all baselines on both drag accuracy and image quality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FlowDrag outperforms all five baselines on VFD-Bench across every metric. Against GoodDrag (the closest competitor), FlowDrag gains +1.1 PSNR, +0.03 1-LPIPS, and −1.14 MD, showing better geometric fidelity and point alignment. On DragBench, FlowDrag achieves the best MD (22.88) but trails DiffEditor and DragDiffusion on 1-LPIPS (0.82 vs. 0.89), which the authors attribute to those methods making minimal edits that trivially preserve the original image. GoodDrag is the nearest competitor on DragBench with MD 22.96 (nearly tied) but slightly higher 1-LPIPS (0.86). FreeDrag and DragNoise are consistently the weakest baselines, with MD values 35–38 on DragBench and 36–38 on VFD-Bench, indicating poor handle-point tracking. DiffEditor, despite competitive 1-LPIPS on DragBench, has the lowest PSNR (21.6) and 1-LPIPS (0.72) on VFD-Bench, revealing that its gradient-guided approach sacrifices structural fidelity for surface-level similarity.

## Relation to Prior Work

FlowDrag extends the motion-based drag-editing pipeline established by GoodDrag, FreeDrag, and DragDiffusion, which iteratively shift handle points toward targets via motion supervision and point tracking in a UNet denoiser. Its key departure is replacing the sparse, point-only optimization with a continuous 2D vector flow field derived from progressive SR-ARAP (Levi & Gotsman, 2014) deformation of a 3D mesh reconstructed from the input image. The method also introduces VFD-Bench, a video-frame-based benchmark with explicit ground-truth edits, addressing the absence of GT in prior benchmarks like DragBench.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The geometric inconsistency problem—where existing drag methods distort rigid structures like hats, arms, or faces during rotation or relocation—is a practical pain point for interactive editing, and FlowDrag's mesh-guided flow field offers a principled remedy. VFD-Bench is a useful contribution for the community since it enables direct PSNR/LPIPS comparison against real GT frames, something DragBench cannot support. However, the method is limited to moderate rigid edits, loses 3D information through 2D projection, and depends on Stable Diffusion 1.5's lack of native 3D understanding; future work with video or 3D-aware diffusion models could address these gaps.
