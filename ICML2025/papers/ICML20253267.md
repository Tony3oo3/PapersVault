## Problem

Certified robustness techniques are widely promoted as providing guaranteed protection against adversarial examples, but the paper identifies a fundamental misalignment: certifications bound the distance to the nearest class-changing example, not the nearest adversarial example, meaning they cannot distinguish between clean and attacked samples. This creates a 'paradox of detection without distinction' where the security guarantees implied by the term 'certification' do not match actual capabilities, potentially producing more security theater than real security for deployers who rely on these claims.

## Input / Output

The paper takes existing certified robustness schemes—randomized smoothing, interval bound propagation, convex relaxation, and global Lipschitz constraints—as its subject of analysis, considering them under ℓ_p-norm threat models applied primarily to classifiers with recent extensions to reinforcement learning and regression. Rather than producing a new certification, the paper outputs a structured critique comprising two formal theorems characterizing certification limitations and six open questions spanning practical deployment, evaluation methodology, coverage optimization, generalization to new model types, and secure code development.

## Method

The core analytical contribution is Theorem 1, which proves that a certification of size ε for input x could correspond to either a correctly classified clean sample or an adversarial example whose class expectation is large enough to produce a certification, meaning the existence of a certificate provides no information about whether the sample has been attacked. Theorem 2 formalizes the norm-mismatch vulnerability, showing that an ℓ_p-certification of radius r_p leaves adversarial examples uncovered when the attacker operates in an ℓ_q-norm with q > p, specifically for r_q > d^(1/q − 1/p) · r_p. The paper further demonstrates that certifications themselves can be exploited by attackers to guide adversarial search beyond gradient-based methods, and argues that the presentation of certifications as absolute security guarantees creates an exploitable attack surface between perceived and actual protection levels.

## Results

This is a position paper with no empirical experiments or quantitative results tables. Its key contributions are two theorems: Theorem 1 establishes that a certification of size ε for input x provides no information about whether x has been attacked (a certification can correspond to either a correct or incorrect class prediction); Theorem 2 shows that an ℓ_p-certification of radius r_p does not cover ℓ_q adversarial examples for q > p, with uncovered attacks existing at distances r_q > d^(1/q−1/p) · r_p. The paper further argues that certifications can be exploited by attackers to guide adversarial search (citing Cullen et al., 2024b), and that the only reliably actionable signal from a certification is its *absence*.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not propose a new method or run empirical comparisons against baselines. Instead, it surveys and contrasts three families of certification mechanisms—randomized smoothing, interval bound propagation (IBP)/convex relaxation, and global Lipschitz—along qualitative axes: architectural flexibility, computational cost (time, memory, parallelism), and the type of robustness guarantee (local vs. global, isotropic vs. anisotropic). The paper notes that randomized smoothing is embarrassingly parallel and architecture-agnostic but requires many model evaluations; IBP/convex relaxation gives tighter anisotropic bounds but is limited to specific architectures (historically ReLU-only) and scales poorly in memory; and Lipschitz-based methods (e.g., GloRoNets) are an order of magnitude faster but less flexible and typically yield smaller certified radii. No head-to-head quantitative benchmark is presented; the authors point to VNN-COMP as a proof-of-concept for structured IBP comparisons but note it excludes randomized smoothing and Lipschitz approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper does not introduce a new method but critically re-examines the conceptual foundations of the entire certified robustness literature, spanning randomized smoothing, interval bound propagation, convex relaxation, and Lipschitz-based approaches. It departs from the standard framing in prior work—where certifications are described as bounding the distance to the nearest adversarial example—by arguing the correct characterization is the distance to the nearest *class-changing* example, a distinction that fundamentally undermines the security interpretation. The paper draws on Cullen et al. (2024b), which demonstrated that certifications can be exploited to guide adversarial attacks, and analogizes to the differential privacy community's experience with 'privacy theater' to argue that the current presentation of certifications risks creating a false sense of security rather than genuine protection.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central claim—that a certification provides no intrinsic information about whether a sample has been attacked (Theorem 1)—challenges the foundational assumption underpinning the certified robustness field and raises the possibility that current deployment practices may create security theater rather than security. It poses six open questions spanning practical deployment, computational cost comparison, real-world testing, coverage across threat models, generalization beyond classifiers, and secure code development, calling for multidisciplinary research integrating game theory, HCI, and information security frameworks (NIST AI RMF, ISO/IEC 27001). The practical stakes are high: if model deployers treat certifications as blanket security guarantees, they may neglect complementary defenses, and the certification mechanism itself may hand attackers an information advantage. The paper's main limitation is that it is a position piece without new empirical evidence beyond citing prior work, so its arguments, while logically sound, await experimental validation at scale.
