## Problem

Existing preference-based RL and RLHF methods assume an underlying numerical signal (a hidden reward or utility) of which human preferences are stochastic manifestations, yet no principled theory exists for what can be learned when only a (partial) preorder over trajectories is available. This paper asks three foundational questions: (a) what dominance and optimality notions are well-defined when no numerical signal is assumed, (b) how to approximate a preference relation with a utility under minimal assumptions, and (c) how to convert a non-Markovian utility into a tractable Markovian reward with quantified error.

## Input / Output

The method takes a finite-horizon MDP without rewards (state space S, action space A, horizon H, transition model, initial distribution) together with a (partial) preorder over the trajectory set T, which may contain incomparable pairs reflecting genuine human uncertainty. It produces: (i) a multi-dimensional compatible utility whose minimum dimension equals the order dimension of the preorder, (ii) a Markovian reward vector r ∈ R^{SAH} obtained by solving a quadratic program that jointly optimizes the utility and reward to minimize approximation error, and (iii) a preference-based policy dominance relation and corresponding set of optimal policies. Notably, the dominance check for total orders reduces to O(|T|) cumulative-sum comparisons of sorted trajectory probabilities, while for partial orders it requires verification over all linear extensions (conjectured NP-hard).

## Method

The paper introduces the PbMDP setting (MDP + preorder over trajectories) and defines preference-utility compatibility via component-wise ordering of multi-dimensional utilities, proving that the minimum compatible dimension equals the order dimension (Theorem 4.1) and that computing a minimal utility is NP-hard (Theorem 4.2). A polynomial-time heuristic constructs a compatible utility of width w dimensions by solving a minimum path cover on the preorder DAG and extending each chain to a linear extension. For policy dominance, the key insight is that a policy π dominates π′ if and only if it does so under every compatible utility; for total orders this reduces to checking that cumulative sums of trajectory probabilities (sorted by preference) are component-wise non-negative (Theorem 5.1), while for partial orders it requires the condition to hold for all linear extensions (Theorem 5.3). Finally, a quadratic program jointly optimizes the utility values and Markovian reward to minimize ‖u − Br‖² subject to order-preserving constraints, and Theorem 6.1 bounds the resulting Pareto-frontier dissimilarity by 2√(mη*), where η* is the QP optimum.

## Results

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical experiments or benchmark evaluations. The key quantitative results are: (1) constructing a minimal compatible utility is NP-hard (Theorem 4.2), with no polynomial-time approximation within a factor of O(|T|^{1-ε}); (2) policy dominance under a total order reduces to checking O(|T|) cumulative-sum inequalities (Theorem 5.1); (3) a polynomial-time heuristic constructs a compatible utility with width(≤_T) dimensions in O(|T|(|T|+w²)) time; and (4) the approximation error between a non-Markovian utility and its Markovian reward approximation is bounded by L(u,û) ≤ 2√(mη*) (Theorem 6.1), where η* is the QP objective value.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper is a theoretical contribution and does not include empirical experiments, baselines, or head-to-head comparisons against other methods. The authors explicitly position their work as laying a theoretical foundation for future algorithmic development, noting that 'our work does not tackle the statistical complexity of learning with preference feedback.' The closest comparisons are conceptual: the PbMDP setting is contrasted with the MDPP framework of Wirth et al. (2017) (which assumes stochastic preference generation) and with RLHF approaches (which assume an underlying hidden reward or utility), but no quantitative benchmarks are provided. The paper's contributions are entirely in the form of definitions, theorems, and complexity characterizations rather than empirical performance numbers.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper draws on the classical utility representation theory from mathematical economics (Ok, 2002; Evren & Ok, 2011), which establishes that partial preorders can be represented by multi-dimensional utilities, and transplants it into the sequential decision-making setting. It departs from the dominant RLHF paradigm (Christiano et al., 2017; Xu et al., 2020) by explicitly rejecting the assumption of a hidden scalar reward or probabilistic preference model, instead working with deterministic (partial) preorders over trajectories that allow incomparabilities. The PbMDP framework also generalizes the MDPP setting of Wirth et al. (2017) by removing the stochastic preference generation process and the policy-likelihood-based preference definition, working with arbitrary preorders instead.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic theoretical treatment connecting preferences, utilities, and rewards in sequential decision making, establishing that the common RLHF practice of learning a reward model from preferences is fundamentally limited: constructing the minimal compatible utility is NP-hard, and the natural notion of policy dominance is intractable for partial orders. The clean reduction of total-order dominance to cumulative-sum conditions (Theorem 5.1) and the QP-based utility-to-reward approximation with a tight error bound (Theorem 6.1) offer concrete tools for future algorithm design. Key open questions include whether less demanding dominance notions (e.g., over a subset of compatible utilities) are tractable, and how to handle the statistical complexity of learning the preference relation itself from finite samples.
