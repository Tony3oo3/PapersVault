## Problem

Existing backdoor purification defenses are largely confined to supervised learning and rely on labeled data or adversarial-training-style procedures, making them inapplicable to self-supervised or unsupervised settings where backdoor attacks have also been shown to succeed. Moreover, many defenses remain vulnerable to low poisoning rates and adaptively designed triggers, leaving a gap for a unified, data-format-agnostic purification method.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251954/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The defender receives a backdoored model of any architecture (e.g., PreAct-ResNet18, ResNet50, CLIP visual encoder) together with a small fraction (5%) of clean training samples and knowledge of the original training procedure (SGD with cross-entropy, SimCLR contrastive loss, or CLIP fine-tuning). No labels are required in self-supervised settings, and the clean samples are used only for computing permutation matrices and training the Bezier curves. The output is a purified model whose weights have been iteratively updated over multiple TSC rounds (typically 2–3), with the final model selected at a fixed curve index (t=0.4 for supervised, t=0.25 for self-supervised).

## Method

TSC exploits permutation invariance to project a copy of the backdoored model into a distinct loss basin by solving a maximization problem over layer-wise permutation matrices (the anti-alignment step), then trains a quadratic Bezier curve between the original and permuted endpoints using only clean samples. Because the endpoints reside in different loss basins and the curve is trained exclusively on benign data, the adversarial loss on poisoned samples is amplified along the curve (first stage). In the second stage, the model selected from the first curve is re-aligned back into the original loss basin via a minimization over permutations, and a second Bezier curve is trained between the backdoored model and this re-aligned point to recover clean accuracy while preserving elevated poisoned-sample loss. The two-stage process is repeated for E_TSC rounds, with a brief fine-tuning step between rounds. Theoretically, the paper proves via Lipschitz-continuity arguments that the anti-alignment strictly increases the upper bound of loss on poisoned samples relative to any aligned or unaligned baseline, while the re-alignment tightens the bound for clean samples, providing a formal guarantee for the circumvention mechanism.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with PreAct-ResNet18, TSC reduces ASR below 15% for all 11 evaluated attacks, achieving ASR as low as 1.26% (BadNet, 1% poisoning) and 10.53% (Blended, 5% poisoning) while maintaining ACC above 88%. On ImageNet-100 with ResNet50, TSC achieves ASR below 13% across all attacks, with particularly strong results on BadNet (0.14–0.22% ASR) and SAPA (1.41–1.74% ASR). In self-supervised settings, TSC reduces ASR to below 11% across all SimCLR downstream tasks and below 2% for CLIP (both linear probing and zero-shot), where MCR completely fails (ASR > 96% on CLIP).

## Baselines & Comparisons

TSC outperforms all 8 baselines on the hardest cases: Blended attacks with small poisoning rates (1% on CIFAR-10, 0.5% on ImageNet-100), where FP, NC, MCR, ANP, and FT-SAM all fail (ASR > 25%), while TSC achieves 12.46% and 12.63% respectively. Against MCR (the closest methodological baseline using mode connectivity), TSC is consistently superior, particularly at low poisoning rates where MCR's ASR spikes to 18–100% (e.g., 99.86% on ImageNet-100 BadNet 1%) while TSC stays below 1%. SAU shows competitive ASR on CIFAR-10 but suffers catastrophic ACC drops (e.g., 65.38% on BadNet 1%), whereas TSC maintains ACC above 88% in the same setting. On ImageNet-100, ANP, FT-SAM, and I-BAU degrade significantly (ASR 20–97% on Blended/LF), while TSC keeps ASR below 13% across all attacks. In self-supervised learning, TSC dominates: MCR fails entirely on CLIP (ASR > 96%) and on SVHN under SimCLR (ASR 65.58%), while TSC achieves ASR < 2% on CLIP and < 11% on all SimCLR tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

TSC builds on two established properties of neural networks—permutation invariance (Entezari et al., 2022) and quadratic mode connectivity (Garipov et al., 2018)—but repurposes them in a novel way: rather than aligning models to merge them in the same loss basin (as MCR does), TSC deliberately *un-aligns* a model with its own permuted copy to project it into a distinct loss basin, then trains a Bezier curve between the two endpoints using only clean samples. This departs from prior post-purification defenses (ANP, I-BAU, SAU) that rely on labeled data and adversarial-training-like procedures, and from MCR which, while data-format agnostic, operates within a single loss basin and shows limited robustness. The two-stage design (amplify adversarial loss, then recover clean accuracy via re-alignment) is the key methodological departure, supported by a new theoretical bound (Corollary 4.3) that generalizes and corrects a prior result from Tatro et al. (2020).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TSC provides one of the first theoretically grounded backdoor purification methods that is genuinely data-format agnostic, extending effective defense to self-supervised learning frameworks (SimCLR, CLIP) where most prior defenses are inapplicable due to their reliance on labeled data. The practical requirement of only a small fraction of clean samples and the original training procedure makes it deployable in realistic settings (e.g., purifying a third-party pre-trained encoder). The main limitation acknowledged is the ACC/ASR trade-off—TSC occasionally sacrifices clean accuracy for backdoor removal—and the open question of whether this trade-off can be tightened. Broader implications include the potential extension to other learning paradigms (federated learning, unsupervised) and the demonstration that weight symmetry can be exploited as a defense primitive rather than merely a training artifact.
