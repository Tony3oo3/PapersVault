## Problem

Existing OOD detection methods focus on designing powerful score functions but rely on empirical threshold-based decision rules (e.g., fixing TPR at 95% on a validation set) that lack rigorous statistical guarantees. This paper asks how to design an OOD detection decision rule with provable error control, reframing the task as an online multiple hypothesis testing problem where test examples arrive sequentially in a stream.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a high-dimensional RGB test image and a fixed calibrated set of in-distribution images. A score function (maximum normalized logit) reduces each image to a univariate score, and an empirical p-value is computed by ranking the test score against the calibrated set. Decisions are made sequentially as examples arrive: each test image receives a binary ID/OOD label, and the decision threshold adapts dynamically based on the running count of prior OOD rejections, rather than using a fixed threshold.

## Method

The paper proposes the g-LOND algorithm, which generalizes the LOND online FDR-control procedure by introducing a transformation function f(·) from specific function classes (F1 or F2) applied to p-values before comparison with adaptive significance levels α_i = αγ_i(D(i−1)+1). The key theoretical advance is proving FDR control at a pre-specified level α without requiring independence or PRDS among the empirical p-values (which are inherently dependent through the shared calibrated set), by exploiting the integral constraint ∫₀¹ 1/f(x)dx ≤ 1. Additionally, under a generalized Gaussian-like distribution family for the test statistic, the paper proves the false positive rate converges to zero in probability as the number of hypotheses grows. In practice, the score function is the maximum normalized logit, and the algorithm operates online with a geometric sequence γ_i = C/i^v for the significance allocation.

## Results

On CIFAR-100 as ID data, g-LOND achieves FPR of 29.76% on SVHN and 30.62% on Place365, substantially lower than the best baselines (43.77% and 45.63% respectively), while maintaining TPR above 91.6%. On ImageNet-200 as ID data, g-LOND reaches FPR of 17.38% on OpenImage-O and 20.38% on iNaturalist, with F1-scores of 91.26% and 86.73%. In terms of classical metrics, g-LOND achieves the best FPR95 across all OOD datasets (e.g., 20.88% on iNaturalist vs. 29.53% for ASH) and competitive AUC/AUPR (e.g., 96.72% AUC on iNaturalist). The method trades a ~2-3% TPR decrease for a 10-15% FPR reduction, yielding consistently higher F1-scores.

## Baselines & Comparisons

g-LOND outperforms all 13 baselines on FPR and F1 across every OOD dataset in both Table 1 (CIFAR-100) and Table 2 (ImageNet-200). The most dramatic gains are on Place365 (FPR: 30.62% vs. 45.63% for KLM, the best baseline; F1: 52.87% vs. 42.85%) and SVHN (FPR: 29.76% vs. 43.77% for KLM; F1: 69.94% vs. 52.53%). On ImageNet-200, g-LOND achieves the lowest FPR on all five OOD sets, with particularly strong results on iNaturalist (FPR 20.38%, FPR95 20.88%) and OpenImage-O (FPR 17.38%, F1 91.26%). The cost is a modest TPR reduction of ~2-3% (e.g., 92.4% vs. 94.68% for MSP on CIFAR-10), which the authors frame as an acceptable trade-off for the FDR-controlled decision rule. On classical metrics, g-LOND achieves the best FPR95 on all datasets and competitive or best AUC/AUPR (e.g., 96.72% AUC on iNaturalist, 91.88% AUC on Texture), confirming that the statistical decision rule does not sacrifice the underlying score's discriminative power.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the LOND algorithm (Javanmard & Montanari, 2015) for online multiple hypothesis testing, extending it to handle dependent p-values through a generalized transformation f(·) that removes the mutual independence requirement. It departs from the dominant OOD detection literature (MSP, ODIN, Energy, KNN, etc.), which focuses on designing powerful score functions followed by an empirical 95%-TPR threshold, by instead treating the decision rule itself as the core problem and grounding it in FDR control. The empirical p-value construction from a calibrated ID set connects to nonparametric statistics (Arlot et al., 2010), while the asymptotic FPR analysis draws on the sparse-regime framework of Donoho & Jin (2004).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first rigorous FDR guarantee for OOD detection under dependent p-values, addressing a gap where prior methods offer only empirical performance without statistical guarantees. The asymptotic result that FPR converges to zero in probability under the generalized Gaussian-like family gives practitioners a theoretical foundation for trusting the method in safety-critical streaming settings. However, the practical gains come at a consistent 2–3% TPR cost, and the theoretical FPR result requires distributional assumptions (generalized Gaussian-like tails) that may not hold for all real-world score distributions. Open questions include how the choice of f(·) and the sequence {γ_i} affect the TPR–FPR trade-off in practice, and whether the framework extends naturally to sequential or adaptive OOD settings.
