## Problem

The Sinkhorn algorithm for optimal transport requires quadratic space and time in the number of points, making it infeasible for datasets with millions of samples. Low-rank OT achieves linear complexity but by definition cannot produce a one-to-one correspondence, while mini-batch OT avoids the quadratic cost at the expense of introducing systematic bias. The paper addresses how to compute a full-rank, bijective Monge map at scale—matching or exceeding Sinkhorn's primal cost—without incurring its quadratic memory footprint or the local-approximation bias of mini-batching.

## Input / Output

The method takes two equally-sized point datasets X and Y (each of size n) in a common metric space, along with a cost function c, and a rank-annealing schedule (r₁, …, r_κ) whose product equals n. It outputs a bijective Monge map T: X → Y as a collection of exactly n point-to-point pairs, yielding a coupling with precisely n non-zero entries (versus O(n²) for Sinkhorn). For log-linear scaling, the cost matrix must admit a low-rank factorization C = UVᵀ (e.g., squared Euclidean cost in d dimensions gives a (d+2)-rank factorization); for general costs with d = O(n), the method falls back to O(n²) space to store C.

## Method

The core insight (Proposition 3.1) is that the optimal low-rank coupling factors Q*, R* co-cluster each source point x with its image T*(x) under the Monge map, i.e., q*(x) = r*(T*(x)), under a strict r-Monge separability condition on the cost. Hierarchical Refinement exploits this invariant recursively: at each scale t, it solves rank-r_{t+1} low-rank OT subproblems (via the FRLC solver) within each co-cluster from the previous scale, using the argmax of Q and R rows to assign hard cluster labels that automatically place source and target partitions in bijective correspondence. The rank-annealing schedule (r₁, …, r_κ) factorizes n, with effective rank ρ_t = ∏_{s≤t} r_s controlling partition granularity; the recursion terminates when all blocks reach the base rank (typically 1), yielding the full bijective map. Under strict Monge separability at every level, the algorithm is provably optimal (Proposition 3.2), and the cost decrease between successive scales is bounded by the Lipschitz constant times the mean co-cluster diameter (Proposition 3.4). The overall complexity is O(n) space and O(ndr log_r n) time for constant rank r and cost-factorization dimension d, enabling alignment of over a million points.

## Results

On the MOSTA mouse organogenesis transcriptomics dataset (up to 121,767 cells), HiRef achieves the lowest Kantorovich cost across all four embryonic timepoints (e.g., 12.79 at E15–16.5 vs. 12.91 for MB-1024 and 14.00 for FRLC). On the 1.28M-point ImageNet alignment task, HiRef achieves a primal OT cost of 18.97, outperforming FRLC (24.12) and the best mini-batch variant MB-1024 (19.58). On the MERFISH brain atlas expression-transfer task (~84k spots), HiRef achieves a cosine similarity of 0.8098 for gene Slc17a7, more than double the scores of FRLC (0.2373) and LOT (0.3390). HiRef scales to over 2 million points with log-linear time and linear space, whereas Sinkhorn and ProgOT are limited to ~16K points due to quadratic memory.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Sinkhorn and ProgOT (full-rank entropic solvers), HiRef matches or slightly beats primal OT cost on synthetic datasets (4 of 6 evaluations) while scaling to 100× more points (2M vs. 16K), since Sinkhorn/ProgOT require O(n²) memory for the coupling matrix. Against mini-batch OT (batch sizes 128–2048), HiRef consistently achieves lower cost at every timepoint on MOSTA and on ImageNet (18.97 vs. 19.58 for MB-1024), and avoids the batch-size-dependent bias inherent to local mini-batch alignments. Against the low-rank solvers FRLC and LOT, HiRef achieves substantially lower cost (e.g., 18.97 vs. 24.12 on ImageNet; 12.79 vs. 14.00 on MOSTA E15–16.5) and dramatically better expression-transfer fidelity on MERFISH (cosine similarity 0.8098 vs. 0.2373 for FRLC and 0.3390 for LOT on Slc17a7), despite using FRLC as its internal subroutine. Against the multiscale method MOP (Gerber & Maggioni, 2017), HiRef outperforms on both primal cost (MOP's cost is ~2× higher on Checkerboard) and MERFISH expression transfer (0.8098 vs. 0.5211 for Slc17a7). Against the exact dual revised simplex solver, HiRef is competitive on 512-point instances where the simplex method is still tractable, confirming that the hierarchical refinement recovers near-optimal couplings.

## Relation to Prior Work

HiRef sits at the intersection of low-rank optimal transport (Scetbon et al., 2021; Halmos et al., 2024) and multiscale OT (Mérigot, 2011; Gerber & Maggioni, 2017). The key departure from Gerber & Maggioni is that HiRef constructs its multiscale partitions on the fly via low-rank OT subproblems rather than requiring them as input, and it operates intrinsically to the data without ambient-space meshes. The central theoretical contribution—Proposition 3.1, showing that optimal low-rank factors co-cluster each point with its image under the Monge map—provides the invariant that makes recursive refinement valid, a structural property not previously identified in the low-rank OT literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By reducing OT from quadratic to log-linear time and quadratic to linear space, HiRef makes full-rank, bijective optimal transport feasible on datasets with over a million points—enabling applications in spatial transcriptomics, large-scale image alignment, and precomputing global OT pairs for generative model training that were previously infeasible. Key limitations include the requirement for equal-sized datasets, dependence on the (nonconvex) low-rank solver's quality, and the absence of geometric guarantees on the induced partition diameters (Proposition 3.4 gives a bound but no decay rate). Open questions include extending to unequal-sized or unbalanced settings, accelerating the low-rank subroutine, and characterizing the geometry of OT-induced partitions to sharpen cost-decay bounds.
