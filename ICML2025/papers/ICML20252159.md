## Problem

In RLHF, reward models are trained from human preference annotations that are expensive, noisy, and binary, making annotation budget allocation a critical bottleneck. While active learning offers a principled framework for selecting informative comparisons, the optimal scoring strategy for preference pair selection in the Bradley-Terry reward modeling context remains underexplored, with prior work limited to heuristic designs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252159/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes pre-computed joint prompt-response embeddings (from Gemma 2B/7B or LLaMA3-8B) and an unlabeled pool of ~20,000 candidate comparisons (drawn from ~22,500 in-prompt or ~25M cross-prompt pairs per round of 500 prompts × 10 responses). It outputs a selected subset of comparisons for annotation and an iteratively retrained 3-layer MLP reward model with BT loss; the final linear layer has no bias since the BT model is identified only up to translation. The reward model operates entirely in embedding space, decoupling representation learning from preference learning.

## Method

The core contribution is adapting classical D-optimal experimental design to the last linear layer of a deep BT reward model. Under a linear reward assumption on embeddings, the BT model reduces to logistic regression, and the Fisher Information matrix decomposes into an embedding-diversity term (design matrix) and a comparison-informativeness term p(1−p), so maximizing det(FI) simultaneously encourages diverse embedding coverage and non-trivial comparisons. For nonlinear reward models, the paper applies this linear design criterion to the penultimate-layer features (a heuristic from Bayesian last-layer and core-set literature), then jointly trains all layers. A first-order Taylor expansion around the all-ones weight vector provides an efficient gradient approximation for the otherwise intractable combinatorial subset selection. A past-aware variant (PA D-opt) incorporates previously collected data into the FI, connecting the approach to Bayesian entropy minimization. The paper benchmarks 8 scoring rules (D-opt, PA D-opt, Entropy, Maxdiff, det(XtX), Coreset, BatchBALD, Random) across 2 datasets and 3 LLMs, showing D-opt and PA D-opt achieve state-of-the-art annotation efficiency and stability.

## Results

Across 3 LLMs (Gemma-2B, Gemma-7B, LLaMA-3-8B) and 2 datasets (Anthropic Harmless, Helpful), D-opt and Past-Aware D-opt consistently achieve the lowest 1-Spearman's rank correlation and highest Best-of-N reward among 8 active selection strategies, with notably lower variance across 5 random seeds. The methods maintain their advantage across annotation batch sizes (125–1000), with smaller batches (more online) yielding further gains. Under cross-prompt annotation, all methods improve, but D-opt variants retain the largest margin. The paper reports that D-opt reaches comparable reward-model quality to random selection of 20,000 comparisons using only ~1,000 informative comparisons, a ~20× reduction in annotation cost.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks 8 strategies; the four most directly comparable baselines are: (1) Entropy and Maxdiff from Muldrew et al. (2024), which select pairs near the decision boundary or at reward extremes respectively; (2) det(XtX) (Optimizing Design Matrix) from Mukherjee et al. (2024), which maximizes the determinant of the embedding-difference covariance without using model predictions; (3) Coreset from Huggins et al. (2016), a sparse-approximation approach for logistic regression; and (4) BatchBALD from Kirsch et al. (2019), a full Bayesian mutual-information criterion. D-opt and Past-Aware D-opt outperform all four on both 1-Spearman correlation and Best-of-N reward across every model–dataset combination, and they exhibit substantially lower run-to-run variance (stability) than the alternatives, which the authors attribute to the empirical-Bayes regularization inherent in applying the design criterion only to the final linear layer. Entropy sampling shows the largest relative gain when moving from in-prompt to cross-prompt annotation, but still trails D-opt in absolute performance. Coreset and det(XtX) behave similarly to each other (both emphasize embedding-space diversity) and are consistently outperformed by D-opt, which additionally weights each comparison by the model's prediction variance p(1−p). BatchBALD, despite being a full Bayesian method, is outperformed by the simpler last-layer D-opt criterion, which the authors explain via the empirical-Bayes interpretation that avoids over-optimization on hyper-priors.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges classical experimental design (D-optimality, Fisher information) from the 1950s–90s statistics literature with modern deep reward modeling, applying these design criteria to the final linear layer of a deep BT reward network rather than the full model. It builds directly on Muldrew et al. (2024) for active preference learning (entropy and maxdiff baselines), Mukherjee et al. (2024) for design-matrix optimization, and Kirsch et al. (2019) for BatchBALD, while distinguishing itself from concurrent work by Feng et al. (2025) by using last-layer FI as the direct optimization target (acting as regularization) and by jointly selecting prompts and responses rather than conditioning on prompts. The gradient-approximation technique for the combinatorial subset-selection problem is adapted from the coreset and robustness literature (Huggins et al., 2016; Campbell & Broderick, 2018).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: the proposed D-opt strategy achieves comparable reward-model quality with roughly 1,000 informative comparisons versus ~20,000 random ones, a ~20× reduction in annotation cost that is critical for scaling RLHF pipelines. The finding that cross-prompt annotations amplify gains across all methods (most dramatically for entropy-based selection) opens a path toward more efficient annotation protocols. Open questions include whether the last-layer linear approximation remains valid for non-BT reward models (e.g., quantile regression), whether embeddings optimized for human-value capture differ fundamentally from generation-oriented embeddings, and whether the approach transfers to multimodal or vision-language reward modeling.
