## Problem

Existing prediction-powered inference (PPI) methods can combine small observed datasets with large model-imputed datasets to estimate means, single quantiles, or regression coefficients, but they cannot handle the broader family of quantile-based distributional measures (QBDMs) such as CVaR, interval VaR, or population-segment averages. These measures are central to economics (wealth inequality), finance (tail risk), genomics (extreme expression), and LLM safety (worst-case toxicity), yet no principled framework existed for estimating them with valid confidence intervals from hybrid observed/imputed data.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a small set of n observed (X_i, Y_i) pairs with ground-truth labels, a large set of N imputed (X_j^u, Ŷ_j^u) pairs from a predictive model g, a user-specified metric M(X,Y) mapping input-observation pairs to real values, and a weighting function ψ(p) that defines the target QBDM (e.g., a Dirac delta for VaR, a uniform indicator for CVaR or interval VaR). It outputs a point estimate of Q_ψ(F) and a finite-sample (1−α) confidence interval; in the multivariate extension, it outputs a joint k-dimensional confidence region via a covariance matrix. Notably, the method requires no user-tuned hyperparameters: the blending weight λ has a closed-form solution, and the user may optionally clip λ to [0,1] for small-sample stability.

## Method

QuEst rewrites each QBDM as an L-statistic (a weighted sum of order statistics), enabling a central limit theorem derivation that existing PPI tools (which only handle M-estimators) cannot provide. The estimator Q̂_ψ(λ) = λQ_ψ(F̃_N^u) + (Q_ψ(F_n) − λQ_ψ(F̃_n)) blends the imputed-data estimate with a bias-correction term computed on the observed sample, and is asymptotically unbiased for any fixed λ. The optimal λ is obtained in closed form by minimizing the asymptotic variance ρ_ψ²(λ, F, F̃), yielding a variance that is provably no larger than the classical observed-only estimator. A multivariate extension derives a joint CLT with a full k×k covariance matrix, producing tighter joint confidence regions than naive Bonferroni corrections. A further extension (QuEst-Opt) replaces the scalar λ with a parameterized adaptive weighting function ψ̃_ξ on the imputed data, optimized via convex minimization with a mild regularization term to achieve additional variance reduction in low-data regimes.

## Results

QuEst consistently reduces both estimation error and confidence interval width compared to using observed data alone across all three research datasets (PovertyMap, GeneExpression, OpinionQA). The gains are most pronounced in the low-data regime: on GeneExpression with only 100 gold-standard examples, QuEst achieves roughly a 50% reduction in both estimation error and interval width. In the multi-dimensional news summarization task (100 observed, 10,000 imputed examples), multivariate QuEst reduces the 90% confidence region volume by 7% and MSE by 7% relative to classical estimation, while univariate QuEst reduces volume by 34%. In the LLM red-teaming toxicity experiment, QuEst-based model selection yields higher rank correlation with true toxicity ordering than observed-only or imputed-only baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baselines are (1) classical estimation using only the small observed dataset (equivalent to λ=0), (2) imputed-only estimation using only the large model-predicted dataset, and (3) for the individual-quantile experiment, the original PPI framework (Angelopoulos et al., 2024) with a fixed λ. QuEst outperforms the observed-only baseline across all datasets, with the largest gains when observed samples are scarce (e.g., ~50% error reduction at n=100 on GeneExpression); the observed-only method becomes competitive only around n≈1000. QuEst also dominates the imputed-only baseline, which performs reasonably on PovertyMap but is markedly worse on GeneExpression and OpinionQA due to model bias. In the direct comparison with PPI on the β=0.75 quantile, QuEst's adaptive λ selection yields lower estimation error overall, while confidence interval tightness is similar between the two methods, indicating the primary benefit is improved point-estimate accuracy rather than narrower intervals.

## Relation to Prior Work

QuEst extends the Prediction-Powered Inference (PPI) framework of Angelopoulos et al. (2023) and its power-tuning variant PPI++ (Angelopoulos et al., 2024) from M-estimators (means, single quantiles, regression coefficients) to the broader class of quantile-based distributional measures (QBDMs) such as CVaR, Interval-VaR, and multi-quantile segments. The key theoretical departure is leveraging L-statistics asymptotic theory to establish CLTs for estimators involving order statistics, which PPI's M-estimator framework cannot handle. The paper also introduces a multi-dimensional extension for simultaneous estimation of multiple QBDMs and an adaptive weighting function optimization (QuEst-Opt) that generalizes the scalar λ power-tuning to a parameterized function.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

QuEst opens the hybrid inference paradigm to distributional measures that are central in economics (wealth inequality), finance (tail risk), medicine, and LLM safety evaluation, where understanding tails and population segments is critical but prior tools were inapplicable. The multi-dimensional extension is particularly relevant for LLM auto-evaluation, where developers must simultaneously assess multiple quality criteria under budget constraints. Open questions include characterizing precisely when the method provides gains (minimum N, required correlation between observed and imputed data), and ensuring responsible deployment given dependence on model prediction quality. The paper acknowledges that performance depends on imputation quality, raising fairness and robustness concerns in high-stakes settings.
