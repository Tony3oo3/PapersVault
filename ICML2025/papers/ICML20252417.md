## Problem

Transformer training on in-context n-gram tasks exhibits prolonged plateaus and stage-wise progression (unigram → bigram → trigram → …), yet no theoretical explanation existed for why intermediate sub-n-gram solutions act as attractors in the loss landscape. The paper asks what structural property of the population cross-entropy loss causes training to stagnate at these intermediate solutions before transitioning to the next hierarchical level.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252417/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes a token sequence x^T sampled from an n-gram language model (drawn from a mixture prior P over transition matrices) and produces a next-token probability distribution via a two-layer disentangled attention-only transformer with (n-1) heads in the first layer and one head in the second. The key theoretical output is a gradient norm bound at k-gram parameter configurations: ‖∇L(θ_*^k)‖ = √c · E[O(‖p_τ − p̂_k‖²)] + O(T√c · e^{−c}), which vanishes as the attention scale c and sequence length T grow. The architecture uses orthogonal token embeddings and a fixed value matrix in the second layer to isolate the attention mechanism from residual-stream confounds.

## Method

The core tool is a sufficient stationarity condition (Proposition 3.2): if a model's score function depends only on a suffix x_t^T of the input, the population CE gradient reduces to an inner product with the error against p_τ(·|x_t^T) rather than the full conditional p_τ(·|x^T), so the gradient vanishes whenever the model correctly estimates the shorter conditional. The paper then constructs explicit parameter configurations θ_*^k in a simplified disentangled transformer where (n-1) first-layer heads retrieve (−h)-tokens and a second layer compares (k-1)-histories, implementing the k-gram MLE estimator in the limit c→∞. By exploiting the self-bounding property of softmax (derivative magnitude scales with the output value), they show attention scores at θ_*^k are highly sparse, restricting gradient contributions to positions whose (k-1)-history matches that of the query token. This yields the bound ‖∇L(θ_*^k)‖ = O(√c · ‖p_τ − p̂_k‖²) + O(T√c · e^{−c}), establishing sub-n-grams as near-stationary points and providing a mechanistic explanation for stage-wise learning: training pauses at each k-gram solution because the gradient vanishes there before escaping to the next hierarchical level.

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 4.1) establishes that the gradient norm at k-gram parameter configurations θ*^k satisfies ‖∇L(θ*^k)‖ = √c · E[O(‖p_τ(·|x_{T-k+2}^T) − p̂_k)²)] + O(T√c·e^{−c}), which vanishes as c = Θ(T) → ∞, proving sub-n-grams are near-stationary points. Empirically, on a trigram task (n=3, S=5, T=32), the model exhibits clear stage-wise learning: it first reaches a plateau matching the bigram estimator loss, then transitions to the trigram estimator, with attention heads progressively learning to attend to the (−1)-token and then the (−2)-token. The test loss plateaus align precisely with the cross-entropy losses of the corresponding k-gram estimators (Figure 1).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is primarily a theoretical paper and does not compare against competing methods on a shared benchmark. Instead, the empirical validation compares the transformer's learned behavior at each training plateau against the analytical k-gram MLE estimators. The key finding is that at the first plateau, both attention heads attend uniformly to the (−1)-token, reproducing the bigram estimator; at the second plateau, the second head shifts to the (−2)-token, reproducing the trigram estimator. The test loss at each plateau closely matches the corresponding k-gram estimator's cross-entropy loss, confirming the theoretical prediction that sub-n-gram configurations are near-stationary. The paper positions its contribution relative to Edelman et al. (2024), who observed similar stage-wise dynamics but provided only a one-step gradient descent analysis for binary bigrams, whereas this work gives a general near-stationarity characterization for unrestricted n and vocabulary size.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the empirical observations of Edelman et al. (2024) on stage-wise learning dynamics in in-context n-gram tasks by providing a general theoretical characterization: sub-n-gram configurations are near-stationary points of the population cross-entropy loss for unrestricted n and vocabulary size, whereas prior theory was limited to one step of gradient descent on binary bigrams. The analysis leverages the disentangled attention-only architecture formalized by Friedman et al. (2023) and Nichani et al. (2024), and builds on the gradient-of-cross-entropy framework from Bietti et al. (2024) and Makkuva et al. (2024). The key conceptual departure is shifting from analyzing training dynamics (how the model moves) to analyzing the loss landscape geometry (why the model pauses), using a sufficient stationarity condition that applies broadly beyond n-grams.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first general theoretical explanation for the widely observed plateaus and stage-wise transitions in transformer training on sequential data, linking them to the vanishing gradient at sub-hierarchical solutions. The practical limitation is that results hold only in the asymptotic regime (infinite sequence length and parameter norm) for a simplified two-layer architecture, leaving open how these insights transfer to finite contexts, deeper networks, and practical training with regularization. A crucial open question the paper highlights is characterizing the escape dynamics—how stochastic gradient methods navigate between these near-stationary points—which would complete the picture of stage-wise learning.
