## Problem

Per-subpopulation performance guarantees are a standard desideratum for prediction systems, but the definition of 'relevant subpopulation' has typically relied on predefined feature combinations. This paper argues that meaningful subpopulations should instead be defined endogenously as mixture components of the data distribution, where group membership is uncertain and must be inferred. The central technical question is how to provide per-component guarantees without first explicitly learning the cluster structure, which the natural cluster-then-predict approach does at the cost of O(T^{2/3}) rates and a requirement for cluster separation.

## Input / Output

The method receives a sequential stream of feature vectors x_t ∈ ℝ^d drawn i.i.d. from an unknown mixture of k densities (e.g., Gaussian components) with unknown mixing weights, and produces calibrated predicted probabilities ŷ_t ∈ [0,1] at each timestep. Group membership is never observed; it is characterized only through the conditional likelihoods f(g|x) of the mixture components. Performance is measured by two novel per-component calibration errors—discriminant calibration error (hard assignment to the most likely component) and likelihood calibration error (soft assignment weighted by posterior probability)—rather than standard marginal calibration.

## Method

The paper introduces a multi-objective approach that simultaneously provides calibration guarantees for all plausible mixture structures, bypassing the need to explicitly recover the true clustering. The key insight is that both discriminant and likelihood calibration error can be upper-bounded by multicalibration error over a carefully constructed class of real-valued distinguishers (indicator functions of argmax assignments for DCE, and posterior likelihood functions for LCE). The complexity of these distinguisher classes is characterized by the pseudodimension of likelihood ratios, which for exponential families is bounded by the dimension of the sufficient statistic (e.g., d(d+1)/2 for Gaussians). An online multicalibration algorithm (Haghtalab et al., 2023) is then run on an empirical cover of the distinguisher class computed from a short exploration phase, yielding O(√T) calibration error without any separation assumption between mixture components—improving over the O(T^{2/3}) rate of cluster-then-predict and demonstrating that providing per-cluster guarantees is strictly easier than learning the clusters themselves.

## Results

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical experiments. The headline result (Theorem 4.1) shows that the multi-objective approach (Algorithm 2) achieves O(√(T(dk log(T) log(k) + log(λ/δ)))) discriminant calibration error and O(√(T(dk log(T) log²(dk) + log(λ/δ)))) likelihood calibration error, both with probability 1−δ, without requiring any separation between mixture components. In contrast, the cluster-then-predict baseline (Proposition 3.1) incurs O(d^{1/3} T^{2/3} γ^{−4/3} + √(T log(1/δ))) discriminant calibration error, where γ is the minimum mean separation between components. For likelihood calibration error, cluster-then-predict requires Õ(T^{2/3} √d) with separation or Õ(T^{12/13} √d) without separation (Theorem B.2).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares two algorithmic paradigms rather than empirical baselines: the natural cluster-then-predict approach (first learn the mixture structure, then calibrate within each cluster) versus the proposed multi-objective approach (simultaneously provide guarantees for all plausible cluster structures via multicalibration). The multi-objective approach strictly dominates on both error rate and assumptions: it achieves the optimal Õ(√T) rate versus Õ(T^{2/3}) for cluster-then-predict, and it eliminates the dependence on cluster separation γ entirely. For discriminant calibration error, this is particularly striking because Theorem 4.10 (Azizyan et al., 2013) proves that learning the cluster assignment function itself has an irreducible Ω(d/(ε²γ⁶)) sample complexity, meaning the T^{2/3} rate is minimax optimal for any cluster-then-predict method. For likelihood calibration error, the gap is even wider without separation: the multi-objective approach still achieves √T while cluster-then-predict degrades to T^{12/13} (based on the ε^{−12} lower bound of Hardt & Price, 2015 for learning Gaussian mixture parameters). The paper thus demonstrates that providing per-subgroup guarantees can be fundamentally easier than learning the subgroups themselves.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the multicalibration framework (Hébert-Johnson et al., 2018; Haghtalab et al., 2023) by redefining subpopulations as endogenous mixture-model components rather than exogenous feature combinations, and by leveraging the online multicalibration algorithms of Haghtalab et al. (2023) and Gupta et al. (2022) as building blocks. It departs from the natural cluster-then-predict paradigm (which combines Gaussian mixture clustering à la Azizyan et al., 2013 with per-cluster calibration à la Foster & Vohra, 1998) by instead constructing a covering of all plausible cluster structures and providing guarantees simultaneously via a multi-objective approach. The key technical tool is bounding the pseudodimension of likelihood-ratio function classes, which for exponential families reduces to the dimension of the sufficient statistic.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that per-subgroup calibration guarantees can be achieved at O(√T) rates without any separation between mixture components, while explicitly learning those components requires separation-dependent sample complexity—challenges the assumption that resolving cluster structure is a prerequisite for subgroup-aware prediction. This opens a path toward multi-objective approaches for other downstream tasks (conformal prediction, calibeating) where group membership is uncertain. Limitations include the focus on calibration as the sole case study, restriction to exponential families, and purely theoretical treatment with no empirical validation; extending to non-exponential-family densities and to settings where the number of components k is unknown remains open.
