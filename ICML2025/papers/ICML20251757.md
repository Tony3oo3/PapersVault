## Problem

The only known unbiased loss function for computing Nash equilibria in normal-form games via stochastic optimization (Gemp et al., 2024) requires estimating the inner product of two independent random variables, causing its variance to scale as O(σ²) rather than O(σ). This high variance significantly degrades convergence rates and can cause the algorithm to fail to converge entirely on some games. The paper asks whether a surrogate loss can be designed that remains unbiased while eliminating the need for this high-variance two-sample inner product.

## Input / Output

The method takes a normal-form game specified only through a payoff simulator (since the full payoff matrix grows exponentially as O(nmⁿ) and cannot be stored in memory), along with the number of players and their action sets. Strategies are parameterized by a 3-layer MLP (1024 hidden neurons, Softmax output heads per player), and the output is the DNN parameter vector θ whose forward pass yields the Nash equilibrium strategy profile. The algorithm operates under sampled play, drawing S joint actions per iteration from the current strategy to estimate gradients via importance sampling with an exploration-augmented distribution.

## Method

The core contribution is the Nash Advantage Loss (NAL), which exploits the observation that standard ML optimizers (Adam, SGD) only require unbiased first-order gradient estimates, not unbiased loss estimates. NAL is defined as Σᵢ ⟨sg[Fᵢ^{τ,x} − ⟨Fᵢ^{τ,x}, x̂ᵢ⟩1], xᵢ⟩, where the stop-gradient operator ensures the gradient w.r.t. xᵢ equals Fᵢ^{τ,x} − ⟨Fᵢ^{τ,x}, x̂ᵢ⟩1 — a quantity estimable via importance sampling with a single random variable rather than the inner product of two, reducing estimation variance from O(σ²) to O(σ). The algorithm trains the DNN via Adam with progressively decreasing entropy regularization τ and learning rate η, using an exploration-augmented sampling distribution (1−ε)x^θ + εx^u to keep importance sampling weights bounded. Theoretical results (Theorems 4.2, 4.3) bound the duality gap in both the regularized and original games by the norm of NAL's gradient, guaranteeing convergence to an NE as τ→0.

## Results

Across eight normal-form games (Kuhn Poker, Goofspiel, Blotto, Liars Dice, Bertrand Oligopoly, Guess Two Thirds Ave, Minimum Effort, Majority Voting), the algorithm minimizing NAL achieves the fastest empirical convergence rate and the lowest variance in estimating the loss value. The variance in estimating NAL is reduced by at least two orders of magnitude compared to the existing unbiased loss function (Gemp et al., 2024) across all tested games, reaching up to six orders of magnitude in Liars Dice. The difference between estimated and true loss values for NAL is typically two orders of magnitude smaller than for all other tested loss functions, confirming its unbiasedness.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares NAL against three baselines: the existing unbiased loss function from Gemp et al. (2024), ADI from Gemp et al. (2022), and NashApr from Duan et al. (2023). NAL outperforms all three baselines on convergence rate across all eight games, with the most dramatic advantage over the Gemp et al. (2024) unbiased loss, which fails to converge entirely in Goofspiel and Minimum Effort. ADI (Gemp et al., 2022) fails to converge in Blotto, and NashApr (Duan et al., 2023) fails in Liars Dice, while NAL converges in all cases. In terms of variance, NAL reduces the estimation variance by at least two orders of magnitude relative to Gemp et al. (2024) in every game, with a six-order-of-magnitude reduction in Liars Dice. The only case where Gemp et al. (2024) shows competitive variance is Bertrand Oligopoly, where its variance is slightly lower than NAL's, but its convergence rate remains slower due to extremely high early-stage variance. The biased baselines (ADI and NashApr) also exhibit larger gaps between estimated and true loss values compared to NAL, further confirming NAL's unbiasedness advantage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Gemp et al. (2024), which introduced the only known unbiased loss function for NE computation under sampled play, by reusing its core insight (all action gradients equal iff at NE) and entropy regularization. The key departure is recognizing that standard ML optimizers (SGD, Adam) require only unbiased first-order gradient estimates rather than unbiased loss estimates, allowing the authors to reformulate the problem to avoid the inner product of two independent random variables that causes the high variance in Gemp et al.'s loss. The stop-gradient operator, borrowed from self-supervised learning (BYOL, SimCLR), is repurposed to decouple the gradient computation from backpropagation, and the importance sampling framework is adapted from standard ML practice to the game-theoretic setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies and resolves a critical practical bottleneck: the only previously unbiased loss function for NE computation via stochastic optimization suffers from variance that is O(σ²) versus O(σ) for NAL, causing convergence failures in several games (Goofspiel, Minimum Effort) where NAL succeeds. This makes large-scale NE computation via ML feasible in settings where the payoff matrix cannot be loaded into memory. Open questions include: (1) the paper provides no convergence guarantees for the algorithm itself, deferring this to the optimization literature; (2) extension to imperfect-information extensive-form games is mentioned as future work; (3) the reliance on a DNN parameterization and the specific entropy-regularization schedule (progressively decreasing τ) limits generality.
