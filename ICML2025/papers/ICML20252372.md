## Problem

Existing methods for evaluating intermediate Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning steps in LLMs either require expensive human-annotated step-by-step labels (Process Supervision) or infer step correctness from final-output accuracy (ORM, Math-Shepherd), both of which suffer from high false-positive rates when errors are correlated with input features. The paper asks: can we detect which specific sub-task in a CoT chain fails to contribute meaningful information toward the correct answer, without any step-level annotations?

## Input / Output

The method consumes the model's full CoT trace (a sequence of intermediate states X_0^M, X_1^M, …, X_T^M) together with the ground-truth final answer Y, requiring no step-level correctness labels. It outputs a per-step information-gain score (the conditional mutual information I(Y; X_j^M | X_{j-1}^M)) and, via a threshold, a binary correct/incorrect classification for each step. The information-gain is estimated as the difference in cross-entropy loss of a supervisor model predicting Y from consecutive CoT prefixes, making it computable from a single forward pass per step.

## Method

The paper formalizes CoT as sequential application of primitive tasks under a compositionally consistent update rule, introducing the notion of task unidentifiability (a task outside the span of learned primitive tasks). The central result (Theorem 3.3) proves that once the model encounters an unidentifiable sub-task at step k, all subsequent steps are conditionally independent of the true output Y given the prior state, so information-gain drops to zero. To operationalize this, the authors train a supervisor LLM (e.g., GPT-2 or LoRA-finetuned Llama-3-8B) to predict Y from any CoT prefix, then compute information-gain at step j as the reduction in cross-entropy loss between prefixes X_{j-1}^M and X_j^M (Proposition 3.4). A sample-wise variant replaces the expectation with a per-instance log-likelihood difference, enabling step-level error localization for individual prompts. This avoids the spurious-correlation pitfalls of ORM and the completion-based false positives of Math-Shepherd, as demonstrated on toy arithmetic, Llama-3-8B arithmetic, controlled GSM8K, and PRM800K.

## Results

On the PRM800K dataset, the proposed Information-Gain (IG) method achieves 74% sample-wise accuracy with 84% TPR and 37% FPR, outperforming the ORM baseline (69% accuracy, 55% TPR, 18% FPR). On GSM8K, IG reaches 72% accuracy with 95% TPR and 62% FPR, compared to ORM's 58% accuracy, 100% TPR, and 100% FPR. On the controlled Toy Arithmetic experiment with Llama-3-8B, IG achieves 76% accuracy with only 2% FPR, while ORM and Math-Shepherd achieve 56% and 53% accuracy respectively, with MS exhibiting an 86% FPR. The IG method correctly localizes the erroneous step (e.g., step 3 in arithmetic, multiplication in GSM8K) by showing a sharp drop in information-gain, whereas baselines either fail to distinguish steps or misattribute errors to unrelated operations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: Outcome Reward Model (ORM, Havrilla et al. 2024) and Math-Shepherd (MS, Wang et al. 2024b). Against ORM, IG wins on sample-wise accuracy across all three datasets (76% vs 56% on Toy, 72% vs 58% on GSM8K, 74% vs 69% on PRM800K) and on TPR for PRM800K (84% vs 55%), but ORM achieves a lower FPR on PRM800K (18% vs 37%). Against MS (evaluated only on Toy Arithmetic), IG achieves substantially lower FPR (2% vs 86%) and higher accuracy (76% vs 53%), though MS has a higher TPR (99% vs 51%) at the cost of flagging nearly all steps as incorrect. A key qualitative finding is that ORM and MS fail to correctly localize errors when spurious correlations exist (e.g., LLM₃ in Toy, subtraction in GSM8K), while IG consistently identifies the true unidentifiable step by showing near-zero or negative information-gain only at the erroneous operation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the formal LLM-as-execution-machine framework of González & Nori (2023) to introduce an information-theoretic lens on Chain-of-Thought reasoning. It departs from process supervision (Lightman et al., 2023), which requires expensive step-level human annotations, and from outcome-based methods like ORM (Havrilla et al., 2024) and Math-Shepherd (Wang et al., 2024b), which avoid annotations but are shown here to be unsound for step-level error detection. The key conceptual departure is framing each CoT step's quality as conditional mutual information (information-gain) with the ground-truth answer, grounded in a formalism of primitive tasks, compositionality, and unidentifiability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, annotation-free metric for identifying which specific step in a CoT chain fails, addressing a practical bottleneck in LLM reasoning evaluation. Its controlled experiments demonstrate that widely-used outcome-based baselines can systematically misattribute errors to the wrong step due to spurious correlations, a limitation that could misdirect model improvement efforts. Limitations include the computational cost of training a supervisor model and the need to categorize steps by sub-task type; open questions include whether in-context learning can replace the supervisor, and whether the framework extends cleanly to non-mathematical domains like planning or commonsense QA.
