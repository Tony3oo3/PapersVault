## Problem

Standard RL training for language models optimizes mean reward, implicitly assuming a single-sample inference procedure, yet deployment often uses k-sample algorithms like pass@k or majority voting. This paper asks whether explicitly optimizing for these inference-time objectives during training improves overall model efficacy, and characterizes the resulting trade-off between mean performance and k-sample performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252344/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt (a math question or competitive programming problem) and generates k distinct responses per prompt during training. For math, each response is a step-by-step chain-of-thought followed by a final answer scored via Sympy symbolic matching (+1/−1); for code, each response is a Python solution scored by test-case execution. The k responses are aggregated through an objective function f (max for pass@k, majority vote for voting) to produce a single scalar that drives the policy gradient update.

## Method

The core contribution is a general k-sample objective framework where an aggregation function f processes k i.i.d. samples from the policy, with the key insight that the samples are coupled through f so the gradient must sum (not average) weighted log-probability terms. An unbiased gradient estimate uses a leave-one-out control variate: each sample's advantage is f(y) − f(y_{−i}), measuring its marginal contribution to the aggregate. For pass@k this yields a sparse signal only on the best generation (the gap between top-1 and top-2 rewards); for majority voting it is non-zero only when a sample can flip the majority vote. A biased variant subtracts the mean advantage for further variance reduction, trading a small objective shift (optimizing a convex combination of top rewards rather than pure max) for smoother, lower-variance gradients. The framework is instantiated in both REINFORCE-style online policy gradient and PPO, where the k-sample advantage replaces the standard per-sample reward in the clipped surrogate objective.

## Results

On CodeContests (Llama 3.1 70B Instruct, trained with k=8), the pass@k policy gradient achieves 54.9% pass@100 on the valid set versus 38.1% for the mean policy gradient baseline (+16.8 points), while the biased pass@k variant reaches 55.8% pass@100 when integrated with PPO. The trade-off is clear: mean policy gradient retains the best pass@1 (24.6% valid, 26.2% test), whereas pass@k variants sacrifice 3–8 points on pass@1 to gain 9–17 points on pass@10 and 16–23 points on pass@100. Improvements generalize to the TACO benchmark, where biased pass@k with PPO reaches 53.8% pass@100 on TACO/easy and 21.3% on TACO/hard, compared to 39.5% and 11.1% for PPO mean.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baseline families: standard mean policy gradient (REINFORCE-style, Williams 1992) and PPO (Schulman 2017). Against mean policy gradient, the proposed pass@k variants consistently win on pass@10 and pass@100 metrics (e.g., 42.2% vs 32.9% pass@10, 54.9% vs 38.1% pass@100 on CodeContests valid) but lose on pass@1 (21.7% vs 24.6%), confirming the intended trade-off between single-sample and multi-sample inference performance. Against PPO mean, the gap is even wider on pass@k metrics (55.8% vs 33.5% pass@100 for biased pass@k PPO), while PPO mean retains a modest pass@1 advantage (20.0% vs 21.6% for biased pass@k PPO on valid). The biased pass@k variant (which subtracts the mean advantage to reduce variance at the cost of a small bias) generally matches or slightly exceeds the unbiased pass@k variant, particularly when combined with PPO's clipping mechanism. On the harder TACO/hard split, the pass@k advantage is most pronounced: biased pass@k PPO achieves 21.3% pass@100 versus 11.1% for PPO mean, a near-doubling of performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the leave-one-out control variate, previously applied to mean-reward policy gradients (Kool et al., 2019) and variational inference (Mnih & Rezende, 2016), to a general class of k-sample aggregation objectives including pass@k and majority voting. The theoretical framework builds on REINFORCE-style policy gradients (Williams, 1992) and draws conceptual parallels to k-sample objectives in variational inference and meta-learning, but the specific formulation of optimizing inference-time algorithms during RL training is novel. Concurrent work (Balashankar et al., 2024; Amini et al., 2024; Chow et al., 2024) pursues related inference-aware alignment but through different mechanisms (pairwise win rates, log-transformed scores, verifier-based best-of-k), whereas this paper provides a unified, unbiased gradient estimator for arbitrary k-sample objectives.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that explicitly training for inference-time objectives creates a predictable trade-off: improved pass@k at the cost of slightly reduced mean performance, with gains concentrated on harder problems and larger models. The finding that the effect vanishes when the task is too easy for the model (70B on MATH) or too hard (8B on HARP) provides practical guidance on when this approach is worthwhile. Open questions include extending the framework to more complex inference-time algorithms (self-correction, reflection, non-uniform sample weighting), reducing the computational overhead of k-sample gradient estimation, and understanding the training-evaluation gap observed where the policy gradient baseline sometimes recovers at test time despite underperforming during training.
