## Problem

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) has shown promise in molecular machine learning, but the optimal way to integrate retrieved references into structure-property models remains unclear. Simple strategies like concatenating reference spectra to model inputs yield minimal or even negative improvements because models cannot adequately account for structural differences between retrieved and target molecules. The paper asks whether explicit, learned structural matching between reference and target molecules can make RAG effective for molecular tasks.

## Input / Output

The model takes a molecular structure (as a molecular graph) and a continuous collision energy value as input, and produces a mass spectrum as a 15,000-dimensional intensity vector binned at 0.1 Da resolution (covering m/z up to 1500 Da). During inference, the model also retrieves the most structurally similar reference molecule (via Tanimoto similarity on Morgan fingerprints) along with up to three reference spectra at the closest collision energies from a reference library. The intermediate representation is a fragmentation DAG where each node is a molecular fragment, and the matching module produces a soft assignment matrix between target and reference fragments.

## Method

MARASON builds on the ICEBERG two-stage simulator (ICEBERG-Generate for fragmentation DAGs, ICEBERG-Score for intensities) and inserts a neural graph matching module between retrieval and intensity prediction. A nested GNN architecture learns fragment-level embeddings (encoding each fragment's structure, its parent molecule, and the neutral loss) and DAG hierarchical embeddings (via forward and reverse message passing over the fragmentation DAG), using separate GNNs for target and reference fragments plus a shared GNN for matching. The learned embeddings are compared via cosine similarity to form an affinity matrix, which is passed through a differentiable Softmax matching layer to produce a continuous matching matrix that aligns reference fragments and their annotated peak intensities to target fragments. The matched triplets (target fragment, reference fragment, reference intensity) along with matching scores and molecular Tanimoto similarity are fed into a set transformer to predict final peak intensities. The key distinction from traditional graph matching is that the affinity metric is learned end-to-end rather than fixed (e.g., Tanimoto), making the matching robust to non-identical but functionally similar fragments.

## Results

On the NIST 2020 dataset (random split), MARASON achieves 27.8% top-1 retrieval accuracy, a 48% relative improvement over the next-best method (MassFormer at 19.1%) and a 47% relative improvement over ICEBERG (18.9%). In terms of spectral cosine similarity, MARASON reaches 0.765 on the random split and 0.725 on the scaffold split, outperforming ICEBERG-with-collision-energy (0.735 / 0.711) by a relative 4.1% and 1.8% respectively. On the more challenging MassSpecGym benchmark, MARASON achieves 34.03% top-1 retrieval accuracy, surpassing the previous state-of-the-art FraGNNet (31.93%) by a 6% relative margin.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MARASON outperforms all eight baselines across every top-k retrieval accuracy on NIST 2020. The largest gap is against 3DMolMS (5.5% top-1) and NEIMS-FFN (10.5% top-1), which lack graph-based fragmentation modeling. Against the strongest non-RAG baseline ICEBERG (18.9% top-1), MARASON gains 8.9 percentage points; even ICEBERG augmented with collision energy (20.2%) trails MARASON by 7.6 points, confirming the gain is attributable to retrieval augmentation rather than the collision-energy input alone. MassFormer (19.1%) and SCARF (18.7%) are the closest non-RAG competitors but remain ~9 points behind. On MassSpecGym, MARASON (34.03% top-1) beats FraGNNet (31.93%), the only other method evaluated on that benchmark, by 2.1 percentage points. In the ablation (Table 3), naive spectrum concatenation actually degrades cosine similarity (−0.3%), while traditional graph matching solvers (Hungarian, RRWM) yield only +0.4–0.9%, confirming that the end-to-end neural graph matching module is the critical component.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the ICEBERG fragmentation-based MS/MS simulator by integrating retrieval-augmented generation through neural graph matching, drawing on the learned-affinity-metric framework of Wang et al. (2022) and the nested GNN architecture of Zhang & Li (2021). It departs from prior RAG-in-molecular-ML work (which typically concatenates retrieved features naively) by formulating fragment alignment as a differentiable graph matching problem over fragmentation DAGs, and from traditional graph matching (Hungarian, RRWM) by replacing fixed Tanimoto-based affinities with end-to-end learned metrics. The paper positions itself at the intersection of the RAG literature (Lewis et al., 2020) and geometric deep learning for molecules, arguing that structural alignment is the missing piece for effective retrieval in small-molecule tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 48% relative improvement in top-1 retrieval accuracy (19%→28%) on NIST and consistent gains on MassSpecGym and scaffold splits make MARASON a practically significant advance for structural elucidation campaigns in metabolomics, drug discovery, and environmental science. The finding that naive spectrum concatenation actively hurts performance, while learned graph matching helps, provides a clear design principle for future RAG systems in molecular ML. Open questions include whether the neural graph matching strategy generalizes to other structure-property prediction tasks beyond MS/MS, and how the method performs when collision energy labels are unavailable (a common real-world constraint). The authors also note the potential to expand simulated spectral libraries from ~27K to 111M PubChem compounds.
