## Problem

While Transformers excel at pattern discovery, it remains unclear whether they can learn the underlying mathematical structure of pseudo-random number generators or merely detect spurious correlations. This paper addresses this gap by systematically studying whether GPT-style Transformers can predict LCG sequences with unseen parameters (a, c) and even unseen moduli (m), and by reverse-engineering the specific algorithms and internal representations the models develop to solve these tasks.

## Input / Output

The model receives an LCG sequence (x_0, …, x_{L-1}) as context and autoregressively predicts the next number x_L, having to infer the generator parameters (m, a, c) entirely in-context. For small moduli (Sections 3–4), each integer is a single token from a vocabulary of size m; for large moduli (Section 5, m up to 2^32), each integer is decomposed into ⌈log_b m⌉ base-b digit tokens (e.g., 4 bytes for m=2^32) with abacus positional embeddings encoding both the integer's sequence position and each digit's position within the integer. Test evaluation is performed on sequences satisfying the Hull-Dobell full-period condition with unseen (a, c) pairs, and in the UM setting, unseen m_test values.

## Method

The paper trains decoder-only GPT-style Transformers (1–6 layers, 1–4 heads) on LCG sequences and then performs mechanistic interpretability to uncover the learned algorithm. In the fixed-modulus setting, a single-layer single-head model suffices: the embedding layer develops principal components that cluster numbers by their residues modulo powers of 2 (i.e., a binary/RNS representation), attention heads look back r = 2^k steps to copy the lowest k bits and simplify higher-bit prediction, and MLP neurons exhibit sparse periodic activations that constructively interfere to resolve the correct output. In the unseen-modulus setting, a minimum depth of 3 layers is required; the model first uses a dedicated attention head to greedily estimate m_test from the largest in-context values, then employs specialized first-layer heads that group numbers by residues modulo different primes (2, 3, 5, 7) to construct the appropriate RNS representation, before applying the same r-step copying and higher-digit prediction strategy. The number of in-context elements needed for high accuracy scales sublinearly with modulus (m^γ, γ ≈ 1/4 for FM, 0.24–0.33 for UM), and test accuracy exhibits a sharp transition at the critical depth d=3.

## Results

For the Fixed Modulus (FM) task, a minimal 1-layer, single-head Transformer (d_model=768) achieves 100% test accuracy on LCG sequences with unseen (a,c) parameters, scaling up to m=2^32. For the more challenging Unseen Modulus (UM) task, a minimum depth of 3 layers is required (with a sharp accuracy transition below this threshold), and the model generalizes to unseen moduli up to m_test=2^16. The number of in-context sequence elements needed to reach 100% test accuracy scales sublinearly with modulus as m^γ with γ≈1/4 in the FM setting, and as m_test^γ with 0.24≤γ≤0.33 for 60% accuracy in the UM setting. The model's per-digit accuracy exhibits a ladder-like structure with transitions at 2^k-th token positions, and overall accuracy multiplicatively depends on per-digit accuracies.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper is an interpretability and scaling study rather than a method comparison, so there are no external baselines or competing architectures being benchmarked. The primary internal comparisons are between the two task paradigms (FM vs. UM) and across architectural choices. The FM task is dramatically easier: a single attention head in one layer suffices, whereas the UM task requires at least 3 layers with a sharp performance cliff below this depth (Figure 1, Appendix D). Increasing attention heads from 1 to 2 yields substantial gains in the UM setting, attributed to the model's improved capacity to capture additional prime factors for RNS construction. In the FM setting, prime moduli (e.g., m=2039) are significantly harder than composite moduli (e.g., m=2048) because the digit-wise periodic structure is absent, yet in the UM setting prime and power-of-two moduli show comparable test performance, suggesting diverse training moduli help the model rely on more generalizable structure. The paper also compares tokenization bases in the UM scaling experiments, showing that base-2^8 tokenization favors m=2^k moduli while base-3^5 favors m=3^k moduli, confirming that alignment between tokenization and modulus structure aids learning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the interpretability literature on Transformers performing modular arithmetic (Power et al., 2022; Gromov, 2023; He et al., 2024) from simple operations like addition to the more complex sequential prediction task of LCG sequences, and departs from the prior PRNG-prediction work of Amigo et al. (2021), which used a fully-connected network on a fixed-parameter LCG, by systematically studying unseen parameters and moduli with GPT-style Transformers. The base-b tokenization and abacus positional embeddings from McLeish et al. (2024) are adopted to handle large moduli, and the grokking phenomenon (Power et al., 2022; Nanda et al., 2023) provides the training-dynamics framework for understanding when generalization emerges. The paper also connects to the formal-grammar view of LCGs within the Chomsky hierarchy, positioning LCG sequences as a controlled synthetic task for probing Transformer capabilities.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a detailed mechanistic account of how Transformers develop Residual Number System representations, prime factorization, and r-step iteration strategies to predict LCG sequences, offering concrete evidence that Transformers implement algorithmic (rather than purely statistical) solutions for structured sequential data. The critical depth of 3 for unseen-modulus generalization and the sublinear (m^γ, γ≈1/4) scaling of required context length with modulus are useful quantitative characterizations. Limitations include the restriction to LCGs (not PCGs or truncated variants), moduli up to 2^32, and the acknowledged gap in understanding the exact mechanism for higher-digit prediction in the unseen-modulus case. Open questions include whether these findings extend to cryptographically relevant PRNGs and whether the learned RNS-based algorithm generalizes to broader classes of arithmetic sequences.
