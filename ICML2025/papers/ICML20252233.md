## Problem

Standard preference optimization algorithms for LLMs (DPO, IPO) assume human preferences are stationary, treating all training datapoints as equally informative regardless of when they were collected. When preference data is gathered over extended periods, temporal drift causes older labels to become stale, and stationary methods interpret this drift as label noise, leading to degraded alignment. This paper addresses the gap by asking how to perform direct preference optimization on offline datasets where the underlying reward function changes over time, without knowing which specific preferences shift.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a preference dataset where each triple (prompt, preferred response, rejected response) is annotated with a collection timestamp t_i, ordered so that t_i ≤ t_j for i < j, along with a reference LLM policy π_ref. The only additional hyperparameter beyond standard DPO is a single discount factor γ ∈ (0,1) controlling how aggressively older datapoints are down-weighted. The output is a standard fine-tuned LLM policy (e.g., LoRA-adapted Llama-2-7b-chat-hf or full-parameter Llama-3.2-1b-it) that generates text responses aligned to the most recent preference distribution at the current time step T.

## Method

NS-DPO replaces the stationary Bradley-Terry model with a Dynamic Bradley-Terry model in which the reward r(x,a,t) is explicitly time-dependent, then derives an exponentially weighted DPO loss: each datapoint's contribution to the negative log-likelihood is scaled by γ^(T−t_i−1), so that losses from more recent preferences dominate the gradient. The key insight is that this single multiplicative weight on the standard DPO loss is sufficient to track a non-stationary reward without needing to know which individual preferences change—only an upper bound B_T on total parameter drift (the variation budget) is required. Theoretically, with log-linear policies and standard offline coverage assumptions, the paper proves an estimation error bound decomposed into a learning term (O(√(d/n))) and a tracking term (O(B_T·√(d·m̄/n)/(T(1−γ)^(3/2)))), yielding a regret of Õ(d·B_T^(3/4)·n^(−1/4)) under the optimal choice γ = 1−(B_T/T)^(3/4). In the stationary limit B_T→0, the bound recovers the standard O(n^(−1/2)) rate, confirming NS-DPO subsumes DPO as a special case.

## Results

On the UltraFeedback-LM dataset (Llama-3.2-1b-it, AlpacaEval2 LCWR), NS-DPO achieves 7.36–9.00 LCWR across varying drift strengths (ρ_diff ∈ {0.7, 1.0}) and change points (t_cp ∈ {21, 51, 81}), consistently outperforming DPO (6.49–8.23) and SW-DPO (4.63–6.71). In the stationary setting (t_cp = 0), NS-DPO matches DPO at 9.12 vs. 8.81 LCWR. On the TV-HH dataset with gradual preference drift, NS-DPO exceeds DPO reward accuracy by over 10%, and on the 2C NSGO dataset it maintains reward accuracy above 60% while stationary methods drop to ~55%. Theoretically, NS-DPO achieves Õ(d·B_T^{3/4}·n^{-1/4}) regret under non-stationarity, recovering O(n^{-1/2}) when B_T → 0.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NS-DPO is compared against stationary DPO (Rafailov et al., 2024) and IPO (Azar et al., 2024), plus a sliding-window variant (SW-DPO) and an in-context-learning variant (tDPO). Against DPO, NS-DPO wins consistently under non-stationarity: on UltraFeedback-LM it gains 0.6–1.2 LCWR points at early change points and up to 1.5 points at later ones (e.g., 7.36 vs. 6.49 at ρ_diff=1.0, t_cp=81); on TV-HH it achieves >0.5 win rate against DPO at all ρ_diff values for later change points, and exceeds DPO reward accuracy by >10% under gradual drift. Against IPO, NS-DPO matches performance for early change points (t_cp ∈ {10, 30}) but pulls ahead as the change point approaches the present (t_cp = 81, 100), where IPO still treats stale labels as equally valid. In the stationary regime (t_cp = 0), NS-DPO ties or slightly exceeds DPO (9.12 vs. 8.81 LCWR), confirming no performance penalty for the added discount parameter. SW-DPO is more sensitive to its window-size hyperparameter and trains slower, while tDPO (appending time-step info to prompts) shows no meaningful improvement over plain DPO.

## Relation to Prior Work

NS-DPO extends Direct Preference Optimization (Rafailov et al., 2024) by incorporating the Dynamic Bradley-Terry model (Cattelan et al., 2013; Bong et al., 2020) to handle time-varying preferences, adding a single exponential discount parameter γ to the DPO loss. The discounting mechanism is borrowed from the non-stationary bandit literature on exponentially weighted estimators (Faury et al., 2021; Russac et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2023), which had previously been applied to generalized linear bandits but not to offline preference learning. The paper is the first to provide theoretical guarantees (regret bounds) for offline DPO when the underlying reward parameter is allowed to drift over time, bridging the gap between stationary preference optimization theory (Chowdhury et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2023) and non-stationary bandit analysis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As preference datasets accumulate over longer time horizons, temporal drift becomes an increasingly realistic concern that stationary methods silently treat as label noise, potentially degrading alignment. The practical significance is amplified by the minimal implementation cost—NS-DPO requires only one additional hyperparameter (γ) on top of standard DPO, making it easy to adopt without architectural changes. Key limitations include the restriction to log-linear policies in the theoretical analysis, the offline-only setting, and the acknowledgment that the discount parameter may amplify biases present in newer data. Open questions include extending to the online setting, adapting to learn at arbitrary (non-present) time steps, and handling cases where the drift structure is partially known.
