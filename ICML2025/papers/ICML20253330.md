## Problem

Current AI evaluation relies on static, large-scale benchmarks where all items are treated as equally important, incurring enormous computational costs while remaining vulnerable to data contamination, annotation errors, and low-quality items. This paper argues that the field should adopt the adaptive testing paradigm from human psychometrics, which estimates item characteristics and tailors evaluation to each model rather than administering a fixed test to all.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20253330/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes a benchmark dataset (e.g., MATH, MedQA, GSM8K) along with response data from a population of AI models to first annotate each item with psychometric parameters (difficulty β, discrimination α, guessing factor c). During evaluation, it takes a target model's sequential responses and produces a latent ability estimate θ with associated uncertainty, an adaptively selected item subset (as few as 3–20% of the full benchmark), and a model ranking. The output is structured as a posterior distribution over ability rather than a single accuracy score, enabling statistical comparison across benchmarks via scale linking.

## Method

The proposed adaptive testing system operates in two phases. Phase 1 (Item Characteristics Annotation) fits a 3-parameter IRT model to population response data via MLE or Bayesian estimation, yielding per-item difficulty, discrimination, and guessing parameters that reveal annotation errors (negative discrimination), guessable items (high c), and potential contamination. Phase 2 (Interactive Dynamic Model Evaluation) sequentially selects the next item by maximizing Fisher Information I_j(θ̂) at the current ability estimate θ̂, updating θ̂ after each response via MLE on the IRT likelihood. This binary-search-like process converges to a precise ability estimate using a fraction of the full benchmark, while the item selection naturally avoids re-exposing models to items they may have seen (mitigating contamination). The paper validates this on MATH with 20 LLMs, showing that Fisher-information-guided selection with IRT achieves higher rank correlation with full-benchmark accuracy than random selection at every subset size, and that as little as 50 items from ~1,000 can reproduce 90% Kendall's rank correlation.

## Results

The paper's key quantitative findings come from simulation and prior empirical work: using Fisher Information–based item selection with IRT, only 50 items from a ~1,000-item benchmark achieve 90% Kendall's rank correlation with full-test rankings (Rodriguez et al., 2021). In the authors' own simulation on MATH with 20 LLMs, the adaptive method reaches the same ability-estimation accuracy as random sampling using at most 20% of items, and achieves the highest rank correlation with full-dataset rankings using only ~60% of items. Kipnis et al. (2024) further show that as little as 3% of items across six benchmarks (ARC, GSM8K, HellaSwag, MMLU, TruthfulQA, WinoGrande) can reconstruct original benchmark scores. Polo et al. (2024) select 100 curated items from MMLU to accurately estimate and reconstruct LLMs' original benchmark scores.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not introduce a new method to benchmark against specific competing baselines in a head-to-head table. Instead, it contrasts the proposed adaptive-testing paradigm (Fisher Information item selection + IRT ability estimation) against two implicit baselines: (1) traditional accuracy-based evaluation on the full benchmark, and (2) random item selection. In the MATH simulation (Appendix B), the adaptive Fisher+IRT method consistently outperforms random selection in rank correlation at every tested subset size (10%–80%), and even random selection combined with IRT ability estimation yields higher rank correlation than traditional accuracy (ACC) scoring. The adaptive method achieves the highest ranking consistency at ~60% of items, whereas random selection requires substantially more items to approach the same level. The paper also notes that IRT-based ability estimates are more stable across random subsets than raw accuracy, which is unstable and cannot guarantee that a subset's performance reflects the full dataset.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper unifies a scattered body of prior work that has independently applied psychometric techniques to AI evaluation—item response theory for performance estimation (Lalor et al., 2016; Rodriguez et al., 2021), sparse benchmark construction (Polo et al., 2024; Kipnis et al., 2024), and dataset quality auditing (Wang et al., 2024a)—under a single adaptive testing framework. It builds directly on the IRT formalism of Lord et al. (1968) and the Fisher-information item selection criterion of Lord (1980), both developed for human computerized adaptive testing, and on the broader CAT paradigm formalized by Chang (2015). Rather than introducing a new algorithm, the paper's contribution is conceptual: it argues that these individually applied techniques constitute a coherent paradigm shift from static, full-benchmark evaluation to dynamic, item-aware, ability-oriented assessment of AI systems.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a pressing practical bottleneck: full-benchmark evaluation of LLMs is prohibitively expensive (4,000+ GPU hours for HELM) and increasingly unreliable due to contamination, annotation errors, and low-quality items. If the adaptive testing paradigm scales, it could reduce evaluation costs by an order of magnitude while simultaneously improving reliability through item-level quality control and contamination detection. Key open questions remain: whether psychometric assumptions (e.g., unidimensional latent traits, uniformity across models) hold for increasingly heterogeneous and agentic AI systems, whether a distinct 'Machine Psychometrics' discipline is needed, and how to handle non-ability traits (bias, safety, robustness) within the same framework. The empirical evidence here is preliminary—simulation on a single benchmark (MATH) with 20 LLMs—so the paradigm's viability at scale and across diverse task types remains to be demonstrated.
