## Problem

Two distinct mechanisms—induction heads (which copy tokens following repeated patterns) and function vector (FV) heads (which encode latent task representations)—have each been proposed to explain in-context learning in LLMs, but it remains unclear which actually drives few-shot ICL performance. Previous studies reached conflicting conclusions, partly due to conflating different ICL metrics and failing to control for the correlation between the two head types.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252900/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis operates on 12 pre-trained decoder-only transformers (Pythia 70M–6.9B, GPT-2 117M–1.6B, Llama2 7B) and 8 intermediate training checkpoints from 7 Pythia models. Induction scores are computed on synthetic repeated-token sequences, while FV scores are measured via causal mediation analysis on 37 ICL tasks with 100 prompts each (10 demonstration pairs + 1 query). Ablation experiments use 19 held-out ICL tasks (100 prompts per task) for few-shot accuracy and 10,000 Pile examples for token-loss difference, with a 2% top-score threshold defining 'important' heads.

## Method

The paper systematically identifies and compares induction heads (ranked by attention weight on the token following a repeated token) and FV heads (ranked by causal contribution to recovering correct outputs on label-shuffled prompts when their activation is replaced with the mean task-conditioned activation) across all 12 models. The key methodological innovation is 'ablation with exclusion': when ablating n FV heads, the top-n heads by FV score that are *not* in the top 2% by induction score are selected (and vice versa), isolating each mechanism's unique causal contribution. Ablations use mean replacement (swapping a head's output with its dataset average) to avoid out-of-distribution artifacts. Training dynamics are tracked by measuring per-head induction and FV scores at 8 checkpoints, revealing that many FV heads initially exhibit high induction scores before transitioning to the FV mechanism—a unidirectional evolution never observed in reverse. The study also disentangles two ICL metrics (few-shot accuracy vs. token-loss difference) that prior work conflated, showing they respond differently to each head type.

## Results

Across 12 decoder-only transformer models (70M–7B parameters), ablating the top 2% FV heads causes substantially larger drops in few-shot ICL accuracy than ablating the top 2% induction heads, with the gap widening as model scale increases. When controlling for the correlation between the two head types via ablation with exclusion, ablating pure induction heads (low FV score) has minimal impact on ICL accuracy—comparable to random ablation in models exceeding 1B parameters—while ablating pure FV heads (low induction score) still significantly degrades performance. Induction heads emerge at approximately step 1,000 of training, while FV heads appear much later at around step 16,000, and 7 of 12 models show zero direct overlap between the top 2% induction and FV head sets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between the causal contributions of induction heads, FV heads, and random heads to few-shot ICL performance. Ablating FV heads consistently produces the largest degradation in ICL accuracy across all 12 models, outperforming (i.e., being more critical than) induction head ablation by a wide margin, especially in larger models (e.g., Pythia 6.9B, GPT-2 1.6B, Llama2 7B). Ablating induction heads without exclusion shows a moderate effect, but this is shown to be largely an artifact of the correlation between induction and FV scores: once only pure induction heads (those outside the top 2% FV) are ablated, the effect drops to random-ablation levels in models above 1B parameters. Conversely, ablating pure FV heads (outside the top 2% induction) still causes significant ICL degradation. On the token-loss difference metric (used in prior work like Olsson et al., 2022), the pattern reverses in models above 345M parameters, where induction head ablation affects token-loss difference more than FV head ablation—highlighting that the two ICL metrics capture fundamentally different phenomena.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly compares two competing mechanistic explanations for in-context learning that have each been supported by separate lines of evidence: induction heads (Elhage et al., 2021; Olsson et al., 2022) and function vector heads (Todd et al., 2024; Hendel et al., 2023). It builds on the induction score methodology from the TransformerLens framework and the causal mediation analysis framework from Todd et al. (2024), but departs from prior work by performing the first systematic head-to-head ablation comparison across 12 models while explicitly controlling for the correlation between the two mechanisms. The paper also distinguishes between two conflated ICL metrics—few-shot task accuracy and token-loss difference—that prior studies treated as interchangeable, showing they capture different phenomena.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that FV heads, not induction heads, are the primary causal drivers of few-shot ICL challenges a widely-held narrative in the interpretability community and reframes how we understand the developmental trajectory of ICL capabilities in transformers. The discovery that many FV heads evolve from induction heads during training suggests a staged learning process where simpler mechanisms bootstrap more complex ones, with implications for training efficiency and model design. Key limitations include the restriction to models under 7B parameters, the unmeasured hydra effect in ablations, and anomalously low FV scores in Llama2 that raise questions about architectural generality. Open questions include whether induction heads are truly necessary as a developmental precursor, what role residual induction heads serve in fully trained models, and whether additional mechanisms beyond these two provide a more complete account of ICL.
