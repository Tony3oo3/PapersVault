## Problem

Despite strong performance on visual question answering and object recognition, current AI models lack 'conceptualization'—the ability to recognize the same underlying concept when its visual representation changes (e.g., the same graph drawn in different layouts). No existing benchmark isolates this specific form of representation-invariant reasoning from broader out-of-distribution generalization challenges, leaving a critical gap in evaluating whether models truly understand abstract visual structures or merely memorize surface patterns.

## Input / Output

Inputs are rendered graph images (8–9 nodes, 700×700 px canvas, resized to 384×384 for model consumption) drawn via NetworkX in specific layouts: Kamada-Kawai, planar, or random. Isomorphism tasks present two graphs side-by-side in a single image; shortest-path tasks mark two target nodes with squares. Outputs are binary labels (isomorphic/not, Hamiltonian path/cycle present/absent) or four-class labels (shortest path length 1–4, largest chordless cycle size 3–6). Crucially, training and test sets use different layouts (e.g., Kamada-Kawai train → planar test), so correct answers require layout-invariant structural reasoning rather than pattern matching.

## Method

The Visual Graph Arena comprises six datasets (27K–155K training samples each) organized around three graph concepts— isomorphism, path, and cycle—each split into an easy and a hard subtask. Graphs are drawn from the known set of connected 8- and 9-node graphs, with layout deliberately varied between training (Kamada-Kawai or random) and testing (planar or mixed) to force conceptual transfer. Vision models (ViT, Swin-T, ConvNeXt, SigLIP, DINOv2) are fine-tuned via transfer learning from ImageNet pretraining for 10 epochs with Adam (lr=1e-4, batch 32, CrossEntropyLoss), while MLLMs (GPT-o1, GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet) are evaluated zero-shot on 100 samples per task. The paper further introduces two diagnostic anomalies—the Middle-Score Anomaly and the Easier-Worse Anomaly—to distinguish genuine conceptual understanding from pseudo-intelligent pattern matching, demonstrating that even GPT-o1's above-chance performance on shortest path (55%) and Hamiltonian cycle (66%) is explainable by exploiting trivial structural cues (e.g., leaf-node detection) rather than true graph-theoretic reasoning.

## Results

On the Visual Graph Arena benchmark, human participants achieve near-perfect accuracy (88.2–100%) across all six graph tasks, while all tested vision models (ViT, Swin-T, ConvNeXt) completely fail on both isomorphism tasks (near-random performance). Among non-failing tasks, ConvNeXt Base achieves the highest accuracy, reaching 82.4% on shortest path (Kamada-Kawai layout) and 74.9% on Hamiltonian cycle, still well below human performance. MLLMs perform even worse: GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet fail on all tasks, while GPT-o1 shows only limited above-random success on shortest path (55%) and Hamiltonian cycle (66%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks three pre-trained vision architectures (ViT Base, Swin-T Base, ConvNeXt Base) fine-tuned on VGA tasks. All three fail identically on both isomorphism tasks, confirming a shared architectural limitation in recognizing structural equivalence across layouts. On the remaining tasks, ConvNeXt consistently outperforms the transformer-based baselines: it leads by 15.9 points over ViT and 7.1 points over Swin-T on Hamiltonian path (72.9% vs. 57.0% vs. 65.8%), by 15.7 points over ViT on shortest path in the Kawai layout (82.4% vs. 66.7%), and by 7.1 points over ViT on Hamiltonian cycle (74.9% vs. 67.8%). The gap is most pronounced on shortest path, where ConvNeXt's 82.4% (Kawai) approaches but still falls short of the human 100%. The authors conclude that convolutional architectures capture local visual structure in graphs more effectively than self-attention mechanisms, though none approach human-level conceptualization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by reframing well-known graph-theoretic tasks (isomorphism, path finding, cycle detection) as purely visual reasoning problems where the critical variable is layout invariance. It explicitly departs from existing graph datasets (OGB, TU dataset) that are structured for GNN consumption as abstract data, and from prior visual reasoning benchmarks (CLEVR, FigureQA, Vision Graph) that do not isolate the specific challenge of recognizing identical structure across different visual presentations. The 'conceptualization' framing—a defined subset of OOD generalization focused on representational invariance—represents a new problem formulation that repurposes standard vision architectures (ViT, Swin, ConvNeXt) and MLLMs as test subjects rather than as the contribution itself.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a stark and well-evidenced gap: all tested vision models and MLLMs fail completely on graph isomorphism detection while humans achieve 91–95% accuracy, suggesting that current architectures lack genuine structural understanding of visual representations. The identification of the 'Easier-Worse Anomaly' in GPT-o1 (28% on path length 1 vs. 69% on length 2) raises a broader question about whether probabilistic training paradigms can achieve conceptualization at all, or whether alternative learning mechanisms (e.g., reinforcement learning) are needed. Limitations include the small human study (15 subjects, 24 questions), the restriction to 8–9 node graphs, and the MLLM evaluation on only 100 samples per task, which constrains the statistical strength of the negative results. Future work extending the benchmark to chemical structures, logic circuits, and larger graphs would strengthen the generalizability of these findings.
