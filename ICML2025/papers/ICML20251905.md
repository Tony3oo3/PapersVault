## Problem

Reinforcement learning for combinatorial optimization suffers from diminishing reward signals: as the policy improves, advantage values shrink toward zero, causing vanishing gradients and slow convergence. Additionally, the exponential combinatorial action spaces make traditional trajectory-level entropy regularization intractable, and local search heuristics used as post-processing incur extra inference cost. The paper addresses all three issues by replacing absolute reward magnitudes with relative preference comparisons.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251905/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a problem instance encoded as a graph (2D node coordinates for TSP/CVRP, or job-machine processing time matrices for FFSP) and produces a combinatorial solution as a sequence of actions (a tour, a set of vehicle routes, or a job schedule). It is architecture-agnostic, plugging into existing encoder-decoder neural solvers (POMO, Sym-NCO, Pointerformer, AM, MatNet, DIMES, ELG) by replacing their REINFORCE-based training loop. During fine-tuning, local search (2-Opt for TSP, swap* for CVRP) refines sampled solutions to generate additional preference pairs without adding inference-time cost.

## Method

Starting from an entropy-regularized RL objective, the paper derives the optimal policy in closed form and reparameterizes the latent reward as r̂(x,τ) = α log π(τ|x) + α log Z(x), where the intractable partition function Z(x) cancels out when computing reward differences between trajectories. This transforms policy optimization into a pairwise classification problem: for each pair of sampled solutions, the model maximizes the log-likelihood of the correct preference label (determined by the grounding reward) under a statistical preference model (Bradley-Terry, Thurstone, or Plackett-Luce). The resulting gradient uses a quantity-invariant advantage signal g(τ,τ′,x) − g(τ′,τ,x) that remains informative even when absolute reward differences are tiny. Local search is integrated into the fine-tuning phase rather than post-processing, treating LS-refined solutions as expert demonstrations in an imitation-learning-style objective, allowing the policy to internalize heuristic improvements without extra inference cost.

## Results

On TSP-100, PO consistently reduces the optimality gap across all neural solvers: POMO improves from 0.15% (RF) to 0.07% (PO) and 0.03% (PO+Finetune), Sym-NCO from 0.39% to 0.28%, and Pointerformer from 0.15% to 0.06%. On CVRP-100, POMO's gap drops from 1.76% to 1.19% with fine-tuning, and AM's gap improves from 7.69% to 6.82%. On FFSP-100, MatNet(PO+Aug.) achieves a 0.00% gap versus 0.56% for MatNet(RF+Aug.). PO also achieves 1.5×–2.5× faster convergence, reaching RF-level performance in only 40%–60% of training epochs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PO outperforms REINFORCE (RF) across all tested neural solvers on both TSP and CVRP. On TSP-100, POMO(PO) achieves a 0.07% gap versus 0.15% for POMO(RF), and with local-search fine-tuning reaches 0.03%, approaching the 0.00% of exact solvers like Concorde. On CVRP-100, the improvement is more pronounced: POMO(PO+Finetune) reduces the gap from 1.76% to 1.19%, and AM(PO) improves from 7.69% to 6.82%. On FFSP, MatNet(PO) consistently beats MatNet(RF) (e.g., 2.13% vs 2.58% gap on FFSP-100), and with data augmentation, MatNet(PO+Aug.) reaches 0.00% gap, surpassing all heuristic baselines including Particle Swarm Optimization (9.09%). For zero-shot generalization on TSPLib/CVRPLib, ELG(PO) marginally improves over ELG(RF) (3.00% vs 3.08% on TSPLib; 5.94% vs 6.03% on CVRPLib). On large-scale TSP (500–10000 nodes) using DIMES, PO-trained heatmaps reduce gaps by 1–2 percentage points across all decoding strategies (e.g., DIMES-S: 13.29% vs 15.47% on TSP-500). PO also converges 1.5×–2.5× faster than RF, reaching comparable performance in 40%–60% of training epochs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate lines of work: entropy-regularized RL for combinatorial optimization (Haarnoja et al., 2017; Ziebart et al., 2008) and preference-based optimization from the RLHF/DPO literature (Rafailov et al., 2024). The key conceptual move is reparameterizing the COP reward function in terms of the policy itself, then using statistical comparison models (Bradley-Terry, Thurstone, Plackett-Luce) to convert quantitative rewards into qualitative pairwise preferences — a transformation that is affine-invariant and thus immune to the diminishing-advantage problem that plagues REINFORCE-based solvers. Unlike traditional preference-based RL, which requires learning a latent reward function from preference labels, PO directly optimizes the policy against preferences derived from the known grounding reward, avoiding the intermediate reward-learning step. The paper also departs from the standard practice of using local search as a post-processing step by integrating it into the fine-tuning phase, where the preference-based objective naturally absorbs off-policy LS-refined solutions in an imitation-learning-like manner.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PO offers a general, architecture-agnostic drop-in replacement for REINFORCE in neural combinatorial optimization, validated across six solver architectures (AM, POMO, Sym-NCO, Pointerformer, DIMES, MatNet) and three problem families (TSP, CVRP, FFSP), making it immediately useful to the RL4CO community. The 1.5–2.5× convergence speedup and consistent gap reductions are practically significant for training budgets, and the local-search fine-tuning integration eliminates a common inference-time overhead. Open questions remain: the optimal preference model (BT vs. Exponential vs. Thurstone) appears to depend on problem scale and difficulty in ways not yet theoretically characterized, and the authors acknowledge that stability of the reparameterized reward across diverse COPs warrants further investigation. Extending PO to settings where reward signals are hard to design but preferences are naturally available (e.g., multi-objective optimization) is identified as a promising direction.
