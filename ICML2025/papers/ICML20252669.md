## Problem

In overparameterized logistic regression, gradient descent diverges in norm while converging in direction to the maximum ℓ₂-margin solution, making it unclear whether the asymptotic predictor is well-calibrated or statistically consistent. While early stopping is known to provide implicit regularization in linear regression, its statistical benefits for logistic regression—where the loss is non-quadratic and the GD path diverges—had not been formally characterized. This paper asks whether early stopping rescues calibration and sample efficiency in the overparameterized regime where asymptotic GD fails.

## Input / Output

The analysis takes n i.i.d. samples (x_i, y_i) where x_i follows an anisotropic Gaussian N(0, Σ) with tr(Σ) < ∞ and y_i is drawn from a logistic conditional model with true parameter w* satisfying ‖w*‖_Σ < ∞. The method consumes a fixed step size η and an oracle-chosen stopping time t (depending on the true parameter and sample size), producing the GD iterate w_t as the parameter vector. The output induces a conditional probability p(w_t; x) = 1/(1+exp(−xᵀw_t)) and a binary classification decision, with the key regime being rank(Σ) ≥ n (overparameterized) and ‖w*‖_Σ ≳ 1 (constant noise).

## Method

The core technical tool is an implicit bias lemma (Lemma 3.3) showing that for any comparator u, early-stopped GD at time t satisfies ‖w_t − u‖²/(2ηt) + L̂(w_t) ≤ L̂(u) + ‖u‖²/(2ηt), meaning early stopping simultaneously achieves small empirical risk and controlled norm. Building on this, the paper derives two risk upper bounds: a bias-dominating bound (Theorem 3.1) valid under the weakest condition ‖w*‖_Σ < ∞ using Rademacher complexity, and a faster variance-dominating bound (Theorem 3.2) requiring ‖w*‖ < ∞ via local Rademacher complexity. For lower bounds, the paper proves that any estimator diverging in norm (including asymptotic GD) must have unbounded logistic risk and constant calibration error (Theorem 4.1), and that interpolating estimators require exponentially many samples to achieve small zero-one error while early-stopped GD needs only polynomially many (Theorem 4.2). Finally, the paper establishes that the GD path and ℓ₂-regularization path differ in angle by at most π/4 and in norm by a factor between 0.585 and 3.415 globally (Theorem 5.1), with their ℓ₂-distance tending to zero under a support-vector coverage condition (Theorem 5.2) but potentially diverging to infinity without it (Theorem 5.3).

## Results

The paper establishes that early-stopped GD achieves vanishing excess logistic risk (Theorem 3.1: $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{\max\{1,\text{tr}(\Sigma)\|\mathbf{w}_{0:k}^*\|^2\}/n) + \|\mathbf{w}_{k:\infty}^*\|_\Sigma^2$) and, under the stronger condition $\|\mathbf{w}^*\|<\infty$, a faster rate (Theorem 3.2: $\|\mathbf{w}^*\|(k/n + \sqrt{\sum_{i>k}\lambda_i/n} + \text{tr}(\Sigma)^{1/2}\ln(n\|\mathbf{w}^*\|\text{tr}(\Sigma)/\delta)/n)$). In contrast, asymptotic GD (the interpolating limit) suffers unbounded logistic risk and a calibration error bounded below by $\exp(-C\|\mathbf{w}^*\|_\Sigma)$ (Theorem 4.1). For zero-one error, early-stopped GD needs only polynomially many samples while any interpolating estimator requires at least exponentially many (Theorem 4.2: excess error $\gtrsim 1/\sqrt{\ln(n)\ln(1/\delta)}$). The GD path and $\ell_2$-regularization path differ in angle by at most $\pi/4$ and in norm by a factor between 0.585 and 3.415 (Theorem 5.1), with their $\ell_2$-distance tending to zero under the support-vector coverage condition (Theorem 5.2).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between early-stopped GD and asymptotic GD (the maximum $\ell_2$-margin interpolator). Early-stopped GD is calibrated and consistent for every well-specified logistic regression problem, while asymptotic GD is statistically inconsistent (unbounded logistic risk) and poorly calibrated (constant calibration error) in the overparameterized regime. For zero-one error, early-stopped GD achieves small excess error with polynomially many samples, whereas any interpolating estimator—including asymptotic GD—requires exponentially many samples under the conditions of Theorem 4.2. The paper also compares the GD path to the explicit $\ell_2$-regularization path: globally, the two paths are within a $\pi/4$ angle and a norm ratio of [0.585, 3.415] (Theorem 5.1), and asymptotically their $\ell_2$-distance vanishes under Assumption 2 (Theorem 5.2) but can diverge to infinity without it (Theorem 5.3). No empirical baselines are compared; all results are theoretical bounds.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the implicit bias analysis of GD (Soudry et al., 2018; Ji & Telgarsky, 2018) from characterizing the asymptotic limit (max-margin direction) to quantifying the statistical cost of reaching that limit in the overparameterized noisy regime. It builds on the benign overfitting literature for linear regression (Bartlett et al., 2020; Tsigler & Bartlett, 2023) and the early-stopping-as-regularization line (Bühlmann & Yu, 2003; Zou et al., 2023), but departs from the logistic regression benign overfitting results (Montanari et al., 2019; Chatterji & Long, 2021) by showing those are special cases where the max-margin solution happens to work, while in general the asymptotic GD is statistically inconsistent. The connection to L2-regularization extends Suggala et al. (2018) from strongly convex to general convex problems and provides the first asymptotic absolute (not just relative) comparison for logistic regression.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that early stopping is not merely a practical heuristic but a fundamental statistical necessity in overparameterized classification: without it, GD's implicit bias toward the max-margin solution causes unbounded logistic risk and persistent calibration error, while with it, GD achieves consistency and calibration for every well-specified problem. The polynomial-versus-exponential sample complexity separation between early-stopped GD and interpolating estimators is a strong negative result that clarifies why overfitting is genuinely dangerous in the classification (as opposed to regression) setting. Open questions include the sharp bias-variance tradeoff for early-stopped GD in logistic regression (which resists the closed-form tools available for least squares), whether the L2-distance between GD and regularization paths is always zero or infinity (a conjectured phase transition), and whether early stopping can strictly outperform explicit L2-regularization in some regimes.
