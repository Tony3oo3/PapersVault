## Problem

Existing hallucination-mitigation work for MLLMs targets mismatches between model outputs and inputs, but overlooks a more fundamental source: intrinsic conflicts between the visual and textual inputs themselves (e.g., a question presupposing an object absent from the image). This paper formally defines 'modality conflict' at the level of objects, attributes, and relationships, and shows that prevalent MLLMs—including GPT-4o—exhibit hallucination rates above 40% when faced with such conflicted inputs.

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image (sourced from Visual Genome) paired with a text question that deliberately conflicts with the image content in one of three ways: object, attribute, or relationship mismatch. The expected output is a free-form text answer that identifies the conflict (e.g., 'The image does not contain a ball') rather than answering the question as stated. The MMMC dataset provides 20K such image-question-answer triples (18K train / 2K test), with answers generated by an LLM conditioned on the known conflict rather than by a vision-language model, to avoid propagating hallucinations into ground truth.

## Method

Three progressively stronger methods are proposed to make MLLMs recognize and respond correctly to modality conflict. (1) Prompt engineering prepends a simple instruction ('Please check if the image contains mentioned information…') to the question, requiring no additional data or compute. (2) Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) uses LoRA with a standard language-modeling loss on the MMMC training set to align the model's response style with conflict-aware answers. (3) Reinforcement learning (RL) formulates token generation as an MDP and optimizes with REINFORCE++, using a binary reward (+1/−1) assigned by a frozen Llama-3.3-70B judge that checks semantic consistency between the generated answer and the ground-truth. RL outperforms SFT because it samples diverse responses from the model itself, encouraging broader conflict-recognition behavior, while SFT tends to overfit to the narrow answer style in the training data. All fine-tuned variants use LoRA; RL is trained on only 1,000 samples with a KL coefficient of 0.01 to prevent model collapse.

## Results

On the MMMC dataset, all base MLLMs exhibit Hallu-Rate above 40% (ranging from 40.6% for Qwen2-VL-Instruct-2B to 93.3% for LLaVA-v1.5-7B), confirming widespread vulnerability to modality conflict. The proposed RL method achieves the largest Hallu-Rate reductions, e.g., dropping LLaVA-v1.5-7B from 93.3% to 33.9% (Llama judge) and Qwen2-VL-Instruct-2B from 46.6% to 18.0%. SFT provides stable improvements with low variance (e.g., Qwen2-VL-Instruct-7B: 52.4% → 28.6% Hallu-Rate, std 0.34), while PE is model-dependent and can even increase hallucination for smaller models (Qwen2-VL-Instruct-2B: 46.6% → 62.1%). LLM-Judge scores improve by 0.4–0.9 across methods, with RL reaching up to 2.97 (GPT judge) for Qwen2-VL-Instruct-2B.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates four open-source MLLMs (InstructBLIP-7B, LLaVA-v1.5-7B, LLaVA-NeXT-7B, Qwen2-VL-Instruct-7B/2B) and GPT-4o as baselines under three intervention methods (PE, SFT, RL). RL consistently achieves the lowest Hallu-Rate across all models, reducing it by 20–60 percentage points relative to base (e.g., LLaVA-v1.5-7B: 93.3%→33.9%; Qwen2-VL-Instruct-2B: 46.6%→18.0%), though with higher variance than SFT. SFT offers the most stable gains (std < 1.0 on Hallu-Rate) and is the best method for InstructBLIP, where RL causes model collapse (response length explosion and repeated text). PE is the weakest and most inconsistent: it helps LLaVA-NeXT-7B (69.7%→50.5%) and Qwen2-VL-Instruct-7B (52.4%→40.1%) but actively harms Qwen2-VL-Instruct-2B (46.6%→62.1%) and GPT-4o (59.4%→60.1%). GPT-4o, despite being the strongest base model, still hallucinates on 57–59% of MMMC samples and shows minimal improvement from PE, indicating that even frontier models lack robust modality-conflict recognition.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by identifying modality conflict (inconsistency between the visual and textual inputs themselves) as a distinct source of MLLM hallucination, separate from the more commonly studied output-input mismatch. It draws conceptually on the knowledge conflict literature in LLMs (Longpre et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2022) and the substitution-based data construction framework from that line of work, but repurposes these ideas for the multimodal setting where the conflict is between two input modalities rather than between context and parametric knowledge. The three mitigation methods (prompt engineering, SFT, RL) are standard techniques from the alignment and robustness literature, applied here to a newly formalized problem rather than being novel in themselves.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The MMMC benchmark fills a gap in MLLM evaluation by isolating input-input conflict, a scenario that existing hallucination benchmarks (HallusionBench, MME) do not specifically target. The finding that even GPT-4o hallucinates ~57% of the time under modality conflict suggests that current alignment strategies are insufficient for handling intrinsically contradictory inputs, pointing to a fundamental limitation in how MLLMs process conflicting evidence. Open questions include whether the capability generalizes beyond the 20K-sample dataset, whether larger or differently-architected models would handle relationship conflicts (the hardest type) better, and how to mitigate the alignment tax observed in weaker models like InstructBLIP without sacrificing the robustness gains.
