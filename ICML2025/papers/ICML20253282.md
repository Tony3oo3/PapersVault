## Problem

Humans and machines conceptualize the world differently, creating a fundamental communication gap in AI interpretability: existing human vocabulary cannot concisely reference machine-specific concepts (M−H) or precisely specify human concepts to machines (H−M). The paper argues that this gap will not close through scaling alone and that progress in interpretability requires developing neologisms—new words that encode either human or machine concepts at a useful level of abstraction.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20253282/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a natural-language text prompt in which a single new neologism token (e.g., ensure_w^h, diverse_w^h, good_w^m) is embedded at a specific position, and produces a standard text response from a frozen LLM (Gemma 2B). The neologism token is a single vocabulary entry with its own d-dimensional embedding; when the token is absent from the prompt, the model's output is guaranteed identical to the unmodified base model. During generation, the model is prevented from emitting the neologism token itself (its logit is set to −∞), so the token functions purely as a conditioning signal within the prompt.

## Method

Neologism embedding learning adds one new word w to the tokenizer vocabulary of a frozen language model and trains only its d-dimensional embedding E_w using a preference-based objective (APO-up, a variant of DPO that adds a saturating term to prevent text degeneration). Preference data D = {(x_i, y_i^c, y_i^r)} is constructed so that each prompt x_i contains the new word w, with chosen and rejected responses encoding the target concept—e.g., length-constrained vs. unconstrained responses for a length neologism, or model-self-scored high vs. low quality responses for a machine-preference neologism. Critically, the approach differs from soft prompting in that the new word is a discrete, user-selectable token that composes with natural language, rather than a fixed prepended embedding; the user decides when and in what syntactic context to invoke it. Only E_w is optimized (learning rate 0.02 with Adafactor, batch size 1), leaving all other model parameters untouched, which guarantees zero behavioral change when the neologism is not used.

## Results

In the length control experiment, base Gemma 2B never generates responses meeting the 400–600 or 600–1000 word constraints when prompted with standard English instructions, whereas the length neologism (ensure_w^h) consistently produces responses within the target range. In the diversity experiment (number guessing 1–9), baseline Gemma is heavily biased toward guessing 5 or 7, yielding only ~20% probability of finding the correct number after 100 guesses; the diversity neologism (diverse_w^h) substantially increases response variety and the probability of success. For the model-preference neologism (good_w^m), Gemma's self-evaluation scores 'extremely good' responses at 3.8/5 on average versus 3.2/5 for 'extremely not good' responses across 50 samples each.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against other fine-tuning or prompting methods (e.g., LoRA, full fine-tuning, standard soft prompting); the sole baseline is the unmodified base Gemma 2B model. The neologism embedding learning approach is evaluated as a single-token intervention: only the embedding of one new vocabulary token is trained while all other model parameters remain frozen. In the length experiment, the baseline (standard English prompting like 'Ensure the response is between 600–1000 words') completely fails to elicit compliant-length responses, while the neologism token reliably produces them. In the diversity experiment, the baseline model's strong positional bias (toward 5 and 7) caps success at ~20% even after 100 guesses, whereas the diversity neologism breaks this bias and yields near-uniform sampling. The model-preference experiment has no direct baseline comparison but demonstrates that the learned token activates a distinct quality axis (3.8 vs 3.2 self-scores) that is not accessible through standard prompting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the mechanism of soft prompting (Lester et al., 2021) — learning a small set of embeddings while keeping the model frozen — and couples it with preference-based optimization (DPO/APO) to introduce a new conceptual framing: interpretability as a human-machine communication problem solvable through neologisms. It positions itself between mechanistic interpretability (circuits, neurons) and behavioral benchmarking, arguing that concept-level neologisms occupy a useful middle abstraction. The chess concept-transfer work of Schut et al. (2025) on AlphaZero provides the closest empirical precedent for teaching machine-specific concepts to humans, while the broader interpretability-as-communication framing traces to Kim (2022).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The neologism framing offers a principled way to think about the right level of abstraction for interpretability and provides a concrete, lightweight method (training a single word embedding) that preserves the base model when the neologism is unused. However, the proof-of-concept is limited to a single 2B model (Gemma), a single dataset (LIMA), and toy tasks (length control, number guessing), leaving open whether the approach scales to larger models, composes across multiple neologisms, or yields genuinely faithful representations of machine concepts. The paper itself acknowledges it is a 'first step' and a 'high-level research direction,' so the main contribution is conceptual rather than empirical.
