## Problem

Real-world time series are inherently non-stationary, with frequency content that fluctuates over time, yet most existing forecasting models focus on temporal correlations and overlook frequency-domain characteristics. The time-frequency uncertainty principle prevents precise frequency observation at a single moment, and selecting a single optimal patch size for short-time Fourier analysis is suboptimal—larger patches capture global features while smaller patches reveal local details. This paper addresses the gap of how to simultaneously capture multi-scale frequency variation patterns without committing to one patch size.

## Input / Output

The model takes a multivariate historical window X ∈ R^{D×T} (e.g., T=96 look-back) and produces a future forecast X̂ ∈ R^{D×τ} for horizons τ ∈ {96, 192, 336, 720}. Each variable is processed independently under a channel-independence strategy, and the input is first standardized via Revin (mean/variance normalization). A key structural constraint is that all patch sizes in the Patch Size List must be divisors of T, and the list is dataset-specific (e.g., [96,48,32,24,16,12] for ETTh1).

## Method

TimeStacker sequentially stacks patches from largest to smallest across L StackerBlocks, where each block processes patches of a fixed size p_l. Within each block, a Smooth Layer (1D convolution with kernel size p_l) suppresses outliers, followed by a FreqAttention module that computes inter-patch similarity in the frequency domain via FFT and learnable Hadamard-product filters (W_q, W_k), then aggregates patches in the time domain using the resulting correlation matrix. This design grounds the architecture in the time-frequency uncertainty principle (Δt·Δf ≥ 1/4π): larger patches provide high frequency resolution for global periodicity, while smaller patches provide high time resolution for local dynamics. The FreqAttention module achieves O(L log₂ p) complexity versus O(Lp) for standard self-attention, and the Hadamard product mitigates spectral leakage by acting as learnable band-pass filters on the Fourier coefficients.

## Results

On the main benchmark (input length 96, averaged over prediction horizons {96, 192, 336, 720}), TimeStacker achieves the best MSE on 6 of 8 datasets: ETTh1 (0.433), ETTh2 (0.368), ETTm1 (0.381), ETTm2 (0.274), Weather (0.243), and Exchange (0.336). It also attains the best MAE on the same six datasets, with notable margins such as 7.07% MAE reduction over iTransformer on ETTm1 and 6.67% MSE reduction on Exchange. TimeStacker underperforms SOFTS on Traffic (MSE 0.508 vs. 0.409) and Electricity (MSE 0.194 vs. 0.174), which the authors attribute to the high number of variables (862 and 321, respectively). The model also requires only 24.9 MB GPU memory and 115 ms/iteration, substantially less than Transformer-based baselines like Crossformer (5214 MB, 238 ms) and TimeMixer (518 MB, 180 ms).

## Baselines & Comparisons

TimeStacker outperforms all nine main baselines (SOFTS, SparseTSF, iTransformer, TimeMixer, SAMformer, PatchTST, Crossformer, DLinear, RLinear) on 6 of 8 datasets in both MSE and MAE. The largest margins are against Crossformer (e.g., MSE 0.368 vs. 0.942 on ETTh2) and DLinear (MSE 0.274 vs. 0.350 on ETTm2). Against the closest competitors, TimeStacker beats iTransformer by 7.07% MAE on ETTm1 and 6.67% MSE on Exchange, and beats SOFTS by 2.4% MSE on ETTh1. However, TimeStacker loses to SOFTS on Traffic (MSE 0.508 vs. 0.409) and Electricity (MSE 0.194 vs. 0.174), which the authors link to the high variable counts (862 and 321) challenging the channel-independent design. In additional comparisons (Table 9), TimeStacker beats N-HiTS, FEDformer, and FiLM on ETTm2 and Weather, but N-HiTS edges it out on Electricity (MSE 0.186 vs. 0.194) and Traffic (MSE 0.452 vs. 0.508). In terms of efficiency, TimeStacker uses 24.9 MB memory and 115 ms/iteration, far below Crossformer (5214 MB, 238 ms) and TimeMixer (518 MB, 180 ms), while only slightly above the linear baselines DLinear (13.7 MB, 45 ms) and SparseTSF (13.7 MB, 108 ms).

## Relation to Prior Work

TimeStacker extends the patching paradigm introduced by PatchTST by replacing a single fixed patch size with a sequential stacking of patches from large to small, motivated by the time-frequency uncertainty principle. Its FreqAttention module departs from standard self-attention by computing query-key similarity in the frequency domain via Hadamard products with learnable spectral filters, while performing value aggregation in the time domain—a design that echoes the frequency-domain emphasis of FEDformer and FiLM but applies it to inter-patch attention rather than global decomposition. The channel-independence strategy and Revin normalization are inherited directly from prior work, positioning TimeStacker as a methodological contribution within the established patch-based forecasting lineage rather than a new architectural family.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The multilevel stacking mechanism offers a principled, uncertainty-principle-grounded solution to the long-standing patch-size selection problem, and the O(L log p) attention complexity makes frequency-aware modeling practical at scale. However, the authors acknowledge a clear limitation: performance degrades as the number of variables increases (notably on Traffic with 862 channels and Electricity with 321), suggesting the channel-independent design struggles to exploit inter-variable correlations that competitors like SOFTS and iTransformer capture. An open question is whether the stacking mechanism can be extended to multivariate settings without sacrificing its efficiency advantage, and whether the frequency-domain attention generalizes beyond the univariate-per-channel formulation.
