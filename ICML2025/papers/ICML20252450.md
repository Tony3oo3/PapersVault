## Problem

GaussianImage achieves competitive 2D image reconstruction and compression using 2D Gaussian splatting, but its representation does not support user-driven modification of scene objects—a key capability of 3D Gaussian Splatting. The paper addresses this gap by asking whether 2D images can be encoded in a way that preserves high reconstruction quality while enabling intuitive, physically grounded editing in 3D space, mimicking how a human perceives a photograph as a flat object in a room.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252450/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single 2D RGB image (Kodak at 768×512, DIV2K at 408×1020 to 1020×1020) and its horizontally flipped mirror as training targets. The image is placed on the XZ plane at a fixed camera distance (a hyperparameter controlling perceived scale). The output is a set of flat 3D Gaussian components, each parameterized as a triangle via GaMeS, forming a 'triangle soup' that can be rendered back to 2D, manipulated vertex-by-vertex in 3D space, or fed into physics engines (Taichi_elements for 2D MPM, Blender for 3D rigid-body simulation).

## Method

MiraGe represents a 2D image as a collection of flat 3D Gaussians (one scale axis forced to ε≈0) initialized on the XZ plane, with each Gaussian mapped to a triangle via GaMeS parametrization, enabling direct geometric manipulation. Three Gaussian control modes are introduced: Amorphous (free 3D movement during training), 2D (constrained to the XZ plane with rotation alignment), and Graphite (XZ-plane constrained with a trainable Y-offset to resolve depth-ordering artifacts). A key architectural choice is the mirror camera setup—two opposing cameras along the Y axis simultaneously reconstruct the original image and its horizontal flip, which confines Gaussians to a bounded spatial region and substantially improves reconstruction fidelity (e.g., 59.52 PSNR on Kodak vs. 51.56 without the mirror). Training minimizes a weighted L1 + D-SSIM loss over both views, using 3DGS-style adaptive densification and pruning. The resulting triangle soup supports real-time 3D manipulation, 2.5D parallax effects via camera movement, and physics-based deformation through MPM or rigid-body simulation.

## Results

MiraGe achieves state-of-the-art reconstruction quality on both Kodak and DIV2K datasets. With 100K initial Gaussians and 30K iterations, MiraGe reaches 59.52 PSNR / 0.9999 MS-SSIM on Kodak and 54.54 PSNR / 0.9998 MS-SSIM on DIV2K, substantially outperforming the closest baseline (GaussianImage-100k at 38.93/41.48 PSNR). Notably, even with only 5K iterations, MiraGe-100k achieves 51.04 PSNR on Kodak in just 59 seconds of training, surpassing GaussianImage-70k (44.12 PSNR, 116 s) by ~7 dB while training 2× faster.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MiraGe decisively outperforms all INR-based baselines (WIRE, SIREN, I-NGP, NeuRBF) by 5–24 dB in PSNR on both datasets, while also training orders of magnitude faster (e.g., 560 s vs. 25,684 s for WIRE on DIV2K). Against the closest Gaussian-based competitor, GaussianImage, MiraGe-100k at 5K iterations already exceeds GaussianImage-100k by ~10 dB on Kodak (51.04 vs. 38.93 PSNR) and ~5 dB on DIV2K (46.23 vs. 41.48 PSNR), with shorter training time. Compared to 3DGS, MiraGe achieves higher PSNR (59.52 vs. 43.69 on Kodak) while also enabling 2D image editing that 3DGS does not support. In qualitative editing comparisons, MiraGe surpasses DragGAN by preserving local details (e.g., a facial dimple) during targeted manipulations, whereas DragGAN's generative nature causes unintended global changes. Against PhysGen for physics-based animation, MiraGe produces more physically plausible results (e.g., correct bounce direction, no object overlap), though the comparison is qualitative rather than metric-based.

## Relation to Prior Work

MiraGe extends the 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) framework from Kerbl et al. into the 2D image domain by constraining Gaussians to be flat (one scale parameter set to zero) and positioning them in 3D space, building directly on the GaMeS mesh-based parametrization from the same group. It departs from GaussianImage, which uses 2D Gaussians purely for compression and offers no editing capability, by introducing a mirror-camera training setup and three distinct Gaussian control modes (Amorphous, 2D, Graphite) that enable spatial manipulation. The conceptual departure is treating a 2D image as a flat physical object in 3D space—motivated by human perception of photographs—rather than as a pixel grid, which simultaneously improves reconstruction fidelity and unlocks 3D-aware editing and physics-based animation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that flat 3D Gaussians with a mirror-camera augmentation substantially outperform both 2D Gaussian (GaussianImage) and classical INR baselines in reconstruction quality (up to ~15 dB PSNR gain on Kodak), while uniquely enabling interactive editing, 2.5D camera effects, and physics-engine-driven animation of 2D images—capabilities absent from prior 2D image representation methods. Key limitations include the non-generative nature of the model (displacing foreground elements reveals gaps), the need for more parameters than GaussianImage for comparable animation quality, and the absence of physically accurate lighting. Open questions include whether the approach scales to multi-image scenes, whether style-transfer and relighting techniques from the 3DGS literature can be integrated, and whether the mirror-camera trick generalizes beyond single-image reconstruction.
