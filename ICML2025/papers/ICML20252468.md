## Problem

The Kernel Max-Sliced (KMS) Wasserstein distance, which finds an optimal nonlinear RKHS projector to maximize the 1D Wasserstein distance between two distributions, was introduced without sharp statistical or computational guarantees. Prior statistical results required strong regularity assumptions (Poincaré inequality, log-concavity) that are difficult to verify, and the computational problem lacked any NP-hardness characterization or provable guarantees for convex relaxations. This paper fills both gaps with dimension-free finite-sample bounds under a mild bounded-kernel assumption and a full computational analysis including NP-hardness, an efficient SDR algorithm, and rank/relaxation-gap guarantees.

## Input / Output

The method takes two empirical distributions supported on n points each in R^d (e.g., 100-dim Gaussian samples, MNIST/CIFAR-10 images, or 80-attribute sensor sequences) and a positive-definite kernel (typically Gaussian with median bandwidth). Via the representer theorem, the infinite-dimensional RKHS optimization is reduced to a finite-dimensional max-min problem over a 2n-dimensional vector ω and a transport plan π ∈ Γ_n. The output is the KMS p-Wasserstein distance value, the optimal nonlinear projector f*(z) = Σ a_{x,i}K(z,x_i) − Σ a_{y,i}K(z,y_i), and a binary test decision (reject/fail to reject H₀: μ=ν) using the critical value Δ(n,α) = 4A(C+4√(log(2/α)))^{1/p}·n^{-1/(2p)}.

## Method

The paper establishes three pillars. Statistically, it proves a dimension-free finite-sample bound KMS_p(μ̂_n,ν̂_n) ≤ KMS_p(μ,ν) + O(n^{-1/(2p)}) under only the assumption √K(x,x) ≤ A, leveraging the reformulation KMS_p(μ,ν) = MS_p(Φ_#μ, Φ_#ν) on Hilbert space and a functional Hoeffding inequality. Computationally, it proves NP-hardness of the exact KMS 2-Wasserstein problem (via reduction from Fair PCA/Partition), then proposes a semidefinite relaxation that drops the rank-1 constraint on S = ωωᵀ, solved by an inexact mirror ascent algorithm using von Neumann entropy Bregman divergence and biased supgradient oracles (Katyusha-momentum stochastic OT solver), achieving Õ(n²δ⁻²·max(n,δ⁻¹)) complexity. Finally, it proves the SDR admits an optimal solution of rank at most k = 1+⌊√(2n+9/4)−3/2⌋ (via Birkhoff-von Neumann and convex geometry), provides a rank-reduction algorithm (Hungarian assignment + greedy null-space descent) to extract such low-rank solutions, and bounds the relaxation gap as εn⁻⁴·Optval(SDR) ≤ Optval(KMS) ≤ Optval(SDR).

## Results

On the MSRC-12 human activity detection task (false alarm rate 0.05), KMS achieves the lowest mean detection delay of 11.4 (std 5.56), outperforming the next-best GSW (12.9) and substantially beating MMD (50.6) and ME (65.4). In generative modeling, KMS attains the best FID score of 105.98, compared to 113.08 (MMD), 113.92 (SW), 114.36 (Sinkhorn), 115.21 (MS), and 128.07 (GSW). In high-dimensional two-sample testing (type-I error 0.05), KMS achieves the highest testing power on 3 of 4 scenarios (Gaussian mixture shift, MNIST, CIFAR-10 abundance change), while MS is best on the Gaussian covariance shift scenario. The SDR-Efficient solver matches or exceeds BCD in solution quality while offering lower theoretical complexity (O~(n²δ⁻²·max(n,δ⁻¹)) vs O~(n³δ⁻³)).

## Baselines & Comparisons

KMS Wasserstein outperforms all six statistical-divergence baselines (Sinkhorn, MMD, SW, GSW, MS, ME) on the human activity detection task, achieving a mean detection delay of 11.4 versus 12.9 (GSW, closest competitor), 16.5 (Sinkhorn), 17.2 (SW), 17.8 (MS), 50.6 (MMD), and 65.4 (ME). In generative modeling, KMS achieves the lowest FID (105.98), beating MMD (113.08), SW (113.92), Sinkhorn (114.36), MS (115.21), and GSW (128.07). In high-dimensional two-sample testing, KMS wins on 3 of 4 scenarios (Gaussian mixture shift, MNIST, CIFAR-10) but loses to MS on the Gaussian covariance shift scenario, where linear projections are provably optimal for separating high-dimensional Gaussians. Against the BCD algorithm from Wang et al. [75], the proposed SDR-Efficient solver produces solutions with larger means and smaller variations in estimated KMS distance, while BCD is slightly faster for large-n instances; SDR-IPM is significantly slower than both for small n. Overall, KMS consistently matches or exceeds every baseline across all three application domains, with the sole exception of MS on the specific Gaussian covariance shift testing task.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the lineage from sliced Wasserstein (random linear projections) through max-sliced Wasserstein (optimal linear projection) to the kernel max-sliced (KMS) Wasserstein distance introduced by Wang et al. [75], which replaces the linear projector with an optimal nonlinear map in an RKHS. Statistically, it builds on Boedihardjo [9]'s sharp dimension-free bounds for MS Wasserstein on Hilbert space, adapting them to the KMS setting via the canonical feature map reformulation. Computationally, it departs from the gradient-based BCD algorithm of [75] (which lacks convergence guarantees) and the interior-point SDR of Xie and Xie [80] (which has O(n^{6.5}) complexity) by proposing a first-order inexact mirror ascent method with a novel rank bound derived from Birkhoff-von Neumann and convex geometry arguments borrowed from [43, 44, 58].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper closes a significant theoretical gap by providing the first dimension-free, worst-case-optimal finite-sample guarantees for KMS Wasserstein (rate n^{-1/(2p)}) under only a bounded-kernel assumption, making the distance practically usable for high-dimensional two-sample testing without unverifiable regularity conditions like Poincaré inequalities. The NP-hardness result combined with the SDR rank bound (O(√n) vs. trivial O(n)) and the efficient first-order solver gives a complete computational picture that was previously missing. Open questions include whether the very conservative relaxation gap (ε·n^{-4}) can be tightened, whether the rank-reduction step is necessary in practice, and how the method scales to very large n where the O(n^5) rank-reduction complexity becomes prohibitive.
