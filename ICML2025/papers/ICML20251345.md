## Problem

Diffusion models require hundreds of sampling steps for high-quality generation, and while fast samplers reduce steps via jump sampling, this inevitably causes quality degradation due to information loss over unvisited steps. The paper asks whether it is possible to reformulate the iterative denoising process to achieve lossless acceleration across a wide range of sampling step budgets (hundreds-step, ten-step, and few-step) for any pre-trained diffusion model regardless of its sampler or architecture.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251345/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes Gaussian noise (and optionally a text prompt or class label for conditional generation) as input and produces a generated image at resolutions ranging from 32×32 (CIFAR-10) to 1024×1024 (LCM-SDXL). A key structural detail is that the Dot model operates at a resolution reduced by 4^m times (typically 16×) relative to the Dash model, which is what makes it N times faster (N≈5–10) per step. The total latency is measured in LSDs (Latency per Step of the baseline DM), and Morse achieves the same FID/CLIP score as the baseline at n LSDs while completing the generation in fewer than n LSDs.

## Method

Morse reformulates the generation process as a time-interleaved interaction between two models: Dash (the frozen pre-trained DM operating in a jump-sampling regime) and Dot (a lightweight model N times faster that generates residual feedback to compensate for information loss between adjacent jump points). The Dot model is constructed by adding m trainable lightweight down-sampling and up-sampling blocks to the pre-trained Dash model, with LoRA applied to the shared layers for fast adaptation; only the new blocks and LoRA modules are trained while shared weights remain fixed. During inference, at each step the noise estimate is either computed directly by Dash (at jump points) or by Dot as z_{t_s} + η(x_{t_s}, x_{t_i}, z_{t_s}, t_s, t_i), where η is conditioned on the trajectory observations (input sample, output sample, time steps, and noise estimate) at the most recent Dash point. This adaptive residual feedback strategy, combined with the weight-sharing mechanism that lets Dot inherit most of Dash's learned knowledge, allows Dot to closely approximate Dash's next-step estimate without jump sampling, yielding a lossless speedup of 1.78×–3.31× on average.

## Results

Morse achieves a lossless speedup of 1.78× to 3.31× on average across 9 baseline diffusion models on 6 image generation tasks. On CIFAR-10 with DDIM, average speedups range from 2.01× to 2.94× across different samplers (DDPM, DDIM, DPM-Solver, SDE). On Stable Diffusion v1.4 (MS-COCO, 512×512), Morse attains an average speedup of 2.29× (best-FID scheme) while the Dot model is trained with only 0.1% of the original training data and 0.1% of the training cost (190 A100-hours vs. 150,000). On LCM-SDXL (1024×1024), Morse further improves the already-distilled model with a 1.43× average speedup on CLIP score.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against PFDiff (Wang et al., 2025), a training-free feature-reuse method, Morse achieves consistently lower FIDs on MS-COCO with Stable Diffusion: at 10 LSDs, Morse reaches FID 11.67 vs. PFDiff's 13.06, and at 6 LSDs, 13.88 vs. 15.47. Against AYS (Sabour et al., 2024), a time-step schedule optimization method, Morse also wins on ImageNet 64×64 with DDIM: at 10 LSDs, Morse achieves FID 25.76 vs. AYS's 29.23, and at 5 LSDs, 46.63 vs. 50.38. Against DeepCache (Ma et al., 2024), a feature-caching method, Morse consistently achieves better throughput-FID tradeoffs on both CIFAR-10 and MS-COCO (Fig. 9); critically, DeepCache is lossy in generation quality due to reusing features across N−1 steps, whereas Morse is lossless. All three comparisons confirm that Morse's dual-sampling with adaptive residual feedback outperforms both training-free reuse strategies and schedule-optimization approaches across diverse step budgets.

## Relation to Prior Work

Morse builds on the jump sampling (JS) paradigm established by fast samplers like DDIM and DPM-Solver, which visit only a subsequence of diffusion time steps for efficiency but suffer from information loss over unvisited steps. Rather than designing a new sampler or distilling a student model, Morse reformulates the single-model iterative denoising loop into a dual-model interaction (Dash + Dot), where the Dot model learns residual feedback conditioned on trajectory observations to compensate for JS information loss. The training procedure leverages LoRA and weight sharing to make the Dot model cheap to train, and the method is explicitly positioned as complementary to distillation-based approaches (e.g., it can further accelerate LCM-SDXL) and orthogonal to feature-reuse methods like DeepCache and PFDiff.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Morse offers a practical, lossless acceleration path (1.78×–3.31×) that is broadly applicable across architectures (U-Net, self-attention), samplers (DDPM, DDIM, SDE, DPM-Solver), and step budgets (hundreds to few steps), with the Dot model trainable at ~0.1% of the original training cost. A key open question is how the speedup factor N (and thus the achievable speedup) behaves on heterogeneous or emerging hardware, since the method's benefit is directly tied to the latency gap between Dash and Dot. The paper does not explore video, 3D, or audio generation, leaving unclear whether the residual-feedback strategy generalizes to higher-dimensional or temporally structured outputs. Additionally, the method introduces extra parameters and a second model to manage, which may complicate deployment in resource-constrained settings despite the small parameter overhead.
