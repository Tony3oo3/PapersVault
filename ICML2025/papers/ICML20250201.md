## Problem

Hierarchical clustering objectives such as Dasgupta's cost and Moseley-Wang revenue are subject to strong hardness-of-approximation barriers: no constant-factor approximation exists in polynomial time for Dasgupta's objective under the Small Set Expansion hypothesis, and no (1−C)-approximation exists for Moseley-Wang under the same assumption. This paper asks whether a natural learning-augmented oracle providing noisy triplet split-away information can provably break these complexity-theoretic limits.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250201/cropped_page5_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The algorithm takes a weighted undirected graph G=(V,E,w) on n vertices and a splitting oracle O:V×V×V→V that, on a triplet (u,v,w), returns the vertex that splits away from the other two in the optimal tree with probability p=9/10 (fixed per triplet, no boosting). The output is a binary dendrogram whose leaves are the individual vertices. The strong partial tree construction uses O(n³) oracle queries and O(n³ log n) time, while the weak partial tree uses only O(n²) queries and near-linear time; super-vertices in the partial tree are bounded at 50000·log n vertices, and the algorithm assumes n≥200 log n.

## Method

The core technical contribution is the construction of partial hierarchical clustering trees—binary trees whose leaves may be 'super-vertices' of size O(log n) representing unresolved local clustering—that are provably consistent with the optimal tree T*. Two consistency notions are defined: strong (each super-vertex induces a maximal subtree in T*) and weak (super-vertices may span consecutive regions with out-degree at most 2). The construction uses a 'small-tree splitting order' to identify good baseline vertices, concentration inequalities to handle oracle noise, and for the weak case, a 'horizon set' mechanism plus candidate-vertex root tests to maintain consistency while achieving near-linear time. For Dasgupta's objective, the strong partial tree is filled by recursive sparsest cuts (exact or O(√log s)-approximate) on each super-vertex, yielding O(1) or O(√log log n) approximation respectively. For Moseley-Wang, the weak partial tree suffices: super-vertices are partitioned arbitrarily since n−O(log n) leaves lie outside each, and a new structural result bounds the contribution of 'less significant' edges to o(1) of the optimal revenue, giving a (1−o(1)) approximation in near-linear time.

## Results

For Dasgupta's minimization objective, the paper achieves an O(1) approximation in polynomial time (Theorem 1) and an O(√(log log n)) approximation in Õ(n³) time (Theorem 2), both using O(n³) oracle queries. For the Moseley-Wang maximization objective, the algorithm achieves a (1−o(1)) approximation in O(n²·polylog n) time with O(n²) queries (Theorem 3). In sublinear settings, a single-pass streaming algorithm uses O(n·log³ n) space with polynomial time (Theorem 4), and a PRAM algorithm achieves (1−o(1)) approximation with O(n²·polylog n) work and log³ n depth.

## Baselines & Comparisons

For Dasgupta's objective, the O(√(log log n)) approximation (Theorem 2) improves upon the best oblivious O(√(log n)) bound by Charikar & Chatziafratis (2017b) and Cohen-Addad et al. (2019); the paper notes this is roughly 2.3× better for n = 10¹⁰. More strikingly, the O(1) approximation (Theorem 1) breaks the Small Set Expansion hardness barrier that rules out any constant-factor approximation in polynomial time without oracle advice. For the Moseley-Wang objective, the (1−o(1)) approximation (Theorem 3) dramatically surpasses the 1/3 approximation of the average-linkage heuristic (Moseley & Wang 2017) and overcomes the APX-hardness of (1−C) approximation under SSE shown by Chatziafratis et al. (2020b). In the streaming setting, the paper's polynomial-time O(1)-approximation algorithm improves upon the exponential-time algorithms of Assadi et al. (2022) and Agarwal et al. (2022), which also achieve O(1) approximation but with intractable running time. In the parallel (PRAM) setting, the (1−o(1)) approximation with O(n²·polylog n) work and log³ n depth improves upon the polylog n approximation of Agarwal et al. (2024), though the objectives differ (Moseley-Wang vs. Dasgupta).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of hierarchical clustering (HC) approximation and the learning-augmented algorithms framework. It builds directly on the recursive sparsest cut procedure of Charikar & Chatziafratis (2017b) and the streaming/parallel HC frameworks of Assadi et al. (2022), using them as subroutines to resolve the 'unknown' clustering inside super-vertices of a partial HC tree. The splitting oracle (triplet split-away queries) draws on a long line of phylogenetic reconstruction work (Aho et al. 1981, Chatziafratis & Makarychev 2023) but is repurposed here as a noisy ML oracle in the learning-augmented sense of Mitzenmacher & Vassilvitskii (2020). The key departure from prior HC work is that all known hardness barriers (no constant approximation for Dasgupta under SSE, APX-hardness for MW under SSE) are overcome by augmenting the algorithm with this oracle, following the broader trend of learning-augmented algorithms breaking NP-hardness limits for graph problems (Cohen-Addad et al. 2024, Braverman et al. 2024).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a simple, natural triplet oracle (which vertex splits away from the other two) is sufficient to break known inapproximability barriers for two of the most studied HC objectives, providing the first constant-approximation for Dasgupta's cost and (1-o(1))-approximation for Moseley-Wang revenue in polynomial time. The partial HC tree is a novel intermediate data structure that may be of independent interest beyond this paper. Key limitations include the 'embarrassingly large' O(n^50002) runtime of the O(1)-approximation algorithm, and the open question of whether the weak partial tree construction can be improved to Õ(n) time. The practical O(√log log n) algorithm for Dasgupta's objective offers a ~2.3× improvement over the state-of-the-art O(√log n) for n=10^10, and the sublinear (streaming/PRAM) extensions show the oracle's value in modern computation models.
