## Problem

Fine-tuning LLMs on tabular classification tasks produces multiple equally well-performing models that make conflicting predictions on the same inputs (fine-tuning multiplicity), raising reliability concerns in high-stakes domains like finance and healthcare. Existing multiplicity measures require retraining and ensembling multiple models, which is computationally prohibitive for LLMs with billions of parameters. The paper asks whether prediction consistency under fine-tuning multiplicity can be quantified preemptively from a single model without expensive retraining.

## Input / Output

Given a single fine-tuned Tabular LLM and a serialized tabular data point x, the method samples k points from a truncated Gaussian distribution over a hypersphere of radius σ centered at x in the model's embedding space, then evaluates the model's predicted probability for the target class at each sampled point. The output is a scalar local stability score S_{k,σ}(x, f) ∈ [0,1] that estimates how consistently the prediction for x would be maintained across a broad class of equally well-performing fine-tuned models. The neighborhood radius σ is set data-drivenly as a fraction of the median k-NN distance in the embedding space, and k is chosen to fit within a single GPU inference pass (k=30 in experiments).

## Method

The paper introduces a local stability measure S_{k,σ}(x, f) = (1/k)Σ f(x_i) − (1/k)Σ|f(x) − f(x_i)| that combines the mean predicted probability in a local embedding-space neighborhood with a penalty for prediction variability within that neighborhood. This is motivated by the observation that Tabular LLMs exhibit noisy, non-smooth decision boundaries (unlike standard neural networks), making high confidence alone an unreliable indicator of prediction consistency. The authors provide a probabilistic guarantee (Theorem 1) via Hoeffding's inequality showing that under mild smoothness and bounded-error assumptions on the fine-tuned model class, a high local stability score certifies that the target model's prediction will remain above S − ε with probability at least 1 − exp(−kε²/32). Empirically, the measure is evaluated on BIGSCIENCE T0 and FLAN-T5 fine-tuned via T-Few and LoRA across six tabular datasets, showing stronger Spearman correlation with actual multiplicity metrics (Arbitrariness, Pairwise Disagreement, Prediction Variance, Prediction Range) than prediction confidence or dropout-based baselines, at a fraction of the computational cost of retraining 40 models.

## Results

The proposed local stability measure achieves higher Spearman correlation with evaluated multiplicity metrics than both baselines across most datasets. On the 128-shot setting, Stability reaches 0.97 correlation with Arbitrariness on Car (vs. 0.63 Drop-Out, 0.56 Pred. Prob.) and 0.97 with Prediction Variance on Heart (vs. 0.74 Drop-Out, 0.50 Pred. Prob.). Computationally, Stability requires only 19.4 minutes of evaluation time on the Adult test set, compared to 102 minutes for Drop-Out and 977.6 minutes for AWP, while maintaining superior correlation (e.g., 0.96 vs. 0.78 for Pairwise Disagreement). On the large-scale Hospital Readmission dataset (~100k points), Stability achieves a mean separation of 0.298 between correctly and incorrectly classified points, outperforming Pred. Prob. (0.103) and Drop-Out (0.097).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the proposed Stability measure against two baselines: the Drop-Out method (Hsu et al., ICLR 2024), which explores the Rashomon set by dropping random model weights, and Adversarial Weight Perturbation / AWP (Hsu & Calmon, NeurIPS 2022), which uses gradient-based optimization to enforce Rashomon-set constraints. Against Drop-Out, Stability wins decisively on most datasets: on Car it achieves 0.97 vs. 0.63 Spearman correlation with Arbitrariness, on Heart 0.89 vs. 0.64, and on Bank 0.79 vs. 0.62; the only near-tie is on German Credit (0.54 vs. 0.50 for Arbitrariness). Against AWP, Stability dominates on both correlation and runtime: on Adult 128-shot, Stability achieves 0.81/0.96/0.80/0.93 across the four multiplicity metrics versus AWP's 0.65/0.71/0.55/0.72, while requiring only 19.4 minutes of evaluation time compared to AWP's 977.6 minutes (a ~50× speedup). The Drop-Out method consistently outperforms raw prediction probability, confirming that some form of perturbation is necessary, but Stability's embedding-space neighborhood sampling proves more informative for LLMs, likely because the embedding space is more compact and structured than the full parameter space that Drop-Out perturbs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the predictive multiplicity / Rashomon effect literature (Breiman 2003; Marx et al. 2020; Hsu & Calmon 2022; Watson-Daniels et al. 2023) to the specific setting of fine-tuned Tabular LLMs, where existing measures require retraining and ensembling multiple models—prohibitively expensive for billion-parameter LLMs. The closest prior work is Hsu et al. (2024), which uses dropout to explore the Rashomon set for neural networks, and Gomez et al. (2024), which empirically studies prediction arbitrariness in text-classification LLMs. The key departure is a single-model, embedding-space perturbation approach (local stability) that avoids retraining entirely, combined with a Hoeffding-based probabilistic guarantee linking local stability to cross-model consistency under mild smoothness assumptions on the fine-tuned model class.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that 20% of test predictions are simultaneously high-confidence and low-stability directly challenges the common practice of trusting model confidence scores in high-stakes tabular deployments (finance, healthcare, hiring), and provides a cheap diagnostic (one forward pass with k neighborhood samples) to flag unreliable predictions. Limitations include the requirement of embedding-space access (restricting applicability to open-source models), sensitivity to the neighborhood radius σ, and the fact that the theoretical bound's gap ε′ is not directly computable in practice. Open questions include whether the measure transfers to closed-source LLMs, how to estimate ε′ empirically, and whether mitigation strategies (e.g., constrained fine-tuning) can reduce multiplicity at the source.
