## Problem

Existing methods for inferring user writing preferences from demonstrations (e.g., CIPHER) produce generic preference descriptions that fail to capture individualized style, because they rely on a single inference step without the ability to reflect on and refine the inferred description. Additionally, the PRELUDE benchmark used to evaluate such methods suffers from weak correlation between preference-quality and generation-quality metrics, an ambiguous binary editing procedure, and overlapping preference sets that match default LLM behavior.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250064/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

PROSE takes a task description (e.g., 'summarize this article' or 'write an email from these notes') paired with the user's own completed writing sample as input. It produces a structured preference description decomposed into discrete components (e.g., 'use ALLCAPS to emphasize words', 'adopt a step-by-step structure'), which is stored in an interaction memory and retrieved for future tasks. At inference time, the preference description conditions the LLM to generate writing aligned with the user's style, requiring approximately 1/10 the prompt tokens of in-context learning baselines.

## Method

PROSE (Preference Reasoning by Observing and Synthesizing Examples) builds on CIPHER by adding two mechanisms. First, iterative refinement: the LLM generates a writing sample conditioned on the current preference description, compares it to the user's demonstration, and updates the description to reduce the gap, repeating up to S steps (S=5 in experiments). Second, consistency verification: the final preference description is decomposed into individual components, and each component is scored against multiple previously observed user demonstrations via LLM-as-a-Judge on a [-2, +2] scale; components whose mean score falls below a threshold v are pruned to prevent overfitting to a single example. The method is entirely prompt-based (no fine-tuning), operates in-context, and is evaluated across four LLMs (Qwen2.5-7B/72B-Instruct, GPT-4o-mini, GPT-4o) on summarization and email writing tasks, where PROSE outperforms CIPHER by 33% and combines with ICL for up to 9% further improvement.

## Results

On the PLUME benchmark (summarization + email writing), PROSE Full achieves a task-mean PPCM of 1.07±0.05 and Preference Similarity of 1.49±0.06, outperforming CIPHER-1 (PPCM 0.14) and CIPHER-5 (PPCM 0.09) by 33% on the percentile scale. The combined PROSE Full+ICL method reaches PPCM 1.49±0.04, exceeding ICL alone (1.37) by up to 9% and CIPHER by 51%. The Oracle upper bound sits at PPCM 1.83, while the no-preference baseline (NPC) scores −1.00, giving a total dynamic range of ~2.83 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CIPHER (Gao et al., 2024), PROSE Full substantially outperforms both CIPHER-1 and CIPHER-5 on generation quality (PPCM 1.07 vs. 0.14 and 0.09 respectively), a 33% improvement on the percentile scale, while achieving comparable or slightly higher Preference Similarity (1.49 vs. 1.44/1.46). Against ICL, the picture is more nuanced: in aggregate across all four LLMs, ICL achieves higher PPCM (1.37) than PROSE Full (1.07), but PROSE scales better with LLM quality and surpasses ICL when using GPT-4o (1.35 vs. 1.32 task-mean PPCM). The two methods are complementary: PROSE excels on nuanced tone preferences (e.g., 'be sharply critical') while ICL excels on structural preferences (e.g., 'write in the style of a tweet'), and combining them (PROSE Full+ICL) yields PPCM 1.49, a 9% gain over ICL alone and 51% over CIPHER. On the original PRELUDE benchmark, PROSE also outperforms all baselines on PPCM for both summarization (1.18 vs. 0.82 for CIPHER-5) and email writing (1.10 vs. 1.09 for CIPHER-5), though the metric correlation issues in PRELUDE limit interpretability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on CIPHER (Gao et al., 2024), which established that learning a natural-language preference description from user edits and conditioning an LLM on it outperforms in-context learning for writing alignment. PROSE departs from CIPHER in two key ways: it learns from user demonstrations rather than edits, and it replaces CIPHER's single inference step with iterative refinement (comparing the agent's generation to the user's demonstration across multiple rounds) plus a consistency-verification step that prunes preference components unsupported by prior examples. The paper also positions itself against the broader personalization literature (RLHF, SFT, activation steering, prompt optimization) by emphasizing that PROSE requires no fine-tuning, uses interpretable preference descriptions, and operates from implicit user signals rather than explicit feedback.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The PLUME benchmark addresses concrete, well-documented flaws in PRELUDE (weak metric-to-generation correlation, ambiguous binary editing, overlapping preference sets), making it a more reliable evaluation environment for preference-inference research. The finding that PROSE and ICL are complementary rather than competing—yielding up to 9% gains over ICL alone—suggests practical systems should combine explicit preference descriptions with in-context examples. Key open questions include the substantial token overhead (5.87× more prompt tokens than a single-step method), the reliance on a synthetic GPT-4o human rather than real users, and whether the iterative-refinement gains transfer to more complex or open-ended writing tasks beyond the controlled summarization/email settings tested here.
