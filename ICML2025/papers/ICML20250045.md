## Problem

Current video quality metrics like FVD are biased toward frame-level appearance rather than motion, failing to capture the poor temporal coherence that remains a major weakness of generative video models. Additionally, these distribution-level metrics require access to a reference dataset and cannot evaluate individual videos or video pairs, limiting their practical utility during training or for models without available training data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250045/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

TRAJAN takes as input a set of 4096 point tracks (x, y, occlusion flag per frame) extracted by BootsTAPIR from 150-frame clips at 256×256 resolution. The tracks are orderless and contain missing data due to occlusions, so the model must produce a permutation-invariant representation that is invariant to both occluded points and the specific subset of points sampled. It outputs a fixed-size 128×64 motion latent, a per-video reconstruction score (Average Jaccard), and per-point spatiotemporal error maps that localize where and when motion inconsistencies occur.

## Method

TRAJAN is a trajectory autoencoder that encodes a variable-sized set of point tracks into a fixed-size latent and decodes held-out tracks from arbitrary query points. The encoder applies sinusoidal space-time embeddings, per-track self-attention with occlusion-aware masking (1−o), and a Perceiver-style cross-attention from 128 learned latent tokens to produce the 128×64 representation. The decoder up-projects the latent, uses a query point (x_q, y_q, t_q) as a readout token via self-attention, and predicts the full track passing through that point at all timesteps with L1 position loss and sigmoid cross-entropy on occlusion. The key design choice is training on held-out query tracks rather than the input tracks, forcing the latent to encode a dense motion field invariant to the specific sampled points. Trained self-supervised on 15M real 150-frame video clips, the model's reconstruction error (Average Jaccard) serves as a per-video quality metric because unrealistic motions are harder to reconstruct, while the latent space supports Fréchet distance for distribution comparisons and L2 distance for video-pair comparisons.

## Results

On per-video evaluation (Table 1), TRAJAN achieves the highest Spearman rank correlations with human ratings across all four motion dimensions on both EvalCrafter (0.29 Motion Consist., 0.29 App. Consist., 0.27 Realism, 0.19 Interacts) and VideoPhy (0.25, 0.32, 0.29, 0.09), outperforming all baselines. At the model level, TRAJAN achieves a Spearman rank coefficient of 0.90 in predicting which of 11 generative video models humans prefer for realism, versus only 0.07 for VideoMAE (Figure 3). At the distribution level on UCF-101 with elastic corruptions, TRAJAN shows the highest sensitivity to spatiotemporal distortions (highest ST/S ratio) among all tested methods (Figure 8).

## Baselines & Comparisons

TRAJAN consistently outperforms all six baselines on per-video human-rating correlation. VideoMAE_PT and I3D (appearance-based) show near-zero or negative Spearman correlations (≤0.06) across nearly all dimensions, confirming their known content bias. RAFT-Warp (optical flow) is the closest competitor, achieving 0.28/0.27/0.25/0.13 on EvalCrafter versus TRAJAN's 0.29/0.29/0.27/0.19, but TRAJAN pulls ahead more clearly on VideoPhy (e.g., 0.32 vs 0.26 for appearance consistency, 0.29 vs 0.18 for realism). MooG-PSNR (next-frame prediction) lags behind both TRAJAN and RAFT across all dimensions. At the distribution level, TRAJAN and RAFT-Warp are the most sensitive to spatiotemporal distortions, while motion histograms (Liu et al., 2024a) and appearance-based methods (VideoMAE, I3D) show equal sensitivity to spatial-only and spatiotemporal corruptions. On VideoPhy physical consistency (Figure 6), TRAJAN matches or exceeds VideoCon (which is fine-tuned on human labels) in ROC-AUC without any fine-tuning, demonstrating its generality.

## Relation to Prior Work

TRAJAN builds on the point-tracking paradigm established by BootsTAPIR (Doersch et al., 2024) and the Perceiver architecture (Jaegle et al., 2021), but departs from prior video evaluation metrics by replacing pixel-level or classification-based features (I3D, VideoMAE) with a learned autoencoder over point trajectories. While Liu et al. (2024a) also use point tracks, they compute hand-crafted motion histograms; TRAJAN instead learns a dense, permutation-invariant motion representation that supports reconstruction-based scoring. The paper also unifies three previously separate evaluation paradigms—per-video quality, paired video comparison, and distribution-level Fréchet distance—under a single model, whereas FVD and its successors addressed only the last.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TRAJAN offers a practical, reference-free alternative to FVD that is markedly more sensitive to temporal distortions and can localize errors in space and time, which is valuable for debugging generative video models during training. However, the per-video correlations with human ratings remain modest (Spearman ρ ≈ 0.25–0.32), and the method fundamentally cannot detect physically implausible but smooth motions (e.g., an object collapsing into a surface), since it only measures how well motion is *reconstructable*, not whether it *should* occur. The paper also reveals significant inter-rater disagreement, raising questions about whether human judgements are a reliable gold standard for video quality. Future work will need to incorporate physical priors or world-model expectations to close this gap.
