## Problem

High-dimensional black-box optimization is critical in scientific applications but remains challenging: Bayesian optimization suffers from the curse of dimensionality and struggles to scale to thousands of evaluations, while existing generative-model-based approaches underperform due to limited model expressivity and difficulty of uncertainty estimation in high-dimensional spaces. The core difficulty is that directly maximizing an acquisition function over a highly non-convex landscape in hundreds of dimensions tends to select samples far from the data manifold, leading to overemphasized exploration at search-space boundaries.

## Input / Output

The method operates on continuous design vectors in bounded boxes [lb, ub]^D (D ranging from 100 to 400 in experiments) and consumes an initial dataset of 100–200 input-output pairs. Each round produces a batch of B candidates (B=50–100) for evaluation, and the dataset is updated with new observations while maintaining a buffer of at most L high-scoring samples. The final output is the best design vector and its function value discovered over R rounds of batched queries.

## Method

DiBO casts candidate selection as posterior inference: it trains a diffusion model as a prior over the data distribution and a deep ensemble of MLP proxies to predict function values with epistemic uncertainty, then defines a target posterior p_tar(x) ∝ p_θ(x)·exp(β·r_φ(x)) where r_φ is a UCB score combining proxy mean and standard deviation. To sample from this intractable posterior, the diffusion model is fine-tuned via the Relative Trajectory Balance (RTB) loss, which enables off-policy training with both on-policy trajectories and noised samples from the dataset for mode coverage. After generating M candidates from the amortized sampler, two post-processing steps refine them: gradient-based local search using the PF-ODE (with Skilling-Hutchinson trace estimation) moves each sample toward a high-density mode of the unnormalized posterior, and top-B filtering by unnormalized posterior density selects diverse, high-quality candidates. A reweighted training scheme (exponential in function value) is applied to both the diffusion model and proxy ensembles to concentrate learning on high-scoring regions.

## Results

DiBO is evaluated on four synthetic benchmarks (Rastrigin, Ackley, Levy, Rosenbrock at D=200 and D=400) and three real-world tasks (HalfCheetah-102D, RoverPlanning-100D, DNA-180D), with results presented as best-so-far performance curves over up to 10,000 (synthetic) or 2,000 (real-world) evaluations. The paper reports that DiBO significantly outperforms all nine baselines across every task, with particularly large gaps on Rastrigin and Ackley where numerous local optima near the global optimum make optimization challenging. On Ackley-200D, the default configuration achieves a best-so-far score of −0.643, which improves to −0.260 with increased local search steps (J=50) and buffer size (L=2000). DiBO also achieves competitive per-round runtime (~40 s at 200D, ~72 s at 400D on a single RTX 3090), faster than all BO-based baselines (TuRBO: 244 s, CMA-BO: 643 s at 200D) and comparable to other generative-model methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DiBO outperforms all nine baselines on every synthetic and real-world task. Among BO methods, TuRBO, LA-MCTS, MCMC-BO, and CMA-BO all show competitive early-stage performance but plateau well below DiBO, particularly on Rastrigin and Ackley where their local-region or partitioning strategies struggle to escape local optima. The evolutionary baseline CMA-ES is the weakest performer overall, confirming its limitations in high-dimensional non-convex landscapes. Among generative-model baselines, CbAS and MINs (which use VAEs) perform reasonably in the first few rounds but fail to improve further, reflecting the limited expressivity of their underlying models. The closest competitors are the diffusion-based DDOM and Diff-BBO, which show consistent improvement across tasks but still trail DiBO by a noticeable margin; the paper attributes this gap to DiBO's posterior-sampling framework (amortized inference via RTB loss, local search, and filtering) rather than to the use of diffusion models alone. On real-world tasks, DiBO is the only method that consistently surpasses all baselines across HalfCheetah, RoverPlanning, and DNA, whereas other methods excel on some tasks but underperform on others.

## Relation to Prior Work

DiBO sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) generative-model-based black-box optimization (CbAS, MINs, DDOM, Diff-BBO), which learns an inverse mapping from function values to inputs; (2) amortized posterior inference in diffusion models via the relative trajectory balance (RTB) loss (Venkatraman et al., 2024), originally developed for vision and control; and (3) entropy-regularized reward-weighted sampling (Nair et al., 2020) that yields the closed-form posterior p_tar(x) ∝ p_θ(x)·exp(β·r(x)). The key departure from prior generative-model-based optimizers is reframing candidate selection as amortized posterior inference rather than conditional generation or classifier-free guidance, and from BO methods (TuRBO, LA-MCTS) by replacing argmax acquisition search with sampling from a learned posterior, thereby avoiding the non-convex optimization over the acquisition function in high dimensions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that the posterior-sampling framework—not merely the use of diffusion models—is what drives consistent outperformance across seven diverse high-dimensional tasks, offering a principled alternative to acquisition-function maximization that naturally prevents over-exploration in data-sparse regions. A notable limitation is the need to retrain the diffusion model from scratch each round, which the authors acknowledge as a computational bottleneck; whether trained models can be incrementally updated or reused across rounds remains an open question. The finding that reweighted training, local search, and filtering are each individually critical (per ablations) suggests the method's success depends on a carefully calibrated pipeline rather than a single architectural insight, which may limit straightforward transfer to other domains.
