## Problem

Concept Activation Vectors (CAVs) are a foundational tool for concept-based explainability, but computing them via linear SVM classifiers becomes prohibitively expensive in modern high-dimensional architectures (e.g., EVA-02-L/14 has ~1M activation dimensions). This computational bottleneck restricts practical applications such as analyzing many layers simultaneously, interpreting numerous concepts at once, or tracking concept evolution during training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252121/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Given a trained network f split at layer l, a set of concept images D_c (typically 60 images from a curated dataset like Broden), and a set of random images D_r (60 images from the training distribution), FastCAV computes activations g_l(x) for all samples and produces a unit-norm CAV v_c^l in the d-dimensional activation space of layer l, along with the corresponding linear decision boundary intercept b. The CAV is a direction vector whose dot product with gradients indicates whether the concept influences a target class prediction, and multiple CAVs are typically computed across resampled D_r sets for statistical significance testing.

## Method

FastCAV exploits the superposition hypothesis that neural network features are encoded as nearly orthogonal directions in activation space. Instead of training a linear SVM, it computes the global mean μ̂ over all activations from D_c ∪ D_r, then takes the normalized mean of (g_l(x) − μ̂) over concept examples x ∈ D_c, yielding a unit vector pointing from the global mean toward the concept mean. Theoretically, under isotropic Gaussian assumptions for both concept and random activations, this is equivalent to Fisher discriminant analysis, and in high-dimensional regimes (d ≫ n) where most samples become support vectors, it coincides with the linear SVM solution used in prior CAV work. The resulting time complexity is O(nd) with very small constants, compared to O(max(n,d)·min(n,d)²) for SVM, yielding speedups of up to 63.6× (average 46.4×) while maintaining comparable CAV accuracy and inter-method cosine similarity (0.8–0.9) across diverse architectures.

## Results

FastCAV achieves up to 63.6× speedup (average 46.4×) over SVM-CAV in CAV computation time across 8 model architectures, e.g., reducing computation from 301.6s to 6.1s for EVA-02-L/14 (1,049,600 dimensions). CAV accuracy is comparable or slightly higher: FastCAV achieves 0.95±0.06 vs. 0.93±0.09 for Inception-v3 and 0.89±0.15 vs. 0.87±0.15 for ResNet50. Inter-method cosine similarity between FastCAV and SVM-CAV vectors ranges from 0.80 to 0.90 across all models, confirming they identify similar directions in activation space. In downstream TCAV and ACE evaluations, FastCAV produces equivalent qualitative insights with lower variance in scores.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the primary baseline SVM-CAV (Kim et al., 2018), FastCAV wins decisively on computation time (up to 63.6× faster) and intra-method robustness (pairwise CAV similarity of 0.60–0.83 vs. 0.22–0.45 for SVM-CAV), while matching or slightly exceeding accuracy in 7 of 8 models (losing only on ConvNeXt-XXL: 0.90 vs. 0.93). For the medical task (Table 2), FastCAV (0.006s, 0.72 accuracy, 0.79 similarity) matches SVM (Ghosh et al., 2023: 0.439s, 0.71 accuracy) and sparse logistic regression (Singla et al., 2021: 6.37s, 0.72 accuracy) on accuracy while being ~70× faster than S-LR and ~70× faster than SVM, with substantially higher intra-method similarity (0.79 vs. 0.46–0.50). In the broader comparison (Tables 5–7), FastCAV is the fastest method by a large margin (0.005–0.039s vs. 0.145–54.6s for all other methods), achieves the highest intra-method similarity (0.75–0.91 vs. 0.13–0.70 for others), and matches or exceeds accuracy of logistic regression (Pfau et al., 2021) and ridge classification (Pedregosa et al., 2011) in most settings. The only case where FastCAV is clearly outperformed is on a small subset of CAVs (2.8% of all computed vectors) where the isotropic Gaussian assumption is violated, with SVM achieving up to 40% higher accuracy in individual cases.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the CAV framework of Kim et al. (2018), which computes concept directions by training linear SVMs in activation space, and on the superposition literature (Olah et al., 2020; Elhage et al., 2022) showing that features are encoded as nearly orthogonal directions. The key conceptual move is recognizing that, under isotropic Gaussian assumptions, the SVM solution reduces to a Fisher discriminant, which in turn reduces to a simple mean-difference vector—making the SVM's iterative optimization unnecessary. The theoretical bridge via Shashua (1999), who showed Fisher discriminant on support vectors equals the linear SVM solution, combined with the high-dimensional observation that nearly all points become support vectors, provides the justification for replacing SVM training with a single vector computation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 63.6× speedup (average 46.4×) makes CAV computation practical for modern architectures with activation spaces exceeding one million dimensions, where SVM-CAV becomes infeasible (e.g., RegNetY exceeded four days). This enables previously impractical analyses such as tracking concept evolution across training epochs and network layers, which the paper demonstrates with ResNet50. However, the equivalence relies on strong assumptions (isotropic within-class covariance, Gaussian activations) that are violated in ~2.8% of cases with accuracy gaps exceeding 25 percentage points, and the authors acknowledge that properties like locality, consistency, and entanglement of FastCAV CAVs remain uncharacterized. The method is a drop-in replacement rather than a new interpretability paradigm, so its impact is primarily computational rather than conceptual.
