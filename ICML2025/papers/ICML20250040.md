## Problem

While LLMs have been shown to match the first-order statistics (log-perplexity) of natural language, it remains unclear whether they replicate the fractal structure—self-similarity and long-range dependence—observed in the information-theoretic complexity of human text. This paper investigates whether and under what conditions (decoding temperature, prompting strategy, model size, instruction-tuning) LLMs fail to capture these second-order fractal properties, and whether the resulting differences could serve as signals for detecting synthetic text.

## Input / Output

The analysis operates on log-perplexity sequences extracted from documents clipped to 400 tokens (with the first 64 tokens discarded to remove warm-up effects), scored by pretrained models. Fractal parameters are estimated by fitting power laws over time scales τ ∈ {8, 16, …, 320} with bootstrapped confidence intervals, and are computed over corpora of texts—each corpus corresponding to a specific combination of generating model, temperature, prompt, scoring model, and domain—rather than over individual documents. Quality ratings are produced by Gemini 1.0 Pro on a 1–5 scale.

## Method

The paper constructs the GAGLE dataset of over 240,000 LLM-generated articles by prompting three architectures (Gemini 1.0 Pro, Mistral-7B, Gemma-2B) in both pretrained and instruction-tuned modes across seven prompting strategies ordered by information density, three decoding temperatures, and five text domains. A key theoretical contribution is a causal model in which a latent context generates both a prefix and a suffix, showing that prompting corresponds to an interventional (do) query while LLMs learn the conditional distribution—this mismatch provides a mechanistic explanation for why LLMs may distort fractal structure. Empirically, the authors find a 'double descent' in self-similarity: providing a summary in the prompt yields texts less fractal-similar to natural language than either fewer (unordered keywords) or more (ordered excerpts) contextual information. They further show that natural language occupies a narrow range of Hölder and Hurst exponents while LLM outputs span a much wider range, and that the Hurst exponent is a substantially stronger predictor of text quality than log-perplexity or the Hölder exponent.

## Results

The paper finds that natural language maintains Hölder exponent S and Hurst exponent H within a narrow range, while LLM-generated outputs vary widely, suggesting fractal parameters could help detect a non-trivial portion of synthetic texts. LLM-generated texts are systematically biased toward higher S (less self-similarity) and higher H (more temporal dependence) than natural language, with the bias diminishing as model size increases (Gemini 1.0 Pro > Mistral-7B > Gemma-2B). Prompting has the largest impact on fractal parameters, contributing 19.7% of the Shannon mutual information with H and 8.4% with S, and exhibits a surprising 'double descent' for self-similarity where unordered keywords outperform summaries. The Hurst exponent H emerges as a much stronger predictor of text quality than log-perplexity or S, with a Pearson correlation of 0.68 between S and H.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical analysis paper rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so the key contrasts are internal. On model size, larger pretrained models (Gemini 1.0 Pro) replicate the fractal parameters of natural language more faithfully than smaller ones (Mistral-7B, Gemma-2B), with Gemma-2B being a notable exception that still shows lower log-perplexity even at β=1. On training paradigm, instruction-tuned models produce texts with lower log-perplexity and higher Hurst exponent H at low temperatures (β<1) compared to their pretrained counterparts, while self-similarity (S) remains largely unaffected by instruction tuning. On prompting strategy, the paper reveals a non-monotone 'double descent' effect: providing unordered keywords yields fractal parameters closer to natural language than providing a summary, while ordered excerpts (most information) eventually recover the original article's structure. On domain, encyclopedic (Wikipedia) and legal (BillSum) texts are closest to natural language in both S and H, while poetry in the RAID dataset shows the largest S deviation (0.50±0.02), indicating LLMs struggle most with highly self-similar creative forms. All findings are validated across both the GAGLE dataset (240K+ articles) and the independent RAID benchmark (11 models, 7 domains).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Alabdulmohsin et al. (2024), which established that natural language exhibits fractal structure (self-similarity and long-range dependence) in its information-theoretic complexity, by asking whether LLMs can replicate these second-order statistical properties. It positions itself within the broader LLM detection and evaluation literature (GLTR, DetectGPT, perplexity-based methods) but departs from first-order statistics by introducing Hölder and Hurst exponents as new analytical probes, following Meister & Cotterell's (2021) argument that evaluation should go beyond log-perplexity. The causal inference framing of prompting as a do-query (vs. a conditional query) draws on Pearl-style causal modeling to explain why LLMs may systematically fail to match human fractal structure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that natural language occupies a narrow range of fractal parameters while LLM outputs vary widely offers a potential statistical signal for detecting synthetic text that complements perplexity-based methods, particularly as larger models close the first-order gap. The counterintuitive 'double descent' in prompting (unordered keywords outperform summaries for fractal fidelity) has practical implications for how practitioners should structure prompts if statistical naturalness is a goal. Key limitations include the need for long documents (≥400 tokens) to estimate fractal parameters reliably, preventing application to individual short texts, and the restriction to autoregressive architectures. Open questions include whether these findings generalize to state-space models and whether fractal parameters can be effectively integrated into real-time detection pipelines.
