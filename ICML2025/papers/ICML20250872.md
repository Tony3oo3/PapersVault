## Problem

In marketplaces, recommender systems, and other sequential settings, units' outcomes are affected by shared states (prices, inventory, algorithmic recommendations) that mediate interference between individuals. Existing causal inference methods either impose parametric models, require domain-specific assumptions, or treat the shared state as an ordinary covariate—each yielding biased or inconsistent treatment effect estimates. This paper formalizes 'shared-state interference' as a general interference structure and provides the first general-purpose double machine learning framework for efficient, nonparametric inference under this structure.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sequence of T observed units, each with covariates X_t ∈ ℝ^{p_X}, a binary treatment D_t, a (possibly vector-valued) shared state H_t ∈ ℝ^{p_H}, and a real-valued outcome Y_t, together with an independent auxiliary dataset of comparable size used to train nuisance functions (outcome model f and propensity score m). It outputs a point estimate of the target estimand—average direct effect (ADE) in observational settings or global average treatment effect (GATE) in switchback experiments—along with a consistent variance estimate that supports asymptotically valid confidence intervals. Unlike standard DML, sample splitting is replaced by the auxiliary-data requirement because folds of a Markov chain are not independent.

## Method

The core contribution is a DML theorem (Theorem 3.1) that extends Chernozhukov et al. (2018) to dependent data by replacing the iid CLT with a Markov chain CLT, under either geometric ergodicity with detailed balance or m-dependence. The estimator is an AIPW-style score where the outcome nuisance f takes the shared state H_t as an argument, and Neyman orthogonality ensures first-order insensitivity to nuisance estimation errors. Nuisance functions are trained on an auxiliary sample independent of the inference data (rather than via cross-fitting), and the proof controls cross-time covariances using exponential mixing (Lemma C.8) or the finite-range property of m-dependence. The framework is instantiated for two estimands: ADE in observational settings (Theorem 4.1) and GATE in switchback experiments (Theorem 5.1), where the latter achieves substantial variance reduction over the standard switchback Horvitz-Thompson estimator by using all data and regression adjustments learned by flexible ML models.

## Results

The paper validates its DML4SSI estimator through simulations rather than reporting a single numerical benchmark. For ADE estimation (Section 4.3), the DML estimator is centered around the true treatment effect while all naive alternatives (Horvitz-Thompson, plug-in, naive DML treating data as iid) exhibit substantial, idiosyncratic bias. For GATE estimation in switchback experiments (Section 5.3), the DML estimator is approximately unbiased and achieves substantially lower variance than the unbiased switchback Horvitz-Thompson estimator of Bojinov et al. (2022), because it uses all data (rather than dropping the first m observations after each switch) and leverages regression adjustments via machine learning to explain outcome variation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares DML4SSI against four naive estimators for ADE: the Horvitz-Thompson (HT) estimator, a plug-in estimator, a naive DML estimator (DML-N) that treats the data as iid, and a shared-state-as-covariates (SSAC) estimator. All four naive estimators are substantially biased in the simulations, while DML4SSI is centered at the true effect; the HT and DML-N biases arise from ignoring the shared state, and the SSAC bias arises from controlling for spillover effects rather than incorporating them into the estimand. For GATE in switchback experiments, DML4SSI is compared against the unbiased switchback HT estimator from Bojinov et al. (2022). Both estimators are centered at the true GATE, but DML4SSI achieves substantially lower variance because it uses all observations (the switchback HT drops the first m units after each switch) and because the AIPW-style regression adjustment explains outcome variation through the shared state and covariates. The paper does not report a single numerical win/loss metric; the comparisons are validated via density plots of estimator bias and spread across simulation replications.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the double machine learning framework of Chernozhukov et al. (2018) from iid data to sequentially arriving units with dependent outcomes, replacing the standard iid CLT with Markov chain CLTs (geometric ergodicity or m-dependence). It formalizes 'shared-state interference' as a structural model that unifies settings previously studied in isolation—marketplace congestion (Johari et al., 2021), switchback experiments (Bojinov et al., 2022), and recommender system spillovers (Zhan et al., 2024)—by requiring all interference to be channeled through a low-dimensional shared state with a Markov property. A key methodological departure from prior DML work is the use of auxiliary training data rather than sample splitting to ensure independence between nuisance estimators and inference data, since sample splitting does not guarantee independence in dependent data. The AIPW-style debiasing term (Robins et al., 1994) is adapted to condition on the shared state, and the GATE estimator in switchback experiments builds directly on the m-dependent design of Bojinov et al. (2022).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This provides the first general semiparametric framework for efficient (√T-rate) treatment effect estimation under shared-state interference, which is the dominant interference structure in marketplaces, ride-sharing, and recommender systems. The practical impact is that practitioners can now use expressive ML (random forests, neural networks) for nuisance estimation in these settings while obtaining valid confidence intervals, rather than relying on parametric models or domain-specific assumptions. Open questions include extending to other estimands (average indirect effects, time-discounted effects), handling cases where the shared state influences treatment assignments, and empirical validation on real marketplace data. A limitation is that the framework assumes the shared state does not affect treatment assignment, which may not hold in adaptive experimental designs.
