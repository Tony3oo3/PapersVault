## Problem

The standard n-gram overlap definition is widely used to determine whether a text sequence was included in an LLM's training data, underpinning privacy, copyright, and safety evaluations. This paper asks whether that definition is robust: can an LLM verbatim complete a target sequence even when no n-gram of that sequence appears in the training set? The answer is yes, and the paper demonstrates both natural occurrences (near-duplicates, generalizable patterns) and systematic adversarial strategies that exploit this gap.

## Input / Output

The completion test takes a target sequence x split into a prefix p and suffix s (each |x|/2 tokens) and checks whether greedy decoding from p reproduces s exactly, within a normalized Levenshtein distance of 1−r, or case-insensitively. For the pre-training experiments, GPT-2-architecture models (304M–2.8B) are trained from scratch on 33.6B tokens of FineWeb-Edu, and sequences of length k=50 (or k=100) are identified, filtered by n-gram overlap, and re-tested after retraining. For the adversarial fine-tuning experiments, Gemma-2 and Qwen 2.5 models (0.5B–9B) are fine-tuned on ~2,000 constructed examples per target text (~1,000 characters, ~250 tokens) using batch size 32 and learning rate 1e-5, with the output being whether the model completes the held-out target verbatim.

## Method

The paper has two complementary experimental pillars. First, it pre-trains LLMs from scratch, extracts all verbatim-completable sequences, removes them from the training data via sliding-window n-gram filtering (varying n from 5 to |x|), retrains a counterfactual model, and shows that up to 40% of removed sequences (exact filter) or ~1% (n=5 filter) are still completed—attributable to near-duplicates evading the filter or low-entropy generalizable patterns. Second, it adversarially constructs fine-tuning datasets D_ft from a chosen unseen target x using three transformations that provably eliminate all n-gram overlap: (1) chunk stitching (splitting x into overlapping segments of size c < n padded with random tokens), (2) token dropouts (replacing tokens at intervals ≤ n with random tokens so no contiguous n-gram survives), and (3) case flipping (randomly inverting letter casing to alter BPE tokenization). After only a few gradient steps of fine-tuning on D_ft, the model verbatim completes x despite zero n-gram membership, demonstrating that n-gram definitions fail to account for auxiliary information embedded in the dataset construction itself.

## Results

After removing all n-gram members from pre-training data and retraining from scratch, LLMs still verbatim complete ~40% of the removed sequences under exact (n=50) filtering, and ~1% under strong (n=5) filtering, consistently across model sizes from 304M to 2.8B parameters. In the adversarial fine-tuning experiments (Section 5), token dropout is the most effective method: even the smallest model (Qwen-2.5 0.5B) achieves verbatim completion of target texts with a drop interval of d=2 (50% token dropout), while chunking is largely ineffective at small chunk sizes (c=25) and only succeeds at c=100 for some models. Completion success generally improves with model size across all adversarial methods, suggesting frontier models are more capable of synthesizing n-gram non-members into target texts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external baselines; instead, it ablates the effectiveness of different n-gram filtering strengths and adversarial construction methods. On the filtering side, stronger filters (smaller n) reduce the lingering fraction from ~40% (n=50) to ~1% (n=5), but never eliminate it entirely, and the nature of lingering sequences shifts from semantically meaningful text to simple pattern continuations (e.g., counting, alphabet sequences). On the adversarial construction side, token dropout (Section 5.2) is dramatically more effective than chunk stitching (Section 5.1): at chunk size c=25, models mostly fail, while token dropout at d=2 succeeds even on 0.5B models. Case flipping (Section 5.3) is also generally effective, and composing dropout with case flipping (Section 5.4) increases task difficulty for smaller models while still enabling verbatim completion for larger ones. The paper also notes that completion success scales with model size across all methods, with Gemma-2 9B and Qwen-2.5 7B/9B consistently outperforming their 0.5B–2B counterparts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of the LLM memorization/extraction literature (Carlini et al. 2021, 2022b; Nasr et al. 2023) and the n-gram-based data deduplication and contamination detection literature (Lee et al. 2021; Sainz et al. 2023; Dubey et al. 2024). Rather than proposing a new membership test, it repurposes the standard completion test as a black-box membership probe and demonstrates a systematic mismatch with n-gram-based ground-truth definitions. The adversarial constructions in Section 5 are closely related to concurrent data poisoning work (Panaitescu-Liess et al. 2024) and the goldfish loss defense (Hans et al. 2024), but the paper's framing is fundamentally about the inadequacy of the membership *definition* rather than the attack or defense itself.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that n-gram membership is adversarially gameable has direct implications for privacy (machine unlearning via retraining may not suppress target sequences), copyright (deduplication pipelines may miss near-duplicates that still enable verbatim completion), and AI safety (harmful content removal may be incomplete). The paper leaves open the question of what a robust membership definition should look like, noting that the root cause is the failure to account for auxiliary information available to the training algorithm. A key open direction is whether 'natural' (non-adversarially-constructed) data can forge completions, which would further undermine n-gram-based contamination reporting by model developers.
