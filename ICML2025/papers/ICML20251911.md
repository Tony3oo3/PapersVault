## Problem

Quantization-aware training (QAT) for LLMs has been shown to be Pareto-optimal only at 8-bit precision for both weights and activations (Kumar et al., 2024), leaving a large gap between hardware-supported 4-bit/1-bit integer arithmetic and achievable training accuracy. Existing QAT methods relying on the Straight-Through Estimator (STE) or learned clipping factors fail to converge stably at very low bit-widths, particularly when both operands are quantized simultaneously.

## Input / Output

The method consumes raw text tokens from the C4 corpus (tokenized with the Llama 2 tokenizer) and produces standard next-token prediction losses during training. During the forward pass, both weight matrices and activation tensors are projected onto 1-8 bit integer grids (INT or FP4), while the backward pass operates in BF16 precision. The trained models are deployed as low-precision (e.g., INT4) decoder-only Llama-2 transformers (30M–800M parameters) that can be executed with custom GPU kernels for 1.2×–3.9× per-layer speedups over BF16.

## Method

QuEST restructures QAT into two coordinated steps. On the forward pass, it applies a Hadamard Transform to both weights and activations to whiten their distributions toward Gaussian, then performs MSE-optimal uniform quantization with a clipping factor α* derived analytically from the N(0,1) distribution (replacing learned normalization). On the backward pass, it replaces STE with a trust gradient estimator: gradients are masked (set to zero) for any entry whose quantization error exceeds T = α*/(2^b − 1), and the mask is applied in the Hadamard domain before an inverse transform propagates gradients back to the standard domain. This decomposition bounds the gradient bias by separating persistent small errors (controlled by distribution fitting) from outlier errors (suppressed by the trust mask), while the Hadamard transform ensures the mask is non-binary in the original domain so no weights are permanently excluded from optimization.

## Results

On C4 validation loss, QuEST achieves 3.272 (W4A4) and 3.945 (W1A1) for a 30M Llama model, outperforming all baselines at every tested bit-width. At 50M parameters, QuEST reaches 3.135 (W4A4) and 3.791 (W1A1), again the best across all methods. Scaling-law fitting shows QuEST's effective parameter efficiency eff(4)=0.70 vs. LSQ's 0.56 at 4-bit, and eff(1)=0.02 for both at 1-bit, confirming 4-bit as the Pareto-optimal precision. Zero-shot evaluation on an 800M model shows QuEST INT4 closely matches BF16 (e.g., 39.18% vs. 39.51% on HellaSWAG, 71.16% vs. 71.65% on PiQA).

## Baselines & Comparisons

QuEST outperforms all four baselines across every bit-width and model size tested. Against STE (the standard straight-through estimator), QuEST reduces W1A1 loss from 5.256 to 3.945 at 30M and from 6.867 to 3.791 at 50M — a gap of over 1.3 nats at the extreme 1-bit regime. Against LSQ (the strongest general-purpose QAT baseline), QuEST consistently wins by 0.04–0.07 nats at W4A4 and by 0.05–0.07 nats at W1A1, with the gap widening at lower bit-widths. Against the QAT extension of QuaRot (which uses AbsMax scaling instead of MSE-optimal distribution fitting), QuEST leads by 0.07 nats at W4A4 (30M) and by a dramatic 0.99 nats at W1A1 (50M: 3.791 vs. 5.007). Against AdaBin, the specialized binary-training method, QuEST achieves a marginal but consistent edge at W1A1 (3.945 vs. 3.988 at 30M; 3.791 vs. 3.843 at 50M). In the scaling-law analysis, QuEST's fitted efficiency eff(4)=0.70 exceeds LSQ's 0.56 by ~25%, confirming the advantage persists at scale.

## Relation to Prior Work

QuEST extends the quantization-aware training (QAT) paradigm established by Jacob et al. (2018) and Esser et al. (2019, LSQ), but departs from prior methods in two key ways: it replaces learned clipping factors (PACT-style) with a Hadamard-transform + MSE-optimal Gaussian fitting step borrowed from PTQ (QuaRot, QuIP#) and distributed optimization, and it replaces the Straight-Through Estimator with a 'trust gradient estimator' that masks gradients for entries whose quantization error exceeds a threshold. The scaling-law analysis builds on Hoffmann et al. (2022) and Frantar et al. (2025), extending the effective-parameter-count framework to quantized training. Kumar et al. (2024) had identified 8-bit as the Pareto-optimal precision for QAT on LLMs; QuEST pushes this frontier to 4-bit and demonstrates stable convergence at 1-bit.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By showing that 4-bit QAT models can Pareto-dominate BF16 models of nearly 4× the size, QuEST challenges the prevailing assumption that low-precision training is inherently lossy and suggests that the fundamental limits of quantized LLM training are much lower than previously thought. The practical GPU kernel support (1.3–3.9× per-layer speedups) makes these results immediately actionable for deployment. Key open questions include whether the 4-bit optimality and 1-bit stability hold at much larger scales (the paper's largest model is 1.6B parameters), whether the approach extends to encoder-decoder or multimodal architectures, and how the method interacts with other compression techniques (e.g., MoE, mixture-of-experts) at scale.
