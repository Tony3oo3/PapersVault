## Problem

LLM-based reward design methods like Eureka suffer from two key limitations: greedy exploitation that discards potentially valuable reward function candidates by only improving from the single best individual, and suboptimal parameter assignment where LLMs directly set reward weights and coefficients without principled optimization. This leads to inefficient exploration of the reward design space and imprecise guidance for policy learning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251354/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a natural-language task description, environment code (exposing variable names and numerical state values), and structured prompts as inputs to GPT-4o. It produces modular reward function code with fine-grained optimized parameters (e.g., weights like 1.5603480339050293 rather than simple integers), a population of rule-based critic functions that output step-wise binary labels (1, 0, -1) for paired trajectory comparison, and a labeled trajectory buffer used for preference-based parameter tuning. Reward functions are decomposed into modules via AST parsing of the returned reward dictionary, enabling module-level recombination.

## Method

R* decomposes reward design into two coupled components. For structure evolution, it maintains a population of 16 modular reward functions, uses LLM reflection to mutate individuals (modify, delete, or add modules), and introduces a module-level crossover operator that inserts a reward module from one high-fitness parent into another, selected via softmax over success rates from a reward function archive. For parameter alignment, it generates a population of 5 LLM-written critic functions that perform step-wise numerical comparisons on paired trajectories; a voting mechanism (relaxing from 5/5 to 3/5 critic agreement) assigns high-confidence labels, and consecutive same-label steps are grouped into segments of length ≥5. These labeled segments are then used to optimize reward function parameters via Bradley-Terry preference loss (70/30 train/validation split, 1000 iterations with early stopping on validation accuracy), aligning the reward function's pairwise trajectory rankings with the collected ground-truth preferences.

## Results

On 8 robotic manipulation tasks (Franka-Cabinet, Swing-Cup, Hand-Over, Hand-Scissors, Allegro-Hand, Door-Open-Outward, Kettle, Pen), R* achieves success rates of 73%–100% under the original Eureka parallel-environment settings, substantially outperforming Eureka (33%–100%) on 5 of 6 reported tasks. Under the reduced 40 GB GPU-memory setting, R* also surpasses the human-designed Oracle reward in most tasks, while sparse rewards fail to produce effective policies in nearly all tasks. In terms of evolutionary efficiency, R* reaches higher best-policy success rates at earlier generations compared to Eureka, indicating faster convergence of the reward design process.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is Eureka (Ma et al., ICLR 2024), the state-of-the-art LLM-based reward design method. R* outperforms Eureka on 5 of 6 tasks with reported numbers: Franka-Cabinet (73% vs. 33%, +40 pp), Swing-Cup (96% vs. 53%, +43 pp), Hand-Over (93% vs. 83%, +10 pp), Kettle (95% vs. 70%, +25 pp), and Door-Open-Outward (100% vs. 98%, +2 pp), while tying on Hand-Scissors (both 100%). R* also surpasses the human-designed Oracle reward in most tasks, indicating that the automated framework can match or exceed expert-level reward engineering. Sparse rewards (binary success signal) fail to guide policy learning effectively in almost all tasks, underscoring the importance of the dense, structured rewards that R* produces. In evolutionary efficiency comparisons (Figure 3), R* consistently reaches higher best-policy success rates at earlier generations than Eureka, confirming that the module-level crossover and parameter alignment optimization accelerate the search for high-quality reward functions.

## Relation to Prior Work

R* extends Eureka (Ma et al., 2024), which pioneered LLM-driven evolutionary reward design, by decoupling the process into two distinct sub-problems: reward structure evolution and parameter alignment optimization. Where Eureka relies on whole-function LLM reflection and lets the LLM set all reward parameters directly, R* introduces modular-based reward functions with a module-level crossover operator for structure search, and a critic-based step-wise voting mechanism combined with Bradley-Terry preference learning for parameter tuning. The work also situates itself within the broader Evolutionary Reinforcement Learning (ERL) literature, specifically the Synergistic Optimization branch, and contrasts with IRL, RLHF, and earlier EA-based reward design methods that depend on human priors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses two concrete limitations of Eureka—greedy exploitation of only the best individual and suboptimal LLM-assigned parameters—yielding reward functions that surpass human-designed baselines across 8 manipulation tasks. The critic-based voting mechanism achieving 100% annotation accuracy (vs. unreliable LLM/VLM comparisons) offers a reusable pattern for automated trajectory labeling. Key open questions include extending to real-robot and vision-only settings where low-level state is inaccessible, and whether the modular crossover approach generalizes beyond manipulation tasks. The finding that increasing crossover individuals from 4 to 8 degrades performance highlights a non-trivial exploration-exploitation tradeoff that practitioners should consider.
