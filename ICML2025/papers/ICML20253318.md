## Problem

Current measurement instruments for evaluating GenAI systems suffer from conceptual confusion, insufficient validity interrogation, and apples-to-oranges comparisons because the ML community typically conflates what is being measured (systematization) with how it is measured (operationalization). The authors argue that evaluating GenAI systems is fundamentally a social science measurement challenge, since the concepts of interest—capabilities, behaviors, and impacts—are abstract, contested, and deeply intertwined with people and society, much like the constructs social scientists have rigorously measured for over fifty years.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a background concept (a broad, contested constellation of meanings related to a GenAI system's capabilities, behaviors, or impacts) and a measurement context as input, and produces a systematized concept (an explicit definition specifying observable phenomena and their relationships), measurement instruments (datasets, classifiers, annotation guidelines, scoring rubrics, aggregation functions), and structured validity evidence. The four-level hierarchy—background concept → systematized concept → measurement instruments → measurements—is linked by four processes (systematization, operationalization, application, interrogation), with validity lenses applied at each level to generate evidence for and against the validity of each component.

## Method

The paper proposes a four-level measurement framework adapted from Adcock & Collier (2001), which explicitly separates systematization (narrowing a background concept into an explicit definition specifying observable phenomena and their interrelationships) from operationalization (developing measurement instruments that instantiate the systematized concept). This separation enables distinct conceptual debates (what is being measured and why) and operational debates (how it is measured), broadening stakeholder participation in the former. Validity is interrogated using seven lenses adapted from Messick (1987)—face, content, convergent, discriminant, predictive, hypothesis, and consequential validity—each constituting a different source of evidence applied throughout both systematization and operationalization. The framework is illustrated through a running example of measuring stereotyping in LLM chatbot outputs and applied to four diverse measurement tasks (stereotyping, mathematical reasoning, memorization, and refusal), demonstrating its generality across measurement approaches including benchmarking, LLM-as-a-judge, and real-world evaluation.

## Results

This is a position paper that proposes a four-level measurement framework (background concept → systematized concept → measurement instruments → measurements) grounded in Adcock & Collier's (2001) measurement theory, linked by four processes: systematization, operationalization, application, and interrogation. The framework recommends seven lenses of validity (face, content, convergent, discriminant, predictive, hypothesis, and consequential validity) adapted from Messick (1987) for interrogating both conceptual and operational debates. The paper illustrates the framework through a running example (measuring stereotyping in LLM chatbot outputs) and a case study on memorization, showing how conflating systematization and operationalization in current ML practice leads to conceptual confusion and insufficient validity interrogation. No quantitative experiments are reported; the contributions are entirely methodological and conceptual.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not perform quantitative comparisons against baselines. Instead, it systematically positions its framework relative to four existing lines of work: (1) psychometrics-inspired benchmark design (Wang et al. 2023b; Alaa et al. 2025; Salaudeen et al. 2025), which the authors view as narrower in scope since it focuses primarily on capability/behavior benchmarks rather than all measurement tasks; (2) Responsible AI literature on fairness measurement (Blodgett et al. 2020, 2021; Jacobs & Wallach 2021), which the authors argue has mostly been used to critique instruments rather than standardize the measurement process; (3) benchmark consolidation efforts (HELM, BIG-bench, DecodingTrust, TrustLLM), which provide standardized conditions but lack a shared measurement process and rigorous validity interrogation; and (4) critiques of existing measurement instruments (Raji et al. 2021; Hutchinson et al. 2022; Roose 2024), which identify problems but do not offer a unifying framework. The authors argue their framework unifies and reinterprets all four lines of work into a single, actionable standard for the measurement process.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies and reinterprets several disparate threads in the ML evaluation literature—critiques of benchmark validity (Raji et al., 2021; Blodgett et al., 2021), psychometrics-inspired benchmark design (Wang et al., 2023b; Alaa et al., 2025), Responsible AI measurement arguments (Jacobs & Wallach, 2021), and benchmark consolidation efforts (HELM, BIG-bench, DecodingTrust)—under a single measurement-theoretic framework adapted from Adcock & Collier (2001) and Messick (1987). Unlike prior work that typically critiques specific instruments or advocates for psychometric rigor in one measurement approach, this paper argues for a general, four-level framework (background concept → systematized concept → measurement instruments → measurements) applicable to all GenAI evaluation tasks regardless of approach. The key departure is the explicit separation of systematization from operationalization, which the authors argue has been conflated throughout the ML community, making it impossible to know precisely what any given instrument measures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete, actionable path for standardizing GenAI evaluation that goes beyond the current 'apples-to-oranges' state, with specific key actions (engage in conceptual debates, interrogate validity, share systematized concepts, share validity evidence) that researchers can adopt at varying levels of comprehensiveness. Open questions include whether the ML community will actually adopt this framework given resource constraints, how to handle the tension between the ideal of full rigor and the practical need for rapid evaluation, and whether partial adoption meaningfully improves the field. The authors candidly note the framework is not a panacea—better measurement does not automatically yield better policy—and that organizational support and incentives will be critical for uptake.
