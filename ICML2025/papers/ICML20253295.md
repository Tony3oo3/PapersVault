## Problem

The open ML model ecosystem (Hugging Face, Kaggle) faces significant supply chain risks—models can be tampered with, replaced with malware, or trained on poisoned data—yet existing protections like model cards are self-reported and unverifiable. While software supply chain solutions such as Sigstore and Certificate Transparency have been deployed for code, no equivalent cryptographic protections exist for ML models, and real attacks (namesquatting, pickle deserialization exploits, leaked API tokens) have already been exploited.

## Input / Output

For model signing, the input is a trained model (single file or directory, with directory contents hashed in alphabetical order by full path) plus the publisher's OIDC identity; the output is a Sigstore bundle containing an ECDSA P256 signature, a short-lived (~10 min) Fulcio certificate, and a Merkle inclusion proof from Rekor. For data provenance, the input is a set of training data elements (individual points or entire datasets) and a queried data point; the output is a zero-knowledge set commitment (intended for storage in an AI BOM) and a succinct (non-)inclusion proof that reveals no information about other training entries. Hashing supports SHA-256 and BLAKE2, with an optimized chunked approach (1 GB default chunks) for files exceeding available RAM.

## Method

The paper proposes two complementary mechanisms built on standardized cryptographic primitives. First, model signing adapts Sigstore's full workflow: the publisher obtains an OIDC token, generates an ephemeral keypair, receives a short-lived certificate from Fulcio binding the key to their verified identity, signs the model hash, and submits to Rekor's transparency log, yielding a verifiable bundle that any consumer can check offline. Second, for training data provenance, they construct a zero-knowledge set that combines a DDH-based VRF (Melara et al., 2015) to randomly permute entry positions in a Patricia Merkle tree accumulator, with Pedersen-style commitments H(D_i, r_i) stored at leaves instead of plain hashes. This design, inspired by SEEMless (Chase et al., 2019) but simplified for static sets, lets the trainer prove a specific data point was or wasn't in the training set while revealing nothing about other entries. The 4500-line Python implementation on top of sigstore-python shows signing overhead of 19% for a 1 TB model and ZKS commit times of ~96 s for 100 million entries, with (non-)inclusion proofs in tens of microseconds.

## Results

For model signing, hashing a 1 TB file using the list-based approach takes 800 s (13.3 min) on the most powerful test machine, representing a 19% overhead relative to the 4,151 s (69 min) cost of saving the file. Additional signing overhead (dominated by the Rekor API call) ranges from 478 ms (35% overhead for small models) to 602 ms (0.03% overhead for the 2.3 TB Llama-3.1-405B), while verification is consistently around 6 ms since it is fully offline. For the zero-knowledge set (dataset verifiability), committing to 100 million entries takes 96 s, whereas proving and verifying (non-)inclusion remains on the order of tens of microseconds even at that scale.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position/systems paper rather than a method-comparison paper, so there is no single head-to-head benchmark table. The authors position their Sigstore-based model signing against Hugging Face's existing GPG commit signing, which has been adopted by fewer than 2% of models on the hub, and against PyPI's former PGP signature support (removed in 2023 due to low adoption and unverifiable signatures). Among modern transparency solutions, they select Sigstore over Sigsum (requires user-managed keys), Notary/Notation (no transparency log, requires key management), and SCITT (mandates COSE format, described as not production-ready), citing Sigstore's maturity, transparency log, and flexibility in key management. For dataset verifiability, the authors contrast their zero-knowledge set construction with full zero-knowledge proofs of training (Choi et al. 2023; Garg et al. 2023; Eisenhofer et al. 2025; Abbaszadeh et al. 2024), noting that those approaches either require revealing the full training dataset or incur prohibitive proof sizes and prover runtimes that do not scale to large models. Their ZKS approach achieves the same (non-)inclusion guarantees with orders-of-magnitude lower query/verify cost (microseconds vs. potentially minutes for ZK proofs) at the cost of only committing to a set of data elements rather than proving the full training process.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts two well-established cryptographic frameworks to the ML model ecosystem: Sigstore (Newman et al., 2022) for model signing and the SEEMless/Parakeet line of work (Chase et al., 2019; Malvai et al., 2023) for zero-knowledge set commitments over training data. It draws a direct analogy to Certificate Transparency for the overall threat model, while departing from prior data provenance work (Longpre et al., 2024; Gebru et al., 2021) by shifting from self-reported documentation to cryptographically verifiable claims. The model integrity component is positioned as more overlooked than data provenance, and the paper explicitly contrasts its approach with proofs-of-training-data methods (Choi et al., 2023; Garg et al., 2023) that require revealing the full dataset, arguing for privacy-preserving alternatives.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses documented, real-world attacks (malware on Hugging Face, namesquatting, the ByteDance training compromise) and provides a deployable open-source implementation benchmarked on models up to 405B parameters, making it one of the first concrete proposals for ML model supply chain security. The critical open question it identifies is ensuring the validity of the data commitment itself—i.e., proving the commitment was computed over the actual training data—which requires either trusted execution environments or scalable zero-knowledge proofs of training that do not yet exist. The paper also cautions that transparency mechanisms risk becoming 'a check-box exercise that provides a false sense of transparency' if not carefully designed, highlighting the tension between verifiability and practicality in a rapidly evolving ecosystem.
