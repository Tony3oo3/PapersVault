## Problem

Rectified Flow (ReFlow) models like FLUX enable fast ODE-based sampling, but their inversion—mapping real images back to structured noise for downstream editing—remains unsolved at practical speed. Existing ReFlow inversion methods either rely on first-order Euler solvers (inaccurate, requiring 28–30 steps) or second-order solvers that double the number of function evaluations per step, negating any step-count reduction. The paper asks whether second-order inversion accuracy can be achieved at the computational cost of a first-order solver.

## Input / Output

The method takes a reference RGB image (tested at 512×512 and 1024×1024) and a text prompt as input, and produces three types of output: structured noise (inversion), a pixel-level reconstruction of the source image, and a semantically edited image. A key operational detail is that during inversion, the V features of self-attention layers at the final step are cached to GPU memory and later injected into the denoising pass for editing, so the editing pipeline consumes both the inverted noise and these stored attention features. The entire pipeline is training-free and requires no auxiliary model.

## Method

FireFlow proposes a modified midpoint ODE solver that reuses the previous step's midpoint velocity approximation (loaded from memory) instead of computing a fresh one, reducing each step to a single model forward pass—matching Euler's NFE while retaining O(Δt³) local truncation error. The theoretical justification rests on the observation that well-trained ReFlow models learn nearly constant velocity fields, so the approximation error of the reused velocity is provably bounded by O(Δt) (Proposition 4.1), which preserves the second-order global error of the standard midpoint method (Theorem 4.2). For semantic editing, the method follows the RF-Solver strategy of replacing V features in MMDiT self-attention during denoising with pre-stored V features from inversion, but achieves comparable or superior results in only 8 steps (18 NFE total) versus 15–30 steps for baselines, yielding a 3× wall-clock speedup on an RTX 3090.

## Results

On PIE-Bench (700 images, 10 edit types), FireFlow with 8 steps and 18 NFE achieves a structure distance of 0.0271, PSNR of 23.03, SSIM of 0.8249, and CLIP similarity of 26.02 (whole) / 22.81 (edited), outperforming RF-Solver (15 steps, 60 NFE) and RF-Inversion (28 steps, 56 NFE) on all preservation metrics while using only 30% of the NFE. For inversion-reconstruction on DCI, FireFlow at 8 steps/18 NFE achieves LPIPS 0.4111 and PSNR 16.01, beating RF-Solver (LPIPS 0.5010, PSNR 14.72) and RF-Inversion (LPIPS 0.7574, PSNR 13.37) at comparable budgets. On MSCOCO T2I, FireFlow (10 steps, 11 NFE) reaches FID 25.16 and CLIP 31.42, surpassing FLUX-dev (FID 26.77) and RF-Solver (FID 25.93). Inference time is 7.70 s per image at 512×512, a 3.09× speedup over vanilla ReFlow (23.76 s).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FireFlow outperforms all diffusion-model-based editing baselines (Prompt-to-Prompt, Pix2Pix-Zero, MasaCtrl, PnP, PnP-Inv) on PSNR, SSIM, and CLIP similarity while using only 18 NFE versus their 100 NFE; PnP-Inv retains a slight edge on structure distance (0.0243 vs. 0.0271) but lags on all other metrics. Against the two ReFlow-specific baselines, FireFlow beats RF-Inversion (Rout et al., 2025) decisively on every metric (e.g., SSIM 0.8249 vs. 0.7192, PSNR 23.03 vs. 20.82) with 3× fewer NFE. Compared to RF-Solver (Wang et al., 2024), FireFlow achieves lower structure distance (0.0271 vs. 0.0311), higher PSNR (23.03 vs. 22.90), higher SSIM (0.8249 vs. 0.8190), and higher whole-image CLIP (26.02 vs. 26.00), while RF-Solver edges ahead on edited-region CLIP (22.88 vs. 22.81); critically, FireFlow uses 18 NFE versus RF-Solver's 60 NFE. For reconstruction on DCI, FireFlow at 8 steps/18 NFE achieves LPIPS 0.4111 and PSNR 16.01, substantially outperforming RF-Inversion (LPIPS 0.7574, PSNR 13.37) and vanilla ReFlow inversion (LPIPS 0.8145, PSNR 15.29) at the same NFE budget. Add-it (Tewel et al., 2025) is compared only in Table 1 on solver properties (30 steps, 60 NFE, O(Δt²) local error, requires auxiliary model) rather than in the editing benchmark; FireFlow matches its second-order error with 8 steps, 18 NFE, and no auxiliary model. DDIM inversion (Song et al., 2021) is included as a reconstruction baseline in Table 5 (LPIPS 0.2342, PSNR 19.72 at 50 steps/100 NFE); FireFlow at 30 steps/62 NFE achieves LPIPS 0.1579 and PSNR 23.87, surpassing DDIM with fewer steps.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Rectified Flow framework (Liu et al., 2023) and the editing strategy from RF-Solver (Wang et al., 2024), which replaces V-features in self-attention during denoising with those stored during inversion. Its key departure is a re-interpretation of the midpoint ODE solver: rather than computing a fresh midpoint velocity (costing 2 NFEs per step), it reuses the previous step's midpoint velocity, exploiting the near-constant velocity dynamics that well-trained ReFlow models learn. This positions FireFlow as a solver-level contribution orthogonal to the editing strategy, contrasting with RF-Inversion's optimal-control formulation and RF-Solver's Taylor-expansion approach, both of which require more steps or NFEs to achieve comparable accuracy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 3× speedup for FLUX-based inversion and editing (8 steps, 18 NFEs vs. 28 steps, 56 NFEs) is practically significant for real-time and interactive editing applications, and the training-free, solver-only nature of the method makes it a drop-in replacement for any ReFlow pipeline. However, the paper acknowledges that the simple V-replacement editing strategy fails on color changes and uncommon scenes, and that adding K-features to self-attention resolves these at the cost of reduced structural preservation—an open trade-off that limits the method's generality. Broader open questions include whether the velocity-reuse trick extends to other flow-based architectures beyond ReFlow, and whether more principled attention manipulation can close the gap between editing fidelity and preservation without requiring auxiliary models.
