## Problem

Existing video LLMs sample frames at low rates (≤2 FPS), effectively treating each 1-second clip as a handful of static images. This discards rapidly changing visual cues such as body movements, micro-expressions, and fast motion, severely limiting comprehension of dynamic scenes. No prior work has explored processing videos at high frame rates (16 FPS) within a multimodal LLM framework.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251417/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

F-16 takes a video sampled at 16 FPS (up to 1,760 frames for 110-second clips; longer videos are uniformly subsampled to 1,760 frames) and a text prompt. Each frame is encoded by a frozen SigLIP image encoder, and frames are grouped into 16-frame (1-second) processing windows. The model outputs a free-form textual response, supporting both multiple-choice QA and open-ended video captioning. A training-free variable-frame-rate decoding scheme allows inference at lower FPS (e.g., 8 FPS) by repeating each sampled frame k times to match the aligner's expected input dimension.

## Method

The core contribution is a high-frame-rate aligner: a 3-layer MLP (two linear layers with GELU) that concatenates the visual features of all 16 frames in a 1-second window along the feature dimension, then compresses them into a single set of visual tokens per window via a spatial 2×2 max-pooling step (≈4× token reduction). To transfer knowledge from a pre-trained image LLM (LLaVA-OneVision with SigLIP + Qwen2-7B), the aligner's weight matrices are initialized via block-matrix decomposition of the image LLM's single-frame aligner, so that at initialization the aligner output equals the average of per-frame representations. The aligner and LLM are trained jointly (encoder frozen) on general video data, followed by LoRA fine-tuning on high-speed sports datasets (gymnastics, diving, basketball, football). A key empirical finding is that post-pooling (applying max-pool after the aligner) substantially outperforms pre-pooling at 16 FPS, confirming that the aligner relies on fine-grained inter-frame feature variations to extract motion information.

## Results

F-16-7B achieves state-of-the-art performance among 7B-parameter video LLMs on Video-MME (65.0% avg accuracy), NeXT-QA (84.1%), TemporalBench (37.2% multiple binary accuracy), and MotionBench (54.5%), surpassing the previous 7B SOTA by up to 13.5% on TemporalBench. On high-speed sports tasks, F-16 (16 FPS) outperforms GPT-4o and Gemini-1.5-Pro on NBA (92.9% F1 vs. 76.7% and 80.6%) and SoccerNet (57.7% vs. 36.8% and 43.1%), while also achieving 64.1% on gymnastics and 86.5% on diving where both proprietary models fail entirely. The variable-frame-rate decoding method preserves most performance at lower test FPS (e.g., 63.9% Video-MME at 8 FPS vs. 65.0% at 16 FPS) without retraining.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among 7B open-source models, F-16 outperforms LLaVA-Video-7B (63.3% Video-MME, 83.2% NQA) and NVILA-7B (64.2% Video-MME, 82.2% NQA) on general video QA, and dramatically surpasses LLaVA-OV-7B (58.2% Video-MME, 21.2% TPB) and Qwen2-VL-7B (63.3% Video-MME, 24.7% TPB) on fine-grained temporal benchmarks. On TemporalBench, F-16's 37.2% exceeds the next-best 7B model (Qwen2-VL-7B at 24.7%) by 12.5 points, and on MotionBench its 54.5% leads all 7B baselines. Against proprietary models, F-16 trails GPT-4o (71.9%) and Gemini-1.5-Pro (75.0%) on Video-MME overall but matches or exceeds them on TemporalBench (37.2% vs. 38.0% and 26.6%) and MotionBench (54.5% vs. 33.0% and 51.0%). On high-speed sports, F-16 at 16 FPS decisively beats both GPT-4o and Gemini-1.5-Pro on NBA (92.9% F1) and SoccerNet (57.7% accuracy), while the proprietary models cannot answer gymnastics or diving questions at all. F-16 shows a slight drop on long-video benchmarks (MLVU 70.3 vs. 70.8 for LLaVA-Video-7B; LVB 57.6 vs. 58.2), attributed to sparser effective sampling over longer durations.

## Relation to Prior Work

F-16 extends the LLaVA-OneVision image LLM into a video model by replacing its single-frame aligner with a high-frame-rate aligner that processes 16 frames per 1-second window through a 3-layer MLP, initialized via block matrix decomposition from the pre-trained image aligner. This departs from the dominant paradigm in video LLMs (Video-LLaVA, Qwen2-VL, LLaVA-Video) that samples at ≤2 FPS and treats each frame independently, and from temporal compression approaches (NVILA, Espresso, RLT) that focus on reducing token count for long videos rather than increasing temporal resolution. The variable-frame-rate decoding method is a training-free inference technique with no direct prior in the video LLM literature, though it is conceptually related to frame interpolation and adaptive sampling strategies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By demonstrating that a 16 FPS video LLM with a simple MLP aligner can outperform much larger proprietary models (GPT-4o, Gemini-1.5-Pro) on fine-grained temporal and sports tasks, the paper shifts the field's attention from model scaling to temporal resolution as a key axis for video understanding. The variable-frame-rate decoding provides a practical path to deploying high-frame-rate models without prohibitive inference costs, making the approach accessible. Open questions include whether the 16 FPS ceiling can be pushed further, how the approach interacts with longer video contexts (where F-16 already shows a slight drop), and whether the block matrix initialization strategy generalizes to other base architectures beyond LLaVA-OneVision.
