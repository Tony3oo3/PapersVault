## Problem

Standard instruction-tuned LLMs lack the ability to explicitly think before responding, and while Chain-of-Thought prompting helps on math and logic tasks, meta-analyses show it is unhelpful or even harmful for general instruction following. Training a model to think is further complicated by the absence of supervised thought data: existing post-training datasets contain only responses or response preferences, with no information on internal thought processes. The paper asks whether an existing LLM can be converted into a Thinking LLM for broad instruction following without any additional human-annotated thought data.

## Input / Output

The model takes a user instruction as text input and produces a two-part autoregressive output: a thought segment followed by a response segment, separated by fixed keyword markers (e.g., 'Here is my thought process:' / 'Here is my response:'). Only the response segment is shown to the end user; the thought segment is internal and can contain drafts, self-evaluations, planning, or mistakes. At inference time the full token sequence is post-processed by simple string matching to extract and return only the response part.

## Method

Thought Preference Optimization (TPO) iteratively trains an instruction-tuned LLM (Llama-3-8B-Instruct) to generate useful internal thoughts without any supervised thought data. At each iteration, the current model is prompted with a thought template (generic or specific draft-and-evaluate format) to sample K=8 thought+response pairs per instruction. A judge model (ArmoRM or STE) scores only the response parts—never the thoughts—so thought quality is optimized implicitly through its effect on response quality. The highest- and lowest-scoring full outputs (thought + response) form a DPO preference pair, and the model is updated via the DPO loss. A length-control mechanism normalizes and penalizes response length in the judge scores to prevent verbosity drift across iterations. Over up to 4 iterations, the model learns to produce shorter, more targeted thoughts that improve response quality on general instruction-following benchmarks (AlpacaEval, Arena-Hard), with gains observed even in non-reasoning categories like marketing, health, and creative writing.

## Results

TPO achieves a 52.5% length-controlled win rate on AlpacaEval and 37.3% on Arena-Hard, outperforming the direct-response baseline (48.4% and 33.0% respectively) by +4.1% and +4.3%. This represents a +27.6% improvement over the untrained Llama-3-8B-Instruct seed model (24.9% AlpacaEval). Notably, the 8B TPO model surpasses GPT-4 (06/13) on AlpacaEval (52.5% vs. 30.2%) and is competitive with much larger models like Llama-3-70B-Instruct (46.6%) and Mistral Large (37.7%) on Arena-Hard. Fine-grained evaluation on 20 instruction categories reveals that thinking benefits not only reasoning tasks but also non-reasoning categories such as marketing, health, and general knowledge.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is TPO against a direct-response baseline trained identically (same Llama-3-8B-Instruct seed, same judge, same data, same DPO loss) but without the thought-generation step. TPO wins on both benchmarks: +4.1% on AlpacaEval (52.5% vs. 48.4%) and +4.3% on Arena-Hard (37.3% vs. 33.0%). Against the untrained seed model, TPO gains +27.6% on AlpacaEval, while simply prompting the seed model to write thoughts (without any training) actually degrades performance to 17.3%, confirming that thought generation must be optimized. On Arena-Hard, TPO (8B) reaches 37.3%, matching GPT-4 (06/13) at 37.9% and Mistral Large at 37.7%, while on AlpacaEval it substantially outperforms all listed larger models except Llama-3-70B-Instruct (46.6%) and Qwen2 72B (38.1%). The paper also notes that TPO's math performance on GSM8K degrades (from 79.2% to as low as 10.2% in answer-only mode), indicating the general instruction-following training setup is not suited for math-specific tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the RLAIF/DPO training paradigm (Bai et al., 2022; Rafailov et al., 2024) to the setting of teaching LLMs to generate internal thoughts before responding, without any human-annotated thought data. It departs from prior 'training to think' methods like STaR (Zelikman et al., 2022), Quiet-STaR, and IRPO (Pang et al., 2024), which rely on correctness labels or gold answers, by instead using a response-only judge model to implicitly optimize thoughts through preference pairs. It also contrasts with self-correction and self-refinement approaches (Kumar et al., 2024b; Snell et al., 2024) that directly supervise the thought process, whereas TPO lets the model discover its own thinking strategy. The key conceptual departure from standard CoT prompting (Wei et al., 2022) is that thoughts are hidden from the user and optimized for general instruction following rather than being visible reasoning steps for math/logic tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that trained thinking improves performance on non-reasoning categories (marketing, health, general knowledge, language) challenges the prevailing view from Sprague et al. (2024) that CoT is only useful for math and logic, suggesting a broader role for internal computation in LLMs. However, the evidence is limited to a single 8B model, the fine-grained gains come from a self-constructed benchmark, and math performance degrades significantly under the training setup. Open questions include whether the approach scales to larger models, whether thought length can be made steerable for cost control, and whether incorporating math-specific training data and judges would resolve the observed math degradation.
