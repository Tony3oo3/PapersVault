## Problem

Most inverse optimization literature learns only the objective function, assuming constraints are known, yet this objective-only approach cannot capture discontinuous or switched decision policies (e.g., in power dispatch). Learning the constraint function instead leads to nonconvex training programs that are difficult to solve, and existing constraint-learning methods either face intractability or impose restrictive assumptions (single observations, feasibility-only data, or linear programming only).

## Input / Output

The method takes N input-decision pairs (s_i, x_i) where s_i is a signal vector (e.g., generator costs and regional demands) and x_i is the observed optimal decision, along with a known linear objective c(s)ᵀx. It produces constraint parameters θ = ({A_k, b_k}_{k=0}^K) that define the feasible region via a latent-variable mapping x = A_θ(s)z + b_θ(s) with z constrained to a conic primitive set Z. The learned model then generates optimal decisions for unseen signals, and performance is evaluated via both sample-based and true predictability/suboptimality losses.

## Method

The paper introduces a hypothesis class g_θ(x,s) = min_{z∈Z} ‖x − A_θ(s)z − b_θ(s)‖ where the conic primitive set Z (simplex, ball, box, etc.) controls the shape of the feasible region and A_θ(s) performs scaling, rotation, and projection. Two loss functions are proposed: a predictability loss that shifts x to the nearest feasible-and-optimal point, and a suboptimality loss that independently penalizes infeasibility and suboptimality via slack variables. For linear objectives, the training problem admits exact reformulations as a convex program (when A_θ(s) is restricted to a scalar) or a MILP (when z or A is binary, using McCormick inequalities). For the general bilinear case, a block coordinate descent algorithm alternates gradient updates on {A_k} with inner optimization over {z_i}, augmented with an adaptive Nesterov smoothing technique that progressively tightens relaxation penalties. A key theoretical insight is that the suboptimality loss is exactly the Nesterov-smoothed counterpart of the predictability loss, explaining its superior numerical stability under vanilla gradient descent.

## Results

On the 5-node power system (Problem 1), the proposed Adaptive Smoothing Algorithm 2 with p=9 achieves the best out-of-sample predictability loss of 1.58 and suboptimality loss of 0.05, substantially outperforming Gurobi's non-convex solver (6.66 and 4.25 respectively) and the quadratic-cost baseline (2.58 and 2.56). The convex reformulation is 3 orders of magnitude faster (0.31s vs. ~600s) but yields worse solution quality (predictability loss 3.36). On the network structure recovery task (Problem 2), both loss formulations achieve zero training loss, and the method solves the MILP in 23.5s versus an estimated ~2700s for the Aswani et al. enumeration approach.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against four baselines: (1) the Aswani et al. (2018) enumeration method for constraint learning, (2) a quadratic-cost objective-learning approach, (3) the paper's own convex reformulation (restricted hypothesis class), and (4) Gurobi's non-convex quadratic solver on the same bilinear formulation. Algorithm 2 decisively outperforms Gurobi across all dimensions of the primitive set: at p=9, the predictability loss drops from 6.66 (Gurobi) to 1.58 (Alg 2), and the suboptimality loss drops from 4.25 to 0.04, while Gurobi hits its 1800s time limit in every case. Against the quadratic-cost baseline, the constraint-learning approach is superior on both losses (1.58 vs. 2.58 predictability; 0.05 vs. 2.56 suboptimality), confirming that learning feasible regions captures discontinuous policies that objective-only learning cannot. The convex reformulation is the fastest (0.31s) but sacrifices solution quality (predictability loss 3.36 vs. 1.58). On the network structure recovery task, the proposed MILP formulation solves in 23.5s versus an estimated 2.64×2^10 ≈ 2700s for Aswani et al.'s exhaustive enumeration, while both achieve zero loss.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the inverse optimization literature, which has predominantly focused on learning objective functions (Keshavarz et al., 2011; Bertsimas et al., 2015; Mohajerin Esfahani et al., 2018), to the underexplored problem of learning feasible regions. Prior constraint-learning IO work (Aswani et al., 2018; Tan et al., 2020; Chan & Kaw, 2020; Ghobadi & Mahmoudzadeh, 2020) either estimated parameters within a fixed constraint structure or faced intractability, whereas this paper introduces a fundamentally different hypothesis class that parameterizes the feasible region as a conic-primitive mapping with latent variables, enabling rotation, scaling, projection, and translation. The two proposed losses (predictability and suboptimality) generalize earlier formulations by jointly penalizing infeasibility and suboptimality, and the key theoretical contribution is showing that the suboptimality loss is a Nesterov-smoothed counterpart of the predictability loss, which explains its superior numerical behavior under vanilla gradient descent.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The hypothesis class's ability to capture discontinuous input-decision mappings (demonstrated in the power dispatch example) addresses a fundamental limitation of objective-only IO, where the induced policy is necessarily smooth. The adaptive smoothing algorithm provides a practical path to solving the nonconvex training problem, and the convex/MILP reformulations for restricted cases offer tractable alternatives for large-scale applications. Key limitations include the restriction to linear objectives, reliance on local search for the general hypothesis class, and the non-trivial choice of primitive set that governs policy expressiveness. Open questions include extending to quadratic or nonlinear objectives, characterizing the primitive set–policy relationship more formally, and identifying additional structural assumptions that yield globally solvable reformulations.
