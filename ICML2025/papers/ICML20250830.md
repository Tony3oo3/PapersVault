## Problem

Asynchronous time series (irregularly spaced events with natural language descriptions) have traditionally been modeled by Temporal Point Processes that require a small fixed set of event categories, discard textual event descriptions, and are difficult to extend beyond forecasting. While LLMs have been applied to regular (evenly-spaced, numerical) time series, their use for textual asynchronous time series across multiple tasks—forecasting, anomaly detection, and imputation—remains unexplored.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250830/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a comma-separated sequence of (inter_arrival_time, event_description) tuples, where event descriptions are free-form natural language strings (e.g., 'carry_bowl', 'GolfSwing') or integer indices for non-textual TPP benchmarks, and inter-arrival times are in milliseconds. The output is always a single Python tuple: for forecasting, the predicted next (time, event); for imputation, the filled-in missing element; for anomaly detection, the identified out-of-place element. The prompt follows a system-user-assistant structure, with the system prompt specifying the task and dataset description, and the user prompt containing the event sequence.

## Method

LASTS encodes asynchronous time series as natural language tuples within a structured LLM prompt, preserving event semantics that traditional TPP methods discard. The key training innovation is Stochastic Soft Prompting (StoP): a continuous soft prompt of length L is prepended to the LASTS representation, but during each training batch only a random prefix of length l (drawn uniformly from [1, L]) is used for both forward and backward passes, while the full prompt is used at inference. This coarse-to-fine training regime—inspired by Matryoshka Representations and stochastic depth—forces early prompt tokens to encode broad, task-level information and later tokens to refine predictions, yielding more diverse and generalizable representations than standard soft prompting. The Llama-3-8B-Instruct backbone is entirely frozen; only the ~1.6M soft prompt parameters (0.02% of the model) are trained via next-token prediction loss, making StoP more parameter-efficient and ~25% faster to train than QLoRA while achieving 12.69% average Macro-F1 improvement over standard soft prompting.

## Results

On the three textual datasets (Breakfast, MultiTHUMOS, EPIC-KITCHENS), LASTS + StoP achieves the best Macro-F1 across all tasks: 0.2633/0.3947/0.0797 for forecasting, 0.2064/0.2213/0.0610 for imputation, and 0.7198/0.6045/0.6603 for anomaly detection. Against the strongest LLM-based baselines (LLMTime, LLMProcesses), LASTS Zero Shot alone already outperforms them on Macro-F1 for forecasting (e.g., 0.0604 vs. 0.0240 on Breakfast) and by a wide margin on EPIC-KITCHENS (0.0105 vs. 0.0040–0.0049). Against TPP models on five standard benchmarks, LASTS + StoP wins 13 of 18 event-type/time evaluations and is top-2 on 17 of 18, with the best Macro-F1 on all event-type predictions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the time-series foundation model Chronos, LASTS Zero Shot outperforms it on every Macro-F1 evaluation (e.g., 0.0604 vs. 0.0011 on Breakfast forecasting) and on most MAE evaluations, confirming that textual event descriptions are far more informative than the numerical encodings Chronos requires. Against LLM-based time-series methods LLMTime and LLMProcesses, LASTS Zero Shot wins on Macro-F1 across all three datasets for forecasting and imputation, with the largest gaps on EPIC-KITCHENS (~2–3× higher M-F1), while LLMTime/LLMProcesses occasionally match or slightly beat LASTS on MAE for MultiTHUMOS forecasting. Against the five TPP baselines (RMTPP, NHP, SAHP, THP, AttNHP) on the five standard TPP benchmarks, LASTS + StoP achieves the best Macro-F1 on all event-type predictions and wins 13 of 18 total evaluations, but lags on time prediction (RMSE) for three datasets (Amazon, Taobao, StackOverflow) where TPP models' explicit time-distribution priors give them an edge. Notably, all five TPP models either diverge, perform poorly, or hit out-of-memory errors on the three textual datasets (Breakfast, MultiTHUMOS, EPIC-KITCHENS), whereas LASTS + StoP handles them natively. Among the PEFT techniques compared internally, StoP yields an average 12.69% Macro-F1 improvement over vanilla Soft Prompting and 13.55% over QLoRA across all tasks and datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends soft prompt tuning (Lester et al., 2021) with a stochastic prefix-selection mechanism (StoP) inspired by Matryoshka Representations and dropout, while departing from prior LLM-for-time-series work (Gruver et al., 2023; Jin et al., 2024) that encodes observations as numeric strings. For asynchronous time series specifically, the paper positions itself against the dominant Temporal Point Process paradigm (Hawkes processes, neural TPPs like RMTPP, NHP, SAHP, THP), arguing that those methods cannot handle large or open-ended event vocabularies, lose natural language semantics, and are difficult to extend beyond forecasting. The LASTS prompt representation is the key conceptual bridge: it retains event descriptions as natural language so that an LLM's world knowledge can be leveraged, rather than reducing events to small categorical indices.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that TPP models simply fail (OOM, divergence) on datasets with hundreds to tens of thousands of event types, while the LLM-based approach handles them with only ~1.6M trainable parameters (0.02% of the backbone). The StoP mechanism's coarse-to-fine structure and the finding that all prefixes remain valid standalone prompts suggest a broadly applicable PEFT technique beyond time series. Key limitations include weaker time prediction than TPP models (no explicit inter-arrival time distribution), evaluation on only eight datasets, and reliance on a single LLM family (Llama-3). Open questions include whether hybrid architectures combining TPP time modeling with LLM event modeling could close the time-prediction gap, and whether StoP's benefits transfer to other modalities or task families.
