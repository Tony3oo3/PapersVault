## Problem

Prior LLM-for-research benchmarks either focus on implementation-heavy tasks (code writing, model training) or on highly subjective activities (idea generation) that resist quantitative evaluation. There is no systematic benchmark targeting the expertise-intensive, daily research activities—verifying equations, designing experiments, and identifying paper weaknesses—that require deep domain knowledge and expert-level reasoning, and that can be assessed with clear input-output specifications.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251623/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each task consumes LaTeX source (not parsed PDF text) to avoid parsing noise: EQINFER takes ~2,000 surrounding words plus one equation and outputs a binary correctness label; EXPDESIGN takes the pre-experiment paper context plus figures and outputs a paired list of experiment steps and one-to-one motivation explanations; WEAKNESS takes the full paper text (parsed via VILA) plus extracted figure/table images and outputs a list of reviewer-style weaknesses. Because WEAKNESS inputs average ~9,800 words, a split-combine strategy (processing 2,000–3,000-word windows then merging) is used, and EXPDESIGN explanations are generated with oracle experiments to prevent error propagation from the design step.

## Method

AAAR-1.0 decomposes research assistance into three standalone tasks with curated, expert-annotated ground truth: 1,049 equation pairs (positive from peer-reviewed ACL papers, negatives synthesized by GPT-4 and filtered for context alignment and compilation correctness by senior PhD students), 100 experiment plans annotated by 10 senior researchers through multi-round peer discussion, and 993 weakness lists extracted verbatim from ICLR 2023 reviewer comments. The evaluation introduces task-specific metrics beyond ROUGE: LLM-as-judge entailment scores (En-Precision/En-Recall) for experiment lists, SentenceBERT-based S-Match for explanation quality, and a novel ITF-IDF diversity metric for weaknesses that penalizes both intra-paper redundancy (informativeness) and cross-paper genericity (specificity). The benchmark is designed so that even human experts require substantial research accumulation to perform well, making it a genuine test of domain expertise rather than surface-level language modeling.

## Results

On EQINFER, the best model (o3-mini) achieves only 47.98% F1, barely above the 40% all-positive baseline, while most open-source LLMs (OLMo-7B at 13.64%, Mistral-7B at 28.45%) fall well below chance. On EXPDESIGN, the top models (o1-preview, o3-mini) reach ~30% En-F1 and ~58.5% S-Match for explanation, indicating LLMs consistently miss ground-truth experiments and generate many novel but often trivial ones. On WEAKNESS, Gemini 1.5 Pro leads with 48.75% S-F1, but the ITF-IDF diversity metric reveals a large gap: human reviewers score 7.69 versus at most 5.95 for GPT-4o, showing LLM-generated weaknesses remain vague and broadly applicable rather than paper-specific.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Closed-source LLMs consistently outperform open-source LLMs across all three tasks: on EQINFER, o3-mini (47.98% F1) and Gemini 1.5 Pro (46.74%) lead, while the best open-source model Mixtral-8x22B (40.90%) barely exceeds the naive all-positive baseline and its extreme recall (93.80%) with low precision (26.15%) suggests it is effectively predicting all-positive. On EXPDESIGN, o1-preview and o3-mini achieve the highest En-F1 (~30.1%) and S-Match (58.55% and 54.01% respectively), while open-source models like OLMo-7B trail at 14.80% En-F1. On WEAKNESS, Gemini 1.5 Pro (48.75% S-F1) and o1-preview (48.62%) edge out GPT-4o (47.73%), but all LLMs fall far short of human reviewer diversity (ITF-IDF 7.69 vs. ≤5.95). Notably, the AI-SCI agent framework (Lu et al., 2024) underperforms its backbone GPT-4o on WEAKNESS (45.05% vs. 47.73% S-F1; 2.23 vs. 5.95 ITF-IDF), indicating that advanced prompting techniques like self-reflection do not adequately address the weakness-identification challenge. InternVL2-26B, the only open-source multimodal model tested, performs comparably to or worse than text-only models in figure/table ablations, suggesting current LMMs cannot effectively leverage scientific figures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by shifting the LLM-for-research evaluation paradigm from end-to-end pipelines (AI-Scientist, MLAgentBench, MLE-Bench) to individual, expertise-intensive cognitive tasks that mirror a researcher's daily workload. While prior work has demonstrated LLMs' ability to generate research ideas (Si et al., 2024) or summarize papers (Du et al., 2024), no prior benchmark systematically evaluates LLMs on the granular reasoning steps—equation verification, experiment design, and weakness identification—that underpin those higher-level activities. The paper also departs from the task-chain evaluation style by isolating each step with clear input-output expectations, enabling transparent assessment of intermediate model outputs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

AAAR-1.0 provides the first rigorous, reproducible evaluation of LLMs on expertise-intensive research tasks, revealing that even frontier models (o3-mini at ~48% F1) barely exceed chance on equation inference and that LLM-generated weaknesses remain substantially less specific and diverse than human reviews (ITF-IDF gap of ~2 points). The finding that multimodal inputs (figures, tables) fail to improve—and sometimes degrade—performance challenges the assumption that richer scientific context automatically helps LLMs. Open questions include whether longer training on scientific corpora or specialized reasoning architectures can close the expertise gap, and how to design LMMs that effectively leverage information-dense scientific figures and tables.
