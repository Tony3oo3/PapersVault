## Problem

Adaptive optimizers like Adam require tracking exponential moving average states (first and second moments), inflating memory to 2–3× the model footprint and constraining scalability in distributed LLM training. While SGD is fully stateless and memory-optimal, it underperforms Adam on LLM pre-training. The paper asks whether a stateless optimizer can match Adam's performance by pre-processing instantaneous gradients with matrix-level operations rather than accumulating history.

## Input / Output

At each step the optimizer receives the raw stochastic gradient matrix G ∈ ℝ^{m×n} (m ≤ n, corresponding to input and output dimensions of a linear layer) and the current weight matrix W ∈ ℝ^{m×n}, and produces an updated W^{(t+1)} = W^{(t)} − η·ΔW^{(t)}. No optimizer state is stored between steps; the only per-step computation is a chain of two matrix operators on G. The GradWhitening step is applied to all linear modules in attention and MLP blocks, and the NSDS variant reduces the whitening cost from O(m³) to O(m²) by replacing one matrix product in each Newton-Schulz iteration with its diagonal approximation.

## Method

SWAN composes two stateless operators applied to the instantaneous gradient: (1) GradNorm, a row-wise RMS normalization (equivalent to RMS-Norm without learnable parameters applied to backward gradients) that stabilizes the covariance structure of stochastic gradients across time, and (2) GradWhitening, which computes (GGᵀ)^{−1/2}G via Newton-Schulz iterations, orthogonalizing the gradient matrix and approximating a non-diagonal second-order update under a Kronecker-factored Hessian assumption (H = I⊗H̃). A rescaling step restores the Frobenius norm of the whitened gradient to √(mn), preventing the orthogonalization from collapsing the effective learning rate. Theoretically, GradNorm removes time-heterogeneity in gradient covariance for a simplified transformer block (Theorem 1), while GradWhitening yields convergence rates robust to the local condition number (Theorem 2). The proposed NSDS scheme further accelerates whitening to O(m²) per step, enabling competitive throughput without model parallelization.

## Results

On the memory-efficient LLaMA pre-training benchmark (C4, effective batch size 130K tokens), SWAN† achieves the best validation perplexity across all model sizes: 30.59 (60M), 22.61 (130M), 16.63 (350M), and 13.56 (1.3B), compared to Adam's 33.02, 24.44, 19.24, and 16.44 respectively. SWAN† requires only 2.98 GB of total memory for the 1.3B model versus Adam's 7.48 GB (~50% reduction), while achieving >2.3× and >2.4× token-efficiency speedups on the 350M and 1.3B models. Even the untuned SWAN-0 variant (no warmup, Adam-like learning rates) surpasses Adam on all four model sizes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SWAN‡ outperforms Adam on all four model sizes in both perplexity and memory: e.g., on the 1.3B model it reaches 13.56 PPL at 2.98 GB versus Adam's 16.44 PPL at 7.48 GB, while also achieving >2.4× token-efficiency speedup. Against the closest stateless competitor Apollo-mini (same 2.98 GB memory), SWAN‡ wins on every size (13.56 vs 14.17 on 1.3B; 16.63 vs 17.18 on 350M), and also beats full Apollo (13.56 vs 14.20 on 1.3B) at lower memory (2.98 GB vs 4.43 GB). SWAN‡ dominates Momentum+GradWhitening (a Muon-like baseline) across all sizes (e.g., 13.56 vs 16.08 on 1.3B) at substantially lower memory (2.98 GB vs 5.23 GB). Against low-rank methods, SWAN‡ achieves far better perplexity than Galore (13.56 vs 15.64 on 1.3B), LoRA (13.56 vs 19.21), ReLoRA (13.56 vs 18.33), and Low-Rank factorization (13.56 vs 142.53), while using less or comparable memory. Notably, even the deliberately untuned SWAN-0 variant (no warmup, Adam-like LR) beats Adam on all sizes, demonstrating robustness to hyperparameter choices.

## Relation to Prior Work

SWAN composes two well-known matrix operators—row-wise RMS-Norm (Zhang & Sennrich, 2019) and decorrelated batch normalization's whitening step (Huang et al., 2018; Song et al., 2022)—applied to instantaneous stochastic gradients, rather than to historical EMA states as in Adam. This departs from the broader line of memory-efficient optimizers (Muon, Apollo, Galore, Adam-mini) that progressively reduce but never eliminate optimizer states, by showing that matrix-level preprocessing of the raw gradient is sufficient to recover Adam's key properties (gradient invariance, smoothing, and whitening) without any temporal memory. The theoretical grounding draws on the Simplified Transformer Block dynamics (Tian et al., 2023) to justify why these specific operators align with transformer gradient structure, and the NSDS scheme (O(m²) diagonal-substitution Newton-Schulz) is a novel computational contribution for scalable whitening.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SWAN provides the first empirical evidence that a fully stateless optimizer can match or exceed Adam on LLM pre-training, with ~50% total memory reduction and 2× token efficiency at 350M–1.3B scale. This opens a fundamentally different design axis for optimizers: exploiting structural redundancies in transformer gradient geometry (shared Hessian blocks, row-wise covariance stability) rather than accumulating historical statistics. Key open questions include whether the O(m²) NSDS scheme and the 2× speedup hold at frontier scale (70B+), whether the STB-based theoretical guarantees transfer to real multi-layer architectures, and how SWAN interacts with other training regimes (long context, multi-task, RLHF). The authors explicitly acknowledge the gap between their 1.3B/C4 benchmark and industry-scale training.
