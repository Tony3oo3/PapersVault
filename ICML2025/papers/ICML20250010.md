## Problem

Existing scaling laws for language models treat total parameter count as a proxy for compute, but Mixture-of-Experts architectures decouple parameters from FLOPs per example through sparsity, leaving the optimal trade-off between these two dimensions uncharacterized. This paper asks whether scaling laws can be drawn for the optimal sparsity level in MoEs under fixed training compute and parameter constraints, and how that optimum shifts with model size and compute budget.

## Input / Output

The method consumes total parameter count N, sparsity level S (ratio of inactive to total experts, ranging 0–98%), training compute budget C in FLOPs (3e19–1e21), and dataset size D. It outputs a predicted pretraining loss via a fitted polynomial IsoFLOP surface, from which optimal sparsity S*, optimal total parameters N*, and optimal active parameters N_a* are derived as argmin solutions. The fitting operates on log-transformed variables (log N and −log(1−S)) with a degree-(2,2,2) polynomial including the N×S interaction term, and the proposed parametric scaling law (Eq. 6) augments the Kaplan et al. form with two sparsity-dependent terms c/(1−S)^λ and d/((1−S)^δ N^γ).

## Method

The paper conducts a large-scale empirical study training sparse MoE transformers (Megablocks with GLU experts, top-k routing, z-router loss, and QK-norm) across sparsity levels 0–98%, multiple model sizes, and five compute budgets on RedPajamaV1. They fit 3D IsoFLOP surfaces mapping (N, S) → L via polynomial regression, then decompose the joint optimization into two sub-problems: (i) optimal N for fixed S, revealing N* increases while N_a* decreases as sparsity grows, and (ii) optimal S for fixed N, revealing a parabolic profile where S* rises with N and converges to 1 for sufficiently large models. They propose a parametric scaling law extending Kaplan et al. with sparsity terms, fitted via L-BFGS with Huber loss, achieving R²=99% on training data and 68% on held-out S=0.98 points. Downstream evaluation across four task categories (language understanding, commonsense reasoning, reading comprehension, world knowledge) shows sparsity does not alter the upstream–downstream loss correlation for most tasks, but denser models outperform sparser ones on reading comprehension; a length-controlled CoT experiment on GSM8k further demonstrates that MoEs benefit more from increased inference-time compute than dense models at matched active parameters.

## Results

The fitted IsoFLOP surface (polynomial in log N, log S, and their interaction) achieves an MSE of 0.0001 on held-out pretraining loss predictions. The proposed parametric scaling law (Equation 6) achieves R² = 99% on fitting data and R² = 68% on the held-out validation set (S = 0.98 models), with MSE of 0.00056 (fit) and 0.0058 (validation). Key empirical findings: for a fixed compute budget, pretraining loss decreases monotonically with sparsity; the optimal total parameter count N* increases with sparsity while the optimal active parameter count Na* decreases; and the optimal sparsity S* increases with model size, converging to 1.0 for sufficiently large models. In CoT experiments on GSM8k, the 14B dense model improves from 20% to 70% accuracy as generated answer length increases, while the 5×2.7B MoE exhibits a steeper performance-per-word slope than expected from dense model interpolation, suggesting MoEs benefit more from increased inference-time compute.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical scaling-law study rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so no external baselines are benchmarked. Internally, the paper compares models across sparsity levels (0%, 25%, 50%, 75%, 90%, 95%, 98%), total parameter counts, and training compute budgets (3×10¹⁹ to 1×10²¹ FLOPs). The central finding is that under a fixed compute budget, sparser MoEs consistently achieve lower pretraining loss than denser models of the same total size, with the benefit persisting across all compute budgets tested (no diminishing returns observed). On downstream tasks, language understanding, world knowledge, and commonsense reasoning show tight upstream–downstream correlation regardless of sparsity, while reading comprehension tasks (SQuAD, CoQA) favor denser models at matched perplexity, likely due to higher inference-time FLOPs. The 6NaD FLOPs estimator is validated against a detailed per-module FLOP calculation (Equation 9), confirming the ratio stays close to 1 across all sparsity levels and model sizes, supporting the use of active parameters as a FLOPs proxy in MoE scaling laws.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the IsoFLOP methodology of Hoffmann et al. (2022) from 2D curves (model size vs. loss at fixed compute) to a 3D surface that jointly incorporates MoE sparsity, and generalizes the Kaplan et al. (2020) parametric scaling law by adding sparsity-dependent terms. It builds directly on Ludziejewski et al. (2024), who introduced granularity and showed MoEs outperform dense models across compute budgets, but those prior MoE scaling studies typically held sparsity or active expert count fixed. The key departure is treating sparsity as a first-class optimization variable alongside total parameters, asking explicitly how to trade off N and FLOPs per example under a fixed compute budget.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides actionable design guidance for MoE practitioners: under a fixed compute budget, increasing sparsity while expanding total parameters consistently lowers pretraining loss, and the optimal sparsity converges to 1 for sufficiently large models. However, the finding that denser models outperform perplexity-matched sparse MoEs on reading comprehension tasks, and that MoEs benefit disproportionately from CoT-style inference-time compute, highlights an open question about how to best allocate inference FLOPs for reasoning-heavy workloads. Memory and communication overheads are not modeled, and whether these scaling relationships hold at much larger scales or with different sparsity mechanisms (e.g., weight sparsity, parameter sharing enabling 'negative sparsity') remains to be established.
