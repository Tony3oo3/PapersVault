## Problem

Fairness-aware learning methods impose fairness constraints during training but lack formal guarantees that the achieved fairness generalizes to unseen data. While prediction overfitting is well-studied, the analogous phenomenon of 'fairness overfitting'—the gap between training and test fairness—has received little theoretical attention, and existing bounds (e.g., KL-based) are not computable for modern deep networks. This paper provides the first information-theoretic framework for bounding fairness generalization error that is both tight and practically estimable.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a training set of (feature, sensitive attribute, label) tuples, the learned model weights W, and a tunable subset-size hyperparameter m ∈ {2,…,n} that controls how many samples are used to decompose the group-based fairness loss. It produces a hierarchy of upper bounds on the expected fairness generalization error, expressed in terms of mutual information I(W; V_u) or conditional mutual information I_{z[2n]}(ΔL_u^{Φ_u}; R_{u1}). A notable structural property is that for Demographic Parity, the MI bound is label-independent (involving only V=(X,T), not Y), and the sensitivity term is governed by a shifted harmonic mean of group sizes, making the bound explicitly sensitive to group imbalance.

## Method

The core technical contribution is a variance-based bounding technique built on the Efron-Stein inequality (Lemma 2) that replaces the traditional sub-Gaussian or boundedness assumptions used in standard information-theoretic generalization bounds. Because fairness losses are inherently group-dependent (requiring multiple samples per group rather than being an average over individual samples), the paper establishes a bounded-differences condition (Lemma 3) linking the sensitivity of the fairness loss to individual sample perturbations with group sizes via a shifted harmonic mean. This variance bound is then combined with Hoeffding's lemma to tightly control the log-moment generating function inside Donsker-Varadhan's variational representation of KL divergence, yielding the MI bound (Theorem 1). In the CMI super-sample setting, the same machinery produces a hierarchy of increasingly tight bounds—CMI, f-CMI, e-CMI, and ΔL-CMI (Theorems 2–5)—where the final ΔL-CMI bound reduces both the selection variable and the loss to one-dimensional quantities via an XOR-based loss-difference construction, making it computable for deep networks. The framework extends naturally to Equalized Odds (Theorems 6–7) and multiclass settings by adapting the bounded-differences condition to the joint (T, Y) subgroup structure.

## Results

The paper's primary contribution is theoretical: it derives tight information-theoretic bounds (via MI and CMI) for fairness generalization error under Demographic Parity and Equalized Odds. Empirically, on the COMPAS dataset, the proposed ΔL-CMI bound (Theorem 5) for DP and the EO bound (Theorem 7) closely track the observed fairness generalization error across training set sizes from 250 to 2500 samples, with a strong linear correlation confirmed in scatter plots. The proposed batch-balancing technique reduces test DP by up to an order of magnitude (e.g., HSIC drops from 0.098 to 0.008 at n=2500 on COMPAS/gender; DiffDP from 0.026 to 0.003), consistently outperforming unbalanced training across all fairness methods and both sensitive attributes (gender and race).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates five in-processing fairness algorithms—HSIC (Baharlouei et al., 2020), DiffDP (Mroueh et al., 2021), PRemover (Kamishima et al., 2012), DiffOdd, and DiffEopp—against standard ERM on COMPAS and Adult. All fairness methods exhibit fairness overfitting (a gap between training and test fairness error), with PRemover showing the worst generalization (test DP ~0.13–0.15 on COMPAS, barely improving with more data) while DiffDP achieves the best test DP among DP methods. The proposed batch-balancing strategy consistently reduces test DP across every method: HSIC benefits most dramatically (e.g., 0.098→0.008 at n=2500, gender), DiffDP also improves substantially (0.026→0.003), while PRemover sees a more modest reduction (0.136→0.126), suggesting PRemover's overfitting is driven by factors beyond group imbalance. The theoretical bounds (Theorems 5 and 7) are validated as tight, tracking the observed fairness generalization error closely across all methods and sample sizes, unlike prior KL-based bounds (Oneto et al., 2020) which are not computable for deep networks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the information-theoretic generalization bounds lineage (Xu & Raginsky 2017; Harutyunyan et al. 2021; Hellström & Durisi 2022; Wang & Mao 2023) to the specific challenge of fairness losses, which are group-dependent and cannot be decomposed into per-sample terms. It builds on Dong et al. (2024)'s permutation-based decomposition for non-pointwise losses but replaces their reliance on boundedness/sub-Gaussianity with a novel Efron-Stein variance bound (Lemma 2) that captures the sensitivity structure of fairness metrics. In the fairness-specific literature, it generalizes beyond the KL-based bounds of Oneto et al. (2020b) (which are intractable for deep networks) and the sample-complexity-only analyses of Woodworth et al. (2017) and Agarwal et al. (2018), providing computable MI and CMI bounds for both Demographic Parity and Equalized Odds.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first general information-theoretic framework for fairness generalization error that is both tight and computable for modern deep networks, filling a gap where prior guarantees were either intractable or restricted to specific algorithms. The derived batch-balancing technique (reducing DP by up to 10×) demonstrates direct practical utility from the theoretical analysis. Open questions include extending to non-binary or continuous sensitive attributes, other group-based non-i.i.d. loss structures, and whether the bounds can guide adaptive algorithm design beyond the simple balancing heuristic. The framework's reliance on estimating CMI between loss differences and selection variables remains computationally nontrivial for very large m.
