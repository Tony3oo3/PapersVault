## Problem

While backdoor attacks on CLIP have been demonstrated, there is no systematic understanding of which internal model components (attention heads vs. MLPs, which layers) are actually infected by different attack types. This lack of mechanistic insight limits the design of targeted, low-cost defenses that preserve clean accuracy.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250894/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates at inference time on a pre-trained (or fine-tuned) backdoored CLIP with ViT-B/32 backbone. It consumes test images and a small set of clean validation images (20% of the validation split) to construct head-specific prototypes. For the repair task, it outputs a modified image representation that yields a class label; for the detection task, it outputs a binary flag indicating whether the input is a backdoor sample, based on the count of heavily-infected attention heads exceeding a threshold ζ.

## Method

The paper decomposes CLIP's image representation into per-attention-head and per-MLP contributions using the residual structure of ViTs, then applies mean-ablation to identify which components carry backdoor information. Key findings: local patch-based attacks (BadNet, BadCLIP) concentrate in the last layer's attention heads, while global perturbation attacks (Blended, ISSBA) spread across MLPs in the last five layers, and not all heads in the last layer are infected. For defense, Decomp-Rep constructs head-specific prototypes from clean validation data, selects heavily-infected heads per image via a cosine-similarity threshold ε, and replaces their representations with the prototypes (or directly mean-ablates the last five MLPs for global attacks). Decomp-Det instead counts the number of selected infected heads per image and flags samples exceeding threshold ζ as backdoor samples, achieving AUROC 0.944 on average versus 0.704–0.826 for prior detectors.

## Results

On ImageNet-1K, Decomp-Rep reduces the ASR of BadCLIP (a state-of-the-art attack) from 99.78% to 0.94% while maintaining CACC at 56.08% (vs. 60.73% baseline). For global perturbation attacks, Blended ASR drops from 99.56% to 0.47% and ISSBA from 70.12% to 6.33%. For the detection task, Decomp-Det achieves an average AUROC of 0.944 across ImageNet-1K, Caltech-101, and Oxford Pets, substantially outperforming all three detection baselines. The method is also plug-and-play with CleanCLIP, further reducing BadNet ASR from 54.23% to 41.49%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Base-Decomp (Gandelsman et al., 2024), the original representation decomposing provides virtually no defense: it barely changes ASR (e.g., BadNet 86.09→88.58, BadCLIP 99.78→99.59) while slightly reducing CACC. Decomp-Rep dramatically outperforms it by selectively repairing only heavily-infected attention heads, reducing BadCLIP ASR from 99.78% to 0.94% and Blended ASR from 99.56% to 0.47%. Against CleanCLIP (Bansal et al., 2023), Decomp-Rep acts as a plug-and-play improvement: stacking it on top of CleanCLIP further reduces BadNet ASR from 54.23% to 41.49% and BadCLIP ASR from 69.03% to 37.09%. For the detection task, Decomp-Det achieves an average AUROC of 0.944, beating STRIP (0.826) by 0.118, SCALE-UP (0.704) by 0.220, and TeCo (0.757) by 0.187, with the largest margin against the strongest attack (BadCLIP: 0.990 vs. 0.637 for TeCo).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the representation decomposition and mean-ablation framework from Gandelsman et al. (ICLR 2024), originally designed for interpretability, as a tool for backdoor defense in CLIP. Prior backdoor defense work on CLIP operates at the training stage (CleanCLIP, RoCLIP), the input stage (trigger inversion via TIJO), or the output stage (STRIP, TeCo), but none inspect internal ViT components. The paper's conceptual departure is treating the ViT's attention heads and MLPs as independently inspectable units whose representations can be selectively repaired or used as detection signals, bridging the interpretability and security literatures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The mechanistic finding that local patch triggers concentrate in last-layer AHs while global noise triggers disperse across late-layer MLPs gives practitioners a principled basis for targeted, low-cost defenses that preserve clean accuracy far better than blanket ablation. The plug-and-play nature of Decomp-Rep (improving even on top of CleanCLIP) makes it immediately deployable. Key limitations include reliance on clean validation data for prototype construction, evaluation only on ViT-B/32, and the acknowledged gap that representation decomposition ignores indirect cross-layer effects. Open questions include robustness to adaptive attacks that specifically avoid the detected AHs, generalization to larger ViT variants or non-ViT CLIP encoders, and whether the AH/MLP split holds for text-side backdoors.
