## Problem

Existing learnware specification methods (RKME, LANE) rely solely on distribution alignment to characterize a model's specialties, capturing only the training data's feature distribution while ignoring the model's inherent discriminative performance. This omission results in specifications that inadequately represent what the model can actually discriminate, degrading the accuracy of learnware identification and the effectiveness of model reuse in the learnware dock system.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250329/cropped_page7_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

At the submitting stage, the method takes a well-trained model f and its private training dataset D (n samples, K classes) and produces a specification R of Km reduced points (m per class, Km << n), each carrying a class label. The specification is initialized by random sampling from D and then optimized in-place. At the deploying stage, a user requirement dataset is similarly converted into a requirement specification, which is matched against stored learnware specifications via quadratic programming (for learnware ranking) and the Hungarian algorithm (for class-to-class assignment), ultimately returning reusable models per requirement class.

## Method

DALI generates specifications by jointly minimizing two alignment losses over numerous randomly initialized ConvNetBN networks. The discriminative alignment term uses H-discrepancy (Konstantinov & Lampert, 2019) in the pseudo-label space: it measures the worst-case cross-entropy loss gap between training data (labeled by the model's own predictions) and the specification across random networks, encoding the model's discriminative ability. The distribution alignment term computes per-class MMD in the random neural embedding space between true-label training data and the specification, preserving the mastered feature distribution. The specification points are optimized via Adam while the random networks remain frozen, and the paper provides Rademacher-complexity-based upper bounds for both loss components. The dual mechanism ensures the specification simultaneously mirrors the model's discriminative performance and its class feature geometry, yielding superior learnware identification (100% Pre@1 in homogeneous settings) and higher specification quality than distribution-only baselines.

## Results

On the homogeneous label space setting, DALI achieves 100% Pre@1 and Pre@2 for both K×5 and K×10 specification sizes, matching RKME-W and outperforming RKME (50.00%/40.91% Pre@1) and LANE (90.91%/95.94% Pre@1). On the more challenging heterogeneous label space setting, DALI (K×5) achieves 68.18% Pre@1 and 72.73% Pre@2, surpassing RKME (45.46%/59.10%), RKME-W (63.64%/63.64%), and LANE (63.64%/63.64%). In the mixed task setting, DALI achieves 100% superclass accuracy and 94.05% specification quality at K×10, outperforming LANE's 90.23% quality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against RKME, DALI achieves a substantial improvement in both settings: 100% vs. 50.00% Pre@1 (homogeneous) and 68.18% vs. 45.46% Pre@1 (heterogeneous), confirming that distribution-only alignment is insufficient for adequate specialty characterization. Against RKME-W, DALI matches performance in the homogeneous setting (both reach 100% Pre@1) but gains a 4.54-point Pre@1 advantage in the heterogeneous setting (68.18% vs. 63.64%), showing that discriminative alignment helps when label spaces differ. Against LANE (the closest baseline, which also uses neural embeddings), DALI improves Pre@1 by 9.09 points in the homogeneous setting (100% vs. 90.91% at K×5) and by 4.54 points in the heterogeneous setting (68.18% vs. 63.64% at K×5). In the mixed task setting, DALI matches LANE's 100% superclass accuracy but achieves higher specification quality (94.05% vs. 90.23% at K×10), indicating that the dual alignment produces more discriminative class-level specifications. The ablation study further confirms that both components contribute: removing discriminative alignment drops Pre@1 to 59.09% (heterogeneous), while removing distribution alignment drops it to 63.64%, versus 68.18% with both.

## Relation to Prior Work

DALI extends the learnware specification literature, which has progressed from RKME (kernel mean embedding in RKHS) to LANE (neural embedding space with class-aware MMD alignment). All prior methods rely solely on distribution alignment—matching the specification's feature distribution to the training data's—while ignoring the model's discriminative performance. DALI introduces a second alignment axis, discriminative alignment, built on H-discrepancy (Konstantinov & Lampert, 2019) evaluated over ensembles of randomly initialized neural networks, using pseudo-labels from the well-trained model to encode its inherent class-separability. This positions DALI as the first specification method to jointly optimize both distributional and discriminative fidelity in a single objective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The learnware paradigm aims to make model reuse practical at scale, and specification quality directly governs whether the dock system can correctly match user requirements to useful models. By showing that distribution alignment alone under-characterizes model specialties, DALI provides a principled recipe for richer specifications that should improve identification accuracy, particularly in heterogeneous label-space and mixed-task settings. However, the empirical gains are modest (a few percentage points over LANE in the hardest setting), the theoretical proofs rely on simplifying assumptions (two-layer networks, single-class analysis), and the experiments are confined to two mid-scale image datasets with 11 domains—scalability to larger, more diverse learnware collections remains untested. Open questions include whether the random-network ensemble size and architecture choices generalize, and whether the approach extends to non-vision modalities or generative models.
