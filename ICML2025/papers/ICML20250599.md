## Problem

State-of-the-art concept-based models (CBMs) that use bypass mechanisms to overcome concept incompleteness suffer from a previously unexplored failure mode called leakage poisoning: when inputs go out-of-distribution, the leaked information in the bottleneck itself becomes OOD, causing concept interventions to fail to improve task accuracy. This undermines the real-world usability of these interpretable models, since interventions are precisely the mechanism by which experts help the model handle distribution shifts.

## Input / Output

The model takes RGB images of varying resolution (299×299 for CUB, 224×224 for AwA2, 64×64 for CelebA, 32×32 for CIFAR-10) processed by a pretrained ResNet-18/34 backbone, along with optional expert concept interventions at test time that override individual concept probabilities. It outputs a task class label, per-concept probability scores (calibrated via Platt scaling), and a k×m-dimensional concept bottleneck. At inference, the model samples M=50 bottleneck realizations via Monte Carlo Dropout over residual embeddings and averages predictions, making the output stochastic by design.

## Method

MixCEM decomposes each concept's embedding into a global (sample-agnostic) pair c̄_i^(+), c̄_i^(−) and a residual (sample-specific) pair r_i^(±)(x) computed by linear projections of the backbone features. The key mechanism is an entropy-based gating: the residual is scaled by (1 − H(p̂_i)), where H is the Bernoulli entropy of the predicted concept probability, so that when a sample is OOD and concept predictions become uncertain, the residual contribution is automatically suppressed, preventing leakage poisoning. The training objective includes a 'prior error' term L_task(y, f(c̄)) that maximizes task accuracy using only global embeddings mixed with ground-truth concept labels, ensuring the global embeddings encode sufficient task information even when all leakage is blocked. Non-deterministic fallbacks—randomly intervening on concepts with probability p_int=0.25 and zeroing residuals with probability p_drop during training—further improve intervenability and robustness, while at inference the model averages over M=50 bottleneck samples to mitigate overfitting and residual poisoning.

## Results

On task accuracy, MixCEM is competitive with the best baselines across all six tasks, achieving 76.54% on CUB (vs. CEM's 76.67%), 74.54% on CUB-Incomplete (vs. CEM's 74.42%), and 88.68% on AwA2-Incomplete (vs. CEM's 90.12%), with drops of at most ~2% relative to the best-performing CM. Its mean concept ROC-AUC is slightly below the best in four tasks but remains within ~2% (e.g., 88.00 on CUB vs. Hybrid CBM's 94.53, 93.35 on AwA2 vs. CEM's 94.84). The headline advantage appears in OOD intervention accuracy: MixCEM achieves up to ~48% higher OOD intervention accuracy than CEM and ~41% higher than IntCEM on AwA2-Incomplete, and is the only completeness-agnostic baseline whose OOD intervention curves remain bounded by the Bayes Classifier across nearly all tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CEM (the strongest completeness-agnostic baseline), MixCEM trades a small task-accuracy margin (e.g., 76.54 vs. 76.67 on CUB, 88.68 vs. 90.12 on AwA2-Incomplete) for dramatically superior OOD intervention performance, achieving up to ~48% higher OOD intervention accuracy on AwA2-Incomplete. Against IntCEM, MixCEM matches or slightly exceeds ID intervention accuracy without requiring expensive training-time sampling, while substantially outperforming it on OOD interventions (up to ~41% improvement on AwA2-Incomplete). Against Vanilla CBM, MixCEM is far superior on concept-incomplete tasks (74.54% vs. 56.46% on CUB-Incomplete), confirming completeness-agnosticism, whereas Vanilla CBM's OOD intervention curves converge to the same points as ID (no leakage poisoning but also no incompleteness gap closure). Against Hybrid CBM, MixCEM is competitive on task accuracy but lags on concept ROC-AUC for CUB (88.00 vs. 94.53); however, Hybrid CBM is not properly intervenable, making it unsuitable for the human-in-the-loop setting this paper targets. Against ProbCBM and P-CBM/Residual P-CBM, MixCEM is consistently better on concept-incomplete tasks and OOD interventions, while ProbCBM retains a narrow edge on unintervened OOD accuracy in the concept-complete TravelingBirds setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Concept Bottleneck Model (CBM) lineage from Koh et al. (2020) and the Concept Embedding Model (CEM) architecture from Espinosa Zarlenga et al. (2022), which introduced dynamic high-dimensional concept embeddings to overcome the incompleteness gap. It departs from this line by identifying that the very bypass mechanisms (residual channels, dynamic embeddings) that enable completeness-agnosticism become a liability under distribution shifts, coining the term 'leakage poisoning.' The proposed MixCEM draws on IntCEM's intervention-aware training, ProbCBM's global embedding structure, and OOD detection's entropy-based uncertainty signals (Hendrycks & Gimpel, 2016) to gate residual information flow, while its prior-error loss term provides a principled MLE derivation from a probabilistic graphical model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of leakage poisoning reveals a fundamental tension in CM design: the information bypasses needed for completeness-agnosticism actively undermine intervenability under OOD shifts, casting doubt on the real-world deployability of current state-of-the-art CMs. The formal property of bounded intervenability provides a computable lower bound for post-intervention accuracy that prior work had overlooked. Open questions remain: MixCEM cannot block leakage when a concept itself becomes incomprehensible under shift (no expert can intervene), it does not always recover full ID performance in incomplete tasks under OOD, and it requires more parameters and hyperparameters than CEMs. Extending the embedding decomposition to other architectures (e.g., ECBMs) and devising mechanisms to block unwanted leakage without requiring concept labels are natural next steps.
