## Problem

Self-supervised learning methods based on joint embedding architectures produce representations with strong hierarchical clustering properties, yet these properties have remained largely untapped for improving SSL itself. The paper addresses three questions: which component of the JEA (encoding, embedding, or projector hidden layers) exhibits the most stable and superior clustering, how to leverage that clustering to create a positive-feedback learning loop, and whether doing so further enhances clustering at both fine-grained and coarse-grained levels.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252654/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR, 224×224 for ImageNet) and produces two augmented views per sample: one with weak augmentation (ResizedCrop + HorizontalFlip only) used for clustering, and one with standard augmentation. The encoder outputs an encoding H ∈ R^{d_e×m} that serves as the downstream representation, while the projector outputs an embedding Z ∈ R^{d_g×m} used only during pretraining. The key intermediate output is a doubly stochastic self-assignment matrix A_H ∈ R^{m×m} computed via Sinkhorn-Knopp on the encoding's cosine self-similarity, which acts as soft clustering targets for the loss.

## Method

ReSA (Representation Self-Assignment) creates a positive-feedback SSL loop by computing online self-clustering on the encoder's output (encoding) and using the resulting assignment to guide the loss on the projector's output (embedding). Specifically, L2-normalized encodings are used to form a cosine self-similarity matrix, which is then passed through the Sinkhorn-Knopp algorithm (3 iterations, ε=0.05) to yield a doubly stochastic assignment matrix A_H. This matrix replaces the identity matrix in the InfoNCE-style cross-entropy loss, so that the gradient for every sample pair (i,j) becomes (1/τ)(P_{i,j} − A_H^{(i,j)}) rather than the rigid positive/negative split of contrastive learning. This eliminates the harmful repulsion of same-class negatives and adapts the optimization to the model's own learned cluster structure. The method uses a momentum network, a standard 3-layer MLP projector (2048 hidden, 512 output), and a higher temperature (τ=0.4) than typical contrastive methods, reflecting its tolerance for a broader range of samples.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, ReSA achieves 93.53% linear and 93.02% k-nn accuracy, surpassing the best baselines (INTL at 92.60%/90.03%, MoCoV3 at 93.10%/89.47%). On CIFAR-100, ReSA reaches 72.21% linear and 66.83% k-nn, with a particularly large k-nn margin of ~5% over INTL (61.90%) and ~7% over Barlow Twins (59.40%). On ImageNet with ResNet-50, ReSA attains 75.2% linear accuracy at 800 epochs (batch 1024), exceeding SwAV (71.8%), MoCoV3 (73.8%), BYOL (74.3%), and INTL (73.3%), and notably surpasses 800-epoch SimCLR/SwAV/SimSiam with only 100 epochs of training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ReSA outperforms all 13 baselines across every dataset and both evaluation protocols (linear and k-nn). The k-nn gap is the most striking: on CIFAR-100, ReSA's 66.83% exceeds INTL (61.90%) by nearly 5 points and Barlow Twins (59.40%) by over 7 points, reflecting superior clustering structure. On ImageNet (Table 2), ReSA at 100 epochs (71.9%, batch 256) already surpasses SimCLR, SwAV, and SimSiam trained for 800 epochs, and at 800 epochs reaches 75.2%—a level that SwAV and DINO only match with the multi-crop trick. Among the closest competitors, INTL (the authors' prior work) is consistently outperformed by 0.5–1.0% on linear and 1–5% on k-nn. On COCO detection/segmentation (Table 5), ReSA leads all baselines by 0.2–1.3 AP points, and on fine-grained CUB-200-2011 (Table 6), ReSA's k-nn accuracy of 56.5% (k=5) exceeds the next best (MoCoV3 at 46.8%) by nearly 10 points, underscoring its advantage in capturing fine-grained structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

ReSA extends SwAV's online self-clustering mechanism by replacing prototype-based assignment on embeddings with a self-similarity computation directly on encodings, eliminating the need for learnable prototypes and reducing the Sinkhorn-Knopp calls from multiple (one per view) to a single execution. Conceptually, it operationalizes the empirical finding from Ben-Shaul et al. (2023) that SSL representations exhibit hierarchical clustering structure, closing the loop so that the model's own clustering properties guide its training in a positive-feedback manner. The gradient analysis positions ReSA as a soft-assignment generalization of InfoNCE, where the identity matrix (hard positive/negative distinction) is replaced by the doubly stochastic self-assignment matrix, thereby avoiding the known pathology of pushing same-class negatives apart.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The well-evidenced finding that encodings consistently outperform embeddings in clustering quality across diverse SSL methods provides a principled justification for where to extract clustering signals in JEA-based training, a question the community has lacked clear guidance on. The practical impact is notable: ReSA's simplicity (no prototypes, single Sinkhorn call, weak augmentation) combined with large performance and efficiency gains (100-epoch ReSA matching 800-epoch SimCLR/SwAV/SimSiam) makes it an attractive pretraining recipe. Open questions include whether the approach scales to very large datasets without any hand-crafted augmentations (the paper speculates this aligns with recent augmentation-free SSL work), and whether the positive-feedback mechanism generalizes beyond vision to other modalities or non-JEA architectures.
