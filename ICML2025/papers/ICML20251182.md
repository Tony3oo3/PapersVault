## Problem

Existing AVSR systems have not kept pace with model scalability seen in ASR or LLMs, as simply increasing model size disproportionately enhances audio semantic understanding without similarly improving visual processing. Moreover, fixed expert activation strategies (hard routing) lack the adaptability needed when noise conditions and modality reliability vary across inputs, making it difficult to scale AVSR capacity without incurring prohibitive computational costs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251182/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes synchronized audio speech signals and video frames of a speaker's lip movements, processed as 500-frame audio-visual sequences with 50 text tokens. During training, 25% of audio is corrupted with noise (babble, speech, music, or natural) at SNR sampled from N(0,5), and either audio or video is randomly dropped with 25% probability to encourage modality-aware expert specialization. Outputs are transcribed text for AVSR or English translations for X-to-English AVS2TT, evaluated across 8 non-English languages on MuAViC and English on LRS3.

## Method

MoHAVE replaces every FFN layer in the AV-HuBERT decoder with a sparse MoE module containing 8 experts split into two modality-specific groups (4 audio, 4 visual), activating only top-1 expert per group per token. A two-layer hierarchical gating mechanism orchestrates routing: an inter-modal router (a learned linear projection) assigns dynamic weights to the audio and visual expert groups, while intra-modal routers select the single best expert within each activated group. A novel load biasing loss trains the inter-modal router to preferentially route audio-only sequences to audio experts and video-only sequences to visual experts, while allowing free adaptation for audio-visual inputs. This enables the model to shift reliance toward visual experts under high auditory noise and toward audio experts in cleaner conditions, achieving 1B total parameters with only 553M activated (LARGE) while matching or exceeding the performance of dense 477M AV-HuBERT-LARGE.

## Results

On the noisy LRS3 benchmark, MoHAVE-BASE (189M activated / 359M total params) achieves an N-WER of 5.8% and C-WER of 1.8%, while MoHAVE-LARGE (553M activated / 1.0B total) reaches N-WER 4.5% and C-WER 1.5%. MoHAVE-BASE matches the performance of the much larger AV-HuBERT-LARGE (477M params, N-WER 5.7%) while activating only 189M parameters. When combined with cross-modal attention (CMA), MoHAVE achieves a state-of-the-art N-WER of 4.2% on the noisy LRS3 benchmark, outperforming all prior methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MoHAVE-LARGE (N-WER 4.5%) outperforms AV-HuBERT-LARGE (5.7%) by 1.2 points and the hard-routing MoE variant (4.8%) by 0.3 points, while activating only 553M of its 1.0B parameters. Against prior SOTA methods on the noisy LRS3 benchmark (Table 3), MoHAVE with CMA achieves N-WER 4.2%, beating CMA alone (4.6%), UniVPM (5.2%), MIR-GAN (5.6%), and AV-HuBERT (5.7%); it also improves BRAVEn from 11.0% to 9.8%. On the multilingual MuAViC benchmark under babble noise, MoHAVE-LARGE achieves 37.4% average WER and 19.5% average BLEU, outperforming the re-implemented mAV-HuBERT (38.9% WER, 18.5% BLEU) and XLAVS-R2B (41.2% WER, 18.7% BLEU), while Whisper large-v2 (170.8% WER) fails catastrophically due to its lack of visual input. In clean environments (Table 7), MoHAVE-LARGE (29.2% WER) is competitive with but does not significantly surpass mAV-HuBERT (29.3%) or XLAVS-R2B (26.4%), confirming that its advantage is primarily in noisy conditions. On unimodal tasks (Table 9), MoHAVE-LARGE slightly improves VSR over AV-HuBERT (28.2% vs. 28.6%) but does not match specialized VSR models like BRAVEn (26.6%) or Llama-3.1-8B-AVSR (25.3%), as its design targets multimodal robustness rather than unimodal specialization.

## Relation to Prior Work

MoHAVE extends the sparsely-gated MoE framework (Shazeer et al., 2017) and the hierarchical mixture-of-experts concept (Jordan & Jacobs, 1994) to the audio-visual speech recognition domain, building on the AV-HuBERT encoder (Shi et al., 2022). It departs from prior multimodal MoE work (V/T-MoE, Uni-Perceiver-MoE) that uses static hard routing—manually activating modality-specific expert groups—by introducing a learned inter-modal router that dynamically weights audio versus visual expert groups per token. The novel load biasing loss bridges the gap between fully learned routing (which fails to specialize groups) and hard routing (which lacks adaptability), training the inter-modal router to develop modality-aware group preferences without explicit activation constraints.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MoHAVE addresses a concrete scalability gap: AVSR models have stagnated below 0.5B parameters while ASR has scaled to 1.6B+ (Whisper), partly because naively increasing AVSR model size disproportionately benefits audio over visual processing. The hierarchical gating provides a principled mechanism to maintain balanced modality utilization at scale, and the empirical finding that optimal audio/visual weighting varies by noise type (e.g., 0.3 audio weight under babble vs. ≥0.5 under natural noise) validates the need for dynamic routing. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes beyond speech to other audio-visual tasks, whether the language-dependent routing patterns observed in multilingual settings can be exploited explicitly, and whether the decoder-only MoE placement advantage holds at even larger scales. The honest negative result—that MoHAVE provides no benefit in clean environments—clarifies that the contribution is specifically a robustness mechanism rather than a general capacity improvement.
