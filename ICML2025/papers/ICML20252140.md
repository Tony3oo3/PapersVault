## Problem

Existing musical version matching approaches learn and evaluate at the full-track level, yet real-world applications require matching short segments (10–20 s) where ground-truth labels are only available per track. This mismatch between track-level supervision and segment-level inference causes dramatic performance degradation, and prior methods rely on classification or triplet losses while ignoring more recent contrastive objectives that could better exploit the few-items-per-class structure of version datasets.

## Input / Output

The method consumes 16 kHz mono audio, from which 2.5-minute blocks are randomly sampled and split into eight non-overlapping 20-second segments (the last 10-second remainder is repeat-padded). Each segment is converted to a constant-Q spectrogram (20 ms hop, 7 octaves, 12 bins/octave, time-downsampled by 5) and fed through the network to produce a 1024-dimensional embedding. At inference, overlapping segments with a 5 s hop are extracted at variable query lengths (5–90 s), and pairwise dimension-normalized Euclidean distances are reduced to track-level scores for retrieval ranking.

## Method

CLEWS introduces two coupled contributions. First, a family of segment-distance reduction functions—most notably bpwr-r (best-pair without replacement)—that collapse a nu×mv segment distance matrix into an n×m track distance matrix by iteratively selecting the minimum distance and masking its row and column, thereby preventing both consecutive-segment and full-track matching artifacts; different reductions are applied to positive (bpwr-5) and negative (min) pairs. Second, a supervised contrastive loss derived from the A&U objective that strictly decouples alignment (mean squared distance over positive pairs) from a smoothed uniformity term (log(ε + mean exp(−γd²)) over negative pairs), operates in plain Euclidean space rather than on the hypersphere, and uses dimension-normalized distances. The backbone is a pre-activation ResNet-50 with ReZero and instance-batch normalization, preceded by a two-layer strided-convolution frontend and followed by generalized-mean pooling and a linear projection to 1024 dimensions, trained with Adam (lr 2×10⁻⁴) on batches of 100 tracks (800 segments) using SpecAugment, time-stretch, and pitch-roll augmentations.

## Results

On the track-level evaluation, CLEWS achieves a NAR of 2.70 and MAP of 0.774 on DVI-Test, and a NAR of 1.27 and MAP of 0.876 on SHS-Test, setting new state-of-the-art on both datasets. On the segment-level evaluation (DVI-Test), CLEWS maintains a NAR of approximately 2.84–3.02 across all query lengths τ ≥ 10 s, while the next-best method (DVINet+) degrades to NAR 3.76 at τ = 90 s and CQTNet to 6.60. The CLEWS loss also outperforms SupCon (NAR 2.69, MAP 0.676), SigLIP (NAR 2.79, MAP 0.684), and Triplet (NAR 3.08, MAP 0.717) in ablation experiments on DVI-Valid.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CLEWS outperforms all ten baselines on both track-level and segment-level evaluations. On DVI-Test (track-level), CLEWS improves NAR from 3.69 (DVINet+, the strongest existing method) to 2.70 and MAP from 0.643 to 0.774; on SHS-Test it improves MAP from 0.863 (ByteCover2) to 0.876 and NAR from 1.91 (ByteCover3†) to 1.27. The gap is most dramatic on DVI-Test, where classification-based methods (CQTNet, CoverHunter-Coarse) suffer severely due to the small number of items per class, while CLEWS's contrastive formulation is robust to this. In the segment-level evaluation, CLEWS maintains near-constant performance (NAR ≈ 2.85) across all query lengths τ = 10–90 s, whereas ByteCover3† degrades from 8.66 to 7.06 and CQTNet from 16.35 to 6.60, confirming that partial-match learning is critical. In loss-function ablations, the CLEWS loss beats SupCon (MAP 0.804 vs 0.676), SigLIP (0.804 vs 0.684), Triplet (0.804 vs 0.717), and cross-entropy classification (0.804 vs 0.205) on DVI-Valid, with the largest margin over classification.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the musical version matching literature, which has predominantly relied on classification and triplet losses at the full-track level, by introducing the first supervised contrastive learning approach for this task. Conceptually, it builds on the A&U loss of Wang & Isola (2020) by reformulating it for supervised contrastive learning with multiple positives per anchor, incorporating decoupling principles from Yeh et al. (2022) and geometric considerations from Koishekenov et al. (2023). The key methodological departure is the segment distance reduction framework (particularly the bpwr-r operator), which addresses the mismatch between track-level labels and segment-level representations — a problem that prior segment-based approaches like ByteCover3's max-mean operator handle only partially by forcing full-track matches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Segment-level musical version matching is critical for real-world applications including plagiarism detection, near-duplicate identification, and assessing data memorization in generative music models, yet prior methods suffered dramatic performance degradation when moving from track-level to segment-level evaluation. The bpwr reduction strategy and the modified A&U loss are general enough to apply to any weakly-labeled sequence matching problem beyond music. Open questions include whether the specific reduction strategies and loss modifications transfer to other domains (speech, video), and whether the approach can handle even shorter query segments or more extreme structural variations between versions.
