## Problem

Despite widespread use of representational similarity analysis to compare neural codes across artificial and biological systems, there was no analytical understanding of how a network's internal representations relate to its input-output function. Specifically, it was unclear whether representational similarity implies functional similarity, whether stable behaviour requires stable representations, or what computational pressures select for task-specific over arbitrary representations. This paper shows that function and representation are fundamentally dissociable in overparameterised networks.

## Input / Output

The analytical framework operates on two-layer linear networks trained via supervised learning on input-target pairs (x_n ∈ R^{N_i}, y_n ∈ R^{N_o}) with hidden dimension N_h ≥ min(N_i, N_o), producing a complete partition of the solution manifold into four subregions (GLS, LSS, MRNS, MWNS) each with an exact parametric equation and a characterised representational similarity matrix. For nonlinear validation, two-layer ReLU networks (784→1024→10) are trained on MNIST digit images, and function-preserving transformations generate expanded parametrisations whose noise robustness and test error are measured. The key quantitative outputs are RSMs, linear-predictivity R² scores, and expected loss under input/parameter noise for each solution type.

## Method

The paper derives a complete parametric equation (Theorem 3.1) for the full solution manifold of two-layer linear networks, then partitions it into four subregions—GLS, LSS, MRNS, MWNS—each with distinct constraints on the image and kernel of the weight matrices. GLS and LSS support nearly arbitrary hidden-layer representations with task-agnostic RSMs, while MRNS and MWNS constrain representations up to orthogonal transformations, yielding unique task-specific RSMs determined entirely by training data. The paper then proves that input-noise robustness selects LSS (task-agnostic) and parameter-noise robustness selects MRNS (task-specific), identifying parameter noise as the mechanism that explains convergence to task-specific representations. Nonlinear validation exploits function-preserving invariances of ReLU networks (permutation, scale, nuisance-neuron, duplication) to construct expanded parametrisations of MNIST-trained models, confirming that task-specific representations are more robust to parameter noise while generalisation error does not constrain representations to be minimal.

## Results

The paper's central analytical result is that function and representation are fully dissociable in two-layer linear networks: GLS and LSS admit nearly arbitrary hidden-layer representations (task-agnostic RSMs), while MRNS and MWNS yield unique, task-specific RSMs invariant to the choice of input weights. On the computational side, robustness to input noise selects exclusively for LSS (task-agnostic), whereas robustness to parameter noise selects exclusively for MRNS (task-specific), and minimising secondary (generalisation) error also permits task-agnostic solutions. In nonlinear ReLU networks on MNIST (1024 hidden units, 50k training images), minimal task-trained solutions degrade more slowly under parameter noise than expanded (scaled, nuisance, or duplicated) parametrisations, while input-null perturbations are the only transformation that increases sensitivity to input noise, consistent with the linear theory.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper that does not benchmark against external baseline methods; instead, it compares four analytically defined solution subregions (GLS, LSS, MRNS, MWNS) within a unified framework. The key internal comparison is between task-agnostic solutions (GLS/LSS) and task-specific solutions (MRNS/MWNS): the former permit arbitrary RSMs and are favoured by input-noise robustness and secondary-error minimisation, while the latter have data-determined RSMs and are uniquely favoured by parameter-noise robustness. In the nonlinear MNIST experiments, the task-trained (minimal) network outperforms all three expanded variants (scaled, nuisance, duplicate) under parameter noise, with the duplicated expansion being the most robust among the expanded types due to noise averaging. Input-null perturbations, in contrast, only impair input-noise robustness and leave parameter-noise robustness unchanged, mirroring the linear result that unobserved null directions decouple the two noise channels.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the solution manifold analysis initiated by Baldi and Hornik (1989) and formalized for deep linear networks by Laurent and Brecht (2018), and generalizes the Q-transformation framework of Saxe et al. (2014) and the MWNS derivation of Saxe et al. (2019) by removing their strong assumptions (whitened inputs, full-rank targets, equal dimensions). For the nonlinear extension, it repurposes function-preserving invariances identified by Simsek et al. (2021) and Martinelli et al. (2023) to construct controlled reparametrisations of ReLU networks, enabling empirical validation of the linear theory. The key departure from prior work is the complete, assumption-free parametric partition of the solution manifold into GLS, LSS, MRNS, and MWNS subregions, each with precisely characterised representational degrees of freedom.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a rigorous analytical foundation for a critical methodological concern in computational neuroscience: representational similarity analysis (RSA) and linear predictivity may reflect solution-type artifacts rather than genuine functional alignment, undermining common interpretations in brain-scoring benchmarks and cross-species comparisons. The finding that parameter noise robustness (not input noise or generalization) is the selective pressure for task-specific representations offers a concrete mechanistic hypothesis for why biological and artificial systems converge on aligned representations. Limitations include the restriction to two-layer architectures, the absence of analysis for how gradient descent actually selects among solution subregions, and the fact that the nonlinear validation relies on exact invariances rather than a full manifold characterisation. Open questions include whether these dissociation principles persist in deeper, more realistic architectures and how implicit regularisation of optimisation interacts with the solution-type structure.
