## Problem

Job seekers increasingly use LLMs to enhance their resumes, but unequal access to premium models (e.g., GPT-4o vs. GPT-3.5-Turbo) creates systematic hiring disparities that existing strategic classification frameworks cannot address. Prior work assumes deterministic manipulations and known classifiers, whereas LLM manipulations are inherently stochastic and the deployed scorer is opaque to candidates. The paper asks whether hiring algorithms can be designed to be robust to these stochastic, group-differentiated LLM manipulations while simultaneously improving fairness and accuracy.

## Input / Output

Each candidate's resume is modeled as a d-dimensional feature vector partitioned into style features (writing quality, vocabulary, formatting) and fundamental features (skills, experience, education). LLM manipulation stochastically redistributes style features according to model-specific distributions while preserving fundamental features, reflecting the empirical observation that LLMs rewrite presentation without altering factual content. The scorer is a monotonically non-decreasing function producing a real-valued relevance score, and the hiring decision is a threshold classifier; under the two-ticket scheme, the effective score is the maximum of the submitted resume's score and the hirer-LLM-manipulated resume's score.

## Method

The core contribution is the two-ticket hiring scheme: the hirer applies their own LLM to each submitted resume and accepts the candidate if either the original or the hirer-manipulated version exceeds the threshold, effectively giving every candidate a second 'ticket' to acceptance. LLM quality is formally ordered via multivariate stochastic dominance, and the key theorem proves that when the hirer's LLM stochastically dominates the weaker group's LLM, the two-ticket scheme strictly reduces TPR disparity and increases TPR for both groups under the No False Positives Objective (maximizing TPR subject to zero FPR). The scheme generalizes to an n-ticket variant where the hirer's LLM is applied n times, and Banach's Fixed Point Theorem guarantees that the hiring outcome converges exponentially to a fixed, group-independent decision as n→∞. The framework is formalized as a Stackelberg game where the hirer commits to an unknown scorer and threshold, and candidates best-respond by submitting the higher-scoring of their original or LLM-manipulated resume. Empirical validation on 520 real resumes from the Djinni dataset using Resume Matcher as the scorer confirms TPR improvements and disparity reductions, with the largest gains when the unprivileged group has no LLM access.

## Results

On the Djinni dataset (520 resumes each for PM and UX Designer roles), the Two-Ticket scheme consistently improves TPR and reduces TPR disparity compared to Traditional hiring. The largest gains occur when the unprivileged group has no LLM access: for the UX Designer role, TPR increases from 0.22 to 0.38 while TPR disparity drops from 0.27 to 0.01. For the PM role under the same condition, TPR rises from 0.11 to 0.14 and disparity halves from 0.10 to 0.05. Accuracy also improves across all conditions (e.g., UX accuracy goes from 0.633 to 0.689 when U has no LLM), confirming the theoretical guarantee that the Two-Ticket scheme simultaneously enhances fairness and accuracy under the No False Positives Objective.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between the proposed Two-Ticket scheme and the Traditional hiring scheme (where the Hirer does not apply an additional LLM manipulation). The Two-Ticket scheme wins on both TPR and TPR disparity across all three LLM-access conditions for both job roles. The advantage is most pronounced when the unprivileged group has no LLM access (U: No LLMs, P: GPT-4o), where UX TPR disparity drops from 0.27 to 0.01 (a 96% reduction) and UX TPR nearly doubles from 0.22 to 0.38. When the unprivileged group has access to a weaker model (GPT-3.5-Turbo), improvements are more modest (UX disparity: 0.15→0.08). When both groups have access to similar-quality models (GPT-4o-MINI vs. GPT-4o), the disparity is already near zero under Traditional hiring, and the Two-Ticket scheme provides only marginal gains (UX disparity: 0.00→-0.01), consistent with the theoretical prediction that the scheme's benefit scales with the LLM quality gap between groups.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the strategic classification framework (Hardt et al., 2016a) to the LLM era, where candidate manipulations are stochastic rather than deterministic and the classifier cannot detect whether or how a resume was modified. It draws on prior work modeling disparate manipulation abilities (Hu et al., 2019b; Milli et al., 2019), techniques for random classifiers under stochastic manipulations (Braverman & Garg, 2020), and the unknown-classifier setting (Ghalme et al., 2021; Cohen et al., 2024). The key conceptual departure is the 'two-ticket' scheme, where the hirer applies their own LLM manipulation to each submitted resume, which has no direct analogue in prior strategic classification literature where the classifier is fixed and passive.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first formal treatment of how unequal LLM access creates hiring disparities, a problem with immediate practical relevance given that 57% of job seekers now use AI for resumes and 98.4% of Fortune 500 companies deploy ATS scoring. The two-ticket scheme offers a simple, group-agnostic intervention that requires no knowledge of candidate demographics, which is a significant practical advantage over membership-based fairness corrections. Key limitations include reliance on a single open-source scorer (Resume Matcher), simple job-agnostic prompts, the best-responding candidate assumption, and the assumption that LLMs preserve fundamental features without hallucination. Open questions include how the scheme performs under variable or adversarial prompts, with blackbox commercial ATS systems, and whether the exponential convergence guarantee of the n-ticket scheme holds in practice with finite, non-ideal LLMs.
