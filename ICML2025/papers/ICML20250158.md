## Problem

Existing LLM reasoning methods such as Chain-of-Thought, Tree-of-Thought, and Graph-of-Thought perform only a single complete reasoning pass, meaning flawed intermediate steps are never revisited and alternative paths are prematurely abandoned. This single-pass limitation, combined with the absence of real-time error correction and inefficient allocation of computational resources across reasoning paths, caps the accuracy achievable on complex mathematical and logical problems.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text reasoning problem (e.g., a mathematical word problem or a Game of 24 instance) as input, which is first augmented by retrieving the most relevant example from a pre-constructed knowledge base B via similarity matching and concatenating it to the original query. The output is a single final answer selected through a two-stage decision process: majority consensus voting across activated trees, followed by an LLM expert evaluation when results are inconsistent. No fine-tuning or backpropagation is required; all computation occurs at inference time.

## Method

Forest-of-Thought (FoT) integrates n independent reasoning trees (e.g., ToT or MCTSr variants) that each approach the problem from a different perspective, then aggregates their results through three key mechanisms. First, sparse activation evaluates intermediate outputs at every layer using a validation function and prunes trees that produce invalid or low-quality nodes, concentrating computation on high-confidence paths and reducing LLM invocations. Second, a dynamic self-correction strategy monitors predicted logits scores at each reasoning step; when the score falls below a threshold (0.5 optimal), correction is triggered either via predefined mathematical rules (e.g., verifying number provenance in Game of 24) or by re-prompting with knowledge-base context. Third, the Consensus-Guided Expert Decision (CGED) strategy first applies majority voting across activated trees, and if results are inconsistent, invokes an LLM expert to compare reasoning processes and select the most accurate answer. The framework demonstrates a scaling law where accuracy improves predictably with the number of activated subtrees across multiple base models (Llama3-8B, Mistral-7B, GLM-4-9B, Qwen2.5-Math-7B, QwQ-32B).

## Results

On the Game of 24 benchmark, FoT with 8 activated subtrees achieves 96.84% success rate, substantially outperforming ToT (74.00%), BoT (82.40%), and XoT (85.40%). On the broader mathematical benchmarks (Table 4), FoT(n=4) built on QwQ-32B-preview reaches 97.33% on GSM8K, 91.20% on MATH-500, and 53.33% on AIME 2024, surpassing GPT-4o (92.90/76.60/9.30) and rStar-Math^64 (95.20/90.00/53.30). Even with the smaller Qwen2.5-Math-7B base, FoT(n=8) attains 96.89% on GSM8K and 46.67% on AIME 2024, demonstrating strong scaling with the number of activated subtrees.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FoT consistently outperforms all compared baselines across every benchmark. On Game of 24, FoT (96.84%) surpasses ToT (74.00%) by 22.84 points, BoT (82.40%) by 14.44 points, and XoT (85.40%) by 11.44 points, while single-step methods like CoT (4.38%) and IO (10.22%) are far behind. On GSM8K, FoT(n=4) with QwQ-32B (97.33%) exceeds GPT-4o (92.90%) by 4.43 points and rStar-Math^64 (95.20%) by 2.13 points. On MATH-500, FoT(n=4) on QwQ (91.20%) edges out rStar-Math^64 (90.00%) by 1.2 points and GPT-4o (76.60%) by 14.6 points. On the hardest benchmark, AIME 2024, FoT(n=4) on QwQ (53.33%) matches rStar-Math^64 (53.30%) and dramatically outperforms GPT-4o (9.30%) by 44 points, while even the smaller FoT(n=8) on Qwen2.5-7B (46.67%) nearly matches rStar-Math (46.70) with a 7B model versus rStar-Math's 14B combined system.

## Relation to Prior Work

FoT extends the XoT reasoning family (CoT → ToT → GoT) by replacing a single reasoning tree with an ensemble of independent trees, each potentially using a different search strategy (ToT, MCTSr with varying rollout counts). It draws on sparse activation and pruning ideas from the MCTS literature to filter low-quality paths, and on dynamic, logit-threshold-based self-correction that departs from fixed-iteration approaches like Self-Refine. The Consensus-Guided Expert Decision (CGED) strategy synthesizes majority voting with LLM-as-judge evaluation, a combination not previously formalized in the XoT setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides empirical evidence that scaling test-time compute through path diversity (more subtrees) follows a predictable scaling law across multiple 7–9B base models, offering a practical recipe for improving reasoning accuracy without fine-tuning. However, the computational overhead is substantial (25+ LLM invocations for Game of 24), and the approach has only been validated on mathematical and logical puzzles, leaving open whether the forest structure generalizes to open-ended generation or multi-modal reasoning. The sensitivity of self-correction to the confidence threshold (optimal at 0.5) and the diminishing returns beyond ~8 subtrees suggest that further gains will require more principled activation criteria rather than brute-force scaling.
