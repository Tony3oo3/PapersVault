## Problem

Existing weight matrix compression methods for deep neural networks rely on the Population Unit Bulk (PUB) model, which assumes a single homogeneous variance σ² for all bulk eigenvalues of W^T W. This assumption is too restrictive and fails to accurately capture the empirical eigenvalue distribution, leading to overly aggressive compression that discards informative bulk eigenvalues and degrades generalization performance.

## Input / Output

The method takes a trained weight matrix W ∈ R^{n×p} and operates on the eigenvalues of W^T W obtained via SVD (W = UD^{1/2}V). It produces a compressed weight matrix W_new = UD_new^{1/2}V whose rank is reduced to K̂ + (p−K̂)t̂, along with the estimated PDB parameters {σ̂₁², σ̂₂², t̂}, the refined spike count K̂, recovered population spikes {α̂₁,…,α̂_K̂}, and the two boundary points λ̂₊ and β̂. For convolutional layers, kernels are reshaped into 2D matrices of size nd×cd before compression and reshaped back afterward.

## Method

The paper proposes a Population Double Bulk (PDB) model where E[W^T W] = diag(α₁,…,α_K, σ₁² repeated (p−K)t times, σ₂² repeated (p−K)(1−t) times), generalizing the PUB model by allowing two heterogeneous bulk variances. A four-step PDBLS algorithm estimates all model parameters: (1) initial spike count K₀ via a gap-based criterion, (2) bulk parameters {σ₁², σ₂², t} via least-squares fitting of the Stieltjes transform equation, (3) boundary points λ₊ (spike/bulk1) and β (bulk1/bulk2) with refined spike count K, and (4) population spikes recovered from sample spikes via the mapping g(α_j). The key compression insight is that bulk1 eigenvalues (between β and λ₊) contain learned information, so the PDB Noise-Filtering algorithm only zeros out bulk2 eigenvalues (below β) while retaining bulk1 and replacing sample spikes with denoised population spikes, yielding a lower-rank matrix that preserves or even improves test accuracy, particularly under label noise.

## Results

On the primary comparison (Table 7), PDB achieves the best average test accuracy of 0.7891 across all networks and datasets, outperforming the uncompressed Base (0.7783), PUB (0.7827), SLR (0.7858), and Naive SVD (0.7789). The largest single improvement is on T5-base/RTE, where PDB reaches 0.7536 versus the Base of 0.7174 (a 5% relative gain). In terms of compression, PDB retains a higher rank than PUB (e.g., 15.72% vs. 8.57% for FCNN/MNIST) while still achieving lower rank than SLR and Naive SVD, striking a better accuracy–rank trade-off. Under 60% label noise, PDB consistently improves over the uncompressed model (e.g., 0.5544 vs. 0.5455 on FCNN/MNIST).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PDB outperforms all four baselines on average test accuracy (0.7891 vs. 0.7827 for PUB/BEMA, 0.7858 for SLR, and 0.7789 for Naive SVD). Against the PUB-based methods (BEMA and Kernel), PDB achieves higher accuracy while retaining a meaningfully higher rank (e.g., 21.48% vs. 10.42% for ResNet18/CIFAR10), indicating that the PUB model's single-bulk assumption discards informative bulk1 eigenvalues. Compared to Naive SVD (fixed 55% compression), PDB achieves lower rank (e.g., 13.54% vs. 55% for VGG16/CIFAR10) while matching or exceeding its accuracy, showing that the data-driven β boundary is more precise than a fixed ratio. Against SLR, PDB wins on accuracy in most settings (e.g., 0.7536 vs. 0.7174 on T5-base/RTE) and achieves lower rank in several cases (e.g., 35.42% vs. 52.08% for ResNet18/ImageNet). The advantage is most pronounced on large pre-trained models (BERT, T5-base, ViT-L), where PDB gains 1–5% over Base, while on smaller networks (FCNN/MNIST) the differences are marginal (<0.1%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the RMT-based weight matrix compression framework established by Martin & Mahoney (2021) and Staats et al. (2023), which model the trained weight matrix under a Population Unit Bulk (PUB) assumption of homogeneous variance σ². The key departure is generalizing to a Population Double Bulk (PDB) model with two distinct bulk variances (σ₁², σ₂²), motivated by empirical observations that the PUB model's single-variance assumption fails to capture the eigenvalue distribution of real trained networks. The estimation procedure (PDBLS) adapts the least-squares spectral distribution fitting of Li et al. (2013) and the spiked eigenvalue recovery of Bai & Yao (2012) to the two-bulk setting, while the compression algorithm (PDB Noise-Filtering) differs from prior PUB-based methods by retaining the bulk1 eigenvalues as informative rather than discarding all bulk eigenvalues.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The PDB model provides a more principled, theory-driven alternative to the ad-hoc compression ratios (e.g., the 55% threshold in Shmalo et al.) and cross-validation-based boundary selection in Staats et al., offering a hyperparameter-free way to determine how much of the weight matrix spectrum to retain. The finding that compression can improve generalization under label noise suggests the bulk2 eigenvalues encode noise rather than signal, which has practical implications for robust training. However, the method is post-hoc (applied after training), only addresses the bulk+spikes phase (not the heavy-tailed phase), and the justification for M=2 bulks over M≥3 rests on a single empirical observation (Table 1) rather than a theoretical argument. Open questions include whether the PDB structure is stable across training dynamics and whether the two-bulk decomposition has a meaningful interpretation in terms of network architecture (e.g., different layer types contributing different variance scales).
