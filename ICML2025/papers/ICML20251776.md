## Problem

Traditional learning rates quantify changes in parameter space, not the functional impact on network outputs, making it difficult to understand how different layers contribute to learning or to transfer hyperparameters across model scales. Existing hyperparameter transfer methods (μP, Modula) rely on restrictive assumptions such as proximity to random initialisation, specific architectures, or manual specification of per-parameter 'masses', limiting their practical applicability. This paper asks whether function-space learning rates can be directly measured and controlled in arbitrary neural networks at any point during training.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a minibatch of data, the current parameter tensors, the optimizer's per-step parameter updates (ΔW), and (for FLeRM) function-space learning rates previously recorded from a small base model. It produces per-layer function-space learning rates (the RMS norm of the output change induced by each parameter tensor's update) and, under FLeRM, adjusted per-layer parameter-space learning rates for the scaled model. Computationally, each estimate requires only a single additional forward and backward pass on a fresh data batch, with 3 scalar exponential moving averages maintained per matrix parameter (R+1 for rank-R tensors), making the overhead negligible when run periodically (e.g., every 100 steps).

## Method

The core estimator projects the network output onto a random Gaussian direction φ = (1/√NK)Σ ω_nk f_nk, so that the change Δ_ℓφ from a parameter update ΔW^ℓ is a single backward pass whose variance equals the squared RMS function-space learning rate. To reduce Monte-Carlo variance, the paper assumes a Kronecker-factored covariance over the elementwise product Z = ΔW ⊙ (dφ/dW), which collapses the full covariance sum into a ratio of three scalar expectations (Frobenius norm, row-sum-of-squares, column-sum-of-squares) tracked via EMAs. FLeRM then records these function-space learning rates in a cheap base model and, when training a scaled model, sets each layer's parameter-space learning rate as η^ℓ = η₀ · ‖Δ_ℓf‖_RMS^(base) / ‖Δ_ℓf‖_RMS^(current), keeping functional updates scale-invariant. For depth scaling, base-model function-space learning rates are shared (divided) across the additional residual blocks. The approach requires no assumptions about initialisation, width, or architecture, and works with any PyTorch network and any optimiser.

## Results

FLeRM eliminates or dramatically reduces the shift in optimal learning rate when scaling model width, depth, initialisation scale, and LoRA rank, keeping the user-defined learning rate hyperparameter stable across scales. In the PostNorm transformer, standard practice causes training instability to shift to lower learning rates as depth increases (worsening optimal loss), while FLeRM keeps instabilities at the same location as the base model. In LoRA fine-tuning of Llama-3.2-1B, the optimal learning rate increases by more than an order of magnitude as rank goes from 2 to 32 under standard AdamW, but FLeRM (using rank-2 as base) eliminates this shift. Analysis of function-space learning rates over time reveals an implicit scheduling effect: FSLRs decay monotonically for most parameters under fixed-LR Adam, and the second feedforward weight matrices in self-attention have FSLRs an order of magnitude larger than the readout layer.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is against standard practice (no hyperparameter transfer), where FLeRM consistently aligns the loss-vs-learning-rate curves across different model scales, whereas standard practice shows significant shifts in the optimal learning rate. Compared to μP (Yang & Hu, 2022), FLeRM is more flexible: it works far from random initialisation, with any architecture, and at any point during training, without requiring the restrictive assumptions of wide networks or specific parametrisations. Compared to Modula (Large et al., 2024), FLeRM does not require the user to manually specify per-parameter 'masses' (analogous to FSLRs), instead measuring them empirically from a base model; an ablation (Section 4.2.5) confirms that measured FSLRs yield slightly better training loss than naively uniform FSLRs. The paper also shows that FLeRM works with multiple optimisers (SGD+momentum, SignSGD, AdamW, Adamax, Adagrad) and with cosine annealing schedulers, generalising beyond the Adam-only setting used in the main experiments.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant analytical approach to hyperparameter scaling (μP, tensor programs, dynamical mean-field theory) by empirically measuring function-space learning rates via a Monte Carlo estimator with a Kronecker-factored covariance structure (borrowed from Martens & Grosse, 2015, and extended to tensors via Hoff, 2010). Unlike μP and Modula, which require restrictive assumptions (near-random initialisation, wide networks) or user-specified per-parameter 'masses,' FLeRM directly measures the relevant quantities in a base model and transfers them, working at any point in training with arbitrary architectures. The paper also contrasts with Chizat & Netrapalli's feature-speed formula and Everett et al.'s empirical alignment analysis, positioning its autodiff-based measurement as a more general and assumption-free alternative.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FLeRM offers a practical, architecture-agnostic tool for zero-shot learning-rate transfer across width, depth, initialisation scale, and LoRA rank, which is directly relevant to the multi-million-dollar cost of LLM pre-training where full-scale hyperparameter sweeps are infeasible. The key open question is whether the ad hoc depth-scaling heuristic (splitting base FSLRs evenly across new residual blocks) can be replaced by a more principled scheme, as the ablations suggest performance gains are possible. Experimental validation is limited to small-to-medium models (≤814M parameters, CIFAR-10, Wikitext-103 subsets, GPT-2, Llama-3.2-1B), leaving open whether the approach retains its advantages at frontier scale where training dynamics may differ substantially.
