## Problem

Existing confounder-removal methods for deep networks (MDN, P-MDN, BR-Net) rely on batch-level statistics or pre-computed global memories, making them incompatible with continual learning where data arrives sequentially under shifting distributions, and with architectures like vision transformers that parallelize over individual examples. There is no mechanism to incrementally residualize confounding effects from intermediate features as both data and confounder distributions change over time.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252146/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes images (dermatoscopic RGB at 64×64, 3D T1w MRI volumes at 64×64×64) together with per-sample confounder metadata (e.g., age, sex, pubertal development score) that is available as auxiliary information. The R-MDN layer is inserted at one or more intermediate layers of a CNN or ViT backbone, where it operates on the learned feature embeddings rather than raw pixels, producing a residualized feature vector that is passed forward to the next layer. The final output is a class label, with the side benefit that all intermediate representations are decorrelated from the confounders.

## Method

R-MDN is a plug-in normalization layer that maintains an internal state consisting of regression coefficients β and an inverse covariance matrix P, updated incrementally via the recursive least squares (RLS) algorithm. For each incoming sample (or mini-batch), it computes the Kalman gain using the Sherman-Morrison rank-1 update (or Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury for batches) to update P, then updates β by the prediction error e = z − Xᵀβ. The layer outputs the residual r = z − x̃β_x̃, stripping the confounder-explained component from the feature while preserving label-relevant variance. Because all updates are per-example and closed-form, R-MDN requires no batch-level aggregation, no pre-computed global statistics, and no adversarial or gradient-based sub-optimization, making it directly insertable into vision transformers and naturally adaptive to distribution shifts across continual learning stages. A small regularization term λI is added to P to stabilize updates against noise and ensure numerical well-conditioning.

## Results

On the HAM10000 skin lesion classification task (5 continual stages, ViT backbone), R-MDN(C) achieves the lowest average squared distance correlation with the confounder (dcor² = 0.0475) among all methods, compared to 0.0864 for the ViT baseline, 0.0544 for BR-Net, and 0.2595 for P-MDN, while maintaining competitive accuracy (0.692). On synthetic continual learning benchmarks, R-MDN achieves an average accuracy distance (ACCd) of 0.02–0.03 from the theoretical unbiased optimum, versus 0.18–0.28 for the CNN baseline and 0.28–0.29 for analytic continual learning methods (ACIL, F-OAL). On the ADNI longitudinal neuroimaging task, R-MDN reduces dcor² to 0.05–0.09 across all three diagnostic groups (MCI, CN, AD), compared to 0.20–0.25 for the ViT baseline, demonstrating effective confounder removal under changing age and sex distributions over multiple acquisition years.

## Baselines & Comparisons

R-MDN(C) achieves the lowest dcor² (0.0475) on HAM10000, outperforming the ViT baseline (0.0864), BR-Net (0.0544), P-MDN (0.2595), EWC (0.0938), LwF (0.0512), and PackNet (0.0470, nearly tied), confirming effective confounder removal. On forward transfer, R-MDN(C) (FWT = 0.3997) substantially outperforms the ViT baseline (0.5125), BR-Net (0.5592), LwF (0.5277), and PackNet (0.4965), indicating better generalization to earlier training stages. Against analytic continual learning methods ACIL and F-OAL on the synthetic benchmark, R-MDN achieves ACCd of 0.02 versus 0.29 and 0.28 respectively, showing it learns confounder-free features approaching the theoretical unbiased optimum while those methods exploit confounders for higher raw accuracy. On the ADNI longitudinal task, R-MDN reduces dcor² to 0.05–0.09 across all diagnostic groups, whereas P-MDN fails to reduce correlation for the AD group (0.24) and the ViT baseline remains high (0.20–0.25). In the static ABCD sex classification setting, R-MDN achieves near-equal dcor² for boys (0.0099) and girls (0.0090) with a TPR−TNR difference of −0.002, indicating no sex bias, while the CNN baseline shows a larger gap (0.0127 vs 0.0218) and P-MDN, though achieving lower dcor², remains biased toward girls (TPR−TNR = −0.043). R-MDN also composes well with existing continual learning frameworks: combining it with LwF further reduces dcor² to 0.0354 (best overall) while maintaining competitive accuracy (0.7186).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Metadata Normalization (MDN, Lu et al. 2021) from a static, batch-level OLS formulation to a recursive, per-sample update using the recursive least squares (RLS) algorithm and Sherman-Morrison/Woodbury rank-update formulas. The key conceptual departure is that prior confounder-removal methods (MDN, P-MDN, BR-Net) require either pre-computed batch statistics over the full dataset or adversarial training, making them incompatible with continual learning where data arrives sequentially under shifting distributions, and with parallelized architectures like vision transformers. R-MDN reframes the residualization as an online state-update problem, maintaining an internal inverse-covariance matrix and regression coefficients that adapt as new examples arrive, thereby unifying confounder removal with the continual learning paradigm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

R-MDN fills a practical gap for longitudinal and multi-site clinical studies (e.g., ADNI, HAM10000) where confounder distributions shift over years and the full dataset is unavailable at training time. As a drop-in normalization layer compatible with CNNs and ViTs, it offers a low-overhead path to equitable predictions across demographic groups without retraining stage-specific models. Limitations include the assumption that confounders are available as structured metadata, the reliance on linear regression in feature space (mitigated by multi-layer insertion), and the need to tune the regularization hyperparameter. Open questions include extending to non-medical domains (video, audio), handling high-dimensional or latent confounders, and integrating with more complex continual learning frameworks beyond EWC/LwF.
