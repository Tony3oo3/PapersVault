## Problem

Large pre-trained foundation models achieve strong compression on their training distributions, but when their parameter size is factored into the compression ratio (since weights must be communicated for decompression), they underperform standard algorithms like gzip and LZMA2. The open question is whether small pre-trained transformers (millions of parameters) can achieve competitive adjusted compression ratios across multiple modalities, and whether multimodal training yields cross-modal transfer to unseen modalities.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250908/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes raw byte streams with no tokenization: UTF-8 text, flattened 512×512×3 uint8 RGB images, and 16 kHz int16 audio waveforms, all concatenated into continuous byte sequences. At inference, the frozen transformer produces next-byte probability distributions that are fed directly into a non-adaptive arithmetic coder, yielding a lossless compressed bitstream. The reported compression ratio includes the model's float16-encoded parameter size (0.8–40.3 MB) in the numerator, and all evaluation is performed on 1 GB of out-of-distribution data distinct from any training source.

## Method

The paper trains decoder-only transformers (SwiGLU activations, post-layer norm, 8 heads, embedding dim 64, 4096-byte context) on 165 GB of raw byte sequences across seven unimodal and multimodal mixtures, minimizing standard next-byte log-loss for 2.5 M Adam steps. At compression time, the frozen model's autoregressive byte predictions are passed directly to an arithmetic coder (no separate head or extraction step), making the training objective equivalent to a maximum-compression objective. The core empirical contribution is a systematic sweep over model size, dataset size, context length, and training mixture composition to identify the regime where small pre-trained transformers beat both general-purpose (gzip, LZMA2) and domain-specific (FLAC, PNG, JPEG-XL) compressors on OOD data, while revealing that cross-modal transfer to unseen modalities is negligible at this scale.

## Results

On 1GB of OOD data, the best pre-trained transformer achieves compression ratios of 0.487 (audio), 0.285 (image), and 0.217 (text), consistently outperforming general-purpose compressors (gzip, LZMA2) and domain-specific ones (FLAC, PNG, JPEG 2000) across all modalities. On multimodal evaluations (e.g., audio+image+text at 0.349), the method remains competitive with the state-of-the-art online adaptive transformer of Bellard (2021) (0.331). The compression ratio is computed as (|compressed data| + |compressor|) / |uncompressed data|, factoring in model parameter size (0.8–40.3 MB in float16).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against general-purpose compressors, the pre-trained transformer dominates across all modalities: on audio it achieves 0.487 vs. gzip's 0.813 and LZMA2's 0.699; on images 0.285 vs. 0.698 and 0.545; on text 0.217 vs. 0.394 and 0.286. Against domain-specific compressors, the method beats FLAC (0.487 vs. 0.538 on audio), PNG (0.285 vs. 0.426 on images), and JPEG 2000 (0.285 vs. 0.390 on images), but is outperformed by JPEG-XL on images (0.285 vs. 0.260). Against the online adaptive transformer of Bellard (2021), the method is broadly on par: it wins on audio (0.487 vs. 0.509) and audio+image (0.393 vs. 0.395) but trails slightly on text (0.217 vs. 0.204), image (0.285 vs. 0.281), and all other multimodal evaluations. The paper notes that Bellard's online approach does not need to communicate model weights for decompression, whereas the pre-trained approach must factor parameter size into the ratio, making the comparison inherently asymmetric in favor of the online method.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Delétang et al. (2024) 'Language modeling is compression,' which established that log-loss minimization is equivalent to a lossless compression objective and demonstrated that billion-parameter LLMs compress data well. However, Delétang et al. found that when model size is factored into the compression ratio, LLMs are not competitive with standard compressors, and their small-model pilot (3.2M params on 1GB Wikipedia) performed poorly on non-text modalities. This work departs by systematically studying small (millions-of-parameters) decoder-only transformers pre-trained on 165GB of raw byte sequences across text, image, and audio, using non-adaptive arithmetic coding with frozen weights, and explicitly accounting for parameter size. It also contrasts with Bellard (2021), whose online adaptive transformer is state-of-the-art on the Large Text Compression Benchmark but does not require communicating model weights, whereas this paper's offline pre-training regime does.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper answers a clear open question: small pre-trained transformers can achieve competitive (and sometimes superior) compression ratios versus standard and domain-specific compressors on 1GB of OOD data, even after accounting for model size. A key negative finding is that cross-modal transfer is weak at this scale—unlike LLMs, small transformers do not compress unseen modalities well—suggesting a qualitative difference between small and very large models. Practical impact is limited by the authors' own admission that these models are orders of magnitude slower than standard compressors, so the contribution is primarily scientific (understanding inductive biases, scaling behavior, and modality interactions). Open questions include whether more sophisticated architectures (Perceivers, MegaByte, Byte Latent Transformers) or transfer learning strategies could enable cross-modal transfer at small scale, and whether lossy weight compression could make larger models competitive.
