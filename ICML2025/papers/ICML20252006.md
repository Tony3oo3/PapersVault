## Problem

Parameter sharing in cooperative MARL improves sample efficiency but produces homogeneous policies that limit joint performance. The paper identifies a previously unexplored mechanism behind this limitation: gradient conflicts among agents cancel out updates for specific neurons (termed 'futile neurons'), degrading both learning efficiency and the expressiveness of the shared value factorization network.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252006/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

Each agent receives its local observation concatenated with an agent identity embedding, processed through a shared network of two fully-connected layers and a GRU to produce an action. A special gradient detection layer (a fixed all-ones matrix) is inserted at activation layers to compute per-agent partial derivatives of the loss without altering forward-pass values; these per-agent gradients are accumulated over T target-network update intervals and cleared before the optimizer step, enabling conflict measurement without changing the training dynamics.

## Method

GradPS monitors per-agent neuron gradients over a sliding window of T target-network updates and computes a Neuron Gradient Efficiency (NGE) score; neurons with NGE below a threshold α are flagged as futile. For each futile neuron, the inter-agent gradient conflict matrix is converted to an affinity matrix via a Gaussian kernel, and spectral clustering partitions agents into K groups that minimize intra-group conflict. The futile neuron is then cloned K times, with each clone's input weights and bias updated independently by the agents in its assigned group, so that conflicting gradients no longer cancel within a single parameter set. Periodically, clones whose parameter variance has shrunk below a threshold are merged back into a single shared neuron using a regularization loss optimized by a separate SGD optimizer, enabling dynamic regrouping as agent policies evolve.

## Results

GradPS achieves the best test win rate across all evaluated SMAC environments (2c_vs_64zg, 27m_vs_30m, 3s5z_vs_3s6z, 5m_vs_6m, MMM2, 1c3s8z_vs_1c3s9z) and the highest return on all three Predator-Prey configurations (small, medium, large), consistently outperforming six parameter-sharing baselines. GradPS also significantly reduces the percentage of futile neurons in the shared network compared to other PS methods, and generalizes across different value factorization architectures (QMIX, QPLEX, DMIX, RMIX). In terms of computational overhead, GradPS adds only 3–16% extra parameters over full PS and requires less wall-clock training time than SePS, SNP, and NoPS.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GradPS outperforms all six baselines (FuPS, FuPS+id, SePS, SNP, Kaleidoscope, NoPS) on every SMAC and Predator-Prey environment in terms of final win rate or return. Against SePS (Christianos et al., 2021), which clusters agents into groups based on trajectories at the start of training, GradPS achieves higher win rates on all SMAC scenarios and higher returns on all Predator-Prey maps, while also requiring less training time (e.g., 9.74h vs. 12.56h on 5m_vs_6m). Against SNP (Kim & Sung, 2023), which uses structured network pruning to create agent-specific subnetworks, GradPS consistently wins on both performance and training time (e.g., 13.83h vs. 14.95h on MMM2). Against Kaleidoscope (Li et al., 2024), which learns dynamic agent-specific masks over a shared network, GradPS achieves superior win rates and returns across all tested environments. GradPS also uses fewer total parameters than SePS (e.g., 105% vs. 112% of FuPS on Predator-Prey small) and NoPS (e.g., 105% vs. 159%), while maintaining lower training overhead than both.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of multi-task learning's gradient conflict literature (PCGrad, CAGrad, GradNorm) and MARL parameter sharing, but at a granularity neither line has explored: the individual neuron. While prior PS methods (SePS, SNP, Kaleidoscope, ROMA) address homogeneity through agent-level grouping, pruning, or masking, GradPS operates at the neuron level, identifying 'futile neurons' whose updates are canceled by inter-agent gradient conflicts. The concept is explicitly distinguished from dormant neurons (Sokar et al., 2023; Qin et al., 2024), which suffer from low activation rather than conflicting gradients. Spectral clustering is repurposed from its standard role in graph partitioning to group agents by gradient-conflict affinity, and the neuron-cloning mechanism is a novel architectural intervention not present in prior PS or MTL work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The futile neuron concept provides a new diagnostic lens for understanding why PS networks underperform in heterogeneous MARL settings, and the method's low overhead (3–22% extra parameters, comparable training time) makes it practically deployable. However, the approach is tied to value-based factorization methods and relies on a fixed threshold α for identifying futile neurons, limiting its generality. Open questions include whether the number of groups K can be learned adaptively to reflect true agent heterogeneity, whether the technique extends to policy-based MARL, and whether the dynamic restore mechanism can be made more principled than the current variance-based heuristic.
