## Problem

The Lottery Ticket Hypothesis has been theoretically validated for CNNs but only empirically for GNNs, with the critical link between GNN expressivity (the ability to distinguish non-isomorphic graphs) and the existence of winning tickets remaining unexplored. This paper asks whether preserving 1-WL-level expressivity in sparsely initialized GNNs is a necessary condition for finding winning tickets that maintain predictive performance, and whether expressivity loss from pruning can be irrecoverable.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251257/cropped_page5_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes graphs represented as adjacency matrices and one-hot encoded node feature matrices from 10 TU-Dataset benchmarks, along with a binary pruning mask applied to the MLP weights within each message-passing layer at a specified sparsity ratio ρ ∈ [10%, 90%]. It produces pruned GNNs (GIN or GCN) whose expressivity τ is measured as the fraction of non-isomorphic graph pairs in the dataset that the network's final-layer node embeddings can distinguish at FLOAT32 precision, and whose post-training classification accuracy is compared against the dense unpruned baseline to determine winning-ticket status (accuracy drop < 0.05).

## Method

The paper establishes Criterion 1 (a GNN must distinguish all non-isomorphic graph pairs of different classes to classify correctly) and then proves the Strong Expressive Lottery Ticket Hypothesis (SELTH, Theorem 3.2): for any sufficiently overparameterized moment-based GNN with an aggregation rule that separates ego from neighbor features, there exist subsets of maximally expressive computational paths whose trainable weights achieve 1-WL-level expressivity on any finite graph dataset. The proof proceeds by showing (via Lemma A.1) that random pruning of an overparameterized MLP preserves injectivity with high probability, and (via Lemma A.2) that the resulting weights receive non-zero gradients through backpropagation. Theorem 3.3 further links embedding orthogonality to gradient diversity, arguing that expressive initializations accelerate convergence and improve generalization. Lemma 3.5 identifies irrecoverable expressivity loss for Structurally Isomorphic, Feature-Divergent Graphs (SIFDGs) when first-layer pruning cancels feature differences before the update step. Empirically, 13,500 training runs across 10 datasets with GIN and GCN confirm that pre-training expressivity strongly predicts winning-ticket probability and post-training accuracy, while recovery from low expressivity during training is rare.

## Results

Across 13,500 training runs on 10 TU-Dataset benchmarks (GIN and GCN, pruning ratios 10–90%), the paper shows that up to 80% pruning, highly expressive sparse GINs outperform dense unpruned models in post-training accuracy, while less expressive sparse models fail to match dense quality. The probability of a pruned model being a winning ticket (accuracy degradation < 0.05) is high for high pre-training expressivity and low otherwise. Critically, Table 1 shows that the probability of a low-expressivity sparse GNN recovering to high expressivity during training is at most 0.04 (typically 0.00–0.03), confirming that expressivity loss from pruning is largely irrecoverable. A statistically significant Pearson correlation exists between pre-training expressivity (τ_pre) and post-training accuracy across all pruning levels.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against other LTH or pruning methods; instead, it validates its theoretical predictions through internal comparisons. Sparse high-expressivity GINs (up to 80% pruning) outperform the dense unpruned baseline in post-training accuracy, while sparse low-expressivity models at the same pruning ratio underperform the dense model. GIN (moment-based) and GCN (spectral) are compared to confirm generality across GNN families, with GIN showing stronger alignment to the 1-WL expressivity theory. The key empirical contrast is between sparse initializations with high τ_pre versus low τ_pre at matched pruning ratios: high-expressivity tickets consistently achieve better post-training accuracy and are more likely to qualify as winning tickets. The paper also demonstrates that expressivity rarely increases during training (τ_post < τ_pre in nearly all cases), contrasting with the expectation that training could recover lost expressivity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously disconnected research threads: GNN expressivity (benchmarked via the 1-WL test, with GIN as the canonical architecture from Xu et al. 2019 and rooted in the Deep Sets framework) and the Lottery Ticket Hypothesis (Frankle & Carbin 2018; SLTH for MLPs/CNNs from Malach et al. 2020). Prior GNN LTH work (Chen et al. 2021a, Tsitsulin & Perozzi 2023, Sui et al. 2023) focused on pruning efficiency and performance preservation but largely overlooked whether pruning preserves the model's ability to distinguish non-isomorphic graphs. The paper introduces the Strong Expressive Lottery Ticket Hypothesis (SELTH) as a GNN-specific extension of SLTH, formalizing critical computational paths whose removal irrecoverably degrades expressivity, and connects embedding geometry to gradient diversity (Theorem 3.3) to explain why expressive initializations converge faster.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical foundation linking GNN pruning to expressivity preservation, identifying irrecoverable failure modes (SIFDGs) where a single first-layer pruning decision can permanently prevent a GNN from distinguishing toxic from non-toxic stereoisomers—a critical concern in drug discovery. Empirically, across 13,500 runs on 10 datasets, pre-training expressivity is shown to be a strong predictor of winning-ticket status, and training rarely recovers lost expressivity, implying that pruning strategies must be expressivity-aware from the outset. Open questions include extending the analysis to attention-based GNNs (GAT), node-level tasks, and developing practical layer-wise sparsification algorithms that provably preserve injectivity. The theoretical bounds (Lemma 3.6) require knowledge of all isomorphism types and uniform class distributions, limiting direct practical applicability.
