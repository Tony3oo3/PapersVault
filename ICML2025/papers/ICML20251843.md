## Problem

Despite rapid practical progress in spiking neural networks (SNNs), their theoretical understanding remains far behind that of conventional ANNs, particularly for the discrete-time LIF model that dominates current implementations on digital neuromorphic hardware. This paper addresses the fundamental question of what functions discrete-time LIF-SNNs can represent, how many neurons are required for approximation, and how the temporal dimension (latency) and spatial dimension (depth) jointly shape the input space partitioning—questions that had no rigorous answers for the discretized setting.

## Input / Output

The theoretical framework takes a static input vector x ∈ R^{n_in} and produces a real-valued output vector in R^{n_out}. The input is consumed via direct encoding, where the same vector is fed identically at every time step t ∈ [T] (no spike conversion), and the output is decoded via membrane potential outputs—a weighted sum of binary spike activations across all T time steps with learnable coefficients. For empirical validation, the framework is instantiated on CIFAR-10 (32×32 RGB images) with standard classification outputs, where the first hidden layer is deliberately bottlenecked to 20 neurons to isolate the effect of latency and depth on expressivity.

## Method

The paper formalizes the discrete-time LIF-SNN as a modular tuple of spatial architecture, temporal dynamics, coding schemes, and (hyper)parameters, then proves three main theoretical results. First, it establishes universal approximation: with T=1 and L=2 layers, the network realizes any piecewise-constant function on polyhedral regions, yielding order-optimal neuron counts of O(Γ·diam(Ω)/ε) in the first layer and O((Γ·diam(Ω)/ε)^{n_in}) in the second for Γ-Lipschitz targets. Second, it characterizes the input partitioning: each neuron in the first hidden layer generates a family of parallel hyperplanes (because the spatial weight vector is shared across all time steps, producing only shifted copies), and the total number of activation regions scales as Θ(T^{2n_1}) or Θ((T²n_1)^{n_in})—quadratic in T rather than the 2^T that naive counting would suggest. Third, it shows that deeper layers do not create new regions (they only merge them), in stark contrast to ReLU ANNs where regions grow exponentially with depth. Empirically, this predicts that low-latency SNNs with narrow first layers are bottlenecked in ways that deeper layers cannot fix, while high-latency SNNs benefit substantially from richer first-layer partitioning.

## Results

The paper's primary contributions are theoretical: Theorem 3.2 establishes that discrete-time LIF-SNNs with L=2, T=1 are universal approximators of continuous functions on compact domains, with width n₁ = (⌈diam_∞(Ω)/ε · Γ⌉ + 1)·n_in and n₂ = ⌈diam_∞(Ω)/ε · Γ⌉^{n_in} for Γ-Lipschitz functions. Theorem 4.3 shows the maximum number of activation regions scales as Θ(T^{2n₁}) for shallow networks (n₁ < n_in) or Θ((T²n₁)^{n_in}) for wide networks, growing quadratically in T per neuron rather than exponentially. Empirically, on CIFAR-10 with a 20-neuron bottleneck first layer, widening subsequent layers from 20 to 200 neurons yields only ~7% training accuracy gain for T=1, ~20% for T=4/8, and ~30% for T=16, compared to ~21% for the equivalent ANN.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external SNN or ANN methods in a standard benchmark sense; instead, it compares discrete-time LIF-SNNs with varying latency T (1, 4, 8, 16) against a standard ReLU ANN with identical spatial architecture (4 layers, 20-neuron bottleneck) on CIFAR-10 training accuracy. The ANN achieves a ~21% training accuracy gain when widening subsequent layers from 20 to 200 neurons. The SNN with T=16 surpasses the ANN with a ~30% gain, while T=1 lags significantly at ~7%, and T=4/8 reach ~20%. This confirms the theoretical prediction (Theorem 4.3) that higher latency enables richer input partitioning in the first hidden layer, making subsequent layers more effective. The region-counting experiment (Table 1) further validates theory: pre-training region counts approach the theoretical upper bound (e.g., 59 vs. 67 for T=2, n₁=4), while post-training counts drop substantially as the network converges to a simpler decision boundary.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the linear-regions and universal-approximation framework from ANNs (Montufar et al., 2014; Pascanu et al., 2014; Hanin & Rolnick, 2019b) to discrete-time LIF-SNNs, building directly on Khalife et al. (2024) for the T=1 Heaviside-ANN special case. It departs from prior SNN expressivity work (Maass, 1994; Singh et al., 2023; Neuman et al., 2024), which analyzed continuous-time spike-response models with temporal coding schemes, by targeting the discrete-time LIF framework that is actually deployed on digital neuromorphic hardware (Loihi 2, SpiNNaker 2). The key conceptual departure is showing that the temporal dimension (latency T), rather than spatial depth, is the primary driver of input-space partitioning complexity in these networks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This provides the first rigorous theoretical foundation for the most widely used SNN architecture, filling a gap the authors explicitly identify between the rapid practical progress in neuromorphic computing and the lack of solid theory. The finding that regions scale quadratically (not exponentially) with latency, and that depth does not increase region count (unlike ReLU ANNs), reframes how practitioners should think about SNN capacity: latency is the critical knob, but its practical benefit is tempered by vanishing region widths at large T. Open questions include whether random initialization achieves the theoretical region bound, whether the theory extends to dynamic/event-driven inputs, and whether the latency-depth tradeoff can be exploited for energy-efficient inference on neuromorphic hardware.
