## Problem

Developing foundation models for 3D medical volumes is hindered by the cubic-or-higher computational cost of 3D architectures and the scarcity of large-scale volumetric training data (the largest 3D medical dataset has ~160K volumes, orders of magnitude smaller than 2D image datasets). Existing approaches require expensive pretraining on domain-specific 3D data, limiting accessibility for researchers in data-scarce and compute-constrained biomedical settings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250056/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a 3D medical volume of dimensions D×D×D (typically 256³ voxels from MRI or CT) and produces a single flattened embedding vector of size 3Kp² (e.g., 76,800 dimensions for K=100, or 7,680 for K=10), which is ~99% smaller than the raw voxel representation. The volume is consumed as 3×D orthogonal 2D slices (axial, coronal, sagittal), each processed independently by a frozen 2D ViT, so no 3D convolutional or attention operations are ever performed on the volume directly.

## Method

Raptor (Random Planar Tensor Reduction) is a fully train-free pipeline that extracts semantically rich embeddings from 3D volumes by (1) applying a frozen DINOv2-L ViT (304M parameters) to all 3×256 cross-sectional slices to obtain patch-level token embeddings of size 3×D×d×p², (2) mean-pooling across the D slices in each of the three orthogonal directions to reduce to 3×d×p², and (3) multiplying by a random projection matrix R∈ℝ^{K×d} (entries drawn i.i.d. from N(0,1)) to compress the token dimension from d=1024 to K (e.g., 100 or 10), yielding a final flattened vector of size 3Kp². The approach is grounded in the Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma, which guarantees approximate preservation of pairwise distances under random projection, and the authors further derive explicit upper and lower bounds on Raptor distances in terms of raw embedding distances under a slice-smoothness assumption. The overall complexity is sub-cubic in the volume dimension (O(p²K)), and no parameters are trained or fine-tuned at any stage, making the method model-agnostic and directly upgradeable as better 2D foundation models emerge.

## Results

On the 3D Medical MNIST benchmark (6 classification tasks), Raptor (K=100) achieves the best AUROC on 5 of 6 categories (Organ 0.999, Nodule 0.929, Adrenal 0.926, Vessel 0.966, Synapse 0.943) and best accuracy on 4 of 6. On the UK Biobank brain MRI regression benchmark (10 brain regions), Raptor achieves the highest mean r² of 0.389, outperforming the next-best method (Merlin, 0.313) by +24%. Across all 9 classification datasets combined, Raptor improves average AUROC by +3% over SuPreM, +6% over MISFM, +10% over Merlin, +13% over VoCo, and +14% over SLIViT, while remaining entirely train-free.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Raptor outperforms all five pretrained medical volume baselines (SuPreM, MISFM, Merlin, VoCo, SLIViT) on average across the 9 classification datasets, with the largest margins over SLIViT (+14% AUC) and VoCo (+13% AUC). On the 3D MedMNIST benchmark specifically, Raptor achieves the best AUROC on 5 of 6 tasks, losing only on Fracture (0.677 vs. VoCo's 0.699 and Merlin's 0.691), where the paper attributes the gap to violations of the slice-smoothness assumption. On the UK Biobank regression tasks, Raptor dominates with mean r²=0.389 vs. Merlin (0.313, +24%), SuPreM (0.299, +30%), and MISFM (0.242, +61%), winning 9 of 10 individual brain regions. The compact Raptor-B variant (K=10, 10× smaller embedding) matches SuPreM's average classification accuracy and surpasses it on regression, while being 28.8× smaller in embedding size. Notably, Raptor achieves these results without any 3D training, using only a frozen DINOv2-L encoder and random projections, whereas all baselines require extensive pretraining on 5K–160K medical volumes.

## Relation to Prior Work

Raptor departs fundamentally from the dominant paradigm of training 3D architectures (3D ResNet, 3D SwinUNETR, 3D ViT) on large medical volume datasets, as pursued by SuPreM, MISFM, Merlin, VoCo, and SLIViT. Instead, it repurposes a frozen 2D foundation model (DINOv2-L) to extract semantic tokens from orthogonal cross-sections, then compresses the resulting high-dimensional tensor via random projections grounded in the Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma. The approach is conceptually closer to classical dimensionality reduction (random projection, mean-pooling) than to any prior 3D medical imaging method, and it is explicitly model-agnostic, designed to inherit improvements from future 2D foundation models without retraining.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By eliminating the need for costly 3D model training and large curated 3D datasets, Raptor dramatically lowers the barrier to state-of-the-art medical volume analysis, making it accessible in data-scarce and compute-constrained biomedical settings. Key limitations include degraded performance on tasks where slice-level embedding differences are not 'smooth' (e.g., Fracture3D, where the paper's own α_j analysis shows violations of the alignment assumption) and reduced sensitivity to small spatial features below ~16px. Open questions include whether the mean-pooling aggregation is optimal, whether the approach extends to non-rectangular or sparse volumetric data (point clouds, meshes), and whether the smoothness assumption can be relaxed or replaced with learned aggregation.
