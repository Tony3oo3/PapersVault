## Problem

LLM-based formal theorem proving via expert iteration or RL quickly plateaus because the number of correct proofs generated per iteration shrinks exponentially as the model improves, leaving the model with almost no new training signal. Moreover, the model's capability is fundamentally bounded by the difficulty of the fixed training dataset, so it cannot learn beyond that level without collecting new theorems. The paper asks: can a model self-improve indefinitely by generating its own appropriately-difficult training problems?

## Input / Output

The conjecturer receives a formatted prompt containing a system instruction, a lemma extracted from a seed proof, and the seed theorem with its proof, and outputs a new formal conjecture in Lean 4 or Isabelle. The prover receives a single formal statement (either from the training dataset or a generated conjecture) and outputs a complete formal proof, with K=32 independent samples drawn per statement per iteration. The conjecturer's input is deliberately structured around a specific lemma to anchor the generated conjecture to a particular proof technique, and a trivial lemma is sometimes substituted to allow unconstrained generation.

## Method

STP runs a self-play loop between two roles of the same LLM: a conjecturer that proposes new theorems from seed statements and a prover that attempts to prove them. The conjecturer is trained only on conjectures whose empirical pass rate falls in (0, 1/4], ensuring they are barely provable by the current prover, and further filtered by an elegancy criterion (minimum proof length relative to conjecture length) and re-weighted via a Wasserstein-distance projection onto the distribution of unproved dataset statements to prevent mode collapse. The prover is trained via standard expert iteration on correct proofs with pass rate below 1/2, using a replay buffer of the last three iterations. The full pipeline has three stages—SFT initialization on proof libraries, iterative self-play (48–58 iterations generating hundreds of millions of proofs), and a final re-training from the base model on all accumulated correct proofs—yielding a 28.5% pass rate on LeanWorkbook, doubling the 13.1% achieved by expert iteration alone.

## Results

STP achieves 28.5% cumulative pass rate on LeanWorkbook (the training dataset), doubling the previous best of 13.1% from expert iteration. On standard benchmarks, STP reaches 65.0% pass@3200 on miniF2F-test and 23.9% pass@3200 on ProofNet-test, setting state-of-the-art among whole-proof generation methods. On PutnamBench, STP solves 8/644 problems at pass@3200, outperforming the prior best of 6/644. Training required 51.3B tokens (241M proofs, 3.6M conjectures) over 48 iterations in Lean.

## Baselines & Comparisons

STP outperforms all whole-proof generation baselines across every sample budget. At 3200 samples, STP achieves 65.0% on miniF2F-test vs. 54.9% for DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-RL (a 10.1-point gap) and 23.9% on ProofNet-test vs. 22.0% for the same baseline. Even the STP variant trained without miniF2F-valid and ProofNet-valid (57.2% at 128 samples) already exceeds the full DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-RL at 3200 samples (54.9%), demonstrating strong out-of-domain generalization. Against tree search methods, STP at 3200 proofs (~32K steps) matches InternLM2.5-StepProver's 65.9% (at 4.9M steps) with roughly 150× fewer LLM calls. On PutnamBench, STP solves 8/644 problems (pass@3200) versus 6/644 for InternLM2.5-StepProver and 4/640 for DSP. The ablation in Table 3 confirms that the conjecture-based training contributes ~3% additional gain on miniF2F-test (61.2% vs. 58.3% at 128 samples) beyond what is achievable by re-training only on proved LeanWorkbook statements.

## Relation to Prior Work

STP extends the expert iteration paradigm (Anthony et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2024) for LLM theorem proving by adding a conjecturer role that generates new, related statements to provide denser training signals, addressing the sparse-reward bottleneck that causes expert iteration to plateau. The closest prior work is Poesia et al. (2024), which also alternates conjecturing and proving in a self-play loop, but operates in a simplified finite action space and trains from scratch; STP instead works with practical formal languages (Lean, Isabelle) having infinite action spaces and starts from strong pretrained models. The conjecturer's reward design—filtering for barely-provable, elegant, and diverse conjectures via Wasserstein re-weighting—draws on curriculum learning (Portelas et al., 2020) and optimal transport, while the prover's training follows standard RL/expert iteration as in DeepSeek-Prover (Xin et al., 2024b).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

STP demonstrates that a model can self-improve indefinitely on formal theorem proving without collecting new data, by generating its own curriculum of conjectures that are calibrated to the prover's current capability. The doubling of LeanWorkbook pass rate (13.1% → 28.5%) and SOTA results on miniF2F, ProofNet, and PutnamBench validate the approach, and the ablation showing that conjecture training transfers to out-of-domain benchmarks suggests the model learns generalizable proof techniques. Limitations include dependence on a formal verifier, the fact that generated conjectures are variants of existing theorems rather than genuinely novel mathematics, and the observation that many LeanWorkbook statements are unprovable (capping the achievable pass rate well below 100%). Open questions include whether this self-play curriculum can escape difficulty plateaus to tackle truly open problems, and whether the approach generalizes to natural-language reasoning tasks without a formal verifier.
