## Problem

Existing DPO-based RLHF methods such as CPL implicitly treat all observed trajectories as if generated by an optimal policy, causing a likelihood mismatch when data actually comes from diverse or suboptimal behavior policies. This misattribution of policy suboptimality to environmental stochasticity distorts learned likelihoods and degrades performance, especially in offline settings with heterogeneous datasets. The paper asks whether preference data alone suffices for sequential decision-making or whether explicit behavior-policy information is required to disentangle stochasticity from suboptimality.

## Input / Output

The method consumes pairs of trajectory segments (length 64 offline, 32 online) from continuous-control environments, each annotated with a preference label and a behavior-policy label; when the true policy is unknown in offline data, deterministic pseudo-labels (Dirac deltas on observed actions) are used instead. Preference labels are generated by a pretrained SAC oracle that scores segments by estimated regret rather than cumulative return, avoiding the sparsity problem of reward-based scoring. The output is a single learned policy π_ψ (a [256,256] MLP Gaussian actor with ~76k parameters) that directly optimizes preference alignment without any separate reward model, Q-function, or critic network.

## Method

PPL replaces the optimal-advantage score in CPL with a regret-based score that explicitly incorporates the behavior policy, decomposing negative regret into a log-likelihood term (increasing probability of preferred actions) and a sequential forward KL divergence term (quantifying the gap between the behavior policy and the optimal policy). The central theoretical contribution, the Policy Deviation Theorem, proves that the difference between optimal and non-optimal soft Q-functions equals α times the sequential forward KL divergence, yielding a closed-form policy-based expression for regret that is invariant to reward-shaping transformations. This decomposition produces a contrastive KL regularization that aligns the learned policy toward the preferred behavior policy while pushing it away from the less-preferred one, approximated in practice by L-horizon undiscounted KL sums computed over the sampled segments themselves. When L=0 the framework reduces exactly to CPL, and when all behavior policies are assumed to be a shared reference policy it recovers standard DPO, establishing PPL as a strict generalization that requires only ~76k parameters versus ~859k for reward-based baselines like P-IQL.

## Results

On the MetaWorld benchmark across six robotic manipulation tasks, PPL consistently achieves the highest success rates among all methods across four dataset configurations (homogeneous/heterogeneous × dense/sparse). On the most challenging task (Bin Picking), PPL reaches 83.5–87.2% on homogeneous datasets and 59.7–83.8% on heterogeneous datasets, compared to CPL's 1.2–26.5% and P-IQL's 48.0–72.4%. PPL uses only 76k parameters versus P-IQL's 859k (~6.3%), and in the online setting it matches PEBBLE's performance while using only 8.8% of its parameters.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CPL (the primary DPO-based baseline), PPL wins on nearly every task across all four dataset configurations, with the most dramatic gaps on heterogeneous data: on Heterogeneous Dense Bin Picking, PPL achieves 59.7% versus CPL's 1.2%, and on Heterogeneous Sparse Button Press, 83.5% versus 50.8%. Against P-IQL (a reward-based method that jointly learns reward, Q, and value functions), PPL outperforms on most tasks—e.g., 87.2% vs 72.4% on Homogeneous Sparse Bin Picking and 83.5% vs 71.0% on Heterogeneous Sparse Button Press—while using only ~6.3% of P-IQL's parameters (76k vs 859k). PPL also consistently surpasses SFT across all settings. In the online setting, PPL matches PEBBLE (which leverages unsupervised pretraining) in success rate while using only 8.8% of its parameters, demonstrating that policy-labeled preference learning can serve as an efficient online RLHF algorithm without a separate reward model or critic.

## Relation to Prior Work

PPL extends the DPO/CPL lineage by identifying a critical assumption gap: DPO and CPL treat all observed trajectories as if generated by the optimal policy, which is valid in deterministic LLM token-level MDPs but breaks down in stochastic sequential decision-making. The paper replaces CPL's optimal-advantage score with a regret-based score that explicitly incorporates the behavior policy, and derives a contrastive KL regularization from this decomposition. Theoretically, it strengthens DPO's reward-to-policy mapping from a surjection to a bijection (Lemma 3.2) and proves that the Q-function gap between any policy and the optimal policy equals the sequential forward KL divergence (Theorem 3.4), results that prior work (Shaikh et al., Zeng et al.) had only established for contextual bandits or deterministic transitions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The likelihood mismatch diagnosis is practically important for anyone applying DPO-style methods to general RL with stochastic dynamics, and the finding that PPL outperforms CPL by large margins on heterogeneous offline data (e.g., 59.7% vs. 1.2% on Bin Picking) underscores that policy labels matter when data comes from diverse sources. However, the evaluation is confined to six MetaWorld manipulation tasks, the Door-Open environment reveals reward-exploitation issues that limit interpretability, and the online RLHF results, while promising, are sensitive to preference query sampling. Open questions include whether the approach scales to higher-dimensional or language-based RLHF, how to handle genuinely unknown behavior policies beyond deterministic pseudo-labels, and whether the contrastive KL regularization's computational overhead (multi-step rollouts) remains tractable in longer-horizon tasks.
