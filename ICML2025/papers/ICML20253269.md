## Problem

The ML fairness literature relies on rigid, U.S.-centric racial taxonomies (e.g., U.S. Census categories) as categorical 'ground truth' for discrimination auditing, despite race being a social construct with no biological basis. These categories fail to represent mixed-race identities, lack global applicability (particularly in Europe where racial classification is legally restricted under GDPR), and reify race as a fixed biological attribute within digital infrastructures, potentially exacerbating the very disparities they aim to address.

## Input / Output

The empirical components process face images from the Chicago Face Database (597 individuals) and its mixed-race extension CFD-MR (88 individuals) through CLIP ViT-B/32 for embedding analysis; feed text prompts ('a mixed-race person', 'a mulatto person') to Stable Diffusion 3.5 Large and Midjourney to generate 30 images per prompt; and present grayscale Face Race Lightness Illusion stimuli to ChatGPT-4 and Gemini-2.0 (N=10 trials each). The literature review examines 78 ICML and CVPR papers from 2023–2024 for their use of racial categories. Outputs include t-SNE visualizations of embedding space, cosine similarity distributions comparing generated versus real mixed-race images, and VQA brightness judgments.

## Method

The paper's core argument is that ML should abandon categorical race labels in favor of 'constitutive features'—context-dependent attributes that shape the social construct of race rather than treating race as a fixed variable. For tabular data, it proposes connecting the 'race function' approach from economics (a context-specific mapping of individual characteristics into a perceived-race space) with individual fairness and multi-calibration frameworks adapted to context-relevant constitutive features, avoiding the assumption of a causal link between proxies and race. For visual data, it argues phenotypic attributes (skin tone, nose shape, eyelid type, lip shape, hair texture) should be treated as components of racial discrimination rather than proxies for race, using the Face Race Lightness Illusion to demonstrate how multiple facial features interact in racial perception. Three empirical experiments support the position: CLIP embedding analysis showing mixed-race individuals occupy intermediate space between categorical groups; generative AI experiments showing models produce stereotyped, low-diversity images for mixed-race prompts (cross-prompt cosine similarity 0.82–0.87 vs. 0.79 within real CFD-MR); and VQA experiments confirming models replicate human perceptual biases. The paper concludes with EDTAL principles (Early engagement, Decision-making power, Transparency, Localized approaches) for participatory AI implementation.

## Results

In the generative AI experiment, cosine similarity between images generated from 'a mixed-race person' and 'a mulatto person' prompts was 0.8190 (Stable Diffusion 3.5 Large) and 0.7687 (Midjourney), indicating both models produce nearly identical outputs for the two prompts. Generated images exhibit higher internal similarity (0.8694 for SD, 0.8265 for Midjourney) compared to real mixed-race images from CFD-MR (0.7883), revealing reduced phenotypic diversity in AI-generated content. In the Face Race Lightness Illusion test, Gemini-2.0-flash-exp consistently identified one face as darker across all 10 trials, while ChatGPT-4-0 reproduced the illusion in 8 out of 10 trials, confirming that VQA models replicate the perceptual bias observed in humans.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not propose a new method to benchmark against baselines; instead, it uses targeted experiments to illustrate the limitations of categorical race labels. The generative AI experiment compares two prompts ('mixed-race person' vs. 'mulatto person') across three models (Stable Diffusion 3.5 Large, Midjourney, DALL-E 3), finding that SD and Midjourney produce nearly indistinguishable outputs for both prompts (cosine similarity 0.82 and 0.77), while DALL-E 3 refused the 'mulatto' prompt entirely. The generated images are less phenotypically diverse than real mixed-race faces from CFD-MR (internal similarity 0.87/0.83 vs. 0.79), suggesting generative models collapse mixed-race identity into a narrow Black/White binary. The CLIP embedding analysis on CFD/CFD-MR shows significant overlap between Mixed-Race samples and other racial groups, undermining the assumption of discrete racial categories. The FRL VQA experiment compares model perception against the well-established human perceptual illusion, with both Gemini-2.0 and ChatGPT-4 reproducing the bias, indicating that phenotypic features beyond skin tone influence brightness perception in AI systems.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper extends the growing critical literature on racial categories in AI (Crawford & Paglen, 2021; Abdu et al., 2023; Khan & Fu, 2021) by arguing that not only are U.S.-centric census taxonomies inappropriate, but even proposed alternatives like skin tone or phenotypic features risk reifying race if framed as proxies for a fixed category. It departs from prior fairness work that treats race as a categorical protected attribute (Barocas & Selbst, 2016; Angwin et al., 2016) and from subgroup/multi-calibration approaches (Kearns et al., 2018; Hébert-Johnson et al., 2018) by proposing 'constitutive features' drawn from Hu & Kohler-Hausmann's constructivist framework, where the relevant attributes are context-dependent rather than pre-specified. The paper also situates its argument within the European legal-institutional context (GDPR, EU AI Act), where racial classification is largely rejected, contrasting this with the U.S. disparate-impact tradition that dominates ML fairness research.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a foundational assumption in algorithmic fairness—that race can be operationalized as a discrete categorical variable—and provides concrete (if preliminary) empirical illustrations of why this fails, particularly for mixed-race individuals. Its proposed research agenda connecting the 'race function' from economics (Rose, 2023) to individual fairness and multi-calibration offers a promising but unimplemented path forward. Key open questions remain: how to define context-relevant constitutive features without circularity, how to ensure fairness interventions remain effective without any group-level comparison, and how to navigate the tension between strategic essentialism (where categorical labels may be politically necessary) and constructivist critique. The empirical evidence is limited (small sample sizes in the CLIP and generative AI experiments, N=10 in the VQA test), so the conceptual argument carries more weight than the data.
