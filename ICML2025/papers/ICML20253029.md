## Problem

While offline kernel methods like ridge regression have well-characterized learning curves in high-dimensional settings, the performance of online first-order algorithms—particularly single-pass SGD—over specific kernels remained largely unexplored. A central open question is whether SGD can match the minimax optimal rates of offline methods and, crucially, whether it avoids the saturation effect that causes KRR to fail for smooth target functions. This paper fills that gap by providing exact excess risk orders for SGD in kernel regression under source conditions that permit model misspecification.

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes a single pass of n i.i.d. labeled pairs (x_t, y_t) where x_t is drawn uniformly from the unit sphere S^d and y_t = f_ρ*(x_t) + ε_t with Gaussian noise. The kernel is a dot-product kernel K(x,y) = Φ(⟨x,y⟩) on the sphere, which encompasses the NTK of ReLU networks as a special case. The output is the final iterate f_n (or its average over n iterates), and the theoretical contribution is the exact order of the expected excess risk ‖f_n − f_ρ*‖²_{L²} as a function of d, n, and the source smoothness parameter s, in both the high-dimensional regime n ≈ d^γ and the asymptotic regime n ≫ d.

## Method

The paper analyzes single-pass SGD in the RKHS framework using a bias-variance decomposition that separates the population-level dynamics (governed by the covariance operator Σ) from empirical fluctuations (governed by the rank-one operator K_x ⊗ K_x). Two step size schedules are studied: an exponentially decaying schedule η_t = η_0/2^{ℓ−1} (piecewise constant, halving every m = ⌈n/log₂n⌉ iterations) and a constant step size with Polyak-Juditsky iterate averaging. The key mechanism is that the exponentially decaying schedule induces implicit regularization—exponential shrinkage along top eigendirections in early phases followed by variance reduction in later phases—which eliminates the saturation effect that bounds KRR's rate at d^{−min{γ−p, (γ−p+p·s̃+1)/2, s̃(p+1)}} for s > 1. For well-specified problems (s ≥ 1), the decaying schedule achieves the minimax rate Ω(d^{−min{γ−p, s(p+1)}}) up to logarithmic factors; for misspecified problems (0 < s < 1), the averaged-iterate variant achieves the same rate. In the asymptotic regime n ≫ d, the decaying schedule remains optimal for all s ≥ 1, while the averaged variant is limited to s ≥ 1/(d+1) due to the polynomial eigenvalue decay of the NTK.

## Results

The paper establishes that single-pass SGD with exponentially decaying step size achieves minimax-optimal excess risk rates for all well-specified problems (s ≥ 1) in both high-dimensional (n ≈ d^γ) and asymptotic (n ≫ d) settings, specifically O(d^{-min{γ-p, s(p+1)}}) in the high-dimensional regime and O(log^s(n) · n^{-s(d+1)/(s(d+1)+d)}) in the asymptotic regime, matching the minimax lower bounds up to constants. For mis-specified problems (0 < s < 1), SGD with constant step size and averaged iterates achieves the same minimax-optimal rates. Crucially, SGD with exponentially decaying step size avoids the saturation effect that plagues KRR, which is suboptimal for s > 1 in high-dimensional settings and s > 2 in asymptotic settings regardless of the regularization parameter.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is against Kernel Ridge Regression (KRR). In the high-dimensional setting (n ≈ d^γ), KRR suffers from the saturation effect: for s > 1, no choice of regularization parameter λ can achieve the minimax rate, with a lower bound of Ω(d^{-min{γ-p, (γ-p+p·s̃+1)/2, s̃(p+1)}}) where s̃ = min{s,2}. SGD with exponentially decaying step size achieves the full minimax rate d^{-min{γ-p, s(p+1)}} for all s ≥ 1, strictly outperforming KRR in the saturation region. In the asymptotic setting (n ≫ d), KRR is suboptimal for s > 2 (bounded below by n^{-2(d+1)/(3d+2)}), while SGD with decaying step size achieves the optimal n^{-s(d+1)/(s(d+1)+d)} for all s ≥ 1. The paper also compares two SGD variants: exponentially decaying step size vs. constant step size with iterate averaging. The decaying schedule is provably superior for smooth problems (s > 1), as the averaged-iterate variant is inherently limited by a 1/(n²η₀^s) bias factor that prevents optimality when s is large. For mis-specified problems (0 < s < 1), the averaged-iterate variant is preferred since it only requires the basic kernel boundedness property (Property 1c) rather than additional kurtosis conditions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the SGD-in-RKHS analysis initiated by Dieuleveut & Bach (2016), who studied single-pass SGD with constant step size and iterate averaging, by introducing the exponentially decaying step size schedule (from Ge et al., 2019) into the kernel regression framework. It builds on the high-dimensional learning curve characterization of Zhang et al. (2024b) and the eigenvalue decay properties of dot-product kernels from Lu et al. (2023), but departs from prior work by showing that the decaying schedule's implicit regularization eliminates the saturation effect that limits KRR and iterate-averaged SGD for smooth targets. The analysis also connects to the NTK literature (Jacot et al., 2018; Bietti & Bach, 2021) by treating the ReLU NTK as a special case of the dot-product kernel on the sphere.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result provides the first provable minimax optimality of an online first-order algorithm across the full smoothness spectrum (all s > 0) in high-dimensional kernel regression, resolving a gap where only offline gradient flow with early stopping was known to be optimal. The identification of the exponentially decaying step size as the mechanism that overcomes saturation offers a concrete, implementable explanation for why common DNN training schedules outperform naive averaging. However, the analysis is restricted to dot-product kernels on the sphere and the n ≈ d^γ scaling; in the asymptotic regime (n >> d), SGD loses optimality for highly misspecified problems (s < 1/(d+1)), leaving open whether momentum or multi-pass strategies can close this gap. The empirical validation is limited to three simulation settings, so broader kernel and distributional robustness remains untested.
