## Problem

Sampling-based search—generating multiple candidate responses and selecting the best—is a natural paradigm for scaling test-time compute, but its practical utility has been bottlenecked by the difficulty of verification. Prior work demonstrated that Pass@k scales well with parallel sampling, yet it remained unclear how much of the Pass@k − Pass@1 gap can actually be closed in practice, how verification accuracy evolves as the number of candidates grows, and whether simple self-verification (without RL-trained verifiers or process reward models) can be made reliable at scale.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single reasoning question (e.g., an AIME math problem, a LiveBench logic puzzle, or a MATH word problem) as input and produces a single selected best response with a final answer. Internally, it generates k_inf=200 candidate solutions via random sampling at temperature 1.5, then scores each candidate with k_verif=50 independent verification attempts (each a multi-turn conversation that rewrites the solution in theorem-lemma-proof format and systematically scans for errors). When the top candidates score within 5% of each other and disagree on the final answer, pairwise comparison matchups (k_tie=100 each) resolve the tie. The entire pipeline uses only parallelizable blackbox queries to a single language model with no external tools or ground-truth access.

## Method

The paper proposes a minimalist, embarrassingly parallel sampling-based search pipeline (Algorithm 1) that requires no fine-tuning, RL, or custom-trained verifiers. Its key technical contributions are: (1) an implicit scaling phenomenon where sampling more responses indirectly improves verification accuracy because a larger pool contains higher-quality (more rigorous, more complete) correct solutions that are easier to verify; (2) two principles for scaling verification with test-time compute—directly comparing candidate responses to localize errors (exploiting the fact that LLMs have low recall for finding mistakes but high precision when given their locations), and rewriting responses from chain-of-thought into a formal theorem-lemma-proof format that is easier to verify; and (3) a multi-turn verification protocol that decomposes each candidate into lemmas, writes a main proof referencing those lemmas, checks each step for errors, and determines whether any identified error is fatal to the final answer. At sufficient scale (200 samples × 50 verification attempts), this approach lifts Gemini v1.5 Pro above o1-Preview on AIME, MATH, and LiveBench benchmarks, demonstrating power-law scaling on AIME even after self-consistency saturates.

## Results

Using sampling-based search with self-verification (Verification@200), Gemini v1.5 Pro achieves 8/15 (53.3%) on AIME 2024, 467/500 (93.4%) on MATH, 135/200 (67.5%) on LiveBench Math, and 97/140 (69.3%) on LiveBench Reasoning. These results surpass o1-Preview (7/15, 428/500, 131/200, 95/140 respectively) on all four benchmarks without any fine-tuning, distillation, RL, or custom-trained verifiers. Verification@200 also consistently outperforms self-consistency (Consistency@200) across all benchmarks, with the largest relative gains on AIME (8/15 vs. 4/15) and LiveBench Reasoning (97/140 vs. 75/140).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against self-consistency (Wang et al., 2023), Verification@200 wins on all four benchmarks: AIME 8/15 vs. 4/15, MATH 467/500 vs. 460/500, LiveBench Math 135/200 vs. 118/200, and LiveBench Reasoning 97/140 vs. 75/140. Notably, Consistency@1,000 provides no further improvement over Consistency@200 on MATH and even slightly degrades on AIME and LiveBench Reasoning, while Verification@200 continues to scale. Against o1-Preview (OpenAI, 2024), Verification@200 with Gemini v1.5 Pro wins on all four benchmarks: AIME 8/15 vs. 7/15, MATH 467/500 vs. 428/500 (a 7.8-point gap), LiveBench Math 135/200 vs. 131/200, and LiveBench Reasoning 97/140 vs. 95/140. The largest margin over o1-Preview is on MATH, where the non-reasoning Gemini model with sampling-based search substantially outperforms the model explicitly trained for reasoning. The paper also shows that using the cheaper Gemini v1.5 Flash for verification (Pro+Flash) still exceeds Pro's Consistency@200 on all benchmarks, highlighting the cost-effectiveness of the approach.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the best-of-N sampling and self-consistency paradigm (Wang et al., 2023; Cobbe et al., 2021) by replacing plurality voting with language-model self-verification as the selection mechanism, and by systematically studying how both the number of sampled responses and the number of verification attempts scale. It departs from the prevailing assumption that self-verification is fundamentally unreliable and requires process reward models (Lightman et al., 2024), RL-trained verifiers (Snell et al., 2024), or other complex interventions, showing instead that a minimalist blackbox implementation suffices at scale. The paper also connects to the broader test-time compute scaling literature (OpenAI o1, DeepSeek-R1, chain-of-thought, tree-of-thoughts) by positioning sampling-based search as a complementary, embarrassingly parallel strategy rather than a replacement for RL-based reasoning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that sampling-based search with self-verification provides a simple, parallelizable, and arbitrarily scalable baseline that practitioners can adopt without RL, distillation, or custom-trained verifiers, making it a useful reference point for evaluating other test-time compute strategies. The 'implicit scaling' finding—that sampling more responses improves verification accuracy by widening the pool of well-written candidates—offers a mechanistic explanation for why this approach works and suggests it should benefit further from more capable base models. The introduced verification benchmark exposes a critical and underappreciated gap in frontier models' out-of-box verification capabilities, identifying a concrete target for post-training. Limitations include the high per-question cost (~$650), evaluation on a single model family (Gemini v1.5), and the small margin over o1-Preview on some benchmarks, leaving open whether the approach generalizes to substantially harder or more open-ended reasoning tasks.
