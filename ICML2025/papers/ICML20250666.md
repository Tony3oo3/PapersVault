## Problem

Government agencies increasingly deploy risk prediction tools to identify and support the most vulnerable individuals, yet there is no overarching framework to systematically compare the welfare impact of improving prediction accuracy versus expanding screening capacity. This paper fills that gap by developing the prediction-access ratio (PAR) for the specific setting of identifying the worst-off, providing both theoretical characterizations and practical tools for policymakers to decide which lever to invest in.

## Input / Output

The method consumes tabular administrative records (56 numerical and 24 categorical variables covering demographics, labor market histories, and job details) and produces a continuous predicted welfare outcome (unemployment duration in days, capped at 24 months). The screening policy then maps this prediction to a binary decision by thresholding at the α-quantile of predicted outcomes. The key analytical outputs are the policy value V(α, β) — the fraction of truly worst-off individuals identified — and the PAR, which quantifies the marginal welfare gain from expanding access relative to improving prediction, expressed in the Gaussian setting via a bivariate normal CDF parameterized by R².

## Method

The paper formalizes a screening problem where the optimal policy ranks individuals by predicted outcomes and screens the bottom α-fraction, deriving a closed-form policy value V(α, β, R²) = Φ₂(Φ⁻¹(α), Φ⁻¹(β); ρ)/β under a Gaussian error model where prediction quality is fully captured by R². The core analytical contribution is the prediction-access ratio (PAR), defined as the ratio of marginal value from expanding screening capacity to marginal value from improving R², for which the paper proves three key results: (1) PAR diverges as α→0 (Theorem 3.1), (2) local prediction improvements are maximally effective at R²→0 and at R²→1 with α=β (Theorem 3.2), and (3) PAR≥1 for moderate R² ∈ [0.15, 0.85] and α≤β (Proposition 3.3). Empirically, the framework is validated on 274,515 German jobseekers using a CatBoost model (R²=0.15) and a 4-depth decision tree, with prediction improvements simulated by uniformly scaling residuals to achieve target ΔR² increments, confirming that expanding screening capacity dominates prediction improvements in the typical operating regime.

## Results

On the German SIAB-R dataset (274,515 jobseekers, 553,980 unemployment spells), the CatBoost model achieves R² = 0.15 on the test set, consistent with typical social prediction tasks. At the German long-term unemployment cutoff (β ≈ 0.15), the policy value is 0.28 at α ≈ 0.1 and 0.56 at α ≈ 0.25, closely matching Bach et al. (2023) who report 0.29 and 0.58 respectively. The additional screening capacity Δα needed to ensure ≥75% of high-risk jobseekers are identified is consistently ~0.25 across different β values. Theoretically, the prediction-access ratio (PAR) exceeds 1 for R² ∈ (0.15, 0.85) with α ≤ β, confirming that expanding screening capacity is more impactful than improving predictions in the standard operating regime.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against competing prediction methods in the traditional sense; instead, it compares policy levers (screening capacity vs. prediction accuracy) and model complexity. CatBoost outperforms a 4-depth decision tree by ~5% in R², translating to a 1–8% reduction in policy value for the simpler model, but the tree's practical advantages (interpretability, minimal infrastructure) may justify its use if the cost of the CatBoost model exceeds the cost of the additional screening capacity Δα* needed to close the gap. Empirically, the CatBoost results (0.28 at α≈0.1; 0.56 at α≈0.25) closely match the independent findings of Bach et al. (2023) (0.29 and 0.58), validating the experimental setup. Across the three R² scenarios tested (0, 0.15, 0.9), the PAR consistently shows that expanding screening capacity dominates prediction improvements in the moderate-R² regime (R²=0.15) where most real systems operate, while prediction improvements become relatively more valuable only at the extremes (R²→0 or R²→1 with α=β).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Perdomo (2024, ICML), which introduced the prediction-access ratio (PAR) for binary welfare outcomes in allocation problems, to a continuous welfare metric and a distinct policy objective: measuring the fraction of truly worst-off individuals identified rather than changes in overall expected welfare. It also situates itself within the broader prediction-policy-problem literature (Kleinberg et al., 2015) and the applied public-sector profiling literature on unemployment assistance (Desiere et al., 2019; Kortner & Bonoli, 2023; Bach et al., 2023). The key departure is providing both theoretical bounds (Gaussian setting) and a practical empirical methodology for computing PAR, whereas Perdomo (2024) is purely theoretical and restricted to binary outcomes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that prediction is a 'first and last-mile effort' whose marginal value peaks at very low and very high R², while expanding screening capacity dominates in the typical operating regime (moderate R², α ≤ β)—directly challenges the prevailing focus in empirical public policy on incremental model improvements. The paper provides a concrete decision rule (compare cost ratio to PAR) and a practical methodology for computing PAR from administrative data, giving policymakers a principled way to allocate budgets between model development and capacity expansion. Open questions include how the framework extends to non-uniform prediction improvements (e.g., targeted error reduction for subgroups), complex cost structures with fixed and recurring components, and dynamic settings with distributional shift.
