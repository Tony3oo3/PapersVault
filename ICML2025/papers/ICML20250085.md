## Problem

Existing offline hierarchical RL methods rely on learning an explicit high-level policy to generate subgoals, but this approach suffers from three critical failures: subgoal sampling ignores temporal dependencies between states, performance collapses as task horizons grow (e.g., antmaze-giant-stitch scores drop to ~1.0), and there is no mechanism to stitch partial transitions from different trajectories into coherent plans. The paper asks whether subgoal selection can be reformulated as a graph search problem that inherently supports cross-trajectory stitching without requiring a learned high-level policy.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a reward-free offline dataset of state-action transitions (29-dim state vectors for AntMaze, 40-dim for Scene, or 64×64×3 RGB images for pixel variants) and a goal state at test time. It first pretrains a 32-dimensional Temporal Distance Representation (TDR) mapping states to a latent space where Euclidean distance equals minimum transition steps. At execution, given a current state and goal, the system outputs a sequence of subgoals (selected via Dijkstra on the precomputed graph) and, at each step, a primitive action vector (8-dim for AntMaze, 5-dim for Scene) produced by a low-level policy conditioned on a unit direction vector in TDR space pointing toward the selected subgoal.

## Method

GAS eliminates the high-level policy entirely by constructing a graph in TDR space where nodes are cluster centroids of states grouped at uniform temporal-distance intervals H_TD, and edges connect nodes within H_TD. A Temporal Efficiency (TE) metric—defined as the cosine similarity between the optimal transition direction (to the state at temporal distance H_TD) and the actual reached state direction—filters out suboptimal states before clustering, retaining only 2–8% of dataset states and reducing node counts to under 1% of total transitions. Subgoal selection at test time uses Dijkstra's shortest path from the goal to all nodes, then greedily picks the nearest reachable node minimizing total distance to goal. The low-level policy is trained with DDPG+BC using TD-aware subgoal sampling (subgoals placed at fixed temporal distance H_TD rather than fixed step count) and represented as normalized direction vectors in TDR space, ensuring temporal consistency between training and execution. Value function learning uses a TD-aware intrinsic reward (inner product of TDR displacement with a random unit direction) optimized via IQL expectile regression.

## Results

GAS achieves the best normalized return across all 10 state-based tasks in OGBench and D4RL. The most striking result is on antmaze-giant-stitch, where GAS scores 88.3 compared to the previous state-of-the-art HIQL score of 1.0 — an 88x improvement. On antmaze-large-explore (a low-quality, high-coverage dataset), GAS reaches 94.2 while the next-best baselines (HHILP, HIQL) score only 2.4 and 2.9. In pixel-based environments (Table 2), GAS is the only method achieving meaningful performance on visual-antmaze-giant-stitch (55.8 vs. 3.6 for HHILP) and visual-antmaze-large-explore (15.1 vs. 0.0 for all baselines).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GAS outperforms all seven baselines (GCBC, GCIQL, QRL, CRL, HGCBC, HHILP, HIQL) on every state-based task. The largest margins appear in stitching and exploratory settings: on antmaze-giant-stitch, GAS (88.3) surpasses HIQL (1.0) by 87.3 points, and on antmaze-large-explore, GAS (94.2) exceeds HHILP (2.4) and HIQL (2.9) by over 90 points. Among flat goal-conditioned baselines, CRL is the strongest on navigation tasks (95.3 on antmaze-medium-navigate) but collapses on stitching (0.0 on antmaze-giant-stitch) and exploratory tasks. Among hierarchical baselines, HIQL is the strongest overall but still trails GAS by 10.3 points on antmaze-giant-navigate (67.3 vs. 77.6) and by 24.6 points on antmaze-large-stitch (71.7 vs. 96.3). HHILP, which also uses TDR, performs competitively on medium-scale navigation (96.3 on antmaze-medium-navigate, matching GAS) but degrades sharply on larger or noisier datasets. In pixel-based environments, GAS maintains its lead over all baselines, with the gap widening on the hardest tasks (e.g., 55.8 vs. 3.6 on visual-antmaze-giant-stitch).

## Relation to Prior Work

GAS builds directly on the Temporal Distance Representation (TDR) framework from HHILP (Park et al., 2024c), which embeds states into a latent space where Euclidean distance encodes optimal temporal distance. However, it departs fundamentally from prior offline HRL methods (HIQL, HHILP, HGCBC) by eliminating the learned high-level policy entirely, replacing it with a graph constructed via TD-aware clustering and a Dijkstra shortest-path search for subgoal selection. The graph construction approach draws from online graph-based RL (Eysenbach et al., 2019; Lee et al., 2022) but adapts it to offline settings by using TDR-space distances instead of online rollouts for reachability, and introduces the Temporal Efficiency (TE) metric as a novel node-filtering criterion not present in prior graph-based methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a clean, interpretable alternative to learned high-level policies for offline HRL, with the practical advantage that subgoal selection becomes a deterministic graph search rather than a stochastic policy output. The dramatic improvement on antmaze-giant-stitch (88.3 vs. 1.0) demonstrates that explicit graph-based stitching can overcome a failure mode that learned high-level policies simply cannot address in long-horizon settings. Key limitations include the need for per-task tuning of H_TD, degraded performance in visual environments (suggesting TDR quality is the bottleneck for pixel inputs), and the quadratic cost of edge construction that may limit scalability to very large state spaces. Open questions include whether the graph approach generalizes to continuous manipulation with high-dimensional action spaces and whether learned TDRs can be improved to close the visual-environment gap.
