## Problem

Time series residuals, even after standard stationarization (differencing, detrending), exhibit pronounced leptokurtic (fat-tailed) distributions that amplify model variance, destabilize gradients, and cause oscillatory or divergent training dynamics. Existing normalization approaches—instance-wise z-scores, RevIN, Box-Cox—address mean/variance shifts but leave excess kurtosis unaddressed, and the paper demonstrates that stationarization itself can amplify tail-heaviness. This creates a fundamental tension: aggressive clipping erases critical extremal values needed for forecasting rare events, while retaining raw fat-tailed distributions introduces unstable gradients that impede convergence.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a multivariate time series X ∈ R^{C×T} (C channels, T historical steps, typically T=336) and produces F-step-ahead predictions Ŷ ∈ R^{C×F} for F ∈ {96, 192, 336, 720}. The MoF module is applied reversibly: MoF transforms the stationarized residual before the backbone, and MoF⁻¹ inverts the prediction back to the original scale afterward, so the backbone always operates in a bounded, near-Gaussian latent space. The module is channel-wise (each of C channels gets its own spline parameters) and is designed as a plug-and-play wrapper that can be inserted before any existing backbone without architectural modification.

## Method

MoF combines two synergistic components. The Flow layer is a strictly monotonic, invertible piecewise-linear spline with B learnable bins per channel: softplus-activated bin widths and heights are normalized to sum to 2T, knot positions are computed via cumulative sums, and the forward/inverse maps are closed-form linear interpolations within each bin—reducing excess kurtosis from values like 29.66 to below 1 while preserving differentiability and invertibility. The Morph layer is a test-time-trained adaptation module that, at inference, constructs a self-supervised contrastive task (low-rank linear projections with a Bernoulli mask) to iteratively update a small weight matrix W_test via gradient descent; this matrix is up-projected to a B×2 modulation vector and element-wise multiplied with the Flow parameters, effectively re-shaping the spline to counteract instance-level distribution drift. The entire pipeline is trained end-to-end with standard MSE loss and no auxiliary objectives, and the reversible design (MoF before, MoF⁻¹ after the backbone) ensures the backbone sees and emits features within a stable kurtosis range. Paired with a patch-based Mamba backbone, MoF achieves 6.3% average MSE improvement over the closest competitor and 21.7% reduction on the highest-kurtosis dataset (Exchange Rate).

## Results

On eight multivariate time series forecasting benchmarks (ETTh1/h2, ETTm1/m2, Electricity, Exchange, Traffic, Weather), MoF with a Mamba backbone achieves an average MSE of 0.298, outperforming the closest competitor (GLinear, 0.317) by 6.3% and iTransformer (0.343) by 14.9%. On the Exchange dataset, which has the highest median excess kurtosis (74.2), MoF-Mamba reduces MSE to 0.305 versus 0.394 for GLinear—a 21.7% improvement. With a simple linear backbone, MoF-Linear (avg. MSE 0.317) matches GLinear and outperforms iTransformer and PatchTST by 7.6% and 9.2% respectively, while using 21× fewer parameters than iTransformer.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MoF-Mamba achieves first place on 43 of 64 dataset-horizon configurations and second on 27, while no other model exceeds 9 first-place finishes. Against the strongest recent baselines, MoF-Mamba outperforms GLinear by 6.3% average MSE, LiNo by 9.9%, and TimeMachine by 8.1%, with the largest gap (21.7%) on the fat-tailed Exchange dataset (K_excess=74.2). Against established architectures, MoF-Mamba beats iTransformer by 14.9%, PatchTST by 16.9%, DLinear by 9.4%, Autoformer by 84%, Informer by 160%, and the original Transformer by 106%. In the plug-and-play normalization comparison (Fig. 8), MoF with a DLinear backbone outperforms FAN, RevIN, SAN, and DishTS across all 8 datasets and 4 horizons without dataset-specific tuning. In the extended analysis, MoF surpasses IN-Flow (which struggles on high-dimensional inputs like Electricity and Traffic) and FITS (which is sensitive to hyperparameter settings under the unified protocol). MoF-Linear, despite its minimal architecture, matches GLinear's average MSE (0.317) and beats iTransformer and PatchTST, demonstrating that the Flow+Morph normalization contributes most of the gain rather than backbone complexity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of normalizing-flow transformations, test-time training (inspired by Sun et al., 2024), and time series normalization. While prior non-stationarity methods (RevIN, FAN, SAN, DishTS) address mean/variance shifts, and gradient-clipping or adaptive-optimizer work (Gorbunov et al., Hübler et al.) tackles heavy-tailed noise in optimization, none explicitly target the kurtosis amplification that stationarization itself introduces. MoF repurposes piecewise-linear spline flows (from the normalizing-flow literature) as a kurtosis-reducing, invertible feature transform, and couples it with a lightweight TTT mechanism to handle instance-level drift—positioning itself as a distributional preconditioner rather than a backbone architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The demonstration that standard stationarization (differencing, detrending) systematically increases excess kurtosis reframes a widely assumed preprocessing step as a potential source of training instability, opening a new axis of analysis for time series pipelines. The plug-and-play design and strong results on fat-tailed datasets (21.7% on Exchange) suggest broad practical utility, but open questions remain: the TTT module's diminishing returns on long horizons, the compute overhead of test-time gradient updates, and whether the channel-shared parameterization used for high-dimensional settings (Electricity, Traffic) limits per-channel expressiveness. Whether the kurtosis-reduction mechanism generalizes beyond the eight standard benchmarks to truly non-stationary, regime-shifting real-world deployments (e.g., financial crises, grid failures) remains to be validated.
