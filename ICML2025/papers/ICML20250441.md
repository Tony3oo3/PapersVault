## Problem

Prior work on edge-colored clustering (ECC) has focused almost exclusively on minimizing total unsatisfied edges (MINECC), leaving two significant gaps: (1) the equivalent-at-optimality but harder-to-approximate MAXECC objective had no approximation algorithm for hypergraphs and only a 0.3622-approximation for graphs, and (2) all standard objectives treat all colors symmetrically, ignoring whether certain colors are disproportionately unsatisfied. This paper addresses both gaps by providing the first hypergraph MAXECC approximation, improving the graph MAXECC bound, and introducing new fairness-aware ECC variants.

## Input / Output

The method takes an edge-colored hypergraph H=(V,E,ℓ) with node set V, hyperedge set E of maximum rank r, and a color mapping ℓ:E→[k], optionally with nonnegative edge weights ω_e. For the PROTECTED-COLOR variant, the input additionally specifies a protected color c₁ and an integer bound b on unsatisfied edges of that color. The output is a node coloring λ:V→[k] produced by rounding an LP relaxation, along with per-color unsatisfied edge counts m_c that quantify fairness. The LP is solved to optimality (via Gurobi) and the rounding step is negligible in runtime (<0.3s), making LP solving the computational bottleneck.

## Method

For MAXECC, the paper introduces a randomized LP rounding algorithm that generates independent uniform random thresholds α_c per color and a global random color permutation π; each node is assigned the highest-priority color it 'wants' (i.e., whose threshold falls below its LP value). The key technical insight is a representative-node observation (Observation 4) that, for each edge and competing color, identifies a single node whose wanting event dominates, making the per-node events mutually independent and enabling the analysis to extend from graphs to arbitrary-rank hypergraphs with a (2/e)^r/(r+1) approximation. For the graph case, a refined algorithm partitions each node's colors into 'strong' (LP value ≥ 2/3) and 'weak' sets, prioritizing weak colors via the permutation, and achieves a 154/405 ≈ 0.38 approximation through a detailed case analysis. For fairness variants, the ℓ_p-NORM MINECC objective is approximated by rounding a convex relaxation (2-approx for p≥1, 2^{1/p} for 0<p<1), while COLOR-FAIR MINECC (p=∞) is shown FPT in the total number of unsatisfied edges t with an O(2^t · r · |E|) algorithm, and PROTECTED-COLOR MINECC receives a (1/ρ, 1/(1−ρ)) bicriteria approximation via LP rounding with a tunable parameter ρ.

## Results

On six benchmark hypergraphs, the COLOR-FAIR MINECC (CF) algorithm achieves color-fair objective ratios between 1.00 and 1.66, far below the theoretical 2-approximation bound, while maintaining standard MINECC objective ratios between 1.00 and 1.37. The standard MINECC (ST) algorithm achieves optimal (1.00) MINECC objective on all datasets but degrades to 2.48 on the color-fair objective for Walmart-Trips. On average across datasets, CF is only 10.66% worse than ST on the standard MINECC objective, while ST is 32.62% worse than CF on the color-fair objective. For PROTECTED-COLOR MINECC, the PC algorithm satisfies the protected-color constraint in most cases (e.g., on DAWN at 10% budget, PC satisfies the constraint while ST violates it by a factor of 10.3).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its COLOR-FAIR MINECC (CF) and PROTECTED-COLOR MINECC (PC) algorithms against the standard MINECC LP rounding baseline (ST) from Amburg et al. (2020). On the color-fair objective, CF dominates ST across all six datasets: CF achieves ratios of 1.00–1.66 versus ST's 1.26–2.48, with the largest gap on Walmart-Trips (1.56 vs. 2.48, a 37% improvement). On the standard MINECC objective, ST is optimal (1.00) on all datasets, while CF is at most 1.37 (Trivago-Clickout), representing a modest 37% degradation in the worst case. For the PROTECTED-COLOR setting, PC dramatically outperforms ST on constraint satisfaction: on DAWN at a 10% protected-edge budget, ST violates the constraint by a factor of 10.3 while PC satisfies it entirely; on MAG-10 at the same budget, ST violates by 3.60× while PC violates by only 1.01×. CF's runtime is modestly higher than ST (e.g., 314s vs. 157s on Walmart-Trips) due to solving a slightly larger LP, but the rounding step is negligible (<0.3s). Overall, the paper demonstrates that incorporating fairness into ECC incurs a small cost on the standard objective while yielding substantial improvements on the color-fair objective.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the edge-colored clustering (ECC) framework along two axes: (1) it provides the first approximation algorithm for hypergraph MAXECC, building on the LP-rounding techniques of Amburg et al. (2020) and Veldt (2023) for MINECC and the graph-specific MAXECC algorithms of Angel et al. (2016) and Ageev & Kononov (2020), and (2) it introduces entirely new fairness-oriented objective functions (ℓ_p-NORM MINECC, COLOR-FAIR MINECC, PROTECTED-COLOR MINECC) that have no direct precedent in the ECC or chromatic correlation clustering literature. The key technical departure is Observation 4, which replaces the delicate dependent-event arguments in prior graph MAXECC proofs with a representative-node reduction that yields mutually independent events, enabling a clean extension to arbitrary hyperedge sizes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper closes a significant gap by showing that non-trivial constant-factor approximations exist for hypergraph MAXECC when rank is bounded, and it improves the long-standing graph MAXECC bound from 0.3622 to 0.38 — a small but highly non-trivial gain in a problem where even minor improvements have required years of work. The COLOR-FAIR MINECC formulation is notable for being NP-hard at k=2 colors while standard MINECC is polynomial at k=2, revealing that fairness constraints fundamentally alter the complexity landscape. Open questions include whether the MAXECC approximation can be improved beyond LP-rounding techniques, whether COLOR-FAIR MINECC remains hard for bounded k, and whether the 2^{1/p} approximation for p<1 can be tightened. Practically, the experiments show that LP-based fairness algorithms achieve near-optimal solutions (within 1–7% of LP lower bounds) on real datasets, suggesting the theoretical gaps are largely artifacts of the analysis.
