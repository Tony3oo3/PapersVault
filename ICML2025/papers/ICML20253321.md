## Problem

Deep learning phenomena such as benign overfitting, double descent, and overparametrization are widely perceived as mysterious, unique to neural networks, and requiring a fundamental rethinking of generalization theory. This position paper argues that these phenomena are reproducible with simple model classes (linear models, Gaussian processes) and are formally characterized by decades-old generalization frameworks—PAC-Bayes and countable hypothesis bounds—that the deep learning community has largely overlooked.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20253321/cropped_page1_idx5.webp)

## Input / Output

The analytical framework takes a trained model (neural network, GP, or linear model) and its stored parameters as input, and produces a non-vacuous generalization bound as output. The bound is computed empirically in four steps: train the model, measure the training loss (empirical risk), measure the model's stored filesize (upper-bounding Kolmogorov complexity), and substitute into the countable hypothesis or PAC-Bayes formula. Unlike VC dimension or Rademacher complexity, these bounds do not penalize the model for having a hypothesis space that can fit noise, and they become tighter rather than looser for larger models.

## Method

The core contribution is the unifying principle of soft inductive biases: rather than restricting the hypothesis space with hard architectural constraints, embrace an arbitrarily flexible hypothesis space combined with a soft preference for simpler solutions (e.g., order-dependent regularization on polynomial coefficients, Bayesian priors, or compression biases). This is formalized through PAC-Bayes and countable hypothesis bounds where generalization is controlled by the prior probability—or equivalently the Kolmogorov complexity—of the specific hypothesis found, not the size of the hypothesis space. The paper demonstrates this with concrete reproductions: a 150th-order polynomial with order-dependent regularization exhibits benign overfitting, a GP exactly reproduces the CIFAR-10 noisy-label experiment from Zhang et al. (2016), and effective dimensionality of the Hessian tracks the second descent in double descent for both ResNets and linear random feature models. The key mechanistic insight is that increasing model parameters simultaneously increases flexibility (lower empirical risk) and compression bias (lower Kolmogorov complexity of the found solution), because the volume of flat, compressible solutions grows with parameter count. The paper contrasts these frameworks with Rademacher complexity and VC dimension, which penalize hypothesis space size and thus cannot explain the phenomena in question.

## Results

This is a position paper without a single empirical benchmark table; its key quantitative demonstrations include: (1) a 150th-order polynomial with order-dependent regularization (Σ 2^j w_j²) reproduces both benign overfitting and double descent on 1D regression; (2) a Gaussian process with an RBF kernel exactly reproduces the CIFAR-10 noisy-label experiment of Zhang et al. (2016), achieving reasonable generalization while perfectly fitting corrupted labels; (3) PAC-Bayes compression bounds yield a non-vacuous upper bound of 16.6% classification error on CIFAR-10 (95% confidence) for a multi-million-parameter model (Lotfi et al., 2022a). The paper's central claim is that these phenomena are fully characterized by long-standing frameworks (PAC-Bayes, countable hypothesis bounds) rather than requiring new generalization theory.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between generalization frameworks that do and do not explain deep learning phenomena. PAC-Bayes and countable hypothesis bounds (the paper's advocated frameworks) successfully characterize benign overfitting, double descent, and overparametrization, while Rademacher complexity, VC dimension, and fat-shattering dimension do not. Empirically, the GP from Wilson & Izmailov (2020) exactly reproduces the CIFAR-10 noisy-label results originally reported by Zhang et al. (2016) for a ResNet-20, with both models showing marginal likelihood (a PAC-Bayes quantity) that decreases monotonically with more corrupted labels. For double descent, the linear random-feature model (Xw = y with noisy features) reproduces the same U-then-U loss curve as the ResNet-18 from Maddox et al. (2020), with effective dimensionality of the Hessian tracking the second descent in both cases. The paper's key finding is that no phenomenon is unique to neural networks: every 'mystery' result can be matched or exceeded by a simple linear model or GP, undermining the claim that deep learning requires rethinking generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes decades of generalization theory—PAC-Bayes bounds (McAllester, 1999), countable hypothesis bounds (Valiant, 1984), and Solomonoff induction (1964)—to argue that deep learning's 'anomalous' generalization phenomena are already formally characterized by these long-standing frameworks. It departs from the 'rethinking generalization' narrative of Zhang et al. (2016, 2021) by showing that PAC-Bayes and countable hypothesis bounds do explain benign overfitting, double descent, and overparametrization, while Rademacher complexity and VC dimension do not. The unifying concept of 'soft inductive biases' connects Residual Pathway Priors (Finzi et al., 2021), order-dependent regularization, and overparametrization under a single principle, with effective dimensionality (Maddox et al., 2020) providing mechanistic intuition for why larger models generalize better.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central claim—that deep learning's generalization behavior requires no new theory, only better cross-community communication—challenges a dominant narrative and provides a practical design prescription: embrace flexible hypothesis spaces with soft preferences for simpler solutions. Open questions remain about why larger models develop stronger compression biases, the precise mechanism of implicit regularization in neural networks, and whether these bounds can be tightened to more closely track empirical behavior. The paper also acknowledges genuinely distinctive aspects of deep learning (representation learning, mode connectivity, universal in-context learning) that remain poorly understood, positioning this reframing as complementary rather than a complete explanation.
