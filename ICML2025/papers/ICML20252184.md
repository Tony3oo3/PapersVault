## Problem

Industrial designers need to control prototype models through simple natural language instructions, but LLM-based CAD tools fail to achieve targeted control due to three fundamental gaps between designers' high-level, ambiguous, domain-specific language and the low-level, precise, domain-agnostic modeling commands: mismatched abstraction levels, fluctuating semantic precision, and divergent lexical scopes across product categories.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252184/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system takes iterative natural language instructions (e.g., 'make the spout narrower toward the tip') from industrial designers working within a specified product design domain and produces structured DSL programs encoding parts with semantic identifiers, physical subparts, properties, and permissible operations, plus inter-part relationships. These DSL programs are translated into FreeCAD modeling commands that generate rendered 3D models, with the pipeline operating over up to 10 fast-prototyping iterations per design task across eight product domains (teapot, pan, fork, toaster, cleaver knife, sofa, wheelbarrow, unicycle).

## Method

The paper introduces an intermediate-level Domain-Specific Language (DSL) as a latent variable that decomposes the intractable direct mapping from designers' intentions to modeling commands into two tractable sub-mappings (intention→DSL and DSL→commands). The DSL employs a hierarchical probabilistic model where parts, physical subparts, operations, and quantitative values are conditionally generated top-down, with concept-operation bindings enforcing semantic coherence (e.g., 'length' of a 'spout' permits 'increase' but not 'twist'). Domain adaptation is automated through an EM-like reciprocative optimization: an E-step expands the construct space via MCMC sampling from GPT-4o commonsense priors with Dirichlet Process Mixture Model clustering into hierarchical semantic units, while an M-step validates constructs against FreeCAD's actual capabilities using RAG over parsed library documentation, categorizing each as implementable, requiring finer decomposition, or incompatible for pruning. This unsupervised process iteratively refines the DSL until it achieves balanced granularity between designers' semantic richness and modeling commands' geometric precision.

## Results

The paper evaluates targeted control across 8 product design domains (teapot, pan, fork, toaster, cleaver knife, sofa, wheelbarrow, unicycle) with 50 participants over 10 iterations each. On rendering consistency, Our-Int significantly outperforms LLM-Int (t(7183)=80.60, p<.0001) and w/o-Int (t(7246)=63.81, p<.0001) across all domains. On information clarity (measured via cumulative AST depth), Our-Int outperforms LLM-Int (t(14)=4.413, p<.001) and w/o-Int (t(14)=3.277, p<.01). LLM-Int itself significantly beats w/o-Int on rendering consistency (t(7127)=12.37, p<.0001), confirming the necessity of an interface structure.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three methods: (1) w/o-Int, which feeds designers' natural language directly to the LLM-based CAD generator without any intermediate interface; (2) LLM-Int, which uses pure LLM prompt engineering as a lightweight interface; and (3) Our-Int, the proposed hierarchically structured DSL interface with automated domain adaptation. Our-Int wins decisively on both rendering consistency and information clarity against both baselines across all 8 domains, with extremely large effect sizes (t>63 for rendering consistency). LLM-Int in turn significantly outperforms w/o-Int on rendering consistency, demonstrating that some form of interface is necessary. Qualitatively, Our-Int handles fine-grained spatial instructions (e.g., 'attach', 'opposite') and domain-specific part references correctly, while w/o-Int and LLM-Int frequently reference incorrect objects, apply inappropriate operations, or produce physically implausible results (e.g., oversized wheels conflicting with the frame). The information clarity metric further confirms that Our-Int's DSL produces more deterministic, less ambiguous modeling programs than the alternatives.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as an auxiliary interface layer atop existing LLM-based CAD generators (CAD-LLM, OpenECAD) rather than a replacement, introducing a latent-variable formulation where an automatically designed Domain-Specific Language (DSL) mediates between designers' natural language and low-level modeling commands. The DSL design draws on domain-specific language theory (Fowler, 2010) and cognitive science of concept acquisition, while the automated domain specification repurposes MCMC sampling, Dirichlet Process Mixture Models, and EM-style iterative optimization in a novel combination for linguistic interface construction. The paper explicitly distinguishes its unsupervised, commonsense-knowledge-driven approach from program synthesis, which relies on structured exemplar programs and supervised I/O pairs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The work addresses a practical bottleneck in industrial design workflows where designers' high-level, domain-specific instructions are poorly translated into precise modeling commands, and demonstrates that an automatically designed intermediate DSL (at ~$10 per domain) can significantly improve targeted control. The impact statement extends the methodology to smart manufacturing supply chains, where aligning design-team requirements with production capabilities is a costly communication problem. Open questions include whether the Markov-process assumption on iterative design interactions is sufficient, whether non-textual inputs (sketches, gestures, digital clay) can be incorporated, and how to reduce the cost of ground-truth annotation for broader product coverage.
