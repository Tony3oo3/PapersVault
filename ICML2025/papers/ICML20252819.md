## Problem

Despite growing awareness of AI's energy footprint, there is no comprehensive, empirically grounded understanding of inference energy consumption across vision model architectures. Prior studies evaluated only 50–100 models on outdated GPUs, used batch size 1 (unfairly favoring larger models), and relied on naive FLOPs-based energy estimates that the authors show underestimate actual consumption by a geometric mean factor of ~3×. This paper asks: how much energy does inference truly cost, what model and hardware factors drive it, and how should practitioners trade off accuracy against energy?

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes standard RGB images (typically 224×224, with some models supporting variable input sizes) and produces 1,000-class ImageNet predictions. Beyond the class label, the framework outputs per-image energy consumption in millijoules, throughput in images/second, and a composite energy-efficiency score. The evaluation spans six test sets (ImageNet-1K, Real Labels, V2, Sketch, Adversarial, Rendition) to capture robustness, and results are reported across four inference configurations (A100/H100 × PyTorch FP32/TensorRT FP16).

## Method

The authors build an automated benchmarking pipeline that evaluates 1,200 pre-trained models from the Timm library on A100 and H100 GPUs under both PyTorch FP32 and TensorRT FP16, iteratively doubling batch size from 1 to the memory limit to ensure fair hardware utilization. Energy is measured via nvidia-smi onboard power sensors sampled at 100 Hz, following the protocol of Yang et al. (SC24). Key empirical findings include: (1) a log-log relationship between energy and accuracy showing steep diminishing returns (a 10,000× energy range yields only ~50 percentage points of accuracy gain); (2) a multiple linear regression on log(params), log(GMACs), and log(activations) that estimates energy with 34% MAPE, and a throughput×TDP formula with <1% MAPE; (3) TensorRT FP16 reduces energy by a geometric mean of ~4× over PyTorch FP32. The paper also introduces a weighted Manhattan-distance efficiency score and an interactive web application for comparing models under user-defined accuracy/energy thresholds.

## Results

Across 1,200 ImageNet classification models on H100 TensorRT FP16, energy consumption spans four orders of magnitude while accuracy follows a nested logarithmic curve: a 10× energy increase roughly doubles accuracy (40%→80%), the next decade adds only 7% (to 87%), and the third adds just 3% (to 90%). Extrapolating the efficient frontier suggests 100% accuracy would require ~207 MWh per image. The naive FLOPs/TDP-based energy estimation (Desislavov et al.) underestimates actual consumption by a geometric mean factor of 3.13× (A100) and 3.16× (H100), while the paper's throughput-based estimator achieves only 0.97% MAPE and the MLR model (params, Gmacs, activations) achieves 34.73% MAPE. Switching from PyTorch FP32 to TensorRT FP16 yields a geometric mean energy reduction of 3.89× on A100 and 4.02× on H100.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline comparison is against the naive FLOPs-based energy estimation method of Desislavov et al. (2023), which divides model FLOPs by GPU FLOP/s and multiplies by TDP. The paper replicates this calculation and shows it underestimates actual measured energy by a geometric mean factor of 3.13× on A100 and 3.16× on H100, with worst-case underestimation approaching 40×. The paper's two proposed estimation methods substantially outperform this baseline: the MLR model using params, Gmacs, and activations achieves 34.73% MAPE (an improvement of nearly an order of magnitude over the naive method), while the throughput-and-TDP method achieves just 0.97% MAPE, making it effectively exact. Additionally, the paper corrects Henderson et al. (2020) who ran all models at batch size 1, showing that this underutilizes modern GPUs and unfairly favors larger models; the paper's maximum-batch-size protocol reveals that energy consumption correlates strongly with activations (PCC = 0.9023) and FLOPs (PCC = 0.8021), contradicting Henderson's claim of no strong correlation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of inference energy benchmarking initiated by Li et al. (2016), Canziani et al. (2016), Henderson et al. (2020), and Desislavov et al. (2023), scaling from 20–94 models to 1,200 and from older GPUs to A100/H100. It departs methodologically from prior work in two key ways: replacing TDP-based energy estimates with actual nvidia-smi power measurements (following Yang et al., 2024) and optimizing batch size to saturate GPU utilization rather than running at batch size 1. Conceptually, it positions itself within the Green AI and Sustainable AI movements (Schwartz et al., 2020; Van Wynsberghe, 2021) while explicitly correcting the misconception from Henderson et al. that FLOPs and parameters have no strong correlation with energy consumption.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 1,200-model benchmark is orders of magnitude larger than any prior study and provides the first comprehensive, methodologically rigorous map of the accuracy–energy trade-off across modern vision architectures, making it likely to become the reference dataset for efficiency-aware model selection. The finding that the widely-used FLOPs/TDP estimation underestimates real energy by a geometric mean of ~3× (up to 40×) corrects a systematic error in the literature, while the throughput-based estimation (<1% error) offers a practical shortcut when hardware is unavailable. Open questions include generalization to downstream tasks (detection, segmentation), LLM inference, non-NVIDIA hardware, and whether the slowing GPU generational efficiency gains (RTX 5090 only 5.7% over 4090) will persist, all of which the authors flag for future work.
