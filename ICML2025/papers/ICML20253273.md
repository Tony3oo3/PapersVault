## Problem

Political neutrality in AI is widely treated as a design goal, yet the paper argues it is theoretically impossible (due to the subjective, paradoxical nature of impartiality) and technically infeasible (due to biased training data, algorithmic design, and probabilistic generation). What has been missing is a structured framework that replaces the unattainable ideal of absolute neutrality with a taxonomy of achievable approximations, each with explicit trade-offs, so that developers can make informed, context-sensitive choices.

## Input / Output

The evaluation takes curated political text prompts across six categories (voting questions, universal rights statements, harmful questions, political opinion questions, opinion questions with forced left/right viewpoint prefixes, and conspiracy-theory questions in good or bad faith formats), totaling over 6,000 prompts. Each model response is classified into one of six labels—refusal, avoidance, reasonable pluralism, output transparency, no approximation used, or bias—via GPT-4o annotation verified by two human raters (75% average agreement). The paper also proposes a 'Political Nutrition Label' as a system-level output that decomposes a model's ideological leanings along dimensions such as economic vs. social ideology and pro- vs. anti-establishment stance.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a three-level taxonomy (output, system, ecosystem) containing eight approximation techniques, each given a formal mathematical definition and analyzed across five characteristics: utility, safety, clarity, fairness, and user agency. At the output level, techniques include refusal (M(x)=∅), avoidance (semantic distance from direct answer exceeds threshold k), reasonable pluralism (output is a set of m reasonable viewpoints), and output transparency (biased output paired with a bias description). At the system level, uniform neutrality requires consistent outputs across metadata sets, reflective neutrality matches the model to the user's bias, and system transparency documents the system's inherent bias. At the ecosystem level, neutrality through diversity requires variance across n systems to exceed a threshold. For empirical evaluation, the authors curate a U.S.-centric dataset and prompt ten LLMs (GPT-4, GPT-4o mini, Claude-3.5 Sonnet, Gemini-1.5 Pro/Flash, OLMo-2, R1, R1-Distill-Llama, Llama-3.3, Qwen-2.5), annotating responses with GPT-4o using a chain-of-thought prompt template and verifying via human raters, finding that GPT-4 aligns most closely with desired approximations while open-source models like Llama and R1 produce more biased or non-refusal responses to harmful inputs.

## Results

Across six question types (voting, universal rights, harmful, political opinion, political opinion + viewpoint, and conspiracy), GPT-4 aligns most closely with the desired output-level approximations: 88.6% direct factual answers on voting questions, 64.6% on universal rights, 100% refusal on harmful questions, 99.3% reasonable pluralism on political opinion, and 82.4% on good-faith conspiracy questions. Claude-3.5 Sonnet is the most cautious, with 16.7% avoidance and 68.8% pluralism on universal rights (where direct answers are expected). Llama-3.3 70B and R1 are the least restrictive, with 30% and 83% non-refusal rates on harmful questions respectively, and the highest bias rates (Llama: 20.8% on universal rights; R1: 20.81% on political opinion).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 10 LLMs (GPT-4, GPT-4 Mini, Claude-3.5 Sonnet, Gemini-1.5 Pro/Flash, OLMo-2 13B, R1, R1-Distill-Llama 70B, Llama-3.3 70B, Qwen-2.5 72B) against the proposed output-level approximation framework. GPT-4 outperforms all other models in alignment with desired approximations, achieving 100% refusal on harmful questions (vs. 93.3% Claude, 70% Llama, 16.7% R1) and 99.3% reasonable pluralism on political opinion (vs. 98.0% Claude, 92.0% Llama, 69.1% R1). Claude-3.5 Sonnet is the most over-cautious, over-avoiding universal rights questions (16.7% avoidance where direct answers are expected) and over-using output transparency on conspiracy questions (35.3%). Llama-3.3 70B exhibits the highest bias rate on universal rights (20.8%) and political opinion (8.1%), while R1 shows the highest political opinion bias (20.8%) and the lowest harmful-question refusal rate (16.7%). Among open-source models, Qwen-2.5 72B performs best with 0% bias on voting and universal rights, while R1-Distill-Llama 70B shows the worst harmful-question safety (only 4.2% refusal, 25% bias).

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper repurposes concepts from political philosophy (Rawls' reasonable pluralism, Raz's degree-based neutrality, Holmes' marketplace of ideas) and political science to reframe the well-studied problem of political bias in LLMs. Rather than building on a specific technical lineage, it synthesizes existing safety mechanisms (refusal, RLHF, Constitutional AI), fairness-aware training, and pluralistic alignment work (Sorensen et al. 2024, Feng et al. 2024) into a unified three-level taxonomy of 'approximation techniques.' The key conceptual departure from prior bias-mitigation work is the explicit argument that true neutrality is impossible, shifting the goal from elimination to graded approximation, and the introduction of ecosystem-level neutrality through diversity as a distinct concern beyond individual model behavior.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a structured vocabulary and decision framework (8 techniques across output/system/ecosystem levels) that could guide developers navigating trade-offs between safety, fairness, utility, and user agency in politically sensitive contexts. The proposed Political Nutrition Label is a concrete, actionable transparency mechanism that complements existing model cards. However, the empirical evaluation is limited to output-level behavior of 10 models using GPT-4o as annotator (75% human agreement), is US-centric, and does not test system- or ecosystem-level approximations, leaving the most ambitious claims largely unvalidated. Open questions include who should define 'reasonable' viewpoints, how to operationalize the Political Nutrition Label across cultures, and whether approximation techniques at one level conflict with those at another.
