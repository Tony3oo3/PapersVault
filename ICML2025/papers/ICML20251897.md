## Problem

Measuring which covariates drive heterogeneity in treatment effects (CATE) with statistical guarantees remains an open problem. The established LOCO method, adapted to the causal setting by Hines et al. (2022), requires refitting the CATE model for every covariate, incurring high computational cost and, more critically, introducing finite-sample variance that degrades statistical power—particularly with complex deep-learning estimators and the small-to-medium sample sizes typical of biomedical studies. No prior work had analyzed how estimation error from model refitting propagates into the variance of variable importance estimates in the CATE context.

## Input / Output

PermuCATE consumes two independent datasets (D_train, D_test) of (X, A, Y) triples, along with pre-fitted nuisance functions (μ̂₀, μ̂₁, π̂), a CATE estimator τ̂ (e.g., DR-learner), and per-covariate conditional predictors ν̂_j fitted on D_train. It outputs a vector of d importance scores (consistent estimates of total Sobol indices) and associated p-values via a Nadeau-Bengio corrected t-test across cross-fitting folds. The importance is scored using a feasible pseudo-outcome risk (PO-risk) since the oracle CATE is unobservable, and the procedure is agnostic to the specific meta-learner used for τ̂.

## Method

PermuCATE generalizes Conditional Permutation Importance (CPI) to CATE estimation by perturbing the j-th covariate through conditional resampling—estimating ν̂_j = E[X^j | X^{-j}] on D_train, computing residuals r_j = X^j − ν̂_j, and shuffling them to construct a perturbed design matrix—then measuring the increase in a feasible causal risk (PO-risk) relative to the unperturbed prediction. The key theoretical contribution is a finite-sample variance analysis showing that PermuCATE's bias depends only on the error in estimating the conditional distribution ν̂_j (a typically simpler regression problem), whereas LOCO's bias depends on the difference in CATE estimation errors between the full and reduced models, which fluctuates more with dimension and model complexity. This structural difference yields lower variance and higher statistical power for PermuCATE, an effect that is most pronounced with stochastic deep-learning CATE estimators (e.g., CateNet) and small sample sizes. The method is model-agnostic, supports type-I error control via cross-fitting, and avoids the per-covariate refitting cost of LOCO by reusing a single fitted CATE estimator.

## Results

On the IHDP benchmark (747 subjects, 25 covariates), PermuCATE achieves significantly better AUC and statistical power for identifying important variables than LOCO across all three CATE estimators (Causal Forest, CateNet, TabPFN). In the high-dimensional non-linear (HP) simulation with d=50 and d=100 covariates, PermuCATE demonstrates greater statistical power at α=0.05 and suffers less degradation from increased dimensionality. In the low-dimensional (LD) benchmark, PermuCATE requires a smaller minimum sample size to detect all three truly important variables (theoretical Sobol indices Ψ¹=0.75, Ψ²=3, Ψ³=0.75) while both methods converge to the correct asymptotic values. In non-linear scenarios with complex estimators (CateNet, TabPFN), PermuCATE exhibits lower variance, sometimes by orders of magnitude, particularly in small-sample regimes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is LOCO (Hines et al., 2022), the established leave-one-covariate-out procedure adapted for CATE. PermuCATE consistently outperforms LOCO on statistical power: on the LD benchmark it requires fewer samples to detect each important variable, and on the HP dataset it maintains higher power as dimensionality increases from d=50 to d=100. On the IHDP benchmark, PermuCATE achieves better AUC for variable importance ranking and higher power at α=0.05 for all three CATE models (CF, CateNet, TabPFN), while both methods maintain type-1 error below 0.05—except LOCO with CateNet, which shows inflated type-1 error due to stochastic optimization noise from per-covariate refitting. In the linear setting with simple models (Ridge), the two methods show comparable variance, with LOCO having a slightly faster asymptotic decrease; however, in non-linear settings and with deep learning estimators, PermuCATE's variance advantage becomes pronounced. The theoretical explanation is that LOCO's finite-sample bias depends on the CATE estimation error (‖τ₋ⱼ − τ̂₋ⱼ‖² − ‖τ − τ̂‖²), while PermuCATE's depends only on the conditional covariate prediction error (‖νⱼ − ν̂ⱼ‖²), which is typically a simpler estimation problem.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Conditional Permutation Importance (CPI) framework of Chamma et al. (2023) from standard prediction to the CATE estimation setting, creating PermuCATE. It is positioned against the Leave-One-Covariate-Out (LOCO) method adapted to causal inference by Hines et al. (2022), and builds on the theoretical foundations of Sobol-CPI (Reyero Lobo et al., 2025) for the conditional sampling step. A key departure from prior work is the explicit finite-sample variance analysis: while earlier studies focused on type-1 error control and asymptotic consistency, this paper characterizes how estimation error from finite training samples differentially inflates the variance of LOCO versus CPI, a distinction that had been neglected in the variable importance literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

In biomedical and clinical applications where sample sizes are limited and complex deep learning CATE estimators are increasingly used, the lower finite-sample variance of PermuCATE translates directly into higher statistical power for detecting predictive biomarkers — a practically consequential difference that could determine whether informative signals are found. The paper also demonstrates that PermuCATE avoids the computational burden of per-covariate refitting, making it compatible with expensive models like CateNet and TabPFN. Open questions include validation on fully real-world interventional data (the paper relies on semi-synthetic benchmarks), extension to truly high-dimensional settings beyond a few hundred variables, and whether the variance advantage persists under more extreme model misspecification.
