## Problem

Existing LLM reasoning benchmarks predominantly evaluate passive reasoning, where all necessary information is provided upfront and the model simply derives a solution. Active reasoning—where a model must iteratively ask questions and gather missing information from external sources to solve a problem—has received little systematic evaluation, leaving a critical gap in understanding whether LLMs can handle real-world agentic scenarios with incomplete information.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20253170/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model under evaluation receives a partial problem description (a murder case with five suspects, a lateral thinking puzzle, or a 4-digit number to guess) plus task rules, and must generate targeted questions over 25 interaction rounds. Feedback arrives from NPC agents—Llama-3.1-405B for detective cases and situation puzzles (narrative and yes/no responses), and a rule-based oracle for guessing numbers (exact-match and partial-match digit counts). Evaluation uses dual metrics: outcome scores (accuracy, character-level F1, exact match) and process scores measuring the fraction of predefined key questions resolved at each conversational state, enabling fine-grained analysis of question quality over time.

## Method

AR-Bench comprises 6,040 puzzles across three task families (detective cases, situation puzzles, guessing numbers) generated via a four-stage automated pipeline—core sampling, tree-based story expansion, key question extraction, and puzzle synthesis—followed by human verification for logical consistency and solvability. The evaluation simulates multi-round conversations where the player model interrogates NPC agents, with performance assessed through both the informativeness of questions asked (process score) and the correctness of the final answer (outcome score). The paper systematically evaluates eight LLMs (Llama-3.1 8B/70B/405B, Qwen-2.5 3B/7B, QwQ-32B, GPT-4o-mini, GPT-4o) under six reasoning strategies (zero-shot, few-shot, ToT, SFT, DPO) plus two active-reasoning-specific methods (Proactive CoT, UoT), revealing that even state-of-the-art models achieve as low as 35% exact match, that process-score gains plateau sharply after the first few rounds, and that unreliable verifiers and low-quality question generation are the primary bottlenecks.

## Results

On AR-Bench, state-of-the-art LLMs exhibit severe active reasoning deficits: GPT-4o achieves only 35% exact match on Guessing Numbers (GN), while SFT yields a 0 score on GN and DPO underperforms zero-shot on both SP and GN. Process scores show rapid early gains (+7.7% in rounds 5–10) that plateau sharply (+2.5% in rounds 20–25), and even scaling interaction rounds from 25 to 100 lifts GN from 8% to only 18%. GPT-4o reaches just 44.0% and 51.2% process scores on SP and DC respectively within 25 rounds, roughly half the maximum, while human evaluators substantially outperform all tested models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Tree-of-Thought (ToT; Yao et al., NeurIPS 2023), which excels in passive reasoning, fails to provide consistent gains in active reasoning: with Llama-3.1-70B it achieves 52% on SP, underperforming the zero-shot baseline, and gains are task-dependent (strong in GN with a reliable verifier, weak in DC/SP with heuristic verifiers). Proactive CoT (Deng et al., EMNLP 2023) yields only a marginal improvement on SP and no meaningful gain elsewhere, as it optimizes step-wise question prompts but neglects global reasoning. Uncertainty-of-Thought (UoT; Hu et al., NeurIPS 2024) performs worse than the zero-shot baseline, struggling with AR-Bench's large (GN: 5,040 options) or infinite (SP) answer spaces where its elimination-based strategy is inapplicable. DPO (Rafailov et al., NeurIPS 2023), trained on process-score-labeled data, underperforms zero-shot on SP and GN, indicating that preference optimization on question-quality signals does not transfer to active reasoning. Overall, none of the four baselines closes the gap to human performance, confirming that existing passive-reasoning and limited active-reasoning methods are insufficient for the multi-round, information-seeking demands of AR-Bench.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by formally defining 'active reasoning' (AR) as a distinct paradigm from the dominant 'passive reasoning' (PR) evaluation paradigm, where models receive all information upfront. It unifies and extends prior fragments—proactive questioning (Qulac, Abg-CoQA), information-seeking games (20 Questions, Guess My City, MediQ), and lateral thinking puzzles (AgentBench)—into a single benchmark that additionally requires symbolic feedback (GN) and complex multi-step reasoning, dimensions absent from all existing AR datasets. Methodologically, it evaluates the full spectrum of current approaches (zero/few-shot, ToT, SFT, DPO, Proactive CoT, UoT) under a common interactive protocol, rather than proposing a new algorithm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a stark and well-evidenced gap: GPT-4o achieves only 35% exact match on the relatively simple GN task, and every advanced method (ToT, SFT, DPO, Proactive CoT, UoT) fails to close the gap, with SFT scoring 0 on GN. This negative result challenges the assumption that passive-reasoning techniques transfer to interactive, information-gathering settings and points to concrete open problems—reliable verifiers for open-ended tasks, long-context degradation during multi-round interaction, and the absence of training objectives that reward iterative questioning. The benchmark is publicly released and provides a clear target for future work on interactive learning, environment-aware training, and agentic reasoning.
