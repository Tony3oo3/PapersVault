## Problem

The Lottery Ticket Hypothesis identifies sparse masks that match dense model performance, but these masks only work with the exact random initialization used to train the original dense model. When applied to a new random initialization, the mask fails to produce competitive accuracy. This paper hypothesizes that the failure stems from a misalignment between the mask's optimization basin and the new initialization's basin in weight space, and proposes a permutation-based fix.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250018/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method requires two fully trained dense models (to compute the permutation via activation matching on training data), a sparse mask obtained through Iterative Magnitude Pruning, and a new random initialization (rewound to an earlier epoch k) from which sparse training begins. The output is a permuted sparse mask π(m_A) that, when applied to the new initialization, yields a trained sparse model with accuracy approaching the LTH baseline. The permutation is computed layer-by-layer as a Linear Assignment Problem solved via the Hungarian algorithm, and the mask is permuted by permuting both the output dimension of each layer and the input dimension of the next layer.

## Method

The core mechanism is to find a permutation π that aligns the loss basin of a reference dense model A with a new random initialization B, then apply that same permutation to the LTH/IMP sparse mask so it is consistent with B's basin. Specifically, two dense models are trained to convergence, and activation matching (solving a per-layer assignment problem to maximize activation correlation) yields the permutation π. The IMP mask m_A is then permuted as π(m_A), and model B is trained from a rewound checkpoint (epoch k ≪ T) using this permuted mask during both forward and backward passes. The paper further shows that after correcting for variance collapse via REPAIR, the LTH solution and the dense solution remain in the same linearly connected mode, and that wider models yield more accurate permutation matching (lower loss barriers), which explains why the permuted solution approaches LTH accuracy as width increases.

## Results

On ResNet20×{1}/CIFAR-10 at 90% sparsity and rewind point k=25, the permuted mask achieves 89.97% test accuracy versus 88.78% for the naive (non-permuted) mask, while LTH reaches 91.43%. The gap between permuted and naive widens with sparsity: at 97% sparsity (k=25), permuted reaches 86.16% vs. 85.70% for naive. On ResNet50/ImageNet at 95% sparsity (k=25), the permuted solution achieves 62.77% Top-1 accuracy, outperforming the naive baseline (60.77%) by ~2%, though both remain below LTH (68.07%). Permuted models also exhibit higher functional diversity than LTH, with ensemble accuracy of 91.75% vs. 91.43% on CIFAR-10 and 77.85% vs. 75.99% on CIFAR-100.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three sparse training approaches: LTH (original mask + original initialization), Naive (original mask + new random initialization), and Permuted (permutation-aligned mask + new random initialization). The permuted mask consistently outperforms the naive baseline across all sparsity levels (80–97%), model widths (w=1,4,8,16), datasets (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, ImageNet), and rewind points. The advantage grows with sparsity: at 97% sparsity on CIFAR-10 (k=25), permuted achieves 86.16% vs. 85.70% for naive, and on ImageNet at 95% sparsity the gap reaches ~2% (62.77% vs. 60.77% Top-1). However, both permuted and naive solutions remain below LTH, which benefits from training within the original solution basin; the gap to LTH narrows as model width increases (e.g., at w=16, CIFAR-10, 90% sparsity, k=25: permuted=96.11% vs. LTH=96.19%). For functional diversity, permuted models match or exceed IMP solutions (disagreement 0.107 vs. 0.093 on CIFAR-10) and substantially outperform LTH (disagreement 0.035), yielding better ensemble accuracy (91.75% vs. 91.43% on CIFAR-10).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate research threads: the Lottery Ticket Hypothesis (Frankle & Carbin, 2019), which identifies sparse subnetworks trainable to dense-level performance but only with the original initialization, and the permutation-symmetry / linear mode connectivity literature (Ainsworth et al., 2023; Jordan et al., 2023), which shows that independently trained networks occupy the same loss basin modulo neuron permutations. The key conceptual move is diagnosing LTH's failure to transfer across random initializations as a basin misalignment problem and applying activation-based permutation matching (Git Re-Basin) to realign the sparse mask with the new initialization's basin. The paper also extends Paul et al. (2023) by demonstrating that, once variance collapse is corrected via REPAIR, the LTH solution remains linearly connected to the dense solution—a conclusion Paul et al. missed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The LTH is a foundational hypothesis in sparse training, but its practical utility has been severely limited by the requirement to reuse the exact winning-ticket initialization; this paper provides both a mechanistic explanation for that limitation and a concrete (if computationally expensive) fix. The finding that permuted-mask models are substantially more functionally diverse than LTH solutions—approaching the diversity of independently pruned IMP solutions—opens a path toward diverse sparse ensembles without the full cost of iterative pruning. However, the method requires training two dense models to convergence plus permutation matching, which is prohibitive at scale; on ImageNet the permuted solution still lags LTH by several points, suggesting the greedy permutation search is insufficient for large models. Open questions include whether cheaper or earlier matching strategies (the paper's Appendix B hints at this) can reduce the overhead, and whether the approach extends to structured sparsity or non-CNN architectures.
