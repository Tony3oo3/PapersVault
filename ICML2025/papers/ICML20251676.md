## Problem

Designing effective reward functions for RL typically requires extensive human effort, and while VLM-based feedback offers a scalable alternative, prior methods rely on pairwise preference queries that are sample-inefficient, computationally expensive (doubled token processing), and prone to hallucinated preferences when trajectories are of similar quality. Additionally, existing rating-based RL frameworks suffer from training instability due to class imbalance in early-stage feedback and noisy labels from VLMs, which has not been adequately addressed.

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB image observations (224×224 for MetaWorld and ALFRED; 480×480 downsampled to 224×224 for the real robot) paired with a natural-language task description, and in ALFRED also includes a 4D robot pose vector. For MetaWorld, the robot is cropped from images before VLM querying to simplify analysis. The output is a learned reward function (a 4-layer CNN for MetaWorld/real robot, or a transformer for ALFRED, with an ensemble of three models) that assigns per-step rewards to state-action pairs, which then drives policy training via SAC or IQL.

## Method

ERL-VLM queries Gemini-1.5-Pro for absolute Likert-scale ratings (e.g., Bad/Average/Good) of individual trajectory segments using a two-stage prompt that first elicits a behavioral analysis before assigning a rating, rather than comparing trajectory pairs. The core technical contribution is three enhancements to the rating-based RL objective: (1) stratified sampling to ensure every minibatch contains samples from all rating classes, mitigating the severe class imbalance that causes the reward model to collapse onto the dominant class; (2) a class-frequency-weighted loss to further correct for imbalance; and (3) replacing cross-entropy with Mean Absolute Error (MAE) loss, which is provably more robust to the noisy and stochastic labels produced by VLMs. The reward model is trained to match the predicted rating distribution to the collected ratings via a probabilistic model with learned class boundaries, and the resulting reward function is used to relabel the replay buffer for off-policy policy optimization.

## Results

ERL-VLM outperforms all baselines in 6 out of 7 simulated tasks across MetaWorld (3 manipulation tasks) and ALFRED (20 vision-language navigation tasks). On the real-robot evaluation, ERL-VLM achieves success rates of 0.73±0.06 (Sweep Bowl), 0.60±0.10 (Drawer Open), and 0.47±0.12 (Pickup Banana), substantially exceeding both Behavior Cloning and Sparse Rewards baselines. Notably, on ALFRED's PickupObject and PutObject tasks, ERL-VLM surpasses even the environment's sparse reward function, indicating that the learned reward provides useful shaping signals beyond binary success/failure.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CLIP Score and RoboCLIP Score (similarity-based reward signals), ERL-VLM consistently outperforms across all MetaWorld and ALFRED tasks; the similarity-based methods produce noisy, fluctuating reward signals that lead to unstable policy optimization. Against RL-VLM-F (preference-based VLM feedback), ERL-VLM achieves comparable performance on MetaWorld's Drawer Open task but significantly outperforms it on all other tasks, particularly in ALFRED where RL-VLM-F nearly fails to learn meaningful policies due to the limited information content of pairwise preferences under a fixed query budget (only ~75 preference samples per task). Against the environment's own sparse reward (used as an upper-bound baseline in ALFRED), ERL-VLM actually surpasses it on PickupObject and PutObject tasks, suggesting the VLM-derived dense reward captures task-relevant shaping information that the sparse signal misses. Overall, ERL-VLM's use of absolute Likert-scale ratings rather than pairwise comparisons yields more informative and sample-efficient reward learning under the same VLM query budget.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the rating-based RL framework of White et al. (2024) by replacing human raters with a large VLM (Gemini) as the feedback source, and departs from the preference-based VLM feedback approach of RL-VLM-F (Wang et al., 2024a) by querying for absolute Likert-scale ratings of individual trajectories rather than pairwise comparisons. The key methodological contributions are practical stabilizations—stratified sampling, class-weighted loss, and MAE loss in place of cross-entropy—that address data imbalance and label noise inherent to VLM-generated ratings. Unlike similarity-score-based reward methods (CLIP, RoboCLIP), ERL-VLM leverages the reasoning capabilities of large VLMs rather than embedding-space alignment, and unlike code-generation approaches (EUREKA, Text2Reward), it requires no access to environment source code or privileged state.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By demonstrating that a single language task description plus image observations suffice to train effective reward functions across low-level manipulation, high-level navigation, and real-robot settings, the paper offers a practical path toward reducing the human bottleneck in reward design. The finding that learned VLM-based rewards can outperform the environment's own sparse rewards on ALFRED tasks suggests that VLM feedback can inject useful shaping signal beyond what the benchmark provides. Open questions include how well the approach scales to longer-horizon or multi-objective tasks, how to mitigate inherited VLM biases in safety-critical deployments, and whether the current reliance on a single large VLM (Gemini) can be replaced by smaller, cheaper models without significant performance loss.
