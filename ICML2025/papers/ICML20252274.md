## Problem

Prior work on non-stationary infinite-armed bandits (Kim et al., 2022; 2024) only studied rotting rewards, required algorithmic knowledge of non-stationarity parameters, and achieved optimal regret bounds only for certain reservoir regularity regimes (β≥1). This paper provides the first parameter-free optimal regret bounds under an adaptive adversary, relaxes distributional assumptions on the reservoir, and introduces a 'significant shift' measure that captures only the rotting changes truly necessitating re-exploration, yielding tighter bounds when rising non-stationarity is present.

## Input / Output

The agent operates over an infinite arm space where each newly sampled arm's initial mean reward is drawn from a β-regular reservoir distribution (tail mass Θ(x^β) near 1), rewards are [0,1]-bounded, and an adaptive adversary sets subsequent rewards for played arms. The output is a high-probability regret upper bound expressed in terms of realized, agent-dependent non-stationarity measures: the number of significant shifts L̃, total rotting variation V_R, and number of rotting changes L_R — rather than a priori worst-case bounds. Notably, the elimination algorithm's bound does not require an upper bound on reservoir tail mass (κ₂), a relaxation unknown even in the stationary setting.

## Method

Two algorithms are proposed. Algorithm 1 (blackbox) subsamples arms at a doubling-epoch schedule, runs a finite-armed MAB base algorithm (e.g., UCB) on each subsample, and restarts when cumulative empirical regret exceeds a threshold calibrated to the subsample size; the base algorithm only needs a gap-dependent bound in 'mildly corrupt' environments. Algorithm 2 (elimination) subsamples arms and performs randomized successive elimination using importance-weighted gap estimates, restarting when all candidate arms are eliminated; its analysis uses a variance-aware per-arm regret decomposition with self-bounding via Freedman's inequality. The key insight is that empirical regret concentration allows detecting non-stationarity without parameter knowledge, and the significant shift definition — an arm is 'safe' if its cumulative gap over any interval is O(t^{β/(β+1)}) — ensures only rotting changes that actually degrade the best subsampled arm trigger restarts, making rising non-stationarity benign.

## Results

The paper establishes the first parameter-free (adaptive) optimal regret bounds for non-stationary infinite-armed bandits. For β≥1, Algorithm 1 achieves Õ((L+1)^(1/(β+1))·T^(β/(β+1)) ∧ (V^(1/(β+2))·T^((β+1)/(β+2)) + T^(β/(β+1)))), matching the minimax lower bound up to log factors without requiring knowledge of L or V. Algorithm 2 (elimination) further improves the bound to depend only on rotting measures (L̃ significant shifts and V_R total rotting variation), showing that rising non-stationarity is benign. On synthetic rotting data with β=1, both algorithms achieve Õ(T^(2/3)) regret versus AUCBT-ASW's Õ(T^(3/4)), and in piecewise-stationary settings with L=√T changes, the elimination algorithm shows empirically better regret than the √(LT)=T^(3/4) bound of prior work.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines on synthetic data: SSUCB (Bayati et al., 2020) and AUCBT-ASW (Kim et al., 2024). In the rotting scenario (Figure 1, O(1/t) decay rate), both Algorithm 1 (blackbox with UCB) and Algorithm 2 (elimination) outperform all benchmarks across β∈{0.6, 0.8, 1}, with the elimination algorithm achieving the best performance. The theoretical advantage is Õ(T^(2/3)) vs. AUCBT-ASW's Õ(T^(3/4)) for β=1. In the piecewise-stationary setting (Figure 2, L=√T rotting changes, β=1), both proposed algorithms show empirically better regret than AUCBT-ASW's √(LT)=T^(3/4) bound, with the blackbox algorithm performing comparably to the elimination procedure — suggesting the blackbox may also adapt to significant shifts. The performance gap between the elimination and blackbox algorithms increases as β decreases, consistent with the theoretical suboptimality of the blackbox for β<1.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the subsampling framework of Bayati et al. (2020) from stationary to non-stationary infinite-armed bandits, replacing the explore-then-exploit strategies of Kim et al. (2022; 2024) with a blackbox that wraps any finite-armed MAB algorithm (e.g., UCB) or a successive elimination procedure (Even-Dar et al., 2006). It introduces the notion of 'significant shifts'—adapted from the finite-armed setting of Suk and Kpotufe (2022)—as a tighter, agent-dependent measure of non-stationarity that tracks only the most performance-relevant rotting changes. The key methodological departure is a variance-aware per-arm regret analysis using importance-weighted estimates and Freedman-type concentration, which avoids the density regularity conditions on the reservoir that prior works required.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This resolves open questions from Kim et al. (2022; 2024) by delivering the first parameter-free, high-probability, optimal regret bounds for non-stationary infinite-armed bandits, and achieves adaptive rates of the form V_R^{1/(β+2)} T^{(β+1)/(β+2)} ∧ (L̃+1)^{1/(β+1)} T^{β/(β+1)} that are unknown even in the finite-armed setting. The conceptual finding that rising non-stationarity is entirely benign (only rotting variation V_R and rotting change count L_R matter) is a clean structural insight specific to the infinite-armed model where the optimal reward value is known. Open questions include extending these techniques to non-stationary linear or kernel bandits and designing more computationally efficient change-detection procedures.
