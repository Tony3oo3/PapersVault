## Problem

Multi-domain fine-tuning of LLMs suffers from a trade-off where gains in one capability (e.g., code) come at the expense of others (e.g., math, general instruction following). Existing mitigations like DMT and MoS rely on empirical schedules or heuristic reward signals without a fundamental understanding of how individual samples interact, yielding only marginal improvements at high trial-and-error cost. The paper identifies that prior work implicitly assumes inter-sample interactions are static and domain-homogeneous, an assumption the authors show to be false.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a multi-domain mixed dataset (CodeAlpaca for code, GSM8K-RFT for math, Alpaca-GPT4 for general instruction; ~182K samples total) and a pretrained base LLM (Mistral-7B, Llama-3.1-8B, or Qwen2.5-14B), applying LoRA (rank 128, α=512) for parameter-efficient fine-tuning. Internally, it computes Johnson-Lindenstrauss-projected (8192-dim) Adam gradients for every sample to build an N×N interaction matrix, then selects a subset of samples per iteration for training. The output is a single fine-tuned checkpoint evaluated on HumanEval (Pass@1/10), GSM8K-test (accuracy), and AlpacaEval 2.0 (length-controlled win rate and win rate).

## Method

EVIC models the interaction between two samples as the inner product of one sample's projected gradient with the other's projected Adam gradient (a first-order Taylor approximation of loss change), capturing how learning one sample helps or hurts another. The key empirical finding is that these interactions evolve dynamically during training—their signs and magnitudes shift—so the interaction matrix must be recomputed periodically rather than computed once. The training loop iterates: (1) compute JL-projected gradients for the full dataset at the current checkpoint, (2) build the N×N interaction matrix, (3) select all samples whose row sum (total influence on the dataset) is non-negative, (4) fine-tune on that subset, and (5) repeat. A 5% random warm-up phase precedes the iterative stages to adapt the model from the pretraining distribution to the SFT distribution. This dynamic, sample-level curriculum yields a higher performance-to-sample ratio (up to 2.11× fewer steps than MTL for Qwen2.5-14B) while covering nearly all samples over the course of training.

## Results

EVIC achieves the best average (AVG) score across all three model architectures: 37.4 on Mistral-7B-v0.3, 44.3 on Llama-3.1-8B, and 57.0 on Qwen2.5-14B, outperforming the strongest baseline by 4.1 points (Mistral), 0.9 points (Llama), and 1.0 points (Qwen). On Mistral-7B, EVIC reaches 57.1% GSM8K accuracy, 37.8% Pass@1, and 56.9% Pass@10 on HumanEval, alongside 9.8% length-controlled win rate on AlpacaEval 2.0. Notably, EVIC achieves these results with fewer training steps than all baselines, yielding a performance-to-sample ratio of 1.29× (Mistral), 1.33× (Llama), and 2.11× (Qwen) over MTL.

## Baselines & Comparisons

EVIC outperforms both DMT and MoS on the average metric across all three model architectures. Against DMT (Dong et al., 2023), EVIC gains 13.2 points on Mistral-7B (37.4 vs. 24.2), 0.8 points on Llama-3.1-8B (44.3 vs. 43.5), and 2.4 points on Qwen2.5-14B (57.0 vs. 54.6); DMT's weakness is coding (P@1 of 3.1 on Mistral vs. EVIC's 37.8), while it retains a slight edge on general-domain win rates. Against MoS (Wu et al., 2024), EVIC leads by 4.1 points on Mistral-7B (37.4 vs. 33.3), 0.9 points on Llama-3.1-8B (44.3 vs. 43.4), and 1.0 points on Qwen2.5-14B (57.0 vs. 56.0); MoS handles imbalanced domain distributions better than DMT but its heuristic reward signals do not directly capture the model's optimization dynamics. Additionally, EVIC requires fewer total training steps than both baselines (e.g., 3,318 vs. 4,269 steps on Mistral-7B), confirming a superior performance-to-sample ratio.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the gradient inner-product formulation from LESS (Xia et al., 2024a), which was designed to estimate train-test influence for targeted data selection, to compute train-train interactions for multi-domain curriculum learning. It departs from prior multi-domain fine-tuning approaches (DMT, MoS) that rely on empirical domain-level scheduling or heuristic reward signals by operating at sample-level granularity and, crucially, by updating the interaction matrix iteratively during training. The key conceptual departure from the broader influence-function literature is the observation that inter-sample interactions are not static properties of the data but evolve as the model's parameters change, motivating a dynamic rather than one-shot selection strategy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that inter-sample conflicts are dynamic rather than purely semantic challenges a widely held assumption in multi-domain fine-tuning and provides a principled mechanism for sample-level data management that outperforms domain-level heuristics. The 1.29–2.11× performance-to-sample ratio improvement is practically significant for practitioners with limited compute budgets, especially for larger models like Qwen2.5-14B. However, the theoretical understanding of why EVIC naturally covers nearly all samples without explicit constraints remains open, and the method has only been validated on three domains with LoRA fine-tuning, leaving questions about scalability to more domains, full fine-tuning, and larger model scales.
