## Problem

Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) have proven effective for interpreting text-only LLMs, but extending them to multimodal LLMs (MLLMs) is non-trivial due to modality fusion and the difficulty of isolating cross-modal representations. Moreover, existing interpretability methods are largely disconnected from practical alignment: they explain model internals but do not directly improve training. The paper asks whether a bottom-up multimodal interpretability tool can simultaneously reveal cross-modal feature structure and serve as a data-quality signal for alignment.

## Input / Output

SAE-V consumes the hidden-state tensor H ∈ R^{l×m} from a single layer of an MLLM (layer 16 for LLaVA-NeXT-7B, layer 8 for Chameleon-7B), where l spans both text tokens (vocabulary size 32,000) and vision tokens (vocabulary size 64). It produces a sparse activation matrix Z ∈ R^{l×n} (n = 65,536 or 131,072 features), per-feature cross-modal weights computed as cosine similarity between top-K text-token and top-K vision-token embeddings, and a ranked dataset or filtered image-patch set. Notably, no auxiliary scoring model is required—the MLLM itself generates the hidden states that SAE-V decodes, making the filtering pipeline fully intrinsic to the target model.

## Method

SAE-V adopts the standard SAE architecture (linear encoder with ReLU, feature-dictionary decoder) but is trained on multimodal data (Obelics) rather than text-only corpora, yielding features that are sensitive to both text and vision tokens. The key contribution is a three-stage cross-modal data-filtering algorithm: (1) a small sample of the dataset is passed through the MLLM and SAE-V to collect, for each feature, the set of text and vision tokens that activate it above a threshold δ; (2) each feature's cross-modal weight is the cosine similarity between its top-K text-token embeddings and top-K vision-token embeddings, quantifying how well the feature captures joint image-text semantics; (3) every data point is scored as the sum of cross-modal weights of its activated features, and the dataset is ranked by this score. This intrinsic metric requires no additional model or annotation, and when the top-scoring subset is used for DPO fine-tuning, it achieves >110% of full-dataset performance with <50% of the data across LLaVA-NeXT-7B, Chameleon-7B, and LLaVA-NeXT-Vicuna-13B.

## Results

On LLaVA-NeXT-7B with the Align-Anything dataset, SAE-V-based data filtering (cosine similarity) achieves a LLaVA-Bench score of 104.20 (108% of the full-dataset baseline) using only 20% of the data, while the co-occurrence filter peaks at 108.17 (115%) with 50% data. On Chameleon-7B, the cosine similarity filter reaches 52.13 (120% of full-dataset performance) with 70% data, and on RLAIF-V it achieves 54.23 (125%) with just 10% data. SAE-V models also demonstrate superior reconstruction over standard SAE, with reconstruction loss 38.3% lower on MLLMs and 50.6% lower when transferred to base LLMs, and a correlation coefficient of 0.84 between average cross-modal cosine similarity score and MLLM benchmark performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is random data selection, against which SAE-V-based filtering consistently outperforms at every data proportion across all tested models and datasets. On LLaVA-NeXT-7B, the cosine similarity filter scores 106.8 at 20% data versus 97.0 for random (a 9.8-point gap), and the co-occurrence filter peaks at 108.17 at 50% data versus 93.7 for random. On the weaker Chameleon-7B model, the gap widens further: SAE-V reaches 52.13 (120% of full-dataset) at 70% data while random plateaus around 43–44. The only external method comparison is against the IFD metric (Li et al., 2024a), which the authors replicate in the multimodal setting; SAE-V achieves comparable peak performance to IFD but without requiring training an additional cherry-picking model, making it more generalizable and computationally efficient. Across ablations on LLaVA-NeXT-Vicuna-13B (116.67 at 40% data) and on RLAIF-V (125% of full-dataset at 10% data), SAE-V maintains its advantage over random, confirming architecture- and dataset-agnostic effectiveness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Sparse Autoencoder (SAE) paradigm from Bricken et al. (2023) and Huben et al. (2024), which decompose polysemantic LLM representations into monosemantic features, to the multimodal setting. While Zhang et al. (2024) first attempted SAE analysis on MLLMs, SAE-V goes further by introducing cross-modal feature weighting (cosine similarity between text and vision token activations) and repurposing the interpretability signal as a data filtering metric for alignment. On the data filtering side, it positions itself alongside LIMA, ALPAGASUS, and IFD (Superfiltering), but distinguishes itself by requiring no additional annotation model or cherry model—relying solely on the MLLM's own latent representations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical value lies in providing a self-contained, interpretable data filtering pipeline for MLLM alignment that avoids the overhead of training separate scoring models, which is significant given the cost of multimodal annotation. The 0.84 correlation between average cross-modal cosine similarity scores and MLLM benchmark performance suggests a measurable link between internal feature structure and downstream capability, opening a path toward diagnosing alignment failures. Key limitations include evaluation restricted to text-vision modalities and two architectural families (CLIP-based and early-fusion), with no theoretical grounding for why cosine similarity of top-K token activations should proxy data quality. Extending to audio, video, and embodied settings, and establishing the mathematical relationship between cross-modal feature geometry and model performance, remain open.
