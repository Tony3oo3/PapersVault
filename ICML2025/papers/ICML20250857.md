## Problem

While prior work has localized individual attention heads or FFN neurons to specific functions, it remains unclear how combinations of modules inside an LLM form structured groupings that collectively enable task execution. Existing methods typically require carefully designed prompts or task-specific instructions to elicit functionality, leaving the mechanism by which a single model internally organizes its attention heads to support different tasks unexplored.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250857/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference, the method takes raw task input text (e.g., a sentence to translate or a word to classify) with no instruction prompt, combined with a pre-trained binary attention head mask of shape n×h (layers × heads per layer). The model then directly produces task-specific outputs—translations, classifications, or extractions—without any explicit task description. The mask itself is learned by freezing all model parameters and optimizing only a small set of head weights, making the intervention parameter-free and reversible.

## Method

The core method trains a discrete binary mask over attention heads using a Gumbel-Softmax relaxation: all model parameters are frozen, and a weight matrix M (initialized positive so σ(M)≈1) is optimized on task data fed as input||output without instructions, with Gumbel noise and a decreasing temperature schedule encouraging convergence to 0/1 values. The key finding is that the selected heads form interdependent 'functional pathways'—removing heads in any order other than the learned pathway delays or disrupts the behavior shift, and the pathway reconstructs the information flow that explicit instructions would normally trigger, as evidenced by increasing cosine similarity of layer hidden states between masked and instructed models in deeper layers. A secondary contribution shows that scaling down (rather than zeroing) heads outside the pathway via a factor α∈[0,1] can further improve task performance, suggesting the pathway acts as a de-noising mechanism that suppresses interference from heads serving other functions.

## Results

On Llama3.1-8B-inst, the learned attention head mask (removing ~15-20% of heads) achieves translation PPL of 0.504–1.322 across 6 English→X language pairs without any instruction prompt, matching or beating the instructed model (PPL 0.550–1.303) and substantially outperforming the uninstructed original (PPL 2.172–2.730). On 6 representative simple tasks, the mask lifts accuracy from near-zero (0.00–0.15) to 0.76–1.00, exceeding the instructed baseline in most cases (e.g., sentiment 0.86 vs. 0.59, conll2003_location 0.92 vs. 0.41). On IWSLT2017, applying the mask alongside instructions further improves BLEU by 0.017–0.023 over the instructed-only model (e.g., en-zh 0.347 vs. 0.322).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external methods but instead establishes four internal baselines: (1) the original model with no instructions or mask, (2) the model with explicit task instructions, (3) a random mask selecting the same number of heads, and (4) cross-model mask transfer (applying the base model's mask to the instruct model and vice versa). The learned mask (w/ M) dramatically outperforms the uninstructed original on all tasks (e.g., PPL drops from 2.643 to 1.017 on en-zh; accuracy jumps from 0.01 to 0.86 on sentiment) and matches or exceeds the instructed model on most metrics (ROUGE-L 0.748 vs. 0.599 on en-es; accuracy 0.92 vs. 0.41 on conll2003_location). The random mask consistently worsens performance relative to the original (PPL 2.909 vs. 2.643 on en-zh), confirming that the learned selection is non-trivial. Cross-model transfer shows partial functionality: applying the base model's mask to the instruct model still reduces PPL (2.269 vs. 2.643 original) but falls well short of the model's own mask (1.017), indicating that instruction fine-tuning introduces additional head-specific functionality beyond what pre-training alone provides.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the differentiable binary mask training paradigm from Csordas et al. (2021) and Fang et al. (2024, MaskLLM) to the specific problem of identifying which attention heads in a frozen LLM are responsible for a given task. It departs from prior attention-head interpretability work (Todd et al., Jin et al., Wu et al.) in a key way: those methods require task-specific prompts or gradient signals to locate important heads, whereas here the mask is trained on raw task data with no instructions, revealing that the model's inherent head combinations already encode task functionality. The paper also contrasts with FFN-neuron localization literature by showing that attention heads alone (not just FFN key-value memories) can carry sufficient functional information to trigger task behavior.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that ~15–20% of attention heads form interdependent 'functional pathways' that can be activated without any instruction prompt offers a new lens on LLM interpretability and suggests a practical route to parameter-efficient inference: scaling down heads outside the pathway can actually improve task performance (lower PPL, higher BLEU). Limitations include that the effect is weaker or absent in very small models (<1.5B), the analysis is restricted to attention heads (FFN layers are not masked), and the 'functional pathway' concept, while empirically supported, lacks a formal graph-theoretic or mechanistic characterization. Open questions include whether similar pathways exist in FFN layers or MoE experts, whether these pathways are stable across fine-tuning, and whether they can be exploited for structured pruning or task-routing in multi-task deployments.
