## Problem

Existing test-time adaptation (TTA) methods rely on self-supervised signals like entropy or confidence that become unreliable under severe distribution shifts, leading to adaptation failures. While active TTA with full-class labels can mitigate this, the labeling cost is prohibitively expensive for real-world deployment. The paper introduces TTA with binary feedback—a setting where an annotator only indicates whether the model's prediction is correct or incorrect—reducing labeling effort by log(num_class) bits per sample while still providing a meaningful adaptation signal.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pre-trained classifier, a streaming batch of 64 unlabeled test images, and k=3 binary feedback labels per batch (less than 5% of the batch) that are queried on the model's own predictions for the most uncertain samples. The binary feedback is a single bit (correct/incorrect) on the model's predicted class, not a full-class label. Output is the updated model parameters after E=3 adaptation epochs per batch, producing a class prediction for each test sample. The method operates in a continual/streaming setting where the test distribution evolves over time (e.g., corruption type changes), and uses MC-dropout (N=4 stochastic forward passes) for uncertainty estimation throughout.

## Method

BiTTA formulates test-time adaptation as a reinforcement learning problem using the REINFORCE policy gradient, where the model prediction is the action and MC-dropout softmax is the policy. It employs a dual-path optimization: (1) Binary Feedback-guided Adaptation (BFA) selects the k least-confident samples via MC-dropout, queries binary feedback, and applies ±1 rewards (minimize cross-entropy for correct, maximize for incorrect) stored in FIFO memory pools; (2) Agreement-Based self-Adaptation (ABA) identifies confident unlabeled samples where the deterministic prediction agrees with the MC-dropout prediction and applies a reward of 1 (minimize cross-entropy). The combined loss L_BiTTA = α·L_BFA + β·L_ABA is optimized via gradient descent with BN statistics frozen per batch. This design lets BFA explore new patterns through sparse feedback while ABA stabilizes existing knowledge on the majority of unlabeled samples, avoiding the over-reliance on noisy self-supervised signals that causes standard TTA to fail.

## Results

BiTTA achieves 87.20% average accuracy on CIFAR10-C (severity 5), 62.49% on CIFAR100-C, and 40.85% on Tiny-ImageNet-C, outperforming all TTA and active TTA baselines by an average of 13.3 percentage points. On the PACS domain generalization benchmark, BiTTA reaches 75.57% (domain-wise) and 72.18% (mixed stream), surpassing the active TTA baseline SimATTA by 12.9 and 3.0 points respectively. Notably, BiTTA outperforms full-class active TTA (SimATTA with oracle labels) under equal labeling cost, achieving up to 32% higher accuracy while using only binary (correct/incorrect) feedback.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BiTTA outperforms all 11 baselines across every dataset. On CIFAR10-C, it leads the strongest TTA baseline SAR* (83.78%) by 3.4 points and the active TTA baseline SimATTA* (81.09%) by 6.1 points. The gap widens dramatically on CIFAR100-C, where BiTTA (62.49%) beats SAR* (60.70%) by 1.8 points and SimATTA* (43.69%) by 18.8 points, while several baselines (EATA* at 2.24%, TENT* at 20.89%) collapse under the harder 100-class setting. On Tiny-ImageNet-C, BiTTA (40.85%) edges out SAR* (39.82%) and EATA* (39.09%), and substantially outperforms SimATTA* (26.06%) and SoTTA* (12.84%). On PACS, BiTTA achieves 75.57% domain-wise accuracy versus SimATTA*'s 62.63%, and 72.18% mixed-stream versus 69.21%. On large-scale ImageNet-C, BiTTA (36.59%) marginally beats SoTTA* (36.02%), and on ColoredMNIST it reaches 96.75% versus SimATTA*'s 93.66%. The paper also shows that replacing the oracle with GPT-4o for full-class labels degrades SimATTA significantly (40% classification error), whereas BiTTA's binary feedback remains robust.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the REINFORCE policy gradient framework (Williams, 1992) and Monte Carlo dropout for Bayesian uncertainty estimation (Gal & Ghahramani, 2016), repurposing both for test-time adaptation. It departs from existing unsupervised TTA methods (TENT, EATA, SAR, SoTTA) that rely solely on self-supervised confidence metrics, and from active TTA (SimATTA) that requires expensive full-class labels, by introducing a middle-ground setting where only a single bit of binary feedback (correct/incorrect) per selected sample guides adaptation. The RLHF literature in NLP (Ouyang et al., 2022; Shankar et al., 2024) motivates the use of sparse binary signals, but this is the first work to formalize binary feedback as a TTA setting and to unify it with unlabeled-sample self-adaptation in a single policy gradient objective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a single bit of binary feedback can outperform full-class labels in TTA challenges the assumption that more label information is always better, and offers a practical path to real-world deployment where annotators can quickly verify predictions without selecting among many classes. Limitations include the continued need for an oracle (even if lightweight), the computational overhead of MC-dropout (4× forward passes), and sensitivity to feedback quality when using automated models like GPT-4o. Open questions include reducing oracle involvement through more dynamic sample selection, handling systematically biased or noisy feedback, and extending the approach to on-device or memory-constrained settings.
