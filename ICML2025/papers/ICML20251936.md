## Problem

Most automated LLM jailbreaking methods search for a single adversarial prompt, overlooking the multi-turn conversational dynamics that real users exploit. While human red teamers dynamically adapt techniques across turns—suppressing refusals, escalating hypotheticals, and layering attacks—no prior automated system combines multiple adversarial prompting strategies in extended conversations with the target model.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251936/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

GOAT takes a violating conversation objective (drawn from JailbreakBench's 100 behaviors) and a set of 7 predefined attack definitions (name, definition, exemplar conversations) embedded in the attacker LLM's system prompt via in-context learning. The attacker LLM (a general-purpose Llama 3 model with no red teaming fine-tuning) produces structured four-part reasoning outputs (observation, thought, strategy, response) at each turn, while the target LLM only receives the final user-facing prompt, simulating a closed-box setting. Conversations are capped at 5 turns by default, and success is scored post-hoc by an external Llama 3.0 70B judge against the original goal, reporting ASR@k over k independent conversation attempts.

## Method

GOAT is an agentic red teaming system that pairs a general-purpose attacker LLM with a target LLM in a multi-turn conversation loop, where the attacker dynamically selects, combines, and layers 7 adversarial prompting techniques (Hypothetical, Persona Modification, Dual Response, Response Priming, Refusal Suppression, Topic Splitting, Opposite Intent) based on the target's responses. The key mechanism is Chain-of-Attack-Thought reasoning: at each turn, the attacker LLM is instructed to produce an observation of the conversation trajectory, a reflective thought on progress toward the goal, a chosen attack strategy, and the actual adversarial reply—extending standard Chain-of-Thought prompting to adversarial decision-making. Attacks are injected purely through in-context learning in the system prompt, requiring no fine-tuning of the attacker model and enabling easy extensibility by adding new attack definitions in plain language. Unlike Crescendo, which requires a separate judge model call at every turn to assess escalation, GOAT uses the attacker model's own reasoning to track progress, reducing LLM calls per turn by a factor of three while achieving higher ASR within the same 5-turn budget.

## Results

GOAT achieves ASR@10 of 96% against Llama 3.1 8B, 91% against Llama 3.1 70B, and 94% against GPT-4o on the JailbreakBench dataset, outperforming the prior multi-turn baseline Crescendo across all target models. On the AILluminate benchmark, GOAT reaches 43% ASR versus Crescendo's 25%, and on JailbreakBench 55% versus 28%. GOAT achieves these high success rates within just 5 conversational turns, using three times fewer LLM calls per turn than Crescendo since it does not require a separate judge model during the conversation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Crescendo (Russinovich et al., 2024), the primary multi-turn baseline, GOAT achieves higher ASR@1 and ASR@10 across all target models, with the gap widening on the AILluminate benchmark (43% vs. 25%) and JailbreakBench (55% vs. 28%). In an extended 50-turn experiment against Llama 3.1 70B, GOAT reaches 75% ASR@1 while Crescendo plateaus at 56%, and GOAT requires only one LLM call per turn versus Crescendo's two (attacker + judge). Against PAIR (Chao et al., 2025) on the JailbreakBench leaderboard, GOAT achieves 30% ASR on Llama 2 7B and 46% on GPT-4, outperforming PAIR (0% and 34%) and AIM (0% and 0%). GOAT also uses a significantly lower query budget, achieving its peak performance within 5 turns compared to Crescendo's need for more turns to reach comparable (but still lower) success rates.

## Relation to Prior Work

GOAT extends the Chain-of-Thought prompting paradigm (Wei et al., 2023b) into an adversarial 'Chain-of-Attack-Thought' reasoning loop, where an attacker LLM observes, reflects, selects a strategy, and generates a follow-up prompt at each turn. It departs from the dominant single-turn jailbreaking literature (gradient-based suffix optimization, tree search, universal prompts) by framing red teaming as a multi-turn conversational problem, building directly on Crescendo (Russinovich et al., 2024) as the prior multi-turn baseline. The key conceptual shift is treating a repertoire of human-discovered attack techniques (dual response, hypotheticals, persona modification, etc.) as a composable toolbox injected via in-context learning, rather than optimizing a single adversarial string.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is that LLM safety training must account for multi-turn, multi-technique adversarial conversations rather than only single-prompt robustness, and that a general-purpose model with no red-teaming fine-tuning can automate this at scale. Limitations include a plateau in attack success after roughly 15–20 turns, resistance from Claude 3.5 Sonnet v2, and a constrained set of only seven attacks, leaving open whether richer attack chains or longer contexts would substantially change the picture. The paper also raises the question of whether training on GOAT-generated adversarial data is sufficient to close the gap, and whether the approach generalizes to multimodal or tool-augmented LLMs.
