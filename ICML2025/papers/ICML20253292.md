## Problem

Current self-improving LLM agents rely on fixed, human-designed meta-processes (extrinsic metacognition) that fail to generalize across shifting task domains and become increasingly misaligned as agent capabilities evolve. The paper argues that sustainable, generalized self-improvement requires agents to develop intrinsic metacognitive learning—the ability to autonomously evaluate, plan, and refine their own learning processes—rather than depending on externally imposed, static mechanisms.

## Input / Output

As a position paper, this work does not propose a new algorithm with standard I/O. It takes the landscape of existing self-improving LLM agents (STAR, Voyager, Generative Agents) as its analytical input and produces a formal three-component framework (metacognitive knowledge, planning, evaluation) that reinterprets these systems along an intrinsic-to-extrinsic spectrum. The output is a structured taxonomy of metacognitive responsibilities, two identified failure modes of extrinsic approaches, and a set of open questions for shared metacognition, evaluation, and scalable oversight.

## Method

The paper introduces a formal framework defining metacognitive learning as a bi-level process where a metacognitive layer monitors, evaluates, and regulates a lower-level learning process, decomposed into three components: metacognitive knowledge (assessing capabilities, task demands, and learning strategies), metacognitive planning (deciding what and how to learn), and metacognitive evaluation (tracking progress and reflecting on learning effectiveness). Through detailed case studies of STAR, Voyager, and Generative Agents, the paper maps each system onto this framework, revealing a progression from fully extrinsic to partially intrinsic metacognitive control. The key analytical contribution is identifying two failure modes of extrinsic metacognition—domain/task distribution shift and capability-mechanism mismatch—and arguing that intrinsic metacognition, where the agent performs metacognitive functions internally, is necessary for scalable self-improvement. The paper further proposes shared metacognition as a practical middle ground where metacognitive responsibilities are optimally distributed between humans and agents, and outlines open challenges in finetuning, evaluating, and safely overseeing intrinsic metacognitive capabilities.

## Results

This is a position paper and does not report quantitative experimental results or benchmark metrics. Instead, it presents a formal framework for metacognitive learning (comprising metacognitive knowledge, planning, and evaluation) and qualitatively analyzes three existing self-improving agents—STAR, Voyager, and Generative Agents—through case studies to illustrate a spectrum from extrinsic to intrinsic metacognitive processes. The paper's central claim is that intrinsic metacognitive learning is necessary for scalable, generalized self-improvement, supported by qualitative reasoning rather than empirical measurements.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not conduct head-to-head experimental comparisons between methods. The closest analog to baselines are the three case-study systems analyzed qualitatively: STAR (extrinsic metacognition, fixed task ordering and learning strategy), Voyager (partially intrinsic metacognition with internalized knowledge and planning but fixed learning strategy), and Generative Agents (intrinsic metacognitive reflection at the meta-level). The paper argues qualitatively that Voyager outperforms extrinsically guided baselines including an expert-designed curriculum, and that agents with greater intrinsic metacognitive functions achieve more sustained progress in capability diversity and novelty. However, the authors explicitly note that substantiating these claims 'will require rigorous, systematic studies,' confirming that no quantitative comparisons are provided.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper does not introduce a new method but reframes the entire landscape of self-improving LLM agents (STAR, Voyager, Generative Agents, Reflexion, OS-Copilot) through the lens of human metacognition (Flavell, 1979; Pintrich, 2002). It draws on the metareasoning tradition (Russell & Wefald, 1991) to formalize a three-component framework—metacognitive knowledge, planning, and evaluation—and argues that existing self-improvement systems rely on extrinsic, human-designed metacognitive loops that fail under domain shift and capability growth. The paper carves new conceptual ground by distinguishing intrinsic from extrinsic metacognition and showing, through case studies, that agents with more internalized metacognitive functions (Voyager over STAR; Generative Agents' reflection over Reflexion) achieve more sustained and diverse progress.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies two concrete failure modes—domain/task distribution shift and capability-mechanism mismatch—that explain why current self-improvement pipelines stall, and positions intrinsic metacognitive learning as the necessary remedy. Key open questions remain: how to optimally distribute metacognitive responsibilities between humans and agents (shared metacognition), how to finetune and evaluate intrinsic metacognitive abilities themselves, and how to build scalable oversight that evolves alongside agent capabilities. As a position paper, its impact lies in providing a structured vocabulary and research agenda for the next generation of self-improving agents, particularly the observation that many foundational metacognitive capabilities already exist in current LLMs but are not yet harnessed in a closed-loop, adaptive manner.
