## Problem

Standard bandit algorithms produce opaque exploration strategies whose regret scales with √(KH), making them unsuitable for high-stakes settings like clinical diagnosis where fast, interpretable decision plans are essential. While prior knowledge of a finite collection of bandits can improve worst-case rates, no existing framework connects the classification complexity of identifying the test task to the achievable regret, nor does it yield interpretable exploration plans. This paper bridges that gap by showing that under a separation condition, the regret of meta learning bandits is fundamentally governed by how hard it is to classify the test task.

## Input / Output

The method takes offline access to M linear contextual bandit simulators (each mapping a context x ∈ ℝ^d and arm k ∈ [K] to a reward distribution) and a fixed context distribution P, with a separation parameter λ ensuring pairwise distinguishability. It produces a pre-computed decision tree exploration plan that, at test time, adaptively probes arms to classify which bandit in the collection is the unknown test task, then switches to exploiting the optimal arm for that task. The plan uses soft splits (λ-bands around thresholds) to remain statistically robust to estimation errors from meta training, and the entire plan is interpretable as a sequence of threshold tests on empirical mean rewards.

## Method

The core contribution is a classification view of meta learning bandits: the test task is identified by running a greedy decision tree classifier over the collection, where each node tests whether the empirical mean reward of an arm exceeds a threshold, and the tree is built to maximize the evenness of hypothesis splits. A novel classification-coefficient C_λ(Π_C) quantifies the worst-case number of hypotheses that survive per round, and the paper proves that the Explicit Classify then Exploit (ECE) algorithm achieves regret O(λ^{-2} C_λ(Π_C) log²(MH)) with a nearly matching lower bound via the decision-estimation coefficient. The practical DT-ECE variant estimates bandit parameters via linear regression during offline meta training, builds the tree with a greedy approximation (giving O(log M) depth overhead), and uses soft splits to tolerate misspecification. The key insight is that separation between bandits transforms the exploration problem into a classification problem, breaking the √(MH) barrier and yielding interpretable plans analogous to clinical diagnosis flowcharts.

## Results

The paper's primary quantitative contribution is theoretical: DT-ECE achieves a test regret of O(λ⁻² C*λ(M) log²(C*λ(M)·M·H)) over H rounds, matching a near-optimal lower bound of Ω(λ⁻² C̃(M)) up to logarithmic factors. Empirically, on the hardest instance (M=40, K=40, λ=0.4), DT-ECE identifies the correct test task using fewer than 1000 samples on average, after which regret plateaus near zero. On the hard family (M=5, K=10), DT-ECE commits to the optimal policy faster than mTS, though it incurs slightly higher initial regret due to its purely exploratory classification phase.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares DT-ECE against two latent-bandit baselines: mUCB (Azar et al., 2013) and mTS (Hong et al., 2020a). On the hard instances where optimal and informative arms do not overlap, mUCB fails catastrophically—its optimism drives it to pull nearly-optimal but non-informative arms, causing regret to grow steadily, while DT-ECE and mTS both classify the task quickly and plateau. On the random instances (rand-10-20 and rand-40-40), mUCB performs considerably better than on hard instances but still does not match the classification efficiency of DT-ECE or mTS. DT-ECE is on par with mTS in overall regret on both families, with the trade-off that DT-ECE's exploration plan is fully interpretable (a decision tree) and pre-computable offline, whereas mTS's posterior-based exploration is opaque and adaptive. The authors note that DT-ECE and mUCB/mTS are 'orthogonal solutions for different applications rather than direct challengers,' with DT-ECE prioritizing interpretability and worst-case guarantees.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the latent bandits / meta learning bandits framework (Hong et al., 2020a; Kveton et al., 2020) by introducing a separation assumption and, crucially, a classification view that was absent from all prior work in this area. The regret analysis builds on the decision-estimation coefficient (Chen et al., 2022a) to derive a lower bound, while the algorithmic contribution (DT-ECE) departs from the UCB/TS-style approaches used in prior latent bandits work by prescribing an interpretable decision tree plan. The paper explicitly positions itself as orthogonal to mUCB and mTS: rather than competing on regret alone, it targets interpretability and fast exploration, with the classification-coefficient providing a new theoretical lens that no prior bandits paper had considered.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The classification-coefficient provides a fundamental and tight characterization of when meta learning bandits is easy versus hard, bridging two previously disconnected areas (classification complexity and bandit regret). The interpretable decision tree plans are directly relevant to high-stakes applications like clinical diagnosis where opaque statistical strategies are unacceptable. Key open questions include extending the framework to MDPs and reinforcement learning (where the decision tree would resemble a hierarchical option-based strategy), designing practical versions that go beyond worst-case guarantees, and generalizing the classification-regret link to low-rank and structured bandits. The empirical validation is limited to small non-contextual instances, and the authors acknowledge that a fully practical ECE implementation is future work.
