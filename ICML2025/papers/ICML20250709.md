## Problem

Classical dimensionality reduction results for Euclidean optimization problems have target dimensions that grow with dataset size n, even when the data has low intrinsic dimensionality. Prior work tying the Johnson-Lindenstrauss transform's performance to the doubling dimension λ (a data-dependent quantity capturing intrinsic dimensionality) had only been established for minimization problems like facility location, k-center, and nearest-neighbor search. This paper asks whether a similar data-dependent target dimension of O(λ) suffices for a broad class of maximization problems, including maximum matching, max-TSP, max-spanning tree, and various diversity measures.

## Input / Output

The method takes a point set P ⊂ R^d with doubling dimension λ and a target dimension t = O(ε^{-2}λ log(1/ε)) (or O(ε^{-2}λk² log(k/ε)) for k-hypermatching), and produces a projected point set G(P) ⊂ R^t via a Gaussian JL map. The guarantee is that every (1+ε)-approximate solution to the optimization problem on G(P) is automatically a (1+O(ε))-approximate solution on P, meaning any solver can be applied in the projected space without further analysis. The paper also establishes matching lower bounds showing that t = Ω(λ) is necessary, and a sharp threshold phenomenon where dropping below O(λ) dimensions causes √2 distortion for max-matching.

## Method

The core mechanism is a hierarchical ball decomposition: for a maximum matching M of P, the authors construct a nested sequence of balls B_0 ⊃ B_1 ⊃ ... where each ball's radius halves, and every matching edge at level i has length Ω(radius of B_i). At each level, an ε-net of size (2/ε)^λ is used, and the JL lemma bounds both pairwise distance preservation on the net and ball expansion (via Lemma 1.2 from Indyk & Naor 2007). The total distortion is controlled by a Markov's inequality argument showing the accumulated error across all levels is O(ε·opt(P)) with high probability. For the √2 lower bound, the authors analyze the projected standard basis vectors as i.i.d. Gaussians on the unit sphere and construct a large matching via a bipartite graph whose edges connect near-antipodal pairs, using degree concentration and LP rounding. The unconditional √2-approximation (without doubling assumption) leverages Tverberg graphs: a perfect matching whose diametral balls have a common intersection point, combined with the fact that the 1-median cost is a √2-approximation to max-matching.

## Results

The paper proves that a Gaussian JL map to target dimension t = O(ε^{-2}λ log(1/ε)) preserves (1+ε)-approximate solutions for max matching, max TSP, max spanning tree, max k-coverage, and subgraph diversity measures, where λ is the doubling dimension of the input set. This dependence on λ is shown to be tight via Ω(λ) lower bounds. Empirically, projecting to dimension 20 yields speedups of 6.01×–121.34× across three problems (max matching, remote clique, max k-coverage) and three datasets (MNIST, CIFAR embeddings, synthetic basis vectors), with the CIFAR dataset showing the largest speedup (121.34× for max k-coverage).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is primarily a theoretical paper without direct method-to-method comparisons; the empirical section instead contrasts high-intrinsic-dimension versus low-intrinsic-dimension versions of the same datasets under identical projection dimensions. On MNIST (784-dim), the low-doubling-dimension version (digit '2' only) achieves the same relative error at ~20 projected dimensions that the high-doubling-dimension version (digit '2' + Gaussian noise) requires at ~50 dimensions. On the synthetic dataset, the low-doubling-dimension version (cumulative sums of basis vectors, doubling dim O(1)) dramatically outperforms the high-doubling-dimension version (basis vectors, doubling dim Ω(log n)), even though the low-doubling set has twice the cardinality—demonstrating that doubling dimension, not dataset size, governs the quality of dimension reduction. The speedup table (Table 2) shows that computing in the projected 20-dimensional space is 6–121× faster than in the original ambient dimension, with CIFAR embeddings (6144-dim) benefiting most due to the large ambient dimension.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the framework of data-oblivious dimension reduction with data-dependent target dimension, pioneered by Indyk & Naor (2007) for nearest-neighbor search and subsequently applied to facility location and single-linkage clustering by Narayanan et al. (2021). The key conceptual departure is moving from minimization problems (clustering, facility location) to maximization problems (max matching, max TSP, max spanning tree, diversity measures), where the analysis requires fundamentally different tools such as Tverberg graph structure (Pirahmad et al., 2024) and a novel multi-scale ball decomposition. The probability bounds from Makarychev et al. (2019) on Gaussian JL maps serve as a technical backbone, but the paper's main contribution is showing that the doubling dimension λ_X, rather than dataset size |X|, governs the required target dimension for a broad class of maximization objectives.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that for maximization problems, the intrinsic geometry of the dataset (captured by doubling dimension) — not its cardinality — determines how much dimension reduction is possible while preserving solution quality, which has direct practical implications for high-dimensional data analysis and diversity maximization in machine learning. The sharp threshold phenomenon (√2 distortion appearing when dimension drops slightly below O(λ), yet persisting at O(1) dimensions) is a new structural insight with no known analogue in prior work. Open questions include whether the √2 threshold extends to other problems (the authors conjecture it does for max TSP), whether Tverberg graph results generalize to tours (which would yield √2 rather than 2-approximation for max TSP without doubling assumptions), and whether the framework extends to non-Euclidean or non-linear settings.
