## Problem

Despite strong empirical evidence that GNNs can solve or guide QP solvers, the theoretical understanding of what GNNs can and cannot represent for QP tasks remains largely absent. This paper asks whether message-passing GNNs, known to be non-universal approximators for general graph functions, nonetheless possess sufficient expressive power to predict the feasibility, optimal objective value, and optimal solution of linearly constrained quadratic programs (and their mixed-integer variants).

## Input / Output

The input is a bipartite LCQP-graph (or MI-LCQP-graph) where constraint nodes and variable nodes are connected by edges weighted by entries of A, and variable nodes are additionally connected by edges weighted by entries of Q; node attributes encode constraint RHS/type, objective coefficients, bounds, and (for MI-LCQP) integer indicators. Outputs come in two forms: a graph-level scalar (feasibility indicator or optimal objective value) computed by aggregating all node features, and a node-level vector (optimal solution) where each variable node receives its own predicted value. The graph-level output uses a single readout function over all node embeddings, while the node-level output conditions each variable's prediction on both its own embedding and the global graph context.

## Method

The core technical contribution is a separation-based analysis: the authors prove that for convex LCQPs (Q ⪰ 0), any two LCQP-graphs indistinguishable by the WL test (and hence by all GNNs) must share identical optimal objective values and optimal solutions, via a key lemma showing that averaging over WL-equivalent variable classes under a PSD quadratic form never increases the objective. This separation result, combined with Lusin's theorem and the Stone-Weierstrass theorem, yields universal approximation guarantees for feasibility, objective, and solution mappings. For MI-LCQPs, the authors construct explicit counterexamples (symmetric constraint structures with integer constraints) where WL-indistinguishable instances have different feasibility, objectives, or solution sets, proving GNNs are not universal. They then identify two precisely defined subclasses—GNN-analyzable (constant A and Q entries within WL color classes) and GNN-solvable (all variable nodes receive distinct WL colors)—on which universal approximation is restored, with efficient O((m+n)·(nnz(A)+nnz(Q))) verification via the WL test.

## Results

The paper's primary contributions are theoretical: GNNs universally approximate feasibility, optimal objective, and optimal solution for convex LCQPs (Theorems 3.2–3.4), while for MI-LCQPs they succeed only on identified GNN-analyzable/solvable subclasses (Theorems 4.6–4.7). Numerically, a trained GNN achieves an average relative error of 6.31×10⁻⁴ on 1,000 synthetic LCQP instances, slightly better than OSQP run to 10⁻³ relative error. In terms of speed, the GNN with batch size 1,000 processes each instance in 0.089 ms on average, roughly 50× faster than OSQP's 4.48 ms per instance, while batch size 100 already reaches 0.504 ms (~9× faster). On real datasets (Maros-Mészáros convex LCQPs and 73 GNN-solvable QPLib MI-LCQPs), GNNs with embedding sizes up to 512 achieve near-zero fitting errors for both objective and solution mappings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The only direct solver baseline is OSQP (Stellato et al., 2020), an operator-splitting QP solver. OSQP solves each instance in 4.48 ms on average (single-instance, no batching), while the GNN at batch size 1 is actually slower (53.62 ms) due to GPU overhead. However, the GNN's advantage grows dramatically with batch size: at batch 100 it is ~9× faster (0.504 ms vs 4.48 ms), and at batch 1,000 it is ~50× faster (0.089 ms vs 4.48 ms). Importantly, the GNN achieves a slightly better solution quality (average relative error 6.31×10⁻⁴) than OSQP's target tolerance of 10⁻³. The paper does not compare against other GNN-based QP solvers or learning-to-solve methods; its empirical focus is on validating the theoretical expressive-power guarantees rather than benchmarking against competing neural approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the GNN expressive power analysis for linear programs (Chen et al., 2023a) and mixed-integer linear programs (Chen et al., 2023b) to the quadratic setting, where the nonlinear term x^T Q x fundamentally alters the separation properties. The proof technique builds directly on the WL-test equivalence for GNN expressive power (Xu et al., 2019) combined with Stone-Weierstrass-type arguments, but the key new ingredient is Lemma A.4, which shows that for convex QPs, WL-indistinguishable instances must share the same optimal objective via a convexity-based averaging argument. The paper also distinguishes itself from the concurrent Wu et al. (2024) study of QCQPs by using a bipartite (rather than tripartite) graph construction and by providing a complete treatment of the mixed-integer case with explicit characterizations of GNN-friendly subclasses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first theoretical characterization of exactly when message-passing GNNs can and cannot represent QP properties, resolving a gap between widespread empirical success and known GNN limitations. The convexity boundary is a clean and actionable insight: practitioners working with nonconvex QPs or highly symmetric MI-LCQPs should expect fundamental GNN failures, while the efficiently verifiable GNN-analyzable/GNN-solvable criteria (O((m+n)·(nnz(A)+nnz(Q))) via WL test) provide a practical pre-check before investing in GNN training. Open questions include training dynamics, generalization guarantees, size/depth requirements, and extension to QCQPs and higher-order polynomial optimization via hypergraph GNNs, which the authors identify as promising future directions.
