## Problem

It remained an open problem whether a localized Rademacher complexity tool analogous to Local Rademacher Complexity (LRC) could be designed for transductive learning to yield sharp excess risk bounds consistent with the inductive setting. The existing state-of-the-art bound (Tolstikhin et al., 2014) contains undesirable n/m and n/u prefactors before the fixed points, causing the bound to diverge when m or u grows slower than n, in stark contrast to the inductive LRC bound which always converges to zero under standard learning models.

## Input / Output

The framework operates on a fixed full sample of n distinct points in R^d, from which m training features (with labels) and u test features (unlabeled) are drawn uniformly without replacement via the RANDPERM algorithm. The method produces high-probability excess risk bounds for the empirical minimizer that depend on fixed points of sub-root functions bounding the Transductive Complexity of localized function classes, with the bound scaling as c₀(r_u + r_m) + 4Bc₂r*/K + c₃x/min{m,u} rather than the divergent n/m·r_u* + n/u·r_m* form of prior work. The analysis requires the asymmetry condition m ≫ u² or u ≫ m² to control chain events in the sampling process.

## Method

The paper introduces Transductive Local Complexity (TLC), built on a novel concentration inequality for the test-train process g(d) = sup_h(U_h^(u) − L_h^(m)), the supremum of the gap between test and training loss over a function class under uniform sampling without replacement. The key technical insight is a combinatorial property (Lemma B.1–B.2) showing that changing a single random variable in the RANDPERM permutation alters the test-train process by at most one element pair, which enables a tight variance bound via two applications of the exponential Efron-Stein inequality. A peeling strategy over geometrically scaled function classes, combined with a new surrogate variance operator T̃_n(h) that upper-bounds the empirical variance through the population loss, yields the sharp excess risk bound free of n/m and n/u factors. As an independent contribution, the test-train concentration inequality is extended to a general supremum-of-empirical-process concentration inequality for sampling without replacement, which is sharper than both versions in Tolstikhin et al. (2014).

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 3.6) establishes a sharp excess risk bound for generic transductive learning: E(f̂) ≤ c₀(rᵤ + rₘ) + 4Bc₂r*/K + c₃x/min{m,u}, where rᵤ, rₘ, r* are fixed points of sub-root functions that all converge to 0 at fast rates under standard learning models. For Transductive Kernel Learning (Theorem 4.1), the excess risk bound is E(f̂) ≤ c₅(min_{0≤Q≤n} r(u,m,Q) + x/min{m,u}), which is free of the undesirable n/u and n/m multiplicative factors present in prior work. The concentration inequality for the test-train process (Theorem 3.1) and the general supremum of empirical processes under sampling without replacement (Theorem 5.1) are both sharper than existing results.

**Comparison of excess risk bounds for Transductive Kernel Learning (TKL).**

| Bound | Expression | Divergence condition |
|---|---|---|
| Tolstikhin et al. (2014), Eq. (18) | Θ(n/u · rₘ* + n/m · rᵤ* + 1/m + 1/u) | Diverges when u = o(n^{1/(2α+1)}) or m = o(n^{1/(2α+1)}) |
| This paper, Eq. (17) | c₅(min_{0≤Q≤n} r(u,m,Q) + x/min{m,u}) | Always converges to 0 as u,m → ∞ |

where r(u,m,Q) = Q(1/u + 1/m) + √(Σ_{q=Q+1}^n λ̂_q / u) + √(Σ_{q=Q+1}^n λ̂_q / m), and for dot-product kernels with α > 1/2, min_{0≤Q≤n}(Q/n + √(Σ_{q=Q+1}^n λ̂_q/n)) ≍ n^{-2α/(2α+1)}.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is with Tolstikhin et al. (2014), the current state-of-the-art for local complexity based transductive learning. Both bounds are derived under the same assumptions (Assumption 1(1) and Assumption 2), but the paper's bound (17) eliminates the multiplicative factors n/u and n/m that appear before the fixed points in Tolstikhin et al.'s bound (18). Concretely, for standard TKL with dot-product kernels on the unit sphere (where the Rademacher complexity scales as n^{-2α/(2α+1)}), the Tolstikhin et al. bound diverges when u = o(n^{1/(2α+1)}) or m = o(n^{1/(2α+1)}), while the paper's bound always converges to 0 as u, m → ∞. The paper also provides a sharper concentration inequality (Theorem 5.1) for the general supremum of empirical processes under sampling without replacement, improving upon both versions in Tolstikhin et al. (2014, Theorems 1 and 2), whose RHS diverge when u = o(√n) and m = w(√n) respectively, whereas the paper's bound converges to 0 under standard learning models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Local Rademacher Complexity (LRC) framework of Bartlett et al. (2005) from the inductive to the transductive setting, building on the transductive Rademacher complexity and RANDPERM sampling procedure of El-Yaniv & Pechyony (2009). The key departure from the prior state-of-the-art (Tolstikhin et al., 2014) is a shift in the analytical target: rather than bounding test loss and training loss separately against the population loss (which introduces undesirable n/m and n/u prefactors), the paper directly concentrates the test-train gap via a novel concentration inequality derived using the exponential Efron-Stein inequality applied twice. This yields a peeling-based excess risk bound that is structurally consistent with the inductive LRC bound, free of the divergent factors that plagued the previous transductive result.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper resolves a concrete open problem: whether a localized Rademacher-complexity-based tool can produce a sharp transductive excess risk bound matching the inductive LRC form. The practical implication is that the previous state-of-the-art bound (Tolstikhin et al., 2014) could diverge under standard kernel learning models when one of m or u grows slower than n^{1/(2α+1)}, whereas the new bound always vanishes as both grow. Limitations include the requirement that m ≫ u² or u ≫ m² (an asymmetry assumption), and the results are purely theoretical with no empirical validation. Open questions include whether the asymmetry condition can be removed and whether the test-train concentration technique extends to other sampling schemes or to unbounded loss functions.
