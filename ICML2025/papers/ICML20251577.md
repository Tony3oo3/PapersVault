## Problem

Existing open-set grounded segmentation methods (VLM- and MLLM-based) require expensive training or fine-tuning and produce unstable, confounded masks when the same object is described with different textual expressions (e.g., 'apple pieces' vs. 'apple slices'). This paper identifies through a motivational study that the misalignment between visual mask representations and linguistic embeddings is the root cause, and asks whether a training-free, test-time approach can robustly localize arbitrary words to their corresponding objects.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251577/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image and a free-form text reference expression as input. The text is parsed (via an LLM or NLP tool) into noun phrases, adjectives, and other syntactic components; each target noun word is treated as a separate segmentation query. The output is a set of pixel-level segmentation masks, one per identified noun, with object class labels inherited directly from the text. Cross-attention maps operate at 16×16 resolution, and a frozen SAM ViT-H refines the coarse attention-based prompts into precise boundaries.

## Method

Segment Anyword inverts an input image into a frozen diffusion model's latent space via direct (PnP) inversion, then performs test-time optimization of only the textual embeddings (V) while keeping the denoising network and text encoder frozen, using a multi-concept textual inversion loss. During the denoising reconstruction, token-level cross-attention maps are collected and averaged across all timesteps to produce per-word localization priors; points are sampled from high-activation regions and fed as positive prompts to a frozen SAM. The key novelty is a linguistic-guided visual prompt regularization that (1) clusters positive adjective prompts with their governing nouns to improve mask completeness, and (2) recurrently binds negative prompts from mutually exclusive noun phrases to sharpen inter-object boundaries. A LoRA fine-tuned BERT encoder (trained on 500 domain samples) reduces the embedding update from 1100 to 50 steps, cutting inference from 470s to 28s per image with minimal accuracy loss.

## Results

On the most challenging open-set grounded segmentation benchmark GranDf, Segment Anyword achieves 31.3 AP50 and 67.4 mIoU on the validation split, outperforming the best fine-tuned MLLM (GLaMM_f: 66.3 mIoU) by +1.1 mIoU while requiring zero training images. On gRefCOCO multi-object reference segmentation, the method reaches 67.73 cIoU (Val), surpassing the best fine-tuned baseline SAM4MLLM (66.33 cIoU) by +1.40 cIoU. On Pascal Context 59 open-vocabulary segmentation, Segment Anyword achieves 52.5 mIoU, exceeding the previous best training-free method EmerDiff (45.7 mIoU) by +6.8 mIoU. On single-object RefCOCO/+/g, the method trails the best MLLM (GLaMM: 79.5 cIoU on RefCOCO Val) but substantially outperforms other training-free baselines (CLIPasRNN: 33.57 cIoU).

## Baselines & Comparisons

On GranDf, Segment Anyword (training-free, 0 training images) outperforms all end-to-end MLLM baselines including GLaMM (65.8 mIoU), LISA (62.0), and OMG-LLaVA (64.6), as well as fine-tuned variants GLaMM_f (66.3) and OMG-LLaVA_f (65.5), achieving the best AP50 (31.3) and mIoU (67.4) on validation. On gRefCOCO, the method decisively beats all baselines: it surpasses fine-tuned SAM4MLLM (66.33 cIoU Val) by +1.40 cIoU, GSVA_f (63.29) by +4.44, and zero-shot PSALM (42.00) by +25.73 cIoU. On Pascal Context 59, Segment Anyword (52.5 mIoU) exceeds all training-free diffusion methods—EmerDiff (45.7), CaR (39.5), OVDiff (32.9)—by 6.8–19.6 mIoU, though it remains below the fine-tuned ODISE (57.3). On single-object RefCOCO/+/g, the method underperforms the best MLLM GLaMM (79.5/72.6/74.2 cIoU on Val) by a wide margin (55.32/55.57/58.43), but still substantially outperforms other training-free methods like CLIPasRNN (33.57/34.22/36.67) and GL-CLIP (26.20/27.80/33.52). The method's key advantage is its training-free nature: it requires no supervised training data and only 500 image-text pairs for optional LoRA text-encoder adaptation, yet matches or exceeds methods trained on 11M–130M images.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes a frozen text-to-image diffusion model's token-level cross-attention maps as segmentation localization priors, building directly on multi-concept prompt learning (Jin et al., 2024) for the embedding optimization objective and PnP inversion (Ju et al., 2024) for image reconstruction. It departs from the dominant CLIP-based and MLLM-based segmentation paradigms (CRIS, GLaMM, LISA, GSVA) by requiring no training or fine-tuning of any segmentation-specific module, instead using a frozen SAM purely as a post-processing mask generator. The key conceptual departure is the linguistic-guided visual prompt regularization—binding positive adjective prompts and negative mutual-exclusive prompts via sentence dependency and syntax structure—which has no direct analogue in prior diffusion-based segmentation work (OVDiff, EmerDiff, Peekaboo) that relied on prototype matching or feature perturbation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a training-free, test-time-only pipeline can match or exceed fine-tuned MLLMs on the most complex open-set grounded segmentation benchmark (GranDf), offering a practical path for deploying open-set segmentation in low-resource or rapidly evolving domains where retraining is infeasible. The motivational study quantifying segmentation instability under text variation across VLMs and MLLMs provides useful empirical grounding for why adaptive test-time alignment is necessary. Key limitations include the 16×16 cross-attention resolution (failing on tiny/thin-structured objects), substantial inference time (470s per image without LoRA, 28s with), and dependence on accurate text parsing for the linguistic regularization. Open questions include whether higher-resolution diffusion backbones or accelerated samplers (e.g., Hyper-SD) can close the speed gap, and whether the predicate segmentation capability generalizes beyond the demonstrated examples.
