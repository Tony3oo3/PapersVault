## Problem

Existing fairness methods guarantee only static fairness and do not ensure fair predictions under imperceptible adversarial perturbations, while adversarial training has been studied exclusively for accuracy robustness and not for fairness. The paper asks whether adversarial attacks against fairness and accuracy can be unified, and whether robustness to one form of attack inherently provides robustness to the other.

## Input / Output

The method operates on tabular feature vectors (Adult, COMPAS, German Credit) or 40-attribute face descriptors (CelebA), each paired with a binary label and a sensitive attribute (race, sex, or age). Adversarial perturbations are applied at the input feature level under a bounded L∞ norm (ε₀ varies per dataset: 0.2 for Adult, 0.005 for COMPAS, 0.01 for German, 0.1 for CelebA). The classifier outputs a binary prediction, and evaluation reports both accuracy and group fairness metrics (demographic parity and equalized odds) measured at a 0.5 cutoff under both fairness and accuracy attacks.

## Method

The paper proposes a unified framework for fairness attacks showing that DP and EOd attacks are equivalent up to multiplicative constants, and that for four of eight prediction subgroups (TN/FP in the advantaged group, TP/FN in the disadvantaged group) the fairness attack and accuracy attack operate in the same direction, making them gradient-equivalent. For the remaining four subgroups where the attacks oppose each other, the paper proves via Lipschitz-gradient bounds that the robustness gap between fairness and accuracy is upper-bounded by the spatial distance between cross-group samples, establishing theoretical alignment between the two robustness notions. The resulting 'fair adversarial training' framework minimizes cross-entropy loss on accuracy adversarial samples (generated by PGD) subject to a fairness constraint L ≤ γ, where L can be instantiated as preprocessing reweighting, in-processing relaxed EOd regularization, or post-processing group-dependent thresholds. This single training objective simultaneously improves fairness robustness (by reducing the accuracy-robustness term in the upper bound) and maintains static fairness, without requiring explicit fairness adversarial samples during training.

## Results

Under fairness attacks on the Adult dataset, a successful attack (ε=0.3) drives accuracy to 0 while DP barely changes (14.34%→15.13%) and EOd drops to 0, demonstrating that standard accuracy attacks do not capture fairness degradation. On CelebA under ε=0.1 fairness attack, the baseline classifier exhibits a cross-entropy loss change of 0.16±0.03 for advantaged FN samples, while fair adversarial training (preprocessing) reduces this to 0.07±0.02 (a ~56% reduction). Under larger perturbation (ε=0.3), the gap widens further: baseline 0.23±0.02 vs. 0.09±0.02 for the proposed method. Across all four datasets (Adult, COMPAS, German, CelebA), the three variants of fair adversarial training consistently achieve lower DP and EOd under fairness attacks while maintaining higher accuracy compared to both normal training and vanilla adversarial training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four baseline categories: (1) normal training with fairness methods (Preprocessing via Yu et al. 2024, In-processing via Wang et al. 2022, Post-processing via Jang et al. 2022), (2) vanilla adversarial training (PGD-based, Madry et al. 2017) without fairness, and (3) the proposed fair adversarial training in three variants. Under fairness attacks, all normal-training baselines show a sharp increase in DP and EOd as perturbation grows, confirming that static fairness does not imply fairness robustness. Vanilla adversarial training (accuracy-only) provides some improvement in fairness robustness over normal training, validating the theoretical alignment, but the proposed fair adversarial training achieves significantly lower DP and EOd with higher accuracy across all four datasets. On CelebA at ε=0.1, the preprocessing variant reduces the FN loss change from 0.16 (baseline) to 0.07, a 56% improvement; at ε=0.3 the reduction is from 0.23 to 0.09 (61%). Under accuracy attacks, adversarial training w.r.t. fairness (using fairness adversarial samples) also improves accuracy robustness, confirming the bidirectional alignment. The ablation on CelebA further shows that using fairness or accuracy adversarial samples alone without the fairness regularization constraint fails to maintain fairness robustness at larger perturbations, highlighting the necessity of the combined objective.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate lines of work: adversarial training for accuracy robustness (Madry et al., 2017; Goodfellow et al., 2014) and group fairness enforcement via preprocessing/in-processing/post-processing (Zafar et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2022; Jang et al., 2022). It departs from prior fairness-attack literature (Solans et al., 2020; Mehrabi et al., 2021b), which focused on poisoning attacks during training, by formalizing feature-level adversarial perturbations that target fairness measures on test data. The closest prior work, Chhabra et al. (2022), considers perturbation against sensitive attributes rather than input features, and the paper explicitly positions its framework as more general. The theoretical alignment between fairness and accuracy robustness builds on Lipschitz-based analysis techniques from adversarial robustness theory but applies them to a new cross-domain setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is significant: a model that appears fair under clean data can be rendered maximally unfair by perturbations as small as 1.5% of input norm, and standard adversarial training for accuracy inadvertently improves fairness robustness. This suggests a unified defense (fair adversarial training) that addresses both concerns simultaneously, reducing the need for separate fairness and robustness pipelines. Limitations include the binary-classification, two-group setting, white-box threat model, and reliance on Lipschitz gradient assumptions that may not hold tightly for deep networks. Open questions include whether the alignment extends to multi-class, multi-group, or intersectional fairness settings, and whether black-box fairness attacks are as destructive as the white-box formulation suggests.
