## Problem

Despite LLMs' impressive performance on reasoning tasks, there is no formal characterization of whether Transformer architectures can perform deductive logical reasoning on NP-complete problems, and the fundamental limitations of their reasoning capabilities remain poorly understood. This paper asks whether decoder-only Transformers can decide 3-SAT (an NP-complete problem) using Chain-of-Thought, and whether such reasoning can be learned from training data rather than hand-coded.

## Input / Output

The model receives a DIMACS-encoded 3-SAT formula as a token sequence (positive/negative integers for literals, 0 as clause separators, bracketed by [BOS] and [SEP]), with each formula containing 16.4p to 17.6p tokens for p variables. The autoregressive output is a Chain-of-Thought trace that simulates the DPLL procedure—generating variable assignment tokens (T/F), unit propagation tokens, [BackTrack] tokens on conflicts, and finally a terminal SAT or UNSAT label. The construction requires at most p·2^(p+1) CoT steps in the worst case, though empirically the bound is 8p·2^(0.08p).

## Method

The paper proves by construction that a decoder-only Transformer with 7 layers, 5 heads, O(p) embedding dimension, and O(p²) parameters can decide all 3-SAT instances with at most p variables and c clauses via CoT, in a non-uniform model where parameters depend on p. The key mechanism is encoding each clause and the evolving partial assignment as 2p-dimensional binary vectors, then using attention heads to compute satisfiability checks, conflict detection, and unit propagation across all c clauses in parallel via dot-product operations—avoiding sequential clause-by-clause evaluation. The CoT emulates the abstract DPLL state transition system: at each step the model decides a variable, applies unit propagation, and backtracks on conflicts. The authors implement this construction as a working PyTorch model using their PARAT tool (which compiles NumPy-style specifications into Transformer weights) and verify perfect accuracy on random 3-SAT instances. Training experiments with 70M/160M LLaMA models on generated CoT traces show near-perfect out-of-distribution generalization within the same variable count but sharp degradation beyond it, consistent with the size-dependent parameter requirement.

## Results

The compiled Transformer (theoretical construction via PARAT) achieves 100% SAT/UNSAT accuracy on all 51 evaluation datasets (p=4–20, three distributions), validating the theoretical construction. Trained LLaMA models (70M/160M params) achieve 99.11%–100% SAT/UNSAT prediction accuracy when tested on the same variable range as training, even under out-of-distribution formula sampling (e.g., 99.88% on Marginal test when trained on Marginal, 99.96% when trained on Random). Full trace correctness (every CoT token follows valid DPLL) ranges from 86.06% (Skewed test, p∈[11,15]) to 99.40% (Random test, p∈[6,10]). However, performance degrades drastically beyond the training variable range, confirming the non-uniformity limitation predicted by the theory.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against external SAT-solver baselines or other LLM reasoning methods; instead, its primary comparison is between the theoretically compiled Transformer and the trained LLaMA models. The compiled model achieves perfect (100%) accuracy on all datasets, while trained models reach 99.11%–100% SAT/UNSAT accuracy within the training variable range but fail to generalize to unseen variable counts (performance drops sharply in Figure 3). Among trained models, training on the Random distribution yields the best cross-distribution full-trace accuracy (99.04%–99.16% on Random test), while the Skewed distribution is hardest, with full-trace accuracy dropping to 86.06%–91.70%. The Marginal dataset (where SAT and UNSAT formulas differ by a single token) is the most stringent test, yet trained models still achieve ≥99.11% binary accuracy, indicating genuine logical reasoning rather than statistical shortcutting. The key negative result is that no trained model generalizes beyond its training variable range, consistent with the theoretical requirement that model size must scale with p.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the attention-layer building blocks (COPY and MEAN lemmas) from Feng et al. (2023) and the abstract DPLL state-transition formalism of Nieuwenhuis et al. (2005), combined with the binary vector encoding scheme from MatSAT (Sato & Kojima, 2021), to construct a decoder-only Transformer that decides 3-SAT via Chain-of-Thought. It departs from prior expressiveness results (Liu et al. 2023a, Merrill & Sabharwal 2024, Li et al. 2024) that established Transformers can solve P/poly problems with CoT, by tackling an NP-complete problem and showing the construction is necessarily non-uniform (parameters scale with problem size). The PARAT tool extends the compilation approach of Tracr (Lindner et al., 2023) from RASP programs to NumPy-style specifications, enabling more complex theoretical constructions to be instantiated as runnable PyTorch models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete, quantifiable mechanism—compounding softmax approximation error—for the widely observed but poorly understood length generalization failure in LLM reasoning, grounding an empirical phenomenon in a specific architectural limitation. The empirical validation (near-perfect intra-length OOD generalization but catastrophic failure beyond training length) tightly couples theory and practice, though the models are small (70M–160M) and the problem is restricted to 3-SAT with at most 20 variables. Open questions include whether any uniform Transformer architecture can solve SAT with polynomial CoT, whether the softmax-error mechanism generalizes to other reasoning tasks, and whether architectural modifications (e.g., saturated attention, larger precision) could overcome the length barrier.
