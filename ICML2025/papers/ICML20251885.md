## Problem

Mechanistic interpretability relies on text explanations for individual neurons, but the field lacks a standardized evaluation framework: different papers use different metrics (Recall, IoU, Correlation, AUC, MAD, etc.) without theoretical justification, and it is unclear how these metrics compare or whether they are reliable. The paper asks which evaluation metrics can be trusted to distinguish a correct neuron explanation from one that is too specific or too generic.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251885/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The NeuronEval framework takes a probing dataset D of n inputs, a neural network f (yielding a neuron activation vector a_k ∈ R^n), and a text explanation t (yielding a concept activation vector c_t ∈ R^n from crowdsourced ratings, model pseudo-labels, or ground-truth annotations), and produces a scalar score s_M(a_k, c_t) for any of 18 unified metrics. The sanity checks output a Decrease Acc percentage per metric (pass if >90%), and the meta-evaluation outputs a meta-AUPRC ranking of all 18 metrics on neurons with known ground-truth concepts. Binarization of continuous activations uses a top-α threshold for neurons and 0.5 rounding for concept vectors, with α tuned per metric on a validation split.

## Method

The paper formalizes all existing neuron explanation evaluations as s_M(a_k, c_t), a function of the neuron activation vector and concept activation vector, unifying 20 methods from 19 studies under the NeuronEval framework. It then proposes two sanity checks: the Missing Labels Test (randomly zeroing half of c_t to simulate an overly specific explanation) and the Extra Labels Test (randomly doubling c_t to simulate an overly generic explanation), measuring whether the score decreases by more than ε=0.001 on >90% of neurons. Theoretical analysis on ideal neurons with varying activation frequencies reveals that test failures are caused by poor handling of concept imbalance, explaining why metrics like Recall, AUC, and Accuracy fail while F1-score, IoU, Correlation, Cosine, and AUPRC pass. A second meta-evaluation computes AUPRC between scores of correct and incorrect (neuron, explanation) pairs on final-layer neurons with known concepts, confirming that the passing metrics (Correlation, Cosine, AUPRC, F1/IoU) also achieve the highest discrimination performance.

## Results

Out of 18 evaluation metrics tested, only 5 pass both the Missing Labels Test and Extra Labels Test: Correlation, Cosine Similarity, AUPRC, IoU, and F1-score. In the meta-evaluation on neurons with known ground-truth concepts (Table 4), Correlation achieves the highest meta-AUPRC of 0.8765 (avg. rank 1.60), followed by Cosine (0.8666, rank 2.30) and AUPRC (0.8406, rank 3.90). Widely used metrics such as Recall (0.6722), Correlation with top-and-random sampling (0.6606), and Spearman Correlation (0.0853) perform substantially worse, with Spearman ranking near the bottom (16.20 out of 17).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is across 18 evaluation metrics unified under the NeuronEval framework, tested on 8 experimental settings (vision: ImageNet, Places365, CUB200; language: OpenWebText) and theoretical ideal neurons. Recall, the most commonly used metric in the literature (Zhou et al. 2015; Bau et al. 2017; Oikarinen & Weng 2023), completely fails the Extra Labels Test (0.00% Decrease Acc), meaning it cannot distinguish a correct concept from an overly generic one. Precision and MAD fail the Missing Labels Test, being biased toward overly specific concepts. Correlation with top-and-random sampling (Bills et al. 2023; Bricken et al. 2023) drops from passing to failing the Extra Labels Test (99.92% → 60.26%) purely due to the biased sampling strategy. In the meta-AUPRC evaluation on known-concept neurons (Table 4), Correlation (0.8765) and Cosine (0.8666) clearly outperform Recall (0.6722), AUC (0.7652), and MAD (0.6952), while Spearman Correlation is nearly useless (0.0853). The passing metrics (Correlation, Cosine, AUPRC, F1-score/IoU) consistently rank in the top 4, confirming that metrics robust to class imbalance are the reliable choice for neuron explanation evaluation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper does not build on a single prior method but rather unifies 20+ disparate evaluation approaches from the mechanistic interpretability literature (Network Dissection, CLIP-Dissect, SAE auto-interp, CoSy, MAIA, etc.) under a single mathematical formalism called NeuronEval. The sanity-check paradigm is directly inspired by Adebayo et al. (2018) for saliency maps, repurposed here to validate neuron explanation metrics. The paper departs from the field's ad-hoc practice of using 1–2 metrics per paper without justification, instead performing systematic meta-evaluation of 18 metrics across vision and language models, hidden and final layers, and multiple concept sources.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that most standard metrics in the field (Recall, AUC, MAD, top-and-random Correlation) fail basic sanity checks is a significant methodological correction for the interpretability community, which has relied on these metrics for years. The identification of concept imbalance as the root cause of failures connects the results to well-known statistical limitations and provides a clear diagnostic. Open questions include extending the framework to output-based explanations, developing effective sampling strategies for non-Recall metrics that control labeling cost, and exploring combinations of metrics (e.g., pairing generative-model Precision with crowdsourced Recall into F1). The paper's guidelines are immediately actionable: researchers should switch to Correlation, Cosine, AUPRC, F1-score, or IoU.
