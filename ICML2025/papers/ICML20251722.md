## Problem

Despite extensive work on parameter estimation and uncertainty quantification for generalized Thurstone models (GTMs), there is no rigorously analyzed hypothesis test to determine whether given pairwise comparison data actually conforms to a GTM with a specified choice function F. This gap is critical because GTMs are parametric models that can be too restrictive for real-world data (e.g., failing to capture home-advantage effects or intransitivities), and practitioners need a principled way to decide when to rely on GTM-based inference versus resorting to non-parametric or alternative parametric models.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input a collection of independent Bernoulli pairwise comparison outcomes Z, an undirected connected observation graph G specifying which agent pairs are compared, a known choice function F (a CDF such as Gaussian or logistic), and a uniform number of comparisons k per edge. The data is split into two roughly equal halves: Z1 for maximum-likelihood estimation of the utility vector w, and Z2 for computing the test statistic T. The output is a binary hypothesis decision (accept or reject H0: data is generated by some GTM), the value of T, a critical threshold ε_c that depends on graph topology (scaling as Θ((nk)^{-1/2}) for complete graphs), and time-uniform confidence intervals valid for all sample sizes simultaneously.

## Method

The core contribution is a likelihood-based test statistic T that estimates the squared Frobenius distance between the empirical pairwise probability matrix and the best-fitting GTM, constructed via sample splitting to ensure unbiasedness. The key theoretical insight (Theorem 3.1) is that the separation distance to the entire class of GTMs is within a constant factor of the distance to the single MLE-fitted GTM, making the problem tractable. Upper bounds on the critical threshold are derived by establishing parameter estimation error bounds in the graph Laplacian semi-norm under potential model mismatch (Theorem 3.3), then applying Chebyshev's inequality to control both type I and II errors. Lower bounds use the Ingster-Suslina method on super-Eulerian graphs with cycle decompositions, generalizing the IIA-testing construction of Seshadri & Ugander (2019) to arbitrary GTMs and broader graph classes. In the sequential setting, a reverse martingale property of T (Proposition 3.6) combined with a specialized time-uniform Hanson-Wright inequality yields confidence sequences and error bounds that hold uniformly over all stopping times.

## Results

For complete observation graphs, the critical threshold ε_c for distinguishing a generalized Thurstone model from a general pairwise comparison model scales as Θ(1/√(nk)), where n is the number of agents and k is the number of comparisons per pair — matching upper and lower bounds. For sparser topologies (d-regular, single cycle, toroidal grid), the upper bound remains O(1/√(nk)) while the lower bound degrades to Ω(1/√(n²k)) or Ω(1/√(n^{7/4}k)). In the LMSYS Chatbot Arena experiment, both BTL and Thurstone models fit well for small n (only ~10% of bootstrap samples exceed the 95th-percentile threshold), but for n=21 approximately 60% of samples exceed the threshold, indicating significant model misspecification at larger group sizes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its likelihood-based testing framework against the spectral-method-based tests of Makur & Singh (2023, IEEE ISIT) and Makur & Singh (2024, arXiv), which were developed specifically for the BTL model. For complete graphs, the upper bound on ε_c recovers the same Θ(1/√(nk)) scaling as the spectral approach, confirming matching minimax optimality. However, the lower bound for single-cycle graphs improves from Ω(1/√(n³k)) (as in Seshadri & Ugander, 2019, for BTL with TV-distance separation) to Ω(1/√(n²k)) when separation is quantified in Frobenius norm. The key methodological distinction is that the present work uses a maximum-likelihood framework with sample splitting and reverse martingale techniques, whereas the spectral baselines rely on eigendecomposition of the comparison matrix. In the LMSYS experiments (Appendix F), the likelihood-based test and the spectral test are compared directly, with the paper noting that both approaches yield qualitatively similar conclusions about model fit across different n values.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the hypothesis testing framework for pairwise comparison models from the BTL-specific setting (Makur & Singh, 2023; 2024, using spectral methods) and the IIA-testing lower bounds of Seshadri & Ugander (2019, using the Ingster-Suslina method on Eulerian graphs) to the full class of generalized Thurstone models with arbitrary choice functions. The key methodological departure is a maximum-likelihood-based test statistic with sample splitting, replacing the spectral approaches of prior BTL testing work, while the lower bound construction generalizes the Ingster-Suslina technique from BTL to any GTM and from Eulerian to super-Eulerian graphs. The paper also builds on the parameter estimation literature for GTMs (Shah et al., 2016; Vojnovic & Yun, 2016) by deriving error bounds under model mismatch, which is essential for the testing analysis but absent from prior estimation results.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first rigorous minimax hypothesis testing framework for the general GTM class, filling a gap the authors explicitly identify in the literature where prior work focused almost exclusively on parameter estimation and uncertainty quantification. The time-uniform error bounds via reverse martingales make the test applicable in sequential/online settings, and the LMSYS chatbot leaderboard experiment demonstrates practical relevance for LLM evaluation, showing that Thurstone/BTL models fit well for small agent sets but deviate significantly as n grows. Open questions include extending to multi-way comparisons, developing active testing strategies, and handling dependent data, while the gap between upper and lower bounds on the critical threshold for non-complete graphs (e.g., d-regular, toroidal grids) remains unresolved.
