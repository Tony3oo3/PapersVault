## Problem

Existing fair GNN methods assume training and test graphs share the same distribution, which rarely holds in practice. Under distribution shifts, two compounding issues arise: sensitive group imbalance skews generalization toward majority groups, and unintended correlations between target and sensitive attributes create a fairness–performance conflict that degrades both objectives simultaneously.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252519/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a graph G=(V,E,X) where the node feature matrix X∈R^{N×d} has its t-th dimension encoding the binary sensitive attribute, along with binary node labels Y∈{0,1}^N. It operates on ego-graphs for node-level prediction and produces binary classification labels plus disentangled node representations Z∈R^{N×d'} that are invariant to the sensitive attribute. The sensitive attribute is not provided as a separate label at inference time but is extracted from the feature matrix, and the method generates synthetic nodes and adversarial perturbations internally during training.

## Method

DANCE simulates distribution shifts from a data-centric view via dual graph expansion: (1) an unbiased Mixup identifies hard minor-group nodes by confidence, synthesizes new node features via linear interpolation, and connects them only to minority-group neighbors sampled through a graph diffusion process (PPR or Heat Kernel) that provably blocks information flow from non-minority to minority groups; (2) an MLP-based adversarial generator produces feature perturbations that maximize both classification loss and an EO-based fairness loss, regularized by an L2 term to stay near the original features. On the expanded graph, a group-acquired alignment loss treats the original and generated graphs as two views and applies a contrastive objective over four similarity groups (intra-group, sensitive inter-group, target inter-group, target&sensitive inter-group), explicitly prioritizing negative pairs that share the same sensitive label with group-wise normalization. A representation disentanglement module uses a second GNN to extract a sensitive-aware negative view, trains a sensitive discriminator on it, and applies a contrastive loss to decorrelate sensitive information from task representations. The overall training is a min-max game: the encoder/classifier minimizes a composite loss (classification + sensitive classification + synthetic classification + alignment + disentanglement), while the adversarial generator maximizes classification and fairness losses minus the regularization term.

## Results

DANCE achieves rank 1 (best combined score) across all three datasets: Credit-Cs, Pokecs, and Bail-Bs. On Credit-Cs (C1), it reaches 78.58% ACC and 72.18% ROC-AUC while maintaining ΔDP = 0.74% and ΔEO = 0.70%, substantially outperforming the runner-up FatraGNN (65.41% ROC-AUC). On Pokec-n, DANCE attains 66.58% ACC, 72.88% ROC-AUC, ΔDP = 0.83%, and ΔEO = 0.29%. On Bail-Bs, it achieves near-perfect ROC-AUC (99.75% on B1, 99.81% on B2) with ΔDP ≤ 3.99% and ΔEO ≤ 3.17% across all test communities.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DANCE outperforms all 10 baselines on the combined metric (ACC + ROC-AUC − ΔDP − ΔEO) across all three datasets, achieving rank 1 in every case. The closest competitor, FatraGNN (rank 2), is significantly outperformed on ROC-AUC: DANCE achieves 72.18% vs. 65.41% on Credit-Cs C1 and 77.56% vs. 67.66% on C4, a gap of 6–10 points, while maintaining comparable or better fairness (ΔDP 0.74% vs. 0.50% on C1). Fair GNN baselines (FairVGNN, NIFTY, EDITS, CAF) improve fairness over traditional methods (MLP, GCN) but sacrifice classification accuracy under distribution shifts, with ROC-AUC dropping to 60–68% range. OOD methods (EERM, SAGM) achieve moderate classification but exhibit substantially higher fairness gaps (ΔDP 4–5%, ΔEO 4–6%), confirming their inability to learn fair representations across environments. On Pokec-n, DANCE leads with 66.58% ACC and 72.88% ROC-AUC, surpassing the next-best FatraGNN (62.00% ACC, 67.82% ROC-AUC) by over 4 points in both metrics while also achieving the lowest ΔDP (0.83%) and ΔEO (0.29%).

## Relation to Prior Work

DANCE sits at the intersection of three research threads: fair GNNs (FairVGNN, NIFTY, EDITS), graph OOD generalization (EERM, SAGM), and fairness under distribution shifts (RFR, FatraGNN). It departs from prior fair GNNs by explicitly rejecting the i.i.d. assumption and from prior OOD methods by incorporating fairness constraints. The group-acquired alignment module draws directly from fair contrastive learning (Park et al., 2022) and fair visual prompt tuning (Park & Byun, 2024), while the data expansion leverages mixup (Zhang et al., 2018) and graph diffusion (Gasteiger et al., 2019) in a novel dual-space formulation. The key conceptual departure from FatraGNN, the closest prior work, is the data-centric perspective: rather than modifying the model or loss to handle shifts, DANCE synthesizes virtual graph data in both structural and feature spaces to simulate shifts during training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically critical gap: real-world graph deployments (credit scoring, criminal justice, social networks) routinely encounter distribution shifts that break fairness guarantees, yet most fair GNN literature ignores this. The dual expansion strategy—using graph diffusion to control information flow between sensitive groups while adversarially perturbing features—offers a principled mechanism for simulating worst-case shifts without access to test data. Limitations include evaluation on only three datasets with binary classification, high variance on Bail-Bs (±12.94 ACC on B1), and a theoretical analysis that is largely standard (Lipschitz convergence, diffusion propagation). Open questions include scalability to multi-attribute fairness, multi-class settings, and whether the adversarial generator's dual objective (maximizing both classification and fairness loss) remains stable at larger scale.
