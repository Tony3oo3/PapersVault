## Problem

Single-stage RCTs waste samples on suboptimal arms, reducing the statistical power to certify treatment effects, while fully adaptive designs (e.g., UCB, Thompson sampling) are often infeasible in practice due to logistical constraints and delayed outcomes. The paper asks whether a deliberately simplified two-stage design can capture most of the statistical gains of full adaptivity while remaining implementable in standard batch-based RCT settings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250436/cropped_page5_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes n treatment arms with unknown means, a total sample budget T split into first-stage budget s1 and second-stage budget s2, a confidence level δ, and optionally a joint prior distribution over arm means. It outputs a pruned arm set (top-k policy), the optimal number of retained arms k selected via sample splitting, and a high-probability lower bound (certificate) computed from second-stage data using Hoeffding's or sub-Gaussian tail bounds. The certificate l satisfies P(l ≤ μ_i) ≥ 1−δ for at least one retained arm, and the design optimizes for maximizing l in expectation.

## Method

The core design is a two-stage RCT where stage 1 uniformly explores all n arms and a top-k policy prunes to the k arms with highest empirical means, while stage 2 uniformly randomizes over the retained arms to compute a concentration-inequality-based certificate. A sample splitting procedure divides stage-1 data into a training half (for ranking arms) and a validation half (for simulating stage-2 certificates at each candidate k), making the design adaptive to the unknown optimal k without requiring knowledge of the true means. Under a first-order stochastic dominance assumption on arm distributions, the paper proves that top-k policies are globally optimal (Theorem 3.7) and provides an explicit approximation bound for the sample splitting design (Proposition 3.2) that makes the s1/s2 tradeoff explicit. In the Bayesian extension, the method greedily constructs the retained arm set by sampling from the posterior and solving a cardinality-constrained submodular optimization, achieving a (1−1/e−ε) approximation guarantee, with a matching hardness result via reduction from maximum coverage.

## Results

On synthetic Bernoulli-arm experiments (n=10, δ=0.1), the proposed sample-splitting two-stage design outperforms single-stage RCTs by 7% when the first stage uses 30% of the budget, and approaches the omniscient upper bound within 0.5% at T=40,000. Against the fully adaptive UCB baseline, sample splitting captures up to 69% of the performance gap between single-stage and UCB (within 3% of UCB at s₁=30%). On the real-world gerontology meta-analysis dataset, the Bayesian prior-based two-stage design beats UCB by 23%, while the prior-free sample-splitting design matches UCB within 2%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its two-stage designs against UCB (Auer, 2002a) and Successive Elimination (Even-Dar et al., 2006), both fully adaptive methods that update assignment probabilities after every sample. On synthetic benchmarks, sample splitting performs within 3% of UCB (capturing 69% of the single-stage-to-UCB gap) and significantly outperforms both the two-stage and fully adaptive versions of Successive Elimination. In the Bayesian setting with an informative prior (β=4), the prior-based two-stage design surpasses UCB by 18%, demonstrating that domain knowledge can outweigh the benefit of high adaptivity. On the real-world gerontology dataset, the prior-based method beats UCB by 23%, while the prior-free sample-splitting design matches UCB within 2%. The paper also notes that sample splitting is within 1.5% of two-stage Thompson Sampling, suggesting that the simpler uniform-allocation two-stage framework captures most of the benefit of more complex adaptive schemes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of best-arm identification (Jamieson & Nowak, 2014; Bubeck et al., 2013) and adaptive RCT design (Kasy & Sautmann, 2021; Athey et al., 2022), but deliberately departs from both. Rather than pursuing fully adaptive allocation (which updates probabilities after each sample) or exhaustive arm identification, the authors introduce a certificate-maximization objective and show that a simple two-stage top-k design with sample splitting captures most of the statistical benefit. The Bayesian extension reduces the policy selection problem to cardinality-constrained submodular optimization, leveraging the classical Nemhauser et al. (1978) greedy guarantee, while the frequentist analysis builds on Hoeffding-type concentration inequalities.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is substantial: RCTs in policy, medicine, and development often face logistical constraints (delayed outcomes, batch processing) that make fully adaptive designs infeasible, yet the paper shows a two-stage design can capture up to 69% of the improvement over single-stage designs and even outperform fully adaptive UCB when an informative prior is available. Key limitations include sensitivity to prior misspecification, the stochastic dominance assumption required for top-k optimality in the frequentist setting, and the restriction to bounded or sub-Gaussian outcomes. Open questions include whether the optimal policy is always top-k without stochastic dominance, how multi-stage designs (beyond two stages) interact with the i.i.d. requirement for concentration bounds, and whether the framework extends to heterogeneous treatment effects or covariate-adaptive settings.
