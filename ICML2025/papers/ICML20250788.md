## Problem

In bandit clustering, prior work (Yang et al., 2024; Thuot et al., 2025) requires observing the full d-dimensional feature vector of a chosen item at each step, which is wasteful when only a few features discriminate between groups. This paper studies the more efficient setting where the learner selects a single (item, feature) pair per query, and provides the first tight, instance-dependent characterization of the sample complexity for two-group clustering under this adaptive single-feature observation model, with matching upper and lower bounds.

## Input / Output

The learner receives n items, each with a d-dimensional feature vector, partitioned into two unknown groups sharing identical feature vectors. At each round the learner adaptively selects one item i and one feature j, and receives a single 1-subGaussian noisy sample of M_{i,j}. The output is a binary label vector ĝ ∈ {0,1}^n recovering the partition with probability ≥ 1−δ, together with the total number of observations T (the budget). The key constraint distinguishing this from prior bandit clustering is that only one scalar entry is revealed per step, not the full row.

## Method

BanditClustering operates in two phases. First, CandidateRow (CR) fixes item r₀=1 and uses an exponential budget grid T_k=2^{k+1} to run CompareSequentialHalving (CSH) over all other items, identifying a representative r₁ from the second group; CSH is a variant of Sequential Halving that compares each candidate entry against the reference row r₀ and incorporates a sub-sampling parameter L to balance detection of small gaps against the risk of missing informative entries. Second, ClusterByCandidates (CBC) runs CSH restricted to the pair (r₀, r₁) to find the feature j with the largest gap |Δ_j|, then classifies all n items by sampling only the j-th feature of each item. The exponential grid over budgets allows the algorithm to adapt to the unknown effective sparsity s* and gap magnitude without prior knowledge, yielding a budget of order log(1/δ)·[d/(θ‖Δ‖²) + min_s (d/s + n)(1/Δ_{(s)}² + 1)], which is shown to be optimal up to polylogarithmic factors in the sparse constant-magnitude regime.

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 3.1) establishes that BanditClustering recovers the correct two-group partition with probability ≥ 1−δ using a budget T ≤ C̃·log(1/δ)·H, where H = (d/θ)(1/‖Δ‖² + 1/s*) + min_s (d/s + n)(1/Δ_(s)² + 1). In the sparse setting (Corollary 3.2, Δ ∈ {0,h}^d with s non-zero entries), this simplifies to T ≤ C̃·log(1/δ)·(d/(θ‖Δ‖²) + n/h²), which is matched up to poly-logarithmic factors by the information-theoretic lower bound (Theorem 4.1). Numerically, in Experiment 1 (n=20, d=1000, varying sparsity s), BanditClustering requires substantially fewer observations than uniform sampling + K-means in the sparse regime, with the CR step budget being nearly independent of s while the CBC step grows approximately linearly with s. In Experiment 3 (n=30, d up to 32), BanditClustering maintains a consistently low error rate (~0.01) with a stable average budget, whereas the fixed-budget Adaptive Clustering baseline from Ariu et al. (2024) shows increasing error as d grows.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares BanditClustering against two baselines. First, against uniform sampling + K-means (Experiments 1, 2, 4): in the sparse regime (n=20, d=1000, s-sparse Δ with h_s=15/√s), BanditClustering requires fewer total observations to achieve <1% error, with the advantage growing as sparsity increases; in Experiment 2 (n up to 5000, d=10n, s=10, h=5), BanditClustering's budget scales linearly with n while uniform sampling would require budget proportional to nd, making accurate clustering infeasible at smaller budgets. Second, against the Adaptive Clustering algorithm of Ariu et al. (2024) (Experiment 3, n=30, d_γ=2^γ for γ=1..5, Bernoulli observations, δ=0.8): BanditClustering maintains a consistently low error rate (~0.01) with a largely unchanged average budget as d increases from 2 to 32, while Adaptive Clustering (run at a fixed budget of T=400,000) shows increasing error with d. The paper also provides a theoretical comparison to Yang et al. (2024) and Thuot et al. (2025), showing that adaptive feature selection removes the high-dimensional term d^{3/2}√(n·log(1/δ))/‖Δ‖² and yields a potential budget reduction by a factor of n ∧ d/s in the sparse, balanced setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Sequential Halving algorithm (Karnin et al., 2013) and its Bracketing variant (Zhao et al., 2023), repurposing them as a subroutine (CompareSequentialHalving) to identify discriminative features in a clustering context. It departs from prior bandit clustering work (Yang et al., 2024; Thuot et al., 2025; Yavas et al., 2025) where the entire feature vector of a chosen item is observed per step, by restricting observations to a single feature of a single item, which enables a fundamentally different exploration-exploitation trade-off. The problem is framed as a special case of the crowdsourcing model in Ariu et al. (2024), for which the authors provide the first theoretical guarantees for an adaptive procedure, and draws on adaptive sensing (Castro, 2014) and dueling bandits literature for the identification sub-problems.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first tight, instance-dependent sample complexity characterization for adaptive bandit clustering with single-feature observations, with matching upper and lower bounds in the sparse setting. The practical implication is a potential budget reduction by a factor of n∧(d/s) compared to methods that must observe full feature vectors, which is significant in high-dimensional crowdsourcing scenarios. Open questions include whether the trade-off in the general (non-sparse) gap vector is truly optimal, how to extend the K>2 cluster case beyond the current K² scaling, and whether the method can handle within-group heterogeneity without degrading guarantees.
