## Problem

DPO's underlying Bradley-Terry model does not guarantee a unique maximum likelihood estimator in the infinite candidate space of responses, meaning the DPO loss can have multiple minimizers of which only one satisfies the slope-1 linearity condition required to minimize the RLHF objective. This theoretical flaw implies that DPO may converge to a suboptimal solution that fails to properly align the policy with the true reward, a problem that cannot be fixed by simply collecting more data.

## Input / Output

The method consumes pairwise preference data (prompt, preferred response, less-preferred response) or multi-response data (prompt with multiple responses ranked by quality, as in Ultrafeedback). Beyond the standard positive/negative pair, EPA additionally draws weak negatives by sampling mismatched responses from other prompts within the same mini-batch, making the loss a batch-level contrastive objective rather than a purely per-pair one. The output is a LoRA-fine-tuned policy (Mistral-7B or Llama3-8B) that produces aligned text responses, evaluated via win-rates on Alpaca-Eval 2.0 and average scores on MT-Bench.

## Method

The paper introduces the Infinite Preference Model (IPM), an energy-based model that posits a Boltzmann distribution over the probability that a response is preferred over all other candidates (one-to-infinite), in contrast to BTM's one-to-one sigmoidal model. Theorem 3.1 proves that IPM's MLE is guaranteed to be unique and equivalent to the RLHF minimizer, resolving BTM's non-uniqueness pathology. For practical training, the authors derive the Energy Preference Alignment (EPA) loss from the energy discrepancy framework of Schröder et al. (2023), yielding a negative log-softmax objective where the denominator contains the positive, strong negatives (same-prompt less-preferred responses), and weak negatives (mismatched responses from other prompts in the batch). The gradient decomposes into a strong contrast term (identical to DPO when only one strong negative is present) plus a weak contrast regularization term that prevents the policy from undesirably increasing likelihood of unrelated responses. Ablations confirm that balancing strong and weak negatives is critical: removing either degrades performance, and the optimal ratio depends on the number of available strong negatives per prompt.

## Results

On UF-BINARIZED (pair-wise data), EPA achieves 19.20/19.26% length-controlled/raw win-rate on Alpaca-Eval 2.0 and 7.71 on MT-Bench, outperforming DPO (17.43/15.24%, 7.55) by ~1.8 points on AE 2.0 and 0.16 on MT-Bench. On UF-ALL (multi-response data), EPA reaches 22.03/21.44% on AE 2.0 and 7.58 on MT-Bench, beating the strongest baseline InfoNCA (17.30/16.25%, 7.50) by ~4.7 points on AE 2.0. EPA also shows better KL-Reward frontier behavior and slower overfitting during training compared to DPO.

## Baselines & Comparisons

EPA consistently outperforms all baselines on both Alpaca-Eval 2.0 and MT-Bench. Against DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023), the most direct BTM-based competitor, EPA gains +1.77 points on AE 2.0 length-controlled win-rate (19.20 vs. 17.43) and +0.16 on MT-Bench (7.71 vs. 7.55) with UF-BINARIZED; with UF-ALL, EPA beats DPO-PL by +6.08 points on AE 2.0 (22.03 vs. 15.95). EPA also surpasses IPO (12.97/10.13%), KTO (12.62/11.29%), and NCA (14.64/11.27%) by 4–6 points on AE 2.0. In the multi-response setting, EPA outperforms InfoNCA (17.30/16.25%) by +4.73 points on AE 2.0. When loss modification tricks (CPO, R-DPO, SimPO, WPO) are applied to both BTM and IPM, EPA+tricks still matches or exceeds DPO+tricks (e.g., EPA+WPO: 22.80/22.26% vs. DPO+WPO: 21.90/21.04% on AE 2.0), confirming the theoretical advantage of the EBM formulation is preserved even with practical heuristics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023), which established the equivalence between minimizing the RLHF objective and maximum likelihood estimation under the Bradley-Terry preference model, and on Schröder et al. (2023), who provided the energy discrepancy framework for fitting discrete EBMs via contrastive losses. The key departure is identifying that BTM's MLE is not guaranteed to be unique in the infinite candidate space of y|x, thereby breaking DPO's theoretical guarantee that its minimizer coincides with the RLHF minimizer. The authors repurpose the energy discrepancy framework to define the Infinite Preference Model (IPM), an EBM that models one-to-infinite preference and provably has a unique MLE, then derive the practical EPA loss as a contrastive approximation of that MLE.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of BTM's non-unique MLE in infinite spaces is a fundamental theoretical gap that undermines the justification for all BTM-based offline alignment methods (DPO, IPO, DPO-PL, etc.), suggesting their theoretical guarantees are weaker than previously assumed. Practically, EPA consistently outperforms DPO across multiple benchmarks and base models, and the ablation showing that adding weak negatives to DPO does not replicate EPA's gains isolates the benefit to the EBM structure rather than extra computation. Open questions include finding more efficient MLE approximation methods (EPA's linear scaling in negative count is a limitation), developing loss modifications tailored to IPM, and understanding whether the slightly higher alignment tax on GSM8k/MMLU is an artifact of aggressive preference optimization or inherent to the EBM formulation.
