## Problem

Obtaining small specialist language models for scarce, domain-specific data typically requires retraining a full model from scratch for each specialization target (as in CRISP), making cost scale linearly with the number of downstream tasks. The paper asks how to leverage a large multi-domain pre-training set so that specialized models can be instantiated in a flash once the target data is revealed, without any additional training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250009/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At specialization time, the method takes a small specialist dataset (as few as 3–1000 samples) and estimates domain weights by embedding documents with SentenceBERT and matching them to pre-computed k-means centroids over 4096 pre-training domains. These domain weights are passed through a small 2-layer MLP to produce expert coefficients, which are then used to linearly combine the expert parameters into a single 110M-parameter stand-alone model. The output is a fully merged, self-contained model requiring no access to the expert bank at inference time.

## Method

The Soup-of-Experts architecture consists of shared parameters S, n expert parameter matrices E_1,…,E_n (each the same size as the base model), and a 2-layer MLP φ_ω that maps domain weights h ∈ R^k to expert coefficients α ∈ R^n. The instantiated model is Θ = S + Σ_j α_j E_j. Training proceeds by sampling random sparse domain weights h from a meta-distribution π at each step, instantiating the model, sampling a mini-batch from the corresponding data mixture mix(h), and backpropagating the next-token-prediction loss. A key computational insight is that gradients with respect to each expert are simply the merged-model gradient rescaled by its coefficient (∇_{E_i} L = α_i ∇_Θ ℓ), so the backpropagation overhead from n experts is negligible compared to the base model's gradient computation. At specialization time, no training is needed: the domain weights estimated from the target dataset are passed through the MLP and the experts are merged in closed form, yielding a small specialist model in a single forward pass.

## Results

The Soup-of-Experts (110M instantiated model, 128 experts, 14B total parameters) achieves the best average specialized loss across 16 PILE domains while remaining only slightly worse than generic pre-training on the generic RedPajama v2 loss. On downstream tasks (MMLU, ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge) with a 335M model and 32 experts, the SoE consistently outperforms generic pre-training at equal training time and reaches a higher accuracy plateau. The SoE achieves 77% of the training throughput of generic pre-training, and training 128 experts of 110M parameters takes approximately one day on 8 A100 GPUs. Only 3 samples from a specialist dataset are sufficient to instantiate a model matching generic pre-training quality, and 100–1000 samples recover the optimal SoE instantiation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Domain Experts (Gross et al., 2017), the Soup-of-Experts achieves lower specialized loss and scales far better: domain experts require training one model per pre-training domain (infeasible at 4096 domains), while the SoE trains a single model. Against CRISP (Grangier et al., 2024b), the SoE matches or beats the specialized loss without requiring a full retraining run per specialization domain; CRISP's cost scales linearly with the number of target domains. Against standard model merging (Wortsman et al., 2022), the SoE becomes advantageous when mixing more than 4 domains, and requires substantially fewer total training steps (64,000 vs. 64,000 + 64×10,000 for the merging baseline). On the generic pre-training loss, the SoE is only marginally worse than a model explicitly trained to minimize that loss, confirming it retains general knowledge while specializing. In fine-tuning experiments, the SoE maintains its advantage across all 16 PILE domains regardless of the number of fine-tuning tokens available, and on the uspto domain, the generic model requires 10M fine-tuning tokens to match the SoE's out-of-the-box performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the model-merging / model-soups literature (Wortsman et al., 2022; Rame et al., 2023) by moving the linear combination of parameters from a post-hoc fine-tuning step into the pretraining objective itself, learning a bank of experts designed from the start to be averaged. It also builds directly on CRISP (Grangier et al., 2024b), which showed that importance-sampling domain weights yields strong specialists, but replaces CRISP's per-task retraining with a single pretrained Soup-of-Experts that can be instantiated for any weight vector via a small MLP. The architecture is conceptually adjacent to Mixture-of-Experts (Fedus et al., 2022; Shazeer et al., 2017) but differs fundamentally: rather than routing at inference time over a large parameter set, it produces a small, standalone model with no routing overhead, and the 'experts' are full model copies rather than sub-networks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Soup-of-Experts addresses a concrete and growing practical need: deploying many small, domain-specific models under strict size and latency budgets without paying the linear-in-numbers-of-tasks retraining cost of CRISP or domain experts. The key open question is scalability — all experiments use 110M–335M parameter models and 4,096 domains from a single web-crawl corpus, so it remains unclear whether the approach retains its advantage at the 7B+ scale where most production LLMs live, or whether the meta-distribution must be carefully tuned per deployment. The paper also notes that low-rank experts underperform dense ones, leaving the parameter-efficiency frontier unresolved, and the natural next step of combining the Soup-of-Experts with a MoE base architecture is left as future work.
