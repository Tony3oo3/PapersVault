## Problem

Substructure-based graph grammars offer interpretability and data efficiency for molecular generation, but learning the grammar requires either costly expert annotation or unreliable algorithmic heuristics that produce uninterpretable production rules. While multi-modal foundation models (MMFMs) possess broad chemical knowledge, their potential for advanced chemistry reasoning in grammar induction has remained unvalidated by domain experts. The paper asks whether an MMFM can be embedded within a sound algorithmic framework to induce interpretable molecular grammars without any expert intervention.

## Input / Output

The method takes a small class-specific molecule dataset (as few as 11 examples) and renders each molecule as an RDKit image with specific substructures highlighted in color, paired with dynamically generated textual descriptions of each motif. At each decomposition step, the MMFM receives a grid of annotated image cells and must make a discrete selection (a single clique or a pair of cliques), with all intermediate states re-rendered as the decomposition progresses. The output is a pooled hyperedge replacement grammar (HRG) where production rules are frequency-weighted across all molecules in the dataset, which can then stochastically generate new molecules as SMILES strings via rule-applicability sampling.

## Method

FMG formulates grammar induction as clique tree decomposition, serializing the construction into four MMFM-guided selection steps: triangulating the clique graph, merging cliques into chemically meaningful motifs, eliminating spanning tree edges, and selecting a root motif. At each step, intermediate states are rendered as annotated RDKit images with dynamic textual descriptions, and GPT-4o is prompted to make single or pair selections using chain-of-thought reasoning, with hard constraints (tree decomposition soundness) enforced algorithmically while soft constraints (chemical meaningfulness, synthesizability) are delegated to the MMFM's cross-modal understanding. To handle the stochasticity of repeated runs, FMG employs an LLM-based Swiss tournament where 'design stories' composed from logged chain-of-thought artifacts are pitted against each other, with outcomes consolidated via the Bradley-Terry model to rank decompositions by chemical soundness. Images are used as the primary input modality over SMILES/SELFIES because they naturally support substructure-level highlighting and bond-disjoint clique annotation that text-based molecular encodings cannot express.

## Results

On small datasets (Isocyanates, Acrylates, Chain Extenders), FMG achieves 100% validity and uniqueness across all three, with Retro* synthesizability scores of 61.7%, 93.0%, and 99.1%, and near-perfect class membership (99.6–100%). On real-world datasets (HOPV, PTC), FMG maintains 100% validity and uniqueness, achieves 70%/78% Retro* score (surpassing expert-annotated RW by 12%/18%), and ties for best diversity (0.93/0.93). FMG is one of only two methods to reach 100% uniqueness on real-world data, while VAE baselines collapse to <45% uniqueness and CLM baselines (MolT5, Text+Chem T5) drop below 62% validity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FMG decisively outperforms the grammar-based baseline DEG on synthesizability (RS), achieving 70%/78% vs. 19%/38% on HOPV/PTC, and 61.7%/93.0%/99.1% vs. 27.2%/43.9%/67.5% on small datasets, indicating that MMFM-guided decomposition captures chemical semantics that neural sampling cannot. Against the expert-annotated Random Walk (RW) baseline, FMG surpasses RS by 12% (HOPV) and 18% (PTC) while matching its 100% uniqueness, showing that MMFM reasoning can replace costly expert annotation. VAE methods (JT-VAE, Hier-VAE) collapse on small datasets with <10% uniqueness, and even on real-world data their uniqueness (8–43%) is far below FMG's 100%. CLM baselines (MolT5, Text+Chem T5) fail on validity (42–62%) and uniqueness, while GPT-4 ICL achieves high RS/Memb but only 71–91% validity and 35–95% uniqueness, confirming that in-context generation without a formal grammar framework is unreliable. MoLeR (FT) is the closest competitor on real-world data, matching FMG on uniqueness but trailing significantly on RS (42%/71% vs. 70%/78%), suggesting scaffold-based neural models lack the synthesizability awareness that MMFM reasoning provides.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the grammar-based molecular generation line (Kajino 2019, Guo et al. 2022b, Sun et al. 2024) by replacing expert annotation or neural parameterization of grammar construction with multi-modal foundation model (GPT-4o) decision-making within a sound clique tree decomposition algorithm. The algorithmic backbone draws on classical chordality testing (Tarjan & Yannakakis 1984) and hyperedge replacement grammar conversion (Aguinaga et al. 2018), while the judgment steps leverage chain-of-thought prompting (Wei et al. 2022) and LLM debate/tournament mechanisms (Khan et al. 2024). Unlike prior multi-modal molecular models that focus on encoding or sequence generation, FMG repurposes MMFM reasoning as a plug-in module for a formal combinatorial procedure, with rendered molecular images (via RDKit) serving as the primary visual input rather than SMILES/SELFIES strings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FMG addresses a practical bottleneck in grammar-based molecular generation: the reliance on costly expert annotation to construct interpretable substructure vocabularies. The expert-validated LLM judge (71% agreement, p=1.1e-5) and the 'design narrative' artifacts offer a path toward automated, interpretable molecular discovery workflows without human-in-the-loop annotation. Key limitations include reduced diversity on very small datasets (≤30 samples), a sharp membership-vs-coverage tradeoff controlled by the ensemble size k, and dependence on GPT-4o API availability and cost. Open questions include whether the image-based substructure identification generalizes to 3D conformations or reaction mechanisms, and whether the tournament-based selection scales to thousands of molecules without prohibitive API costs.
