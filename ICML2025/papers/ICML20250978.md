## Problem

Classifying single-molecule force spectroscopy (SMFS) force curves into no-molecule, single-molecule, and multiple-molecule classes currently relies on expert visual inspection, requiring 12–24 hours per session of 2,000–5,000 curves and demanding significant domain expertise. No publicly available time-series datasets exist for non-specific pulling of multi-domain protein molecules, and no automated method has been demonstrated to handle the variable-length, noisy, multi-domain nature of these curves without requiring chemically functionalized probes or engineered molecular fingerprints.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250978/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each input is a pair of variable-length time series: a force trace F and an extension trace X (lengths range from 736 to 8,890 points across datasets). The force trace is resampled to 400 points via linear interpolation and min-max normalized for the force-trace branch; the extension trace is used to compute per-point contour length estimates via a polymer elastic model (WLC by default, with fixed persistence length) for the physics-based branch, where values exceeding a threshold (default 1000 nm) are filtered. The output is a 3-class probability vector (no molecule / single molecule / multiple molecules) from a softmax layer, with the model requiring only ~30 annotated experimental samples for competitive performance.

## Method

PemNN employs a dual-branch architecture: a force-trace branch processes the normalized force signal through three 1D-CNN blocks (128/256/128 filters, kernels 8/5/3) plus an LSTM layer, while a physics-based branch converts force-extension pairs into contour-length estimates using the worm-like-chain model (or FJC/FRC), filters by a length threshold, and processes the resulting series through an identical CNN+LSTM stack. The two branches are fused via early fusion (default: Early-Conv, which concatenates feature maps along the filter dimension and applies a 1×1 convolution to enable cross-branch information exchange) or late fusion (GAP concatenation or gated weighting). Fused features pass through two additional CNN blocks, are globally average-pooled, concatenated with the LSTM outputs, and fed to a 3-neuron softmax classifier trained with categorical cross-entropy. A key enabling contribution is a physics-based Monte Carlo simulation engine that generates realistic SMFS datasets for all three classes (including stochastic adhesion/detachment and partial molecule attachment), enabling a physics-guided pretraining strategy where the model is trained entirely on simulated data and transferred to experimental data without any experimental labels, achieving 72.0% average accuracy versus 79.6% with ~30 experimental training samples.

## Results

PemNN achieves 98.9±1.9% average accuracy across four simulated SMFS datasets, outperforming the best baseline (LSTMFCN) by 6%. On experimental datasets with only ~30 training samples (5% training proportion), PemNN attains 79.6±5.2% average accuracy, surpassing the best baseline by 11.4%. Even without any experimental annotations, physics-guided pretraining on simulated data yields 72.0±5.9% average accuracy. In applied SMFS analysis of Titin I27O, PemNN produces an interquartile range of 52.63 pN for unfolding forces—only a quarter of the IQR from heuristic or raw-data methods—while recovering the expected most probable force of 206.68 pN (expected: 204±26 pN).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PemNN is statistically significantly more accurate than all five baselines (Friedman test + Wilcoxon signed-rank with Holm correction). At the headline 5%/80% split (~30 training samples), PemNN (79.6%) outperforms LSTMFCN (68.3%) by 11.4%, Triplet (55.6%) by 24.0%, ResNet (37.5%) by 42.1%, FCN (35.9%) by 43.7%, and InceptionTime (35.3%) by 44.3%. LSTMFCN is the closest competitor, narrowing the gap to ~0.9% at the 80%/20% split (88.5% vs. 87.7%). On simulated datasets, PemNN reaches 98.9% accuracy versus 92.9% for LSTMFCN (the best baseline), with the largest gap (15%) on Titin I27O and the smallest (6%) on Insect UtrN-R3. The Triplet network, designed specifically for SMFS data with functionalized probes, performs worst among baselines on non-specific pulling data, confirming that prior SMFS-specific methods do not transfer to the harder no-fingerprint setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends prior SMFS classification work (Waite et al., 2023; Doffini et al., 2023) that relied on chemically functionalized probes and molecular fingerprints to the more challenging setting of non-specific pulling of multi-domain proteins without engineered fingerprints. Methodologically, it departs from standard time series classifiers (FCN, ResNet, InceptionTime, LSTMFCN) by introducing a dual-branch architecture (PemNN) that fuses raw force-trace features with physics-derived features (contour length estimated via worm-like-chain, freely-jointed-chain, or freely-rotating-chain models), and it introduces a Monte Carlo simulation engine that generates training data for all three classes (no, single, multiple molecules) using the Dudko-Hummer-Szabo transition rate model. The physics-guided pretraining strategy, which substitutes labeled experimental data with simulated data augmented via linear combinations of reference curves, draws on data augmentation ideas from image and sound classification but applies them in a physics-constrained setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial for the SMFS community: reducing single-molecule data extraction from a full day of expert visual inspection to under an hour, and providing the first publicly available time-series datasets for non-specific pulling of multi-domain proteins (titin, utrophin, dystrophin). A key open question is whether the physics-guided pretraining can be made agnostic to energy landscape parameters (k0, Δx‡, ΔG‡), which currently require separate experiments to determine; the simulation's assumption of homogeneous domains also limits fidelity for proteins with heterogeneous domains like utrophin and dystrophin. Future work on extracting individual domain energy landscapes from small experimental datasets could close this gap and extend the approach to a broader range of multi-domain proteins.
