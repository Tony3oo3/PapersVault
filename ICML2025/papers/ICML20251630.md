## Problem

Existing 3D molecular pretrained representation (MPR) models treat molecules as discrete sets of atoms, ignoring the surrounding 3D space that physically contains electrons, electromagnetic fields, and quantum phenomena relevant to molecular properties. The paper asks whether explicitly modeling this space beyond atom positions can improve MPR, and demonstrates that even naively adding random virtual points yields non-trivial gains, motivating a principled grid-based framework.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251630/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a 3D molecular conformation and discretizes the enclosing cuboid into uniform grid cells of 0.49Å edge length, where each cell is either an atom cell (atom type + precise 3D coordinates) or a non-atom cell (NULL type + cell center). After adaptive grid merging (recursively combining 2×2×2 blocks of empty cells) or random sampling, the input is a set of ~1K cells processed by a Transformer. The output is a molecular representation embedding fine-tuned for regression (HOMO, LUMO, GAP, energies, dipole, solubility, etc.) or classification (BBBP, BACE) on limited labeled data.

## Method

SpaceFormer is a Transformer-based architecture that treats a molecule as a 3D 'image' of grid cells rather than a point set of atoms. Three key components enable efficient processing: (1) grid-based discretization at 0.49Å ensuring at most one atom per cell; (2) adaptive grid merging that recursively combines 2×2×2 blocks of empty cells into larger cells (reducing ~8K cells to ~1K without hyperparameter tuning); and (3) linear-complexity 3D positional encoding combining RoPE extended to 3D continuous coordinates for directional information and random Fourier features for pairwise distance. Pretraining uses a Masked Auto-Encoder objective where a portion of grid cells are masked and the model predicts both whether each masked cell contains an atom and, if so, its type and precise sub-cell offset — a stronger task than denoising-based approaches used by prior 3D MPR models.

## Results

Across 15 downstream molecular property prediction tasks (computational and experimental), SpaceFormer achieves the best result on 10 tasks and ranks within the top 2 on 14 tasks. On key computational properties, SpaceFormer surpasses the runner-up by approximately 20%: HOMO (0.0042 vs. 0.0050 MAE), LUMO (0.0040 vs. 0.0057), GAP (0.0064 vs. 0.0080), and Dipmom (0.0083 vs. 0.0106). On the QM9 benchmark (Table 8), SpaceFormer further reduces MAE to 0.000594 (HOMO), 0.000544 (LUMO), and 0.00106 (GAP), outperforming Uni-Mol by 30–35%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SpaceFormer is compared against five baselines: two 2D graph-based models (GROVER, GEM), one 3D GNN (3D Infomax), and two 3D atom-based Transformer models (Uni-Mol, Mol-AE). Against the primary baseline Uni-Mol, SpaceFormer wins on 12 of 15 tasks, with the largest margins on computational properties (e.g., HOMO 0.0042 vs. 0.0052, a ~20% improvement; Dipmom 0.0083 vs. 0.0106, ~22% improvement), but loses on BBBP (0.8605 vs. 0.9066 AUC) and is marginally worse on MME (0.2933 vs. 0.2901 MAE). Against Mol-AE (Uni-Mol + atom-based MAE), SpaceFormer wins on 13 of 15 tasks, confirming that the gains come from modeling 3D space rather than merely adopting MAE pretraining. GEM is competitive on BACE (0.9851 vs. 0.9657 AUC) and BBBP (0.8863 vs. 0.8605) but is outperformed on all computational property tasks. GROVER and 3D Infomax are consistently outperformed across nearly all tasks, with 3D Infomax showing particularly large gaps on Dipmom (0.0291 vs. 0.0083) and D3_disp_corr (0.2285 vs. 0.0053). The paper attributes the BBBP loss to measurement variability and known MoleculeNet dataset quality issues.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in 3D molecular pretrained representation (MPR), where models like Uni-Mol, Mol-AE, and 3D Infomax treat molecules as discrete sets of atom positions and model only inter-atomic interactions. SpaceFormer instead treats a molecule as a 3D 'image' by discretizing the surrounding space into grid cells, drawing an analogy to pixel grids in computer vision, and processes them with a Transformer augmented by novel 3D positional encodings (RoPE for direction, Random Fourier Features for distance). The MAE pretraining objective is adapted from vision (He et al., 2022) but reinterpreted for molecular grid cells, where the model must predict both cell occupancy and sub-cell atom position. The paper also connects conceptually to virtual points in 3D point cloud fusion and auxiliary tokens in ViT/LLMs, but distinguishes its use by grounding empty grid cells in physical principles (electron density, field distributions) rather than treating them as purely learned artifacts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that modeling the 3D space beyond atoms yields consistent gains across 14/15 tasks validates a physically motivated design choice that prior 3D MPR work had overlooked, and the adaptive grid merging strategy provides a practical recipe for scaling grid-based molecular models without hyperparameter tuning. However, the approach remains limited to small organic molecules (ZINC, GDB-17 scale); extending to larger systems like proteins or force-field tasks is explicitly left as future work. The computational cost, while improved by linear-complexity PE and FlashAttention, still exceeds that of atom-only baselines, and the BBBP result suggests that experimental-property tasks with high measurement noise may not benefit as much. Open questions include whether the grid-based formulation transfers to multi-scale or periodic systems and whether the MAE advantage over denoising persists at larger pretraining scales.
