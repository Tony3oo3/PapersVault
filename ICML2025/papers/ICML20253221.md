## Problem

Dropout is the most widely used regularization technique in deep learning, yet its role in Multiple Instance Learning (MIL) has never been formally investigated. The standard two-stage MIL training scheme (frozen pre-trained backbone + trainable aggregator) suffers from noisy feature embeddings and weak supervision, but it remains unclear whether, how, and which instances should be dropped to regularize MIL effectively.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20253221/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on a bag of N instance feature embeddings (e.g., 1024-dim ResNet-50 vectors or 512-dim SimCLR vectors per WSI tile, with N ≈ 5000–10000 per bag) and produces a single bag-level binary label. Unusually, the dropout acts at the instance level by zeroing entire rows of the feature matrix rather than individual neuron entries, and it is applied within a 3-layer shallow feature extractor (1024→512→256→128 or 512→256→128→64) placed between the frozen backbone and the MIL aggregator, not inside the aggregator itself.

## Method

MIL-Dropout systematically drops the top-k most important instances plus G similar instances per top-k instance, based on two key components: (i) a non-parametric averaging-based attention (avgpool over embedding dimension followed by sigmoid) that computes instance importance at each layer of the shallow feature extractor, avoiding the instability of using attention scores from the previous optimization iteration; and (ii) a query-based selection mechanism that computes cosine similarity between each top-k instance and all remaining instances to identify G highly similar instances for co-dropping. The counterintuitive core insight is that dropping the most important (not random or least important) instances reduces gradient direction error during training and drives convergence to flatter local minima, improving both accuracy and robustness to noise. A normalization factor γ = N/(N−K(1+G)) stabilizes training, and the method adds only two hyperparameters (K and G) with negligible computational overhead (O(ND^(l)) per iteration since K ≪ N).

## Results

On five MIL benchmark datasets, ABMIL+MIL-Dropout achieves SOTA accuracy of 96.4% (MUSK1), 95.4% (MUSK2), 78.9% (FOX), 91.7% (TIGER), and 93.4% (ELEPHANT), yielding an average improvement of 9.72% over the ABMIL baseline. On WSI classification, MIL-Dropout plugged into four aggregators (ABMIL, DSMIL, TransMIL, DTFD-MIL) delivers average gains of 2.3% accuracy, 2.46% F1, and 2.48% AUC on CAMELYON16, and 2.05% accuracy, 2.44% F1, and 1.80% AUC on TCGA-NSCLC. The method adds only two hyperparameters (K and G) with negligible computational overhead (O(ND^(l)) per iteration).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ABMIL+MIL-Dropout outperforms all 11 baselines on all five MIL benchmark datasets, with the largest margins over the simplest aggregators: +7.2% over ABMIL on FOX (78.9% vs. 61.5%) and +9.6% over ABMIL-Gated on MUSK1 (96.7% vs. 90.0%). Against the strongest prior method DSMIL (Li et al., 2021), MIL-Dropout gains +3.2% on MUSK1, +2.4% on MUSK2, +6.0% on FOX, +4.8% on TIGER, and +0.9% on ELEPHANT. On WSI datasets (Table 2), MIL-Dropout consistently improves every aggregator it is plugged into: ABMIL gains up to +4.1% AUC on CAMELYON16, TransMIL gains +2.9% AUC, and DTFD-MIL(MaxS) gains +3.2% AUC. The method also beats TransMIL (Shao et al., 2021) and DTFD-MIL (Zhang et al., 2022) when applied to the simpler ABMIL backbone, showing that regularization can close the gap to more complex aggregation architectures. On the EBRAINS dataset (rebuttal), MIL-Dropout still improves ABMIL by +5.0% accuracy and TransMIL by +3.9%, though gains are smaller with foundation-model (UNI) features.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper formalizes and extends the informal 'DropInstance' regularization used in DSMIL (Li et al., 2021) into a systematic MIL-specific dropout method. It draws on standard dropout theory (Hinton et al., 2012; Srivastava et al., 2014) and attention-based dropout (Choe & Shim, 2019), but repurposes the concept from dropping neurons to dropping entire instances within a bag, which is the natural granularity in MIL. The theoretical analysis leverages gradient direction error (Liu et al., 2023; Jastrzebski et al., 2020) and flat-minimum/sharpness literature (Dinh et al., 2017; Keskar et al., 2016) to explain why dropping the most important instances—counterintuitively—improves optimization and generalization. The method is positioned as orthogonal to existing MIL aggregators (ABMIL, DSMIL, TransMIL, DTFD-MIL), serving as a plug-in regularization rather than a new aggregation architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MIL-Dropout offers a simple, parameter-free, plug-and-play regularization that consistently improves a range of MIL aggregators with negligible computational overhead, addressing the well-known noisy-feature problem in two-stage WSI training without requiring self-supervised pretraining. The counterintuitive finding that dropping the most important instances forces the model to discover diverse positive representations rather than over-relying on a single discriminative subset has broader implications for weakly-supervised learning more generally. Limitations include failure to integrate with transformer-based MIL blocks (likely due to interference with layer normalization), restriction to shallow feature extractors, and no accounting for instance-level uncertainty. Open questions include extension to video frame detection, 3D point cloud classification, and whether the top-k dropping principle generalizes beyond attention-score-based importance.
