## Problem

Preference-based reward learning methods that rely on the Bradley-Terry model degrade substantially when human feedback contains even a small fraction of erroneous labels, yet existing robust approaches either require explicit identification of false preferences, prior distributional assumptions, or additional classification modules. This paper asks whether one can design a reward alignment procedure that is provably robust to unknown false preferences without any of these requirements, while also providing a certifiable upper bound on the number of human queries needed.

## Input / Output

The method consumes batches of N trajectory segment pairs (each segment being a short rollout of Z steps generated by sampling-based MPPI in the MJX simulator) along with binary human preference labels indicating which segment is preferred. The output is not a single reward network but a maintained ensemble of M reward parameters (3-layer MLPs, 256 hidden units, ReLU activations, Tanh output in [-1,1]) that collectively define a shrinking hypothesis space; the planning reward is the ensemble mean. The hypothesis space is updated per batch via a voting-based cut rather than gradient descent on a loss, and the final reward is used to drive MPC-based control.

## Method

HSBC maintains a hypothesis space Θ of reward parameters and iteratively refines it through three steps: (1) sampling an ensemble from the current space, (2) actively querying a human on trajectory pairs that exhibit disagreement across the ensemble, and (3) applying a batch cut defined by a voting function V_i(θ) that counts how many of the N preference constraints each θ satisfies. The key robustness mechanism replaces the strict requirement V_i(θ)=N with a conservative threshold V_i(θ) > ⌊(1−γ)N⌋−0.5, where γ is a user-set conservativeness level bounding the maximum fraction of false labels per batch; this guarantees the true reward θ_H remains in the hypothesis space regardless of which specific preferences are false. The voting function is made differentiable via sigmoid approximations of the Heaviside step, enabling gradient-based ensemble sampling with warm-starting, filtering, and densification. A PAC-style theorem bounds the total query complexity as O(ζ(d log ζ + log(log(1/ε)/δ)) log(1/ε)), where ζ is the disagreement coefficient and d is the VC-dimension of the reward class.

## Results

Across 6 control tasks (Cartpole, Walker, Humanoid, two dexterous manipulation tasks, and Go2 quadruped standup), HSBC achieves performance close to the oracle reward at 0% false preference rate (e.g., 137.8 vs. oracle 148.6 on Cartpole; 459.4 vs. oracle 472.9 on Walker). Under 30% false preferences, HSBC retains strong performance (111.3 on Cartpole, 417.2 on Walker) while the PEBBLE baseline degrades severely (42.8 and 277.0 respectively). In the multi-method comparison (Table 2), HSBC outperforms all five baselines (PEBBLE, RIME, SURF, MAE, T-CE) on both Cartpole and Walker at 20% and 30% false rates, with the largest margin on Cartpole-30% where HSBC scores 111.3 versus the next-best RIME at 81.0.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HSBC clearly dominates all five baselines under high false-preference rates. On Cartpole-30%, HSBC (111.3) outperforms RIME (81.0), SURF (62.3), MAE (59.9), T-CE (52.0), and PEBBLE (42.8) by 20–68 reward points. On Walker-30%, HSBC (417.2) beats T-CE (345.6), RIME (310.2), SURF (292.0), MAE (288.3), and PEBBLE (277.0) by 72–140 points. At 20% false rate, the gap narrows: on Walker, MAE (425.5) and T-CE (410.8) are competitive with HSBC (447.0), while on Cartpole HSBC still leads by a wide margin (130.7 vs. 98.6 for MAE). At 0% false rate, HSBC matches or slightly exceeds the PEBBLE baseline across all six tasks (e.g., 459.4 vs. 444.4 on Walker; 137.8 vs. 143.3 on Cartpole), confirming no robustness-accuracy trade-off. The paper also notes that HSBC handles 'Mistake' and 'Myopic' B-Pref teacher types well (122.6 and 127.0 vs. oracle 148.6) but struggles with the 'Stoc' teacher (93.6), where late-stage near-tie trajectories produce highly noisy labels.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the hypothesis space cutting paradigm from Jin et al. (2022) and Xie et al. (2024), which were restricted to linear weight-feature reward parameterizations, to general neural network rewards where cuts become non-linear. It also builds on the batch active preference learning framework of Biyik & Sadigh (2018), but replaces Bayesian posterior updates with a deterministic voting-based cutting mechanism. The key conceptual departure from prior robust PbRL methods (RIME, CANDERE-COACH) is that HSBC avoids explicit false-label identification or filtering entirely, instead using a conservative voting threshold to guarantee the true reward is never cut out of the hypothesis space.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The provable robustness guarantee—ensuring θ_H remains in the hypothesis space regardless of the number of false preferences up to a known bound—without requiring any prior on the error distribution or additional classification modules, is a clean and practical contribution for human-in-the-loop robotics. Limitations include evaluation only in simulation with MPC-based trajectory generation, a fixed conservativeness hyperparameter γ that must be set a priori, and demonstrated failure under the 'Stoc' teacher model where late-stage trajectories are too similar for reliable labeling. Open questions include whether the approach scales to higher-dimensional continuous control tasks, whether RL-based trajectory generation (rather than MPC) preserves the theoretical guarantees, and how the method behaves when the false preference rate exceeds the assumed γ.
