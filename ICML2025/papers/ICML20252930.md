## Problem

Brain graph datasets from multi-site fMRI collections suffer from two intertwined challenges: (1) low signal-to-noise ratio and high dimensionality make it difficult for models to capture local discriminative biomarkers, and (2) distribution shifts across sites and individuals cause models to rely on spurious correlations, degrading generalization. Existing graph contrastive learning methods for brain data use random augmentations (truncating BOLD signals, randomly modifying edges) that destroy the local topological structure carrying critical saliency information, failing to address either problem effectively.

## Input / Output

The method takes resting-state fMRI BOLD time series as input, which are parcellated using the AAL atlas (90 cortical + 26 cerebellar ROIs) and converted into undirected brain functional connectivity graphs via Pearson correlation, then sparsified to the top 5% edges. Node features are extracted through a three-layer BrainNetCNN-style module (one E2E cross-shape filter layer followed by two E2N convolutional layers). The model outputs a binary disease class label (e.g., ASD vs. TC), a learned brain invariant subgraph identifying causally relevant local structures, and a ranked list of important ROIs for interpretability.

## Method

CIA-GCL introduces a causal decoupling mechanism that learns a Gumbel-Softmax edge mask to partition each brain graph into an invariant subgraph G^inv (causally linked to the label) and a spurious subgraph G^s (driven by environmental factors like site differences). Around G^inv, a novel invariance-aware augmentation strategy generates two positive views: the original graph (G^inv ⊕ G^s) and a mixed view (G^inv ⊕ G^ms) where G^ms is sampled from spurious subgraphs of different-label subjects, preserving local topology while injecting diverse distributional shifts. Training combines three losses: a causal loss that maximizes mutual information between same-label invariant subgraphs while minimizing I(G^inv, G^s), a contrastive loss using G^inv as anchor with augmented views as positives (plus an orthogonality penalty between the two augmented views), and an invariant loss that minimizes prediction risk across the three environment views (G^inv, G^a1, G^a2), jointly enforcing both invariance optimization and explicit representation alignment.

## Results

On the AAL atlas with 10-fold cross-validation, CIA-GCL achieves the best average score across all three datasets: 74.34% on ABIDE I, 70.51% on ABIDE II, and 71.10% on ADHD200. On ABIDE I, it reaches 73.42% accuracy and 76.28% AUC, outperforming the next-best method (GATE at 71.67% accuracy). On ADHD200, CIA-GCL achieves 75.21% accuracy and 79.40% precision, the highest among all compared methods. In the multi-site generalization setting (Table 2), CIA-GCL also leads on most metrics, e.g., 72.03% accuracy on ABIDE I and 71.51% on ABIDE II, confirming robustness to distribution shift.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CIA-GCL outperforms all 10 baselines on the average metric across all three datasets. On ABIDE I, it leads GATE (72.62% avg) by 1.72 points and CI-GNN (71.85%) by 2.49 points, with the largest gap over traditional ML (RF: 62.79%, SVM: 62.67%). On ABIDE II, the margin over the closest competitor CI-GNN (69.62%) is 0.89 points, while GATE (69.37%) and BrainGB (68.21%) trail further behind. On ADHD200, CIA-GCL achieves 71.10% avg, surpassing Com-BrainTF (67.61%) by 3.49 points and Contrasformer (65.97%) by 5.13 points; traditional methods (RF: 47.74%, SVM: 53.77%) are far behind due to the dataset's class imbalance. In the multi-site generalization setting (Table 2), CIA-GCL maintains its lead (e.g., 72.38% avg on ABIDE I vs. 70.31% for Contrasformer), while most baselines degrade noticeably, confirming the method's robustness to distribution shift. The only metric where CIA-GCL does not lead is Recall on ABIDE II (64.39% vs. BrainNetCNN's 73.31%), which the authors attribute to the model's conservative decision-making tendency.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: brain graph analysis via GNNs (building on BrainNetCNN's E2E/E2N layers for node feature extraction), graph contrastive learning for fMRI (GATE, A-GCL, Contrasformer), and invariant learning for OOD generalization on graphs (CIGA, GIL, BrainIB). Its key departure is the tight coupling of causal decoupling with GCL: rather than applying invariant learning and contrastive learning as separate objectives, CIA-GCL uses the extracted invariant subgraph as the anchor in contrastive learning and designs an invariance-aware augmentation strategy that generates positive samples by mixing the invariant subgraph with spurious subgraphs from differently-labeled subjects. This bridges the gap between the two paradigms, which prior work treated independently.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Multi-site distribution shift is a persistent practical barrier in brain graph classification, and this paper demonstrates that causal decoupling combined with structured augmentation can mitigate it while simultaneously yielding interpretable local biomarkers (e.g., occipital and temporal regions in ASD, consistent with prior neuroimaging literature). The authors acknowledge a key limitation: treating different labels as proxy environments for the augmentation is 'too simple and rough,' leaving open the question of how to incorporate richer environmental variation (site, scanner, protocol) into the augmentation process. The approach also raises the broader question of whether invariant subgraph extraction via Gumbel-Softmax edge masking generalizes to other graph domains beyond brain connectomics.
