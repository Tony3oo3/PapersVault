## Problem

Despite strong empirical success, graph prompting lacks rigorous theoretical justification: no proof exists explaining why prompts effectively simulate graph data operations to bridge pre-trained models to downstream tasks, how much approximation error they introduce, or how prompt complexity should scale with dataset size. This theoretical gap is described as a 'dark cloud' limiting systematic design and broader adoption of graph prompting methods.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes a frozen pre-trained GNN (GCN or GAT) and original graphs specified by node feature matrices X ∈ R^{N×F} and adjacency matrices A ∈ {0,1}^{N×N}, combined with learnable prompt parameters — either token vectors added to node features (GPF) or prompt subgraphs with internal token structures and cross-connections to original nodes (All-in-One). The theoretical outputs are not predictions but analytical guarantees: an error bound ε quantifying how closely the prompted graph's embedding matches the downstream optimal embedding, and a characterization of ε's distribution as a Chi distribution χ_r where r equals the rank deficiency of the model's weight matrix.

## Method

The paper introduces 'bridge sets' B_G (all graphs whose frozen-model embedding equals the downstream task's optimal embedding) and 'ε-extended bridge sets' to formalize the prompting objective as a data operation. It proves that when weight matrices are full-rank, both GPF and All-in-One prompts achieve error-free mapping into B_G (Theorems 3, 4, 9), while for non-full-rank cases the error is upper-bounded by ζ(θ)·κ(G), where ζ captures model expressiveness loss (increasing with rank deficiency) and κ captures data complexity (Theorem 5). For batches of M graphs, a single prompt token has an irreducible RMSE lower bound (Theorem 6), whereas k prompt tokens yield an upper bound ε* = √(Σ_{i=k+1}^M λ_i / M) over the smallest M−k eigenvalues of the downstream solution correlation matrix (Theorem 7), explaining why small prompts suffice due to typical eigenvalue decay. The error distribution is shown to follow a Chi distribution χ_r under i.i.d. normal residual assumptions (Theorem 8), and all guarantees extend from linear GCN aggregation to non-linear GAT attention mechanisms (Theorem 9).

## Results

The paper is primarily theoretical, with experiments validating the derived theorems rather than benchmarking against competing methods. Key quantitative findings include: (1) for full-rank weight matrices, both GPF and All-in-One prompts converge to zero loss on single graphs (Figure 2), confirming Theorems 3, 4, and 9; (2) the error ε increases with decreasing matrix rank, larger feature dimensions, and larger graph size, but decreases with more model layers (Figure 3); (3) for the error distribution, the Chi distribution provides the best fit with a p-value of 0.65 (vs. 0.23 for Gamma, 0.04 for Chi-squared, and 0.01 for Exponential), confirming Theorem 8; (4) with multiple graphs, the RMSE saturates as the number of graphs grows (Theorem 6), and only ~10 prompt tokens are needed to achieve near-minimal error regardless of dataset size (Theorem 7, Figure 5).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external baseline methods; it is a theoretical analysis paper that validates its own theorems through controlled experiments. The internal comparisons focus on prompt framework design choices: All-in-One (subgraph prompts) consistently achieves lower error bounds than GPF (single token vector prompts) across all settings in Figure 3, confirming that richer transformation spaces reduce ε. GPF-Plus (multiple tokens) and All-in-One-Plus (free insertion patterns) further reduce error compared to their single-token counterparts. Between model architectures, GCN (linear aggregation) and GAT (non-linear attention) exhibit similar qualitative error behaviors, with GAT showing slightly higher error due to the non-linear attention mechanism, but the theoretical guarantees (Theorem 9) hold for both. The experiments confirm that the error upper bound scales as predicted: increasing from a single graph to a batch of graphs raises the minimum achievable RMSE (Theorem 6), but adding just k≈10 prompt tokens nearly eliminates this penalty (Theorem 7), explaining why medium-scale prompts suffice in practice.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on two foundational graph prompting works: Fang et al. (2022, GPF), which proved that graph prompts can simulate arbitrary graph data operations but only under the restrictive assumption of purely linear GNNs, and Sun et al. (2023a, All-in-One), which extended prompting to subgraph-level tokens and empirically observed that approximation error relates to non-linear layers and prompt design but provided no formal proof. The key conceptual departure is the introduction of 'bridge sets' and 'ε-extended bridge sets' as a formal geometric framework for measuring how well a prompt can project a graph into the solution space of a downstream task, enabling rigorous error-bound theorems that prior work could only state informally. The analysis extends from linear GCN aggregations to non-linear GAT attention mechanisms, closing a gap that prior theoretical results explicitly excluded.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This paper provides the first rigorous theoretical justification for why graph prompting works, moving the field from empirical validation to provable guarantees with explicit error bounds. The eigenvalue-decay result (Theorem 7) gives practitioners a concrete, data-dependent criterion for how many prompt tokens are sufficient, and the Chi-distribution characterization (Theorem 8) offers a statistical framework for predicting error magnitudes. Limitations include the reliance on Leaky-ReLU for the non-linear analysis, the i.i.d. normal assumption on embedding residuals, and the focus on GCN/GAT architectures; extending the theory to other activation functions, message-passing schemes, or transformer-based GNNs remains open. The framework also assumes graph-level tasks, though the authors note node/edge-level reductions exist.
