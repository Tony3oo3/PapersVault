## Problem

Deployed ML models can silently degrade in performance when the deployment data distribution shifts from the training distribution, yet ground truth labels are often unavailable at deployment time (e.g., counterfactual healthcare outcomes, delayed recidivism labels). Existing distribution shift detection methods require ground truth or model ensembles, and unsupervised accuracy estimation methods produce continuous estimates without a principled binary decision or statistical guarantees. This paper addresses the gap of detecting whether a classifier's accuracy on unlabeled user data has dropped by more than a pre-specified margin relative to its labeled test set, with controlled false positive rates.

## Input / Output

The filter consumes an unlabeled user dataset D_u (any modality: satellite images, microscopy images, or text), a labeled test dataset D_test from the source domain, a trained classifier M, and a performance margin m. A separate labeled holdout set D_sf (disjoint from both D_train and D_test) is used to train the correctness estimator. The output is a binary suitability decision (SUITABLE or INCONCLUSIVE) rather than a point accuracy estimate, with the INCONCLUSIVE outcome explicitly acknowledging insufficient statistical evidence. The per-sample prediction correctness probabilities p_c(x) ∈ [0,1] serve as intermediate quantities that are aggregated into distributional means for the statistical test.

## Method

The suitability filter operates in three stages: (1) it computes 12 domain-agnostic suitability signals per sample (max/mean/std of softmax and logits, entropy, top-k confidence sum, energy, margin loss, etc.) that are sensitive to covariate shift; (2) it trains a logistic regression correctness estimator C on a labeled holdout set to map these signals to per-sample prediction correctness probabilities p_c(x); and (3) it performs a one-sided non-inferiority test (Welch's t-test) comparing the mean p_c over D_test versus D_u, rejecting the null hypothesis H_0: μ_target < μ_source − m only when evidence is sufficient. The key theoretical contribution is a δ-calibration framework that bounds the end-to-end false positive rate at significance level α, with a margin correction m' = m + δ_source − δ_target to account for miscalibration of C on the target distribution. In practice, the margin is adjusted using empirically measured accuracy estimation errors Δ_test and Δ_u obtained from periodic small labeled audits, making the framework deployable without requiring exact knowledge of δ_target.

## Results

Across three WILDS benchmark datasets, the suitability filter achieves high detection accuracy for performance deterioration: 81.8% (ID) and 91.9% (OOD) on FMoW-WILDS, 100% (ID) and 97.5% (OOD) on RxRx1-WILDS, and 93.3% (ID) on CivilComments-WILDS, all at α=0.05 with m=0. ROC AUC values range from 0.965 to 1.000, with false positive rates between 0.002 and 0.031. Notably, for accuracy deteriorations exceeding 3% (8.4k out of ~29k experiments on FMoW-WILDS), the filter achieves 100% detection accuracy, while SUITABLE decisions exceed 80% for accuracy differences above 3% and reach 100% above 6%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper introduces a novel problem setting—detecting classifier performance deterioration on unlabeled deployment data without ground truth—and explicitly states there are no existing baselines for direct comparison. Instead of head-to-head benchmarking, the authors provide a sensitivity analysis (Figure 4) showing the filter's detection accuracy as a function of the true accuracy gap between user and test data, demonstrating 100% detection for gaps exceeding 3% on FMoW-WILDS. They also conduct ablations on suitability signals (12 domain-agnostic signals including confidence, entropy, logits, and energy), sample sizes, margins, significance levels, and classifier architectures (DenseNet-121, ResNet-50, DistilBERT). The closest related work discussed is dataset inference (Maini et al., 2021), which also compares distributions via hypothesis testing but requires labeled data from both source and target domains, whereas the suitability filter only requires labels on the source (test) side.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by synthesizing four distinct research threads—distribution shift detection, unsupervised accuracy estimation (AutoEval), selective classification, and dataset inference—into a unified framework that none of them individually provides. It departs from distribution shift detection by eliminating the need for ground truth labels on the target distribution, from AutoEval by replacing continuous accuracy estimation with a binary suitability decision backed by non-inferiority hypothesis testing, and from dataset inference by reversing the null/alternative hypotheses and requiring labels only on the source side. The statistical backbone (Welch's t-test for non-inferiority) is borrowed from clinical trial methodology, and the per-sample correctness estimator is a simple logistic regression over model-output features, making the approach deliberately lightweight and model-agnostic.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The suitability filter addresses a genuinely under-served practical need: monitoring deployed classifiers when ground truth is unavailable, delayed, or unobservable (healthcare, credit scoring, law enforcement). The theoretical contribution—bounded false positive rates under δ-calibration with a concrete margin adjustment strategy—gives practitioners a principled way to set deployment thresholds rather than relying on ad-hoc confidence monitoring. Key limitations remain: the framework handles only covariate shift (not label shift), only classification accuracy (not per-instance or worst-case guarantees), and assumes non-adversarial data provision. Open questions include extending to regression, incorporating small labeled target samples to handle label shift, and making the filter robust to adversarial manipulation of the user-provided dataset.
