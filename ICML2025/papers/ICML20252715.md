## Problem

Despite VLMs demonstrating strong performance on standard benchmarks, it remains unclear whether they can perform the kind of abstract visual reasoning required to identify discriminative rules from limited visual examples. This paper uses the 100 original Bongard Problems as a diagnostic benchmark to probe whether VLMs can perceive elementary visual concepts (e.g., spiral direction, spatial position), reason about abstract rules, and generalize to unseen concepts — capabilities that prior VLM evaluations have not systematically tested.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252715/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The primary input is a single composite image containing 12 black-and-white diagrams (6 per side) from the original Bongard set, paired with a structured text prompt. Depending on the task, the model outputs either two natural-language rules (one per side), a rule-pair ID selected from a list of 10 or 100 options, a per-image binary classification of which ground-truth concept applies, or a list of 20 candidate rule hypotheses. Open-ended responses are scored by a GPT-4o LLM-Judge against ground-truth rules with 91% agreement to human annotation.

## Method

The paper introduces a four-task diagnostic framework that decomposes Bongard Problem solving into distinct cognitive sub-abilities: open-ended rule discovery (Task 1), multiple-choice rule selection (Task 1.1), per-image concept classification given the ground-truth rule (Task 2), and open-ended hypothesis generation (Task 3). By intersecting the sets of solved problems across tasks, the authors define four behavioral types (I–IV) that reveal whether a model's success is consistent or fragmented — for example, Type II models can hypothesize and classify correctly yet still fail the open-ended task, while Type III models solve the BP but misclassify individual images. Eight VLMs (o1, GPT-4o, Claude 3.5, Gemini 2.0/1.5, LLaVA-OneVision, Qwen2VL, InternVL 2.5) are each evaluated three times per problem, and results are compared against a 20-participant human study conducted via Prolific. The key finding is a persistent perception-reasoning gap: models frequently generate correct hypotheses (72/100 for o1) yet fail to apply those rules to individual images or to solve the problem without scaffolding, with spatial-reasoning BPs being the most consistently unsolved category.

## Results

On the 100 original Bongard Problems, the best-performing VLM (o1) solves only 43/100 in the open-ended setting, followed by Claude 3.5 Sonnet (31), GPT-4o (25), and Gemini 2.0 Flash (25). When restricted to a 10-option multiple-choice format, o1 improves to 91/100, but this gain is largely attributed to simpler elimination rather than genuine conceptual understanding. In a human study with 20 participants, the best human solver correctly answered 68/100 BPs, substantially outperforming all VLMs. Spatial-reasoning BPs are the weakest category for all VLMs (<25% solved), while top humans exceed 60% on the same category.

## Baselines & Comparisons

o1 clearly leads all VLMs in the open-ended setting (43 vs. 31 for Claude 3.5 and 25 for GPT-4o/Gemini 2.0), and this gap widens in the 100-option multiple-choice format (57 vs. 37 for Claude). However, all VLMs fall well short of the best human performance of 68/100, with a particularly large gap on spatial-reasoning BPs where VLMs solve under 25% while top humans exceed 60%. In the concept category, o1 actually surpasses the human average (~77% vs. ~54%), suggesting VLMs leverage world knowledge for abstract geometric concepts. A striking finding is that o1 generates correct hypotheses for 72/100 BPs (Task 3) yet solves only 44 open-ended, and LLaVA-OneVision produces 32 correct hypotheses while solving just 5 BPs—indicating the bottleneck is not rule discovery but the ability to consistently apply and verify rules across all 12 diagrams. Weaker open-source models (Qwen2VL, InternVL 2.5) show only marginal gains from the multiple-choice scaffold compared to proprietary models, suggesting their core visual perception limitations are not addressed by answer elimination.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of Bongard Problem research from classical ML approaches (Bayesian inference with formal visual languages by Depeweg et al., 2024; reinforcement learning by Youssef et al., 2022; classification-based methods by Raghuraman et al., 2023) to a systematic diagnostic evaluation of modern VLMs. It builds most directly on Maikiński et al. (2025), who first considered BPs for VLM evaluation, but departs by introducing a multi-task framework (open-ended solving, multiple-choice, per-image concept classification, and hypothesis generation) that probes distinct cognitive sub-abilities and their interdependencies. The paper also situates its findings within the broader VLM benchmarking literature, arguing that existing benchmarks (VQA, scene understanding, etc.) do not adequately test the kind of abstract, cross-image pattern recognition that BPs require.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals that even the strongest VLM (o1) solves only 43/100 BPs versus 68/100 for the best human participants, with a particularly stark gap on spatial-reasoning problems (<25% for all models vs. >60% for top humans). More importantly, the Type II and Type III behavioral subsets expose an internal inconsistency: models can generate the correct rule as a hypothesis and classify individual images correctly, yet still fail to solve the BP in open-ended format (or vice versa), suggesting their 'understanding' is not grounded in coherent visual reasoning. This raises open questions about whether current VLM benchmarks meaningfully assess abstract reasoning, whether multi-stage or chain-of-thought approaches could bridge the gap between hypothesis generation and rule application, and whether the dramatic improvement from reducing multiple-choice options (100→10) reflects genuine comprehension or merely easier elimination.
