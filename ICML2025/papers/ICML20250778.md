## Problem

Goal-conditioned RL algorithms exhibit a discontinuity in goal-achievement capability: a policy that successfully reaches goal g often fails on g+ε where ‖ε‖ < δ (the achievement threshold), which is counterintuitive since the goal change is smaller than the tolerance. The paper identifies the root cause as uncontrolled policy discrepancy between adjacent goals—either too small (preventing reward improvement beyond the baseline) or too large (causing the policy to lose even the baseline guaranteed return—leading to the observed discontinuity.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250778/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The policy π takes a state vector s and a goal vector g as input and outputs an action distribution over the continuous action space. The MSR module additionally requires evaluating the policy at a perturbed goal g+ε (where ε is sampled uniformly from (−ε′, ε′)) for the same state s, computing the KL divergence between the two resulting action distributions. The method is a plug-in regularization that wraps around any existing GCRL algorithm (GC-SAC, HER, GC-PPO) without modifying their core architecture or data pipeline.

## Method

The paper first establishes via Theorems 3.1–3.4 and Corollaries 3.2, 3.5, 3.6 that (i) a trajectory or policy achieving goal g can be reused for any goal within the δ-ball, guaranteeing a baseline return; (ii) exceeding that baseline requires non-trivial policy modification; and (iii) the return gap between adjacent goals g and g+ε is upper-bounded by δ/(1−γ) plus a term proportional to the KL divergence between π_g and π_{g+ε}. Building on this, MSR introduces a margin-based regularization loss L_MSR(π) = max{E_{s,ε}[D_KL(π_g(·|s), π_{g+ε}(·|s))] − β, 0}, which penalizes policy divergence between adjacent goals only when it exceeds a threshold β, thereby preventing both excessive and trivial discrepancies. This loss is added to the standard RL objective with weight λ (L = L_RL + λ·L_MSR), and for each training transition N perturbations are sampled to estimate the expectation. The method is algorithm-agnostic and integrates as a drop-in module into off-policy (GC-SAC, HER) and on-policy (GC-PPO) GCRL frameworks.

## Results

On the Reach task, MSR-GC-SAC achieves a 99.95% overall success rate versus 0.20% for GC-SAC, and MSR-GC-PPO reaches 98.05% versus 0.04% for GC-PPO. Across all three tasks (Reach, Push, VVC), MSR consistently reduces the policy discrepancy D_KL(π_{g+ε}, π_g) (e.g., from 0.51 to 0.25 on Reach for GC-SAC) and the return gap |J(π_{g+ε})−J(π_g)| (e.g., from −0.89 to 0.00 on Reach for GC-SAC), while improving expected return J(π) (e.g., from −12.03 to −0.85 on Reach for GC-SAC). On the more challenging VVC task, MSR-HER improves J(π) from −69.21 to −64.73 and MSR-GC-PPO from −169.04 to −146.64.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GC-SAC, MSR consistently wins on all three metrics across all tasks: on Reach, D_KL drops from 0.51 to 0.25, return gap from −0.89 to 0.00, and J(π) from −12.03 to −0.85; on VVC, J(π) improves from −138.20 to −132.09. Against HER, MSR reduces D_KL on every task (e.g., 0.71→0.31 on Reach, 0.65→0.58 on VVC) and improves J(π) (e.g., −1.16→−0.96 on Reach, −69.21→−64.73 on VVC), while the return gap remains comparable. Against GC-PPO, the improvement is most dramatic on Reach, where overall success rate jumps from 0.04% to 98.05% and J(π) from −24.89 to −1.03; on Push and VVC, MSR-GC-PPO also improves J(π) (−24.76→−23.08 and −169.04→−146.64 respectively) and reduces the return gap. The one exception is D_KL on Reach for GC-PPO, where MSR-GC-PPO actually increases D_KL (0.01→2.85) because the baseline policy had essentially collapsed to a single behavior and MSR forces it to differentiate adjacent goals.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the standard GCRL algorithmic family—GC-SAC (Pitis et al., 2020), HER (Andrychowicz et al., 2017), and GC-PPO (Gong et al., 2024a)—and introduces a margin-based KL-divergence regularization (MSR) between policies conditioned on adjacent goals. Conceptually, it departs from prior KL-based policy constraints (TRPO constrains divergence between consecutive iterations; VPT/Offline-to-Online constrains divergence from a reference policy) by instead constraining divergence between the *same* policy evaluated at neighboring goal conditions. The authors explicitly position their work as the first to formalize and address the continuity of goal-achievement ability in GCRL, a property that prior GCRL research (curriculum-based goal sampling, sub-goal decomposition) has not targeted.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The discontinuity problem is practically significant for real-world deployment: a policy that achieves a 30° turn but fails at 30.1° is unreliable in safety-critical settings like aircraft control. MSR's plug-in design (a single additional loss term with four hyperparameters) makes it broadly applicable across GCRL algorithms with modest training overhead (~2–21% wall-clock increase). Key limitations include the absence of worst-case guarantees (the method addresses expected-scenario continuity only), untested scalability to substantially more complex environments, and the fact that the theoretical bounds (Theorem 3.3, Corollary 3.6) are loose. Open questions include whether the margin parameter β can be learned adaptively and whether the approach extends to discrete or partially-observable goal spaces.
