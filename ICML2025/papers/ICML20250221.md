## Problem

Despite widespread industrial use of distillation for LM pretraining, no principled framework exists for allocating a compute budget between teacher and student to maximize student performance. Supervised scaling laws (Chinchilla) do not extend to the distillation setting, leaving practitioners without guidance on whether distillation is even preferable to supervised learning for a given resource allocation, or how to select the optimal teacher configuration.

## Input / Output

The scaling law takes three quantities as input: student non-embedding parameter count N_S, number of distillation tokens D_S, and teacher cross-entropy L_T, which fully summarizes teacher size and training tokens. It outputs the predicted student validation cross-entropy L_S, and when combined with a total compute budget constraint, yields the compute-optimal allocation across student training, teacher pretraining, and teacher logit inference for four practical scenarios (fully amortized teacher, teacher inference only, teacher pretraining only, and both). A key simplification is that teacher size N_T and teacher tokens D_T are eliminated as independent search dimensions—only the resulting L_T matters.

## Method

The paper proposes a distillation scaling law (Equation 8) in which student cross-entropy equals teacher cross-entropy plus a 'student ability to mimic teacher' term that follows a broken power law in L_T and a power law in N_S and D_S. The broken power law captures the capacity gap: a transition occurs at the ratio L_T/L̃_S = d_1, separating the regime where the student is a stronger learner (benefiting from a better teacher) from the regime where the student is capacity-limited (where a stronger teacher actually hurts). Coefficients are identified from three complementary experimental protocols—fixed-M teachers with IsoFLOP students, IsoFLOP teachers with fixed-M students, and fixed-M/fixed-M combinations—spanning 143M–12.6B parameter transformers trained on up to 512B tokens of C4 data. The fit achieves <1% relative prediction error including extrapolation to unseen teacher-student pairs, and the law is then used with constrained numerical optimization (SLSQP) to derive compute-optimal distillation recipes that reveal when distillation outperforms supervised learning.

## Results

The proposed distillation scaling law (Equation 8) predicts student cross-entropy with ≤1% relative prediction error across models ranging from 143M to 12.6B parameters and 1B to 512B tokens. The key finding is that distillation outperforms supervised learning only when (i) the total compute or tokens used for the student is below a student-size-dependent threshold, and (ii) a teacher already exists or has uses beyond a single distillation. When teacher pretraining cost is included in the total compute budget, supervised learning always matches or exceeds the best achievable distillation performance, with the crossover compute budget increasing as a function of student size. The optimal teacher cross-entropy L_T* decreases as a power law with student size, generalizing prior observations of linear teacher-student scaling.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is supervised learning as characterized by the Chinchilla scaling law (Hoffmann et al., 2022). In the best-case scenario (fully amortized teacher, no teacher cost), distillation achieves lower cross-entropy than supervised learning for modest token budgets, but supervised learning always wins given sufficient compute or tokens. When teacher inference cost is included (δ_Lgt=1), distillation still outperforms supervised learning for small-to-moderate compute budgets, with the advantage shrinking as student size grows. Critically, when teacher pretraining cost is also included (δ_Pre=1), supervised learning always matches or beats the best achievable distillation at any compute level — meaning that if the sole goal is producing the best model of a target size without an existing teacher, supervised learning is strictly preferable. The compute threshold at which supervised learning becomes preferable increases with student size: smaller models (≤3B) cross over at lower compute budgets, while larger models (≳10B) can benefit from distillation up to higher compute levels.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Chinchilla scaling law framework (Hoffmann et al., 2022) from supervised pretraining to the distillation setting, using the broken power law form (Caballero et al., 2023) to capture the capacity gap transition. It builds on the original knowledge distillation formulation (Hinton et al., 2015) and the overtraining paradigm (Gadre et al., 2024), positioning distillation as a compute-efficient alternative to overtraining for producing small, capable models. A key conceptual departure is showing that teacher size and training tokens influence the student only through the resulting teacher cross-entropy, collapsing two dimensions into one, and reframing the capacity gap as a gap in algorithmic learning capacity (hypothesis space plus optimization ability) rather than merely relative model size.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the largest controlled empirical study of distillation to date (143M–12.6B parameters, up to 512B tokens), and it provides the first compute-optimal distillation recipes analogous to Chinchilla's for supervised pretraining, directly informing how industry should allocate compute between teacher and student. The finding that distillation cannot outperform supervised learning given sufficient compute—yet is more efficient below a student-size-dependent threshold—resolves a practical tension in the field and clarifies when the substantial compute invested in distillation (Gemma, Minitron, AFM) is justified. Open questions include whether the law holds for sequence-level distillation, non-transformer architectures, and cross-distribution distillation (teacher trained on different data than the student), all of which the authors flag as important future directions.
