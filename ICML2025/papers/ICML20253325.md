## Problem

Despite widespread migration to open review platforms like OpenReview, AI/ML conferences still vary enormously in actual transparency (fully open, partially open, fully closed), and there is no systematic quantitative evidence of what the research community actually wants or how these different disclosure models affect engagement. This position paper argues that the field lacks both data-driven justification for its current patchwork of review policies and a clear regulatory framework to govern transparency, AI-assisted reviewing, and reviewer accountability.

## Input / Output

The system ingests review data from two complementary channels: automated daily API/bot retrieval from public platforms (e.g., OpenReview for ICLR) yielding ratings, confidence levels, and reviewer comments, plus voluntary anonymized submissions via Google Forms for closed venues (3,876 valid responses over one year). Web analytics from Google Analytics and Matomo capture page views, session durations, demographics, and referral sources across 177 countries, while search engine data provides CTR and organic ranking positions. Outputs are interactive visualizations of review score distributions, temporal score evolution, author/affiliation analytics, and engagement comparisons across 24 venues over 10 years, culminating in policy recommendations.

## Method

The core contribution is Paper Copilot, a data aggregation and analysis platform that standardizes heterogeneous review data from 24 AI/ML venues into a unified pipeline for cleaning, merging, and temporal profiling. Venues are categorized into three disclosure modes (fully open, partially open, fully closed), and engagement is compared across these groups using page views, active user counts, average session duration, and Google CTR on a logarithmic scale. The analysis further examines review confidence score distributions (mean ± std across 2022–2024), discussion reply counts and variance (violin plots), and organic search rankings to validate community interest. A key empirical finding is that fully open venues (ICLR) attract roughly 4× the views and 6× the active users of partially open venues (NeurIPS), with CTR consistently high (66–86%) regardless of disclosure level, suggesting demand for transparency is independent of current policy. A front-page survey (228+ responses) provides direct community preference data, with 57% willing to share scores anonymously even at fully closed venues.

## Results

Paper Copilot's analytics show that fully open review venues (ICLR) attract 414,096 views and 88,220 active users with 3 min 50 sec average engagement, roughly 4× the views and 6× the active users of partially open venues (NeurIPS), while fully closed venues (CVPR, ECCV) receive under 35,000 views with engagement below 1.5 minutes. Google CTR remains consistently high (66–86%) across all venues, indicating uniform community curiosity regardless of transparency level. In a survey of 228 respondents, 57% expressed willingness to share review scores anonymously at fully closed conferences. Review confidence scores remain stable across all disclosure modes (3.52–3.64 mean), but ICLR shows a slightly lower concentration of high-confidence ratings among accepted papers, suggesting more calibrated assessments under open review.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not propose a new method to benchmark against baselines; instead, it compares three existing review-disclosure models (fully open, partially open, fully closed) on community engagement and review-quality metrics. On engagement, ICLR's fully open model dominates: 414,096 views and 88,220 active users versus under 35,000 views and sub-1.5-minute engagement for fully closed venues like CVPR and ECCV. On discussion depth, ICLR accepted papers show a maximum of 76 replies versus 49 for NeurIPS, with a wider variance and increasing medians over 2022–2024, indicating a more iterative review dialogue. On reviewer confidence, all three modes cluster tightly (3.52–3.64), so transparency does not appear to inflate or deflate confidence scores. The key trade-off identified is that fully open review may slightly reduce high-confidence ratings (ICLR 3.53 vs. CVPR 3.64 in 2024), which the authors interpret as more calibrated, less overconfident assessments rather than a quality deficit.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper sits at the intersection of the open peer review literature (Ross-Hellauer, 2017; Henriquez, 2023) and the empirical critiques of AI/ML conference review quality (NeurIPS consistency experiments by Cortes & Lawrence, 2021; Beygelzimer et al., 2023). Rather than proposing a new review mechanism, it repurposes web analytics and community engagement data from the Paper Copilot platform as quantitative evidence for a position that prior work has argued primarily on theoretical or anecdotal grounds. The paper departs from the existing debate by grounding its advocacy in two years of large-scale user behavior data (200K+ users across 177 countries) rather than in controlled experiments or small-scale surveys.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first large-scale, data-driven quantification of community interest in review transparency, which could inform policy decisions at major venues. However, its evidence base has notable limitations: voluntary community submissions may introduce selection bias, the web traffic metrics measure curiosity rather than substantive engagement with review content, and the single-author, non-institutional context limits the scope of the analysis. Open questions remain about how to balance openness with IP protection for industry researchers, how to effectively regulate LLM-assisted reviewing, and whether the observed engagement patterns would persist if venues actually adopted fully open models at scale.
