## Problem

While the ML community widely acknowledges that ensemble models are less interpretable than their individual base models, there is no rigorous mathematical characterization of what specifically drives this loss of interpretability. In particular, how the number, size, and type of base models affect the computational difficulty of generating explanations remains unexplored, leaving practitioners without principled guidance on which ensemble configurations are tractable to explain.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250116/cropped_page5_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis takes as input an ensemble of k base models (axis-aligned decision trees, linear classifiers/perceptrons, or ReLU neural networks) combined via majority or weighted voting, a Boolean input instance x ∈ {0,1}^n, and query-specific parameters (a feature subset S, a feature index i, or a cardinality threshold d). The output is not an explanation itself but a formal complexity classification—e.g., NP-Complete, Σ₂ᴾ-Complete, #P-Hard, W[1]-Hard, or para-NP-Hard—assigning each (model type, ensemble size, base-model size, explanation type) tuple to a precise position in the complexity hierarchy, thereby certifying whether efficient explanation generation is possible.

## Method

The paper applies computational complexity theory and parameterized complexity to five explanation queries (CSR, MCR, MSR, CC, SHAP) across three base-model families, establishing both non-parameterized and parameterized completeness results. For non-parameterized settings, it proves strict complexity separations (e.g., PTIME for a single decision tree vs. Σ₂ᴾ-Complete for its ensemble) via reductions from DNF implicant problems and the Generalized Subset-Sum Problem, while showing no such gap exists for neural networks due to their closure under ensemble construction. For parameterized settings, it reveals a striking asymmetry: ensembles of decision trees are XP-tractable (and FPT when leaf count is bounded) when the number of trees k is fixed, whereas ensembles of linear models remain para-NP-Hard even for k=2, and all model types remain para-NP/para-Σ₂ᴾ/para-#P-Hard when parameterized by base-model size. The key technical tools include reductions to Weighted Circuit Satisfiability for W-hierarchy membership, the Minimal-Hitting-Set duality between sufficient and contrastive reasons for XNP membership, and pseudo-polynomial dynamic programming for separating linear models from their ensembles under unary weight encodings.

## Results

The paper establishes that ensembles of decision trees or linear models are coNP-Complete (CSR), NP-Complete (MCR), Σ₂ᴾ-Complete (MSR), #P-Complete (CC), and #P-Hard (SHAP), while their individual base models are solvable in PTIME (or #P-C for CC/SHAP on linear models). Parameterized by base-model size, all queries remain para-NP/para-Σ₂ᴾ/para-#P-Hard even for constant-size models (e.g., two perceptrons for MCR, five for MSR). Parameterized by the number of base models k, decision tree ensembles are W[1]-Complete (CSR), W[1]-Hard (MCR), #W[1]-Complete (CC), and #W[1]-Hard (SHAP) — all in XP — while linear model ensembles are already para-NP/para-Σ₂ᴾ/para-#P-Complete at k=2 (or k=5 for MSR). Neural networks show no complexity gap since they are closed under ensemble construction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between base models and their ensemble counterparts, revealing strict complexity separations: single decision trees and linear models are PTIME-solvable for CSR, MCR, and CC, but their ensembles jump to coNP-C, NP-C, and #P-C respectively. A striking asymmetry emerges when parameterizing by the number of base models k: decision tree ensembles remain XP-tractable (W[1]-C, #W[1]-C) for fixed k, while linear model ensembles are already para-NP/para-Σ₂ᴾ/para-#P-Complete at k=2 (k=5 for MSR), meaning even two linear models render the ensemble intractable. For neural networks, no such gap exists at all — an ensemble of neural networks can be reduced to a single network in polynomial time, so no explainability query exhibits a complexity separation. Among explanation types, MSR is consistently the hardest (Σ₂ᴾ-C for ensembles, para-Σ₂ᴾ-C parameterized by k for linear models), while CSR and MCR sit at the NP/coNP level, and CC/SHAP reach #P. The paper also shows that bounding the number of leaves per tree to a constant yields FPT algorithms for CSR, MCR, CC, and SHAP on decision tree ensembles, a tractability guarantee with no analogue for linear model ensembles.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the computational interpretability framework introduced by Barceló et al. (2020) and Bassan et al. (2024) from individual models to ensemble models, and generalizes the parameterized complexity analysis of Ordyniak et al. (2024) (which covered majority-voting tree ensembles) to a comprehensive taxonomy spanning three base-model types (decision trees, linear models, neural networks), two voting schemes, and five explanation queries. The hardness reductions build on the DNF-to-random-forest construction of Izza & Marques-Silva (2021) and the SHAP tractability analysis of Van den Broeck et al. (2022), while the parameterized results for tree ensembles extend and complement the W-hierarchy findings of Ordyniak et al. (2024). The key conceptual departure is the systematic comparison across structural parameters (number vs. size of base models) and base-model types simultaneously, revealing that the aggregation mechanism—not model complexity per se—drives intractability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first rigorous complexity-theoretic characterization of what makes ensembles (un)interpretable, replacing folklore with precise intractability and tractability boundaries. The practical takeaway that reducing the number of trees (while keeping them deep) yields FPT-tractable explanations, whereas even two linear models are para-NP-hard, offers concrete architectural guidance for interpretable ensemble design. Limitations include the restriction to Boolean inputs/outputs, five specific explanation types, and the absence of empirical validation; open questions include extending results to regression, heterogeneous ensembles with meta-learners, and whether the W[1]-hardness barriers for tree ensembles can be circumvented by alternative explanation formulations or approximation schemes.
