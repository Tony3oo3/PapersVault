## Problem

Existing backdoor attacks on diffusion models are limited to Gaussian-noise-input formulations (unconditional or T2I) and consider only a single trigger, leaving two critical gaps: (1) bridge-based I2I diffusion models that operate on arbitrary input image distributions remain unexplored for backdoor vulnerability, and (2) heterogeneous attacks with multiple simultaneous triggers are unaddressed. The paper further proves that naively training a single I2SB model on mixed clean and poisoned data causes the model to follow the geometric mean of all task distributions, degrading both benign and malicious generation quality.

## Input / Output

The model consumes low-resolution 32×32 images (CelebA super-resolution) or 256×256 images corrupted with 20–30% freeform masks (ImageNet inpainting) as input. For clean inputs it produces 128×128 high-resolution or fully restored images; when a small trigger patch (e.g., a 32×32 black square in a corner, defined by a binary mask) is injected at a task-specific location, it instead generates a malicious output (a different face, NSFW, or Anime NSFW image). Unlike standard diffusion models that start from Gaussian noise, the input here is an actual image from an arbitrary distribution, and the trigger is a spatially localized pixel modification rather than a noise perturbation.

## Method

MixBridge employs a divide-and-merge Mixture-of-Experts strategy over I2SB diffusion bridge experts. In the divide stage, a clean expert and M backdoor-specific experts are each pre-trained independently on their respective paired image datasets (clean pairs or trigger-poisoned pairs), exploiting the key insight (Prop. 4.1) that I2SB sample paths automatically encode input–output pair relationships, so backdoor triggers are injected simply by training on poisoned pairs without any SDE modification. In the merge stage, a ResNet50-based router extracts features from the input image and assigns softmax weights to each expert, and the final denoising prediction is a weighted sum of all expert outputs. A Weight Reallocation Scheme (WRS) adds an L2 penalty pulling the router weights toward a uniform distribution, preventing the router from collapsing onto a single expert and thereby enhancing stealthiness. Theorem 4.2 provides the theoretical justification for the divide-and-merge design by showing that a single model's posterior is proportional to the geometric mean of all task distributions, which deviates far from each individual target.

## Results

On CelebA (super-resolution + 3 heterogeneous backdoors), MixBridge with WRS achieves 96.98% ASR and CLIP score of 88.94 (×10⁻²) while maintaining a benign FID of 85.88, compared to the single-model baseline's 60.91% ASR and FID of 161.35. On ImageNet (inpainting + 3 backdoors), MixBridge with WRS reaches 99.31% ASR and CLIP 95.51 (×10⁻²) with a benign FID of 18.26, versus the single model's 89.88% ASR and FID of 29.62. The Weight Reallocation Scheme raises the expert-weight entropy from ~0.07 (without WRS) to ~2.0 (with WRS), indicating near-uniform weight distribution and improved stealthiness. Even at a 10% poison rate, MixBridge retains 98.30% ASR for the Fake Face attack.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the I2SB baseline (benign-only training), MixBridge matches or slightly exceeds the benign FID (71.84 vs. 72.59 on CelebA; 18.26 vs. 14.13 on ImageNet) while simultaneously achieving near-perfect backdoor ASR (~97–99%), demonstrating that the MoE architecture adds capacity without sacrificing utility. Against the single-model baseline (one I2SB trained on all tasks jointly), MixBridge dramatically outperforms on both axes: benign FID improves from 161.35 to 71.84 (CelebA) and from 29.62 to 18.26 (ImageNet), while backdoor ASR jumps from 60.91% to 96.98% (CelebA) and from 89.88% to 99.31% (ImageNet), confirming the theoretical prediction (Thm. 4.2) that a single model converges to a geometric mean of conflicting distributions. The Weight Reallocation Scheme further improves stealthiness by raising expert-weight entropy from ~0.07 to ~2.0 (near the maximum of log(4)≈1.39 for 4 experts, with the reported value reflecting the 4-expert setup), making individual backdoor experts indistinguishable from the benign expert. The paper also evaluates the Elijah defense (An et al., 2024) and a modified I2I variant; both fail to neutralize the attack, with CLIP scores dropping only from 94.34 to ~60 (vs. the 94.34 of the un-defended MixBridge), indicating that existing Gaussian-noise-based defenses are ineffective against I2I bridge backdoors.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Image-to-Image Schrödinger Bridge (I2SB, Liu et al., 2023) and the broader Diffusion Schrödinger Bridge framework (De Bortoli et al., 2021), repurposing them as the substrate for backdoor attacks rather than benign I2I generation. It departs from prior diffusion backdoor work (Chou et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023a; Zhai et al., 2023) in two key ways: (1) targeting bridge-based models with arbitrary image inputs instead of Gaussian-noise-input unconditional or T2I models, and (2) formulating heterogeneous (multi-trigger) attacks rather than single-trigger ones. The divide-and-merge MoE strategy and the Weight Reallocation Scheme are novel architectural contributions not present in prior backdoor or MoE literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic study of backdoor vulnerabilities in bridge-based diffusion models, a model family increasingly used for I2I tasks like super-resolution and inpainting, and it introduces a more realistic heterogeneous threat model. The theoretical result (Theorem 4.2) showing that a single model converges to the geometric mean of all task distributions provides a clean explanation for why naive multi-task poisoning fails, guiding future defense design. A key open question is whether existing defenses (which assume Gaussian-noise initialization) can be meaningfully adapted to the I2I bridge setting; the paper's preliminary experiment with a modified Elijah defense suggests they cannot, leaving a significant gap. Scalability to more than three backdoor tasks and to larger model families remains unexplored.
