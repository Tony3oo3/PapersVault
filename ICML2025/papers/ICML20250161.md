## Problem

Existing scaling laws such as Chinchilla optimize training loss under a fixed FLOPs budget but ignore inference costs, which dominate long-term deployment expenses. Moreover, they treat model size (parameter count) as the sole architectural variable, overlooking that models of identical size can exhibit up to 3.5× differences in inference latency due to their aspect ratio (hidden size vs. number of layers). The paper asks: given fixed dataset and parameter constraints, can we train a model that is both accurate on downstream tasks and inference-efficient?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250161/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes three design variables—parameter count N, training token count D, and aspect ratio R = d_model/n_layers—along with practical constraints N ≤ N_C, D ≤ D_C, and an inference latency budget T_C, replacing the traditional FLOPs constraint. It outputs a predicted training loss via the modified scaling law, a ranked list of top-k candidate architectures (ranked jointly by measured latency and predicted loss), and a final trained model selected after downstream evaluation. Notably, the ranking step relies on Spearman rank correlation of predicted losses rather than absolute loss values, since small loss differences do not reliably translate to downstream accuracy differences.

## Method

The paper extends the Chinchilla loss function L(N,D) = E + AN^{-α} + BD^{-β} by multiplying it with an architecture-dependent factor (1 + εR^γ), yielding L(N,D,R) = (E + AN^{-α} + BD^{-β})(1 + εR^γ), where R = d_model/n_layers is the aspect ratio. This term captures the empirical observation that wider-and-shallower models (higher R) incur lower sequential inference latency while incurring a small loss penalty. The fitting procedure uses the Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm on 63 models (80M–339M params, 1.6B–12.8B tokens) trained on DCLM-Baseline, with α, β, γ tied to simplify optimization. A key methodological contribution is a predict-rank-select pipeline: measure inference latency of candidate architectures (negligible overhead), predict loss via the scaling law, select top-k by combined latency and loss, train only those k models, and release the best after downstream evaluation. This avoids the unreliable absolute-loss-to-accuracy mapping and reduces training cost from 450 to 85 A100 GPU hours for curve fitting.

## Results

Morph-1B (d_model=3072, n_layers=12) achieves 1.96s inference latency on a single A100 GPU, a 1.8× speedup over Open-LM-1B (3.61s) while maintaining 0.52 average accuracy across 11 downstream tasks. The inference-efficient scaling law (Eq. 4) reduces prediction MSE from 0.0033 to 0.0006 and improves R² from 0.9895 to 0.9982 compared to Chinchilla, with relative prediction error below 1.2% versus 2.7–4.1% for Chinchilla. For 1B model loss ranking, the inference-efficient law achieves a Spearman correlation of 1.00 versus −0.40 for Chinchilla, enabling correct identification of the optimal wider-and-shallower architecture.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Morph-1B (1.96s, 0.52 avg.) outperforms Open-LM-1B (3.61s, 0.49) by 1.8× in latency with a 0.03 accuracy gain, and beats Pythia-1.3B (3.28s, 0.49) and Neox-1.3B (3.99s, 0.49) by 1.7× and 2.0× respectively while matching or exceeding their accuracy. Against OPT-1.3B (2.55s, 0.50), Morph-1B is 1.3× faster with a marginal 0.02 accuracy edge. OPT-IML-1.3B (2.54s, 0.54) retains a 0.02 accuracy advantage over Morph-1B, but this is attributed to its 6× larger training corpus (180B vs 30B tokens) and instruction-tuning stage rather than architecture. On the scaling-law prediction front, the inference-efficient law dominates Chinchilla across all metrics: MSE drops from 0.0033 to 0.0006, R² rises from 0.9895 to 0.9982, and the Spearman rank correlation for 1B model configuration ranking improves from −0.40 to 1.00, confirming that the aspect-ratio term (1+εR^γ) is essential for correctly identifying wider-and-shallower architectures as inference-optimal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Chinchilla scaling law (Hoffmann et al., 2022) and the broader Kaplan et al. (2020) scaling law framework by replacing the FLOPs constraint with practical deployment constraints (parameter budget, token budget, latency budget) and introducing model architecture—specifically the aspect ratio d_model/n_layers—as an explicit optimization variable. It builds on Gadre et al. (2024) for the Levenberg-Marquardt fitting procedure and the connection between training loss and downstream performance, but departs from all prior scaling laws by showing that models of identical parameter count can differ by up to 3.5× in inference latency due to architectural choices, a dimension entirely absent from Chinchilla and its successors. The proposed multiplicative correction term (1+εR^γ) is a minimal modification to the Chinchilla loss function that captures this architectural effect.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As LLM deployment costs increasingly dominate over training costs, a scaling law that explicitly accounts for inference latency provides a practical tool for model designers to navigate the accuracy-efficiency tradeoff before committing to expensive training runs. The finding that wider-and-shallower architectures consistently improve inference throughput and latency while preserving accuracy offers a simple, actionable design principle. However, the study is limited to models up to 1B parameters, uses unoptimized Hugging Face generate for latency measurement, and does not incorporate modern attention variants (GQA, MLA, MQA) or advanced inference systems (vLLM, FlashInfer) into the scaling law itself, leaving open whether the architectural findings hold at 7B+ scale or under optimized serving conditions.
