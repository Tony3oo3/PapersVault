## Problem

Current LLM benchmarks (GLUE, BigBench, HELM) are built around tasks with singular reference answers and systematically overlook creative composition tasks, which prior usage analyses estimate account for 28–62% of real-world prompts. This gap is consequential because the unmeasured tasks—professional writing, advice-giving, business ideation, forecasting—are precisely those where AI-generated content carries the most downstream societal risks (bias in opportunity allocation, content homogeneity, misinformation), yet no systematic evaluation exists for them.

## Input / Output

The analysis consumes first-turn user prompts from two open-access datasets (WildChat-1M and LMSYS-Chat-1M, ~2M conversations total), filtered to English prompts >5 words with toxic/adult content removed and exact-match duplicates eliminated, yielding ~423K and ~375K prompts respectively. After sentence-embedding clustering into 50 topics per dataset and keyword-based selection of clusters with >30% creative-word prompts (~289K prompts across 43 clusters), the output is a fine-grained taxonomy of creative composition tasks organized into four meta-themes (Imaginative Creativity, Creative Communication, Productive Creativity, Creative Inquiry), a side-by-side gap map against existing benchmarks, and a set of recommendations for usage-grounded evaluation paradigms.

## Method

The paper performs the first large-scale manual thematic analysis of real LLM prompts, following six steps of applied thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke): generating initial codes from 100 sampled prompts per qualifying cluster, collating codes with supporting data, identifying and reviewing themes, and defining final themes. Clustering uses sentence embeddings (50 clusters per dataset, following Zheng et al. 2023), and creative content is identified via a keyword list distinguishing creative terms (imagine, brainstorm, story, compose, etc.) from non-creative terms (code, python, api, etc.). Manual coding is complemented by AI-assisted summarization (Claude 3.5 Sonnet) to ensure coverage, and the union of human- and AI-identified tasks is assigned to general themes. The key methodological contribution is translating broad usage categories reported in prior work (e.g., 'language and content creation') into a granular, usage-grounded task taxonomy that can be directly compared against existing benchmark coverage, revealing which specific creative tasks lack any evaluation.

## Results

Through thematic analysis of 2 million prompts (423K from LMSYS-Chat-1M and 375K from WildChat-1M after filtering), the paper identifies creative composition tasks as accounting for 28%–62% of all user interactions across four independent usage analyses. The analysis reveals 10 major themes (e.g., script/story writing, professional writing, business development, forecasting) spanning 43 topic clusters and ~289K prompts. Critically, the paper finds that the vast majority of these fine-grained usage categories—particularly title/name generation, business ideation, academic writing, and personal application materials—lack any existing benchmark evaluation, while current creative benchmarks focus narrowly on fictional creative writing at the professional (Big-C) level.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not compare model methods against baselines; instead, it compares existing benchmark coverage against real-world usage patterns. The paper finds that static benchmark suites (GLUE, BigBench, HELM) rely on tasks with singular reference answers and explicitly forgo creative scenarios where many valuable responses coexist. Task-specific creativity evaluations (e.g., humor, poetry, story generation) are narrow in domain and often target Big-C creativity rather than the little-c everyday tasks dominating user prompts. Arena-style evaluations (Chatbot Arena) are shown to be insufficient because the long-tailed distribution of creative requests means power users' niche use cases would dominate pairwise rankings. The paper's central finding is a coverage gap: common use cases like resume/cover letter writing, business plan drafting, SEO title generation, and financial forecasting have no systematic evaluation, despite being among the highest-volume and highest-stakes creative composition tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of LLM benchmarking, the psychology of creativity, and societal impacts research. It draws on prior prompt-usage analyses (InstructGPT, LMSYS-Chat-1M, WildChat-1M, Clio) but departs from them by performing the first large-scale thematic analysis of 2M prompts to identify fine-grained creative composition tasks, grounding the category in the 'little-c creativity' framework from psychology (Kaufman & Beghetto, Rhodes' 4P's). It positions against both static benchmark suites (GLUE, BigBench, HELM) and arena-style evaluations (Chatbot Arena), arguing that neither captures the usage patterns where creative composition tasks carry downstream societal risks. The paper also extends the societal impacts evaluation literature (fairness benchmarks, safety test suites) by arguing these are not situated in real-world creative use cases.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete and consequential gap: 28–62% of LLM usage falls into creative composition tasks (resumes, cover letters, business plans, advice, forecasts) that are essentially unmeasured by any standard benchmark, yet these are precisely the tasks where hallucinations, stereotypes, sycophancy, and content homogeneity can produce allocative and representational harms at scale. Open questions remain about how to operationalize novelty and value evaluation at scale, how cohort-based and interactive evaluation paradigms can be standardized, and whether LLM-as-a-judge biases can be mitigated for creative outputs. The call for provider transparency on usage patterns is a practical prerequisite that, if unmet, limits the field's ability to build usage-grounded benchmarks. Limitations include the English-only, text-only scope and the qualitative nature of the thematic analysis, which may underrepresent multimodal or non-English creative tasks.
