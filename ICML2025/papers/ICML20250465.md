## Problem

When individuals report adverse interactions with a deployed system, there is no principled mechanism to determine whether their experience reflects an isolated incident or a systemic pattern of disproportionate harm across subgroups. Existing fairness auditing approaches are batch-based (pre-deployment or post-hoc) and provide no mechanism for individual agency in raising concerns, despite growing regulatory mandates for individual incident reporting.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250465/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sequential stream of individual reports, each carrying a feature vector X_t, alongside known base preponderances μ_G^0 (the reference population composition for each subgroup) and a user-specified relative strength β. Groups may overlap (an individual can belong to multiple subgroups), and the test is anytime-valid: it can be evaluated at every timestep without inflating the family-wise false positive rate. The output is a set of flagged subgroups whose reporting preponderance exceeds β·μ_G^0, along with the stopping time T (the number of reports needed to trigger the first alarm).

## Method

The paper formalizes subgroup harm detection as a sequential hypothesis test H_0^G: μ_G < βμ_G^0 vs. H_1^G: μ_G > βμ_G^0 for each group G, where μ_G is the group's share among reports and μ_G^0 is its share in the reference population. Two algorithmic instantiations are provided: (1) a sequential Z-test that counts group membership and applies a finite-time Law of the Iterated Logarithm threshold (with C=1/2 for finite-sample validity or C=√(βμ_G^0(1−βμ_G^0)) for asymptotic validity), and (2) a betting-style test that accumulates log-wealth via an adaptive λ_t updated by Online Newton Step, with a fixed threshold ln(|G|/α) justified by Ville's inequality on the resulting supermartingale. Both use a Bonferroni correction across groups, which the paper shows incurs only an additive ln(|G|) term in stopping time. The preponderance signal is then translated into interpretable harm metrics: Proposition 3.2 converts overrepresentation into a lower bound on relative risk under bounded report-to-incidence ratio assumptions, while Proposition 3.4 converts it into a lower bound on true incidence rate under assumptions on group-conditional true/false reporting rates.

## Results

On VAERS vaccine reports, all three algorithms correctly identify the (Male, 18-29) group as disproportionately affected by myocarditis within 34–111 reports (depending on algorithm and β), and the (Male, 12-17) group within 241–612 reports. Over 100 random permutations at β=2, the asymptotic Z-test identifies harm in all 100 trials by t=100, the betting-style test in ~80, and the finite-sample Z-test in ~20. On HMDA mortgage data, the asymptotic Z-test achieves the shortest stopping times (e.g., 60–85 reports at β=1.2) while identifying groups with true relative risks of 1.50–1.70; the betting-style test stops later (403–638 reports) but identifies groups with slightly higher relative risks (1.65–1.70). Stopping times increase by orders of magnitude as β rises from 1.2 to 1.8, with some algorithm/reporting-model combinations failing to stop within 40,000 reports at β=1.8.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external baseline methods; instead, it compares three algorithmic instantiations of the same sequential testing framework: the asymptotically-valid Z-test, the finite-sample Z-test, and the betting-style test. The asymptotic Z-test consistently achieves the shortest stopping times (e.g., 34 vs. 61 vs. 69 reports for (M,18-29) at β=2.0 on VAERS) but is less conservative, requiring a minimum stopping time to avoid early false rejections. The betting-style test offers a middle ground, stopping moderately faster than the finite-sample Z-test (e.g., 61 vs. 69 on VAERS; 403 vs. 1065 on HMDA at β=1.2, Anti-Correlated) while identifying groups with comparable or slightly higher true relative risks. The finite-sample Z-test is the most conservative, requiring 10–30× more reports than the asymptotic variant on HMDA (e.g., 2002 vs. 85 at β=1.2, Correlated), but provides the strongest finite-sample validity guarantee without asymptotic approximations. The authors conclude that the betting-style test offers the best practical balance between speed and confidence in the severity of identified harm.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of sequential hypothesis testing (Ville's inequality, LIL-based concentration bounds, e-value/betting frameworks from Waudby-Smith & Ramdas 2024, Vovk & Wang 2021, Shafer 2021) and fairness auditing. It departs from prior sequential fairness tests (Chugg et al. 2024, Feng et al. 2024) by introducing a fundamentally different test structure: rather than comparing means across groups, it tests within-group overrepresentation of reports relative to a known reference population composition. The Online Newton Step update (Cutkosky & Orabona 2018) is borrowed from online learning to optimize the betting-style test statistic, and the LIL-based threshold follows the lineage of Jamieson et al. 2014 and Balsubramani 2014. The reporting model itself—where individuals self-report adverse events and the database only sees a biased subset—is the key conceptual departure from both batch fairness auditing and prior sequential monitoring approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first formal framework for using individual incident reports as collective evidence of subgroup disparity, directly responding to regulatory mandates (EU AI Act Articles 85/87, UN AI Resolution 78/265) that require post-market surveillance with individual reporting. The practical demonstration on VAERS and HMDA data shows that known disparities can be detected with a fraction of the reports previously used for post-hoc analysis, making real-time monitoring feasible. Key open questions remain: how to handle time-varying base preponderances, how to set β without ground-truth reporting rates, how individuals' incentives shape reporting behavior, and how to legally contextualize a statistical alarm. The framework also raises the question of whether groups should be pre-specified or discovered online, and whether the Bonferroni correction can be replaced by tighter multiple-testing procedures that exploit dependence across hypotheses.
