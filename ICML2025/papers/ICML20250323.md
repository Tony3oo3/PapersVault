## Problem

Current event stream processing methods rely heavily on fixed data structures (voxel grids, spatiotemporal histograms, or end-to-end learned representations), which limits their stability and generalization across diverse datasets and real-world conditions. These approaches struggle with sparse data, high dynamic range, and are tightly coupled to specific representational frameworks, hindering robust deployment.

## Input / Output

The method consumes asynchronous event streams where each event carries spatial coordinates (x, y), a binary polarity, and a timestamp. Events are grouped by spatial coordinate (used as a key), and the corresponding polarity-timestamp sequence at each coordinate is fed into the CCNN. The output is a binary 2D event frame (pixel values 255 or 0) that encodes the spatial location of moving objects, which is then passed to a ResNet-34 classifier to produce a class label.

## Method

The framework encodes event streams using a Continuous-Coupled Neural Network (CCNN) inspired by the dorsal visual pathway: constant-polarity sequences produce periodic neuron outputs while varying-polarity sequences produce chaotic outputs, enabling intrinsic separation of stable and dynamic events. Continuous wavelet transform (Gaussian wavelet, scales 1–10) is then applied to the CCNN neuron outputs, and the sum of real parts of all wavelet coefficients distinguishes polarity-changing from constant sequences (negative vs. positive). A linear low-pass filter thresholds this value at zero to produce a binary event frame. The resulting frame is classified by a ResNet-34 backbone pre-trained on ImageNet, trained for five epochs with Adam (lr=1e-4) and cross-entropy loss, achieving 84.4% on N-Caltech101 and 99.9% on N-CARS.

## Results

The proposed CCNN-based chaotic dynamics framework achieves state-of-the-art classification accuracy on N-Caltech101 (84.4%) and N-CARS (99.9%), surpassing the previous best (TOKEN) by 2.8% and 4.5% respectively. On N-MNIST it reaches 97.4% and on ASL-DVS 99.2%, both competitive but slightly below the top methods (TOKEN at 99.9% and TORE/EvT at 99.9%). Inference is fast at 2.12 ms per sample (≈472 samples/s) with 21.9 M parameters and 3.7 G MACs, outperforming EST (6.4 ms) and MVF-Net (10.1 ms) in speed.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The framework clearly outperforms all 16 baselines on N-Caltech101 (84.4% vs. TOKEN 81.6%, TORE 79.8%, EST 75.3%) and N-CARS (99.9% vs. TOKEN 95.4%, TORE 94.5%, EV-VGCNN 95.3%), with margins of 2.8–17.5% and 4.5–24.0% respectively. On N-MNIST it trails TOKEN (99.9%) and VMV-GCN (99.5%) by 2.5 and 2.1 percentage points, and on ASL-DVS it is 0.7 points below TORE and EvT (both 99.9%). In terms of efficiency, the method's 2.12 ms inference time is roughly 3× faster than EST/M-LSTM (6.4 ms), 5× faster than MVF-Net (10.1 ms), and 50× faster than PointNet++ (103.9 ms), while using fewer MACs (3.7 G) than EST (4.3 G) and MVF-Net (5.6 G). The IoU comparison on N-Caltech101 (Table 3) further shows the framework surpassing Event Count, Time Surface, and LIF representations at all event counts (e.g., 0.6450 vs. 0.5937 at 100k events), confirming superior spatial localization of moving objects.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the CCNN model from Liu et al. (2022), originally a neuroscience model of primary visual cortex dynamics, as a fixed (non-learned) front-end encoder for event streams. It departs from the dominant paradigm in event processing—end-to-end learned representations (EST, M-LSTM, GNNs, transformers) and handcrafted accumulations (voxel grids, time surfaces, contrast maximization)—by instead using a nonlinear recurrence to map polarity sequences into periodic or chaotic signals, followed by continuous wavelet transform and a binary threshold to produce event frames. The dorsal-pathway ('where' pathway) framing positions the CCNN as a biologically motivated motion-extraction stage, with a standard ResNet-34 handling the subsequent 'what' (recognition) stage, echoing the brain's MT→IT routing.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework's appeal lies in its fixed, interpretable front-end (no gradient-based learning in the CCNN/CWT stage) combined with very fast inference (2.12 ms, ~472 samples/s), which could ease deployment in latency-sensitive or resource-constrained settings. However, the SOTA claims hold on only two of four datasets (N-Caltech101, N-CARS), and the absence of ablation studies makes it difficult to attribute gains to the CCNN dynamics versus the CWT or the threshold. Open questions include whether the fixed CCNN parameters generalize to unseen domains or sensor types, whether the 'chaotic' regime is truly necessary versus a simpler nonlinear filter, and whether the approach extends beyond classification to dense tasks like optical flow or segmentation.
