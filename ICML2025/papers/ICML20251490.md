## Problem

Standard speculative decoding enforces strict unbiasedness, requiring exact token-level matching between draft and target models, which wastes computation by rejecting high-quality draft outputs that merely differ in distribution. For multi-step reasoning tasks (math, science), this rigidity is especially costly: a correct intermediate step generated by the draft model may be discarded simply because its probability under the target model is low, negating the speedup. The paper asks whether a controlled, reward-guided bias can simultaneously reduce inference cost and improve accuracy over both the target model alone and standard speculative decoding.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a text prompt (e.g., a math word problem), a lightweight draft model, a larger target model, and a process reward model (PRM) that scores each reasoning step on a 0-to-1 scale. Reasoning steps are delineated by double-newline (\n\n) boundaries, and the PRM evaluates each step independently. The output is a complete step-by-step reasoning response; the threshold δ (default 0.7) controls how many steps are resolved by the draft model alone versus requiring target-model intervention, effectively performing automatic compute allocation across questions of varying difficulty.

## Method

RSD defines a dynamic mixture distribution P_RSD(y|z) = ω_r(y|z)·P_m(y|z) + ν·P_M(y|z), where the weighting function ω_r maps the PRM score of the current draft step to [0,1] and ν is a normalizing constant. At each decoding step, the draft model generates a candidate; if its PRM score exceeds threshold δ, the step is accepted (draft-only path), otherwise the target model generates the step (fallback path). The paper proves that under a constrained sampling budget, the optimal weighting is a binary step function (accept if r ≥ δ, reject otherwise), and that the resulting expected reward under P_RSD is guaranteed to exceed that of the draft model alone whenever ω is non-decreasing and the target model has higher expected reward. This controlled-bias mechanism allows RSD to retain high-reward draft steps that standard speculative decoding would reject, yielding up to 4.4× fewer FLOPs than target-only inference while achieving higher accuracy than both the target model and standard SD on average.

## Results

On the primary math-model setting (Qwen2.5-Math 7B target, 1.5B draft, 7B PRM), RSD with optimized threshold δ* achieves 66.5 average accuracy across six reasoning benchmarks, surpassing the single 7B target model (65.3) by +1.2 points and standard speculative decoding (64.6) by +1.9 points. At the 72B scale (7B draft, 7B PRM), RSD reaches 72.9 average accuracy vs. 71.8 for the single 72B model (+1.1) and 71.1 for SD (+1.8). In terms of efficiency, RSD (7B/72B/7B) achieves 88.0 on MATH500 with up to 4.4× fewer FLOPs than the 72B target model alone, while also outperforming Best-of-N (N=64) by 1.8 points at significantly lower cost.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against standard speculative decoding (Leviathan et al., 2023), RSD consistently wins: at the 7B scale, RSD (7B/1.5B/7B, δ*) achieves 66.5 average accuracy vs. SD's 64.6 (+1.9), and at the 72B scale, RSD (72B/7B/7B, δ*) reaches 72.9 vs. SD's 71.1 (+1.8). SD actually underperforms the single target model in both settings (−0.7), a known artifact of floating-point errors and strict unbiasedness, whereas RSD reliably exceeds the target model. Against test-time scaling baselines (Majority Voting and Best-of-N from Brown et al., 2024), RSD is clearly superior: majority voting at maj@16 drops to 62.3 (−3.0 below the 7B target), and Best-of-N at N=16 reaches only 64.8 (−0.5), while RSD achieves 66.1–66.5. Against search-based methods (Beam Search and Process Best-of-N from Chen et al., 2024), RSD (1.5B/7B/1.5B) scores 82.6 on MATH500, 94.5 on GSM8K, and 34.6 on Minerva Math, outperforming Beam Search (beam=4: 78.2/88.9/33.5) and Process Best-of-N (N=16: 76.0/87.9/31.2) by 4.4, 5.6, and 3.1 points respectively, demonstrating that targeted model invocation via PRM guidance outperforms exhaustive search in high-complexity reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends speculative decoding (Leviathan et al., 2023) by replacing its strict token-level unbiasedness requirement with a reward-guided, step-level acceptance criterion driven by a process reward model (PRM). While standard speculative decoding guarantees the output distribution exactly matches the target model, RSD deliberately introduces controlled bias, accepting high-reward draft outputs even when they deviate from the target model's distribution. The theoretical contribution (Proposition 2.3) shows that under a compute budget constraint, the optimal acceptance rule is a binary threshold on reward, connecting the method to optimal stopping and resource allocation theory. The approach also positions itself against test-time scaling methods (Best-of-N, majority voting, beam search) by showing that targeted model invocation outperforms exhaustive sampling of a smaller model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 4.4× FLOP reduction while matching or exceeding target-model accuracy makes RSD practically relevant for deploying large reasoning models at scale, where inference cost dominates. The emergent property of automatic compute allocation—simpler questions are solved by the draft model alone while harder questions invoke the target model—offers a principled alternative to uniform test-time scaling. Key open questions include: the absence of general-domain PRMs (the method currently relies on math-specific PRMs), whether specialized PRM fine-tuning aligned to the draft model can further boost acceptance rates, and how RSD interacts with emerging long-chain-of-thought reasoning models where step boundaries are less clear. The modest accuracy gains (+0.6 to +1.2 average) suggest the method's primary value is efficiency rather than quality improvement.
