## Problem

Existing diffusion-model-based generative steganography methods (CRoSS, DiffStega) rely on text prompts to guide stego image generation and serve as cryptographic keys. This leads to three critical failures: stego images remain visually similar to secret images due to limited prompt control, revealed secret images suffer quality degradation from DDIM inversion inconsistency, and descriptive text prompts are trivially guessable, compromising both key and secret image security.

## Input / Output

The method takes a 256×256 secret image and a 256×256 reference image as inputs, producing a 256×256 stego image that should closely resemble the reference image while being maximally dissimilar to the secret image. During reveal, the receiver uses the same reference image plus a public key (transmitted alongside the stego image) to regenerate the symmetric key and recover the secret image. The reference image acts as a generation guide rather than a container, and the private key derived from the reference image is never transmitted, ensuring an attacker who intercepts the stego image and public key cannot recover the secret.

## Method

SSHR replaces text prompts with reference images as guiding conditions within a modified Perona-Malik nonlinear diffusion framework, operating in the wavelet frequency domain via DWT. The conceal process iterates a coupled z/y update (inspired by EDICT) with a learnable mixing parameter p∈[0.90,1.0], where the diffusion term uses Conditional Reparameterization Convolution (CRC) whose weights are dynamically generated from the symmetric key, and the condition reaction term is modulated by a Condition Information Guidance Module (CIGM) that produces timestep-varying AdaLN-Zero parameters. The symmetric key is generated via an ECDHE-inspired protocol: AlexNet extracts features from both images, MLPs produce private keys, and a Hadamard product with a secret-image-dependent weight matrix yields the shared key. The exact reveal process deterministically inverts every step of the conceal iteration, eliminating the DDIM inversion inconsistency. Training uses a combined frequency loss (L1 in wavelet domain matching stego to reference and revealed to secret) and perceptual loss (AlexNet feature distance).

## Results

On the UniStega dataset, SSHR achieves a PSNR of 47.04 dB and SSIM of 0.99 for secret/revealed image pairs with the correct key, surpassing DiffStega† (23.92 dB / 0.79) by 23.12 dB and 20% SSIM. For stego/secret dissimilarity (lower is better), SSHR reduces PSNR to 8.96 and SSIM to 0.11, far below CRoSS (19.03 / 0.66) and DiffStega (18.61 / 0.59), confirming strong concealment. Against cover-based methods on ImageNet, SSHR reaches 49.52 dB PSNR and 0.9986 SSIM for revealed images, outperforming HiNet (46.78 / 0.9947) by 2.74 dB. In steganalysis, SSHR achieves the lowest detection accuracy (50.64% SRM, 51.05% SRNet, 50.93% XuNet), closest to the random-guess baseline of 50%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SSHR dominates all generative steganography baselines (CRoSS, DiffStega, DiffStega†) on every metric in Table 2: it achieves a 23.12 dB PSNR gain over DiffStega† for correct-key recovery (47.04 vs. 23.92) while simultaneously reducing stego/secret similarity by 10.77 dB in PSNR (8.96 vs. 19.73), meaning the stego image is far less recognizable as derived from the secret. For wrong-key attacks, SSHR drops recovery PSNR to 5.70 dB (vs. 20.68 for DiffStega†), confirming that intercepted public keys are insufficient for decryption. Against cover-based methods (Table 1), SSHR outperforms the strongest baseline HiNet by 2.74 dB PSNR on ImageNet (49.52 vs. 46.78) and 1.92 dB on DIV2K (48.56 vs. 46.64), with ISN and UDH trailing by over 10 dB. In steganalysis (Table 4), SSHR achieves the lowest detection accuracy across SRM, SRNet, and XuNet (50.64%, 51.05%, 50.93%), beating CRoSS and DiffStega by 1–2 percentage points and approaching the 50% random-guess floor. For naturalness (NIQE, Table 3), SSHR scores 5.46, slightly better than CRoSS (5.60) and DiffStega (5.58) but marginally above HiNet (5.35) and the original image (5.14).

## Relation to Prior Work

SSHR repurposes the Perona-Malik anisotropic diffusion PDE (originally for image restoration) as the core conceal/reveal mechanism, extending the condition-reaction-term framework of Chen & Pock (2016) with a key-conditioned convolution (CRC) and a reference-image-guided CRT. It inherits the frequency-domain (DWT) steganography paradigm from HiNet (Jing et al., 2021) and adopts the coupled z/y mixing structure from EDICT (Wallace et al., 2023) to enable exact inversion. The key conceptual departure from recent diffusion-model steganography (CRoSS, DiffStega) is replacing DDIM-based text-prompt conditioning with PM-diffusion-based image conditioning and an ECDHE-inspired symmetric key, shifting the security model from guessable text prompts to a key-exchange protocol.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to integrate a key-exchange protocol (modeled on ECDHE) into image steganography, addressing a genuine security gap where prior methods relied on guessable text prompts as keys. The exact reveal process, grounded in the invertibility of the PM iteration, yields a 24 dB PSNR advantage over diffusion-based baselines for secret recovery, which is practically significant for applications requiring faithful reconstruction. However, the ECDHE analogy is implemented via Hadamard products rather than true elliptic-curve arithmetic, so the cryptographic security guarantees are more aspirational than formally proven. Open questions include scalability beyond 256×256, robustness to adversarial steganalysis beyond the tested detectors, and whether the PM-diffusion framework generalizes to video or multi-image steganography.
