## Problem

Existing data selection methods for LLM pretraining focus on general-domain quality signals (linguistic coherence, noise reduction, surface-level heuristics) and largely neglect the specific structure of mathematical reasoning. In particular, they fail to account for the underlying mathematical skills embedded in training texts and the compositional relationships among those skills, which are critical for building strong reasoning capabilities.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251452/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a target math dataset (e.g., OpenWebMath with 14.7B tokens, OpenWebMath-pro with 5B tokens, or synthetic Jiuzhang3.0 with 3.8B tokens) and a small reference dataset (100K sampled points from NuminaMath). It produces a scalar quality score for every data point in the target set, from which a top-k% subset is extracted (k varies by dataset: 30% for OpenWebMath, 60% for OpenWebMath-pro, 70% for Jiuzhang3.0). The selected subset is then repeated proportionally to match the original token budget before continual pretraining.

## Method

MASS operates in two stages. First, it prompts Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct-GPTQ-Int4 to extract 1–10 abstract mathematical skill labels from each of 100K reference data points, then merges semantically similar skills (cosine > 0.9 via bge-large-en-v1.5) into 46,490 unique nodes and builds a skill graph with 1.18M co-occurrence edges; the adjacency matrix encodes skill importance on the diagonal (softmax-normalized occurrence counts) and compositional relationships off-diagonal (softmax-normalized pair co-occurrence counts). Second, for each target data point it computes a similarity to every skill as the maximum cosine similarity (via bge-large-en-v1.5) between the target text embedding and the embeddings of all reference texts containing that skill, forming an N×|V| similarity matrix. This matrix is then multiplied by the adjacency matrix (S_agg = S·A) to propagate compositional information through the graph, and the final quality score is the row-wise sum of the aggregated similarities. The key insight is that this single matrix product simultaneously rewards data points that cover more important individual skills (diagonal contribution) and those that capture richer skill-combination patterns (off-diagonal contribution), enabling a skill-aware ranking that outperforms surface-quality baselines.

## Results

MASS achieves the best average performance across all three target datasets and both model sizes. For TinyLlama-1.1B, MASS reaches 23.9% (OpenWebMath), 26.9% (OpenWebMath-pro), and 40.8% (Jiuzhang3.0) average accuracy, outperforming the original datasets by 3.6%, 3.3%, and 4.2% respectively. For Mistral-7B, MASS achieves 58.4%, 62.0%, and 65.5% average, improving over the originals by 4.8%, 5.9%, and 4.1%. In terms of efficiency, MASS-selected subsets require 50%–70% fewer trained tokens to match the performance of full-dataset training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MASS outperforms all six baselines across every dataset and model size tested. On OpenWebMath with TinyLlama-1.1B, MASS (23.9% avg) beats AutoDS (22.7%), DSIR (22.1%), ProX (23.6%), and the FineWeb heuristic RULE (18.4%). On OpenWebMath-pro with Mistral-7B, MASS (62.0%) surpasses DSIR (57.5%), BM25 (56.6%), and the original dataset (56.1%) by a substantial 4.5–5.9 percentage points. On Jiuzhang3.0, the largest gains appear: MASS improves over AutoDS by 2.5% (TinyLlama) and over the original by 4.1% (Mistral-7B). RHO-1 is only partially reported (no average score), but where comparable, MASS leads on GSM8K (9.0 vs 7.1) and MATH (4.4 vs 5.0 is the sole exception where RHO-1 edges ahead on MATH for TinyLlama). The consistent margin over AutoDS and DSIR—both of which also target mathematical data—suggests that the skill-graph aggregation captures compositional structure that flat scoring methods miss.

## Relation to Prior Work

MASS departs from general-purpose data selection methods (heuristic filters, importance resampling, token-level selection) by introducing a domain-specific, skill-centric scoring mechanism for mathematical pretraining data. It is conceptually closest to AutoDS, which also targets math data but scores texts on surface-level mathematical intelligence without modeling the compositional structure of underlying skills. The skill-graph construction draws on graph-based approaches in LLM data curation—Skill-it's skills graph for training order and MathScale's concept graph for instruction generation—but repurposes the graph for data *selection* during continual pretraining rather than for curriculum ordering or data generation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 50–70% token reduction while matching or exceeding full-dataset performance is practically significant for teams with limited compute budgets in math-focused pretraining. The skill-graph framework is naturally extensible to other domains with well-defined skill taxonomies (coding, biomedicine), though the authors acknowledge that domains with less structured skills (creative writing, role-playing) remain challenging. Key open questions include incorporating skill difficulty (rare but complex skills may need more data), explicitly modeling diversity alongside quality, and adapting the approach to instruction tuning or RL settings where small-scale selection is critical.
