## Problem

When the number of treatment levels K is large, existing ITR learning methods suffer from data sparsity within each treatment group and severe covariate shifts across groups, making propensity-score-based balancing unstable and complex outcome models prone to overfitting. Prior fusion approaches (e.g., Ma et al., 2022) require both a correctly specified linear outcome model and balanced covariates, limiting their applicability in observational settings with heterogeneous treatment assignment.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20253209/cropped_page8_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes tabular observational data with p-dimensional covariates X, a K-level discrete treatment A, and a scalar outcome Y. It first produces a data-driven group mapping δ: A → B that reduces K treatments to M groups (M < K), then outputs a depth-D policy tree d^B: X → B assigning patients to treatment groups. Notably, the final policy tree may use a strict subset of covariates (excluding confounders like race, region, and SES proxies for fairness) while calibration weighting and nuisance estimation use the full covariate set.

## Method

The procedure has two stages. First, calibration-weighted treatment fusion: for each treatment group, Cressie-Read discrepancy minimization yields weights that balance the group's covariate distribution to the overall sample mean; these weights feed into a weighted fused Lasso objective that groups treatments whose linear projection vectors ζ_a onto X are similar, with the number of groups determined by EBIC. Second, Cross-Fitted AIPW Learning (CAIPWL) with policy trees is applied on the M grouped treatments to learn the optimal individualized rule. The key theoretical contribution is double robustness: the true group structure is recovered if either the calibration weighting model or the linear outcome model is correctly specified, because the group structure is encoded in the linear projection of potential outcomes onto X, and the projection residual is orthogonal to X by construction regardless of model form.

## Results

In the primary simulation (K=16 treatments, n=1800, 4 true groups), the proposed CW+fusion+policy tree achieves an Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) of 0.96 (SE 0.06) for group recovery and a policy value of 8.89 (SE 0.11), recovering on average 4.34 groups (oracle = 4). The uncalibrated fusion baseline achieves only ARI 0.26 and recovers 10.7 groups on average, while the Ma et al. (2022) method yields ARI 0.26 and a policy value of 8.51, actually underperforming the no-fusion policy tree baseline (8.77). On the Flatiron Health CLL/SLL dataset (10,346 patients, 7 treatments), the method fuses treatments into clinically interpretable groups and produces a depth-5 policy tree assigning patients to grouped treatments based on age, time since diagnosis, Rai stage, ECOG score, and lymphadenopathy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: (1) the CAIPWL policy tree of Zhou et al. (2023) without any fusion or calibration weighting, and (2) the fused Lasso approach of Ma et al. (2022) that uses linear working models without calibration weighting. The proposed CW+fusion+policy tree decisively outperforms both on fusion quality: ARI of 0.96 versus 0.26 for both baselines, recovering 4.34 groups on average (near the oracle of 4) versus 10.73 for the uncalibrated methods. On policy value, the proposed method achieves 8.89, exceeding the no-fusion baseline (8.77) by 0.12 and the Ma et al. method (8.51) by 0.38. Notably, Ma et al. (2022) actually underperforms the simple policy tree baseline (8.51 vs. 8.77) because its linear outcome model is misspecified in the simulation, whereas the proposed method's double robustness protects against this misspecification. In the additional simulation with correctly specified outcome models (Table 10), all methods perform well on ARI, but the proposed method still achieves the best policy value (8.80 vs. 8.66 baseline and 6.39 for Ma et al. with their linear policy learning step).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the treatment fusion framework of Ma et al. (2022, 2023), which used fused Lasso to group similar treatments for ITR learning, by introducing calibration weighting as a robust covariate-balancing mechanism that replaces the more fragile IPW approach. The key conceptual departure is a double robustness guarantee: the oracle group structure is recovered if either the calibration weighting model or the linear outcome model is correctly specified, whereas Ma et al. required the outcome model to be correctly specified. The downstream policy learning step directly adopts the CAIPWL framework of Zhou et al. (2023) with policy trees, and the calibration weighting draws on the Cressie-Read discrepancy family from the balancing literature (Lee et al., 2023; Wu & Yang, 2023).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method addresses a practically critical bottleneck in precision medicine where dozens of treatment options (e.g., 16+ regimens for CLL/SLL) make direct multi-armed policy learning infeasible due to data sparsity and covariate shift. The double robustness property and the separation of covariates used for fusion versus those used in the final decision rule (enabling fairness constraints) are practically valuable design choices. Limitations include instability when some treatment arms have very few observations, the single-pass (non-iterative) fusion procedure, and the reliance on a linear working model for the interaction terms. Open directions flagged by the authors include multi-source data integration, uncertainty quantification via conformal prediction, and extension to heterogeneous treatment effect estimation.
