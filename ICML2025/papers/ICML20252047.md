## Problem

Prior backdoor attacks against deep reinforcement learning (e.g., TrojDRL, SleeperNets) assume the adversary can implant arbitrarily large reward perturbations to bias the agent's Q-values toward a target action. In practice, RL environments have natural reward bounds and training pipelines apply clipping or normalization, rendering these unconstrained attacks both brittle and trivially detectable. This paper asks: can a backdoor attack achieve guaranteed success while respecting the environment's reward constraints?

## Input / Output

The victim agent consumes environment observations as input—32×32 RGB frames for Atari games (Q*Bert, Frogger, Pacman, Breakout), one-hot vectors for CAGE-2, or lidar+proprioceptive vectors for Safety Car—and outputs a discrete action. The adversary operates on the agent's replay memory: it inserts a trigger (a 6×6 checkerboard in the top-left image corner, or a boolean flag appended to vector observations) into stored states and rewrites the associated actions and rewards before the agent's PPO policy update. The poisoned agent, at test time, receives the same observation format but is conditioned to output the target action whenever the trigger is present.

## Method

The core mechanism is 'adversarial inception': rather than inflating rewards beyond the environment's natural [L, U] bounds, the attack selectively poisons high-return time steps in the replay memory. A parallel DQN approximates the optimal Q-function from the agent's benign interactions, and a metric F_Q̂ identifies time steps where the agent took near-optimal (high positive) or near-worst (high negative) actions. The adversary then inserts the trigger into the prior observation, replaces the recorded action with the target action a⁺ (for high-return steps) or leaves it as a non-target action (for low-return steps), and sets the reward to U or L respectively. This causes the agent to associate the target action with optimal outcomes in triggered states while keeping all rewards within the benign range. The paper proves that under this construction, the target action is provably optimal in every poisoned state (Theorem 1) and the optimal policy in the adversarial MDP coincides with the optimal policy in the benign MDP for all untriggered states (Theorem 2), guaranteeing both attack success and stealth simultaneously.

## Results

Q-Incept achieves 100% Attack Success Rate (ASR) on 5 of 7 environments (Q*Bert, Frogger, Pacman, Breakout, Safety Car) at the higher poisoning rate, while maintaining Benign Return (BR) scores comparable to unpoisoned agents. On the two hardest environments, Q-Incept still substantially outperforms baselines: 93.2% ASR on CAGE-2 (vs. <6% for both baselines) and 61.6% ASR on Highway Merge (vs. <2%). Q-Incept is the only method whose ASR consistently improves as the poisoning budget β increases, whereas SleeperNets and TrojDRL sometimes degrade at higher β.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Q-Incept decisively outperforms both SleeperNets (dynamic reward poisoning) and TrojDRL (static reward poisoning + forced action manipulation) on 6 of 7 environments in ASR under constrained rewards, with the largest gaps on CAGE-2 (93.2% vs. 0.06% and 5.6%) and Highway Merge (61.6% vs. 1.5% and 1.2%). On Frogger, Q-Incept achieves 100% ASR while SleeperNets drops to 0% and TrojDRL to 4.4%, demonstrating that prior attacks fundamentally fail when rewards are clipped to the environment's natural [0,1] range. The only environment where all three methods tie is Breakout (~100% ASR), likely because the No-Op target action has minimal impact on gameplay. In terms of stealth (BR), Q-Incept maintains returns within or above unpoisoned baselines on most environments, while SleeperNets and TrojDRL show larger BR drops on CAGE-2 (−50.2 and −53.1 vs. −45.2 unpoisoned). Q-Incept is also the only method whose ASR monotonically improves with increasing β, whereas both baselines sometimes degrade at higher poisoning rates (e.g., Frogger, Pacman).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the DRL backdoor poisoning line of work initiated by TrojDRL (Kiourti et al., 2019) and advanced by SleeperNets (Rathbun et al., 2024), which established the outer-loop threat model and dynamic reward poisoning strategies. The key departure is identifying that all prior attacks implicitly assume unbounded reward manipulation, and proving this assumption is both unrealistic and necessary for their success. The paper introduces 'adversarial inception'—a qualitatively different mechanism that manipulates actions in stored training trajectories rather than (or in addition to) rewards—leveraging a DQN-based Q-function approximation (Mnih et al., 2013) to identify high-return time steps for poisoning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that the entire prior line of DRL backdoor attacks is fundamentally brittle under the reward constraints that real environments enforce, which could give practitioners a false sense of security. The theoretical guarantees (Theorems 1 and 2) showing that inception-based attacks simultaneously achieve attack success and stealth under bounded rewards are a clean contribution to the adversarial RL literature. However, the threat model still requires the adversary to have access to the agent's replay buffer during training, and no effective defense against inception attacks is proposed—existing 'universal' defenses like BIRD and State Sanitization are shown to be evadable. Open questions include whether inception attacks generalize to continuous action spaces, multi-agent settings, and whether training-time detection of action manipulation is feasible.
