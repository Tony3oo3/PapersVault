## Problem

Existing cognitive simulation approaches either model human behavior under ideal conditions or treat environmental stimuli as a static, binary factor, failing to capture how dynamic, time-varying stimuli (e.g., animated time-pressure feedback) continuously modulate the fine-grained cognitive process. The paper asks how to simulate the granular, frame-by-frame impact of such dynamic environmental perturbations on human evidence accumulation during decision-making.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252793/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes three input streams per trial: a one-hot encoded math question string (11×17 vector), time-pressure video frames segmented at 5 fps and encoded via a CNN feature extractor, and a numeric trial ID. It produces a predicted response time (determined when the DDM evidence accumulator crosses the boundary threshold) and an interpretable time-pressure effect trajectory — a per-frame sequence of accumulated actions showing how each visual frame shifted the evidence accumulation process. The DDM trajectory is initialized from SVM-predicted baseline response time and choice probability, then modulated step-by-step by the DRL agent.

## Method

CogReact is a four-stage hybrid pipeline: (1) an LSTM sequence-to-sequence math agent (256 hidden units) is trained to solve modular arithmetic correctly, extracting task-difficulty features from its intermediate layer; (2) these 256-dim features plus trial ID feed SVM models (SVC for choice probability, SVR for baseline response time) that map agent representations to human performance; (3) a drift-diffusion model generates a fine-grained evidence accumulation trajectory using a sigmoid curve perturbed by Brownian motion, with the SVC probability as boundary threshold; (4) a PPO-based DRL agent with an MLP policy observes each time-pressure frame and outputs a continuous action in [-1,1] that biases the evidence accumulator (positive, neutral, or negative drift per frame), with reward defined as the relative error reduction over the SVM baseline at the terminal state. The key architectural insight is embedding DDM's sequential evidence accumulation inside the DRL loop so the agent modulates cognition frame-by-frame rather than predicting a single response-time offset, yielding both interpretability (action trajectories) and ~10× faster convergence than a pure DRL baseline.

## Results

On the Math Reasoning Dataset, CogReact achieves the lowest MAPE of 0.2703 (STD 0.2224) in the numeric encoding setting (Type IV) and 0.2999 (STD 0.2318) in the string+video setting (Type II), outperforming all baselines by 3–6 percentage points. On the CPC18 decision-making dataset and the PeerEdu learning dataset, the hybrid DRL model also achieves the lowest MAPE across all baselines, with statistical significance at p<0.001. The hybrid DRL agent also converges ~10× faster than the pure DRL agent (4.42 min vs. 38.30 min on an RTX 2080 Ti).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CogReact outperforms all four baseline families across both primary input settings. Against the hGRU model of Goetschalckx et al. (NeurIPS 2024), CogReact in Type IV (0.2703) reduces MAPE by ~6.3 points over hGRU in Type I (0.3335), though the input modalities differ. Against the LSTM-based latent dynamical systems of Jaffe et al. (Nature Human Behaviour 2023), CogReact in Type II (0.2999) beats the best LSTM variant (LSTM-V1+3D ResNet, 0.3334) by 3.4 points. Against the MLP+3D ResNet cognitive model prior of Bourgin et al. (ICML 2019), CogReact in Type II (0.2999) improves over MLP+3D ResNet (0.3331) by 3.3 points. Against the Transformer architecture of Vaswani et al. (NeurIPS 2017), CogReact in Type IV (0.2703) beats Transformer (0.3052) by 3.5 points, and in Type II CogReact (0.2999) beats Transformer+3D ResNet (0.3306) by 3.1 points. All improvements are statistically significant (p<0.001) under Kolmogorov-Smirnov and permutation tests, except CogReact Type II vs. LSTM/Transformer Type IV where the modality mismatch limits direct comparability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of classical cognitive modeling (drift-diffusion model, Ratcliff & McKoon 2008) and data-driven human behavior simulation (Bourgin et al. 2019; Jaffe et al. 2023; Goetschalckx et al. 2024). Prior work on response time prediction predominantly models cognition under ideal, stimulus-free conditions or treats environmental feedback as a static, trial-level constant. CogReact departs from this by embedding the DDM's evidence accumulation process as the state space within a PPO-based DRL loop, where the agent modulates accumulation frame-by-frame in response to dynamic visual stimuli — a fine-grained temporal coupling that neither pure DDM (posterior estimation) nor pure DRL (black-box output) provides. The LSTM-based math agent for feature extraction follows the RNN-for-cognition line (Yang et al. 2019; Mickey & McClelland 2014), and the SVM transfer step borrows from perceptual time-perception modeling (Roseboom et al. 2019).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a principled bridge between interpretable cognitive theory and flexible deep learning, producing per-trial evidence accumulation trajectories that reveal *how* (not just *that*) dynamic stimuli modulate cognition — a capability absent in end-to-end response time predictors. Generalization across three tasks (math reasoning, decision making, learning) and two feedback modalities (visual, numeric/textual) suggests the hybrid DDM-DRL pattern is not task-specific. Key limitations include the exclusive focus on response time (accuracy is held constant by design), the relatively small human dataset (36 participants, 21K trials), and the reliance on a single stimuli type (time pressure). Open questions include whether the framework extends to multi-dimensional cognitive measures, more complex adaptive interventions, or larger-scale behavioral data where the DDM's two-boundary assumption may break down.
