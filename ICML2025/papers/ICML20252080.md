## Problem

Standard imbalanced learning remedies such as oversampling, SMOTE, and bagging are widely used to handle skewed class distributions, but their interaction with differential privacy has not been formally characterized. This paper shows that these methods either explode downstream privacy sensitivity (SMOTE's loss scales exponentially in dimension) or yield privacy parameters too small to be meaningful (bagging), leaving a gap in DP-compatible tools for imbalanced classification.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252080/cropped_page7_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The methods consume a tabular dataset of n d-dimensional feature vectors bounded in [-R,R]^d with binary labels, where the minority class has n₁ ≪ n₀ examples (imbalance ratio r = n₀/n₁ up to 130). Outputs are either a binary classifier trained under an (ε,δ)-DP guarantee, or a class-balanced synthetic dataset (n₀ = n₁) drawn from a privately fitted distributional model. All experiments use datasets from the imblearn repository with n ∈ [336, 11183] and r ∈ [8.6, 130], and privacy budgets ε ∈ {0.05, 0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 5.0} with δ = 1/n².

## Method

The paper provides both negative and positive results. Negatively, it proves that SMOTE pre-processing inflates the downstream ε by a factor of 2^{0.4042d}·⌈N/n₁⌉/k (Theorem 3, via kissing-number geometry and Chernoff bounds), and that bagging's claimed intrinsic DP forces ε ≤ 1/n when δ = n^{-c} (Proposition 4). Positively, it introduces three DP-compatible alternatives: (1) a pre-processing pipeline that fits a DP synthetic data model (GEM or PrivBayes) via the Select-Measure-Project paradigm, then draws balanced samples for a non-private XGBoost classifier—privacy is preserved trivially by post-processing; (2) a class-weighted private ERM (Algorithm 1) that adapts Chaudhuri et al.'s objective-perturbation mechanism by inflating the perturbation sensitivity from 2 to 3 to account for weight changes when a minority label flips between neighboring datasets (Theorem 5); and (3) a weighted DP-SGD variant (Algorithm 4) that incorporates inverse-class-frequency weights into the cross-entropy loss while maintaining the standard (ε,δ)-DP guarantee via a refined gradient-sensitivity bound of 2C/B (Proposition 6). Empirically, the GEM+XGBoost pipeline ranks first on 7 of 8 metrics, while weighted ERM achieves the best recall among private in-processing methods.

## Results

Across 8 imbalanced datasets (imbalance ratios r∈[8.6, 130], sizes n∈[336, 11183]) and privacy budgets ε∈{0.05, 0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 5.0}, GEM+XGBoost achieves the best average rank (1.45) on 7 of 8 metrics (AUC, F1, Balanced Accuracy, Precision, Worst-ACC, Macro-ACC, G-Mean). Priv. Weighted LogReg achieves the best average Recall (2.11) among all private in-processing models and outperforms its unweighted counterpart on 3 of 4 imbalanced-specific metrics. Priv. Weighted FTT (DP-SGD-trained FTTransformer) consistently ranks last or near-last across all metrics, with average ranks of 3.34–3.70, confirming that neural models are poorly suited for small-to-medium private imbalanced tabular classification.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four pipelines: GEM+XGBoost (DP synthetic data + non-private XGBoost), Priv. LogReg (unweighted DP ERM logistic regression from Chaudhuri et al., 2011), Priv. Weighted LogReg (the paper's class-weighted DP ERM, Algorithm 1), and Priv. Weighted FTT (DP-SGD-trained FTTransformer with weighted cross-entropy). GEM+XGBoost dominates, ranking first on 7 of 8 metrics with average ranks of 1.45–1.48, substantially ahead of all in-processing alternatives. Priv. Weighted LogReg improves over Priv. LogReg on imbalanced-specific metrics—Recall (2.11 vs. 2.20), Worst-ACC (2.59 vs. 2.89), Macro-ACC (2.59 vs. 2.89), and G-Mean (2.59 vs. 2.86)—but slightly worsens on overall metrics like AUC (3.19 vs. 2.77), consistent with the known trade-off that weighting shifts performance toward the minority class. SMOTE (Chawla et al., 2002) is shown to be fundamentally incompatible with DP: Theorem 3 proves the privacy loss scales as Θ(ε·2^{0.4042d}·⌈N/n₁⌉/k), making the required ε′ impractically small (e.g., ε′=0.00469 needed to achieve ε=1 at d=25), and empirical results in Figure 2 confirm severe performance degradation after proper sensitivity adjustment. Priv. Weighted FTT underperforms all other methods across every metric, with average ranks of 3.34–3.70, suggesting that even a strong neural architecture cannot overcome minority-class sparsity under DP in these tabular settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two well-developed literatures—differentially private machine learning and imbalanced classification—and is the first to systematically analyze which standard imbalanced-learning remedies are compatible with DP. It extends the DP-ERM framework of Chaudhuri et al. (2011) by proving a class-weighted variant (Theorem 5), adapts DP-SGD (Abadi et al., 2016) with weighted cross-entropy (Proposition 6), and leverages the GEM synthetic-data generator (Liu et al., 2021) as a private pre-processing step. Crucially, it departs from Liu et al. (2020), who claimed bagging is 'intrinsically' DP, by showing the resulting privacy parameters are vacuous in practice (Proposition 4), and from the widespread use of SMOTE by proving its sensitivity scales exponentially in dimension via kissing-number bounds (Theorem 3).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides practitioners with a clear decision framework: use DP synthetic data (e.g., GEM) plus a strong non-private tabular learner for low-dimensional settings, or class-weighted DP-ERM for higher-dimensional data where synthetic data suffers from the curse of dimensionality. The negative results for SMOTE and bagging are practically important because these are the most commonly deployed imbalanced-learning techniques, and the paper quantifies exactly why they fail under DP. Open questions include extending the analysis to multiclass settings, exploring hybrid pipelines (DP synthetic data in lower-dimensional embeddings feeding weighted ERM), and whether personalized/individualized DP budgets could relax the uniform privacy constraint that disproportionately penalizes minority-class samples.
