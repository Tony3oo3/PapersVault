## Problem

While biologically plausible learning rules for RNNs have been shown to match BPTT in task performance, it remains unclear whether they also produce brain-like neural activity comparable to BPTT-trained models. This paper asks whether a local, gradient-truncation-based rule (e-prop) can achieve neural data similarity on par with BPTT when both are matched for task accuracy, addressing a critical but underexamined criterion for evaluating brain models.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251605/cropped_page4_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

RNNs (200–400 hidden units, retanh activation) are trained on synthetic inputs derived from primate neuroscience datasets (Mante 2013: 34 timesteps, 400 units; Sussillo 2015: 186 timesteps, 200 units) using various learning rules. The trained hidden-state trajectories are then compared against actual multi-electrode neural recordings via Procrustes distance (primary metric), supplemented by CCA, CKA, and Dynamical Similarity Analysis. Comparisons are made at matched normalized accuracy levels, and a data-to-data noise floor (splitting neurons within the same subject) provides a baseline for interpreting model-data distances.

## Method

The paper trains RNNs with e-prop (a three-factor local rule that truncates nonlocal terms in the RTRL gradient, retaining only eligibility traces modulated by a top-down instructive signal) and compares its neural similarity to BPTT, truncated BPTT, ModProp, node perturbation, and evolutionary strategies across two primate tasks. The key finding is that e-prop achieves Procrustes distances statistically indistinguishable from BPTT at equal accuracy, while older methods (node perturbation, ES) show significantly larger distances. The authors further show that initial weight gain and architecture exert a larger effect on model-data distance than the choice of learning rule, and that e-prop's similarity to BPTT is partly explained by its decomposition into a lower-learning-rate BPTT component plus approximation error. A 1D linear RNN toy analysis (Proposition B.1) provides a formal convergence/divergence characterization showing e-prop's success depends critically on initialization relative to the BPTT solution.

## Results

On the Sussillo 2015 task, e-prop achieves a Procrustes distance of 0.407±0.008 at normalized accuracy ≈0.8, statistically indistinguishable from BPTT (0.398±0.007, p=0.173). Additional similarity metrics confirm this: CCA scores are 0.273±0.009 (e-prop) vs. 0.272±0.009 (BPTT, p=0.953) and CKA scores are 0.154±0.006 vs. 0.152±0.015 (p=0.817). Trained models significantly outperform untrained initializations (e.g., Sussillo 2015: 0.406 vs. 0.498 for BPTT; Mante 2013: 0.721 vs. 0.800) and approach the data-to-data noise floor (0.373 for Sussillo, 0.704 for Mante). Initial weight gain has a larger effect on model-data distance than the choice of learning rule (p<0.001 across gains vs. p>0.07 across rules at fixed gain).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between e-prop (Bellec et al., 2020) and BPTT: at matched task accuracy, e-prop achieves Procrustes distances statistically indistinguishable from BPTT across all tested initializations (p-values range from 0.078 to 0.985), confirming that gradient truncation does not sacrifice neural data similarity. ModProp (Liu et al., 2022b), which recovers terms omitted by e-prop via cell-type-specific modulatory signals, also matches BPTT under Dale's law and ReLU activation (Appendix Figure 7). In contrast, older learning rules—node perturbation (Werfel et al., 2003) and evolutionary strategies (Salimans et al., 2017)—produce significantly higher Procrustes distances than both BPTT and e-prop at equivalent accuracy levels (Appendix Figure 9), demonstrating that not all learning rules are equally effective at producing brain-like activity. The paper further shows that initial weight gain (a model architecture/initialization factor) exerts a larger influence on model-data distance than the learning rule choice itself, with p<0.001 across gains versus p>0.07 across rules at fixed gain.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the e-prop/RFLO line of truncation-based bio-plausible learning rules (Bellec et al., 2020; Murray, 2019) and the broader RNN-for-brain-modeling tradition (Mante et al., 2013; Sussillo et al., 2015), but shifts the evaluation criterion from task accuracy to neural data similarity — a question the authors explicitly identify as underexamined. It leverages established similarity metrics (Procrustes distance, CCA, CKA, DSA) and applies them systematically to compare bio-plausible rules against the BPTT benchmark, departing from prior work that primarily assessed bio-plausible rules on task performance alone. The theoretical convergence analysis (Proposition 4.1) extends the understanding of e-prop's behavior by showing initialization-dependent convergence in a simplified 1D setting, complementing the empirical findings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides important validation that bio-plausible gradient-truncation rules can achieve neural data similarity comparable to BPTT, strengthening the case for their use in brain modeling without sacrificing representational fidelity. The finding that initial weight magnitude exerts a larger influence on model-data alignment than the learning rule itself is a practical and somewhat counterintuitive result that should reshape how the community prioritizes hyperparameter choices. Key limitations include the restriction to two primary datasets, a fixed architecture, and the fact that BPTT itself is not biologically plausible — raising the open question of whether a truly bio-plausible benchmark would yield different conclusions. Future work on learning rule–architecture co-design, broader dataset coverage, and non-temporal domains (e.g., vision) is needed to generalize these findings.
