## Problem

Previous precision-aware scaling laws (e.g., Kumar et al., 2024) treat total bit width as a single scalar, which fails to capture the distinct and asymmetric roles of exponent bits (dynamic range) versus mantissa bits (precision) in floating-point quantization. As a result, these laws exhibit significant fitting errors—especially in low-bit FP regimes like E1M1—and cannot guide practitioners on how to allocate bits between exponent and mantissa, choose scaling-factor block sizes, or determine when additional training data becomes harmful under low precision.

## Input / Output

The Capybara scaling law takes five continuous/discrete inputs—model parameter count N, trained token count D, exponent bits E, mantissa bits M, and scaling-factor block size B—and outputs a predicted validation loss. Beyond the loss prediction, the framework analytically derives three actionable quantities: the optimal E/M split for a fixed total precision P (e.g., E4M3 for FP8), a critical data size D_crit beyond which validation loss increases (over-training under low precision), and the compute-optimal precision P_opt as a function of total FLOP budget C. The block size B enters the formula logarithmically (log₂B), unifying tensor-wise, channel-wise, and block-wise scaling strategies under a single parameter.

## Method

The paper proposes the Capybara scaling law, which augments the Chinchilla loss (n/N^α + d/D^β + ε) with a precision-loss term (D^β/N^α)·log₂B / [γ(E+0.5)^δ(M+0.5)^ν]. The key structural insight is that the precision penalty is modulated by the 'knowledge intensity' ratio D^β/N^α, so the same FP format causes proportionally more damage when a model has absorbed more data relative to its size. Exponent and mantissa enter as separate power-law factors with distinct fitted exponents (δ≈3.19, ν≈2.95), rather than being collapsed into a single total-bit term as in prior work. The authors validated the law by training 366 LLaMA-architecture models (41M–679M parameters, 10B–100B tokens) with QPyTorch-simulated FP formats, quantizing only the GEMM inputs P2 (forward weights), P4 (backward weights), and P6 (backward inputs) after ablation showed these targets minimize performance degradation. From the fitted law they derive closed-form expressions for optimal E/M allocation, critical data size, and compute-optimal precision, predicting that the best cost-performance precision lies in the 4–8 bit range across a wide compute budget (10²¹–10³¹ FLOPs).

## Results

The Capybara scaling law is fitted on 366 training runs (N ∈ {41M, 85M, 154M, 679M} params; D ∈ {10B, 20B, 50B, 100B} tokens) with hyperparameters n=69.23, α=0.237, d=68973, β=0.516, ε=1.906, γ=11334.5, δ=3.193, ν=2.954. Validation on unseen 1.2B, 7B, and 70B models confirms consistent predictive accuracy. Key derived quantities include optimal float layouts (FP4→E2M1, FP8→E4M3, FP16→E8M7), critical data sizes (D_crit = 1730T for BF16-1B, 27T for FP8-E4M3-1B, 0.4T for FP4-E2M1-1B), and an optimal cost-performance precision of 4–8 bits across compute budgets of 10²¹–10³¹ FLOPs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is Kumar et al. (2024), whose precision-aware scaling law (Eq. 6) treats total bit width P = E+M+1 as a single scalar via an exponential saturation term N_eff = N(1 − e^{−P/γ}). The paper demonstrates that Kumar et al.'s formulation exhibits considerable fitting bias in the E1M1 (FP2) regime, where the joint treatment of exponent and mantissa fails to capture their asymmetric contributions (Figure 1a vs. 1b). The Capybara law, by decomposing precision into separate power-law factors (E+0.5)^δ and (M+0.5)^ν with distinct exponents (δ=3.19 vs. ν=2.95), fits the same 366 experimental points substantially better, particularly at low bit widths. Additionally, the paper compares the Chinchilla (Hoffmann et al., 2022) and OpenAI (Kaplan et al., 2020) base scaling law forms, finding Chinchilla's additive n/N^α + d/D^β + ε structure provides a tighter fit to BF16 training losses than OpenAI's multiplicative form, and adopts it as the foundation for the precision-aware extension.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Chinchilla scaling law (Hoffmann et al., 2022) by adding a precision-dependent loss term, and specifically departs from Kumar et al. (2024) 'Scaling laws for precision,' which treated total bit width P = E+M+1 as a single scalar. The key conceptual departure is decomposing FP precision into its constituent exponent (E) and mantissa (M) bits with separate power-law exponents (δ and ν), plus the block size (B) of scaling factors, revealing that the single-P formulation fails to fit low-bit FP training data. The resulting 'Capybara' scaling law unifies all five factors (N, D, E, M, B) under a shared set of hyperparameters, with the precision-related term taking the form D^β/N^α · log₂(B) / [γ(E+0.5)^δ(M+0.5)^ν].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides actionable guidance for hardware designers (optimal E:M ratios like E2M1 for FP4, E4M3 for FP8) and training practitioners (the critical data size D_crit, which drops to 0.4T tokens for a 1B model in FP4, meaning more data actively degrades performance). The finding that optimal cost-performance precision stays in the 4–8 bit range across a 10^21–10^31 FLOP compute budget offers a concrete target for future training pipelines. Limitations include fitting on models up to 679M parameters (with 1.2B/7B/70B only as validation), restriction to the classical Transformer architecture, and reliance on a single dataset (Dolma V1.7 subset); whether the law transfers to SSM/Mamba architectures or newer quantization schemes remains open.
