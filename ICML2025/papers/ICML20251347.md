## Problem

Fine-grained rule-based preference annotation for RLHF reward models faces a fundamental tension: applying all rules from a large pool (e.g., 133 constitutions) is computationally expensive and introduces bias from irrelevant rules, while using a small fixed or randomly sampled set fails to capture data diversity. No existing method dynamically selects the most informative rules for each specific pair of responses, leaving a gap between annotation quality and efficiency.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251347/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The Rule Adapter takes a trio (prompt x, response y_A, response y_B) and outputs indices of 5 critical rules from a pool of 100. The reward model (RAMO) takes a prompt-response pair and outputs a scalar reward score. Notably, rule-based ratings are computed via Llama3-70B logits as P(Yes) − P(No) in [−1, 1] rather than direct 0–1 scores, with a third 'Irrelevant' option added to handle off-topic rules robustly. Preference labels are binary (chosen/rejected) derived from the mean of the 5 selected rule scores.

## Method

The core insight is that since a reward model learns the difference between two responses, the most informative rules are those along which the two responses exhibit the largest score discrepancy. The paper proves (via Jensen-Shannon divergence) that selecting rules maximizing this discrepancy is equivalent to maximizing mutual information between rule-based preference labels and hidden ground-truth preferences. A relevance regularization term (cosine similarity between rule embedding and prompt) is added to avoid selecting high-discrepancy but off-topic rules. A Rule Adapter (Llama3.2-3B multi-label classifier) is trained on 63K synthetic trios to predict the top-5 critical rules per trio, and an 8B reward model (RAMO) is then trained on just 1K trios labeled using these dynamically selected rules, achieving the top safety score on RewardBench.

## Results

RAMO, an 8B reward model trained on just 1K preference pairs labeled via the Rule Adapter, achieves a Safety score of 95.1 on RewardBench-Safety, ranking first on the leaderboard as of May 25, 2025, ahead of 160+ models. Its per-task scores are 91.2 (Do Not Answer), 98.0 (Refusals Dangerous), 99.0 (Refusals Offensive), 97.4 (XTest Should Refuse), and 93.2 (XTest Should Respond). After RLHF with PPO using RAMO, the aligned Llama3.2-1B and Llama3.2-3B models achieve SafetyBench average scores of 56.8 and 77.4 respectively, surpassing larger 6B–13B baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RAMO (8B) outperforms all 16 baselines on the overall Safety metric. Against the closest explicit multi-attribute competitors, it beats SteerLM-70B (92.8) by 2.3 points and Nemotron-340B (91.5) by 3.6 points, despite being roughly 9–42× smaller. Among same-backbone models, RAMO improves over Skywork-8B (92.7) by 2.4 points and over the raw Llama3.1-8B (64.0) by 31.1 points, showing the Rule Adapter labeling adds substantial value on top of the backbone. Against larger Llama-based models, RAMO surpasses Llama3.1-405B (77.6) by 17.5 points and Tulu2-70B (84.5) by 10.6 points. Among non-Llama architectures, RAMO leads GPT-4 (87.6) by 7.5 points, Claude3.5 (81.6) by 13.5 points, and Gemini1.5 (74.0) by 21.1 points. The only sub-task where RAMO is not the top scorer is XTest Should Respond (93.2 vs. QRM's 97.2), though it remains competitive.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the rule-based preference annotation paradigm established by Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022b) and multi-attribute reward modeling (SteerLM, HelpSteer2) by replacing fixed or randomly-sampled rule sets with a dynamic, data-adaptive selection mechanism. The Bradley-Terry formulation for reward model training is standard, and the DPP-based rule deduplication follows Li et al. (2024), but the key departure is the max-discrepancy criterion for selecting which rules to apply per sample, backed by a mutual information maximization theorem via Jensen-Shannon divergence. The Rule Adapter (a 3B multi-label classifier) operationalizes this selection, distinguishing the approach from prior work that either applies all rules (inefficient) or a fixed subset (inflexible).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that principled, data-adaptive rule selection with only 1K synthetic training samples can push an 8B reward model to the top of the RewardBench safety leaderboard, outperforming models up to 304B parameters. This suggests that annotation quality (via smart rule selection) can partially substitute for model scale in reward modeling, with practical implications for resource-constrained safety alignment. However, the framework is currently confined to the safety domain with a fixed rule budget (r=5), and the theoretical guarantee relies on a conditional independence assumption across rules that may not hold in practice. Extending to flexible rule counts, other alignment domains (reasoning, helpfulness), and validating the mutual information bound empirically remain open.
