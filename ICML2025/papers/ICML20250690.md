## Problem

Distributional Compositional Generalization (DCG) requires models to recombine knowledge from previously learned distributions to handle novel combinations, yet the statistical properties and constraints governing this process remain largely unexplored. Prior work has examined DCG through identifiability and group invariance lenses, but no unified statistical framework exists to answer whether methods transfer across DCG problems or what properties determine a learning algorithm's capacity for knowledge composition.

## Input / Output

The experimental setup uses compositional text sequences of the form 'e1, e2, r1, r2, r3, #', where e1 and e2 are drawn from two 1000-element word sets partitioned into 500-element subsets, and r1–r3 are random filler words simulating noise. The model must complete the sequence according to a hidden composition rule (e.g., (e1,e2)→e1,e2,e1,e2,e1,e1) from 64 possible rules. The theoretical outputs are a generalization error bound decomposed into IID and CG components, and an invariant μ-measure that is constant across learning algorithms, enabling trade-off analysis across tasks.

## Method

The paper introduces two main theoretical tools. First, an invariant μ-measure defined as Q^{(A,β)}(T̃=T | P_S^(T)) / α_{A,β}(T, P_S^(T)), where α is constructed via Bayes' rule to ensure the measure is identical for all learning algorithms; this reveals that non-trade-off improvements across DCG tasks require the learning algorithm's adaptivity, quantified by the mutual information I_{A,β}(T̃=T; P_S^(T)). Second, a generalization bound that decouples the IID error (bounded by any standard PAC bound) from the CG error, yielding |E[err(P_U, f_{D_n}) − err(D_n, f_{D_n})]| ≤ GenIID + Φ_n(I_A(f_S; T | P_S^(T))) + τ(T, A), where Φ_n involves a Wasserstein-to-TV-to-KL chain and τ(T,A) measures incompatibility between the algorithm and composition rule. The proof leverages the discrete metric on function space, L-boundedness of the error, and a bijection between composition rules and target distributions (Assumption 5.5). Empirically, GPT-2 models (4-layer/128-dim and 6-layer/256-dim) pretrained on different rule subsets validate that higher adaptivity (mutual information) correlates with better average compositional accuracy (GACC), and the proposed bound is tighter than Ben-David et al. (2010) across rule complexities 6–12.

## Results

The paper's primary quantitative result is a generalization bound for DCG tasks, evaluated across rule complexities of 6, 8, 10, and 12. The proposed bound (Ours) tracks the actual CG Error closely, with gaps of only 0.048–0.030 (e.g., bound 0.271 vs. actual 0.223 at complexity 6; bound 0.372 vs. actual 0.342 at complexity 12). In contrast, the Ben-David et al. (2010) bound is substantially looser, exceeding the actual error by 0.348–0.399 across all complexities. Additionally, Table 1 verifies the theoretical link between adaptivity (measured by I_{A,β}(T̃=T; P_S)) and average task performance (GACC), showing a positive correlation from 0.073→0.564 in mutual information corresponding to GACC rising from ~0.60 to ~0.78.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its generalization bound against the out-of-distribution bound from Ben-David et al. (2010), which is the standard reference for domain-shift generalization. The proposed bound is consistently tighter across all four rule complexities tested: at complexity 6, the paper's bound is 0.271 versus Ben-David's 0.622 (a 2.3× improvement in tightness), and at complexity 12, it is 0.372 versus 0.690 (1.9× tighter). The paper's bound also correctly captures the increasing trend of CG Error with rule complexity, whereas Ben-David's bound plateaus (0.701 at complexity 10, then drops to 0.690 at complexity 12), failing to reflect the true difficulty progression. The authors note in Appendix B.4 that the relative tightness depends on whether the problem is dominated by the learning algorithm or the function space: in learning-algorithm-dominated settings, the paper's bound is substantially better, while in function-space-dominated settings with small support sets, Ben-David's bound can be competitive.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the out-of-distribution generalization framework of Ben-David et al. (2010) to the specific setting of Distributional Compositional Generalization, where the distribution shift is structured by a known composition rule. It departs from prior DCG analyses (identifiability, group invariance, disentanglement) by adopting a purely statistical lens—mutual information, Wasserstein distance, and total variation—rather than architectural or representational assumptions. The invariant μ-measure draws conceptual parallels to No Free Lunch theorems but is scoped specifically to DCG composition rules and explicitly characterizes non-trade-off improvements, which NFL does not address.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The decoupling of IID generalization error from compositional generalization error provides a reusable analytical template that lets practitioners plug in any standard IID bound (VC, Rademacher, stability) and focus only on the DCG-specific term. The finding that non-trade-off improvement across tasks requires data adaptivity (quantified via mutual information) offers a concrete, if abstract, criterion for data-centric DCG design. However, the experiments are limited to toy sequence-completion tasks with small GPT-2 models, and the invariant measure, while elegant, is constructed to be invariant by design, limiting its practical diagnostic power. Open questions include whether the compatibility term τ(T, A) can be estimated or controlled in practice, and whether the framework extends beyond two-component compositions.
