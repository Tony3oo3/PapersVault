## Problem

While in-context learning (ICL) has been extensively studied, the phenomenon of LLMs performing multiple computationally distinct ICL tasks simultaneously within a single inference call—termed 'task superposition'—had not been thoroughly investigated. The paper asks whether this is a genuine capability or merely prediction noise, whether it emerges when models are trained on one task at a time, and whether transformers possess the theoretical expressivity to support it.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252755/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The input is a text prompt that deliberately interleaves in-context examples from K different tasks (e.g., 20 randomly ordered examples each from numerical addition, English addition, French addition, and Spanish addition) followed by a single query. Rather than expecting a single answer, the output is analyzed by computing the joint token-sequence probability for each task's correct answer on the query, yielding a probability vector over tasks. The key diagnostic is whether this vector assigns non-negligible mass to multiple task answers and whether it approximates the in-context task example distribution (e.g., uniform 0.25 per task for K=4).

## Method

The paper combines empirical, theoretical, and mechanistic analyses. Empirically, it tests pretrained LLMs (GPT-3.5, Llama-3 70B, Qwen-2.5 72B) with mixed-task prompts and trains a small GPT-2 from scratch on one task at a time, showing superposition emerges without ever seeing multi-task prompts. Theoretically, it constructs a 7-layer transformer with O(d + log(mn)) embedding dimension and K attention heads that executes K tasks in parallel via independent head streams, identifies each task by computing L1-norm differences between task predictions and in-context labels, averages the resulting indicator vectors to obtain task proportions, and weights each task's output accordingly. Mechanistically, it extracts task vectors (Hendel et al., 2023) for individual tasks and shows that the model's internal representation during superposition lies on the convex hull of individual task vectors, and that patching convex combinations of task vectors into the model reproduces the superposition effect. Finally, scaling experiments on the Qwen-2.5 family (0.5B–14B) demonstrate that larger models solve more tasks in parallel and better calibrate their output distribution to the in-context task example distribution.

## Results

Across GPT-3.5, Llama-3 70B, and Qwen-2.5 72B, all models assign non-negligible probability mass to correct answers of multiple tasks simultaneously when prompted with mixed in-context examples (Figure 2). In the accuracy comparison (Table 2), moving from single-task (K=1) to four-task (K=4) prompts causes moderate accuracy drops: e.g., GPT-3.5 drops from 100% to 97.3% on addition, 99.7% to 90.7% on English addition, 90% to 80% on French addition, and 95% to 80.3% on Spanish addition. A from-scratch 14M-parameter GPT-2 trained on one task at a time generalizes to task superposition, producing approximately [0.5, 0.5] output probabilities for a 50/50 mixture of two retrieval tasks. Within the Qwen-2.5 family, larger models (7B, 14B) achieve higher r (number of tasks with correct answers in top-K outputs) and lower KL-divergence to the in-context task distribution, confirming better calibration with scale.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing methods in the traditional sense; instead, it establishes task superposition as a new phenomenon and characterizes it across multiple dimensions. The primary comparison is between single-task (K=1) and multi-task (K>1) prompting: all three models show accuracy degradation when tasks are mixed, with Llama-3 70B being most robust (drops of 0–6%) and Qwen-2.5 72B showing the largest drop on Spanish addition (−33%). A second comparison is between task vector interpolation (patching convex combinations of individual task vectors) and actual mixed in-context prompts: both produce task superposition, but vector interpolation yields higher 'other' (irrelevant) output probabilities and a less linear relationship between mixture ratio λ and output probabilities, indicating it is sufficient but not a complete explanation. A third comparison is across model scale within the Qwen-2.5 family (0.5B to 14B): larger models consistently achieve higher r values (more tasks solved simultaneously) and lower KL-divergence, demonstrating that superposition capability and calibration improve with scale. Finally, the from-scratch GPT-2 experiment shows that superposition emerges without explicit multi-task training, contrasting with the Bai et al. (2023b) framework which requires training on all algorithms simultaneously.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the in-context learning literature by introducing a new experimental setting where a single prompt contains examples from multiple distinct tasks, departing from the standard assumption that all in-context examples belong to one task. It builds on the task vector framework of Hendel et al. (2023) to analyze internal mechanisms of superposition, and leverages the ReLU approximation results from Bai et al. (2023b) to construct a theoretical proof that transformers have the expressivity to perform task superposition. The authors explicitly distinguish their 'task superposition' from feature superposition (Elhage et al., 2022), model superposition (Cheung et al., 2019), and data multiplexing (Shen et al., 2024a), positioning it as a phenomenon most apparent in the final output distribution rather than in internal representations or parameter sharing.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that task superposition emerges even when models are trained on one task at a time, and that it improves with model scale, provides concrete evidence for the 'LLMs as superposition of simulators' hypothesis and suggests that pretraining implicitly encodes a rich multi-task capability. However, the practical utility is currently limited by 'generation collapse,' where autoregressive decoding converges to a single task after the first token, making it difficult to actually extract multiple task outputs. Key open questions include developing decoding strategies that maintain the multi-task state throughout generation (as attempted by Shen et al., 2024b), understanding why convex combinations of task vectors only partially reproduce the effect, and whether this capability can be harnessed for efficient multi-task inference in practice.
