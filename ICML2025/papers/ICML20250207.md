## Problem

Existing causal discovery methods for time series either assume causal graphs are static over time or restrict relationships to linear patterns, making them inadequate for systems like the brain that exhibit nonlinear, state-dependent interactions. This paper addresses the gap by proposing a method that simultaneously captures both nonlinearity and temporal dynamics in causal structure, enabling hypothesis generation for dynamic causal graphs without restrictive linearity or stationarity assumptions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250207/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes regularly sampled multi-channel scalar time series (n_c channels, T time steps) as input, with each factor receiving a window of τ_in past time steps and the state model receiving an extended context of τ_in + τ_cl steps. Optionally, per-time-step behavioral state labels (B-dimensional) are provided as supervised targets. The output is a set of n_k factor adjacency matrices (each n_c × n_c × τ_in, capturing time-lagged Granger causal weights) combined with a time-varying coefficient vector α per time step that determines each factor's contribution, plus predicted behavioral labels when supervised.

## Method

REDCLIFF-S models the dynamic system as a conditionally weighted superposition of n_k static nonlinear causal graphs, where each factor is a cMLP (component-wise MLP) that learns Granger causal relationships via time series forecasting. A separate state model g_θ, trained on an extended historical window, generates time-varying factor scores that are broadcast-multiplied against factor outputs to produce the composite forecast. Training uses a composite objective combining MSE forecasting loss, log-weighted L1 sparsity on time-lagged adjacency matrices, a cosine similarity penalty to encourage factor dissociation, L1 sparsity on factor scores, and (in the supervised variant) MSE on behavioral label predictions. The training procedure follows a three-phase strategy: pre-train the state model, freeze it while acclimating the generative factors, then jointly optimize all parameters. The key architectural insight is that the state model's role as a noisy classifier of which causal regime is active requires more historical context than the generative factors need for forecasting, motivating the asymmetric context window design.

## Results

On the Synthetic Systems benchmark, REDCLIFF-S achieves average optimal f1-score improvements of roughly 22–28% over all baselines, with individual improvements reaching well over 60% (e.g., 0.797 vs. 0.544 ROC-AUC on the 6-2-2 system). On the D4IC HSNR dataset, REDCLIFF-S attains the highest average optimal f1-score among all methods tested. In the multi-metric supervised comparison (Table 2), REDCLIFF-S achieves the best mean comparative placement (2.0/5) across F1, ROC-AUC, AID, and SHD, with a ROC-AUC of 0.705±0.022 and Parent AID (upper) of 3.9±0.607.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the single-factor neural baselines (cMLP, cLSTM from Tank et al. 2021; DGCNN from Song et al. 2018), REDCLIFF-S consistently wins on both f1-score and ROC-AUC across all Synthetic Systems configurations (e.g., 0.797 vs. 0.544/0.612/0.556 ROC-AUC on 6-2-2), with the single-factor models appearing to learn an 'average factor graph' that is inaccurate for any individual state. Against dCSFA-NMF (Talbot et al. 2023), REDCLIFF-S achieves 0.743 vs. 0.557 ROC-AUC on the 12-11-5 system. In the supervised multi-metric comparison (Table 2), REDCLIFF-S outperforms LASAR, QRBS, and SLARAC (Weichwald et al. 2020) on every metric except SLARAC's Parent AID (lower), where SLARAC's 0.7±0.179 is lower than REDCLIFF-S's 3.1±0.727. Against Regime-PCMCI (Saggioro et al. 2020), the closest competitor, REDCLIFF-S wins on ROC-AUC (0.705 vs. 0.673) and both AID metrics (3.9 vs. 6.1 upper; 3.1 vs. 4.8 lower), while Regime-PCMCI edges ahead on optimal F1 (0.407 vs. 0.383) and SHD (9.1 vs. 10.9 upper). On the D4IC HSNR dataset, REDCLIFF-S achieves the highest average optimal f1-score, outperforming DYNOTEARS (Pamfil et al. 2020) and NAVAR (Bussmann et al. 2021), though the margin is modest since most D4IC factors are low-complexity where REDCLIFF-S's advantage is smallest.

## Relation to Prior Work

REDCLIFF-S sits at the intersection of two prior lines of work: (1) factor-model approaches to dynamic causal discovery (Mague et al., 2022; Gallagher et al., 2021; Talbot et al., 2023), which capture time-varying graph structures but are restricted to linear VAR-based relationships, and (2) neural-network-based nonlinear Granger causal discovery (Tank et al., 2021; Song et al., 2018), which captures nonlinear edges but assumes static graphs. The paper's core architectural contribution is wrapping nonlinear cMLP factors inside a conditionally-weighted superposition framework (informed by State-Dependent Causal Inference, Balsells-Rodas et al., 2022), so that each factor learns a distinct nonlinear causal graph whose activation is modulated by a separate state model. This departs from Regime-PCMCI (Saggioro et al., 2020), the closest prior method for regime-dependent causal discovery, by replacing its linear regime-switching with learned nonlinear factors and by incorporating supervised behavioral labels through invertible transformations of factor scores.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine methodological gap: brain systems exhibit nonlinear, state-dependent causal interactions, yet existing ML tools force a choice between linearity (factor models, VARs) and staticity (neural Granger methods). The identifiability analysis in Appendix A.1 is a valuable and honest theoretical contribution, showing that even a 2-node system with ReLU/nReLU edges is fundamentally non-identifiable, which tempers expectations for the entire task. Practical limitations include expensive hyperparameter tuning (only one repeat used for grid search), the absence of a true nonlinear DeltaCon0 metric for evaluating graph similarity, and the reliance on cosine similarity as a proxy. The TST case study's alignment with independently published findings on the lateral habenula and anxiety networks provides encouraging external validation, but a single real dataset limits generalizability claims.
