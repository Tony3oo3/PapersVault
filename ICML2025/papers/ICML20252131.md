## Problem

Deep Ensembles are widely deployed to boost predictive performance, yet their impact on algorithmic fairness—how model accuracy varies across groups defined by protected attributes such as age, gender, or race—remains largely unexplored. This paper identifies and characterizes the 'disparate benefits effect,' where ensembling unevenly favors different protected groups, often worsening fairness violations for already disadvantaged groups while improving overall accuracy.

## Input / Output

The models consume facial images (FairFace, UTKFace) or chest X-ray images (CheXpert) and produce binary class predictions (e.g., young/old, male/female, white/non-white) with calibrated predictive probability distributions. Protected group attributes partition the test set for computing per-group fairness metrics (SPD, EOD, AOD), and the key analytical output is the trajectory of performance and fairness violations as ensemble size grows from 1 to 10 members, revealing which groups gain or lose from ensembling.

## Method

The paper trains 1,000 individual DNNs (ResNet18/34/50, RegNet-Y 800MF, EfficientNetV2-S) across 15 binary classification tasks on three vision datasets, then forms Deep Ensembles by averaging the predictive distributions of independently trained members. The authors identify per-group differences in average predictive diversity (DIV) as the key driver of the disparate benefits effect, validated through controlled synthetic experiments on FashionMNIST where input construction directly manipulates diversity levels. For mitigation, they demonstrate that Deep Ensembles are better calibrated (lower ECE) than individual members, making them more sensitive to prediction thresholds, and show that Hardt Post-Processing—group-dependent threshold optimization applied to the ensemble's predictive distribution—effectively restores fairness to individual-member levels while preserving the ensemble's performance gains.

## Results

Across 15 tasks on FairFace, UTKFace, and CheXpert, Deep Ensembles (10 members) consistently improve performance over individual members (ΔAccuracy +0.009 to +0.022 on facial datasets, +0.005 AUROC on CheXpert), but fairness violations (SPD, EOD, AOD) increase in 4 out of 6 target/group combinations on FairFace and 4 out of 6 on UTKFace. The largest fairness degradation occurs for the age/gender task on FairFace (ΔSPD +0.022, ΔEOD +0.017, ΔAOD +0.017) and UTKFace (ΔSPD +0.017, ΔEOD +0.015, ΔAOD +0.012). After applying Hardt Post-Processing (HPP) to the Deep Ensemble, fairness violations are reduced to the level of individual members (e.g., SPD drops from 0.194 to 0.176 on FF age/gender) while accuracy is maintained or slightly improved (0.816 → 0.818).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare different ensemble methods or fairness interventions against each other; instead, it compares the Deep Ensemble (10 members) against the average individual ensemble member as the baseline. The Deep Ensemble always wins on performance (accuracy or AUROC) across all 15 tasks, with gains of 0.9–2.2 percentage points on facial datasets and 0.5 percentage points on CheXpert. However, the Deep Ensemble loses on fairness in a majority of tasks where individual members already exhibit substantial violations: for example, on FF age/gender the ensemble's SPD worsens from 0.173 to 0.190 and EOD from 0.153 to 0.165, while on UTK age/gender SPD worsens from 0.296 to 0.309. In tasks where individual members are already relatively fair (e.g., FF gender/race, FF race/gender), the ensemble either matches or slightly improves fairness. The paper further shows that Hardt Post-Processing applied to the Deep Ensemble recovers the fairness level of individual members (e.g., SPD 0.176 vs. 0.173 for members on FF age/gender) while retaining the ensemble's accuracy advantage (0.818 vs. 0.794), making it a Pareto improvement over both the unmitigated ensemble and the individual members.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of Deep Ensembles (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017) and algorithmic fairness, building on the predictive diversity framework of Jeffares et al. (2023) to explain why ensembling benefits groups unevenly. It extends the ensemble fairness literature, which has focused on shallow models and model multiplicity, to the Deep Ensemble setting with protected-attribute-based group definitions. The closest prior work, Ko et al. (2023), studied Deep Ensembles and subgroup performance but defined groups as subsets of the target space rather than protected attributes and did not consider established group fairness metrics. For mitigation, the paper repurposes the classical Hardt post-processing method (Hardt et al., 2016), which had not previously been applied to Deep Ensembles, leveraging their superior calibration as the key enabling property.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that Deep Ensembles—a widely adopted, 'safe' performance boost—can systematically decrease group fairness challenges a common practitioner assumption and has direct implications for high-stakes deployments in healthcare, finance, and law. The predictive diversity explanation provides a mechanistic account that could inform future ensemble design, while the HPP mitigation offers a practical, low-cost remedy that preserves ensemble performance gains. Limitations include the focus on vision tasks and convolutional architectures, the use of only three group fairness metrics, and the absence of analysis under prior in-processing fairness interventions. Open questions include whether the effect persists in language models, how it interacts with individual fairness notions, and whether ensemble diversity can be explicitly controlled per group at training time.
