## Problem

Label distribution learning (LDL) represents instances solely by relative label proportions, ignoring the absolute intensity of each label. This causes fundamentally different instances (e.g., two faces with identical emotion proportions but vastly different emotional intensity) to receive identical label distributions, losing critical information. The paper introduces 'background concentration'—the description degree of the complementary set of all known labels—to recover this missing absolute information and form a more complete representation called the concentration distribution.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252334/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes standard feature vectors from existing LDL datasets (gene expression profiles, facial image features, scene descriptors) where the last class of the ground-truth label distribution is treated as the hidden background concentration during training. It outputs a concentration distribution vector c_d = [b, μ] ∈ R^{c+1}, where b ∈ R^c is the real label distribution and μ > 0 is the scalar background concentration, satisfying Σb_i + μ = 1. The network replaces the conventional softmax layer with a RELU activation to produce non-negative confidence values, which are then analytically decomposed into b and μ via closed-form expressions.

## Method

CDL-LD uses a neural network with RELU output to produce a dataset-side confidence vector e, then models the apparent label distribution p as Dirichlet(α) with α_i = e_i + 1. By equating the Dirichlet expectation E[p_i] = (e_i+1)/(Σe_j + c) with the decomposition E[p_i] = b_i + μ/c, the method derives closed-form expressions b_i = e_i/(Σe_j + c) and μ = c/(Σe_j + c), linking the network output directly to the concentration distribution. Training uses an adjusted MSE loss (AMSE) that integrates ||y − p||² over the Dirichlet distribution, decomposing into a prediction-error term and a variance-reduction term that jointly minimize both the point estimate error and the uncertainty of the sampled distribution. The paper further provides a Rademacher complexity-based generalization bound proving the model is learnable directly from LDL datasets, and constructs the first real-world CDL dataset (SJA_c) by computing background concentration from the raw 5-level emotion ratings in SJAFFE.

## Results

CDL-LD achieves the best average rank across all four evaluation metrics on 12 datasets: 1.00 (Clark), 1.00 (KL), 1.50 (Cosine), and 1.67 (Chebyshev), ranking 1st in 45 out of 48 individual dataset-metric comparisons (93.75%). On the newly constructed concentration distribution dataset SJA_c, CDL-LD reports Chebyshev 0.1393, Clark 0.5336, KL 0.0722, and Cosine 0.9740, substantially outperforming the best baseline (LCLR: 0.2504 / 0.7821 / 0.4076 / 0.8434). The Friedman test rejects the null hypothesis of equal performance at the 0.05 level for all four metrics (F_F = 22.26–43.94 vs. critical value 2.913), and the Bonferroni-Dunn post-hoc test confirms CDL-LD is significantly superior to every baseline on Clark and Cosine.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CDL-LD outperforms all six baselines (LDLLC, SA-IIS, LCLR, LDLFs, LDLLDM, DLDL) in 45 of 48 dataset-metric comparisons. On the purpose-built CDL dataset SJA_c, the gap is largest: CDL-LD's KL of 0.0722 is roughly 2.8× lower than the next-best SA-IIS (0.2021), and its Cosine similarity of 0.9740 exceeds the runner-up SA-IIS (0.8824) by over 9 points. On the original LDL dataset SJAFFE, CDL-LD is competitive on Clark (0.9992), KL (0.4841), and Cosine (0.8161) but trails LCLR on Chebyshev (0.4335 vs. 0.3086), reflecting the difficulty of recovering background concentration from a dataset not originally designed for it. On SBU_3DFE, CDL-LD leads on Clark (0.9682) and KL (0.4641) but is outperformed by LCLR on Chebyshev (0.3362 vs. 0.2972) and Cosine (0.6310 vs. 0.7578). The Bonferroni-Dunn test at α=0.05 (CD=2.3265) confirms that CDL-LD's advantage over every baseline is statistically significant on Clark and Cosine, while on Chebyshev and KL the significance is less uniform, with LCLR and LDLLDM occasionally falling within one critical difference.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Label Distribution Learning (LDL) paradigm introduced by Geng (2016), which replaces binary multi-label assignments with real-valued description degrees summing to one. The key conceptual departure is the introduction of 'background concentration'—the description degree of the complementary set of all labels in the label space—transforming LDs into richer 'concentration distributions' (CDs). Methodologically, the CDL-LD model combines a Dirichlet distribution framework (where the neural network outputs confidence parameters via RELU rather than softmax) with an adjusted MSE loss that jointly minimizes prediction error and Dirichlet variance. The theoretical contribution provides a Rademacher complexity-based generalization bound proving that CDL is learnable directly from standard LDL datasets, a result not previously established.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The background concentration concept addresses a genuine representational gap: instances with identical LDs can differ substantially in absolute intensity (e.g., two faces with the same emotion proportions but different overall emotional strength). The construction of SJA_c as the first CDL dataset and the theoretical learnability proof give the paradigm some grounding, but the practical impact remains limited by the small dataset sizes, the single true CDL benchmark, and the somewhat ad hoc assumption that background concentration spreads uniformly across classes. Open questions include whether the uniform-spreading assumption (Eq. 7) holds in practice, how the method scales to high-dimensional label spaces, and whether the recovered background concentration is genuinely informative for downstream tasks beyond the demonstrated emotion-recognition example.
