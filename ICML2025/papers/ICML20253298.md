## Problem

A prevalent claim in the machine learning literature holds that causal inference questions (interventional and counterfactual) cannot be answered with standard probabilistic modelling tools and require bespoke causal frameworks such as the do-operator, do-calculus, or Structural Causal Models. This paper argues that this perceived insufficiency stems from a semantic confusion where 'statistical' is used too narrowly to mean only associational modelling of the observed distribution, and that the full probabilistic modelling framework is in fact sufficient for all causal inference tasks.

## Input / Output

The method takes an observational dataset (e.g., N samples of (Z, T, Y)), a Bayesian Network specification over the observed variables, and a query specifying an intervention (e.g., assign dose t*) or a counterfactual scenario (e.g., what if the same individual had taken a different dose). The practitioner must additionally specify which generative mechanisms are shared between the observed and hypothetical settings and which are altered. The output is computed via standard marginalisation and conditioning on the joint: for interventions, an expected effect estimate E[Y*|D]; for counterfactuals, a full conditional distribution p(y*|t, y, D) over the hypothetical outcome given individual-level observations.

## Method

The core contribution is the 'twin model approach': define a joint distribution over both the observed world (Z, T, Y) and the intervened-upon or counterfactual world (Z*, T*, Y*) by explicitly specifying which conditionals are shared (e.g., p_θ(y|t,z) = q_θ(y|t,z), reflecting unchanged physical mechanisms) and which are altered (e.g., q(t) = δ(t*−t), reflecting the intervention). For counterfactuals, individual-level variables (e.g., headache severity Z) are shared across settings rather than group-level variables, and the posterior over shared variables conditioned on observed data serves as the prior in the counterfactual world. The paper then demonstrates that Pearl's intervention operation, do-notation, and the SCM counterfactual construction (Def. 3.2) are all recoverable as syntactic shorthands or special cases of this joint model, showing that the do-calculus and adjustment criteria are convenience shortcuts built on top of standard probability theory rather than a separate mathematical framework.

## Results

This is a position paper with no empirical experiments or quantitative benchmarks. The key analytical results are: (1) the naive conditional expectation E[Y|T] = exp((a(b−c)σ_Z² − cσ_T²)/(σ_Z² + c²σ_T² + σ_Y²) · (T − aμ_Z) + const.) incorrectly shows headache duration increasing with aspirin dose even when c=0 (Simpson's paradox), while the correct interventional expectation E[Y*|θ] = (t*)^(−c) · exp(bμ_Z + (b²σ_Z² + σ_Y²)/2) correctly isolates the causal effect to parameter c alone. (2) The counterfactual query p(y*|t,y,D) is shown to reduce to a standard posterior-weighted integral over the shared latent Z, requiring no causal-specific machinery. (3) The paper formally demonstrates that the do-operator, intervention operation (Def. 2.1), and SCM counterfactual construction (Def. 3.2) are all special cases of writing down a joint distribution over multiple settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work contains no empirical baselines or head-to-head method comparisons. Instead, the 'comparison' is conceptual: the paper shows that the probabilistic twin-model approach (writing a joint over observed and intervened/counterfactual settings) is mathematically equivalent to the standard causal frameworks. Specifically, the do-calculus and intervention operation (Def. 2.1) are recast as syntactic shorthand for the same joint model, and Structural Causal Models (Def. 3.1–3.2) are shown to be a restricted subclass of Bayesian Networks on X∪N where all noise variables are shared. The paper argues the probabilistic approach is strictly more general because it does not require sharing all noise variables (Section 3.1) and can handle settings expressible in probabilistic programming but not in graphical models (Section 4.1). No method 'wins' or 'loses' — the claim is one of sufficiency and equivalence, not superiority in performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper reinterprets the entire causal inference toolkit (do-operator, do-calculus, Structural Causal Models) as syntactic shorthands for specifying joint distributions within standard probabilistic modelling, building directly on the twin-network construction of Balke & Pearl (1994) and the Bayesian Network formalism. It engages explicitly with the Pearl–Gelman debate over whether causal questions require tools beyond probability theory, arguing the disagreement is primarily semantic: the term 'statistical' has been narrowed to mean 'associational/observational' only. The paper also situates itself within the growing identifiability literature in deep learning (Locatello et al., von Kügelgen et al., Hyvärinen et al.), noting that those works already operate in a purely probabilistic framework while being motivated by causal concerns.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By demonstrating that interventional and counterfactual questions reduce to writing down a joint distribution over all settings of interest, the paper lowers the barrier to entry for ML practitioners who are already fluent in probabilistic modelling but intimidated by causal-specific notation. The authors acknowledge significant open challenges: the 'devil is in the details' of specifying correct independence assumptions, the Markov equivalence class problem when using the do-operator shorthand, and the contextual question of which latent variables to share in counterfactual settings. A key limitation is that the paper is a position paper with toy examples rather than a comprehensive formal proof of equivalence, and it does not address computational scalability of the joint-model approach for complex causal graphs.
