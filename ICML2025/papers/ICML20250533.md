## Problem

Standard DP-SGD privacy accounting treats all randomness in training as noise injection, ignoring the structured randomness already present in techniques like model parallelism, dropout, and non-i.i.d. subsampling. This paper asks whether the hidden randomness in which submodel or iteration a sample touches can be formally quantified as additional privacy amplification, reducing the noise required for a given (ε,δ)-DP guarantee.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250533/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a training dataset, a model architecture, a target privacy budget (ε,δ), and a training configuration specifying the number of submodels d (or total iterations T), per-sample participation count k, gradient clipping norm c, and noise variance σ². It outputs a numerical RDP bound via Theorem 3.1's closed-form expression, which is then converted to (ε,δ)-DP to determine the minimum noise standard deviation needed. The random assignment structure (which submodel each sample receives, or which iterations each sample participates in) must remain hidden from the adversary for the amplification to hold.

## Method

The core result (Theorem 3.1) bounds the Rényi divergence between a uniform mixture of Gaussians centered at binary vectors in S_{d,k} (representing the noisy gradient when a sample is used with a random submodel/iteration assignment) and a single zero-mean Gaussian (representing the gradient when the sample is absent). This mixture-of-Gaussians structure is fundamentally different from the i.i.d. subsampling structure handled by prior privacy amplification results, requiring new tools: Lemma 3.13 exploits symmetry of the mixture centers to reduce computation from O(d^{kα}) to O(dk), and Lemma 3.15 decomposes the reverse divergence into a tractable Gaussian-vs-Gaussian term plus a covariance-mismatch term. The theorem is then instantiated for disjoint model splitting (k=1, d submodels), partial splitting with shared layers, dropout as implicit 2-way model parallelism, and Balanced Iteration Subsampling (each sample used in exactly k of T iterations), showing that all these structured-randomness sources yield tighter privacy bounds than standard DP-SGD accounting or Poisson subsampling.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with ResNet-101 under (8, 10⁻⁵)-DP, the paper's privacy amplification via model splitting improves validation accuracy by 2.6–3.7% over standard DP-SGD accounting while using less noise. In the centralized setting with 3 submodels, amplification achieves 82.43% accuracy (noise σ=5.44×10⁻⁴) versus 79.80% for the baseline (σ=6.62×10⁻⁴); with 8 submodels, the gap widens to 80.52% vs 76.80%. In the federated setting, amplification yields 80.28% (3 submodels) and 79.11% (8 submodels) versus 78.47% and 76.96% for the baseline. Balanced Iteration Subsampling matches Poisson Subsampling within <1% noise difference (e.g., σ=10.17 vs 10.20 for WideResNet-40-4 from scratch).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against standard DP-SGD RDP accounting (Abadi et al., 2016), the paper's model-parallelism amplification consistently wins: with 3 submodels it gains +2.6% accuracy (centralized) and +1.8% (federated) while reducing noise by ~18–19%; with 8 submodels the gains grow to +3.7% (centralized) and +2.2% (federated) with ~25% less noise. The amplification method narrows the gap to the full-model relaxation baseline (no splitting) from ~5% to ~2.5% accuracy, making model splitting a more practical choice under compute constraints. Against Poisson Subsampling (Zhu & Wang, 2019), Balanced Iteration Subsampling is essentially tied: noise standard deviations differ by less than 1% (σ=10.17 vs 10.20 for WideResNet-40-4; σ=2.36 vs 2.34 for ResNet-101 finetuning), and validation accuracies are within 0.13%. The paper's theoretical analysis shows BIS is always at least as private as Poisson Subsampling under the tighter bound (2), with a larger margin when T is small (e.g., T=10), where Poisson's variance in per-sample usage causes a sharp RDP spike at large α.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the privacy amplification literature—which has focused on i.i.d. subsampling, shuffling, and random gradient compression—to a new and previously unstudied source of randomness: model partitioning (splitting, dropout). It builds on DP-SGD as the base mechanism, Rényi DP as the analytical framework, and generalizes random check-ins (Balle et al., 2020) into Balanced Iteration Subsampling. The key departure is that the randomness in model parallelism is structured (each sample updates only a random subnetwork) and cannot be reduced to simple masking of full gradients, so prior amplification analyses do not apply. Theorem 3.1 provides a unified bound for both model and data partitioning, revealing they share the same underlying mathematical structure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that model parallelism techniques already deployed in federated learning for compute and memory efficiency now come with a quantifiable privacy bonus 'for free,' reducing the noise needed for a given (ε,δ)-DP guarantee and thus improving utility. Balanced Iteration Subsampling offers a deployment-friendly alternative to Poisson subsampling with guaranteed equitable participation, addressing operational concerns in federated systems. Limitations include the bounds being designed for numerical evaluation rather than closed-form interpretation, experiments restricted to CIFAR-10 with ResNet-101/WideResNet-40-4, and the open question of how model and data partitioning interact when applied simultaneously. Future work could tighten the bounds (Remark 3.6), extend to overlapping subnetworks with shared parameters, and validate on larger-scale tasks.
