## Problem

Preference learning lacks the unifying conceptual foundation that tasks like classification enjoy through maximum likelihood, resulting in a fragmented landscape of algorithms and evaluations without clear guiding principles. This paper asks what underlying principles actually ground preference learning and proves that win rate is the only evaluation of a generative model that respects both preferences and prevalences in the pairwise preference data distribution.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20253046/cropped_page6_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework operates on pairwise preference data consisting of a text query x, two candidate responses y0 and y1 (typically generated by the same or different models), and a binary preference label indicating which response is preferred. The output is a finetuned generative language model evaluated by its h-win rate against a chosen anchor distribution under the query-preference environment, where h is any strictly increasing transformation of the preference probability. The paper experiments with Pythia-2.8b on OpenAssistant and Anthropic HH datasets, using an oracle judge model for both relabeling preferences and evaluating final win rates.

## Method

The paper establishes a win rate-centric framework by first proving (Proposition 3.3) that the only evaluation satisfying both preference-consistency and prevalence-consistency is h-win rate, then partitioning all preference learning methods into WRO (directly optimizing win rate, e.g., RLHF, NLHF, and generalized variants with arbitrary h and anchor) and non-WRO (e.g., DPO, SFT). WRO methods are shown to satisfy win rate-correspondence (improving the objective improves win rate) and win rate-consistency (the optimum achieves maximum possible win rate), while DPO fails correspondence due to offline data breaking prevalence-consistency and SFT additionally fails consistency because its target distribution is bounded by the initial model's preference variance (Theorem 5.1). Empirically, the paper shows that across WRO-KL variants differing in h, β, and preference classifier estimation, no design choice systematically outperforms others, but training loss correlates far more strongly with test win rate than any objective design parameter, identifying optimization success as the dominant practical factor.

## Results

On OASST, the best WRO-KL variants achieve ~66–67% win rate over the reference model (e.g., BT logit β=0.1: 66.72%, oracle identity β=0.01: 66.69%), while on HH the best variants reach ~69–70% (e.g., BT logit β=0.001: 70.46%, non-BT logit β=0.001: 69.15%). Critically, no choice of h (identity, log, logit), β (0.001–1), or preference classifier estimate (non-BT, BT, oracle) systematically outperforms all others. Training loss predicts test win rate far better than any objective design choice (Spearman p-value 8.27e-5 for loss vs. win rate, vs. p=0.968 for classifier choice and p=0.133 for β), indicating optimization success is the dominant practical factor.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares RLHF (Christiano et al., 2017; implemented as WRO-KL with h=logit under the Bradley-Terry assumption) and DPO (Rafailov et al., 2024c) against SFT on preferred samples, all evaluated by win rate over the initial model. RLHF substantially underperforms its theoretical expectations: even replacing the learned reward model with an oracle preference classifier (WRO-logit-oracle) performs worse, suggesting optimization difficulty rather than reward estimation error is the bottleneck. DPO with strong regularization (β=1) performs closer to expectations, but with weaker regularization it also underperforms, and the paper proves DPO fails win rate-correspondence (lower DPO loss does not guarantee higher win rate). SFT performance aligns with its theoretical prediction (Theorem 5.1), with win rate improvement bounded by the variance in average preference probability under the initial model. Across all 18 WRO-KL configurations in Table 1, no single method dominates: the best OASST result (66.72%, BT logit β=0.1) and best HH result (70.46%, BT logit β=0.001) come from different settings, and oracle preference classifiers do not consistently beat estimated ones.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Ψ-Preference Optimization framework of Azar et al. (2023) and Nash Learning from Human Feedback (Munos et al., 2023), but departs from both by proving that win rate is the *only* evaluation grounded in the preference data sampling distribution (Proposition 3.3), then using this result to partition all preference learning methods into WRO and non-WRO families. Rather than proposing a single new algorithm, it provides a unifying theoretical lens: RLHF and NLHF are WRO instances, while DPO and SFT are non-WRO, with the latter provably failing win rate-correspondence (and SFT additionally failing win rate-consistency). The paper also generalizes WRO beyond the specific h=logit/BT setting of RLHF to arbitrary strictly increasing h functions and anchor distributions, connecting the regularized WRO objective to blackbox variational inference.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework gives the fragmented preference learning landscape a single organizing principle (win rate) and concrete diagnostic criteria (win rate-correspondence and consistency) for evaluating any new method. The empirical finding that optimization success predicts win rate far better than any objective design choice (h, β, classifier estimation) is practically important: it suggests the field should invest in better optimization of WRO objectives rather than endlessly proposing new surrogate losses. Open questions include developing better surrogates for WRO, non-constant-sum WRO games, fully online direct alignment algorithms, and whether the optimization-dominance finding holds at larger scales. Limitations include empirical validation on a single 2.8B model and two datasets, and the theoretical results assume discrete support.
