## Problem

State-of-the-art video generation models produce visually impressive but physically inaccurate outputs, failing even at the basic task of object freefall. This paper investigates whether and how post-training can remedy this gap, and characterizes the limitations of such post-training in generalization and distributional fidelity.

## Input / Output

The task is image-to-video: a single 1:1 aspect-ratio frame showing an object suspended in midair (held by an invisible wire) is provided, optionally accompanied by a text caption like '{object description} falls.' The model generates a video of the object falling and potentially colliding with objects on the ground. Evaluation compares generated and ground-truth videos via segmentation masks (SAM 2), computing trajectory L2 (centroid distance), Chamfer Distance (shape fidelity), and IoU (object permanence) after fps alignment. Real-world test videos are 120fps slow-motion cellphone recordings; simulated test videos are 32 frames at 16fps rendered by Kubric.

## Method

The paper introduces a two-stage post-training pipeline on top of Open-Sora v1.2. Stage 1 (PSFT) fine-tunes the model on ~5k simulated dropping videos generated with Kubric (PyBullet physics + Blender rendering, GSO 3D objects), using the standard rectified flow objective; this alone is sufficient to induce realistic freefall and outperform all proprietary baselines. Stage 2 (ORO) applies reward-gradient optimization inspired by VADER but modified to compare generated video against ground truth (not just conditioning) and backpropagate through all denoising timesteps. Three interchangeable reward functions—segmentation IoU via SAM 2, optical flow L1 via RAFT, and depth L1 via Depth-Anything-V2—target shape, 2D motion, and 3D motion accuracy respectively, demonstrating modular and interpretable physical improvement. The paper further shows that pretraining is essential (random initialization fails), that only 5k samples suffice, and that the model generalizes poorly to unseen depths/heights and fails to match the ground-truth dropping-time distribution p(t|y) as measured by KS tests.

## Results

On the real-world PisaBench test set, Open-Sora+PSFT achieves L2=0.076, CD=0.188, and IoU=0.139, substantially outperforming the best proprietary model (Kling-V1.5: L2=0.155, CD=0.424, IoU=0.058) and the best open-source model (CogVideoX-5B-I2V: L2=0.138, CD=0.366, IoU=0.080) using only 5,000 simulated training samples. Adding ORO with segmentation reward further improves IoU to 0.142, while optical flow and depth rewards reduce L2 to 0.067 and CD to 0.159–0.164. On the simulated seen test set, the gap widens dramatically: PSFT achieves L2=0.036 vs. 0.112 for the best open baseline, and ORO(Flow) pushes L2 down to 0.026.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's PSFT model (fine-tuned on just 5k simulated samples) decisively outperforms all seven baselines across every metric and every test split. On the real-world set, PSFT halves the L2 error of the best proprietary model (Kling-V1.5: 0.155 vs. 0.076) and nearly doubles its IoU (0.058 vs. 0.139); the best open-source baseline CogVideoX-5B-I2V (L2=0.138, IoU=0.080) is also clearly beaten. Sora, despite being the most prominent commercial model, performs mid-pack among proprietary models (L2=0.174, IoU=0.065) and is outperformed by PSFT by a factor of ~2.3× on L2. ORO provides targeted improvements: segmentation reward boosts IoU (0.139→0.142 real, 0.165→0.170 sim-seen), while optical flow and depth rewards reduce L2 and CD (e.g., L2 drops from 0.076 to 0.067 on real, and from 0.036 to 0.026 on sim-seen), confirming that different reward functions can selectively improve different physical aspects. The advantage is most pronounced on the simulated seen set, where PSFT achieves L2=0.036 versus 0.112 for CogVideoX, a 3.1× improvement, reflecting the sim-to-real gap that limits transfer to real-world data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper draws a direct analogy between LLM post-training (SFT + reward modeling) and video generation, applying the two-stage pipeline of supervised fine-tuning followed by reward optimization to the specific problem of physical accuracy. It builds on Open-Sora as the base diffusion model and adapts the VADER reward-gradient framework (Prabhudesai et al., 2024) into Object Reward Optimization (ORO), modifying it to use ground-truth videos rather than conditioning signals and propagating gradients through all denoising steps. In the broader landscape of physics evaluation for video generation (VideoPhy, PhyGenBench, Cosmos, Physics-IQ), this work is distinctive in providing a controlled, simulation-grounded diagnostic task (freefall) with exact ground truth, rather than relying on MLM-based VQA scoring or uncontrolled scenes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that all eight evaluated SOTA models (including Sora, Kling, and Runway Gen3) fail at basic freefall—objects float, move upward, or remain static—challenges the assumption that internet-scale pretraining on video implicitly encodes physical laws. The demonstration that just 5k simulated samples of fine-tuning dramatically outperforms all baselines suggests post-training is a necessary and tractable step toward world-model capabilities. However, the paper also reveals critical open problems: the model fails to generalize to out-of-distribution depths and heights, and more fundamentally, it does not learn the correct conditional distribution p(t|y) of dropping times (50/50 KS test rejections), indicating that per-sample physical plausibility is insufficient for faithful world simulation. PisaBench provides a reusable diagnostic tool for tracking physical accuracy as video models evolve.
