## Problem

Despite superhuman performance on standard benchmarks, DNNs catastrophically fail to recognize objects when their contours are fragmented into disconnected elements—a task humans perform near-ceiling. Existing benchmarks lack the systematic control over fragmentation levels and the model-scale diversity needed to identify the specific computational mechanism (contour integration) underlying this human-model gap.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251619/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The task is a 12-alternative forced-choice object recognition test with 20 conditions: full RGB images, contour-extracted images, and 9 fragmentation levels (12%–100%) of two stimulus types—directionless phosphenes and directional segments—spanning 8×8 degrees of visual angle. Models are evaluated zero-shot by mapping ImageNet-1k labels to the 12 categories via WordNet, or with a linear decoder fit on penultimate-layer activations using 120 supervised trials. The key derived output is 'integration bias,' defined as the performance difference between the segment and phosphene conditions, which quantifies a model's sensitivity to directional alignment.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a large-scale empirical dissection of contour integration across 1,038 DNNs (13 architecture families, 18 training datasets ranging from ~500 to 5B images) and 50 human participants. They show that training dataset size (r=0.814) explains model performance far better than architecture or compute, and that integration bias scales with dataset size (r=0.594), indicating contour integration is learned from data rather than hardwired into architecture. To test causality, they train ResNet-18 models on ImageNet-1k augmented with fragmented stimuli (phosphenes, segments, or both) using a multi-task loss, finding that this induces both integration bias and a shape bias exceeding that of the Stylized-ImageNet-trained model from Geirhos et al. (2018). Finally, they demonstrate that integration bias predicts robustness to ImageNet-C perturbations (r=0.671) and ImageNet classification accuracy, establishing contour integration as a mechanistic driver of human-like object recognition.

## Results

On the 12-alternative forced-choice contour integration task (chance = 8.33%), humans achieved ~59% average accuracy across all conditions, with performance scaling log-linearly with fragment density (accuracy = 0.29·log(x) − 0.51, R²=0.73). In contrast, zero-shot DNNs averaged only ~16% (accuracy = 0.04·log(x) − 0.03, R²=0.23), and decoder-fit models averaged ~34% (accuracy = 0.09·log(x) + 0.03, R²=0.39) across 1,038 models. Training dataset size was the strongest predictor of model performance (r=0.814, p<0.001), far exceeding model compute (r=0.334) or architecture. Integration bias (segment minus phosphene performance) correlated with overall task accuracy, ImageNet-C robustness (r=0.671, p<0.001), and ImageNet top-1 accuracy (r=0.67, p<0.01).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline comparison is against the shape-bias-trained ResNet-50 from Geirhos et al. (2018), which was trained on Stylized ImageNet to increase shape bias. The paper's contour-trained ResNet-18 models (IN+Segments, IN+Phosphenes, IN+Segments+Phosphenes) all achieve higher shape bias (measured by cue conflict accuracy) than the Geirhos et al. shape-biased model, while simultaneously acquiring a meaningful integration bias. However, the Geirhos et al. shape-biased model retains a larger integration bias than the contour-trained models, and the contour-trained models suffer minor drops in full-color (RGB) ImageNet recognition compared to the IN-only baseline. GPT-4o, evaluated zero-shot, is the closest model to human performance, reaching near-human accuracy on the fragmented object task, whereas all other zero-shot DNNs (621 models) remain substantially below human levels, with most barely exceeding the 8.33% chance level.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the shape-bias and Gestalt-grouping literature (Geirhos et al., 2018; Baker et al., 2018; Bowers et al., 2023) by isolating contour integration as a specific, measurable mechanism and introducing 'integration bias' (directional-segment minus directionless-phosphene performance) as a new metric. Prior DNN contour-integration studies (Linsley et al., 2018; Funke et al., 2021) used abstract line-segment tasks with small model sets; here the authors scale to 1,000+ models across 13 architectures and 18 training datasets, and ground the task in naturalistic object recognition with 20 controlled fragmentation conditions. The Brain-Score benchmarking framework (Schrimpf et al., 2020) provides the infrastructure for zero-shot and decoder-fit model evaluation, while the BOSS dataset supplies the object imagery.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that contour integration, traditionally attributed to horizontal connectivity in V1, emerges purely from training-data scale without any architectural inductive bias—challenges a long-standing assumption in computational neuroscience and vision modeling. Practically, the paper provides the largest human-model comparison to date and a new benchmark that cleanly separates integration ability from general recognition, giving researchers a diagnostic tool for model failures. Key open questions include whether human-like training data (rich in occlusions, as in egocentric video) can close the remaining gap, and whether the trade-off observed between fragmented-stimulus performance and full-color accuracy can be resolved. Limitations include the observational (non-causal) nature of most findings, limited VLM evaluation, and the possibility of subtle data leakage despite careful stimulus synthesis.
