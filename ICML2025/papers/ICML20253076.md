## Problem

When LLM multi-agent systems fail on a task, identifying which agent and which specific step caused the failure (failure attribution) is currently a manual, labor-intensive process requiring domain expertise to parse complex conversation logs. This paper formalizes automated failure attribution as a new research problem, introducing the concept of a 'decisive error'—the earliest agent-step pair whose correction would flip the outcome from failure to success—and benchmarks how well LLMs can identify these errors automatically.

## Input / Output

The input is a real-world query (from GAIA or AssistantBench) paired with the full multi-agent failure log (a turn-based conversation of up to 130 steps across 1–5 agents), optionally augmented with the ground-truth answer and system metadata (agent names, prompts, tools). The output is a structured triple: the name of the failure-responsible agent, the integer step index of the decisive error, and a free-text explanation of why that step was the critical mistake. The decisive error is defined counterfactually: correcting that single action (and adjusting downstream actions accordingly) must change the trajectory outcome from failure to success.

## Method

The paper proposes three prompt-based LLM-as-judge strategies for failure attribution: (1) All-at-once, which feeds the entire failure log to a single LLM call and asks for agent, step, and reason; (2) Step-by-step, which processes the log incrementally, querying at each step whether an error has occurred and stopping at the first detected mistake; and (3) Binary search, which iteratively halves the log to localize the error in O(log n) calls. A hybrid variant chains all-at-once (to identify the responsible agent) with step-by-step (to pinpoint the step within that agent's actions), achieving the best step-level accuracy at the cost of doubled token usage. The key empirical finding is a context-length tradeoff: agent-level accuracy favors full-context methods (all-at-once) because identifying the responsible agent requires comparing multiple agents' behaviors, while step-level accuracy favors incremental methods (step-by-step) because LLMs struggle to retrieve a specific step from long contexts (the 'space-in-the-needle' problem). Experiments across GPT-4o, Llama, Qwen, OpenAI o1, and DeepSeek R1 show that even SOTA reasoning models achieve only ~53% agent-level and ~14% step-level accuracy, far below practical usability.

## Results

On the Who&When dataset with GPT-4o, the best agent-level accuracy is achieved by All-at-Once at 55.17% (hand-crafted, with ground truth) and 54.33% (algorithm-generated, with ground truth), substantially above the random baseline of 12.00% and 29.10% respectively. For step-level accuracy, Step-by-Step performs best at 25.51% (algorithm-generated, with GT) but drops to only 8.77% on hand-crafted systems without ground truth, while All-at-Once falls below the random baseline (19.06%) on algorithm-generated systems. Even SOTA reasoning models (OpenAI o1, DeepSeek R1) fail to close the gap, with DeepSeek R1 underperforming GPT-4o in 3 out of 4 cases. Overall, the best achievable performance is ~53.5% agent-level and ~14.2% step-level accuracy, far from practical usability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three LLM-based failure attribution methods (All-at-Once, Step-by-Step, Binary Search) against a random baseline. For agent-level accuracy, All-at-Once dominates, outperforming Step-by-Step by 19–25 percentage points and Binary Search by 10–21 points across all settings, benefiting from its access to the full conversation context. For step-level accuracy, the ranking inverts: Step-by-Step leads (25.51% on algorithm-generated with GT), followed by Binary Search (23.98%), with All-at-Once performing worst (12.50%) and even falling below random (19.06%). A hybrid method combining All-at-Once (for agent identification) and Step-by-Step (for step detection) achieves the best step-level accuracy at 12.28% on hand-crafted systems but at 2.4× the token cost of All-at-Once alone. Stronger reasoning models (OpenAI o1, DeepSeek R1) do not consistently outperform GPT-4o, suggesting that the bottleneck is task complexity rather than model capability. Ground truth availability improves accuracy across all methods, confirming that external answer signals provide useful anchoring for failure detection.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out a new problem space by formalizing 'decisive error' as a counterfactual intervention in multi-agent trajectories and proposing automated failure attribution as a distinct research task. Conceptually, it repurposes the LLM-as-a-judge paradigm (Zheng et al., 2023; Gu et al., 2024) from output-quality evaluation to multi-agent system debugging, and draws on process-level reward model ideas (Math-Shepherd, ProcessBench) that evaluate intermediate reasoning steps—but extends these from single-agent mathematical reasoning to multi-agent collaborative trajectories with turn-based protocols. It departs from existing evaluation benchmarks (SWE-Bench, DevAI) that provide aggregate metrics but leave failure attribution as a manual, expert-dependent process, and from self-reflection work (Reflexion) that focuses on single-agent correction rather than cross-agent causal diagnosis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper exposes a critical gap in the AI development cycle: despite the rapid proliferation of LLM multi-agent systems, the debugging step that identifies *which* agent and *which* step caused a failure remains entirely manual and expert-dependent. The negative results (best method: 53.5% agent-level, 14.2% step-level accuracy; reasoning models like o1 and R1 fail to close the gap) establish that this is a genuinely hard problem, not one solvable by simply prompting a stronger model. The finding that methods perform better at a statistical/aggregate level than at the instance level (Finding 6) suggests a practical near-term use case for system-level diagnostics, while the 'space-in-the-needle' explanation for step-level failures points toward context-management strategies as a promising research direction. Open questions include whether specialized training (beyond prompting) can improve attribution, how to handle increasingly long and complex agent interactions, and whether the decisive-error formulation generalizes to non-turn-based or parallel agent architectures.
