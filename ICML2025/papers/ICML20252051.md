## Problem

Deciding when to retrain a machine learning model under distribution shift requires balancing the cost of retraining against the cost of degraded performance, but existing approaches either ignore retraining costs (distribution shift detection), rely on strong stationarity assumptions (CARA), or are infeasible in the low-data regime typical of this problem (offline RL). No prior work provides a comprehensive, cost-aware formulation that works with only a handful of historical models and datasets.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a short offline sequence of w≈7 datasets with their corresponding trained models, plus a cost-to-performance ratio α, and at each online timestep receives new data and optionally a newly trained model. Performance observations form a triangular matrix pe_{i,j} (model i evaluated on dataset j, i≤j), which is the key structural constraint exploited by the forecaster. The output is a binary decision at each online timestep (retrain or keep), collectively forming a retraining schedule θ over the T-step online horizon.

## Method

UPF (Uncertainty-Performance Forecaster) models each unknown performance entry pe_{i,j} as a Beta-distributed random variable (for bounded metrics like accuracy), parameterized by a compact regression model (ElasticNet) over features [i, j, j−i, z_shift] that capture temporal position and distribution shift magnitude. A Gaussian approximation of the Beta enables stable likelihood-based training via standard MSE minimization on the small offline regression set. Decisions are made by comparing δ-level quantiles (default 95%) of the recursively defined total cost under retrain versus keep, where the cost random variables sum the Beta-distributed performance terms along the predicted future decision path. When no closed-form quantile exists, Monte Carlo bootstrapping provides the estimate. The approach also derives a theoretical upper bound on the optimal number of retrains (r* ≤ T − √(α/L)), enabling practitioners to determine a priori whether retraining is even worth considering.

## Results

The primary metric is the AUC of the combined cost C_alpha(theta) (lower is better), computed over a range of cost-to-performance ratios alpha. UPF achieves the best AUC on 6 out of 7 datasets: electricity (2.5782), Gauss (0.3829), circles (0.0668), yelpCHI (0.1293), epicgames (0.3189), and iWildCam (3.0498), with statistically significant improvements over the next-best baseline on all six. On airplanes, CARA edges out UPF (2.2753 vs. 2.2865), though the gap is small. UPF also most closely tracks the oracle's retraining count across varying alpha, adapting its retraining frequency appropriately (e.g., 0.1 retrains at high cost vs. 2.5 at low cost on electricity, compared to the oracle's 0.0 and 5.6 respectively).

## Baselines & Comparisons

UPF outperforms all four baseline families on 6 of 7 datasets. Against the distribution shift detection baselines (ADWIN, FHDDM, KSWIN), UPF achieves substantially lower AUC cost: on electricity UPF reaches 2.5782 versus 2.8099 (ADWIN-5%) and 3.1525 (FHDDM-5%), and on iWildCam 3.0498 versus 3.7371 (ADWIN-5%) and 4.4267 (FHDDM-5%). The shift detection methods cannot adapt to the retraining cost parameter alpha, producing identical retraining counts regardless of cost, which makes them impractical when retraining is expensive. Against CARA (Mahadevan and Mathioudakis, 2024), the closest competitor, UPF wins on 6/7 datasets with statistically significant margins on electricity (2.5782 vs. 2.7198), Gauss (0.3829 vs. 0.3841), circles (0.0668 vs. 0.0726), yelpCHI (0.1293 vs. 0.1294), epicgames (0.3189 vs. 0.3202), and iWildCam (3.0498 vs. 3.9506). CARA narrowly wins on airplanes (2.2753 vs. 2.2865), but the difference is marginal. UPF also more closely tracks the oracle's retraining frequency across alpha values, whereas CARA's fixed-threshold strategy is less sensitive to cost changes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of distribution shift detection, cost-aware retraining, and offline reinforcement learning, but does not directly build on any single prior method. It departs from distribution shift detection (ADWIN, FHDDM, KSWIN) by explicitly incorporating retraining costs into the decision, from CARA (Mahadevan & Mathioudakis, 2024) by avoiding assumptions of stationarity and constant task difficulty, and from offline RL by exploiting the highly structured, low-data nature of the retraining problem to use compact regression models instead of large Q-function approximators. The UPF method repurposes ideas from Bayesian performance forecasting (analogous to Freeze-Thaw in HPO) and quantile-based decision theory into a novel retraining-specific formulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Model retraining is a ubiquitous but underexplored practical problem in industry, and this paper provides the first comprehensive formulation that jointly handles cost trade-offs, distribution shift, and data scarcity. The theoretical bound (Proposition 3.1) gives practitioners a simple rule for when retraining is even worth considering, while the UPF algorithm offers a lightweight, deployable solution. Key open questions include handling abrupt (rather than gradual) performance shifts, transferring learned retraining schedules across datasets and tasks, and adapting hyperparameter optimization techniques to improve the performance forecaster. The method's reliance on temporal correlation in performance limits its applicability to highly non-stationary environments.
