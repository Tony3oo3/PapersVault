## Problem

Despite the proliferation of transformer architectures for time series forecasting, it remains unclear why simpler models like iTransformer and PatchTST consistently outperform more complex counterparts on standard benchmarks, why point-wise transformers underperform, and why intra-variate and inter-variate attention mechanisms yield similar results. This paper investigates the underlying mechanisms—specifically the relative importance of intra-variate versus inter-variate dependencies and the role of architectural components such as skip connections and normalization—in determining forecasting performance.

## Input / Output

The models under analysis consume multivariate time series (7–862 variates at 10-min to hourly resolution) and produce fixed-horizon future predictions (96–720 steps for benchmarks; 48–192 steps for healthcare data). Beyond standard MAE/MSE, the paper introduces model-agnostic mutual information scores (Intra MI, Avg Inter MI, Max Inter MI) computed by augmenting each sample into N=5 perturbed versions (zero, original, and three Gaussian-noise levels) and measuring the conditional variance of predictions. The synthetic evaluation sets use two variates with controlled autocorrelation (γ ∈ {0.5, 0.95}) and inter-variate dependency (α ∈ {0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.8}) to isolate these effects.

## Method

The paper proposes a mutual information estimation framework that quantifies how much a model's prediction for variate j depends on variate i by measuring the standard deviation of predictions when only variate i is perturbed, yielding Intra MI (i=j) and Inter MI (i≠j) scores comparable across architectures. To systematically test the role of dependency structure, the authors design synthetic two-variate datasets with tunable autocorrelation and cross-variate coupling, alongside ablation studies on iTransformer that remove encoder skip connections or replace the variate-independent linear decoder with a variate-dependent one. A Z-score normalization/denormalization ablation across four models reveals that this technique substantially improves performance on stationary benchmark data but degrades it on non-stationary synthetic data with monotonic trends. Validation on real-world dementia-care datasets (TIHM, MINDER) with 8 hourly location-activity variates confirms that the optimal architectural choice (e.g., variate-dependent vs. independent decoder) depends on the specific dependency structure of the target data.

## Results

The paper's central finding is that intra-variate mutual information (Intra MI) is the dominant predictor of forecasting accuracy: MAE–Intra MI correlations are strongly negative on all benchmark datasets (e.g., −0.875 on ETTh1, −0.935 on ETTh2, −0.87 on Traffic), while MAE–Inter MI correlations are weaker and positive (e.g., +0.616 on ETTh1, +0.266 on Traffic). On synthetic data with high autocorrelation (γ=0.95) and low inter-variate dependency (α≤0.4), the negative MAE–Intra correlation persists, but it flips to positive when autocorrelation drops to γ=0.5. Z-score normalization is a critical enabler: without it, iTransformer's MAE on ETTh2 degrades from 0.407 to 0.668, yet on non-stationary synthetic data (γ=0.5, α=0) Z-norm slightly hurts performance (0.785→0.792 MAE).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares seven transformer architectures (Transformer, Autoformer, FEDformer, Crossformer, PatchTST, iTransformer, TimeXer) across benchmark and synthetic datasets. Point-wise models (Transformer, Autoformer, FEDformer) are consistently less competitive because they achieve lower Intra MI scores, meaning they fail to capture within-variate temporal patterns that drive most of the predictive signal on these benchmarks. PatchTST (patch-wise, intra-variate attention) and iTransformer (variate-wise, inter-variate attention) achieve nearly identical MAE on all benchmarks despite fundamentally different attention scopes, confirming that both are primarily exploiting intra-variate structure. Crossformer, which explicitly models both intra- and inter-variate dependencies via two-stage attention, outperforms PatchTST, iTransformer, and TimeXer on synthetic datasets with strong inter-variate dependency (α=0.8) or low autocorrelation (γ=0.5), where inter-variate patterns matter more. Ablation on iTransformer shows that removing skip connections causes the largest degradation (Traffic MAE: 0.283→0.591), while replacing the variate-independent decoder with a variate-dependent one has minimal impact on benchmarks but helps on high-dependency synthetic data (γ=0.5, α=0.8: MAE 0.765→0.761). Z-score normalization is the single most impactful architectural choice: without it, ETTh2 MAE for iTransformer jumps from 0.407 to 0.668, and TimeXer from 0.404 to 0.891, yet it degrades performance on non-stationary synthetic data, revealing that benchmark success is partly an artifact of stationarity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper is a diagnostic empirical study that does not propose a new architecture but instead analyzes the behavior of seven existing transformer-based time series forecasting models (Transformer, Autoformer, FEDformer, Crossformer, PatchTST, iTransformer, TimeXer) through a novel lens: model-agnostic mutual information metrics that quantify intra- and inter-variate information flow. It builds on the tokenization taxonomy (point-wise, patch-wise, variate-wise) established by Wang et al. (2024b) and connects to prior observations by Zeng et al. (2023) on linear models and Tan et al. (2024) on LLMs, offering mechanistic explanations for their findings. The paper departs from RevIN (Kim et al., 2021) by using standard non-learnable Z-score normalization and from Zhao & Shen (2024) by showing that inter-variate dependencies are largely irrelevant on standard benchmarks, while still acknowledging their importance in specific settings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central practical implication is that benchmark performance of time series transformers is heavily confounded by the self-dependent, stationary nature of standard datasets and by the use of Z-score normalization, meaning that architectural comparisons on these benchmarks may not reflect true generalization capability. The validation on real-world healthcare data (TIHM, MINDER) demonstrates that models excelling on benchmarks can fail on data with different dependency structures, and that a single universal architecture is insufficient. Open questions include developing adaptive evaluation protocols, designing decoders that better capture inter-variate dependencies in non-stationary settings, and expanding benchmarks to include diverse, non-stationary, and event-driven time series.
