## Problem

RLHF requires expensive, time-consuming human annotations, while RLAIF (AI feedback) cannot fully replicate human judgment, especially on hard-to-annotate samples that are most critical for fine-tuning. The paper addresses how to achieve full-human-annotation-level alignment while reducing human annotation effort to a small fraction, without requiring full visibility into customer data for prompt optimization.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252760/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an unlabeled preference dataset (prompt + two candidate responses) and a general-purpose LLM for initial labeling, operating on a randomly down-sampled 1/4 shard of the corpus. It produces aligned preference labels for the full dataset, a trained reward model (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct with LoRA), and a DPO- or PPO-fine-tuned LLM (Qwen2.5-3B) for downstream tasks. Unusually, the method automatically flips LLM labels that are confidently wrong (very negative reward differences) without human input, and only routes the genuinely ambiguous 'knee' region samples to human annotators.

## Method

RLTHF operates in three stages: (1) a general-purpose LLM provides coarse preference labels on a 1/4 data shard; (2) an iterative loop trains a Bradley-Terry reward model on the LLM-labeled data, analyzes the reward distribution curve to identify the 'elbow' (high-confidence correct samples) and 'knee' (low-confidence, likely mislabeled samples), then applies targeted human annotation only to the knee region while automatically flipping samples with strongly negative reward differences; (3) the final reward model labels the full corpus for DPO or is integrated into PPO. Two hyperparameters control the iteration: an amplification ratio (α) that repeats human-annotated samples to reinforce their preference features, and a back-off ratio (β) that trims the training set to exclude noisy middle-region samples. Across 5 iterations with 4% human annotation per iteration, RLTHF converges to full-human-annotation-level alignment using only 6–7% of total human annotations, and the sanitized training data actually yields better downstream DPO performance than fully human-annotated data.

## Results

On downstream DPO fine-tuning (Qwen2.5-3B, evaluated via AlpacaEval win rate against SFT), RLTHF achieves 58.1% on HH-RLHF and 62.3% on TL;DR using only 6–7% human annotations, surpassing the fully human-annotated baseline (55.7% and 60.2% respectively). In reward modeling, RLTHF lifts test preference accuracy from GPT-4o's 74.7% to 89.6% on HH-RLHF and from 78.8% to 88.0% on TL;DR, approaching the full-human RM accuracy of 91.8% and 89.6%. The method achieves a 15.9× and 5.3× higher return-on-investment (accuracy gain per unit of human annotation) compared to random human annotation on HH-RLHF and TL;DR, respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the RLAIF baseline (AI-only labeling with GPT-4o), RLTHF improves downstream win rate by 8.9 points on HH-RLHF (58.1% vs. 49.2%) and 3.1 points on TL;DR (62.3% vs. 59.2%), demonstrating that targeted human correction of LLM mislabels yields substantial gains over pure AI feedback. Against the full-human-annotation RLHF baseline, RLTHF actually outperforms by 2.4 points on HH-RLHF (58.1% vs. 55.7%) and 2.1 points on TL;DR (62.3% vs. 60.2%), which the authors attribute to RLTHF's back-off sanitization filtering out noisy and biased human labels that are present in the raw fully-annotated dataset. Notably, even when the initial AI labeler is the weaker GPT-4o-mini, RLTHF closes most of the gap (56.1% vs. 58.1% on HH-RLHF; 62.4% vs. 62.3% on TL;DR), showing robustness to initial labeling quality. The random-annotation baseline, which uses the same number of human labels but applied uniformly at random, achieves only marginal improvement over AI-only labeling (52.5% vs. 49.2% on HH-RLHF), confirming that the strategic targeting via reward distribution is critical to RLTHF's effectiveness.

## Relation to Prior Work

RLTHF positions itself as a bridge between RLAIF (Lee et al., 2023), which uses LLMs as annotators but is limited by model bias and prompt sensitivity, and full RLHF, which is costly and slow. It builds on the Bradley-Terry reward model formulation (David, 1963) and the DPO/PPO preference optimization pipelines (Rafailov et al., 2024; Schulman et al., 2017), but its core departure is using the reward model's own reward distribution over the training set as a diagnostic signal to identify which samples are likely mislabeled by the LLM and which are safely correct. This contrasts with self-improvement methods (SER, Self-Rewarding LMs) that the authors argue are bounded by the LLM's initial capability, and with random human annotation baselines that waste effort on already-correct samples.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: an 84–86% cost reduction while matching or exceeding full-human-annotation quality on downstream tasks makes targeted human feedback a viable strategy for industry fine-tuning services where data access is restricted and SME time is scarce. The finding that RLTHF's sanitized dataset outperforms the fully human-annotated baseline (58.1% vs. 55.7% win rate on HH-RLHF) challenges the assumption that more human annotation is always better, suggesting that curation quality can outweigh data volume. Open questions include generalizability beyond the two evaluated datasets, sensitivity to the heuristic hyperparameters (α, β), and whether the reward-distribution-based targeting transfers to domains where the initial LLM alignment is very poor or the preference structure is multi-dimensional rather than binary.
