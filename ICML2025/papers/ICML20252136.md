## Problem

Algorithm-of-Thoughts (AoT) substantially improves LLM planning accuracy over Chain-of-Thought by exploring multiple reasoning paths, but at the cost of solutions that are up to 80% longer, increasing computational overhead and token consumption. The paper asks whether the solution-length gap between CoT and AoT can be closed without sacrificing the planning accuracy gains that diverse reasoning paths provide.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252136/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a classical planning problem specified as an initial state and a goal state (e.g., five numbers plus a target value for Game of X, a tile grid for N-Puzzle, or two words for Word Ladder) and produces a single-query sequence of state-transition triplets of the form (state → thinking/action → next state). Unlike Tree-of-Thoughts, no external search framework or multiple API calls are needed; the branching exploration is encoded within the generated text itself, with the model free to backtrack from any intermediate state. Solutions are constrained by the model's context window, and the RL stage explicitly optimizes to stay within that budget.

## Method

AoT-O3 uses a two-stage training pipeline. First, supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on AoT-style plans constructed from random search trajectories combined with valid solution segments, using standard cross-entropy loss to teach the model the state-thinking-transition format. Second, a reinforcement learning stage (RLOO) optimizes a non-LLM, objective value function that treats each transition step as an RL action, assigning reward +1 for correct solutions minus a per-step penalty nα, with a cutoff β preventing long correct solutions from scoring below incorrect ones, and an optional κ bonus for transitions lying on the ground-truth solution path. The key design choice is framing the reward as a per-transition value model rather than a per-solution reward model, which prevents the policy from hacking the objective by prolonging solutions to accumulate more total reward. The α penalty is calibrated so that a correct solution at maximum context length still slightly out-rewards an incorrect one, and a KL divergence to the SFT reference model provides entropy regularization to avoid mode collapse.

## Results

AoT-O3 achieves up to 80% reduction in solution length compared to AoT-SFT while simultaneously improving accuracy across all three benchmarks (Game of X, N-Puzzle, Word Ladder) and all three model sizes (Gemma2-2B, Llama3-1B, Llama3-3B). On Game of X, AoT-O3 reaches 78–82% accuracy with 9.7–16.3 steps, versus 55–66% accuracy with 31.9–37.2 steps for AoT-SFT. On N-Puzzle, solution length drops from 24.8–33.1 to 14.2–17.9 steps while accuracy improves from 64–71% to 68–75%. On Word Ladder, Llama3-1B sees a 47.1% length reduction (26.1→13.8 steps) with accuracy rising from 47% to 52%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CoT-SFT, AoT-O3 achieves substantially higher accuracy on all benchmarks (e.g., 78% vs. 32% on Game of X with Gemma2-2B; 75% vs. 42% on N-Puzzle with Llama3-3B), though CoT-SFT produces shorter solutions (4–8 steps) at the cost of much lower success rates. Against AoT-SFT, AoT-O3 wins on both accuracy and solution length simultaneously: on Game of X, accuracy improves by 11–17 percentage points (e.g., 72% vs. 55% for Llama3-1B) while solution length drops by 51–62% (9.7 vs. 31.9 steps for Llama3-1B). On N-Puzzle, AoT-O3 reduces solution length by 28–46% while matching or exceeding AoT-SFT accuracy (e.g., 75% vs. 71% for Llama3-3B). On Word Ladder, the gains are most pronounced for smaller models: Llama3-1B improves from 47% to 52% accuracy with a 47% length reduction. The κ=0 ablation confirms the solution-path bonus provides modest additional gains (1–5% accuracy) and slightly shorter solutions, but the core length-reduction benefit comes from the RL reward structure itself.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Algorithm of Thoughts (AoT) framework (Sel et al., ICML 2024; Sel et al., 2025) by adding a reinforcement learning stage that explicitly optimizes for solution conciseness, moving beyond the prompting-only or SFT-only paradigms of prior AoT work. A key conceptual departure is the use of a simple, non-LLM objective reward model (step-count penalty plus correctness) rather than the learned or LLM-based reward models typical in RLHF pipelines, and the framing of each state transition as an individual action (making the feedback a value model rather than a single-step reward). The paper also positions itself against Tree-of-Thoughts and LLM-Modulo by emphasizing single-query efficiency, and against standard CoT by retaining the multi-path exploration structure that AoT provides.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The demonstrated 80% reduction in solution length while maintaining or improving accuracy addresses a critical scalability bottleneck for LLM-based planning in resource-constrained or high-throughput deployment settings, with the paper quantifying potential energy savings at scale. However, the evaluation is limited to small open-source models (1B–3B parameters) and three relatively constrained planning benchmarks, leaving open whether the RL length-optimization stage transfers to larger models or more complex, open-ended planning domains. The finding that random demonstrations are effective for in-context prompting but actively harmful for fine-tuning (Table 1) raises an important open question about the boundary between format elicitation and strategy learning in LLMs. The Jevons paradox concern—efficiency gains potentially accelerating overall LLM usage—remains an unresolved societal question the authors flag but do not address.
