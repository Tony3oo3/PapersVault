## Problem

Existing virtual agent benchmarks suffer from three critical limitations: uncontrollable and fixed task complexity that cannot scale with agent capabilities, heavy reliance on manual annotation that limits scenario coverage, and coarse-grained evaluation that only measures final task success without assessing intermediate steps or fine-grained capabilities. No prior benchmark systematically defines composable task complexity or provides a multi-dimensional evaluation framework that quantifies distinct agent capabilities such as planning, decision-making, and instruction understanding.

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes GUI screenshots (standardized to 1024×1024 via bicubic interpolation), accessibility trees, natural-language task instructions, and high-level task intents. Tasks are represented as directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) where nodes are subtasks with explicit input/output resource lists and edges encode execution dependencies. The evaluation produces two graph-based metrics — Coverage Rate (depth-weighted fraction of completed subtasks) and Logical Consistency (ratio of the agent's application-switching coherence to the maximum possible) — along with per-capability scores across 10 dimensions, all computed at the subtask level rather than the whole-task level.

## Method

OmniBench introduces a bottom-up automated synthesis pipeline that first discovers subtasks across 49 applications, then iteratively generates trajectories and evaluation functions via a cross-verification loop between an MLLM and a Code LLM. Subtasks are composed into task graphs guided by extracted task intents (e.g., 'create a personal introduction PowerPoint for Emily'), with a consistency validator ensuring the summarized instruction preserves the graph's nonlinear dependency structure. Five fundamental complexity dimensions — dependency (edges), instruction (nodes), knowledge (application categories), hierarchy (depth), and branch (width) — are controlled by constraining the DAG composition rules, enabling systematic construction of test tasks for 10 capability dimensions. The OmniEval framework tracks each graph node through Completed/Evaluating/Waiting states in topological order, and the two novel metrics (CR and LC) capture intermediate progress and operational-logic alignment that traditional success-rate or trajectory-similarity metrics miss. Fine-tuning agents on the synthesized graph-structured trajectories improves both OmniBench scores and generalization to external benchmarks (AndroidControl, OmniAct) while reducing sensitivity to instruction reordering.

## Results

On OmniBench, GPT-4o achieves the highest overall Coverage Rate (CR) of 38.7 and Logical Consistency (LC) of 49.0 among all evaluated agents, while humans reach 80.1 CR and 92.8 LC. The best fine-tuned agent, Omni-UGround-V1-7B, achieves 34.4 CR and 37.4 LC, outperforming its base UGround-V1-7B (25.0 CR, 26.3 LC) by 9.4 and 11.1 points respectively. On graph-structured tasks specifically, even GPT-4o drops to only 20.5% accuracy versus 80.1% for humans, revealing a critical gap in handling non-linear task dependencies. Across all 10 capability dimensions, Subtask Identification (SI) and Long Instruction Following (LIF) remain the weakest, with GPT-4o scoring only 30.6 and 32.2 respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among closed-source MLLMs, GPT-4o leads all baselines with 38.7 CR, outperforming Claude-3.5-Sonnet (27.6), Gemini-2.0-Flash (25.9), and Qwen-VL-Max (18.4) by 11.1, 12.8, and 20.3 points respectively. Among visual agents, UGround-V1-7B* (25.0 CR) surpasses ShowUI-2B (23.2), Aguvis-7B (22.9), and OS-Atlas-Pro-4B (19.1), while open-source MLLMs (Qwen2-VL-7B at 14.8, InternVL2.5-8B at 17.4) trail significantly behind all agent architectures. The paper's fine-tuned agents demonstrate clear gains over their base models: Omni-UGround-7B improves from 25.0 to 34.4 CR (+9.4) and Omni-OS-Atlas-4B from 22.2 to 29.7 CR (+7.5) on OmniBench. On AndroidControl, Omni-UGround-7B achieves 83.57% SR (Low) and 70.83% SR (High), marginally beating UGround-7B-V1 (83.29/70.31) and SeeClick (75.00/59.11). On OmniAct-Desktop, Omni-UGround-7B reaches 92.10% SR, outperforming UGround-7B-V1 (91.89) and Qwen2-VL-7B (91.77). However, even the best fine-tuned agent (Omni-UGround-7B at 34.4 CR) remains far below GPT-4o (38.7) and humans (80.1), and all agents struggle most on Subtask Identification and Long Instruction Following, where the gap to human performance exceeds 35 points.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the graph-based evaluation paradigm introduced by CRAB (Xu et al., 2024a), which first represented agent tasks as DAGs and tracked subtask completion states, but relied on handcrafted graphs and lacked systematic task complexity control. OmniBench departs from CRAB by introducing a fully automated bottom-up synthesis pipeline with five-dimensional complexity constraints (dependency, instruction, knowledge, hierarchy, branch) and a 10-capability evaluation framework, moving beyond the coarse grounding/planning dichotomy used in prior agent benchmarks. The environment design draws from OSWorld's interactive Windows setup, while the evaluation metrics (Coverage Rate, Logical Consistency) are novel contributions that address the limitations of both trajectory-based (AndroidInTheWild, Mind2Web) and result-based (WebArena, OSWorld) evaluation paradigms.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 36k-task scale (40× larger than most environment-based benchmarks) and automated synthesis pipeline make scalable, reproducible GUI agent evaluation practical for the first time, removing the manual annotation bottleneck that has limited prior work. The finding that even GPT-4o drops to 20.5% on graph-structured tasks versus 80.1% for humans reveals a fundamental structural limitation in current agent training (predominantly on linear trajectories) that the field has not previously quantified. Open questions include whether the graph-structured training data generalizes beyond the tested AndroidControl and OmniAct benchmarks, whether the 10-capability decomposition is the right granularity, and how the framework extends to truly open-ended tasks where the task graph is not known a priori.
