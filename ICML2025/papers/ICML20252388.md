## Problem

Existing particle-based Bayesian inference methods (SVGD, SGLD) approximate only the empirical posterior, which risks overfitting to the training set. Meanwhile, generalization guarantees for flat minimizers like SAM were established solely in finite-dimensional Euclidean spaces, leaving no theoretical foundation for sharpness-aware sampling in the infinite-dimensional functional spaces where kernel methods naturally operate. This paper bridges both gaps by extending generalization bounds to RKHS and proposing a particle-sampling algorithm that targets the population posterior.

## Input / Output

The method fine-tunes a pre-trained ViT-B/16 (ImageNet-21K) using LoRA adapters on 224×224 RGB images from 19 VTAB-1K datasets spanning natural, specialized, and structured domains. It maintains m=4 LoRA particles simultaneously, and the final prediction is the average output across all particles, yielding a class label or regression value per input image. The particle transport operates in the LoRA parameter space, so the computational overhead scales with the number of lightweight adapter modules rather than the full model.

## Method

FHBI performs an iterative two-step update in a vector-valued RKHS: (1) an adversarial functional ascent step that displaces each particle along the normalized functional gradient of the KL divergence (analogous to SAM's perturbation), and (2) a functional descent step that minimizes the KL divergence to the empirical posterior evaluated at the perturbed location. The functional gradient is computed via a Stein-operator-like expression involving kernel-weighted averages of log-posterior gradients across all particles, which couples their trajectories. This coupling produces both spatial repulsion (preventing mode collapse, as in SVGD) and angular repulsion (diversifying gradient directions, as in SAM), so FHBI subsumes SVGD when ρ=0 and SAM when m=1. The theoretical contribution (Theorems 4.2–4.3) shows that minimizing the sharpness-aware empirical KL loss implicitly bounds the population KL loss, providing the justification for targeting the population posterior without direct access to it.

## Results

On the VTAB-1K benchmark (19 datasets across Natural, Specialized, and Structured domains), FHBI achieves a mean Top-1 accuracy of 73.7%, outperforming all nine baselines. The closest competitors are BayesTune (72.2%), SADA-JEM (72.1%), and AdamW (72.0%), while the strongest Bayesian particle-sampling baselines SVGD (70.9%) and SGLD (70.4%) trail by 2.8% and 3.3% respectively. FHBI also achieves the best Expected Calibration Error (ECE) of 0.12 on average, compared to 0.16 for SAM and 0.18 for SVGD, indicating superior calibration alongside higher accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FHBI outperforms all seven compared baselines on the VTAB-1K average. Against SAM (70.5%), FHBI gains +3.2%, demonstrating that the multi-particle interactive gradient mechanism provides substantial benefits over single-particle sharpness-aware optimization. Against SVGD (70.9%), the closest particle-sampling baseline, FHBI improves by +2.8%, with the gap widening to +3.3% over SGLD (70.4%). FHBI also surpasses the hybrid methods SA-BNN (68.1%, +5.6%) and SADA-JEM (72.1%, +1.6%), confirming that FHBI is not merely a combination of SVGD and SAM but introduces genuinely new angular diversity dynamics. Against BayesTune (72.2%), a variational Bayesian fine-tuning method, FHBI leads by +1.5%. DeepEns (67.0%) is the weakest baseline, trailing FHBI by 6.7%, highlighting the limitations of independent ensemble training without sharpness-awareness or kernel-based interaction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate research threads: Sharpness-Aware Minimization (SAM), which improves generalization by minimizing loss sharpness in finite-dimensional Euclidean spaces, and Stein Variational Gradient Descent (SVGD), which transports a set of particles toward a target distribution using kernel-based functional gradients. The key conceptual departure is extending SAM's generalization bounds from Euclidean spaces to infinite-dimensional reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHS), introducing the notion of 'functional sharpness,' and then embedding the sharpness-aware ascent step directly into SVGD's particle transport mechanism. The resulting algorithm, FHBI, is shown to be a strict generalization of both SVGD (recovered when the ascent step size ρ=0) and SAM (recovered when the number of particles m=1), but is not reducible to their naive combination because the kernel-mediated gradient interactions create angular repulsive forces between particles.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theoretical extension of flat-minimizer generalization bounds to RKHS provides a principled justification for why sharpness-aware particle transport should improve generalization beyond what SVGD alone achieves, and the empirical validation on 19 diverse VTAB-1K datasets (consistently outperforming 9 baselines including SGLD, SVGD, SA-BNN, and SADA-JEM) demonstrates practical utility for fine-tuning pretrained vision transformers. A key limitation is the memory cost of storing multiple particle models, which becomes prohibitive for larger architectures; the authors identify extending functional sharpness to variational inference (avoiding explicit particle storage) as a natural future direction. The finding that angular diversity in particle gradient directions—not merely spatial diversity—contributes to generalization is a mechanistic insight that could inform other ensemble or particle-based methods.
