## Problem

Standard RLHF reward modeling treats annotator disagreements as noise to be resolved by majority vote, yet the paper shows that over 30% of preference pairs exhibit genuine diverging preferences driven by legitimate factors such as task underspecification, response style, and safety judgments. Consequently, standard Bradley-Terry and MSE-regression reward models assign decisive reward differences even when human preferences truly diverge, and LLM-as-Judge evaluators unduly punish models that follow consistent pluralistic policies (e.g., clarifying on ambiguous prompts or refusing on safety-sensitive ones).

## Input / Output

The reward model takes a prompt and a single response as input and outputs a reward distribution parameterized by a mean μ and variance σ². When comparing two responses A and B, the model computes the distribution of r_A − r_B (accounting for inter-response correlation ρ) and maps it to three preference labels—tied, slightly preferred, or significantly preferred—via fixed threshold boundaries at ±0.5 and ±1.5. For divergence detection, a scalar score |μ_A − μ_B| − λ(σ_A + σ_B) is computed, where larger values indicate high-agreement preferences and smaller (or negative) values flag divisive response pairs.

## Method

The core contribution is the Mean-Var Reward Model (KL), which parameterizes each response's reward as a normal distribution N(μ_A, σ_A²) rather than a single scalar. A key design choice is modeling correlation ρ between two responses, set to the fraction of annotators who labeled the pair as a tie and scaled by a hyperparameter η, to capture the fact that similar responses receive similar judgments. Training uses KL-divergence loss over all individual annotator labels (not majority-aggregated), where the predicted distribution over r_A − r_B is discretized into three preference categories and compared against the empirical annotator label distribution. A complementary Classification (KL) variant predicts the full 5-way Likert score distribution per response, using the joint probability of extreme scores (1 and 5) as a divergence indicator. All models are fine-tuned from Llama-3-8B-Instruct via LoRA with 8-bit quantization, and the variance head applies an absolute-value activation plus a 0.1 offset to ensure non-negative, stable σ predictions.

## Results

On the Diverging ID AUROC metric (the paper's key evaluation for detecting annotator disagreement), all single-value reward models perform at or below random (0.400–0.494), confirming they cannot distinguish diverging from high-agreement preferences. The proposed Mean-Var (KL) distributional reward model achieves 0.615 AUROC on MultiPref and 0.582 on HelpSteer2, while Classification (KL) reaches 0.648 on HelpSteer2 — a 0.16 improvement over the best single-value baseline. On Preference Accuracy, all methods perform comparably (0.64–0.68), indicating that distributional modeling does not sacrifice the ability to identify which response is preferred.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All single-value baselines — Bradley-Terry (both aggregated and all-labels variants), MSE Regression, and the large-scale SOTA models Skywork (0.494) and Nemotron (0.400) — perform at or below random (0.5) on Diverging ID AUROC, confirming they are fundamentally unable to detect when annotators disagree. The prior distributional approach, Mean-Var (NLL, Independent) from Siththaranjan et al. (2023), improves to 0.549/0.573 AUROC but sacrifices Preference Accuracy (0.533/0.574 vs. ~0.66–0.68 for other methods), indicating its NLL loss and independence assumption are suboptimal. The proposed Mean-Var (KL) outperforms the NLL baseline on both metrics simultaneously (0.615 vs. 0.549 AUROC on MultiPref; 0.684 vs. 0.574 Pref. Acc. on HelpSteer2), demonstrating that modeling correlation between responses and using KL-divergence over preference labels is critical. Classification (KL), which leverages the full Likert-5 annotations from HelpSteer2, achieves the highest Div. AUROC (0.648) on that dataset, suggesting richer annotation granularity further improves disagreement detection.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the distributional reward modeling framework of Siththaranjan et al. (2023), which proposed Mean-Var reward models trained with NLL loss under an independence assumption, by introducing correlation modeling between response rewards and switching to KL-divergence loss over all annotator labels. It departs from prior pluralistic alignment work (Chen et al., 2024; Poddar et al., 2024) that relied on synthetic, author-defined disagreement simulations, instead grounding its analysis in real human annotation disagreement from MultiPref and HelpSteer2. The paper also positions itself against the standard RLHF pipeline (Bradley-Terry, MSE regression) and LLM-as-Judge evaluation (ChatbotArena, WildBench), showing both fail to account for diverging preferences, and connects to a broader NLP literature on annotator disagreement in safety, NLI, and discourse tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that over 75% of preference annotation disagreements stem from legitimate individual predilections (verbosity, format, aesthetic taste, refusal strategy) rather than noise directly challenges a foundational assumption in reward model design where dissenting labels are treated as undesirable variance to be averaged away. Practically, the paper provides a concrete method (distributional reward models) for identifying divisive prompts in LLM-as-Judge benchmarks, with demonstrated application to WildBench where 42% of the top-divisive examples involved Comply-vs-Refuse disagreements that the judge resolved 100% in favor of compliance. Open questions include how to actually leverage distributional rewards during RLHF training to produce pluralistically aligned models, whether the taxonomy generalizes across languages and domains, and how to design evaluation protocols that fairly assess models trained with consistent divergence-handling policies.
