## Problem

Deep learning survival models outperform classical methods in time-to-event prediction but their opacity hinders clinical adoption in personalized medicine. While model-agnostic post-hoc XAI methods like SurvLIME and SurvSHAP(t) exist for survival analysis, no gradient-based explanation methods have been formally extended to survival neural networks, leaving a gap in model-specific, computationally efficient interpretability for time-to-event functional outcomes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251277/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a trained survival deep learning model (DeepSurv, CoxTime, or DeepHit) with multi-modal inputs—tabular clinical/genetic features and 226×226 histopathological image patches processed through a ResNet34 backbone—and produces attribution values R_j^{t_k} for each feature j at each discretized time point t_k, rather than a single scalar per feature. For image inputs this yields time-varying saliency maps; for tabular features it produces relevance curves, stacked contribution plots, and force plots that decompose the prediction-to-reference difference across the survival curve. A key structural difference from standard attribution is that explanations form an ensemble across time, capturing how feature effects evolve rather than summarizing a single prediction.

## Method

The paper extends six gradient-based attribution methods (Grad, SmoothGrad, Gradient×Input, Integrated Gradients, GradSHAP, and their survival counterparts) to functional survival outcomes by computing attributions at each discretized time point, addressing practical challenges such as gradient accumulation in models that replicate instances across time (e.g., CoxTime). The key novel contribution is GradSHAP(t), which replaces the single baseline in Integrated Gradients with the expectation over randomly drawn reference values from the training data via Monte Carlo integration, yielding a gradient-based approximation of SHAP values at selected time points with computational complexity O(n·n_samples·n_int). This makes it a model-specific counterpart to the sample-based SurvSHAP(t), achieving comparable local accuracy at a fraction of the runtime, particularly in high-dimensional settings. The paper also develops temporal visualization techniques—relevance curves, normalized contribution plots, and force plots—that incorporate the time dimension to show how feature effects evolve across the survival curve, and systematically analyzes how implicit or explicit baselines in these methods influence survival explanations.

## Results

On the real-world multi-modal brain cancer survival task (TCGA LGG/GBM, 1,239 train / 266 test), the CNN-based DeepHit model achieves a C-index of 0.713 and an integrated Brier score of 0.092. GradSHAP(t) with 100 baseline samples and 20 integration points produces a full time-dependent explanation for a single instance in approximately 12 minutes. In the controlled comparison (Sec. 5.2), GradSHAP(t) matches or slightly exceeds SurvSHAP(t) in time-averaged instance-wise local accuracy while being dramatically faster—completing in seconds where SurvSHAP(t) requires several minutes at comparable accuracy. On global feature rankings across 300 test samples, GradSHAP(t) and SurvSHAP(t) produce nearly identical rankings aligned with the data-generating process, whereas SurvLIME yields a near-uniform distribution that fails to differentiate feature importance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SurvSHAP(t) (Krzyziński et al., 2023), GradSHAP(t) achieves comparable or marginally better local accuracy (Fig. 6–7) while reducing runtime by orders of magnitude: in the high-dimensional image-input setting (32×32 ResNet18), GradSHAP(t) completes in a few seconds versus several minutes for SurvSHAP(t) at the same accuracy level. On global feature importance rankings (Fig. 8, 300 test samples, p=5 features with known ordering), GradSHAP(t) and SurvSHAP(t) are nearly indistinguishable and both correctly recover the ground-truth hierarchy, confirming the gradient-based approximation is faithful. Against SurvLIME (Kovalev et al., 2020), GradSHAP(t) clearly outperforms: SurvLIME produces a near-uniform ranking distribution that cannot separate features of differing importance, while GradSHAP(t) consistently concentrates high-importance features at the top of the ranking. The paper also notes that on time-dependent simulation data, CoxTime and DeepHit correctly capture time-varying feature effects via gradient-based explanations, whereas DeepSurv (constrained by the PH assumption) cannot—demonstrating that the explanation methods faithfully reflect model architecture limitations rather than data properties.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the well-established family of gradient-based feature attribution methods (Saliency, SmoothGrad, Gradient×Input, Integrated Gradients, GradSHAP) from their original scalar-output formulation to survival neural networks with functional, time-dependent outputs. It builds directly on Lundberg & Lee (2017) and Erion et al. (2021) for GradSHAP, Sundararajan et al. (2017) for Integrated Gradients, and Simonyan et al. (2014) for saliency maps, adapting each to handle the additional temporal dimension and the specific computational challenges of survival architectures (e.g., CoxTime's time-as-feature violating sample independence). The key departure from prior survival XAI work (SurvLIME, SurvSHAP(t)) is the introduction of model-specific, gradient-based methods that exploit the differentiability of the survival NN itself, rather than relying on model-agnostic sampling or surrogate fitting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic framework for gradient-based, time-dependent explanations of survival deep learning models, filling a gap that limited practitioners to model-agnostic methods with poor scalability for high-dimensional or multi-modal inputs. The practical demonstration on TCGA glioma data (tabular + histopathological images) shows these methods can surface clinically meaningful temporal dynamics and visual patterns, supporting transparent clinical decision-making. Open questions include extension to competing risks, multistate models, and recurrent events, as well as capturing feature interactions rather than only main effects. The authors explicitly caution that these explanations do not imply causal relationships, a limitation inherent to all post-hoc attribution methods.
