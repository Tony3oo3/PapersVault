## Problem

Learning sequential tasks from a single video demonstration is difficult when the demonstration is temporally misaligned due to variations in execution speed, embodiment, or pauses. Existing frame-level matching approaches (optimal transport, DTW, TemporalOT) treat imitation as a distribution-matching problem over individual frames, which fails to enforce correct subgoal ordering or consistent progress, causing agents to revisit subgoals, skip ahead, or stall without penalty.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single video demonstration (a sequence of RGB frames with no state or action labels) and a learner's visual trajectory as input, with no requirement that the two be temporally aligned. It produces a dense per-timestep reward signal where each reward is a scalar in [0,1] representing the probability that the learner has covered all demonstration subgoals in the correct order up to that timestep. The reward is non-Markovian, depending on the full trajectory history, and is computed via a visual encoder (ResNet50) that extracts frame embeddings with cosine distance as the pairwise metric.

## Method

ORCA (ORdered Coverage Alignment) formulates the reward as an ordered coverage problem solved via dynamic programming: a coverage matrix C_{t,j} is computed recursively as C_{t,j} = max{C_{t-1,j}, C_{t,j-1} · P_{t,j}}, where P_{t,j} = exp(−λ·d(o_t, ñ_o_j)) is the probability of occupying subgoal j at time t. The final per-timestep reward is R = C_{t,T̃−1} · P_{t,T̃}, which requires the agent to both have covered all prior subgoals in order AND currently occupy the final subgoal, preventing the agent from 'banking' coverage and then drifting away. Training uses a two-stage procedure: first pretraining with TemporalOT rewards (which assume temporal alignment) to initialize the policy in a good basin, then refining with ORCA rewards. The paper provides formal proofs that ORCA satisfies two key desiderata—enforcing subgoal ordering and enforcing full subgoal coverage—that frame-level matching methods provably violate, and achieves 4.5× and 6.6× improvements over the best baselines on Meta-world and Humanoid-v4 tasks respectively.

## Results

On Meta-world tasks with temporally misaligned demonstrations, ORCA achieves an average expert-normalized return of 0.50, a 4.5× improvement over the best frame-level matching baseline TemporalOT (0.11). On Humanoid-v4 tasks, ORCA(NP) achieves an average return of 62.9 versus 6.55 for TemporalOT (a 9.6× improvement), while ORCA with pretraining achieves 43.3 (6.6× improvement). ORCA also outperforms all baselines on temporally aligned demonstrations, reaching 0.57 average normalized return on Meta-world versus 0.47 for OT and 0.43 for TemporalOT.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ORCA decisively outperforms all frame-level matching baselines on temporally misaligned demonstrations. Against TemporalOT (Fu et al., 2024c), the strongest baseline, ORCA achieves 4.5× higher average normalized return on Meta-world (0.50 vs. 0.11) and 6.6× higher return on Humanoid-v4 (43.3 vs. 6.55). OT (Tian et al., 2024) and DTW (Sakoe & Chiba, 1978) perform near-zero on Meta-world (0.04 and 0.01 respectively), confirming their inability to handle temporal misalignment. RoboCLIP (Sontakke et al., 2024) also fails on most Meta-world tasks (0.05 average) and achieves only 11.94 on Humanoid. On the additional baselines tested on a subset of tasks, GAIfO (Torabi et al., 2019) and LIV (Ma et al., 2023) both reach 0.21 average normalized return, well below ORCA's 0.70. ORCA's only notable loss is on the Push task, where the small target object makes the visual distance metric noisier, benefiting the sparser Threshold baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant frame-level matching paradigm in IRL-based imitation learning, where methods like OT (Tian et al., 2024), TemporalOT (Fu et al., 2024c), and DTW (Sakoe & Chiba, 1978) align individual learner and demonstration frames via transport cost or temporal warping. While these approaches inherit the IRL formulation of matching learner and expert distributions (Swamy et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2019), ORCA redefines matching at the sequence level using a dynamic-programming-based ordered coverage probability, making the reward explicitly non-Markovian. The paper also builds on the practical setup of Fu et al. (2024c) for visual reward design with ResNet50 embeddings and DrQ-v2/SAC policies, but replaces the entropic OT objective with a recursive coverage computation that enforces both subgoal ordering and full coverage.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies and formally proves a fundamental limitation of all frame-level matching rewards (OT, DTW, TemporalOT) for sequence-matching tasks: they cannot enforce temporal ordering or full subgoal coverage, leading to agents that stall, skip, or reverse subgoals. ORCA's 4.5x and 6.6x improvements over the best frame-level baselines demonstrate that this is not a tuning issue but a structural failure. Open questions include whether ORCA extends to cross-embodiment settings (where temporal misalignment is most severe), whether the visual distance metric can be improved via online fine-tuning, and how the approach generalizes to language-conditioned subgoal sequences rather than purely visual frames. The reliance on a good off-the-shelf encoder and the need for pretraining to avoid local minima are practical limitations that constrain immediate deployment.
