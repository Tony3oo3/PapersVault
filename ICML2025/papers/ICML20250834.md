## Problem

Existing data selection methods for deep learning—static coreset selection, adaptive reselection, and online batch selection—all require the full dataset to be available upfront or make only temporary selection decisions that discard samples after a single update. This is impractical in real-world streaming scenarios (on-device learning, web-scraped data) where examples arrive continuously, cannot be shuffled, and must be filtered under resource constraints. The paper introduces Incremental Data Selection (IDS), a new problem setting where a learner must incrementally build a fixed-size training set from a stream while simultaneously training, retaining selected examples for repeated use.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a continuous stream of labeled RGB image examples (x, y) from a data source of unknown size, observed one at a time, along with a pretrained ViT-B/16 model (DINO self-supervised on ImageNet-1k). Each example is evaluated immediately upon arrival and either permanently retained in the growing training set or discarded; there is no revisit. The output is a selected training subset of predefined size k (ranging from 2.5k to 400k examples depending on dataset) and a fine-tuned classifier. Images are normalized with ImageNet statistics and upscaled to 224×224 for CIFAR-100; no data augmentation is applied during fine-tuning.

## Method

PEAKS derives its scoring function from a first-order Taylor expansion of model updates in the kernel regime, showing that the change in validation logits induced by training on a new example factorizes into a prediction error term E(x_p) and a kernel similarity term (inner product of penultimate-layer features). Under a last-layer training approximation, the kernel reduces to the inner product of the example's features with the class weight vector, yielding the compact score s(x_p, y_p) = E(x_p) · f(x_p, θ_t)[y_p] / c_{y_p}(t), where the denominator normalizes by the count of already-selected samples from that class to prevent imbalanced selection. Selection decisions use a dynamic percentile threshold over a rolling cache of recent scores (refreshed every τ updates), accepting examples whose score exceeds the (100−p)-th percentile. The overall IDS procedure has three phases: initialization on m random examples, iterative selection-and-update (collecting δ new examples per update mixed with older ones in a batch), and final fine-tuning on the complete selected set.

## Results

On the primary fine-tuning setting (ViT-B/16 pretrained with DINO), PEAKS achieves 59.0% top-1 accuracy on WebVision (100k samples), 72.9% on Food101 (10k), and 62.2% on Food101-N (10k), consistently outperforming random selection (50.1%, 69.2%, 55.8% respectively) and all embedding- and error-based baselines. In scaling experiments, PEAKS with 50k and 100k WebVision samples matches the accuracy of random selection requiring 200k and 400k samples—a 4× data reduction. On the clean CIFAR100 dataset, PEAKS (82.7%) is competitive but slightly below the 'Wrong Low Confidence' strategy (83.9%) at the 10k budget, reflecting that simpler datasets offer less room for principled selection to shine.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PEAKS decisively outperforms EL2N and GraNd (both from Paul et al.) on noisy and imbalanced datasets: on Food101-N ×4, PEAKS reaches 62.2% versus EL2N's 44.7% and GraNd's 50.0%, and on WebVision ×4, PEAKS achieves 59.0% versus EL2N's 41.5% and GraNd's 48.8%. Against the Moderate Embedding baseline (Xia et al.), PEAKS wins on Food101-N (62.2% vs. 62.0%) and WebVision (59.0% vs. 54.8%) but trails on CIFAR100 (82.7% vs. 74.9%—actually PEAKS wins here too) and is close on Food101 (72.9% vs. 70.5%). The Easy Embedding (Welling et al.) and Hard Embedding (Sorscher et al.) baselines are consistently outperformed across all datasets, with Hard Emb. performing particularly poorly on noisy data (e.g., 21.4% on Food101-N ×4). The Uncertainty baseline (Guo et al.) is competitive on clean Food101 (71.5% vs. 72.9%) but falls well behind PEAKS on WebVision (45.6% vs. 59.0%). The one notable exception is CIFAR100, where the simple 'Wrong Low Confidence' strategy (83.9%) edges out PEAKS (82.7%), which the authors attribute to CIFAR100's clean labels making misclassification a reliable difficulty signal.

## Relation to Prior Work

PEAKS derives its scoring function from a first-order Taylor expansion of model updates, where the Neural Tangent Kernel (Jacot et al., 2018) provides the geometric coupling term and the cross-entropy gradient provides the prediction error term—effectively unifying the EL2N/GraNd lineage (Paul et al., 2021) with kernel-based representation geometry in a single product form. Conceptually, the paper departs from static and adaptive coreset selection (which require full dataset access) and online batch selection (which discards samples after one update) by introducing the Incremental Data Selection (IDS) formulation: a streaming, fixed-budget setting where selected examples are permanently retained and repeatedly used. The key methodological departure is that PEAKS multiplies prediction error by kernel similarity rather than treating them as independent criteria, a choice the authors argue selectively promotes hard-but-clean examples over noisy or atypical ones.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 4× data reduction on WebVision (50k PEAKS ≈ 200k random) is practically significant for large-scale real-world training where data collection and compute costs dominate. The IDS formulation opens a new axis of study—how training data size, sample repetition, and selection quality interact under streaming constraints—that connects to neural scaling laws and on-device learning. Limitations include the stationary-distribution assumption, the gap to upper bounds on noisy/imbalanced datasets (suggesting the need for better plasticity or regularization), and the observation that on clean, well-curated data (CIFAR100), simple heuristics like 'wrong and low confidence' can match or beat PEAKS, limiting the method's advantage in idealized settings.
