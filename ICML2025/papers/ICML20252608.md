## Problem

Foundation models in NLP and vision learn transferable generalities (tokens, contours) across tasks, but graph-structured data lacks an analogous unified learning unit because node-, edge-, and graph-level tasks operate on fundamentally different sets of nodes, creating task heterogeneity that prevents a single model from generalizing across all graph tasks. Prior approaches using subgraphs or graphons either incur high computational cost, rely on strong generative assumptions, or fail to capture substructures that message-passing GNNs can actually learn.

## Input / Output

The method takes text-attributed graphs whose node features are projected into a shared 768-dimensional space via Sentence-BERT, along with a task specification (node, edge, or graph-level) that determines which nodes are 'task-relevant.' A virtual task node is appended to the original graph and connected to all task-relevant nodes; the GNN encoder then produces the virtual node's embedding as the task-tree representation. During pretraining, two corrupted views (random edge and attribute masking) of each task-tree are generated for reconstruction; during downstream use, the task-tree embedding feeds a linear head for classification or link prediction scores.

## Method

The core contribution is the task-tree: for any graph task, a virtual node is connected to all task-relevant nodes, and the L-layer computation tree rooted at this virtual node serves as a unified learning instance across node-, edge-, and graph-level tasks. Encoding a task-tree is operationally equivalent to running a shared GraphSAGE encoder on the original graph augmented with the virtual node, making it more efficient and more learnable than extracting and storing subgraphs. Pretraining uses a negative-free contrastive reconstruction objective (inspired by BGRL) where two corrupted task-tree views are reconstructed from each other with stop-gradient, L2 normalization, and a KL regularizer that pulls embeddings toward a shared domain subspace. Domain specialization is achieved via instruction tuning: the pretrained model is fine-tuned on a small set of domain-specific task-trees using LLM-generated label descriptions as regression targets. The paper provides theoretical guarantees—stability, transferability, and generalization bounds—showing that pretraining on diverse task-trees yields representations that transfer to downstream tasks with minimal fine-tuning.

## Results

In the fine-tuning setting, GIT-G achieves an average of 75.37% across five domains (Academic 75.82, E-commerce 78.55, KG 75.73, Molecule 74.57, Temporal 64.59), outperforming OFA (73.08%), GraphMAE (72.79%), and supervised GNNs (72.25%). With domain-specific instruction tuning, GIT-S further improves to 75.72% average. In the 3-shot in-context setting, GIT-G reaches 57.82% average vs. OFA's 53.70% and GraphMAE's 52.07%, while GIT-S achieves 60.15%. In zero-shot, GIT-S attains 36.32% average (Molecule 62.83), substantially exceeding OFA (26.14%) and GraphMAE (25.11%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GIT-G outperforms all graph foundation model baselines in the fine-tuning setting: it beats OFA (Liu et al., ICLR 2024) by 2.29 points on average (75.37 vs. 73.08) and GraphMAE (Hou et al., KDD 2022) by 2.58 points (75.37 vs. 72.79), with the largest gains in KG (+3.35 over OFA) and Temporal (+2.28 over GraphMAE). Against supervised GNNs (GCN/GAT/GIN), GIT-G improves by 3.12 points on average (75.37 vs. 72.25), with a notable +8.87 margin on KG. In the 3-shot in-context setting, GIT-G leads OFA by 4.12 points (57.82 vs. 53.70) and GraphMAE by 5.75 points (57.82 vs. 52.07), with the gap widening to +10.58 over OFA on KG. In zero-shot, GIT-S achieves 36.32% average, surpassing OFA by 10.18 points and GraphMAE by 11.21 points, driven primarily by Molecule (62.83 vs. 50.49 for OFA). Against recent SOTA graph foundation models (Table 3), GIT-G leads GraphPrompt+ (75.82 vs. 74.80 Academic), All in One (75.82 vs. 75.25), OpenGraph (75.82 vs. 74.64), and AnyGraph (75.82 vs. 75.01) on Academic, and similarly dominates on KG and Molecule.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on GFT (Wang et al., 2024b), which first introduced computation trees as transferable units for aligning heterogeneous graph tasks, and on BGRL (Thakoor et al., 2022), whose negative-free reconstruction loss (stop-gradient, normalization, KL regularizer) is adapted as the pretraining objective. The key conceptual departure is reframing the learning instance: rather than the subgraph-based units used by OFA, All in One, and most prior graph foundation models, or the model-centric tree vocabulary of GFT, GIT treats the computation tree rooted at a virtual task node as the fundamental learning unit, making it natively compatible with message-passing GNNs. The paper also positions itself against graphon-based transferability theory (Ruiz et al., 2020) and substructure-motif approaches (Zhao et al., 2023), arguing both rely on assumptions or inductive biases that message-passing GNNs cannot effectively exploit.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first theoretical framework providing stability, transferability, and generalization guarantees specifically for cross-task transfer in graph learning, filling a gap that prior empirical graph foundation models (OFA, GFT, All in One) left unaddressed. Practically, GIT demonstrates that a lightweight GraphSAGE encoder pretrained on task-trees from a small set of source graphs can generalize across 30+ datasets in five domains under fine-tuning, few-shot, and zero-shot settings, with instruction tuning closing the gap to domain-specific experts. Key limitations include reliance on text-attributed graphs for feature alignment (sidestepping rather than solving feature heterogeneity), the 1-WL expressiveness ceiling of the message-passing backbone, and the absence of a clear data scaling law—leaving open whether task-trees can scale to truly large, diverse graph corpora or whether more expressive architectures are needed to capture higher-order structural generalities.
