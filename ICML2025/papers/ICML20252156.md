## Problem

Explainable AI metrics such as SHAP values are vulnerable to a form of p-hacking—termed X-hacking—where analysts exploit model multiplicity to select models that produce a predetermined, potentially misleading explanation while maintaining acceptable predictive accuracy. Prior work on model multiplicity was restricted to specific model families and did not cover modern end-to-end AutoML pipelines that jointly search over preprocessing, model class, and hyperparameters, leaving open whether off-the-shelf or custom automated tools could systematically and efficiently find such 'defensible' but contrarian models at scale.

## Input / Output

The method takes a tabular dataset (binary classification from OpenML-CC18), a target feature whose SHAP explanation the analyst wishes to manipulate, and a baseline random forest model whose accuracy defines the defensible threshold. It produces a model from the confirmatory Rashomon set—i.e., one with accuracy at least matching the baseline—whose SHAP-based feature importance ranking or partial-dependence slope for the target feature has been shifted (e.g., the feature drops out of the top-k, or its SHAP sign flips). The directed-search variant additionally outputs a Pareto front trading off accuracy against mean absolute SHAP of the target feature, and the vulnerability analysis outputs a redundancy-based characterization of which features are susceptible.

## Method

The paper formulates X-hacking as a multi-objective optimisation problem with two objectives: maximising predictive accuracy and minimising (or flipping) the mean absolute SHAP of a target feature. Two strategies are implemented: (1) a post-hoc 'cherry-picking' approach that runs auto-sklearn for one hour, then filters the resulting models for those in the Rashomon set whose SHAP rankings confirm the desired hypothesis; and (2) an ad-hoc directed search using a custom AutoML solution that combines auto-sklearn's search space with Optuna's MOTPESampler for Bayesian multi-objective optimisation and Ray Tune for distributed execution over 12 hours. The key empirical finding is that Bayesian MOO reaches the minimum mean absolute SHAP roughly 3× faster than random sampling for susceptible features, and that directed search finds the first confirmatory defensible model in under 6 minutes on average—10× faster than the post-hoc setting. A simulation study further shows that a feature's vulnerability to X-hacking is governed by information redundancy: SHAP attribution can be transferred between collinear features without accuracy loss, but not for independent features.

## Results

Bayesian multi-objective optimisation (MOTPESampler) finds defensible models with manipulated SHAP values on average 3 hours earlier than random sampling over a 12-hour budget, translating to a ~3× speedup for the majority of susceptible features. In the ad-hoc (directed search) setting, a confirmatory defensible model is found in under 6 minutes for 14 of 23 datasets, versus up to 1 hour in the post-hoc (cherry-picking) setting — a ~10× speedup. For 20 out of 92 features tested, no defensible model was found by either optimiser, indicating inherent robustness to X-hacking due to lack of information redundancy. In the post-hoc experiment, roughly one-third of datasets yielded no more-accurate model that displaced the baseline's top feature, while a similar proportion had almost all more-accurate models with different top features.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against external XAI-manipulation baselines; instead it compares two internal strategies. (1) Bayesian multi-objective optimisation (MOTPESampler via Optuna) versus uniform random sampling over the auto-sklearn search space: Bayesian MOO reaches the cumulative minimum mean |SHAP| for the target feature ~3 hours earlier on average across the 92 features tested, while random sampling is marginally faster (~55 min) on the minority of features where it ultimately achieves a lower minimum. (2) Post-hoc cherry-picking (run auto-sklearn for 1 hour, then filter the Rashomon set) versus ad-hoc directed search (custom Ray Tune + Optuna + auto-sklearn pipeline optimising accuracy and mean |SHAP| simultaneously): directed search finds the first confirmatory defensible model in under 6 minutes for 14 of 23 datasets, compared to the full 1-hour budget required in the post-hoc setting, yielding an average ~10× speedup. The baseline model throughout is a default scikit-learn RandomForestClassifier; 'defensible' models are those whose accuracy meets or exceeds this baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes three previously separate research threads—model multiplicity (Rashomon sets, D'Amour et al. 2022; Brunet et al. 2022), p-hacking as a questionable research practice (Head et al. 2015; Gelman & Loken 2013), and adversarial XAI attacks such as fairwashing (Slack et al. 2020; Laberge et al. 2023a)—into a unified threat model called X-hacking. It departs from prior multiplicity work, which studied specific model families in isolation, by extending the search to the full end-to-end AutoML pipeline (preprocessing, model class, hyperparameters) and framing it as a deliberate confirmatory search. The technical implementation builds on auto-sklearn's search space, repurposed with Optuna's MOTPESampler for multi-objective Bayesian optimisation and Ray Tune for distributed execution, a combination the authors note is not available in any single off-the-shelf package.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that XAI explanations—particularly SHAP-based feature importance—are not robust to the many defensible modelling choices available in modern AutoML pipelines, raising fundamental concerns about the reliability of post-hoc explanations in high-stakes domains (healthcare, criminal justice, public policy). The finding that information redundancy among features determines a dataset's vulnerability to X-hacking provides a practical diagnostic, while the 3× speedup from Bayesian optimisation and 10× speedup from directed search over cherry-picking show the threat is not merely theoretical but operationally feasible within modest compute budgets. Open questions include: whether the results generalise beyond SHAP and binary tabular classification, how to effectively detect X-hacking in published work (the proposed explanation-histogram and pipeline-similarity methods remain nascent), and whether pre-registered analysis plans can realistically constrain the AutoML search space. The paper explicitly notes it presents no evidence of X-hacking 'in the wild,' limiting its impact to a proof-of-concept warning.
