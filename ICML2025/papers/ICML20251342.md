## Problem

The Fisher Information Matrix diagonal is a standard measure of parameter importance used in model merging, pruning, sparse training, task embedding, and continual learning, but computing it requires additional per-example gradient computations over training data that are costly and often infeasible (e.g., when training data is unavailable). This paper asks whether the squared gradient accumulator already maintained by adaptive optimizers like Adam can serve as a zero-cost approximation of the Fisher diagonal, and rigorously validates when and why this substitution works.

## Input / Output

The method consumes the squared gradient accumulator v^(t) (an exponential moving average of squared mini-batch gradients) already stored in the optimizer state at the end of training, requiring no additional forward or backward passes. It produces a per-parameter importance vector that serves as a drop-in replacement for the empirical Fisher diagonal. In applications invariant to global rescaling (Fisher merging, pruning, FISH Mask, Task2Vec), the accumulator is used as-is; in EWC, a rescaling by dataset size N is applied to match the regularization strength of the original Fisher-based formulation.

## Method

The key theoretical insight connects the squared gradient accumulator to the joint empirical Fisher: while the standard empirical Fisher computes a sum of squared per-sample gradients, the joint Fisher (Lin et al., 2024) computes the square of the summed gradient, matching the structure of the accumulator's square-of-average operation. The Squisher simply reuses v^(t) from Adam/AdamW as the Fisher diagonal proxy, with the understanding that it introduces three approximations: (1) an EMA over training history rather than a single-point computation, (2) mini-batch gradients rather than full-dataset gradients, and (3) a factor-of-N scale difference. The paper empirically validates the Squisher across six settings spanning five Fisher-based applications (Fisher merging, UBGM merging, Fisher pruning, FISH Mask, Task2Vec, and EWC), showing comparable performance to the true Fisher and consistent outperformance of Fisher-free baselines, with ablations isolating the impact of each approximation.

## Results

Across six experimental settings, the Squisher (squared gradient accumulator) achieves performance comparable to the empirical Fisher diagonal while consistently outperforming Fisher-free baselines. In Fisher Merging on T5-Large, the Squisher reaches 58.0% average accuracy versus 53.6% for the Fisher and 48.5% for parameter averaging. In FISH Mask on BERT-Large (GLUE), the Squisher attains 82.3% average accuracy, nearly identical to the Fisher's 82.4%, compared to 73.2% for random masking. For EWC continual learning on Split CIFAR-100 (task-incremental), the Squisher achieves 75.30% versus 75.96% for the Fisher and 61.82% for the unregularized baseline. Computing the Squisher costs under 0.1 seconds (loading pre-computed optimizer state) versus 2.5 seconds to 51,900 seconds for the Fisher depending on the setting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Fisher Merging method of Matena & Raffel (2022), the Squisher outperforms the Fisher by 4.4 points in average accuracy (58.0 vs. 53.6), though the authors attribute this to instability in multitask merging rather than inherent superiority. In UBGM Merging (Daheim et al., 2024), the Squisher essentially ties the Fisher (94.00 vs. 93.99 average accuracy on RoBERTa text classification). For Fisher Pruning (Theis et al., 2018), the Squisher slightly underperforms the Fisher at 75% sparsity (63.9 vs. 64.8 on VGG-13/CIFAR-100) but still beats random pruning by 5.1 points. In FISH Mask (Sung et al., 2021), the Squisher matches the Fisher almost exactly (82.3 vs. 82.4 average GLUE accuracy). For Task2Vec embeddings (Achille et al., 2019), the Squisher achieves a higher in-class MRR (0.468 vs. 0.421) and lower average rank (3.8 vs. 4.6) than the Fisher. In EWC (Kirkpatrick et al., 2017), the Squisher is within 0.7 points of the Fisher on Split CIFAR-100 task-incremental (75.30 vs. 75.96) and actually outperforms it on Split MNIST domain-incremental (69.87 vs. 65.75). Both the Squisher and Fisher substantially outperform the parameter averaging baseline of Utans (1996) across all merging settings, and the Squisher dominates the dataset-size heuristic of Vu et al. (2020) on task transferability prediction (MRR 0.468 vs. 0.136 in-class).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Lin et al. (2024), whose joint Fisher formulation provides the theoretical bridge showing that the square of aggregated gradients corresponds to the diagonal of a Fisher information matrix, making it structurally compatible with the squared gradient accumulator in adaptive optimizers. It also connects to the broader Fisher-based methods literature (Fisher merging, Fisher pruning, FISH Mask, Task2Vec, EWC) and to recent work linking adaptive optimizers to second-order methods (IVON, AdaFisher, FAdam). The key departure is that while prior work either computed the Fisher explicitly at additional cost or used optimizer statistics in a modified optimizer, this paper shows the standard Adam/AdamW squared gradient accumulator can be recycled as-is as a zero-cost Fisher diagonal proxy across diverse downstream applications.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: computing the Fisher diagonal can take hours (e.g., ~8 hours for Fisher merging, ~14 hours for Task2Vec), requires access to training data, and is not supported in popular tooling like mergekit. The Squisher eliminates all of these barriers since the accumulator is already available at the end of any Adam/AdamW training run. Limitations include sensitivity to training duration (the EMA needs sufficient steps to stabilize) and the need for careful rescaling in magnitude-sensitive settings like EWC. Open questions include whether non-diagonal Fisher approximations from advanced optimizers yield further gains, and whether the community will adopt sharing optimizer state dicts alongside model weights to enable post-hoc Squisher-based methods.
