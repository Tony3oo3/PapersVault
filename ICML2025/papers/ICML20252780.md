## Problem

Standard LLM-as-a-Judge frameworks (e.g., AlpacaEval) rank models via pairwise comparisons against a fixed baseline, implicitly assuming the judge's preferences are transitive. This paper shows that LLM judges exhibit both hard and soft non-transitive preferences, making rankings highly sensitive to baseline choice—only 20% of models maintain consistent rank positions across 20 different baseline selections. The impact of this non-transitivity on evaluation reliability had been largely unexplored.

## Input / Output

The method consumes pre-generated outputs from 20 LLMs on AlpacaEval instructions, evaluated by a judge LLM (GPT-4-Turbo or GPT-3.5-Turbo) that produces continuous preference scores via softmax over log-probabilities of model identifier tokens. Each comparison is run in both output orders (position switching) with two API calls per order to mitigate position bias and API randomness. Outputs include per-instruction non-transitivity metrics (PNT for hard violations, SNTD via Jensen-Shannon divergence from transitivity-predicted win rates), a full 20×20 win-rate matrix, and final Elo rankings derived from Bradley-Terry coefficients.

## Method

The paper introduces SNTD, which quantifies soft non-transitivity by computing the Jensen-Shannon divergence between observed pairwise win rates and win rates predicted under a transitivity assumption (derived from Bradley-Terry quality gaps of the other two pairs in a triplet). To produce robust rankings, it replaces the fixed-baseline paradigm with a round-robin tournament where every model is compared against every other, then scores models using a soft Bradley-Terry model that uses continuous judge preferences as soft labels rather than discrete win/loss counts, converting coefficients to Elo ratings. To address the O(m²) comparison cost of round-robin, the authors propose SWIM (Swiss-Wise Iterative Matchmaking), which dynamically matches a new model against the already-ranked model whose BT coefficient is closest to its own, iterating in a binary-search-like fashion to reduce comparisons to O(m log m) while preserving ranking quality. Experiments confirm that non-transitivity is jointly driven by position bias and the judge's inherent reasoning limitations, and that position switching (rather than random assignment) reduces non-transitivity by 17–44% for stronger judges.

## Results

The round-robin tournament with Bradley-Terry scoring improves correlation with Chatbot Arena rankings over AlpacaEval 2.0: Spearman correlation increases from 81.4% to 85.4% (without length control) and from 95.0% to 96.4% (with length control); Kendall correlation increases from 63.2% to 68.4% and from 82.1% to 86.3% respectively. Non-transitivity is most pronounced when model performance gaps are small (MM scenario: PNT=8.45%, SNTD=0.1431 for GPT-4-Turbo), and weaker judges (GPT-3.5-Turbo) exhibit consistently higher non-transitivity across all scenarios (PNT≈21–23%, SNTD≈0.26). Position switching reduces non-transitivity by 17–44% for stronger judges, and SWIM tournaments achieve nearly identical ranking quality to full round-robin while reducing comparisons from O(m²) to O(m log m).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's sole baseline is AlpacaEval 2.0 (Li et al., 2023), the standard baseline-fixed pairwise comparison framework using GPT-4-Turbo as judge. The proposed round-robin + Bradley-Terry method outperforms AlpacaEval on all four correlation metrics with Chatbot Arena: +4.0% Spearman and +5.2% Kendall without length control, and +1.4% Spearman and +4.2% Kendall with length control. The gains are larger without length control, suggesting that the round-robin framework's advantage is partly independent of verbosity debiasing. The SWIM variant, designed to reduce computational cost from O(m²) to O(m log m) comparisons, achieves nearly identical correlation to full round-robin across all model pool sizes tested (Figure 6-Right), confirming that the efficiency gain does not come at the cost of ranking quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM-as-a-Judge evaluation paradigm (Zheng et al., 2023) and specifically the AlpacaEval framework (Li et al., 2023) by questioning its foundational assumption of transitive preferences. It draws a conceptual bridge from non-transitive game theory in multi-agent reinforcement learning (Balduzzi et al., 2019; Czarnecki et al., 2020), where tournament-based evaluation against diverse opponents is standard practice, and applies this to LLM ranking. The Bradley-Terry model (Bradley & Terry, 1952) serves as the scoring mechanism, and the SWIM tournament borrows from Swiss-system and binary-search matchmaking to reduce computational cost from O(nm²) to O(nm log m).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that only 20% of models maintain consistent rank positions when the baseline model is changed in AlpacaEval-style frameworks is a significant methodological concern for a widely-used evaluation tool, suggesting that leaderboard rankings may be more arbitrary than previously recognized. The counterintuitive result that position switching reduces non-transitivity for strong judges but increases it for weak judges provides nuanced guidance for practitioners choosing debiasing strategies. Open questions remain about whether non-transitivity manifests in pointwise evaluation, whether the Bradley-Terry model's transitive scalar assumption is sufficient for capturing multi-dimensional model capabilities, and whether human preferences in Chatbot Arena themselves exhibit non-transitivity that limits achievable alignment.
