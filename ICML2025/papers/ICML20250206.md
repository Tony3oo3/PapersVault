## Problem

Standard pooling methods (AvgPool, MaxPool, ClsToken) used to aggregate transformer output embeddings are treated as arbitrary design choices without theoretical justification, and they suffer catastrophic performance drops when the signal-to-noise ratio of inputs fluctuates. The paper asks: can a single pooling mechanism robustly retain task-relevant signal while attenuating distractors across the full spectrum of noise levels, and what are the theoretical guarantees for such a mechanism?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250206/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a set of N transformer output embeddings (a matrix X ∈ R^{N×d}) and a query vector x_q selected from the signal subset of the input, and produces a single pooled d-dimensional vector via cross-attention. The query selection is a critical design choice: in RL it is the agent's own state token, in vision it is a central or focal patch embedding, and a residual connection adds the pooled output back to the query. The pooled vector then feeds into downstream heads for class labels or action distributions, with the key constraint that the query must contain task-relevant information to enable effective signal-noise separation.

## Method

AdaPool performs global vector pooling via cross-attention with a single query, computing softmax-normalized weights from a scaled dot-product relation kernel between the query and each input embedding, then taking a weighted sum of the value-projected inputs. The paper frames pooling as a degenerate single-cluster vector quantization problem and defines signal loss as MSE between the pooled output and the signal-subset centroid, proving that the signal-optimal quantizer assigns weight 1/k to signal vectors and 0 to noise. They show AvgPool and MaxPool are special cases of AdaPool (via zero query projection and infinite-temperature multi-head limits, respectively) and derive explicit error bounds on AdaPool's approximation of the optimal quantizer in terms of the margin M between signal and noise relation scores and the neighborhood widths ε_s, ε_η. The key practical insight is selecting the query from the signal subset rather than a learned embedding or input centroid, making the relation kernel invariant to global rotations and robust to varying signal-noise ratios. Theoretical results are validated on a synthetic KNN-centroid task, multi-agent RL (MPE simple centroid and simple tag with increasing obstacle noise), relational reasoning (BoxWorld with entity vs. pixel observations), and CIFAR-10/100 image classification.

## Results

On the synthetic KNN-Centroid task (N=128, d=16), AdaPool achieves the lowest signal loss (MSE) at every SNR level, with the largest margins at low SNR: 0.037 vs. 0.093 (AvgPool), 0.066 (MaxPool), and 0.102 (ClsToken) at SNR=0.01. On CIFAR-10, AdaPool with a Focal query reaches 87.98% top-1 accuracy, outperforming MaxPool (87.65%), AvgPool (87.15%), and ClsToken (84.52%); on CIFAR-100, AdaPool (Mean query) reaches 61.23% vs. MaxPool (60.55%), AvgPool (59.63%), and ClsToken (55.56%). In MPE Simple Tag, AdaPool suffers the smallest mean-reward decline (50.9%) as noise obstacles increase, compared to AvgPool (77.4%), ClsToken (70.4%), and MaxPool (60.7%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

AdaPool consistently outperforms ClsToken (the BERT/ViT standard) across all benchmarks: on CIFAR-10, Ada-Focal achieves 87.98% vs. ClsToken's 84.52% (+3.46 pts), and on CIFAR-100, Ada-Mean reaches 61.23% vs. 55.56% (+5.67 pts). On the synthetic KNN-Centroid task, AdaPool beats ClsToken at every SNR level, with the gap widening at low SNR (0.037 vs. 0.102 at SNR=0.01, a 2.7× improvement). In MPE Simple Tag, AdaPool's mean-reward decline under increasing noise (50.9%) is substantially smaller than ClsToken's (70.4%) and AvgPool's (77.4%), confirming its robustness advantage. In BoxWorld, MaxPool initially outperforms AdaPool on entity-level observations (exploiting the white goal gem's maximal pixel values per Corollary 3.8), but collapses to the worst performance under pixel-level observations, while AdaPool maintains the smallest performance decline. Overall, AdaPool wins or ties on every benchmark, with its advantage most pronounced in low-to-mid SNR regimes where standard pooling methods suffer their greatest signal loss.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the AdaPool technique from Stergiou & Poppe (2023), which applied attention-based pooling to vision, and on the vector quantization framework of Gray (1984), to reframe transformer output pooling as a lossy compression problem. It draws a conceptual bridge to the associative memory literature (Hopfield networks, Dense AMs, and the equivalence between attention and Hopfield update rules shown by Ramsauer et al., 2021), positioning adaptive pooling as a mechanism for selective recall under interference. The key departure from prior work is the provision of explicit error bounds on AdaPool's approximation of the signal-optimal quantizer for arbitrary SNR, along with a principled query selection strategy (using a signal vector rather than a learned embedding or input centroid) that prior attention-pooling methods lacked.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first theoretical framework for analyzing how pooling method choice interacts with input noise characteristics, giving practitioners a principled tool for selecting aggregation strategies rather than relying on intuition or trial-and-error. The consistent empirical validation across synthetic, RL (MPE), relational reasoning (BoxWorld), and vision (CIFAR) benchmarks strengthens the practical relevance. Key limitations include the binary signal/noise partition assumption (a vector is entirely signal or entirely noise), the requirement for domain knowledge to select an appropriate query vector, and the dependence of tight bounds on a large margin between signal and noise relation scores. Open questions include generalization to mixed signal-noise vectors (partially addressed via multi-head partitioning), applicability to non-transformer relational architectures, and whether the query selection strategy transfers to settings where no obvious signal-rich token exists.
