## Problem

Machine-learned interatomic potentials (MLIPs) require large, expensive training datasets generated via ab initio or DFT calculations, and they exhibit poor generalization and robustness during molecular dynamics simulations when data is sparse. A critical gap is that high-accuracy reference methods like CCSD(T)/CBS often cannot provide force labels, yet existing weakly supervised approaches either require those force labels or fail to encode the physical constraints (conservative forces, Taylor-expansion consistency) that govern atomic interactions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252314/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes atomic configurations as point clouds (3D positions r_i ∈ R³ and atomic numbers Z_i ∈ N) and outputs a scalar potential energy plus per-atom force vectors in R³. During training, the method internally generates perturbed copies of each configuration by adding small displacement vectors δr (magnitude typically 0.01–0.025 Å, constrained to less than ~30% of the shortest bond length) to create weak supervision signals. Forces may be computed as negative energy gradients or predicted via a separate branch; the PIWSL losses are agnostic to this choice and operate on the predicted energy and force values at both original and perturbed configurations.

## Method

The paper introduces two physics-informed weakly supervised loss functions that augment the standard supervised MLIP objective. The PITC loss enforces consistency between the model's directly predicted energy at a perturbed configuration S_{δr} and the energy estimated via a (first- or second-order) Taylor expansion from the original configuration S, using the model's own predicted forces as expansion coefficients—requiring no reference force labels. The PISC loss enforces path-independence of energy differences (the conservative-force property) by comparing the energy at a target point reached via a single perturbation δr against the energy reached via two sequential perturbations δr' and δr'' along a different spatial path. Perturbation directions are sampled uniformly at random (or adversarially via gradient ascent on the energy loss), and the combined PIWSL objective is trained jointly with the standard energy/force regression loss. This framework is architecture-agnostic, applies to any equivariant message-passing MLIP, and enables effective fine-tuning of foundation models on sparse energy-only datasets.

## Results

On the ANI-1x heterogeneous molecular dataset with 100 training configurations, PIWSL reduces energy RMSE by 10–61% across models (e.g., Equiformer v2: 398.71→154.98 kcal/mol; PaiNN: 168.01→109.89 kcal/mol) and force RMSE by up to 12% (PaiNN: 21.33→18.76 kcal/mol/Å). With 1000 training configurations, gains remain substantial (Equiformer v2 energy: 54.52→20.89 kcal/mol; PaiNN: 56.62→24.53 kcal/mol). On the TiO₂ inorganic dataset with 2000 configurations, PIWSL still reduces energy RMSE from 1.78 to 0.90 kcal/mol (eSCN) and force RMSE from 0.42 to 0.16 kcal/mol/Å. For fine-tuning MACE-OFF on MD17(CCSD) without force labels, PIWSL achieves ~40% lower RMSE (energy: 1.21→0.72; force: 6.90→3.77 kcal/mol/Å) and matches the accuracy of a model trained on nearly 2× more data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PIWSL consistently outperforms the NoisyNode data-augmentation baseline (Godwin et al., 2022) across nearly all model–dataset–size combinations. NoisyNode frequently *deteriorates* accuracy because it does not model the proper energy/force response to coordinate perturbations: on ANI-1x with 100 samples, PaiNN energy RMSE worsens from 168.01 (baseline) to 464.55 (NoisyNode), while PIWSL achieves 109.89; Equiformer v2 energy worsens from 398.71 to 632.38, while PIWSL reaches 154.98. On TiO₂ with 2000 samples, NoisyNode degrades eSCN energy RMSE from 1.78 to 20.90 kcal/mol, whereas PIWSL improves it to 0.90. Compared to the Taylor-expansion weak-label method of Cooper et al. (2020), PIWSL replaces reference energy/force labels with model-predicted values, enabling training without force labels (critical for CCSD(T)/CBS fine-tuning) and additionally improving force RMSE, which the Cooper et al. approach does not address. The only case where PIWSL does not clearly win is SchNet on ANI-1x at N=1000, where all three methods yield nearly identical energy RMSE (~31.5 kcal/mol), likely due to SchNet's limited capacity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Taylor-expansion-based weak label approach of Cooper et al. (2020), which used reference energy and force labels to generate approximate labels for perturbed configurations, by replacing those reference values with model-predicted energies and forces, making the supervision truly self-consistent and eliminating the need for force labels. It also departs from NoisyNode (Godwin et al., 2022), which adds random perturbations to atomic coordinates as a denoising task, by instead enforcing the physical constraint that energy changes along a perturbation must be consistent with the predicted force (negative energy gradient). The spatial consistency loss (PISC) is a novel addition grounded in the path-independence property of conservative forces, with no direct prior equivalent in the MLIP literature. The method is architecture-agnostic and applies to existing equivariant message-passing models (SchNet, PaiNN, eSCN, Equiformer, MACE) without modification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PIWSL addresses a critical practical bottleneck: the high cost of generating ab initio training data for MLIPs, particularly for new molecular or material systems where datasets are inherently sparse. The ability to improve force predictions without explicit force labels opens the door to fine-tuning foundation models using CCSD(T)/CBS energies, where force calculations are computationally prohibitive. Limitations include evaluation only on homogeneous inorganic datasets (TiO2, LMNTO) for materials, and the method is restricted to MLIPs predicting energies and forces. Open questions include whether combining PIWSL with architectural modifications like DeNS yields further gains, whether higher-order Taylor expansions or ODE-based formulations improve accuracy, and how the method generalizes to heterogeneous inorganic systems.
