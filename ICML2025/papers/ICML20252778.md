## Problem

The state-of-the-art kernel solver for nonlinear PDEs (DAKS) embeds differential operators within the kernel, requiring manual construction of a large Gram matrix whose dimension exceeds the number of collocation points. This makes computation intractable when tens of thousands or millions of collocation points are needed to capture complex solution features such as sharp shocks or high frequencies. The paper asks whether one can retain the theoretical guarantees of kernel-based PDE solving while eliminating the operator-augmented Gram matrix entirely.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a nonlinear PDE specified by its interior and boundary operators (each decomposed into linear differential operators), source and boundary functions, and a regular grid of collocation points (e.g., 600×200 for Burgers' equation). Kernel hyperparameters (per-dimension length-scales, nugget) and regularization strengths α, β are tuned jointly. The output is the approximate solution u(x;η) in standard kernel interpolation form, with all required derivatives obtained by analytically differentiating the interpolant rather than estimating them as separate variables.

## Method

The key insight is to model the solution using a standard kernel interpolation u(x;η) = κ(x,M) K_MM^{-1} η where η are free solution values at collocation points, then approximate PDE derivatives by differentiating this interpolant directly—completely removing differential operators from the kernel. To scale to massive collocation counts, the method places points on a tensor-product grid and uses a product kernel κ(x,x') = ∏ κ_j(x_j, x_j'), which makes K_MM a Kronecker product of small per-dimension matrices. Solution evaluation and derivative computation then reduce to mode-k tensor-matrix multiplications with only the local m_j×m_j inverses, avoiding the full M×M matrix entirely (e.g., three 100×100 inverses instead of one 10⁶×10⁶). The learning objective minimizes the RKHS norm plus residual and boundary losses, solved via ADAM with automatic differentiation in JAX. A convergence proof establishes an H^l error bound of order h^{min(k,t,τ)} under standard PDE stability and Sobolev regularity assumptions, matching the rate of the richer DAKS model space.

## Results

On challenging PDEs requiring massive collocation points, SKS achieves L² errors of 2.28×10⁻³ on Burgers' equation (ν=0.001, 120K points), 4.44×10⁻⁶ on Allen-Cahn (a=15, 40K points), and 2.98×10⁻⁵ on Allen-Cahn (a=20, 40K points). On simpler benchmarks with ~2400–4800 points, SKS matches or beats DAKS in most cases (e.g., 2.51×10⁻⁵ vs. 7.78×10⁻⁵ on Eikonal PDE). PINN fails entirely on Allen-Cahn (errors ~10⁰) while SKS reaches 10⁻⁵–10⁻⁶. DAKS becomes computationally infeasible beyond ~4800 collocation points, whereas SKS scales to 120K via Kronecker product structure.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DAKS (Chen et al., 2021), SKS achieves lower L² error on the Eikonal PDE (2.51×10⁻⁵ vs. 7.78×10⁻⁵ at 2400 points) and the nonlinear elliptic PDE at 1200 points (6.80×10⁻⁶ vs. 8.65×10⁻⁴), but DAKS edges out SKS on Burgers' at 4800 points (9.76×10⁻⁵ vs. 3.21×10⁻⁴) and the nonlinear elliptic PDE at 2400 points (1.68×10⁻⁷ vs. 1.83×10⁻⁶). The critical advantage emerges at scale: DAKS is computationally infeasible beyond ~4800 collocation points due to its enlarged Gram matrix, while SKS handles 120K points efficiently. Against PINN (Raissi et al., 2019), SKS wins decisively on the Allen-Cahn equation, where PINN's spectral bias causes it to fail completely (L² errors of 4–6) while SKS achieves 10⁻⁵–10⁻⁶. On Burgers' with ν=0.02, PINN slightly outperforms SKS at 600–2400 points, but SKS pulls ahead at 4800 points and at the challenging ν=0.001 setting. Overall, SKS's key differentiator is its ability to scale to tens of thousands of collocation points without sparse or low-rank approximations, a regime where both baselines either degrade or become infeasible.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the DAKS framework of Chen et al. (2021), which embeds differential operators directly into the kernel to jointly estimate solution values and their derivatives, by instead using a standard kernel interpolation form and differentiating the interpolant to obtain derivatives. This eliminates the need to construct a large Gram matrix with Q×Q operator-pair sub-blocks, reducing the matrix size from (Q_Ω·M_Ω + (Q−Q_Ω)·(M−M_Ω))² to M×M. For efficient computation, the paper leverages the Kronecker product structure from structured GP literature (Wilson & Nickisch 2015, Saatcci 2012) but applies it in the PDE-solving context where grid-based collocation is natural. The convergence analysis inherits the roadmap and stability assumptions from Batlle et al. (2023) but requires a more sophisticated proof to show that the reduced model space still achieves comparable convergence rates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that kernel-based PDE solvers can now scale to tens of thousands or even millions of collocation points without sparse or low-rank approximations, which is critical for capturing sharp features in low-viscosity Burgers' or high-frequency Allen-Cahn solutions where PINNs and DAKS fail or become infeasible. The theoretical result that a reduced model space (no operator-augmented basis) matches the convergence rate of the richer DAKS framework is reassuring and simplifies implementation. Open questions include developing faster second-order optimizers (Gauss-Newton diverges in this setting), reducing sensitivity to kernel hyperparameters (length-scale changes cause order-of-magnitude error shifts), and extending to 3D or higher-dimensional PDEs where the Kronecker product cost grows with dimensionality.
