## Problem

Existing tools beyond conditional independence constraints (rank constraints, high-order moments, matrix decompositions) enable latent variable causal discovery but none have been adapted to handle selection bias, forcing practitioners to revert to basic CI-based methods like FCI that cannot identify latent structures. This paper bridges the gap by developing the first statistical tool beyond CI constraints that simultaneously handles both latent variables and selection bias.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250469/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method requires tabular observational data with a known one-factor measurement structure (i.e., which observed variables are measurements of which latent variables, with each latent having at least two observed children). It assumes a linear Gaussian data-generating process and linear selection mechanisms (truncation, probit, logistic, etc.). The output is a partial ancestral graph (PAG) over the latent variables, from which both latent causal relations and selection bias structures (e.g., which latent variables are ancestors of selection) can be read off.

## Method

The core contribution is a generalized rank constraint with a precise graphical criterion (Theorem 1): for data generated by a linear Gaussian model and subjected to linear selection, the rank of any covariance submatrix in the biased data equals the minimum t-separation set size in a selection-augmented graph (where selection response variables Y are added as nodes) minus the number of selection variables. The proof proceeds in three steps—pointwise Gaussian conditioning, multi-valued Gaussian selection sets, and non-Gaussian selection noise—showing that the correction term from selection cannot increase the rank beyond the t-separation bound. For the one-factor model, Proposition 3 shows that d-separations among unobserved latent variables persist as low-rank structures in observed covariances even under selection, enabling constraint-based algorithms like FCI to recover the latent PAG directly from rank tests on observed data.

## Results

On synthetic data (5–20 latent variables with 2–3 measurements each, under truncated probit selection), the proposed method achieves the lowest total number of differing edge marks between the estimated and ground-truth PAG across all variable counts and both Gaussian and exponential selection noise, with the performance gap over baselines widening as the number of latent variables increases. On real-world data, the method recovers interpretable selection structures: in the World Value Survey it identifies Social Trust as a potential ancestor of selection across Canada, China, and Germany, and in the Big Five Personality dataset it detects a selection mechanism driven by Agreeableness. Runtime is under one second for ≤15 latent variables and under five minutes for 20.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against three baselines on synthetic data: FCI (Spirtes et al., 1999/2000), PC (Spirtes & Glymour, 1991), and BOSS (Andrews et al., 2023). The proposed method outperforms all three on the edge-mark-difference metric across every setting (5, 10, 15, 20 latent variables; Gaussian and exponential noise). FCI is the most relevant baseline since it is the only existing method that simultaneously handles latent variables and selection bias, but it relies solely on CI constraints and thus cannot exploit the additional rank information that the generalized rank constraints provide. PC does not model latent variables or selection at all, and BOSS, while a strong score-based DAG learner, also operates only on observed variables. The advantage of the proposed method grows with problem size, indicating that the rank constraints become increasingly informative as the latent structure becomes more complex. On real-world data (WVS, BIG5), no direct numerical comparison is made; instead, the method's output PAGs are interpreted qualitatively to identify plausible selection mechanisms.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate lines of work: the rank constraints and t-separation criterion of Sullivant et al. (2010), which extend CI constraints to handle latent variables in linear Gaussian models, and the selection-augmented graph framework of Bareinboim & Pearl (2012), which encodes selection mechanisms into causal DAGs. The key conceptual departure is showing that the algebraic rank structure of covariance submatrices survives selection bias—even though selection destroys the underlying linear Gaussian distribution—thereby bridging a gap where FCI (handling both latent variables and selection via CI only) was the sole available tool. The application to the one-factor model of Silva et al. (2003) demonstrates that d-separations among latent variables persist as recoverable rank constraints in observed data under selection.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first statistical tool beyond conditional independencies that simultaneously handles latent variables and selection bias, unblocking latent variable causal discovery in settings where FCI's CI-only approach is insufficient. The main open question is a complete characterization of rank equivalence classes (analogous to MAGs for CI constraints), which the authors explicitly leave for future work. Practical impact is demonstrated on the World Value Survey and Big Five Personality datasets, where the method recovers interpretable selection mechanisms (e.g., Social Trust as a selection ancestor, Agreeableness-based selection) that were previously invisible to CI-based methods. Limitations include the requirement of linear Gaussian data generation and linear selection mechanisms, and the method currently applies to the one-factor model specifically rather than general latent structures.
