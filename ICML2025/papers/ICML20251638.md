## Problem

Existing DPO-based alignment methods for multimodal LLMs apply a static β uniformly across all preference data, leading to imbalanced responsiveness: the model overfits on easy-to-distinguish samples (large image-text similarity gaps) while underfitting on hard-to-distinguish ones (small gaps). This imbalance manifests as stagnating reward gaps on difficult data during training, limiting the model's ability to distinguish subtle hallucinations from correct descriptions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251638/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on preference pairs (image, prompt, preferred response y_w, rejected response y_l) from the RLAIF-V dataset (22k samples). A key preprocessing step decomposes each response into self-contained sub-sentences of at most 77 tokens (to fit CLIP's token limit) using LLaMA-3, then computes per-sub-sentence CLIP image-text similarity scores offline. At training time, the model consumes a batch of N=16 preference instances and produces updated policy parameters; the output is a text response generated by the aligned MLLM given an image and prompt.

## Method

DAMA dynamically modulates the DPO penalty β per-sample through two multiplicative factors. The data-aware factor α_D is computed offline: CLIP similarity scores for sub-sentences of y_w and y_l are normalized via softmax into probabilities, their difference δ is sigmoid-transformed and ratio-normalized against the dataset mean, yielding a hardness estimate that scales β inversely (larger β for easy data to prevent overfitting, smaller β for hard data to relax constraints). The model-aware factor α_M is computed online per batch: implicit reward gaps between y_w and y_l are calculated, outliers are filtered via a mask based on squared distances from the batch mean (keeping K=12 of N=16 samples), and the filtered mean gap is sigmoid-normalized against a moving-average estimate (γ=0.9). The two factors are combined element-wise (α = α_D · α_M) to produce a per-instance β_C = β · α, which is then used in the standard DPO loss, enabling the model to simultaneously adapt to data difficulty and its own real-time learning progress.

## Results

On Object HalBench, DAMA-7B achieves 90.9% response-level and 95.3% mentioned-level non-hallucination rates, surpassing GPT-4V (86.4% and 92.7%) and reducing hallucination by 90.0% and 95.3% respectively over the LLaVA-1.5-7B baseline. On AMBER, DAMA-13B reaches 84.3% Accuracy and 88.1% F1, exceeding GPT-4V (83.4% and 87.4%). On MMHal-Bench, DAMA-7B improves the hallucination score by 28.4% over DPO (2.76 vs 2.15), and on MM-Vet, DAMA-7B scores 32.8 vs 30.4 for DPO. Overall, DAMA-7B gains +16.1% on Object HalBench Non-Rsp and +10.3% on AMBER ACC compared to vanilla DPO.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DAMA-7B outperforms all hallucination-specific baselines (VCD, Less-is-More, OPERA, CCA-LLaVA) by a wide margin on Object HalBench Non-Rsp (90.9% vs. 51.2–59.7%) and AMBER ACC (83.3% vs. 71.8–77.7%). Among preference-optimization baselines, DAMA-7B surpasses RLAIF-V (90.9 vs. 89.5 Non-Rsp; 95.3 vs. 94.8 Non-Men), TPO-7B (83.3 vs. 79.3 ACC; 87.0 vs. 85.0 F1), POVID (83.3 vs. 82.9 ACC, though POVID edges ahead on F1 at 87.4 vs. 87.0), and all other DPO variants (HA-DPO, CSR, V-DPO, RLHF-V) by 5–10+ points on AMBER. At 13B scale, DAMA-13B beats TPO-13B on AMBER (84.3 vs. 83.9 ACC; 88.1 vs. 88.0 F1) and RLHF-V-13B on Object HalBench (89.1 vs. 87.8 Non-Rsp). Notably, DAMA-7B surpasses GPT-4V on Object HalBench (90.9 vs. 86.4 Non-Rsp; 95.3 vs. 92.7 Non-Men) and DAMA-13B surpasses GPT-4V on AMBER (84.3 vs. 83.4 ACC; 88.1 vs. 87.4 F1), though GPT-4V remains substantially stronger on LLaVA Bench (98.0 vs. 68.0) and MM-Vet (67.7 vs. 32.8).

## Relation to Prior Work

DAMA extends DPO (Rafailov et al., 2024) for multimodal LLM alignment by replacing the static β hyperparameter with a dynamically computed one that reflects both data hardness (estimated via CLIP image-text similarity on decomposed sub-sentences) and real-time model responsiveness (estimated via implicit reward gaps). This is conceptually related to β-DPO (Wu et al., 2024b), which introduced dynamic β for text-only DPO, but DAMA grounds the adaptation in multimodal-specific signals (CLIP scores, vision-anchored reward gaps) and combines the two awareness signals via element-wise multiplication. The paper positions itself within the MLLM hallucination-mitigation literature, specifically in the preference-optimization sub-area that includes RLAIF-V, V-DPO, TPO, and CSR, departing from those methods by targeting the optimization dynamics rather than data construction or token-level regularization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies and quantifies a concrete failure mode of DPO in MLLMs—overfitting on easy-to-distinguish pairs while stagnating on hard-to-distinguish ones—and shows that a simple β-modification strategy can substantially close this gap, achieving >90% non-hallucination on Object HalBench (7B) and surpassing GPT-4V on several benchmarks. Limitations include reliance on a single offline preference dataset (RLAIF-V, 22k pairs), testing only on LLaVA-1.5 backbones, and a CLIP-based hardness proxy that may not capture all forms of visual inconsistency. Open questions the authors raise include extending DAMA to online/iterative settings, using richer hardness and responsiveness estimators (e.g., semantic entropy, fine-grained visual features), and validating scalability to commercial and omni-modal architectures.
