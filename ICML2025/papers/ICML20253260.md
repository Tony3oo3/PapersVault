## Problem

While there is broad consensus that generative AI requires regulation, there is strong disagreement on whether to extend existing laws or create entirely new frameworks, and the regulatory landscape remains a fragmented patchwork. This paper argues that the two-decade evolution of social media regulation—spanning self-regulation, oversight boards, transparency mandates, and formal legislation—offers concrete, tested lessons that can prevent avoidable mistakes and accelerate the development of effective generative AI policy.

## Input / Output

The analysis takes as input a structured set of affordance features (spatial separation, interactivity, recording, personalization, content moderation, black-box opacity, etc.) drawn from Clark's (1996) medium/control/immediacy taxonomy and Rafaeli & Sudweeks' (1997) interactivity concept, applied to representative generative AI applications (ChatGPT, Claude) and social media platforms (Facebook, X). It also draws on the regulatory history of social media, including the EU Digital Services Act, Section 230, the Christchurch Call, and corporate initiatives like Meta's Oversight Board and TikTok's Research API. The output is a 22-row affordance comparison table identifying shared versus divergent features, plus four policy recommendations each mapped to specific generative AI regulatory measures and their social media precedents.

## Method

The paper employs a two-stage comparative analysis. First, it constructs an affordance comparison grounded in Clark's (1996) three-category framework (medium, control, immediacy) augmented with Rafaeli & Sudweeks' interactivity, identifying which features generative AI and social media share (e.g., content moderation, AI-driven personalization, black-box opacity, abstraction) and where they diverge (e.g., direct conversation partner vs. user-to-user connection, invisible pre-generation moderation vs. post-hoc visible moderation, single output vs. infinite scroll). Second, it maps the regulatory evolution of social media onto generative AI, arguing that shared affordances make the analogy valid for regulatory purposes while differences are mostly in degree rather than kind. The core contribution is four policy recommendations—counter bias via transparency and researcher access, invest in trust and safety for specific harms, promote computational social science research, and adopt a global perspective—each justified by specific social media precedents (Meta Transparency Center, TikTok Research API, Mastodon's decentralized moderation, Christchurch Call) and translated into concrete generative AI regulatory measures such as researcher API access, mandatory audits, dedicated trust and safety teams, and regionally adapted safety policies.

## Results

This is a position paper with no quantitative experimental results. The core analytical contribution is an affordance comparison (Table 1) identifying 22 features across three categories (medium, control, immediacy) shared or differentiated between generative AI and social media. The paper derives four policy recommendations for generative AI regulation: (1) counter bias via transparency, oversight boards, researcher access, and democratic input; (2) address specific regulatory concerns (youth wellbeing, election integrity) and invest in trust and safety; (3) promote computational social science research; and (4) adopt a more global perspective. No numerical metrics, benchmarks, or performance comparisons are reported.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not propose a method to be benchmarked against baselines, so no empirical comparisons are made. The paper's analytical comparison is between generative AI and social media affordances, identifying 14 shared features (e.g., content moderation, use of AI, black-box nature, interactivity, recording, personalization) and 8 differentiating features (e.g., direct connection vs. user connections, invisible vs. visible content moderation, single output vs. infinite content, pre-generation vs. post-generation moderation). The author explicitly acknowledges the analogy is imperfect, noting differences in business models (freemium vs. advertising), potential harm scale, and the probabilistic nature of generative AI models versus deterministic social media algorithms. The paper positions social media regulation not as a baseline to beat but as a source of lessons—both successes (e.g., Meta Transparency Center, TikTok Research API, Mastodon's decentralized moderation) and failures (e.g., Meta's 2025 policy shift ending fact-checking, delays in the Facebook/Instagram Election Study)—to inform generative AI policy design.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper repurposes Clark's (1996) framework for media affordances—augmented with Rafaeli & Sudweeks' (1997) interactivity dimension—to systematically compare generative AI and social media, a comparison not previously formalized in the AI governance literature. It draws on the growing body of work documenting bias in large language models (Durmus et al., 2023; Röttger et al., 2024; Rozado, 2023) and the extensive social media regulation literature (Persily & Tucker, 2020; Appel et al., 2023) to argue that two decades of social media regulatory evolution offer a concrete playbook for the still-fragmented generative AI regulatory landscape. Rather than proposing a new regulatory framework from first principles, the paper positions itself as a bridge, mapping specific social media regulatory precedents (Meta's Transparency Center, TikTok Research API, Mastodon's decentralized moderation, the Christchurch Call) onto analogous generative AI policy needs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses an urgent gap: generative AI regulation is globally fragmented and still in early stages, while social media regulation has already gone through a full cycle of self-regulation, public backlash, and formal legislation. By grounding recommendations in concrete precedents rather than abstract principles, the paper offers policymakers a shorter path to effective regulation and a checklist of mistakes to avoid (e.g., Meta's 2025 reversal of fact-checking, the failure of the original Facebook Oversight Board to satisfy critics). Key open questions remain: whether the analogy holds as generative AI's capabilities diverge further from social media (e.g., agentic behavior, multimodal generation), how to implement recommendations amid deep political polarization, and whether the pace of generative AI development outstrips the iterative regulatory cycles that social media experienced. The paper's main limitation is its reliance on an acknowledged imperfect analogy, and it does not provide empirical evidence that the recommended interventions would be effective for generative AI specifically.
