## Problem

Classical split conformal prediction guarantees exact marginal coverage but fails to provide conditional coverage, which adapts prediction regions to local data structure. Existing remedies either require estimating the full conditional distribution of conformity scores (computationally expensive and hard in high dimensions), partition the covariate space (yielding overly large regions), or rely on localizer functions that degrade in high-dimensional settings. RCP targets the specific gap of achieving approximate conditional validity while preserving exact marginal guarantees, without needing the full conditional distribution.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a basic conformity score function V(x,y), a calibration dataset of N points split into two subsets (m points for conditional quantile estimation and n points for the SCP quantile), a parametric transformation family f_t, and a miscoverage level α. It outputs a prediction set C_α(x) defined by thresholding the rectified score f_{τ̂(x)}^{-1}(V(x,y)) against an empirical quantile computed from the n rectified calibration scores. The calibration split is a key structural requirement: the quantile estimator τ̂(x) must be fit on data disjoint from the SCP calibration set to preserve the exchangeability argument underlying marginal validity.

## Method

RCP's core insight is that marginal and conditional coverage coincide precisely when the conditional (1−α)-quantile of the conformity score is covariate-independent. It constructs 'rectified' scores by applying a transformation f_{τ̂(x)}^{-1} to the basic score, where τ̂(x) is an estimate of the conditional quantile Q_{1−α}(P_{V_φ|X=x}) obtained via quantile regression (minimizing the pinball loss). Standard SCP is then applied to these rectified scores, which automatically restores exact marginal validity while the quantile-based transformation drives conditional coverage toward the nominal level. Unlike prior score-transformation methods that estimate the full conditional distribution or use least-squares residual fitting, RCP only requires a univariate conditional quantile estimate, making it applicable to multi-output problems where standard conformal quantile regression has limited applicability. The paper provides a theoretical lower bound on conditional coverage that is explicitly governed by the approximation error of τ̂(x), and shows asymptotic conditional validity under optimal bandwidth selection for local quantile regression.

## Results

Across 12 multi-output regression datasets, RCP consistently improves worst-slab coverage (WSC) toward the nominal 90% level: base methods (PCP, DCP, ResCP) typically achieve WSC of 0.72–0.85, while their RCP counterparts reach 0.84–0.91. RCP also reduces median prediction set volume in most cases (e.g., on meps_19, RCP-DCP achieves median volume 0.303 vs. DCP's 2.10; on scm1d, RCP-PCP achieves 2.05 vs. PCP's 116). All methods maintain correct marginal coverage. Computationally, RCP is 200–100,000× faster than CPCG while achieving comparable WSC.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RCP outperforms all three base conformal methods (ResCP, PCP, DCP) on worst-slab coverage across nearly all 12 datasets, closing the gap to the nominal 90% level by 5–15 percentage points (e.g., DCP on rf1: 0.715 → RCP-DCP: 0.863). Against SLCP (Han et al., 2022), RCP achieves comparable or better WSC, with the advantage growing on larger datasets (Figure 4). Compared to CQR (Romano et al., 2019), RCP-DCP matches conditional coverage but produces substantially smaller median prediction set volumes due to more flexible set shapes. Against CPCG (Gibbs et al., 2025), RCP achieves similar WSC (within ~1–2 percentage points) but is 200–100,000× faster in wall-clock time, making CPCG impractical for large-scale use; CPCG also failed to converge on two datasets (house, calcofi). RCP additionally reduces median prediction set volume relative to its non-rectified counterparts on most datasets, indicating sharper and more adaptive prediction regions.

## Relation to Prior Work

RCP extends the split conformal prediction framework and the normalized nonconformity score literature (Papadopoulos et al., 2008; Papadopoulos & Haralambous, 2011) by introducing a parametric family of score transformations whose parameter is the conditional quantile of the score distribution. Unlike methods requiring full conditional density estimation (DCP, PCP, Colombo's normalizing flows) or kernel-based localization (Guan, 2023), RCP only needs a conditional quantile regression, making it applicable to multi-output settings where CQR is limited. It also differs from Gibbs et al. (2025) in operating on a transformed score with a simpler calibration procedure, and from prior normalized conformal predictors (Johansson et al., 2021; Lei et al., 2018) by using quantile regression rather than least-squares residual fitting, which is essential for satisfying the rectification condition.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

RCP provides a clean, general wrapper that can be applied on top of existing conformal methods (ResCP, PCP, DCP) to improve conditional coverage while preserving exact marginal guarantees, with a theoretical bound explicitly linking coverage error to quantile estimation accuracy. Its practical significance is underscored by the 200–100,000× speed advantage over CPCG and its applicability to multi-output regression where CQR-based approaches are less natural. Open questions include how the method behaves in very high-dimensional covariate spaces where quantile regression becomes harder, and whether the calibration-set split (or cross-validation variant) can be further optimized for small-sample regimes.
