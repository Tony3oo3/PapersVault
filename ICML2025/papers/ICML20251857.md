## Problem

Existing hallucination detection methods for LLMs—whether retrieval-based, fine-tuning-based, or uncertainty-estimation-based—lack formal statistical guarantees, making them unsuitable for high-stakes domains where precise control over Type I errors (incorrectly classifying hallucinations as truthful) is essential. No prior work provides distribution-free, finite-sample guarantees for LLM factuality testing, leaving a gap between practical detection heuristics and the rigorous error control needed for reliable deployment.

## Input / Output

FACTTEST takes a question q, the LLM's generated answer M(q) (potentially multiple samples for entropy-based scoring), and a calibration dataset of n question-answer pairs with human-verified correctness labels. It outputs a binary decision—answer or abstain—along with a provable guarantee that the Type I error (false positive rate) stays below a user-specified significance level α with probability at least 1−δ. The method is model-agnostic (works with any black-box or white-box LM) and distribution-free, requiring no assumptions about the underlying data distribution and functioning with any number of calibration samples, including very small ones where it conservatively abstains from all questions.

## Method

FACTTEST formulates hallucination detection as a Neyman-Pearson hypothesis testing problem: H₀ (model is incorrect) vs. H₁ (model is correct), with the goal of controlling Type I error below α while minimizing Type II error. Given a score function η̂ measuring answer correctness (e.g., negative semantic entropy, kernel language entropy, or vanilla entropy), the method selects a threshold τ̂_α as the k-th order statistic of scores computed on the calibration subset of incorrect answers, where k is chosen to satisfy a binomial tail bound v(k) ≤ δ. This yields a distribution-free, finite-sample Type I error guarantee (Theorem 2.1) and, under mild conditions on score quality, a Type II error bound relative to the Bayes-optimal NP classifier (Theorem 2.2). The paper further establishes a formal duality between this NP classification threshold and PAC-style conformal prediction (Lemma 1), and extends the framework to covariate shifts by applying rejection sampling with estimated density ratios to transform source-distribution calibration samples into target-distribution samples before threshold selection.

## Results

On ParaRel with OpenLLaMA-13B, FACTTEST-kle15 achieves 83.84% accuracy versus 42.21% for the base model and 59.62% for SelfCheckGPT, a 41.6-point improvement over the base. On FEVER with OpenLLaMA-3B, FACTTEST-se5 reaches 82.40% accuracy compared to 39.74% (base) and 41.97% (SelfCheckGPT), an over-40% gain. Type I error is maintained below the α=0.05 threshold across all datasets and models (e.g., 0.0331–0.0520 on ParaRel). When compared to the training-based R-Tuning baseline, FACTTEST-t (using only half the training data) achieves 34% and 28% higher accuracy on HotpotQA and FEVER respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FACTTEST consistently outperforms SelfCheckGPT across all four datasets and model sizes; for example, on ParaRel-3B it achieves 78.45% vs. 53.60%, and on FEVER-3B it reaches 82.40% vs. 41.97%, with the gap widening for smaller models. Against the training-based R-Tuning baseline, FACTTEST-t (calibrated on only half the training data) surpasses R-Tuning by 34 percentage points on HotpotQA and 28 points on FEVER, while halving the training cost. Compared to SAPLMA (a classifier-based truthfulness scorer), FACTTEST-saplma achieves 84.77% on ParaRel-3B versus SAPLMA's 67.33%, though on HotpotQA both methods struggle (31.96% vs. 25.13%). On the out-of-distribution ParaRel-OOD test set, FACTTEST-O maintains its Type I error control and outperforms training-based methods without any fine-tuning. The key distinction is that FACTTEST provides finite-sample, distribution-free Type I error guarantees at a user-specified α, whereas none of the baselines offer such formal statistical guarantees.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Neyman-Pearson (NP) classification framework (Tong et al., 2018; Scott & Nowak, 2005) for LLM hallucination detection, a domain previously addressed by retrieval-based methods, fine-tuning approaches (R-Tuning), and uncertainty estimation (SelfCheckGPT, Semantic Entropy) that lack formal error guarantees. It establishes the first formal equivalence between NP classification and PAC-style conformal prediction (Vovk, 2012), unifying two previously separate methodological threads. The covariate shift extension draws on density ratio estimation and rejection sampling from the distribution-free inference literature, adapting them to the LLM setting where calibration data may come from a different domain than deployment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By providing distribution-free, finite-sample Type I error guarantees, FACTTEST enables principled deployment of LLMs in high-stakes domains (medical, legal) where controlling false positives is critical, and it does so without requiring fine-tuning or external knowledge bases. The framework's model-agnosticism and compatibility with black-box APIs (GPT-4o, Claude 3.5) broadens its practical reach. Key limitations include the offline-only calibration requirement, dependence on the quality of the chosen score function for Type II error control, and the need for human-annotated calibration data. Open questions include extending to online/sequential testing, exploring richer score functions, and whether the i.i.d. assumption can be further relaxed beyond covariate shift.
