## Problem

Average scores on benchmark subsets are confounded with question difficulty, making LLM comparisons unreliable when different subsets are used. Item Response Theory (IRT) deconfounds ability from difficulty but requires expensive per-question calibration that scales linearly with question bank size, making it impractical for the 10^3–10^6 questions typical in generative model evaluation. The paper asks how to make IRT-based evaluation both reliable and affordable at scale.

## Input / Output

The difficulty predictor takes a question's text and dataset context, featurized into a 4096-dimensional embedding via Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, and outputs a scalar difficulty on the logit scale through a linear regression head. The question generator takes a dataset description and a target difficulty scalar as input and produces a free-text question, with 64 candidates generated and the best-matching one selected. The adaptive testing loop consumes a binary response matrix (M test takers × N questions) and outputs a per-model ability estimate on the logit scale, which can further be predicted from a model's pretraining FLOPS via a power-law relationship.

## Method

The core contribution is amortized calibration: a content-based difficulty predictor (linear model on LLM embeddings) replaces the per-question difficulty parameter in the Rasch model's EM algorithm, reducing calibration cost from O(M) per question to O(1) by sharing parameters across questions and datasets. The modified EM alternates between an E-step that marginalizes over ability using the predicted difficulty and an M-step that updates the predictor's weights to maximize the marginal likelihood. For adaptive testing, Fisher information guides question selection from a calibrated bank, but the bank must be large and diverse to avoid suboptimal selections. To address this, a conditional question generator is trained in two stages: SFT on existing questions followed by PPO with a reward of negative distance between the predicted difficulty of the generated question and the target difficulty. The Rasch model (1PL) is validated as sufficient over 2PL/3PL variants, and a single unidimensional ability parameter per model generalizes well across all 22 datasets.

## Results

Across 22 NLP benchmarks and 183 LLMs, the Rasch IRT model achieves 0.83 test AUC (simple) and 0.87 test AUC (dataset-specific), substantially outperforming the average-score baseline (0.5 AUC, equivalent to random guessing) for out-of-sample generalization. Adaptive testing with Fisher information reduces the number of questions needed to reach 95% reliability by an average of 50% and up to 82% compared to random sampling. Amortized calibration (predicting difficulty from Llama-3.1-8B embeddings) matches traditional calibration in both model fit and ability estimation quality across all datasets. The conditional question generator (SFT + PPO) achieves a mean difficulty prediction error of 0.12 (train) / 0.15 (test), reducing error by nearly 10× over SFT-only, and generated questions preserve ability estimates (ρ ≈ 0.96 calibration, ρ ≈ 0.81 test).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is IRT (Rasch) versus the classical average-score (CTT) approach: IRT achieves 0.78 ± 0.07 AUC for predicting out-of-sample responses from 50-question subsets, while the average score yields 0.50 ± 0.07 (random-guessing level), and IRT consistently outperforms across all 22 datasets. For efficiency, adaptive testing with Fisher information on a full question bank reaches 95% reliability with ~31 queries on AIR-Bench, whereas random sampling requires the full 400-question budget; the average reduction is 50% and peaks at 82% across datasets. Amortized calibration (linear regression on Llama-3.1-8B embeddings) produces model-fit and ability-correlation metrics that align almost perfectly with traditional per-question EM calibration (all points above 0.99 correlation), confirming that content-based prediction is a valid substitute. The 2PL and 3PL IRT variants do not outperform the simpler 1PL Rasch model, likely due to limited test-taker counts amplifying overfitting. For question generation, the SFT+PPO pipeline reduces mean difficulty prediction error by nearly 10× compared to SFT-only, and generated questions (from both Llama-3.1-8B and Mistral-7B) preserve model ability rankings (ρ ≈ 0.81–0.96) when concatenated with original benchmark questions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper operationalizes Item Response Theory (specifically the Rasch/1PL model) for large-scale LLM evaluation, building on the EM-based calibration framework of Bock & Aitkin (1981) and the Rasch model itself. It departs from the dominant classical test theory (average-score) paradigm in LLM benchmarking and extends prior IRT-for-NLP work (Vania et al., 2021; Lalor et al., 2018; Maia Polo et al., 2024) by introducing two practical innovations: amortized calibration that replaces per-question difficulty estimation with a content-based predictor, and a PPO-trained conditional question generator for automated question bank construction. Unlike Flash-HELM or anchor-point methods that subsample existing questions, this approach reframes evaluation as a sequential decision problem over a calibrated item bank.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 50–82% reduction in query complexity via adaptive testing and the near-zero marginal cost of amortized calibration make reliable model-based evaluation feasible for iterative LLM development and governance monitoring, where frequent re-evaluation is essential. The finding that a single FLOPS-predictable ability parameter explains performance across 22 diverse datasets suggests 'difficulty' may be an out-of-distribution measure relative to pretraining data, offering a unifying interpretive lens. Key limitations include restriction to binary (dichotomous) responses, dependence on embedding quality for difficulty prediction, and the acknowledged risk of bias in AI-generated questions requiring human oversight. Open questions include extending IRT to non-binary assessments, incorporating prompting/sampling confounds, and validating the OOD-difficulty hypothesis.
