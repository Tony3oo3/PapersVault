## Problem

While empirical studies have reported that tokens in deep transformers collapse into a single cluster, the theoretical understanding of this phenomenon was limited to results relying on stochastic or mean-field techniques with restrictive assumptions (single head, identity matrices). This paper asks whether consensus convergence can be rigorously established under weaker, more general conditions using deterministic geometric and control-theoretic tools, and whether the phenomenon persists in practical architectures with feedforward layers.

## Input / Output

The theoretical analysis takes ℓ token vectors constrained to an n-dimensional ellipsoid (or sphere), time-varying Q/K/V weight matrices defining P(t)=Q(t)ᵀK(t) and U(t) matrices, and a number of attention heads h, and produces Lyapunov-based convergence proofs to consensus equilibria with explicit domains of attraction (hemispheres or conull sets). For empirical validation, the method consumes 100 random prompts (200 tokens each, uniformly sampled from the GPT-2 vocabulary) fed through pretrained GPT-2 XL (48 layers) and GPT-Neo 2.7B (32 layers) models, producing the average cosine-similarity metric E across all prompts as a function of effective depth (multiple passes through the model).

## Method

The paper derives a continuous-time ODE model of attention as the first-order Taylor approximation of the discrete-time transformer layer, where tokens evolve on an ellipsoid via projection dynamics. The key analytical tool is a Lyapunov function V(y)=max_i(1−vᵀy_i) whose upper Dini derivative (computed via Danskin's theorem to handle the non-differentiable max) is shown to be strictly negative outside the consensus set, establishing attractivity for full attention (Theorem 3.2) under only boundedness and uniform continuity of P(t). For causal (auto-regressive) attention, the triangular structure is exploited: the first token is fixed (or converges to an eigenvector of U), and an input-to-state-stability argument propagates convergence inductively to subsequent tokens, yielding asymptotic stability for almost all initial conditions (Theorem 4.2) or within a hemisphere defined by the dominant eigenvector of a symmetric U (Theorem 4.5). This geometric control-theoretic approach avoids the stochastic/mean-field machinery of prior work and extends results to multiple heads and time-varying P matrices.

## Results

The paper proves that all tokens in a transformer asymptotically converge to a single consensus equilibrium (i.e., all token representations become identical) under three sets of assumptions: (1) full attention with U=I and bounded, uniformly continuous P for any number of heads (Theorem 3.2), (2) causal attention with U=I and bounded P for any number of heads, with asymptotic stability for almost all initial conditions (Theorem 4.2), and (3) causal attention with a symmetric time-invariant U having a simple largest eigenvalue, single head (Theorem 4.5). Empirically, the convergence metric E (1 minus average cosine similarity to the first token) decays to 0 across repeated passes through GPT-2 XL (48 layers) and GPT-Neo 2.7B (32 layers) on 100 random prompts, both with and without feedforward layers, and with both pretrained and randomly initialized weights. Simulations of the continuous-time model (10 tokens on S², 50 tokens on S⁴⁹⁹) confirm the theoretical predictions, with E converging to zero along all 100 random trajectories.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's theoretical contributions are positioned against two prior works. Compared to Geshkovski et al. (2023a, Lemma 4.2), which requires both U and P to be the identity matrix and a single attention head, Theorem 3.2 here only requires U=I with P being bounded and uniformly continuous, and extends to any number of heads h≥1. Compared to Karagodin et al. (2024, Theorem 4.1), which imposes time-invariance of P and a single head, Theorem 4.2 here allows time-varying P and any number of heads while still establishing asymptotic stability (not just convergence). Theorem 4.5 addresses the case U≠I (symmetric, simple largest eigenvalue), for which the authors note no prior result exists in the literature—Karagodin et al. (2024) conjecture but do not prove this case. Empirically, the paper shows that consensus convergence is a structural property of the transformer architecture rather than an artifact of specific weights: convergence to E→0 occurs identically with pretrained weights, fixed random weights, and freshly randomized weights before each pass, and persists even when feedforward layers are included (though the rate varies: feedforward layers speed up convergence in GPT-2 but slow it in GPT-Neo).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the mathematical modeling of transformer attention initiated by Geshkovski et al. (2023a) and the multi-particle dynamics perspective of Lu et al. (2019) and Dutta et al. (2021), but departs methodologically by replacing the stochastic/mean-field techniques used in prior work with control-theoretic tools: consensus dynamics on manifolds, Lyapunov stability, and Input-to-State Stability. Theoretically, it generalizes existing convergence results from single-head, time-invariant, identity-matrix settings to multi-head, time-varying, and non-identity value matrix cases. Empirically, it goes beyond prior theoretical scope by validating convergence on full GPT-2 XL and GPT-Neo 2.7B architectures including feedforward layers, which no prior theoretical result covers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first rigorous proof that token convergence to a single cluster is a structural property of the attention mechanism itself, independent of weight initialization or training, which reframes the empirically observed 'model collapse' as an architectural inevitability rather than a training artifact. A key open question is what mechanisms in practice (residual connections, specific normalization choices, or finite depth) prevent full collapse in deployed models, since the theory predicts collapse asymptotically. The finding that feedforward layers do not prevent consensus—and may even slow convergence—raises fundamental questions about the expressiveness limits of the transformer architecture at depth.
