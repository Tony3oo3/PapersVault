## Problem

Existing algorithms for optimizing generalized metrics (F-β, AM measure, Jaccard, weighted accuracy) in binary classification rely on characterizing the Bayes-optimal classifier and applying threshold-based methods, which lack finite-sample guarantees and are not tailored to restricted hypothesis sets. The best predictor within a given hypothesis class can differ substantially from the Bayes-optimal threshold, as demonstrated by the paper's linear classifier example where the optimal F-β boundary is not even parallel to the SVM boundary. This paper addresses the gap by providing hypothesis-set-specific, non-asymptotic guarantees for metric optimization.

## Input / Output

The method takes labeled binary classification data (in experiments, 32×32 RGB images from CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and SVHN with two classes extracted per dataset) and produces a binary classifier whose predictions are optimized for a user-specified linear-fractional metric rather than standard accuracy. The optimization target is a ratio of two expected losses over the confusion matrix statistics, which is non-differentiable and NP-hard to optimize directly; the algorithm instead minimizes a smooth surrogate loss parameterized by a scalar λ that is itself estimated via binary search or cross-validation over the training data.

## Method

The key reformulation (Theorem 3.1) shows that minimizing a generalized metric L(h) over a hypothesis set H is equivalent to minimizing a single cost-sensitive loss ℓ^{λ*}(h) where λ* = L*(H) is the best-in-class metric value. The paper then defines a new family of general cost-sensitive surrogate losses L_Φ (Eq. 6) that extend standard margin-based losses (logistic, hinge, exponential, etc.) to arbitrary four-cost settings, and proves H-consistency bounds for these surrogates (Theorem 4.1) with explicit rates depending on the loss type. The resulting METRO algorithm performs binary search over λ, using an empirical surrogate minimizer at each step to determine the search direction, yielding a finite-sample guarantee that the returned hypothesis achieves L(ĥ) ≤ L*(H) + O(ε_m / ℓ̲_β). A cross-validation variant (Algorithm 3) replaces the theoretical ε_m threshold with empirical metric evaluation, trading logarithmic for linear complexity while maintaining the same asymptotic guarantee.

## Results

METRO consistently outperforms all four baselines across F_β (β=1, 0.5, 1.5) and Jaccard similarity on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and SVHN. The largest gains appear on CIFAR-100 for F_0.5 (0.9759 vs. 0.9515 for the strongest baseline Parambath, a +2.4% improvement) and on CIFAR-10 for F_1 (0.9359 vs. 0.9185, +1.7%). For Jaccard similarity on CIFAR-10, METRO achieves 0.6575 versus 0.6428 for Parambath and 0.5746 for Koyejo Algorithm 2. The per-epoch computational cost of METRO is comparable to Koyejo Algorithm 2, as both involve a single hyperparameter and standard surrogate loss optimization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

METRO (Algorithm 3, cross-validation variant) is compared against four baselines: standard ERM, two threshold-based algorithms from Koyejo et al. (2014), and the cost-sensitive F_1 optimizer from Parambath et al. (2014). METRO wins on every metric–dataset combination, with the most substantial margins on CIFAR-100 for F_0.5 (0.9759 vs. 0.9515 for Parambath, +2.44%) and on CIFAR-10 for F_1 (0.9359 vs. 0.9185, +1.74%). Among the baselines, Parambath et al. consistently outperforms both Koyejo algorithms, confirming that the two-threshold cost-sensitive approach is stronger than single-threshold methods. Koyejo Algorithm 2 (cost-sensitive logistic regression with a single threshold) generally outperforms Koyejo Algorithm 1 (standard classifier + threshold search), though the ordering varies slightly across datasets. The authors note that the per-epoch computational cost of METRO is comparable to Koyejo Algorithm 2, since both reduce to optimizing a single hyperparameter with a standard surrogate loss.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the H-consistency framework of Awasthi, Mao, Mohri, and Zhong (2022a) from standard binary classification to a general cost-sensitive learning setting, which in turn subsumes the optimization of linear-fractional metrics (F-β, Jaccard, AM measure, weighted accuracy) studied by Koyejo et al. (2014) and Parambath et al. (2014). The key conceptual departure is from the two-stage threshold-based paradigm of prior work: rather than estimating class probabilities and then searching for an optimal threshold δ*, METRO directly minimizes a general cost-sensitive surrogate loss whose label-dependent costs encode the best-in-class metric value λ*, yielding H-consistency and finite-sample guarantees that are hypothesis-set-specific. The paper also generalizes Scott's (2012) θ-weighted surrogate losses to the full four-cost setting, showing that the existing θ-weighted framework is insufficient for the broader metric family.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first non-asymptotic, hypothesis-set-specific guarantees for optimizing generalized metrics, addressing a gap explicitly left open by Koyejo et al. (2014). The demonstration (Figure 1) that the best-in-class linear classifier for F_0.5 can be non-parallel to the best-in-class SVM classifier is a concrete argument that threshold-based methods are fundamentally inadequate for restricted hypothesis sets, not merely suboptimal. Open questions include extending the theory to multi-class and multi-label settings, handling over-parameterized models where Rademacher-based bounds become loose, and evaluating on genuinely imbalanced datasets (the current experiments use balanced CIFAR/SVHN subsets). The practical impact is moderate: the algorithm is computationally comparable to standard training, but the theoretical guarantees depend on the minimizability gap being small, which may not hold in all regimes.
