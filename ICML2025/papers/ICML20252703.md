## Problem

Existing algorithm selection methods model empirical correlations between meta-features and performance, ignoring the underlying mechanism of what characteristics an algorithm must possess to solve a problem with specific feature values. This correlation-based approach limits explainability and makes models vulnerable to marginal distribution shifts in both problem and algorithm sets, which are inherent in real deployment as new algorithms and problem domains continuously emerge.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252703/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes tabular problem features (PF) and algorithm features (AF) as concatenated vectors; algorithm features are typically 75-dimensional representations extracted from code (e.g., AST features or LLM-extracted code characteristics). Beyond the final algorithm ranking, the model produces reconstructed algorithm features representing the 'ideal' feature profile for the best algorithm, a learned causal DAG encoding inter-feature dependencies, and counterfactual explanations specifying minimal perturbations to problem features that would flip the selection decision.

## Method

DAG-AS models the conditional distribution P(AF|PF, S=1) via a structural causal model, arguing this is more robust than modeling P(S|PF,AF) because it decouples from marginal distribution shifts. The architecture is a Causal Learning Network with two multi-layer reconstruction sub-networks (f for PF, g for AF) whose first-layer weights directly define the DAG adjacency matrix (non-zero entries = directed edges). Training minimizes a four-term loss: Frobenius-norm reconstruction error, L0/L1 sparsity penalty on the adjacency matrix, a continuous acyclicity constraint (tr(e^{M⊙M}) − |V|), and a BPR ranking loss that compares similarity scores between reconstructed and original algorithm representations. At inference, the reconstructed AF and each candidate's original AF are mapped to representation vectors by separate MLPs, and a similarity module scores their match to rank algorithms. Explainability is achieved at the model level through the learned DAG (centrality analysis, edge inspection) and at the instance level through counterfactual do-calculus interventions (abduction → intervention → prediction) that identify minimal feature perturbations altering the selection.

## Results

DAG-AS is evaluated on 10 ASlib benchmark datasets using the PAR10 score (lower is better), which penalizes timeouts by 10× the cutoff time. DAG-AS achieves the lowest PAR10 in 8 out of 10 datasets, with particularly large margins on GRAPHS-2015 (5.42×10⁶ vs. 7.56×10⁶ for the best baseline SUNNY), MAXSAT19-UCMS (2.15×10³ vs. 3.37×10³), and TSP-LION2015 (0.48×10³ vs. 0.78×10³). It underperforms on GLUHACK-18 (15.53×10³ vs. MCC's 8.10×10³) and SAT03-16-INDU (8.15×10³ vs. SATzilla11's 4.27×10³), attributed to limited training samples making causal structure harder to learn.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DAG-AS outperforms all five baselines (ISAC, MCC, SATzilla11, SNNAP, SUNNY) on 8 of 10 datasets in PAR10. Against SUNNY, the closest competitor, DAG-AS wins on 7 datasets with margins ranging from 0.04×10³ (SAT18-EXP: 8.07 vs. 8.11) to 3.14×10³ (MAXSAT19-UCMS: 2.15 vs. 3.37). Against SATzilla11, DAG-AS achieves the largest relative gains on SAT11-INDU (3.06 vs. 6.79×10³, a 55% reduction) and SAT11-HAND (4.69 vs. 7.54×10³, a 38% reduction). DAG-AS loses to MCC on GLUHACK-18 (15.53 vs. 8.10×10³) and to SATzilla11 on SAT03-16-INDU (8.15 vs. 4.27×10³), which the authors attribute to insufficient training samples for learning causal structure in those settings. SNNAP is consistently the weakest baseline across all datasets, with PAR10 scores often 2–10× higher than DAG-AS.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant correlation-based paradigm in algorithm selection (SATzilla, ISAC, SNNAP, SUNNY, MCC), which learns empirical mappings from meta-features to algorithm performance or rankings. It instead introduces a causal structural equation model over a learned DAG to capture the conditional distribution P(AF|PF, S=1) — the feature characteristics the optimal algorithm must possess given a problem — rather than the joint P(S|PF, AF). The technical machinery is directly inherited from Zheng et al. (2018) for the continuous acyclicity constraint and from He et al. (2022) for the combined causal-reconstruction-plus-ranking loss architecture, repurposed here for the algorithm selection domain. The counterfactual explanation component extends Pearl's do-calculus and abduction-intervention-prediction pipeline to a neural-network-parameterized SCM, a setting where analytical solutions are unavailable and numerical gradient-based abduction is required.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically critical weakness of existing algorithm selection methods: vulnerability to distribution shift as new algorithms and problem domains emerge, which is inherent in real deployment. By modeling invariant causal mechanisms rather than marginal correlations, DAG-AS offers a principled path to robustness, and the multi-level explainability (DAG visualization + counterfactual interventions) makes selection decisions auditable — a significant step for trust in automated algorithm selection. Key limitations remain: the method requires informative algorithm features (e.g., AST or LLM-extracted code features), underperforms on datasets with limited training samples where causal structure is hard to learn (GLUHACK-18, SAT03-16-INDU), and the counterfactual computation via iterative gradient descent may be computationally expensive at scale. Open questions include whether interventions on algorithm features can guide the design of novel algorithms and whether more advanced causal learners (nonlinear, multivariate) can handle the complex causal structures the authors acknowledge as a current limitation.
