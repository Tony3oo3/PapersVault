## Problem

Decoder-only LLMs cache key/value vectors during autoregressive generation, and this cache grows linearly with sequence length, creating severe memory bottlenecks (e.g., 86 GB for 512K tokens on a 65B model). Existing dimension-level pruning methods such as SliceGPT can only remove a small fraction of head dimensions before performance collapses, because the attention vectors contain hidden linear redundancy that makes every individual direction appear important. The paper asks whether a principled reparameterization can expose this redundancy so that a much larger fraction of dimensions can be safely pruned or fine-tuned at low cost.

## Input / Output

The method consumes the pre-trained projection matrices W_Q, W_K, W_V, W_O (each D×H×d) from any transformer model and, for the fine-tuning variant, a small amount of task-specific training data. It outputs a pruned model where each attention head's dimension is reduced from d to r (r ≤ d) with the singular values merged back into the orthogonal basis matrices so that no extra parameters or FLOPs are introduced at inference time. For the PEFT variant, only the diagonal singular-value matrices (bounded by H×d×d per head pair) are updated, and the final model is obtained by absorbing these values into U and V, yielding a standard full-rank weight matrix.

## Method

CLOVER treats the Q-K and V-O projection pairs within each attention head as mutual low-rank factors: it forms the cross-layer product W_QK^h = W_Q^{[:,h,:]}(W_K^{[:,h,:]})^T ∈ R^{D×D} and applies SVD to obtain orthogonal matrices U, V and a diagonal S with at most d nonzero singular values. Because the vectors are now orthogonal, a small number of dominant singular vectors span nearly the entire head space while the remainder approach zero, making them safe to prune without fine-tuning. For parameter-efficient fine-tuning, CLOVER freezes U and V and updates only the singular values S, which enables full-rank updates (unlike LoRA's fixed low-rank delta) while keeping the trainable parameter count comparable to a rank-32 LoRA adapter. This cross-layer decomposition is the key distinction from SVFT, which factorizes each projection matrix individually into D×D components and thus introduces far more parameters; CLOVER's per-head factorization keeps the tunable matrix size at O(H·d²) rather than O(D²).

## Results

On GPT-2-XL, pruning 70% of the Q-K head dimension with CLOVER yields a perplexity comparable to vanilla pruning at only 8%, and pruning 50% without fine-tuning increases perplexity by just 1.38× versus 21.9× for vanilla pruning. On LLaMA-2-7B commonsense reasoning (8 tasks), CLOVER achieves 85.2% average accuracy with 0.83% trainable parameters, outperforming LoRA (77.6%), DoRA (79.7%), HiRA (81.4%), and PiSSA (84.5%). On LLaMA-3-8B, CLOVER reaches 87.9% average with only 0.47% trainable parameters (vs. 0.70% for others). Combined with TransMLA, CLOVER achieves up to 11.1× inference speedup over the original LLaMA-2-7B at 8K context length.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SliceGPT on DeepSeek-V2-Lite, CLOVER prunes 50% of the head dimension while maintaining 57.25 average benchmark score, compared to SliceGPT's 57.30 at only 6.25% hidden-size pruning; CLOVER also delivers a 1.25× inference speedup (177.02 ms prefill vs. 191.91 ms) where SliceGPT provides no acceleration. Against TransMLA on LLaMA-2-7B, CLOVER adds an additional 50% head-dimension pruning on top of TransMLA's 92.97% KV-cache reduction while recovering performance to 59.13 average (vs. 58.68 for TransMLA alone). In the PEFT comparison on LLaMA-2-7B, CLOVER (85.2% avg) outperforms LoRA (77.6%) by 7.6 points, DoRA (79.7%) by 5.5 points, HiRA (81.4%) by 3.8 points, and PiSSA (84.5%) by 0.7 points, all at the same 0.83% trainable parameter budget. On LLaMA-3-8B, CLOVER uses only 0.47% trainable parameters (vs. 0.70% for others) yet still leads: 87.9% vs. LoRA 80.8%, DoRA 85.2%, HiRA 86.7%, and PiSSA 87.3%. CLOVER also consumes less GPU memory (104.75 GB vs. 110.84 GB) and trains faster (2h22m vs. 2h42m) than LoRA under identical settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

CLOVER repurposes SVD in a cross-layer context that departs from both standard pruning (L2-norm-based, SliceGPT) and prior SVD-based PEFT methods. Unlike SVFT, which factorizes each projection matrix (W_Q, W_K, W_V, W_O) individually and introduces large U, S, V components, CLOVER treats the Q-K and V-O pairs as cross-layer products (W_QK = W_Q·W_Kᵀ) and applies SVD to the merged matrix, yielding a compact set of singular values that simultaneously guide pruning and serve as trainable parameters. This cross-layer orthogonalization also distinguishes it from PiSSA, which initializes adapters with principal singular vectors of individual matrices but still operates within a single-layer, fixed-basis framework. The method is compatible with existing KV-cache compression (TransMLA) and structured pruning approaches, positioning it as an orthogonal initialization layer rather than a standalone compression pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: CLOVER enables 70% head-dimension pruning on GPT-2-XL with perplexity comparable to 8% vanilla pruning, and achieves up to 11.1× inference speedup on LLaMA-2-7B when combined with TransMLA. The finding that significant linear redundancy is hidden within attention heads and only revealed through cross-layer SVD offers a new lens for understanding model structure that could inform future architecture design. Key limitations include the incompatibility with RoPE for Q-K cross-layer orthogonalization (requiring a fallback to head-wise decomposition), the absence of comparisons with other state-of-the-art pruning techniques beyond SliceGPT, and evaluation limited to perplexity and commonsense benchmarks rather than broader capability suites. Open questions include whether CLOVER's orthogonal initialization can be combined with quantization to eliminate outliers, whether it scales to MoE architectures beyond DeepSeek-V2-Lite, and whether the cross-layer perspective generalizes to other paired operations in transformer blocks.
