## Problem

While Occam's razor dictates that the simplest model explaining the data generalizes best, standard ML approaches only indirectly promote simplicity through regularization or inductive biases, and no tractable objective directly minimizes model complexity alongside training error. This paper asks whether in-context learning (ICL) in sequence models provides such a principled mechanism, and if so, what its theoretical guarantees and practical limitations are.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250589/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For supervised tasks, the sequence model receives a context of (x, y) pairs followed by a novel query x*, and outputs a predicted label y*; for nonstationary HMM tasks, it receives a token sequence and predicts the next token. Unusually, the same network plays a dual role: its fixed weights φ parameterize the learner T_φ, while its hidden activations given the context implicitly encode the fitted model p_θ, so no explicit parameter update occurs at inference. The meta-training objective is the cumulative next-token prediction loss over a meta-dataset of tasks, which the paper shows equals the prequential code length.

## Method

The core theoretical contribution is proving that the next-token prediction loss used to train ICL sequence models is exactly equivalent to prequential coding (Blier & Ollivier, 2018), a compression technique that upper-bounds Kolmogorov complexity. Minimizing this loss over a meta-dataset of tasks thus yields a learner T_φ that jointly minimizes training error and model complexity, operationalizing Occam's razor without explicit regularizers. Empirically, the paper instantiates T_φ with bottleneck Transformers, standard causal Transformers, and Mamba 1/2 SSMs, and compares prequential ICL against a train-risk ICL control (predicting past context tokens) and SGD-based learners across linear regression, sinusoidal regression, Mastermind, and HMM tasks. Key findings include that prequential ICL outperforms train-risk ICL and SGD in low-data regimes, that architecture choice substantially affects prequential code length minimization, and that even GPT-4 fails to minimize prequential code length on the novel Mastermind task, revealing underfitting and task-generalization limitations of current ICL methods.

## Results

The paper's main empirical finding is that prequential ICL (next-token prediction) consistently achieves lower prequential code length than train-risk ICL (past-token prediction) in low-data regimes across all three iid tasks (linear regression, sinusoid regression, Mastermind), with the gap widening as task difficulty increases. In large-data regimes, generalization error converges between prequential and train-risk ICL, and SGD-based learners match or exceed ICL performance (notably on Mastermind, where SGD outperforms ICL due to ICL underfitting). On the nonstationary HMM task, prequential ICL generalizes at all context lengths while both train-risk ICL and SGD fail entirely. GPT-4, despite massive pretraining, performs worse than a small ICL model trained on Mastermind tasks and even worse than a naive marginal-distribution baseline on the novel Mastermind task.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against train-risk ICL (the paper's primary control), prequential ICL wins decisively in low-data regimes on all tasks, with the advantage growing from linear regression (small gap) to sinusoid regression (moderate gap) to Mastermind (large gap); the two methods converge at large context lengths. Against SGD-based learners, prequential ICL wins in low-data regimes across all tasks but loses to SGD in large-data regimes on Mastermind, where SGD's greater model capacity (N² weights vs. N activations) and unlimited compute budget overcome ICL's underfitting. Against GPT-4 (Achiam et al., 2023), the paper's small bottlenecked Transformer trained on a distribution of Mastermind tasks substantially outperforms GPT-4 in prequential code length, and GPT-4 even underperforms a trivial baseline that predicts the empirical marginal class distribution. Among architectures (Figure 4), the Transformer without bottleneck is the only one that performs well on Mastermind, while Mamba 1 (Gu & Dao, 2024) and Mamba 2 (Dao & Gu, 2024) outperform all Transformer variants on the HMM task, demonstrating that the next-token prediction objective alone does not guarantee successful prequential code length minimization without the right architecture.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions ICL within the Kolmogorov-complexity / minimum-description-length tradition, showing that the next-token prediction objective is mathematically equivalent to prequential coding (Blier & Ollivier, 2018), thereby providing a compression-based normative account of why ICL fits simple models. It departs from the dominant Bayesian framing of ICL (Xie et al., 2022; Müller et al., 2022) and the gradient-descent-emulation perspective (Von Oswald et al., 2023; Ahn et al., 2023) by identifying the precise meta-objective as joint minimization of training error and model complexity, and argues in Appendix H that the Kolmogorov perspective subsumes the Bayesian one. Empirically, it builds on controlled ICL benchmarks (Garg et al., 2022; Akyürek et al., 2023; Mahankali et al., 2024) and extends them with a train-risk ICL control, SGD baselines, and architecture comparisons (Mamba 1/2, bottlenecked vs. standard Transformers).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first principled, compression-theoretic explanation for ICL's strong low-data generalization and simultaneously identifies concrete failure modes: underfitting in large-data regimes on difficult tasks, strong architecture dependence of prequential code length minimization, and task-generalization failures even for massive pretrained models (GPT-4 underperforms a naive marginal-distribution baseline on a novel Mastermind task). These findings suggest actionable directions—variable-compute 'optimization layers' in sequence models, mixture-of-learners combining ICL with SGD, and meta-level ICL that minimizes the learner's own Kolmogorov complexity. Open questions include whether optimal data ordering (curricula) can further reduce prequential code length, and whether novel architectures can be designed to explicitly target compression rather than relying on implicit inductive biases.
