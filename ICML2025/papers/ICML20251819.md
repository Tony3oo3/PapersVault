## Problem

Existing text-to-image alignment frameworks rely on large-scale global data and single-objective criteria (aesthetics, content moderation), failing to capture the nuanced, multi-criteria, intersectional values of local communities in domains like urban public space design. This gap risks systematically excluding or misrepresenting historically marginalized groups in generative depictions of public spaces, as no prior dataset integrates community-driven, intersectional feedback across multiple coexisting design norms.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20251819/cropped_page14_idx9.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes free-form text prompts describing urban public-space scenarios (e.g., parks, plazas, pedestrian promenades) and produces 1024×1024 RGB images via Stable Diffusion XL. The LIVS dataset itself consists of 37,710 pairwise image comparisons across 13,462 images, where each comparison is annotated on three randomly selected criteria (out of six) using a continuous slider from −1 to +1, yielding ~113,130 criterion-level annotations. For DPO training, continuous slider values are discretized to binary preferred/not-preferred labels, and multi-criteria conflicts are resolved via majority voting into a single binary signal per image pair.

## Method

The core contribution is a two-year participatory action research process with 30 Montreal community organizations that produced 634 initial design concepts, iteratively distilled through workshops into six criteria (Accessibility, Safety, Comfort, Invitingness, Inclusivity, Diversity), and 440 human-authored prompts augmented to ~3,350 total via three GPT-4o prompting strategies. SDXL generates up to 20 images per prompt, from which the 4 most visually distinct are selected using a greedy CLIP-similarity algorithm, yielding 13,462 images for annotation. The fine-tuning procedure applies Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) to SDXL with binary preference labels derived from majority-voted multi-criteria annotations, using a learning rate of 1×10⁻⁸, β=5000, batch size 64, and 500–1500 training steps on a single A100 80GB GPU. The key methodological insight is that multi-criteria community feedback can be aggregated into a single DPO training signal, though the resulting ~50% neutral evaluation rate reveals the fundamental tension between collapsing heterogeneous, intersectional preferences into a binary objective.

## Results

On the held-out evaluation set of 2,200 pairwise image comparisons, the DPO-finetuned SDXL was preferred in 700 cases (32%), the baseline SDXL in 300 cases (14%), and approximately 1,100 cases (50%) were rated neutral. Criteria with higher training annotation volume (Comfort, Invitingness) showed stronger DPO preference gains, while Inclusivity and Diversity exhibited the highest neutral rates. The dataset comprises 37,710 image-level comparisons (≈113,130 criterion-level annotations) across 13,462 images, distilled from 634 community-defined concepts into six criteria.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between the DPO-finetuned Stable Diffusion XL and the unmodified baseline SDXL, evaluated on 2,200 new pairwise comparisons annotated by community participants. The DPO model achieves a modest net advantage (32% vs. 14% preference), but the 50% neutral rate indicates that DPO alone is insufficient to capture the full complexity of multi-criteria, intersectional community preferences. No external T2I alignment baselines (e.g., HPS, ImageReward, MPS) are directly compared; the evaluation is limited to the DPO vs. baseline contrast within the LIVS annotation protocol. Secondary comparisons show that human-authored prompts yield fewer neutral annotations than GPT-4o-generated prompts (Case Study III), and that intersectional identity groups assign systematically different raw scores across criteria (Case Study IV), further underscoring that a single global alignment objective cannot reconcile all local preferences.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on DPO for diffusion model alignment (Wallace et al., 2023; Rafailov et al., 2024), applying the same binary preference-optimization recipe to Stable Diffusion XL. It departs from prior T2I alignment datasets (Simulacra, Pic-a-Pic, ImageReward, HPS) that target single global objectives like aesthetics or harmlessness, instead constructing a multi-criteria, intersectional preference dataset through two years of participatory action research with 30 community organizations in Montreal. Conceptually, it extends the multi-criteria preference learning line (MPS, ImageReward) by grounding criteria in locally co-defined, identity-aware annotations rather than expert or crowdworker judgments, and positions itself within the pluralistic alignment framework of Sorensen et al. (2024).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that roughly 50% of evaluation comparisons remain neutral after DPO fine-tuning is a sobering signal that current binary preference optimization is insufficient for capturing the heterogeneity of community values in spatial design. The intersectional scoring patterns—where groups with mobility challenges systematically rate Accessibility and Safety lower—concretely demonstrate why single-objective alignment risks erasing marginalized perspectives. Open questions include how to incorporate neutral annotations as informative signals, how to resolve multi-criteria conflicts without collapsing them via majority vote, and whether the methodology generalizes beyond a single mid-sized city. The dataset, while modest in scale (37,710 comparisons, 13,462 images), provides a rare benchmark for evaluating context-aware, stakeholder-driven T2I alignment in urban planning.
