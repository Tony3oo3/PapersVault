## Problem

Voting-based LLM evaluation platforms like Chatbot Arena rely on anonymous model responses to ensure unbiased user votes, but no prior work had examined whether an adversarial user could break this anonymity and strategically manipulate rankings. This paper demonstrates that the anonymity assumption is fragile: a user can identify which model generated a given response with >95% accuracy and then cast targeted votes to shift leaderboard positions at the cost of roughly a thousand votes.

## Input / Output

The de-anonymization classifier takes a prompt-response pair (P, R) where the prompt is attacker-chosen to maximize inter-model distinguishability (e.g., math problems, low-resource language queries, or safety-violating instructions) and outputs a binary label indicating whether R was generated by the target model. The downstream attack consumes this binary signal to decide whether to cast a strategic vote (upvote/downvote the target) or abstain, ultimately producing a manipulated Bradley-Terry coefficient ranking. The attacker controls the prompt but not the model selection, which is sampled uniformly from the arena's model pool.

## Method

The attack proceeds in two stages. First, a target-model detector is built: either via identity-probing prompts (e.g., 'Who are you?') or, more robustly, via a supervised logistic regression classifier trained on bag-of-words and TF-IDF features of 50 responses per model per prompt, achieving >95% binary detection accuracy across 22 models. Second, the attacker iteratively submits prompts, uses the detector to identify the target model among the two anonymous responses, and casts a vote for (or against) it when detected, abstaining otherwise. The paper quantifies the attack cost through simulations on 1.67M real Chatbot Arena votes, showing ~1,000–5,000 adversarial votes suffice to shift a model by 1–4 positions. Mitigations are then evaluated via a formal cost model (account cost × number of accounts + action cost × number of actions + detector training cost), with likelihood-ratio tests and perturbed leaderboard releases shown to detect adaptive adversaries.

## Results

The training-based de-anonymization detector achieves >95% accuracy for most of 22 evaluated models using simple bag-of-words features and logistic regression, with domain-specific prompts (Math, Coding) and non-English languages (Chinese, Indonesian) yielding the highest accuracy (up to 98.1%). Simulations on 1.67M real Chatbot Arena votes show that moving a high-ranked model up by one position requires only ~557–1,880 adversarial votes, while low-ranked models need as few as 126–381 votes for equivalent rank shifts. The one-time cost of building the detector is approximately $440 (200 prompts × $2.20/prompt), making the attack economically feasible.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares two de-anonymization strategies: identity-probing (asking 'Who are you?') and a training-based classifier. Identity-probing achieves >90% accuracy for most models but is easily filtered by Chatbot Arena's existing post-processing, making it impractical for real attacks. The training-based detector with BoW features consistently outperforms length-based features (e.g., 95.7% vs. 77.7% for llama-3.1-70b-instruct) and TF-IDF features (95.7% vs. 94.4%), confirming that simple bag-of-words representations are sufficient. Across prompt types, domain-specific tasks (Math: up to 97.8%, Code: up to 98.1%) and low-resource languages (Indonesian: up to 97.5%) yield higher detection accuracy than general English chat (92–96%). For the manipulation cost, low-ranked models (ranks 125–129) require only ~30% of the votes needed for high-ranked models (ranks 1–5) to achieve equivalent rank shifts, indicating that models with fewer accumulated votes are significantly more vulnerable to adversarial manipulation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two well-studied areas: attacks on voting/reputation systems (Kamvar et al., 2003; Hoffman et al., 2009) and model/authorship attribution (Sun et al., 2020; Tay et al., 2020; Munir et al., 2021). It repurposes the de-anonymization problem from the authorship-attribution literature—where the goal is identifying which model generated text—as a stepping stone for a new attack on LLM leaderboards. The concurrent work by Zhao et al. (2024) explores a similar targeted-detection-plus-adversarial-voting pipeline but on a much smaller scale (55k votes, <5 models) and without mitigation analysis. The paper's distinctive contribution is the end-to-end empirical demonstration at scale (22 models, 1.7M historical votes) combined with a cost model and co-designed defenses with the Chatbot Arena team.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Chatbot Arena is the most widely cited and trusted LLM evaluation platform, and this work shows its rankings can be shifted by a few thousand adversarial votes at a total cost under $500—undermining a key signal that model developers and the public rely on. The paper's practical impact is amplified by its responsible-disclosure collaboration, which led to concrete mitigations (authentication, rate limiting, perturbed leaderboards, prompt uniqueness) being implemented. Open questions remain: whether these defenses suffice against well-resourced adversaries who can hire human voters, whether the shift from objective to subjective LLM evaluation introduces other unexplored failure modes, and whether watermarking or backdoor-based detection (discussed in the appendix) could make de-anonymization trivially easy for model owners.
