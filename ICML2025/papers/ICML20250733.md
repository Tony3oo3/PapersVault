## Problem

Existing learning progress (LP) estimation methods for curriculum learning either require exhaustive re-evaluation of every goal (computationally intractable for large discrete spaces) or depend on brittle expert-defined goal groupings that cannot capture semantic competence transfer in high-dimensional language spaces. For LLM agents trained with online RL in evolving goal spaces containing millions of natural language goals, no prior method can efficiently estimate LP while generalizing competence predictions to unseen goals.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a textual prompt combining the goal instruction and full scene state (e.g., 'Goal: Grow lion. You see: water, carrot seed, baby lion, baby cow. You are standing on: none. Inventory (0/2): empty.') and produces a scalar success-probability estimate per goal via an MLP head on the LLM's final hidden state. From a buffer of N=100 past weight snapshots, it computes absolute learning progress (ALP) per goal, which drives a multi-armed bandit goal selector with annealing ε-greedy sampling (ε from 1 to 0.2). The Little-Zoo goal space contains ~20 million instruction-scene combinations, 80% of which are infeasible, making efficient prioritization essential.

## Method

MAGELLAN trains a second LoRA-adapted copy of the policy LLM (Flan-T5 248M) as a goal-conditioned competence estimator, where the LLM's latent representation is jointly optimized with a single-layer MLP (128 units, Tanh) to predict binary success outcomes via cross-entropy on a sliding buffer of M=5000 recent goal-outcome pairs. The key mechanism is that updating the LLM's representation space during competence training causes semantically similar goals to cluster in latent space, so estimating competence on one goal implicitly updates nearby goals—achieving dynamic, learned grouping without any expert-defined categories. A buffer of N=100 past weight snapshots enables ALP computation as the absolute difference between current and N-steps-ago competence estimates. Goal selection follows the Lopes & Oudeyer multi-armed bandit scheme, sampling goals proportionally to their estimated ALP. Ablations confirm that separate LoRA adapters for the policy and the competence estimator (rather than shared or frozen representations) are critical for stable learning and full mastery of the hardest goal categories.

## Results

On the held-out test set in Little-Zoo, MAGELLAN achieves a mean competence estimation error of 0.11±0.06, compared to 0.53±0.06 for Online-ALP and 0.09±0.03 for EK-Online-ALP (Table 2). In the main training experiment (500k episodes, 25k goals), MAGELLAN is the only method without expert-defined grouping to reach ≥90% success rate in all four goal categories, mastering each category significantly earlier than Online-ALP and Uniform (p < 8×10⁻⁴). MAGELLAN matches Eval-ALP's competence estimation accuracy (Figure 3) while incurring zero additional evaluation cost, and in adaptation tests it outperforms Online-ALP and Uniform while reaching parity with EK-Online-ALP in high-LP scenarios.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MAGELLAN decisively outperforms Online-ALP (Baranes & Oudeyer, 2013) on all metrics: on the held-out test set, MAGELLAN's mean estimation error is 0.11 vs. Online-ALP's 0.53, and in training MAGELLAN masters all goal categories while Online-ALP fails to master 'Grow carnivore' entirely. Against Eval-ALP (Kanitscheider et al., 2021), MAGELLAN achieves comparable competence estimation accuracy (Figure 3) but eliminates the need for periodic full-space evaluations, making it the only method combining high efficiency with full competence-transfer tracking. Compared to EK-Online-ALP (Stout & Barto, 2010), MAGELLAN achieves nearly identical test-set estimation error (0.11 vs. 0.09) without requiring expert-defined goal groupings, though EK-Online-ALP learns slightly faster in training because its predefined groups help discard impossible goals more efficiently. Against EK-Eval-ALP (Colas et al., 2019), MAGELLAN matches estimation quality while avoiding both the expert knowledge requirement and the computational cost of periodic evaluations. Overall, MAGELLAN is the only method that simultaneously achieves high efficiency, full competence-transfer tracking, and no expert knowledge requirement (Table 1).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the autotelic agent and learning-progress (LP) curriculum learning literature by addressing a specific gap: efficiently estimating LP over large, high-dimensional, structured (natural language) goal spaces. Prior LP-based methods either require exhaustive per-goal evaluation (computationally prohibitive at scale) or rely on brittle expert-defined goal groupings that assume no cross-group transfer. MAGELLAN departs from both by leveraging the LLM's semantic representation (via LoRA adapters) to dynamically learn competence transfer between goals, replacing predefined groups with a data-driven, generalizable clustering. The RL agent backbone is SAC-GLAM, and the goal selection mechanism follows the multi-armed bandit scheme from Lopes & Oudeyer (2012).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MAGELLAN demonstrates that an LLM agent can self-organize an efficient curriculum over a ~20M-goal language space without any expert-defined categories, achieving full mastery where all non-expert baselines fail. The ability to generalize competence estimates to unseen goals and adapt to evolving goal spaces is a key step toward truly open-ended LLM agents. However, experiments are limited to a small LLM (Flan-T5 248M) and a single controlled textual environment, leaving open whether the approach scales to larger models, richer environments, or real-world open-ended settings. The authors also note potential applications to code goal spaces and educational technologies, but these remain untested.
