## Problem

ML-based quantum error mitigation (QEM) methods lack standardized benchmarks with diverse noise profiles and circuit types, hindering fair comparison across studies. Additionally, existing ML-QEM architectures (vanilla MLPs or GNNs) use feature encoders that either scale exponentially with qubit count or capture only coarse global statistics, failing to model both short-range gate context and long-range circuit topology simultaneously.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250108/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a quantum circuit encoded as a directed acyclic graph (DAG) where each node carries a feature vector combining gate-type one-hot encoding, qubit multi-hot encoding, and continuous rotation angles, along with a global circuit feature vector comprising 8 circuit statistics, 3N_q single-qubit Pauli expectation values (X, Y, Z on each qubit), and the raw noisy expectation value. The output is a single scalar: the predicted ideal (error-mitigated) expectation value of the target observable. The DAG structure preserves the directed flow of operations along qubits, enabling topology-aware message passing.

## Method

QEMFormer employs a two-branch architecture that processes the circuit DAG in parallel. The MLP branch performs global mean-pooling over node features and feeds the result through K sequential layers with batch normalization, ReLU, and dropout to capture short-range, gate-specific context. The Graph Transformer branch applies L layers of multi-head self-attention restricted to graph neighbors (i.e., attention is computed only over adjacent nodes in the DAG), with gated residual connections and layer normalization to model long-range dependencies and topological structure. The two branch outputs are concatenated with the global circuit feature vector, projected via a learnable linear layer, and passed through a final 2-layer MLP regressor to predict the ideal expectation value. This design explicitly separates local gate-level information from global topological patterns, addressing the limitation of prior methods that rely on a single homogeneous architecture.

## Results

On the standard QEM-Bench settings (Table 3), QEMFormer achieves the lowest RMSE across most of the nine circuit×noise configurations: 0.087 (Random/Incoherent), 0.054 (QAOA/Incoherent), 0.024 (Trotter/Incoherent), and 0.033 (QAOA/Provider). On the advanced zero-shot generalization settings (Table 2), QEMFormer attains RMSE of 0.025 on Trotter-Step Zero-Shot (Incoherent) and 0.069 on Random-Size Zero-Shot (Provider), both best-in-class. On real 50-qubit IBM Kyiv and 63-qubit IBM Brisbane devices (Table 4), QEMFormer achieves the lowest MAE of 0.018 / RMSE of 0.026 (Kyiv Pre) and MAE of 0.123 / RMSE of 0.272 (Brisbane Raw), outperforming all baselines including GTranQEM and MLP.

## Baselines & Comparisons

QEMFormer consistently outperforms the closest ML-based competitor GTranQEM (Bao et al., ICLR 2025) on 7 of 9 standard settings, with the largest gaps on QAOA/Provider (0.033 vs 0.045 RMSE) and Trotter/Incoherent (0.024 vs 0.033); GTranQEM edges ahead only on Trotter/Provider (0.051 vs 0.062). Against the MLP and GNN baselines from Liao et al. (Nature Machine Intelligence 2024), QEMFormer wins on 8 of 9 standard configurations, e.g., Random/Incoherent (0.087 vs 0.093 MLP, 0.111 GNN) and QAOA/Incoherent (0.054 vs 0.056 MLP, 0.063 GNN). The non-ML baselines ZNE (Temme et al., PRL 2017) and CDR (Czarnik et al., Quantum 2021) are substantially outperformed: on Trotter/Coherent, QEMFormer achieves 0.189 RMSE versus 0.438 for ZNE and 0.222 for CDR, and on QAOA circuits ZNE and CDR are excluded entirely due to poor performance. On the real-device large-scale datasets (50-qubit Kyiv, 63-qubit Brisbane), QEMFormer achieves the best MAE and RMSE across all four settings, with a 62% RMSE reduction over the noisy baseline on Kyiv Pre (0.026 vs 0.069) and a 64% reduction on Brisbane Raw (0.272 vs 0.760). On the advanced zero-shot generalization tasks, QEMFormer leads on 7 of 9 settings, with particularly strong results on Trotter-Step Zero-Shot/Incoherent (0.025 RMSE, beating the next-best GNN at 0.024 by a narrow margin) and Random-Size Zero-Shot/Provider (0.069, best overall).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the ML-based QEM line initiated by Kim et al. (2020a), whose neural-network approach scales exponentially with qubit count, and Liao et al. (2024), who evaluated MLPs, random forests, and GNNs but relied on coarse global features (e.g., binned gate-angle counts) and vanilla architectures that ignore circuit topology. QEMFormer departs from both by encoding circuits as directed acyclic graphs with a feature encoder that preserves gate-level, global-level, and topological information, and by introducing a two-branch architecture (MLP for short-range context + Graph Transformer for long-range dependencies) rather than a single monolithic model. The paper also positions itself against non-ML QEM methods (ZNE, CDR) and the closely related GTranQEM (Bao et al., 2025), a non-message-passing graph transformer for QEM, while contributing a standardized 22-dataset benchmark (QEM-Bench) that prior studies lacked.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

QEM-Bench addresses a critical gap in the ML-QEM literature: the absence of standardized datasets and consistent evaluation protocols, which has made cross-study comparison difficult and limited coverage of diverse noise models and circuit types. The inclusion of real-device experiments on 50- and 63-qubit IBM processors (Kyiv, Brisbane) is practically important, though the results reveal that even the best method retains substantial residual error (RMSE ≈ 0.27 on Brisbane Raw), indicating the problem is far from solved. Open questions include whether the two-branch design's advantage over a single Graph Transformer persists at larger scales, how well these methods handle non-Markovian noise, and whether the DAG-based encoding generalizes to circuits with mid-circuit measurements or adaptive operations.
