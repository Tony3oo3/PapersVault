## Problem

Induction heads, the circuit underlying in-context learning in transformers, are known to require at least two layers of attention and sufficient hidden-state width to function, yet prior work has focused almost exclusively on interpreting these circuits rather than modifying the architecture to make them easier to learn. This paper asks whether a lightweight modification to the attention mechanism can reduce both the depth and width requirements for induction, thereby accelerating the model's acquisition of in-context learning capabilities during pre-training.

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard token sequences for decoder-only language modeling, producing next-token prediction logits as output. The modification is confined to the key and value projections: each token's key and value are replaced by a learnable convex combination of the original and a one-position-shifted version (discarding the last token, padding zero at the front), adding only 4 scalar parameters per attention head. The causal mask is preserved, and the additional computation is O(ND), negligible relative to the O(ND² + N²D) attention cost.

## Method

KV shifting attention decouples keys from values by mixing each token's K and V with its immediate predecessor's K and V via learnable weights (α₁, α₂ for keys; β₁, β₂ for values), so that attending to the i-th token's key retrieves the (i−1)-th token's value and vice versa. This single architectural change allows a one-layer, single-head attention with D=d dimensions to exactly represent the induction heads function (Theorem 3.3), whereas vanilla attention requires two layers and D=2d to approximate it (Theorem 3.2). The initialization sets α₁, β₁ ~ U(0,1) with α₂=1−α₁, β₂=1−β₂ to preserve the original attention at step zero. The paper further provides a theoretical analysis (Theorem 3.4) showing that under simplified conditions, gradient descent on the four shift parameters drives the model toward the induction-head configuration (α₁,β₁)→(0,1), and validates the approach empirically from toy induction tasks through 19B-parameter pre-training, demonstrating consistent loss reductions and benchmark improvements over vanilla, differential, and selective attention baselines.

## Results

On the 2.9B model trained on 500B tokens, KV shifting attention achieves an average benchmark score of 38.57 versus 36.45 for vanilla attention, with notable gains on MMLU (40.88 vs 37.26), CMMLU (40.78 vs 38.22), and ARC-Easy (39.02 vs 36.20). At the 19B scale (200B tokens), KV shifting reaches 38.83 average versus 38.06 for vanilla, with the largest gap on ARC-Challenge (29.32 vs 25.78) and MATH (3.20 vs 2.60). A key efficiency finding is that KV shifting at 340B tokens already matches vanilla at 500B tokens (36.46 vs 36.45 average), suggesting ~32% token savings. On iGSM math reasoning, KV shifting improves accuracy from 0.8154 to 0.8909 (TR12-TE15) and from 0.8711 to 0.9062 (TR21-TE24).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the primary vanilla (standard multi-head attention) baseline, KV shifting attention consistently outperforms at every scale tested (1.5B, 2.9B, 6.7B, 13B, 19B) and at every training checkpoint, with the 2.9B model showing a +2.12 average benchmark improvement at 500B tokens and the 19B model showing +0.77 at 200B tokens. The convergence advantage is particularly striking: KV shifting at 340B tokens already matches vanilla at 500B tokens, and at 1e-2 learning rate vanilla diverges while KV shifting remains stable, suggesting a flatter optimization landscape. Against Differential Transformer (Ye et al., 2024), KV shifting achieves lower validation loss across nearly all learning-rate and context-length settings in the 0.2B model comparison (e.g., 3.2404 vs 3.3187 at LR=2e-3, context=4096), and Differential Transformer exhibits instability at higher learning rates (loss spikes to 7.83 at LR=4e-3, context=256). Against Selective Attention (Leviathan et al., 2024), KV shifting achieves lower validation loss at all tested settings and, critically, Selective Attention runs out of memory beyond 1024 context length while KV shifting handles 4096 without issue (peak memory 43360 MiB vs 69500 MiB for Selective Attention at 1024 context).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the induction heads mechanism (Elhage et al., 2021; Olsson et al., 2022) and the virtual attention heads framework, but departs from prior work by using mechanistic interpretability insights to *modify* the attention architecture rather than merely analyze it. While Wang et al. (2024) and Sanford et al. (2024a) established that standard transformers require at least two layers and doubled width to implement induction heads, this paper proposes KV shifting attention as a minimal architectural change (4 learnable scalars per head) that provably reduces both requirements to one layer and single width. It also connects to the broader line of attention modifications (Differential Transformer, Selective Attention) and non-transformer architectures that use short convolutions (RWKV, Mamba, DeltaNet), positioning the shift operation specifically as a key-value decoupling mechanism rather than a general local-fusion operation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is high: KV shifting attention adds negligible parameters and compute (O(ND) vs O(ND²+N²D)) yet consistently improves language modeling across 1.5B–19B scales, with the added benefit of greater training stability at high learning rates. Open questions include how the mechanism interacts with multi-layer induction circuits, whether it facilitates length generalization, and whether the theoretical single-layer result extends to deeper architectures. A key limitation is the use of non-public training data, which hinders independent verification, and the theoretical analysis is restricted to the single-layer, single-head case with Alibi RPE.
