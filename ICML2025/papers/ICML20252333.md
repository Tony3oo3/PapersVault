## Problem

Previous studies of the Hessian in deep learning reported only a coarse two-component structure (a few large eigenvalues and many near-zero ones), leaving the statistical distribution of the bulk spectrum unexplored. This paper identifies that a simple power-law structure has been overlooked in well-trained networks, and further reveals that conventional sharpness-based generalization measures, which work well for CNNs, largely fail as predictors of LLM generalization during training.

## Input / Output

The method takes the parameters of a trained (or randomly initialized) neural network and a target dataset, then uses Stochastic Lanczos Quadrature (SLQ) with a fixed batch size (256 for LLMs, 1000 samples per run for ViT) to approximate the top ~1000 Hessian eigenvalues above a cutoff. Outputs include the fitted power-law exponent β and Zipf slope s, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance d_ks against a critical value d_c (at α=0.05), the eigengap spectrum, and Pearson correlations between these spectral quantities and test loss or sharpness metrics. The analysis is restricted to eigenvalues above a minimal cutoff to ensure convergence and avoid inaccurate estimation of near-zero eigenvalues.

## Method

The paper first empirically discovers that the Hessian eigenvalue spectrum of well-trained DNNs (LeNet, ResNet18, GPT-2, TinyLlama, ViT) follows a power-law distribution p(λ) ∝ λ^{-β}, validated via MLE parameter estimation and formal KS goodness-of-fit tests, while random or underparameterized networks do not. A maximum-entropy principle combining spectral entropy and volume entropy (log det H^{-1}) is shown to analytically yield the power-law form (Theorem 1). The paper further demonstrates that Hessian eigengaps also follow a power law with exponent s+1, and applies a Davis-Kahan theorem variant to prove that the top eigenspace is robustly stable while tailed eigenspaces are unstable, explaining the empirically observed low-dimensional learning subspace. For LLMs, the KS distance d_ks (measuring power-law adherence) is shown to correlate with test loss (Pearson r up to 0.98) while largest-eigenvalue and trace-based sharpness measures fail, suggesting that power-law goodness captures generalization in the far-from-minima regime typical of LLM training.

## Results

The paper's headline finding is that well-trained deep networks (CNNs and LLMs) exhibit a power-law Hessian spectrum that passes the Kolmogorov-Smirnov goodness-of-fit test (d_ks well below the critical value d_c = 0.043 at α=0.05), while randomly initialized networks fail the test. For example, LeNet on MNIST trained with SGD yields d_ks = 0.009 (power-law confirmed, β̂ = 1.991, ŝ = 1.009), whereas the same architecture at random initialization gives d_ks = 0.0796 (power-law rejected). For LLMs, the KS distance serves as a strong generalization predictor during training: Pearson correlation with test loss reaches 0.923 for GPT2-nano pretraining on Shakespeare and 0.980 for GPT2-small fine-tuning, while conventional sharpness-based measures (λ₁ and Tr(H)) yield correlations of −0.714 and 0.247 respectively—effectively useless or harmful. The power-law structure also holds across 11 different optimizers and exhibits a sharp phase transition in large-batch training (power-law breaks when batch size increases from 640 to 768 on MNIST/LeNet).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new training method to benchmark against baselines; instead, it compares analytical perspectives on the Hessian spectrum. The key comparison is between the proposed power-law spectral analysis (quantified by KS distance d_ks) and conventional sharpness-based generalization measures (largest eigenvalue λ₁ and Hessian trace Tr(H)). For CNNs trained to convergence, both approaches agree: a smaller ŝ correlates with smaller λ₁ and Tr(H), consistent with prior work (Jiang et al., 2019). However, for LLMs—which are extremely over-parameterized and often far from well-trained minima—the comparison is stark: during GPT2-small fine-tuning on Shakespeare, the KS distance tracks test loss with Pearson r = 0.980, while λ₁ and Tr(H) yield r = −0.714 and r = 0.247, making sharpness-based measures actively misleading. A second comparison is between trained and randomly initialized networks: the power-law hypothesis is statistically rejected for all random initializations (d_ks > d_c across all models and datasets) but accepted for all well-trained checkpoints, confirming that the structure emerges from optimization rather than architecture. Finally, the paper compares 11 optimizers (SGD, Adam, AMSGrad, AdaBound, Yogi, RAdam, Adai, PNM, Lookahead, DiffGrad, Vanilla SGD) and finds the power-law structure holds universally as long as the optimizer trains the network well, with ŝ ranging from 1.009 (SGD) to 1.363 (RAdam) on CIFAR-10.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of Hessian spectrum analysis in deep learning (Sagun et al., 2016; Wu et al., 2017; Ghorbani et al., 2019), which previously characterized the spectrum as a coarse 'two-component' structure (few large eigenvalues, many near-zero). The authors depart from this by identifying a finer-grained power-law distribution within the non-zero eigenvalues, supported by formal KS goodness-of-fit tests and a maximum-entropy derivation borrowed from statistical physics (Visser, 2013). The eigenvector robustness analysis builds directly on a Davis-Kahan theorem variant (Yu et al., 2015), and the power-law fitting methodology follows Clauset et al. (2009) and the Powerlaw library (Alstott et al., 2014). A key conceptual departure is extending Hessian spectral analysis from CNNs to LLMs (GPT-2 family, TinyLlama), where prior work had largely not ventured.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that conventional sharpness-based generalization measures (largest eigenvalue, Hessian trace) fail as predictors for LLMs while power-law goodness (KS distance) correlates strongly with test performance challenges a widely held assumption in the field and suggests that generalization theory for LLMs needs rethinking. The power-law structure also provides a unifying lens connecting overparameterization, batch-size phase transitions, and scaling behavior. Key limitations include the inability to test on state-of-the-art LLMs due to Hessian computation cost, and the authors themselves note that power-law goodness is not a universally robust generalization measure. Open questions include whether the power-law structure persists in much larger models, its relationship to scaling laws, and a deeper generalization theory for LLMs that accounts for the different training regimes (far-from-minima vs. close-to-minima).
