## Problem

Standard user-level differential privacy assumes each training example is attributed to a single user, but many real datasets (e.g., emails, collaborative papers) contain examples that carry sensitive information about multiple users simultaneously. This paper initiates the study of private ML under a multi-attribution model where each example is associated with a set of users, requiring both a new privacy definition and new data-selection algorithms that existing single-attribution reductions cannot handle.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20250701/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an attributed dataset D = ((x₁,e₁),…,(xₘ,eₘ)) where each example xᵢ is paired with a hyperedge eᵢ ⊆ V specifying the users it is attributed to, along with a privacy budget (ε, δ) and a contribution bound k. The hypergraph (V, E) is treated as fixed public structure; the DP guarantee protects against adversarially replacing all example contents adjacent to any single user. The output is a trained model (fine-tuned BERT-Tiny for masked language modeling on arXiv abstracts, or a logistic regression classifier on synthetic data) together with the contribution-bounded subset S used for training. The contribution bounding step is performed independently of example contents, incurring no additional privacy cost.

## Method

The paper introduces fixed-graph DP, a privacy definition where two datasets are adjacent if they share the same hypergraph but differ in all examples touching a single user, and then reduces private training to a two-phase procedure: (1) solve the contribution bounding problem to select a subset S where each user appears in at most k examples (or satisfies (k,b)-min-sep for DP-MF), and (2) run standard DP-SGD with Poisson sampling and group-level privacy accounting, or DP-MF (BandMF) with min-sep batch selection, on S. Since maximizing |S| under the contribution bound is NP-hard (reducible from independent set), the paper proposes greedy baseline algorithms that sort examples by increasing user count and iteratively add examples while respecting the per-user cap, with variants allowing or disallowing duplicates. The key empirical finding is a bias-variance tradeoff: the greedy algorithm inherently favors examples with fewer users (reducing bias toward high-arity examples), and the optimal strategy depends on the privacy regime—noise reduction (larger S) dominates at low ε, while bias mitigation becomes important at higher ε for skewed graphs.

## Results

On the arXiv dataset (BERT-Tiny, masked language modeling, δ=10⁻¹⁰), DP-SGD with duplication consistently outperforms DP-MF across all ε ∈ {2²,…,2⁶} and all contribution bounds. The greedy contribution bounding algorithm selects ≈98.7% of the examples found by an exact ILP solution (e.g., 370,019 vs. 374,663 at k=2; 475,831 vs. 482,128 at k=3), translating to at most a 1.25% increase in noise multiplier and 0.25% increase in test loss. On the synthetic logistic regression task, DP-MF gains an edge over DP-SGD at larger ε, on more skewed graphs, and when users attribute more samples on average. The greedy algorithm's inherent bias toward fewer-user examples costs roughly a √2 multiplicative factor in ε on arXiv compared to an unbiased random subset of the same size.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is DP-SGD (with Poisson sampling and privacy amplification) versus DP-MF/BandMF (with min-sep batch selection, no amplification). On arXiv, DP-SGD outperforms DP-MF at every ε value tested, which the authors attribute to the small number of iterations per epoch reducing the effective min-sep parameter b, thereby minimizing DP-MF's advantage. On the synthetic logistic regression task, DP-MF reverses the outcome and wins when ε is larger (weaker amplification), the graph is more skewed (DP-SGD's duplication introduces more bias), or users attribute more samples on average (min-sep avoids high-privacy-loss multi-contribution events). The greedy contribution bounding algorithm is compared against an exact ILP: it achieves ≈98.7% of the optimal solution size (≈1.3% gap), with negligible impact on test loss (≤0.25%). Notably, when duplicates are allowed, the ILP actually selects fewer *distinct* examples (231k vs. 370k at k=2) because it over-duplicates single-author examples, leading to worse training outcomes than the greedy algorithm. For bias mitigation, the paper finds that on arXiv the optimal strategy is to simply maximize dataset size (no bias correction), while on logistic regression a carefully tuned degree-size tradeoff yields non-negligible accuracy gains over the naive greedy solution.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends user-level differential privacy from the standard single-attribution model (where each example belongs to one user, enabling reductions to example-level DP via DP-FedSGD) to a multi-attribution model where each example can be attributed to multiple users, formalized as fixed-graph DP over a hypergraph. The key algorithmic departure is the contribution bounding problem—selecting a data subset where each user contributes at most k examples—which is trivial in the single-attribution case but NP-hard here. For privacy accounting, the paper repurposes existing results: Poisson sampling amplification for DP-SGD (Charles et al., 2024) and min-separation analysis for BandMF (Choquette-Choo et al., 2024), applying them once the contribution bound is satisfied. The paper positions fixed-graph DP as a practical middle ground between example-level DP and the much harder node-level DP for hypergraphs (Fang et al., 2022), arguing that the two-phase algorithm structure (graph pruning then content-agnostic training) makes hypergraph leakage unlikely in practice.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The multi-attribution model captures realistic scenarios (emails, collaborative documents, social media) where a single data point contains sensitive information about multiple parties, making the single-attribution assumption inadequate. The paper's main open questions include whether node-DP algorithms can match the accuracy of fixed-graph DP algorithms, whether tight amplification guarantees exist for DP-MF in the multi-attribution setting, and whether the two-phase structure admits actionable attacks on the hypergraph structure. The finding that a simple greedy algorithm achieves within 1.3% of the ILP optimum suggests the NP-hardness is largely pathological, but the significant bias-variance tradeoff (bias toward fewer-user examples costs ~√2 in ε) means practical deployments will need careful tuning. The empirical scope is limited to BERT-Tiny on arXiv abstracts and synthetic logistic regression, leaving open whether these findings transfer to larger-scale or more complex training tasks.
