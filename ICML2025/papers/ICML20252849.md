## Problem

Neurons in transformer LLMs suffer from polysemanticity, encoding multiple unrelated concepts simultaneously and obscuring mechanistic interpretability. Post-hoc methods like Sparse Autoencoders are computationally expensive, incomplete, and introduce performance loss. No prior architecture was designed to be intrinsically interpretable at scale while maintaining competitive language modeling performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252849/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes text token sequences (chess PGN strings up to 1023 characters for interpretability evaluation, or natural language tokens with 1024-token context for performance evaluation) and produces standard next-token predictions. Unusually, the internal MLP hidden activations are structured to be both wide (MD dimensions across M experts) and sparse (only top-k experts active, with ReLU zeroing within each expert), making individual neurons directly interpretable without any post-hoc decomposition. The routing scores themselves encode an estimate of each expert's expected activation sparsity.

## Method

MoE-X reformulates a standard Sparse MoE layer as an equivalent wide-and-sparse MLP by concatenating all expert decoders into a 'mega-decoder' and scaling each expert's hidden vector by its gating weight. Two key modifications enforce interpretability: (1) ReLU activation within each expert induces intrinsic activation sparsity, reducing the ℓ₀ norm from ~4091 (GELU) to ~166; (2) Sparsity-Aware Routing replaces standard top-k gating with a score of -erf(μ_h/(√2 σ_h)), where μ_h and σ_h are estimated from column-wise statistics of each expert's encoder weights under a Gaussian assumption, so the router selects experts predicted to produce the sparsest activations. This routing implicitly regularizes experts toward sparser representations during training since gradients flow through the encoder weights. The model is trained via upcycling from a dense GPT-2 checkpoint with a load-balance auxiliary loss, achieving GPT-2-level perplexity while surpassing SAE-based interpretability scores.

## Results

On the chess interpretability benchmark (Table 1), MoE-X achieves the best Reconstruction Score of 0.840 and Coverage of 0.428, substantially outperforming the GPT-2 dense baseline (0.608 / 0.356) and the Switch Transformer (0.734 / 0.424), while maintaining a competitive validation loss of 0.211. On natural language modeling (Table 2), MoE-X-M (555M active params) achieves PPL of 14.78 on OpenWebText, 21.34 on LAMBADA, 35.01 on WikiText103, and 35.16 on WikiText2, beating GPT-2 Medium on all four benchmarks and matching or exceeding Switch-M. MoE-X also surpasses SAE-based interpretability (GPT-2+SAE) in both coverage and reconstruction while achieving 100% loss recovery versus SAE's ~96%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MoE-X decisively outperforms the GPT-2 dense baseline (Radford et al.) on interpretability, improving Reconstruction from 0.608 to 0.840 (+37%) and Coverage from 0.356 to 0.428, while on language modeling MoE-X-M beats GPT-2 Medium on all four PPL benchmarks (e.g., 14.78 vs 17.19 on OpenWebText). Against GEGLU (Shazeer) and SoLU (Elhage), which were previously proposed for interpretability, MoE-X achieves far higher scores (Reconstruction 0.840 vs 0.394 and 0.343 respectively), and the paper notes SoLU actually underperforms the plain GELU baseline. MoE-X also substantially beats the fine-grained MoE baselines MONET (Park et al.) and PEER (He), which claim monosemanticity improvements but score only 0.528/0.482 and 0.426 in Reconstruction respectively. Against the closest competitor, Switch Transformer (Fedus et al.), MoE-X wins on Reconstruction (0.840 vs 0.734) and ties on Coverage (0.428 vs 0.424), though Switch has a slight PPL edge on language tasks (e.g., 18.36 vs 19.42 on OpenWebText for the small model). Finally, MoE-X surpasses the SAE post-hoc baseline (Gao et al.) in interpretability while maintaining perfect model fidelity (100% loss recovery) versus SAE's ~96%, and the paper argues SAE fundamentally cannot capture all model features.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Sparsely-Gated MoE (Shazeer et al., 2017) and Switch Transformer (Fedus et al., 2022) architectures, but repositions them from an efficiency tool to an interpretability mechanism. The key conceptual departure is the reformulation of MoE as a wide, sparse MLP (building on Liu et al., 2023c's algebraic equivalence), combined with two targeted modifications: ReLU experts for intrinsic activation sparsity and a sparsity-aware routing function that replaces standard top-k gating. This contrasts with prior interpretability efforts—SAEs (Gao et al., 2024), SoLU (Elhage et al., 2022a), GEGLU (Shazeer, 2020), and Monet (Park et al., 2024)—which either operate post-hoc or were validated only on toy-scale tasks, whereas MoE-X embeds interpretability into the architecture itself and validates on language-modeling-scale experiments.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MoE-X offers a practical, training-time solution to polysemanticity that avoids the computational cost and incompleteness of post-hoc SAEs, and the sparsity-aware routing is computationally cheap (O((N+D)Md) vs O(NMDd) for naive evaluation). However, all experiments use relatively small models (124M–555M active parameters) and the primary interpretability evaluation relies on chess PGN, a somewhat artificial proxy; whether the interpretability advantage persists at the scale of modern LLMs (billions of parameters) remains an open question. The negative results showing that SoLU and Monet do not deliver their claimed interpretability gains also suggest the field needs more rigorous empirical validation of architectural interpretability claims.
