## Problem

Traditional GNNs cannot generalize zero-shot to test graphs whose node attributes differ from training attributes in type, name, semantics, or cardinality, because they rely on absolute attribute values that have no correspondence across domains. The core gap is the absence of a unified input space for node attributes that enables transfer of learned prediction patterns across entirely different attribute spaces without retraining or adaptation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20252172/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

STAGE takes an attributed graph G=(V,E,X) where node attributes can be continuous, categorical, or mixed, and produces fixed-dimensional edge embeddings that replace the original node attributes before a downstream GNN computes link prediction scores or node classification labels. The STAGE-edge-graph construction is linear in graph size but quadratic in the number of attributes per node, making it suited to small-to-medium graphs (up to ~4k nodes, 50k edges, 16 attributes). The two GNNs (M1 for intra-edge, M2 for inter-edge) are trained end-to-end on the target task, and the final representation is invariant to the specific attribute values, names, and ordering.

## Method

STAGE transforms node attributes from their absolute natural space into a relative space of statistical dependencies by constructing, for each edge (u,v), a fully connected weighted directed graph (STAGE-edge-graph) with 2d nodes (one per attribute of each endpoint) where node attributes are empirical marginal CDFs/PDFs and edge weights are empirical conditional probabilities between attribute pairs. A shallow GNN M1 encodes each STAGE-edge-graph into a fixed-dimensional edge embedding, and a second GNN M2 processes the original graph topology augmented with these edge embeddings (but without original node attributes) to produce the final representation. The key theoretical contribution is proving that STAGE is invariant to COGGs (component-wise order-preserving groupoids for graphs), which formalize invariance to order-preserving transformations of attribute values, permutations of attribute order, and permutations of node identities—thereby providing provable zero-shot generalization guarantees across a family of attribute domain shifts. The use of groupoids rather than groups is essential because transformations act between distinct attribute spaces rather than within a single space.

## Results

On zero-shot link prediction over E-Commerce Stores (trained on 4 domains, tested on the 5th), NBFNet-STAGE achieves 0.4606 Hits@1 and 0.4971 MRR, outperforming the strongest baseline (NBFNet-normalized: 0.3269 Hits@1) by 41% in Hits@1 and 29% in MRR. On the cross-dataset H&M evaluation, STAGE reaches 0.4666 Hits@1 and 0.4703 MRR, a 103% relative improvement over the best parametric baseline (NBFNet-llm: 0.2302 Hits@1) and 202% over a supervised structural method trained directly on H&M. For zero-shot node classification (Friendster→Pokec, gender prediction), GINE-STAGE achieves 65.2% accuracy, a 10.3% improvement over the best baseline GraphAny (59.1%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

STAGE consistently outperforms all baselines across both link prediction and node classification tasks. Against the LLM-based baseline (PRODIGY / NBFNet-llm, Huang et al. 2023), STAGE achieves 103% higher Hits@1 on H&M (0.4666 vs 0.2302) and 43% higher on held-out E-Commerce domains (0.4606 vs 0.3226), demonstrating that converting attributes to text and encoding with a language model is inferior to learning statistical dependencies directly. The Gaussian-noise baseline (Abboud et al. 2021; Sato et al. 2021) performs poorly, especially cross-dataset (0.0925 Hits@1 on H&M), confirming that random features cannot capture meaningful attribute relationships. For node classification, STAGE surpasses GraphAny (Zhao et al. 2024b) by 10.3% (65.2% vs 59.1% accuracy on Pokec) and GCOPE (Zhao et al. 2024a) by 21.9% (65.2% vs 53.5%), indicating that the analytical/cross-domain-pretraining approaches are less effective than STAGE's unified statistical-dependency representation. Notably, STAGE also beats a supervised structural method trained and tested on H&M by 202% in Hits@1, showing that even with in-domain supervision, ignoring node attributes is far less effective than STAGE's zero-shot attribute encoding.

## Relation to Prior Work

STAGE departs from two dominant strategies for handling unseen node attributes: ignoring attributes entirely (structural-only GNNs) and converting attributes to text for LLM-based encoding (PRODIGY, LLaGA). Instead, it repurposes the classical statistical insight that rank tests for dependency are invariant to order-preserving transformations (Bell 1964; Berk & Bickel 1968), translating this into a GNN-compatible representation via STAGE-edge-graphs whose edge weights are empirical conditional probabilities. The theoretical formalization using groupoids (rather than groups) to capture invariances *across* distinct attribute spaces is a novel algebraic tool not previously applied to GNN generalization, and the bijective correspondence between STAGE-edge-graphs and feature hypergraphs (Theorem 3.3) bridges statistical testing theory and graph representation learning in a way that prior graph generalization work (domain adaptation, OOD GNNs) did not attempt.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

STAGE provides the first provable invariance guarantees (COGG) for zero-shot transfer across attribute domains in graphs, offering a principled unified input space that could underpin future graph foundation models. However, the quadratic complexity in the number of attributes and the restriction to pairwise dependencies limit applicability to small/medium graphs (≤4k nodes, ≤16 attributes), and higher-order attribute interactions remain unaddressed. The finding that a simple statistical dependency representation outperforms LLM-based text encodings on numerical and categorical attributes is practically relevant, but whether this advantage persists at scale or with more complex attribute interactions is an open question.
