## Problem

Standard transformers lack an inherent iterative structure, making it difficult to emulate the iterative algorithms (e.g., gradient descent) that underpin traditional machine learning solvers. While prior work showed a simplified one-layer linear self-attention transformer implicitly performs one step of gradient descent, the gap between that minimal architecture and the standard multi-layer decoder transformer used in practice remains unresolved. This paper asks whether a looped (weight-shared) transformer can emulate iterative learning algorithms more efficiently than a standard non-recursive transformer.

## Input / Output

The model receives a prompt sequence of k in-context input-output pairs (x_i, f(x_i)) followed by a test query x_test, all drawn from a function class (linear, sparse linear, decision tree, or 2-layer ReLU network) with problem dimension d=20. The looped transformer is a single-layer GPT-2 block (D=256, h=8 heads, 0.79M parameters) that is unrolled for t iterations at inference, producing a scalar prediction f̂(x_test) for the test query. This contrasts with the standard 12-layer transformer (9.48M parameters) that processes the same prompt in one forward pass, yielding a 12× parameter reduction.

## Method

The core contribution is a training methodology for a single-layer looped transformer that emulates iterative algorithms. The architecture uses input injection (Y_{t+1} = M(Y_t + P)) rather than pure weight-tying, which prevents the solution from diverging beyond the trained number of loop iterations. Training minimizes a truncated loss over a window of T consecutive loop iterations (from b−T to b), analogous to truncated backpropagation through time, with b controlling the maximum unroll depth and T controlling the memory footprint. The authors show that choosing b sufficiently large (e.g., b=20 for linear regression, b=70 for decision trees) allows the looped transformer to discover a stable fixed-point solution that generalizes beyond the trained iterations, effectively emulating a convergent iterative algorithm. Empirically, this single-layer looped transformer matches or outperforms the 12-layer standard transformer across linear regression, sparse linear regression, decision trees, and 2-layer ReLU networks, while using less than 10% of the parameters.

## Results

The looped transformer (1 layer, 0.79M parameters) matches the standard 12-layer GPT-2 transformer (9.48M parameters) on in-context linear regression, using less than 10% of the parameter count. On sparse linear regression with 40 in-context samples, the looped transformer achieves a normalized error of 6.12e-04, outperforming both the standard transformer (0.0017) and Lasso (1.32e-04). On OpenML binary classification tasks, the looped transformer achieves higher test accuracy than the standard transformer on 5 out of 10 datasets (e.g., 0.757 vs 0.668 on 'wind', 0.72 vs 0.656 on 'space_ga'), while remaining competitive on the rest.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the standard 12-layer GPT-2 transformer (Garg et al. 2022 setup, 9.48M params), the 1-layer looped transformer (0.79M params) matches performance on linear regression and 2-layer ReLU NNs, and outperforms it on sparse linear regression across all sparsity levels (s=1,3,6,9,12), particularly when in-context samples are limited. On decision trees, the looped transformer also matches the standard transformer. Against Lasso (Tibshirani 1996) on sparse linear regression, the looped transformer achieves lower error than Lasso when fewer than 10 in-context samples are provided, though Lasso still achieves lower absolute error at 40 samples (1.32e-04 vs 6.12e-04). The paper attributes the looped transformer's advantage on sparse tasks to an inductive bias toward simpler (sparser) solutions. On OpenML datasets, the looped transformer wins on 5/10 tasks, ties on 2, and loses narrowly on 3, suggesting comparable real-world utility at a fraction of the parameter cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two research threads: (1) Garg et al. (2022), who established that standard 12-layer GPT-2 transformers can be trained from scratch to solve in-context data-fitting problems (linear regression, sparse linear, decision trees, 2-layer NNs), and (2) Giannou et al. (2023), who demonstrated that looped (weight-shared) transformers can function as programmable computers. The paper departs from Garg et al. by replacing the 12-layer architecture with a single-layer looped transformer and developing a training methodology (input injection + truncated loss over loop iterations) that enables the looped model to converge to a stable fixed-point solution. It also connects to the deep equilibrium model literature (Bai et al., 2019) on fixed-point iteration and to von Oswald et al. (2022), who showed that linear self-attention implicitly performs one step of gradient descent, by asking whether a looped architecture can emulate the full iterative algorithm rather than a single step.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that a 1-layer looped transformer with ~0.79M parameters can match a 12-layer standard transformer (~9.48M parameters) on in-context data-fitting, offering a concrete recipe for parameter-efficient iterative computation in transformers. However, the paper is candid about limitations: the learned fixed-point solution carries an inherent error floor, generalizes poorly out-of-distribution (e.g., scaled inputs), and does not truly discover the underlying algorithm—only emulates it within the training distribution. Open questions include whether adaptive loop iteration counts (token-level or task-level) can further improve efficiency, whether the inductive bias toward simpler solutions can be controlled, and whether the approach scales to more complex or higher-dimensional tasks. The finding that b and T values serve as a proxy for task difficulty is an interesting but preliminary observation that could inform curriculum design.
