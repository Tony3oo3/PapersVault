## Problem

Mixture-of-regressions problems arise in federated learning, crowdsourcing, and recommendation systems, but existing algorithms are highly model-specific, require knowledge of problem parameters (hypercontractivity bounds, spectral norms, noise levels, number of subpopulations), and are brittle under model misspecification. This paper asks whether a general-purpose transformer architecture, trained with standard gradient descent, can learn a near-Bayes-optimal predictor for mixtures of linear regressions without such specialized assumptions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240601/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The input is a prompt P = (x_1, y_1, ..., x_k, y_k, x_{k+1}) of length k+1, where each x_i ∈ R^d is drawn from N(0, I_d) and y_i | x_i ~ N(⟨w, x_i⟩, σ²) with w sampled from a discrete uniform mixture of m point masses on the sphere of radius √d. The transformer processes this as a sequence of 2k+1 tokens (interleaved x and y vectors plus the final query x_{k+1}) and outputs a single scalar prediction ŷ_{k+1}. Experiments use d=20, m ∈ {5, 20}, and prompt lengths k from 1 to 60.

## Method

The paper provides both a constructive representation proof and empirical validation. Theoretically, it shows the Bayes-optimal posterior mean predictor f_π*(P) = ⟨ŵ(P), x_{k+1}⟩ — which computes residuals for each mixture component, squares and sums them over the prompt, applies a softmax to obtain posterior weights, and forms a weighted prediction — can be implemented by a 9-layer autoregressive transformer using a sequence of primitive operations (copy_down, copy_over, mul, aff, scaled_agg, soft), each realizable in a single transformer layer via the RAW operator framework. Empirically, 12-layer decoder-only transformers (hidden dim 256, 8 attention heads, GeLU nonlinearity, layer normalization) are trained with Adam (lr=0.1) and curriculum learning over increasing prompt lengths. The trained transformers achieve normalized MSE close to the oracle posterior mean and argmin procedures, outperform OLS and ridge regression, and are competitive with model-specific algorithms (EM, subspace algorithm) at fixed training set sizes.

## Results

The paper evaluates transformers on mixture-of-linear-regression tasks with d=20, m∈{5,20} components, and noise levels σ∈{0,1}. The trained transformer (12 layers, p=256, 8 heads) achieves normalized MSE nearly matching the Bayes-optimal posterior mean across prompt lengths k=1 to 60, substantially outperforming OLS and ridge regression. At fixed training set sizes (n=15,000 to 60,000), the transformer's inference-time MSE is competitive with the state-of-the-art Subspace Algorithm (SA) and EM-based plug-in methods. Inference-time probing (squared distance metric) confirms the transformer's predictions are closest to the posterior mean with oracle weights, regardless of the number of mixture components.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The transformer substantially outperforms OLS and ridge regression (with optimal penalty from Dicker 2016 / Pathak et al. 2023) across all prompt lengths, as OLS is known to be suboptimal for mixture settings. Against the oracle posterior mean and oracle argmin procedures (which know the true mixture weights {w_j*}), the transformer achieves nearly identical normalized MSE, indicating near-Bayes-optimal performance without explicit knowledge of the mixture components. At fixed training set sizes (Figure 3), the transformer matches or slightly outperforms both the EM-based plug-in estimator and the Subspace Algorithm (SA) from Jain et al. 2023, demonstrating sample efficiency comparable to model-specific methods. The inference-time distance analysis (Figure 4) shows the transformer is closest to the posterior mean with oracle weights (d_k^sq smallest), rather than to argmin or to plug-in variants using estimated weights. For large m (Appendix G), the ratio of ridge regression MSE to transformer MSE decreases, consistent with the theoretical prediction that the optimal estimator converges to ridge regression as m→∞.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two research threads: (1) model-specific algorithms for mixture of linear regressions (Kong et al. 2020; Jain et al. 2023), which require knowledge of problem parameters like noise level and number of components, and (2) transformer in-context learning for simple function classes (Garg et al. 2022; Akyürek et al. 2023), which showed transformers can learn linear regression but not mixture settings. The key departure is showing that a general-purpose autoregressive transformer, trained with standard gradient descent, can learn the Bayes-optimal posterior mean procedure for mixture models—something no prior model-specific algorithm achieves without explicit parameter knowledge. The constructive proof (Theorem 1) builds directly on the RAW operator framework from Akyürek et al. (2023) to show the optimal procedure is transformer-representable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides evidence that transformers serve as general-purpose predictors for statistically complex mixture settings, with practical implications for federated learning, recommendation systems, and crowdsourcing where data naturally arrives in heterogeneous batches. The connection to LLM pretraining data mixtures—where models must select relevant subpopulations from diverse training data—suggests a broader principle about how attention mechanisms perform implicit model selection. Open questions include whether the in-context setting (where the mixture distribution itself is sampled per prompt) remains tractable, how explicitly the transformer stores mixture parameters, and whether these results extend to more complex mixture structures. A limitation is sensitivity to weight scaling shifts, suggesting the learned solution is not fully distribution-robust.
