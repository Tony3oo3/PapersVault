## Problem

Existing LLM leaderboards suffer from training-set contamination, 'cramming for the leaderboard,' and rapid saturation, making them poor measures of genuine general-purpose language capability. A critical open question is whether models can truly combine skills in novel ways they never encountered during training (going beyond 'stochastic parrot' behavior), and no existing evaluation can reliably test this while remaining resistant to memorization.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241680/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The evaluation randomly samples a subset of k skills (each with a definition and illustrative example) from a curated list of N=101 skills and one topic from T=100 low-frequency topics (unigram probability ~10^-6), then prompts the student model to produce a minimal text of at most k-1 sentences demonstrating all k skills in that topic context. Grading is performed by a separate LLM judge (GPT-4 or LLaMA-2-70B-Chat) using a per-criterion rubric that awards 1 point per skill, 1 for topic adherence, 1 for coherence, and 1 for sentence-limit compliance, with scores aggregated via a Python script to avoid LLM arithmetic errors. Each (k skills, topic) combination is generated 3 times and graded 3 times, with majority voting across grading rounds.

## Method

SKILL-MIX exploits combinatorial explosion: with N skills, there are C(N,k) possible skill subsets, so for N=1000 and k=5 the space exceeds 10^12 combinations, making it vanishingly unlikely that any specific skill-topic pairing appeared in the training corpus. The evaluation is administered via a two-turn generation prompt (initial answer followed by a self-improvement turn) and a structured grading prompt that forces the judge to output per-criterion scores in a fixed format. The paper provides a theoretical guarantee that if a model's Ratio of Full Marks α_k exceeds (3/2)·p_s^k·p_t·L (where p_s, p_t are average skill/topic frequencies and L is corpus size), then more than one-third of its correct outputs involve novel skill-topic combinations unseen in training; GPT-4 satisfies this bound at k=5 and k=6. Ablations show that deducting points for explicitly naming skills and filtering out 17 high-frequency skills (≥5% in RedPajama) makes the evaluation substantially harder and more discriminative, with all non-GPT-4 models saturating by k=3 under the strictest variant.

## Results

On SKILL-MIX(k) graded by GPT-4, GPT-4 achieves the highest Ratio of Full Marks across all k values: 0.94 (k=2), 0.68 (k=3), 0.52 (k=4), 0.36 (k=5), and 0.29 (k=6). GPT-3.5-turbo is second at 0.53 (k=2) and 0.20 (k=3), while Xwin-LM-70B-V0.1 leads among open models at 0.42 (k=2) and 0.22 (k=3). Most models saturate (Ratio of Full Marks drops below 0.05) by k=3 or k=4, with only GPT-4, GPT-3.5-turbo, and Xwin-LM-70B-V0.1 sustaining non-trivial performance at k=5.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4 dominates all other models by a wide margin at every k value, with a Ratio of Full Marks of 0.36 at k=5 where the next-best model (GPT-3.5-turbo) scores only 0.04. Among open models, Xwin-LM-70B-V0.1 (0.42 at k=2) outperforms LLaMA-2-70B-Chat (0.28) and Falcon-180B-Chat (0.27), despite Falcon-180B-Chat ranking higher on the Hugging Face Open LLM Leaderboard. Notably, LLaMA-2-70B-Chat outperforms both Falcon-180B-Chat and Tigerbot-70B-Chat on SKILL-MIX, contradicting their leaderboard rankings and suggesting leaderboard-specific overfitting. Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.1 (0.05 at k=2) underperforms LLaMA-2-13B-Chat (0.22) despite the Mistral team's claims of superiority over LLaMA-2-13B. Qwen-14B-Chat (0.16 at k=2) also trails LLaMA-2-70B-Chat (0.28) despite outperforming it on MMLU, HumanEval, and GSM8K. The LLaMA-2 family shows a clear scaling trend: saturation point increases from k=2 (7B) to k=3 (13B and 70B), consistent with the skill-emergence theory of Arora & Goyal (2023).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes the skill-emergence theory of Arora & Goyal (2023)—which showed that scaling LLMs improves proficiency at k-tuples of basic skills—with insights from human pedagogy literature on compositional skill acquisition, to create a practical evaluation framework. It departs from existing LLM benchmarks (MMLU, AlpacaEval, Open LLM Leaderboard) by replacing fixed question sets with randomly sampled skill-topic combinations, directly addressing the contamination and 'cramming' vulnerabilities that plague static evaluations. The LLM-as-judge grading methodology builds on the broader trend of using strong models (GPT-4, LLaMA-2-70B) as automated evaluators, but introduces a structured rubric with per-criterion scoring and majority voting to mitigate known arithmetic and sycophancy failures of grader models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SKILL-MIX provides a contamination-resistant, difficulty-scalable evaluation that reveals significant gaps between leaderboard rankings and genuine compositional generalization ability, with practical implications for how the field assesses model progress. The probabilistic argument that GPT-4's k=5 performance exceeds what memorization could explain offers the first quantitative evidence (albeit model-specific) for going beyond 'stochastic parrot' behavior, a question Hinton identified as a key blocker in AI risk discussions. Open questions include whether cramming shortcuts exist for SKILL-MIX itself (the fine-tuning experiment in Appendix E suggests they do), how to grade harder future versions without circularity, and whether the framework extends to multi-modal settings. The proposed ecosystem of independent, partially-secret evaluations could become a meaningful input to AI policy if adopted by trusted research groups.
