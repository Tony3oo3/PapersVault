## Problem

Existing transformer-based MVS methods have not systematically investigated how different attention mechanisms suit different MVS pipeline components, have not effectively injected cross-view information into pre-trained ViTs without degrading their robust features, and suffer from poor length extrapolation when training at lower resolutions but testing at higher ones. These gaps limit the depth estimation accuracy and generalization of transformer-based MVS to high-resolution outdoor scenes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241562/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes N calibrated RGB images (5 views for DTU, 10 for ETH3D) with known camera intrinsics and extrinsics, resized to resolutions ranging from 576×768 (training) up to 1152×1536 (DTU testing) or 1088×1920 (Tanks-and-Temples). It produces per-view depth maps through a 4-stage coarse-to-fine cascade (32-16-8-4 inverse depth hypotheses), which are then fused via Gipuma or DPCD into a dense 3D point cloud. The frozen DINOv2-base backbone processes half-resolution images, while the trainable SVA and CVT modules operate at 1/8 scale with sequence lengths up to ~32,640 tokens.

## Method

MVSFormer++ assigns distinct attention mechanisms to different MVS modules based on their feature characteristics: linear attention (feature-level aggregation) for the Side View Attention (SVA) module that injects cross-view information into frozen DINOv2 via a side-tuning approach with separate self-attention for reference and cross-attention for source views, and vanilla attention (spatial aggregation) for the Cost Volume Transformer (CVT) that denoises the 3D cost volume. To handle the large gap between training (~6,000) and testing (~27,000–32,000) sequence lengths in CVT, the paper introduces Frustoconical Positional Encoding (FPE), which normalizes 3D positions into a frustum-shaped [0,1]³ space, and Adaptive Attention Scaling (AAS), which scales the attention logits by κ·log(n)/d to maintain invariant entropy across sequence lengths. Additional design choices include normalized 2D positional encoding for high-resolution generalization, Pre-LN for the feature encoder (faster convergence with DINOv2's high-variance features) versus Post-LN for the cost volume, and learnable adaptive layer scaling coefficients to balance contributions from different DINOv2 layers.

## Results

On DTU, MVSFormer++ achieves the best overall error of 0.2805 mm (accuracy 0.3090 mm, completeness 0.2521 mm), outperforming the previous best MVSFormer (0.289 mm) and all other learning-based methods. On Tanks-and-Temples, MVSFormer++ attains mean F-scores of 67.03 (Intermediate) and 41.70 (Advanced), ranking top-1 on both sets. On ETH3D, it achieves an F1-score of 82.99, surpassing MVSFormer (82.85) and CostFormer (77.36).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MVSFormer++ outperforms all 13 compared baselines on DTU overall error, beating the closest competitor MVSFormer (0.289 mm) by 0.0085 mm and WT-MVSNet/GeoMVSNet (0.295 mm) by 0.0145 mm. On Tanks-and-Temples, it surpasses MVSFormer (66.37/40.87) by 0.66/0.83 F-score points on Intermediate/Advanced sets, and leads D-MVSNet (64.66/41.17) by 2.37 points on Intermediate while also winning on Advanced (41.70 vs. 41.17). On ETH3D, MVSFormer++ edges out MVSFormer (82.85 F1) by 0.14 points and substantially outperforms CostFormer (77.36) and COLMAP (73.01). Traditional methods (Gipuma, PMVS, COLMAP) are consistently far behind on all three benchmarks, with COLMAP achieving only 42.14 F-score on Tanks-and-Temples Intermediate versus 67.03 for MVSFormer++. The paper also demonstrates that its components (CVT, SVA) transfer to other baselines: adding CVT to CasMVSNet* reduces overall error from 0.320 to 0.305, and adding CVT+SVA to MVSFormer-P reduces it from 0.296 to 0.288.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends MVSFormer (Cao et al., 2022), the first method to integrate pre-trained ViTs into the MVS pipeline, by replacing the DINOv1 backbone with frozen DINOv2 and systematically redesigning the transformer components around it. It departs from prior transformer-based MVS methods (TransMVSNet, WT-MVSNet, CostFormer) by assigning distinct attention mechanisms to different MVS modules—linear attention for cross-view feature encoding and vanilla attention for cost volume regularization—rather than using a single attention type throughout. The Adaptive Attention Scaling mechanism is derived from Su's (2021) entropy-invariance analysis of attention, repurposed here to address the train-test resolution gap in MVS.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides practical, well-ablated guidance on transformer design choices (LN placement, PE normalization, attention type selection) that are often overlooked in MVS research, making it a useful reference for practitioners building transformer-based MVS systems. The finding that linear attention excels at feature-level aggregation but fails at cost volume denoising offers a clear heuristic for module-specific attention selection. However, the method still inherits the coarse-to-fine error accumulation problem for tiny foreground objects, and the overall SOTA margins (e.g., 0.289→0.2805 on DTU) are modest, suggesting that further gains may require addressing the fundamental cascade architecture rather than incremental transformer refinements.
