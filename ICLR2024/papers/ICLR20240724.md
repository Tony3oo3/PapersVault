## Problem

Loss scaling laws predictably describe how training loss decreases with model size, but task performance appears discontinuous—small models score near zero until a threshold is crossed, a phenomenon labeled 'emergent abilities.' The paper hypothesizes that this apparent discontinuity is an artifact of insufficient evaluation resolution: small models do show consistent, measurable improvements (on the order of 10⁻⁵) that conventional fixed-sample evaluation cannot capture, making task performance seem unpredictable when it is in fact governed by a strict scaling law.

## Input / Output

The method takes a trained LLM checkpoint and a task instance (e.g., a HumanEval code problem or a BigBench prompt) and performs up to 10⁵ random-sampling decodes, evaluating each generated answer against ground-truth tests after every single sample. It outputs a PASSUNTIL score PU = r/K (where r is a small constant like 1 or 2 and K is the total number of samples drawn until r passes), which is a maximum-likelihood estimate of the per-instance pass probability with theoretically infinite resolution. Aggregating PU across instances yields dataset-level or instance-level scaling-law parameters (c, α) that enable prediction of task performance at unseen model sizes before training begins.

## Method

PASSUNTIL replaces fixed-budget random sampling with an adaptive stopping rule: after each decode, the answer is immediately evaluated, and sampling halts once r correct answers have been collected, recording the total draw count K. This yields PU = r/K, proven to be the MLE of the true pass probability under a negative-binomial model, and can resolve success rates as low as 10⁻⁵. From the standard loss scaling law L = cN^{-α} + L₀, the paper derives a task scaling law PU(N) ∝ exp(−cN^{-α}), implying log(−log PU) is linear in log N; this is validated empirically on models from 0.03B to 2.4B with prediction deviations as low as 0.05%. To handle heterogeneous instance difficulty, an instance-level fit assigns each test sample its own (c_s, α_s) and averages the resulting IPU curves, with test loss on ground-truth answers used as an auxiliary signal for instances that remain at zero PU. Finally, the paper identifies 'accelerated emergence'—tasks whose log(−log PU) vs. log N curve is concave (super-scaling-law growth)—and proves that multi-step reasoning produces convex (sub-scaling) curves, while a 'multiple circuits' hypothesis (inspired by grokking explanations) naturally yields the observed concavity.

## Results

Using PASSUNTIL with instance-level fitting, the paper predicts the 2.4B model's task performance from 0.03B–1.5B models with a mere 0.05% deviation on HumanEval (Series 1) and 1.7% deviation on Date Understanding (Series 2). The dataset-level fit achieves larger deviations (9.3% on HumanEval-1, 9.0% on Date Understanding-2), while instance-level fitting substantially narrows the gap. The paper also identifies that 5 out of 8 Unnatural In-context Learning tasks exhibit concave (super-scaling) curves in log(-log(PU)) vs. log(N), which cannot be fitted by the standard task scaling law.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external baseline methods; instead, it contrasts two variants of its own fitting strategy (dataset-level vs. instance-level) against ground-truth 2.4B model performance. Instance-level fitting dramatically outperforms dataset-level fitting on HumanEval (0.05% vs. 9.3% deviation, Series 1) and Date Understanding (1.7% vs. 9.0% deviation, Series 2), confirming that per-instance scaling curves are necessary when test difficulty is heterogeneous. However, on Emoji Movie (Series 2), dataset-level fitting (8.7% deviation) is actually closer to the real value than instance-level fitting (19.3% deviation), suggesting the instance-level approach can overfit on very small test sets. Conceptually, the paper positions itself against Schaeffer et al. (2023), who proposed 'change of metrics' to smooth emergence; the authors argue their infinite-resolution approach directly targets the original target metric (exact match) rather than substituting a proxy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the loss scaling law of Kaplan et al. (2020) to task performance by deriving a task scaling law of the form PU(N) ~ exp(-cN^{-α}), and builds on Schaeffer et al. (2023) by extending their 'increase resolution' approach to a practical, infinite-resolution evaluation strategy (PASSUNTIL) rather than their 'change of metrics' alternative. It positions itself as the first open-source, systematic verification of the predictable task scaling claimed (but not detailed) in the GPT-4 technical report, and introduces a theoretical framework distinguishing sub-scaling (multi-step reasoning) from super-scaling (multiple circuits) growth patterns.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The PASSUNTIL evaluation strategy and the derived task scaling law provide a concrete, reproducible methodology for predicting task performance before training, with a demonstrated 0.05% deviation on HumanEval — a practical tool for compute planning. However, the accelerated emergence finding (concave scaling curves in 5/8 UICL tasks) rests on small test sets (20 questions) and a superficially validated 'multiple circuits' hypothesis, leaving the mechanistic explanation open. Key limitations include the 2.4B parameter ceiling, restriction to tasks with zero random baselines, and the acknowledged gap between fitting a scaling curve and understanding the underlying circuit dynamics that drive super-scaling behavior.
