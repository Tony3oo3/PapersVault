## Problem

Most bandit algorithms assume reward variances are known and homogeneous, leading to suboptimal regret from variance overestimation or linear regret from underestimation. Prior variance-adaptive algorithms (e.g., UCB-V, TS14, TS20) are frequentist and cannot exploit informative prior knowledge about reward variances. This paper establishes the first Bayesian framework for K-armed Gaussian bandits with unknown heterogeneous reward variances, yielding regret bounds that decrease with lower variances and more informative priors.

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes a Gaussian-Gamma prior specified by per-arm hyperparameters (μ₀, κ₀, α₀, β₀) and sequentially observes scalar Gaussian rewards from the pulled arm. When the true prior is unavailable, hyperparameters are estimated via method of moments from prior samples, making the method applicable to non-Gaussian reward distributions (Bernoulli, Beta). The output at each round is an arm selection, and the analysis produces a finite-time Bayes regret bound whose constant C depends explicitly on the prior parameters, vanishing as priors become more informative.

## Method

VarTS maintains a joint Gaussian-Gamma posterior over each arm's mean reward μᵢ and reward precision λᵢ = σᵢ⁻². In each round, it samples λₜ,ᵢ ~ Gam(αₜ,ᵢ, βₜ,ᵢ) and then μ̃ₜ,ᵢ ~ N(μ̂ₜ,ᵢ, 1/(κₜ,ᵢ λₜ,ᵢ)), pulling the arm with the highest sampled mean. The key analytical novelty is bounding confidence intervals whose widths are themselves random (due to unknown σᵢ²), requiring careful conditioning on posterior-sampled precisions combined with Jensen's and Cauchy-Schwarz inequalities. The resulting Bayes regret bound scales as √(n log n) · √(Σᵢ [β₀,ᵢ/(α₀,ᵢ−1)] · log(1 + n/κ₀,ᵢ)), where β₀,ᵢ/(α₀,ᵢ−1) is the mean of the Inverse-Gamma variance prior and κ₀,ᵢ⁻¹ plays the role of the prior-to-reward variance ratio, capturing the same trade-off structure as the known-variance case.

## Results

In the Bernoulli bandit (K=10, n=2000), VarTS matches the best baseline (Bernoulli ε-TS) in cumulative regret, while frequentist baselines UCB1 and UCB-V show the highest regret due to over-conservatism. In the Beta bandit (10× lower reward variances), VarTS outperforms Bernoulli ε-TS by exploiting the lower variance structure. In the scalability experiment at K=32, VarTS achieves at least 3× lower regret than any baseline on Bernoulli, 5× lower on Beta, and 2× lower on Gaussian bandits, with the gap widening as K increases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VarTS outperforms all six baselines in the Beta and Bernoulli bandits at K=32, with gaps of 3×–5× in regret. Against UCB1 and UCB-V (frequentist, non-adaptive), VarTS consistently wins across all three reward distributions because these algorithms are overly conservative with fixed [0,1] bounds. Against UCB1-Tuned (frequentist, heuristic), VarTS wins in Bernoulli and Beta bandits but is roughly tied in the Gaussian bandit, where UCB1-Tuned's heuristic happens to be well-calibrated. Against Bernoulli ε-TS (Bayesian, designed for Bernoulli rewards), VarTS matches performance in the Bernoulli setting but clearly wins in the Beta setting by learning the 10× lower variances. Against TS14 (Honda & Takemura, 2014) and TS20 (Zhu & Tan, 2020), both frequentist variance-adaptive TS algorithms, VarTS wins in all settings because it leverages informative priors while TS14/TS20 use fixed uninformative priors (e.g., TS20 uses α₀=0.5, β₀=0.5). The key differentiator is that VarTS's Bayesian design allows it to exploit prior knowledge, reducing regret as priors become more informative—a property no frequentist baseline can capture.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of variance-adaptive bandit algorithms (UCB-V, UCB1-Normal) into the Bayesian Thompson sampling framework, building on the Gaussian TS analysis of Kveton et al. (2021) and the frequentist variance-adaptive TS of Honda & Takemura (2014) and Zhu & Tan (2020). The key departure is the shift from frequentist per-instance guarantees to Bayes regret bounds that explicitly depend on prior parameters, which is only possible in the Bayesian setting. The algorithmic novelty lies in maintaining a joint Gaussian-Gamma posterior over both mean and precision, enabling closed-form posterior updates that the frequentist baselines lack.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first Bayes regret bound that simultaneously decreases with lower reward variances and more informative priors, establishing that Bayesian analysis is necessary to capture the full benefit of prior knowledge in variance-adaptive bandits. The practical robustness to model misspecification (Gaussian-designed algorithm performing well on Bernoulli and Beta rewards) and the growing performance gap over baselines with more arms suggest broad applicability. Open questions include extending to infinite arms, contextual bandits, and time-varying variances, as well as deriving tight finite-time lower bounds or logarithmic upper bounds for this setting.
