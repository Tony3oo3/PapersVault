## Problem

Despite numerous perturbation-based GNN explainers being proposed, their empirical evaluations lack diversity in interrogative aspects: comparative analysis of counterfactual reasoners, stability under variational factors (noise, stochasticity, architecture changes), and feasibility under domain-specific topological constraints have not been formally investigated. This is the first benchmarking study to systematically evaluate counterfactual explainers for GNNs alongside factual ones under a unified protocol.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20242072/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes a trained black-box GNN (GCN, GAT, GIN, or GraphSAGE with 3 layers, 20 hidden dims) and an input graph with node features, producing either a factual explanation subgraph (minimal edge set preserving the prediction) or a counterfactual perturbed graph (minimal edge edits flipping the prediction). Explanations are extracted via greedy top-k edge selection from continuous scores, and evaluated across 13 datasets spanning molecular, social, and text-derived graphs. Stability is measured as Jaccard similarity between explanations under controlled perturbations (topological noise, feature noise, adversarial edge flips, different seeds, different architectures), while feasibility is assessed via p-values on connectivity distributions in molecular counterfactuals.

## Method

The paper constructs a unified benchmarking framework that evaluates seven factual explainers (GNNExplainer, PGExplainer, TAGExplainer, GEM, SubgraphX, CF², RCExplainer) and four counterfactual explainers (RCExplainer, CF², CF-GNNExplainer, CLEAR) across five evaluation axes: comparative efficacy (sufficiency vs. size Pareto front), stability to topological noise, stability to explainer stochasticity (multiple seeds), stability to GNN architecture changes, and feasibility of counterfactual recourses. For factual explainers, inductive methods are trained on training data and evaluated on test data, while transductive methods run on the full dataset; counterfactual methods follow analogous protocols. The key finding is that RCExplainer emerges as the Pareto-optimal method across most datasets and stability axes, yet all methods suffer significant stability degradation under noise, and counterfactual explainers frequently produce infeasible recourses that violate domain topological constraints (e.g., disconnected molecules). The authors further show that reproducibility experiments reveal explanations capture only partial data signals, motivating ante-hoc joint training paradigms.

## Results

For counterfactual graph classification, RCExplainer achieves the lowest sufficiency (0.4±0.12) and smallest edit size (1.1±0.22) on Mutag, and consistently near-zero sufficiency with size ≈1.0 across Mutagenicity, AIDS, Proteins, IMDB-B, and OGBG-MolHIV. On node classification, CF-GNNExplainer reaches 100% ground-truth accuracy on Tree-Cycles and 92.7% on Tree-Grid, outperforming CF² (74.7% and 14.3% respectively). For factual explanations, no single method dominates: RCExplainer excels on Mutag while GNNExplainer leads on Reddit-B and NCI1. Stability analysis shows RCExplainer maintains Jaccard similarity of 0.95–0.97 across random seeds on AIDS, whereas GNNExplainer drops to 0.44–0.47, and feasibility tests reveal statistically significant (p < 0.00001) deviations in connected-component structure for counterfactuals on Mutagenicity and AIDS.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RCExplainer emerges as the Pareto-optimal counterfactual explainer, achieving the lowest sufficiency and smallest edit size on 5 of 6 graph-classification datasets (e.g., 0.40 sufficiency / 1.1 size on Mutag vs. CF²'s 0.90 / 1.0 and CLEAR's 0.55 / 17.15). On node classification, CF-GNNExplainer decisively outperforms CF², reaching 100% ground-truth accuracy on Tree-Cycles versus CF²'s 74.7%, and 92.7% vs. 14.3% on Tree-Grid, contradicting the original CF² paper's claim of superiority. Among factual explainers, GNNExplainer achieves the highest sufficiency on Reddit-B and NCI1 but exhibits the worst cross-seed stability (Jaccard ≈ 0.44–0.47 on NCI1) and the poorest stability against GNN architecture changes (Jaccard ≈ 0.17 on IMDB-B). PGExplainer and TAGExplainer show moderate stability but consistently lower sufficiency than RCExplainer and GNNExplainer. CLEAR achieves the best (lowest) counterfactual sufficiency on AIDS (0.84) but requires an impractical 164.9 edge perturbations and fails with OOM errors on Mutagenicity, Proteins, and OGBG-MolHIV. GEM and SubgraphX are limited to graph classification and suffer from scalability issues (SubgraphX requires ~26.5 hours for 435 graphs on Mutagenicity), restricting their practical utility.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the GNN explainability benchmarking line initiated by GraphFrameX (Amara et al., 2022) and Agarwal et al. (2023) by being the first to systematically evaluate counterfactual explainers (RCExplainer, CF², CF-GNNExplainer, CLEAR) alongside factual ones (GNNExplainer, PGExplainer, TAGExplainer, GEM, SubgraphX) under a unified protocol. It departs from prior benchmarks by introducing evaluation dimensions that had not been formally investigated: stability against topological noise, random seeds, and GNN architecture changes; feasibility of counterfactual recourses under domain constraints; and reproducibility of predictions from explanation-only or residual-only retraining. The individual explainer methods are all pre-existing; the contribution is the comparative framework and the new metrics (Jaccard-based stability, p-value-based feasibility, reproducibility ratios) applied uniformly across them.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals two critical, previously unmeasured deficiencies: (1) all perturbation-based explainers suffer significant stability degradation under even minor topological noise or seed changes, and (2) counterfactual explainers systematically produce infeasible recourses (e.g., disconnected molecules) that violate domain-specific topological constraints. The reproducibility finding—that retraining a GNN on residual graphs (with explanations removed) recovers similar accuracy—challenges the assumption that factual explanations capture the essential data signal. These findings open concrete research directions: integrating generative models into counterfactual optimization for feasibility, and shifting from post-hoc to ante-hoc (jointly trained) explanation paradigms for stability. For practitioners deploying GNNs in healthcare, finance, or regulatory settings, the paper provides the first evidence-based guidance on which explainer to trust under which conditions.
