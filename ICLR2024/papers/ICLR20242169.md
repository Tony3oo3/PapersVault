## Problem

Existing multimodal patient representation learning either assumes all modalities are present for every patient or handles only missing modalities, while in practice both modalities and labels are frequently absent due to clinical workflow variations, patient dropout, and data linkage issues. Additionally, multimodal models suffer from modality collapse, over-relying on a subset of modalities during training and degrading sharply when those key modalities are missing at inference.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20242169/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes heterogeneous multimodal patient data (demographics, ICD diagnosis/procedure/medication codes, lab time-series, clinical note text, vital signals, or MRI/PET/DTI biomarker features) with arbitrary per-patient modality absence patterns, along with binary modality-missingness and label-missingness matrices. It outputs a fixed-dimension patient representation embedding (128-d) and a downstream clinical prediction (binary mortality/readmission or 3-class Alzheimer's progression). A key design choice is that the unsupervised contrastive objective requires no label, so patients with missing labels can still contribute to training through the edge-dropout augmentation branch, effectively expanding the usable training pool.

## Method

MUSE represents the multimodal patient dataset as an undirected bipartite graph with patient nodes and modality nodes, where edges exist only for present modalities and carry modality-specific encoded features as edge attributes. A Siamese GraphSAGE encoder (2 layers, edge-attribute variant) processes both the original graph and an edge-dropout-augmented version (15% dropout rate) to produce two sets of patient embeddings. The mutual-consistent contrastive loss has two components: an unsupervised InfoNCE-style loss that aligns the same patient's representations across the two graphs (learning modality-agnostic features without requiring labels), and a supervised contrastive loss that pulls together patients sharing the same label while pushing apart different-label patients (learning label-decisive features). A standard cross-entropy classification loss on an MLP head is added for the downstream task. The critical architectural insight is that edge dropout on the bipartite graph naturally simulates arbitrary modality absence patterns without imputation, and the unsupervised branch's label-free supervision enables MUSE+ to incorporate unlabeled patients, yielding an additional ~4% absolute AUC-ROC gain.

## Results

On MIMIC-IV mortality prediction, MUSE achieves 0.9004 AUC-ROC, outperforming the best baseline M3Care (0.8896) by ~1.1% absolute. MUSE+ further elevates this to 0.9201 AUC-ROC (~3% absolute gain) by incorporating patients with missing labels into the unsupervised contrastive loss. On eICU mortality, MUSE reaches 0.9017 AUC-ROC vs. M3Care's 0.8964, and MUSE+ reaches 0.9332. On the ADNI Alzheimer's progression task, MUSE achieves 0.9158 AUC-ROC and 0.7973 balanced accuracy, while MUSE+ reaches 0.9309 AUC-ROC and 0.8291 accuracy, surpassing the best baseline M3Care (0.9101 / 0.7822).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MUSE outperforms all six baselines across every task and dataset. Among modality imputation methods, CM-AE and SMIL perform worst (e.g., 0.8530 and 0.8607 AUC-ROC on MIMIC-IV mortality), as they rely on strong distributional assumptions to reconstruct missing modalities. The direct prediction method MT (0.8739) improves slightly by using a Transformer for cross-modal fusion but ignores inter-patient relationships. Among graph-based methods, HGMF (0.8710) underperforms due to its complex hypergraph design being prone to overfitting, while GRAPE (0.8837) and M3Care (0.8896) achieve the strongest baseline results. MUSE (0.9004) surpasses M3Care by ~1.1% AUC-ROC on MIMIC-IV mortality and by ~1.5% on eICU mortality, with all improvements statistically significant (p < 0.05). MUSE+ extends the gap to ~3–4% absolute AUC-ROC by leveraging the unsupervised contrastive loss on patients with missing labels, a capability none of the baselines possess since they all require label supervision.

## Relation to Prior Work

MUSE directly builds on GRAPE (You et al., 2020), adopting its bipartite patient-modality graph structure and GraphSAGE-with-edge-attributes GNN variant, but departs by introducing a Siamese architecture with edge-dropout augmentation and a dual mutual-consistent contrastive loss (unsupervised for modality-agnostic features, supervised for label-decisive features). It positions itself against three prior families—modality imputation (CM-AE, SMIL), direct prediction (MT, MulT, ViLT), and graph-based fusion (GRAPE, HGMF, M3Care)—arguing that all either ignore cross-patient relationships, oversimplify the patient-modality structure, or require complete labels. The key conceptual departure is framing missing labels as a first-class problem alongside missing modalities, enabling the unsupervised contrastive branch to exploit previously discarded unlabeled patients.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically important gap in clinical ML: real EHR data routinely lacks both modalities and outcome labels, yet prior methods discard unlabeled patients entirely. By showing that the unsupervised contrastive component can leverage these patients (MUSE+ gaining ~4% absolute AUC-ROC), the work offers a concrete recipe for expanding training scope without additional annotation. Limitations include the assumption of missing-completely-at-random (acknowledged in Appx. F), the lack of guarantee that multimodal models outperform unimodal baselines for a given task, and the relatively narrow evaluation to three datasets. Open questions include whether the mutual-consistent contrastive framework generalizes to more than six modalities, to temporal/longitudinal settings, and to scenarios where modality and label missingness are strongly correlated.
