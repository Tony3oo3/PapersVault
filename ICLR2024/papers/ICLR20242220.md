## Problem

Pre-trained transformers achieve strong performance on clinical NLP tasks, yet little is understood about how their feature spaces evolve during fine-tuning, when they produce unreliable predictions, or how to build trust with medical personnel. No systematic framework existed to diagnose the interpretability of fine-tuned transformer features in high-stakes clinical settings, particularly regarding the impact of pre-training data choices on downstream robustness and failure modes.

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes five 110M-parameter encoder-based transformer models (BERT, TNLR, BioBERT, Clinical BioBERT, PubMedBERT) in both their pre-trained and fine-tuned states, along with clinical text (2,907 prostate pathology reports and MedNLI subsets). Activations are extracted at the classification token across all 12 encoder layers at 25 training checkpoints. Outputs include layer-wise RSA similarity curves, 2D PCA scatterplots tracking feature disambiguation across layers and epochs, probing F1 scores, and a set of outlier reports categorized into five clinically meaningful failure modes (wrongly labeled, inconsistent, multiple sources, truncated, boundary) validated by a urology domain expert.

## Method

SUFO is a four-component diagnostic pipeline: (1) Supervised probing freezes all pre-trained weights and trains only the final linear layer to quantify how much task-relevant information the pre-trained features already encode; (2) Unsupervised Representational Similarity Analysis (RSA) computes Pearson correlations between flattened pairwise Euclidean distance matrices of pre-trained vs. fine-tuned activations at each layer, revealing where in the network fine-tuning causes the most reconfiguration; (3) Feature Dynamics applies PCA to activations across both the layer axis (1–12) and the time axis (epochs 1–25) to visualize how class clusters disambiguate during training; (4) Outlier Analysis exploits the finding that fine-tuning sparsifies the last-layer feature space so that the first two principal components explain ~95% of variance, then constructs 2D rectangular clusters from independent 1D projections onto PC1 and PC2, flagging reports outside these clusters as outliers for expert review. The key empirical insight is that in-domain pre-training accelerates feature disambiguation but can cause overfitting to minority classes under imbalance, while mixed-domain pre-training provides greater robustness.

## Results

On the four pathology classification tasks, Clinical BioBERT achieves the best average macro F1 of 0.959, followed by BioBERT (0.950), BERT (0.924), and TNLR and PubMedBERT tied at 0.913. The critical finding emerges on the highly imbalanced Path-PG task (67%/30%/3% class distribution), where Clinical BioBERT (0.959) and BioBERT (0.933) substantially outperform BERT (0.858), TNLR (0.763), and PubMedBERT (0.770). Under supervised probing (frozen encoder, linear probe only), PubMedBERT achieves the highest average F1 (0.494), indicating its pre-trained features contain the most task-relevant information, yet this advantage reverses after fine-tuning on imbalanced data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Clinical BioBERT (mixed-domain) achieves the best overall fine-tuning performance (0.959 avg F1), outperforming BERT by 3.5 points and PubMedBERT by 4.6 points, with the largest gap on the imbalanced Path-PG task (0.959 vs. 0.858 and 0.770 respectively). BioBERT (mixed-domain) is a close second at 0.950 average, also robust to class imbalance on Path-PG (0.933). PubMedBERT (domain-specific) paradoxically achieves the best supervised probing score (0.494 avg F1, vs. 0.493 for BERT and 0.487 for Clinical BioBERT), confirming its pre-trained features encode the most task-relevant information, yet it degrades to 0.770 F1 on Path-PG after fine-tuning due to overfitting on the 3% minority class (per-class accuracy of only 0.67 for class 5). BERT (general-domain) provides a solid middle-ground baseline at 0.924 average, while TNLR (general-domain with different pre-training objective) performs worst on Path-PG (0.763) and has the lowest probing score (0.378), failing to even beat the random-initialized BERT baseline (0.421). On MedNLI, the same pattern holds: Clinical BioBERT outperforms PubMedBERT under high class imbalance (0.76 vs. 0.71 accuracy), while PubMedBERT leads under balanced conditions (0.83 vs. 0.73).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper integrates established feature analysis techniques—supervised probing (Tenney et al., 2019; Hewitt & Manning, 2019), representational similarity analysis (Kriegeskorte et al., 2008), and cluster-based feature dynamics (van Aken et al., 2019; Voita et al., 2019; Merchant et al., 2020)—into a unified interpretability pipeline (SUFO) tailored for clinical transformer models. Prior feature analysis work typically examined either cross-layer or cross-temporal dynamics in isolation, and focused on unsupervised LMs or general NLU tasks; SUFO is the first to conduct extensive joint layer-and-time cluster analysis on text features and to ground the analysis in domain-expert validation for clinical deployment. The paper positions itself against the growing body of domain-specific pre-training work (BioBERT, Clinical BioBERT, PubMedBERT) by asking not just whether in-domain pre-training helps, but what happens to the feature space during fine-tuning and why performance differences emerge.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SUFO provides clinicians and NLP practitioners with a concrete, multi-faceted diagnostic toolkit for evaluating fine-tuned transformers before deployment in high-stakes medical settings, addressing a critical gap between strong empirical performance and actionable interpretability. The finding that domain-specific pre-training can paradoxically increase overfitting risk under class imbalance—despite yielding the richest pre-trained features—has direct practical implications for model selection in clinical NLP. Limitations include the study's scope (110M-parameter models, four pathology classification tasks, one non-public dataset), and the authors note that generalizing to broader clinical tasks, larger models, and optimally combining general and domain-specific pre-training corpora remain open questions.
