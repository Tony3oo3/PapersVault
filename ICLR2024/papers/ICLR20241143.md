## Problem

Non-convex optimization problems often admit multiple global optima for the same input, creating a multi-valued input-solution mapping that standard supervised NN training (e.g., MSE loss) cannot learn, producing averaged, infeasible, or suboptimal solutions. Existing remedies—data generation, hindsight loss, clustering, or data augmentation—either require problem-specific prior knowledge, demand knowing the number of solution branches in advance, or lack formal optimality guarantees. The paper asks whether a generative paradigm that learns the full input-conditioned solution distribution can resolve this multi-valued mapping challenge with bounded optimality loss.

## Input / Output

The method takes a contextual parameter vector c ∈ R^d (e.g., a 2-D target position for inverse kinematics, a K×K channel matrix for wireless power control, or a graph adjacency matrix for combinatorial problems) and produces a decision variable x ∈ R^n satisfying an input-dependent compact constraint set K_c. At inference, the model does not output a single point: it samples m candidate solutions from the learned distribution via ODE integration and returns the one with the lowest objective value and constraint violation. For combinatorial problems, an additional greedy decoding step converts the relaxed continuous output into a discrete feasible solution, guaranteeing 100% feasibility.

## Method

The core idea is a paradigm shift from learning a deterministic input→solution mapping to learning an input→solution-distribution mapping, implemented with a RectFlow (ODE-based) generative model. The target distribution is assumed to follow a Boltzmann form p_d(x|c) ∝ exp(−βf(x,c)), and the RectFlow vector field u(x,t,c) = E[x₁−x₀ | x_t=x] is the explicit solution to the Fokker-Planck equation transporting a Gaussian prior to p_d(x|c). A neural network v_θ(x,t,c) approximates this vector field by minimizing a regression loss ‖v_θ(x_t,t,c)−(x₁−x₀)‖² over sampled (x₀, x₁, t) triples, where x₁ comes from the training dataset and x₀ from the Gaussian prior. At inference, m initial points are drawn from the Gaussian, each is propagated through k Euler steps of the learned ODE, and the best candidate is selected. The paper proves that the probability of the best-of-m solution exceeding an optimality gap δ decays exponentially in m, with the base governed by the data quality (Pr(S_c^δ|c)), the NN approximation error ε_θ^{1/4}, and the ODE discretization error k^{−1/2}.

## Results

On the wireless power control problem (d=40², n=40), RectFlow achieves a 3.96% optimality gap with 7× speedup, substantially outperforming the simple NN (30.64%, 49×), Hindsight (28.67%, 28×), Cluster (27.15%, 28×), GAN (36.39%, 16×), and Diffusion (13.77%, 6×) baselines. On the inverse kinematics problem (d=2, n=4), RectFlow achieves 0.04 constraint violation with 258× speedup, compared to 2.82 (NN), 1.06 (Hindsight), 0.23 (GAN), and 0.05 (Diffusion). For combinatorial problems on 100-node graphs, RectFlow achieves 1.35% optimality gap on Maximum Cut (vs. 8.21% for Diffusion and 12.06% for NN) and 4.87% on Maximum Clique (vs. 5.73% for Diffusion and 22.77% for NN).

## Baselines & Comparisons

RectFlow decisively outperforms all deterministic-mapping baselines (NN, Hindsight [Li et al., 2018], Cluster [Courts & Kvinge, 2021]) on optimality across every problem, with gaps of 15–27 percentage points on wireless power control and 5–100× lower constraint violation on inverse kinematics. Against the GAN baseline [Goodfellow et al., 2020], RectFlow achieves 4–10× smaller optimality gaps on wireless power control (2.94% vs. 19.31% at d=20²) and 6× lower constraint violation on inverse kinematics (0.04 vs. 0.23). The closest competitor is the Diffusion model (DDIM [Song et al., 2020a; Sun & Yang, 2024]): RectFlow wins clearly on wireless power control (2.94% vs. 12.08% opt. gap) and Maximum Cut (1.35% vs. 8.21%), but Diffusion edges out RectFlow slightly on inverse kinematics n=7 (0.35 vs. 0.37 error) and Maximum Independent Set (5.40% vs. 5.59% opt. gap). In terms of speedup, RectFlow is competitive with Diffusion (5–7× vs. 4–6× on wireless power control) while offering better optimality, and both are slower than the simple NN baseline but far more accurate.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in ML-based optimization of learning deterministic input-solution mappings (single-valued via data generation/selection, or multi-valued via hindsight loss, clustering, or initial-point augmentation) and instead learns a conditional solution distribution using Rectified Flow, an ODE-based generative model. It builds directly on the RectFlow formulation (Liu et al., 2022) and the Fokker-Planck equation framework, while positioning itself against GANs (which suffer mode collapse on multi-modal targets) and diffusion models (which are slower due to stochastic backward SDE integration). The Boltzmann-distribution assumption on the solution set connects to prior work on stochastic solvers (MCMC, simulated annealing) and the β→∞ limit recovers the multi-global-optimum setting studied by Nandwani et al. and Kotary et al.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to formalize and theoretically analyze the input-to-solution-distribution paradigm for continuous non-convex constrained optimization, providing an exponential optimality-gap bound that decays with the number of sampled candidates. The practical implication is a clean recipe: collect high-quality (large-β) training data, train a RectFlow vector field, then sample-and-select at inference for guaranteed feasibility and bounded suboptimality. Key limitations include the dependence on ground-truth training data (requiring expensive offline solver runs), the assumption of a compact constraint set, and the open question of extending to unsupervised or reinforcement-learning settings where no optimal solutions are available. The framework also raises the question of whether the distributional approach generalizes to problems with unbounded or non-compact feasible sets.
