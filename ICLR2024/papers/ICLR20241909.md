## Problem

The Gaussian Process (GP) limit for infinitely wide neural networks was previously proven only under IID weight assumptions, leaving important practical initialization schemes—orthogonal, low-rank, and structured sparse weights—without a rigorous GP convergence guarantee despite their computational advantages. This paper asks whether the GP limit extends to these non-IID but structured weight distributions that violate the independence assumption.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241909/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes weight matrices or convolutional tensors whose entries are row- and column-exchangeable (not necessarily independent), centered, uncorrelated with variance σ²/n, and satisfying specific 8th-moment and 4th-cross-moment bounds; input data are vectors or 2D multi-channel images propagated through the network. The output is a closed-form GP covariance kernel depending only on variance parameters σ_W², σ_b², and the activation function—identical in recursive form to the IID case but now valid for orthogonal, low-rank, and structured sparse initializations, enabling direct computation of Edge-of-Chaos parameters and exact Bayesian posterior distributions.

## Method

The paper introduces PSEUDO-IID distributions, a class of weight matrices that are row- and column-exchangeable with uncorrelated entries and controlled higher-order moments, replacing the full independence requirement of standard IID analysis. The proof follows the simultaneous width scaling framework of Matthews et al. (2018) but substitutes the standard CLT with an exchangeable CLT (Blum–Rosenblatt type) that requires only exchangeability and pairwise uncorrelatedness of summands. The critical technical step is showing that the 8th-moment condition and the vanishing 4th-cross-moment condition ensure the CLT summands interact asymptotically as if independent, even when entries are dependent. The authors verify that orthogonal matrices, low-rank factorizations A = CP with orthonormal C, and randomly permuted block-sparse masks all satisfy PSEUDO-IID, yielding GP limits for both fully connected networks (Theorem 1) and CNNs (Theorem 2). The resulting covariance kernel has the same recursive form as the IID case, establishing that all PSEUDO-IID networks are equivalent up to variance rescaling and enabling tractable Edge-of-Chaos analysis and exact Bayesian posterior computation across these initialization schemes.

## Results

The paper proves two main theorems: Theorem 1 establishes that fully connected networks with PSEUDO-IID weights converge to a centered Gaussian Process in the simultaneous infinite-width limit, with covariance kernel K^(ℓ)(x,x') = σ_b² + σ_W² E[φ(u)φ(v)] (for ℓ≥2), and Theorem 2 extends this to CNNs with an additional spatial overlap factor. Numerical simulations on 7-layer networks with widths n=3, 30, and 300 confirm convergence, with excellent histogram agreement at n=30 and near-perfect agreement at n=300. A key practical consequence is that structured sparse or low-rank initializations are signal-propagation-equivalent to their dense/full-rank counterparts up to a fractional variance rescaling by the sparsity or rank ratio, enabling tractable Edge-of-Chaos computation for these architectures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper that does not benchmark against competing methods in the standard sense; instead, it unifies and generalizes prior GP-limit results. The PSEUDO-IID framework strictly encompasses the IID Gaussian case (Matthews et al. 2018; Neal 1996), the orthogonal initialization case (Huang et al. 2021, which the authors note left the first-layer treatment incomplete), and the CNN GP limit (Novak et al. 2020; Garriga-Alonso et al. 2019), recovering their exact covariance kernels as special cases. The novel contribution is extending the GP limit to low-rank and structured sparse weight matrices, for which no rigorous proof existed previously. In the numerical simulations (Section 3.2), orthogonal initialization converges to the limiting Gaussian Process fastest among the tested PSEUDO-IID distributions, while IID uniform, low-rank, and structured sparse all show good agreement at n=30 and near-exact agreement at n=300. The paper also validates that the NNGP posterior and Edge-of-Chaos calculations from prior work remain valid under the broader PSEUDO-IID regime, since the resulting covariance kernel is identical.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the simultaneous-width-scaling Gaussian Process proof of Matthews et al. (2018) by replacing the IID independence assumption with a weaker PSEUDO-IID condition (row/column exchangeability plus moment bounds), thereby unifying and strictly generalizing prior results for IID weights (Matthews et al., Hanin), orthogonal weights (Huang et al., who left the first-layer treatment incomplete), and CNNs (Novak et al., Garriga-Alonso et al.). It also provides the missing GP-limit proof that Nait Saada & Tanner (2023) assumed for low-rank networks, and extends the framework to structured sparse initializations for which no prior GP result existed. The proof machinery relies on an exchangeable CLT (Blum & Rosenblatt) rather than the classical IID CLT, which is the key technical departure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that Edge-of-Chaos initialization and Bayesian NNGP inference become tractable for sparse, low-rank, and orthogonal architectures — settings that are increasingly important for efficient deployment on hardware accelerators. The paper shows that, in terms of signal propagation, structured sparse or low-rank initializations are equivalent to dense/full-rank counterparts up to a variance rescaling by the sparsity or rank fraction, which simplifies the design of such networks. Open questions include whether the PSEUDO-IID conditions are minimal (some may be redundant), whether the first layer can also be PSEUDO-IID rather than IID, and whether the results extend to architectures with residual connections or attention mechanisms. The numerical validation is limited to modest widths (n ≤ 300) and small networks, so the practical convergence rates at production scale remain untested.
