## Problem

In online recommendation, vendors strategically choose item descriptions (and thus click-through rates) to maximize their exposure, creating a fundamental tension with the platform's goal of consistent, high-quality recommendations. Standard multi-armed bandit algorithms treat CTRs as fixed parameters and fail to account for this strategic behavior, suffering linear regret even with oracle knowledge of all parameters. The paper introduces the strategic click-bandit problem, where arms are self-interested agents who freely choose their click-rates, and shows that incentive-aware mechanism design is necessary to achieve sublinear regret.

## Input / Output

The learner interacts with K arms over T rounds, observing only Bernoulli click indicators and (conditionally on a click) a post-click reward in [0,1]. Crucially, the click-rate s_i is not a fixed parameter but a strategic action chosen by each arm before the interaction begins, so the learner must simultaneously estimate both s_i and μ_i from partial feedback. The output each round is an arm selection from a dynamically shrinking active set A_t; arms that are detected as deviating from the desired strategy s*(μ_i) are permanently eliminated, and once all arms are eliminated the algorithm falls back to uniform random selection.

## Method

UCB-S (UCB with Screening) combines optimistic UCB-style arm selection with a screening rule that permanently eliminates any arm whose confidence interval on its click-rate is incompatible with the desired strategy s*(μ_i) given the current confidence interval on μ_i. The key insight is that the threat of elimination is credible because it is triggered only when confidence bounds (of width √(2 log T / n)) rule out the desired strategy, so a truthful arm playing s_i = s*(μ_i) is eliminated with probability at most 1/T². This creates a mechanism-design effect: arms that are selected more frequently (e.g., optimal arms expected to be pulled ~T/K times) are forced to keep their click-rates within Õ(√(K/T)) of s*(μ_i), while suboptimal arms can deviate by Õ(Δ_i). The paper proves Õ(√(KT)) strong strategic regret under every Nash equilibrium and an almost-matching Ω(√(KT)) lower bound, and validates the design via simulations where arms adapt via gradient ascent.

## Results

The main theoretical result is that UCB-S achieves Õ(√(KT)) strong strategic regret (Theorem 5.3), matching an almost-tight Ω(√(KT)) lower bound for weak strategic regret (Theorem 5.5). In simulations with K=4 arms (μ₁=0.75, μ₂=0.725, μ₃=0.7, μ₄=0.675, λ=5) over 20 epochs of T=50k rounds, the optimal arm under UCB-S converges to a strategy close to the desired s*(μ₁)=0.825, while suboptimal arms deviate proportionally to their optimality gap Δᵢ. In contrast, under incentive-unaware standard UCB, all arm strategies remain near s=1 throughout all epochs, and UCB suffers substantially larger regret than UCB-S after the initial transient phase.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares UCB-S against two categories of baselines. First, incentive-unaware oracle algorithms (μ-Oracle and (s,μ)-Oracle) are shown to suffer Ω(T) linear regret in every Nash equilibrium (Proposition 4.1), even with full knowledge of all parameters, because arms' dominant strategy is to set s=1 regardless of the learner's objective. Second, in simulations, standard UCB (the natural incentive-unaware bandit baseline) fails to incentivize desirable arm strategies: all arms remain near s=1 across all 20 epochs (Figure 3), and UCB's strategic regret stays high throughout (Figure 4). UCB-S initially suffers larger regret than UCB due to its elimination rule triggering uniform random selection when arms are notably untruthful, but this threat incentivizes arms to adapt their strategies, and UCB-S's regret improves substantially over epochs to fall below UCB's. The key empirical finding is that UCB-S's screening mechanism is robust even under gradient-ascent arm behavior (a realistic non-equilibrium model), confirming that the incentive design works beyond the Nash equilibrium assumption.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the classical UCB framework (Auer, 2002) and the algorithmic mechanism design tradition (Nisan and Ronen, 1999), but departs from prior strategic MAB work in a key way. Braverman et al. (2019) first studied strategic arms in MABs but assumed the principal did not need to learn reward distributions; Feng et al. (2020) and Dong et al. (2022) assumed bounded manipulation budgets rather than designing incentive mechanisms; and auction-based MAB work (Babaioff et al., 2009; Devanur and Kakade, 2009) relied on payments. The strategic click-bandit is, to the authors' knowledge, the first formulation where arms' strategies and other parameters are simultaneously unknown and must be learned from interaction while incentivizing arms under uncertainty without payments, making the mechanism design necessarily approximate.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean theoretical resolution to a practically important problem (clickbait in recommendation platforms) by showing that a simple screening mechanism achieves minimax-optimal √(KT) regret while incentivizing near-truthful arm behavior, and that incentive-unaware algorithms fundamentally fail with linear regret even under oracle knowledge. The conceptual insight that more frequently selected arms are forced to be more truthful (a trade-off between exploration and incentive alignment) is a useful design principle. Open questions include whether the mechanism remains effective under adaptive (non-equilibrium) arm strategies and whether a dominant-strategy equilibrium can be constructed, both of which would strengthen practical applicability.
