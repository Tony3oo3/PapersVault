## Problem

Bootstrapping-based offline RL algorithms (CQL, TD3+BC, IQL, ATAC) suffer from inconsistent performance across datasets, sometimes underperforming even behavior cloning. While blending Monte-Carlo heuristics into bootstrapping is a known technique in online RL, it has not been rigorously extended to the offline setting, where the theoretical analyses from online work break down due to the absence of new data collection.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241618/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

HUBL takes a standard offline trajectory dataset D = {(s, a, s', r, γ)} and a base bootstrapping-based offline RL algorithm as input. It first converts tuples back into trajectories to compute per-step Monte-Carlo returns h_t and a trajectory-level blending factor λ_t, then relabels each transition with modified reward r̃ = r + γλ'h' and reduced discount γ̃ = γ(1−λ'), producing a new dataset D̃. The base algorithm is then run unmodified on D̃ to produce the final policy, so the only change to the pipeline is the data relabeling step.

## Method

HUBL partially replaces bootstrapped values in the Bellman equation with Monte-Carlo return heuristics by reshaping the MDP: the reward is augmented with γλh' and the discount is shrunk to γ(1−λ), where λ ∈ [0,1] is a trajectory-dependent blending factor. Three λ designs are provided (constant, sigmoid of mean return, and rank-based), all controlled by a single hyperparameter α ≈ 0.1 that requires minimal tuning. The key theoretical contribution is a bias-regret decomposition (Theorem 1) showing that HUBL trades a controllable bias (proportional to the gap V* − V^μ, i.e., behavior policy suboptimality) for reduced regret (smaller effective discount makes the reshaped MDP easier to solve with finite data). The trajectory-dependent λ is critical: it is large on high-return trajectories (trusting the heuristic) and small on low-return ones (relying on bootstrapping), which keeps bias small while still reducing learning complexity. This is formalized via a transition-dependent discount factor, a novel analysis technique that makes the offline setting tractable where prior online analyses fail.

## Results

HUBL achieves an average relative normalized score improvement of 9% across 27 datasets (9 D4RL + 18 MetaWorld) when combined with four state-of-the-art offline RL algorithms (ATAC, CQL, TD3+BC, IQL). The largest per-method gains are seen with CQL (16% average on D4RL) and TD3+BC (9% on D4RL), while ATAC shows more modest improvements (2% on D4RL). In the most challenging datasets where base methods underperform, HUBL delivers over 50% relative improvement (e.g., 70% for TD3+BC on hopper-medium-v2, 58% for IQL on hopper-medium-replay-v2, 39% for CQL on hopper-medium-expert-v2).

## Baselines & Comparisons

HUBL is evaluated as a plug-in enhancement to four base offline RL methods (ATAC, CQL, IQL, TD3+BC), so the primary comparison is each base method against itself augmented with HUBL. CQL benefits the most from HUBL, with a 16% average relative improvement on D4RL and up to 39% on hopper-medium-expert-v2, where the base CQL score (78.4) is far below the expert-level scores of other methods. TD3+BC shows the single largest per-dataset gain (70% on hopper-medium-v2, base score 59.1), and IQL achieves 58% improvement on hopper-medium-replay-v2 (base 54.0). ATAC, which is already the strongest base method on most D4RL datasets (scores 85–112), sees only modest 1–2% average gains, consistent with less room for improvement. HUBL slightly hurts performance in a few expert-data settings (e.g., −2% for CQL on halfcheetah-medium-v2, −16% for TD3+BC on hopper-medium-expert-v2), but these are cases where the base method is already near-optimal. Across all 108 dataset-method combinations, the Rank blending strategy wins in 46 cases, outperforming Sigmoid (30) and Constant (32) blending.

## Relation to Prior Work

HUBL extends the heuristic-guided RL framework of Cheng et al. (2021) from the online to the offline setting, where the prior theoretical analysis breaks down due to the absence of new data collection. The trajectory-dependent blending factor λ is inspired by White's (2017) transition-dependent discount factor, repurposed here as both a performance mechanism and an analytical tool. The paper also positions itself against discount regularization (Hu et al., 2022), showing that reducing the discount without heuristic compensation introduces an unavoidable pessimistic bias, and against multi-step return blending in TD methods, which operates online rather than as a one-time data relabeling step.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

HUBL addresses a practical pain point in offline RL—performance inconsistency across datasets—with a technique that requires only a data relabeling step and one hyperparameter, making it broadly applicable to any bootstrapping-based offline RL algorithm. The theoretical contribution (first finite-sample bound for RL with heuristics in the offline setting) clarifies the bias-regret trade-off and provides principled guidance for designing the blending factor. Limitations include the theory being restricted to tabular MDPs with constant λ, and the empirical gains, while consistent, are modest on average (9%) with some negative results on expert datasets. Open questions include extending the analysis to model-based offline RL and to function-approximation settings.
