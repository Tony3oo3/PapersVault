## Problem

CLIP-based OOD detection methods rely exclusively on positive prompts (similarity matching), which fail when OOD samples share surface features with ID classes (e.g., tigers resemble cats in ear shape). A natural fix—using hand-crafted negative prompts like 'not a photo of a [class]'—does not work because CLIP cannot semantically comprehend the word 'not,' as the authors demonstrate empirically. The paper asks how to learn effective negative prompts that capture the diverse set of features absent from a given class.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241812/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes 224×224 RGB images, encoded by a frozen CLIP image encoder (B/16, B/32, or RN50x4). At test time it produces a scalar OOD score computed as the difference between a Maximum Concept Matching (MCM) score from positive prompts and a Minimum Concept Matching (MinCM) score from negative prompts; a threshold on this score yields the ID/OOD decision. ID class predictions are made separately using only the positive prompts. Training requires only a small subset of ID samples (128 per class for most benchmarks, 64 for ImageNet-1k).

## Method

LSN (Learn to Say No) learns two sets of prompt vectors on top of frozen CLIP encoders: a shared positive prompt (CoOp/CoCoOp style) and K=3 class-specific negative prompts per ID class. The key insight is that negative features are diverse and cannot be carried by the class name alone, necessitating multiple class-specific negative prompts rather than a single shared one. Negative prompts are trained with a novel loss that inverts the cosine similarity (maximizing dissimilarity to the correct class while maximizing similarity to all other classes) plus a semantic orthogonality regularization that penalizes cosine similarity between the K negative prompt embeddings of the same class, encouraging them to attend to different negative features. At inference, the OOD score is S(x) = MCM_positive(x) − MinCM_negative(x), where MCM takes the max softmax probability over classes and MinCM takes the min softmax probability over classes, so a sample is flagged OOD when it matches no positive prompt well or matches at least one negative prompt strongly.

## Results

On ImageNet-100 (the primary benchmark), CoCoOp+LSN achieves an average FPR95 of 8.56% and AUROC of 98.05%, reducing FPR95 by 24.02 percentage points compared to the MCM baseline (32.58%). On the larger ImageNet-1k benchmark, CoCoOp+LSN reduces average FPR95 from 43.55% (MCM) to 30.22%, a 13.33-point improvement. On small-scale CIFAR benchmarks, CoCoOp+LSN achieves the best FPR95 across all five datasets (e.g., 5.92% on CIFAR+10, 7.65% on CIFAR+50), consistently outperforming both prompt-learning baselines (CoOp, CoCoOp) and the zero-shot OOD method ZOC.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LSN outperforms all 17 compared baselines across every benchmark. Against the closest CLIP-based OOD baselines, CoCoOp+LSN reduces average FPR95 from 14.86% (CoCoOp) and 12.40% (NPOS) to 8.56% on ImageNet-100, and from 31.10% (CLIPN) to 30.22% on ImageNet-1k. Compared to traditional post-hoc methods (MSP, ODIN, Energy, GradNorm, Vim, KNN), LSN achieves substantially lower FPR95 (e.g., 30.22% vs. 47.75% for ODIN and 80.82% for GradNorm on ImageNet-1k). On small-scale CIFAR benchmarks, LSN beats ZOC by 2–3 AUROC points (e.g., 95.17 vs. 93.00 on CIFAR-10) and improves FPR95 by 8–10 points over CoOp/CoCoOp. In the appendix comparison with DualCoOp (Table 7), LSN achieves 19.70% average FPR95 versus 23.47% for DualCoOp on ImageNet-100, and post-hoc methods (NNGuide, ASH, KNN, LINE) applied to raw CLIP perform poorly (FPR95 62–97%), confirming that LSN's prompt-based approach is better suited to CLIP's feature space.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the CLIP-based OOD detection line initiated by MCM (Ming et al., 2022), which matches image features against positive text prompts, by introducing learned negative prompts that capture dissimilarity. It builds on the prompt-learning framework of CoOp/CoCoOp (Zhou et al., 2022) but departs fundamentally in that negative prompts must be class-specific and multi-set per class, unlike positive prompts which can be shared. The work contrasts with CLIPN (Wang et al., 2023a), which fine-tunes the text encoder to understand negation, and with RPL (Chen et al., 2020), which constructs negative representations from classification features rather than leveraging CLIP's visual representations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method offers a lightweight, training-efficient path to substantially improve CLIP-based OOD detection (reducing FPR95 by ~24 points on ImageNet-100) without fine-tuning the backbone, making it practically attractive for open-world deployment. The empirical demonstration that CLIP fundamentally misinterprets the word 'not' and that shared negative prompts catastrophically fail (FPR95 > 80%) while class-specific multi-set prompts succeed provides useful guidance for anyone working with VLMs and negation. Limitations include dependence on CLIP's feature quality, doubled inference cost, and diminishing returns with more training data, leaving open whether the approach transfers cleanly to newer or larger VLMs.
