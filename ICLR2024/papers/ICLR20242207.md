## Problem

Standard strategic classification and causal strategic learning assume agents manipulate features x that causally affect outcomes y, but many real-world settings (e.g., labor markets) involve the reverse: agents change their outcomes (e.g., skill level) which then causally shifts their observable features (e.g., skill assessments). This paper formalizes this reverse causal strategic learning problem and studies the welfare and equity consequences of performative versus reactive hiring policies in labor market models.

## Input / Output

The learner receives the reverse causal response map Y_+(f,Y) and the feature generating mechanism Φ(x|y) a priori (justified by the learner designing the assessment process), while the base distribution p(y) of agent outcomes is unknown and must be learned from data. The output is a threshold hiring policy θ that anticipates the aggregate strategic response of the labor force, along with a REINFORCE-based gradient estimator for the performative loss that accounts for both the direct effect of the policy on classification and the indirect effect through the shifted feature distribution.

## Method

The paper introduces a reverse causal strategic learning framework where agents update outcomes via Y → Y_+(f,Y) = argmax W(f,y') - c(y',Y), and features shift accordingly via X ~ Φ(·|Y_+(f,Y)), distinguishing it from prior causal strategic classification where x → y. In the Coate-Loury labor market model, the performative employer solves a Stackelberg game (firm as leader, labor force as follower) to find the optimal threshold θ_opt, while a reactive employer settles at a Nash-equilibrium stable threshold θ_stab. The paper proves that under regularity conditions, optimal policies yield higher employer utility, greater labor force skill, and smaller inter-group discrimination gaps than stable policies. For stochastic optimization, Algorithm 1 derives the gradient of the performative loss via the implicit function theorem (to handle ∂_θ G_θ(y) from the argmax response) and approximates the resulting integrals with REINFORCE sampling from the known Φ(x|G_θ(y)). Empirical experiments in a general equilibrium model with continuous skills, non-linear utility, and Nash-determined wages show that while performative employers always benefit themselves and raise skill levels, they can reduce aggregate worker welfare and the fairness guarantees are brittle to market assumptions.

## Results

In the Coate-Loury model, Theorems 3.1–3.2 establish that performatively optimal hiring policies always yield a higher proportion of skilled workers (π(θ_opt) > π(θ_stab)) and, in low-wage markets, strictly higher employer utility (U_perf(θ_stab) ≤ U_perf(θ_opt)) compared to stable (reactive) policies. Theorems 3.3–3.4 further show that in markets with good skill separation, stable policies can produce discriminatory equilibria with qualification gaps exceeding 1−c, while optimal policies enforce near-perfect equity (gap < ε, or exactly 0 in low-wage markets). In the more complex general equilibrium model (Section 4), performative employers still increase labor force skill and employer reward, but they reduce aggregate worker welfare and the fairness benefits become brittle under non-linear utility or group-dependent costs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between two employer strategies: stable (reactive/Nash) policies, which maximize utility on their own induced distribution, and optimal (performative/Stackelberg) policies, which anticipate the labor force's strategic response. On employer welfare, the optimal policy always wins: Theorems 3.1–3.2 guarantee U_perf(θ_opt) ≥ U_perf(θ_stab), and the empirical experiments in Section 4 confirm this across all market configurations tested. On labor force skill, the optimal policy also wins in both the simple Coate-Loury model and the general equilibrium model, producing a higher fraction of qualified workers. On equity, the optimal policy wins decisively in the Coate-Loury model (Theorems 3.3–3.4 show discriminatory stable equilibria coexist with fair optimal ones), but in the general equilibrium model with non-linear utility (u = y·H^α) or asymmetric group costs, the fairness advantage of optimal policies disappears. On worker aggregate welfare (cost-adjusted), the stable policy wins in the general equilibrium experiments, as performative employers extract more surplus from workers. Thus the performative employer is a Pareto improvement for the employer and for skill, but a welfare loss for workers in aggregate, with mixed effects on equity depending on market structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the strategic classification and performative prediction literature by introducing a reverse causal mechanism: agents manipulate their outcomes Y, which then causally shift their features X, as opposed to the standard direction where agents game features X to affect outcomes Y. It directly complements Horowitz and Rosenfeld (2023) on causal strategic classification, which permits agents to modify labels through a forward causal model (X→Y), by studying the opposite propagation (Y→X). The application to the Coate-Loury labor market model connects to a rich economics literature on statistical discrimination, while the stochastic optimization algorithm (Algorithm 1) builds on the REINFORCE-based performative gradient descent of Izzo et al. (2021) but in a setting where the performative map is known a priori.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean game-theoretic interpretation (Stackelberg vs. Nash equilibrium) for performative vs. stable policies in labor markets, and shows that anticipatory hiring can simultaneously benefit employers and improve labor force skill and equity in the simple Coate-Loury model — an alignment that contrasts with the typical trade-off in strategic classification. However, the authors demonstrate that these desirable properties are brittle: in a more realistic general equilibrium model with non-linear utility and non-flat wages, performative employers harm aggregate worker welfare and fail to prevent discrimination. Open questions include whether the alignment results can be recovered under weaker assumptions, how the framework extends to multi-employer settings with strategic interaction, and whether the stochastic optimization algorithm scales to realistic labor market data.
