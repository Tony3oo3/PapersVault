## Problem

While transformers are known to struggle with arithmetic, it remains unclear what they actually learn when they do succeed at exact integer computations, and whether their predictions can be fully characterized. This paper asks whether small transformers trained to compute GCD can be made to perform exact calculations, and whether their predictions obey a small set of interpretable rules rather than behaving as inscrutable black boxes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241977/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives two positive integers (up to 10^6) encoded as digit sequences in a chosen base B, separated by a '+' sign token (e.g., '+160+120' in base 10), and produces the GCD as a digit sequence (e.g., '+40'). The base B is a key design choice: composite bases (30, 210, 420) enable simple divisibility tests via trailing digits, while large bases (1000–10000) allow grokking of small primes not dividing B. All data is generated on the fly from a 10^12-pair problem space, ensuring no train/test overlap.

## Method

The paper trains 4-layer sequence-to-sequence transformers (512 dimensions, 8 heads, Adam at 1e-5) to minimize cross-entropy on GCD prediction, then fully characterizes model predictions by probing inputs with known GCD values. The key finding is that predictions obey three rules: (R1) deterministic per-GCD prediction, (R2) correct predictions are exactly products of prime divisors of B (and small primes after grokking), and (R3) the model outputs the largest correct prediction dividing the true GCD — effectively a sieve algorithm. Training distribution is critical: log-uniform operands (balancing small and large examples) and log-uniform outcomes (balancing small and large GCD) boost correct GCD count from 13 to 91 for base 10, while uniform outcomes break explainability by causing the model to predict arbitrary class members rather than the smallest (correct) one.

## Results

With log-uniform operands and outcomes, 4-layer transformers (512 dim, 8 heads) trained for 1000 epochs correctly predict 87–91 of the first 100 GCD values for bases ≥ 1000 (e.g., 91 for bases 2025, 2187, 2744, 3125, 3375, 1000), with accuracy of 97–98% on the natural test set. With log-uniform operands only, the best result is 73 correct GCD ≤ 100 (base 2401, 99.1% accuracy). With uniform operands (the baseline setting), performance drops to 38 correct GCD ≤ 100 (base 420, 96.8% accuracy). Training from a uniform distribution of GCD outcomes pushes correct GCD to 95–99 but breaks the deterministic prediction rules that make the model fully explainable.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares 4-layer transformers against LSTM and GRU recurrent architectures (1024 and 2048 dimensions, 4 layers) on the same GCD task. With uniform operands (500 epochs), all three architectures achieve similar results: transformer and LSTM both reach 38 correct GCD at base 420, while GRU reaches 34. With log-uniform operands (500 epochs), the transformer reaches 59 GCD at base 420, outperforming LSTM (50) and GRU (44). The gap widens substantially with log-uniform operands and outcomes (450 epochs): the transformer achieves 88 GCD at base 420, versus 64 for LSTM (1024 dim) and 53 for GRU (1024 dim). The paper notes that LSTM and GRU results are lower than transformers after comparable training time, and that there is no clear advantage of LSTM over GRU or of 2048 over 1024 dimensions within the recurrent family.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on neural networks for arithmetic (Siu & Roychowdhury 1992; Kalchbrenner et al. 2015; Zaremba et al. 2015) and the author's own prior work on transformers for linear algebra and polynomial roots (Charton 2021, 2022a), but departs sharply from prior explainability efforts (Nanda et al. 2023; Zhong et al. 2023) that inspect model weights. Instead, it characterizes predictions purely through input-output behavior, revealing a deterministic 'sieve algorithm' the model implements. The grokking of non-divisor primes connects to Power et al. (2022) and Gromov (2023), but here grokking is shown to be a natural consequence of the sieve mechanism rather than a mysterious generalization phase.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that transformer predictions for integer divisibility can be fully characterized by a handful of simple rules, offering a new template for explainability that avoids weight inspection. The counterintuitive finding that balanced (uniform) outcome distributions break explainability while log-uniform distributions improve both performance and interpretability has potential implications for fine-tuning LLMs on arithmetic. Open questions include whether the sieve algorithm generalizes to other number-theoretic tasks (factorization, primality testing), whether the model-size independence observed here holds for more complex computations, and whether the explainability-by-input-output approach scales to tasks where the underlying algorithm is less transparent.
