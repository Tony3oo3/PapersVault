## Problem

While dense scaling laws for Transformers are well established, the impact of weight sparsity on the scaling behavior of foundation models trained on massive datasets remains largely unexplored. In particular, it is unclear whether sparse models can be competitive with dense ones under fair compute-matched comparisons in the regime where dense models continue to improve with prolonged training, as opposed to saturating benchmarks like ImageNet.

## Input / Output

The scaling law takes three continuous inputs — sparsity level S ∈ [0,1), number of non-zero parameters N, and training data amount D (images for ViT, tokens for T5) — and outputs a predicted pretraining validation loss L. From this law, the paper analytically derives the optimal sparsity S_opt(N,C) for a given inference size and training compute budget C, as well as a limit-performance gain factor expressing the equivalent dense model size needed to match a sparse model's loss. The law contains 7 free parameters (a_S, b_S, c_S, b_N, a_D, b_D, c) fitted via Huber-loss minimization on log L using BFGS.

## Method

The paper derives a joint scaling law L(S,N,D) = (a_S(1-S)^{b_S} + c_S)·(1/N)^{b_N} + (a_D/D)^{b_D} + c, where the sparsity term is a saturating power law in remaining density (1-S) that multiplicatively modulates the size-scaling term while leaving the data-scaling term unchanged. This functional form is motivated by three empirical observations from T5/C4 sweeps: parallel loss-vs-size curves across sparsity levels, quickly diminishing sparsity gains, and independence between sparsity and data scaling. The law is validated on ViT/JFT-4B (112 runs, 7 sizes × 4 data amounts × 4 sparsities) and T5/C4 (48 runs), with successful extrapolation to models ~6.75× larger than the fitting data. Sparsification is performed via gradual magnitude pruning with a cubic schedule (25%–75% of training), and the framework is extended to hardware-friendly n:m patterns, decoder-only architectures, and pruning from pretrained checkpoints.

## Results

The paper derives and validates a joint scaling law L(S,N,D) that predicts validation loss as a function of sparsity S, non-zero parameter count N, and training data D, achieving fitting errors of 4.93e-4 (ViT/JFT-4B) and 7.60e-6 (T5/C4). The headline finding is that 75% sparsity yields a ~2.15–2.17× equivalent dense capacity gain, consistent across both vision and language domains (Table 1). Optimal sparsity increases with training: 50% sparsity becomes optimal at <2× Chinchilla compute for ViT and <3× for T5 (in sparse FLOPs). Pruning from a pretrained checkpoint is >4× more data-efficient than sparsifying from scratch at 50–75% sparsity (Table 2).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is against dense models of equivalent non-zero parameter count: a 75%-sparse ViT with N non-zeros matches a dense model with ~2.17N parameters, and the same holds for T5 (2.16×), confirming domain-invariant sparsity gains. Against the AC/DC sparse training method (Peste et al., 2021), the paper's GMP-based approach performs comparably or slightly better: at 42M non-zeros and 75% sparsity, GMP achieves 5.87 perplexity vs. AC/DC's 6.05, and at 87.5% sparsity GMP reaches 5.81 vs. 6.11 for AC/DC. At 1.3M parameters the two methods are essentially tied at 50% (17.11 vs. 17.11) but GMP pulls ahead at 75% (15.64 vs. 15.96). The paper notes that AC/DC requires hyperparameter reconfiguration at larger scales, making GMP more practical for systematic scaling sweeps. Overall, the paper's contribution is not a new pruning algorithm but a scaling law framework; the pruning method (GMP) is chosen for robustness across scales rather than peak accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the dense scaling law framework of Kaplan et al. (2020) and Hoffmann et al. (2022, Chinchilla) by introducing weight sparsity as a third scaling dimension, yielding the first joint L(S,N,D) law for foundation models. It departs from the bulk of prior sparsity/pruning literature (Gale et al., 2019; Singh & Alistarh, 2020; Sanh et al., 2020), which evaluated on small benchmarks like ImageNet or GLUE where dense models saturate, by moving to the massive-dataset regime (JFT-4B, C4) where dense models continue improving and fair resource-equivalent comparison becomes essential. The n:m structured sparsity extension builds on hardware-friendly patterns (Mishra et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2021), while the pruning methodology relies on well-established gradual magnitude pruning (Zhu & Gupta, 2017) chosen for robustness across scales rather than peak performance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This provides the first principled tool for predicting when and how much sparsity helps in the foundation model regime, showing that optimal sparsity increases monotonically with training duration and that sparse models form a half-plane of optimal configurations rather than a single line. The finding that sparsity acts as a clean multiplicative factor on the capacity term (with no interaction with data scaling) and that gains are remarkably consistent across vision and language (~2.15× at 75%) gives practitioners a simple rule of thumb. Key limitations include the focus on pretraining with infinite data (eliminating overfitting effects where sparsity might help more), moderate maximum sparsity (87.5%), and the assumption that sparsity provides no training-time speedup in the dense-cost analysis. Open questions include whether the law holds at extreme sparsity (>90%), how it interacts with quantization or MoE routing, and whether hardware-specific speedup profiles alter the optimal sparsity landscape.
