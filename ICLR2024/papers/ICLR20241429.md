## Problem

While automated prediction of future numerical data (weather, stock prices) is well-studied, predicting future textual content remains largely unexplored in NLP. This paper formalizes the task of future language modeling—probabilistically modeling future texts given a temporal history of documents—and demonstrates that temporal information can be effectively incorporated into language models to generate text that better reflects upcoming trends.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241429/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a temporal sequence of document collections (one per year, e.g., ACL paper abstracts from 2003–2019) and generates text for the next year (2021). During generation, each token's probability is conditioned not only on previously generated tokens but also on all prior years' documents, with the temporal signal encoded as a per-word bias added to the softmax logits. The output is a full generated abstract (~164 tokens on average) that should reflect emerging topics, methods, and terminology of the target year.

## Method

The core mechanism augments a fine-tuned GPT-2 decoder by adding a temporal bias term to the output softmax, up-weighting or down-weighting vocabulary items based on their trajectory across prior years. Three variants are proposed: (1) a Word Frequency Model that feeds log-frequencies through a shared LSTM to produce a scalar bias per word; (2) a Temporal Contextual Model that replaces raw frequencies with per-year pooled contextualized embeddings (from RoBERTa) fed through the same LSTM; and (3) a Doubly Contextualized Model that introduces a sigmoid-attention gating mechanism between the transformer decoder's hidden state and the projected temporal bias, allowing the model to dynamically decide when to rely on temporal trends versus local generation context. All models are trained with standard cross-entropy loss via fine-tuning, and the LSTM uses a sliding window of m prior years to capture multi-year trends.

## Results

On the test set (2021 ACL abstracts), the Doubly Contextualized Model (Context²) achieves the best performance across all three automatic metrics: PPL 19.81, Content METEOR 20.12, and Content Perplexity 82.43. This represents a 2.95-point PPL improvement, a 3.9-point CM improvement, and a 19.6-point CPL improvement over the strongest non-temporal baseline (Baseline-all: PPL 22.76, CM 16.22, CPL 102.03). The Word Frequency Model also outperforms Baseline-all on all metrics (PPL 20.20, CM 19.98, CPL 87.68), while the Temporal Contextual Model (Context) degrades fluency (PPL 23.21) despite competitive content matching, highlighting the importance of the gating mechanism in Context².

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against three categories of baselines: (1) non-temporal GPT-2 fine-tuned on all previous years (Baseline-all) or only the n most recent years (Baseline-2, Baseline-3); (2) a word-frequency bias without LSTM (Frequency-NoLSTM); and (3) the paper's own simpler temporal models (Frequency, Context). Context² wins on all three metrics against every baseline. Against Baseline-all, Context² improves PPL by 2.95 points, CM by 3.9 points, and CPL by 19.6 points. Against the best recent-window baseline (Baseline-2), Context² still leads on all metrics (PPL 19.81 vs 21.53, CM 20.12 vs 19.69, CPL 82.43 vs 94.37). The Frequency model also beats all baselines but trails Context², showing that contextualized temporal information beyond raw word counts provides additional benefit. Notably, the Context model (without gating) actually worsens PPL relative to Baseline-all (23.21 vs 22.76), demonstrating that naively injecting temporal bias without a gating mechanism harms fluency.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by formalizing 'future language modeling' as a distinct task, explicitly noting that no prior work constructs generative language models for future text from temporal document history. While related work on diachronic NLP (Röttger & Pierrehumbert, 2021; Lazaridou et al., 2021; Hofmann et al., 2021) incorporates temporal information into language models, those efforts focus on adaptation or evaluation rather than generative prediction of future content. Methodologically, the paper repurposes well-known components—GPT-2 as a backbone, LSTM for temporal sequence modeling, and sigmoid attention for gating—into a novel architecture that injects a learned temporal bias into the softmax, but the combination and task framing are new.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper opens a new research direction for automated textual forecasting, with potential applications in trend prediction, technology forecasting, and domain-specific content generation. However, the evidence is limited to a single domain (NLP conference abstracts) with modest improvements over strong baselines (a few points on content metrics), and the 'future' horizon is only 1–2 years. Key open questions include whether the approach generalizes to other domains (news, scientific literature, business reports), whether it scales to larger language models, and what the fundamental upper bound is given that truly novel entities and random events are inherently unpredictable. The paper itself acknowledges that experts are often wrong at prediction, leaving the machine upper-bound unknown.
