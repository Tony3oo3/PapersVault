## Problem

Longitudinal treatment effects inference has been dominated by neural network architectures that serve as black-box inference machines, limiting interpretability, robustness to irregular sampling, and flexibility in identification assumptions. This paper introduces a fundamentally different solution type—closed-form ordinary differential equations discovered from observed trajectories—as a viable alternative, arguing that the resulting equation (rather than a trained network) is the inference machine itself.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240223/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes discrete-time patient trajectories (features x_t, treatments a_t, outcomes y_t) along with static covariates v, where treatments can be binary, categorical, multiple, or continuous-valued and either static or dynamic. It produces patient-specific closed-form ODEs F^(i) with individualized numeric constants, a population ODE F̄ whose constants carry parametric distributions (e.g., Gaussian), and counterfactual outcome predictions for hypothetical future treatment plans. Unlike neural network baselines, the output is a human-readable equation that supports extrapolation to irregularly sampled observations and further scientific reasoning about treatment dynamics.

## Method

INSITE (Individualized Nonlinear Sparse Identification Treatment Effect) operates in two stages: first, a population ODE F̄ is discovered from the full dataset using a deterministic ODE discovery method (SINDy by default), with separate equations learned per categorical treatment value to handle discrete treatment plans; second, each patient's numeric constants β^(i) are fine-tuned via BFGS minimization of a loss combining trajectory MSE with an L2 regularization toward the population constants β̄, borrowing a transfer-learning principle to prevent overfitting. The framework resolves three key discrepancies between ODE discovery and treatment effects literature: substituting ODE assumptions (existence/uniqueness, observability, functional space) for standard TE assumptions (consistency, overlap, ignorability), incorporating diverse treatment types through piecewise ODEs or action variables, and modeling between-subject variability through per-patient numeric constants rather than learned representations. This yields a model that is fully interpretable, naturally handles irregular sampling, and can relax the overlap assumption when the correct functional form is specified.

## Results

INSITE achieves the lowest counterfactual 6-step-ahead normalized RMSE across all ten synthetic datasets. On the one-compartmental PKPD model (eq. 5, where INSITE is correctly specified), it reaches near-perfect n-RMSE of 0.02–0.05, compared to 0.10–0.15 for adapted ODE baselines and 0.72–2.09 for LTE baselines. On the more complex tumor-growth PKPD model (eq. 6) and the standard Cancer PKPD benchmark (where INSITE is misspecified due to a limited feature library), it still achieves 0.79–0.94 n-RMSE, outperforming the best LTE method (CRN at 0.92 on Cancer PKPD) and the adapted SINDy baseline (1.23). INSITE also maintains low error under increasing time-dependent confounding (γ up to 6), where LTE methods degrade substantially.

## Baselines & Comparisons

INSITE outperforms all five LTE baselines (MSM, RMSN, CRN, G-Net, CT) on every dataset. The gap is most dramatic on the one-compartmental PKPD model (eq. 5), where INSITE achieves 0.02–0.05 n-RMSE versus 0.72–2.09 for the best LTE method (G-Net/CT), a >15× improvement. On the standard Cancer PKPD benchmark, INSITE (0.79) beats the strongest LTE baseline CRN (0.92) by ~14%. Against the adapted ODE-discovery baselines, INSITE also wins across the board: on eq.(5) it improves over A-SINDy (0.11) by 5–6×, and on eq.(6)/Cancer PKPD it reduces A-SINDy's error from 1.23–1.51 to 0.79–0.94. A-WSINDy, which uses a variational (weak) formulation, is only applicable to the simpler eq.(5) datasets and is outperformed by INSITE there as well (0.10 vs. 0.05 on eq.(5).D). The ablation (Table 4) confirms that both per-categorical-treatment ODE discovery and patient-specific fine-tuning contribute substantially, with removing either component degrading eq.(5).D n-RMSE from 0.05 to 0.15 or 0.43.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in longitudinal treatment effects (LTE), where neural network architectures (RMSN, CRN, Causal Transformers, G-Net) serve as black-box inference machines trained with propensity weighting or adversarial balancing. Instead, it repurposes ODE discovery—specifically SINDy (Brunton et al., 2016)—as the inference engine, producing closed-form differential equations rather than learned representations. The key conceptual move is a framework that systematically translates any ODE discovery method into a TE method by reconciling three discrepancies: different identification assumptions, discrete treatment plans, and between-subject variability. INSITE instantiates this framework with a two-step procedure: population-level SINDy discovery followed by BFGS fine-tuning of numeric constants per patient, borrowing a transfer-learning regularization idea.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a genuinely different type of solution to longitudinal TE estimation—one that yields interpretable equations, handles irregular sampling naturally through continuous-time formulation, and operates under a distinct (and in some settings weaker) set of identification assumptions that can relax the overlap requirement. However, the method is constrained to sparse ODEs, requires a correctly specified feature library, and is evaluated exclusively on synthetic PKPD datasets, leaving open whether the approach transfers to real-world clinical data with unobserved confounders or high-dimensional covariates. Future work on stochastic differential equations, latent variable handling, and cross-population transfer would be needed to move toward practical deployment.
