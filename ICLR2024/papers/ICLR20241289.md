## Problem

Prior work claimed that LLMs can self-correct their reasoning, but these gains relied on oracle labels for stopping criteria, unfair comparisons that ignored equivalent inference costs, or sub-optimal initial prompts that made the feedback step appear informative. This paper asks whether LLMs can genuinely self-correct reasoning intrinsically—without any external feedback—and finds they cannot, with performance often degrading after self-correction.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241289/cropped_page5_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The evaluation pipeline takes a text question, the model's initial response, and a feedback prompt as input, producing a revised answer through a three-step prompting protocol (generate, review/feedback, revise). Each self-correction round consumes 2 additional model calls (3 total for round 1, 5 for round 2), and the paper explicitly tracks call counts to enable fair comparison against self-consistency baselines at matched inference budgets. Outputs are accuracy scores on GSM8K, CommonSenseQA, HotpotQA, and CommonGen-Hard, with per-instance answer-change analysis (correct→incorrect, incorrect→correct, no change).

## Method

This is an empirical study that defines and evaluates 'intrinsic self-correction'—a setting where the LLM must judge its own answer correctness without oracle labels. The authors replicate RCI (Kim et al., 2023), Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023), and Self-Refine (Madaan et al., 2023) across GPT-3.5-Turbo, GPT-4, GPT-4-Turbo, and Llama-2-70B, showing that removing oracle labels causes self-correction to consistently decrease accuracy. They further demonstrate that multi-agent debate (Du et al., 2023) underperforms simple self-consistency at equivalent response counts, and that apparent Self-Refine gains on constrained generation vanish when the initial prompt is made equally informative. The key insight is that the self-correction feedback prompt acts as an additional, potentially biasing instruction rather than a genuine error-detection mechanism, and that LLMs lack the metacognitive ability to reliably distinguish correct from incorrect reasoning steps.

## Results

The central finding is that intrinsic self-correction (without oracle labels) consistently *decreases* reasoning accuracy across all tested models and benchmarks. For GPT-3.5, GSM8K accuracy drops from 75.9% (standard prompting) to 74.7% after two rounds of self-correction, while CommonSenseQA plummets from 75.8% to 41.8%. GPT-4 shows a similar pattern: GSM8K falls from 95.5% to 89.0% and HotpotQA from 49.0% to 43.0%. In the multi-agent debate comparison on GSM8K, self-consistency with 9 responses (88.2%) substantially outperforms multi-agent debate with 9 responses (83.0%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper replicates the self-correction protocols of Kim et al. (2023, RCI) and Shinn et al. (2023, Reflexion) and shows that their reported improvements (e.g., +7% on GSM8K, +15% on CommonSenseQA) depend entirely on oracle labels; without them, both methods degrade performance. Against Du et al. (2023, Multi-Agent Debate), the paper demonstrates that at equivalent inference costs, self-consistency (Wang et al., 2022) consistently outperforms multi-agent debate: 85.3% vs. 83.2% at 6 responses and 88.2% vs. 83.0% at 9 responses on GSM8K, indicating the debate mechanism adds no value over simple majority voting. For Madaan et al. (2023, Self-Refine), the paper shows that the apparent self-correction gain on Constrained Generation (67.0% vs. 44.0%) is an artifact of a sub-optimal initial prompt; when the constraint is included in the initial instruction, standard prompting reaches 81.8% while self-correction drops to 75.1%. Overall, the paper's method (intrinsic self-correction) loses to its own standard prompting baseline on every benchmark, and multi-agent debate loses to self-consistency at matched compute.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper does not introduce a new method but critically re-examines the self-correction literature (RCI, Reflexion, Self-Refine, Multi-Agent Debate) by isolating the role of oracle labels, inference-cost fairness, and prompt design. It carves new conceptual ground by defining 'intrinsic self-correction'—self-correction without any external feedback—as the proper setting for evaluating whether LLMs can genuinely fix their own reasoning, a distinction that prior work had blurred. The paper repurposes existing prompting and evaluation infrastructure to show that the celebrated improvements in prior self-correction papers largely dissolve once these confounds are controlled.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that intrinsic self-correction consistently degrades reasoning performance across four models and three benchmarks challenges a widely-held assumption in the LLM community and redirects research toward external-feedback-based correction (code execution, tool use, trained verifiers) and toward fairer evaluation protocols (comparing against self-consistency at matched inference cost). A key open question is whether any prompting or architectural change can enable true intrinsic self-correction for reasoning, or whether the inability to judge one's own reasoning correctness is a fundamental limitation of current LLMs. The paper also highlights that the scope is limited to reasoning tasks; self-correction may still be effective for style, safety, or format-alignment tasks where the model can more reliably assess its own output.
