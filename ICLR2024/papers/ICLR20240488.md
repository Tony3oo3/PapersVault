## Problem

Sampling the learned distributions of Restricted Boltzmann Machines is extremely slow when the distribution is multimodal, as standard Alternating Gibbs Sampling gets trapped in energy valleys and parallel tempering struggles with well-separated modes. While deep representations were known to aid mixing on intuitive grounds, no practical method systematically exploited hierarchical RBM representations for accelerated sampling, and no theoretical analysis quantified the resulting speedup.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240488/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a sequentially trained stack of RBMs with nested architecture (hidden units of RBM n equal visible units of RBM n+1, with visible layer sizes decreasing with depth, e.g., 784→200→100→25→10 for MNIST) and produces samples from the bottom RBM's learned data distribution. The input data can be binary images (MNIST), discrete sequences (lattice proteins with 20-letter alphabet), or spin configurations (2D Ising lattice), and the output is a sequence of visible-layer configurations from the bottom RBM that mix efficiently across all modes of the distribution.

## Method

Stacked Tempering (ST) trains a stack of RBMs sequentially bottom-up, where each RBM's hidden representations serve as training data for the next, with decreasing hidden-unit counts and L2 regularization producing increasingly clustered, simplified representations at greater depths. During sampling, each RBM is updated independently via Alternating Gibbs Sampling, but after each step a Metropolis swap is attempted between the hidden configuration of RBM n and the visible configuration of RBM n+1, with an acceptance probability that satisfies detailed balance without requiring intractable partition functions. The key mechanism is that the deepest RBM, operating on a highly compressed and clustered representation, mixes rapidly and propagates non-local moves down the stack through exchanges, enabling the bottom RBM to jump between distant modes. A theoretical analysis in the overparametrized limit (N,M→∞) identifies a phase transition between pattern-separation and pattern-completion regimes controlled by aspect ratio and regularization, and derives exact exponential scaling of mixing and swap times, showing an optimal trade-off between representation compression and swap acceptance rate.

## Results

On MNIST0/1, Stacked Tempering (ST) with 4 RBMs (784→200→100→25→10 units) achieves a linear increase in digit-class transitions over sampling time, while AGS remains stuck in a single class and PT (even with many intermediate temperatures) shows minimal improvement. On Lattice Proteins, ST generates high-quality sequences with P_nat > 0.9 for over 80% of samples and mixes efficiently between the two target structures S_A and S_B, whereas AGS and PT almost never switch between the two sequence families. On the 2D Ising model (32×32 grid), ST with 3 RBMs (50, 20, 1 hidden units) performs effective cluster moves that flip the magnetization sign, while AGS and PT remain trapped at a fixed magnetization. The theoretical analysis (Section 4) shows that ST provides an exponential speedup in data dimension N over AGS, with the optimal speedup governed by a trade-off between representation compression and swap acceptance rate.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares ST against two baselines: Alternating Gibbs Sampling (AGS), the standard single-RBM sampler, and Parallel Tempering (PT), which runs multiple RBM copies at different effective temperatures with Metropolis-style exchanges. ST decisively outperforms both on all four test datasets. On MNIST0/1, AGS never transitions between digits 0 and 1, and PT with up to many intermediate temperatures still shows very few transitions, while ST produces a steady linear accumulation of class switches (Fig. 3a–c). On full MNIST, AGS can only transition between visually similar digit pairs (e.g., 0→5, 8→3), whereas ST achieves transitions across many more pairs, including dissimilar digits, at a rate of at least one transition per 10^5 MC steps for a substantially larger set of digit pairs (Fig. 3h). On Lattice Proteins, both AGS and PT fail to switch between the S_A and S_B sequence families (cumulative transitions remain near zero), while ST mixes efficiently and maintains high folding probability (P_nat > 0.9 for >80% of samples). On the 2D Ising model near the critical temperature, AGS and PT both get trapped at a fixed magnetization sign set by initialization, while ST's stacked architecture enables effective cluster-like moves that flip the global magnetization. The paper attributes PT's underperformance to a phenomenon analogous to critical slowing down, where separate modes require a large number of temperatures near a critical point to be adequately bridged.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the parallel tempering (replica exchange) framework of Swendsen & Wang (1986) from physical systems to data-driven RBM models, and adapts the Deep Tempering architecture of Desjardins et al. (2014) in two key ways: RBMs are trained sequentially rather than simultaneously, and layer widths decrease with depth rather than remaining uniform. Unlike Deep Tempering, which was designed as a training procedure for deep belief networks, Stacked Tempering is purely a sampling algorithm where the nested structure enables configuration exchanges between adjacent RBM layers. The paper also provides the first analytical treatment (in an overparametrized, K=2 regime) connecting the hierarchical clustering of representations to mixing-time speedups, building on the intuitive argument of Bengio et al. (2013) that deep representations improve mixing.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method offers a practical, easy-to-implement sampling procedure that substantially outperforms both Alternating Gibbs Sampling and Parallel Tempering across diverse domains (handwritten digits, protein sequences, spin systems), with the added benefit that deeper RBMs have fewer parameters and converge faster during training. The theoretical analysis identifies a phase transition between pattern-separation and pattern-completion regimes in RBM representations, providing concrete guidance on hyperparameter choices (aspect ratios, L2 regularization) that practitioners can use. Key limitations include the very restricted theoretical setting (overparametrized, K=2 data points) and the additional training cost of the full stack, while open questions include whether exchange efficiency can serve as a joint training objective and how the framework extends to regimes where the number of data points is comparable to their dimension.
