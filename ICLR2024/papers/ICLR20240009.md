## Problem

Despite the widespread use of soft-prompt tuning for adapting vision-language models like CLIP to downstream tasks, no prior work has investigated the role of the norms of learned soft-prompt vectors in model performance. This paper poses the question 'Do we need to normalize the soft prompts in VLMs?' and uncovers the previously unexplored Low-Norm Effect, where reducing the norms of prompt vectors at specific positions improves performance while increasing them degrades it.

## Input / Output

The method operates on RGB images (e.g., 224×224) paired with class-name text, producing a class label over C categories. The image is encoded by a frozen image encoder (ResNet-50 or ViT-B/16), while the text side concatenates L learnable soft-prompt vectors (each of dimension D) with the class-name word embedding, encoded by a frozen text encoder. Classification is performed via cosine similarity between the image feature and all C text features, with the only trainable parameters being the L×D soft-prompt matrix.

## Method

Nemesis introduces two corruption operations (REPLACE with Gaussian vectors and RESCALE by a factor s) to systematically probe how norm changes at each prompt position affect performance, revealing the Low-Norm Effect. To exploit this, it proposes two regularization losses added to the standard cross-entropy objective: the Position-Uniform Normalization (PUN) loss, which applies a uniform ℓp-norm penalty to all L prompt positions, and the Position-Aware Normalization (PAN) loss, which first runs a pre-inference step each batch to rescale N randomly selected positions by a threshold τ and compares corrupted versus original predictions to identify which positions actually benefit from norm reduction. Only those identified positions receive the normalization penalty in PAN, avoiding unnecessary constraint on unaffected positions. The total objective is L = L_CE + β·L_PUN + (1−β)·L_PAN, where β selects the variant, and the method integrates into any existing soft-prompt framework with negligible additional parameters.

## Results

On few-shot image classification across 11 datasets, CoOp+Nemesis (PUN) achieves up to +3.84% over CoOp on ImageNet at 2 shots, and +4.42% on Food101 at 16 shots (78.62% vs. 74.20%). In the domain generalization setting (16-shot ImageNet training), CoOp+Nemesis (PUN, ω=10) improves over CoOp on all four target domains, e.g., ImageNet-Sketch from 32.64% to 34.34% and ImageNet-R from 54.60% to 56.78%. In the base-to-new setting, CoCoOp+Nemesis (PAN, ω=20) boosts new-class accuracy by 1.3% over CoCoOp (73.9% vs. 72.6% average).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CoOp (the primary baseline), Nemesis consistently wins on both few-shot classification and domain generalization: on ImageNet 16-shot, PUN (ω=10) reaches 63.08% vs. CoOp's 62.70%, and on Food101 16-shot the gap widens to 78.62% vs. 74.20%. Against PLOT (an ensemble-based prompt-tuning method), PLOT+Nemesis outperforms PLOT on all three tested datasets (Caltech101, DTD, Flowers102) across all shot settings, e.g., on DTD 16-shot: 64.68% (PAN) vs. 64.16% (PLOT). Against CoCoOp in the base-to-new setting, CoCoOp+Nemesis (PAN, ω=20) improves new-class accuracy by 1.3% on average (73.9% vs. 72.6%) while slightly reducing base-class accuracy (80.3% vs. 80.8%), yielding a higher harmonic mean (77.0 vs. 76.5). Notably, the PAN loss achieves better source-domain performance but slightly worse target-domain transfer than PUN, suggesting PAN's position-aware selection overfits to intra-domain patterns.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on CoOp (Zhou et al., 2022b) and its conditional variant CoCoOp (Zhou et al., 2022a), which established soft-prompt tuning as the dominant paradigm for adapting CLIP to few-shot visual recognition. While prior work has investigated prompt interpretability (mapping to nearest words), attention mechanisms in prompt-tuning (Oymak et al., 2023), and alignment strategies (PLOT, Chen et al., 2023), none had examined the norms of learned prompt vectors as a performance-relevant property. Nemesis departs from this lineage by reframing the question from 'what should prompts encode?' to 'how should the magnitude of prompt vectors be constrained?', introducing two corruption operations (REPLACE, RESCALE) as diagnostic tools and two normalization losses (PUN, PAN) as remedies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of the Low-Norm Effect provides a new lens for understanding why soft-prompt tuning can degrade, particularly in low-data regimes, and offers a lightweight, plug-and-play regularization that improves CoOp by 1–4% on datasets where the effect is pronounced. However, the effect is highly dataset-dependent (absent on Flowers102, strong on OxfordPets/Food101), the mechanistic explanation remains speculative, and the PAN loss introduces non-trivial computational overhead (up to 4× training time) that scales with the number of corruption positions N. Open questions include: why norms stabilize before accuracy plateaus, how to automatically select ω per dataset, and whether the effect generalizes to other PEFT paradigms (prefix-tuning, adapter-tuning) beyond text-side prompt-tuning.
