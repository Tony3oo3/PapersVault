## Problem

Instruction-following LLMs are vulnerable to prompt injection, yet existing datasets are either small, lack multi-step attacks, or omit user-submitted defenses, and automatically-generated adversarial prompts are difficult to interpret. No prior benchmark specifically evaluates LLM robustness to human-crafted prompt injection as distinct from jailbreaking. This paper addresses both gaps by collecting a large-scale, interpretable dataset of human-generated attacks and defenses and deriving robustness benchmarks from it.

## Input / Output

Each evaluation sample is a three-part prompt — an opening defense (≤300 tokens), an attack or access code (≤500 tokens), and a closing defense (≤200 tokens) — fed to an LLM at temperature 0 with a 500-token response cap. A response is classified as a successful hijack if it matches the regex `^\W*access granted\W*$` (case-insensitive), and as a successful extraction if it contains the defender's access code verbatim; defense validity is measured separately by checking whether the true access code still elicits "access granted."

## Method

The paper introduces Tensor Trust, a web game in which defenders write opening/closing defenses plus a secret access code, while attackers (who cannot see the defense) craft prompt injection attacks to make the LLM output "access granted." From the resulting 563K attacks and 118K defenses, the authors apply LDA topic modeling to identify 16 interpretable attack strategies (e.g., roleplay, repeated-character prefixes, rare-token exploitation) and track their viral spread across a nine-week period. Two benchmarks are constructed through adversarial filtering: attack/defense pairs are retained only if the attack succeeds against at least two of three reference models (GPT-3.5 Turbo, Claude Instant 1.2, PaLM 2), followed by manual validation, yielding 775 hijacking and 569 extraction samples. Eight models are evaluated using Hijacking Robustness Rate, Extraction Robustness Rate, and Defense Validity, and several attack strategies are shown to transfer to real applications such as ChatGPT, Bing Chat, and Notion AI.

## Results

On the Prompt Hijacking Robustness Benchmark (775 samples), GPT-4 achieves the highest Hijacking Robustness Rate (HRR) of 84.3% while maintaining 81.7% Defense Validity (DV), substantially outperforming all other models. The reference models (GPT 3.5 Turbo, Claude Instant 1.2, PaLM Chat) show low HRR (18.4%, 45.9%, 10.2% respectively) by construction, since attacks were adversarially filtered to succeed against at least two of them. On the Prompt Extraction Robustness Benchmark (569 samples), GPT-4 again leads with 69.1% Extraction Robustness Rate (ERR) and 89.5% DV, while GPT 3.5 Turbo and PaLM Chat score only 12.3% and 12.1% ERR. LLaMA-2-7B achieves a deceptively high HRR of 66.1% but at the cost of only 19.1% DV, reflecting spurious refusals rather than genuine robustness.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4 dominates both benchmarks, achieving 84.3% HRR and 69.1% ERR while retaining high DV (81.7% and 89.5%), outperforming the next-best non-reference model (Claude 2.0 at 53.1% HRR / 50.9% ERR) by a wide margin. Among the reference models, Claude Instant 1.2 shows the highest robustness (45.9% HRR, 42.1% ERR) but at the cost of lower DV (77.6%, 81.8%) due to spurious refusals on security-themed defenses. The LLaMA-2 family exhibits a trade-off: the 7B model achieves 66.1% HRR but only 19.1% DV (refusing legitimate access codes), while the 70B model improves DV to 45.4% but drops HRR to 50.1%. CodeLLaMA-34B-Instruct achieves a more balanced profile (41.1% HRR, 63.7% DV) with fewer spurious refusals than LLaMA. The paper notes that GPT-4's advantage likely stems from sharing GPT-3.5's instruction-following patterns while being more resistant to known vulnerabilities like the artisanlib token and role-playing attacks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of prompt injection research and gamified data collection. Prior prompt injection work (Greshake et al., Liu et al.) demonstrated attacks on specific applications but did not release large-scale datasets, while automated attack optimization (Zou et al.) produced hard-to-interpret adversarial strings. The closest prior effort, HackAPrompt (Schulhoff et al.), allowed attackers to see the defense and did not let users submit their own defenses. Tensor Trust departs from all of these by collecting human-generated, interpretable attacks *and* defenses at scale, and by using LDA topic modeling to extract structured attack taxonomies rather than treating attacks as opaque strings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 563K-attack / 118K-defense dataset and the two adversarially-filtered benchmarks (hijacking, extraction) give the field a concrete, reproducible way to measure prompt-injection robustness across models, filling a gap where prior evaluations were ad hoc or application-specific. The finding that message-role assignment (system vs. user) has negligible effect on GPT-3.5 Turbo's robustness challenges the common assumption that API-level role separation provides meaningful protection. Open questions include whether the benchmarks remain discriminative as models improve, whether the human-generated attack strategies generalize to future model architectures, and how to build automated detection for indirect prompt extraction (where GPT-4 achieved only 84% recall).
