## Problem

Self-consistency (SC) decoding for chain-of-thought reasoning requires a fixed, preset number of samples (e.g., 64 for MATH), incurring substantial API costs—roughly $2000 to evaluate the full MATH test set with GPT-4. The paper asks whether this sampling budget can be adaptively reduced per input without sacrificing the voting-based performance gains that make SC effective over greedy chain-of-thought.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240589/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a text reasoning prompt (with few-shot demonstrations) and a language model, and produces a final answer selected by majority vote over the generated samples. Unusually, the number of samples consumed is not fixed: it is an adaptive quantity (denoted L-hat) that varies per input, ranging from as few as one window (w samples) up to the preset maximum L. The output thus includes both the voted answer and the actual sample count, enabling direct cost accounting per problem.

## Method

ESC partitions the fixed sampling budget L into sequential observation windows of size w. After each window, it checks whether all w sampled answers are identical (i.e., the window's entropy is zero); if so, sampling stops immediately and that unanimous answer is returned. If no window is unanimous, sampling continues until the maximum L is reached, at which point standard SC voting applies. A one-proportion z-test provides a theoretical upper bound on the probability that ESC's vote disagrees with SC's vote (≤ 2×10⁻³ for w = 8), and the bound tightens as w grows. A control scheme further uses the first window (w₀ ≈ 5) to estimate the expected sampling cost and performance degradation for any (w, L) pair, allowing practitioners to select parameters that meet a given budget-performance trade-off without prior knowledge of model capability or task difficulty.

## Results

ESC reduces the average number of samples by 33.8% on MATH, 80.1% on GSM8K, 76.8% on StrategyQA, 78.5% on CommonsenseQA, 84.2% on Coin Flip, and 67.4% on Last Letters (averaged across GPT-4, GPT-3.5-Turbo, and Llama-2 7B), while maintaining accuracy within ±0.2% of full self-consistency. For example, on GSM8K with GPT-4, ESC achieves 89.29% accuracy using only 7.98 samples on average versus 40 for SC. The control scheme predicts sampling cost and performance with Pearson correlations of 0.81–1.00 across models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CoT (Wei et al., 2022), ESC substantially outperforms: on MATH with GPT-4, ESC reaches 60.32% versus CoT's 50.44%, and on GSM8K with GPT-3.5-Turbo, ESC achieves 85.67% versus CoT's 75.83%. Against full Self-Consistency (Wang et al., 2023), ESC matches accuracy to within 0.01–0.19 percentage points across all six benchmarks and three models while reducing average samples by 33–84%; the L̂−SC row confirms that running SC with only L̂ samples would drop accuracy by 0.13–1.96 points, validating that ESC's early stopping preserves the voting benefit. Against Adaptive Consistency (Aggarwal et al., 2023), ESC achieves equal or slightly higher accuracy at lower cost: on GSM8K with GPT-4, ESC scores 89.29% at $0.0769/item versus AC's 89.28% at $0.1501/item, and on Letter with GPT-3.5, ESC scores 83.15% at $0.0012/item versus AC's 83.15% at $0.0020/item. Against Progressive-Hint Prompting (Zheng et al., 2023), ESC achieves higher accuracy (85.67% vs. 85.08%) at lower cost ($0.0052 vs. $0.0072 per item) on GSM8K with GPT-3.5-Turbo, and when ESC is applied on top of PHP, it reduces sampling from 40 to 10.26 on average while maintaining PHP's accuracy (86.98% vs. 87.00%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends Self-Consistency (Wang et al., 2023) by replacing its fixed-size sampling with an adaptive early-stopping mechanism based on observation windows. It departs from Adaptive Consistency (Aggarwal et al., 2023), which also reduces SC cost but requires a tuned hyperparameter threshold and generates samples one-by-one (incurring repeated prompt overhead). The theoretical grounding draws on the Law of Large Numbers and one-proportion z-tests to bound the probability that ESC's voting outcome diverges from SC's, and the control scheme for dynamically selecting window size and maximum samples is a novel addition not present in prior SC variants.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ESC offers a drop-in, hyperparameter-free replacement for SC that cuts sampling costs by 30–84% across arithmetic, commonsense, and symbolic reasoning with negligible accuracy loss, making SC-based inference far more accessible for researchers and production systems. The control scheme enables practitioners to set explicit cost/performance budgets without prior knowledge of model capability or task difficulty. Limitations include reduced savings on harder tasks where the model is genuinely uncertain (e.g., only 33.8% on MATH), and the strict all-same-in-window criterion may be overly conservative for tasks where a near-unanimous window would suffice. Open questions include how ESC interacts with test-time compute scaling trends and whether the window-based stopping criterion generalizes to iterative or multi-turn reasoning settings.
