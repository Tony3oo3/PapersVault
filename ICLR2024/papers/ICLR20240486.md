## Problem

Existing meta-IRL methods learn reward functions from few demonstrations but cannot explicitly handle constraints, and none provide theoretical guarantees on convergence or generalization to new tasks. This paper formulates the first meta-learning framework that jointly learns meta-priors over both reward functions and constraints, with provable convergence and generalization bounds.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240486/cropped_page8_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes expert demonstration trajectories from m related tasks sharing the same CMDP (state, action, transition dynamics), where each task has its own reward, cost, and budget. Each task's dataset is partitioned into three subsets: a small training set for computing task-specific adaptations, an evaluation set for hyper-gradient computation, and a second-order set for finite-difference approximations. The output consists of a reward meta-prior θ and cost meta-prior ω; adaptation to a new task requires one gradient step on θ (MAML-style) and solving a λ-regularized optimization on ω (iMAML-style), yielding task-specific reward and cost functions.

## Method

M-ICRL formulates a bi-level optimization where the upper level learns a reward meta-prior via MAML-style one-step adaptation and the lower level learns a cost meta-prior via iMAML-style regularized optimization, unifying both paradigms in a single framework. The core technical challenge is computing hyper-gradients that involve intractable second-order terms (requiring gradients of inverse-Hessian expressions) and expensive inverse-of-Hessian products. The paper addresses this with three mechanisms: (i) a first-order finite-difference approximation to avoid computing second-order terms directly, (ii) iterative gradient descent to approximate the inverse-Hessian-vector product without explicit matrix inversion, and (iii) Lipschitz continuity arguments to bound the resulting approximation errors. The algorithm achieves O(1/ε²) iteration complexity to reach ε-stationary points, and the generalization error to a new task is bounded by the L1 distance between the new task's stationary state-action distribution and the training tasks' average distribution.

## Results

M-ICRL achieves near-expert performance across all four experiments with only one demonstration per test task. On the physical drone navigation task, M-ICRL reaches a success rate of 0.96 (vs. expert 1.00) and a constraint violation rate of 0.02 (vs. expert 0.00). On Mujoco tasks, M-ICRL attains cumulative rewards of 322.56 (Swimmer), 228.78 (HalfCheetah), and 712.40 (Walker), approaching expert values of 376.10, 264.00, and 752.40 respectively, while maintaining CVR below 0.04 in all cases. The method outperforms all baselines on every metric in every experiment.

## Baselines & Comparisons

M-ICRL dominates all three baselines on every metric across all four experiments. Compared to ICRL (Liu & Zhu, 2022), which learns from a single demonstration without meta-priors, M-ICRL improves success rate from 0.62 to 0.96 on the drone task and cumulative reward from 76.44 to 322.56 on Swimmer (a 4.2× improvement). Against Meta-IRL (Xu et al., 2019), which only learns a reward meta-prior via MAML, M-ICRL reduces constraint violation rate from 0.33–0.42 to 0.00–0.04, demonstrating the critical benefit of jointly learning both reward and constraint meta-priors. The ICRL(pre) baseline, which naively pre-trains by maximizing likelihood across all training demonstrations, performs only marginally better than ICRL alone (e.g., 0.71 vs. 0.62 SR on drone), confirming that the bi-level meta-learning design with task-specific adaptation is essential rather than simple parameter averaging.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate lines of work: single-task inverse constrained reinforcement learning (ICRL, Liu & Zhu 2022), which learns reward and cost functions via a bi-level optimization with a constrained soft Bellman policy, and meta-learning frameworks (MAML for reward adaptation, iMAML for cost adaptation). The key departure from prior meta-IRL work (Xu et al. 2019; Yu et al. 2019) is the explicit treatment of constraints in the meta-learning loop and the provision of convergence and generalization guarantees, which those works lack. The bi-level formulation is designed so that the lower-level cost learning (iMAML-style) does not add extra computational burden because ICRL is already bi-level, while the upper-level reward learning uses MAML-style one-step adaptation to avoid a three-level problem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first theoretical framework for meta-ICRL, providing both an O(1/ε²) convergence guarantee and a generalization bound that depends on the distance between the new task's stationary state-action distribution and the training tasks'. The practical implication is that a robot or agent can learn to navigate with constraints from a single demonstration in a new environment, provided the environment is structurally similar to training environments. A key limitation is the full observability assumption; extending to POMDPs is identified as future work. The generalization analysis via stationary state-action distribution distance, rather than reward/cost function distance, is a reasonable but somewhat conservative choice that may not capture all relevant task similarities.
