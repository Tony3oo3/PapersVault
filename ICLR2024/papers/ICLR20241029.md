## Problem

Real-world super-resolution methods operate over a vast degradation space (combinations of blur, noise, resize, and compression), yet are evaluated only on small randomly-sampled test sets, producing inconsistent and potentially misleading rankings. A method can outperform another on 60% of degradation types while still showing a lower mean PSNR, and different random samplings of the same space yield contradictory conclusions. This paper identifies the need for a systematic, distributed evaluation framework that captures overall generalization rather than a single average score.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a real-SR model as input and evaluates it on 100 representative degradation cases (SE test sets) constructed from Set14 or DIV2K_val ground-truth images. Each case applies a specific degradation parameter vector (derived from cluster centers) to generate low-quality inputs. The output is a structured set of metrics per case—distributed PSNR/LPIPS/SSIM scores, a binary acceptance indicator, and a sigmoid-mapped RPR value—aggregated into AR, RPR_I, RPR_A, RPR_U, and a final coarse-to-fine rank. The acceptance line (FSRCNN-mz, 0.4M params) and excellence line (SRResNet-mz, 1.5M params) are non-blind models individually fine-tuned on each of the 100 representative degradations.

## Method

SEAL first models the degradation space by generating 10,000 degraded images (combining BSRGAN's shuffled and RealESRGAN's high-order degradation models with equal probability), representing each via a 768-bin RGB histogram, and applying spectral clustering to extract 100 cluster centers whose degradation parameters define the representative test set. It then introduces two relative metrics: Acceptance Rate (AR), the fraction of cases where the model exceeds the acceptance line, and Relative Performance Ratio (RPR), which maps the model's improvement over the acceptance line relative to the gap to the excellence line through a sigmoid. A coarse-to-fine ranking protocol sequentially compares models by AR, then RPR_I (interquartile range measuring robustness), RPR_A (mean RPR on acceptable cases), and RPR_U (mean RPR on unacceptable cases), with predefined thresholds to decide when to advance to the next metric. The framework is modular: the clustering algorithm, similarity metric, IQA metric, and reference lines are all swappable to suit different evaluation needs.

## Results

Under the SEAL framework on Set14-SE (PSNR metric), BSRNet achieves the highest Acceptance Rate (AR = 0.59) and is ranked 1st, while RealESRNet-GD attains the highest average PSNR (22.82 dB) but ranks 2nd (AR = 0.43). SwinIR shows the best robustness with the lowest interquartile range RPR_I = 0.24. For GAN-based methods on Set14-SE (LPIPS metric), MMRealSR is ranked 1st (AR = 0.80, RPR_I = 0.08) and SwinIR ranks 2nd (AR = 0.81, RPR_I = 0.24). Notably, conventional random-test-set evaluation yields contradictory rankings: RealESRNet appears superior to BSRNet (24.93 vs 24.77 dB on random DIV2K_val), yet SEAL reveals BSRNet outperforms RealESRNet in 55% of representative degradation cases (AR 0.55 vs 0.15).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SEAL reveals that BSRNet (AR = 0.59) substantially outperforms RealESRNet (AR = 0.27) across representative degradation cases, directly contradicting the conventional evaluation where RealESRNet achieves a higher mean PSNR on a randomly sampled DIV2K_val set (24.93 vs 24.77 dB). RealESRNet-GD attains the highest average PSNR (22.82 dB) and best RPR_A (0.74), but its lower AR (0.43) places it behind BSRNet in the coarse-to-fine ranking. SwinIR demonstrates the most consistent performance (lowest RPR_I = 0.24) yet has the weakest RPR_A (0.58) among ranked methods, indicating it excels at robustness but not peak quality. SRResNet, DASR, and RDSR all fail the acceptance threshold (AR < 0.25), meaning they cannot even beat the small FSRCNN-mz network on most degradation cases. Among GAN-based methods, MMRealSR achieves the best robustness (RPR_I = 0.08) and is ranked 1st, while SwinIR leads in AR (0.81) but trails in RPR_I (0.24), illustrating a trade-off between coverage and consistency.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper does not build on a specific prior SR method but rather addresses a methodological gap in how real-world super-resolution (real-SR) models are evaluated. Prior real-SR works (BSRGAN, RealESRGAN, DASR) introduced increasingly complex degradation models but relied on randomly sampled small test sets with average PSNR for evaluation. SEAL repurposes spectral clustering (a standard unsupervised technique) to partition the vast degradation space into representative cases, and introduces relative metrics (AR, RPR) anchored to acceptance/excellence reference lines, departing from the absolute-average paradigm that dominates the field.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SEAL reveals that conventional evaluation of real-SR methods can produce contradictory and misleading rankings depending on which random test set is used, undermining confidence in published comparisons. The framework provides a more stable, distributed, and relative view of model performance that could become a standard for the real-SR community. Open questions include whether the clustering-based representation generalizes to even larger or more diverse degradation spaces, whether the fixed acceptance/excellence lines remain appropriate as models improve, and how to extend the protocol to perceptual or task-specific quality criteria beyond PSNR/LPIPS/SSIM.
