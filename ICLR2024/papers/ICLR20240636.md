## Problem

Transformers exhibit strong length generalization on some algorithmic tasks (e.g., counting) but fail on others (e.g., addition, parity), yet no unified framework explains when and why generalization occurs. Prior work provides scattered, task-specific evidence without a principled way to predict a Transformer's out-of-distribution behavior on a new task.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240636/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

All tasks are deterministic symbolic sequence-to-sequence problems with character-level tokenization. Inputs are prompts such as 'SoS 25>2345 EoS' (counting) or 'a5b4+a3b7>' (addition with index hints), and the model produces the next token autoregressively via greedy decoding. At train time, the context window is packed with multiple independent task samples and randomly shifted to encourage position-symmetric learning; at test time, single examples are evaluated without packing.

## Method

The paper introduces RASP-L, a restricted subset of the RASP programming language that captures algorithms a decoder-only Transformer can both represent and learn, with each RASP-L line compiling to at most one Transformer layer. The central RASP-Generalization Conjecture states that a Transformer trained to completion will length generalize if (1) the task is realizable by a single Transformer at all lengths, (2) the solution is expressible in RASP-L, and (3) training data is diverse enough to prevent shorter shortcut programs. Leveraging this framework, the authors design targeted interventions—index hints to eliminate index-arithmetic, reverse-order output to make carry propagation causal, and scratchpads that 'unroll' sequential loops into parallelizable next-token steps—that reduce RASP-L program complexity and unlock length generalization on previously intractable tasks like decimal addition and parity.

## Results

The paper's central empirical finding is that tasks admitting a short RASP-L program achieve near-perfect length generalization (100% exact match at +10 length beyond training), while tasks without such programs fail (0–50% EM). Specifically, Count, Mode, and Unique Copy all reach 96–100% EM at +10 length, and the newly constructed Addition (with index hints) and Parity (with scratchpad) also reach 100%. In contrast, standard Addition and Repeat Copy achieve 0% EM, and standard Parity plateaus at 50%. On the count task, models trained on sequences up to length 60 generalize near-perfectly up to length 150, and reverse-order addition with index hints generalizes perfectly on 50-digit hard-carry examples once training length exceeds 40.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against external methods; instead, it compares task formulations within a unified RASP-L framework. Standard addition (no index hints, forward order) achieves 0% EM at +10 length, while reverse-order addition with index hints reaches 100% once training length exceeds 40, and forward addition with balanced-carry training also generalizes on hard-carry examples. Standard parity (no scratchpad) plateaus at 50% EM, whereas parity with a running-parity scratchpad reaches 100% at training length 45. Conversely, adding a frequency-counting scratchpad to the Mode task (which already generalizes at 100% without one) degrades performance to 0% EM, demonstrating that scratchpads can hurt when they increase RASP-L program complexity. The paper also contrasts its RASP-based simplicity measure with the min-degree-interpolator model of Abbe et al. (2023), showing on a boolean conjunction task that the RASP framework correctly predicts 100% out-of-distribution accuracy while the min-degree model does not.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the RASP programming language (Weiss et al., 2021) into RASP-L, a 'learnable' subset that captures which algorithms standard decoder-only Transformers can both represent and learn, and uses it to propose a unifying conjecture for length generalization. It departs from the prevailing narrative (Dziri et al., 2023) that Transformers only perform 'analogical pattern matching' by demonstrating that standard Transformers trained from scratch do exhibit strong length generalization on tasks with short RASP-L solutions. The simplicity notion also contrasts with architecture-agnostic measures like minimum polynomial degree (Abbe et al., 2023), which the authors show can make incorrect predictions for Transformer behavior. The scratchpad analysis builds on Nye et al. (2021) and Wei et al. (2022) but reframes their utility through RASP-L program complexity rather than information-theoretic arguments.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The RASP-Generalization Conjecture provides a practical diagnostic: before investing in new architectures or training tricks for a task, check whether a short RASP-L program exists. This reframes length generalization from a per-task puzzle into a predictable property of the task's algorithmic structure. Key open questions include whether a formal complexity-theoretic characterization of 'simple for Transformers' exists (analogous to regular languages for RNNs), whether the conjecture extends to multi-task and compositional settings, and whether RASP-L's restrictions (int8, limited index arithmetic) are truly necessary or merely sufficient. The finding that scratchpads can hurt generalization by increasing RASP-L complexity is a cautionary result for the widespread assumption that chain-of-thought always helps.
