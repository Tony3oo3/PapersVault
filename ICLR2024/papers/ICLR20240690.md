## Problem

Large vision-language models (VLMs) consume substantial computational resources during inference, making them vulnerable to availability attacks that exhaust resources by inducing high energy consumption and latency. Prior energy-latency manipulation methods (sponge samples, NICGSlowDown) were designed for LLMs or smaller-scale captioning models and cannot be directly transferred to VLMs, which use advanced sampling policies that randomize output sequences and incorporate a vision modality that introduces new attack surfaces.

## Input / Output

The method takes a standard RGB image (224×224 for BLIP-2/InstructBLIP/MiniGPT-4, 384×384 for BLIP) and a text prompt (a placeholder for captioning, or a question for VQA/visual reasoning) and produces a single perturbed image constrained within an l∞ ball of radius ε=8. The perturbation is imperceptible (LIPIS ≈ 0.036 at ε=8), and when the verbose image is fed to a VLM, it triggers generation of a sequence up to 8.56× longer than the original, approaching the model's maximum token limit of 512.

## Method

The paper proposes three complementary loss objectives optimized via projected gradient descent (PGD) over 1,000 iterations: (1) a delayed EOS loss that minimizes the EOS token probability at every generated position to force continued generation; (2) an uncertainty loss that minimizes the KL divergence between each token's output distribution and a uniform distribution, breaking token-level output dependency; and (3) a token diversity loss that maximizes the nuclear norm of the concatenated hidden-state matrix across all generated tokens, breaking sequence-level output dependency. A temporal weight adjustment algorithm balances the three losses by normalizing each loss by the L1 norm of the uncertainty loss and applying logarithmic temporal decay functions with model-specific coefficients, plus a momentum term (m=0.9) to smooth weight updates across iterations.

## Results

Verbose images increase the average length of generated sequences by 7.87× on MS-COCO and 8.56× on ImageNet relative to original images, averaged across four VLMs (BLIP, BLIP-2, InstructBLIP, MiniGPT-4). For BLIP-2 on MS-COCO, verbose images produce sequences of length 226.72 (vs. 8.82 for original images), with latency of 7.97 s and energy of 321.59 J, compared to 0.39 s and 16.08 J for original images. The method consistently achieves the highest energy-latency cost across all four VLMs and both datasets, outperforming both sponge samples and NICGSlowDown on length, latency, and energy metrics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares verbose images against two prior energy-latency attack methods: sponge samples (Shumailov et al., 2021) and NICGSlowDown (Chen et al., 2022). Sponge samples, designed for LLMs to maximize the L2 norm of activations, achieve only modest length increases (e.g., 22.53 vs. 226.72 for BLIP-2 on MS-COCO) because their mechanism of increasing per-token computation does not transfer well to VLMs. NICGSlowDown, designed for smaller RNN/LSTM captioning models, minimizes logits of specific EOS and output tokens; it performs better than sponge samples (103.54 length for BLIP-2 on MS-COCO) but still falls well short of verbose images (226.72), partly because VLMs use stochastic sampling (nucleus sampling) that makes token-specific logit optimization unreliable. Verbose images win on all three metrics (length, latency, energy) across all four VLMs and both datasets, with the largest relative gains on BLIP-2 where they achieve ~22× the sequence length of original images. The gap is most pronounced on InstructBLIP, where sponge samples and NICGSlowDown perform nearly identically (~93 length) while verbose images reach 140.35, suggesting that instruction-tuned models are particularly resistant to the simpler baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the energy-latency attack line initiated by sponge examples (Shumailov et al., 2021), which maximizes activation L2 norms in LLMs, and NICGSlowDown (Chen et al., 2022c), which minimizes EOS and output token logits in smaller RNN/CNN captioning models. The key departure is that both prior methods target LLMs or small-scale architectures and cannot be directly transferred to large VLMs, which use advanced sampling policies (nucleus sampling) that make token-specific logit optimization infeasible. The paper introduces two new loss objectives beyond NICGSlowDown's approach—an uncertainty loss (KL divergence to uniform) at the token level and a nuclear-norm-based token diversity loss at the sequence level—along with a temporal weight adjustment algorithm to balance the three objectives during PGD optimization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic study of energy-latency attacks specifically targeting large VLMs, revealing that imperceptible perturbations can inflate inference cost by nearly 8×, a concrete availability threat for VLM service providers. The paper argues that the intuitive defense of capping generation length is impractical because users have diverse length requirements and providers are actively increasing token limits (e.g., GPT-4-turbo allows 8,192 tokens), making the vulnerability more pressing as models scale. Open questions include whether effective defenses exist that do not degrade legitimate long-form generation, how the attack scales to even larger or proprietary VLMs, and whether the dispersed-attention mechanism identified via Grad-CAM can be exploited for other attack surfaces.
