## Problem

Standard propensity scores handle a single train-to-test distribution shift, but real-world models face many successive gradual shifts (e.g., evolving user preferences, aging brain scans, mutating viruses). No existing method can selectively reweight past data based on how the distribution has evolved over time, rather than merely comparing two fixed snapshots. This paper asks: can we design a propensity score that is a function of time, enabling the model to identify which past data evolved in a similar fashion to the present?

## Input / Output

The method consumes a timestamped dataset D = {(x_i, t_i)} where each observation carries the time at which it was collected; for supervised tasks x_i includes a label y_i, and for RL tasks x_i is a (state, action) pair. It outputs per-sample weights ω(x, T, t) = dp_T/dp_t(x) that are specific to the current time T and the collection time t of each past sample, which are then multiplied into the standard training loss (classification loss or TD error). The weights are produced by a learned function g_θ(x,t) — a small 4-layer FC network for RL or a 5-layer CNN for image tasks — and are clipped at 1 to prevent extreme reweighting.

## Method

The core contribution is the time-varying propensity score ω(x, T, t) = dp_T/dp_t(x), the Radon-Nikodym derivative between the current distribution p_T and each past distribution p_t, which generalizes the standard two-sample propensity score by making the comparison time-indexed. The method models p_t(x) = p(x)·exp(g_θ(x,t)) (deviations from the marginal) and estimates g_θ by training a binary classifier on triplets (x_i, t', t) where the label indicates whether t' or t is the true collection time of x_i; this is done via logistic regression (Algorithm 2) with randomly sampled negative time pairs. The resulting weights are then plugged directly into the existing training objective — a weighted cumulative loss for supervised learning (Eq. 15) or a weighted Q-value update in SAC for RL (Eq. 16) — with no other architectural changes. A key property is that the method automatically detects shifts without any prior knowledge of when or where they occur, and it requires no assumption on the data-generating distribution beyond gradual evolution (bounded total variation between consecutive time steps).

## Results

The paper evaluates its time-varying propensity score across continuous supervised learning (Gaussian, CIFAR-10a/b/c, CLEAR) and reinforcement learning (ROBEL D'Claw, Half-Cheetah WindVel) benchmarks, with results presented primarily in figures rather than numerical tables. In supervised learning, the method consistently outperforms Everything, Recent, and Fine-tune baselines across all four image/synthetic benchmarks, with the advantage being most pronounced on CIFAR-10b and CLEAR where label shifts are more complex. In reinforcement learning, the method achieves higher average undiscounted return with better sample complexity and stability compared to SAC and SAC + Context on both ROBEL D'Claw and Half-Cheetah WindVel. The method also outperforms standard propensity scoring and Meta-Weight-Net, and matches Everything's performance when no distribution shift is present.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against standard propensity scoring (Agarwal et al., 2011), the time-varying propensity score clearly wins: Fig. 3 and Fig. 5 show that standard propensity largely ignores temporal shifts in data distribution, while the proposed method appropriately re-weights data based on how it evolved over time, yielding higher classification accuracy on CIFAR-10b and Gaussian benchmarks. Against Meta-Weight-Net (Shu et al., 2019), the proposed method achieves higher relative test accuracy on both CIFAR-10 and CLEAR (Fig. 4), demonstrating superior sample weighting under continuous label shift. In reinforcement learning, the method outperforms standard SAC (Haarnoja et al., 2018) on both ROBEL D'Claw and Half-Cheetah WindVel in terms of sample efficiency and return stability (Fig. 2, 10). It also surpasses the stronger SAC + Context baseline (Caccia et al., 2022; Fakoor et al., 2020c), which uses a context variable to infer task information, showing that the time-varying propensity score provides complementary and additional benefit beyond context-based task identification (Fig. 11). The method's key advantage over all baselines is its ability to automatically detect gradual distribution shifts without requiring prior knowledge of when or where they occur, and it degrades gracefully to match Everything's performance when no shift is present (Fig. 14).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the classical propensity score framework (Shimodaira, 2000; Agarwal et al., 2011), which reweights a single training distribution to match a fixed test distribution, into a time-varying setting where the data distribution evolves gradually over a continuous time index. The core idea is to replace the static Radon-Nikodym derivative between two fixed densities with a time-dependent weight ω(x, T, t) = dp_T/dp_t(x), estimated via a logistic classifier over (x, t) pairs. The paper positions itself against concept drift, online meta-learning, and non-stationary RL literatures, noting that none of these employ time-varying propensity weights, and provides a Markov-process semi-group formalization (via the infinitesimal generator) to justify the 'gradual evolution' assumption. The method is deliberately minimal: it inserts a single weighting term into existing training objectives (cross-entropy for supervised learning, TD-error for SAC) without altering architecture or optimization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The time-varying propensity score offers a lightweight, assumption-light plug-in for any pipeline that accumulates timestamped data under gradual drift, and its demonstrated generality across supervised image classification and off-policy RL (SAC) makes it practically attractive. A key open question is how the method behaves when the 'gradual' assumption is violated by abrupt regime changes, since the total-variation bound in Eq. (4) becomes uninformative. The paper also leaves open the question of how the estimator's quality degrades when the time index is sparse or irregularly sampled, and whether the exponential-family parameterization of g_θ(x,t) is sufficient for highly non-linear drift patterns. The comparison with standard propensity scoring (Fig. 3, 5) is the most informative result, showing that ignoring temporal structure in the weight leads to systematically poor data selection.
