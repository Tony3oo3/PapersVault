## Problem

Reinforcement learning in large action spaces suffers from the curse of dimensionality, and existing abstraction methods that group similar actions only analyze the performance loss from approximation while ignoring the sample and computational complexity costs. This paper reveals a counter-intuitive trade-off: finer grouping reduces approximation error but increases estimation error under limited samples or computation, making the grouping granularity a critical design choice that prior work has not addressed.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input a finite-horizon discounted MDP with a large discrete action space, a generative model that produces independent (s, h, r, s') quadruples for each state-group pair, and a feasible set of surjective grouping functions mapping actions to groups. It outputs a two-level policy (a higher-level policy selecting a group and a lower-level policy selecting an action within that group), the optimal grouping function from the feasible set, and a high-probability upper bound on the performance gap between the optimal policy and the grouped policy. The lower-level policy can be domain-specific or a uniform distribution over group members.

## Method

The core mechanism is a linear decomposition model that expresses each action's transition kernel and reward as a convex combination of a group-common component and an action-specific deviation, parameterized by deviation factors β_P and β_R. A model-based RL algorithm (Algorithm 1) samples from the grouped MDP, estimates the group-level transition and reward, and runs dynamic programming (value or policy iteration) to obtain a nearly-optimal grouped policy. The key theoretical contribution (Theorem 1) decomposes the performance loss into an approximation error (governed by β_P* and β_R*, which decrease with finer grouping) and an estimation error (governed by S|g|/K, which increases with finer grouping), establishing a minimax-optimal sample complexity bound. The paper then formulates a utility-maximization problem (P1) jointly over the grouping function, sample size, and iteration count, and proposes a practical approximation (P2) that estimates deviation factors from a random subset of actions per group, reducing computational complexity to be independent of the full action space size.

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 1) establishes that the performance loss decomposes into an approximation error of 2(γβ_P*/(1−γ)² + β_R*/(1−γ)) and an estimation error of 20γ√(S|g|log(8S|g|/(δ(1−γ)))/(K(1−γ)³) + 4ε_opt/(1−γ)), with the required sample complexity Õ(S|g|/((1−γ)³ε²)) being minimax optimal. Empirically, in a downlink transmission scenario with A=1000 actions and G=10 groups, grouping with limited samples (K'=10) yields significantly lower performance loss than the non-grouping setting, while with abundant samples (K'=500) the non-grouping setting performs better. The counter-intuitive finding is that the optimal number of groups depends on the sample budget: with K=10⁴, G=20 is preferred, whereas with K=10⁷, no grouping (G=A) is optimal. The approximation error bound is shown to be tight up to a constant factor of 2 via a constructed example.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against named algorithmic baselines in a standard benchmark sense; instead, it compares grouping strategies against the non-grouping (full action space) setting and against prior theoretical bounds. Against the non-grouping setting, grouping with G=10 groups dramatically reduces computational complexity (from A=1000 to G=10 state-action pairs to estimate) while maintaining comparable or better performance when samples are limited (K'=10), as shown in Figure 1. Against Wang et al. (2021, NeurIPS), the paper improves the approximation error bound for transition kernel differences by a factor of 22/γ and additionally characterizes the reward model deviation, which was not considered in the prior work. Against Agarwal et al. (2020b, COLT), the leave-one-out analysis is extended from tabular MDPs to grouped MDPs, achieving a minimax-optimal estimation error bound. The key practical comparison is between different grouping granularities: coarser grouping (fewer groups) is preferred under sample/computation constraints, while finer grouping (more groups, up to no grouping) is preferred when resources are abundant, as demonstrated in Figure 2.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the abstraction selection framework of Jiang et al. (2015) and the model-based RL analysis of Agarwal et al. (2020b) and Wang et al. (2021) by introducing a linear decomposition model to quantify intra-group action similarity and, crucially, by jointly accounting for both approximation error (from grouping) and estimation error (from finite samples and limited computation). Prior abstraction work (Abel et al., 2020; Jiang et al., 2015) characterized performance loss from approximate abstractions but explicitly ignored the sample and computational costs of estimating the abstracted model. The leave-one-out analysis technique from Agarwal et al. (2020b) is adapted here to the grouped MDP setting, and the paper improves upon Wang et al. (2021) by incorporating reward deviation and tightening the transition kernel approximation bound by a factor of 22/γ.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The counter-intuitive finding that finer grouping can worsen overall performance under limited resources provides a principled, new degree of freedom (grouping granularity) for practitioners to tune alongside sample size and iteration count. The optimization framework (P1/P2) and its computationally efficient approximation offer a practical recipe for selecting near-optimal groupings without exhaustive search over the action space. Limitations include the assumption of deterministic rewards, the requirement for a generative model, and the need for a pre-specified feasible grouping set D; extending to stochastic rewards, learning D from data, and applying the framework to continuous or high-dimensional action spaces remain open.
